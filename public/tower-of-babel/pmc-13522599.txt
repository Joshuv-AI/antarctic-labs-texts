Hyperdimensional in-memory computing with analogue memristive crossbar arrays
Authors: Yi Huang, Alireza Jaberi Rad, Daniel Belkin, Ning Ge, J. Joshua Yang, Miao Hu, Qiangfei Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deploying large-scale artificial intelligence models for language processing on edge devices is limited by constraints in computational capacity and energy efficiency. To address this challenge, we present a hardware-algorithm co-design framework that leverages analog in-memory computing and hyperdimensional computing for efficient language identification at the edge. By exploiting the inherent randomness and multistate properties of analog memristors, we implement a vector matrix multiplication-based language feature encoding with much reduced hardware complexity. Language classification is then realized with a single-layer perceptron on analog memristive crossbar arrays, eliminating inter-layer activation functions and backward propagation during training that are required in deep neural networks. Experimental implementation on a multicore memristive system-on-a-chip demonstrates a 90% reduction in hardware resources while achieving 95.24% language identification accuracy, the highest reported among hyperdimensional computing implementations on emerging hardware platforms. This work provides a scalable, energy-efficient approach for high-accuracy language processing on edge devices.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms has accelerated their integration into various aspects of daily life. Meanwhile, the rise of the Internet of Things (IoT) has connected billions of mobile and edge devices to the Internet, thereby enabling them to leverage AI models for complex tasks. This interconnected ecosystem creates opportunities to harness the full potential of edge devices1,2, where written language serves as a major interface for a wide range of applications3. However, transferring data between cloud and edge devices poses challenges such as high energy consumption and transmission latency4, hindering the realization of efficient edge AI. A promising solution lies in the on-device implementation of lightweight AI models that allow real-time data processing at the edge5–7, which demands innovations in both algorithm design and hardware implementation8.

Hyperdimensional computing (HDC) has emerged as a brain-inspired algorithm particularly well-suited for edge AI applications where computing resources are constrained9. HDC encodes data features into hyperdimensional vectors, known as hyper vectors, and performs classification through computations in hyperdimensional spaces, as shown in the right panel of Fig. 1a. By relying primarily on bit-wise operations and vector similarity comparisons between hyper vectors, HDC enables parallel and distributed processing thereby rendering it highly efficient for digital hardware implementation. To deploy HDC-based algorithms on edge devices, in-memory computing (IMC) hardware based on memristor devices offers an energy-efficient platform10–12. As HDC algorithms continue to evolve in both encoding16,17 and classification18–20 methods, and memristive hardware progresses from device-level innovations21 and peripheral circuit designs22 to IMC integrated chips10–12 and hardware-algorithm co-designs23–25, there has been growing exploration into enabling edge AI by leveraging the advantages of HDC algorithms and IMC hardware. At the device level, resistive switching devices have been integrated into three-dimensional structures to increase device density thus enabling the storage and computation of hyper vectors26,27. Phase-change memory (PCM)28 and ferroelectric field-effect transistors (FeFETs)29 have also been integrated with customized peripheral circuits for encoding and classification, further enhancing the energy efficiency of HDC systems. Furthermore, hardware-algorithm co-designs have been proposed to harness the growing computing capacity of memristive hardware for HDC with the goal of balancing energy efficiency and classification accuracy30,31. However, existing approaches are primarily limited to binary memristor devices and conventional HDC algorithms, which do not fully exploit the multistate capability of analog memristor devices28,32–34. With the development of analog IMC (AIMC) based on multistate memristors, several works have begun to exploit this property to enhance the efficiency of HDC implementations35–37. However, these HDC systems have so far been validated only through simulations or partially through hardware prototypes that rely on off-chip peripherals such as a host personal computer (PC) or field-programmable gate array (FPGA). This dependency on external components limits their practicality and hinders full system-level deployment for edge AI applications28,36–38. Moreover, most existing implementations typically adopt convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for feature extraction while only using HDC implemented on IMC hardware as a classifier35–37. The inclusion of deep neural network architectures introduces additional complexity and hardware overhead, making it challenging to deploy such systems efficiently on edge devices.

Here, we present a hardware-algorithm co-design that leverages the intrinsic characteristics of memristor devices to optimize both the encoding and classification stages of HDC, and we implement the entire system on a memristive system-on-a-chip (SoC) for energy-efficient and high-accuracy language identification. We propose a VMM-based hyper vector mapping scheme that harnesses the inherent randomness and multistate characteristics of analog memristors for language feature encoding. This approach eliminates the hardware required for generating and storing random hyper vectors and reduces hyper vector dimensionality by nearly 90%. We further exploit the parallel analog vector-matrix multiplications (VMMs) enabled by memristive crossbar arrays to implement a single-layer perceptron for classification, removing the inter-layer activation function and backward propagation of deep neural network architectures. The co-design achieves 98.87% accuracy in identifying 21 European languages in simulation, the highest reported among existing HDC designs16,17,28,31. The full hardware implementation on the SoC achieves 95.24% accuracy, higher than prior memristive HDC implementation28. These substantial gains in hardware efficiency and identification performance underscore the potential of the co-design for energy-efficient edge AI applications.

Results

Hardware-algorithm co-design for language identification on edge devices

Figure 1b illustrates the hardware-algorithm co-design leveraging the AIMC hardware and HDC for energy-efficient language identification on edge devices. The system includes two main modules for language identification, a VMM-based encoding stage that projects language features into a hyperdimensional space for robust and efficient processing, and a single-step classification stage that categorizes input data into different classes based on the encoded hyper vectors.

The encoding in conventional HDC involves bit-wise vector operations such as multiplication, addition, and permutation, which are applied between binary feature vectors representing the data features and a set of basis hyper vectors16,17,28. These basis hyper vectors are (pseudo) random vectors with independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) components. To leverage the intrinsic characteristics of analog memristors for language feature encoding, we propose the VMM-based hyper vector mapping, which represents the feature vectors as voltages applied to the rows of memristive crossbar arrays and projects them into hyper vectors through analog VMM. In this process, feature vectors as input voltages are multiplied by a random matrix represented by the conductance values of memristor devices by leveraging their inherent randomness. The mapped hyper vectors are determined by analog-to-digital converter (ADC) results converted from resulting currents that flow from the columns of the crossbar array. We also propose a hardware-aware processing method (see Methods) for the mapped hyper vectors to mitigate the noise in the encoded hyper vectors introduced by hardware non-idealities.

After feature encoding, classification in conventional HDC relies on comparing the similarity between encoded hyper vectors from testing samples and those from training samples stored in associative memory16,17,28. By using the VMM-based hyper vector mapping, we manage to use a memristive single-layer perceptron trained with encoded hyper vectors to perform classification. We map encoded hyper vectors to voltages applied to the rows of a memristive crossbar array and the trained weights of the single-layer perceptron to the conductance values of the memristor devices within the array. We implement the single-layer perceptron through analog VMM within the crossbar array, and the resulting output currents correspond to the classification results. By leveraging the single-step analog VMM enabled by memristive crossbar arrays, the single-layer perceptron improves classification accuracy while using the same hardware resources required for associative memory. Meanwhile, it eliminates the need for inter-layer activation functions and backward propagations required for deep neural network architectures.

VMM-based hyper vector mapping

As illustrated in Fig. 2a, encoding of conventional HDC implemented in digital hardware relies on (pseudo) random number generators to produce basis hyper vectors, which are stored in memory, and combinational logic circuits to perform bit-wise operations between basis hyper vectors and feature vectors. During both training and inference, the basis hyper vectors are transferred from memory to processing units for hyper vector mapping across all data samples. To reduce hardware resources required for (pseudo) random number generation and minimize data movement between memory and processing units, we leverage the inherent randomness and multistate characteristics of analog memristors, combined with the parallel VMM capabilities of memristive crossbar arrays, for efficient hyper vector mapping. In the VMM-based hyper vector mapping shown in Fig. 2b, we represent feature vectors as voltages applied to the rows of a memristive crossbar array. By applying Reset-Set cycles to the memristors in a crossbar array, we generate random conductance that naturally forms a random matrix (see Supplementary Note 1). The analog VMMs project the feature vectors from the feature space into a hyperdimensional space using the random matrix. The output currents from the columns of the crossbar array are converted to voltages through trans-impedance amplifiers and then digitized by ADCs. In our VMM-based hyper vector mapping, we use the VMM output current to determine whether a binary hyper vector element is “1” or “−1”. Therefore, only a single bit of the ADC output is required to determine this binary state. Specifically, the least significant bit of the ADC output is used to determine whether each hyper vector element is “1” or “−1”. The accuracy achieved (confusion matrix shown in Fig. 2d) with a lower hyper vector dimension demonstrates the effectiveness of the VMM-based hyper vector mapping for language feature encoding.

Memristive single-layer perceptron for classification

For classification based on encoded hyper vectors during inference, cosine similarity is commonly used to compare the similarity between the encoded hyper vectors and those stored in associative memory. The final classification result is then determined by a winner-take-all module. From computing perspective, this process closely resembles the operations of a single-layer perceptron since both involve VMMs between the input vectors and a trained weight matrix, followed by a winner-take-all module. This similarity in operations implies comparable hardware resources when implemented on memristive crossbar arrays where analog VMM can be performed in a single step, as shown in Fig. 3a. Moreover, using neural networks for classification has been shown to improve classification accuracy in HDC18,19,38. Therefore, we employ a single-layer perceptron for classification in our HDC-based system. Hyper vectors generated from the VMM-based mapping for each data sample are accumulated into a single encoded hyper vector which serves as an input for the single-layer perceptron. In the hardware implementation of the memristive single-layer perceptron, these encoded hyper vectors are converted into analog voltages and applied to the rows of memristive crossbar arrays. The weight matrix of the single-layer perceptron, pre-trained offline (see Methods), is mapped to the conductance values of the memristors within the crossbar array. To evaluate the performance of the single-layer perceptron with the VMM-based hyper vector mapping, we compare its classification accuracy against associative memory with cosine similarity for the language identification task through simulations. As illustrated in Fig. 3b, the single-layer perceptron consistently outperforms associative memory across all tested hyper vector dimensions (d). Note that even at 1024 hyper vector dimensions, the single-layer perceptron achieves a classification accuracy of 98.87%, which exceeds the previously reported 98.23% accuracy in HDC39. These results demonstrate that employing the single-layer perceptron for classification, when combined with VMM-based hyper vector mapping, achieves higher accuracy than associative memory while maintaining similar hardware resource requirements. Moreover, the use of a single-layer perceptron for classification eliminates both the inter-layer activation function and backward propagation during training, the bottlenecks for edge deployment in prior works, which integrate HDC with multilayer CNNs or multilayer perceptron (MLP) for classification18,19,38.

Hardware implementation for language identification

Building on the promising simulation results, we validate the proposed hardware-algorithm co-design framework through experimental implementations of the proposed VMM-based hyper vector mapping and memristive single-layer perceptron within a memristive SoC25 (TetraMem MX100, see “Methods”) for language identification. The SoC employs a multicore architecture that integrates ten memristive computing cores, a CPU and on-chip peripheral circuits (See “Methods”). Each memristive computing core is equipped with a 248 × 256 one-transistor-one-memristor (1T1R) crossbar array thus enabling distributed hyper vector mapping and single-layer perceptron implementation across multiple computing cores. Balancing the limited on-chip hardware resources and accuracies observed from simulations, we select a hyper vector dimension of 1024 for hardware implementation of the proposed co-design in the language identification task, which requires memristive crossbar arrays with 78 × 1024 and 1024 × 42 dimension for VMM-based hyper vector mapping and the single-layer perceptron, respectively.

Figure 4a illustrates the workflow of encoding and classification stages for the 21 European language identification while Fig. 4b shows the hardware implementation distributed across nine memristive computing cores and the on-chip CPU. Since the dimensions of the memristive crossbar arrays on chip are 248 × 256, whereas the encoding requires a matrix of dimensions 78 × 1024, the VMM-based hyper vector mapping distributes the computation across four memristive computing cores, each employing a 78 × 256 subarray as a random projection matrix to generate hyper vectors. The hyper vectors from the columns of memristive crossbar arrays across these four computing cores are transferred to the on-chip memory and CPU via a data interconnect. In the CPU, these hyper vectors are processed and accumulated to form the encoded hyper vectors for the single-layer perceptron. During simulations, we use the least significant bit of ADC outputs to determine the encoded hyper vector elements. However, non-idealities in the memristors and on-chip peripheral components, such as trans-impedance amplifiers and ADCs, introduce noise to the ADC outputs corresponding to the VMM results. This noise leads to accuracy degradation when transitioning from simulation to hardware implementation (see Supplementary Note 2). To address this challenge, we develop a hardware-aware processing approach (see “Methods”) to mitigate the impact of hardware non-idealities. Following hardware-aware processing and accumulation, we distribute the encoded hyper vectors to five additional memristive computing cores where the 1024 × 21 weights of the single-layer perceptron are mapped and programmed into the conductance of the memristive crossbar arrays for classification. We achieve an average language identification accuracy of 95.24% across all languages with the VMM-based hyper vector mapping, hardware-aware processing and memristive single-layer perceptron implemented within the memristive SoC, which is 2.43% higher than the accuracy achieved using conventional HDC on a PCM-based IMC chip28. Figure 4c shows the identification accuracy for each language obtained from the fully hardware-based implementation. Figure 4d compares results from pure software simulations, hardware implementations and mixed setups to further understand the impact of hardware non-idealities on classification accuracy. In the VMM-based hyper vector mapping, cycle-to-cycle variation in the VMM results impact encoding performance, as demonstrated by the identification accuracy degradation from 98.87% to 95.76% and from 98.45% to 95.24% when switching from software-based to hardware-based hyper vector mapping while keeping the single-layer perceptron unchanged, as shown in Fig. 4d. In the single-layer perceptron for classification, device-to-device variation causes the trained weight matrix to deviate from its ideal values, which may degrade system performance. Cycle-to-cycle variation, on the other hand, has a very limited impact on classification owing to the winner-take-all mechanism used to determine classification results. Experimental results incorporating both variation types shown in Fig. 4d demonstrate that the single-layer perceptron is highly robust to device variations as the identification accuracy decreased only marginally, from 98.87% to 98.45% and from 95.76% to 95.24%, when switching from a software-based to a hardware-based single-layer perceptron while keeping the encoding unchanged. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the single-layer perceptron is robust against noise introduced by hardware non-idealities, whereas the VMM-based hyper vector mapping process is more sensitive to such imperfections. Notably, even when the combined impact of both the VMM-based hyper vector mapping and the single-layer perceptron is considered, the overall accuracy degradation from 98.87% to 95.24% remains modest.

Discussion

We propose a hardware-algorithm co-design framework that leverages the intrinsic characteristics of analog memristors and crossbar arrays for encoding and classification of HDC. We experimentally evaluate the co-design framework by implementing the proposed VMM-based hyper vector mapping and single-step single-layer perceptron across multiple computing cores within a memristive SoC. Compared to conventional HDC designs and implementations using IMC, our approach differs in both algorithm design and hardware implementation. Specifically, our method leverages intrinsic randomness and multistate characteristics of memristors and analog VMMs while prior implementations utilize binary vectors and bit-wise operations. Additionally, previous efforts lack system-level implementations on experimental IMC hardware. For a fair comparison, we focus on accuracy when evaluating algorithm design and compare hyper vector dimensions, which dominate hardware resource requirements, when assessing energy efficiency. The accuracy achieved with only 1024-dimensional hyper vectors (98.87%) exceeds that of existing HDC designs16,17,28,31 which typically use a 10,000-dimensional hyper vector on the same dataset for language identification (see Supplementary Note 3). For comparisons involving IMC hardware implementations, our experimental results demonstrate higher accuracy (95.24%) with the same hyper vector dimensions than previous IMC works26–28 (see Supplementary Note 4) where overall accuracies were derived from simulations. To compare system-level metrics with system-level hardware implementations, we benchmark our implementation against system-level accelerator40,41 and application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs)42–44 implementations of HDC based on complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) devices (Supplementary Note 5). Our hardware implementation achieves the highest energy efficiency (2.58 TOPS/W) among the existing works. The latency (302.5 µs) is lower than that of accelerators built on conventional processors and is slightly higher than that of ASICs specifically designed for HDC acceleration.

The system performance can be further enhanced in several aspects. The hyper vector mapping is currently distributed across multiple computing cores where the outputs are concatenated after VMMs. In future development, this process might be implemented in a single array of a larger size. Since feature vectors are generally sparse, hyper vector mapping remains robust against performance degradation in VMM results as the size of the arrays increases16. In addition, the on-chip 8-bit ADCs used to determine hyper vector elements and classification results are the speed and energy bottlenecks of the memristive SoC. Future designs could adopt low-precision ADCs, since only the third bit to the least significant bit of the ADC outputs is required to determine hyper vector elements (see Methods and Supplementary Note 2) and a simple current winner-take-all circuit is sufficient for obtaining classification results. Employing lower-precision ADCs significantly enhance energy efficiency and reduce computing latency45–47. Furthermore, the training of single-layer perceptron does not require backward propagation compared to multilayer neural networks used in previous HDC design18. Future memristive SoCs could integrate edge learning capabilities48 to enable the on-chip training of single-layer perceptron, which would greatly enhance the energy efficiency of single-layer perceptron training.

The proposed approach is also constrained by its relatively simple architecture, which comprises only one VMM-based encoding stage and a single-layer perceptron, and is therefore well suited to high-dimensional sequential features with inter-feature correlations. The proposed approach also achieves a classification accuracy of 98.12% with a hyper vector dimension of 1024 on the tumor classification task using the Gene Expression Cancer RNA-Seq dataset49 (Supplementary Note 6), which exhibits feature characteristics analogous to those of the language identification task. However, extending this architecture to tasks involving features with spatial correlations, such as image classification, requires additional feature extraction layers, as widely adopted in existing HDC-based classifiers19,36,37,50. Such additional layers introduce extra energy consumption and latency, which conflicts with our design objective of efficient language processing on edge devices. Future work on the encoding method is therefore needed to expand the application of the proposed co-design to a wide range of edge AI tasks51–53.

In summary, we propose a hardware-algorithm co-design framework that harnesses analog memristive crossbar arrays and the HDC-based algorithm, and experimentally demonstrate it on a memristive SoC for language identification. By leveraging the intrinsic randomness and multistate characteristics of analog memristors and parallel analog VMMs on crossbar arrays, we distribute the simplified system architecture, a VMM-based language feature encoding and the memristive single-layer perceptron, across multiple computing cores within the memristive SoC. Simulations of the co-design framework achieve 98.87% accuracy in language identification, outperforming existing HDC designs while demonstrating a nearly 90% reduction in hyper vector dimensionality. Experimental results further confirm a classification accuracy of 95.24%, surpassing state-of-the-art memristive hardware implementation while requiring 90% fewer hardware resources. These findings validate that our co-design achieves higher accuracy with energy-efficient hardware, paving the way for future edge AI applications.

Methods

Hardware-aware processing

The non-idealities of memristor devices in crossbar arrays, when combined with imperfections in peripheral circuits, introduce noise to the VMM results used for hyper vector mapping (see Supplementary Note 2). These noise sources manifest as fluctuations in the output currents of crossbar array columns and directly affect the ADC results. This effect is particularly pronounced in the least significant bit of the ADC output, which determines the hyper vector elements. In our hardware-based encoding and software-based classification experiments, the inversion of the least significant bit in ADC results led to a significant accuracy degradation from 96.19% to 74.17% when transitioning from simulation to hardware implementation of VMM-based encoding. Through analysis of the hardware imperfections in VMM results, we observe that small deviations from ideal outputs to experimental outputs result in inversions of either the least significant bit or the second least significant bit of the ADC output. Note that the higher-order bits of the ADC results remain unaffected by these minor shifts. To mitigate this issue, we redefine the hyper vector elements using the third least significant bit of the ADC results. This adjustment effectively reduces the impact of noise and results in 95.76% accuracy in the hardware implementation of encoding, which underscores the importance of hardware-aware processing in compensating for hardware imperfections.

Language identification datasets and feature vectors

The training text samples are sourced from the Wortschatz Corpora54 which provides a large number of sentences across 21 European languages. The testing text samples are sourced from another independent dataset: the Europarl Parallel Corpus55. Both datasets are pre-processed for 21 language identification tasks17 with approximately 10,000 sentences per language used for training and 1000 sentences per language for testing. Identification accuracy is defined as the percentage of sentences correctly classified.

To generate feature vectors from the text samples, each letter is first mapped to a binary vector where “1” corresponds to high voltage and “0” to low voltage when applied to memristive crossbar arrays for hyper vector mapping. Each character feature is represented as a 26-dimensional vector that corresponds to the 26 possible alphabet characters and a space character (all zeros). Every three consecutive letters are combined to form “trigram” vectors since trigrams have been demonstrated to balance classification accuracy with hardware resource efficiency17. The resulting 78-dimensional trigram vectors serve as the feature representations for texts across different languages.

Single-layer perceptron training and weight mapping

The hyper vectors mapped from trigram feature vectors in a sentence are accumulated to form a single encoded hyper vector, which serves as the input sample for the single-layer perceptron. Due to the 8-bit precision limitations of the on-chip DACs and ADCs, the elements of the encoded hyper vectors are quantized to approximately 8 bits, corresponding to approximately 255 distinct voltage states. This approach differs from the typical normalization of binary vectors for classification found in conventional HDC designs16,17,28. The 8-bit encoded hyper vectors from 10,000 sentences per language are used for the offline training of the single-layer perceptron. The training of the single-layer perceptron employs the stochastic gradient descent algorithm with a cross-entropy loss function. The trained weights are quantized to 8 bits, corresponding to 255 conductance states of memristor devices within the crossbar arrays, to accommodate the on-chip ADCs. The mapped conductance weight matrix is then programmed into the memristive crossbar arrays across multiple computing cores through a write-verify programming process (as detailed in Supplementary Note 7). Inference of the single-layer perceptron is fully implemented on-chip within the memristive SoC.

Memristive SoC and evaluation kit

The hardware platform used to evaluate our hardware-algorithm co-design is the TetraMem MX100 SoC evaluation kit25. This kit includes the MX100 SoC and a software development kit (SDK, TetraMem Instinct 1.4). The memristive SoC is fabricated using the 65-nm CMOS process and integrates ten memristive computing cores, each comprising a 248 × 256 1T1R crossbar array of Ta/HfO₂/Pt memristors21,25,56,57, for analog VMMs. The VMM operations for hyper vector mapping and single-layer perceptron are distributed across multiple computing cores. The digital peripherals of the SoC include a RISC-V CPU for general-purpose processing that is utilized for hardware-aware processing, memory for instruction and data storage to store intermediate hyper vectors, and a direct memory access (DMA) engine that is used to transfer hyper vectors between computing cores. The TetraMem Instinct 1.4 SDK consists of three major components: the machine learning (ML) model quantizer, the ML compiler and the deployment tool. We employ the ML model quantizer to quantize the offline-trained single-layer perceptron model and use the ML compiler and deployment tool to enable on-chip inference of the single-layer perceptron model for classification.
