A Case of Temporal Lobe Hemorrhagic Infarct Secondary to Tentorial Sinovenous Thrombosis: An Unusual and Underrecognized Site of Venous Thrombosis
Authors: Sindhu V Nambiar, GG Sharath Kumar, Vishal Sharma, Richard Berly, Sai Kanth Deepalam, Thomas Mathew
Published: 2025

Tentorial sinovenous thrombosis (TSVT) is rarely considered in usual neurology practice. We found only one case report of tentorial venous thrombosis in the literature, suggesting its rarity or lack of awareness leading to underreporting. Here, we report a case of TSVT to raise awareness of this entity as a cause of hemorrhagic infarcts.

A 43-year-old woman with no known comorbidities presented to the emergency with an acute, severe holocranial headache of 4 hours duration. There was no history of head trauma or bleeding diasthesis. On admission, she was conscious and oriented, with a pulse of 80 beats per minute and a blood pressure of 150/90 mmHg. Neurological examination revealed bilateral papilledema with no lateralizing signs.

A noncontrast computed tomography (CT) scan of the brain done 4 h after the onset of symptoms showed a left temporal hemorrhage [Figure 1a]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain done immediately after the CT scan showed hemorrhage in the left temporal lobe and along the left tentorium [Figure 1b]. Following this, a 3 Dimension (3D) time-of-flight (TOF) MR venography (MRV) performed 5 h after the brain MRI showed normal-appearing major dural venous sinuses, including the vein of Labbé [Figure 1c]. Axial susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) [Figure 1d] showed curvilinear hypointensities suggesting thrombosed tentorial veins transitioning into the tentorial sinus along the medial edge of the tentorium, and coronal SWI [Figure 1e] showed two discrete hypointensities suggesting a “cord sign” in the sinovenous system. Additionally, the coronal phase images [Figure 1f] showed a hypointense signal of paramagnetic dipole (deoxyhemoglobin) suggesting thrombosis.

(b) Axial T2-FLAIR images showing temporal lobe hemorrhage (arrowheads) and hyperintensity along the tentorium, which could suggest subdural hemorrhage. (c) 3D MR venogram shows normal venous sinuses, including the vein of Labbé (curved arrows). (d and e) Axial SWI showing curvilinear hypointensities (solid arrowheads) suggesting thrombosed tentorial veins transitioning into the tentorial sinus along the medial edge of the tentorium, linear blooming (long arrow) suggesting tentorial hemorrhage, and coronal SWI showing two discrete hypointensities (solid arrowheads) suggesting a “cord sign” in the sinovenous system. (f) Coronal phase images of a right-handed MRI system showing a hypointense signal suggestive of a paramagnetic dipole (deoxyhemoglobin) of thrombus. Laboratory workup revealed severe iron deficiency anemia (hemoglobin 7.0 g/dl, iron level 17 µg/dl, ferritin 6 ng/dl, and transferrin saturation 2.8%] with normal vitamin B12 and homocysteine levels. Stool occult blood and parasites were negative. Ultrasound of the pelvis was normal. The workup for antiphospholipid antibodies was negative. The patient was managed with anticoagulation, a short course of antiepileptics, antiedema measures, and parenteral iron supplementation (Encicarb [ferric carboxymaltose] 500 mg). She was stable at discharge and was continued on oral anticoagulation (dabigatran 150 mg bd) and iron supplements [Tab. Orofer XT (ferrous ascorbate equivalent to elemental iron 100 mg) once daily]. At a 3-month follow-up, she had full neurological recovery with a hemoglobin of 10 g/dl. Repeat imaging at 8 months of follow-up did not identify any other cause of temporal lobe hemorrhage, such as vascular malformations or an underlying mass lesion.

TSVT are rarely recognized and may be overlooked as a cause of cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT). A literature search identified only one case report by Nadkarni et al.,[1] describing a 26-year-old woman with sickle cell disease who presented with a 2-day history of headache and vomiting. In our case, the patient had severe iron deficiency anemia, a known risk factor for CVT.[2] The likely cause of iron deficiency anemia in our patient was nutritional.

The tentorial sinovenous system, located in the posterior cranial fossa, is essential for draining the occipital and temporal lobes as well as the upper cerebellum. They consist of two primary components: the lateral tentorial sinus, which facilitates venous drainage into the transverse-sigmoid junction, and the medial tentorial sinus, which connects directly to the straight sinus. The medial tentorial system primarily drains the superior cerebellar lobes and occipital lobes and results in occipital lobe hemorrhage. The temporal hemorrhage in our case, however, suggests involvement of the lateral tentorial venous system. In our case, delineation of the lateral tentorial veins was challenging due to the concomitant tentorial hemorrhage. We were able to identify only a few thrombosed tentorial veins in the region of the medial tentorial system. Nevertheless, there are reports indicating that medial tentorial veins may also contribute to temporal lobe drainage through accessory or collateral pathways in otherwise normal individuals.[3] These venous pathways may play a role in the venous drainage of the temporal lobes.[4] The temporal hemorrhage in our case may be due to the involvement of these accessory or collateral pathways. Thrombosis in the tentorial venous sinuses disrupts normal venous outflow, leading to venous congestion, ischemia, and hemorrhagic infarction in the temporal or occipital lobes. Unlike thrombosis in larger venous sinuses, TSVT may remain clinically silent until complications such as venous infarcts or hemorrhage arise. As isolated TSVT may present atypically and spare the major venous sinuses, it can be missed on standard 3D TOF MRV.[5]

SWI plays a crucial role in detecting TSVT, as it may not be visible on conventional MRV. A contrast-enhanced MRV may be more sensitive than 3D TOF MRV for delineating thrombosed tentorial veins. However, this was not performed in our case, as contrast MRV was not part of our routine MRV protocol. When evaluating temporal hemorrhagic infarcts, clinicians should consider TSVT in the differentials when the dural venous sinuses and the vein of Labbé appear normal. This entity should be differentiated from posterior cerebral artery territory infarcts with hemorrhagic transformation, tentorial subdural bleeds, and pachymeningitis.[6]
