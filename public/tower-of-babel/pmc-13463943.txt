Feasibility and preliminary safety of urologic telesurgery using Chinese surgical systems: a single-center exploratory case series
Authors: Tao Xu, Zixing Wang, Ao Qi, Meiling Guo, Jianwei Wang, Pengyu Guo, Ziqi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTelesurgery offers a solution to the uneven distribution of surgical resources by enabling telesurgery through surgical robotic systems. This study aimed to describe the technical feasibility and preliminary short-term safety of urologic telesurgery using Chinese surgical systems (CSS).MethodsThis single-center exploratory descriptive case series evaluated urologic telesurgery using CSS between January 2023 and October 2025. A total of 6 consecutive eligible telesurgeries were included: 3 robot-assisted radical prostatectomies (RARP) and 1 robot-assisted partial nephrectomy (RAPN) were performed with the Edge MP1000 (MP1000) system, and 1 RAPN and 1 robot-assisted radical cystectomy (RARC) were performed with the KangDuo SR-2000 (KD-SR-2000) system. Six locally performed RARP cases using the da Vinci Xi (DV-Xi) system during the same study period were retrospectively collected as a limited contextual reference for the RARP subgroup. The primary outcome was technical success, defined as completion without conversion. Secondary descriptive outcomes included perioperative safety events, 30-day Clavien-Dindo complications (CDC), network performance, and, in the RARP subgroup, positive surgical margin (PSM) rate, estimated blood loss (EBL), operative time, suture-per-stitch time, and 4-week urinary continence recovery after catheter removal.ResultsAll six telesurgeries were completed without intraoperative conversion or major complications. In the RARP subgroup, remote MP1000 procedures were summarized alongside locally performed DV-Xi procedures as a limited contextual reference. PSM rate and early urinary continence recovery showed no obvious unfavorable signal in this small contextual cohort, whereas operative time and suture-per-stitch time were longer, and EBL was numerically higher, in remote procedures. Mean Network round-trip latency for telesurgery ranged from 6.13 ± 0.71 ms (Harbin-Harbin, 2 km) to 54.12 ± 0.58 ms (Harbin-Hangzhou, 2200 km), with no frame loss.ConclusionIn this small selected case series, urologic telesurgery using CSS was technically feasible and preliminary safety under stable telecommunication conditions.

Introduction

In 2001, the first human telesurgery was conducted when a surgeon in the United States completed a laparoscopic cholecystectomy remotely on a patient located in France using the ZEUS robotic system [1]. This pioneering achievement demonstrated the feasibility of telesurgery, yet widespread implementation was hindered by the absence of high-speed and reliable network infrastructure.

In recent years, significant advances in medical technology have occurred alongside widening gaps in healthcare access. Global crises, such as the pandemic and ongoing regional conflicts, have emphasized the growing need for remote surgical capabilities [2–6]. In areas with limited healthcare infrastructure, patients often experience delays in accessing specialized surgical services, resulting in disease progression and deferred treatment.

Urology has been a leading specialty in robotic-assisted surgery, and nearly all major urologic surgeries are now performed with robotic assistance [7]. Meanwhile, the advent of 5G networks has enabled ultra-fast, stable, and reliable data transmission, making telesurgery increasingly feasible [7–10]. This technology has the potential to impact hundreds of millions of patients worldwide, with an estimated global economic effect of USD 3.5 trillion and 22 million new jobs by 2035 [8]. CSS offer significant cost advantages, and China’s comprehensive development of 5G technology and related infrastructure provides a fertile foundation for the advancement of telesurgery, with the potential to benefit patients worldwide [11, 12].

Although research on telesurgery in urology is expanding, robust real-world clinical evidence remains limited [13–15]. Therefore, we report a single-center exploratory case series of urologic telesurgery using two CSS platforms across RARP, RAPN, and RARC. The aim was to describe technical feasibility, short-term perioperative outcomes, network performance, and safety safeguards. The locally performed DV-Xi RARP cohort was used only as a limited contextual reference for the RARP subgroup and was not intended to support formal comparative conclusions.

Methods

Patients

This was a single-center, exploratory descriptive case series evaluating the technical feasibility and preliminary short-term safety of telesurgery using two CSS platforms (Edge MP1000 and KangDuo-SR-2000). From January 2023 to October 2025, all consecutive eligible telesurgical cases performed at our center were included. The telesurgery cohort comprised 3 RARP and 1 RAPN using the MP1000 system, as well as 1 RAPN and 1 RARC using the KD-SR-2000 system. To provide limited perioperative context for the RARP subgroup, six patients who underwent conventional locally performed RARP using the DV-Xi system during the same study period were retrospectively selected. Local RARP cases were first required to meet the same predefined RARP eligibility criteria as the telesurgery cohort and were then selected based on temporal proximity, with preference given to cases performed closest in time to the telesurgical procedures. Given the limited number of RAPN and RARC telesurgery cases, these procedures were reported descriptively only.

The primary outcome for all telesurgeries was technical success, defined as completion of the planned operation without conversion to open or conventional laparoscopic surgery. Secondary descriptive outcomes included perioperative safety events, 30-day CDC, network performance, and selected RARP outcomes, including PSM rate, EBL, operative time, suture-per-stitch time, and urinary continence recovery at 4 weeks after catheter removal.

The study enrolled patients undergoing one of three urologic procedures, based on predefined clinical suitability and safety criteria for early-stage telesurgical implementation. For radical prostatectomy, candidates were required to have histologically confirmed prostate adenocarcinoma, clinical stage T1-2N0M0, and be between 18 and 75 years of age. For partial nephrectomy, eligible patients presented with T1-T2a renal tumors, a RENAL score of 9 or less, and were aged 80 years or younger. For radical cystectomy, inclusion required pathologically confirmed bladder urothelial carcinoma without radiological evidence of unresectable local extension, nodal involvement, or distant metastasis, an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification of I or II, and an age range of 18 to 75 years. Patients were excluded if they had contraindications to general anesthesia, severe cardiopulmonary comorbidity, anticipated severe intra-abdominal adhesions, locally unresectable disease, distant metastasis, or inability to comply with follow-up procedures. Additional telesurgery-specific requirements included successful preoperative system and network testing, availability of an experienced bedside surgical team, readiness for immediate local takeover or conversion if needed, and written informed consent specifically acknowledging the telesurgical nature of the procedure (Fig. 1).

Telesurgery system

The telesurgery system was composed of four components: a master-side surgeon console, a slave-side bedside operating unit, a teleconferencing setup, and a telecommunications module. All subsystem signals were routed to a central telesurgery host, which enabled communication via both an Optical Transport Network (OTN) and a dedicated network link (Fig. 2). The KD-SR-2000 is a master–slave robotic system consisting of an open-architecture surgeon console, a four-arm surgical cart, and a vision cart (Fig. 2A). The EDGE-MP1000 system comprises a surgeon console, a patient-side surgical platform, and a 3D high-definition imaging system (Fig. 2B).

Telesurgery process

Telesurgery connections were established between multiple city pairs in China (Fig. 3). Network performance metrics, including real-time round-trip latency, jitter, video encoding and decoding delay, code rate, frame loss, and display delay, were continuously monitored throughout the procedures and summarized as mean ± standard deviation. The operating team reviewed network stability before each operation. Telesurgery proceeded only when the connection remained stable, with latency maintained within the range considered acceptable in previous telesurgery literature.

Both telesurgeries and local surgeries were performed by surgeons who had each completed more than 100 standard robot-assisted operations under the supervision of senior faculty. Both robotic systems were used within their approved clinical-use scope and under the institutional ethical and regulatory framework for this study. During telesurgery, an experienced bedside surgical team was physically present with the patient and remained prepared for immediate local takeover or conversion if technical failure, network instability, uncontrolled bleeding, or other safety concerns occurred.

Results

Six consecutive patients underwent telesurgery using two CSS platforms. 3 RARP and 1 RAPN were performed with the MP1000 system, while 1 RAPN and 1 RARC were completed using the KD-SR-2000 system. Six locally performed DV-Xi Xi RARP cases were included as a limited contextual reference for the RARP subgroup. Technical success was achieved in all telesurgery cases, and no case required conversion to open or conventional laparoscopic surgery.

In the RARP subgroup, three remote MP1000-RARP cases and six local DV-Xi RARP cases were summarized descriptively (Table 1). The remote MP1000-RARP cases had a median operative time of 194.2 min and median suture-per-stitch time of 0.207 min, while the local DV-Xi RARP cases had a median operative time of 163.6 min and median suture-per-stitch time of 0.167 min. EBL was 138 ml in the MP1000 group and 105 ml in the DV-Xi group. PSM occurred in 1 of 3 MP1000 cases and 2 of 6 DV-Xi cases. Urinary continence recovery at 4 weeks after catheter removal was observed in 2 of 3 MP1000 cases and 3 of 6 DV-Xi cases (Table 2). These findings are descriptive only and should not be interpreted as evidence of formal comparative validity.

Surgical, pathological and postoperative outcomes of RARP group

Both RAPN cases (1 MP1000 and 1 KD-SR-2000) and the single RARC case (KD-SR-2000) were completed successfully. The RAPN performed with the MP1000 had an operative time of 128.7 min, a warm ischemia time (WIT) of 18.4 min, and an EBL of 30 ml. The patient recovered uneventfully and had a postoperative hospital stay of 4 days, with no complications or PSM. The RAPN using the KD-SR-2000 required 130.6 min of operative time, with a WIT of 19.1 min and an EBL of 20 ml; the patient had an uncomplicated recovery and a postoperative hospital stay of 4 days. The KD-SR-2000 RARC procedure was completed in 270.4 min with an EBL of 40 ml, and the postoperative hospital stay was 5 days (Table 3).

Patients demographics and clinical outcomes

Distances for the telesurgery procedures varied between 2 km and 2,200 km across four city pairs (Harbin–Harbin, Harbin–Beijing, Harbin–Hefei, and Harbin–Hangzhou). There was a clear increase in mean real-time round-trip latency as distance grew, from 6.13 ± 0.71 ms (Harbin–Harbin) to 54.12 ± 0.58 ms (Harbin–Hangzhou). Network jitter remained < 0.5 ms across all connections, with no frame loss observed. No significant variation was observed across sites in video encoding/decoding latency, code rate, or display latency (Table 4).

Discussion

This pilot study demonstrates the feasibility and preliminary short-term safety of telesurgery using 2 CSS across different city pairs in China. With all procedures successfully carried out and no equipment-related complications, the results point to operational stability of the CSS in telesurgery.

For the RARP subgroup, remote MP1000 procedures were summarized alongside a small retrospective DV-Xi cohort used only as a contextual reference. Operative time and suture-per-stitch time were longer during remote procedures, and EBL was numerically higher, whereas PSM rate and early urinary continence recovery showed no obvious unfavorable signal in this limited series. However, total operative time is influenced by multiple factors, including platform familiarity, robotic setup, bedside coordination, remote communication workflow, and the early learning curve [16, 17]. By comparison, suture-per-stitch time may more directly reflect telemanipulation-related workflow efficiency during remote surgery. Therefore, these descriptive findings cannot isolate the independent effects of telesurgery, network latency, or platform-related factors.

Remote RAPN and RARC were also completed successfully using the MP1000 and KD-SR-2000 systems. These individual cases illustrate the technical breadth of CSS-based telesurgery, but the evidence for RAPN and RARC remains purely descriptive. No conclusions regarding oncologic efficacy, renal functional preservation, continence, recurrence, or long-term safety can be drawn from these limited observations.

Network stability is a critical prerequisite for telesurgery. In this study, OTN and dedicated network connections maintained mean round-trip latency below 60 ms and no frame loss. Previous studies have suggested that end-to-end latency below 200 ms is acceptable for telesurgery, and our findings remained well within this safety threshold [18]. Our observed network parameters were within this previously reported tolerance range. Nevertheless, the present study was not designed to establish a quantitative correlation between latency and surgical performance. Future studies should combine synchronized network logs with task-level surgical metrics to define clinically validated safety thresholds.

Several methodological limitations must be emphasized. First, only six telesurgical cases were included, and the procedures were heterogeneous. Second, the local DV-Xi RARP cohort was retrospectively selected and was not rigorously matched for variables, which introduces selection bias and residual confounding. Third, platform effects, surgeon adaptation, bedside team coordination, and remote-operation effects cannot be separated in this design. Fourth, follow-up was short, and long-term oncologic and functional outcomes were not assessed. Fifth, the study was not powered for inferential statistical analysis or learning-curve assessment. Therefore, all findings should be considered descriptive and hypothesis-generating.

The broader implementation of telesurgery should be considered both a technical and clinical governance issue. Safe adoption requires reliable low-latency communication, secure data transmission, explicit patient consent, surgeon credentialing, institutional oversight, and predefined emergency response pathways [19–21]. Because the console surgeon is physically separated from the patient, standardized protocols are needed for network monitoring, audiovisual communication, local backup, emergency takeover, cybersecurity, data protection, and postoperative audit. For wider international implementation, differences in medical licensure, liability, reimbursement, data regulation, and infrastructure capacity remain major barriers. Therefore, CSS-based telesurgery will require harmonized regulatory frameworks, prospective registries, and stepwise validation across institutions and health care systems.

Recent studies have increasingly explored the clinical application of telesurgery using CSS, with growing evidence supporting their reliability in selected urologic procedures [22]. At the same time, robotic urologic oncology is evolving beyond conventional multiport systems toward next-generation robotic platforms, single-port surgery, artificial intelligence, three-dimensional surgical planning, augmented reality, and 5G-enabled connectivity [21–23]. These advances reflect a broader shift toward more precise, less invasive, and more accessible surgical care, while also highlighting persistent challenges related to training, cost, technical integration, regulatory oversight, network safety, and long-term outcome validation. Within this evolving robotic surgery ecosystem, CSS-based telesurgery may help improve access to specialized urologic care in selected settings. However, the present study should be interpreted only as an early feasibility experience under stable network conditions, and the clinical role of CSS in remote urologic surgery must be defined through larger prospective studies with standardized reporting, robust network-safety assessment, and longer follow-up.

Conclusion

In this small selected case series, urologic telesurgery using CSS was technically feasible under stable telecommunication conditions, with preliminary short-term safety observed. Larger prospective multicenter studies with standardized protocols and longer follow-up are needed to validate these findings.
