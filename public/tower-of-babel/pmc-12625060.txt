Perineural versus intravenous dexamethasone for single-shot interscalene block in arthroscopic shoulder surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies published through May 2021
Authors: Rongcan Wu, Zhi Chen, Gang Chen, Jun Sun, Wenge Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAlthough both perineural and intravenous dexamethasone have been shown to extend the analgesic duration of single-shot interscalene brachial plexus blocks (ISB), the optimal route of administration remains uncertain. This meta-analysis systematically compares the efficacy and safety of perineural versus intravenous dexamethasone as adjuvants to ISB in patients undergoing arthroscopic shoulder surgery.MethodsPrimary outcomes assessed were analgesia duration, sensory block duration, and motor block duration. Secondary outcomes included postoperative pain scores, opioid consumption, and the incidence of adverse events, such as local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST). We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science from inception to May 31, 2021 under a prespecified protocol; studies published thereafter were beyond the scope of this review.ResultsA total of seven studies involving 846 patients were included in this analysis. Compared with intravenous administration, perineural injection of dexamethasone significantly prolonged analgesia duration (weighted mean difference WMD: 1.699 h; 95% confidence interval CI: 0.014–3.384; p = 0.048) and reduced postoperative pain scores at 12 h (WMD: −0.652; 95% CI: −1.129 to −0.176; p = 0.007). No significant differences were observed in sensory or motor block durations, postoperative pain scores, and opioid consumption at 24 h, or the incidence of adverse events, including dyspnea, sleep disturbances, and postoperative glucose level fluctuations.ConclusionsCompared with the intravenous route, perineural dexamethasone modestly prolongs the duration of analgesia after single-shot interscalene block (approximately 2 h). However, the associated reductions in postoperative pain scores are small and unlikely to reach commonly cited MCID thresholds, rendering the overall clinical importance uncertain. These findings support cautious interpretation and individualized route selection rather than generalized claims of superiority.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13741-025-00617-6.Keywords: Perineural, Intravenous, Dexamethasone, Interscalene nerve block, Analgesia

Introduction

Shoulder surgery, particularly arthroscopic procedures, is frequently associated with substantial postoperative pain (Abdallah et al. 2015a). Inadequately treated or unmanaged pain can significantly impair a patient’s quality of life, elevate the risk of postoperative complications, and prolong both rehabilitation and hospital stay. Despite advancements in pain management strategies, achieving optimal postoperative pain control following arthroscopic shoulder surgery remains a considerable clinical challenge (Abdallah et al. 2015b).

Interscalene brachial plexus block (ISB) is considered the gold standard for managing acute shoulder pain (Abdallah et al. 2016). However, a single-shot ISB typically provides analgesia for only 8 to 12 h, which may not be sufficient for the entire postoperative period (Attardi et al. 1993). As such, its duration of analgesia is usually inadequate to provide adequate pain coverage for the majority of patients (Abdallah et al. 2016). Therefore, to prolong and enhance the analgesic effect of a single-shot ISB, various adjuvants have been investigated, with dexamethasone being among the most widely studied. When co-administered with local anesthetics, dexamethasone can extend analgesia duration (Brummett and Williams 2011). Among these, dexamethasone is a widely studied and effective option (Choi et al. 2014)as studies have shown that co-administration of dexamethasone with local anesthetics can significantly extend the duration of analgesia (Chun et al. 2016; Desmet et al. 2013).

Previous research has demonstrated that dexamethasone prolongs analgesia when administered via both perineural and intravenous routes (Fredrickson et al. 2010). However, the efficacy of dexamethasone may vary depending on the route of administration. Recent studies attempting to compare the analgesic efficacy of perineural versus intravenous dexamethasone for ISB have yielded inconsistent results (Holland et al. 2018). To address these discrepancies and provide clearer evidence, this study conducted a meta-analysis to compare the efficacy and safety of perineural versus intravenous dexamethasone for ISB in the context of arthroscopic shoulder surgery. Therefore, this meta-analysis aimed primarily to evaluate the duration of analgesia as the main outcome measure, comparing perineural and intravenous dexamethasone when used in interscalene block.

Material and methods

Search strategy

We searched PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science from inception to May 31, 2021, using terms related to dexamethasone, interscalene block, brachial plexus, and perineural or intravenous administration. Reference lists of relevant articles were screened. Full search strings are provided in the Supplement Material. This review summarizes evidence up to the prespecified cut-off and does not evaluate studies published thereafter. This meta-analysis was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42021253499) and conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Holland et al. 2018). The search terms used were: ((“intravenous” OR “systemic”) AND “perineural”) AND (“dexamethasone” OR “glucocorticoids” OR “corticosteroids” OR “steroids”) AND (“brachial plexus block” OR “interscalene block” OR “infraclavicular block” OR “supraclavicular block” OR “axillary block” OR “nerve block”) AND ((“arthroscopy” OR “arthroscopic” OR “surgery”) AND “shoulder”). The search was restricted to human clinical studies published in English. Additional studies were identified by screening references from relevant reviews and included articles.

Protocol and scope statement

The search horizon (through May 31, 2021) was prespecified to align with the original project timeline and to preserve protocol fidelity. To avoid selective inclusion bias, post-2021 studies were not evaluated in this revision.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Studies fulfilling the following criteria were included: (1) participants: patients receiving single-shot interscalene brachial plexus block (ISB) for arthroscopic shoulder surgery; (2) intervention: perineural dexamethasone administered as an adjunct to local anesthetics; (3) comparator: intravenous administration of an equivalent dose of dexamethasone; and (4) outcomes: duration of analgesia, duration of sensory and motor blocks, postoperative pain scores at 12 and 24 h, opioid consumption within 24 h, and the incidence of adverse events.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies comparing local anesthetics combined with dexamethasone versus local anesthetics alone; (2) studies comparing perineural versus intravenous dexamethasone administration for surgeries other than shoulder arthroscopy; (3) case reports, letters, abstracts, reviews, animal studies, or in vitro studies; (4) studies with insufficient data to extract or calculate required outcomes; and (5) duplicate publications.

Data extraction and quality assessment

All identified studies were independently screened for eligibility by two reviewers according to predefined inclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through consultation with a third investigator. Primary outcomes were block-related measures, including the duration of analgesia, sensory block, and motor block. Secondary outcomes encompassed postoperative pain scores at 12 and 24 h, opioid consumption within 24 h postoperatively, and the incidence of adverse events (dyspnea, sleep disturbances, and postoperative glucose alterations). Additional data extracted included the first author’s name, publication year, country, sample size, patient age, gender, type of local anesthetic used, dexamethasone dosage, and overall study quality. The methodological quality of included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was assessed using the Jadad scale (Hughes et al. 2013); studies scoring greater than 3 were classified as high-quality.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was conducted using STATA software (version 12.0). Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05. Heterogeneity among studies was evaluated using the I2 statistic and chi-square test. A fixed-effects model was employed when heterogeneity was not statistically significant (I2 < 50%, p > 0.1); otherwise, a random-effects model was utilized. For dichotomous outcomes, risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated. For continuous outcomes, either weighted mean differences (WMD) or standardized mean differences (SMD), along with corresponding 95% CI, were determined. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic. In line with our prespecified analysis plan, we considered exploratory subgroup/sensitivity analyses (e.g., by local-anesthetic regimen, dexamethasone dose strata, or perioperative co-analgesics); however, when subgrouping would yield sparse cells (≤ 2–3 studies per stratum), we refrained from post-hoc quantitative splits to avoid unstable estimates and multiplicity. Therefore, in this revision, no additional post-hoc quantitative subgroup or sensitivity analyses were performed.

Results

Literature retrieval results

Figure 1illustrates the literature screening and selection process. The initial search yielded 91 records. After removing 28 duplicates, 63 studies were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 55 were excluded for failing to meet the inclusion criteria. The remaining eight studies underwent full-text review, resulting in the inclusion of seven studies (Choi et al. 2014; Hussain et al. 2018; Jadad et al. 1996; Kahn et al. 2018, 2018; Kawanishi et al. 2014; Liu et al. 2015) for final meta-analysis. The final literature search was conducted in May 2021; studies published thereafter were not evaluated in this review.

Research characteristics and quality assessment

The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. The meta-analysis comprised 846 patients, of whom 426 received perineural dexamethasone, while 420 received intravenous dexamethasone. The mean age of participants in the included studies ranged from 47 to 59.2 years. Considerable variability existed among studies regarding the type, concentration, and dosage of local anesthetics administered. Similarly, dexamethasone dosages varied from 1 to 10 mg, with 4 mg being the most commonly used dose. Notably, one study compared low-dose (4 mg) versus high-dose (8 mg) dexamethasone via perineural and intravenous routes; data from both dosages were included in our analysis (Choi et al. 2014). All included studies were assessed as high-quality based on the Jadad scoring system. While the pooled difference favored the perineural route for duration of analgesia, the absolute magnitude of benefit was modest. Between-group differences in pain intensity were statistically significant but small, generally below commonly cited MCID thresholds (≈1 point on a 0–10 NRS).

Primary outcomes

Heterogeneity statement

Across several comparisons, between-study heterogeneity was considerable, reflecting variability in block techniques and local-anesthetic regimens, dexamethasone dosing and logistics, perioperative pathways, and outcome definitions/time points. Given this heterogeneity, pooled estimates should be interpreted cautiously, prioritizing effect magnitude and direction rather than nominal statistical significance alone.

Duration of analgesia

This outcome was reported in five studies. Pooled analysis indicated that perineural dexamethasone significantly prolonged analgesia duration compared to intravenous dexamethasone (WMD: 1.699 h; 95% CI: 0.014–3.384; I2 = 64.3%; p = 0.048, random-effects model) (Fig. 2a).

a Forest plot depicting the duration of analgesia. b Forest plot depicting the duration of sensory block. c Forest plot depicting the duration of motor block

Duration of sensory block

Reported by two studies, the pooled results revealed no significant difference between perineural and intravenous dexamethasone administration (WMD: 264.579 min; 95% CI: −490.767–1019.924; I2 = 86.7%; p = 0.492, random-effects model) (Fig. 2b).

Duration of motor block

Reported in two studies, no significant difference was observed between perineural and intravenous routes (WMD: 110.48 min; 95% CI: −284.379–505.339; I2 = 74.2%; p = 0.583, random-effects model) (Fig. 2c).

Secondary outcomes

Postoperative pain at 12 h

Analysis of data from three studies indicated significantly lower postoperative pain scores at 12 h in patients receiving perineural dexamethasone compared with intravenous dexamethasone (WMD: −0.652; 95% CI: −1.129 to −0.176; I2 = 43.1%; p = 0.007, fixed-effects model) (Fig. 3a).

a Forest plot illustrating postoperative pain scores at 12 h. b Forest plot illustrating postoperative pain scores at 24 h. c Forest plot illustrating opioid consumption within 24 h

Postoperative pain at 24 h

Reported by three studies, no statistically significant difference was detected between the two administration routes (WMD: −0.309; 95% CI: −1.275–0.658; I2 = 72%; p = 0.531, random-effects model) (Fig. 3b).

Opioid consumption within 24 h

Reported by two studies, pooled analysis found no significant difference between perineural and intravenous dexamethasone groups (SMD: −0.019; 95% CI: −0.244–0.206; I2 = 0%; p = 0.866, fixed-effects model) (Fig. 3c).

Adverse events

There were no statistically significant differences between perineural and intravenous administration for the incidence of dyspnea (three studies, RR: 0.936; 95% CI: 0.27–3.24; I2 = 0%; p = 0.916, fixed-effects model), sleep disturbances (two studies, RR: 1.028; 95% CI: 0.526–2.01; I2 = 28.7%; p = 0.935, fixed-effects model), or postoperative glucose changes (two studies, WMD: −1.5; 95% CI: −4.723–1.722; I2 = 0%; p = 0.361, fixed-effects model).

Discussion

ISB is commonly used to manage postoperative pain, but its analgesic duration is relatively limited, often necessitating adjuvants such as dexamethasone to enhance analgesic efficacy and prolong pain relief. Previous studies demonstrated that dexamethasone (Liu et al. 2015), when combined with medium- or long-acting local anesthetics, approximately doubled postoperative analgesia duration (Kawanishi et al. 2014). However, the optimal route of dexamethasone administration remains uncertain. Abdallah and Desmet reported that analgesic duration is independent of the administration route, finding similar analgesic effects for both perineural and intravenous routes (Holland et al. 2018; Hussain et al. 2018). Conversely, other researchers favored perineural over intravenous dexamethasone for prolonging analgesia duration. Ryosuke et al. indicated that perineural dexamethasone significantly prolonged effective postoperative analgesia compared to intravenous administration following ISB (Kahn et al. 2018). Richard et al. similarly concluded that perineural dexamethasone extends analgesia duration by 2.5 to 5 h (McHardy et al. 2020)compared to intravenous administration. Given these conflicting results, our meta-analysis aimed to clarify the most effective administration route, demonstrating that perineural dexamethasone significantly prolongs analgesic duration compared to intravenous administration. This finding is consistent with a Cochrane review reporting longer analgesic duration of perineural dexamethasone for peripheral nerve blocks (Morita et al. 2020) in both upper and lower extremities.

Literature suggests that perineural dexamethasone’s enhanced efficacy in ISB might arise from local mechanisms (Movafegh et al. 2006)distinct from systemic absorption. Specifically, local administration may suppress ectopic neural discharges, decrease nociceptive C-fiber activity, and reduce neuropeptide-mediated inflammatory responses (Holland et al. 2018; Pehora 2017). Moreover, perineural dexamethasone induces vasoconstriction, slowing local anesthetic absorption and thus extending its analgesic duration (Sakae et al. 2017).

The observed heterogeneity likely arises from clinical differences (single-shot ISB techniques; local-anesthetic type/concentration/volume; dexamethasone dose and route logistics; concomitant multimodal analgesia; surgical complexity and perioperative pathways) and methodological differences (eligibility criteria; outcome definitions and assessment time points; handling of rescue analgesia; risk-of-bias domains). Such variability can attenuate precision and inflate apparent inconsistency, underscoring the need for cautious, context-aware interpretation of pooled effects.

Most existing studies predominantly focused on analgesic duration, with relatively few investigations into sensory and motor block durations. Theoretically, perineural administration might prolong sensory and motor blocks; however, conflicting results have been reported. Thiago et al. observed prolonged sensory and motor blocks with perineural dexamethasone compared to intravenous administration (Kawanishi et al. 2014), whereas another meta-analysis concluded that perineural dexamethasone significantly extended sensory but not motor block duration (Movafegh et al. 2006). Contrastingly, Paul and Faraj reported no differences between administration routes regarding sensory and motor blocks (Liu et al. 2015; Zorrilla-Vaca and Li 2018). Our pooled results concurred with these latter findings, suggesting equivalency between perineural and intravenous dexamethasone for sensory and motor blocks. Given the limited number of studies included, further research is warranted to confirm these results.

Optimizing postoperative pain management and reducing opioid consumption significantly influence recovery outcomes following arthroscopic shoulder surgery. Prior studies showed both perineural and intravenous dexamethasone effectively reduced postoperative pain and opioid requirements, but their comparative efficacies remained controversial. Some studies reported no difference (Holland et al. 2018; McHardy et al. 2020), whereas others supported perineural dexamethasone as superior. Our meta-analysis demonstrated significantly improved pain (Kawanishi et al. 2014)control at 12 h with perineural dexamethasone, but no significant difference at 24 h for pain scores or opioid consumption (Kahn et al. 2018). Recent evidence indicates local interactions between dexamethasone and peripheral nerves play critical roles in controlling acute postoperative pain (Movafegh et al. 2006).

As our synthesis is time-bound through May 2021, post-cut-off evidence was not evaluated.

Although dexamethasone effectively prolongs analgesic duration and reduces postoperative pain, concerns regarding potential adverse effects, particularly steroid-induced hyperglycemia, persist. Several studies reported elevated glucose levels following both intravenous and perineural administration (Hussain et al. 2018; Jadad et al. 1996). Nevertheless, our pooled analysis revealed no significant difference in glucose responses between routes. Additionally, no significant differences were observed regarding dyspnea and sleep disturbances, consistent with previous research (Choi et al. 2014). While delayed wound healing and infection risks associated with dexamethasone remain concerns, existing studies have not found increased risks attributable to either administration route. Thus, perineural dexamethasone appears beneficial for prolonging analgesia and acute pain relief without additional complication risks. Variability in results could be attributed to differences in dexamethasone dosages, types of local anesthetics, and study methodologies. Future studies should focus on standardizing these variables to reduce heterogeneity. Currently, limited data exist regarding dose-dependent adverse effects of dexamethasone. Further research exploring various doses is needed to establish a clear relationship between dexamethasone dosage and incidence of adverse events. Our findings suggest specific clinical scenarios in which perineural dexamethasone may offer significant advantages over intravenous administration, potentially guiding anesthesiologists toward more effective analgesic protocols for arthroscopic shoulder surgery. Although perineural dexamethasone consistently prolonged block duration by ≈2 h, most pooled pain-score differences were below MCID thresholds, which tempers the clinical implications of statistical significance. We therefore emphasize magnitude and relevance rather than statistical positivity alone.

Perineural dexamethasone constitutes an off-label use. Given the modest absolute benefit and practical considerations, some clinicians may reasonably prefer the intravenous route. Route selection should therefore be individualized, integrating patient preferences and institutional practice patterns.

Limitations

A key limitation is the time-bound search horizon (through May 2021); studies published thereafter were not assessed and could affect currency. Heterogeneity was multifactorial (techniques, dosing, outcomes/time points, perioperative pathways), which limits generalizability and warrants cautious interpretation.

Inconsistent definitions of “duration of analgesia” and sparse data for some secondary, patient-centered outcomes (e.g., rebound pain, sleep disturbance) further attenuate precision. Given these constraints, future protocol-based updates are needed to verify the modest magnitude and clinical relevance of effects.

Conclusion

In summary, compared with the intravenous route, perineural dexamethasone modestly prolongs ISB analgesia duration (≈2 h). Reductions in pain scores (e.g., at 12 h) are small and generally below commonly cited MCID thresholds, rendering the overall clinical importance uncertain. These findings support cautious interpretation and individualized route selection rather than generalized claims of superiority. However, uncertainties regarding its precise mechanisms, optimal dosing, and potential neurotoxicity necessitate further research before routinely recommending perineural dexamethasone in ISB for arthroscopic shoulder surgery.
