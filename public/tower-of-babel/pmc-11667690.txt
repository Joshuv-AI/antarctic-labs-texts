Is vector competence testing of Aedes aegypti a valid tool to determine the transmission potential of yellow fever virus?
Authors: Andrew W. Taylor-Robinson
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

Vector competence testing assesses the potential ability of an arbovirus to replicate in both vector and host, as well as providing partial data on vector-host transmission. This well-established technique has yielded insights into the epidemiological risks associated with mosquito-borne diseases. Aedes aegypti is the primary vector for several flaviviruses, including yellow fever virus (YFV), dengue (DENV), chikungunya (CHIKV), and Zika (ZIKV). Evaluation of its vector competence in relation to each of these significant human pathogens is therefore a public health priority [1].

The vector competence of Ae. aegypti for pathogenic arboviruses including YFV has been studied extensively, from which we know much about these vector/virus relationships [2,3]. Yet, more so than with other flaviviruses there are several limitations that specifically affect the applicability of current vector competence testing for YFV. Reduced awareness of these caveats hinders public health surveillance, outbreak prediction, and vector control strategies aimed at combating yellow fever. The key considerations are outlined here for this learned audience.

First, under laboratory conditions the vector competence test does not measure fundamentally important factors, such as synchrony in the natural environment whereby virus, vector and host meet in abundance. Nor does the test consider YFV persistence in urban settings or inter-urban dispersal capacity, contrasting with DENV, which it measures best, and reasonably well for CHIKV and ZIKA. Exploring each of these epidemiological traits is pivotal to understanding YFV transmission potential as, notably unlike DENV, it is not fully adapted to urban persistence and inter-urban dispersal [2].

Second, while Ae. aegypti is known to be permissive to YFV, variability in vector competence across different geographic populations and environmental conditions complicates interpretation of vector competence tests. Ae. aegypti populations from separate regions, such as West Africa and the Asia-Pacific, exhibit differing levels of susceptibility to YFV, influenced by local ecological factors and genetic diversity within mosquito populations [2]. This discrepancy underscores the importance of local context when assessing the risk of YFV transmission, thereby limiting generalizability for predicting YFV outbreaks. In addition, depending on the specific viral strains involved, the emergence of co-infection with other arboviruses may affect the efficiency of transmission of YFV.

Third, the transmission dynamics of YFV are influenced by other mosquito species that can act as competent vector in certain settings, which may create competition with Ae. aegypti where the two species coexist [2,3]. For instance, in sub-Saharan Africa Ae. taylori is involved in YF transmission in transition areas. Such interspecies competition complicates assessment of the role of Ae. aegypti as a primary vector, as the presence of alternative vectors may reduce the overall transmission potential attributed to this mosquito alone.

Fourth, the extrinsic incubation period (EIP) of YFV in Ae. aegypti can be affected by environmental factors such as temperature and humidity, which vary significantly across regions and seasons. The escalating rate and impact of climate change, including major weather events, is an emerging compounding factor of concern. Temperature fluctuations can affect the EIP, potentially modulating transmission risk, but these effects are not uniform across all populations of Ae. aegypti [2]. This variability further complicates the application of vector competence tests, as results obtained in an environmentally-controlled laboratory may not accurately reflect field conditions.

Fifth, the role of Wolbachia in modulating vector competence is an active area of research [4]. This endosymbiotic, Gram-negative bacterium can significantly reduce transmission of other arboviruses, including dengue and Zika, by Ae. aegypti. However, the impact of Wolbachia and other symbiotic bacteria on YFV transmission in endemic hot spots in Africa and Latin America remains to be seen. Theoretically, its presence could alter the outcomes of vector competence tests, making it challenging to draw definitive conclusions about the capacity of Ae. aegypti to transmit YFV.

Lastly, the historical context of yellow fever outbreaks and vaccination strategies also plays a role in the applicability of vector competence testing [5]. Although YFV remains a perennial threat in more than 40 countries, a vaccine that elicits lifelong protective immunity has significantly reduced disease incidence wherever it has been introduced (unfortunately, not yet in all high-risk countries). As global vaccination coverage continues to rise, the potential for YFV transmission may be expected to decrease correspondingly, thus influencing the perceived need for ongoing vector competence testing of Ae. aegypti in locations traditionally recognized as endemic.

In summary, the parameters selected for arbovirus vector competence tests must reflect the most important environmental conditions under which viral persistence and dispersal occur. For YFV, while vector competence testing of Ae. aegypti remains a valid tool for understanding the potential for transmission, several factors combine to reduce its usefulness. Geographic variability, the presence of alternative vectors, co-infections, environmental influences, the role of symbiotic bacteria, and vaccination strategies all must be considered when interpreting vector competence data. As such, to accurately assess the risk of YFV transmission in different contexts, a nuanced perspective should be taken that is informed by local knowledge and which incorporates rather than ignores these variables. Applying a set of parameters in vector competence tests should, as much as possible, be virus-specific; a standardized approach to all arboviruses is undesirable.
