Impact of fabrication history induced disorder on Josephson junction reproducibility and qubit performance
Authors: Dae-Yun Kim, Hyeokshin Kwon, Dae Seok Han, Kiho Kim, Hyoung Kug Kim, Chungsun Lee, Geunyong Kim, June-Young M Lee, Dongmin Kim, Eun-A Kim, Jaeho Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReproducible Josephson junction (JJ) characteristics are essential for frequency-deterministic and scalable superconducting quantum processors. We show that the normal-state resistance and its reproducibility in Al/AlOx/Al JJs are governed not by oxide thickness, but by fabrication-history–induced disorder in the tunneling barrier. By comparing junctions fabricated on bare wafers, lift-off–processed base layers, and dry-etched base layers under identical oxidation conditions, we observe large differences in resistance and wafer-to-wafer variability. Atomic force microscopy reveals distinct surface disorder imprinted by prior fabrication steps, while cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy show that the average AlOx barrier thickness is comparable across the three fabrication histories within experimental uncertainty. The enhanced resistance on rough, plasma-damaged surfaces therefore originates from nanoscale barrier disorder rather than thickness variations. These results establish fabrication history as a key determinant of JJ barrier quality and highlight the need for holistic process control to achieve deterministic qubit frequency targeting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46352-w.Subject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

Scalable superconducting quantum processors require Josephson junctions (JJs) with tightly controlled electrical characteristics and minimal device-to-device variability1–4. In transmon qubits, the normal-state resistance of a JJ directly sets the Josephson energy and thus determines the qubit frequency after circuit integration1,2. As a result, even modest fluctuations in JJ resistance can translate into substantial frequency spread across large qubit arrays, degrading yield and increasing calibration overhead5–8. Beyond frequency control, microscopic nonuniformities in JJ barriers are also known to introduce additional dissipation channels that limit qubit coherence8. Recent wafer-scale efforts in Josephson junction fabrication have demonstrated significant improvements in junction resistance and area uniformity through advances in oxidation control and lithography, including CMOS-compatible processing and 300 mm wafer platforms9–15. However, the reproducibility and uniformity of JJ resistance remain sensitive to fabrication-history-dependent factors.

To date, most efforts to control Al/AlOx/Al JJs have focused on tuning oxidation parameters such as oxygen pressure, exposure time, and temperature, under the implicit assumption that the oxide thickness is the primary determinant of junction resistance16–23. In practice, however, JJs are fabricated late in a complex process flow that includes base-layer formation of shunting capacitors, resonators, and control wiring6–8. These earlier fabrication steps inevitably modify the surface morphology and chemical state of the underlying metal on which the JJ is formed. Consequently, the oxidation process that defines the tunneling barrier does not occur on an idealized, pristine surface, but on one whose properties are shaped by the accumulated fabrication history.

Two representative and widely adopted approaches for base-layer patterning are lift-off and dry etching. Lift-off processes minimize plasma exposure and can preserve relatively smooth surfaces, but may leave organic residues or induce subtle mechanical damage during resist removal. Dry-etch processes, by contrast, provide superior pattern fidelity and cleaner lithographic definition, yet unavoidably introduce plasma-induced roughness and subsurface damage24–26. Although both approaches are routinely employed in state-of-the-art superconducting qubit fabrication, their downstream impact on JJ barrier formation and resistance reproducibility has not been systematically clarified.

Previous studies have shown that interface roughness or barrier disorder can significantly influence tunneling transport in a wide range of junction systems, including Josephson junctions and magnetic tunnel junctions. Such effects have been discussed in the context of unconventional superconductors, ferromagnetic junctions, and metal–insulator–metal tunnel devices, where roughness or microstructural inhomogeneity modifies local barrier transparency and transport characteristics27–29. However, these studies do not address how upstream fabrication history within a complex superconducting-qubit process flow governs the wafer-scale reproducibility of Al/AlOx/Al Josephson junction resistance under otherwise identical junction formation and oxidation conditions.

In this work, we demonstrate that fabrication history (i.e., how the base layer is patterned prior to JJ fabrication) fundamentally governs the effective tunneling-barrier quality of Al/AlOx/Al JJs. By directly comparing junctions fabricated after three distinct pre-JJ substrate/base-layer conditions—(i) bare wafers with no base-layer deposition (as a reference), (ii) lift-off–patterned base layers, and (iii) dry-etched base layers— under otherwise identical conditions, we show that large differences in normal-state resistance and reproducibility arise even when junction geometry and oxidation parameters are unchanged. Through wafer-scale electrical statistics combined with surface and structural characterization, we identify base-layer-fabrication-history–induced barrier disorder as the dominant mechanism controlling JJ resistance, with important implications for deterministic qubit frequency targeting.

Sample fabrication

To isolate the effect of base-layer fabrication history (whether via lift-off or dry etch) on Josephson junction behavior, we fabricated three sets of transmon qubit wafers, with three different fabrication histories: (1) the “bare wafer” condition, in which JJs are fabricated directly on a bare wafer without any base-layer deposition or patterning prior to JJ fabrication; (2) the “lift-off” condition, in which base layers are patterned via a lift-off process and the JJs are subsequently fabricated; and (3) the “dry-etch” condition, in which base layers are patterned via a dry-etch process and the JJs are subsequently fabricated. An overview of the fabrication flow and the three different conditions compared in this study is summarized in Fig. 1(a)–(i).

All devices are fabricated on a 725-µm-thick 8-inch (100)-oriented high-resistivity (> 10 kΩ·cm) intrinsic silicon wafer. Wafers are solvent-cleaned by spin processing in acetone and IPA for 10 min each, followed by a DI water rinse for 10 min. In the bare wafer condition, no base layer is formed; we perform AFM measurements on designated regions to establish the baseline surface condition prior to Josephson junction (JJ) processing.

For the lift-off condition, wafers are cleaned in piranha solution (H₂SO₄:H₂O₂ = 4:1 at 120 °C) for 20 min, followed by quick-dump rinse (QDR) at 70 °C and spin rinse/dry (SRD). A negative photoresist is spin-coated and patterned using an i-Line stepper. An Al film is then deposited using an electron-beam evaporator to a thickness of 120 nm at a deposition rate of 10 Å s⁻¹, with a base pressure below 10⁻⁷ Torr. To form the base layer, we perform lift-off by immersing the wafer in acetone at room temperature and applying sonication for 60 min, followed by rinsing in acetone and IPA for 10 min each; the wafer is then dried by N₂ blowing.

For the dry-etch condition, wafers undergo the same piranha/QDR/SRD sequence. The native oxide is removed immediately prior to metallization by dipping in buffered oxide etch (BOE, 10:1) for 30 s, followed by a 5-min DI water rinse; we immediately load the wafer into the evaporator vacuum chamber after the rinse to suppress re-oxidation. A blanket Al film is deposited under the same electron-beam evaporation conditions described above. The base layer is patterned using positive photoresist and i-Line stepper lithography, and transferred by Cl-based Al reactive ion etching (RIE) (10 mTorr, 500 W inductively coupled plasma, 100 W bias, BCl₃ 100 sccm, and Cl₂ 25 sccm). After etching, an in-vacuo H₂O-based plasma strip is applied to mitigate etch residues and suppress Al corrosion. Subsequently, a chemical resist stripper followed by O₂ ashing is used to remove residual photoresist remaining after etching and plasma strip.

In all cases, the Josephson junctions (Fig. 1g–i) were defined by standard e-beam lithography and fabricated using the same lift-off-based Manhattan-style angled evaporation process16, with identical in-situ oxidation pressure and duration, following well-established Al/AlOx/Al JJ fabrication protocols17–20. By strictly fixing the junction geometry, evaporation angles, and oxidation parameters, we ensured that any systematic differences in electrical characteristics could be attributed to the surface condition inherited from earlier fabrication steps rather than to variations in the junction definition itself. Each wafer contained 64 nominally identical transmon qubits, and five wafers were prepared for each condition, enabling statistically meaningful comparisons of both within-wafer uniformity and wafer-to-wafer reproducibility.

Experimental results

Fabrication-history dependence of JJ reproducibility

Figure 1(d–f) show the wafer-averaged normal-state resistance () of nominally identical Josephson junctions for each base-layer condition, plotted as a function of wafer index. Across these three fabrication histories, pronounced differences are observed in the normal-state resistance of the Josephson junctions. Junctions fabricated directly on bare wafers exhibit the lowest average resistance (1.05 kΩ) and the narrowest within-wafer and wafer-to-wafer distributions, with an RSD of 2.9% and 3.3%, respectively, indicating highly uniform barrier formation. In contrast, junctions fabricated on dry-etched base layers display resistance values that are more than four times larger on average (3.92 kΩ), accompanied by substantial fluctuations both within-wafer and wafer-to-wafer, with an RSD of 4.6% and 28.5%, respectively. Lift-off–processed base layers yield intermediate resistance values (1.79 kΩ) and variability; the within-wafer and wafer-to-wafer RSD are 3.7% and 1.6% (please refer to Table 1 for details). Such sensitivity of JJ resistance to subtle process variations has been widely recognized as a major bottleneck for scalable superconducting circuits5–8. Notably, for the dry-etch condition, some wafers show systematic offsets in mean resistance relative to others, highlighting that plasma-induced surface modification amplifies sensitivity to fabrication nonuniformities that would otherwise be negligible.

To exclude trivial geometric origins of the observed resistance differences, we performed critical-dimension critical dimension scanning electron microscope (CD-SEM) measurements on representative junctions from all wafers. Junction areas, overlap lengths, and electrode widths were found to be statistically indistinguishable across all fabrication histories within measurement uncertainty, as shown by the Fig. 1(m–o). This confirms that the observed changes in resistance do not arise from lithographic bias, resist shrinkage, or etch-induced dimensional chang es. Instead, the data indicate that fabrication history primarily modifies the intrinsic properties of the AlOx tunneling barrier, rather than the physical size or shape of the junction, consistent with earlier studies emphasizing the sensitivity of tunneling transport to barrier quality30–32.

Fabrication history dependence of JJ tunnel barrier oxidation dynamics

We next examined how fabrication history influences the response of the Al surface to oxidation by systematically varying the oxidation time during AlOx barrier formation. Figure 2 shows plots of the normal-state resistance of Josephson junctions as a function of oxidation time for each base-layer condition, with the junction area held fixed. For all fabrication conditions, the normal-state resistance increases monotonically with oxidation time, consistent with progressive barrier formation30. However, the absolute resistance values and their scaling with oxidation time differ markedly between conditions. For a given oxidation time, junctions fabricated on dry-etched surfaces consistently exhibit much higher resistance than those fabricated on bare wafers, while lift-off–processed surfaces again show intermediate behavior. This result demonstrates that oxidation parameters alone do not uniquely determine the effective tunneling barrier, echoing earlier reports that tunneling transport is exponentially sensitive to local barrier properties rather than average thickness alone30–32.

Normal-state resistance () of Josephson junctions with fixed junction area as a function of oxidation time for AlOx barrier formation. Red, blue, and purple symbols correspond to junctions fabricated on bare wafers, lift-off–processed base layers, and dry-etched base layers, respectively.

Please note that all Josephson junctions presented in Figs. 1 and 2 were fabricated using nominally identical oxidation parameters, including oxygen pressure, temperature, and oxidation environment. The only intentional difference is the oxidation time, which was fixed at 10 min for Fig. 1 and systematically varied for Fig. 2. The junctions shown in Figs. 1 and 2 were fabricated in different fabrication batches separated by several months. Because the AlOx tunnel barrier is extremely thin, its effective properties are sensitive to subtle variations in the oxidation-chamber condition over long timescales. As a result, long-term drift of the oxidation chamber can lead to quantitative offsets in the absolute resistance values between different fabrication batches, even when nominal oxidation parameters are set identically. Importantly, within each batch, the resistance exhibits a consistent and reproducible dependence on the relevant control parameters.

Microscopic origin of fabrication-history dependence of JJs

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) provides direct insight into the physical origin of this behavior by revealing distinct surface morphologies associated with each fabrication history. Figure 3(a–c) shows the AFM images of Si wafer surfaces taken immediately after base-layer fabrication; the AFM measurement area corresponds to the Si wafer surface on which the JJ will later be fabricated. Figure 3(d–i) shows the AFM measurement results. As a result, bare wafers exhibit atomically smooth surfaces with minimal height variation, with an RMS roughness of ~ 0.17 nm, reflecting the absence of plasma exposure or aggressive processing prior to junction fabrication. Lift-off–processed surfaces show moderate roughness, with an RMS roughness of ~ 1.67 nm, which can be attributed to residual contamination or mechanical perturbation during resist removal. In contrast, dry-etched surfaces display pronounced nanoscale asperities and significantly increased RMS roughness of ~ 4.73 nm, consistent with plasma-induced damage and micro-masking effects during etching24–26. Similar correlations between surface roughness, oxidation nonuniformity, and electronic transport have been discussed in the context of thin-film oxides and tunnel barriers24,25.

Oxidation on a disordered surface is expected to proceed in a spatially heterogeneous manner. Local variations in surface orientation, defect density, and chemical reactivity can promote preferential oxygen diffusion and nonuniform oxide growth. As a result, even when the nominal oxidation parameters are fixed, the resulting AlOx barrier can exhibit strong nanoscale variations in density, defect concentration, and tunneling transparency. Electron transport through a Josephson junction is exponentially sensitive to such local variations; for example, pockets of low resistance (such as leak channels) can even dominate the overall junction resistance30,33–35. This disorder-dominated tunneling picture naturally explains the enhanced resistance and reduced reproducibility observed for rough, plasma-damaged surfaces.

To quantitatively assess the structural properties of the tunneling barrier, we performed cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) combined with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) on representative Josephson junctions fabricated under each condition, as shown in Fig. 4. Rather than relying on qualitative comparisons, the effective AlOx tunnel-barrier thickness was quantitatively extracted from the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the AlOx-related oxygen peaks in the EDS line profiles. In this analysis, the AlOx contribution was isolated by focusing on the Al-rich and Si-poor region, thereby excluding the substrate-related SiO2 signal. Within experimental uncertainty, the extracted AlOx thicknesses are comparable across the three fabrication histories. Importantly, no systematic or monotonic correlation is observed between the normal-state resistance and the average AlOx thickness. For example, although a thicker AlOx barrier might naively be expected for the dry-etch condition based on its higher resistance, the extracted AlOx thickness does not follow this trend and is in fact largest for the bare-wafer case. This quantitative TEM/EDS analysis demonstrates that the observed resistance differences cannot be attributed to systematic variations in the average AlOx thickness.

Complementary EDS measurements (Fig. 4d–f) nevertheless reveal pronounced variations in the local elemental distributions within the barrier region, indicating that oxidation proceeds differently on smooth and disordered base-layer surfaces even when the average oxide thickness is comparable. Such nanoscale chemical and structural inhomogeneities are known to promote the formation of two-level systems and other defect states in amorphous AlOx barriers36–39. Taken together, these results indicate that Josephson junction resistance in this regime is governed by fabrication-history–dependent disorder within the tunneling barrier, rather than by its nominal thickness. In this disorder-dominated tunneling regime, electron transport is controlled by the least transparent regions of the barrier, naturally leading to both increased average resistance and enhanced device-to-device variability33–35.

Fabrication history dependence of coherence times of superconducting qubits

Finally, we consider the implications of fabrication history for transmon qubit performance. For identical designed parameters of transmon qubits, we compared the energy relaxation time of devices fabricated using lift-off and dry-etch base-layer processes. Figure 5 shows box plots of the energy relaxation time () for two different base-layer processes. The figure shows that devices fabricated using lift-off and dry-etch base layers can exhibit comparable average coherence when other loss mechanisms are well controlled. However, the strong dependence of JJ resistance on fabrication history directly translates into qubit frequency spread, which poses a more immediate and severe limitation for scalable architectures. Even if coherence can be preserved through careful materials and interface optimization, uncontrolled resistance variability undermines deterministic frequency targeting and complicates system-level integration.

Discussion & conclusion

In this work, we have demonstrated that the normal-state resistance and its reproducibility in Al/AlOx/Al Josephson junctions are governed primarily by disorder arising from fabrication history, rather than by variations in the average oxide thickness. By systematically comparing junctions fabricated on bare wafers, lift-off–processed base layers, and dry-etched base layers under otherwise identical junction geometry and oxidation conditions, we observed pronounced and reproducible differences in both the mean resistance and its wafer-scale variability. These differences persist despite statistically indistinguishable junction dimensions and nearly identical AlOx barrier thicknesses, as confirmed by CD-SEM and cross-sectional TEM analyses.

The combined electrical and structural characterization reveals that upstream base-layer processing modifies the surface condition on which the tunnel barrier is formed, thereby influencing oxidation pathways and the resulting barrier uniformity. In particular, AFM measurements show a strong increase in surface roughness for dry-etched base layers, consistent with plasma-induced damage and micro-masking effects. Oxidation on such disordered surfaces is expected to proceed in a spatially heterogeneous manner, producing local variations in barrier density, defect concentration, and tunneling transparency. Because electron transport through ultrathin tunnel barriers is exponentially sensitive to such local variations, these nanoscale inhomogeneities naturally lead to enhanced resistance and increased device-to-device variability, even when the average barrier thickness remains unchanged.

While the present study focuses on room-temperature normal-state resistance statistics, the observed strong dependence of junction resistance on fabrication history has clear implications for defect formation mechanisms and process optimization. From a process-integration perspective, our results suggest several practical mitigation strategies. These include minimizing unnecessary plasma exposure during base-layer patterning, optimizing dry-etch conditions to reduce ion-induced damage and micro-masking effects, and introducing surface-recovery or smoothing steps—such as gentle post-etch cleaning, low-damage plasma treatments, or controlled surface reconditioning—prior to junction oxidation. Such approaches aim to restore a more uniform and chemically well-defined Al surface before AlOx barrier formation, thereby improving both resistance uniformity and reproducibility.

Although a detailed low-temperature transport characterization of individual defects—such as tunnel-barrier I–V spectroscopy or temperature-dependent measurements of critical current—is beyond the scope of the present work, the wafer-scale trends reported here already provide clear guidance for process-level optimization. Future studies combining such process refinements with systematic low-temperature transport measurements will be valuable for further elucidating the microscopic nature of fabrication-history-dependent barrier disorder and its impact on Josephson junction performance.

Finally, we emphasize that the implications of fabrication-history-dependent disorder extend beyond the specific Al/AlOx/Al junction system studied here. In complex device fabrication flows, surface conditions inherited from prior processing steps can fundamentally alter oxidation pathways and barrier uniformity, even when nominal growth parameters are held constant. Our findings therefore highlight fabrication history as a critical and often underappreciated control parameter for deterministic Josephson junction fabrication. Addressing such upstream process effects is essential for achieving wafer-scale reproducibility and reliable frequency targeting in scalable superconducting quantum processors, and may be broadly relevant to other electronic devices relying on ultrathin amorphous tunnel barriers.
