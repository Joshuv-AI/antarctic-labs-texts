Krylov shadow tomography: Efficient estimation of quantum Fisher information
Da-Jian Zhang, D. M. Tong

Abstract
Efficiently estimating the quantum Fisher information (QFI) is pivotal in quantum information science but remains an outstanding challenge for large systems due to its high nonlinearity. In this Letter, we tackle this long-standing challenge by integrating the Krylov subspace method–a celebrated tool from applied mathematics–into the framework of shadow tomography. The integrated technique, dubbed Krylov shadow tomography (KST), enables us to formulate a strict hierarchy of non-polynomial lower bounds on the QFI, among which the highest one matches the QFI exactly. We show that all the bounds can be expressed as expected values of the inverses of Hankel matrices, which are accessible via shadow tomography. Our KST therefore opens up a resource-efficient and experimentally feasible avenue to estimate not only non-polynomial lower bounds but also the QFI itself.

Applying the KST to two physical contexts, we demonstrate that it allows for estimating the QFI itself at a moderate cost of resources, which is a sought-after goal that cannot otherwise be reached using polynomial lower bounds. The KST therefore opens up a resource-efficient and experimentally feasible avenue to estimate not only non-polynomial lower bounds but also the QFI itself.

Background.—
To illustrate the significance of the QFI, it is instructive to revisit its applications in entanglement detection. Let
\rho
be an unknown state of
N
qubits. Its QFI with respect to a Hermitian operator
H
is defined as

\displaystyle F_{Q}=2\sum_{p_{k}+p_{l}>0}\frac{(p_{k}-p_{l})^{2}}{p_{k}+p_{l}}\absolutevalue{\bra{k}H\ket{l}}^{2},

(1)

where
p_{k}
and
\ket{k}
are the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\rho
. It has been shown that the inequality
F_{Q}\leq N
holds for all separable states when
H=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{z}^{(i)}

[
13
]
, with
\sigma_{z}^{(i)}
denoting the Pauli-
Z
matrix for qubit
i
. So a violation of this inequality,
F_{Q}>N
, serves as a criterion certifying that
\rho
is entangled.
This insight has inspired a series of studies that refine and extend the criterion to account for states of given entanglement depth
[
15
,
14
]
, states characterized by Young diagrams
[
19
]
, networked states in distributed sensing
[
23
]
, and situations where
H
is nonlinear
[
24
]
. Apparently, practical implementations of these criteria are hindered by the difficulty in accessing
F_{Q}
. To address this issue, it has been suggested to alternatively estimate lower bounds
B
that satisfy
B\leq F_{Q}
for all states. The underlying rationale is that, to certify that
F_{Q}>N
and thus
\rho
is entangled, it suffices to show
B>N
. A simple lower bound, derived from the Legendre transform of
F_{Q}
, is proposed in Ref.
[
51
]
, given by

\displaystyle B^{(\mathsf{Leg})}=\begin{cases}N^{2}(1-2f_{\textrm{GHZ}})^{2}&\textrm{if}~~f_{\textrm{GHZ}}>\frac{1}{2},\\
0&\textrm{if}~~f_{\textrm{GHZ}}\leq\frac{1}{2},\end{cases}

(2)

where
f_{\textrm{GHZ}}=\bra{\textrm{GHZ}}\rho\ket{\textrm{GHZ}}
denotes the fidelity between
\rho
and the Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger
(GHZ) state. Another simple lower bound, grounded in the concept of the sub-QFI, is introduced in Ref.
[
54
]
, and reads

\displaystyle B^{(\mathsf{Sub})}=-2\tr([\rho, H]^2).

(3)

These bounds are polynomials of
\rho
and easier to access in experiments. Yet, the substantial gap between them and
F_{Q}
severely limits their effectiveness in detecting entanglement; that is, many entangled states detectable via
F_{Q}
cannot be detected using these bounds. To reduce the gap, an infinite number of polynomial lower bounds, obtained from the Taylor expansion of
F_{Q}
, are proposed in Ref.
[
56
]
,

\displaystyle B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Tay})}

\displaystyle=2\tr\Biggl(\sum_{l=0}^{n}\left(\rho\otimes\openone-\openone\otimes\rho\right)^{2}\left(\openone\otimes\openone-\rho\otimes\openone\right.

\displaystyle\quad-\left.\openone\otimes\rho\right)^{l}S(H\otimes H)\Biggr),

(4)

where
S
is the swap operator. Remarkably,
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Tay})}
may increase as
n
increases and eventually converges to
F_{Q}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Unfortunately, it is very difficult, if not impossible, to obtain the QFI itself in this way, as the experimental effort in estimating
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Tay})}
grows exponentially with
n
.

Non-polynomial lower bounds in Krylov subspaces.—
To open the possibility of accessing the QFI, our idea is to employ the Krylov subspace method to formulate non-polynomial lower bounds that not only can effectively approximate the QFI but also be accessible via shadow tomography.

First, we derive a compact expression for the QFI. We define a weighted inner product between two Hermitian operators
X
and
Y
as
\langle X,Y\rangle_{\rho}\coloneqq\tr[\rho(XY+YX)/2]
, where the subscript
\rho
is used to indicate the dependence of the inner product on
\rho
. The corresponding norm then reads
\norm{X}_{\rho}\coloneqq\sqrt{\langle X,X\rangle_{\rho}}
. Inspired by the seminal work of Braunstein and Caves
[
3
]
, we introduce the superoperator

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(X)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(\rho X+X\rho\right).

(5)

When expressed in the eigenbasis of
\rho
, this superoperator takes the form
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(X)=\sum_{p_{k}+p_{l}>0}\frac{p_{k}+p_{l}}{2}X_{kl}\ket{k}\bra{l}
, where
X_{kl}=\bra{k}X\ket{l}
. It follows that the pseudoinverse of
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}
is given by
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{-1}(X)=\sum_{p_{k}+p_{l}>0}\frac{2}{p_{k}+p_{l}}X_{kl}\ket{k}\bra{l}

[
3
]
(see also Ref.
[
66
]
). Letting
L\coloneqq\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{-1}\left(i[\rho,H]\right)
, we obtain

\displaystyle F_{Q}=\norm{L}_{\rho}^{2},

(6)

i.e.,
F_{Q}
can be compactly expressed as the squared norm of
L
.

Second, we construct the Krylov subspaces with the aid of
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}
.
Hereafter, given a set of Hermitian operators
\{X_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
, we denote the span of this set by
\text{span}\{X_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
, which consists of all linear combinations generated by the set,
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}x_{k}X_{k}
, with real coefficients
x_{k}
. Let
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k}
denote the superoperator obtained by applying
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}
iteratively
k
times. By convention,
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{0}
is the identity map. With these notations, we define the
n
th Krylov subspace as follows:

Definition 1
.

\mathcal{K}_{n}\coloneqq\text{span}\left\{\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k}(i[\rho,H])\right\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
.

We refer to
\{\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k}(i[\rho,H])\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
as the generating set of
\mathcal{K}_{n}
. Apparently, the generating set of
\mathcal{K}_{n+1}
is constructed by augmenting that of
\mathcal{K}_{n}
with
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{n}(i[\rho,H])
. Consequently,
\mathcal{K}_{n}
expands as
n
increases. This expansion, however, eventually terminates at a certain integer
n^{*}
, since the dimension of
\mathcal{K}_{n}
cannot exceed that of the entire space of Hermitian operators. Therefore, we have

Observation 1:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{1}\subsetneq\mathcal{K}_{2}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}=\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}+j},

(7)

for any integer
j\geq 1
.
Moreover, we have

Observation 2:

\displaystyle L\in\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}~~\text{but}~~L\notin\mathcal{K}_{n}~~\text{whenever}~~n<n^{*}.

(8)

The proofs of the two observations are presented in Ref.
[
66
]
.

\mathcal{K}_{n}

, where
L_{n}\in\mathcal{K}_{n}

\hat{T}_{0},\cdots,\hat{T}_{2n-1}
and insert them into Eq. (

) to obtain
\hat{B}_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
. Here,
u_{i}

[

.

Third, we associate each
\mathcal{K}_{n}
with a non-polynomial lower bound
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
. To ensure that
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
effectively approximates
F_{Q}=\norm{L}_{\rho}^{2}
, we define
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}=\norm{L_n}_{\rho}^{2}
, where
L_{n}\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
is chosen to be as close to
L
as possible [see Fig.
1
(a)]. Formally,

Definition 2
.

B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}\coloneqq\norm{L_n}_{\rho}^{2}
with
\norm{L-L_n}_{\rho}=\min_{X\in\mathcal{K}_{n}}\norm{L-X}_{\rho}
.

To see that
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
is indeed a lower bound, let us recall that
L_{n}
attains
\min_{X\in\mathcal{K}_{n}}\norm{L-X}_{\rho}
if and only if it satisfies

\displaystyle\langle X,L-L_{n}\rangle_{\rho}=0,

(9)

for any
X\in\mathcal{K}_{n}

[
67
]
. Using Eq. (
9
) and noting that
L_{n}\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
, we have
\norm{L_n+L-L_n}_{\rho}^{2}=\norm{L_n}_{\rho}^{2}+\norm{L-L_n}_{\rho}^{2}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle F_{Q}=B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}+\norm{L-L_n}_{\rho}^{2}.

(10)

This implies
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}\leq F_{Q}
. That
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
is non-polynomial can be seen from its explicit expression presented below. Noting that
\norm{L-L_n}_{\rho}=\min_{X\in\mathcal{K}_{n}}\norm{L-X}_{\rho}\geq\min_{X\in\mathcal{K}_{n+1}}\norm{L-X}_{\rho}=\norm{L-L_{n+1}}_{\rho}
, we have
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}\leq B_{n+1}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
. Actually, we can show that
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}<B_{n+1}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
if
n<n^{*}

[
66
]
, as
\mathcal{K}_{n}
is a proper subset of
\mathcal{K}_{n+1}
(see
Observation 1
). Further, noting that
\norm{L-L_{n^*}}=0
because
L\in\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}
(see
Observation 2
), we deduce from Eq. (
10
) that
B_{n^{*}}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}=F_{Q}
.

Now we can present the first part of our main finding as follows:

\displaystyle B_{1}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}<B_{2}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}<\cdots<B_{n^{*}}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}=F_{Q},

(11)

stating that
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
is an increasingly better lower bound as
n
increases and eventually matches the QFI exactly when
n=n^{*}
.

Estimation of our lower bounds via shadow tomography.—
We need to derive the explicit expression of
B_{n}^{\textsf{(Kry)}}
. Let

\displaystyle T_{k}\coloneqq\tr\left(i[\rho,H]\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k}(i[\rho,H])\right).

(12)

Interestingly,
T_{k}
,
k=0,1,\cdots
, are polynomials of
\rho

[
66
]
. Specifically, the first two of them read
T_{0}=2\tr(\rho^2H^2-\rho H\rho H)
and
T_{1}=\tr(\rho^3 H^2-\rho^2H\rho H)
(see Ref.
[
66
]
for the general expression of
T_{k}
).
We introduce

\displaystyle A=\begin{bmatrix}T_{1}&T_{2}&\cdots&T_{n}\\
T_{2}&T_{3}&\cdots&T_{n+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
T_{n}&T_{n+1}&\cdots&T_{2n-1}\end{bmatrix},~~b=\begin{bmatrix}T_{0}\\
T_{1}\\
\vdots\\
T_{n-1}\end{bmatrix}.

(13)

Notably, each ascending skew-diagonal of
A
is constant, that is,
A
is a Hankel matrix
[
68
]
. When
n\leq n^{*}
,
A
is positive-definite and hence invertible
[
66
]
. With these notations, we can specify the rest part of our main finding as

\displaystyle B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}=b^{T}A^{-1}b,

(14)

showing that the lower bounds
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
, albeit being non-polynomial in nature, can be expressed in terms of the polynomials
T_{k}
through
A
and
b
(see Ref.
[
66
]
for the proof).

To see how to estimate
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
, let us recall that classical shadows can be efficiently generated by applying local randomized unitaries to
\rho
followed by standard measurements in the computational basis [see Fig.
1
(b)]. A key advantage of classical shadows is their ability to predict multiple polynomials of
\rho
simultaneously, provided a sufficient number of them are collected. This feature is particularly well-suited to our needs. Specifically, to estimate
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
, we generate a sufficient number of classical shadows and use them to predict
\hat{T}_{0},\hat{T}_{1},\cdots,\hat{T}_{2n-1}
simultaneously. Here and throughout, quantities with hats denote their estimates. Inserting all these
\hat{T}_{k}
’s into Eq. (
14
) yields an estimate of
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
. Details of this protocol are provided in Ref.
[
66
]
. A thorough analysis
[
66
]
shows that the number of classical shadows required here scales similarly, up to a constant factor, to that needed in the work
[
56
]
, which represents the state of the art in estimating the QFI for large systems. Note that the work
[
56
]
aims to estimate
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Tay})}
, which diverges significantly from the QFI when
n
is small. Note also that, as shown below,
n^{*}
is small in interesting contexts, for which
B_{n^{*}}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}=F_{Q}
. So, compared with the cutting-edge method
[
56
]
, our KST opens up the possibility of efficiently estimating the QFI itself without increasing resource demands.

Application 2: Bound entangled state.—
We now consider the state

\displaystyle\rho_{k}=\lambda P_{k}^{+}+\frac{\lambda}{2}(Q_{k}^{+}+Q_{k}^{-}),

(18)

where
P_{k}^{+}=\sum_{\#1(i)<k}\ket{\phi_i^+}\bra{\phi_i^+}
and
Q_{k}^{\pm}=\sum_{\#1(i)=k}\ket{\phi_i^\pm}\bra{\phi_i^\pm}

1\leq k\leq\lfloor{N/2}\rfloor

\ket{\phi_i^\pm}=\left(\ket{i}\pm\ket{\bar{i}}\right)/\sqrt{2}

. The notation

denotes the Hamming weight counting the number of ones in

. The state in Eq. (

) is a bound entangled state
[

]
, whose entanglement has been acknowledged to be difficult to detect in experiments.

is chosen as

[

n^{*}=1
, implying that

matches the QFI exactly

]
. So, we see again that the KST allows for fully unlocking the effectiveness of the QFI in applications.

Concluding remarks.
—We remark that
n^{*}
is small if
\rho
is of low rank, which arises frequently in practice
[
79
,
80
]
. However, while
n^{*}
is small in many scenarios, there are also cases where
n^{*}
is large. In these cases, it is preferable to work in
\mathcal{K}_{n}
with small
n
, as the current development in shadow tomography only allows for efficiently estimating polynomials of small order
[
58
,
59
]
. Notably, the QFI can usually be well approximated by
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
with small
n
due to the exponentially fast convergence of power iteration
[
81
]
. So, even when
n^{*}
is large, it is still expected that
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
’s with small
n
are quite good lower bounds on the QFI. We present in Ref.
[
66
]
some numerical results demonstrating the practical usefulness of
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}

In conclusion, we have developed the KST to tackle the long-standing challenge of estimating the QFI for large systems. Our idea is to construct the nested sequence of Krylov subspaces
\mathcal{K}_{n}
and associate each
\mathcal{K}_{n}

B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}

can approximate the QFI increasingly better as

\mathcal{K}_{n}

B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
can be expressed in terms of the polynomials
T_{k}
through
A
and
b

B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
to be accessible via shadow tomography.

We highlight that our KST allows for estimating not only non-polynomial lower bounds but, more importantly, the QFI itself in a resource-efficient and experimentally feasible manner. This offers a pathway to fully unlock the effectiveness of the QFI in vast applications. We have applied the KST to two interesting contexts, demonstrating that it opens the possibility of efficiently estimating the QFI itself without increasing resource demands.

Two directions for future works are to experimentally realize the KST and to further refine and extend it for estimating other physical quantities like various resource measures

B
Proofs of the two observations

Observation 1

The Krylov subspaces satisfy
\mathcal{K}_{1}\subsetneq\mathcal{K}_{2}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}=\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}+1}=\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}+2}=\cdots
for some positive integer
n^{*}
.

Proof.

Noting that the generating set of
\mathcal{K}_{2}
is
\left\{i[\rho,H],\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(i[\rho,H])\right\}
whereas that of
\mathcal{K}_{1}
is
\left\{i[\rho,H]\right\}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{1}\subset\mathcal{K}_{2}.

(S10)

\mathcal{K}_{1}
may or may not be a proper subset of
\mathcal{K}_{2}
, depending on whether the generating set of
\mathcal{K}_{2}
satisfies the linear independence condition. If the condition is satisfied, there is

\displaystyle\text{dim}(\mathcal{K}_{2})=2~~\text{whereas}~~\text{dim}(\mathcal{K}_{1})=1,

(S11)

where
\text{dim}(\mathcal{K}_{n})
denotes the dimension of
\mathcal{K}_{n}
.
This implies that

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{1}\subsetneq\mathcal{K}_{2}.

(S12)

Applying the above reasoning iteratively, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{n}\subsetneq\mathcal{K}_{n+1},

(S13)

provided that the generating set of
\mathcal{K}_{n+1}
satisfies the linear independence condition. This means that
\mathcal{K}_{n}

expands as
n
increases. However, this expansion must eventually terminate at some integer
n^{*}
, as the dimension of any Krylov subspace cannot exceed that of
\mathcal{X}
. The termination occurs when the generating set of
\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}
satisfies the linear independence condition whereas that of
\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}+1}
does not. We have
\text{dim}(\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}})=\text{dim}(\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}+1})
, implying that

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}=\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}+1}.

(S14)

Note that the generating set of
\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}+1}
is constructed by augmenting the generating set
\{\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k}(i[\rho,H])\}_{k=0}^{n^{*}-1}
of
\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}
with
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{n^{*}}(i[\rho,H])
.
From Eq. (
S14
), it follows that
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{n^{*}}(i[\rho,H])
can be expressed as a linear combination of
\{\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k}(i[\rho,H])\}_{k=0}^{n^{*}-1}
. Using this fact iteratively, we have that
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{n^{*}+j}(i[\rho,H])
is a linear combination of
\{\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k}(i[\rho,H])\}_{k=0}^{n^{*}-1}
for any
j\geq 1
. This implies
\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}=\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}+j}
for
j\geq 1
.
∎

Observation 2

The Hermitian operator
L\coloneqq\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{-1}(i[\rho,H])
belongs to the Krylov subspace
\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}
but not to
\mathcal{K}_{n}
with
n<n^{*}
.

Proof.

We first prove
L\in\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}
. Let
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}})
denote the subspace

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}})=\left\{\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(X):X\in\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}\right\}.

(S15)

Note that the generating set of
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}})
is
\{\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k}(i[\rho,H])\}_{k=1}^{n^{*}}
, which is contained by the generating set of
\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}+1}
, i.e.,
\{\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k}(i[\rho,H])\}_{k=0}^{n^{*}}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}})\subset\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}+1}.

(S16)

Noting that
\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}=\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}+1}
, we deduce from Eq. (
S16
) that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}})\subset\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}.

(S17)

Moreover, since
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}
is invertible, the dimension of
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}})
must be the same as that of
\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}
. Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}})=\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}.

(S18)

Note that
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(L)=\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{-1}(i[\rho,H]))=i[\rho,H]
.
Thus,
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(L)\in\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}
, which, in conjunction with Eq. (
S18
), leads to

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(L)\in\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}).

(S19)

Using Eq. (
S19
) and the invertibility of
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}
, we obtain

\displaystyle L\in\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}.

(S20)

To prove

with
n<n^{*}
, we assume, by contradiction, that

\displaystyle L\in\mathcal{K}_{n}~~\text{with}~~n<n^{*}.

(S21)

Then there is a linear combination of
\{\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k}(i[\rho,H])\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
such that

\displaystyle L=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}x_{k}\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k}(i[\rho,H]),

(S22)

where
x_{k}
is a real coefficient. Using
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(L)=i[\rho,H]
, we have

\displaystyle i[\rho,H]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k}(i[\rho,H]),

(S23)

meaning that
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k}(i[\rho,H])
,
k=0,1,\cdots,n
, are linearly dependent.
This contradicts the assumption that the generating set of
\mathcal{K}_{n+1}
satisfies the linear independence condition.
∎

C
Proof of the strict hierarchy of our bounds

Theorem 3

The non-polynomial lower bounds we formulate obey the strict hierarchy
B_{1}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}<B_{2}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}<\cdots<B_{n^{*}}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
.

We have shown in the main text that
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}\leq B_{n+1}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
for any
n\geq 1
. To establish that this inequality is strict for
n<n^{*}
, we need the assistance of the following two lemmas.

Lemma 4

There exit Hermitian operators
P_{0}
,
P_{1}
,
\cdots
,
P_{n^{*}-1}
orthonormal with respect to the weighted inner product in Eq. (
S2
), i.e.,
\langle P_{k},P_{l}\rangle_{\rho}=\delta_{kl}
, such that

\mathcal{K}_{n}=\text{span}\{P_{0},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n-1}\}
, for
n=1,2,\cdots,n^{*}
.

This lemma is a simple consequence of the Gram-Schmidt process for constructing an orthonormal basis from a set of vectors in an inner product space. As the proof is straightforward, we omit it. Lemma
C
provides a way to characterize the Krylov subspaces in terms of the orthonormal basis
\{P_{0},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n^{*}-1}\}
. The following lemma provides another way to characterize these subspaces.

Lemma 5

Let
Q_{n}=\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(L-L_{n})
with
n=0,1,\cdots,n^{*}-1
. Here, by convention,
L_{0}
is set to be
0
. Then, there is
\mathcal{K}_{n}=\text{span}\{Q_{0},Q_{1},\cdots,Q_{n-1}\}
, for
n=1,2,\cdots,n^{*}
.

Proof of Lemma
C
.

We have pointed out in the main text that

\displaystyle\langle X,L-L_{n}\rangle_{\rho}=0,

(S24)

for any
X\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
. Using Eq. (
S24
) and the relation between the two inner products given by Eq. (
S3
), we have that

\displaystyle(X,Q_{n})=(X,\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(L-L_{n}))=\langle X,L-L_{n}\rangle_{\rho}=0,

(S25)

for any
X\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
. This implies that
Q_{n}
is orthogonal to the Krylov subspace
\mathcal{K}_{n}
with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product. Here, by saying “
Q_{n}
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{K}_{n}
,” we mean
(Q_{n},X)=0
for all
X\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
. Note that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(L_{n})\in\mathcal{K}_{n+1},

(S26)

which, in conjunction with
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(L)=i[\rho,H]
, further leads to

\displaystyle Q_{n}=i[\rho,H]-\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(L_{n})\in\mathcal{K}_{n+1}.

(S27)

As the dimension of
\mathcal{K}_{n+1}
only increases by one in comparison with that of
\mathcal{K}_{n}
when
n<n^{*}
, we therefore deduce that

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{n+1}=\text{span}\{\mathcal{K}_{n},Q_{n}\},

(S28)

that is, the
(n+1)
th Krylov subspace is obtained by augmenting the
n
th Krylov subspace with
Q_{n}
. Here we have set
\mathcal{K}_{0}=0
by convention. Using Eq. (
S28
) iteratively, we conclude that
\mathcal{K}_{n}=\text{span}\{Q_{0},Q_{1},\cdots,Q_{n-1}\}
for
n\leq n^{*}
.
∎

Now, with Lemmas
C
and
C

Proof of Theorem
C
.

As stated in Lemma
C
, the orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}

\{P_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n^{*}-1}
. Noting that
L\in\mathcal{K}_{n^{*}}

\displaystyle L=\sum_{k=0}^{n^{*}-1}\langle P_{k},L\rangle_{\rho}P_{k}.

Likewise, since

\displaystyle x_{k}=\langle P_{k},L\rangle_{\rho}.

(S31)

Further, noting that

\displaystyle B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\langle P_{k},L\rangle_{\rho}^{2}.

Likewise,

\displaystyle B_{n+1}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\langle P_{k},L\rangle_{\rho}^{2}.

(S33)

Hence, to show that

\displaystyle\langle P_{n},L\rangle_{\rho}\neq 0.

(S34)

To this end, we assume, by contradiction, that
\langle P_{n},L\rangle_{\rho}=0

\displaystyle(P_{n},Q_{k})=(P_{n},\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(L-L_{k}))=\langle P_{n},L-L_{k}\rangle_{\rho}.

(S35)

Besides, note that
P_{n}

P_{0}
,
P_{1}
,
\cdots

P_{n-1}
with respect to the weighted inner product. From Lemma

P_{n}
is orthogonal to the Krylov subspace
\mathcal{K}_{k}

\langle P_{n},L_{k}\rangle_{\rho}=0

.
Then, from Eq. (
S35

\displaystyle(P_{n},Q_{k})=\langle P_{n},L\rangle_{\rho},

(S36)

which, under the assumption made, further implies that

\displaystyle(P_{n},Q_{k})=0,

(S37)

\mathcal{R}_{\rho}(X)=\frac{1}{2}(\rho X+X\rho)

\mu_{l}^{(k)}=\binom{k}{l}-2\binom{k}{l-1}+\binom{k}{l-2}.

(S42)

Here, by convention, we set
\binom{k}{l}=0

\Pi^{(k+2)}\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\ket{\psi_2}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\psi_{k+2}}=\ket{\psi_2}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\psi_{k+2}}\otimes\ket{\psi_{1}},

N

\displaystyle\tr\left(H\rho^{l}H\rho^{k-l+2}\right)=

\displaystyle\tr[\Pi^{(k+2)}\left(H\otimes\openone^{\otimes(l-1)}\otimes H\otimes\openone^{\otimes(k-l+1)}\right)\rho^{\otimes(k+2)}].

Proposition 6

The Hankel matrix
A
is positive-definite when
n\leq n^{*}
.

Proof.

To maintain consistency with the notations in this work, we index the elements of

A
using
k,l\in\{0,1,\cdots,n-1\}
. That is, the
(k,l)

A
is given by

\displaystyle A_{kl}=T_{k+l+1},

(S50)

which, in conjunction with the definition of
T_{k+l+1}
, leads to

\displaystyle A_{kl}=\left(i[\rho,H],\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k+l+1}(i[\rho,H])\right).

(S51)

As
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}
and hence
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k+l+1}
are Hermitian superoperators with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product [see the text below Eq. (
S3
)],

A_{kl}
is real, i.e.,
A
is a real matrix. To prove that
A
is positive-definite, we need to show

\displaystyle v^{T}Av>0,

(S52)

for any nonzero real vector
v=[v_{0},v_{1},\cdots,v_{n-1}]^{T}
. Note that

\displaystyle\left(i[\rho,H],\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k+l+1}(i[\rho,H])\right)=\left\langle\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k}(i[\rho,H]),\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{l}(i[\rho,H])\right\rangle_{\rho},

where we have used the Hermiticity of
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}
with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product and the relation between the two inner product. From Eq. (

), it follows that

\displaystyle A_{kl}=\langle\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k}(i[\rho,H]),\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{l}(i[\rho,H])\rangle_{\rho}.

(S53)

Then, after some simple algebra, we arrive at the equality,

\displaystyle v^{T}Av=\norm{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}v_k\mathcal{R}_\rho^{k}(i[\rho,H])}_{\rho}^{2}.

(S54)

Besides, note that
\{\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k}(i[\rho,H])\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
are linearly independent when

, which implies that
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}v_{k}\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{k}(i[\rho,H])\neq 0

.
∎

Noting that

\displaystyle L_{n}=\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}x_{l}\mathcal{R}_{\rho}^{l}(i[\rho,H]),

(S56)

x_{l}

\displaystyle B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}=\norm{L_n}_{\rho}^{2}

\displaystyle B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}=x^{T}Ax.

\displaystyle\langle X,L-L_{n}\rangle_{\rho}=0,

Details on the shadow tomography protocol

Figure S1:
Schematic of the shadow tomography protocol for estimating
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
. Let
\rho
be the unknown state of
N
qubits. A randomly selected unitary operation
U=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}
is applied to
\rho
, followed by the projective measurement in the computational basis with outcome recorded as a bit string
s=(s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{N})
. This procedure is repeated
M
times. Consequently,
M
classical shadows can be constructed, which can be employed to simultaneously predict
\hat{T}_{0},\cdots,\hat{T}_{2n-1}
. Inserting these
\hat{T}_{k}
into Eq. (
S55
) finally produces an estimate
\hat{B}_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
.

We specify how to estimate
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
using shadow tomography
[
37
,
38
]
.
A schematic representation of the protocol is provided in Fig.
S1
. Let
U
denote a unitary operation that is selected at random from a suitable ensemble of unitary operations. Specifically, we consider the setting that

U=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}u_{j},

(S65)

where
u_{j}
, acting on qubit
j
, is drawn from an ensemble of single-qubit unitary operations that evenly cover the Bloch sphere. Examples of such ensembles include the single-qubit Clifford group and the full unitary group
U(2)
. We apply
U
to the state
\rho
and perform the measurement in the computational basis. The measurement outcome is recorded as a bit string
s=(s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{N})
. We repeat this procedure for
M
randomly selected operations

U^{(m)}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}^{(m)},

(S66)

with
m=1,2,\cdots,M
. Assigned to each
U^{(m)}
, a bit string

s^{(m)}=(s_{1}^{(m)},s_{2}^{(m)},\cdots,s_{N}^{(m)})

(S67)

is stored for recording the measurement outcome when
U^{(m)}
is applied.
We associate each pair of
U^{(m)}
and
s^{(m)}
with

\hat{\rho}^{(m)}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}\left(3u_{j}^{(m)\dagger}\ket{s_j^{(m)}}\bra{s_j^{(m)}}u_{j}^{(m)}-\openone\right),

(S68)

where
\openone
is the identity operator. The operator
\hat{\rho}^{(m)}
,
known as a classical shadow, reproduces the state
\rho
exactly in expectation over both unitaries and measurement outcomes,

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\rho}^{(m)}]=\rho.

(S69)

Intuitively speaking,
\hat{\rho}^{(m)}
can be viewed as a classical snapshot of
\rho
.

To estimate
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
, we need to use the
M
classical shadows to predict
\hat{T}_{0},\hat{T}_{1},\cdots,\hat{T}_{2n-1}
simultaneously
[
39
]
, as clarified in the main text. Here and throughout this work, a quantity with a hat represents its estimate. For example,
\hat{B}_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
is used to denote an estimate of
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
. We divide the
M
classical shadows into
I
equally sized subsamples. The
i
-th subsample consists of the classical shadows
\hat{\rho}^{(m)}
with indexes
m
belonging to the following set

S_{i}=\left\{(i-1)L+1,(i-1)L+2,\cdots,iL\right\},

(S70)

where
L
represents the size of each subsample. Accordingly, there are
M=IL
classical shadows in total. Recall that

T_{k}=\tr[O^{(k+2)}\rho^{\otimes(k+2)}],

(S71)

i.e.,
T_{k}
is a
(k+2)
-order polynomial of
\rho
. The approach based on
U
-statistics
[
39
]
can be used to estimate such polynomials,

\hat{T}_{k}^{(i)}=\frac{1}{(k+2)!}\binom{L}{k+2}^{-1}\sum_{m_{1}\neq\cdots\neq m_{k+2}}\tr[O^{(k+2)}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k+2}\hat{\rho}^{(m_{j})}],

(S72)

where we require that all
m_{j}
,
j=1,2,\cdots,k+2
, belong to
S_{i}
, that is, all the classical shadows appearing in Eq. (
S72
) are drawn from the
i
-th subsample. Using all the
I
subsamples, we obtain a sequence of estimates of
T_{k}
,

\hat{T}_{k}^{(1)},\hat{T}_{k}^{(2)},\cdots,\hat{T}_{k}^{(I)}.

(S73)

To avoid the so-called outlier corruption
[
39
]
, we adopt the median-of-means estimator to obtain a final estimate of
T_{k}
,

\hat{T}_{k}=\text{median}\left\{\hat{T}_{k}^{(1)},\hat{T}_{k}^{(2)},\cdots,\hat{T}_{k}^{(I)}\right\}.

(S74)

The above procedure for obtaining
\hat{T}_{k}
is schematically shown in Fig.
S2
. Notably, all the
\hat{T}_{k}
,
k=0,1,\cdots,2n-1
, can be obtained simultaneously via this procedure. Finally, we insert these
\hat{T}_{k}
in Eq. (
S55
) to yield an estimate
\hat{B}_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
.

Figure S2:

. We divide the

\hat{T}_{k}^{(i)}

is produced from the estimates
\hat{T}_{k}^{(1)},\hat{T}_{k}^{(2)},\cdots,\hat{T}_{k}^{(I)}

To finalize the description of the shadow tomography protocol, it remains to choose appropriate values for
I
and
L
, and figure out the estimation error resulting from these choices. A detailed analysis in Sec.
H
shows that the protocol works well when
I
and
L
are chosen as follows:

I=8\ln\frac{2n}{\delta},

(S75)

and

L=\max_{1\leq l\leq k+2\leq 2n+1}\left[\left(\frac{4(k+2)(k+2)!^{2}\tr(\left[O_l^{(k+2)}\right]^2)}{l!(k-l+2)!^{2}\epsilon^{2}}\right)^{1/l}2^{N}+l-1\right],

(S76)

where
\epsilon
and
\delta
are prescribed accuracy parameters. The maximum in Eq. (
S76
) is taken over all indices
k
and
l
satisfying
1\leq l\leq k+2\leq 2n+1
, and
O_{l}^{(k+2)}
is defined as

\displaystyle O_{l}^{(k+2)}=\tr_{l+1,\cdots,k+2}\left[\overline{O}^{(k+2)}\left(\openone^{\otimes l}\otimes\rho^{\otimes(k-l+2)}\right)\right],

where the partial trace is taken over the copies indexed by
l+1,l+2,\cdots,k+2
, and
\overline{O}^{(k+2)}
is the symmetrized operator described by Eq. (
S49
). Notably,
O_{l}^{(k+2)}
is an operator acting on
l
copies of the
N
qubits. Formally, we summarize the result as a theorem (see Sec.
H
for its proof):

Theorem 8

Let
\epsilon
and
\delta
be prescribed accuracy parameters, and
I
and
L
be given by Eqs. (
S75
) and (
S76
), respectively. Then, the estimate
\hat{B}_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
obtained from the shadow tomography protocol obeys the inequality

\absolutevalue{\hat{B}_n^{(\mathsf{Kry})}-B_n^{(\mathsf{Kry})}}<2n\epsilon\max_{0\leq k\leq 2n-1}\absolutevalue{\frac{\partial}{\partial T_k}B_n^{(\mathsf{Kry})}}

(S78)

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Here,
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
is viewed as a function of
T_{0},T_{1},\cdots,T_{2n-1}
through Eq. (
S55
).

Theorem
G
shows that a collection of
IL
randomly generated classical shadows enables accurate estimation of
B_{n}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}
with high probability. In the main text, we demonstrate that
n^{*}
is small in interesting contexts, for which
B_{n^{*}}^{(\mathsf{Kry})}=F_{Q}
. In these cases, our protocol can accurately estimate the quantum Fisher information (QFI) itself using the
IL
classical shadows. Notably,

is constant. Furthermore, the

in Eq. (

[

[

Error analysis

To prove Theorem
G
, we need the assistance of the following lemmas.

Lemma 9

Let
I=8\ln\frac{2n}{\delta}
. Under the condition that
\text{Pr}\left(\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k^{(i)}-T_k}\geq\epsilon\right)\leq\frac{1}{4}
for all
k=0,1,\cdots,2n-1
,

\text{Pr}\left(\bigcap_{k=0}^{2n-1}\left\{\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k-T_k}<\epsilon\right\}\right)\geq 1-\delta,

(S79)

where
\text{Pr}(\cdot)
is the probability of the event in question.

Lemma
H
provides a sufficient condition for accurately estimating all the
T_{k}
’s simultaneously with probability at least
1-\delta
. Notably, while
T_{k}
is specified by Eq. (
S41
) in the present work, Lemma
H
holds as well if
T_{k}
is used to represent any other physical property, e.g., the expectation value of an observable. This point can be easily seen from the proof presented below. Consequently, Lemma
H
generalizes one of the main results in Ref.
[
39
]
, which establishes a sufficient condition for accurately predicting multiple expectation values of observables simultaneously.

Proof of Lemma
H
.

Noting that the complement of the set
\bigcap_{k=0}^{2n-1}\left\{\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k-T_k}<\epsilon\right\}
is

\bigcup_{k=0}^{2n-1}\left\{\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k-T_k}\geq\epsilon\right\}
, we have

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left(\bigcap_{k=0}^{2n-1}\left\{\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k-T_k}<\epsilon\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\text{Pr}\left(\bigcup_{k=0}^{2n-1}\left\{\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k-T_k}\geq\epsilon\right\}\right).

(S80)

Further, noting that
\text{Pr}\left(\bigcup_{k=0}^{2n-1}\left\{\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k-T_k}\geq\epsilon\right\}\right)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{2n-1}\text{Pr}\left(\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k-T_k}\geq\epsilon\right)
, we deduce from Eq. (
S80
) that

\text{Pr}\left(\bigcap_{k=0}^{2n-1}\left\{\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k-T_k}<\epsilon\right\}\right)\geq 1-\sum_{k=0}^{2n-1}\text{Pr}\left(\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k-T_k}\geq\epsilon\right).

(S81)

Therefore, to prove Eq. (
S79
), it suffices to show that

\text{Pr}\left(\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k-T_k}\geq\epsilon\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{2n},

(S82)

for all
k=0,1,\cdots,2n-1
. Recall that
\hat{T}_{k}
is obtained from
\hat{T}_{k}^{(1)},\hat{T}_{k}^{(2)},\cdots,\hat{T}_{k}^{(I)}
via the median-of-means estimator,

\hat{T}_{k}=\text{median}\{\hat{T}_{k}^{(1)},\hat{T}_{k}^{(2)},\cdots,\hat{T}_{k}^{(I)}\}.

(S83)

Hence, the event
\left\{\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k-T_k}\geq\epsilon\right\}
occurs only when at least
I/2
of the
\hat{T}_{k}^{(i)}
’s lie outside
\epsilon
-distance to
T_{k}
. That is,

\left\{\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k-T_k}\geq\epsilon\right\}\subset\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{I}\mathcal{I}\left(\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k^{(i)}-T_k}\geq\epsilon\right)\geq\frac{I}{2}\right\},

(S84)

where
\mathcal{I}\left(\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k^{(i)}-T_k}\geq\epsilon\right)
denotes the indicator function of the event
\left\{\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k^{(i)}-T_k}\geq\epsilon\right\}
, that is, this function outputs
1
when
\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k^{(i)}-T_k}\geq\epsilon
and
0
otherwise. We introduce

Z_{k}^{(i)}=\mathcal{I}\left(\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k^{(i)}-T_k}\geq\epsilon\right),

(S85)

which represents a random variable with the expectation value

P_{k}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}^{(i)}]=\text{Pr}\left(\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k^{(i)}-T_k}\geq\epsilon\right).

(S86)

It is noteworthy that
\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}^{(i)}]
is independent of
i
, as all the
I
subsamples are identical from a statistical perspective. Using Eqs. (
S85
) and (
S86
), we can rewrite Eq. (
S84
) as

\left\{\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k-T_k}\geq\epsilon\right\}\subset\left\{\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\left(Z_{k}^{(i)}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}^{(i)}]\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}-P_{k}\right\}.

(S87)

Hence,

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left(\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k-T_k}\geq\epsilon\right)\leq

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left(\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\left(Z_{k}^{(i)}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}^{(i)}]\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}-P_{k}\right).

(S88)

Using the one-side Hoeffding’s inequality
[
90
]
, we can bound the probability in the right-hand side of Eq. (
S88
) as

\text{Pr}\left(\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\left(Z_{k}^{(i)}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}^{(i)}]\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}-P_{k}\right)\leq e^{-2I(\frac{1}{2}-P_{k})^{2}}.

(S89)

Substituting Eq. (
S89
) into Eq. (
S88
) and further noting that
P_{k}\leq\frac{1}{4}
according to the condition in Lemma
H
, we have

\text{Pr}\left(\absolutevalue{\hat{T}_k-T_k}\geq\epsilon\right)\leq e^{-\frac{I}{8}}.

(S90)

Inserting
I=8\ln\frac{2n}{\delta}
into Eq. (
S90
), we finally arrive at Eq. (
S82
).
∎
