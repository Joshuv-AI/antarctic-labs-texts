First to reach n n game
Stanislav Volkov, Magnus Wiktorsson

Abstract
We consider a game with two players, consisting of a number of rounds, where the first player to win n n rounds becomes the overall winner. Who wins each individual round is governed by a certain urn having two types of balls (type 1 and type 2). At each round, we randomly pick a ball from the urn, and its type determines which of the two players wins. We study the game under three regimes. In the first and the third regimes, a ball is taken without replacement, whilst in the second regime, it is returned to the urn with one more ball of the same colour. We study the properties of the random variables equal to the properly defined overall net profits of the players, and the results are drastically different in all three regimes. Keywords: Gambler’s ruin problem, Rubin’s construction, Pólya’s urn AMS 2010 subject classifications: 60C05, 60J10, 91A05

1
Introduction

Several real-life games and sports use a format that can be viewed as the “first to exactly
n
wins” rule. Under this rule, two players play a number of rounds, such that there is a clear winner in each round. The match continues until one player wins exactly
n
rounds (or sets), at which point the game ends immediately. The person who won
n
rounds is declared the overall winner and gets a certain reward. This structure is common in fighting games like
Street Fighter
or
Mortal Kombat
, racket sports such as tennis, table tennis, and badminton, as well as in some chess championship formats where the first player to win a set number of games is declared the winner. Unlike games with a fixed number of rounds, these formats feature a variable-length match with a clear win condition based on reaching a target number of victories, making them natural real-world analogues of the probabilistic game model described below, where the game continues until one side reaches
n
wins.

Somewhat relevant models were considered in
Antal, Ben-Naim and Krapivsky 2010
and
Jansson 2016
. In
Antal, Ben-Naim and Krapivsky 2010
, the authors studied when the numbers of balls in the Pólya’s urn become equal, and
Jansson 2016
investigated the question of “eternal lead” of one of the players. Our models also comfortably fall in the general category of time-inhomogeneous Markov chains, see e.g.
Engländer and Volkov 2025
.

The formal description of our initial model is as follows. Given a positive integer
n
, two players engage in the following game, consisting of at most
2n-1
rounds. In round
i=1,2,3,\dots
, Player 1 wins with probability
p_{i}\in(0,1)
, and Player 2 wins with probability
q_{i}=1-p_{i}
. The game continues until the first time one of the players has won exactly
n
rounds. The first player to reach
n
wins is declared the overall winner and receives a reward (from the other player) equal to the difference between their number of wins (which is
n
for the overall winner) and the number of wins accumulated by the opponent, denoted by
W_{n,p}\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
. Let
Z_{n,p}\in[-n,n]
be the (random) net profit of Player 1 as a function of
n
and
p
. Note that

Z_{n,p}=(n-W_{n,p})\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{Player 1 won}\}}+(W_{n,p}-n)\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{Player 2 won}\}}.

The goal of this paper is to study the properties of
W_{n,p}
and
Z_{n,p}
.

We will consider
three
versions of the above setup. In the first model (“Constant model”)
p_{i}\equiv p
is the same for all rounds. The second model (“Pólya model”) assumes that the probabilities of winning are governed by a Pólya urn and thus the probability of winning a round for a player increases with the number of wins so far by that player. The third model (“Anti-OK Corral model”) assumes precisely the opposite. The detailed descriptions of the models will be given further in the paper.

The first model, with constant
p_{i}
, provided the original motivation for this paper and contains our main results.

2
Constant model

In this version of the model, we assume that
p_{i}\equiv p
and
q_{i}\equiv q=1-p
for all
i
. Note that this model can be viewed as an “asymptotic” urn model described in the abstract, with very large initial numbers of balls,
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
, such that
N_{1}/N_{2}\approx p/q
. Formally, if
N_{1}=\lfloor pt\rfloor
and
N_{2}=\lfloor qt\rfloor
, as
t\to+\infty
, we obtain the current setup.

A similar model was also studied in
Addona, Wilfand Wagon 2011
. In this paper, the game setup remained the same, but one player had the option to stop the game before playing all rounds. The authors were interested in finding the optimal strategy to maximise the expected profit.

2.1
Martingale approach

Before stating the exact results, we want to provide some intuition behind them. The intuition is based on constructing a certain martingale, which is a quite usual technique in such models (see e.g.
Kingman 1999
).

Let
X_{k}
be the current difference between wins of the first and the second player after round
k
, with
X_{0}=0
. Then
M_{k}=X_{k}-\mu k
, where
\mu=p-q
, is a martingale. Letting
\tau
be the stopping time when
|X_{k}|
hits the “line”
2n-k
for the first time.
This can at the latest happen when
k=2n-1
. Since
\tau\leq 2n-1
using the Optional Stopping Theorem we get

0=M_{0}=\mathbb{E}M_{\tau}=\mathbb{E}X_{\tau}-\mu\mathbb{E}\tau

where
\tau
is the index of the last round. On the other hand,

|X_{\tau}|=n-(\tau-n)
, implying

\mathbb{E}|X_{\tau}|=2n-\mathbb{E}\tau,\quad\mathbb{E}|X_{\tau}|\geq|\mathbb{E}X_{\tau}|=|\mu|\mathbb{E}\tau

yielding

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\tau\leq\frac{2n}{1+|\mu|}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|\mathbb{E}X_{\tau}|\leq\frac{2n|\mu|}{1+|\mu|}=\frac{n|\mu|}{\max(p,q)}.

(1)

When
p>q
, giving
|\mu|=\mu
, and
n
is large, it seems quite unlikely that the second player might reach
n
wins before the first one does. As a result, with probability close to one
X_{\tau}
will be positive, and hence
\mathbb{E}|X_{\tau}|\approx\mathbb{E}X_{\tau}
, so that we can expect that the inequalities in (
1
) become asymptotically equalities.

Another useful martingale is available in the case
p=q=1/2
. In this case,
\tilde{M}_{k}=X_{k}^{2}-k
is also a martingale, hence

0=\tilde{M}_{0}=\mathbb{E}\tilde{M}_{\tau}=\mathbb{E}X_{\tau}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\tau

On the other hand,
|X_{\tau}|=2n-\tau
as before, hence, by Jensen’s inequality

0=4n^{2}-4n\mathbb{E}\tau+\mathbb{E}(\tau^{2})-\mathbb{E}\tau\geq 4n^{2}-(4n+1)\mathbb{E}\tau+(\mathbb{E}\tau)^{2}

yielding

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\tau\geq 2n-\frac{\sqrt{8n+1}-1}{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbb{E}|X_{\tau}|\leq\frac{\sqrt{8n+1}-1}{2}.

(2)

2.2
Main results

Now, note that by symmetry it suffices to consider only the case
p\geq 1/2
, so we will assume this in the rest of the section. Denote by
\mu=p-q\geq 0
the expected gain of one round.
The following statement gives an exact explicit formula for
E_{n,p}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
E_{n,p}=\mathbb{E}Z_{n,p}
and
z=pq
. Then

\displaystyle E_{n,p}=n\mu\,\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}C_{j}z^{j}

(3)

where
C_{j}=\frac{(2j)!}{j!(j+1)!}
are Catalan numbers.

Remark 2.2
.

Catalan numbers have the generator function

c(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}C_{j}z^{j}=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4z}}{2z}=1+z+2z^{2}+5z^{3}+14z^{4}+42z^{5}+132z^{6}+\dots.

hence
E_{n,p}
are the first
n
terms in the Taylor expansion of
c(z)
.

Corollary 2.3
.

We have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{p\downarrow\frac{1}{2}}\frac{E_{n,p}}{n\mu}=2.

We also want to study the asymptotic behaviour of
W_{n,p}
and
Z_{n,p}
as
n\to\infty
.

Theorem 2.4
.

We have

(a)

For all
0\leq k<n

\mathbb{P}(W_{n,p}=k)=\binom{n+k-1}{n-1}q^{k}p^{n}+\alpha_{n,p}

where
|\alpha_{n,p}|\leq(4pq)^{n}
.

(b)

As
n\to\infty
,

\frac{pZ_{n,p}-\mu n}{\sqrt{nq}}\to\mathcal{N}(0,1)\text{ in distribution.}

Remark 2.5
.

While part (a) of Theorem
2.4
formally applies to the case
p=1/2
, it is meaningful only for
p\in(1/2,1)
.

Remark 2.6
.

Part (b) of Theorem
2.4
immediately implies that
\frac{Z_{n,p}}{n}\to 1-\frac{q}{p}
in probability.

Remark 2.7
.

Part (b) of Theorem
2.4
also implies that in case
p=q=1/2
the expected profit of the winning player is asymptotically
2\sqrt{n/\pi}\approx 1.13\sqrt{n}
(compare this with (
2
)).

3
Pólya’s model

In this version of the game, we assume that we have a classical Pólya urn initially having
N_{i}\geq 1
of balls of type
i=1,2
. At each round, we take a ball from the urn at random, and then return it to the urn with one more ball of the same type. The type of ball drawn determines which of the two players wins the round.

Recall that the Pólya’s urn model can be realised as follows. Let
\xi\sim\mathrm{Beta}(N_{1},N_{2})
and then, given
\xi
, at each round draw a ball of type 1 (2 resp.) with probability
\xi
(
1-\xi
resp), independently of the past. Then at each round the distribution of balls will be exactly as in the Pólya’s urn. Hence, by Theorem
2.4
, part (b), taking into account that we are no longer guaranteed the first player winning,

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\max(\xi,1-\xi)\,\tilde{Z}_{n}-n(2\xi-1)}{\sqrt{n\min(\xi,1-\xi)}}\leq a\mid\xi\right)\to\Phi(a)

where
\tilde{Z}_{n}
is the overall win of the first player.
In particular, this implies that conditionally on
\xi
,

\frac{\tilde{Z}_{n}}{n}\to\frac{2\xi-1}{\max(\xi,1-\xi)}=\begin{cases}\frac{\xi}{1-\xi}-1,&\text{if }\xi<1/2;\\
1-\frac{1-\xi}{\xi},&\text{if }\xi\geq 1/2\end{cases}

(see Remark
2.6
), hence
\tilde{Z}_{n}/n
converges (unconditionally) in distribution
to a random variable
\zeta
with the density

f_{\zeta}(x)=\frac{\Gamma(N_{1}+N_{2})}{\Gamma(N_{1})\Gamma(N_{2})\,(2-|x|)^{N_{1}+N_{2}}}\cdot\begin{cases}(1-|x|)^{N_{2}-1},&\text{ if }x\in[0,1];\\
(1-|x|)^{N_{1}-1},&\text{ if }x\in[-1,0].\end{cases}

and, in particular, the probability that the first player will be a net winner is

\mathbb{P}(\text{Player 1 wins overall})=\frac{\Gamma(N_{1}+N_{2})}{\Gamma(N_{1})\Gamma(N_{2})}\,\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-x)^{N_{2}-1}}{(2-x)^{N_{1}+N_{2}}}dx.

In the special case
N_{1}=N_{2}=n
, the asymptotic expected profit of the winning player is

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}|x|nf_{\zeta}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{(2n)!}{(n-1)!^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x(1-x)^{n-1}}{(2-x)^{2n}}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-1}\left(\frac{(2n)!}{(n-1)!^{2}2^{2n-1}}-n\right)=2\sqrt{\frac{n}{\pi}}-1+O(n^{-1/2}).

This result is not surprising, as for large
n
the transition probabilities in the Pólya’s model approach the ones for the constant case model – please see Remark
2.7
.

4
Anti-OK Corral model

This version of the model is also governed by an urn. Suppose that there are initially
n
balls of each type (
1,2
) in the urn. In each round, we take a ball at random
without replacement
, and the type of ball determines who wins the round. The person who first gets
n
balls of its type (that is, has won
n
rounds) wins the game. This model is reminiscent of the OK Corral model studied in
Kingman 1999
;
Kingman and Volkov 2003
;
Williams and McIlroy 1998
, except instead of favouring those who won the most so far, it favours the other player (hence the name of this section).

It is possible to construct a Markov process that resembles the random walk for the constant case. The issue is that the transition probabilities become both state and time-dependent. Let
D_{k}
be the difference between the number of balls of type one and type two at time
k
. Thus
D_{0}=0
. Now we have

\displaystyle P(D_{k+1}=1+D_{k}|D_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{D_{k}}{2n-k}\right),

\displaystyle P(D_{k+1}=-1+D_{k}|D_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{D_{k}}{2n-k}\right).

This is, in fact, equivalent to a Markov representation of a simple random walk bridge on the time interval
0
to
2n
. We now run this process up to the stopping time
\tau^{(n)}
when
|D_{k}|
hits the “line”
2n-k
for the first time. Since the total number of balls remaining at time
k
is
2n-k
, it is evident that all remaining balls must be of the same type at the first time
|D_{k}|=2n-k
and thus all of the
n
balls of one of the two types have been taken. If
D_{\tau^{(n)}}=2n-\tau^{(n)}
then all the remaining balls are of type one and if
D_{\tau^{(n)}}=-(2n-\tau^{(n)})
then all remaining balls are of type two.

In our case, it turns out that it is possible to solve the problem by direct combinatorial calculations. The probability that the first player wins overall and the other player wins
n-k\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
rounds is

\frac{\displaystyle\frac{n!}{(n-1)!}\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}}{\displaystyle\frac{2n!}{((n-1)+(n-k))!}}=\frac{\displaystyle n(2n-1-k)!n!}{\displaystyle 2n!(n-k)!}.

(4)

In particular, if
k=1
, (
4
) becomes

\frac{n(2n-2)!n!}{(2n)!(n-1)!}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{n}{2n-1}=\frac{1}{4}+o(1).

Applying Stirling’s formula to the expression (
4
) we get

\displaystyle\frac{n\sqrt{2\pi{n}}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}\sqrt{2\pi{(2n-1-k)}}\left(\frac{2n-1-k}{e}\right)^{2n-1-k}}{\sqrt{2\pi{(n-k)}}\left(\frac{n-k}{e}\right)^{n-k}\sqrt{2\pi{2n}}\left(\frac{2n}{e}\right)^{2n}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{n(2n-k)}{(n-k)2n}}\frac{e\left(1-\frac{k+1}{2n}\right)^{2n}}{\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)^{n}}2^{-k-1}\left(\frac{2n-2k}{2n-1-k}\right)^{k}\frac{2n}{2n-1-k}.

As
n
tends to infinity, this converges to

\frac{e^{-k}}{e^{-k}}2^{-1-k}=2^{-k-1}.

By symmetry, the probability for the reversed situation (when the second player is winning) is the same. This means that the limiting distribution of the net profit of a player is an equal mixture of two geometric distributions on
\{1,2,\dots\}
, both with probability
1/2
.

Proofs

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

An easy calculation shows that

E_{n,p}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{P}(\text{Player 1 wins, }W_{n,p}=j)\cdot(n-j)-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{P}(\text{Player 2 wins, }W_{n,p}=i)\cdot(n-i).

To reach
n
wins for one player before the other, the game must last
n+k-1
rounds, and the winner must win the last round. The probability that the winning player whose probability of winning is
\theta
reaches
n
wins while the other player has exactly
k
wins equals

h(\theta,k)=\binom{n+k-1}{k}\cdot\theta^{n}\cdot(1-\theta)^{k},

where
\theta=p
or
\theta=1-p
. Then the expected net profit is:

\displaystyle E_{n,p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(n-k)h(p,k)-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(n-k)h(q,k)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n+k-1}{k}\left(p^{n}q^{k}-q^{n}p^{k}\right)(n-k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n+k-1}{k}(pq)^{k}\left(p^{n-k}-q^{n-k}\right)(n-k)

\displaystyle=(p-q)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n+k-1}{k}(n-k)(pq)^{n}A_{n-k}

where
A_{m}=\frac{p^{m}-q^{m}}{p-q}
. Then for
m=1,2,\dots
, noting that
p+q=1
,

A_{m}=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{m-1}{2}\rfloor}\binom{m-1-i}{i}(-z)^{i},\quad z=pq

(this can be shown by induction as
A_{m+2}=A_{m+1}-zA_{m}
, and
A_{1}=A_{2}=1
).
Hence, with
z=pq
, using the change of order of summation, we have

\displaystyle\frac{E_{n,p}}{p-q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n+k-1}{k}(n-k)z^{k}\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n-k-1}{2}\rfloor}\binom{n-k-1-i}{i}(-z)^{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1+k}{k}(n-k)\sum_{j=k}^{\lfloor\frac{n+k-1}{2}\rfloor}z^{j}\binom{n-1-j}{j-k}(-1)^{j-k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{j}z^{j}\sum_{k=0}^{j}\binom{n-1+k}{k}\binom{n-1-j}{j-k}(-1)^{k}(n-k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{j}z^{j}\times\frac{(-1)^{j}\ n}{j+1}\binom{2j}{j}=n\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{z^{j}}{j+1}\binom{2j}{j}

where the penultimate equality is due to Lemma
5.1
.
∎

Lemma 5.1
.

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{j}\binom{n-1+k}{k}\binom{n-1-j}{j-k}(-1)^{k}(n-k)=\frac{(-1)^{j}\ n}{j+1}\binom{2j}{j}=(-1)^{j}\ n\ C_{j}

(5)

Proof.

First, we will use the following (probably, known) identity.
Assume
a\geq b+m
,
m\geq 0
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{k}\binom{a+k}{a}\binom{b}{m-k}=(-1)^{m}\binom{a-b+m}{m}.

(6)

Indeed, the LHS of (
6
) is the coefficient on
x^{a+m}
in

\displaystyle\sum_{u=0}^{b}\sum_{\ell=a}^{\infty}\binom{\ell}{a}\binom{b}{u}(-1)^{l-a}x^{u+\ell}=\left[\sum_{u=0}^{b}\binom{b}{u}x^{u}\right]\times\left[\sum_{\ell=a}^{\infty}\binom{\ell}{a}(-1)^{l-a}x^{\ell}\right]

\displaystyle=(1+x)^{b}\times\frac{x^{a}}{(1+x)^{a+1}}=x^{a}(1+x)^{-(a-b+1)}=x^{a}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}\binom{a-b+m}{m}x^{m}.

Next,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{j}\binom{n-1+k}{k}\binom{n-1-j}{j-k}(-1)^{k}(-k)

\displaystyle=n\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}(-1)^{k}\binom{n+k}{k}\binom{n-1-j}{j-1-k}.

We will now use twice the identity proven above.
Setting
a=n-1
,
m=j
(
a=n
,
m=j-1
respectively) and
b=n-1-j
in (
6
) gives

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{k}\binom{n-1+k}{k}\binom{n-1-j}{j-k}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j}\binom{2j}{j};

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}(-1)^{k}\binom{n+k}{k}\binom{n-1-j}{j-1-k}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j}\binom{2j}{j-1}

which yields (
5
) as
C_{j}=\binom{2j}{j}-\binom{2j}{j-1}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

We separately consider the cases when
p=1/2
and when
p>1/2
. We will use the Poisson process representation, similar to Rubin’s construction in
Davis 1990
or the one in
Kingman and Volkov 2003
.

Let
\lambda=q/p\leq 1
. Consider two independent Poisson process,
X(t)
with rate
1
, and
Y(t)
with rate
\lambda
. Let

\tau_{X}=\tau_{X}^{(n)}=\min\{t:\ X_{t}=n\},\quad\tau_{Y}=\tau_{Y}^{(n)}=\min\{t:\ Y_{t}=n\},\quad\tau=\tau^{(n)}=\min(\tau_{X},\tau_{Y}).

Then
Z=X(\tau)-Y(\tau)
has the same distribution as our quantity of interest
Z_{n,p}
. Indeed, the embedded Markov chain for the process
(X(t),Y(t))
,
t\geq 0
, has exactly the same transition probabilities as in the game. Also note that
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{X}=\tau_{Y})=0
, and thus we can write

\displaystyle Z_{n,p}=[n-Y(\tau_{X})]\mathbf{1}_{\tau_{X}<\tau_{Y}}+[X(\tau_{Y})-n]\mathbf{1}_{\tau_{X}\geq\tau_{Y}}.

(8)

First, let us deal with the case
p>1/2
, i.e.
\lambda<1
. In this case

\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{X}^{(n)}\leq\tau_{Y}^{(n)}\right)\leq(4pq)^{n}\to 0\text{ as }n\to\infty.

by part (a) of Lemma
5.2
, since
p>1/2
implies
4pq=1-(2p-1)^{2}<1
.
Since

\mathbb{P}(W_{n,p}=k)=\mathbb{P}(Y(\tau_{X})=k\mid\tau=\tau_{X})=\mathbb{P}(Y(\tau_{X})=k\mid\tau_{X}<\tau_{Y})

and for any two events
A,B
such that
\mathbb{P}(B)>0
we have

\left|\mathbb{P}(A)-\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)\right|\leq\mathbb{P}(B^{c}),

from (
7
) we obtain

\displaystyle|\mathbb{P}(W_{n,p}=k)-\mathbb{P}(Y(\tau_{X})=k)|\leq(4pq)^{n}

(9)

Now, the only remaining problem is to estimate the distribution of
Y(\tau_{X})
. This can be computed explicitly, since
\tau_{X}\sim\Gamma(n,1)
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(Y(\tau_{X})=k)&=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}(Y(\tau_{X})=k\mid\tau_{X})\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda u)^{k}e^{-\lambda y}}{k!}\cdot\frac{u^{n-1}e^{-u}}{(n-1)!}du\\
&=\frac{\lambda^{k}}{(\lambda+1)^{n+k}}\frac{(n+k-1)!}{k!(n-1)!}=\binom{k+n-1}{k}q^{k}p^{n},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\end{split}

which is a negative binomial distribution with mean
\lambda n
and variance
\lambda n/p
(this can also be obtained directly using the coin tossing model for the negative binomial distribution).
Combining this with (
9
) finishes the proof of part (a) of the Theorem.

Next, by the Central Limit Theorem applied to the negative binomial distribution,
for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a<b
we get

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(a\leq\frac{Y(\tau_{X})-\lambda n}{\sqrt{\lambda n/p}}\leq b\right)=\Phi(b)-\Phi(a)

(10)

where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution.
At the same time, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{Y(\tau_{X})-\lambda n}{\sqrt{\lambda n/p}}\in[a,b]\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{W_{n,p}-\lambda n}{\sqrt{\lambda n/p}}\in[a,b]\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left[\sqrt{\frac{\lambda n}{p}}(b-a)+1\right](4pq)^{n}\to 0

by (
9
). This, together with (
5
), implies the CLT for
W_{n,p}
and hence also for
Z_{n,p}
, since

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{n,p}\in[a,b]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(n-W_{n,p}\in[a,b],\tau_{X}<\tau_{Y}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(W_{n,p}-n\in[a,b],\tau_{X}>\tau_{Y}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(n-W_{n,p}\in[a,b]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(n-W_{n,p}\in[a,b],\tau_{X}<\tau_{Y}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(n-W_{n,p}\in[a,b],\tau_{X}>\tau_{Y}\right)

together yield

\left|\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{n,p}\in[a,b]\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(n-W_{n,p}\in[a,b]\right)\right|\leq 2\,\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{X}>\tau_{Y}\right)\leq 2\,(4pq)^{n}\to 0

(11)

as
n\to\infty
.

Now, assume
p=1/2
. Here, both Poisson processes are of rate
1
, and in this case, each
\tau_{X}
and
\tau_{Y}
is equally likely to be the minimum one.
From (
8
),

\displaystyle Z_{n,p}

\displaystyle=[n-Y(\tau_{X})](1-\mathbf{1}_{\tau_{X}\geq\tau_{Y}})+[X(\tau_{Y})-n]\mathbf{1}_{\tau_{X}\geq\tau_{Y}}

\displaystyle=n-Y(\tau_{X})+\left[X(\tau_{Y})+Y(\tau_{X})-2n\right]\mathbf{1}_{\tau_{X}\geq\tau_{Y}}.

Similarly to the case
p>1/2
, we know that
Y(\tau_{X})
is a negative binomial, and thus by the CLT (see (
5
))

\frac{n-Y(\tau_{X})}{\sqrt{2n}}\to\mathcal{N}(0,1)\qquad\text{in distribution}.

We will show now that

\frac{\zeta_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\to 0\text{ \ in probability, where }\zeta_{n}=X(\tau_{Y})+Y(\tau_{X})-2n

thus yielding the desired result.

Figure 1:
A path of the process in case
\tau_{Y}<\tau_{X}
.

Indeed, since

\{\tau_{Y}<\tau_{X}\}=\{X(\tau_{Y})=n-k\text{ for some }k\geq 1\}

from the strong Markov property it follows that the behaviour of the processes
X(t),Y(t)
for
t>\tau_{Y}
is independent of the past, hence conditionally on
\nu=n-X(\tau_{Y})\geq 1
the distribution of
\eta:=Y(\tau_{X})-Y(\tau_{Y})\equiv Y(\tau_{X})-n
is negative binomial with parameters
1/2
and
\nu
(see also Figure
1
). By part (b) of Lemma
5.2

\mathbb{P}(|\eta-\nu|>n^{2/5}\mid\nu)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n^{4/5}}{4(\nu+n^{2/5})}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2/15}}{8}\right)\text{ on }\nu<n^{2/3}.

Now,
X(\tau_{Y})
is also a negative binomial with parameters
1/2
and
n
, so again by part (b) of Lemma
5.2

\mathbb{P}(|\nu|\geq n^{2/3})=\mathbb{P}(|X(\tau_{Y})-n|\geq n^{2/3})\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n^{4/3}}{4(n+n^{2/3})}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n^{1/3}}{8}\right)

Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\eta-\nu|\geq n^{2/5})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(|\eta-\nu|\geq n^{2/5},\nu<n^{2/3})+2\exp\left(-\frac{n^{1/3}}{8}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2/15}}{8}\right)+2\exp\left(-\frac{n^{1/3}}{8}\right)\to 0.

Since
\eta-\nu=(Y(\tau_{X})-n)-(n-X(\tau_{Y}))=Y(\tau_{X})+X(\tau_{Y})-2n=\zeta_{n}
and
n^{2/5}=o(\sqrt{n})
, we are done.
∎
