Abstract
. Friezes are infinite arrays of numbers, in which every four neighbouring vertices arranged in a diamond satisfy the same arithmetic rule. Introduced in the late 1960 1960 s by Coxeter, and further studied by Conway and Coxeter in their remarkable papers from 1973 1973 , this topic has been nearly forgotten for over thirty years. But since the discovery of connections to cluster algebras and categories of type A A , interest in friezes has exploded, several generalizations have been studied, and links to geometry and combinatorics have been explored. In this article we will review some of the most striking results connecting the purely combinatorially defined friezes with triangulations of polygons, Grassmannian cluster algebras and (Grassmannian) cluster categories. Then we will focus on Grassmannian cluster categories and some recent results linking them to friezes.

1.
Introduction

This is a survey on frieze patterns from the point of view of representation theory, based on my talk from ICRA2024 in Shanghai. The main idea that I wanted to get across is that frieze patterns appear quite naturally in connection with (Grassmannian) cluster categories and that his yields interesting interactions with other fields, such as algebraic geometry and commutative algebra.

While we will review some of the “classical” correspondence between triangulations of polygons, cluster algebras and categories of type
A
and Conway–Coxeter frieze patterns, the focus is on Grassmannian cluster categories. In particular, we show how to obtain the Conway–Coxeter friezes from them (including the boundary rows), and then survey recent results on the construction of tame integral
SL_{k}
-friezes and infinite friezes from Grassmannian cluster categories of infinite rank.

What is in this survey: In Section
2

Therefore, we roughly follow the historical trajectory, starting with Coxeter’s and Conway–Coxeter’s seminal work on combinatorics of frieze patterns from the 1970s. The topic then long lay dormant, until the introduction of cluster algebras and connections to cluster categories in the 2000s. Since then research on friezes has exploded.

2.2.
Some generalizations

In Section

successive entries of the frieze on its diagonals and all other entries above and below the diagonal accordingly. Finally, if all non-trivial entries
m_{ij}
with
0\leq i,j\leq w
are positive integers, we call the pattern (
41
) an
integral

SL_{k}
-frieze.

We will see in Section
4.2
how these friezes can be interpreted as evaluations of so-called Plücker friezes, whose entries are Plücker coordinates in the Grassmannian
Gr(k,n)
, where
n=w+k+1
.

Other types of friezes

There are also generalizations in a different direction, where the determinant condition is changed. For example, additive friezes have been considered by Coxeter and Rigby, also by Shephard
[
CR
,
She76
]
in the
1970
s - there the diamond rule (
2.1
) is altered to

a+d=c+d+1\ .

Here a representation theoretic description would be interesting. There is also the variant of tropical friezes, which have been studied from a representation theoretic point of view see
[
Rin12
,
Guo13
]
. For even more elaborate rules, let us mention symplectic friezes
[
MG19
]
, Heronian friezes
[
FS21
]
, and most recently noncommutative friezes
[
CHJ25
]
.

3.
Cluster algebras and cluster categories

Here we will explain how to get from cluster algebras and categories to friezes. The basic observation for this is again due to Coxeter: every entry in a closed frieze is a continuant polynomial in elements of the quiddity sequence, see Prop.
3.2
. Continuants appear as exchange polynomials in cluster algebras of type
A
, which will also be defined below. Further, we will look at one prominent example of algebras with a cluster structure: the homogeneous coordinate ring of the Grassmannian
Gr(k,n)
. Then we explain the relation to cluster categories, in particular to Grassmannian cluster categories, which were introduced by Jensen, King, and Su
[
JKS16
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

A

\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&b_{1}&0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\

for all

3.1.
Cluster algebras

Cluster algebras were introduced by Fomin and Zelevinsky in the early 2000s, see
[
FZ02
]
, for more details and the general theory see e.g
[
FWZ16
,
FWZ17
,
FWZ20
,
Nak23
]
.

In this paper we will mostly be interested in Grassmannian cluster algebras and categories, that means in particular that we will define cluster algebras of geometric type with coefficients, mostly following
[
FZ07
]
.

Let
(\mathbb{P},\oplus,\cdot)
be a semifield, i.e., it fulfills the same axioms as a field with the possible exception for the existence of neutral element with respect to
\oplus
and for the existence of an inverse element with respect to
\oplus
.
Fix a positive integer
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and a field
K
7
7

7

In order to avoid possible pathologies, we will assume that
K
is algebraically closed throughout. Mostly we will take
K=\mathbb{C}
.
.
The
ambient field
for a cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}
of rank
N
is a field
\mathcal{F}
isomorphic to the field of rational functions in
N
independent variables with coefficients in
K\mathbb{P}
.

Further recall the notion of a tropical semifield
[
FZ07
, Definition 2.2]
: Let
(\mathbb{P},\cdot)
be a multiplicative free abelian group with a finite free set of generators

(p_{1},\ldots,p_{\ell})
.
We endow
\mathbb{P}
additionally with the auxiliary addition
\oplus
given by

\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}p_{i}^{a_{i}}\oplus\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}p_{i}^{b_{i}}:=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}p_{i}^{\min\{a_{i},b_{i}\}}.

Using this addition,
(\mathbb{P},\oplus,\cdot)
is a semifield,
which is usually denoted by
\operatorname{Trop}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{\ell})
, the
tropical semifield
.

An
N
-tuple
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
of elements in
\mathcal{F}
is called a
free generating set
if

x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
are algebraically independent over
K\mathbb{P}
and
\mathcal{F}=K\mathbb{P}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
.
Moreover, recall that an
N\times N
integer matrix
B=(b_{ij})
is called
skew-symmetrizable

if there exist positive integers
d_{1},\ldots,d_{N}
such that, for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, we have

d_{i}b_{ij}=-d_{j}b_{ji}\ .

In particular, a skew-symmetrizable matrix is sign-skew-symmetric.

We will define cluster algebras via extended exchange matrices. Therefore let

•

\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})
be an
N
-tuple of elements in
\mathbb{P}
, called the
coefficient tuple
,

•

B=(b_{ij})
be a skew-symmetrizable
N\times N
integer matrix,
called the
exchange matrix
,

•

\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
be an
N
-tuple of elements in
\mathcal{F}
forming a free generating set, called the
cluster
.

We are renaming the generators
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{\ell})
of the tropical semifield of
\mathbb{P}
by calling them
x_{N+1},\ldots,x_{M}
, where
M:=N+\ell
,
and set

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N},\ldots,x_{M}).

Since the coefficients
y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}
are Laurent monomials in
x_{N+1},\ldots,x_{M}
, we may define the integers

b_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z},\ \ \ \mbox{for }j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\mbox{ and }i\in\{N+1,\ldots,M\}

via

(42)

y_{j}=\prod_{i=N+1}^{M}x_{i}^{b_{ij}}\ .

In other words,
we are extending the
N\times N
exchange matrix
B
to an
extended exchange matrix
\widetilde{B}
of size
M\times N
,
where

\widetilde{B}:=(b_{ij})_{i\in\{1,\ldots,M\},j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}}\ .

Thus, the new entries below
B
(i.e., those
b_{ij}
with
i\geq N+1
) are determined by the exponents of
x_{i}
in (
42
).
The pair
\Sigma=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{B})
is called a
labeled seed
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\Sigma=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{B})
be a labeled seed and
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
The
seed mutation

\mu_{k}
in direction
k
transforms
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{B})
into the labeled seed
\mu_{k}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{B}):=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime},\widetilde{B}^{\prime})
,
where:

(1)

a new cluster
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{N}^{\prime})
is introduced via
x_{j}^{\prime}:=x_{j}
if
j\neq k

and
x_{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
is defined by the
exchange relation
:

(43)

x_{k}x_{k}^{\prime}=\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
b_{ik}>0\end{subarray}}^{M}x_{i}^{b_{ik}}+\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
b_{ik}<0\end{subarray}}^{M}x_{i}^{-b_{ik}}\ .

Notice that the appearing exponents are the entries of the
k
-th column of
\widetilde{B}
. The polynomial
x_{k}x_{k}^{\prime}-\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
b_{ik}>0\end{subarray}}^{M}x_{i}^{b_{ik}}-\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
b_{ik}<0\end{subarray}}^{M}x_{i}^{-b_{ik}}
is sometimes called
exchange polynomial
.

(2)

the new exchange matrix

\widetilde{B}^{\prime}=(b_{ij}^{\prime})
is of the same size as
\widetilde{B}
and its entries are determined by

b_{ij}^{\prime}:=\begin{cases}-b_{ij}&\mbox{if }i=k\mbox{ or }j=k\ ,\\
b_{ij}+\operatorname{sgn}(b_{ik})[b_{ik}b_{kj}]_{+}&\mbox{otherwise}\ .\end{cases}

Since we are only allowed to mutate in directions
\{1,\ldots,N\}
,
the variables
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
are sometimes called the
mutable variables
of
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{B})
,
while
(x_{N+1},\ldots,x_{M})
are called the
frozen variables
or
coefficient variables
of
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{B})
, cf. e.g.
[
FWZ16
, Definition 3.1.1]
.

Note that seed mutation is well-defined, i.e.,
\mu_{k}(\Sigma)
is again a labeled seed in
\mathcal{F}
.
Furthermore,
\mu_{k}
is an involution, that is, we have
\mu_{k}(\mu_{k}(\Sigma))=\Sigma
.

In order to finally define cluster algebras, we further need
cluster patterns
: Therefore, recall that the
N
-regular tree

\mathbb{T}_{N}
is the infinite simply laced tree such that every vertex
v
has
N
edges and the latter are labeled with the numbers
1,\ldots,N
.
Then a
cluster pattern
is an assignment of a labeled seed
\Sigma_{t}=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},\widetilde{B}_{t})
to every vertex
t\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
such that the following property holds:

For every pair of vertices
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
which are joined by an edge, say with label
k
, we have

\mu_{k}(\Sigma_{t})=\Sigma_{t^{\prime}}
.
Note that we also have
\mu_{k}(\Sigma_{t^{\prime}})=\Sigma_{t}
in the above situation since
\mu_{k}
is an involution.

Definition 3.4

(
[
FZ07
, Definition 2.11]
)
.

For a cluster pattern determined by the seeds
\Sigma_{t}=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},\widetilde{B}_{t})
,
t\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
,
we set

\mathcal{X}:=\bigcup_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=\{x_{i,t}\mid t\in\mathbb{T}_{N},i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\},

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=(x_{1,t},\ldots,x_{N,t})
.
We call
x_{i,t}\in\mathcal{X}
the
cluster variables
.

The
cluster algebra

\mathcal{A}
associated to the given cluster pattern is the
K\mathbb{P}
-subalgebra of the ambient field
\mathcal{F}
generated by all cluster variables,

\mathcal{A}=K\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{X}]\ .

For a labeled seed
\Sigma=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{B})=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},\widetilde{B}_{t})
with
t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
a fixed value,
we also use the notation

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)=\mathcal{A}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\widetilde{B})\ .

By the last part, we may define a cluster algebra also by giving a labeled seed
\Sigma
in
\mathcal{F}
and to create then a cluster pattern by repeatedly mutating in all possible directions.

3.3.
Grassmannian cluster categories

We have already introduced Grassmannian with its cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}(k,n)=\mathbb{C}[Gr(k,n)]
in Example
3.9
. There is also a Frobenius categorification of
\mathcal{A}(k,n)
due to Jensen, King, and Su
[

]
, inspired by the categorification of an affine open cell in the Grassmannian by
[

, which will be the key idea to generalize to the infinite Grassmannian

, see Section

.

Let
\mu_{n}=\langle\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{*}:\zeta^{n}=1\rangle
, where
\zeta
is a primitive root of unity, act on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
via the usual action

(x,y)\mapsto(\zeta x,\zeta^{-1}y)\ .

The element

is a semi-invariant under the action of

\mu_{n}
-action restricts to the plane curve singularity with coordinate ring

. The invariant subring of the
\mu_{n}
-action is

with

.

Then look at the category of
\mu_{n}
-equivariant finitely generated modules over

, namely
\textrm{mod}_{\mu_{n}}(R_{k,n})
, which is tautologically equivalent to the category of finitely generated modules over the skew-group ring
\mu_{n}*R_{k,n}
. The

(k,n)
is defined to be

\mathcal{C}(k,n)=\operatorname{{MCM}}(\mu_{n}*R_{k,n})=\operatorname{{MCM}}_{\mu_{n}}(R_{k,n})\ ,

that is, the category of maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules over
\mu_{n}*R_{k,n}
. In particular, the objects of
\mathcal{C}(k,n)
are modules over the skew-group ring that are free over the invariant subring

.

In the previous sections it was described how to move between a triangulation of an

Lemma 4.4
.

Consider

with a cluster tilting object

Then the entries
of rows 2 to

Proof.

W.l.o.g. assume that all our modules are in the fundamental domain of the AR-quiver, depicted in Fig.

. The AR-sequences are of the form

0\xrightarrow{}M_{i,j}\xrightarrow{}M_{i,j+1}\oplus M_{i+1,j}\xrightarrow{}M_{i+1,j+1}\xrightarrow{}0\

for

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Ext^{1}_{\mathcal{C}(2,n)}(M_{i+1,j+1},M_{ij})=1
.
The corresponding sequence with start and endterm reversed is

0\xrightarrow{}M_{i+1,j+1}\xrightarrow{}M_{i,i+1}\oplus M_{j,j+1}\xrightarrow{}M_{i,j}\xrightarrow{}0\ .

\displaystyle\zeta_{T}(M_{i,j})\zeta_{T}(M_{i+1,j+1})

\displaystyle=\zeta_{T}(M_{i,j+1})\zeta_{T}(M_{i+1,j})+\zeta(M_{i,i+1})\zeta_{T}(M_{j,j+1})

\displaystyle=\zeta_{T}(M_{i,j+1})\zeta_{T}(M_{i+1,j})+p_{i,i+1}p_{j,j+1}\ .

All

are nonzero Laurent polynomials in
