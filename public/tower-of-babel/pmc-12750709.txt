Cervical cancer screening and treatment of VIA positive lesions among women living with HIV in Nairobi and Kajiado counties, Kenya: results from a targeted intervention implemented under the USAID Fahari ya Jamii project in supported HIV clinics
Authors: Lulu Ndapatani, Elizabeth Kubo, Ashford Munene, Collins Odhiambo, Susan Arodi, Reson Marima, Annastacia Kumola, Dennis Osiemo, Teresa Simiyu, Jared Mecha, C F Otieno
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths and the second most common cancer amongst females in Kenya. Women living with HIV (WLHIV) have a six-fold increased risk of developing cervical cancer than women in the general population. As of October 2021, only 23% (34/149) of Fahari ya Jamii (FYJ) project-supported HIV clinics in Nairobi and Kajiado Counties were offering cervical cancer screening for WLHIV. Visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) positive lesions identified were being treated in only 7% (11/34) of facilities. The project implemented an intervention and sought to determine its outcome on cervical cancer screening and treatment uptake.MethodsThis was a retrospective analysis of data from 1st July 2022 to 30th June 2023. The intervention focused on building the capacity of service providers, determining cervical cancer screening eligibility using Kenya National eligibility screening guidelines, providing screening supplies and commodities, creating service demand through health education by mentor mothers and peer educators, providing screening services and on-site treatment of VIA positive lesions. WLHIV aged 25 to 49 years and receiving care in 64 USAID–FYJ–supported facilities in Nairobi and Kajiado Counties were included in the analyses. Descriptive analyses were performed for sociodemographic characteristics, screening and treatment uptake.ResultsA total of 52% (11,089/21,324) WLHIV received cervical cancer screening services, representing a five-fold increase from pre-intervention figures (2,186). The number of facilities offering screening services increased from 23% (34/149) to 43% (64/149). Of the women screened, 2% (245) VIA positive cervical lesions were identified. Treatment uptake improved from 38% pre-intervention to 90%, with 90% (220/245) of identified cases receiving appropriate treatment. The intervention successfully integrated screening into routine HIV care, facilitated same-day treatment in equipped facilities, and strengthened provider capacity, leading to sustained service delivery improvements.ConclusionsThe intervention increased cervical cancer screening and treatment uptake for VIA positive lesions among WLHIV by integrating services into routine HIV care, enhancing provider training, and ensuring the availability of screening supplies. These findings highlight the potential for scaling up similar strategies in resource-limited settings. Sustained mentorship and continuous monitoring will be essential to further strengthen screening and treatment efforts toward cervical cancer elimination.Keywords: Cervical cancer screening, Women living with HIV, VIA positive lesions treatment

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the third most common cancer in women worldwide [1], with approximately 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths recorded in 2022, with over 94% of cervical cancer deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where screening and immunization efforts lag [2]. These include limited access to screening services, due to a lack of healthcare infrastructure for screening and trained personnel, poor quality control and assurance measures for screening tests, access to treatment, financial constraints, and scarcity in the number of facilities offering treatment services, which hinder timely and appropriate care [3]. Globally, 1 to 2% of HIV-negative women develop cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) stages 2 and 3 annually also [4], and HIV-positive women are 10% more likely to do so[4].

The incidence and mortality of cervical cancer are further exacerbated by the high incidence of HIV infection in women, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa [5], where HIV-positive women face a higher risk of developing cervical precancerous lesions that progress to cervical cancer [6]. Women living with HIV (WLHIV) face unique challenges in addition to the general burden of the disease. Due to immune suppression, WLHIV are vulnerable to persistent HPV infection. Additionally, HIV is often associated with other risk factors for cervical cancer, such as poor socioeconomic status, having multiple sexual partners, early age of sexual experience, and cigarette smoking [7]. Access to healthcare services, including regular cervical cancer screenings and treatment, may be limited for HIV-positive women, but stigma and discrimination related to both HIV and cervical cancer further exacerbate the challenges faced by these women, impacting their overall well-being and quality of life [8–10].

In Kenya cervical cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths and the second most common cancer amongst females. Screening is low with only 4% of women in Nairobi County in 2019 having been screened for cervical cancer [1].

In May 2018, the World Health Organization (WHO) called for a global initiative to eliminate cervical cancer as a public health problem [11]. In 2020, the global cervical cancer elimination strategy outlined three key strategies to ending cervical cancer morbidity and mortality by 2030 by ensuring that: 90% of girls are vaccinated against the human papillomavirus (HPV), 70% of eligible women are screened using a high-performance test and 90% of precancerous lesions are treated [11].

Methods

Study design and population

This was a retrospective analysis of data from 1 st July 2022 to 30th June 2023. WLHIV and receiving care in 64 USAID-FYJ-supported facilities in Nairobi and Kajiado Counties who were eligible for cervical cancer screening and had consented to cervical cancer screening were included. Eligible women who declined cervical cancer screening were excluded.

Setting

The intervention was implemented in Nairobi City County and Kajiado County, Kenya. Nairobi City County is one of the 47 counties in Kenya and the most populous with a population estimate of 5 million. According to the 2019 Kenya Population and Housing Census, Kajiado County had an estimated population of 1,117,840 people. Nairobi and Kajiado Counties are neighbors hence people often move between these counties for work, leisure, and residency. The USAID Fahari ya Jamii project implements HIV care and treatment services in 103 facilities in Nairobi County informal settlements and 46 in Kajiado County. Cervical cancer screening services are provided as part of an integrated package of HIV care services for WLHIV.

Target population

The target population was 12,038 WLHIV between the ages of 25 and 49 years, receiving HIV care in supported clinics from July 2022 to June 2023. Women aged 50 years and above were excluded as the Kenya Cancer Screening guidelines do not recommend screening with visual inspection with acetic acid for women 50 years and above as VIA is appropriate to use in women whose squamocolumnar junction (SCJ) is visible, that is those younger than 50. The SCJ gradually recedes into the endocervical canal when menopause occurs, making it possible to miss lesions when relying on visual inspection.

Intervention

The USAID Fahari ya Jamii (FYJ) is a University of Nairobi implemented project that provides HIV prevention, care, and treatment services in 149 health facilities in Nairobi and Kajiado Counties. In the quarters of October to December 2021, only 34 health facilities were offering cervical cancer screening services and only 11 of these were providing treatment for VIA positive lesions. Few healthcare workers were trained in the delivery of this service. Program-level data from the USAID Fahari ya Jamii project showed that between October and December 2021, only 54% (2,186/4,017) of WLHIV received cervical cancer screening services, and just 57% (31/54) of VIA-positive lesions identified were treated. In response to these gaps, the project implemented a targeted intervention to strengthen both screening and treatment services for WLHIV in Nairobi and Kajiado Counties.

The USAID Fahari ya Jamii project implemented a multipronged intervention to improve cervical cancer screening and treatment uptake among women living with HIV in Nairobi and Kajiado County HIV clinics. The approach focused on building the capacity of healthcare providers to assess screening eligibility, delivering screening using visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA), supplying essential health products, creating demand for services among beneficiaries, ensuring timely treatment for clients with VIA-positive lesions, and strengthening monitoring of both screening services and treatment uptake. Beginning in July 2022, and in close collaboration with Nairobi City County and Kajiado County, the project integrated these strategies into routine HIV care in supported facilities to enhance service delivery and improve patient outcomes. Cervical cancer screening services were provided as part of routine care, and all eligible WLHIV who accessed the health facility between July 2022 and June 2023 were included based on predefined inclusion criteria: age, screening eligibility and consent to screening.

Building capacity of service providersThe USAID Fahari ya Jamii project, in partnership with the counties built the capacity of healthcare workers to create demand for cervical cancer screening and treatment of VIA positive lesions which is key to increasing uptake of cervical cancer screening services and management. In addition, the project trained lay health workers in the national algorithm for the identification of women who are eligible for cervical cancer screening. Service providers received a comprehensive training package on knowledge and skills in cervical cancer screening and treatment of pre-cancerous cervical lesions. Pre-cancerous cervical lesions also known as intraepithelial neoplasia are abnormal changes in the cells of the cervix that have the potential to develop into cervical cancer if left untreated. A total of 58 nurses and 37 clinical officers working in 95 HIV clinics received a 5-day training on cervical cancer prevention, screening, and management using the Kenya National training curriculum for screening and management of VIA positive lesions. Post-training, these staff were assigned screening duties and a mentor to build their screening competence.

Monitoring the intervention

A robust monitoring system was implemented which included daily performance tracking through WhatsApp groups, de-identified data entry onto an electronic platform, and weekly facility performance review during multi-disciplinary meetings. Best practice sharing forums and performance reviews were conducted with the program team every fortnight to enhance learning and improve service outcomes.

Data management and analysis

Data was abstracted from the Kenya Ministry of Health Cancer Screening Register (MOH 412), and extracted from the Kenya Electronic Medical Records System (Kenya EMR). Abstracted variables included screening status and treatment uptake. Extracted variables included sociodemographic characteristics such as age, marital status, education level and employment.

Clinical characteristics assessed included duration on ART, viral load suppression status at the time of VIA-positive lesion identification, and comparison of women with and without VIA-positive lesions. All data were de-identified to ensure patient confidentiality.

The main outcome variables of interest were screening uptake and treatment uptake. Descriptive analyses were performed for all variables. Categorical variables were summarized using proportions. Pearson’s Chi square test was used to compare proportions. P-values < 0.05 were statistically significant. Data was analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25 and ‘R’ version 4.2.2.

Ethical considerations

Verbal consent for cervical cancer screening using visual inspection with acetic acid was obtained from eligible clients after educating them on the importance and benefits of screening. This approach aligns with Kenya’s National Guidelines for HIV Service Provision. Approval for these analyses was granted by the Kenyatta National Hospital-University of Nairobi Ethics and Research Committee, approval number P688/09/2024.

Discussion

This study looked at 3 main aspects: (i) Trends in cervical cancer screening uptake, (ii) Screening outcomes among WLHIV, (iii) Trends in treatment uptake for VIA positive lesions. The cervical cancer screening uptake increased soon after the introduction of the intervention and then dipped. Identification of positive lesions increased from 131 before intervention to 245 within the 12 months of intervention. There was an increase in the uptake of treatment of VIA positive lesions from 38% to 90%.

The highest screening proportions were noted in the 35–44 years age category, those who were married and among those who had attained a secondary school education. These findings are consistent with those of a Tanzanian study that documented higher screening rates among older, married, more educated women [12]. This could be attributed to older women having had more contact with reproductive health services because of longer fertility history and more educated women having increased access to information on benefits of cervical cancer screening services [13–15]. A Kenyan study also documented similar findings where older women and more educated women were more likely to undergo screening [16]. Studies conducted in Ethiopia and in Zambia found that 60.8% and 32.2% of the VIA positive cases were identified in women aged between 31 and 40 years respectively [17]. In contrast to our findings, a study conducted in France found high screening rates among younger women aged 25–35 years, during screening services provided during antenatal visits [17]. This calls for the integration of cervical cancer screening into HIV care and treatment services to ensure that there are no missed opportunities [17].

The screening uptake increased soon after the intervention and then dipped. This reduction in client attendance coincided with political tensions surrounding Kenya’s general election in August 2022, during which insecurity led to fewer clinic bookings and limited-service access. This disruption underscores the vulnerability of health service delivery to external political factors. The increase in screening uptake can be attributed to our approach which entailed integration of screening into routine HIV care. Integrating cervical cancer screening services into HIV care is an important strategy for improving access and uptake of this service for WLHIV. Integration of cervical cancer screening into routine HIV care has been endorsed as an effective strategy to increase the uptake of cervical cancer screening and achieve early detection and treatment of pre-cancerous lesions among HIV-infected women in Sub-Saharan Africa where cervical cancer burden parallels that of HIV [18–25]. A Ugandan mixed methods study documented that 64.5% of the women found this approach acceptable [26].

There was a notable increase in the identification of VIA-positive lesions during the intervention period, rising from 131 at baseline to 245 an average positivity of 2.5. While improved training of health workers played a central role, enhancing detection skills and adherence to screening protocols, the observed increase is likely multifactorial. It may also reflect a higher screening volume and a possible shift in the risk profile of the screened population. These additional factors warrant further investigation into future research to better understand their relative contributions.

The low positivity rate of 2.5% from visual inspection with acetic acid is lower than the WHO recommended 5%−10% which raises concerns about its sensitivity in the screened population [26]. This low detection rate could be attributed to several factors, including the skill level of the healthcare providers performing the test, the quality of lighting and visualization, or characteristics of the population being screened. Additionally, the VIA method is known for its subjectivity, which may contribute to under-detection if providers are overly cautious in interpreting results. This calls for continuous retraining of providers and the scaling up colposcopy and transitioning to HPV testing to improve detection rates and ensure that lesions are not missed.

The project documented an increase in the uptake of treatment for VIA positive lesions from 38% to 90%. A possible explanation for this improvement is the ‘same day treatment’ approach in facilities with treatment devices, and treatment within 2 weeks of diagnosis for those without thermal ablative devices. The single-visit, screen-and-treat approach (SV-SAT) with visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) to detect and manage VIA positive lesions has been recommended for cervical cancer prevention in LMICs like Kenya because of its low cost and facilitation of treatment uptake among screen- positive women compared to cytology or HPV testing [23]. The single visit screen and treat approach recommends that the decision to provide treatment is based on a positive primary screening test only. A study conducted in Cote d’Ivoire found a high rate of treatment (96%) among eligible women identified with precancerous lesions, which was attributed to a same-day screening and treatment approach [27].

Treatment of identified lesions is critical in reducing the incidence of cervical cancer. VIA positive lesions were managed using thermocoagulation therapy.

Additionally, 30% of VIA positive lesions in this study were diagnosed in women aged 25–34, emphasizing the need to prioritize and educate women under 35 about the importance of early screening. Early detection of VIA positive lesions enables timely treatment, helping to prevent disease progression.

Limitations

First, our analysis was based on a review of data reported in the District Health Information Software. This may be subject to errors inherent in the process of data capture in the original documents, as well as missing data. The original documents lacked information on the severity of VIA positive lesions and reasons why women diagnosed with VIA positive lesions declined treatment. Second, we did not capture data on age of sexual debut and CD4 count yet these variables may be associated with the risk of developing cervical pre-cancerous lesions. Third, we only included patients who consented to undergo screening, potentially introducing self-selection bias. Fourth, we lacked a comparator group. Fifth, our sample population was drawn from FYJ-supported facilities hence our findings can only be generalized to patients within FYJ-supported facilities. Additionally, the data does not include the number of women who sought services against those that received services therefore limiting our ability to assess service uptake, identify barriers to access, and accurately measure the screening coverage and missed opportunities within the target population. Demand generation activities were only conducted within health facilities excluding women who did not interface with the clinic system during the intervention period. This facility-based approach only benefited primarily those women who were already accessing services, while missing a broader population that could have been reached through community-based or outreach strategies.

Finally, utilizing the ‘test and treat’ approach with the VIA screening method may have resulted in overtreatment, as no confirmatory triage test was conducted to verify VIA-positive lesions. However, in resource limited settings, this approach is often considered acceptable and even preferred given the risk of loss to follow-up and limited access to diagnostic services. In such contexts, the benefit of treating all VIA positive cases may outweigh the risk of overtreatment, especially when the treatment method is low-risk and non-invasive.

To mitigate these limitations, we ensured rigorous data verification by cross-checking records whenever possible, thereby minimizing errors in data capture. While data on age of sexual debut and CD4 count were not captured, we interpreted findings within the context of known risk factors from existing literature. To address potential self-selection bias, we considered demographic and clinical characteristics of the screened population to assess representativeness. Although a comparator group was not included, we compared our findings with regional and national trends to provide context. While our sample was drawn from FYJ-supported facilities, findings offer valuable insights into screening outcomes and service delivery, which can inform improvements within similar healthcare settings. Lastly, the 2nd edition of the Kenya National Cancer Screening and Early Diagnosis Guidelines (2024) has adopted HPV testing as the primary screening method for cervical cancer to improve case detection. The guidelines emphasize high-risk HPV DNA testing including the option of self-collected samples as the first-line approach for women in the target screening age group.

Conclusions

The implementation of targeted interventions significantly improved cervical cancer screening uptake, identification of precancerous lesions, and treatment rates among women living with HIV (WLHIV) in Nairobi and Kajiado Counties. By integrating screening into routine HIV care, training healthcare providers, ensuring the availability of screening supplies, and leveraging peer educators to create demand, the project successfully increased access to life-saving cervical cancer services. The five-fold increase in screening numbers and the substantial improvement in treatment uptake underscore the effectiveness of a structured, patient-centered approach to cervical cancer prevention.

Despite these successes, challenges such as fluctuations in screening uptake, potential reporting inconsistencies, and missed treatment opportunities highlight the need for ongoing mentorship, continuous monitoring, and strengthened community outreach. Expanding similar interventions to other high-burden regions, with policy support and increased investment in diagnostic and treatment technologies could further accelerate progress toward achieving global cervical cancer elimination targets.

Future efforts should focus on sustaining these gains through enhanced healthcare worker training, improved patient follow-up mechanisms, and the integration of emerging screening technologies. Research into long-term outcomes and patient experiences will be crucial in refining strategies to ensure equitable and effective cervical cancer prevention services for WLHIV.
