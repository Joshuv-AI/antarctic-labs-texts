Factors associated with cyberbullying and depression in cybervictimized adolescents and the role of internet gaming disorder
Authors: Hasan Can Ozbay, Doga Sevincok, Mutlu Muhammed Ozbek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the relationships between cybervictimization, cyberbullying, Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD), depression, and Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) symptoms in adolescents with a history of cybervictimization. A clinical sample of 89 adolescents aged 12–17 years was assessed using standardized diagnostic interviews and self-report scales. Correlation and regression analyses revealed that depression severity was independently associated with cybervictimization, age, and IGD severity. Oppositional defiant disorder (ODD) symptoms were significantly associated with cyberbullying perpetration, whereas cybervictimization was only related to depression severity. Additionally, moderation analyses showed that IGD did not significantly moderate the relationship between cybervictimization and depression or between ODD symptoms and cyberbullying. These findings highlight the complex interplay between psychiatric symptoms and problematic online behaviors. Our results emphasize the importance of routine screening for cyberbullying experiences in adolescents with depression or ADHD. Further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify causal pathways and inform targeted interventions aimed at mitigating the negative mental health outcomes associated with cyberbullying and IGD.Keywords: Attention deficit, Cyberbullying, Cybervictimization, Depression, Hyperactivity, Internet gaming disorderSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Virtual environments provide many conveniences for communication and interaction. However, they can also lead to negative consequences. One such consequence is cyberbullying, a modern form of traditional bullying1. Cyberbullying is defined as the use of the Internet or other digital communication technologies with the intent to harm others2. Today’s adolescents may use technological tools to threaten, demean, mock, defame individuals or groups and to gain unauthorized access to others’ social media accounts and passwords. Because of its unique characteristics, cyberbullying is considered more harmful than traditional bullying, as it can cause greater social and emotional challenges for victims, including social isolation and difficulty regulating emotions3. Prevalence estimates vary across studies, but evidence suggests that approximately 10–40% of adolescents experience cybervictimization4.

Cyberbullying causes deleterious psychological effects on both perpetrators and victims. Victims of cyberbullying have been reported to suffer emotional distress including frustration, sadness, anger, and anxiety; mental health issues such as depression; behavioral problems including delinquency and substance use; academic difficulties such as loss of motivation, underachievement, and absenteeism; and severe psychological consequences such as suicidal ideation and attempts1. Considering adolescents’ widespread access to the Internet and social media, these outcomes are even more concerning. Empirical studies have consistently suggested that cybervictimization correlates with increased levels of depression, loneliness, anxiety, diminished self-esteem, self-harm behaviors, and suicidal tendencies5.

A meta-analysis by Kowalski et al.4,, which reviewed 131 studies on cyberbullying conducted between 2002 and 2013, reported that approximately 10% of adolescents have experienced cybervictimization. A 2020 report by the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre, based on a survey of over 6,000 youths aged 10–18 years, found that nearly 50% had experienced at least one form of cyberbullying in their lifetime6. A study on Vietnamese adolescents found that 36.5% reported having been victims of cyberbullying, and about half of the adolescents exhibited symptoms of depression. After controlling for confounding variables, victims of cyberbullying were 1.81-fold more likely to exhibit depressive symptoms than their non-victimized peers7.

Because the risk of cybervictimization increases with more time spent online, Internet gaming disorder (IGD), a common issue among adolescents, stands out as a major risk factor. IGD, defined in the DSM-5 as a pattern of persistent and recurrent gaming behavior leading to significant impairment or distress, has become increasingly prevalent among adolescents, with the prevalence rate of 4.8%8,9. Previous studies have demonstrated a significant association between online game addiction and both perpetration and victimization in cyberbullying10. In online gaming environments, participants communicate via text or voice using either real names or pseudonyms. Adolescents who spend more time gaming online are at a higher risk of either engaging in cyberbullying or becoming victims themselves11. Moreover, extended online activity has been associated with increased symptoms of depression12.

IGD could also potentially contribute to adolescent reactions to online stress through a more indirect route. Overexposure to online gaming might make youth vulnerable to other online interactions that can result from dysfunctional coping behavior and increase the psychological consequences of cybervictimization. On the other hand, extended involvement with gaming can decrease the chances of engaging in face-to-face interactions, leading to isolation and inability to function effectively in daily life. These behaviors were found to contribute to the development of depression13,14. In light of this theory, IGD was examined as a potential moderating factor in the relationship between cybervictimization and depression. These mechanisms constitute a rationale for studying the connection between IGD and the two constructs. However, given the limited theoretical and empirical evidence in this area, this approach should be considered exploratory.

Depression, characterized by apathy, diminished energy, and loss of interest in social activities, often leads to social withdrawal and reduced engagement in face-to-face interactions. Consequently, individuals may gravitate toward activities that circumvent direct interpersonal contact, such as social media use, online shopping, and gaming15. A recent study suggested that attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and depression are direct predictors of IGD16. Additionally, previous studies have indicated an association between ADHD and cyberbullying: one study revealed that adolescents with combined-type ADHD are more likely to perpetrate cyberbullying than those with predominantly inattentive ADHD, and another study reported a positive association between hyperactivity and being a victim of cyberbullying. These findings indicate that impulsivity symptoms associated with ADHD increase the risk of involvement in cyberbullying, both as a perpetrator and as a victim17,18.

IGD, ADHD symptoms, and depressive symptoms contribute to the risk of involvement in cyberbullying and can result from experiencing or perpetrating cyberbullying. Investigating the complex interplay among these variables and identifying key risk factors may facilitate the early identification of vulnerable adolescents and inform targeted interventions. The present study aims to examine the risk factors associated with cybervictimization and cyberbullying among youths, particularly adolescents who have experienced cybervictimization, and to clarify the roles of depression, ADHD symptoms, and IGD in this context. We also aimed to investigate factors associated with depression. Lastly, we hypothesized that IGD may exert a moderating effect on these relationships, such that higher levels of IGD would strengthen the associations between the variables.

Method

Participants and assessment

After receiving approval from the ethics committee and authorization from the hospital administration, adolescents who reported a history of cybervictimization during clinical interviews at the Child and Adolescent Psychiatry outpatient clinics of Yalova Training and Research Hospital were invited to participate. A total of 105 adolescents (50 females and 55 males) who met the inclusion criteria and provided informed consent—along with their parents—were administered the study instruments. However, 16 were excluded because of the incomplete data. Thus, the final sample comprised 89 adolescents (42 females and 47 males), aged 12–17 years, all with documented cybervictimization histories. The sampling strategy was designed to target a clinically vulnerable population, in which cybervictimization is an important concern allowing to examine the differences in symptom severity and related behavioral features as well as psychological correlates.

Each participant was assessed using the Turkish adaptation of the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Age Children—Present and Lifetime Version (K-SADS-PL), aligned with DSM-5 criteria for mood disorders and schizophrenia19. Despite all participants having a documented history of cybervictimization, the level of related experiences did differ between individuals and scores for cybervictimization were treated as continuous variables for all analyses.

Exclusion criteria were having a diagnosis of intellectual disability, psychotic disorders, bipolar disorder, autism spectrum disorder, or neurological illnesses. Detailed data on age, sex, educational attainment, and developmental history were collected using a standardized sociodemographic form. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol (2025/101-18.04.2025) was approved by the Yalova University Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after comprehensive briefing on study procedures, between April 20, 2025, and August 20, 2025.

Measures

Sociodemographic and clinical data form

This form was developed by the researcher based on relevant literature and was completed using information provided by both parents and children. The form was designed to collect sociodemographic information (e.g., participant’s name, date of birth, sex, household composition, parents’ marital status, age, education level), along with prenatal and birth history, developmental milestones, and relevant clinical history.

K-SADS-PL (Present and Lifetime Version)

K-SADS-PL is a semi-structured interview form developed by Kaufman et al., (2016) to ascertain past and current psychopathology in children and adolescents per DSM-5 criteria. The diagnostic screening section evaluates approximately 200 specific symptoms and behaviors. For each symptom, screening questions precede the application of diagnostic criteria. If the screening indicated potential pathology, additional modules covering five diagnostic domains were administered to confirm the diagnoses. These domains included the following: mood disorders, psychotic disorders, anxiety disorders, behavioral disorders, substance use disorders, and other disorders. Each supplementary symptom checklist included criteria for evaluating the most severe current and past presentations of the disorder. The diagnostic criteria for obsessive–compulsive disorder and ADHD are assessed in these sections. The Turkish translation and its back-translation have demonstrated robust reliability and validity19.

Turgay DSM-IV-based screening and evaluation scale for conduct disorders in children and adolescents (T-DSM-IV-S)

T-DSM-IV-S was developed by Turgay to screen for disruptive behavior disorders, and its Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Ercan et al.20,. Among its 41 items, the cutoff thresholds require ratings of 2 (“often”) or 3 (“very often”) for at least 6 of the 9 inattention items, 6 of the 9 hyperactivity-impulsivity items, 4 of the 8 oppositional defiant disorder (ODD) items, and 2 of the 15 conduct disorder items. For combined-type ADHD, at least 12 items across inattention and hyperactivity-impulsivity domains must be rated 2 (“often”) or 3 (“very often”). Item severity is rated on a four-point Likert scale.

Internet gaming disorder questionnaire (IGDQ)

IGDQ was developed by Pontes et al.21,, based on the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria and the components model of addiction. The IGDQ consists of 20 items that assess online and offline gaming behavior over the past 12 months. Responses are recorded on a five-point Likert scale (“strongly disagree,” “disagree,” “neither agree nor disagree,” “agree,” and “strongly agree”). Items 2 and 19 are reverse scored. The Turkish adaptation and the validity-reliability study were conducted by Çakıroğlu and Soylu in 2019. Unlike the original six-factor structure, the Turkish version comprises five subscales (Çakiroğlu & Soylu, 2019). In our study, this scale showed good internal consistency with the Cronbach’s alfa value of 0.881.

Revised cyberbullying inventory-II (RCBI-II)

The original Cyberbullying Inventory was developed by Erdur-Baker and used for the first time by Erdur-Baker and Kavşut in 2007. RCBI-II is the second edition of the revised inventory, obtained through a review and revision performed by Topçu and Erdur-Baker in 201022; Topçu &22. Topçu and Erdur-Baker further validated RCBI-II in 2018 with 1,803 high school students23. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficients were 0.79 for the Cyberbullying Perpetration Form and 0.80 for the Cybervictimization Form, indicating good internal consistency.

Children’s depression inventory (CDI)

Developed by Kovacs24 to assess the severity of depression in children, the CDI is the most widely used self-report measure for childhood depression and has well-established psychometric properties. Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Öy (1991). Suitable for children aged 6–17 years, the CDI demonstrates acceptable item and total-scale correlations, internal consistency, test–retest reliability, and discriminant validity. The inventory consists of 27 items evaluating the child’s feelings over the past 2 weeks; each item presents three statements from which the child selects the one that best describes their experience. Responses are scored between 0 and 2, yielding total scores ranging from 0 to 54, with a clinical cutoff score of 19 and above indicating pathology. In our study, Cronbach’s Alfa value of this scale was 0.885 indicating good internal consistency.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 25. Continuous variables were expressed as mean (M) ± standard deviation (SD); categorical variables as frequency (n) and percentage (%). Normality of the distribution was evaluated using skewness and kurtosis values, along with histogram graphs. Variables that did not follow a normal distribution were corrected through transformation, and outliers were removed. Internal consistency for administered scales was determined by calculating Cronbach’s alpha coefficients. Relationships among variables were examined using Pearson’s correlation for normally distributed data, and Spearman’s correlation for the non-normally distributed (only for T-DSM-IV-S conduct problems subscale) data. To identify predictors of the dependent variables, linear regression analyses were performed using the enter method. The variables used in the regression analyses were chosen considering the correlation results and their theoretical relevance. The presence of multicollinearity was assessed with variance inflation factor (VIF), and no significant multicollinearity was observed. Linear regression assumptions, including normality and linearity, were examined and found to be met before the analysis. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied across all analyses. Simple moderation analyses were performed using Hayes’s PROCESS Macro (Model 1) to test the moderating effects of proposed variables25. All analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics, version 25. Bootstrapping with 5,000 samples was used for parameter estimation, and unstandardized regression coefficients (B) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. CI that did not include zero were considered statistically significant. Preacher and Hayes26,. Discussion

In this study, adolescents who reported a history of cybervictimization identified during clinical interviews were included. This approach allowed the examination of psychological and behavioral correlates within a clinically vulnerable group. Our findings highlight cybervictimization as a significant concern in vulnerable populations such as children and adolescents. Correlation and regression analyses revealed that depression severity was independently associated with cybervictimization, age, and IGD severity. Furthermore, ODD symptoms were significantly associated with cyberbullying perpetration whereas cybervictimization was only related to depression severity. Additionally, moderation analyses showed that IGD did not significantly moderate the relationship between cybervictimization and depression or between ODD symptoms and cyberbullying.

Individuals are more likely to become both victims and perpetrators of cyberbullying as their time spent on the Internet and social media platforms increases. Quintana-Orts and Rey (2018) identified a history of cybervictimization as the strongest predictor of becoming a cyberbully. In a recent study, a moderate positive association was observed between cyberbullying and cybervictimization in adolescents (Lozano-Blasco et al., 2020). In the current study, 86.5% of participants—adolescents with a history of cybervictimization—also reported engaging in cyberbullying. Consistent with these findings, our correlation analyses revealed a moderate positive relationship between cybervictimization and cyberbullying. These results align closely with the previous findings.

The mean CDI score in this study was 20.47, corresponding to a mildly elevated level of depressive symptoms. This finding should be interpreted with caution. Indeed, 54% of participants met the diagnostic criteria for depression on the K-SADS-PL. In a systematic review of students aged 12–17 years, Bottino et al.27, reported an association between cybervictimization and moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms. A Swedish study of 1,214 adolescents aged 13–16 years found that victims of cyberbullying were 3.41-fold more likely to experience depression than nonvictims28. Another study reported that increases in Internet use among adolescents were accompanied by a rise in depression prevalence, with nearly 60% exhibiting symptoms of depression29. Our results are consistent with previous findings, and the higher rates of depression observed may be attributable to the fact that our sample was selected from a clinical population. Although there was a history of cybervictimization, the sample consisted of adolescents who were considered to exhibit symptoms requiring evaluation by a psychiatrist. These findings underscore the importance of assessing cybervictimization as a key risk factor in depressed adolescents.

Previous findings indicate that ADHD is associated with involvement in cyberbullying. Studies have shown that individuals with combined-type ADHD are at greater risk of perpetrating cyberbullying than those with predominantly inattentive ADHD. These findings indicate that impulsivity symptoms associated with ADHD increase the risk of involvement in cyberbullying, both as a perpetrator and as a victim17,18. Although previous studies primarily explain ADHD’s association with cyberbullying perpetration through impulsivity, its role in cybervictimization should not be neglected. Previous studies indicate that individuals with ADHD diagnoses are at an increased risk of cybervictimization because of factors such as lower academic achievement, poor impulse control, difficulties in social interactions, and reduced self-awareness of their behavior30,31. For adolescents with ADHD, online interactions may offer an alternative to difficult face-to-face social situations, potentially increasing their risk of involvement in cyberbullying as both perpetrators and victims. Dawson et al.32, indicated that participation in online activities is associated with increased risks of both cyberbullying and cybervictimization. Consistent with these reports, in the present study, among cybervictimized participants, 23.6% had ADHD only, 18% had comorbid ADHD and depression, and 6.7% had comorbid ADHD and an anxiety disorder.

Regression analyses showed that age and ODD symptoms were significantly associated with cyberbullying when controlling for other variables. In a sample of 195 adolescents with ADHD aged 11–18 years, Liu et al.33, reported that ADHD constitutes a risk factor for both cybervictimization and cyberbullying, with ODD symptoms showing a particularly strong association with cyberbullying. ODD is a disorder characterized by symptoms including negative, defiant, and hostile behavior toward authority figures, refusal to comply with rules, quickness to anger, argumentativeness, and deliberately provocative behaviors, leading to functional impairment during childhood and adolescence8. Because of its frequent comorbidity with ADHD and its characteristic traits, ODD may be considered a risk factor for both cyberbullying and traditional bullying.

Previous studies that examined the relationship between cyberbullying/victimization and age present divergent findings. A meta-analysis by Kowalski et al.4, reported that cyberbullying experiences were most prevalent among middle school– and high school–aged youth. Another study reported that risk of perpetration increases with age34. This association between age and cyberbullying may reflect the increasing autonomy individuals gain in online environments as they grow older. Investigations on the relationship between age and victimization has identified three distinct patterns as follows: rising victimization with age35, decreasing victimization with age36, and a curvilinear peak in mid-adolescence (14–16 years)37. Additionally, a meta-analysis by Lozano-Blasco et al., (2023) of 32 cybervictimization studies found that, despite cross-cultural differences, individuals in American-influenced cultures showed a positive correlation between age and cybervictimization, victimization increased with age.

Although the present study found no significant relationship between age and cybervictimization, a significant association was observed between age and cyberbullying perpetration. Considering the conflicting findings in previous studies, larger-scale studies are needed to better understand the relationship between age and cyberbullying or victimization.

Our regression analyses examining factors associated with IGD indicated that both inattention and depression were significantly related to IGD. A previous study showed that depressive symptoms may contribute to the development of IGD in university students38. A recent study involving 1,362 Chinese adolescents examined bridge symptoms between IGD and depression, finding that pleasure-seeking through gaming, preoccupation with gaming, and using gaming to relieve negative emotions may contribute to increased depressive symptoms39. Although gaming is often pursued for enjoyment, prolonged engagement may impair time management, self-esteem, and social relationships, ultimately undermining daily functioning40.

Impulsivity and time-management difficulties which are characteristics of ADHD have been central to extensive research on the relationship between ADHD and IGD. Recent community-based studies reported robust associations between ADHD symptoms and IGD41. Evren38, reported that individuals with ADHD were approximately three times more likely to meet IGD criteria and that ADHD severity correlated positively with IGD severity. Another study involving 1,032 players of massively multiplayer online games specifically examined the association between inattention symptoms and IGD, revealing a significant association between inattention and IGD among American gamers42. Previous studies have indicated that inattention symptoms associated with ADHD may be key predictors of disorders characterized by internalizing symptoms43,44. Inattention is associated with social withdrawal, an internalizing symptom, indicating that inattentive adolescents may exhibit greater social introversion45. Consequently, these individuals may be drawn to online gaming environments, which are perceived as safer alternatives to traditional forms of social interaction. Thus, our findings demonstrate the relationships of depressive symptoms and inattention with IGD and align with findings from previous studies.

Studies examining the interaction between cyberbullying and IGD among adolescents are limited. Both represent prevalent online behavioral problems. Two longitudinal studies examining problematic Internet use and cyberbullying indicated a bidirectional relationship: problematic Internet use predicted later cyberbullying, whereas cyberbullying predicted subsequent problematic Internet use46,47. A study by Liu et al.33, reported that adolescents who experienced online bullying were more likely to develop Internet addiction within 6 months. Although existing literature has extensively documented an increased risk of cybervictimization with prolonged Internet use, the present study found no association between IGD and cybervictimization, which is a key finding. This may be due to participants in our victimization sample experiencing bullying primarily on social media platforms rather than in gaming environments48.

Regression analyses revealed that depressive symptoms were independently associated with both IGD and cybervictimization, consistent with previous findings. However, no direct relationship was observed between IGD and cybervictimization. Additionally, a reciprocal relationship exists between depression and cybervictimization. Victimization can exacerbate depressive symptoms, and depression can increase vulnerability to victimization. Thus, clinicians should systematically screen for cyberbullying experiences in depressed youth to inform comprehensive assessment and psychosocial interventions. In addition, moderation analyses were performed to determine whether IGD has a moderating role in the relationship between cybervictimization and depression. The results indicated that IGD did not moderate the relationship between cybervictimization and depression. These findings suggest that although IGD was significantly associated with depressive symptoms, it did not moderate the relationship between cybervictimization and depression. Similarly, although ODD symptoms were significantly associated with cyberbullying, IGD did not show a moderating effect on this relationship. The relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical power, particularly for detecting interaction effects in moderation analyses. Therefore, the non-significant moderation findings should be interpreted with caution and require replication in larger samples49.

Limitations

Several limitations must be considered for interpreting this study’s results. For broader adolescent populations, the research’s generalizability is limited by a relatively small sample size in a single clinical center. The sample included only individuals who had been identified as cybervictims and referred to psychiatric evaluation, which likely biased the findings toward adolescents with more severe psychopathology. Additionally, the inclusion of only adolescents with a history of cybervictimization may have restricted the variability of this variable and should be considered when interpreting analyses in which cybervictimization was treated as an outcome. Second, the cross-sectional design is preventing the establishment of temporal or causal relationships because such design involves cybervictimization along with cyberbullying and IGD plus ADHD symptoms as well as depression. We also used clinical interviews with self-report tools to assess all psychiatric symptoms plus behavioral patterns which might lead to recall bias. Fourth, the study did not take into account any contextual factors such as family functioning or peer relationships. These factors along with patterns of technology use outside gaming may influence the observed associations. In future research, longitudinal multi-center designs with larger and more diverse samples should be employed. Additional psychosocial variables should be explored along with objective behavioral measures incorporated for better comprehension of the interplay between psychiatric symptoms and problematic online behaviors50.

Conclusion

This study provides insights into the psychiatric and behavioral correlates of cybervictimization in clinically referred adolescents. Depression severity was primarily associated with cybervictimization, older age, and higher IGD levels while cyberbullying perpetration was related to ODD symptoms. IGD did not moderate the relationships of ODD and cyberbullying or cybervictimization and depression. Clinicians should consider these findings to assess cybervictimized adolescents thoroughly as they focus on problematic gaming, disruptive behavior patterns, and depressive symptoms. Early identification and efficient interventions for online behavioral problems can help prevent negative mental health outcomes resulting from cybervictimization and perpetration in digital environments.
