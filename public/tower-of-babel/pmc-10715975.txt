A Case Series of Pregnant Women With Mitral Stenosis
Authors: Gleiny Vázquez Hernández, Román Vasallo Peraza, Jesús Ernesto Pérez Torga, Luis Oscar Dominguez Choy, Ivette Castillo Arocha, Susana Hernández García, Mónica Meluzá Martín, Yilena Gil Jiménez, Pedro Antonio Román Rubio
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Pregnant women with mitral stenosis have a high cardiovascular risk, and their outcomes vary greatly. The management of these patients should be individualized and carried out by a multidisciplinary team. This case series presents 3 cases of pregnant women with severe mitral stenosis, describing their progression and therapeutic strategies.

Valvular disorders are the most prevalent cardiovascular diagnosis during pregnancy in emerging countries (55%) and the second most frequent in developed countries (32%).1,2 Mitral stenosis (MS) represents the valvular pathology with the poorest hemodynamic tolerance during pregnancy.1, 2, 3 Prognosis depends on the severity of the lesion and the functional class of the pregnant woman.3,4 However, apparently stable patients may experience complications as hemodynamic changes of pregnancy develop.5,6Learning Objectives•To recognize the importance of multidisciplinary and individualized management of pregnant women with mitral stenosis.•To identify mitral stenosis as a high-risk cardiac condition during pregnancy.

Patient 1

Patient 1 was 29-year-old pregnant woman at 22 weeks of gestation with favorable obstetric evolution. The diagnosis of severe MS was confirmed (Videos 1, 2, 3 and 4), and treatment with propranolol 20 mg every 8 hours was initiated. The Wilkins echocardiographic score was determined to be 6 points, indicating that she was a favorable candidate for percutaneous mitral commissurotomy (PMC), which was electively performed at 23.2 weeks of gestation. The procedure was carried out without complications, achieving a valve area of 1.75 cm2 and a decrease in mean gradient to 5.06 mm Hg. After the intervention, the patient returned to NYHA functional class I and remained asymptomatic until term. An elective cesarean was performed at 37 weeks because of cardiovascular indications, resulting in a full-term newborn weighing 2,430 g (10th percentile for gestational age) with Apgar scores of 9/9. There were no maternal or perinatal complications.

Patient 2

Patient 2 was a 45-year-old pregnant woman with a history of chronic arterial hypertension, evaluated for the first time at 33.2 weeks of gestation because of an episode of acute pulmonary edema. She presented with atrial fibrillation on admission with a rapid ventricular response, but sinus rhythm was restored at the time of evaluation. No significant obstetric alterations were noted. The diagnosis of double mitral lesion with severe stenosis and moderate regurgitation was made (Videos 5, 6, 7 and 8), and she was transferred to the Cuban Institute of Cardiology and Cardiovascular Surgery (Instituto de Cardiología y Cirugía Cardiovascular). She was not a favorable candidate for PMC, so treatment with intravenous furosemide, propranolol, and unfractionated heparin was initiated. Cesarean was performed at 34.2 weeks of gestation because of maternal hemodynamic decompensation. The newborn weighed 2,300 g (5th percentile for gestational age) with Apgar scores of 9/9. The patient remained clinically stable and was discharged on postoperative day 6 without complications.

Patient 3

Patient 3 was a 24-year-old pregnant woman at 17 weeks of gestation with a history of recurrent syncope. She was referred to our service after an episode of syncope with falls, requiring emergency department care. The diagnosis of severe MS with moderate regurgitation and mild pulmonary arterial hypertension was made (Videos 9, 10, 11 and 12). She was hospitalized for medical treatment and was evaluated for a possible PMC. However, because of a lack of favorable echocardiographic characteristics (Wilkins score of 8 points, subvalvular apparatus fibrosis, and severe mitral regurgitation), this procedure was not performed. Treatment with propranolol and diuretics was initiated. The patient presented with recurrent palpitations and progressive worsening of dyspnea on exertion. Cesarean was performed at 37 weeks because of cardiovascular indications. The newborn weighed 3,040 g (50th percentile for gestational age) with Apgar scores of 9/9. The patient remained stable postoperatively and was discharged on postoperative day 4 without complications.

Discussion

Virtually all hemodynamic changes that occur in a normal pregnancy are deleterious for pregnant women with MS.2,6 They may cause decompensation of pre-existing heart disease, as in patients 1 and 3, or it may be the first manifestation of a previously unknown disease, as in patient 2.

The drug of choice for pregnant women with severe MS is cardioselective beta-blockers. The dosage should be adjusted according to the blood pressure and heart rate profile of the pregnant women, maintaining a rate between 70 and 80 beats/min. Lower values have been associated with intrauterine growth restriction.7 All patients received treatment with propranolol because of the unavailability of the first-line drug during pregnancy (metoprolol). The disadvantage of propranolol compared to metoprolol lies in its nonselective nature, which increases uterine muscle tone and might lead to a threat of preterm labor.3,7 Loop diuretics are indicated when signs of pulmonary congestion appear, with furosemide being the most commonly used. Excessive volume depletion should be avoided, as it might lead to uteroplacental hypoperfusion.1,2

The use of anticoagulants in pregnant women with severe MS follows the same guidelines as the general population.4,8 In the presented series, patients received anticoagulation because they had a left atrial volume index >60 mL/m2, which in patient 2 was associated with a history of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. A combined anticoagulation regimen using heparin and warfarin was used based on gestational age because it was considered to be the most balanced approach in relation to the risk of maternal and fetal complications.1,2,8 In addition to pharmacologic treatment, PMC represents an important therapeutic option in pregnant women with moderate and severe MS, and currently, it is a procedure performed with a low rate of maternal and perinatal complications.3,4

Interventional procedures during pregnancy should be performed electively between 18 and 24 weeks of gestation. At 18 weeks, the uterus is below the umbilical scar, and organogenesis is complete, reducing the risk of radiation damage. It is not recommended to exceed 24 weeks to minimize fetal thyroid exposure to iodinated contrast effects.9 However, in situations of high maternal risk, PMC may be considered at any time during pregnancy, provided that the valvular anatomy is favorable and in the absence of contraindications.1,3

The timing of pregnancy termination depends on gestational age and, above all, the appearance of signs of maternal or fetal decompensation. It should be performed in a hospital with technical support and trained personnel to manage complications.1,2 In all patients, cesarean was performed at the Cuban Institute of Cardiology and Cardiovascular Surgery, as recommended by the guidelines for the management of pregnant women at very high cardiovascular risk (World Health Organization classification category IV).1

In these particular patients, the immediate postpartum period constitutes a high-risk period for decompensation because of the sudden increase in preload resulting from uterine decompression, which can trigger an episode of acute pulmonary edema.2,6 For this reason, all patients received a 20-mg intravenous dose of furosemide immediately after delivery, a strategy that is protocolized in the National Service of Cardiopathy and Pregnancy and its regional delegations for all pregnant women with severe MS.9

Unfavorable fetal outcomes are more frequent in relation to the severity of MS. The risk of having a small fetus for gestational age varies at 4.3%, 10.8%, and 19.6% and that of preterm delivery at 3.8%, 9.3%, and 21.3% for mild, moderate, and severe MS, respectively.10 In the presented series, no newborns with a weight below 2,000 g were reported, which might be related to multidisciplinary care and obstetric follow-up by trained personnel experienced in managing pregnant women with cardiovascular diseases.

Patients were received on beta-blockers and were followed up at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year after discharge.

Conclusions

The management of pregnant women with MS should be individualized, considering the severity of the lesion and the functional class of the patient. A multidisciplinary approach involving obstetricians, cardiologists, and anesthetists is crucial for optimal outcomes. PMC can be considered in selected cases. Proper management and close monitoring are essential to minimize maternal and fetal risks in these high-risk pregnancies.
