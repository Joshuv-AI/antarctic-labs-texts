Meta-analysis of maternal exposure to heavy metals (lead, cadmium, mercury, chromium) and adverse pregnancy outcomes
Authors: Yuan Ou, Jie Han, Yanfang Wei, Xiaoyan Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe association of heavy metal exposure with adverse pregnancy outcomes (APOs) remains a subject of debate. The goal of the present study was to analyze the correlation of heavy metals (lead [Pb], cadmium [Cd], mercury [Hg], chromium [Cr]) with APOs.MethodsA systematic review was carried out across four databases: The Cochrane Library, PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase up to November 19, 2024. Two independent researchers performed the literature screening and data extraction based on predefined eligibility criteria. Then, based on the selected literature, the researchers extracted the odds ratios (ORs) and confidence intervals (CIs) of heavy metals (Pb, Cd, Hg, and Cr) and pregnancy outcomes (preterm birth, low birth weight [LBW], and small gestational age [SGA]). The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was adopted for quality assessment. Heterogeneity tests, sensitivity checks, subgroup evaluations, and publication bias analyses were implemented via Stata 15.1 software.ResultsThis meta-analysis incorporated 24 studies, involving 52,390 subjects. The results demonstrated a significant association of Pb concentration with preterm birth (OR = 1.4, 95% CI: 1.17, 1.68; P < 0.001) and SGA (OR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.04, 2.66; P = 0.034), though not with LBW. Cd concentration was associated with LBW (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.00, 1.20; P = 0.041) and SGA (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.08, 1.48; P = 0.003), but not with preterm birth. No significant association was observed between Hg concentration and preterm birth. Due to insufficient data, the impact of Cr was not analyzed. Subgroup analysis suggested that region, study design, measurement time, and measurement source may influence the conclusions.ConclusionsThis meta-analysis indicated that Pb exposure was associated with elevated risks of both preterm birth and SGA. Cd exposure exhibited significant associations with LBW and SGA. However, the limited number of articles for other indicators warrants further investigation. Future prospective, high-quality studies are encouraged to explore these relationships further.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08507-x.

Introduction

Adverse pregnancy outcomes (APOs) include preterm birth, low birth weight (LBW), and small for gestational age (SGA). APOs represent a major global public health challenge due to their profound implications for maternal and infant health, socioeconomic burden, and long-term population quality [1]. Worldwide, approximately 13.4 million infants are born preterm each year, accounting for 9.8% of all live births. On average, one in ten newborns is preterm. Complications associated with preterm birth constitute the leading cause of death in children under five years of age. This results in approximately 1 million child deaths annually [2]. LBW (< 2,500 g, regardless of gestational age [GA]) is closely related to preterm delivery. This category largely overlaps with preterm births and also includes full-term infants who are SGA. Infants born both preterm and underweight face particularly elevated mortality risks [3]. Etiologies of APOs are multifactorial, involving genetic predisposition [4], maternal factors [5], and environmental influences [6].

Accumulating evidence suggests that environmental heavy metal exposure may contribute to preterm birth and LBW. Heavy metals—such as lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), mercury (Hg), and chromium (Cr)—are defined by high density (> 5 g/cm³) and may cause chronic toxicity upon bioaccumulation. Early life represents a critical window of development characterized by heightened susceptibility to environmental hazards. Prenatal exposure to adverse environmental factors can readily induce intrauterine developmental abnormalities, leading to APOs.

Exposure levels to these four metals vary across Asia, Europe, and the Americas. Overall, Europe and the Americas exhibit low-to-moderate exposure, whereas Asian regions display considerable heterogeneity, generally falling into moderate-to-high exposure categories. This is partly due to disparities in regulatory stringency and policy implementation among developing countries.

Numerous epidemiological studies suggest a potential link between heavy metal exposure (e.g., Pb, Cd, Hg, Cr) and APOs. However, the precise mechanisms, dose-response relationships, and population-specific effects remain controversial. For instance, some studies indicate that blood Pb levels significantly increase preterm birth risk [7], whereas other cohort studies report no significant association [8]. Similar inconsistencies exist for Cd and Hg. Cd accumulates in the placenta and disrupts zinc metabolism, which could restrict fetal growth [9]. However, some researchers argue that Cd’s effects may be confounded by factors such as smoking or nutritional status [10]. These discrepancies highlight the need for studies incorporating more precise biomarkers (e.g., placental metalomics) and rigorous confounder adjustment to clarify the independent effects of heavy metals on pregnancy outcomes. Thus, the objective of this study is to analyze the association of Pb, Cd, Hg, and Cr with APOs using confounder-adjusted data, and to further explore variations by region, study design, biospecimen type, and timing of exposure assessment.

Methods

This meta-analysis was carried out per the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [11]. (File S1) The study protocol has been registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42024615549).

Search strategies

A retrieval was performed in the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase databases up to November 19, 2024. The retrieval was restricted to English-language articles. It utilized a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text words. The MeSH terms used were as follows: Mercury, Cadmium, Lead, Chromium, Metals, Metal, Premature Birth, prematurity, Infant Low Birth Weight, low birth weight, Infant Small for Gestational Age, small for gestational age, Pregnancy Outcome. The specific strategies are detailed in File S2. Additionally, a secondary search was conducted in the reference lists of published systematic reviews and other relevant articles to ensure comprehensive literature retrieval.

Eligibility criteria

Literature meeting the following criteria was included: (i) The study population consisted of healthy pregnant women. (ii) The heavy metals under study included Pb, Cd, Hg, and Cr. The measurement methods included blood and urine, among others, with no restrictions on sources. The extracted endpoints included preterm birth, LBW, and SGA. (iii) Provision of adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs); (iv) Study design: Cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional studies.

Literature with the following characteristics was excluded: (i) Animal or cell experiments, case reports, scientific experimental plans, reviews, letters, editorials, conference papers, etc.; (ii) Studies with missing data or significant errors; (iii) Duplicate publications; (iv) Research with mismatched objectives and outcomes; (v) Non-English articles. Non-English articles were excluded for the following reason: First, English articles tend to be of higher quality. Second, non-English articles are all published in local journals. In reality, there are many cases where the data and quality do not match.

Data extraction

The searched literature was imported into EndNote. Two researchers (Y.F. Wei and J. Han) independently conducted literature screening according to the eligibility criteria, first by reviewing titles and abstracts, and then by reading full texts. Disagreements were adjudicated through consensus discussion or by involving a third researcher (Y. Ou). Two researchers (Y.F. Wei and J. Han) independently extracted the following information from eligible literature using Excel 2016: study type, first author, number of subjects, publication year, GA, age, country, primiparity, heavy metals exposed, measurement time, measurement source, outcomes, adjustment factors, etc.

Quality evaluation

Two researchers (Y.F. Wei and J. Han) assessed the quality of case-control and cohort studies via the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [12]. NOS scores are calculated with a total score of nine points, with one to three points representing low quality, four to six points representing medium quality, and seven to nine points representing high quality. For the evaluation of cross-sectional studies, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) checklist was employed [13]. The questionnaire has a total score of 11 points, with eight to eleven points representing high-quality studies, four to seven points representing medium quality, and zero to three points representing low quality.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed utilizing Stata 15. Multifactor-adjusted results were extracted. ORs with CIs were summarized to evaluate the correlation of heavy metals with pregnancy outcomes. Heterogeneity was assessed using I² and the p-value of the Cochrane’s Q statistic. Significant and substantial heterogeneity was defined as I² and P < 0.01, in which case a random-effects model (REM) was employed. Otherwise, a fixed-effects model (FEM) was utilized. Subgroup analysis was performed to explore sources of high heterogeneity. When substantial heterogeneity was present, we examined its sources in terms of measurement source, region, and measurement time (T1 = first trimester, T2 = second trimester, T3 = third trimester), as well as study type. Sensitivity analysis was conducted by omitting individual studies one at a time to assess the robustness of the results. If the effect size remained within the original pooled interval after exclusion, the conclusion was considered stable. Otherwise, it indicated that the study significantly impacted the pooled results and should be excluded. When there were more than ten studies, Egger’s test was used to assess publication bias. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Literature retrieval and screening

Literature retrieval yielded 12,168 articles. After removing 1,560 duplicates, 10,452 articles were excluded following initial screening of titles and abstracts. Full-text reviews of the remaining 156 articles were conducted in strict accordance with the predefined eligibility criteria, resulting in a final inclusion of 24 articles. Basic characteristics and quality evaluation of included studies

The 24 [7, 8, 14–35] eligible studies originated from eight countries (Mexico, the United States [U.S.], China, Japan, the United Kingdom [UK], Canada, Myanmar, Suriname), involving 52,390 subjects. The age ranged from 22 to 34. These studies examined four metals (Pb, Cd, Hg, and Cr) and three outcomes (preterm birth, LBW, and SGA). There were 12 articles on Pb and preterm birth, four articles on Pb and LBW, and five articles on Pb and SGA. Six articles examined Cd and preterm birth, three articles examined Cd and LBW, and four articles examined Cd and SGA. Eight articles focused on Hg and preterm birth. Basic characteristics of the eligible literature are illustrated in File S3. The literature comprised seven case-control studies and 17 cohort studies. The quality scores of all studies exceeded 6 points, indicating high quality (File S4).

Meta-analysis results

Preterm birth

Pb and preterm birth

Twelve studies involving 13,923 individuals were included. The pooled results, using an REM (I2 = 79.5%, p < 0.001), indicated a significant correlation between higher Pb levels and an elevated risk of preterm birth (OR = 1.4, 95% CI: 1.17, 1.68; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A).

Cd and preterm birth

Six studies encompassing 7,469 subjects were incorporated. The pooled results, using an REM (I2 = 81.7%, p < 0.001), indicated no significant association of higher Cd levels with preterm birth (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 0.91, 1.67; P = 0.185) (Fig. 2B).

Hg and preterm birth

Eight studies incorporating 7,759 subjects were included. The pooled results, using an REM (I2 = 63.6%, p = 0.008), illustrated no significant association between higher Hg levels and preterm birth (OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 0.91, 1.44; P = 0.24) (Fig. 2C).

LBW

Pb and LBW

Four studies involving 6,646 patients were included. Analysis of this outcome, with I2 = 56.5% and p = 0.075 in heterogeneity analysis, employed an REM. The pooled results displayed no significant association between Pb and LBW (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.74, 1.34; P = 0.998) (Fig. 3A).

Cd and LBW

Three studies encompassing 23,391 subjects were incorporated. Analysis of this outcome, with I2 = 0.00% and p = 0.894 in heterogeneity analysis, utilized an REM. The pooled results demonstrated a significant correlation of higher Cd levels with an elevated risk of LBW (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.00, 1.20; P = 0.041) (Fig. 3B).

SGA

Pb and SGA

Five studies were included (four studies remaining after sensitivity analysis), involving 9,726 individuals. Analysis of this outcome, with I2 = 84% and p < 0.001 in heterogeneity analysis, applied an REM. The pooled results indicated a significant correlation of higher Pb levels with an elevated risk of SGA (OR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.04, 2.66; P = 0.034) (Fig. 4A).

Cd and SGA

Four studies involving 22,239 subjects were encompassed. Analysis of this outcome, with I2 = 41% and p = 0.166 in heterogeneity analysis, adopted an REM. The pooled results indicated a significant correlation of higher Cd levels with an elevated risk of SGA (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.08, 1.48; P = 0.003) (Fig. 4B).

Qualitative overview

Other outcomes, including Hg and LBW/SGA, and Cr and preterm birth/LBW/SGA, were not quantitatively analyzed due to an insufficient number of articles. Only a narrative review was conducted. Regarding preterm birth, a significant association with higher levels of Pb (OR = 1.4, 95% CI: 1.17, 1.68; P < 0.001) and Cd (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 0.91, 1.67; P = 0.185) was observed. No association was identified with higher levels of Hg (OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 0.91, 1.44; P = 0.24). Regarding LBW, a significant correlation with higher levels of Pb (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.74, 1.34; P = 0.998) and Cd (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.00, 1.20; P = 0.041) was discovered. Regarding SGA, a significant association with higher levels of Pb (OR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.04, 2.66; P = 0.034) and Cd (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.08, 1.48; P = 0.003) was identified.

Subgroup analysis

For studies exhibiting significant heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted based on region, study type, measurement time, and measurement source.

Pb and preterm birth

When subgroup analysis was performed by region, elevated Pb concentrations demonstrated significant associations with increased preterm birth risk in both Asia and the Americas. When subgroup analysis was conducted by study type, increased Pb concentrations illustrated significant correlations with elevated preterm birth risk in cohort studies (OR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.13, 1.70; P = 0.02), but no correlation was identified in case-control studies (File S5.1.2). When subgroup analysis was carried out by measurement time, elevated Pb concentrations exhibited significant correlations with increased preterm birth risk in T2 and T1 T2 T3, while no association was identified in T1, T3, T1 T2, T1 T3, or umbilical cord blood (File S5.1.4). When subgroup analysis was implemented by measurement source, increased Pb concentrations were significantly linked to elevated preterm birth risk in blood (OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.49, 2.58; P < 0.001), while no association was discovered in urine or umbilical cord blood (File S5.1.5).

Cd and preterm birth

No significant correlation between higher Cd concentrations and preterm birth was identified when subgroup analyses were conducted by region, study type, measurement time, and measurement source (File S5.2.2–5.2).

Hg and preterm birth

When subgroup analysis was conducted by study type, higher Hg concentrations were significantly linked to a higher risk of preterm birth in case-control studies (OR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.03, 1.67; P = 0.029), but no association was found in cohort studies (File S5.3.2). When subgroup analysis was carried out by region, higher Hg concentrations were significantly correlated with a higher risk of preterm birth in Asia (OR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.06, 1.87; P = 0.019), while no correlation was identified in Europe or the Americas (File S5.3.3). When subgroup analysis was implemented by measurement time, elevated Hg concentrations were significantly correlated with increased preterm birth risk in T3 (OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.08, 1.38; P < 0.001) and T1 T2 (OR = 5.88, 95% CI: 1.72, 20.11; P = 0.005), while no correlation was identified in T1, T2, or T1 T3 (File S5.3.4). When subgroup analysis was performed by measurement source, no association was discovered between higher Hg concentrations and preterm birth in blood or urine.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

The robustness of all outcomes was evaluated via sensitivity analyses. The stability of the results was tested by omitting individual studies one at a time. The results indicated instability in the conclusions for Pb and SGA. After removing one article that led to unstable conclusions, the sensitivity analysis was repeated and the conclusions were found to be stable (File S5.6.1-2.1). Publication bias was assessed for outcomes with ≥ 10 articles using Egger’s test. Publication bias was found in studies examining preterm birth risk and Pb exposure (P = 0.001). Six articles were added via the trim-and-fill approach. The OR after trim-and-fill was 1.058 (95% CI: 0.881, 1.272, P = 0.545). This finding is inconsistent with previous conclusions. It suggests that the original analysis may have been subject to conclusion bias, which occurs when studies with positive results are more likely to be published than those with negative results. The initial analysis indicated a strong association between Pb exposure and SGA. However, the presence of publication bias suggests that this effect may have been overstated (Fig. 5).

Discussion

APOs, including preterm birth, LBW, and SGA, are significant issues in the global public health domain at present. Multiple factors can cause APOs, with prenatal heavy metal exposure being particularly important. Currently, studies investigating the adverse effects of heavy metal exposure on APOs have yielded inconsistent results. This study analyzed 24 eligible datasets and concluded that Pb exposure was associated with an increased risk of preterm birth and SGA; however, it was not associated with LBW. Cd exposure increases the risk of LBW and SGA, but is not linked to preterm birth. Hg exposure is not correlated with preterm birth.

Pb and preterm birth, SGA, and LBW

This meta-analysis confirms the conclusions of Habibian et al. in 2021 that elevated maternal blood Pb levels are significantly associated with the risk of preterm birth. However, there are inconsistencies in the subgroup analysis by measurement time. Previous research suggests that the T2 and T3 periods are linked to preterm birth, but T1 is not. In the current study, the T2 period is related to preterm birth, while T1 and T3 are not. This discrepancy may be due to different article inclusion criteria (this meta-analysis excluded articles whose original data had been logarithmically transformed) and different subgroup categorizations (this meta-analysis placed articles that did not describe when the blood specimen was obtained into the T1 T2 T3 groups). In the subgroup analysis by study type, cohort studies yielded positive results, while case-control studies yielded negative results. This may be related to the small number of case-control studies incorporated in this meta-analysis (one article). The subgroup analysis by specimen source is consistent with previous research results: blood specimens are related to preterm birth, while urine and umbilical cord specimens are not. This suggests that blood specimens are an important indicator for predicting preterm birth. However, the limited sample size warrants cautious interpretation of these findings.

It should be noted that the observed association may be influenced by publication bias. The discrepancy between the pre- and post-trim-and-fill analyses strongly suggests that the original meta-analytic result may have overestimated the true risk of preterm birth from maternal Pb exposure. This is because studies showing null or weak associations are less likely to be published. The adjusted result implies that the link between maternal Pb exposure and preterm birth may be minimal or even absent after accounting for this bias. Substantial heterogeneity across studies indicates that this relationship may vary considerably among different populations and is likely modulated by factors such as exposure levels, nutritional status, and socioeconomic conditions.

The present study also confirms Habibian et al.‘s (2021) conclusion that elevated maternal blood Pb levels are significantly linked to the risk of SGA. However, the present study differs from Bellinger et al.‘s (1991) conclusion regarding umbilical cord blood Pb levels. This discrepancy may be because umbilical cord blood Pb primarily reflects exposure during the final stages of pregnancy and does not capture the effects of Pb exposure during the critical early developmental stages. Moderate heterogeneity was observed (I²=56.5%), but a subgroup analysis was precluded due to the insufficient number of eligible studies. 6.

Notably, maternal blood Pb showed no significant correlation with LBW (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.74–1.34). This finding differs from Wang et al.‘s conclusion, possibly because the present meta-analysis excluded literature with logarithmically transformed original data and because the LBW definition itself has limitations. Some studies excluded premature infants when assessing LBW. Term SGA infants may also have different outcome classifications due to the GA compensation effect (e.g., a 38-week infant weighing 2,500 g does not meet the LBW standard). These findings suggest that the impact of Pb exposure on birth weight requires comprehensive evaluation with GA consideration.

While the precise mechanisms linking Pb exposure to preterm birth require further elucidation, existing evidence suggests multi-pathway involvement, possibly in a synergistic manner. First, maternal Pb exposure may activate the fetal hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, promoting excessive secretion of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH). This leads to premature elevation of fetal cortisol and disrupts placental hormone homeostasis, which is characterized by increased estrogen and decreased progesterone. This disruption can trigger early parturition. Notably, an increase in CRH exceeding 1.5-fold per week after 20 weeks of gestation is associated with a 7.2-fold higher risk of preterm delivery [36]. Additionally, Pb exhibits environmental estrogenic activity by binding to and activating estrogen receptor alpha (ERα), while potentially inhibiting progesterone synthesis or function. This endocrine imbalance creates a state of relative estrogen dominance, enhancing uterine smooth muscle contractility and compromising its physiological quiescence [37]. Pb may also disrupt the delicate balance between matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and their tissue inhibitors (TIMPs) in placental and fetal membrane tissues. This results in excessive extracellular matrix degradation, thereby elevating the risk of preterm premature rupture of membranes [38].

There is a clear dose-response relationship between maternal Pb exposure and reduced fetal birth weight. Mechanistically, Pb exposure can significantly alter the placenta’s ability to transport nutrients, for example. Pb competitively inhibits calcium channels and interferes with placental transport of essential trace elements such as zinc and iron, thereby impairing fetal nutrient supply [39]. Pb also disrupts endocrine balance within the maternal-placental-fetal unit by altering thyroid hormone levels and suppressing insulin-like growth factor signaling pathways. These hormonal disturbances directly impact fetal growth velocity, particularly during mid-to-late gestation [40]. Additionally, Pb-induced reactive oxygen species cause placental oxidative damage. Studies show significantly elevated lipid peroxidation products and reduced antioxidant enzyme activity in placental tissues of Pb-exposed pregnant women. This state of oxidative stress promotes placental cell apoptosis and diminishes functional placental mass [41].

In the context of preterm birth, this study identified a significant association between Pb exposure during the second trimester (T2) and preterm delivery. Associations in the first (T1) and third (T3) trimesters, however, were not significant. This may reflect a critical window of susceptibility. T2 is a key period for fetal HPA axis maturation and placental hormonal programming. Pb exposure during this period may activate the fetal HPA axis, induce premature CRH elevation, disrupt the estrogen-progesterone balance, and initiate the parturition cascade. Concurrently, Pb-induced imbalance between MMPs and TIMPs may increase extracellular matrix degradation, thereby elevating the risk of membrane rupture. This finding is consistent with our observation that blood Pb levels (reflecting recent exposure) more strongly predict preterm birth.

Regarding fetal growth restriction, maternal blood Pb levels were significantly associated with SGA, but not with LBW. This seemingly contradictory result may stem from the confusion inherent in the definition of LBW regarding GA. Pb exposure limits the supply of nutrients to the fetus and its growth rate. It does so by competitively inhibiting placental transport of essential elements (e.g., calcium, iron, and zinc), disrupting insulin-like growth factor signaling, and inducing oxidative damage to the placenta. These effects manifest more directly as an impaired growth trajectory, as indicated by SGA (weight below the 10th percentile for GA), rather than the absolute weight-based LBW threshold (< 2,500 g).

Cd and LBW and SGA

This study confirms, via meta-analysis, that elevated levels of maternal Cd exposure are significantly and positively correlated with LBW and SGA. Cd exposure demonstrated a modest correlation with LBW (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.00–1.20.00.20, P = 0.041), consistent with meta-analysis conclusions by Huang et al. (2019) and Amegah et al. (2020). The association with SGA is more significant (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.08–1.48, P = 0.003), consistent with the research results of Amegah et al. (2020) and Habibian et al. (2021). These associations may have common mechanisms that affect fetal growth through multiple synergistic pathways.

Maternal Cd exposure contributes to reduced fetal birth weight through multiple interconnected mechanisms. Cd alters the DNA methylation status of key genes in the placenta. Higher maternal blood Cd levels are associated with the hypomethylation of imprinted genes, such as PEG3 and MEG3 [42]. These epigenetic modifications may disrupt pathways critical for fetal growth regulation.

Furthermore, Cd competes with essential elements, including zinc and iron, for binding sites, thereby reducing their bioavailability. Interestingly, low iron levels amplify Cd-induced methylation changes in genes such as PLAGL1 and PEG3 (p < 0.05), while zinc may partially mitigate Cd’s toxic effects [42].

Cd also induces oxidative stress in the placenta, disturbing the balance between cell proliferation and apoptosis. Studies on animals demonstrate that exposure to high doses of Cd leads to reduced fetal weight and maternal toxicity [17], supporting the idea that oxidative damage plays a role in fetal growth impairment.

Other findings

Through meta-analysis, this study discovered that maternal Cd and Hg exposure had no statistically significant association with the preterm birth risk. The combined OR value of Cd exposure was 1.23 (95% CI: 0.91–1.67, P = 0.185), inconsistent with prior studies (Amegah et al., 2020; Asefi et al., 2020). This discrepancy may be due to the relatively small number of literature included in this meta-analysis, although all the included data are adjusted. The combined OR value of Hg exposure was 1.15 (95% CI: 0.91–1.44, P = 0.24), consistent with Pan et al.‘s results (2025). While this study confirms significant associations between Pb or Cd exposure and specific APOs, the underlying biological mechanisms are complex and interrelated. Based on the current evidence, a multi-step mechanistic model is proposed. The initial step involves placental transfer and accumulation of heavy metals. This serves as a prerequisite for subsequent toxic effects. Thereafter, intrauterine metal accumulation induces oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, directly compromising placental structure and function. Simultaneously, heavy metals may mimic or antagonize endogenous hormones, disrupting key endocrine pathways, such as the HPA axis, and impairing the hormonal equilibrium necessary for maintaining pregnancy. These mechanisms do not operate in isolation, but rather, they act synergistically, ultimately contributing to distinct APOs, such as preterm birth, LBW, or SGA.

Strengths

The strengths of this meta-analysis lie in its comprehensive investigation of the association between four heavy metals (Pb, Cd, Hg, and Cr) and APOs (preterm birth, LBW, SGA, birth weight, GA at birth). Furthermore, adjusted OR values and CIs derived from multivariate analyses were extracted, enhancing the reliability of the findings. Third, subgroup analyses were performed for factors that may influence the results, such as measurement time and measurement source. Fourth, most of the eligible studies had good quality, with appropriate case/control definitions, control selection, and exposure assessment. There are three main routes of heavy metal exposure to the human body: the digestive tract, the respiratory tract, and the skin. The main exposure route for Pb is through the digestive tract, such as when contaminated water or food enters the body and accumulates in the bones. Avoiding the use of Pb-coated containers, drinking filtered water, and washing hands frequently can help prevent Pb exposure. Cd exposure is relatively common through both the respiratory and digestive tracts, especially through the intake of crustacean seafood or inhalation of Cd dust. Reducing shellfish intake and avoiding smoking can help prevent Cd exposure. Exposure to Hg mainly occurs through the respiratory tract and skin contact. Hg is volatile in its normal state. For example, Hg from broken thermometers can be absorbed into the human body, as can Hg from Hg-containing cosmetics through the skin. Properly handling Hg waste and choosing Hg-free cosmetics can help prevent Hg exposure. Cr exposure is common through the digestive and respiratory tracts. For example, Cr-containing industrial dust can be inhaled, and Cr can migrate from Cr-excessive frying pans to acidic food. Wearing protective masks in industrial settings and avoiding contact between acidic foods and stainless steel or alloy tableware can help prevent Cr exposure.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. First, there is the issue of heterogeneity and bias. High levels of heterogeneity were observed across various outcomes. Despite performing subgroup analyses according to region, study type, measurement time, and measurement source, the source of heterogeneity could not be identified. The association between Pb exposure and preterm birth is potentially influenced by publication bias. While biological mechanisms suggest that Pb may increase preterm birth risk via pathways such as placental dysfunction, the epidemiological evidence from this study appears less robust after bias correction. Further validation of this relationship is required through well-designed prospective cohorts, particularly those that actively publish negative results. Second, some outcomes (e.g., Hg and LBW, and Cr-related outcomes) could not be analyzed due to an insufficient number of included studies; thus, a review of the results was conducted. Third, although we extracted adjusted OR values and CIs from multivariate analyses, the factors influencing the final inclusion of different data in this study vary and are not specifically mentioned. This makes it impossible to conduct further adjustment analyses for confounding factors, such as those strongly associated with gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes, preterm birth, and LBW infants. We hope to obtain more independent results regarding the correlation between heavy metal exposure and pregnancy outcomes in subsequent studies.

Public health implications

Our meta-analysis provides compelling evidence of a link between maternal exposure to Pb and Cd and specific APOs. These findings underscore the urgent need for a multi-level public health response that includes targeted screening, strengthened environmental regulations, and evidence-based preventive strategies.

Towards targeted maternal screening

Based on the observed associations of Pb with SGA and Cd with LBW, and considering that the blood sample serves as an effective biomarker for Pb exposure, we recommend integrating blood Pb and urinary Cd testing into routine prenatal screening for high-risk pregnant individuals. High-risk groups include individuals residing near industrial or mining areas, occupationally exposed women, and individuals with dietary habits that increase exposure (e.g., frequent consumption of shellfish or animal organs). Subgroup analyses in this study indicate that the optimal timing for Pb screening may be the second trimester (T2). Defining reference ranges and safety thresholds for heavy metal exposure during pregnancy will inform clinical decision-making and enable timely intervention.

Strengthening environmental and food surveillance

Heavy metals enter the human body through environmental media (air, water, and soil) and the food chain. Therefore, we propose that environmental protection agencies strengthen their continuous monitoring of metal concentrations in the air and soil in high-risk areas, such as those near lead-acid battery recycling plants, non-ferrous metal smelters, and electronic waste disposal sites. This data should be made publicly accessible. Additionally, market regulatory authorities should enhance the inspection and traceability management of foods prone to heavy metal accumulation, especially Cd, such as rice, leafy vegetables, and aquatic products, to ensure compliance with national safety standards. Furthermore, updating outdated water supply infrastructure can reduce Pb leaching into drinking water.

Implementing Evidence-Based preventive strategies

In light of the mechanisms of heavy metal toxicity, such as oxidative stress and competition with essential nutrients, the following measures are recommended: promote pre-conceptual and prenatal nutrition education to encourage adequate intake of calcium, iron, zinc, and selenium, which may antagonize Pb/Cd absorption and mitigate their bioavailability; conduct health education campaigns advising pregnant people—particularly high-risk groups—to minimize exposure sources (e.g., avoid using non-compliant cosmetics, reduce proximity to polluted areas, cease smoking [a major Cd source], and thoroughly wash vegetables and fruits before consumption); and incorporate heavy metal exposure risk assessment into pre-pregnancy healthcare services to provide couples with personalized guidance and intervention.

Conclusions

This study, through systematic review and meta-analysis, revealed that high concentrations of Pb increased the risk of preterm birth and SGA. Similarly, high concentrations of Cd increased the risk of LBW and SGA. The reproductive toxicity of Hg requires further verification. Future research should integrate multi-omics methods and real-world data to establish a scientific foundation for the prevention and control of global heavy metal exposure, as well as for maternal and child health policies. Due to the limited data on certain heavy metals, the obtained conclusions were not statistically significant and have limited clinical relevance, particularly for Cr and APOs. We hope that in the future, more high-quality studies will be conducted in this field.
