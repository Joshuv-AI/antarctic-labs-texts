Derivative estimation by RKHS regularization for learning dynamics from time-series data
Hailong Guo, Haibo Li

Abstract
Learning the governing equations from time-series data has gained increasing attention due to its potential to extract useful dynamics from real-world data. Despite significant progress, it becomes challenging in the presence of noise, especially when derivatives need to be calculated. To reduce the effect of noise, we propose a method that simultaneously fits both the derivative and trajectory from noisy time-series data. Our approach formulates derivative estimation as an inverse problem involving integral operators within the forward model, and estimates the derivative function by solving a regularization problem in a vector-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert space (vRKHS). We derive an integral-form representer theorem, which enables the computation of the regularized solution by solving a finite-dimensional problem and facilitates efficiently estimating the optimal regularization parameter. By embedding the dynamics within a vRKHS and utilizing the fitted derivative and trajectory, we can recover the underlying dynamics from noisy data by solving a linear regularization problem. Several numerical experiments are conducted to validate the effectiveness and efficiency of our method.

. However, in many cases, systems driven by physical principles may have unknown parameters, such as the mass or size of mechanical components, or their dynamics may be completely unknown. In these scenarios, system identification techniques are employed to estimate the system’s dynamics from the data it generates
[
39
,
26
]
. Consider the following autonomous dynamical system:

\begin{cases}\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}(t))\\
\boldsymbol{x}(0)=\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\end{cases}

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{x}:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the system state and
\boldsymbol{f}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the underlying dynamics. Suppose we have
n
noisy observational states
\{(t_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
with
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=\boldsymbol{x}(t_{i})+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}
,
t_{i}\in[0,T]
, where
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\delta^{2}I_{d})
are i.i.d. Gaussian white noise and
I_{d}
is the identity matrix of order
d
. The system identification aims to learn the dynamics
\boldsymbol{f}
from those time-series observations.

As the collection of massive datasets grows in the era of big data, discovering the underlying dynamics within these data has become a key objective in scientific research. In past years, there have been many attempts to extract dynamical equations from time-series data. For example, Bongard and Schmidt applied symbolic regression to identify nonlinear differential equations
[
4
,
47
]
. A common approach represents
\boldsymbol{f}
as a parameterized vector field and recovers the parameters by integrating the solution along the vector field starting from guessed initial conditions and then comparing the resulting trajectories with the observed time-series data. For example, ODENet represents
\boldsymbol{f}
as a linear combination of polynomials of various orders and determines the coefficients through sparse regression
[
20
]
; alternatively,
\boldsymbol{f}
can be modeled using the units of a deep neural network, as demonstrated in methods proposed in
[
11
,
41
]
. In
[
29
]
, it is assumed that
\boldsymbol{f}
belongs to a vector-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert space (vRKHS) and can be recovered by solving a constrained optimization problem, which is derived from matching the Euler approximations of the ODE solution with the observed data.

On the other hand, if the values of the derivative function
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)
at
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are known, fitting the dynamics from the noisy observation reduces to solving a regression problem, where usually a regularized problem with loss term
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t_{i})\|_{2}^{2}
should be solved. There are many such methods for recovering
\boldsymbol{f}
that assume that
\{\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
are available or can be easily estimated; see e.g.
[
52
,
58
,
56
,
55
,
24
]
. Among these methods, the Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy) method is a representative and successful framework, which assumes that
\boldsymbol{f}
is a linear combination of a set of basic functions
[
6
]
. It first estimates
\{\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
by some noisy removal algorithms, and then recover
\boldsymbol{f}
by solving a linear sparse regression problems. In many real-world applications, however, the observation data are often unevenly distributed and heavily corrupted by noise. This presents a significant challenge in reliably estimating the derivative values, which in turn severely limits the algorithm’s robustness to noise and, consequently, its applicability to real-world data.

Various methods have been proposed to address this challenge. In SINDy, in addition to the finite difference method, a total variation (TV) regularization algorithm is used to estimate the derivatives at the given time points. In
[
37
]
, a method called WEAK SINDy was proposed to deal with observational noise, where
\{\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
are not required. Instead, a weak-form ODE is derived by using carefully designed test functions that help smooth out the random noise through the calculation of integrals. In
[
19
]
, a method that simultaneously identifies and denoises a dynamical system was proposed, where a discretized ODE-constrained optimization problem needs to be solved. In comparison, the method in
[
44
]
uses a neural network to model
\boldsymbol{f}
, learning the dynamics and estimating the observational noise simultaneously. Based on the Runge-Kutta method for solving ODEs, the method proposed in
[
43
]
can simultaneously denoise the data and recover the parameters of the dynamics. Most of these methods require solving a nonlinear optimization problem, where at each step, the ODE needs to be solved explicitly or implicitly. While these methods are generally effective, they often involve solving complex optimization problems or repeatedly solving the ODE, which significantly increases the computational overhead.

Our goal is to estimate derivatives from noisy data as the first step, since more accurate derivative estimation improves the ability to develop dynamic or statistical models and generate precise forecasts. One major challenge in estimating derivatives is that it is an ill-posed problem, meaning that the noise in the data can be significantly amplified when using simple methods like finite differences to calculate the derivatives. In addition to the aforementioned method based on TV regularization
[
10
]
, there are various approaches for estimating derivatives of noisy data. One such method involves applying a smoothing filter to the data first, followed by a finite difference calculation
[
7
]
. The second method relies on building a local model of the data using linear regression and then analytically calculating the derivative based on this model
[
46
,
3
]
. The third one is based on the Kalman filter with unknown noise and process characteristics
[
12
,
1
]
. By formulating the derivative calculation as an inverse problem with an integration operator as the forward model, several methods have been proposed to estimate the derivatives by solving a Tikhonov regularization problem with some appropriate smoothing penalties; see e.g.
[
13
,
36
,
10
]
. It is important to note that selecting an appropriate regularization parameter is crucial for obtaining a satisfactory regularized solution, and unfortunately, determining the optimal regularization parameter is not a trivial task. For example, the method in
[
10
]
relies on grid search to determine a good regularization parameter, which requires solving the regularization problem multiple times with different parameter values in order to select the best one, which can be computationally expensive. Additionally, most of these methods are limited to handling 1-dimensional data, which means for the data
\{(t_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
with
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there are
d
regularization problems that need to be solved, one for each dimension of the data.

In this paper, we estimate the values of
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)
at
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
for a continuous
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
by solving the regularized ill-posed inverse problem:

\min_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(s)\mathrm{d}s-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\phi}(s):=\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)
and
\mathcal{H}
is the hypothesis space. This formulation follows the approach in
[
10
]
, but
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)
is allowed to be vector-valued, and
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{K}
is chosen as an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) with a reproducing kernel
K

[
8
,
9
]
. To obtain the solution of
eq.

2
, we first establish an integral-form representer theorem for the vRKHS, which shows that the solution must be a linear combination of a finite number of basis functions, where the basis functions depend on both the reproducing kernel and the data. With the help of the integral-form representer theorem, we only need to solve a finite-dimensional linear regularization problem to obtain the regularized solution. We demonstrate that by using commonly employed kernels, such as the Gaussian or Matérn kernels, the integral computation required for forming the Gram matrix can be avoided. Furthermore, the optimal regularization parameter can be efficiently estimated using the L-curve method
[
18
]
. The resulting algorithm, which requires solving
eq.

2
only once and does not require computing any integrals, can simultaneously estimate both the values of
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
at
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

With the estimated
\{\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{\boldsymbol{x}(t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we can reconstruct
\boldsymbol{f}
following the procedure of SINDy. Additionally, we can employ a nonparametric inference method to model
\boldsymbol{f}
as an element in an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued RKHS. This approach eliminates the need to specify the function form of
\boldsymbol{f}
using a dictionary of pre-defined basis functions, offering greater flexibility in modeling the dynamics. The vector field
\boldsymbol{f}
can be recovered by solving a linear vRKHS regularization problem, where the representer theorem for vRKHS
[
22
]
enables us to compute the solution by solving a finite-dimensional regularization problem, where the optimal regularization parameter can be efficiently determined by the L-curve method. This approach consists of two steps: first denoising the data, and then learning the dynamics. Each step requires solving only a finite-dimensional linear Tikhonov regularization problem. In contrast to other methods, this approach avoids many complex computations, making the algorithm more efficient and straightforward to implement.

Preliminary

First, we provide a brief review of the SINDy framework for learning dynamics from data, along with the TV regularization method for estimating derivatives from noisy data. Then, we review some basis properties of the vRKHS.

2.1
SINDy and TV derivative estimation method

The SINDy algorithm
[
6
]
has been shown to be successful in learning a sparsely
represented nonlinear dynamics when noise is small and dynamic scales do not vary across multiple orders of magnitude. Suppose
\boldsymbol{f}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{d}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. This framework first chooses a dictionary of basis functions
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
with
\psi_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and assumes that
\boldsymbol{f}
can be represented componentwisely by

f_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{ji}\psi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}),\ \ i=1,\dots,d,

(3)

where
\mathbf{W}=(w_{ji})\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times d}
composed by the coefficients should be a sparse matrix. Substituting
eq.

3
into
eq.

1
with data
\{(t_{i},\boldsymbol{x}(t_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{n}
leads to

\dot{\mathbf{X}}=\Theta(\mathbf{X})\mathbf{W},

where
\mathbf{X}=(\boldsymbol{x}(t_{1}),\dots,\boldsymbol{x}(t_{n}))^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,
\dot{\mathbf{X}}=(\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t_{1}),\dots,\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t_{n}))^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
\Theta(\mathbf{X})=(\psi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}(t_{i})))\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times J}
. Given the noisy observations
\{(t_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, SINDy uses the noisy data
\mathbf{Y}=(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}_{n})^{\top}
to form the matrix
\Theta(\mathbf{Y})
and uses the time derivative
\dot{\mathbf{Y}}
estimated from
\mathbf{Y}
to replace
\dot{\mathbf{X}}
. Then it obtains the sparse coefficients
\{w_{ji}\}
by solving the least squares problem

\min_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times d}}\|\dot{\mathbf{Y}}-\Theta(\mathbf{Y})\mathbf{W}\|_{F}^{2}

by the sequentially-thresholded least squares method. Here
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm of a matrix.

Due to the presence of observational noise, simple methods like finite differences are not robust for estimating time derivatives from
\{(t_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. SINDy leverages the method from
[
10
]
to estimate the derivatives, which utilizes TV regularization to improve robustness in the presence of noise. Given a function
g:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
(for convenience assume
g(0)=0
) where
g\in L^{2}([0,T])
, this method computes the derivative of
g
by solving the TV regularization problem

\min_{u\in\mathrm{BV}([0,T])}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}|Au-g|^{2}\mathrm{d}t+\lambda\mathrm{TV}(u),

(4)

where
(Au)(t):=\int_{0}^{t}u(s)\mathrm{d}s
,
\mathrm{TV}(u)=\int_{0}^{T}|u^{\prime}(t)|\mathrm{d}t
,
\mathrm{BV}([0,T])
is the space of functions of bounded variation, and
\lambda
is the regularization parameter. Using the TV regualrization method, the noise can be suppressed in the derivative, and it does not suppress jump discontinuities, which allows for the computation of discontinuous derivatives. We remark that the TV method is more proper to estimate a piecewise constant derivative function, as it enforces the reconstruction of a function with bounded variation.

For the time-series noisy data
\{(t_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the integral in
eq.

4
should be discretized followed by an iterative method
[
49
,
48
]
. In practical computations, determining an appropriate value for
\alpha
is challenging, and the regularization problem needs to be solved multiple times with different values of
\lambda
to select the best one. Additionally, for
d
-dimensional data,
d
separate regularization problems must be solved, one for each dimension of the data. This process can be computationally expensive and time-consuming.

2.2
Vector-valued RKHS

We review several fundamental properties of the vRKHS, which will be utilized in the subsequent sections. For simplicity, we only review the vRKHS on
\mathbb{R}
, while the definition and properties on
\mathbb{C}
are similar. For a detailed theoretical treatment, we refer the reader to
[
8
,
9
,
22
]
. For any nonempty sets
X
and
Y
, we use
Y^{X}
to denote the set of all maps from
X
to
Y
.

Definition 1
(Reproducing kernel).

Let
X
be a compact metric space. The map
K:X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is called an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued reproducing kernel on
X
if

(1).

for any
x,x^{\prime}\in X
it holds
K(x,x^{\prime})=K(x^{\prime},x)^{\top}
;

(2).

for any
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in X
and
z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
it holds

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle K(x_{i},x_{j})z_{i},z_{j}\rangle_{2}\geq 0,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2}
is the Euclidean inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Definition 2
(Vector-valued RKHS).

A Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{K}\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{X}
is called an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert space if there exist an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-reproducing kernel
K:X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that

(1).

for any fixed
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
x^{\prime}\in X
, the map
\varphi:x\mapsto K(x,x^{\prime})v
belongs to
\mathcal{H}_{K}
;

(2).

for every
x\in X
,
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
, it holds
\langle\varphi,K(x,\cdot)v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}=\langle\varphi(x),v\rangle_{2}
, called the reproducing property.

The following well-known theorem is a generalization for the real-valued RKHS, establishing a one-to-one correspondence between the vector-valued reproducing kernel and the vector-valued RKHS.

Theorem 3
(Moore-Aronszajn).

Suppose
K
is an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued reproducing kernel on
X
. Then there is a unique
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued RKHS
\mathcal{H}\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{X}
for which
K
is the reproducing kernel.

The following representer theorem plays a key role in learning vector-valued functions from finite data using the kernel method. For a more general discussion of the representer theorem for vRKHS, see, e.g.
[
51
,
53
]
.

Theorem 4
(Representer theorem).

Let
\mathcal{H}_{K}\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{X}
be an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued RKHS with kernel
K
. Suppose we have data pairs
(x_{1},z_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},z_{n})\in X\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\lambda>0
. Then the regularized optimization problem

\min_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}}J(\boldsymbol{\varphi}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}(x_{i})-z_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}

(5)

has at least one solution, and any solution must have the representation

\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}K(x_{i},\cdot)v_{i}

(6)

with some
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
i=1,\dots,n
.

The representer theorem is particular useful for solving the regularization problem
eq.

5
, since it transforms this infinite-dimensional optimization problem to a finite-dimensional problem. We only need to compute the coefficient vector
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
to get the regularized solution.

3
Estimating derivative function by vRKHS regularization

For the ODE
eq.

1
, suppose
\boldsymbol{f}
is a Lipschitz continuous vector field and
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
is a continuous
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued function. Then
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)
is continuous. Our aim is to obtain a good estimate of
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)
from the noisy time-series data
\{(t_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. We assume that
\boldsymbol{\phi}(t):=\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)
belongs to an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{K}
with reproducing kernel
K(t,s)
.

From now on, we consider the vRKHS directly related to our focused problem, i.e., we set
X=[0,T]
, but for notational simplicity, in some contexts we also use
X
as an alternative. To estimate
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)
, we consider the following regularization problem in
\mathcal{H}_{K}
:

\min_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(s)\mathrm{d}s-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2},

(7)

where the integral is calculated componentwisely for the
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued
\boldsymbol{\phi}(s)
. Note that for
\boldsymbol{\phi}=\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}
, then
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(s)\mathrm{d}s=\boldsymbol{x}(t_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,n
. Thus,
eq.

7
is a regularized loss function to fit the noisy data
\{(t_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

At first glance,
eq.

7
appears quite similar to
eq.

5
. However, the Representer Theorem cannot be applied to
eq.

7
, as it does not include any
\boldsymbol{\phi}(t_{i})
term. In the following part, we establish a new representer theorem, which asserts that the solution to
eq.

7
can be expressed as a linear combination of certain basis functions.

For any
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{d})^{\top}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
and
t_{i}\in[0,T]
, define the linear map

\mathcal{L}_{i}:\mathcal{H}_{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d},\ \ \ \boldsymbol{\phi}\mapsto\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(s)\mathrm{d}s:=\begin{pmatrix}\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\phi_{1}(s)\mathrm{d}s,\cdots,\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\phi_{d}(s)\mathrm{d}s\end{pmatrix}^{\top}.

(8)

Define the feature map

\Phi:X\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{K},\ \ \ (x,v)\mapsto K(\cdot,x)v.

(9)

For any two metric spaces
X
and
Y
, denote by
C(X,Y)
the set of all continuous maps from
X
to
Y
. If
K\in C(X\times X,\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
, we call
K
a continuous kernel. The following result will be used to establish the new representer theorem.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{K}
be an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued RKHS with a continuous kernel
K
. For any
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
\mathcal{L}_{i}
defined as
eq.

8
, it holds

\langle\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi}),v\rangle_{2}=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,v))\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}.

(10)

Proof.

Since
X
is compact, it follows that the function
x\mapsto\|K(x,x)\|_{2}
is locally bounded and
x\mapsto K(x,x^{\prime})v\in C(X,\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any fixed
x\in X
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Therefore,
K
is a Mercer kernel and
\mathcal{H}_{K}
is continuously embedded in
C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
; see
[
9
, Proposition 2.2]
. We prove
eq.

10
by the following two steps.

Step 1: prove
\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,v))\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
. Fixing
t
and
v
, then
K(s,t)v
is a continuous map with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Thus, there exist countably many positive numbers
w_{j}
and
s_{j}\in[0,t_{i}]
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,v))(t)=\int_{0}^{t_{i}}K(s,t)v\mathrm{d}s=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}K(s_{j},t)v=:\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,m}(t),

for any
t\in[0,T]
, and
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,m}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}K(s_{j},\cdot)v\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
. Note that the integral
\int_{0}^{t_{i}}K(s,t)v\mathrm{d}s
is calculated componentwisely for
K(s,t)v
.
Since
[0,T]
is compact and
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,m}
is continuous, the above convergence is uniform and
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,m}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,y))
in the Banach space
C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Now we prove
\{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,m}\}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathcal{H}_{K}
. For any
m^{\prime}>m
, we have

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,m^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,m}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sum_{j=m+1}^{m^{\prime}}w_{j}K(s_{j},\cdot)v,\sum_{l=m+1}^{m^{\prime}}w_{l}K(s_{l},\cdot)v\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}

\displaystyle=v^{\top}\left(\sum_{j,l=m+1}^{m^{\prime}}w_{j}w_{l}K(s_{j},s_{l})\right)v\rightarrow 0\ \ (m\rightarrow+\infty),

since

\sum_{j,l=1}^{m}w_{j}w_{l}K(s_{j},s_{l})\rightarrow\iint_{[0,t_{i}]^{2}}K(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t<\infty\ \ (m\rightarrow+\infty),

where the above integral and “
<
” are processed componentwisely.
Therefore, there exist a
\bar{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
such that
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,m}\rightarrow\bar{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
with the
\mathcal{H}_{K}
norm. Since the
\mathcal{H}_{K}
norm is stronger than the
C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
norm, thereby
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,m}\rightarrow\bar{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
with the
C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
norm, which leads to
\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,y))=\bar{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
.

Step 2: prove the equality
eq.

10
. Using the above convergence relation, we have

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,v))\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}w_{j}K(s_{j},\cdot)v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},w_{j}K(s_{j},\cdot)v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}w_{j}\langle\boldsymbol{\phi}(s_{j}),v\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle=v^{\top}\cdot\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(s)\mathrm{d}s=\langle\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi}),v\rangle_{2}.

The proof is completed.

Recall that for a Banach space, a functional
f:Z\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is lower-semicontinous if
\liminf_{n\rightarrow n}f(z_{n})\geq f(z)
for any convergent
z_{n}\rightarrow z
in
Z
. The functional
f
is coercive if
\lim_{\|x\|_{Z}\rightarrow+\infty}f(x)=+\infty
. Now we can give the representer theorem for
eq.

7
.

Theorem 6
(Integral-form representer theorem).

Let
\mathcal{H}_{K}
be an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued RKHS with a continous kernel
K
. Suppose we have data pairs
(t_{1},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}),\dots,(t_{n},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{n})\in X\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, linear operators
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{N}:\mathcal{H}_{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined as
eq.

8
, and
\lambda>0
. Then the regularization problem

\min_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}}J(\boldsymbol{\phi}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}

(11)

has at least one solution, and any solution must have the representation

\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,v_{i}))

(12)

with some
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
i=1,\dots,n
.

Proof.

Note that
J(\boldsymbol{\phi})
is bounded from below, coercive and lower-semicontinuous on
\mathcal{H}_{K}
. It follows that
J(\boldsymbol{\phi})
has at least one minimizer. Suppose that
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(x)
is such a minimizer. Define the space

\mathcal{M}=\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{\phi}=\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,v)),\ 1\leq i\leq N,\ v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}.

(13)

It follows from
Lemma

5
that
\mathcal{M}
is a finite-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}_{K}
. Using
Lemma

5
again, we get

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi}),\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\rangle_{2}-2\langle\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi}),\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\rangle_{2}+\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi})-2\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}))\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}+\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}.

Denote by the
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}
the projector operator onto the closed subspace
\mathcal{M}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \langle\boldsymbol{\phi},\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi})-2\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}))\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi},\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi})-2\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}))\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}+\langle(I-\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}})\boldsymbol{\phi},\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi})-2\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}))\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi},\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi})-2\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}))\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi}),\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\rangle_{2}-2\langle\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi}),\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\rangle_{2},

since
\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi})-2\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}))\in\mathcal{M}
. Using the same approach as the above, we can also get
\langle\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi}),\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\rangle_{2}=\langle\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi}),\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi})\rangle_{2}
. Therefore, we obtain

\|\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}.

If
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}\notin\mathcal{M}
, using the above equality, we have

\displaystyle J(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}+(I-\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}})\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}

\displaystyle=J(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})+\lambda\|(I-\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}})\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}

\displaystyle>J(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}),

contradictory with that
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}
is a minimizer. In other words,
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}
must have the finite expansion form
eq.

12
.

Note that
eq.

7
and
eq.

11
are identical by setting
\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
. The Integral-form Representer Theorem (IRT) allows us to get a finite-dimensional optimization problem from
eq.

7
. We can further show that the solution of
eq.

7
is unique.

Corollary 7
.

The regularization problem
eq.

7
has a unique solution, which has the expression
eq.

12
with
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1}^{\top},\dots,v_{n}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}
be an arbitrary solution of

\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}}\|\mathbf{G}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{v},

(14)

where

\mathbf{G}=\begin{pmatrix}G_{11}&\cdots&G_{1n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
G_{n1}&\cdots&G_{nn}\end{pmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{b}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}\\
\vdots\\
b_{n}\end{pmatrix}

(15)

with
b_{i}=\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
and
G_{ij}=\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\int_{0}^{t_{j}}K(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t
.

Proof.

By the IRT, suppose
\boldsymbol{\phi}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,v_{i}))
be a minimizer of
eq.

7
. Then

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(s)\mathrm{d}s-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t_{j}}K(s,t)v_{j}\mathrm{d}t\right)\mathrm{d}s-(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\int_{0}^{t_{j}}K(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t\cdot v_{j}-(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0}).

Let
\mathbf{G}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
be defined in
eq.

19
. Then we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(s)\mathrm{d}s-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}^{2}.

(16)

Now we derive the expression of
\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}
. Using
Lemma

5
we have

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,v_{i})),\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{j}(\Phi(\cdot,v_{j}))\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\langle\mathcal{L}_{i}\mathcal{L}_{j}(\Phi(\cdot,v_{j})),v_{i}\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}v_{i}^{\top}\left(\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\int_{0}^{t_{j}}K(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t\right)v_{j}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{v}.

Therefore, the coefficient vector must be a solution of the least squares problem
eq.

14
.

Note that there may be multiple solutions of
eq.

14
if
\mathrm{rank}(G)<nd
. Now we prove that even for this case, these different minimizers will lead to the same solution of
eq.

7
. Suppose
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}=(v_{1}^{\prime\top},\dots,v_{n}^{\prime\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}
are two solutions of
eq.

14
and the corresponding solutions to
eq.

7
are
\boldsymbol{\phi}
and
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,v_{i}^{\prime}))
, respectively. Then it holds that
\boldsymbol{w}:=\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{G})
, the null space of
\mathbf{G}
. Note that

\boldsymbol{\phi}-\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,v_{i}))-\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,v_{i}^{\prime})))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,v_{i}-v_{i}^{\prime})).

Using the same procedure as the above for deriving the expression of
\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}
, we have

\|\boldsymbol{\phi}-\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}=(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})^{\top}\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})=0.

Therefore, it must hold that
\boldsymbol{\phi}=\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}
.

In the next section, we will discuss the choice of the kernel
K
for practical computations. With an appropriate selection of
K
, a unique solution to
eq.

14
exists, and we will also present an efficient method for estimating the optimal value of
\lambda
.

4
Practical computations for fitting derivative and trajectory simultaneously

We show how to choose an appropriate kernel
K
to avoid the need for integral computations when forming
\mathbf{G}
. Additionally, we provide an efficient approach for determining an optimal regularization parameter
\lambda
without solving
eq.

14
multiple times.

4.1
Choice of kernels

In practical computation, the most common choice of an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued kernel can be a separable kernel
K(s,t)=k(s,t)A
, where
k(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}
is a symmetric positive definite kernel function and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix. Without loss of generality, it can also be written as
K(s,t)=k(s,t)I_{d}
since
A
can be “absorbed” in the coefficients
\{v_{i}\}
.
The following result describes the structure of an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued RKHS with a separable kernel.

Proposition 8
.

Let
X=[0,T]
. Let
k(t,t^{\prime}):X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a real-valued reproducing kernel with the corresponding RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Then
K(t,t^{\prime}):=k(t,t^{\prime})I_{d}:X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued reproducing kernel, and the corresponding
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued RKHS is

\mathcal{H}_{K}=\underbrace{H_{k}\otimes\cdots\otimes H_{k}}_{d},

(17)

where the inner product is
\langle\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{g}^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\langle g_{i},g_{i}^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
for any
\boldsymbol{g}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{d})^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{g}^{\prime}=(g_{1}^{\prime},\dots,g_{d}^{\prime})^{\top}
.

Proof.

Write
\mathcal{H}=\underbrace{H_{k}\otimes\cdots\otimes H_{k}}_{d}
with the inner product defined as the above. Then
\mathcal{H}
is a Hilbert space. It is easy to verify that
K
is a reproducing kernel. By the Moore-Aronszajn Theorem, we only need to verify that
\mathcal{H}
and
K
satisfy the two properties in
definition

2
:

(1).

For a fixed
t^{\prime}\in X
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, consider the map
\boldsymbol{\phi}(t)=K(t,t^{\prime})v=k(t,t^{\prime})\boldsymbol{v}
where
t\in X
and
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If follows that
\boldsymbol{\phi}(t)=(k(t,t^{\prime})v_{1},\dots,k(t,t^{\prime})v_{d})^{\top}
. Since
k(t,t^{\prime})v_{i}\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
for
i=1,\dots,d
, we have
\boldsymbol{\phi}(t)\in\mathcal{H}
.

(2).

For any
t\in X
,
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},K(t,\cdot)v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi},k(t,\cdot)v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\langle\phi_{i},k(t,\cdot)v_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\phi_{i}v_{i}=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi}(x),v\rangle_{2}.

Therefore, we get
\mathcal{H}_{K}=\mathcal{H}
.

The following result shows that if
k(t,t^{\prime})
is a strictly positive definite kernel, then we can make sure that
eq.

14
must have a unique solution. For more discussions about strictly positive definite kernels, see
[
53
, Chapter 6]
.

Theorem 9
.

Following the notations in
Proposition

8
, assume that
k(t,t^{\prime})
is a continuous and strictly positive definite kernel, which means that

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k(t_{i},t_{j})>0

for any integer
n\geq 1
and
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\neq\mathbf{0}
. Then the matrix
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd\times nd}
in
eq.

15
is symmetric positive definite.

Proof.

It is easy to verify that
\mathbf{G}
is symmetric, because

G_{ji}^{\top}=\int_{0}^{t_{j}}\int_{0}^{t_{i}}K(s,t)^{\top}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\int_{0}^{t_{j}}K(t,s)\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}s=G_{ij}.

From the derivation of the expression of
\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}
, we know that for
\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{v}\geq 0
for any
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}
, which implies that
\mathbf{G}
is symmetric positive semi-definite.

To prove the strict positiveness, suppose
\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{v}=0
for some
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}
and write
\boldsymbol{\phi}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\Phi(\cdot,v_{i}))
where
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1}^{\top},\dots,v_{n}^{\top})^{\top}
with
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then
\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}=0
, leading to
\boldsymbol{\phi}=\mathbf{0}
, that is

\mathbf{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t_{i}}K(s,\cdot)\mathrm{d}s\cdot v_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t_{i}}k(s,\cdot)\mathrm{d}s\cdot v_{i}.

(18)

Note that
\int_{0}^{t_{i}}k(s,\cdot)
is a scalar-valued function. Now we prove that
\{\int_{0}^{t_{i}}k(s,\cdot)\}_{i=1}^{n}
are linear independent functions. Suppose there exist real numbers
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}
such that

0=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\int_{0}^{t_{i}}k(s,\cdot)\mathrm{d}s=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\int_{t_{j-1}}^{t_{j}}k(s,\cdot)\mathrm{d}s=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{i=j}^{n}c_{i}\right)\int_{t_{j-1}}^{t_{j}}k(s,\cdot)\mathrm{d}s,

where
t_{0}=0
. By the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals, there exist
\xi_{i}\in(t_{j-1},t_{j})
for
j=1,\dots,n
such that
\int_{t_{j-1}}^{t_{j}}k(s,\cdot)\mathrm{d}s=(t_{j}-t_{j-1})k(\xi_{j},\cdot)
. Since
k(\cdot,\cdot)
is strictly positive definite, it follows that the matrix
(k(\xi_{i},\xi_{j}))_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
is positive definite, and thereby the
n
vectors
\{(k(\xi_{i},\xi_{j}))_{1\leq j\leq n}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are linear independent. Using
[
57
, Theorem 2.1]
, it follows that
\{k(\xi_{i},\cdot)\}_{i=1}^{n}
are linear independent functions. Therefore, we have
\sum_{i=j}^{n}c_{i}=0
for any
1\leq j\leq n
, i.e.

\mathbf{0}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\begin{pmatrix}1&&\\
\vdots&\ddots&\\
1&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix},

leading to
c_{1}=\cdots=c_{n}=0
. This proves that
\{\int_{0}^{t_{i}}k(s,\cdot)\mathrm{d}s\}_{i=1}^{n}
are linear independent. Using
[
57
, Theorem 2.1]
again, we can find
n
difference points
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset[0,T]
such that the
n
vectors
\{(\int_{0}^{t_{i}}k(s,s_{j})\mathrm{d}s)_{1\leq j\leq n}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are linear independent. Now
eq.

18
implies that

\mathbf{0}=(v_{1},\cdots,v_{n})\begin{pmatrix}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}k(s,s_{1})\mathrm{d}s&\cdots&\int_{0}^{t_{1}}k(s,s_{n})\mathrm{d}s\\
\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
\int_{0}^{t_{n}}k(s,s_{1})\mathrm{d}s&\cdots&\int_{0}^{t_{n}}k(s,s_{n})\mathrm{d}s\end{pmatrix}.

Since the above matrix is nonsingular, it follows that
v_{i}=\mathbf{0}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. This proves that
\mathbf{G}
is strictly positive define.

By
Theorem

9
, if we choose a separable kernel
K(s,t)=k(s,t)I_{d}
with a strictly positive definite
k(t,t^{\prime})
, then
\mathbf{G}
is positive definite, thereby the unique minimizer of
eq.

14
is

\boldsymbol{v}_{\lambda}=(\mathbf{G}^{2}+\mathbf{G})^{-1}\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{b}=(\mathbf{G}+\lambda I_{nd})^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}.

Moreover,
\mathbf{G}
is a block diagonal matrix, which has the form

\mathbf{G}=G_{1}\otimes I_{d}

with

G_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}k(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t&\cdots&\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}k(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\int_{0}^{t_{n}}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}k(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t&\cdots&\int_{0}^{t_{n}}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}k(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t\end{pmatrix}.

(19)

In this case, we have

(\mathbf{G}+\lambda I_{nd})^{-1}=[G_{1}\otimes I_{d}+\lambda I_{n}\otimes I_{d}]^{-1}=[(G_{1}+\lambda I_{n})\otimes I_{d}]^{-1}=(G_{1}+\lambda I_{n})^{-1}\otimes I_{d}.

Let
\mathbf{B}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
. Then we have

\boldsymbol{v}_{\lambda}=[(G_{1}+\lambda I_{n})^{-1}\otimes I_{d}]\boldsymbol{b}=\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{B}(G_{1}+\lambda I_{n})^{-1}),

(20)

where
\mathrm{vec}(\cdot)
vectorizes a matrix by stacking its columns from left to right into a single column vector. Write
\boldsymbol{v}_{\lambda}=(v_{\lambda,1}^{\top},\dots,v_{\lambda,n}^{\top})^{\top}
. We eventually obtain the solution of
eq.

7
, which is the estimated derivative function from the noisy data:

\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\approx\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t_{j}}K(s,t)v_{\lambda,j}\mathrm{d}s=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\int_{0}^{t_{j}}k(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\right)v_{\lambda,j}.

(21)

To estimate the values of
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)
at
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, define
\psi_{j}(t_{i})=\int_{0}^{t_{j}}k(s,t)\mathrm{d}s
. It follows from
eq.

21
that
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t_{i})\approx\sum_{j=1}^{n}\psi_{j}(t_{i})v_{j}
, and thereby

\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t_{1})&\cdots&\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t_{n})\end{pmatrix}\approx\begin{pmatrix}v_{\lambda,1}&\cdots&v_{\lambda,n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}(t_{1})&\cdots&\psi_{1}(t_{n})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\psi_{n}(t_{1})&\cdots&\psi_{n}(t_{n})\end{pmatrix}=:\mathbf{V}_{\lambda}\Psi,

(22)

where it follows from
eq.

20
that
\mathbf{V}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{B}(G_{1}+\lambda I_{n})^{-1}
.
To estimate the values of
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
at
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, notice that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}(t_{i})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\approx\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t_{j}}k(s,t)v_{\lambda,j}\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\int_{0}^{t_{j}}k(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t\right)v_{\lambda,j}.

Therefore, we obtain

\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}(t_{1})&\cdots&\boldsymbol{x}(t_{n})\end{pmatrix}\approx\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\end{pmatrix}+V_{\lambda}G_{1}.

(23)

During the computation, in order to form the Gram matrix
\mathbf{G}
and basis functions
\psi_{j}(t)
, more than
n^{2}
integrals with integrand
k(t,t^{\prime})
should be computed. However, by selecting proper kernel functions, analytical expressions for these integrals can be obtained, allowing the costly numerical integrations to be avoided. For the kernel function
k(s,t)
, it should be efficient to compute
\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\int_{0}^{t_{j}}k(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t
and
\psi_{j}(t_{i})=\int_{0}^{t_{j}}k(s,t)\mathrm{d}s
. We exploit two types of commonly used kernel functions—the Gaussian kernel and the Matérn kernel. Both of them are strictly positive definite kernels; see
[
53
, Theorem 6.11]
for how to check whether it is a strictly positive definite kernel.

The Gaussian kernel function

k(s,t)=\exp\left(-\frac{(s-t)^{2}}{2l^{2}}\right)

(24)

with
l
a hyperparameter is a very common choice for fitting continuous curves; for the structure of the RKHS induced by the Gaussian kernel, see
[
53
, Theorem 5.20]
. The parameter
l
determines the length scale of the associated hypothesis space of functions. As
l
increases, the induced functions change less rapidly, and thus get “smoother”. Although the analytic form of the antiderivative of this function can not be expressed with elementary functions, we can still quickly evaluate the value of its integral with the help of special functions. Using the error function

\mathrm{erf}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-t^{2}}\mathrm{d}t,\ \ \ x\in\mathbb{R}

and its antiderivative

h(x):=\int\mathrm{erf}(x)\mathrm{d}x=x\cdot\mathrm{erf}(x)+\frac{e^{-x^{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}+c,

we have

\int_{0}^{t_{j}}k(s,t)\mathrm{d}s=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}l}{2}\left(\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{t_{j}-t}{\sqrt{2}l}\right)+\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}l}\right)\right)

and

\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\int_{0}^{t_{j}}k(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t=\sqrt{\pi}l^{2}\left(h\left(\frac{t_{i}}{\sqrt{2}l}\right)+h\left(\frac{t_{j}}{\sqrt{2}l}\right)-h\left(\frac{t_{i}-t_{j}}{\sqrt{2}l}\right)-h(0)\right).

Therefore, the numerical integrals for forming
G_{1}
and
\Phi
can be avoided.

Sometimes the hypothesis space of the Gaussian kernel is too smooth for fitting the derivative function. In this case, we can choose the Matérn kernel

k_{\nu,l}(s,t)=\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}|s-t|}{l}\right)^{\nu}K_{\nu}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}|s-t|}{l}\right),

(25)

where
\nu,l>0
are hyperparameters,
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the gamma function, and
K_{\nu}(\cdot)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind of order
\nu
. The parameter
\nu
effectively controls the level of smoothness of the function. In fact, for any Sobolev space
H^{\tau}(X)
with
\tau=\nu+\frac{1}{2}
is a positive integer, then the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{k_{\nu,l}}
is equivalent to
H^{\tau}(X)
. Therefore, for the separable kernel
K(s,t)=k_{\nu,l}(s,t)I_{d}
, the corresponding
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued RKHS is
\mathcal{H}_{K}=\underbrace{H^{\tau}(X)\otimes\cdots\otimes H^{\tau}(X)}_{d}
,
meaning that each component of the trajectory belongs to
H^{\tau}(X)
.
For the Matérn kernel with
\nu=\tau-\frac{1}{2}=(\tau-1)+\frac{1}{2}
, a well-known property is that
k_{\nu,l}(s,t)
can be written as

k_{\nu,l}(s,t)=\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}|s-t|}{l}\right)p_{\tau-1}\left(\frac{|s-t|}{l}\right),

where
p_{\tau-1}(\cdot)
is a polynomial of degree
\tau-1
; see
[
54
, §4.2.1]
. Therefore,
k_{\nu,l}(s,t)
is integrable with respect to both
s
and
t
. To efficiently compute
\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\int_{0}^{t_{j}}k(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t
and
\psi_{j}(t_{i})=\int_{0}^{t_{j}}k(s,t)\mathrm{d}s
, we can first derive the analytic forms of the above two functions and then evaluate them at the corresponding points.

4.2
Determining regularization parameter

Now we show how to compute a good estimation of the optimal regularization parameter
\lambda
. Heuristically, a good
\lambda
should balance the loss term and regularization term in
eq.

14
. We estimate the optimal parameter
\lambda
by the L-curve method
[
18
]
. The idea is to plot the following parametrized curve in log-log scale:

l(\lambda)=(x(\lambda),y(\lambda)):=\left(\log(\|\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{v}_{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}),\log((\boldsymbol{v}_{\lambda}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{v}_{\lambda})^{\frac{1}{2}})\right),

which usually has a characteristic “L” shape, and the corner of
l(\lambda)
corresponds to the point where further reduction in the residual comes only at the expense of a drastic increase in the regularization term. Therefore, a good estimate of
\lambda
should maximize the (signed) curvature of
l(\lambda)
. Let the eigen-decomposition of
G_{1}
be
G_{1}=U\Lambda U^{\top}
, where
U=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\Lambda=\diag(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}>0
. In practical computation, we restrict
\lambda
in the spectral range of
G_{1}
, and compute

\lambda^{*}=\mathop{\text{argmax}}_{\lambda_{n}\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{1}}\kappa(\lambda):=\frac{x^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime}x^{\prime\prime}}{(x^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 2})^{3/2}}

(26)

as the optimal regularization parameter. Since

\mathbf{V}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{B}(G_{1}+\lambda I_{n})^{-1}=\mathbf{B}U(\Lambda+\lambda I)^{-1}U^{\top},

(27)

we can quickly obtain
\mathbf{V}_{\lambda}
for different
\lambda
once we have the eigen-decomposition of
G_{1}
. Then we get the analytic expressions of
x(\lambda)
and
y(\lambda)
via the equalities

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{v}_{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}=\|(G_{1}\otimes I_{d})\boldsymbol{v}_{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}=\|\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{V}_{\lambda}G_{1})-\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{B})\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{V}_{\lambda}G_{1}-\mathbf{B}\|_{F}

and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{\lambda}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{v}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}v_{i}^{\top}(G_{ij}I_{d})v_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}^{\top}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}G_{ij}v_{j}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{V}_{\lambda}G_{1}^{\top})_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{trace}(\mathbf{V}_{\lambda}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{\lambda}G_{1})=\mathrm{trace}(\mathbf{V}_{\lambda}G_{1}\mathbf{V}_{\lambda}^{\top}).

\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)

Learning dynamics from the fitted derivative and trajectory

In addition to the SINDy framework, the dynamics
\boldsymbol{f}
can also be embedded within a vRKHS. This strategy is particularly well-motivated due to the strong connection between the regularity properties of a kernel and those of
\boldsymbol{f}
. Specifically, one can select a vRKHS such that the existence and uniqueness of the corresponding ODE are guaranteed
[
29
]
. An additional advantage of this approach is the elimination of the need to select a dictionary of pre-defined basis functions.

Denote the estimated values of
\{\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{\boldsymbol{x}(t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
by
\{\dot{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{\boldsymbol{\phi}(t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, respectively. Assume
\boldsymbol{f}
belong to an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued RKHS on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We recover the dynamics
\boldsymbol{f}
by fitting the ODE
eq.

1
at the time points
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, i.e., we compute
\boldsymbol{f}
by solving the following regularization problem:

\min_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\dot{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t_{i})-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{\phi}(t_{i}))\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}.

(28)

By
Theorem

4
, the solution of this problem has the representation

\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}K(\boldsymbol{\phi}(t_{i}),\cdot)v_{i}

(29)

with some
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
i=1,\dots,n
. Write the coefficient vector as
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1}^{\top},\dots,v_{n}^{\top})^{\top}
.
By a similar discussion as the previous section, it follows that
\boldsymbol{v}
is the solution to the finite-dimensional regularization problem

\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}\boldsymbol{v}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{b}}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}\boldsymbol{v}

(30)

where

\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}=\begin{pmatrix}K(\boldsymbol{\phi}(t_{1}),\boldsymbol{\phi}(t_{1}))&\cdots&K(\boldsymbol{\phi}(t_{1}),\boldsymbol{\phi}(t_{n}))\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
K(\boldsymbol{\phi}(t_{n}),\boldsymbol{\phi}(t_{1}))&\cdots&K(\boldsymbol{\phi}(t_{n}),\boldsymbol{\phi}(t_{n}))\end{pmatrix},\ \ \ \widetilde{\boldsymbol{b}}=\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\dot{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t_{n})\end{pmatrix}.

(31)

Similar to the approach in the previous section, by choosing
K(t,t^{\prime})=k(t,t^{\prime})I_{d}
with a strictly positive definite kernel
k(t,t^{\prime})
, we can write
\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}
as
\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}=\widetilde{G}_{1}\otimes I_{d}
with
\widetilde{G}_{1}=(k(\boldsymbol{\phi}(t_{i}),\boldsymbol{\phi}(t_{j})))\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Therefore, we obtain

\boldsymbol{v}=[(\widetilde{G}_{1}+\lambda I_{n})^{-1}\otimes I_{d}]\widetilde{\boldsymbol{b}}=\mathrm{vec}(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(\widetilde{G}_{1}+\lambda I_{n})^{-1}),

(32)

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}=(\dot{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t_{1}),\dots,\dot{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t_{n}))
.
Similarly, we can use the L-curve method to select a good parameter
\lambda
for
eq.

30
.

Forced vibration of nonlinear pendulum

This is a second-order ODE describing the motion of a simple pendulum. It has the form
\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\theta}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}+\alpha\sin(\theta)=f(\theta)
, where
\theta
is the angle from the vertical to the pendulum, and
\alpha=g/L
with
g
and
L
the acceleration of gravity and the length of the pendulum, respectively. We assume the external force
f
only depends on
\theta
. Let
x_{1}=\theta
and
x_{2}=\dot{\theta}
. We have the following equivalent 2-dimensional (dim) ODE:

\begin{cases}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{1}}{\mathrm{d}t}=x_{2}\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{2}}{\mathrm{d}t}=f(x_{1})-\alpha\sin x_{1}.\end{cases}

(33)

In the experiment, we set
L=5
,
f(x)=\cos(e^{x})
. The initial value is set as
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}=(0,0)^{\top}
.

Lotka–Volterra equation

The Lotka–Volterra equation, also known as the Lotka-Volterra predator-prey model, is a pair of first-order nonlinear differential equations used to describe the dynamics of biological systems in which two species interact, one as a predator and the other as prey
[
35
,
50
]
. The ODE model is as follows:

\begin{cases}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{1}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\alpha x_{1}-\beta x_{1}x_{2}\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{2}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\delta x_{1}x_{2}-\gamma x_{2}.\end{cases}

(34)

In the experiment, we set
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)==(0.7,0.007,1,0.007)
. The initial value is set as
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}=(70,50)^{\top}
.

SIR model

The SIR model
[
27
]
describes the evolution of an epidemic by the follows ODEs:

\begin{cases}\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{dt}=-\beta\frac{SI}{S+I+R}\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}I}{dt}=\beta\frac{SI}{S+I+R}-\gamma I\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}R}{dt}=\gamma I,\end{cases}

(35)

where
S
,
I
, and
R
are the numbers of susceptible, infected and recovered individuals, respectively. The parameter
\beta
is the infection rate and
\gamma
is the rate at which individuals recover. In the experiment, we set
(\beta,\gamma)=(0.4,0.04)
and
(S(0),I(0),R(0))=(900,10,0)
.

Lorenz63 model

The Lorenz63 model was proposed by the American meteorologist E. Lorenz in 1963 for for describing atmospheric turbulence
[
33
]
. It is a simple mathematical system constituted by three ordinary differential equations:

\begin{cases}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{1}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sigma(x_{2}-x_{1})\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{2}}{\mathrm{d}t}=x_{1}(\rho-x_{3})-x_{2}\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{3}}{\mathrm{d}t}=x_{1}x_{2}-\beta x_{3}.\end{cases}

(36)

In the experiment, we set
(\sigma,\rho,\beta)=(10,28,8/3)
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}=(1,1,1)^{\top}
, which will lead to chaotic solutions and in particular, the Lorenz attractor.

Lorenz96 model

The Lorenz96 model is a dynamical system formulated by Edward Lorenz in 1996
[
34
]
, which is a system of ordinary differential equations that describes a single scalar quantity as it evolves on a circular array of sites, undergoing forcing, dissipation, and rotation invariant advection. It is defined as follows. For
i=1,\dots,N
, the equation is

\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=(x_{i+1}-x_{i-2})x_{i-1}-x_{i}+F,\ \ \ 1\leq i\leq N,

(37)

where it is assumed that
x_{-1}=x_{N-1},x_{0}=x_{N}
and
x_{N+1}=x_{1}
. Here
x_{i}
is the state of the system and
F
is a forcing constant. Here we set
N=5
and
F=8
, which is a common value known to cause chaotic behavior. The initial value is set as
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}=(8.01,8,8,8,8)^{\top}
.

7
Conclusion

To mitigate the impact of noise on learning dynamics from data, we have proposed a method that simultaneously fits both the derivative and the trajectory from noisy time-series data. This approach treats the derivative estimation as an inverse problem involving integral operators in the forward model and estimates the derivative function by solving a vRKHS regularization problem. We have established an integral-form representer theorem for vRKHS, based on which we only need to compute the regularized solution by solving a finite-dimensional problem and can automatically select a good regularization parameter by the L-curve method. With the fitted derivative and trajectory, the dynamics can be recovered by solving a linear regularization problems by embedding the dynamics in a vRKHS. Several numerical experiments are performed to demonstrate the effectiveness and efficiency of our method.

When the data size
n
is very large, the implementation of our method becomes challenging since
G_{1}
and
\widetilde{G}_{1}
are large-scale dense matrices and the eigen-decompositions are very expensive to compute. In this case, we could consider scalable RKHS methods to get sparse approximations of
G_{1}
or
\widetilde{G}_{1}
, as referenced in
[
42
,
32
]
. Additional, iterative regularization methods that rely only on matrix-vector products are more efficient, as referenced in
[
30
,
31
]
. These approaches will be the focus of our future research.
