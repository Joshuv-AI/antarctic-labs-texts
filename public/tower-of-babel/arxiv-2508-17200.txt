Large Language Model-Based Automatic Formulation for Stochastic Optimization Models
Amirreza Talebi

Abstract
This paper presents the first integrated systematic study on the performance of large language models (LLMs), specifically ChatGPT, to automatically formulate and solve stochastic optimization problems from natural language descriptions. Focusing on three key categories, joint chance-constrained models, individual chance-constrained models, and two-stage stochastic linear programs (SLP-2), we design several prompts that guide ChatGPT through structured tasks using chain-of-thought and modular reasoning. We introduce a novel soft scoring metric that evaluates the structural quality and partial correctness of generated models, addressing the limitations of canonical and execution-based accuracy. Across a diverse set of stochastic problems, GPT-4-Turbo outperforms other models in partial score, variable matching, and objective accuracy, with cot_s_instructions and agentic emerging as the most effective prompting strategies. Our findings reveal that with well-engineered prompts and multi-agent collaboration, LLMs can facilitate specially stochastic formulations, paving the way for intelligent, language-driven modeling pipelines in stochastic optimization.

Leveraging LLMs for modelling of such problems is important as they offer quick, step-by-step guidance and clear explanations, making complex tasks more understandable. This capability enables rapid testing of multiple scenarios and real-time analysis, while enhancing decision-making with improved explainability and iterative refinement.

Related work

The advent of the systematic study of auto-formulation of OR problems is marked by the paper of
[

Their system, OptGen, uses a two-stage controllable generation pipeline: first mapping text to an intermediate representation (IR), then converting that IR into a canonical linear programming (LP) formulation. They introduce the first dataset of LP word problems (LPWP) across diverse domains to train and evaluate their approach.

Moreover, in
[

, the NL4Opt Competition was introduced to explore approaches for translating natural language descriptions of optimization problems into solver-compatible formulations. It comprises two tasks: entity recognition for tagging semantic components and generation of a logical form (intermediate representation) for LP problems. They also proposed a metric to measure the quality of solutions in their canonical form. With the advancements in LLMs and prompting methods, the reference
[
22
]
proposed a multi-agent framework for OR problems, orchestrating specialized LLM agents through planning and reflection. Although their conductor-agent architecture shares some conceptual similarity with our system, their work addresses general OR problems, whereas our project specializes in stochastic optimization.

Similarly, the recently proposed OptiMUS framework by
[
1
]
employed a modular, multi-agent architecture to automate the formulation and solution of deterministic mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) problems from natural language. It also used a team of specialized agents, leveraging Python with Gurobi, and evaluated the final output solution. However, OptiMUS focuses exclusively on deterministic problems and does not address stochastic models. Besides, they a priori mention the parameters, constraints, and objective function of the problem in their prompts, whereas we only provide instructions in some of our prompts. Similarly,
[
6
]
proposed a framework to formulate MILP models in three phases: variable generation, constraints formation, and finally MILP modeling.

The work of
[
2
]
demonstrates that ChatGPT, when provided with carefully engineered prompts, can automatically formulate, adapt, and solve robust and adaptive robust optimization problems, and include different types of uncertainty in constraints. While their study highlights the model’s potential for handling uncertainty and generating executable code, it remains focused on prompt design for cases without incorporating collaborative reasoning or more stochastic frameworks such as SLP-2. In contrast, our project extends beyond robust optimization into stochastic optimization with SLP-2, joint and individual chance constraints, leveraging multi-agent collaboration and chain-of-thought prompting to tackle deeper modeling challenges. Additionally, we only provide a description of the problem rather than a formulation. We also propose a systematic way to evaluate and quantify the solution quality. However, their work is similar in the sense that they have demonstrated solving a category of stochastic problems using ChatGPT.

As for other applications of LLMs in OR, the reference
[
3
]
introduces OptiChat, a GPT-4-based system aimed at diagnosing infeasible optimization models by explaining structure, identifying infeasibility sources, and suggesting corrective actions for deterministic problems. While their work focuses on post-solution debugging and interpretability, our approach centers on proactive reasoning during the modeling and solution process, with chain-of-thought and multiple agents coordinating to avoid infeasibility and improve formulation quality upfront.

Except for the work of
[
22
]
, which considers feedback loops in their chain-of-experts framework, step-by-step analysis and solution methods in the mentioned works do not consider the fact that an error in the early stages can propagate through the whole process. Motivated by
[
22
]
and
[
8
]
, which introduce the feedback and refinement concept in LLM tasks, we also embed this idea in our multi-agent framework. Moreover, evaluation of the solutions in these works is based on two major categories, namely canonical accuracy and execution accuracy, used primarily in
[
12
,
10
]
. In canonical accuracy, the problem is turned into its canonical shape and then the matching between elements of the ground truth model and the generated one is counted. However, this method is prone to errors as it does not consider permutations of elements in constraints. Additionally, LLMs may not follow the notations in the ground truth model. On the other hand, relying solely on execution accuracy, we are not able to comprehensively assess the capability of LLMs as they can at least generate partially correct solutions. Furthermore, we can still have two LP models with the same optimal solutions while they are not equivalent and the generated model is not correct. The only work tackling these issues partially is
[
7
]
, where they map the formulation of the problems to bipartite graphs and compute the editing costs of converting the generated model to the ground truth. While this tackles the permutation problem, it is still subject to errors caused by notation issues, redundancies, and computational burdens, as it can be seen as a matching problem in bipartite graphs. Additionally, they do not provide detailed information on matching scores for different components of the problem.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that systematically integrates stochastic optimization problems with a variety of prompt strategies tailored specifically for stochastic optimization.
We introduce a novel soft scoring metric that provides a more nuanced evaluation of LLM-generated optimization models. This metric accounts for structural redundancies, notational discrepancies, and permutations in the ordering of elements within constraints and objective functions, limitations that are not fully addressed by existing metrics such as canonical and execution accuracy.
Furthermore, we present an extensive experimental evaluation across multiple ChatGPT variants and a range of carefully designed prompts.

2
Approach

We investigate the ability of LLMs, particularly ChatGPT, to engage with and solve stochastic optimization problems. The focus lies on three core problem types frequently encountered in stochastic optimization: joint chance-constrained models, individual chance-constrained models, and SLP-2 problems including their deterministic counterparts, DLP-2. We first define each model formally with precise mathematical formulations and use them as structured instructions in one of our prompts.

2.1
Chance constraint models

Chance-constrained models are optimization models that incorporate uncertainty by allowing certain constraints to be violated with a probability. Instead of requiring that a constraint always be satisfied (which can be conservative when dealing with uncertain data), a chance-constrained model wants constraint(s) to be satisfied most of the time, with some confidence. In particular, the joint chance constraint model seeks to minimize the cost while ensuring that all constraints are satisfied simultaneously with at least probability

2.3
SLP-2

SLP-2 with recourse is a powerful framework for modeling decision-making problems under uncertainty. In this approach, decisions are made in two stages: the first-stage decisions are taken before the uncertainty is realized, while the second-stage decisions are made after the uncertain outcomes become known. The recourse function captures the cost or adjustment needed in the second stage to respond to different realizations of uncertainty. This structure allows decision-makers to plan ahead while maintaining the flexibility to adapt based on future information. SLP-2 models are widely applied in areas such as supply chain management, energy and water systems, the airline industry, and logistics, where anticipating and mitigating risk is crucial.

Next, we leverage ChatGPT as a language model to interpret a verbal description of stochastic problems. From the narrative input, the model will extract and formalize the relevant components, including sets, parameters, first-stage (deterministic) decision variables, and second-stage (recourse) stochastic variables.

Following the identification of model components, ChatGPT will be prompted to construct the mathematical formulation of models.

This procedure will be conducted through in-context learning, wherein structured prompts are used to sequentially introduce foundational concepts and problem-specific elements. The interaction will follow a chain-of-thought prompting strategy to emulate stepwise reasoning and derivation, enabling the model to incrementally construct, refine, and solve the problem with minimal external intervention, which has been found effective by
[
21
]
.

The steps in our method are as follows (for SLP-2):

1.

Problem description extraction:
We will provide a verbal description of a stochastic problem to ChatGPT. This description will include all essential components required for the mathematical formulation. A prompt will guide ChatGPT to extract relevant sets, parameters, stochastic variables, and deterministic variables.
Benefit: This step structures the problem elements for downstream formalization, enabling precise and complete model construction.

2.

Model formulation:
A prompt will instruct ChatGPT to formulate the SLP-2 by identifying the objective function and constraints, including the recourse function. The inputs for this step will be the initial problem description and the extracted elements from the previous step.
Benefit: This formalization transforms the abstract problem into a solvable optimization model.

3.

Extensive form generation:
A prompt will direct ChatGPT to generate the extensive form of the mathematical formulation developed in the previous step. The inputs will be the original problem description and the compact model formulation.
Benefit: The extensive form facilitates deterministic equivalent modeling and enables solution by standard solvers.

For the chance-constrained model and DLP-2, we provide a general problem description and evaluate whether ChatGPT can solve them in a single prompt without further guidance.

In a similar framework, we apply the same approach across all problem categories, SLP-2, DLP-2, and chance-constrained models, with one key difference: ChatGPT is asked to work entirely within the Python environment using the Gurobi solver, without any textual formulations or explicit instructions. This setup allows us to more directly assess the quality of both the solutions and the underlying model formulations. Additionally, we also adapted standard and chain-of-thought prompts from
[
22
]
for stochastic optimization problems. In particular, we introduce a new scoring system (detailed in the next section) that captures fully correct as well as partially correct components of the generated code, enabling a more nuanced evaluation of ChatGPT’s modeling performance.

2.4
Multi-agent framework

To further extend the reasoning capabilities of LLMs in complex optimization tasks, we designed a multi-agent framework wherein distinct ChatGPT agents collaborate on solving parts of a stochastic optimization problem. Each agent is prompted with tailored context and roles, promoting focused, modular reasoning. After execution, separate reviewer agents evaluate the consistency and coherence of the outputs, identifying formulation issues or logical gaps. This agentic setup reflects a chain-of-experts approach, allowing multiple reasoning paths to converge into a coordinated solution.

2.7
Scoring

The scoring system evaluates ChatGPT’s generated models based on output correctness and structural similarity to a ground-truth model. Exact matches receive full credit, while variables, constraints, and objectives are compared using a matching strategy that assigns partial credit and reports any runtime or compile-time errors.
While a matching optimal solution suggests a high-quality model, it may still lack essential variables or constraints. To prevent false positives, we can investigate matching scores for variables, constraints, and the objective function.
The system also reports the percentage of extra elements in the output to help identify faulty models. Constraint permutations do not affect correctness, as we compare them pairwise using algebraic checks.

3.1
Summary of results and takeaways

GPT-4-Turbo is the most consistent and best-performing model overall, excelling in partial score, variable matching, and objective matching, with minimal compile and runtime errors. GPT-4 follows closely, while GPT-3.5-Turbo-16k performs well on objective matching but shows variability in other metrics. GPT-3.5-Turbo ranks lowest, with the most fluctuations and weakest constraint handling.

Among prompting methods,
cot_s_instructions
is the most robust for SLP-2 problems, showing high consistency and top scores.
cot_s2
performs best for DLP-2 and maintains a strong balance across metrics.
agentic
performs well on partial and objective scores, while
standard_s
ranks consistently lowest across all categories and metrics.

Across problem types, DLP-2 is the most tractable, with the highest partial and variable matching scores. Constraint matching and accuracy remain low across all categories and are highly variable across runs. Instance-level analysis reveals that Instance 2 often has higher compile error rates, while Instance 1 is more prone to runtime issues.

Reproducibility analysis shows that objective and partial scores are generally stable, while constraint matching and error metrics are highly inconsistent across runs, especially for less robust models and prompt styles.

4
Conclusion

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have opened new possibilities for automating tasks in operations research (OR). While prior efforts have primarily focused on deterministic problem formulation, this paper explores the performance of LLMs to stochastic optimization, including chance constraint models, two-stage stochastic programming (SLP-2), and its deterministic counterpart, two-stage deterministic programming (DLP-2).

We developed customized prompts and introduced the first soft scoring metric tailored to stochastic optimization, addressing the limitations of existing evaluation methods such as canonical and execution accuracy. Extensive experiments were conducted on several recent variants of ChatGPT, leveraging our prompting methods and scoring system.

Experimental results show that each combination of ChatGPT model and prompting method excels in modeling specific problem categories and components. Overall, GPT-4-Turbo outperforms other variants in formulating stochastic models, particularly when paired with
cot_s_instructions
prompting. The
agentic
method produced results comparable to
cot_s_instructions
, which is notable given that it was not provided with explicit instructions, highlighting the effectiveness of collaborative LLM reasoning in generating structurally sound and semantically accurate optimization models. These insights would not have been possible without our soft scoring method, which enabled granular analysis of partially correct outputs.

Future research will focus on enhancing the agentic system by introducing dynamic feedback loops, allowing agents to revise outputs based on verification signals or prior errors using our scoring system. Another direction involves integrating symbolic solvers and constraint validation tools to improve real-time feasibility and correctness checks. Finally, extending the framework to accommodate non-linear, integer, or multi-stage stochastic models would broaden its applicability to real-world OR domains.

Additionally, future work could involve systematically testing ChatGPT across a wider range of stochastic optimization problems to validate these findings. It would also be valuable to examine how solutions perform under varying uncertainty scenarios, and to explore which types of prompts tend to trigger scenario-based errors, a limitation we could not address in this study due to compilation errors across all such problems.
