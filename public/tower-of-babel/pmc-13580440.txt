Joint Associations of High-Sensitivity C-Reactive Protein and Albumin with Incident Heart Failure: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Man Li, Yan-xiu Wang, Wei-guo Zheng, Bao-hua Wang, Lu Guo, Li Zhang, Xiao-xuan Wei, Peng Li, Shou-ling Wu, Lixia Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough elevated high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and lower albumin (ALB) are each associated with increased heart failure (HF) risk, and inflammation and hypoalbuminemia are correlated in HF patients, evidence on their joint associations with incident HF is lacking. While C-reactive protein to albumin ratio (CAR) has been associated with HF, it shows no advantage over CRP alone. We therefore aimed to examine the independent and joint associations of hs-CRP and ALB with incident HF and to compare their predictive value with CAR.MethodsIn this prospective observational cohort study, we included 61,488 participants from the Kailuan Study without HF at baseline. hs-CRP and ALB were measured in 2010, and participants were stratified into four groups according to hs-CRP (<2 or ≥2 mg/L) and ALB (<46.1 or ≥46.1 g/L), with 46.1 g/L representing the cohort median. Incident HF was identified through hospital records and insurance data. Cox models estimated hazard ratios (HRs), and predictive performance was assessed using the C-index, Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI), and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI).ResultsOver 11.3 years of follow-up, 1444 participants (2.35%) developed HF. Significant additive interactions were observed between hs-CRP, ALB, and HF risk. Compared with normal hs-CRP and above-median ALB, elevated hs-CRP and below-median ALB were associated with a 2.01-fold higher HF risk (HR 2.01, 95% CI 1.73–2.34). Among the evaluated models, the model incorporating the joint hs-CRP-ALB classification had the highest numerical C-index (0.7803). Compared with the fully adjusted model, the NRI and IDI were 0.2496 and 0.0012, respectively.ConclusionHs-CRP and ALB are independently and jointly associated with HF risk.Keywords: high-sensitivity c-reactive protein, albumin, c-reactive protein to albumin ratio, heart failure, inflammation, synergistic effect, risk factors

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is the final stage of various cardiovascular diseases and remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide.1 The in-hospital mortality rate during the first admission for HF ranges from 2% to 17%. In addition, the one-year mortality rate following HF hospitalization is between 17% and 45%, while the five-year mortality rate exceeds 50%.2 Globally, the prevalence of HF was approximately 1–2% in 2021. Between 2012 and 2030, HF prevalence is projected to increase by 46%, with associated healthcare costs anticipated to rise by 127%.3,4 These figures underscore the need to identify high-risk populations and implement early preventive interventions to mitigate the significant burden of HF on public health and the economy.

C-reactive protein (CRP) and albumin (ALB) are both synthesized by hepatocytes. CRP serves as a well-established biomarker of inflammation, widely used to assess acute and chronic inflammatory responses.5 ALB plays a crucial role in maintaining oncotic pressure, and it also possesses antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties.6–8 Several population-based studies have shown that elevated high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and lower ALB levels are independently associated with an increased risk of incident HF.9–13 For instance, a study analyzed data from four longitudinal community-based cohorts and found that each 1- standard deviation (SD) increase in natural log-transformed CRP was associated with a 15% higher risk of HF.14 In addition, Filippatos et al found that among community-dwelling older adults aged ≥65, baseline hypoalbuminemia was associated with a 25% higher risk of incident HF.12

A meta-analysis of 48 studies established a link between hypoalbuminemia and persistent inflammation in HF patients, as higher CRP levels were observed in those with lower ALB levels.15 Bonilla-Palomas et al further demonstrated that inflammatory activation was the primary factor associated with hypoalbuminemia in patients with acute decompensated HF.16 Given the well-documented association between inflammation and hypoalbuminemia in HF patients, it is important to determine whether the joint association of hs-CRP and ALB increases the long-term risk of incident HF. Previous evidence from the UK Biobank has shown that the C-reactive protein to albumin ratio (CAR) is associated with the incidence of HF; however, its predictive ability was not superior to CRP alone.17 This raises the question of whether directly examining the joint association of hs-CRP and ALB provides greater insight into HF risk than using CAR. To date, no published studies have systematically investigated the interaction and joint associations of hs-CRP and ALB with incident HF. Moreover, existing research on the association between ALB and HF has primarily focused on older populations, and its role in the general population remains unclear. To address these gaps, we utilized data from the Kailuan Study, a large-scale, ongoing prospective community-based cohort, to examine the independent and joint associations of hs-CRP and ALB levels with the risk of incident HF, while also comparing their predictive value with CAR in the general population.

Method

Data Source

This prospective observational cohort study used data from the Kailuan Study, an ongoing, large-scale cohort with biennial health examinations. Details of Kailuan study have been described elsewhere.18,19 In brief, 101,510 individuals were recruited between 2006 and 2007 (referred to as “2006”) in Tangshan, Hebei Province, China. Participants completed structured questionnaires during the interviews and underwent standardized clinical examinations, which were repeated at each biennial follow-up visit. Furthermore, annual follow-ups were conducted to assess the incidence of HF and all-cause mortality. The present analysis was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and within the scope of the Kailuan Study protocol approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Kailuan General Hospital ([2006] Approval No. 5; January 5, 2006). All participants provided written informed consent at enrollment in the Kailuan Study.

Study Participants

During the third physical examination conducted in 2010–2011 (referred to as “2010”), hs-CRP and ALB levels were measured. Because this study aimed to investigate the independent and joint associations of hs-CRP and ALB levels, as well as the association of CAR with HF, participants with complete data on these biomarkers were included. Those with a history of HF were excluded from the analysis.

Exposure and Outcomes

During the “2010” health examination, fasting venous blood samples (5 mL) were collected from participants after 8–12 hours of fasting. Serum hs-CRP levels were measured using turbidimetry, and ALB levels were assessed with the bromocresol green method. All measurements were conducted in the Central Laboratory of Kailuan Hospital.20,21 To avoid zero values in the calculation of CAR, hs-CRP values of 0 mg/L were replaced with 0.01 mg/L. CAR was calculated as the ratio of hs-CRP to ALB.21

The “2010” health examination marked the start of the follow-up period, with the first occurrence of a HF event considered the endpoint. Potential HF events were identified through hospital discharge summaries and social medical insurance records. The medical records of potential cases were reviewed and adjudicated by two physicians according to prespecified diagnostic criteria based on the ESC guidelines.22,23 Clinical evidence of HF included dyspnoea, fatigue, or fluid retention, with New York Heart Association functional class II–IV or Killip class II–IV documented where applicable. Objective evidence included either an LVEF ≤50%, measured by two-dimensional echocardiography using the modified biplane Simpson method, or an elevated plasma NT-proBNP concentration. HF was diagnosed when clinical evidence was accompanied by at least one of the objective criteria. For participants with multiple HF episodes, the first occurrence was recorded as the outcome. For those without HF events during follow-up, December 31, 2022, or the date of death was recorded as the endpoint.

Data Collection

Demographic data (eg, age and sex), socioeconomic status (eg, education), lifestyle information (eg, drinking status, smoking status, and physical activity), medication use (eg, antihypertensive, hypoglycemic, and lipid-lowering drugs), family history (eg, cardiovascular disease) and medical history (eg, HF) were collected through face-to-face interviews using a standardized questionnaire, as previously described.24,25 Anthropometric parameters and blood pressure were measured during biennial health examinations. The definitions of body mass index (BMI), hypertension, diabetes, alcohol consumption, smoking, and physical activity are provided in the Supplementary Methods section of the Supplement. Blood samples were collected from the antecubital vein after an overnight fast of ≥8 hours, and biochemical parameters, including hs-CRP, ALB, total cholesterol, and fasting blood glucose, were measured in the Central Laboratory of Kailuan Hospital.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were described as the mean (SD), median (IQR), or number (percentage), as appropriate. Intergroup comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables and χ2 test for categorical variables. Missing covariate data were handled through multiple imputation by chained equations.

Unadjusted incidence rates (per 1000 person-years) and Kaplan‒Meier curves were used to present the cumulative risk of HF. Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the risk of incident HF associated with hs-CRP (<1, 1–3, or >3 mg/L), ALB (tertiles: <45, 45–47.2, or >47.2 g/L), and CAR (quartiles: <0.015, 0.015–0.029, 0.029–0.065, or ≥0.065). For hs-CRP and ALB, three sequential models were constructed: Model 1 was adjusted for age (continuous variable, years) and sex (men or women). Model 2 adjusted for variables in Model 1 and current smoking (yes or no), current drinking (yes or no), physical activity (active or inactive), education (high school completion and lower or college completion and higher), BMI (continuous variable, kg/m2), TC (continuous variable, mmol/L), hypertension (yes or no), diabetes (yes or no), cardiovascular disease family history (yes or no), antihypertensive (yes or no), hypoglycemic (yes or no), and lipid-lowering drugs (yes or no). Model 3 additionally adjusted for variables in Model 2 with mutual adjustment for hs-CRP and ALB as independent variables. For CAR, only Models 1 and 2 were applied. We applied log-transformation (ln) to hs-CRP and CAR concentrations, and used restricted cubic spline models with three knots at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles to examine the associations of ln hs-CRP, ALB, and ln CAR with the risk of HF. In these spline models, covariates included in Model 2 were adjusted. In addition, the aforementioned multivariate Cox models were used for the following analyses. We first examined the additive and multiplicative interactions between hs-CRP (<2 or ≥2 mg/L) and ALB (median cutoff: <46.1 or ≥46.1 g/L) in association with the risk of HF.26 Based on these groupings, the entire sample was stratified into four mutually exclusive groups, and the Cox proportional hazards models (Models 1 and 2) were then used to analyze the risk of HF across these groups.

To evaluate the incremental predictive performance for HF, the C-index, continuous Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI), and absolute Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) were calculated for Model 2 alone and after the addition of hs-CRP, ALB, CAR, or the joint hs-CRP-ALB classification. Continuous NRI and absolute IDI were reported as proportions, with 95% CIs estimated using 1000 bootstrap resamples.

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). A two-tailed P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Subgroup Analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to investigate the joint association of hs-CRP and ALB with the risk of HF according to age (<50, 50–60, and ≥60 years), sex, BMI (<24, 24–28, and ≥28 kg/m2), hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, LDL-C (<2.6 or ≥2.6 mmol/L), and socioeconomic status (SES). Interactions were assessed by including multiplicative interaction terms between the joint hs-CRP-ALB categories and each stratification variable in Model 2. To account for multiple subgroup comparisons, interaction P values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.

Sensitivity Analyses

To evaluate the robustness of the observed joint association between hs-CRP and ALB and incident HF, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses. First, because medication use, acute inflammatory conditions, and hepatic dysfunction may influence hs-CRP or ALB levels, we repeated the analyses after separately excluding participants who were taking antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, or glucose-lowering medications, those with hs-CRP concentrations >10 mg/L, and those who tested positive for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg). Second, to reduce the likelihood of reverse causation, we repeated the primary analysis after excluding participants who developed HF within the first year of follow-up. Third, to assess the impact of missing-data handling, we performed a complete-case analysis without multiple imputation. Fourth, participants were reclassified according to the median values of hs-CRP and ALB, and the analyses were repeated to examine whether the results were sensitive to alternative grouping strategies. Fifth, E-values were calculated on the basis of the model 2 to estimate the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the exposure and HF to fully account for the observed association.27 Finally, because death may preclude the occurrence of HF, a Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards model was applied to account for the competing risk of death.

Participant Selection

Figure 1 shows the process of participant selection. We included 61,902 participants who were enrolled in the “2010” health examination and had complete data on hs-CRP and ALB. After excluding participants with pre-existing HF, 61,488 participants were included in the final cohort.

Population Characteristics

Among 61,488 participants (mean age 53.14 years, 75.13% men), 18,849 (30.65%) were of normal hs-CRP and above-median ALB levels; 12,054 (19.60%) were of above-median ALB but elevated hs-CRP; 19,282 (31.36%) were of normal hs-CRP but below-median ALB; and 11,303 (18.38%) were of elevated hs-CRP and below-median ALB. Joint Association of Hs-CRP and ALB with HF

Significant interactions between hs-CRP, ALB, and the risk of HF were observed in the additive interaction models, with an RERI of 0.20 (95% CI: 0.10‒0.29) (Supplementary Table 1). The cumulative incidence of HF across the four groups categorized by hs-CRP and ALB levels is shown in Figure 2 (Log rank test, P <0.05). In the Cox proportional hazards regression multivariable analysis, compared with the group with normal hs-CRP and above-median ALB levels (hs-CRP<2 mg/L and ALB≥46.1 g/L), the multivariable-adjusted HRs were 1.35 (95% CI, 1.14–1.60) for the group with above-median ALB but elevated hs-CRP (hs-CRP≥2 mg/L and ALB≥46.1 g/L), 1.39 (95% CI, 1.19–1.61) for the group with normal hs-CRP but below-median ALB (hs-CRP<2 mg/L and ALB<46.1 g/L), and 2.01 (95% CI, 1.73–2.34) for the group with both elevated hs-CRP and below-median ALB (hs-CRP≥2 mg/L and ALB<46.1 g/L) (Table 4).

Prediction Model

The addition of hs-CRP, ALB, CAR, or the joint hs-CRP-ALB classification to Model 2 resulted in small numerical increases in the C-index. The C-index was 0.7758 for Model 2, 0.7777 after the addition of CAR, and 0.7803 after the addition of the joint hs-CRP-ALB classification. For CAR, the continuous NRI was 0.1753 (95% CI, 0.1235–0.2271) and the absolute IDI was 0.0007 (95% CI, 0.0004–0.0010). For the joint hs-CRP-ALB classification, the continuous NRI was 0.2496 (95% CI, 0.1987–0.3008) and the absolute IDI was 0.0012 (95% CI, 0.0007–0.0015) (Supplementary Table 2).

Sensitivity Analyses

A series of sensitivity analyses were performed to examine the robustness of the joint association between hs-CRP, ALB, and the risk of HF. The results of the sensitivity analyses were consistent with the main findings. Furthermore, based on Model 2, we calculated the E-Value for the joint association of hs-CRP and ALB with the risk of HF, which was 3.43. This suggests that any confounding factor associated with the HF would need to have a HR of at least 3.43 to reduce the observed HR for HF to below 1. Meanwhile, the competing risks analysis using the Fine-Gray model showed a trend similar to that of the original analysis (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

Principal Findings

In this large, community-based prospective observational cohort study, we found that hs-CRP and ALB were both independently and jointly associated with the incidence of HF. Participants with elevated hs-CRP and below-median ALB had a two-fold higher risk of developing HF compared to those with normal hs-CRP and above-median ALB levels. The joint association was generally consistent across the examined demographic, clinical, and socioeconomic subgroups. In addition, CAR was independently associated with incident HF.

Comparison with Other Studies

Prospective observational studies on the joint association of hs-CRP and ALB with the risk of HF are lacking, making direct comparisons difficult. Recently, data from the UK Biobank showed that CAR was associated with the incidence of HF; however, they found that CAR did not show superior predictive ability for HF compared to CRP alone.17 Consistent with previous evidence, CAR was independently associated with incident HF in our study. Although the model incorporating the joint hs-CRP-ALB classification had a slightly higher numerical C-index than the model incorporating CAR, the absolute difference was small, and its clinical relevance remains uncertain. Unlike CAR, which summarizes hs-CRP and ALB as a single ratio, joint classification allows the two biomarkers to be considered simultaneously while retaining information on their individual levels. This approach also enabled us to evaluate their additive interaction with incident HF. Our study identified a significant synergistic effect on HF risk associated with the co-occurrence of elevated hs-CRP (≥2 mg/L) and below-median ALB (<46.1 g/L) (RERI =0.20, 95% CI: 0.10–0.29, P <0.01), indicating that the combined effect of elevated hs-CRP and below-median ALB substantially increases the risk of HF beyond the simple additive effects of each marker. Further joint analysis revealed that, compared with individuals with hs-CRP <2 mg/L and above-median ALB, those with elevated hs-CRP and below-median ALB had a 2.01-fold higher risk of HF (95% CI: 1.73–2.34). This finding provides new insights into early screening and intervention for HF, emphasizing the need to consider the joint association of these biomarkers in risk assessment.

While no prior research has specifically examined this joint association, previous studies have independently evaluated the roles of hs-CRP and ALB in incidence of HF. For instance, de Boer et al analyzed data from four longitudinal community-based cohorts and found that each 1-SD increase in natural log-transformed CRP was associated with a 15% higher risk of HF.14 Similarly, a meta-analysis reported that elevated CRP levels were significantly associated with a 9% increased risk of new-onset heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF).28 Moreover, a 9.4-year follow-up study of 2907 participants aged 70–79 years found that low serum ALB levels are associated with increased risk for HF in the elderly in a time-dependent manner.13 Filippatos et al also found that among community-dwelling adults aged ≥65 years, baseline hypoalbuminemia was associated with increased risk of incident HF, independent of CRP.12 However, studies investigating the association between ALB and HF have been limited by smaller samples size and older populations, which may not fully represent the broader at-risk demographic. Furthermore, the studies mentioned above were constrained by shorter follow-up periods, which may have hindered their ability to fully assess the long-term association of prolonged exposure on HF risk. In contrast, our study benefits from a population-based design with a longer follow-up duration and a large-scale sample, covering a broader spectrum of age groups and health statuses. This design enables a more comprehensive and accurate evaluation of the long-term, both independently and jointly, associations of hs-CRP and ALB with the incidence of HF.

Potential Mechanisms

Hs-CRP is a marker of inflammation, and its elevated levels are typically associated with the activation of inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and IL-1β. These cytokines enter cardiomyocytes, inducing hypertrophy and apoptosis, leading to myocardial fibrosis and cardiac remodeling, and ultimately resulting in HF.29 The mechanism by which ALB levels are associated with increased HF risk remains unclear. However, weakened of antioxidant and anti-inflammatory, and fluid retention exacerbation may play key roles in this association. Since ALB has anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties,30 reduced levels impair these protective mechanisms, making the heart more vulnerable to oxidative and inflammatory damage.31 Moreover, a decrease in serum ALB reduces colloid osmotic pressure, leading to fluid retention and the formation of pulmonary edema, which further exacerbates the clinical manifestations of HF.32 Notably, these two mechanisms are complementary to each other. Inflammation suppresses hepatic synthesis of ALB,33 resulting in decreased serum ALB levels, which further render the heart more vulnerable to oxidative and inflammatory damage. This, in turn, establishes a vicious cycle that accelerates the progression of HF. Moreover, inflammation disrupts the integrity of the vascular endothelium and glycocalyx, increasing vascular permeability and promoting the leakage of ALB from blood vessels into surrounding tissues.32 Therefore, the joint association of hs-CRP and ALB in HF—through inflammation and fluid retention—ultimately exacerbates the pathological processes of HF. The interaction between the two provides an important pathological basis for the incidence of HF.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several important strengths. The prospective cohort design and long follow-up period strengthened the temporal interpretation of the association between hs-CRP, ALB, and subsequent HF. The large, well-characterized cohort, together with linkage to social insurance records, hospital discharge databases, and regular health examinations, improved the completeness of outcome ascertainment and reduced the likelihood of substantial loss to follow-up. In addition, comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders and multiple sensitivity analyses enhanced the robustness of the findings.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, HF events were primarily identified through hospitalization records; therefore, milder cases managed only in outpatient settings may not have been captured, which could have led to an underestimation of HF incidence. Second, as with any observational study, residual or unmeasured confounding cannot be entirely excluded, and the present findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Third, hs-CRP and ALB were measured at baseline only, and temporal changes in these biomarkers during follow-up were not assessed, which may have introduced regression dilution bias. Finally, the Kailuan cohort primarily comprised occupational adults from northern China and was predominantly male. The underrepresentation of women and other populations may limit the generalizability of our findings. In addition, the lower proportions of current smoking and drinking observed among participants with elevated hs-CRP and below-median ALB may partly reflect age-related differences, behavioral changes following the development of chronic conditions, or characteristics of the occupational cohort. These baseline differences should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Further validation in more diverse populations is warranted.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings suggest that hs-CRP and ALB were independently and jointly associated with the risk of incident HF. Moreover, the joint hs-CRP and ALB classification provided modest incremental prognostic information when added to the fully adjusted model. These findings support the potential value of jointly assessing hs-CRP and ALB for early HF risk stratification, although further validation in more diverse populations is needed.
