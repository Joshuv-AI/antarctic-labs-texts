Hepatitis B vaccination coverage and associated factors among adults in Somalia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sakariye Abdullahi Hassan –Tuuryare, Kiprotich Kimutai Timothy, Muktar Hassan Mohamud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatitis B virus (HBV) remains a significant global health concern with substantial morbidity and mortality. Although effective vaccines exist, vaccination coverage remains suboptimal in many low- and middle-income countries, including Somalia. This descriptive cross-sectional study aimed to assess Coverage of the complete hepatitis B vaccination series and identify associated demographic, occupational, and health-related factors among Somali adults.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted in Somalia from April 2024 to November 2024. Data were collected through structured face-to-face interviews and validated with hospital records at multiple healthcare institutions, universities, and community settings. Hepatitis B coverage was defined as completion of the full three-dose series. Binary logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with adherence.ResultsAmong 10,300 recruited participants, 9,975 had complete data for analysis. Hepatitis B vaccination adherence was high (81.91%). Females constituted the majority (55.05%), with most being single (56.65%), students (62.50%), and urban residents (89.37%). Significant factors associated with adherence included gender, education level, occupation, residence, and prior health conditions. Females, university graduates, healthcare workers, and hospital staff had notably lower adherence rates. Logistic regression identified male gender (OR = 4.91), student status (OR = 3.64), and urban residence (OR = 3.27) as strong positive predictors of vaccine completion. In contrast, hospital staff (OR = 0.37) and university students (OR = 0.17) had significantly lower odds of adherence. These results highlight the need for targeted interventions among specific subgroups to improve vaccination uptake.ConclusionThis study reveals a promising hepatitis B coverage rate in Somalia, with key disparities linked to gender, occupation, education, and residence. Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a significant global health concern, with an estimated 254 million people living with chronic infection in 2022, and causes approximately 1.1 million deaths annually [14, 16]. Transmission primarily occurs from mother to child at birth or during early childhood [3]. While effective vaccines have been available since 1982, global birth-dose coverage is only 45%, with particularly low coverage in Africa [8]. Antiviral treatments like tenofovir and entecavir can effectively reduce disease progression and improve survival. However, a significant testing and treatment gap persists, with only 13% of those infected diagnosed and 3% treated in 2022 [14, 16, 25]. To achieve the World Health Organization's goal of eliminating viral hepatitis by 2030, strategies for curing chronic hepatitis B and preventing transmission are being developed [3, 8, 14, 16, 25].

Hepatitis B vaccination remains the most effective strategy for preventing infection and its complications, with global coverage reaching 82% in 2021 [4]. However, significant challenges persist, particularly in developing countries with suboptimal coverage rates. Barriers to vaccination include limited healthcare access, lack of awareness, and socioeconomic factors [24]. Monthly income, occupational status, and being a healthcare worker are strong predictors of both knowledge and vaccination status [22, 24]. Despite these challenges, universal vaccination has been implemented in 168 countries, resulting in decreased disease burden and hepatitis B-related morbidity and mortality [31]. To achieve the WHO's goal of eliminating viral hepatitis by 2030, efforts must focus on overcoming social and economic hurdles, improving healthcare access, and increasing public awareness, especially in low- and middle-income countries. Recent studies highlight the challenges of hepatitis B vaccination in Africa, where prevalence rates are among the highest globally [4, 22].

Healthcare workers (HCWs) are at increased risk, yet vaccination rates remain suboptimal. In Kenya, only 48% of HCWs received all three recommended doses, with higher completion rates in hospitals compared to primary care facilities [19]. Barriers to vaccination include cost, limited access, and lack of awareness [9]. In sub-Saharan Africa, 4.6–64.4% of HCWs completed the vaccination regimen, with cost and lack of awareness cited as common reasons for non-uptake [23]. Recommendations include providing free vaccines to student HCWs, education about hepatitis B, and considering mandatory vaccination if other measures fail [9]. Low vaccination coverage among HCWs not only increases their occupational risk but also poses a threat to patient safety and infection control. Unvaccinated HCWs may unknowingly transmit hepatitis B to vulnerable patients, undermining public health efforts to control the disease.

Recent studies in Somalia highlight the low hepatitis B vaccination coverage among healthcare workers and students, despite their high-risk status. In Bosaso, only 2.8% of medical students and 16.4% of healthcare workers were fully vaccinated [5, 17]. While in Mogadishu, 33.4% of health sciences students received a full dose [15]. Common barriers to vaccination include vaccine unavailability, high costs, and fear of side effects. Factors associated with higher vaccination rates include occupation, workplace vaccine availability, and age under 30. Good knowledge about HBV was prevalent, but did not necessarily translate to vaccination uptake [5, 17].

This study aims to assess full hepatitis B vaccination coverage and identify demographic, occupational, and health-related factors associated with it. In addition, the study aims to analyze adherence patterns among high-risk groups, including healthcare workers, students, and urban residents, to provide evidence-based recommendations that can inform targeted public health interventions and strategies to enhance hepatitis B vaccination coverage in Somalia. This study fills a critical gap by examining completion rates and associated sociodemographic factors among a large sample of Somali urban residents.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Somalia between April 2024 and November 2024. Data were collected across multiple healthcare institutions, universities, and community settings, primarily in urban centers in Mogadishu, Somalia.

Study population and sampling

A total of 10,300 participants aged 18 years and above were recruited using convenience sampling during hepatitis B vaccination campaigns and community outreach programs. Participants were invited based on their availability and willingness to participate. The inclusion criterion was receipt of at least one dose of the hepatitis B vaccine, enabling evaluation of adherence to full completion of the vaccination series.

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected using a structured, pre-tested questionnaire developed from existing literature and expert consultation. The questionnaire collected demographic information, vaccination history, chronic medical conditions, and high-risk occupational exposure. Data collection was conducted via face-to-face interviews by trained staff. Vaccination status was validated using hospital records when available. Content validity was established through expert review. Reliability was assessed via a pilot study of 100 participants (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.82).

Sample size

The sample size of 10,300 was based on available participants during the campaigns; no formal sample size calculation was performed.

Definitions of variables

Hepatitis B vaccination coverage was defined as the completion of all three doses among individuals who had received at least one dose. High-risk groups were defined based on potential occupational or social exposure to hepatitis B infection, including healthcare workers, hospital staff, university students (particularly those in clinical programs), and event/conference attendees.

Data analysis

Of the 10,300 participants recruited, 9,975 had complete data and were included in the final analysis. A total of 325 participants were excluded due to missing or incomplete responses. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. The chi-square test was used to assess associations between categorical variables and hepatitis B vaccination coverage, while Pearson's correlation coefficient was used to examine the relationship between age (a continuous variable) and adherence. Binary logistic regression was conducted to identify predictors of vaccine adherence. The dependent variable was adherence to the full three-dose hepatitis B vaccine series (coded as 1 = completed, 0 = not completed). Independent variables were dummy-coded, and the following reference categories were used: female (gender), less than university (education), general population (high-risk group), divorced/widowed/others (marital status), unemployed (occupation), and rural (residence). Regression coefficients were exponentiated to obtain odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study examined hepatitis B vaccination coverage and its associated factors among a large sample of the Somali population. The overall adherence rate to the full vaccine series (three doses) was 81.91%, which is slightly above the global average of 82% reported by the World Health Organization in 2021 [30]. This high rate is encouraging, particularly in the context of Somalia’s historically fragile healthcare infrastructure, vaccine supply challenges, and population displacement due to conflict.

In contrast to local studies showing low uptake, such as 33.4% among health sciences students in Mogadishu [15], 2.8% among medical students in Bosaso [5]. and 16.4% among healthcare workers in Bosaso [17]. This study suggests broader improvements in vaccine coverage. The higher adherence rates observed may be attributed to recent public health campaigns, improved vaccine availability in urban centers, and increased awareness among specific occupational groups. Commonly reported barriers in these earlier studies include vaccine unavailability, cost, and lack of awareness [5, 15, 17]

The Health Belief Model (HBM) has demonstrated strong empirical support across a wide range of health behaviors, with perceived barriers consistently identified as the most influential construct in predicting health-related actions [18]. In the context of hepatitis B vaccination, HBM components reliably predict vaccination behavior across diverse populations. For example, among hemodialysis patients, higher levels of perceived susceptibility and perceived severity were associated with increased vaccine acceptance [29]. Similarly, a study among undergraduate nursing students found that perceived benefits and self-efficacy significantly correlated with vaccination uptake, despite a low overall coverage of just 25.8% in a high-risk group [12]. These findings underscore the critical role of HBM dimensions, particularly perceived barriers, perceived benefits, and self-efficacy, in shaping hepatitis B vaccination behaviors among at-risk populations.

More broadly, the HBM has demonstrated substantial predictive validity for vaccination uptake among healthcare workers and the general population. Systematic reviews indicate that HBM constructs explain 85–95% of influenza vaccination behavior among healthcare workers, with key predictors including beliefs about vaccine efficacy and safety, risk perception, self-efficacy, cues to action, and professional norms [11]. In the case of COVID-19 vaccination, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, and cues to action were among the most frequently cited determinants, with an overall vaccination intention rate of 68.19% [20]. However, healthcare workers also reported barriers such as concerns over vaccine safety and a preference for natural immunity [28, 32]. Overall, the HBM constructs perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, self-efficacy, and cues to action consistently distinguish between vaccinated and unvaccinated individuals across various vaccine types and populations.

Gender was a significant predictor of vaccine adherence. Contrary to trends in several African contexts where females tend to show higher compliance, this study found that males had a higher adherence rate (90.8%) than females (74.7%), a statistically significant difference confirmed by logistic regression (OR = 4.91, 95% CI: 4.30–5.61, p < 0.001). This unexpected reversal may reflect improved male engagement in preventive healthcare or differences in vaccine delivery targeting. It highlights the need for gender-sensitive strategies that encourage vaccination uptake among women, who may face structural or informational barriers. In Ethiopia, females were more likely to complete the vaccination series among healthcare professionals [2], and in Nigeria, low uptake was reported among healthcare workers (14.2%) and was influenced by staff category and age [26]. Interestingly, other Nigerian data showed higher antibody development post-vaccination in males [1]. underscoring the complexity of gender effects across different settings.

Occupational status also strongly influenced adherence. Students (86.0%) and healthcare workers (69.6%) demonstrated relatively high adherence, likely due to increased exposure to health education and institutional policies encouraging vaccination. Logistic regression confirmed significantly higher odds of adherence among students (OR = 3.64, p < 0.001) and other employed individuals (OR = 2.89, p < 0.001) compared to the unemployed. These findings are consistent with other studies in Sub-Saharan Africa, which associate formal employment and occupational exposure with higher vaccine uptake [7, 10].

Although higher education is generally expected to improve health behavior, this study found that university-educated individuals were less likely to complete the vaccine series (OR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.71–0.90, p < 0.001) compared to those with less than university education. This may stem from differing perceptions of risk, time constraints, or mistrust in health interventions. Previous research has shown that while education facilitates access, factors like social influence, attitudes, and perceived safety strongly influence vaccine acceptance [13]. This finding highlights the need for targeted risk communication even among highly educated groups.

Residence was another key determinant of adherence. Urban residents had higher odds of completion compared to rural dwellers (OR = 3.27, 95% CI: 2.75–3.89, p < 0.001), reflecting better access to vaccination services and public health infrastructure. As Somalia continues to urbanize, expanding immunization coverage to rural and underserved areas remains a critical priority. This trend mirrors findings from other studies showing urban residency and flexible vaccine delivery models improve uptake [6, 27].

Regarding health status, individuals with a history of hepatitis infection had lower adherence (76.0%) than those without (82.2%, p = 0.002), while those with a history of liver disease showed slightly higher adherence (88.5%, p = 0.029). Interestingly, chronic medical conditions did not significantly influence adherence (p = 0.228), suggesting that vulnerability alone does not drive vaccine behavior. This underlines the need for targeted counseling and follow-up among patients with chronic illnesses to emphasize the benefits of vaccination.

Among high-risk groups, college students had the highest adherence (98.0%), while hospital staff had the lowest (63.1%). University students and event/conference attendees showed intermediate levels. These differences point to the importance of not treating all high-risk groups as uniform; tailored interventions and institutional policies are needed to improve uptake, particularly in occupational groups with suboptimal adherence.

Although age was not modeled directly in the logistic regression, a moderate positive correlation (r = 0.34) was observed, indicating older individuals may be more likely to complete the vaccine series. This is consistent with international findings that link vaccine completion to older age, perceived susceptibility, and longer health system engagement [6, 27]. Studies also show that accelerated vaccination schedules can improve completion [21], and that adherence remains low in high-risk populations like medical students and drug users [5, 27].

In conclusion, this study reveals a promising hepatitis B vaccination coverage rate in Somalia, with key disparities linked to gender, occupation, education, and residence. Addressing these disparities will require context-specific, multi-pronged strategies that combine expanded access, targeted communication, and flexible delivery methods to reach underserved and high-risk populations.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Second, convenience sampling and recruitment through vaccination campaigns may have introduced selection bias, likely overestimating adherence due to participants’ greater health awareness. Third, reliance on self-reported vaccination history, though partially validated by hospital records, may have led to recall and social desirability bias. Most notably, the urban focus of the sample (89% urban residents) limits generalizability, particularly to rural and nomadic populations, who face distinct barriers such as limited access, mobility, and cultural differences. These groups may have significantly different vaccination behaviors not captured in this study.
