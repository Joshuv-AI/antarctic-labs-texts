Abstract
. G-structures and Cartan geometries are two major approaches to the description of geometric structures (in the sense of differential geometry) on manifolds of some fixed dimension n n . We show that both descriptions naturally extend to the setting of manifolds of dimension ≥ n \geq n which are endowed with a distinguished involutive distribution F F of rank n n . The resulting “partial” structures are most naturally interpreted as smooth families of standard G-structures or Cartan geometries on the leaves of the foliation defined by F F . We prove that for the special class of AHS-structures (also known as | 1 | |1| -graded parabolic geometries) the construction of a canonical Cartan geometry associated to a G-structure extends to this general setting. As an application, we prove that for partial AHS-structures there is an analog of the machinery of BGG sequences. This constructs sequences of differential operators of arbitrarily high order intrinsic to the structures. Under appropriate flatness conditions, these sequence are fine resolutions of sheaves which locally can be realized as pullbacks of sheaves on local leaf spaces for the foliation defined by F F .

1.
Introduction

The concept of G-structures represents the most important approach to a description
of geometric structures in the sense of differential geometry. The idea is to endow
each tangent space of a smooth manifold
M
of dimension
n
with some “structure”
isomorphic to a “model structure” on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(or some vector space of real
dimension
n
) in a way depending smoothly on the base point. This “structure” can
be simple, like an inner product or some tensor of fixed type, or more intricate like
a subvariety in the tangent space or its projectivization, or an identification with
a tensor product of spaces of fixed dimensions. There is a uniform concept based on a
closed subgroup
G_{0}\subset GL(n,\mathbb{R})
, which can be thought of as the group of
linear automorphisms of the model structure. The formal definition of a

G_{0}
-structure (or a G-structure with structure group
G_{0}
) is then via a reduction
of structure group of the (linear) frame bundle of
M
. To include analogs of spin
structures it is preferable to allow
G_{0}
to be a covering of a closed subgroup of

GL(n,\mathbb{R})
, which is easily possible.

The geometric properties of such structures can be very diverse and the general
theory of G-structures relates them to algebraic properties of the structure group

G_{0}
. The main tool here is the study of connections that are compatible with the
given structure and in particular of their torsion. This leads to results on
existence of canonical connections (like for Riemannian metrics, which are equivalent
to
O(n)
-structures) or of families of distinguished connections.

Cartan geometries represent a different general approach to a concept of geometric
structures. The basic idea here is to view a manifold
M
endowed with a geometric
structure as a “curved analog” of a homogeneous space
G/P
. For Cartan geometries,
many properties of general geometries are more closely related to those of the
homogeneous model
G/P
. However, for many choices of
P\subset G
, the resulting
structures are not as interesting from a geometric point of view, since they may
involve data like the choice of a compatible connection which heavily influences
geometric properties.

There are overlaps between the two concepts, which represent many very interesting
geometric structures. A basic example is that
G_{0}
-structures that determine
a canonical connection can always be equivalently described as Cartan
geometries. While the Cartan point does not provide additional technical input in
these cases, it leads to a very nice and conceptual picture (see
[
15
]
) which
generalizes the point of view that Riemannian manifolds are curved analogs of
Euclidean space.

There also are examples of
G_{0}
-structures that do not admit canonical connections
but still admit an equivalent description via a Cartan geometry. The most important
examples of this situation are AHS-structures and among them conformal
pseudo-Riemannian structures. Here the group
P
is an extension of
G_{0}
, but the
group
G
is a new ingredient, which is not easy to guess knowing only
G_{0}
. In the
conformal Riemannian case,
G_{0}=CO(n)
the conformal group in dimension
n
and
P

is a semi-direct product of
G_{0}
with the vector group
\mathbb{R}^{n*}
. However,
G

is the (semi-)simple group
SO(n+1,1)
of conformal isometries of the sphere

S^{n}
. The tools derived from the Cartan description, like tractor bundles and BGG
sequences have lead to many important developments in conformal geometry and related
areas during the last decades.

The aim of this article is to generalize both approaches and the relations between
them to a situation of smooth families of structures. An obvious idea would be to
consider families of structures on the fibers of a locally trivial bundle or, more
generally, of a surjective submersion. It turns out, however, that one can easily
deal with the even more general setting of involutive distributions (of constant
rank) on arbitrary manifolds. The resulting geometries can be interpreted as smooth
families of structures on the leaves of the induced foliation. Locally, they are
equivalent to families on fiber bundles, but, depending on global properties of the
foliation, interesting global phenomena may arise. Using the right approach, the
generalization is relatively straightforward, so we include some background on
G-structures and Cartan geometries in order to clarify this approach. Since there are
already quite a few concepts of geometric structures related to foliations around and
since technically we replace differential forms by partially defined differential
forms, we call the resulting concepts
partial G-structures
and

partial Cartan geometries
.

The main result on these concepts we prove is that one can canonically associate to
any partial AHS-structure a normal partial Cartan geometry. To do this, one can use
the same normalization condition and all the algebraic results available for standard
AHS-structures. Moreover, there is a functorial geometric construction of the partial
Cartan geometry phrased in terms of distinguished partial connections compatible with a
partial AHS-structure (with normalized torsion). This construction is given in the
proof of Theorem
12
, which shows that any partial AHS-structure is induced
by some normal partial Cartan geometry. Theorem
14
is a strong uniqueness
result, showing in particular that a normal partial Cartan geometry is uniquely
determined up to isomorphism by the underlying partial AHS-structure.

In Section
3
of the article, we give an application of the Cartan description
of partial AHS-structures. We show that there is an analog of the machinery of BGG
sequences, as originally introduced in
[
8
]
, in the partial setting. In
particular, this shows that for any type of partial AHS-structure, there are
differential operators of arbitrarily high order intrinsically associated to the
structure, see §
3.6
. The machinery provides an extension of and a new
perspective on the relative BGG machinery introduced in
[
9
]
and

[
10
]
. To some extent, the point of view we obtain is closer to the way
relative BGG resolutions were first used in twistor theory, see in particular the
book
[
1
]
. There the authors considered fibrations between generalized flag
manifolds of the same group, observed that then the fibers are generalized flag
manifolds of a smaller group and used a “BGG resolution along the fibers”. The
point of view of geometric structures on the fibers played only a small role in the
geometric version of the relative BGG machinery and, apart from one case discussed in
the thesis
[
16
]
of the second author, has not been studied in detail
so far. The partial theory we develop actually shows that for the simplest examples
of structures, a “BGG machinery along the fibers” is available in a much more
general setting. We also show that if the partial Cartan geometry has vanishing
curvature, then partial BGG sequences are complexes and resolutions of sheaves that
can be locally described as pullbacks of sheaves on local leaf spaces of the
foliation, see Theorem
22
. The same theorem also provides an analog of the
concept of normal BGG solutions in the partial setting.

2.
Partial AHS-structures and their Cartan description

2.1.
G_{0}
-structures and AHS-structures

A closed subgroup
G_{0}\subset GL(n,\mathbb{R})
gives rise to a geometric structure on
manifolds of dimension
n
. Such a
G_{0}
-structure is defined as a reduction of the
linear frame bundle
\mathcal{P}M\to M
of a smooth
n
-manifold
M
to the structure
group
G_{0}\subset GL(n,\mathbb{R})
. Slightly more generally, one can consider the case
that
G_{0}
is a covering of a closed subgroup
\underline{G_{0}}\subset GL(n,\mathbb{R})

to include structures like spin structures. In this case one has to use the
definition as a principal
G_{0}
-bundle
p:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M
together with a morphism

\mathcal{G}_{0}\to\mathcal{P}M
of fiber bundles, which is equivariant over the obvious
homomorphism
G_{0}\to GL(n,\mathbb{R})
coming from the covering homomorphism

G_{0}\to\underline{G}_{0}
.

It is well known that such a homomorphism can be equivalently encoded as a
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued one-form
\theta\in\Omega^{1}(\mathcal{G}_{0},\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which is strictly horizontal
and equivariant for the natural representation of
G_{0}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
coming from the
homomorphism
G_{0}\to GL(n,\mathbb{R})
from above. Here being strictly horizontal means
that for each
u\in\mathcal{G}_{0}
the kernel of
\theta(u):T_{u}\mathcal{G}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

coincides with the vertical subspace
\ker(T_{u}p)
. Since
M
is assumed to be

n
-dimensional, this means that
\theta(u)
descends to a linear isomorphism

T_{p(u)}M\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
which defines the homomorphism to the frame bundle.

AHS-structures are a special class of such structures that arise from simple Lie
algebras. To specify such a structure, one starts from a Lie group
G
whose Lie
algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is simple and endowed with a so-called
|1|
-grading, i.e. a
vector space decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{1}
such
that
[\mathfrak{g}_{i},\mathfrak{g}_{j}]\subset\mathfrak{g}_{i+j}
with the convention that
\mathfrak{g}_{\ell}=\{0\}
if
|\ell|>1
. Such gradings are well understood from the
classification of Hermitian symmetric spaces, see Sections 3.2.3 and 3.2.10 of

[
5
]
for more details and the classification. The subalgebra
\mathfrak{p}:=\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{1}
is then a parabolic subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
. It is well known
that the normalizer
\{g\in G:\operatorname{Ad}(g)(\mathfrak{p})\subset\mathfrak{p}\}
is a closed subgroup
of
G
that has Lie algebra
\mathfrak{p}
. One then chooses a
parabolic subgroup

P\subset G
corresponding to
\mathfrak{p}
, i.e. a subgroup that lies between this
normalizer and its connected component of the identity. In particular, this implies
that
P
has Lie algebra
\mathfrak{p}
.

It is also well known that the exponential mapping restricts to a diffeomorphism from

\mathfrak{g}_{1}
onto a closed normal subgroup
P_{+}\subset P
, so
P/P_{+}
has Lie algebra

\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{g}_{1}\cong\mathfrak{g}_{0}
. It turns out that

G_{0}:=\{g\in P:\forall i:\operatorname{Ad}(g)(\mathfrak{g}_{i})\subset\mathfrak{g}_{i}\}
, which obviously is a
closed subgroup of
P
, projects isomorphically onto
P/P_{+}
, so
P
is the
semi-direct product of
G_{0}
and
P_{+}
. By construction, the group
G_{0}
acts via the
adjoint action on each
\mathfrak{g}_{i}
and one shows that the resulting map

G_{0}\to GL(\mathfrak{g}_{-1})
is infinitesimally injective, so putting

n:=\dim(\mathfrak{g}_{-1})
there is the concept of a
G_{0}
-structure on manifolds of
dimension
n
. Of course, one can view the equivariant form
\theta
describing such a
structure as having values in
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\cong\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p}
.

Some choices of
G
and
P
show exceptional behavior from the point of
view of the resulting
G_{0}
-structures and we have to exclude them in what
follows. There is a
|1|
-grading of
\mathfrak{sl}(n+1,\mathbb{K})
with
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}=\mathbb{K}^{n}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{0}\cong\mathfrak{gl}(n,\mathbb{K})
for
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or

\mathbb{C}
and this is the only available
|1|
-grading if
n=1
. Hence a

G_{0}
-structure in this case contains almost no information. For
n\geq 2
, the
corresponding pairs
(G,P)
are of interest for the picture of Cartan geometries
that we will discuss below, since they lead to equivalent descriptions of classical
projective structures and an almost complex analog known as
c
-projective
structures, see
[
2
]
. Since these structures need a different description,
we will not consider them in this article. The case of projective structures is
dealt with in in detail in
[
16
]
and we’ll only make short remarks
on this case below. For clarity of terminology, we phrase this as a definition.

Definition 1
.

An
AHS-pair

(G,P)
consist of a simple Lie group
G
and a parabolic
subgroup
P\subset G
corresponding to a
|1|
-grading of the Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}
of
G
, which is different from the grading of
\mathfrak{sl}(n+1,\mathbb{K})

with
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}=\mathbb{K}^{n}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{0}\cong\mathfrak{gl}(n,\mathbb{K})
for

\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. The subgroup
G_{0}\subset P
of all elements whose
adjoint action preserves the grading of
\mathfrak{g}
will be called the structure
group of the AHS-structure determined by
(G,P)
.

The importance of AHS-structures as defined here is visible from the classification
results of S. Kobayashi and T. Nagano in
[
11
]
, which use the concept of
prolongation of
G_{0}
-structures. Equivalently, this can be phrased in terms of the
question whether automorphisms of the structure in question are locally determined by
some finite jet in one point. The classical example of this situation is provided by
pseudo-Riemannian metrics, since local isometries for such metrics are determined
uniquely in a neighborhood of a point by their one-jet in that point. On the other
end of the spectrum, there are (almost) symplectic structures, for which
automorphisms are not locally determined by any finite jet in a point. The main
result of
[
11
]
then is that the AHS-structures are the only
G_{0}
-structures
for which
G_{0}
acts irreducibly on
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
and automorphisms are locally
determined by some finite jet in a point but not by the one-jet. Indeed,
automorphisms for any AHS-structure are always locally determined by their two-jet in
a point, which makes them difficult to deal with. The best known example of an
AHS-structure are conformal structures in dimensions
\geq 3
, see
[
7
]
for
more examples of AHS-structures.

2.2.
Partial
G_{0}
-structures

An obvious idea for defining smooth families of geometric structures is to look at
fiber bundles and geometric structures on each fiber which, in an appropriate sense,
depend smoothly on the base point. It turns out that for
G_{0}
-structures (and also
for Cartan geometries as discussed below), there is a natural generalization, which
can locally be interpreted as a smooth family in that sense. Consider a smooth
manifold
M
of dimension
n+k
endowed with a distinguished distribution

F\subset TM
of rank
n
. Then consider the linear frame bundle

\mathcal{P}F=GL(\mathbb{R}^{n},F)
of the vector bundle
F
. This is a principal fiber bundle
with structure group
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
whose fiber over a point
x\in M
consists of all
linear isomorphisms
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to F_{x}
, the fiber of
F
over
x
. In principle, one
could define partial
G_{0}
-structures for an arbitrary distribution
F
, but as far
as we know interesting results are only available in the case that
F
is involutive,
so we restrict to that case.

Definition 2
.

Let
G_{0}
be a covering of a closed subgroup
\underline{G}_{0}\subset GL(n,\mathbb{R})
,

M
a smooth manifold of dimension
\geq n
, and
F\subset TM
an involutive
distribution of rank
n
. Then a
partial
G_{0}
-structure on
(M,F)
is
given by a principal fiber bundle
\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M
with structure group
G_{0}
, and a
morphism
\Phi:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to\mathcal{P}F
of fiber bundles with base map
\operatorname{id}_{M}
, which is
equivariant over the homomorphism
G_{0}\to GL(n,\mathbb{R})
induced by the covering
homomorphism
G_{0}\to\underline{G}_{0}
. If the dimension of
M
is
n+k
, then we
will call this a
partial
G_{0}
-structure of codimension
k
.

To define morphisms between partial
G_{0}
-structures, we need some
observations. Consider two manifolds
M^{i}
endowed with involutive rank
n

distributions
F^{i}\subset TM^{i}
for
i=1,2
and let
f:M^{1}\to M^{2}
be a smooth
map. Suppose that for each
x\in M^{1}
, the tangent map
T_{x}f:T_{x}M^{1}\to T_{f(x)}M^{2}

restricts to a linear isomorphism
F^{1}_{x}\to F^{2}_{f(x)}
. Then there is natural lift
of
f
to a principal bundle homomorphism
\mathcal{P}f:\mathcal{P}F^{1}\to\mathcal{P}F^{2}
defined by

\mathcal{P}f(u)=T_{x}f\circ u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to F^{2}_{f(x)}
for
u\in\mathcal{P}_{x}F^{1}
, i.e.
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to F^{1}_{x}
. Using this, we can now introduce the natural notion of
morphisms.

Definition 3
.

Let
(\mathcal{G}_{0}^{i}\to M^{i},F^{i},\Phi^{i})
be partial
G_{0}
-structures as in Definition

2
for
i=1,2
. The a
morphism

(\mathcal{G}_{0}^{1}\to M^{1},F^{1},\Phi^{1})\to(\mathcal{G}_{0}^{2}\to M^{2},F^{2},\Phi^{2})
is a
homomorphism
\Psi:\mathcal{G}_{0}^{1}\to\mathcal{G}_{0}^{2}
of principal bundles such that the base
map
\psi:M^{1}\to M^{2}
has the property that for any
x\in M^{1}
, the tangent map

T_{x}\psi
restricts to a linear isomorphism
F^{1}_{x}\to F^{2}_{\psi(x)}
and such that

\Phi^{2}\circ\Psi=\mathcal{P}\psi\circ\Phi^{1}
.

Observe that in the case that
\dim(M)=n
and
F=TM
, we recover the usual concepts
of
G_{0}
-structures and their morphisms (which then automatically are local
diffeomorphisms). Similarly as in that setting, partial
G_{0}
-structures can be
described via an analog of strictly horizontal, equivariant forms, but we need some
background to introduce this. Let
p:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M
be a principal fiber bundle. Then
for each
u\in\mathcal{G}_{0}
with
x=p(u)
the tangent map
T_{u}p:T_{u}\mathcal{G}_{0}\to T_{x}M
is
surjective, and we define
\widetilde{F}_{u}:=(T_{u}p)^{-1}(F_{x})\subset T_{u}\mathcal{G}_{0}
. Evidently, these spaces fit together to define a smooth distribution

\widetilde{F}\subset T\mathcal{G}_{0}
which contains the vertical subbundle
V\mathcal{G}_{0}
of

\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M
. In particular, given a vector space
E
, we can consider the vector
bundle
L(\widetilde{F},E)\to\mathcal{G}_{0}
, whose fiber over a point
u\in\mathcal{G}_{0}
is
the space
L(\widetilde{F}_{u},E)
of linear maps from
\widetilde{F}_{u}
to

E
. Sections of this vector bundle can be considered as “partially defined

E
-valued differential forms” on
\mathcal{G}_{0}
.

For a section
\theta\in\Gamma(L(\widetilde{F},\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, there is an obvious notion of
being
strictly horizontal
, namely that for each
u\in\mathcal{G}_{0}
, the kernel
of
\theta(u):\widetilde{F}_{u}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
coincides with the vertical subspace
V_{u}\mathcal{G}_{0}=\ker(T_{u}p)
. Moreover, for
g\in G_{0}
the principal right action
r^{g}:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to\mathcal{G}_{0}
satisfies
p\circ r^{g}=p
, which readily implies that
\widetilde{F}
is
invariant under the principal right action in the sense that
T_{u}r^{g}:T_{u}\mathcal{G}_{0}\to T_{u\cdot g}\mathcal{G}_{0}
maps
\widetilde{F}_{u}
to
\widetilde{F}_{u\cdot g}
. This
means that given
\alpha\in\Gamma(L(\widetilde{F},E))
for some vector space
E
and
g\in G_{0}
, there is a well defined pull back
(r^{g})^{*}\alpha\in\Gamma(L(\widetilde{F},E))

defined by the usual formula
((r^{g})^{*}\alpha)(u)(\xi)=\alpha(u\cdot g)(T_{u}r^{g}(\xi))
. Given
a representation of
G_{0}
on
E
, one can thus define equivariancy of

\alpha\in\Gamma(L(\widetilde{F},E))
in the usual way. Using these observations, we can
now formulate

Proposition 4
.

Let
G_{0}
be a covering of a closed subgroup
\underline{G}_{0}\subset GL(n,\mathbb{R})

and let
K
be the kernel of the covering homomorphism
G_{0}\to\underline{G}_{0}
.

(1) For a manifold
M
of dimension
\geq n
and an involutive distribution

F\subset TM
, a partial
G_{0}
-structure on
(M,F)
is equivalent to a principal

G_{0}
-bundle
p:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M
endowed with a strictly horizontal,
G_{0}
-equivariant
section
\theta\in\Gamma(L(\widetilde{F},\mathbb{R}^{n}))
.

(2) For two partial
G_{0}
-structures described as in (1), suppose that we have given
a principal bundle morphism
\Psi:\mathcal{G}_{0}^{1}\to\mathcal{G}_{0}^{2}
, whose base map

\psi:M^{1}\to M^{2}
satisfies the condition from Definition
3
. Then there
is a well defined pullback
\Psi^{*}\theta^{2}\in\Gamma(L(\widetilde{F}^{1},\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\Psi

is a morphisms of partial
G_{0}
-structures, if and only if
\Psi^{*}\theta^{2}=\theta^{1}
.

(3) A morphism
\Psi:\mathcal{G}_{0}^{1}\to\mathcal{G}_{0}^{2}
of partial
G_{0}
-structures is
determined by its base map
\psi
up to a locally constant function
\mathcal{G}_{0}^{1}\to K
.

Proof.

These are simple variations of the standard proofs for
G_{0}
-structures.

(1) We have to show that a morphism
\Phi:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to\mathcal{P}F
as in Definition

2
is equivalent to a section
\theta
with the specified properties. One
first observes that on
\pi:\mathcal{P}F\to M
, there is a tautological section

\underline{\theta}
of
L(\widetilde{F},\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which is strictly horizontal and

GL(n,\mathbb{R})
equivariant. This is defined by

\underline{\theta}(u)(\xi):=u^{-1}(T_{u}\pi(\xi))
, taking into account that

u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to F_{\pi(u)}
is a linear isomorphism and
T_{u}\pi(\xi)\in F_{\pi(u)}

for
\xi\in\widetilde{F}_{u}
. Given a morphism
\Phi
as above, one observes that
the tangent maps of
\Phi
send the pre-image of
F
in
T\mathcal{G}_{0}
to the
pre-image of
F
in
T\mathcal{P}F
. Hence we can define
\theta:=\Phi^{*}\underline{\theta}

and verify that this has the required properties.

Conversely, given
(p:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M,\theta)
we observe that for
u\in\mathcal{G}_{0}
,
T_{u}p

restricts to a map
\widetilde{F}_{u}\to F_{p(u)}
which descends to a linear
isomorphism
\widetilde{F}_{u}/V_{u}\mathcal{G}_{0}\to F_{p(u)}
. On the other hand, since

\ker(\theta(u))=V_{u}\mathcal{G}_{0}
it descends to an injective linear map

\widetilde{F}_{u}/V_{u}\mathcal{G}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
which has to be a linear isomorphism since
both spaces have the same dimension. Composing the inverse of this isomorphism with
the above isomorphism, one obtains a linear isomorphism
\Phi(u):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to F_{u}
, and
one directly verifies that these fit together to define a morphism
\Phi:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to\mathcal{P}F
with the required properties.

(2) The base map
\psi
of
\Psi
has the property that for each
x\in M^{1}
, the
tangent map
T_{x}\psi
restricts to a linear isomorphism
F^{1}_{x}\to F^{2}_{\psi(x)}
and,
as discussed above, this implies that there is an induced principal bundle homomorphism

\mathcal{P}\psi:\mathcal{P}F^{1}\to\mathcal{P}F^{2}
. By definition, we have
Tp^{2}\circ T\Psi=T\psi\circ Tp^{1}
, so for each
u\in\mathcal{G}_{0}^{1}
, we get

T_{u}\Psi(\widetilde{F}^{1}_{u})\subset\widetilde{F}^{2}_{u}
and an element in the kernel of
this map has to lie in the kernel of
T_{u}p^{1}
. This shows that the restriction of

T_{u}\Psi
to
\widetilde{F}^{1}_{u}
maps this space injectively and hence bijectively to

\widetilde{F}^{2}_{u}
. This readily implies that
\Psi^{*}\theta^{2}
is well
defined.

For
\xi\in\widetilde{F}^{1}_{u}
and
x=p^{1}(u)
, we get

T_{u}p^{1}(\xi)=\Phi^{1}(u)(\theta^{1}(u)(\xi))
and hence

\mathcal{P}\psi(\Phi^{1}(u))(\theta^{1}(u)(\xi))=T_{x}\psi(T_{u}p^{1}(\xi)).

On the other hand,
\Psi^{*}\theta^{2}(u)(\xi)=\theta^{2}(\Psi(u))(T_{u}\Psi(\xi))
, and

\Phi^{2}(\Psi(u))
maps this element to

T_{\Psi(u)}p^{2}(T_{u}\Psi(\xi))=T_{x}\psi(T_{u}p^{1}(\xi))
. This says that as maps

\widetilde{F}^{1}_{u}\to F^{2}_{\psi(x)}
, we get

\mathcal{P}\psi\circ\Phi^{1}\circ\theta^{1}(u)=\Phi^{2}\circ\Psi\circ(\Psi^{*}\theta^{2})(u).

Since both sides are surjective and have kernel the vertical subspace, we conclude
that
\mathcal{P}\psi(\Phi^{1}(u))=\Phi^{2}(\Psi(u))
is equivalent to

\theta^{1}(u)=(\Psi^{*}\theta^{2})(u)
, so (2) follows.

(3) Let
\Psi,\widetilde{\Psi}:\mathcal{G}_{0}^{1}\to\mathcal{G}_{0}^{2}
be two morphisms with the
same base map
\psi:M^{1}\to M^{2}
. Then there is a smooth function
g:\mathcal{G}_{0}^{1}\to G_{0}
such that
\widetilde{\Psi}(u)=\Psi(u)\cdot g(u)
for any
u\in\mathcal{G}_{0}^{1}
. But
since both maps are morphisms, we know that
\Phi^{2}\circ\widetilde{\Psi}=\Phi^{2}\circ\Psi
,
which implies that
g(u)\in K
for any
u
. But by assumption
G_{0}
is a
covering of
\underline{G}_{0}
, so
K
is discrete and hence the smooth function
g

has to be locally constant.
∎

In particular, given an AHS-pair
(G,P)
and the structure group
G_{0}
of the
corresponding AHS-structure as in Definition
1
, we have the notion of a
partial AHS-structure on a manifold of dimension
\geq n:=\dim(\mathfrak{g}_{-1})
endowed
with an involutive distribution of rank
n
. This can be described as a principal

G_{0}
-bundle
p:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M
endowed with a strictly horizontal,
G_{0}
-equivariant
section
\theta\in\Gamma(L(\widetilde{F},\mathfrak{g}_{-1}))
.

2.3.
Examples

(1) Let us first discuss how smooth families of
G_{0}
-structures fit into the picture
of partial
G_{0}
-structures. Suppose that
f:M\to N
is a surjective submersion with

\dim(N)=k
and
\dim(M)=n+k
and let
F\subset TM
be the vertical subbundle of
f
,
i.e.
F_{x}=\ker(T_{x}f:T_{x}M\to T_{f(x)}N)
. Then it is well known that for each
y\in N
, the fiber
M_{y}:=f^{-1}(\{y\})\subset M
is a smooth submanifold, so we can
naturally view
M
as a smooth family of
n
-dimensional manifolds parametrized by
the
k
-dimensional manifold
N
. Moreover, for
x\in M
with
f(x)=y
, the vertical
subspace
F_{x}
is exactly the tangent space
T_{x}M_{y}
to the fiber. Thus the
restriction
\mathcal{P}F|_{M_{y}}
can be naturally identified with the linear frame bundle
of
M_{y}
. For a partial
G_{0}
-structure
(\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M,\theta)
we can therefore view
the restriction
\mathcal{G}_{0}|_{M_{y}}
endowed with the restriction of
\theta
as a

G_{0}
-structure on
M_{y}
. Hence in this situation a partial
G_{0}
-structure of
codimension
k
can be naturally viewed as a
k
-dimensional smooth family of

G_{0}
-structures.

For a general involutive distribution
F\subset TM
, this picture is available
locally. By the Frobenius theorem there are local leaf spaces, i.e. each
x\in M

has a neighborhood
U\subset M
for which there is a surjective submersion
f:U\to N

onto a smooth manifold
N
such that
\ker(T_{z}f)=F_{z}
for any
z\in U
. The global
picture is more subtle, the best way to think about this is as a smooth family of

G_{0}
-structures on the leaves of the foliation defined by
F
. Consider such a leaf,
i.e. an injective immersion
i:L\to M
whose image is a maximal connected integral
submanifold for
F
. Given a partial
G_{0}
-structure
(\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M,\theta)
, we can
form the pullback
i^{*}\mathcal{G}_{0}\to L
and we can also define
i^{*}\theta
by the usual
formula. Now by definition, for each
x\in L
, the tangent map
T_{x}i
restricts to a
linear isomorphism
T_{x}L\to F_{x}
. Thus
i^{*}\theta
is defined on all of
T_{x}L
, so
\theta

is a strictly horizontal
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued one-form on
i^{*}\mathcal{G}_{0}\to L
, making it
into a
G_{0}
-structure in the standard sense. In this point of view, the smoothness
of the family of
G_{0}
-structures is a much more subtle condition, however. Recall
that there are well known examples of foliations that have a dense leaf, and for such
a foliation, the whole partial
G_{0}
-structure is uniquely determined by the induced
structure on that leaf.

(2) Let us discuss explicit descriptions of partial
G_{0}
-structures in the case of
the most popular AHS-structure, namely conformal structures. The relevant

|1|
-grading here has the form

\mathfrak{so}(n+1,1)=\mathbb{R}^{n}\oplus\mathfrak{co}(n)\oplus\mathbb{R}^{n*},

see Section
1.6.3 of
[
5
]
for details. Choosing an appropriate group
G
, the adjoint
action on
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
identifies the group
G_{0}
with the conformal
group
CO(n)
. Now let
M
be a smooth manifold of dimension
n+k
,
F\subset TM
an
involutive distribution of rank
n
and
\mathcal{P}F\to M
the linear frame bundle of

F
. Completely parallel to standard
G
-structures, a reduction of
\mathcal{P}F
to the
structure group
CO(n)\subset GL(n,\mathbb{R})
is equivalent to the choice of a class

[g_{x}]
of non-degenerate positive-definite bilinear forms
g_{x}
on the fibers
F_{x}

of
F
which are related to each other by multiplication by a positive number. These
have to depend smoothly on the point
x
in the sense that locally there are smooth
sections of
S^{2}F^{*}
whose values in each point lie in the chosen class. Otherwise
put, one has to prescribe a smooth ray-subbundle in
S^{2}F^{*}
. Since any such bundle
is globally trivial, there are global smooth sections, so one can equivalently define
a partial conformal structure as a equivalence class of smooth positive definite
bundle metrics on
F
, with two such metrics
g
and
\hat{g}
being equivalent if and
only if
\hat{g}=e^{2f}g
for some smooth function
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
.

Here it is also relevant to consider coverings of subgroups of
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
. Indeed, choosing
G
to be a spin group, one arrives at
G_{0}=CSpin(n)
and hence
at the concept of a partial conformal spin structure.

2.4.
Cartan description of AHS-structures

The most effective tools for the study of AHS-structures, which take the higher order
phenomena mentioned in Definition
1
into account, are derived from the
equivalent description as Cartan geometries. This makes AHS-structures into the
simplest examples of the class of parabolic geometries that is discussed in the book

[
5
]
. Indeed, as a special case of the results of existences of canonical
Cartan connections in the parabolic case, one obtains canonical Cartan connections
associated to all AHS-structures.

For an AHS-pair
(G,P)
as in Definition
1
, a Cartan geometry of type
(G,P)
on a
smooth manifold
M
of dimension
\dim(G/P)
is given by a principal
P
-bundle

p:\mathcal{G}\to M
endowed with a Cartan connection
\omega\in\Omega^{1}(\mathcal{G},\mathfrak{g})
. This
means that for each
u\in\mathcal{G}
,
\omega(u):T_{u}\mathcal{G}\to\mathfrak{g}
is a linear
isomorphism,
\omega
is
P
-equivariant, and for the fundamental vector field

\zeta_{A}\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{G})
generated by
A\in\mathfrak{p}
, one has
\omega(\zeta_{A}(u))=A
for
any
u\in\mathcal{G}
. There is an obvious associated concept of morphisms: Given

(p^{i}:\mathcal{G}^{i}\to M^{i},\omega^{i})
for
i=1,2
, a morphism is a principal bundle
homomorphism
\Phi:\mathcal{G}^{1}\to\mathcal{G}^{2}
such that
\Phi^{*}\omega^{2}=\omega^{1}
(which
immediately implies that
\Phi
is a local diffeomorphism). Similarly as in
Proposition
4
, one proves that under an assumption on infinitesimal
effectivity, which is satisfied in all cases we consider, a morphism of Cartan
geometries is determined by its base map up to a locally constant function, see
Proposition 1.5.3 of
[
5
]
.

Now it is easy to see that any such Cartan geometry has an underlying AHS-structure
corresponding to
G_{0}=P/P_{+}
. Via the principal right action,
P_{+}\subset P
acts
freely on
\mathcal{G}
and the quotient
\mathcal{G}_{0}:=\mathcal{G}/P_{+}
is easily seen to be a
principal fiber bundle over
M
with structure group
P/P_{+}=G_{0}
. Moreover,
projecting the values of
\omega
to
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p}\cong\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
one obtains an
equivariant element of
\Omega^{1}(\mathcal{G},\mathfrak{g}_{-1})
, which is easily seen to descend
to a strictly horizontal, equivariant form
\theta\in\Omega^{1}(\mathcal{G}_{0},\mathfrak{g}_{-1})
. The
underlying AHS-structure then is
(\mathcal{G}_{0},\theta)
. This construction immediately
implies that a morphism of Cartan geometries induces a morphism between the underling
AHS-structures.

The question is whether one can revert this procedure, i.e. associate to a

G_{0}
-structure canonically a Cartan geometry which has that underlying
structure. Making choices, it is rather easy to construct some Cartan geometry which
has this property, but doing this in a canonical way turns out to be more
subtle. Indeed one can trivially modify a Cartan connection
\omega
without changing
the underlying structure, so to get a canonical Cartan geometry one has to impose
some
normalization condition
on the Cartan connection. The best way to do
this is via the curvature
K\in\Omega^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathfrak{g})
of
\omega
that is defined by

(2.1)

K(\xi,\eta)=d\omega(\xi,\eta)+[\omega(\xi),\omega(\eta)]\quad\text{\ for\ }\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{G}).

So
K
measures the extent to which
\omega
fails to satisfy the Maurer-Cartan
equation. This also leads to the fundamental result that
K
vanishes if and only if

(\mathcal{G}\to M,\omega)
is locally isomorphic (as a Cartan geometry) to
G\to G/P
with
the Maurer-Cartan form of
G
as its Cartan connection. It is easy to see that
K
is

P
-equivariant and horizontal, i.e. it vanishes upon insertion of one vertical
vector field.

Via the Cartan connection
\omega
, the curvature
K
can be equivalently encoded in
the
curvature function

\kappa:\mathcal{G}\to\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\otimes\mathfrak{g}
which is
characterized by
K(u)(\xi,\eta)=\kappa(u)(\omega(u)(\xi),\omega(u)(\eta))
. The properties of

K
imply that
\kappa
is a
P
-equivariant function and actually has values in

\Lambda^{2}(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p})^{*}\otimes\mathfrak{g}
. Via the Killing form
(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p})^{*}\cong\mathfrak{g}_{1}
, so we can also view the values of
\kappa
as lying in
\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}_{1}\otimes\mathfrak{g}
. Now
\mathfrak{g}_{1}\subset\mathfrak{g}
is an abelian Lie subalgebra which
acts on
\mathfrak{g}
by the restriction of the adjoint action, so there is a Lie algebra
homology differential, which for historical reasons is usually called the

Kostant codifferential
and denoted by
\partial^{*}:\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}_{1}\otimes\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}_{1}\otimes\mathfrak{g}
. The construction implies that
\partial^{*}
is

P
-equivariant, the explicit formula is stated in formula (
2.3
) below. A
Cartan geometry of type
(G,P)
is called
normal
if and only if

\partial^{*}\circ\kappa=0
. Now we can state the fundamental theorem on existence and
uniqueness of canonical Cartan connections for AHS-structures, which is proved in
Section 3.1 of
[
5
]
in the more general setting of parabolic geometries:

Theorem 5
.

Consider an AHS-pair
(G,P)
as in Definition
1
and let
G_{0}\subset P

be the structure group of the corresponding AHS-structure. For any
G_{0}
-structure

(\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M,\theta)
, there is a normal Cartan geometry
(p:\mathcal{G}\to M,\omega)
with
this underlying
G_{0}
-structure, so in particular
p
factorizes through

\mathcal{G}_{0}
. Moreover, if
(p^{i}:\mathcal{G}^{i}\to M^{i})
are normal Cartan geometries for

i=1,2
and
\Phi:\mathcal{G}^{1}_{0}\to\mathcal{G}^{2}_{0}
is a morphism of the underlying

G_{0}
-structures, then there is a unique lift
\tilde{\Phi}:\mathcal{G}^{1}\to\mathcal{G}^{2}
of

\Phi
which is a morphism of Cartan geometries. In particular, two normal Cartan
geometries inducing the same underlying
G_{0}
-structure
(\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M,\theta)
are
isomorphic via an isomorphism that induces the identity on
\mathcal{G}_{0}
.

It is this Cartan description that we want to extend to partial
G_{0}
-structures.

2.5.
Partial Cartan geometries

Returning to the setting of §
2.2
, consider a manifold
M
of dimension
k+n

endowed with an involutive distribution
F\subset TM
of rank
n
, a Lie group
G

and a closed subgroup
P\subset G
such that
\dim(G/P)=n
. Then for a principal
fiber bundle
p:\mathcal{G}\to M
with structure group
P
we have the subbundle

\widetilde{F}\subset T\mathcal{G}
from §
2.2
, so
\xi\in\widetilde{F}_{u}
if and only
if
T_{u}p(\xi)\in F_{p(u)}\subset T_{p(u)}M
. We have already observed there that
this subbundle is preserved by the principal right action, i.e. for any
g\in P
and

u\in\mathcal{G}
, we have
T_{u}r^{g}(\widetilde{F}_{u})=\widetilde{F}_{u\cdot g}
. Consequently,
for any partially defined one form
\tau\in\Gamma(L(\widetilde{F},V))
with values in a
vector space
V
and
g\in P
, we can form
(r^{g})^{*}\tau\in\Gamma(L(\widetilde{F},V))
. Having made these observations, there is an obvious partial analog of the
definition of Cartan geometries:

Definition 6
.

A
partial Cartan geometry
of type
(G,P)
on
(M,F)
is given by a
principal fiber bundle
p:\mathcal{G}\to M
with structure group
P
and a

partial Cartan connection

\omega\in\Gamma(L(\widetilde{F},\mathfrak{g}))
such
that

(i)

For any
g\in P
, we have
(r^{g})^{*}\omega=\operatorname{Ad}(g^{-1})\circ\omega
.

(ii)

For the fundamental vector field
\zeta_{A}\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{G})
generated by

A\in\mathfrak{p}
, we get
\omega(\zeta_{A})=A
.

(iii)

For any point
u\in\mathcal{G}
,
\omega(u):\widetilde{F}_{u}\to\mathfrak{g}
is a
linear isomorphism.

There also is a clear way how to define a morphism between two partial Cartan
geometries
(p^{i}:\mathcal{G}^{i}\to M^{i},\omega^{i})
of type
(G,P)
on
(M^{i},F^{i})
for

i=1,2
. Let
\Phi:\mathcal{G}^{1}\to\mathcal{G}^{2}
be a morphism of principal fiber bundles,
whose base map
\varphi
has the property that for any
x\in M^{1}
we get

T_{x}\varphi(F^{1}_{x})\subset F^{2}_{\varphi(x)}
. Then for any
u\in\mathcal{G}^{1}
we get

T_{u}\Phi(\widetilde{F}^{1}_{u})\subset\widetilde{F}^{2}_{\Phi(u)}
and hence we can form

\Phi^{*}\omega^{2}\in\Gamma(L(\widetilde{F}^{1},\mathfrak{g}))
. We call
\Phi
a
morphism
of
partial Cartan geometries if this condition on the base map
\varphi
is satisfied and

\Phi^{*}\omega^{2}=\omega^{1}
. Observe that this implies that for any
u\in\mathcal{G}
, the tangent
map
T_{u}\Phi
restricts to a linear isomorphism

\widetilde{F}^{1}_{u}\to\widetilde{F}^{2}_{\Phi(u)}
, but it does not even force
M^{1}
and

M^{2}
to have the same dimension. As before, we recover the usual concepts of Cartan
geometries and their morphisms in the case that
\dim(M)=n
and
F=TM
.

The general ideas about underlying
G_{0}
-structures of Cartan geometries extend to
the setting of partial structures introduced here. Given a pair
(G,P)
, we can
consider the natural representation
\operatorname{\underline{Ad}}:P\to GL(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p})
of
P
on
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p}
induced by the restriction of the adjoint representation of
G
. Given a
partial Cartan geometry
(p:\mathcal{G}\to M,\omega)
of type
(G,P)
on
(M,F)
we can thus
form the associated bundle
\mathcal{G}\times_{P}(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p})
. The defining properties of
the partial Cartan connection then readily imply that the map
\mathcal{G}\times(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p})\to F
that sends
(u,A+\mathfrak{p})
to
T_{u}p(\omega(u)^{-1}(A))\in F_{p(u)}

induces an isomorphism
\mathcal{G}\times_{P}(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p})\cong F
.

Under weak assumptions, this can be recast in the setting of §
2.2
. We just do
this in the setting of AHS-structures and remark on generalizations. So we take and
AHS-pair
(G,P)
as in Definition
1
and the normal subgroup
P_{+}\subset P

such that
P/P_{+}\cong G_{0}
. For a partial Cartan geometry
(p:\mathcal{G}\to M,\omega)
on

(M,F)
, we can then form
\mathcal{G}/P_{+}
which is a principal bundle over
M
with
structure group
P/P_{+}\cong G_{0}
. We denote by
p_{0}:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M
the corresponding
bundle projection.

Proposition 7
.

Consider a partial Cartan geometry
(p:\mathcal{G}\to M,\omega)
of type
(G,P)
over
(M,F)

and the underlying bundle
p_{0}:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M
from above and let
\widetilde{F}\subset T\mathcal{G}
and
\widehat{F}\subset T\mathcal{G}_{0}
be the distributions determined by

F\subset TM
. Then
\omega
descends to a strictly horizontal section

\theta\in\Gamma(L(\widehat{F},\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p}))
which makes the bundle
\mathcal{G}_{0}
into a
partial
G_{0}
-structure over
(M,F)
. This construction is functorial,
i.e. morphisms of partial Cartan geometries descend to morphisms of partial

G_{0}
-structures.

Proof.

Projecting the values of
\omega
to
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p}
, we obtain a smooth section

\tilde{\theta}
of the bundle
L(\widetilde{F},\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p})\to\mathcal{G}
which by
construction is
P
-equivariant and vanishes on the vertical subbundle
V\mathcal{G}=\ker(Tp)
. It is a classical result that the natural projection
q:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}_{0}
is a principal fiber bundle with structure group
P_{+}
. Since for
Z\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}
, the map
\operatorname{ad}(Z)
maps
\mathfrak{g}
to
\mathfrak{p}
, we conclude that

\exp(Z)\subset\ker(\operatorname{\underline{Ad}})
, so
P_{+}
is contained in
\ker(\operatorname{\underline{Ad}})
. Hence
\tilde{\theta}

is
P_{+}
invariant and of course it is horizontal with respect to the projection to

\mathcal{G}_{0}
. Moreover
T_{q}
maps
\widetilde{F}
onto
\widehat{F}
, so we conclude that

\tilde{\theta}
descends to a smooth section
\theta
of
L(\widehat{F},\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p})\to G_{0}
.

The
P
-action on
\widetilde{F}
descends to the natural action of
P/P_{+}=G_{0}
on

\widehat{F}
and the representation
\operatorname{\underline{Ad}}
also descends to
P/P_{+}
, so

P
-equivariancy of
\tilde{\theta}
implies that
\theta
is
G_{0}
-equivariant. On the other
hand, for
u\in\mathcal{G}
,
\tilde{\theta}(u)
is surjective and vanishes on the vertical
subspace
V_{u}\mathcal{G}
of
p:\mathcal{G}\to M
. This readily implies that
\theta
is strictly
horizontal and hence makes
p_{0}:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M
into a partial
G_{0}
-structure by
Proposition
4
.

Concerning functoriality, it is clear that a principal bundle morphism
\Phi:\mathcal{G}^{1}\to\mathcal{G}^{2}
descends to a principal bundle morphism
\underline{\Phi}:\mathcal{G}^{1}/P_{+}\to\mathcal{G}^{2}/P_{+}
with the same base map. If
\Phi^{*}\omega^{2}=\omega^{1}
then the
construction above immediately implies that
\underline{\Phi}^{*}\theta^{2}=\theta^{1}
, so this
is a morphism of partial
G_{0}
-structures.
∎

Remark 8
.

It is a special feature of the case of AHS-structures that there is a preferred
subgroup
G_{0}
which can serve as the structure group of the underlying
structure. It follows from the structure theory that in the AHS-case the image

\operatorname{\underline{Ad}}(P)\subset GL(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p})
is a closed subgroup
\underline{G}_{0}
and
that
\operatorname{\underline{Ad}}
descends to a finite covering
G_{0}\to\underline{G_{0}}
.

For general pairs
(G,P)
, one has to assume that
G_{0}:=\operatorname{\underline{Ad}}(P)\subset GL(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p})
is a closed subgroup and define
K:=\ker(\operatorname{\underline{Ad}})
. Then one
defines
\mathcal{G}_{0}:=\mathcal{G}/K
and proves similarly as in the proof of Proposition

7
that one can descend
\omega
to a partially defined one-form with values
in
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p}
that makes
\mathcal{G}_{0}
into a partial
G_{0}
-structure. Of course,
functoriality of the construction of the underlying
G_{0}
-structure continues to hold
in this case.

2.6.
Curvature and normality

Let
M
be a smooth manifold of dimension
n+k
,
F\subset TM
an involutive
distribution of rank
n
, and
V
a finite dimensional vector space. Then we refer to
sections of the bundle
L(\Lambda^{r}F,V)
as partial
V
-valued
r
-forms and denote the
space of such forms as
\Omega^{r}_{F}(M,V)
. In this language, the form
\theta
from
Proposition
4
and a Cartan connection are partial
1
-forms with values
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and in
\mathfrak{g}
, respectively.

Now we first observe that involutivity of
F
implies that there is a well-defined
exterior derivative
d:\Omega^{r}_{F}(M,V)\to\Omega^{r+1}_{F}(M,V)
defined by the usual global
formula. Alternatively, one can extend
\alpha\in\Omega^{r}_{F}(M,V)
to a
V
-valued
r
-form

\tilde{\alpha}
and then observe that the restriction of
d\tilde{\alpha}
to entries from
F

depends only on
\alpha
and not on the chosen extension. Second, we observe that for
any principal bundle
p:\mathcal{G}\to M
over
M
, the lifted distribution
\widetilde{F}\subset T\mathcal{G}
admits local frames that consist of projectable vector fields,
which implies that
\widetilde{F}
is involutive, too. Hence the concept of partial

V
-valued forms makes sense on the total space of
\mathcal{G}
and we also have an
exterior derivative for these forms.

Definition 9
.

Let
(p:\mathcal{G}\to M,\omega)
be a partial Cartan geometry of type
(G,P)
on

(M,F)
. The
curvature

K\in\Omega^{2}_{\widetilde{F}}(\mathcal{G},\mathfrak{g})
of
\omega
is defined
by

K(u)(X,Y)=d\omega(u)(X,Y)+[\omega(u)(X),\omega(u)(Y)]

for
u\in\mathcal{G}
and
X,Y\in T_{u}\mathcal{G}
.

From this definition, it follows exactly as for usual Cartan geometries that
K
is

P
-equivariant, i.e.
(r^{g})^{*}K=\operatorname{Ad}(g^{-1})\circ K
for any
g\in P
, and horizontal,
i.e. it vanishes upon insertion of one tangent vector which is vertical for
p:\mathcal{G}\to M
. Thus one can equivalently encode
K
via the
curvature function

\kappa:\mathcal{G}\to L(\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{g})
, which is characterized by

(2.2)

\kappa(u)(X+\mathfrak{p},Y+\mathfrak{p})=K(\omega(u)^{-1}(X),\omega(u)^{-1}(Y)).

This is well defined since
K
is horizontal and it is
P
-equivariant by
construction. Observe that we can project the values of
\kappa(u)
to
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p}

to obtain a function
\tau:\mathcal{G}\to L(\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p})
. This
is also
P
-equivariant and hence defines an element of
\Omega^{2}_{F}(M,F)
, which is
called the
torsion
of the partial Cartan geometry
(p:\mathcal{G}\to M,\omega)
.

For the case of AHS-structures, this readily implies that the standard notion of
normality continues to make sense in our setting. There is a linear map

\partial^{*}:L(\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{g})\to L(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p})
which is defined
using the fact that
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
are dual representations of
P

via the Killing form of
\mathfrak{g}
, see
[
7
]
. For
\varphi\in L(\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{g})
and
X\in\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p}
one defines

(2.3)

(\partial^{*}\varphi)(X):=\sum_{i}[Z^{i},\varphi(X_{i},X)],

where the
Z^{i}
form a basis for
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
and the
X_{i}
are the elements of the
dual basis of
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p}
.

Definition 10
.

Let
(G,P)
be an AHS-pair as in Definition
1
. Then a partial Cartan
geometry
(p:\mathcal{G}\to M,\omega)
of type
(G,P)
is called
normal
if it
curvature function
\kappa
has the property that
\partial^{*}\circ\kappa=0
.

The reason why
\partial^{*}
gives rise to a good normalization condition are its
algebraic properties. First, since
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
are dual
representations of
P
, the definition readily implies that
\partial^{*}
is

P
-equivariant. In particular, its kernel is a
P
-invariant subspace of

L(\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{g})
. Second, the definition immediately implies that if

\varphi
has values in
\mathfrak{g}_{i}\subset\mathfrak{g}
, then
\partial^{*}\varphi
has values in

\mathfrak{g}_{i+1}
. (This property is most naturally phrased as
\partial^{*}
preserving
homogeneities, but we will not use this concept here.)

On the other hand, we can identify
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p}
as a vector space with the
abelian Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\subset\mathfrak{g}
. Consequently, there is a Lie
algebra cohomology differential
\partial:L(\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g})\to L(\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g})
. Since
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
is abelian, the formula for this differential
is just
\partial\psi(X,Y)=[X,\psi(Y)]-[Y,\psi(X)]
. Observe that if
\psi
has values in

\mathfrak{g}_{i}
, then
\partial\psi
has values in
\mathfrak{g}_{i-1}
. In particular, we get
a restriction
\partial:L(\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g}_{0})\to L(\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g}_{-1})
. Viewing
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
as a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g}_{-1})
via
the adjoint action this is exactly the Spencer differential, which plays a crucial
role in the theory of
G_{0}
-structures.

The relevant algebraic properties then follow from the fact that
\partial
and

\partial^{*}
are adjoint with respect to an appropriate inner product, see

[
14
]
or Section 3.3 of
[
5
]
for a much more general version. In
addition, one needs information on the Lie algebra cohomology of
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
with
coefficients in
\mathfrak{g}
, which are obtained as a very special case of Kostant’s
version of the Bott-Borel-Weil theorem from
[
12
]
, see Proposition 3.3.7 of

[
5
]
(taking into account that we excluded some pairs according to Definition

1
). We will need the following facts that can be found in these
references.

Proposition 11
.

Let
(G,P)
be an AHS-pair as in Definition
1
with Lie algebras

\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathfrak{g}
.

(1) For any
i=-1,0,1
,
\ker(\partial^{*})
and
\operatorname{im}(\partial)
are complementary
subspaces of
L(\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{g}_{i})
.

(2) If
\psi\in L(\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g}_{0})
satisfies
\partial\psi=0
, then there is
a unique element
Z\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}
such that
\psi(X)=[Z,X]
for any
X\in\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
.

(3)
\partial:L(\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g}_{1})\to L(\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g}_{0})
is
injective.

2.7.
Existence of normal partial Cartan geometries

In order to prove that normal partial Cartan geometries are an equivalent description
of partial AHS-structures, we first prove that any partial AHS-structure is induced
by some normal Cartan geometry.

Theorem 12
.

Let
(G,P)
be an AHS-pair as in Definition
1
,
G_{0}\subset P
the
structure group of the corresponding AHS-structure, and consider a partial

G_{0}
-structure
(p_{0}:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M,\theta)
over
(M,F)
. Then there is a normal
partial Cartan geometry
(p:\mathcal{G}\to M,\omega)
, which has
(p_{0}:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M,\theta)
as
its underlying
G_{0}
-structure.

Proof.

We use a construction similar to the one in
[
7
]
respectively the one in

[
4
]
(in the special case we consider here). We want to point out in
particular that all the technical verifications we need are the same for partial
Cartan geometries as for usual Cartan geometries, so we keep them rather short.

Step 1: Construct the space
\mathcal{G}
: For a point
u_{0}\in\mathcal{G}_{0}
, we
consider
\theta(u_{0}):\widetilde{F}_{u_{0}}\to\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
and linear maps

\varphi:\widetilde{F}_{u_{0}}\to\mathfrak{g}_{0}
, which extend
\theta(u_{0})
to a linear map

\widetilde{F}_{u_{0}}\to\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}
requiring two more
conditions. First, we require that for the fundamental vector field
\zeta_{A}
generated
by
A\in\mathfrak{g}_{0}
, we get
\varphi(\zeta_{A}(u_{0}))=A
. This in particular implies that

\theta(u_{0})\oplus\varphi
is surjective and hence defines a linear isomorphism

\widetilde{F}_{u_{0}}\to\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}
. Second, for
\xi,\eta\in\widetilde{F}_{u_{0}}\mathcal{G}_{0}
, we consider

(2.4)

d\theta(u_{0})(\xi,\eta)+\varphi(\xi)(\theta(u_{0})(\eta))-\varphi(\eta)(\theta(u_{0})(\xi)).

If
\xi=\zeta_{A}(u_{0})
for some
A\in\mathfrak{g}_{0}
, then this reduces to

d\theta(u_{0})(\zeta_{A},\eta)+[A,\theta(u_{0})(\eta)]
. This coincides with

(\mathcal{L}_{\zeta_{A}}\theta-\operatorname{ad}(A)\circ\theta)(u_{0})(\eta)
and hence vanishes by equivariancy of

\theta
. Thus there is a map
\tau_{\varphi}:\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\to\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
such that the
expression in (
2.4
) equals
\tau_{\varphi}(\theta(u_{0})(\xi),\theta(u_{0})(\eta))
, and the
second condition we require is that
\partial^{*}(\tau_{\varphi})=0
. For fixed
u_{0}
, we
can view the isomorphisms
\theta(u_{0})\oplus\varphi
for which both conditions are satisfied
as an affine subspace of the fiber of the vector bundle

L(\widetilde{F},\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0})\to\mathcal{G}_{0}
over
u_{0}
. The union of
all these subspaces is easily seen to define a smooth submanifold of this total
space, which provides us which projections
q:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}_{0}
and
p:\mathcal{G}\to M
. In what follows we will denote a typical element of
\mathcal{G}
as

u=\theta(u_{0})\oplus\varphi
.

Step 2: Construct a
P
-action on
\mathcal{G}
: We can construct a natural right
action of
P
on
L(\widetilde{F},\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0})
. On the one hand, we
get a natural representation of
P
on
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}
by identifying
this space with
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}_{1}
and observing that
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
is invariant under
the adjoint action of
P
. With a slight abuse of notation we also denote this
representation by
\operatorname{\underline{Ad}}
. On the other hand, as noted in §
2.1
,
P/P_{+}\cong G_{0}
and for
g\in P
, we put
g_{0}:=gP_{+}\in G_{0}
. This justifies our abuse of notation
since viewing
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
as
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p}
, for
g\in P
, the map
\operatorname{\underline{Ad}}(g)

descends to
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
and since
P_{+}
acts trivially on
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p}
, it
coincides with
\operatorname{\underline{Ad}}(g_{0})
there.

Now for a linear map
\psi:\widetilde{F}_{u_{0}}\to\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}
, we define

\psi\cdot g:\widetilde{F}_{u_{0}\cdot g_{0}}\to\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}
by

(2.5)

\psi\cdot g:=\operatorname{\underline{Ad}}(g^{-1})\circ\psi\circ Tr^{g_{0}^{-1}}.

Since
\widetilde{F}
is invariant under the principal right action, this clearly
defines a right action and we claim that the subset
\mathcal{G}
is
P
-invariant. First
observe that equivariancy of
\theta
reads as
\theta\circ Tr^{g_{0}^{-1}}=\operatorname{\underline{Ad}}(g_{0})\circ\theta
. Together with our above observation, this readily implies that for

u=\theta(u_{0})\oplus\varphi\in\mathcal{G}
the
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
-component of
u\cdot g
coincides
with
\theta(u_{0}\cdot g_{0})
. For fundamental vector fields, one has the equivariancy
property
Tr^{g^{-1}_{0}}(\zeta_{A}(u_{0}\cdot g_{0}))=\zeta_{\operatorname{Ad}(g_{0})(A)}(u_{0})
. Since
P_{+}

acts trivially on
\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{g}_{1}
, we conclude as before that on

\{0\}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}
, the map
\operatorname{\underline{Ad}}(g^{-1})
coincides with
\operatorname{Ad}(g_{0}^{-1})
and
hence
u\cdot g
reproduces the generators of fundamental vector fields.

To verify the last property for
u\cdot g
, recall from §
2.1
that
P
is the
semi-direct product of
G_{0}
and
P_{+}
, so we can write
g=g_{0}\exp(Z)
for a unique
element
Z\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}
, see also Theorem 3.1.3 of
[
5
]
. Hence

\operatorname{\underline{Ad}}(g^{-1})=\operatorname{\underline{Ad}}(\exp(-Z))\circ\operatorname{\underline{Ad}}(g_{0}^{-1})
and since
\operatorname{ad}(Z)^{2}=0
we conclude that
for
u=\theta(u_{0})\oplus\varphi\in\mathcal{G}
, the
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-component of
u\cdot g
maps

\xi\in\widetilde{F}_{u_{0}\cdot g_{0}}
to

\operatorname{Ad}(g_{0}^{-1})(\varphi(Tr^{g_{0}^{-1}}(\xi)))-[Z,\operatorname{Ad}(g_{0}^{-1})(\theta(u_{0})(Tr^{g_{0}^{-1}}(\xi)))].

Now if we consider (
2.4
) for
u\cdot g
, then the terms involving
Z
do not
give any contribution, since for
X,Y\in\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
, we get

0=[Z,[X,Y]]=[[Z,X],Y]+[X,[Z,Y]]
. Using this and that equivariancy of
\theta
implies
equivariancy of
d\theta
, a short computation shows that
\tau_{u\cdot g}=g^{-1}_{0}\cdot\tau_{u}
, where the dot denotes the canonical action of
G_{0}
on
L(\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g}_{-1})
induced by
\operatorname{\underline{Ad}}
. But then equivariancy of
\partial^{*}

implies that
\partial^{*}(\tau_{u\cdot g})=0
, which completes the proof that
\mathcal{G}

is invariant under the
P
-action we have constructed.

Step 3: Show that
\mathcal{G}\to M
is a principal
P
-bundle
: We next show that
the
P
-action on
\mathcal{G}
is free and transitive on the fibers of
p:\mathcal{G}\to M
. To prove freeness, assume that
u\cdot g=u
for some
u\in\mathcal{G}
. Then of course
we must have
g_{0}=e
, so
g=\exp(Z)
for some
Z\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}
and
\operatorname{ad}(-Z)
has to
vanish on
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
. But it is well known that
[Z,\mathfrak{g}_{-1}]=\{0\}
implies

Z=0
, see Proposition 3.1.2 of
[
5
]
. Next, we know that
G_{0}
acts
transitively on the fibers of
\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M
, so we can prove transitivity of the
action on the fibers by showing that
P
acts transitively on the fibers of
q:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}_{0}
. If
q(u)=q(\tilde{u})=u_{0}
, we see that
\tilde{u}-u
has to have
values in
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
and has to vanish on vertical vector fields. Hence there is a
linear map
f:\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\to\mathfrak{g}_{0}
such that
\tilde{u}(\xi)=u(\xi)+f(\theta(u_{0})(\xi))
for each
\xi\in T_{u_{0}}\mathcal{G}_{0}
. But looking at
equation (
2.4
) we readily see that
\tau_{\tilde{u}}=\tau_{u}+\partial f
. Since

u,\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{G}
, this implies that
\partial^{*}\partial f=0
. By part (1) of
Proposition
11
, this implies
\partial f=0
which by part (2) of that
Proposition implies that
f=\operatorname{ad}(Z)
for some
Z\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. Hence we obtain
\tilde{u}=u\cdot\exp(-Z)
and transitivity of the action on the fibers is proved.

To prove that
p:\mathcal{G}\to M
is a
P
-principal bundle, it thus remains to construct
local smooth sections of
L(\widetilde{F},\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0})
which have
values in
\mathcal{G}
. Indeed, if
\sigma:U\to\mathcal{G}
is such a section, then
(x,g)\mapsto\sigma(x)\cdot g
defines a diffeomorphism
U\times P\to p^{-1}(U)
, whose inverse is a
principal bundle chart. To do this, we choose a principal connection
\gamma
on
\mathcal{G}_{0}
and a local smooth section
\sigma_{0}:U\to\mathcal{G}_{0}
of
p_{0}:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M
. Then

\hat{\sigma}(x):=\theta(\sigma_{0}(x))\oplus\gamma(\sigma_{0}(x))
is an element of

L(\widetilde{F},\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0})
over
\sigma_{0}(x)\in\mathcal{G}_{0}
. Plugging
this into (
2.4
), we conclude that
x\mapsto\tau_{\hat{\sigma}(x)}
defines a
smooth map
U\to L(\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g}_{-1})
. By part (1) of Proposition

11
, the target space splits as

\ker(\partial^{*})\oplus\operatorname{im}(\partial)
. Choosing left inverse of the surjective linear
map
\partial:L(\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g}_{0})\to\operatorname{im}(\partial)
we obtain a smooth map

\psi:U\to L(\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g}_{0})
such that

\tau_{\hat{\sigma}(x)}-\partial(\psi(x))\in\ker(\partial^{*})
for any
x\in U
. But
(
2.4
) immediately implies that this equals
\tau_{\sigma(x)}
, where

\sigma(x):=\theta(\sigma_{0}(x))\oplus\left(\gamma(\sigma_{0}(x))-\psi(x)\circ\theta(\sigma_{0}(x))\right).

Since this is evidently smooth, we have constructed a local smooth section of
\mathcal{G}
as required.

Step 4: Construct a normal Cartan connection
: From our construction of the
principal bundle
p:\mathcal{G}\to M
, we conclude that there is a canonical section

\hat{\theta}=\hat{\theta}_{-1}\oplus\hat{\theta}_{0}\in\Gamma(L(\widetilde{F},\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0})\to\mathcal{G}):

Since a point
u\in\mathcal{G}
with
q(u)=u_{0}\in\mathcal{G}_{0}
is a linear isomorphism

T_{u_{0}}\mathcal{G}_{0}\supset\widetilde{F}_{u_{0}}\to\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}
we can define

\hat{\theta}(u)(\xi):=u(T_{u}q(\xi))
for
\xi\in T_{u}\mathcal{G}
. This is obviously smooth and
the definition of the
P
-action on
\mathcal{G}
is exactly chosen in such a way that

(r^{g})^{*}\hat{\theta}=\operatorname{\underline{Ad}}(g^{-1})\circ\hat{\theta}
so
\hat{\theta}
is equivariant for the

P
-representation
\operatorname{\underline{Ad}}
on
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}\cong\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}_{1}
.

The final step now is to extend, for a fixed point
u\in\mathcal{G}
, the map
\hat{\theta}(u)

to a linear isomorphism
T_{u}\mathcal{G}\to\mathfrak{g}
in such a way that the normalization
condition is satisfied. So given
u
, we want to define
\alpha:T_{u}\mathcal{G}\supset\widetilde{F}_{u}\to\mathfrak{g}_{1}
such that
\alpha(\zeta_{A}(u))=0
for
A\in\mathfrak{g}_{0}
,
and
\alpha(\zeta_{Z}(u))=Z
for
Z\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. In addition, similarly to (
2.4
), we
consider

(2.6)

d\hat{\theta}_{0}(u)(\xi,\eta)+[\hat{\theta}_{0}(u)(\xi),\hat{\theta}_{0}(u)(\eta)]+[\alpha(\xi),\hat{\theta}_{-1}(u)(\eta)]+[\hat{\theta}_{-1}(u)(\xi),\alpha(\eta)]\in\mathfrak{g}_{0}

for
\xi,\eta\in T_{u}\mathcal{G}
. Inserting
\xi=\zeta_{B}(u)
for
B\in\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{1}
, we see that this reduces to the
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-component of
(\mathcal{L}_{\zeta_{B}}\hat{\theta}+\operatorname{\underline{ad}}(B)\circ\hat{\theta})(\eta)
, which vanishes by equivariancy of

\hat{\theta}
, As before, this shows that there is a map
\rho_{\alpha}\in L(\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g}_{0})
such that (
2.6
) equals

\rho_{\alpha}(\hat{\theta}_{-1}(u)(\xi),\hat{\theta}_{-1}(u)(\eta))
. We claim, there there is a
unique map
\alpha
as above for which
\partial^{*}\rho_{\alpha}=0
. Indeed, by part (1) of
Proposition
11
,
L(\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g}_{0})=\ker(\partial^{*})\oplus\operatorname{im}(\partial)
, so there is a linear map
\psi:\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\to\mathfrak{g}_{1}
such that
\partial^{*}(\rho_{\alpha}+\partial\psi)=0
. As before,

\rho_{\alpha}+\partial Z=\rho_{\beta}
where
\beta(\xi)=\alpha(\xi)+\psi(\hat{\theta}_{-1}(\xi))
,
which proves existence.

To prove uniqueness, we observe that two maps
\alpha
and
\beta
compatible with
fundamental vector fields are related by
\beta(\xi)=\alpha(\xi)+\psi(\theta_{-1}(u)(\xi))

for a linear map
\psi:\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\to\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. Then

\rho_{\beta}=\rho_{\alpha}+\partial\psi
and if

\partial^{*}\rho_{\alpha}=\partial^{*}\rho_{\beta}=0
, we conclude that

\partial^{*}\partial\psi=0
. By part (1) of Proposition
11
this implies

\partial\psi=0
, which by part (3) of the same Proposition implies

\psi=0
. Defining
\omega_{i}=\hat{\theta}_{i}
for
i=-1,0
and letting

\omega_{1}(u):\widetilde{F}_{u}\to\mathfrak{g}_{1}
be the map we just constructed, we obtain a
linear map
\omega(u):\widetilde{F}_{u}\to\mathfrak{g}
which is surjective and hence a linear
isomorphism by construction. It also follows by construction that
\omega(u)
reproduces
the generators of fundamental vector fields.

Now for
g\in P
consider
\operatorname{Ad}(g)\circ\omega(u\cdot g)\circ T_{u}r^{g}:\widetilde{F}_{u}\to\mathfrak{g}
. By equivariancy of
\hat{\theta}
, its component in
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}

coincides with
\hat{\theta}(u)
and equivariancy of fundamental vector fields implies
that it reproduces their generators. Similarly as above, one computes that the
associated map
\rho
is related to the one for
\omega(u)
by the adjoint action of

g_{0}=gP_{+}
and hence lies in the kernel of
\partial^{*}
. Thus we conclude that

\operatorname{Ad}(g)\circ\omega(u\cdot g)\circ T_{u}r^{g}=\omega(u)
and hence
\omega
is
P
-equivariant. But we
also conclude that to deduce smoothness of
\omega
it suffices to deduce smoothness
along the images of local sections of
\mathcal{G}\to M
. But along such sections the
construction above can be carried out depending on a point starting from a principal
connection on
\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}_{0}
. This implies smooth dependence as above, so we
have verified that
\omega\in\Gamma(L(\widetilde{F},\mathfrak{g}))
is a partial Cartan
connection.

By construction the component
\omega_{-1}
coincides with the pullback of

\hat{\theta}_{-1}
and hence with the pullback of
\theta
, while
\omega_{0}
coincides with the
pullback of
\hat{\theta}_{0}
. On the one hand, this implies that the partial
\mathcal{G}_{0}
-structure underlying
(p:\mathcal{G}\to M,\omega)
is
(p_{0}:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M,\theta)
. Plugging into the defining equation of curvature and using compatibility of
the exterior derivative with pullbacks, we deduce information on the components

\kappa_{i}
of the curvature function
\kappa
, which have values in
L(\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g}_{i})
. Namely,
\kappa_{-1}
is the pullback of (
2.4
), while
\kappa_{0}

is the pullback of (
2.6
), so both these components have values in

\ker(\partial^{*})
. But the whole subspace
L(\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g}_{1})
is
contained in
\ker(\partial^{*})
, we have proved that
\omega
is normal and this
completes the proof.

Remark 13
.

(1) The construction in the proof of Theorem
12
is easily seen to be
functorial and hence together with the construction of the underlying partial

G_{0}
-structure defines an equivalence of categories. We will not need this result,
however, since we will prove a stronger uniqueness result below, so we just sketch
the argument.

Suppose that we have given a morphism
\Phi
between partial
G_{0}
-structures

(\mathcal{G}_{0}^{i}\to M^{i},F^{i},\theta^{i})
for
i=1,2
. Then for any
u_{0}\in\mathcal{G}_{0}^{1}
,

T_{u_{0}}\Phi
restricts to a linear isomorphism

\widetilde{F}^{1}_{u_{0}}\to\widetilde{F}^{2}_{\Phi(u_{u})}
. Construct

(\mathcal{G}^{i}\to M^{i},\omega^{i})
for
i=1,2
as in the proof, so a point
u\in\mathcal{G}^{1}

over
u_{0}\in\mathcal{G}^{1}_{0}
is a linear isomorphism

\widetilde{F}^{1}_{u_{0}}\to\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}
. This can be composed with

(T_{u_{0}}\Phi|_{\widetilde{F}^{1}_{u_{0}}})^{-1}
to obtain a linear isomorphism defined
on
\widetilde{F}^{2}_{\Phi(u_{0})}
. Using that
\Phi^{*}\theta^{2}=\theta^{1}
and naturality of
the construction, one verifies that this isomorphism actually lies in
\mathcal{G}^{2}
,
let’s call it
\tilde{\Phi}(u)
.

Hence one obtains a lift
\tilde{\Phi}:\mathcal{G}^{1}\to\mathcal{G}^{2}
of
\Phi
, which by
construction has the property that
\tilde{\Phi}^{*}\hat{\theta}^{2}=\hat{\theta}^{1}
. The
construction also implies that
\tilde{\Phi}
is
P
-equivariant and thus a morphism
of principal bundles. This also implies that for any
u\in\mathcal{G}^{1}
, the tangent
map
T_{u}\tilde{\Phi}
restricts to a linear isomorphism

\widetilde{F}^{1}_{u}\to\widetilde{F}^{2}_{\tilde{\Phi}(u)}
. This implies that

\omega^{2}(\tilde{\Phi}(u))\circ T_{u}\tilde{\Phi}
defines a linear isomorphism

\widetilde{F}^{1}_{u}\to\mathfrak{g}
whose component in
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}

coincides with
\tilde{\Phi}^{*}\hat{\theta}^{2}=\hat{\theta}^{1}(u)
by construction. Naturality of
the normalization condition then implies that
\omega^{2}(\tilde{\Phi}(u))\circ T_{u}\tilde{\Phi}

satisfies the condition that characterizes
\omega^{1}(u)
, and this implies that

\tilde{\Phi}
is a morphism of partial Cartan geometries.

(2) There is a conceptual interpretation of the construction parallel to the case
of usual AHS-structures. The linear maps
T_{u_{9}}\mathcal{G}_{0}\to\mathfrak{g}_{0}
that occur
in the first step can be viewed a values of partial principal connection forms in

u_{0}
. There is a correspondence between partial principal connections and partial
linear connections on
F
, i.e. operators
\Gamma(F)\times\Gamma(F)\to\Gamma(F)
which
satisfy properties analogous to the defining properties of a linear
connection. Involutivity of
F
implies that such a partial linear connection has a
well defined torsion, which is a section of
L(\Lambda^{2}F,F)
. The map
\partial^{*}

induces a bundle map
L(\Lambda^{2}F,F)\to L(F,L(F,F))
which is analogous to a
normalization condition on the torsion in the theory of
G_{0}
-structures. In the
picture of partial principal connections, this torsion is induced by equation
(
2.4
) and so the condition
\partial^{*}\circ\tau_{\varphi}=0
singles out the values
of partial principal connections with normal torsion. So one can view the bundle

\mathcal{G}
as obtained from attaching to each point
u_{0}\in\mathcal{G}_{0}
the values of
the normal partial (principal) connections compatible with the partial

G_{0}
-structure in
u_{0}
. The pullback of a normal compatible partial connection
along a morphism of partial
G_{0}
-structures is a normal compatible partial
connection, so we obtain a lift to the extended bundle. To make this into a
principal bundle and construct canonical Cartan connections, the specific algebraic
setup for the AHS pairs is needed.

(3) In §
2.1
we excluded a specific one-grading of

\mathfrak{sl}(n+1,\mathbb{R})
for
n\geq 2
from our discussion. For this grading,

G=SL(n+1,\mathbb{R})
and
P
the stabilizer of a line in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is a possible
choice of groups. For this choice,
G/P
is the projective space
\mathbb{R}P^{n}
, and
it is well known that this leads to a geometric structure which behaves very
similarly to the AHS-structures we discuss. These structures are called (classical)
projective structures and they are not determined by an underlying

G_{0}
-structure. Given an
n
-dimensional manifold
M
, a projective structure on

M
is defined as an equivalence class of torsion-free linear connections on
TM

with two connections being equivalent iff they have the same geodesics up to
parametrization. Alternatively, this property can be easily characterized by the
fact that the
\binom{1}{2}
-tensor field that represents the difference of the two
connections has a special form. It can then be shown that a projective structure
can be equivalently encoded as a Cartan geometry of type
(G,P)
, which provides
the analogy to the AHS-structures, see Section 4.1.5 of
[
5
]
.

Now we have the necessary concepts at hand to explain that also this case admits a
generalization to the partial setting. Given a manifold
M
of dimension
n+k
and
an involutive distribution
F\subset TM
of rank
n
, we can consider partial
linear connections
\nabla:\Gamma(F)\times\Gamma(F)\to\Gamma(F)
as in (2) above and require
that the partial torsion defined there vanishes identically. Both versions of the
definition of projective equivalence admit an obvious analog in this setting, and
one defines a partial projective structure via an equivalence class of torsion-free
partial connections. As detailed in
[
16
]
, the construction of a
canonical Cartan connection extends to the partial setting. In terms of the proof
of Theorem
12
, one starts from the full linear frame bundle
\mathcal{G}_{0}
of

F
and attaches to
u_{0}\in\mathcal{G}_{0}
the connection forms of the partial principal
connection forms corresponding to the partial linear connections in the class.

The main motivation for these developments in
[
16
]
is that
canonical partial projective structures arise in unexpected situations. The main
example discussed there is a contact manifold
(M,H)
endowed with an involutive
Legendrean distribution
F\subset H
. One then shows that a choice of contact form
gives rise to a partial linear connection on
F
, and different choices lead to
projectively equivalent partial connections. Hence one obtains a canonical partial
projective structure on
(M,F)
, which turns out to always be locally flat. The
construction of partial BGG sequences we obtain in §
3
below thus leads to
differential complexes consisting of (higher order) operators that are intrinsic to
the geometry
(M,H,F)
.

The motivation for these developments came from the theory of Legendrean contact
structures, which belong to the class of parabolic geometries. Such a structure is
given by a decomposition of
H
into a direct sum of two (not necessarily
involutive) Legendrean distributions
E
and
F
, see Section 4.2.3 of

[
5
]
. In that case there is the general concept of Weyl connections, which
produces a distinguished connection for any choice of contact form. Moreover, the
complexes above are obtained using the machinery of relative BGG sequences
developed in
[
10
]
. In
[
16
]
it is shown that these
constructions generalize in the way described above to either a single involutive
Legendrean distribution
F
or a general Legendrean distributions
F
together with
a choice of a (not necessarily Legendrean) complement
E
in
H
.

2.8.
Uniqueness

The construction of a canonical Cartan connection that we used in the proof of
Theorem
12
has the advantage that it is geometric in nature. However, there
are very different constructions, for example parallel to the one in Section 3.1 of

[
5
]
, where one starts the construction by making choices and then just
exploits the algebraic properties of the normalization condition. Finally, there are
constructions for Cartan geometries like Fefferman-type constructions or extension
functors which directly produce normal Cartan geometries. Therefore, it is important
to prove uniqueness results for normal Cartan geometries which do not depend on
specific constructions. In our setting, this result looks as follows.

Theorem 14
.

Let
(G,P)
be an AHS-pair as in Definition
1
. Suppose that
(p^{i}:\mathcal{G}^{i}\to M^{i},\omega^{i})
are normal partial Cartan geometries over
(M^{i},F^{i})
for
i=1,2
with
underlying partial
G_{0}
-structures
(p^{i}_{0}:\mathcal{G}^{i}_{0}\to M^{i},\theta^{i})
. Then any
morphism
\Phi
between the underlying partial
G_{0}
-structures uniquely lifts to a
morphism of the partial Cartan geometries. In particular, any two normal partial
Cartan geometries that have the same underlying partial
G_{0}
-structure are
isomorphic via an isomorphism inducing the identity on the underlying partial

G_{0}
-structure.

Proof.

Start with
(M,F)
and a principal
P
-bundle
\mathcal{G}\to M
and suppose that

\omega,\tilde{\omega}\in\Gamma(L(\widetilde{F},\mathfrak{g}))
are partial Cartan connections on

\mathcal{G}
, which induce the same underlying partial
G_{0}
-structure
(p_{0}:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to M,\theta)
. The latter condition means that for each
u\in\mathcal{G}
and the components in

\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
we get
\omega_{-1}(u)=\tilde{\omega}_{-1}(u)
. We write
\theta(u)
for this
component, since it corresponds to the pullback of
\theta
along the projection
q:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}_{0}
. Now
\tilde{\omega}-\omega\in\Gamma(L(\widetilde{F},\mathfrak{g}))
vanishes on the
vertical subspace and has values in
\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{1}
, so there is a smooth
map
\psi:\mathcal{G}\to L(\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{1})
such that

(2.7)

\tilde{\omega}(u)(\xi)=\omega(u)(\xi)+\psi(u)(\theta(u)(\xi))

for each
\xi\in T_{u}\mathcal{G}
. Moreover, equivariancy of
\omega
and
\tilde{\omega}
easily
implies that
\psi(u\cdot g)=g^{-1}\cdot\psi(u)
where in the right hand side we use
the action of
P
coming from the identification of the target space with
L(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p})
.

Now denote by
\kappa
and
\tilde{\kappa}
the curvature functions of the two Cartan
connections and by
\kappa_{i}
and
\tilde{\kappa}_{i}
their components in
\mathfrak{g}_{i}
for

i=-1,0,1
. Then (
2.7
) easily implies that for the lowest components, we get

\tilde{\kappa}_{-1}(u)=\kappa_{-1}(u)+\partial(\psi_{0}(u)),

where
\psi_{0}
denotes component of
\psi
in
L(\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g}_{0})
. Since both

\tilde{\kappa}
and
\kappa
have values in
\ker(\partial^{*})
, we conclude that

0=\partial^{*}(\partial(\psi_{0}(u)))
. By Proposition
11
, this implies that

\partial(\psi_{0}(u))=0
and hence
\psi_{0}(u)=\partial(Z(u))
for a unique element

Z(u)\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}
.

Next, we claim that we can choose
Z(u)
depending smoothly on
u
and such that for

g\in P
, we obtain
Z(u\cdot g)=\operatorname{Ad}(g_{0}^{-1})(Z(u))
, where, as before,

g_{0}=gP_{+}
. For an open subset
U\subset M
for which there is a smooth section

\sigma:U\to\mathcal{G}
, the map
x\mapsto\psi_{0}(\sigma(x))
is smooth and by uniqueness

Z(\sigma(x))
has to be obtained from
\psi_{0}(\sigma(x))
by composition with the inverse
of the linear isomorphism
\partial:\mathfrak{g}_{1}\to\ker(\partial^{*})\subset L(\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathfrak{g}_{0})
. Thus
x\mapsto Z(\sigma(x))
is smooth and we can uniquely extend
this to a smooth map
p^{-1}(U)\to\mathfrak{g}_{1}
by putting
Z(\sigma(x)\cdot g):=\operatorname{Ad}(g^{-1})(Z(\sigma(x)))
. Of course, this map then satisfies
Z(u\cdot g)=\operatorname{Ad}(g^{-1})(Z(u))
for any
u\in p^{-1}(U)
and
g\in P
and since
P_{+}
acts
trivially on
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
, this coincides with the required equivariancy
condition. Finally, since
\psi_{0}(\sigma(x)\cdot g)=g_{0}^{-1}\cdot\psi_{0}(\sigma(x))
,

G_{0}
-equivariancy of
\partial
implies that
\psi_{0}(u)=\partial Z(u)
for all
u\in p^{-1}(U)
. Doing this for the elements of an open covering of
M
, the corresponding
local maps can be glued to a global map with the required properties using (the
pullback of) a partition of unity on
M
.

Now
Z(u\cdot g)=\operatorname{Ad}(g^{-1})(Z(u))
implies that
\exp(Z(u\cdot g))=g^{-1}\exp(Z(u))g
which shows that
u\mapsto u\cdot\exp(Z(u))
defines a fiber
bundle map
\Xi:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}
which covers the identity on the underlying partial

G_{0}
-structure and is
P
-equivariant, so
\Xi
is a morphism of principal
bundles. Thus
\Xi^{*}\tilde{\omega}
is a Cartan connection on
\mathcal{G}
and its curvature
function is given by
\tilde{\kappa}\circ\Xi
, so it also has values in

\ker(\partial^{*})
. For
u\in\mathcal{G}
and
\xi\in T_{u}\mathcal{G}
, we get

\Xi^{*}\tilde{\omega}(u)(\xi)=\tilde{\omega}(u\cdot\exp(Z(u))(T_{u}\Xi(\xi))
. Now the definition
of
\Xi
readily implies that
T_{u}\Xi(\xi)=T_{u}r^{\exp(Z(u))}(\xi)+\eta
, where

\eta
lies in the vertical bundle of
q:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}_{0}
and hence

\tilde{\omega}(\eta)\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. Equivariancy of
\tilde{\omega}
thus implies that, modulo
elements of
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
, we get

\Xi^{*}\tilde{\omega}(u)(\xi)=\operatorname{Ad}(\exp(-Z(u))(\tilde{\omega}(u)(\xi))=\tilde{\omega}(u)(\xi)-[Z(u),\theta(u)(\xi)].

But the last term coincides with
-\psi_{0}(u)(\theta(u)(\xi))
, so
\Xi^{*}\tilde{\omega}
and

\omega
have the same
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-component.

So let us assume that in the above procedure we know that the components of

\tilde{\omega}
and
\omega
in
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}
agree. Then the map
\psi
in
(
2.7
) has values in
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
and from the definitions it follows readily
that
\tilde{\kappa}_{-1}=\kappa_{-1}
, while

\tilde{\kappa}_{0}(u)=\kappa_{0}(u)+\partial(\psi(u))
. Normality of both Cartan connections
then implies
\partial^{*}(\partial(\psi(u)))=0
, which by Proposition
11

implies
\partial(\psi(u))=0
and therefore
\psi(u)=0
. Hence we conclude that

\Xi^{*}\tilde{\omega}=\omega
so any two normal Cartan connections on
\mathcal{G}
that induce the
same underlying
G_{0}
-structure are related by an automorphism of
\mathcal{G}
which
induces the identity on this underlying structure.

To get the general statement on existence, we assume that
\Phi:\mathcal{G}^{1}_{0}\to\mathcal{G}^{2}_{0}
is a morphism of
G_{0}
-principal bundles with base map
\varphi
such that

\Phi^{*}\theta^{2}=\theta^{1}
. Using that
\exp:\mathfrak{g}_{1}\to P_{+}
is a diffeomorphism, one
shows that the
P_{+}
-principal bundles
q^{i}:\mathcal{G}^{i}\to\mathcal{G}_{0}^{i}
are trivial for

i=1,2
, and there even are
G_{0}
-equivariant smooth sections
\sigma_{i}:\mathcal{G}_{0}^{i}\to\mathcal{G}^{i}
for
i=1,2
, see Proposition 5.1.1 of
[
5
]
. Now there is a
principal bundle homomorphism
\tilde{\Phi}:\mathcal{G}^{1}\to\mathcal{G}^{2}
characterized by

\tilde{\Phi}\circ\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{1}\circ\Phi
, which of course lifts
\Phi:\mathcal{G}^{1}_{0}\to\mathcal{G}^{2}_{0}
. Then
\tilde{\Phi}^{*}\omega^{2}
defines a partial Cartan connection on
\mathcal{G}^{1}

which is normal as the pullback of a normal connection. Hence from above, we know
that there is a morphism
\Xi:\mathcal{G}^{1}\to\mathcal{G}^{1}
that covers the identity on
\mathcal{G}^{1}_{0}
such that
\Xi^{*}\tilde{\Phi}^{*}\omega^{2}=\omega^{1}
and hence
\tilde{\Phi}\circ\Xi
is a
morphism of Cartan geometries that lifts
\Phi
.

To prove uniqueness, we observe that any other homomorphism
\mathcal{G}^{1}\to\mathcal{G}^{2}

that lifts
\Phi
is of the form
\hat{\Phi}(u)=\tilde{\Phi}(u)\cdot\exp(Z(u))
for a
smooth function
Z:\mathcal{G}^{1}\to\mathfrak{g}_{1}
such that
Z(u\cdot g)=\operatorname{Ad}(g^{-1})(Z(u))
.
If
\tilde{\Phi}^{*}\omega^{2}=\omega^{1}
then as above we conclude that for
u\in\mathcal{G}
and

\xi\in T_{u}\mathcal{G}
the
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-component of
(\hat{\Phi}^{*}\omega^{2})(x)\xi
coincides
with the the one of
\omega^{1}(u)(\xi)-[Z(u),\theta(u)(\xi)]
. Thus

\hat{\Phi}^{*}\omega^{2}(u)=\omega^{1}(u)
is only possible of
[Z(u),X]=0
for any
X\in\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
, which implies
Z(u)=0
and hence
\hat{\Phi}=\tilde{\Phi}
.
∎

3.
The partial BGG machinery

Having the setup of partial Cartan geometries corresponding to AHS-pairs
(G,P)
at
hand, we can introduce BGG sequences following ideas from standard BGG sequences for
AHS-structures on the one hand an the relative BGG machinery introduced in

[
10
]
on the other hand. Basically, one can use all the algebraic results
related to standard BGG sequences in the AHS case, while the differential operator
part is best done following the relative machinery.

3.1.
The partial fundamental derivative

Let
(G,P)
be an AHS-pair as in Definition
1
and let

(p:\mathcal{G}\to M,\omega)
be a partial Cartan geometry of type
(G,P)
on
(M,F)
. Then
we define the
partial adjoint tractor bundle

\mathcal{A}_{F}M
as the associated
bundle
\mathcal{G}\times_{P}\mathfrak{g}
. Sections of
\mathcal{A}_{F}M
are then in bijective
correspondence with smooth functions
f:\mathcal{G}\to\mathfrak{g}
such that

f(u\cdot g)=\operatorname{Ad}(g^{-1})(f(u))
for any
u\in\mathcal{G}
and
g\in P
. But by definition,

\omega(u)
defines a linear isomorphism
\widetilde{F}_{u}\to\mathfrak{g}
, so given
f
as
above, there is a unique element
\xi(u)\in\widetilde{F}_{u}
such that

\omega(u)(\xi(u))=f(u)
. This defines a bijection between
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{G},\mathfrak{g})

and
\Gamma(\widetilde{F})
and using equivariancy of
\omega
, the equivariancy condition
on
f
translates to
\xi(u\cdot g)=T_{u}r^{g}(\xi(u))
i.e. to
(r^{g})^{*}\xi=\xi
. Thus
we see that sections of
\mathcal{A}_{F}M
are in bijective correspondence with

P
-invariant sections of
\widetilde{F}\to\mathcal{G}
.

This shows that we get counterparts to all the well-known operations on adjoint
tractor bundles in our setting:

•

A bilinear bundle map
\{\ ,\ \}:\mathcal{A}_{F}M\times\mathcal{A}_{F}M\to\mathcal{A}_{F}M
induced by
the Lie bracket on
\mathfrak{g}
, which makes
\mathcal{A}_{F}M
into a bundle of Lie
algebras.

•

A filtration of
\mathcal{A}_{F}M
by smooth subbundles of the form

\mathcal{A}^{1}_{F}M\subset\mathcal{A}^{0}_{F}M\subset\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{F}M=\mathcal{A}_{F}M
induced by

\mathfrak{g}_{1}\subset\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathfrak{g}
. This is compatible with
\{\ ,\ \}
in the
sense that (with obvious conventions)
\{\mathcal{A}^{i}_{F}M,\mathcal{A}^{j}_{F}M\}\subset\mathcal{A}^{i+j}_{F}M
. Moreover, projecting invariant vector fields to
M
gives rise to
an isomorphism
\mathcal{A}_{F}M/\mathcal{A}^{0}_{F}M\to F
.

•

A Lie bracket
[\ ,\ ]:\Gamma(\mathcal{A}_{F}M)\times\Gamma(\mathcal{A}_{F}M)\to\Gamma(\mathcal{A}_{F}M)
induced
by the Lie bracket of vector fields. Viewing the map
\mathcal{A}_{F}M\to F
from above as
having values in
TM
and using it as an anchor, this makes
\mathcal{A}_{F}M
into a Lie
algebroid over
M
.

•

By construction, the curvature function
\kappa
of
\omega
is equivariant, and
hence it can be equivalently interpreted as a section of

\Lambda^{2}F^{*}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{F}M
, i.e. a partially defined two-form with values in

\mathcal{A}_{F}M
.

Now assume that
\mathbb{W}
is any representation of
P
. Then we get an associated
natural vector bundle
WM:=\mathcal{G}\times_{P}\mathbb{W}\to M
over
M
, and sections of
WM
are
in bijective correspondence with smooth functions
f:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{W}
such that

f(u\cdot g)=g^{-1}\cdot f(u)
. We can use any vector field on
\mathcal{G}
to
differentiate such a function, but the result will not be equivariant in general. But
if we take invariant vector fields and in particular the invariant sections of

\widetilde{F}
corresponding to sections of
\mathcal{A}_{F}M
, we will end up with
equivariant functions. Thus, we get an operator
\mathcal{D}:\Gamma(\mathcal{A}_{F}M)\times\Gamma(WM)\to\Gamma(WM)
which we denote by
(s,\sigma)\mapsto\mathcal{D}_{s}\sigma
in order to
emphasize the analogy to a (partial) covariant derivative. Indeed, the operator is
bilinear and linear over smooth functions in the first variable. In the second
variable, we have a Leibniz rule of the form
\mathcal{D}_{s}(h\sigma)=(\Pi(s)\cdot h)\sigma+h\mathcal{D}_{s}\sigma
for
h\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
, where
\Pi
denotes the tensorial
operator induced by the projection
\mathcal{A}_{F}M\to F
. The construction also implies
immediately that this operation is compatible with all natural bundle maps that come
from
P
-equivariant maps between the inducing representations. Finally, since the
operation is tensorial in the first variable, we can also view it as
\mathcal{D}:\Gamma(WM)\to\Gamma(\mathcal{A}^{*}_{F}M\otimes WM)
via
(\mathcal{D}\sigma)(s)=D_{s}(\sigma)
.

3.2.
Partial tractor connection and partial twisted exterior
derivative

Consider a representation
\mathbb{V}
of
G
that we can view
as a representation of
P
by restriction. The we call the associated bundle

VM:=\mathcal{G}\times_{P}\mathbb{V}
a
partial tractor bundle
. Compared to a general
natural bundle, we now get an additional ingredient, namely a bilinear bundle map

\mathcal{A}_{F}M\times VM\to VM
induced by the infinitesimal representation
\mathfrak{g}\times\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{V}
. We will denote this bundle map and the induced tensorial operation on
sections by
(s,t)\mapsto s\bullet t
. This makes several algebraic operations
available which are the basis for the further developments. First, we get the
standard Lie algebra cohomology differential
\partial_{\mathfrak{g}}:\Lambda^{k}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{V}\to\Lambda^{k+1}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{V}
for each
k=0,\dots,\dim(\mathfrak{g})-1
. Viewing elements of
\Lambda^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{V}
as multilinear alternating
maps, this is explicitly given by

(3.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathfrak{g}}\alpha(A_{0},\dots,A_{k})

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}A_{i}\cdot\alpha(A_{0},\dots,\widehat{A_{i}},\dots,A_{n})

\displaystyle+\textstyle\sum_{i<j}(-1)^{i+j}\alpha([A_{i},A_{j}],A_{0},\dots,\widehat{A_{i}},\dots,\widehat{A_{j}},\dots,A_{k}),

where the dot in the first sum denote the action
\mathfrak{g}\times\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{V}
and the
hats denote omission. Since this only uses the Lie bracket on
\mathfrak{g}
and the
action of
\mathfrak{g}
on
\mathbb{V}
we immediately conclude that
\partial_{\mathfrak{g}}
is

G
-equivariant and hence also
P
-equivariant. Consequently, it induces bundle maps

\Lambda^{k}\mathcal{A}^{*}_{F}M\otimes VM\to\Lambda^{k+1}\mathcal{A}^{*}_{F}M\otimes VM
and we also denote
these as well as the induced tensorial operations on sections by the same symbol

\partial_{\mathfrak{g}}
. Observe that we can view

\varphi\in\Gamma(\Lambda^{k}\mathcal{A}^{*}_{F}M\otimes VM)
as a
k
-linear alternating map

\Gamma(\mathcal{A}^{*}_{F}M)^{k}\to\Gamma(VM)
, which is linear over smooth functions in each
argument. In this picture,
\partial_{\mathfrak{g}}\varphi
is given by a formula completely
analogous to (
3.1
) but with the dot in the first sum replaced by

\bullet
and the bracket in the second sum replaced by
\{\ ,\ \}
.

There also is a natural differential operator acting between the same bundles. Given

\varphi\in\Gamma(\Lambda^{k}\mathcal{A}^{*}_{F}\otimes VM)
, we can form
\mathcal{D}\varphi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A}^{*}_{F}M\otimes\Lambda^{k}\mathcal{A}^{*}_{F}M\otimes VM)
, thus defining a
(k+1)
-linear operator
\Gamma(\mathcal{A}_{F}^{*}M)^{k+1}\to\Gamma(VM)
which is linear over smooth functions in each variable. We
can then make the result alternating by simply forming

(3.2)

(\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{a}}\varphi)(s_{0},\dots,s_{k}):=\textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}(\mathcal{D}_{s_{i}}\varphi)(s_{0},\dots,\widehat{s_{i}},\dots,s_{k})

for
s_{j}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A}^{*}_{F}M)
. We can combine this with the Lie algebra cohomology
differential, by defining

\tilde{d}^{V}_{F}:\Gamma(\Lambda^{k}\mathcal{A}^{*}_{F}M\otimes VM)\to\Gamma(\Lambda^{k+1}\mathcal{A}^{*}_{F}M\otimes VM)\qquad\tilde{d}^{V}_{F}\varphi:=\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{a}}\varphi+\partial_{\mathfrak{g}}\varphi.

The motivation for forming this combination is nicely visible by looking at the
case
k=0
, where we start with
t\in\Gamma(VM)
and then obtain
(\tilde{d}^{V}_{F}t)(s)=\mathcal{D}_{s}t+s\bullet t
. Now suppose that
s
is a section of the subbundle
\mathcal{A}^{0}_{F}M\subset\mathcal{A}_{F}M
. By definition this means that for the invariant vector field

\xi\in\Gamma(\widetilde{F})
corresponding to
s
and a point
u\in\mathcal{G}
, we get

\omega(\xi(u))\in\mathfrak{p}
and putting
\omega(\xi(u))=A
, this means that

\xi(u)=\zeta_{A}(u)
. Denoting by
f:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{V}
the equivariant function
corresponding to
s
, equivariancy implies that
\xi(f)(u)=-A\cdot f(u)
, which
equals the value of the function corresponding to
-s\bullet t
in
u
. Hence we
conclude that
\tilde{d}^{V}_{F}t(s)=0
for any
s\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A}^{0}_{F}M)
and since
\mathcal{A}_{F}M/\mathcal{A}^{0}_{F}M\cong F
, we conclude that
\tilde{d}^{V}_{F}t
actually is a section of

F^{*}\otimes VM
. So we can view this as an operator
d^{V}_{F}:\Gamma(VM)\to\Gamma(F^{*}\otimes VM)
and the Leibniz rule for
\mathcal{D}
immediately implies that this defines a
partial connection, called the
partial tractor connection
on the partial
tractor bundle
VM
.

It is rather easy to extend this to higher degrees. The bundle
\Lambda^{k}F^{*}\otimes VM

can be naturally viewed as the subbundle of
\Lambda^{k}\mathcal{A}^{*}_{F}M\otimes VM
, consisting
of those maps
(\mathcal{A}_{F}M)^{k}\to VM
that vanish upon insertion of one element of
\mathcal{A}^{0}_{F}M
. If
\varphi
is a section of this subbundle, then by the naturality discussed
in §
3.1
also
\mathcal{D}_{s}\varphi
has this property for each
s\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A}M)
. Using this, one can compute explicitly the behavior of
\tilde{d}^{V}_{F}\varphi
under
insertion of a section of
\mathcal{A}^{0}_{F}M
exactly as in the the first part of the proof
of Theorem 4.1 of
[
10
]
to obtain the following result. In what follows, we
use the notation
\Omega^{k}_{F}(M,VM):=\Gamma(\Lambda^{k}F^{*}\otimes VM)
to emphasize the point of
view of “partially defined differential forms”.

Proposition 15
.

Let
(G,P)
be an AHS-pair and let
(p:\mathcal{G}\to M,\omega)
be a partial Cartan geometry
of type
(G,P)
on
(M,F)
. For a representation
\mathbb{V}
of
G
, let
VM
be the
corresponding partial tractor bundle. Then the operators
\tilde{d}^{V}_{F}
restrict to
natural differential operators
d^{V}_{F}:\Omega^{k}_{F}(M,VM)\to\Omega^{k+1}_{F}(M,VM)
for each

k=0,\dots,n-1
.

3.3.
The algebraic Hodge theory

The second main ingredient needed for our version of the BGG construction is the
special case of B. Kostant’s algebraic Hodge theory from
[
12
]
that is used
in the theory of AHS-structures. Since
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
is a Lie subalgebra of

\mathfrak{g}
, we can restrict the infinitesimal action
\mathfrak{g}\times\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{V}
from
§
3.2
to
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
. This makes
\mathbb{V}
into a representation of the
(Abelian) Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
, so there also is a Lie algebra cohomology
differential

\partial:=\partial_{\mathfrak{g}_{-1}}:\Lambda^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{-1}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{V}\to\Lambda^{k+1}\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\otimes\mathbb{V}
. This is defined by formula (
3.1
), but since

\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
is Abelian, only the first sum in the formula survives. Compared to the
case of
\partial_{\mathfrak{g}}
discussed above, we have weaker equivariancy properties,
however. The adjoint action of elements of
G_{0}
by definition maps
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
to
itself, which easily implies that
\partial
is
G_{0}
-equivariant. However, this is
not true for elements of
P
and the standard way to define an action on

\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
is via the identification with
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p}
. It turns out,
however, that this action does
not
lead to
P
-equivariancy of the
infinitesimal representation and of
\partial
. Hence there is no immediate way to
convert
\partial
into a geometric operation, and we’ll discuss below how to deal
with this problem.

On the other hand,
\mathfrak{g}_{1}\subset\mathfrak{g}
also is an Abelian Lie subalgebra of

\mathfrak{g}
and by restriction
\mathbb{V}
becomes a representation of
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. Dually
to Lie algebra cohomology, there is a standard complex computing Lie algebra homology
of
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{V}
. The differential in this complex maps

\Lambda^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{V}
to
\Lambda^{k-1}\mathfrak{g}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{V}
. In the theory of
parabolic geometries, this is traditionally referred to as the
Kostant
codifferential
and denoted by
\partial^{*}
. Explicitly,

(3.3)

\partial^{*}(Z_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge Z_{k}\otimes v)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-1)^{i}Z_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\widehat{Z_{i}}\wedge\dots\wedge Z_{k}\otimes(Z_{i}\cdot v).

Now
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
actually
is
invariant under the adjoint action of elements
of
P
and then the definition readily implies that
\partial^{*}
is

P
-equivariant. As we has noted in §
2.4
, the Killing form of
\mathfrak{g}

induces a
P
-equivariant isomorphism between
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
and
(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{p})^{*}
,
so in particular
\mathfrak{g}_{1}\cong\mathfrak{g}_{-1}^{*}
as a
G_{0}
-module. Thus
\partial^{*}

acts between the same spaces as
\partial
but in the opposite
direction. Indeed, Kostant’s original definition of
\partial^{*}
was as the adjoint
of
\partial
with respect to a certain inner product, the algebraic
interpretation was noted only later (and provides a nice explanation for the better
equivariancy properties). The adjointness easily implies the following result, which
is known as the
algebraic Hodge decomposition
, see
[
12
]
and
Theorem 3.3.1 of
[
5
]
for a proof.

Theorem 16
.

For any
k=0,\dots,n=\dim(\mathfrak{g}_{-1})
consider the
G_{0}
-equivariant map

\square=\square_{k}:=\partial^{*}\circ\partial+\partial\circ\partial^{*}
acting on

\Lambda^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{-1}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{V}
. Then there is a
G_{0}
-invariant decomposition

(3.4)

\Lambda^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{-1}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{V}=\operatorname{im}(\partial)\oplus\ker(\square)\oplus\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*}),

with the first two summands adding up to
\ker(\partial)
and the last two summands
adding up to
\ker(\partial^{*})
.

The
G_{0}
-invariant subspace
\ker(\square)
can be identified with

\ker(\partial)/\operatorname{im}(\partial)
and thus with the Lie algebra cohomology
H^{k-1}(\mathfrak{g}_{-1},\mathbb{V})
. However, we can also identify it with

\ker(\partial^{*})/\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})=H_{k}(\mathfrak{g}_{1},\mathbb{V})
, which endows it naturally
with the structure of a
P
-module. It turns out that
P_{+}\subset P
acts trivially
on this quotient. In
[
12
]
, B. Kostant analyzed the map
\square
in more
detail (see also below) to obtain a complete description of the
G_{0}
-representation

\ker(\square)
in terms of highest weights. These weights are algorithmically
computable, so one can always determine the representations
H_{k}(\mathfrak{g}_{1},\mathbb{V})

explicitly.

3.4.
Converting to geometry

Parts of the developments of §
3.3
can be directly translated to geometric
objects on a partial Cartan geometry
(p:\mathcal{G}\to M,\omega)
of type
(G,P)
. The

P
-equivariant maps
\partial^{*}
give rise to natural bundle maps

\Lambda^{k}F^{*}\otimes VM\to\Lambda^{k-1}F^{*}\otimes VM
. We denote these bundle maps and the
induced tensorial operators on sections all by the same symbol
\partial^{*}
. In
particular,
\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})\subset\ker(\partial^{*})\subset\Lambda^{k}F^{*}\otimes VM
are
natural subbundles and the quotient
\ker(\partial^{*})/\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})
can be
naturally identified with the associated bundle

\mathcal{H}_{k}^{V}:=\mathcal{G}\times_{P}H_{k}(\mathfrak{g}_{1},\mathbb{V})
. As we have noted above,
P_{+}
acts
trivially on these homology spaces, so

\mathcal{H}_{k}^{V}=\mathcal{G}_{0}\times_{G_{0}}H_{k}(\mathfrak{g}_{1},\mathbb{V})
. Once the homology representations
are known, these bundles thus admit a direct interpretation in terms of the
underlying
G_{0}
-structure.

For the remaining ingredients from §
3.3
the translation to geometry is more
subtle and requires an additional ingredient. There is a so-called
grading
element

E\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
[E,A]=jA
for each
A\in\mathfrak{g}_{j}
with

j=-1,0,1
. The definition of
E
implies that it lies in the center of the subalgebra

\mathfrak{g}_{0}
and in the cases we consider here, it actually spans this center. Lie
theory implies that
E
acts diagonalizably on any representation
\mathbb{V}
of
\mathfrak{g}
. Assuming that
\mathbb{V}
is irreducible, the resulting eigenspaces can be used to
define a decomposition
\mathbb{V}=\oplus_{i=0}^{N}\mathbb{V}_{i}
which has the property that
for
A\in\mathfrak{g}_{j}
and
v\in\mathbb{V}_{i}
, one obtains
A\cdot v\in\mathbb{V}_{i+j}
,
c.f. Section 2.5 of
[
9
]
. In particular, each of the subspaces
\mathbb{V}_{i}

is
G_{0}
-invariant while
\mathbb{V}^{i}:=\oplus_{j\geq i}\mathbb{V}_{j}
is even

P
-invariant. Otherwise put,
\mathbb{V}
admits a
P
-invariant filtration
\mathbb{V}=\mathbb{V}^{0}\supset\mathbb{V}^{1}\supset\dots\supset\mathbb{V}^{N-1}\supset\mathbb{V}^{N}
.

Now this filtration of course induces a filtration of the partial tractor bundle
VM

by the natural smooth subbundles
V^{j}M=\mathcal{G}\times_{P}\mathbb{V}^{j}
. We can form the

associated graded vector bundle

\operatorname{gr}(VM)=\oplus_{i=0}^{N}\operatorname{gr}_{i}(VM)
where

\operatorname{gr}_{i}(VM):=V^{i}M/V^{i+1}M
. By construction
\operatorname{gr}_{i}(VM)=\mathcal{G}\times_{P}\operatorname{gr}_{i}(\mathbb{V})
,
where
\operatorname{gr}_{i}(\mathbb{V}):=\mathbb{V}^{i}/\mathbb{V}^{i+1}
, so
\operatorname{gr}(VM)
is the natural vector
bundle associated to
\operatorname{gr}(\mathbb{V})=\oplus\operatorname{gr}_{i}(\mathbb{V})
, the associated graded
representation to
\mathbb{V}
. Observe that by construction the action of
P_{+}
maps
each
\mathbb{V}_{i}
to
\mathbb{V}_{i+1}
, so the induced action on
\operatorname{gr}_{i}(\mathbb{V})
is
trivial, while as a
G_{0}
-module we get
\operatorname{gr}_{i}(\mathbb{V})\cong\mathbb{V}_{i}
and hence

\operatorname{gr}(\mathbb{V})\cong\mathbb{V}
. It is crucial to realize however, that this identification
of
G_{0}
modules has no geometric counterpart. On the geometric side, we only have
the canonical quotient maps
V^{i}M\to\operatorname{gr}_{i}(VM)
but there are no natural injective
bundle maps (in either direction) between
VM
and
\operatorname{gr}(VM)
.

We can now view the
G_{0}
-equivariant maps
\partial
and
\square
as

P
-equivariant maps on the spaces
\Lambda^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{-1}^{*}\otimes\operatorname{gr}(\mathbb{V})
and this
interpretation also makes sense for
\partial^{*}
. The explicit formulae immediately
imply that

(3.5)

\begin{gathered}\partial(\Lambda^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{-1}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{V}_{i})\subset\Lambda^{k+1}\mathfrak{g}_{-1}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{V}_{i-1}\\
\partial^{*}(\Lambda^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{-1}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{V}_{i})\subset\Lambda^{k-1}\mathfrak{g}_{-1}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{V}_{i+1}\\
\square(\Lambda^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{-1}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{V}_{i})\subset\Lambda^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{-1}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{V}_{i}\end{gathered}

Hence we get induced bundle maps defined on the bundles
\Lambda^{k}F^{*}\otimes\operatorname{gr}_{i}(VM)

with analogous homogeneity properties. We will denote these bundle maps and the
induced tensorial operators on sections by the same symbols
\partial
,
\partial^{*}

and
\square
. Notice that for
\partial^{*}
this is an additional abuse of notation
since we denote the operations on the original bundles and on the associated graded by
the same symbol. This is justified by the homogeneity property in (
3.5
)
which implies that they are compatible with the projection to the associated graded,
see below. Thus we obtain a geometric version of the Hodge decomposition from
(
3.4
).

Corollary 17
.

For any
i=0,\dots,N
, there is a decomposition into a sum of natural subbundles

(3.6)

\Lambda^{k}F^{*}\otimes\operatorname{gr}_{i}(VM)=\operatorname{im}(\partial)\oplus\ker(\square)\oplus\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*}).

The bundle map
\square
restricts to a linear isomorphism

\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})\to\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})
and there is a universal polynomial
p^{\mathbb{V}}_{k,i}\in\mathbb{R}[x]
(independent of
(M,F)
)such that the linear map
p^{\mathbb{V}}_{k,i}(\square)
restricts on
\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})
to

(\square|_{\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})})^{-1}
.

Proof.

The decomposition (
3.6
) readily follows from Theorem
16
and
also the remaining statements are of algebraic origin. Since

\partial^{*}\circ\partial^{*}=0
, we see that on
\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})
,
\square
coincides
with
\partial^{*}\circ\partial
, so

\square(\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*}))\subset\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})
. Theorem
16
also shows
that
\ker(\partial^{*})\cap\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})=\{0\}
so
\square
is injective on

\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})
. On the inducing representations, we are dealing with linear maps
on finite dimensional vector spaces, so this implies that

\square:\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})\to\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})
is a linear isomorphism.

To prove the last part, we refer to Kostant’s analysis of
\square
, see

[
12
]
and Section 3.3 of
[
5
]
. If
\mathfrak{g}
is complex and
\mathbb{V}

is a complex representation of
\mathfrak{g}
, the map
\square
on
\Lambda^{k}g_{1}\otimes\mathbb{V}
can be expressed in terms of Casimir operators. This then implies that

\square
acts diagonalizably with real eigenvalues on each
\Lambda^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{V}_{i}
, hence this also holds for the subspace
\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})
. Linear
algebra then shows that, on this subspace, the inverse of
\square
can be written as
a polynomial with real coefficients in
\square
. In the general case, we can
complexify
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathbb{V}
(as needed) and then view
\Lambda^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{V}_{i}
as a linear subspace in the analogous space obtained from the
complexifications. This subspace is invariant under
\square
, which implies that the
same polynomial works.
∎

The conceptual way to interpret the homogeneity properties is via
total
degree
, which is defined to be
k+i
for elements of
\Lambda^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{1}\otimes\operatorname{gr}_{i}(\mathbb{V})
(or of the corresponding bundle). Equation (
3.5
)
then simply says that all three operations preserve the total degree. Similarly, we
will say that an element of
\Omega^{k}_{F}(M,VM)
is homogeneous of degree
\geq k+i
if it
is a section of the subbundle
\Lambda^{k}F^{*}\otimes V^{i}M
. If a section
\varphi
has this
property, then there is a well defined projection (which actually acts only on the
values of
\varphi
) to
\operatorname{gr}_{k+i}(\varphi)\in\Gamma(\Lambda^{k}F^{*}\otimes\operatorname{gr}_{i}(VM))
. This has
homogeneity
k+i
and represents the lowest homogeneous component of
\varphi
. In this
notation, the compatibility of
\partial^{*}
with projection to the associated graded
reads as follows: If
\varphi
is homogeneous of degree
\geq k+i
, then so is

\partial^{*}\varphi
and
\operatorname{gr}_{k+i}(\partial^{*}\varphi)=\partial^{*}(\operatorname{gr}_{k+i}(\varphi))
.

3.5.
The BGG machinery

Now we can quickly develop the BGG machinery in our setting following

[
10
]
. The first step is analyzing the homogeneity properties of the
partial twisted exterior derivative, which suggests the definition of a geometric
version of the Kostant Laplacian.

Lemma 18
.

(1) The twisted exterior derivative
d^{V}_{F}
is compatible with (total) homogeneity
and induces
\partial
on the associated graded. More explicitly, if

\varphi\in\Omega^{k}_{F}(M,VM)
has total homogeneity
\geq k+i
that so has
d^{V}_{F}\varphi\in\Omega^{k+1}_{F}(M,VM)
and for its projection to the associated graded, we get

\operatorname{gr}_{k+i}(d^{V}_{F}\varphi)=\partial(\operatorname{gr}_{k+i}(\varphi))
.

(2) The operator
\square^{R}:=\partial^{*}\circ d^{V}_{F}+d^{V}_{F}\circ\partial^{*}
maps each

\Omega^{k}_{F}(M,VM)
to itself. It also preserves homogeneity and induces
\square
on the
associated graded.

(3) Fix integers
k
with
0\leq k\leq n
and
i
with
0\leq i\leq N
and define

T_{k,i}:=p^{\mathbb{V}}_{k,i}(\partial^{*}\circ d^{V}_{F})
where
p^{\mathbb{V}}_{k,i}
is the
polynomial from Corollary
17
. Then
T_{k,i}
maps

\Gamma(\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*}))\subset\Omega^{k}(M,VM)
to itself and has the following property.
If
\psi\in\Gamma(\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*}))
has homogeneity
\geq\ell:=k+i
, then the element

\tilde{\psi}:=d^{V}_{F}(T_{k,i}(\psi))\in\Omega^{k+1}_{F}(M,VM)
has homogeneity
\geq\ell
and

\operatorname{gr}_{\ell}(\partial^{*}(\tilde{\psi}))=\operatorname{gr}_{\ell}(\psi)
.

Proof.

(1) To prove the homogeneity statement, we assume
\varphi\in\Gamma(\Lambda^{k}F^{*}\otimes V^{i}M)

for some
i
, and we have to show that
d^{V}_{F}\varphi\in\Gamma(\Lambda^{k+1}F^{*}\otimes V^{i-1}M)
. To deal with
d^{V}_{F}
, we have to view
\varphi
as a section of
\Lambda^{k}\mathcal{A}^{*}M\otimes V^{i}M
and use the definition of
\tilde{d}^{V}_{F}
. Naturality of
\mathcal{D}

implies that for any
s\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A}M)
,
\mathcal{D}_{s}\varphi
is a section of the
subbundle
\Lambda^{k}F^{*}\otimes V^{i}M
. Therefore each summand in the definition of
\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{a}}\varphi
in (
3.2
) lies in
V^{i}M\subset V^{i-1}M
, so
D^{\mathfrak{a}}

actually raises total homogeneity by
1
. On the other hand, the action of an
element
A=A_{-1}+A_{0}+A_{1}\in\mathfrak{g}
maps
v\in\mathbb{V}_{i}
to
\mathbb{V}_{i-1}\oplus\mathbb{V}_{i}\oplus\mathbb{V}_{i+1}
and the component in
\mathbb{V}_{i-1}
equals
A_{-1}\cdot v
. This readily implies that
\partial_{\mathfrak{g}}(\varphi)\in\Gamma(\Lambda^{k+1}F^{*}\otimes V^{i-1}M)
, so the homogeneity statement follows. But we also see that

\operatorname{gr}_{k+i}(\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{a}}\varphi)=0
and from the definitions and the above
observation on the lowest component of
A\cdot v
, it immediately follows that

\operatorname{gr}_{k+i}(\partial_{\mathfrak{g}}(\varphi))=\partial(\operatorname{gr}_{k+i}(\varphi))
.

(2) We have noted in §
3.4
that

\partial^{*}:\Omega^{k}_{F}(M,VM)\to\Omega^{k-1}_{F}(M,VM)
preserves homogeneity and induces

\partial^{*}
on the associated graded. Hence both claims follow immediately from
part (1).

(3) Since
\partial^{*}\circ d^{V}_{F}
maps
\Gamma(\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*}))
to itself, so does

T_{k,i}
. Since
\partial^{*}
preserves homogeneities, part (1) shows that
T_{k,i}

preserves being of homogeneity
\geq\ell
for any
\ell
. If

\psi\in\Gamma(\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*}))
is homogeneous of degree
\geq\ell=k+i
then so is

T_{k,i}(\psi)
and this property is preserved by application of
d^{V}_{F}
and then of

\partial^{*}
. Using part (1), we then compute that

\operatorname{gr}_{\ell}(\partial^{*}(d^{V}_{F}(T_{k,i}(\psi))))=(\partial^{*}\circ\partial)(\operatorname{gr}_{\ell}(T_{k,i}(\psi)))).

But for
\operatorname{gr}_{\ell}(T_{k,i}(\psi))
we compute similarly step by step to conclude
that this equals
p^{\mathbb{V}}_{k,i}(\partial^{*}\circ\partial)(\operatorname{gr}_{\ell}(\psi))
. But on

\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})
, we have
\partial^{*}\circ\partial=\square
and then then results
follows since
\square\circ p^{\mathbb{V}}_{k,i}(\square)
is the identity on

\Gamma(\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*}))
by Corollary
17
.
∎

From this, the key elements of the BGG machinery follow quickly.

Theorem 19
.

Consider the operator
\square^{R}:\Omega^{k}_{F}(M,VM)\to\Omega^{k}_{F}(M,VM)
from Lemma

18
.

(1)
\ker(\square^{R})=\Gamma(\ker(\partial^{*}))\cap\ker(\partial^{*}\circ d_{F}^{V})
and the bundle
projection

\ker(\partial^{*})\to\ker(\partial^{*})/\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})=\mathcal{H}^{V}_{k}

induces an isomorphism
\ker(\square^{R})\to\Gamma(\mathcal{H}^{V}_{k})
.

(2) Viewed as an operator
S:\Gamma(\mathcal{H}^{V})\to\Omega^{k}_{F}(M,VM)
, the inverse of the
isomorphism from (1) is a differential operator naturally associated to the given
partial Cartan geometry. For
\alpha\in\Gamma(\mathcal{H}^{V}_{k})
, the element

S(\alpha)\in\Omega^{k}_{F}(M)
is uniquely characterized by the facts that

\partial^{*}(S(\alpha))=0
,
\pi_{H}(S(\alpha))=\alpha
and
\partial^{*}(d^{V}_{F}(S(\alpha)))=0
. Here

\pi_{H}
denotes the quotient projection
\Gamma(\ker(\partial^{*}))\to\Gamma(\mathcal{H}^{V}_{k})
.

Proof.

(1) We first claim that
\square^{R}
is injective
\Gamma(\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*}))
. Any section
of this subbundle can be written as
\partial^{*}(\psi)
for
\psi\in\Omega^{k+1}_{F}(M,V)

and we have to prove that
\square^{R}(\partial^{*}(\psi))=0
implies

\partial^{*}(\psi)=0
. To prove this, we assume inductively that for some
\ell\geq k
,
\partial^{*}(\psi)
is homogeneous of degree
\geq\ell
. Since

\partial^{*}\circ\partial^{*}=0
, we get

0=\square^{R}(\partial^{*}(\psi))=\partial^{*}(d^{V}_{F}(\partial^{*}(\psi)))
and applying

\operatorname{gr}_{\ell}
we conclude that

0=\partial^{*}(\partial(\operatorname{gr}_{\ell}(\partial^{*}(\psi))))
. Hence

\operatorname{gr}_{\ell}(\partial^{*}(\psi))
is a section of the subbundle

\ker(\partial^{*})\cap\operatorname{im}(\partial)\subset\Lambda^{k}F^{*}\otimes\operatorname{gr}_{\ell-k}(VM)
, which is
trivial by the algebraic Hodge decomposition (
3.4
). Since

\operatorname{gr}_{\ell}(\partial^{*}(\psi))=0
, we conclude that
\partial^{*}(\psi)
is homogeneous
of degree
\geq\ell+1
. Since
\partial^{*}(\psi)
automatically is homogeneous of
degree
\geq k
and the filtration of
\mathbb{V}
has finite length, we conclude
inductively that
\partial^{*}(\psi)=0
as claimed.

Now suppose that
\varphi\in\Omega^{k}_{F}(M,VM)
satisfies
\square^{R}(\varphi)=0
. Then

0=\partial^{*}(\square^{R}(\varphi))
and by definition, this equals

\square^{R}(\partial^{*}(\varphi))
. From the injectivity statement above, we conclude

\partial^{*}(\varphi)=0
and hence
0=\square^{R}(\varphi)=\partial^{*}(d^{V}_{F}(\varphi))
. But this
says that
\varphi\in\Gamma(\ker(\partial^{*}))\cap\ker(\partial^{*}\circ d^{V}_{F})
. Since the
opposite inclusion is obvious, this implies the claimed description of

\ker(\square^{R})
.

Next, we claim that the restriction of
\pi_{H}:\Gamma(\ker(\partial^{*}))\to\Gamma(\mathcal{H}^{V}_{k})
to
\ker(\square^{R})
is injective. Suppose that
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in\Omega^{k}_{F}(M,VM)

satisfy
\partial^{*}(\varphi_{i})=0
for
i=1,2
and
\pi_{H}(\varphi_{1})=\pi_{H}(\varphi_{2})
. Then

\varphi_{2}=\varphi_{1}+\partial^{*}(\psi)
for some
\psi\in\Omega^{k+1}_{F}(M,VM)
. But then if

\square^{R}(\varphi_{i})=0
for
i=1,2
implies
\square^{R}(\partial^{*}(\psi))=0
and hence

\partial^{*}(\psi)=0
and
\varphi_{1}=\varphi_{2}
by the injectivity statement above.

Now for
\alpha\in\Gamma(\mathcal{H}^{k}_{V})
, we can choose some representative

\varphi\in\Omega^{k}_{F}(M,VM)
, i.e.
\partial^{*}(\varphi)=0
and
\pi_{H}(\varphi)=\alpha
. Assume
inductively that
\square^{R}(\varphi)=\partial^{*}(d^{V}_{F}(\varphi))
is homogeneous of degree

\geq\ell=k+i
. Define

(3.7)

S_{i}(\varphi):=\varphi-T_{k,i}(\partial^{*}(d^{V}_{F}(\varphi))),

where

is the operator from part (3) of Corollary

. Since

has values in

\partial^{*}(S_{i}(\varphi))=0

and
\pi_{H}(S_{i}(\varphi))=\alpha

\square^{R}(S_{i}(\varphi))=\partial^{*}(d^{v}_{F}(S_{i}(\varphi)))

\varphi

for
\alpha

(2) We can view (
3.7
) as defining a differential operator on all of

\Gamma(\ker(\partial^{*}))\subset\Omega^{k}_{F}(M,VM)
(noting that also there
\square^{R}

coincides with

\Gamma(\ker(\partial^{*}))

Remark 20
.

As with the standard BGG machinery, the construction of the splitting operator in the
proof of Theorem
19
is mainly useful for theoretical purposes. It does not
provide an effective description of the splitting operators or a reasonable way to
compute explicit formulae (except in extremely simple situations). The simplest way
to obtain explicit formulae is to determine
S(\alpha)
by explicitly verifying the
characterizing conditions in part (2) of the theorem. (The best way to do this is
using the analog of the theory of Weyl structures as discussed in
[
6
]
and
Chapter 5 of
[
5
]
in our setting.)

The splitting operator also is of much lower order than one would expect from the
construction in the proof. The first simplification arises from the fact that

\ker(\square)
is a completely reducible representation and each of the irreducible
components is contained in
\Lambda^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{V}_{i_{0}}
for some fixed

i_{0}
. Such a component corresponds to a subbundle in
\mathcal{H}^{V}_{k}
, and for sections
of that subbundle,
S
actually coincides with
S_{N}\circ\dots\circ S_{i_{0}+1}\circ S_{i_{0}}
. But still, the expected order of this is much higher than its actual
order. The expected order of the operator
S_{i}
is
\deg(p^{\mathbb{V}}_{k,i})+1
, which
by Kostant’s results roughly equals the number of
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-isotypical components
in the representation
\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})\subset\Lambda^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{V}_{i}
. So the
expected order of
S_{N}\circ\dots\circ S_{i_{0}+1}\circ S_{i_{0}}
roughly is the number of
isotypical components of
\operatorname{im}(\partial^{*})\subset\Lambda^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{V}^{i_{0}}
. But simple homogeneity considerations show that the actual order is

\leq N-i_{0}
.

3.6.
BGG sequences and BGG resolutions

The further developments mainly use formal properties that we have derived already
and hence are very closely parallel to the usual theory of BGG sequences. Thus we
keep the discussion rather brief. By part (2) of Theorem
19
, the splitting
operators have the property that
d^{V}_{F}(S(\alpha))\in\Gamma(\ker(\partial^{*}))
for any

\alpha\in\Gamma(\mathcal{H}^{V}_{k})
, so these can be projected to the homology bundles to define
the BGG operators.

Definition 21
.

Let
VM
be a partial tractor bundle on
(M,F)
induced by a representation
\mathbb{V}
of
G
and let
S
denote the splitting operators from Theorem
19
. For

\alpha\in\Gamma(\mathcal{H}^{V}_{k})
, we define
D^{V}_{F}(\alpha):=\pi_{H}(d^{V}_{F}(S(\alpha))\in\Gamma(\mathcal{H}^{V}_{k+1})
. For each
k
, this defines a natural differential operator

D^{V}_{F}:\Gamma(\mathcal{H}^{V}_{k})\to\Gamma(\mathcal{H}^{V}_{k+1})
called the
k
th partial BGG operator
associated to
\mathbb{V}
. The sequence
(\Gamma(\mathcal{H}^{V}_{*}),D^{V}_{F})
of operators is
called the partial BGG sequence determined by
\mathbb{V}
.

We do not go into a detailed discussion of examples of partial BGG sequences
here. The point about this is that the bundles that are contained a BGG sequence and
the orders of the BGG operators can be easily deduced from the corresponding
information for the usual BGG sequences associated to AHS structures. In the
description of the bundles, one basically only has to replace the tangent bundle by

F
and then apply algebraic constructions accordingly. The orders of the operators
are exactly the same as for standard BGG sequences. Indeed, an irreducible component

W_{1}\subset H_{k}(\mathfrak{g}_{1},\mathbb{V})
has to be contained in
\Lambda^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{V}_{i}
for some fixed number
i_{1}
. Denoting by
i_{2}
the corresponding number for an
irreducible component
W_{2}\subset H_{k+1}(\mathfrak{g}_{1},\mathbb{V})
, the order of the
component of the BGG operator mapping sections of the bundle associated to
W_{1}
to
sections of the one associated to
W_{2}
simply is
i_{2}-i_{1}+1
. In particular, this
shows that for any type of AHS-structure, even the first operator in the BGG sequence
can have arbitrarily high order. Also the description of the principal parts of BGG
operators in terms of distinguished connections for a AHS structure admits and analog
using distinguished partial connections, but we do not go into this here.

The comparison of the BGG sequence to the sequence
(\Omega^{*}_{F}(M,VM),d^{V}_{F})
is
particularly interesting in the case that the latter sequence is a complex,
i.e.
d^{V}_{F}\circ d^{V}_{F}=0
. However, there is also some interesting information available
in general. In particular, in degree zero we obtain an analog of the concept of
normal solutions of the first BGG operator, see
[
13
]
and
[
3
]
.

Theorem 22
.

For a partial tractor bundle
VM\to M
, consider the partial twisted exterior
derivative
d^{V}_{F}
and the induced partial BGG operators
D^{V}_{F}
. Then we have:

(1) The linear subspace
\ker(d^{V}_{F})\cap\Gamma(\ker(\partial^{*}))\subset\Omega^{k}_{F}(M,VM)

projects isomorphically onto a linear subspace of

\ker(D^{V}_{F})\subset\Gamma(\mathcal{H}^{V}_{k})
. In degree zero, this means that the space of
sections of
VM
that are parallel for the partial tractor connection projects
isomorphically onto a subspace of the kernel of the first partial BGG operator.

(2) If
d^{V}_{F}\circ d^{V}_{F}=0
on
\Omega^{k}_{F}(M,VM)
, then
D^{V}_{F}\circ D^{V}_{F}=0
on
\Gamma(\mathcal{H}^{V}_{k})
. If this holds for all
k
, and hence both sequences are complexes, then
their cohomologies are canonically isomorphic.

Proof.

(1) Theorem
19
(1) shows that

\Gamma(\ker(\partial^{*}))\cap\ker(d^{V}_{F})\subset\ker(\square^{R})
and hence
\pi_{H}
is
injective on this subspace. But for
\varphi\in\ker(\square^{R})
we get

\varphi=S(\pi_{H}(\varphi))
and thus
D^{V}_{F}(\pi_{H}(\varphi))=\pi_{H}(d^{V}_{F}(\varphi))=0
, which implies
the claim. In degree zero, we have
\ker(\partial^{*})=VM
and
d^{V}_{F}
is just the
partial tractor connection, so the second statement follows.

(2) For
\alpha\in\Gamma(\mathcal{H}^{V}_{k})
consider

\varphi:=d^{V}_{F}(S(\alpha))\in\Gamma(\ker(\partial^{*}))
, which by definition satisfies

\pi_{H}(\varphi)=D^{V}_{F}(\alpha)
. But by assumption
d^{V}_{F}(\varphi)=0
so part (1) shows that

\pi_{H}(\varphi)=D^{V}_{F}(\alpha)\in\ker(D^{V}_{F})
and hence
D^{V}_{F}\circ D^{V}_{F}=0
on

\Gamma(\mathcal{H}^{V}_{k})
. From the proof of part (1) we also see that
\varphi=S(\pi_{H}(\varphi))
,
which shows that
d^{V}_{F}(S(\alpha))=S(D^{V}_{F}(\alpha))
. Assuming that
d^{V}_{F}\circ d^{V}_{F}=0
in
all degrees, we see that the BGG sequence is a complex and that the splitting
operators satisfy
S\circ D^{V}_{F}=d^{V}_{F}\circ S
. Hence they define a chain map which in
turn induces a map in cohomology.

To prove the statement about cohomology, we first claim that for any element

\varphi\in\Omega^{k}_{F}(M,VM)
, there exists
\tilde{\varphi}\in\Omega^{k-1}_{F}(M,VM)
such that

\varphi+d^{V}_{F}(\tilde{\varphi})\in\Gamma(\ker(\partial^{*}))
. Since
\varphi
is automatically has
homogeneity
\geq k
, the same holds for
\partial^{*}(\varphi)
. Assuming inductively
that
\partial^{*}(\varphi)
is homogeneous of degree
\geq\ell=k+i
, we consider

d^{V}_{F}T_{k-1,i+1}(\partial^{*}(\varphi))
for the operator from part (3) of Corollary

17
. By that Corollary, this has homogeneity
\geq\ell
and applying

\operatorname{gr}_{\ell}
we obtain
\operatorname{gr}_{\ell}(\partial^{*}(\varphi))
. But this shows that

\partial^{*}(\varphi-d^{V}_{F}T_{k-1,i+1}(\partial^{*}(\varphi)))
has homogeneity
\geq\ell+1
,
and inductively the claim follows.

The claim readily implies that any cohomology class for
d^{V}_{F}
has a
representative which is a section of
\ker(\partial^{*})
. If
\varphi
is such a
representative, we get
\varphi=S(\pi_{H}(\varphi))
and
D(\pi_{H}(\varphi))=0
by part (1). This
shows that the map in cohomology induced by
S
is surjective. To prove injectivity,
take
\alpha\in\ker(D^{V}_{F})
and assume that
S(\alpha)=d^{V}_{F}(\psi)
for some

\psi\in\Omega^{k-1}_{F}(M,VM)
. This implies
\partial^{*}(d^{V}_{F}(\psi))=0
and by the
claim and since
d^{V}_{F}\circ d^{V}_{F}=0
, we may assume that

\psi\in\Gamma(\ker(\partial^{*}))
. But then
\psi=S(\pi_{H}(\psi))
by part (2) of
Theorem
19
and hence
\alpha=\pi_{H}(d^{V}_{F}(\psi))=D(\pi_{H}(\psi))
. This shows
injectivity of the map in cohomology induced by
S
.
∎
