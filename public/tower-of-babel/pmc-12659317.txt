KDM6B inhibition enhances chemotherapeutic response in small cell lung cancer via epigenetic regulation of apoptosis and ferroptosis
Authors: Zhongliang Wang, Ziyuan Liu, Yufan Yang, Wenyang Wang, Qian Chen, Shumei Liang, Linlang Guo, Man Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmall cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a highly aggressive malignancy characterized by rapid progression and the frequent emergence of resistance to standard chemotherapeutic agents such as cisplatin (DDP) and etoposide (VP16), resulting in poor clinical outcomes.Methods and resultsTo elucidate mechanisms underlying chemoresistance, we conducted a genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 knockout screen, which identified the histone demethylase KDM6B as a critical mediator of drug resistance in SCLC. Genetic silencing of KDM6B significantly reduced IC₅₀ values of DDP and VP16, particularly in H69-AR cells, and enhanced chemotherapy-induced apoptosis. Consistently, pharmacological inhibition of KDM6B using the dual KDM6A/B inhibitor Gskj1 markedly potentiated the effects of DDP and VP16, while exhibiting minimal cytotoxicity as monotherapy. Overexpression of KDM6B rescued the chemosensitizing effect of Gskj1, thereby excluding confounding contributions from KDM6A. In vivo, the combination of Gskj1 with chemotherapy synergistically suppressed tumor growth without detectable systemic toxicity. To explore the downstream regulatory pathways, we performed transcriptome analysis via RNA-seq followed by KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, which revealed that Gskj1 treatment modulates key oncogenic signaling pathways. Integration of RNA-seq with H3K27me3 ChIP-seq data identified EGR3 as a direct epigenetic target of KDM6B inhibition. STRING analysis further suggested that EGR3 is co-expressed with c-FOS. Functional assays, including qRT-PCR, Western blotting, Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), and dual-luciferase reporter assays, confirmed that EGR3 transcriptionally activates c-FOS, establishing an EGR3/c-FOS regulatory axis downstream of KDM6B. Mechanistically, inhibition of this axis enhanced chemosensitivity by promoting apoptosis, as evidenced by activation of caspase signaling, and by inducing ferroptosis through downregulation of GPX4, upregulation of ACSL4, lipid peroxidation, and modulation of HO-1. Rescue experiments with Z-VAD and ferrostatin-1 further validated that both apoptosis and ferroptosis contribute to the chemosensitizing effects of KDM6B inhibition.ConclusionFinally, in vivo experiments using patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models demonstrated that Gskj1 effectively enhances the antitumor efficacy of chemotherapy in SCLC, providing compelling evidence for the clinical potential of targeting KDM6B to overcome chemoresistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-025-01496-6.

Introduction

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma that accounts for approximately 15% of all lung cancers, predominantly affecting smokers [1–4]. Characterized by rapid tumor growth, early dissemination, and a high proliferative index, SCLC remains one of the most lethal malignancies. At diagnosis, over 65% of patients present with extensive-stage disease [5, 6]. Although frontline chemotherapy with cisplatin (DDP) and etoposide (VP16) initially achieves high response rates, most patients relapse within months due to acquired chemoresistance, resulting in a median survival of less than one year [7, 8]. Recent advances in immunotherapy have offered some benefit, yet only a subset of patients respond, and resistance to immune checkpoint blockade further limits efficacy [9, 10]. These challenges underscore the urgent need to identify novel therapeutic targets and combination strategies to overcome treatment resistance in SCLC.

One promising approach involves integrating chemotherapy with molecularly targeted agents to enhance tumor cell vulnerability [11, 12]. High-throughput functional genetic screens have proven effective in elucidating mechanisms of drug resistance and identifying synthetic lethal interactions. While RNA interference (RNAi)-based platforms using shRNA libraries have historically facilitated gene silencing studies, their limitations—such as incomplete knockdown and off-target effects—can compromise screening accuracy [13]. In contrast, the CRISPR/Cas9 system enables precise and complete gene disruption, allowing unbiased genome-wide loss-of-function screens to identify essential genes involved in cancer proliferation, survival, and drug resistance [14–20].

In this study, we performed a genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 screen in SCLC cells resistant to DDP and VP16 to uncover novel synergistic targets that could enhance chemotherapeutic efficacy. Our screen identified lysine demethylase 6B (KDM6B), an H3K27-specific demethylase, as a critical modulator of chemoresistance. KDM6B regulates chromatin structure and gene transcription, and has been implicated in differentiation, oncogenesis, and immune modulation [21–24]. While pharmacological inhibition of KDM6B has shown antitumor effects in several cancers, its therapeutic potential in SCLC—particularly in combination with chemotherapy—remains poorly defined.

Here, we demonstrate that targeting KDM6B using the small-molecule inhibitor Gskj1 sensitizes chemoresistant SCLC cells to standard chemotherapy by enhancing apoptosis and ferroptosis. Mechanistically, we show that KDM6B inhibition increases H3K27me3 deposition at the EGR3 locus, suppressing the EGR3/c-FOS axis, a previously unrecognized regulator of chemoresistance. These findings provide a rationale for integrating KDM6B inhibition into therapeutic regimens to overcome drug resistance in SCLC.

Results

CRISPR/Cas9 screening identifies KDM6B as a key mediator of chemoresistance in SCLC

To identify genes that contribute to chemoresistance in small cell lung cancer (SCLC), we performed a genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 loss-of-function screen in the cisplatin (DDP)- and etoposide (VP16)-resistant H69-AR cell line (Fig. 1A). The screen included a total of 20,540 sgRNAs across treatment and control groups. Comparative analysis between DDP/VP16-treated and DMSO-treated groups revealed that 11,241 sgRNAs were negatively enriched, suggesting their potential role in promoting chemoresistance (Supplementary Table S1). Among these, 107 sgRNAs exhibited a log2 fold change ≤ –2 with statistical significance (P < 0.01), including genes such as DCC, KDM6B, IDO1, TRMT12, and AMH (Fig. 1B). To prioritize candidates for further investigation, we focused on KDM6B and IDO1 due to the availability of pharmacological inhibitors. Protein expression analysis using data from The Human Protein Atlas revealed that KDM6B is markedly overexpressed in lung cancer tissues compared to normal tissues, whereas IDO1 expression remained consistently low across both [25] (Supplementary Fig. 1C,D). In addition, pan-cancer analysis based on The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data showed that high KDM6B expression is associated with poor prognosis in multiple solid tumors, including melanoma and ovarian cancer [26] (Supplementary Fig. 1A,B). Given the limited efficacy and low expression profile of IDO1 in SCLC, we selected Gskj1, a selective KDM6B inhibitor with demonstrated antitumor activity and a favorable safety profile in solid tumors, for further study [27, 28]. Analysis of KDM6B expression across four SCLC cell lines using data from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) revealed heterogeneous expression levels, suggesting a possible correlation between KDM6B expression and intrinsic drug resistance (Fig. 1C,D) [29]. Based on these data, we hypothesized that KDM6B may act as a pivotal regulator of chemotherapy resistance in SCLC. To validate this, we selected two drug-resistant SCLC cell lines—H69-AR and H446-DDP—which were previously developed from their parental counterparts, NCI-H69 (A-type) and NCI-H446 (N-type), respectively (Fig. 1D) [30, 31]. Quantitative PCR analysis confirmed that KDM6B expression was significantly upregulated in H69-AR compared to NCI-H69 (Fig. 1E), whereas in H446-DDP, KDM6B expression showed only a modest increase relative to NCI-H446 (Fig. 1F). KDM6B silencing enhances chemotherapeutic sensitivity in drug-resistant SCLC cells

To further investigate the functional role of KDM6B in mediating chemoresistance in small cell lung cancer (SCLC), we employed small interfering RNA (siRNA) to selectively knock down KDM6B expression in two drug-resistant SCLC cell lines, H69-AR and H446-DDP. Prior to genetic manipulation, we confirmed the chemoresistant phenotypes of these cell lines by determining the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values of cisplatin (DDP) and etoposide (VP16) compared to their respective parental counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 2A,B). Following siRNA transfection, both qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses confirmed efficient knockdown of KDM6B at the mRNA and protein levels in both cell lines (Fig. 2A,B). Subsequent drug sensitivity assays revealed that KDM6B silencing significantly reduced the IC₅₀ values for both DDP and VP16 in H69-AR cells (Figs. 2C,D, 3A,C), indicating enhanced chemosensitivity. Although KDM6B knockdown also led to modest reductions in DDP and VP16 IC₅₀ values in H446-DDP cells (Figs. 2E,F, 3B,D), the effect was less pronounced than in H69-AR cells. To assess the functional impact of KDM6B depletion on cellular proliferation, we performed Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), colony formation, and EdU incorporation assays. KDM6B knockdown alone had a limited effect on cell viability; however, its combination with DDP or VP16 resulted in a markedly enhanced suppression of cell growth, surpassing the effects observed with chemotherapy or knockdown alone (Figs. 2G–J, 3E–G). Furthermore, flow cytometry-based apoptosis assays demonstrated that combined treatment elicited the highest proportion of apoptotic cells among all experimental groups (Fig. 3H–J). Interestingly, we consistently observed that the chemosensitizing effects of KDM6B silencing were more substantial in H69-AR than in H446-DDP cells across all phenotypic assays. This differential response may reflect the relative increase in KDM6B expression in resistant cells compared to their parental counterparts, as previously shown (Fig. 1E,F). We hypothesize that the efficacy of KDM6B inhibition in reversing chemoresistance may be contingent upon the magnitude of KDM6B upregulation in resistant sublines. Collectively, these findings support a critical role for KDM6B in maintaining the chemoresistant phenotype of SCLC and demonstrate that its targeted silencing can markedly potentiate the cytotoxic effects of standard chemotherapeutic agents. Gskj1 re-sensitizes chemoresistant SCLC cells to chemotherapy

Our investigation into KDM6B's therapeutic potential in SCLC involved treating the chemoresistant cell lines H69-AR and H446-DDP with the KDM6A/B inhibitor Gskj1. The determined IC50 values for Gskj1 were 430 μM for H69-AR and 327 μM for H446-DDP (Fig. 4A). Notably, applying Gskj1 at an approximate IC20 (~ 50 μM) significantly lowered the IC50 values of both cisplatin (DDP) and etoposide (VP16) in these cell lines (Fig. 4B-E, Supplementary Fig. 2C–F). Further assessments using cell proliferation and colony formation assays revealed that combining Gskj1 with chemotherapy markedly suppressed the proliferation of resistant SCLC cells more effectively than any single treatment or the negative control (Fig. 4F–I). This was corroborated by immunofluorescence EDU assays, which showed the lowest proliferation rates in cells treated with both Gskj1 and chemotherapeutic agents (Fig. 4K,L). To quantify the synergistic effects of combining Gskj1 with chemotherapy, we employed Synergy Finder version 3.0 [32], analyzing cell viability across increasing concentrations of Gskj1, DDP, and VP16.32 The highest single agent (HSA) model indicated a strong synergistic interaction (scores> 10) between Gskj1 and both DDP and VP16 in the studied cell lines (Fig. 4J, Supplementary Fig. 2G,H). Additionally, scratch healing assays demonstrated that the combination of Gskj1 with chemotherapy significantly impeded the migration of H446-DDP cells compared to other treatment groups at 24 and 48 h (Supplementary Fig. 3A,B). Because no selective KDM6B inhibitors are currently available, we sought to exclude the potential confounding effect of KDM6A by overexpressing KDM6B through plasmid transfection in cells treated with Gskj1 and chemotherapy. Western blot analysis confirmed successful KDM6B overexpression and further demonstrated that Gskj1 markedly reduced KDM6A expression (Fig. 4M). Consistent with this, CCK-8 assays showed that the reintroduction of KDM6B significantly restored cell viability, bringing it back to the level observed with DDP + VP16 treatment alone (Fig. 4N). These findings indicate that KDM6A does not substantially contribute to the chemoresistant phenotype or alter the sensitivity of resistant SCLC cells to DDP and VP16. Enhanced in vivo tumor suppression by Gskj1 and chemotherapy combination

Investigating Gskj1's therapeutic potential, we established a subcutaneous model of chemotherapy-resistant SCLC in nude mice using H69-AR cells. Upon reaching an average tumor volume of 100 mm3, mice were allocated into four distinct treatment groups as outlined in Fig. 5A. Following a 21-day treatment period, results demonstrated a pronounced inhibitory effect on tumor growth in the group receiving combined treatment of Gskj1 and chemotherapy, compared to moderate or mild inhibition observed with chemotherapy or Gskj1 alone, respectively (Fig. 5B,C). The weight of the excised tumors further corroborated the enhanced efficacy of the combination treatment, showcasing significant tumor growth suppression (Fig. 5D). Importantly, monitoring of mouse body weight throughout the treatment indicated no adverse effects from any treatment regimen. Notably, the negative control group exhibited considerable variability in weight, highlighting instances of weight loss in untreated mice (Fig. 5E). To validate the expression of KDM6B in each tumor group, immunofluorescence was performed, confirming the effective inhibition by Gskj1 in nude mice (Fig. 5F,G). Subsequently, Ki-67 immunohistochemical staining showed a significantly lower rate of Ki-67 positive cells in the drug combination group compared to other groups (Fig. 5H,I), which is consistent with our in vivo and in vitro experimental results. KDM6B inhibition enhances chemosensitivity via epigenetic suppression of the EGR3–c-FOS axis

To elucidate the mechanistic basis of Gskj1-mediated chemosensitization in drug-resistant small cell lung cancer (SCLC) cells, we first performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on H69-AR cells treated with either Gskj1 or DMSO. Differential expression analysis (cutoff: P < 0.05 and |log₂FoldChange|> 1) identified a substantial number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Fig. 6A; Supplementary Table S2). Subsequent KEGG and GSEA enrichment analyses revealed significant alterations in pathways associated with tumor progression, apoptosis, and cell death (Fig. 6B,C; Supplementary Fig. 4A,B). To further delineate epigenetically regulated targets of KDM6A/B inhibition, we leveraged publicly available ChIP-seq data (GSE150600) [22], focusing on H3K27me3 enrichment in DMSO- versus KDM6A/B inhibitor-treated samples. We observed a global increase in H3K27me3 deposition following KDM6A/B inhibition, with the strongest enrichment localized to broad domains, promoter regions, and ± 3 kb around transcription start sites (TSSs) (Fig. 6D,E). By intersecting these regions with downregulated transcripts from RNA-seq, we identified 13 candidate targets, including LRRN4, SPEF1, and EGR3 (Fig. 6F). STRING-based protein–protein interaction analysis revealed that among these candidates, EGR3 and RBFOX3 were co-expressed with c-FOS, with confidence scores > 0.7 (Fig. 6G). Moreover, predicted co-expression between KDM6B and c-FOS was also observed (Supplementary Fig. 4C). ChIP-seq visualization confirmed marked enrichment of H3K27me3 across the EGR3 promoter and gene body after KDM6A/B inhibition, whereas RBFOX3 showed only modest enrichment restricted to gene body regions. Other oncogenic regulators such as SOX2, MYC, GATA3, and TP53 also displayed altered H3K27me3 signals, suggesting that Gskj1-mediated KDM6B inhibition affects multiple pathways linked to tumor progression, stemness, and apoptosis (Fig. 6H,I). Based on these findings, we prioritized the EGR3–c-FOS axis for experimental validation. To validate this predicted regulatory axis, we first confirmed that Gskj1 treatment significantly reduced both EGR3 and FOS expression in H69-AR and H446-DDP cells, as demonstrated by qRT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. 4D,E). Western blotting further corroborated the downregulation of EGR3 and c-FOS proteins, accompanied by increased H3K27me3 deposition (Fig. 6J). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays verified a direct physical interaction between EGR3 and c-FOS proteins (Fig. 6K). To further examine transcriptional regulation, we performed dual-luciferase reporter assays. Overexpression of EGR3 significantly enhanced c-FOS promoter activity compared with control, whereas mutation of the c-FOS promoter markedly abolished this effect (P < 0.05), confirming that EGR3 directly binds to and transcriptionally activates the c-FOS promoter (Fig. 6L). Gskj1 promotes chemosensitivity by inducing apoptosis and ferroptosis through suppression of the EGR3/c-FOS axis

Given prior evidence implicating c-FOS in the regulation of apoptosis and ferroptosis, we explored these pathways in greater depth [33]. Notably, RNA-seq data identified changes in ferroptosis- and apoptosis-related genes, including AKT2 and HMOX1, which were confirmed by qRT-PCR (Fig. 7A). Western blot analysis revealed that Gskj1 treatment increased levels of pro-apoptotic proteins (cleaved PARP, cleaved caspase-3, Bax) and decreased anti-apoptotic Bcl-2. These effects were significantly amplified when Gskj1 was combined with cisplatin and etoposide (Fig. 7B). Flow cytometry corroborated these findings, showing the highest proportion of apoptotic cells in the triple combination treatment group (Fig. 7C–E). In vivo validation using xenograft tumor tissues (from Fig. 5B) demonstrated consistent protein expression patterns, with immunohistochemistry revealing a pronounced reduction in Bcl-2 expression in the Gskj1 + DDP + VP16 group compared to other treatment arms (Fig. 7F,G). Similarly, TUNEL staining confirmed that the triple combination induced the highest level of apoptosis (Fig. 7H,I). To further evaluate ferroptosis, we analyzed the expression of canonical markers GPX4 and ACSL4 via Western blotting. Gskj1 treatment led to a substantial downregulation of GPX4, an enzyme critical for lipid peroxide detoxification, and upregulation of ACSL4, which promotes the esterification of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), sensitizing cells to lipid peroxidation [34, 35] (Fig. 7B). Using a malondialdehyde (MDA) assay kit to quantify lipid peroxidation, we observed that in both H69-AR and H446-DDP cells, the triple combination treatment produced the highest MDA levels, with statistically significant increases compared to all other groups (Fig. 8A,B). These findings strongly support the induction of ferroptosis by Gskj1 through disruption of lipid metabolism and antioxidant defense mechanisms. Interestingly, we also observed a significant upregulation of HMOX1 (HO-1) in both RNA-seq and qRT-PCR analyses (Fig. 7A), further validated by Western Blot (Fig. 7B) and ELISA (Fig. 8C). While HO-1 is often regarded as a protective response to oxidative stress, its byproduct—labile iron—may paradoxically amplify oxidative damage under conditions of reduced GPX4 activity, thereby exacerbating ferroptotic cell death [36, 37]. To further confirm the roles of apoptosis and ferroptosis, we conducted rescue experiments using the apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD and the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), followed by CCK-8 assays. Both inhibitors significantly improved cell viability in the triple treatment group, and their combined application produced an even greater protective effect, although viability did not return to baseline levels (Fig. 8D). Collectively, these data suggest that Gskj1 enhances SCLC chemosensitivity by epigenetically repressing the EGR3/c-FOS axis and promoting both apoptosis and ferroptosis, representing a promising strategy to overcome drug resistance in SCLC. Materials and methods

Cell line culture

Cell lines representative of small cell lung cancer, including H69, H69AR and H446, were acquired from the American Cell Culture Center (ATCC, USA). H446-DDP is a cisplatin-resistant cell line developed through incremental exposure to increasing concentrations of cisplatin in the culture medium derived from NCI-H446. Its resistance phenotype is sustained in the presence of cisplatin. These cells were propagated in RPMI-1640 medium (Biomed, Israel) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Procell, China), with the exception of H69AR, which was maintained in the same medium but with a increased FBS concentration of 20%. Cultivation of all cell lines occurred under controlled conditions at 37 °C with a 5% CO2 atmosphere in a humidified incubator. Notably, the cisplatin-resistant variants, H446DDP and H69AR, were derived through the gradual escalation of cisplatin exposure, reaching a final concentration of 0.5 µg/mL. This resistance phenotype was subsequently preserved through continuous cisplatin exposure.

Cellular transfection procedure

We initiated the transfection process by acquiring small interfering RNA (siRNA) from Guangzhou Hanyi Biological Technology. For the transfection cocktail, we diluted the siRNA in 1 × transfer buffer (SignaGen, China) to which we added 5 μl of siRNA solution. Subsequently, 2.4 μl of PepMute transfection reagent (SignaGen, China) was introduced into the mixture. After a 15-min incubation at room temperature to allow complex formation, this preparation was applied to target cells seeded in well plates. The cells were then incubated for 48 h, during which their response to the transfection was monitored periodically.

The siRNA sequences targeting the KDM6B gene were as follows:

-si-KDM6B1: 5′-AGACCUCGUGUGGAUUAAUTTAUUAAUCCACACGAGGUCUTT-3′

-si-KDM6B2: 5′-UGCGAUGUGGAGGUGUUUATTUAAACACCUCCACAUCGCATT-3′

-si-KDM6B3: 5′-GAGCACAAACGGAACUAUGTTCAUAGUUCCGUUUGUGCUCTT-3′

CRISPR/Cas9 screening and quantification analysis

We employed the Human CRISPR Knockdown Pooled Library (GeCKO v2) lentivirus provided by GenScript, China, for genome-wide CRISPR knockdown screening. Utilizing a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.3, we infected 3 × 10^7 H69-AR cells per sample, achieving an extensive 250-fold coverage of the library. Following this, cells were selected with 1.5 μg/ml puromycin (Solarbio, China) for 72 h, after which control samples (day 0) were collected for further analysis. Subsequently, the surviving cell population was divided into two groups: a negative control group and a chemotherapy treatment group. The chemotherapy group was further subdivided to receive either VP16 (50 μg/ml) or DDP (1 μg/ml) treatments over a period of 7 days. Post-treatment, genomic DNA was extracted using the Blood and Cell Culture DNA Kit from Qiagen, Germany. This DNA then underwent two successive rounds of PCR amplification to prepare for sequencing. The resulting PCR products were purified using both the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit and the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Purified samples were processed through second-generation sequencing to gather data. For data analysis, we utilized the MAGeCK algorithm, which facilitated the identification and quantification of knockdown effects across the genome.

Chemicals and inhibitors

Cisplatin (DDP) and etoposide (VP16) were purchased from Selleck and dissolved in DMSO to a final concentration of 5 mg/ml and 10 mg/ml. The KDM6B inhibitor, Gskj1, was purchased from MCE and dissolved in DMSO to a final concentration of 10 mM.

Cell viability, synergistic effect and 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50)

Cells were inoculated in 96-well plates at appropriate density, treated with different drug combinations and incubated for a certain period of time, and then incubated for 1 h with CCK8 reagent (MCE), after adding a certain dose according to the instructions, and the absorbance at 450 nm was detected. The cell viability was calculated with the absorbance value of the negative control. The synergistic effect of DDP/VP16 with Gskj1 was calculated by the single-drug model HSA in the SynergyFinder web tool (http: //SynergyFinder.firm.fi).The IC50 value was calculated by GraphPad Prism9 software to calculate the cells concentration of the drug that inhibited the half.

Clone formation assay

After inoculation of cells at appropriate density in 6-well plates, different combinations of chemical reagents were added to the medium and incubated in a cell culture incubator at 5% CO2, 37 °C. After replacing the fresh medium after 24 h, the cell survival and growth status was examined every 2–3 days and 1 ml of fresh medium was replaced. After 14 days of observation, when the number of cells in the colony was about 50, the medium was removed and washed three times with pre-warmed PBS, followed by addition of 4% paraformaldehyde for cell fixation for 20 min. The paraformaldehyde was then removed and washed, and staining was performed using 0.4% crystal violet solution for 20 min, followed by washing with PBS until the background was no longer purple. Photographs were taken of each well and then calculated using ImageJ 8.0.

Scratch healing assay

Appropriate amount of cells were inoculated in six-well plates with different chemical combinations and incubated for 24 h. When the density of cells in the field of view under the microscope reached more than 90%, the tip of the pipette was crossed perpendicularly with the horizontal line at the bottom using a 200 mL pipette tip, and the plate was incubated with 2% low serum for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. Subsequently, the medium was discarded, and the distance of the cells' migration was observed under a bright-field microscope.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Appropriate amount of cells were inoculated into six-well plates with 20 mm cell slides pre-positioned, and when the cell coverage reached 50–60%, the medium was removed and added to low-serum medium for 24 h of starvation to achieve cell cycle normalization, then the medium was replaced with 1640 medium containing 10% FBS with various chemical reagents and drug combinations for 72 h of treatment, during which time the medium with serum was supplemented. medium was supplemented with serum-containing medium. Subsequently, Edu solution was configured and the incubation time was determined based on the cell growth rate. After labeling the cells, the liquid was discarded and washed, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, washed, permeabilized with 5% Trinton X-100 for 10 min, washed again, and then configured with Click-iT reaction solution according to the instructions, and the reaction was carried out for 30 min at room temperature in a light-proof environment. Subsequently DNA re-staining was performed, Hoechsr3334 was diluted according to the instructions, incubated for 20 min at room temperature away from light, washed and blocked by adding an anti-fluorescence quencher. The cells were observed and photographed under a fluorescence microscope.

RNA isolation and real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR)

RNA extraction kit, reverse pathway reagent and real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) dye SYBR Green were purchased from EZBioscience. RNA extraction, RNA reverse transcription and PCR quantification were performed by mixing and proportioning according to the manufacturer's instructions. Primers were purchased from Beijing Liuhe Huada Gene Company.

The sequences of all forward and reactive primers in their experiments were, respectively:

KDM6B F:TTGGGCAACTGTACGAGTCAG

KDM6B R:CCATAGTTCCGTTTGTGCTCAAG

GAPDH F:AAATCAAGTGGGGCGATGCT

GAPDH R:CAAATGAGCCCCAGCCTTCT

Relative quantification was calculated as follows:ΔCt [ΔCt = Ct (A)—Ct (GAPDH)]. The relative expression was determined as 2—ΔΔCt, where ΔΔCt = ΔCt (test sample)—ΔCt (reference sample).

Western blotting assay with antibodies

Cells were collected in 1.5 mL EP tubes, and cytoplasmic proteins were extracted using RIPA lysate buffer (Beyotime, China) mixed with the appropriate protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors (Cwbiotech, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA protein concentration assay kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Equal amounts of proteins were separated in 10% SDSPAGE, and then the proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes, which were closed with 5% skimmed milk powder for 2 h at room temperature, and then incubated with a 1:1000 dilution of protein primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. It was washed 3 times with TBST, then incubated with conventional secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature, and then washed 3 times with TBST. Next, the signal intensity of the membrane was detected using an ECL (enhanced chemiluminescence) Western Substrate (Pierce, Rockford, IL) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) was performed to evaluate the physical interaction between EGR3 and c-FOS in drug-resistant SCLC cell lines. H69-AR and H446-DDP cells were lysed in ice-cold NP-40 lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4; 150 mM NaCl; 1% NP-40; 1 mM EDTA) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Thermo Scientific, #78,441). Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min and then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove debris. For each immunoprecipitation reaction, 500–1000 µg of total protein was incubated overnight at 4 °C with 2–5 µg of anti-EGR3 antibody (Abmart, #PK44695) or anti-c-FOS antibody (Abmart, #T56596). The next day, 30 µL of protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Scientific, #88,802) were added and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Beads were washed four times with lysis buffer and then boiled in 2 × SDS loading buffer for 10 min to elute bound proteins. Immunoprecipitated complexes were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, #IPVH00010). Western blotting was performed using reciprocal antibodies (anti-c-FOS for EGR3 IP and anti-EGR3 for c-FOS IP). Signals were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (Bio-Rad, #1705061) and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Input lysates (5–10% of total protein) were included as positive controls.

Apoptosis assay

Cells were obtained after intervention by different combinations of chemical reagents and were collected and washed after digestion using EDTA-free trypsin. Cells were resuspended using Binding Buffer in Apoptosis Kit (ThermoFisher) and stained using Annexin V for 15 min under light protection and 4 °C, then PI dye was added and stained for 5 min in the same environment. Finally the stained cell samples were assayed for apoptosis using Beckman flow analyzer.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) data analysis

ChIP-seq datasets targeting H3K27me3 were obtained from the GEO database (accession number GSE150600). Raw sequencing reads were first quality-checked using FastQC (v0.11.9) and adapter sequences were trimmed using Trim Galore (v0.6.7) with default parameters. Clean reads were then aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using Bowtie2 (v2.4.5) with default settings. Aligned reads with a mapping quality score (MAPQ) < 30, duplicates, and mitochondrial reads were removed using SAMtools (v1.15) and Picard (v2.27.4). Peaks representing H3K27me3-enriched regions were called with MACS2 (v2.2.7.1) using the broad peak calling mode and a q-value cutoff of 0.05. Differential peak analysis between control (DMSO) and KDM6A/B-inhibited (Gskj1-like) treatment groups was performed using DiffBind (v3.6.5) in R. Peaks with an absolute log₂ fold change > 1 and FDR < 0.05 were considered significantly differential. Peak annotation relative to genomic features (promoters, gene bodies, enhancers) was conducted using ChIPseeker (v1.30.3). Visualization of peak distribution around transcription start sites (TSS ± 3 kb) and gene bodies was performed with EnrichedHeatmap (v1.28.0).

To integrate ChIP-seq and RNA-seq data, genes with significant downregulation (|log₂FC|> 1, P < 0.05) from RNA-seq analysis were intersected with genes harboring increased H3K27me3 peaks to identify potential epigenetically repressed targets.

All statistical analyses and visualization were conducted in R (v4.3.1).

Elisa quantitative assay

Using HMOX1 Elisa test kit. The treated sample, standard, and HRP-labeled detection antibody were added sequentially, incubated at room temperature and washed thoroughly. The color was developed with the substrate TMB, which was converted to blue by catalysis of peroxidase and to final yellow by the action of acid. The color shade is positively correlated with HMOX1 in the sample. Absorbance (OD value) was measured at 450 nm using an enzyme meter, followed by calculation of sample concentration.

Animal experiments

In this experiment, human small cell lung cancer drug-resistant strain H69AR was used to construct a hormonal mouse model. When the cells were grown to 80% fusion rate with good cell morphology, trypsin digestion was performed and centrifuged. Subsequently, the supernatant was discarded and the cells were resuspended with basal medium. The cell density was adjusted to 5 × 107/mL, and the cell suspension was placed on ice to maintain cell viability. Subsequently, 100 μL (i.e., 5 × 106) of H69AR cells were subcutaneously inoculated into the right lower limb of female BALB/c nude mice as soon as possible (within 1 h) to establish a female BALB/c nude mouse model containing H69AR cells. Each nude mouse carrying a tumor was marked with an ear pin, and the swelling of the injection site was observed every other day. Tumor volume (V) was measured with vernier calipers, V = LW2/2 (L and W are the length and width of the tumor, respectively). When the tumor size and volume averaged about 100 mm3, the hormonal mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 6), and all drugs were administered by intraperitoneal injection of saline (control 200 μL), DDP + VP16, Gskj1, VP16 + Ddp + Gskj1 (etoposide + cisplatin + KDM6B inhibitor), with all drugs taken by intraperitoneal administration, in which DDP vs. VP16 was administered every 3 days, GSKJ every 2 days, DDP was administered at a dose of 2.5 mg/kg, VP16 was administered at a dose of 5 mg/kg, and Gskj1 was administered at a dose of 50 mg/kg. a total of 21 days of monitoring was performed. And then the mice were executed using euthanasia, the tumors were collected after dissection and photographed, and the tumors in each group were weighed. All experiments were approved by the Pearl River Municipal Committee for the Protection and Utilization of Animals (LAEC-2022–164).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tumor tissues were fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded series of ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 20 min at 95 °C. After cooling, sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase activity and then incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin to prevent nonspecific binding. The sections were subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific for the target proteins. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), sections were incubated with biotinylated secondary antibodies followed by streptavidin-HRP conjugate. The antigen–antibody complexes were visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen, and counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin. Finally, sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted for microscopic evaluation. The staining intensity and percentage of positive cells were quantified using ImageJ software.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The human c-FOS promoter region (− 2000 to + 1 relative to the transcription start site) was cloned into the pGL3-Basic vector to generate the wild-type reporter (pGL3-FOS-WT). A mutant construct (pGL3-FOS-MUT) was generated by replacing the predicted EGR3-binding motifs with an A/T-rich sequence, and all constructs were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. HEK293T cells were seeded in 24-well plates and co-transfected with 500 ng of reporter plasmid (WT or MUT), 500 ng of pcDNA3.1-EGR3 or empty vector, and 50 ng of pRL-TK (Renilla luciferase) using Lipofectamine 3000. After 24 h, Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured with the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Relative promoter activity was expressed as the ratio of Firefly to Renilla luciferase activity, normalized to the WT + Vector group.

Lipid peroxidation (MDA) assay

Cellular MDA levels were measured using the MDA Assay Kit (microplate method; ADS-W-YH002) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, H69AR and H446DDP cells were seeded to reach ~ 70–80% confluence and treated in biological triplicate with one of four conditions: vehicle (DMSO), Gskj1, DDP + VP16, or Gskj1 + DDP + VP16 (drug concentrations and treatment durations as indicated in the main text). After treatment, cells were harvested and counted; for each sample, ~ 5.0 *10^6 cells were resuspended in 1.0 mL kit-provided extraction buffer and homogenized on ice. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 *g for 10 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was collected for assay. For the colorimetric reaction, 200 µL of sample supernatant was mixed with 300 µL of the kit working solution in an Eppendorf tube, incubated in a preheated water bath at 90–95 °C for 30 min, then cooled on ice and centrifuged at 12,000*g for 10 min; 200 µL of the clarified reaction mixture was transferred to a 96-well plate and absorbance was read at 532 nm and 600 nm. The corrected absorbance was calculated as ΔA = A532 − A600. MDA content was calculated according to the manufacturer’s formula; for normalization we used the kit conversion factor: MDA (nmol /mL) = 32.3 × ΔA. MDA was also normalized to total protein (nmol/mg protein) using BCA-determined protein concentration and the kit formula. All values are presented as mean ± SEM (n = 3 biological replicates). Statistical comparisons among the four groups were performed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, and p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Screening and quantification of RNA sequencing

H69AR cells were cultured in medium containing 20% FBS with 1640 RPMI and processed after transfection of siRNA, followed by sequencing of extracted RNA using RNA-seq technology, and processing of the data using bioinformatics tools, including screening of differentially expressed genes and pathway enrichment analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software 21.0 (Abbott Laboratories, USA) or GraphPad Prism9 (GraphPad software Inc. USA). Differences were compared using independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA. Asterisks are leveraged to depict the significant differences (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ns: nonsignificant).

Discussion

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC), renowned for its aggressive clinical course, presents formidable challenges in treatment due to its inherent resistance to conventional chemotherapy and radiotherapy, alongside a high propensity for recurrence [2]. The prevalent issue of late-stage detection exacerbates these challenges, further complicating therapeutic intervention [5]. While recent strides in molecular targeted therapy and immunotherapy herald a new era in oncology, offering promising avenues for malignancy management, their efficacy in SCLC has been tempered by the tumor's robust chemoresistance mechanisms [38, 39]. This underscores an urgent need for the identification of novel therapeutic targets to enhance treatment outcomes in SCLC cohorts.

The advent of CRISPR/Cas9 technology as a paradigm-shifting gene-editing tool offers a novel lens through which the molecular underpinnings of SCLC can be elucidated [15]. Leveraging this cutting-edge technology, our study embarked on a first-of-its-kind genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 screening, targeting 19,050 genes to unveil potential determinants of chemotherapy resistance in SCLC. Although multiple candidates emerged from this screen, including DCC, TRMT12, and AMH, we prioritized KDM6B because of its marked overexpression in lung cancer tissues compared to normal tissues and the availability of pharmacological inhibitors, making it a more clinically actionable target.

Central to our findings is the KDM6B gene, a histone demethylase implicated in the epigenetic regulation of cancer pathogenesis across various tumor types [40, 41]. Our rigorous in vitro and in vivo assessments demonstrate that silencing KDM6B significantly enhances chemotherapeutic efficacy, mitigates cell proliferation, and augments apoptotic pathways. Mechanistically, we identified EGR3 as a key downstream effector through integrative RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, and protein–protein interaction analyses. Among the 13 candidate genes intersecting these datasets, EGR3 showed the strongest co-expression relationship with c-FOS. Using dual-luciferase assays and co-immunoprecipitation, we confirmed the transcriptional regulation of c-FOS by EGR3, and functional rescue experiments with apoptosis and ferroptosis inhibitors further validated the dual contribution of the EGR3/c-FOS axis.

In addition to apoptosis, our work highlights ferroptosis as an important form of cell death regulated by KDM6B. While initial evidence came from GPX4 and ACSL4 expression changes, we substantiated this with direct measurements of lipid peroxidation using MDA assays [42], as well as rescue experiments with ferroptosis inhibitors. These data strengthen the evidence that KDM6B inhibition simultaneously promotes apoptotic and ferroptotic responses, offering a two-pronged approach to overcoming chemoresistance.

A key consideration in our study is the specificity of Gskj1, which inhibits both KDM6A and KDM6B [21]. Although KDM6B-selective inhibitors are not yet available, we addressed this by combining pharmacological and genetic approaches. siRNA-mediated knockdown of KDM6B phenocopied the effects of Gskj1, and KDM6B overexpression reversed the phenotype. Moreover, we confirmed that KDM6A expression remained largely unchanged under treatment conditions. Together, these findings strongly suggest that KDM6B is the predominant driver of the observed phenotypes, although we acknowledge that a minor contribution from KDM6A cannot be completely excluded.

The implications of our research are twofold. Firstly, it propels the KDM6B gene to the forefront of potential therapeutic targets for SCLC, offering a novel avenue for drug development. Secondly, it reaffirms the utility of CRISPR/Cas9 screening as a robust methodological framework for uncovering the genetic basis of chemoresistance in cancer. Clinically, although analysis of public SCLC cohorts revealed a trend toward poorer survival in patients with higher KDM6B expression, this did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited sample sizes, incomplete treatment annotation, and the rapid clinical course of SCLC. Larger and well-annotated clinical datasets will be required to validate the prognostic and predictive relevance of KDM6B expression.

However, it is imperative to acknowledge the limitations inherent to our study. While our findings are promising, the translation of gene-editing therapies from bench to bedside necessitates comprehensive clinical trials to ascertain safety, efficacy, and optimal delivery mechanisms. Moreover, the lack of KDM6B-selective inhibitors remains a limitation, underscoring the need for future drug development efforts. Further studies should also extend our findings to patient-derived xenografts and investigate how KDM6B-mediated regulation of apoptosis and ferroptosis may influence tumor–immune interactions in the microenvironment.

In conclusion, our study not only sheds light on the potential of KDM6B as a therapeutic target in combating SCLC but also sets the stage for future research to explore the full spectrum of gene candidates identified through CRISPR/Cas9 screening. By demonstrating the mechanistic role of the KDM6B/EGR3/c-FOS axis in coordinating apoptosis and ferroptosis, we provide novel insights into epigenetic vulnerabilities in SCLC. Future work focusing on clinical validation, the development of KDM6B-selective inhibitors, and integration with immunotherapy may help translate these findings into effective therapeutic strategies for patients.
