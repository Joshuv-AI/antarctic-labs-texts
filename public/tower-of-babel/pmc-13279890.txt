Meta-EEGs: A structured approach for processing high-volume EEG data
Authors: Palak Handa, Manya Joshi, Esha Gupta, Ramona Woitek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electroencephalography (EEG) is widely used clinically and in research, including AI-driven applications for cognitive state analysis and neurological disorder detection, such as epilepsy. However, automated seizure detection faces challenges, including inconsistent windowing, timestamp misalignment, label-based signal segmentation, and unstructured large-scale EEG data—especially critical in event-driven settings. To address these, we introduce Meta-EEGs, a structured, domain-agnostic EEG representation for temporally labelled tasks. Meta-EEGs provide consistent windowing with precise time alignment, enable event-based segmentation based on annotations or class labels, and organise raw EEG recordings into a simpler, relatively reduced in volume format suitable for AI model input. They also support the creation and management of hierarchical EEG datasets, currently lacking in the field. As case studies, we applied Meta-EEGs to the CHB-MIT and Siena Scalp EEG Databases, generating structured datasets that are publicly available on Figshare and have been downloaded over 2000 times since 2022. The working code is accessible on GitHub.Main features and applications:•Provides consistent window definition, timestamp alignment, signal segmentation, and standardised structuring for large-scale EEG studies.•Releases two fully annotated, reduced in volume datasets for automated seizure detection, supporting reproducible and generalisable analysis.•Enables AI model development for seizure detection, event classification, patient-specific and cross-patient analysis, and hierarchical EEG tasks without repeated initial preprocessing.

Background

Electroencephalography (EEG) interpretation traditionally relies on expert visual inspection, despite the large data volume generated by routine recordings; for example, a 60-minute scalp EEG can produce approximately 360 printed pages [1]. With growing advances in EEG acquisition, the data and its inspection have become complex and time-consuming [2].

Each year, an estimated 5000–6000 peer-reviewed EEG studies are published [3], with a significant subset focused on automated analysis for epilepsy diagnosis, and seizure detection [[4], [5], [6]]. Despite this volume, the field lacks standardised methods for EEG windowing and segmentation from clinical raw data, limiting the reproducibility and scalability of AI models [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Existing open EEG datasets are heterogeneous in sampling rates, channel configurations, and recording durations, and hierarchical EEG classification datasets remain largely absent [12,13]. A key obstacle is inconsistent input segment lengths across datasets; for example, models trained on short segments from the Bonn dataset cannot be directly applied to long-term recordings such as the Temple University Hospital (TUH) corpus. These inconsistencies, together with severe class imbalance—where normal, abnormal, and seizure-related states are unevenly represented due to patient-specific temporal variability—further hinder cross-dataset training, benchmarking, and generalization.

Moreover, EEG data handling remains cumbersome due to multivariate signals, high temporal resolution, and proprietary file formats. Preparing structured, relatively volume-reduced data for Artificial Intelligence (AI) remains a major bottleneck. Addressing these challenges is essential to enable FAIR-compliant EEG datasets. In this study, we focus on these challenges by introducing the approach of Meta-EEG for automated seizure detection.

Method details

To run the Meta-EEG algorithm on any EEG dataset, the first requirement is to generate a timestamp metadata CSV file that describes how the raw EEG recordings are to be segmented. The timestamp CSV file is then passed into the Meta-EEG processing pipeline, which can be applied to any EEG dataset with appropriate tweaks. In general, for any EEG dataset, the pipeline first loads the relevant raw EEG files, extracts the defined segments, applies windowing, and finally saves each segment as a new CSV file organized by event type or class label. The sampling frequency, start, and end time of the EEG are treated as user-defined parameters during the development of the time-stamped metadata CSV. It does not perform automatic resampling or frequency standardization across datasets with different sampling rates. If cross-dataset standardization is required, an additional step (e.g., external resampling to a common frequency) would need to be applied before using our pipeline. This design choice was intentional to keep the framework modular, lightweight, and adaptable to different experimental settings.

To address the variability in seizure durations across patients in real-time (which also exists in the CHB-MIT Scalp EEG Database and the Siena Scalp EEG Database), we applied a proportional windowing strategy where the duration of all surrounding segments (post-ictal, pre-ictal, and non-seizure) is matched to the length of the ictal event. For example, if the ictal segment is 30 s, each of the corresponding segments is also extracted with a length of 30 s. For the analysis of peri‑ictal data, the full ictal duration is retained and combined with an additional 10 s of pre-ictal and 10 s of post-ictal activity. A corresponding non-seizure segment of the same total length is also extracted for balanced comparison. All the above settings were done based on our prior analysis [14] where we introduced the idea of analysing peri‑ictal EEG signals for automated seizure detection using the original CHB-MIT EEG scalp database. Additionally, a gap of five seconds was taken between ictal and pre-ictal, and ictal and post-ictal EEG segments to further keep the boundaries of each segment distinct. However, researchers can use our approach to cut any window EEG length for any AI use case with minimal modifications. The non-seizure data of the Meta-EEG of Siena Scalp EEG Database v1.0. is not available as the original data only consisted of seizure files.

It is important to note that both short-term segments and long-term temporal context play an important role in AI-EEG analysis. Segmenting EEG data into different states may affect estimated nonlinear features, but it does not alter the underlying signal. Segmentation simply divides a continuous EEG recording into temporal portions for analysis. For example, separating EEG into interictal and ictal periods may yield different nonlinear metrics because brain dynamics differ across states, not because the signal itself has changed. A similar principle of segmentation applies when EEG data are processed by AI models. Signals are typically handled in fixed-length chunks or batches for computational efficiency. This process does not modify the signal content; rather, it provides a practical way to process long sequences of voltage samples, whether in the time domain or after transformation into feature or time–frequency representations. Treating chunks without considering their class labels can lead to incorrect AI predictions and may obscure relationships that span across segment boundaries. By applying this balancing and selecting a fixed EEG window length, the researchers can also conduct cross-dataset evaluations and compare their study with others reliably and transparently. Additionally, the concept of Meta-EEGs shifts the responsibility of handling variable-length data from the model to the data representation itself. This design ensures compatibility with standard neural network architectures while avoiding the potential drawbacks of padding (e.g., introducing artificial values) or truncation (e.g., loss of information).

Analyzing EEG over long time spans with accurate temporal labelling is essential because many clinically relevant patterns—particularly seizure dynamics—unfold over extended periods. Gradual state transitions, preictal changes, circadian rhythms, and evolving background activity cannot always be captured within short, isolated segments. Emphasising segmented data analysis therefore complements, rather than replaces, the use of long-term EEG in AI pipelines. Clear annotations of clinically meaningful states—such as inter-ictal, preictal, peri‑ictal, and ictal periods—remain critical for interpretation, validation, and supervised learning. The appropriate balance between segmented and continuous analysis ultimately depends on the research objective. Our study introduced an approach for handling large-scale EEG data across arbitrary time scales, illustrated through a practical use case.

pyEDFlib was used to read and extract information from EDF (European Data Format) files, commonly used for EEG recordings. NumPy facilitated numerical computations, while Pandas were employed for handling and manipulating structured data using Data Frames. The CSV module was used to read and write CSV files during data export and logging. File and directory operations were managed using both the os and shutil libraries, which helped navigate the file system, create folders, and move files during dataset organization and preprocessing.

We detail the structure of the datasets here to show the hierarchy generated through our approach:

Meta-EEG of CHB-MIT Scalp EEG Database v1.0.0.0

The Meta-EEG of CHB-MIT Scalp EEG Database v1.0.0.0 dataset is organised into two primary folder types: patient-specific and patient non-specific. The patient-specific folder consists of a dual folder structure for both intra-patient and inter-patient analysis. Intra-patient analysis refers to the evaluation of EEG data from the same patient recorded at different time points, where the patterns of EEG signals may vary significantly. For example, patient chb21, whose data was obtained 1.5 years after chb01. Inter-patient analysis refers to the evaluation of patient-specific EEG data divided based on different patients, where the duration over which the EEG data is recorded is not a deciding factor. The inter-patient and intra-patient folders are further divided into seizure and non-seizure folders. Both the seizure and non-seizure folders are divided based on the patients. Each patient's files are bifurcated into pre-ictal, ictal, post-ictal, and peri‑ictal EEG segments-based CSV files. In the non-seizure folder, each patient has two non-seizures i.e., one of ictal EEG length and the other of peri‑ictal EEG length CSV files. The patient non-specific folder combines all 197 annotated seizure events and randomly selected, and duration matched non-seizure EEG segments from every subject into a single pooled dataset. This structure supports generalized, patient-independent model training and evaluation and was designed to resemble the Bonn EEG dataset. Meta-EEG of Siena Scalp EEG Database v1.0.0

In line with the previously described data record, this dataset has been created using the same approach. The Meta-EEG of Siena Scalp EEG Database v1.0.0 dataset is organised into two main folder types: patient-specific and patient non-specific. The patient-specific folders contain only the inter-patient analysis folder with seizures categorised by type, localisation, and lateralisation. The original Siena Scalp EEG database does not contain intra-patient EEG data. Each subject folder follows the naming convention of the original Siena Scalp EEG Database (PN00, PN01, … PN17) and includes CSV files segmented into pre-ictal, ictal, post-ictal, and peri‑ictal intervals. Each CSV file contains a class label in the last row to specify the segment type. The labels are numbered as 1 for the Ictal EEG segment, 2 for the pre-ictal EEG segment, 3 for the post-ictal EEG segment, and 4 for the peri‑ictal EEG segment. The patient's non-specific folder aggregates all 46 annotated seizure events irrespective of seizure type and localisation. This folder follows the same segmentation structure as discussed above for the meta-EEG of CHB-MIT Scalp EEG Database. Data from PN00– 3 was excluded due to incomplete annotations. Method validation

To show the adoption and storage efficiency of our approach and support transparency, reproducibility, and real-time AI-EEG research, we applied it to the above-mentioned two publicly available datasets commonly used for automated epilepsy diagnosis and seizure detection. Both developed datasets are accessible on Figshare [15]. It has been downloaded over 2000 times since 2022 [17,18]. Our approach significantly reduces the size of the raw EEG recordings, for example, achieving a 2.64 × reduction from the original CHB-MIT Scalp EEG Database [19] and a 5.9 × reduction from the original Siena Scalp EEG Database [20], and introduces class labels for each of the EEG segments in these two datasets, thus facilitating the use of AI applications.

We also briefly validated our approach by performing a classification and latent embedding analysis between three classes: non-ictal, intact ictal, and fragmented ictal segments. A synthetic and real EEG from the CHB-MIT EEG database were considered. In the synthetic setup, the three EEG classes were generated mathematically using sinusoidal seizure-like waveforms and Gaussian noise formulations to simulate seizure morphology and transition behavior. For the real EEG setup, EEG recordings from the Meta-EEG of CHB-MIT Scalp EEG Database v1.0.0.0 were used, where a single bipolar channel (FP1-F7) was selected with fixed-length windows of 1024 samples. A simple one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN) was designed to learn latent representations from the three segments. The network consisted of two Conv1D layers followed by a Global Average Pooling layer, a 16-dimensional dense embedding layer, and a final softmax classification layer. The network was trained using the Adam optimizer with categorical cross-entropy loss for 20 epochs and a batch size of 32. The CNN employed kernel sizes of 5 in both convolutional layers, with 16 and 32 filters, respectively, followed by a 16-dimensional latent embedding layer for feature representation learning. The purpose of the above CNN was to briefly study how different EEG segmentation strategies influence the learned latent representations of seizure activity. Latent embeddings extracted from the CNN layer were projected into two dimensions using t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) for visualization of both synthetic and real EEG signals (Fig. 3). Fig. 3. This analysis helps illustrate that intact seizure segments produce more coherent and separable representations, whereas fragmented seizure windows introduce ambiguity and dispersed feature distributions. Limitations

The labelled segments provided here have not undergone preprocessing and may thus contain noise or artefacts such as ocular movements or nerve stimulation. However, additional preprocessing techniques can be easily applied on top of Meta-EEGs to enhance signal quality. Furthermore, for our approach, pro-ictal (periods of heightened seizure risk other than ictal or inter-ictal times) and inter-ictal segments were excluded, as accurate labelling of these segments would require expert clinical input, which will be the aim of future studies. While the current implementation supports only EDF files, the modular design allows straightforward integration of real-time data and other EEG file formats, acknowledging the diversity of existing EEG standards. The proposed approach of Meta-EEGs is intended as a conceptual idea rather than a mandated approach and is independent of any specific hardware acquisition device; however, practical deployment may require adaptation to the device and intended use case.
