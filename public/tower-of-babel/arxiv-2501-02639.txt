Matrix Hessenberg schemes over the minimal sheet
Rebecca Goldin, Martha Precup

Abstract
. We study families of matrix Hessenberg schemes in the affine scheme of complex n × n n\times n matrices, each defined over a fixed sheet in the Lie algebra 𝔤 ​ 𝔩 n ​ ( ℂ ) \mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C}) . It is well known that such families over the regular sheet are flat, and every regular Hessenberg scheme degenerates to a regular nilpotent Hessenberg scheme. This paper explores whether flat degenerations exist outside of the regular case. For each matrix Hessenberg scheme, we introduce a one-parameter family of matrix Hessenberg schemes that degenerates it to a specific nilpotent Hessenberg scheme. Our main theorem states that, when the family lies over the minimal sheet in 𝔤 ​ 𝔩 n ​ ( ℂ ) \mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C}) , this degeneration is flat. The proof leverages commutative algebra on the polynomial ring to identify the structure of the family concretely, and we explore several applications. We conjecture that flatness holds for these families over other sheets as well.



1.
Introduction

Hessenberg varieties form large class of subvarieties of the flag variety that have arisen in diverse contexts across mathematical fields.
Motivated by Hessenberg matrices and algorithms for efficiently calculating eigenvalues in numerical analysis, Hessenberg varieties in the flag variety of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
were first introduced by De Mari and Shayman
[
DS1988
]
and later defined in all Lie types by De Mari, Procesi, and Shayman
[
DPS1992
]
. Many questions regarding their geometry and topology remain open, or are answered in only a few cases.

Examples of Hessenberg varieties include many well-studied cases such as Springer fibers
[
Spaltenstein1976
,
Steinberg
]
, Peterson varieties
[
Kostant1996
]
, and permutohedral varieties
[
DPS1992
]
. Hessenberg varieties also overlap with other important subvarieties of the flag variety such as Schubert varieties
[
EPS2023
,
Tymoczko2006A
,
Abe-Crooks2016
]
and
K
orbits
[
EPS-online
]
. Hessenberg varieties and their generalizations arise in the context of Schubert calculus
[
Carlsson-Oblomkov2019
,
Drellich2015
,
Anderson-Tymoczko2010
,
Insko-Tymoczko-Woo
]
, representation theory
[
GKM-affine
,
Oblomkov-Yun2016
]
, and combinatorics
[
Carlsson-Mellit2021
,
MR4116638
]
. The topology of regular semisimple Hessenberg varieties has been tied to the study of chromatic quasisymmetric functions via an action of the symmetric group on its cohomology
[
Shareshian-Wachs2016
,
Brosnan-Chow2018
]
, motivating new advances in the area; see, for example,
[
Harada-Precup2019
,
Aberu-Nigro2022A
,
Aberu-Nigro2022B
,
MR4692364
]
.

Due to their geometric diversity, the theory of Hessenberg varieties has traditionally been advanced by a focus on special cases. This paper takes a different approach: we study families of Hessenberg varieties united only by a few essential properties of the defining parameters. One-parameter families of regular Hessenberg varieties were studied by Abe–Dedieu–Galetta–Harada
[
ADGH2018
]
and Abe–Fujita–Zeng
[
Abe-Fujita-Zeng2020
]
. Our work generalizes that approach by organizing type A Hessenberg varieties into one-parameter families with the goal of understanding how the members of each family differ. In particular, we examine when these varieties vary continuously, that is, when the families we define are
flat
.

Let
n
be a positive integer,
G=GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, and
B\subset G
be the Borel subgroup of upper triangular matrices. The collection of flags

V_{\bullet}:=(0\subset V_{1}\subset V_{2}\subset\cdots\subset V_{n-1}\subset V_{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n})

with
\dim V_{i}=i
for all

is the
flag variety
of
G
. Recall that this variety can be naturally identified with the homogeneous space
G/B
. A Hessenberg variety
Y_{\mathsf{x},h}
is a subvariety of
G/B
that depends on two inputs: a matrix
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{Lie}(G)
and a non-decreasing function
h:\{1,\ldots,n\}\to\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
h(i)\geq i
, called a
Hessenberg function
. Explicitly,

(1.1)

\displaystyle Y_{\mathsf{x},h}:=\{V_{\bullet}\mid\mathsf{x}(V_{i})\subseteq V_{h(i)}\text{ for all $i$}\}

is the
Hessenberg variety
corresponding to the matrix
\mathsf{x}
and Hessenberg function
h
. If
\mathsf{x}
is a nilpotent (respectively, semisimple) element of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
then we say that
Y_{\mathsf{x},h}
is a
nilpotent
(respectively,
semisimple
)
Hessenberg variety
.

The geometry of the Hessenberg variety
Y_{\mathsf{x},h}
depends on the choice of matrix
\mathsf{x}
and Hessenberg function
h
. In one extreme case, when
h(i)=n
for all
i
, the Hessenberg variety is equal to the flag variety of
G
for all matrices
\mathsf{x}
. In another, when
h(i)=i
for all
i
, the structure of
Y_{\mathsf{x},h}
depends on the matrix
\mathsf{x}
(or its conjugacy class) a great deal. For example, if
\mathsf{x}
is nilpotent then
Y_{\mathsf{x},h}
is the Springer fiber over
\mathsf{x}
and if
\mathsf{x}
is semisimple then
Y_{\mathsf{x},h}
is a disjoint union of products of smaller dimensional flag varieties.

Let
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a regular matrix, that is, a matrix such that the conjugacy class
\mathcal{O}_{\mathsf{x}}
of
\mathsf{x}
in
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is maximum dimensional. When
\mathsf{x}
is regular, we say that
Y_{\mathsf{x},h}
is a
regular Hessenberg variety
. Assume further that
h(i)>i
for all
1\leq i<n
. We call such a Hessenberg function
indecomposable
, because in this case, the regular Hessenberg variety
Y_{\mathsf{x},h}
is not a disjoint union of products of smaller dimensional regular Hessenberg varieties.
A regular Hessenberg variety with indecomposible Hessenberg function is reduced and irreducible, of dimension
\sum_{i}\left(h(i)-i\right)

[
Abe-Fujita-Zeng2020
,
Precup2018
]
. Note that this dimension formula does not depend on the specific regular matrix
\mathsf{x}
. The geometric reason for this “dimension stability” is that the family regular Hessenberg varieties is flat, as we now explain.

A scheme
\mathcal{Y}

admits a flat degeneration to a scheme
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
if there exists a flat morphism
\mathcal{Y}_{t}\to\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[t]
of schemes such that the scheme-theoretic fiber over the closed point
t\neq 0
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{Y}
and the scheme-theoretic fiber over
0
is
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
. We we call
\mathcal{Y}
the
general fiber
and
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
the
special fiber
or
degenerate fiber
. We also refer to the scheme
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
as a
flat family
. Abe, Fujita, and Zeng proved that every regular Hessenberg variety is reduced and admits a flat degeneration to a regular nilpotent Hessenberg variety
[
Abe-Fujita-Zeng2020
, Cor. 6.5]
. The goal of this paper is to explore whether such a degeneration exists outside of the regular case. Outside of the regular case, one needs to disambiguate the
scheme
defined by the equations describing the inclusions (
1.1
)
from its underlying variety. To that end, we define the
matrix Hessenberg scheme
as a lift
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x},h}
of
Y_{\mathsf{x},h}
in
\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[M_{n}]
, where
M_{n}
denotes the set of

matrices with complex entries.

The driving question of this paper is:

Question A
.

Does every matrix Hessenberg scheme
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x},h}
admit a flat degeneration to a nilpotent matrix Hessenberg scheme?

Our main result is that the answer is yes when
\mathsf{x}
in the
minimal sheet
of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Recall that a
partition of
n
is a sequence
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
of non-decreasing positive integers whose sum is
n
. There is a decomposition of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})=\bigsqcup_{\lambda}{\mathfrak{g}}_{\lambda}
into subsets called
sheets
, each indexed by a partition
\lambda
of
n
. The group
G
acts on
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
by conjugation and each conjugacy class is assigned to a particular sheet using the Jordan canonical form; see Section
3
. The minimal sheet is the union of conjugacy classes in
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
of minimum positive dimension and the partition indexing the minimal sheet is
(2,1,1,\dots,1)
, also written
(2,1^{n-2})
. We call any Hessenberg variety
Y_{\mathsf{x},h}
with
\mathsf{x}\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{(2,1^{n-2})}
a
minimal Hessenberg variety
and similarly for the associated matrix Hessenberg scheme. Our main result is the following; see also Theorem
3.16
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
h:\{1,\ldots,n\}\to\{1,\ldots,n\}
be a Hessenberg function. For all
\mathsf{x}\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{(2,1^{n-2})}
there exists a curve
\mathsf{x}_{t}
in the minimal sheet
{\mathfrak{g}}_{(2,1^{n-2})}
and a flat morphism

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}\to\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[t]

with general fiber
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x},h}
and special fiber
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n},h}
,
where
\mathsf{n}
is an element of the minimal nilpotent orbit in
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Furthermore, the flat family
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}
is reduced.

More broadly, we provide the tools to ask Question
A
for other sheets, by defining a curve
\mathsf{x}_{t}
in the sheet
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\lambda}
, and a corresponding family of Hessenberg schemes over
\mathsf{x}_{t}
, for any partition
\lambda
. In Conjecture
3.15
, we conjecture that this family is flat over every sheet.
In the case of the minimal sheet, the proof of Theorem
1
follows from an explicit description of the associated primes for the ideal defining
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}
, stated in Theorem
.

The minimal sheet has two particularly convenient elements, mainly the nilpotent matrix
\mathsf{n}:=E_{1n}
, the matrix with unique nonzero entry equal to
1
in position
(1,n)
, and semisimple matrix
\mathsf{s}=\operatorname{diag}(1,0,\ldots,0)
.
These choices are representative in that each matrix Hessenberg scheme is isomorphic to either
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}
or
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n},h}
by Lemma
3.5
.
The general fiber
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}
is studied in
[
GP-semisimple
]
, where we prove that this matrix Hessenberg scheme is reduced and we identify each irreducible component explicitly.

Though the family
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}
and its general fiber
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}
are reduced, the special fiber
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n},h}
need not be. Since
\mathsf{n}
is
B
-invariant, the Hessenberg variety
Y_{\mathsf{n},h}
is a union of Schubert varieties. Tymoczko identified the particular union when
h
is indecomposable
[
Tymoczko2006A
]
, and Abe and Crooks extended the result to include the case that
h
is decomposable
[
Abe-Crooks2016
]
. In Proposition
, we prove a scheme theoretic version of Tymoczko’s decomposition for the indecomposable clase. We then connect it with our results from
[
GP-semisimple
]
for the semisimple Hessenberg varieties over the minimal sheet, via a flat degeneration defined on each component; see Theorem
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
h:\{1,\ldots,n\}\to\{1,\ldots,n\}
is an indecomposable Hessenberg function. The flat degeneration described in Theorem
1
yields a flat degeneration from each component of the semisimple matrix Hessenberg scheme
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}
to a unique component of the nilpotent matrix Hessenberg scheme
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n},h}
. In particular, there is a bijection between components of each minimal semisimple Hessenberg variety and minimal nilpotent Hessenberg variety when
h
is indecomposable.

A component-wise degeneration does not generally exist outside the indecomposable case. In Example
, we exhibit a (flat) degeneration in which the special fiber consists of three Schubert varieties with nontrivial multiplicity, while the general fiber consists of four reduced components. We also prove
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n},h}
is not reduced when
h
is decomposable; see Theorem
.

Theorem 3
.

The minimal nilpotent matrix Hessenberg scheme
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n},h}
is reduced if and only if the Hessenberg function
h
is indecomposable.

The paper is organized as follows. Section

. Miracle flatness does not apply in the minimal sheet – this failure suggests the need for other methods. One enormous benefit of lifting the Hessenberg variety to the matrix space that the
geometry of the Hessenberg variety can be studied using commutative algebra in the
relatively simple setting of the polynomial ring in

variables.
Section
5
contains the technical heart of the paper, in which we examine the defining ideal of a family of matrix Hessenberg schemes in detail using Gröbner bases. In Section
we use these technical results to examine the family of Hessenberg schemes over the minimal sheet in detail, proving the main theorem. The implications of these results for the special (nilpotent) fiber can be found in Section
.

The Weyl group of

Lemma 2.2

Let

Subvarieties of the flag variety equal to the intersection of a Schubert and opposite Schubert variety are called

and recall that the Richardson variety

is nonempty if and only if

Lemma 2.3

The ideal

is prime for all

and

. Furthermore, when

\mathcal{X}_{u[i]}^{\mathrm{op}}\cap\mathcal{X}_{w_{0}v[j]}=\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]/(w_{0}\cdot\mathcal{J}_{i-1}^{0}+\mathcal{K}_{j})

Proof.

Note that
w_{0}\cdot\mathcal{J}_{0}^{0}=\langle 0\rangle
and otherwise

and

and

It is straightforward to check (using, for example, the tableau criterion from
[

is nonempty. By Lemma

in
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
. Since

is prime, this intersection is reduced and

\overline{\pi^{-1}(X_{u[i]}^{\mathrm{op}}\cap X_{w_{0}v[j]})}=\mathcal{X}_{u[i]}^{\mathrm{op}}\cap\mathcal{X}_{w_{0}v[j]},

as desired.

2.2.
Matrix Hessenberg schemes

We now define our main geometric objects of interest, matrix Hessenberg schemes. Throughout this section,
R
denotes denoted a polynomial ring with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}
. We take
R=\mathbb{C}
and
R=\mathbb{C}[t]

Definition 2.4
.

A function
h:[n]\to[n]
is a
Hessenberg function
if
h(i)\leq h(i+1)
for all
i<n
and
h(i)\geq i
for all
i
.

We denote a Hessenberg function by listing its values, e.g.
h=(h(1),\ldots,h(n))
. We say that
h
is
indecomposable
whenever
h(i)>i
for all
i<n
. Otherwise, we say that
h
is
decomposable
. We adopt the convention that
h(0)=0
.

Let
e_{i}
denote the
n\times 1
column vector with unique nonzero entry equal to
1
in the
i
th row, and define

(2.4)

\displaystyle v_{j}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{ij}e_{i}

for
j=1,\dots,n
.
Throughout this section, let
\mathsf{x}\in M_{n}(R)
denote a

matrix with entries in the polynomial ring
R
.
For each
i\in[n]
such that
h(i)<n
, define
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h,i}\subseteq R[\mathbf{z}]
to be the ideal in
R[\mathbf{z}]
generated by the condition that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{rk}\begin{bmatrix}|&|&&|&|&|&&|\\
\mathsf{x}v_{1}&\mathsf{x}v_{2}&\cdots&\mathsf{x}v_{i}&v_{1}&v_{2}&\cdots&v_{h(i)}\\
|&|&&|&|&|&&|\end{bmatrix}\leq h(i).

That is,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h,i}
is the ideal generated by all
(h(i)+1)\times(h(i)+1)
-minors of the
n\times(h(i)+i)
-matrix above. Set
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h,i}
equal to the zero ideal whenever
h(i)=n
.
We define

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}:=\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h,i}\subseteq R[\mathbf{z}].

Observe that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}
is invariant under action of
B
on
R[\mathbf{z}]
induced by right multiplication on
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
.

Recall that
G=GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a principal open subset of the affine scheme
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. We identify
G
as an affine subscheme of
\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z},y]
with coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z},d^{-1}]:=\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z},y]/\left<yd-1\right>
obtained via localization, where
d\in\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
denotes the determinant function. Let

(2.7)

\displaystyle\iota:R[\mathbf{z}]\hookrightarrow R\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z},d^{-1}]=:R[\mathbf{z},d^{-1}]

denote the canonical injection. Given an ideal
\mathcal{I}\subseteq R[\mathbf{z}]
,
we denote the saturation of
\mathcal{I}
with respect to
d
by
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}
, i.e.

(2.8)

\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}:=\iota^{-1}(\iota(\mathcal{I})R[\mathbf{z},d^{-1}]),

where
\iota(\mathcal{I})R[\mathbf{z},d^{-1}]
is the ideal generated by the image of
\mathcal{I}
under
\iota
. It is immediate that
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}
. We are now equipped to define matrix Hessenberg schemes; note that
R=\mathbb{C}
in the definition below.

Definition 2.5
.

Given a matrix
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and Hessenberg function
h:[n]\to[n]
, the associated
matrix Hessenberg scheme
is the affine scheme

\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x},h}:=\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]/\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{x},h}.

Note that, if
h
is a fixed Hessenberg function, the matrix Hessenberg schemes associated to conjugate matrices are isomorphic
[
GP-semisimple
, Rem. 3.5]
.

Remark 2.6
.

It is immediate from the definition that
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x},h}=\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}^{\prime},h}
if the two ideals
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x}^{\prime},h}
are equal. Thus if
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{x}^{\prime}
result in identical rank conditions (
2.5
), then the corresponding matrix Hessenberg schemes are equal.

In the literature, Hessenberg varieties are typically defined as subvarieties of the flag variety
G/B
using the determinantal equations as defined for each
i\in[n]
in (
2.5
) above. There is some ambiguity regarding whether a Hessenberg variety is the subscheme of
G/B
defined by these equations or the reduced variety supporting this subscheme (see
[
Insko-Tymoczko-Woo
, §4]
for more discussion on this topic). We adopt the former convention in this paper (which is also the convention of
[
Insko-Tymoczko-Woo
]
).

Hessenberg schemes satisfy the following properties; see
[
GP-semisimple
, §2]
for a detailed discussion.

Remark 2.7
.

Given
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and
h:[n]\to[n]
a Hessenberg function, let
Y_{\mathsf{x},h}
denote corresponding the Hessenberg variety in the flag variety
G/B
. Then

(1)

\pi^{-1}(Y_{\mathsf{x},h})=G\cap\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]/\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}
, and

(2)

\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x},h}=\overline{\pi^{-1}(Y_{\mathsf{x},h})}
.

We say
i\in[n]
is a
corner of the Hessenberg function
h
whenever
h(i)>h(i-1)
. Denote the set of all corners by
\mathcal{C}(h)
. Our convention that
h(0)=0
implies that
1
is always a corner of
h
. For each
i\in[n]
we set

i^{*}:=\max\{k\in[n]\mid h(k)=h(i)\}.

Notice that if
i
is a corner of
h
, then
i^{*}+1\in\mathcal{C}(h)
. Furthermore, there is no corner
k
in
\mathcal{C}(h)
such that
i<k<i^{*}+1
. The following statement is proved in
[
GP-semisimple
, Lemma 3.6]
; note that version stated in
[
GP-semisimple
]
assumes
R=\mathbb{C}
, but the proof generalizes immediately to the polynomial setting.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
R
be a polynomial ring with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}
. For all Hessenberg functions
h
and matrices
\mathsf{x}\in M_{n}(R)
,

\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h)}\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h,i^{*}}.

Example 2.9
.

We use Lemma
2.8
to illustrate that a nilpotent matrix Hessenberg scheme
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n},h}
need not be equidimensional. Let
h=(2,4,4,4)
, which is indecomposable. The corner set of
h
is
\mathcal{C}(h)=\{1,2\}
, and we have
1^{*}=1
and
2^{*}=4
. Recall that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h,4}=\left<0\right>
by definition. Thus Lemma
2.8
implies
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h,1}
.

Let
\mathsf{x}=\mathsf{n}:=E_{14}
be the
4\times 4
nilpotent matrix with unique nonzero entry equal to
1
in position
(1,4)
. Note that
\mathsf{n}
is a nilpotent element in the minimal sheet
{\mathfrak{g}}_{(2,1,1)}
.
The rank conditions from (

) generating
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{n},h}
are

(2.9)

\displaystyle\operatorname{rk}\begin{bmatrix}z_{41}&z_{11}&z_{12}\\
0&z_{21}&z_{22}\\
0&z_{31}&z_{32}\\
0&z_{41}&z_{42}\\
\end{bmatrix}\leq 2,

which is equivalent to insisting that all

minors vanish. Thus the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{n},h}
has generators
z_{41}(z_{21}z_{32}-z_{22}z_{31})
,
z_{41}(z_{21}z_{42}-z_{22}z_{41})
and
z_{41}(z_{31}z_{42}-z_{32}z_{41})
.

We use Macaulay2 to check that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{n},h}
is radical, and has associated primes

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{2}

\displaystyle=\langle z_{32}z_{41}-z_{31}z_{42},z_{22}z_{41}-z_{21}z_{42},z_{22}z_{31}-z_{21}z_{32}\rangle\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{1}^{0}

\displaystyle=\langle z_{41}\rangle.

We can identify each prime ideal as the determinantal ideal defining a matrix Schubert variety using Lemma
2.2
. The two relevant permutations in this case are
v[2]=[1423]
and
u[2]
= [2134].
Observe that the vanishing set of both prime ideals intersects
GL_{4}(\mathbb{C})
, so
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{n},h}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{n},h}
. In Section
, we show that
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{x},h}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}
for any
h
when
\mathsf{x}
is in the minimal sheet. For
h=(2,4,4,4)
, we conclude that
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n},h}
is the scheme theoretic union of matrix Schubert varieties:

\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n},h}=\mathcal{X}_{w_{0}[1423]}\cup\mathcal{X}_{w_{0}[2134]}=\mathcal{X}_{[4132]}\cup\mathcal{X}_{[3421]}.

Since
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{X}_{[4132]}=14
and
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{X}_{[3421]}=15
, we have shown that the reduced (but not irreducible) matrix Hessenberg scheme
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n},h}
is
not equidimensional
.

The interested reader can compare this with
[
GP-semisimple
, Ex. 3.9]
which considers the matrix Hessenberg scheme for the semisimple element
\mathsf{s}=(1,0,0,0)\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{(2,1,1)}
and where it is shown that for
h=(2,4,4,4)
we have

\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}=\mathcal{X}_{[4132]}\cup\mathcal{X}_{[2134]}^{\mathrm{op}}.

In particular, we note that
\dim\mathcal{X}_{[4132]}=14
and
\dim\mathcal{X}_{[2134]}^{\mathrm{op}}=15
so
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n},h}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}
have the same number of components of a given dimension. This phenomenon is explained by the existence of a flat degeneration from each component of
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}
to a corresponding component of
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n},h}
; see Theorem
below.
∎

3.
Sheets and Families of matrix Hessenberg schemes

In this section, we consider the stratification of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
by sheets. We make Question
A
more precise by defining, for each matrix
\mathsf{x}
, a curve in the sheet containing
\mathsf{x}
and family of matrix Hessenberg schemes with general fiber
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x},h}
and special fiber equal to a nilpotent matrix Hessenberg scheme. Our main result, stated precisely in Theorem
3.16
below, is that this family is flat whenever
\mathsf{x}
is an element of the minimal sheet.

3.1.
Sheets

Given an arbitrary complex reductive group
G
, we consider the adjoint action of
G
on its Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
. The
sheets of
{\mathfrak{g}}
are irreducible components of the locally closed
G
-stable subvariety
{\mathfrak{g}}^{(d)}:=\{x\in{\mathfrak{g}}\mid\dim Gx=d\}
. That is, each sheet in
{\mathfrak{g}}
is a maximal irreducible subset of
{\mathfrak{g}}
consisting of
G
orbits (with respect to the adjoint action) of a fixed dimension. This decomposition was first considered by Peterson for
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})

[
Peterson-thesis
]
and Borho
[
Borho1981
]
classified the orbits appearing in each sheet of the corresponding Lie algebra. The study of sheets arises naturally in the study of orbits and representation theory of algebraic groups
[
Borho-Kraft1979
,
Katsylo1982
,
Panyushev2001
]
. Although sheets are defined for arbitrary reductive groups, we focus here on
G=GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and leave the study of Question
A
and matrix Hessenberg schemes in other types for future work.

Kraft proved that the sheets in
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
are in bijection with partitions of
n

[
Kraft
, §2]
. Recall that a
partition

\lambda
of
n
is a list
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
of positive integers such that
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{\ell}
and
n=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{\ell}
. We associate a partition to
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
as follows.
Recall that a generalized Jordan block of the matrix
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
consists of all Jordan blocks of
\mathsf{x}
with the same eigenvalue
c\in\mathbb{C}
, and each generalized Jordan block determines a partition
\mu

recording the sizes of these Jordan blocks in decreasing order. Let
\mu_{j}
denote the
j
th entry of
\mu
.
The algebraic multiplicity of the eigenvalue
c
is given by
|\mu|=\sum_{j}\mu_{j}
.

Let
\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{k}\}
be the distinct eigenvalues of
\mathsf{x}
and denote the partition corresponding to
c_{i}
by
\mu^{i}
.
Define
\lambda_{\mathsf{x},j}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu^{i}_{j}
. Observe that
\lambda_{\mathsf{x},j}
does not depend on the order of the eigenvalues chosen, and
\lambda_{\mathsf{x}}:=(\lambda_{\mathsf{x},1},\lambda_{\mathsf{x},2},\ldots)
is a partition of
n
. For each partition
\lambda
the sheet

{\mathfrak{g}}_{\lambda}
is the union of orbits
\mathcal{O}_{\mathsf{x}}
such that
\lambda_{\mathsf{x}}=\lambda
.

Remark 3.1
.

If
\mathsf{x}
is nilpotent, all its eigenvalues are
0
, resulting in only one generalized Jordan block with partition
\mu
. In this case
\lambda_{\mathsf{x}}=\mu
. Thus each sheet
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\lambda}
contains a unique nilpotent conjugacy class, namely the conjugacy class associated to the partition
\mu
.

Example 3.2
.

Consider the matrix

\displaystyle\mathsf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}4&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&4&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&5&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&5&1&0\\
0&0&0&0&5&1\\
0&0&0&0&0&5\end{bmatrix}

with eigenvalues
c_{1}=4
and
c_{2}=5
.
Then
\mu^{1}=(1,1)
and
\mu^{2}=(3,1)
, resulting in
\lambda_{\mathsf{x}}=(1+3,1+1)=(4,2)
so
\mathsf{x}\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{(4,2)}
.
∎

Definition 3.3
.

The
regular sheet

{\mathfrak{g}}_{reg}:={\mathfrak{g}}_{(n)}
consists of all orbits of dimension
n^{2}-n
.
The
minimal sheet

{\mathfrak{g}}_{(2,1^{n-2})}
consists of orbits of minimal nonzero dimension. In particular,
{\mathfrak{g}}_{(2,1^{n-2})}
contains the unique nilpotent orbit of minimal nonzero dimension in
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, called the
minimal nilpotent orbit
.

Remark 3.4
.

The minimal sheet
{\mathfrak{g}}_{(2,1^{n-2})}
contains only two types of conjugacy classes.
If
\lambda_{\mathsf{x}}=(2,1^{n-2})
, then
\mathsf{x}
has at most two eigenvalues. If
\mathsf{x}
has exactly two eigenvalues
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
then
\mu^{1}=(1)
and
\mu^{2}=(1,1,\ldots,1)
. Thus
\mathsf{x}
is conjugate to
\operatorname{diag}(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{2})
. If
\mathsf{x}
has a single eigenvalue, then
\mu^{1}=(2,1,\ldots,1)
, and
\mathsf{x}
is conjugate to
cI_{n}+E_{n-1,n}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Up to isomorphism there are only two matrix Hessenberg schemes over the minimal sheet, namely
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n},h}
where
\mathsf{s}=\operatorname{diag}(1,0,\dots,0)
and
\mathsf{n}:=E_{1,n}
.

Proof.

By Remark
3.4
there are only two types of matrices in
{\mathfrak{g}}_{(2,1^{n-2})}
, up to a choice of eigenvalues. If
\mathsf{x}=\operatorname{diag}(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{2})
for some
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
with
c_{1}\neq c_{2}
then the rank inequalities of (
2.5
) are satisfied for
\mathsf{x}
if and only if they are satisfied for
\mathsf{x}^{\prime}=\operatorname{diag}(c_{1}-c_{2},0,\dots,0)
which are themselves satisfied if and only if they are satisfied for
\mathsf{s}=\operatorname{diag}(1,0,\dots,0)
. It follows from Remark
2.6
that, for any Hessenberg function
h
,

\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{s},h}
. If
\mathsf{x}=cI_{n}+E_{n-1,n}
for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
, then the rank inequalities of (
2.5
) are satisfied for
\mathsf{x}
if and only if they are satisfied for
\mathsf{x}^{\prime}=E_{n-1,n}
and thus
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x}^{\prime},h}
by Remark
2.6
. The desired statement now follows from the fact that
\mathsf{x}^{\prime}
and
\mathsf{n}
are conjugate.
∎

For any
\mathsf{x}\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\lambda}
, we identify a specific conjugate in Jordan canonical form.
Fix an ordering of the generalized Jordan blocks as follows. Let
\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{k}\}
denote the distinct eigenvalues of
\mathsf{x}
. By relabeling, we may assume
i<j
whenever

•

|\mu^{i}|<|\mu^{j}|
, or

•

|\mu^{i}|=|\mu^{j}|
, and
\mu^{i}
is less than
\mu^{j}
in lexicographic ordering.

For each eigenvalue
c_{i}
, consider the unique tableau
\mathcal{T}_{i}
obtained by labeling the Young diagram of shape
\mu^{i}
with entries

\displaystyle|\mu^{1}|+\cdots+|\mu^{{i-1}}|+1,\,|\mu^{1}|+\cdots+|\mu^{{i-1}}|+2,\,\ldots,\,|\mu^{1}|+\cdots+|\mu^{{i-1}}|+|\mu^{i}|

so that all values
increase
from left to right in each row, and so that all values in each row are
greater than
all values in the rows below it (that is, the tableau
\mathcal{T}_{i}
has decreasing columns). Note that this tableau is obtained by labeling the rows sequentially from left to right, starting with the bottom row and moving upward; see Example
3.7
below. Let

\mathsf{n}_{i}=\sum E_{\ell m}

where the sum is taken over all pairs
(\ell,m)
such that
m
appears directly to the right of the box containing
\ell
in
\mathcal{T}_{i}
.

Then

\mathsf{x}
is conjugate to the matrix

\mathsf{x}_{\mathrm{ss}}+\mathsf{x}_{\mathrm{nilp}}
with semisimple and nilpotent parts

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathsf{x}_{\mathrm{ss}}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{diag}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{2},\ldots,c_{k},\ldots,c_{k})

\displaystyle\mathsf{x}_{\mathrm{nilp}}

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{n}_{1}+\mathsf{n}_{2}+\cdots+\mathsf{n}_{k},

where
c_{i}
occurs
|\mu^{i}|
times as a diagonal entry of
\mathsf{x}_{\mathrm{ss}}
.

Remark 3.6
.

If
\mathsf{x}
is in Jordan canonical form with blocks arranged according to
(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
, and such that the nilpotent part within each generalized Jordan block has
increasing
block sizes, then
\mathsf{x}=\mathsf{x}_{\mathrm{ss}}+\mathsf{x}_{\mathrm{nilp}}.
The matrix in Example
3.2
is of this form.

Finally, we identify a particular nilpotent element
\mathsf{n}_{\mathsf{x}}
that lies in the same sheet as
\mathsf{x}.

Given two tableaux
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, let
\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
denote their
concatenation
, the tableau obtained by concatenating the entries in each row of
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. Let
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{x}}
be tableau of shape
\lambda_{\mathsf{x}}
obtained by the concatenation:

\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{x}}=\mathcal{T}_{1}\circ\mathcal{T}_{2}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{T}_{k}.

Definition 3.13
.

Let

\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}:=\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[t,\mathbf{z}]/\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h},

where
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[t,\mathbf{z}]
is the ideal defined in Equation (

) (with
R=\mathbb{C}[t]
) and
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}
is the saturation of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}
with respect to the determinant

. The

\mathsf{x}_{t}
is the morphism

(3.5)

\varphi_{\mathsf{x},h}:\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[t]

induced from the ring homomorphism
\mathbb{C}[t]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[t,{\bf z}]/\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}
. By a slight abuse of notation, we also refer to the scheme
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}
as the
family of matrix Hessenberg schemes
over the sheet line
\mathsf{x}_{t}
.

Lemma 3.14
.

For each
\mathsf{x}\in{\mathfrak{g}}
and Hessenberg function
h:[n]\to[n]
, the morphism
\varphi_{\mathsf{x},h}
has scheme-theoretic fiber over
a\neq 0
isomorphic to the matrix Hessenberg variety
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x},h}
and scheme-theoretic fiber over
0
equal to the nilpotent matrix Hessenberg scheme
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n}_{\mathsf{x}},h}
, where
\mathsf{n}_{\mathsf{x}}
is the associated nilpotent element to
\mathsf{x}
.

Proof.

For each
a\in\mathbb{C}
the fiber of
\varphi_{\mathsf{x},h}
over the prime ideal
\langle t-a\rangle\in\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[t]
consists of prime ideals
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[t,\mathbf{z}]
such that
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}+\langle t-a\rangle\subseteq\mathcal{P}
. Thus

\varphi_{\mathsf{x},h}^{-1}(\langle t-a\rangle)=\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[t,\mathbf{z}]/(\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}+\langle t-a\rangle).

Let
\mathrm{ev}_{a}:\mathbb{C}[t,\mathbf{z}]\to\mathbf{\mathbb{C}[z]}
denote the evaluation homomorphism mapping
t
to
a
. Note that
\mathrm{ev}_{a}
satisfies
\operatorname{ker}(\mathrm{ev}_{a})=\left<t-a\right>
and
\mathrm{ev}_{a}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h})=\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x}_{a},h}
. Thus
\mathrm{ev}_{a}
induces a bijection between between prime ideals in
\mathbb{C}[t,\mathbf{z}]
containing
{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}+\langle t-a\rangle
and prime ideals in
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
containing
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x}_{a},h}
. The fact that
\mathrm{ev}_{a}(d)=d
yields an isomorphism of schemes,

\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[t,\mathbf{z}]/(\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}+\langle t-a\rangle)\simeq\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]/\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{x}_{a},h}

so each fiber of
\varphi_{\mathsf{x},h}
is a matrix Hessenberg scheme.
If
a\neq 0
, the matrix
\mathsf{x}_{a}
is conjugate to
a\mathsf{x}
by Lemma
3.9
and thus we get an isomorphism between the fiber
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}_{a},h}
over
a
and
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x},h}
in that case. Finally, by construction
\mathsf{x}_{0}=\mathsf{n}_{\mathsf{x}}
so the fiber over
0

\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n}_{\mathsf{x}},h}
.
∎

Conjecture 3.15

The one-parameter family
\varphi_{\mathsf{x},h}
is a flat morphism for all
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})

The main result of this paper proves this conjecture for
\mathsf{x}

Theorem 3.16
.

For all Hessenberg functions
h:[n]\to[n]
and all
\mathsf{x}\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{(2,1^{n-2})}
, the morphism
\varphi_{\mathsf{x},h}:\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}\to\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[t]
is flat. In particular, every matrix Hessenberg scheme over the minimal sheet admits a flat degeneration to the minimal nilpotent matrix Hessenberg scheme.

From the theorem, it follows that all matrix Hessenberg schemes over the minimal sheet have the same dimension and cohomology classes (see Lemma
4.2
and Remark
4.3
below). We explore these consequences after the proof of Theorem
3.16
in Section
.

. We describe the family explicitly by identifying the associated ideals of
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}
, and illustrate that
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}
is not equidimensional.

We noted in Example
3.12
that

\mathsf{x}_{t}=\mathsf{s}_{t}=\mathrm{diag}(t,0,0,0)+E_{14}.

As in Example
2.9
, the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}
is a sum over two corner elements, one of which vanishes, so
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h,1}
.
The rank conditions defining
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}
are then

\displaystyle\operatorname{rk}\begin{bmatrix}tz_{11}+z_{41}&z_{11}&z_{12}\\
0&z_{21}&z_{22}\\
0&z_{31}&z_{32}\\
0&z_{41}&z_{42}\\
\end{bmatrix}\leq 2.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<tz_{11}+z_{41}\right>\cdot\left<z_{21}z_{32}-z_{22}z_{31},z_{21}z_{42}-z_{22}z_{41},z_{31}z_{42}-z_{32}z_{41}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<tz_{11}+z_{41}\right>\cdot\mathcal{K}_{2}.

Using Macaulay2, we confirm that

\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}=\mathcal{K}_{2}\cap\left<tz_{11}+z_{41}\right>

and it is clear that each of the ideals
\mathcal{K}_{2}
and
\left<tz_{11}+z_{41}\right>
is prime. Since the vanishing set of each of these ideal intersects

, we have
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}
. We conclude that
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}
is the reduced scheme theoretic union of two irreducible components, one of dimension
\dim\mathbb{C}[t,\mathbf{z}]/\mathcal{K}_{2}=15
and the other of dimension
\dim\mathbb{C}[t,\mathbf{z}]/\left<tz_{11}+z_{41}\right>=16
. In particular, the scheme
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}
is
not equidimensional
.

The fiber of the family
\varphi_{\mathsf{x},h}
over
0
is precisely the nilpotent matrix Hessenberg scheme of Example
2.9
. The fiber over

is isomorphic to the semisimple matrix Hessenberg scheme
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}
. Thus we obtain a flat degeneration from
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}
to
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n},h}
.
∎

Our proof of Theorem

leverages commutative algebra in the polynomial ring

Theorem

4.
Flat morphisms and miracle flatness

In this section, we review properties of flatness, setting the stage for the proof that
\varphi_{\mathsf{x},h}:\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}\rightarrow\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[t]
is flat when
\mathsf{x}
is in the minimal sheet. We explain why standard techniques used in some other cases (miracle flatness) do not apply in the setting of the minimal sheet.

4.1.
Flat morphisms

Suppose
R
and
S
are commutative rings with identity.
Recall that a morphism
\varphi:\mathrm{Spec}\ \!R\to\mathrm{Spec}\ \!S
of affine schemes is
flat
whenever
R
, regarded as a
S
-module via the map
\varphi^{*}:S\to R
, is a flat
S
-module.
In that case, we say that the collection of fibers of
\varphi
is a
flat family
. In this paper we assume
S=\mathbb{C}[t]
, in which case the flat morphism
\varphi
is called a
flat degeneration
. Let
a\neq 0
be a complex number. We call any scheme isomorphic to the scheme-theoretic fiber over the closed point
\left<t-a\right>\in\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[t]
the
general fiber
of
\varphi
and the scheme-theoretic fiber over
0
the
special
or
degenerate fiber
.

A particularly nice criterion for flatness applies for families defined over a principal ideal domain, captured in the following lemma; see
[
Eisenbud
, Cor 6.3, pg. 164]
. We use it in Section
to prove Theorem
1
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
S
be a commutative ring and
M
an
S
-module. If
S
is a principal ideal domain, then
M
is flat as an
S
-module if and only if
M
is torsion free.

Flat families preserve several invariants of the corresponding fibers, resulting in relationships among the fibers. We collect them here for ease of reference.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose
\varphi:\mathcal{Y}_{t}\to\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[t]
is a flat
family with general fiber
\mathcal{Y}
and special fiber
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
.
Then

(1)

\dim\mathcal{Y}=\dim\mathcal{Y}_{0}
, and

(2)

if
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
is a graded scheme, then the multidegree of
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
with respect to this grading are equal.

These properties stem from the observation that graded flat families preserve
the graded Hilbert series
of the fibers
[
Eisenbud
, Exerercise 6.11, pg. 175]
.
In particular, when an ideal
\mathcal{I}
of
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
is homogeneous with respect to a
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
grading of the polynomial ring, the quotient
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]/\mathcal{I}
is has a well defined graded Hilbert series, which depends on the grading. In this case, we associate the graded Hilbert series with the scheme
\mathcal{Y}=\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]/\mathcal{I}
.

We refer the interested reader to
[
MS05
, §8.1,8.2]
for a formal definition of the graded Hilbert series. Importantly, the graded Hilbert series determines a scheme’s
multidegree
, which itself determines the scheme’s dimension and cohomology class. The multidegree is a polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{x}]=\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{d}]
, of degree
\dim(\mathcal{Y})
, defined using a particular shift and truncation of the graded Hilbert series. A precise definition can be found in
[
MS05
, §8.5]
.

Suppose
\mathcal{Y}
is a closed subscheme of the affine scheme
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
of
n\times n
matrices such that
\mathcal{Y}\cap G=\pi^{-1}(Y)
, where
Y\subseteq G/B
is a closed subscheme. Knutson and Miller
[
Knutson-Miller2005
]
proved that the multidegree of the coordinate ring of
\mathcal{Y}
(with respect to the grading
\deg(z_{ij})=e_{j},
the
j
th coordinate vector) is equal to the class of
Y
in the cohomology ring of the flag variety
G/B
(see also
[
Insko-Tymoczko-Woo
, Prop. 3]
). Here the cohomology ring
H^{*}(G/B)
has been identified with a quotient of the polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
via the Borel presentation. In summary, the cohomology classes of fibers in a graded flat family are equal. We summarize this conclusion in the remark below.

Remark 4.3
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{Y}_{t}\subseteq\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[t,\mathbf{z}]
, and that

\varphi:\mathcal{Y}_{t}\rightarrow\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[t]
is a flat family with general fiber
\mathcal{Y}
and special fiber
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
. Let
Y
and
Y_{0}
be closed subschemes of the flag variety
G/B
such that
\pi^{-1}(Y)=\mathcal{Y}\cap G
and
\pi^{-1}(Y_{0})=\mathcal{Y}_{0}\cap G
. If
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
is a graded subscheme of
\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[t,\mathbf{z}]
with respect to the grading induced by that on
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
resulting from
\deg(z_{ij})=e_{j},
then
[Y]=[Y_{0}]
. That is, the cohomology classes of
Y
and
Y_{0}
in
H^{*}(G/B)
are equal.

4.2.
Miracle flatness and applications

Miracle flatness
refers to a specific context in which a family of algebraic varieties is necessarily flat. We shall see that miracle flatness cannot be used to prove Theorem
3.16
; see Remark
4.7
. However, since miracle flatness is the main tool used in the literature for proving that certain families of Hessenberg varieties are flat, it makes sense to address it here.
We highlight two cases in which it applies, and provide clear illustration for the failure of miracle flatness for the family of Hessenberg schemes over the minimal sheet.

The following version of miracle flatness can be found in
[
Matsumura
, §23, Thm. 23.1]
. Roughly speaking, this result allows us to conclude a morphism is flat provided that the fibers are all the same dimension.

Theorem 4.4

(Miracle Flatness)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are irreducible schemes and
\varphi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
a morphism. Suppose
\mathcal{Y}
is regular,
\mathcal{X}
is Cohen–Macaulay,
\varphi
takes closed points of
\mathcal{X}
to closed points of
\mathcal{Y}
, and for every closed point
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, the fiber
\varphi^{-1}(y)
has dimension
\dim\mathcal{X}-\dim\mathcal{Y}

(
or is empty
)
. Then
\varphi
is flat.

Flatness for regular Hessenberg varieties was originally proved for
\mathsf{x}
semisimple in
[
ADGH2018
]
and in full generality by Abe, Fujita, and Zeng in
[
Abe-Fujita-Zeng2020
, §6.2]
. Hessenberg varieties associated to the Hessenberg function
h=(n-1,n,\ldots,n)
are studied in detail by the second author, Escobar, and Shareshian in
[
EPS-online
]
. In both cases, miracle flatness can be used to deduce that the family
\varphi_{\mathsf{x},h}
is flat. The proof of the following propositions may be found in the appendix (Section
).

Proposition 4.5
.

Given
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and
h:[n]\to[n]
a Hessenberg function, let
\varphi_{\mathsf{x},h}:\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}\to\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[t]
be the morphism (
3.5
). Suppose that for each
a\in\mathbb{C}
,

•

\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}_{a},h}
is irreducible, and

•

\dim\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}_{a},h}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(i)
.

Then
\varphi_{\mathsf{x},h}
is flat.

This dimension criterion may be used to identify several cases in which there is a flat family of matrix Hessenberg schemes:

Proposition 4.6
.

Suppose
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and
h:[n]\to[n]
is a Hessenberg function. Assume further that either

(1)

\mathsf{x}
is an element of the regular sheet
{\mathfrak{g}}_{reg}
and
h(i)>i
, or that

(2)

h=(n-1,n,\ldots,n)
.

Then
\varphi_{\mathsf{x},h}
is flat.

Remark 4.7
.

When
\mathsf{x}
is in the minimal sheet, Theorem
4.4
does not apply to
\varphi_{\mathsf{x},h}
. Any Cohen–Macaulay scheme must be equidimensional
[
Eisenbud
, Cor. 18.11]
, yet Example
3.17
shows that this is not always the case over the minimal sheet.

The identification of two cases in which miracle flatness applies, and one case in which it does not, leads to the following open question.

Question B
.

For which
\mathsf{x}
and
h
does miracle flatness apply to the family
\varphi_{\mathsf{x},h}:\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x}_{t},h}\rightarrow\mathrm{Spec}\ \!\mathbb{C}[t]
? More specifically, in which cases does Proposition
4.5
apply?

5.2.
Gröbner background

We recall essential facts about Gröbner geometry needed for the technical arguments of the next section.
Let
R
be a polynomial ring over an algebraically closed field
k
. Let
\preceq
be a fixed monomial ordering on
R
. Denote the leading term of a nonzero polynomial
f\in R
by
\mathrm{LT}(f)
and let
\mathrm{LM}(f)
be the monic monomial such that
\mathrm{LT}(f)=c\,\mathrm{LM}(f)
for some
c\in k
, called the leading monomial of
f
.

Given a nonzero ideal
\mathcal{I}
we let
\mathrm{LT}_{\preceq}(\mathcal{I})
be the ideal generated by the leading terms of the elements of
\mathcal{I}
.
A finite subset
G=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\}
of a nonzero ideal
\mathcal{I}\subseteq R
is a
Gröbner basis
if

\mathrm{LT}_{\preceq}(\mathcal{I})=\left<\mathrm{LT}(g_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{LT}(g_{r})\right>.

By convention, the empty set is a Gröbner basis of the zero idea.

Given two polynomials
f_{1},f_{2}\in R
, we let
\mathrm{M}(f_{1},f_{2})
denote the (monic) least common multiple of
\mathrm{LM}(f_{1})
and
\mathrm{LM}(f_{2})
. The
S
-polynomial
of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
is

(5.3)

\displaystyle S(f_{1},f_{2}):=\frac{\mathrm{M}(f_{1},f_{2})}{\mathrm{LT}(f_{1})}f_{1}-\frac{\mathrm{M}(f_{1},f_{2})}{\mathrm{LT}(f_{2})}f_{2}.

Buchberger’s Criterion states that
G=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\}
is a Gröber basis of
\mathcal{I}=\left<g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\right>
if and only if for all pairs
i\neq j
the remainder of
S(g_{i},g_{j})
on division by
G
(with respect to some fixed order) is zero. We make use of a refinement of Buchberger’s Criterion; to state the refinement, we need the following definition.

Let
G=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\}\subseteq R
. Given
f\in R
we say
f

reduces to zero modulo
G
, denoted by
f\rightarrow_{G}0
, if
f
has a
standard representation
:

f=a_{1}g_{1}+\cdots+a_{r}g_{r},\;a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}\in R

such that
a_{i}g_{i}\neq 0
implies
\mathrm{LM}(a_{i}g_{i})\preceq\mathrm{LM}(f)
for all
i
. The following is
[
CLO
, Chap. 2, §9, Thm. 3]
.

Theorem 5.4
.

A generating set
G=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\}
of an ideal
\mathcal{I}
in
R
is a Gröbner basis if and only if
S(g_{i},g_{j})\rightarrow_{G}0
for all
i\neq j
.

We use the following lemmas to simplify our arguments below; the following is
[
CLO
, Chap. 2, §9, Prop. 4]
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
G\subseteq R
be a finite set of polynomials and suppose
f,g\in G
such that the leading monomials of
f
and
g
are relatively prime. Then
S(f,g)\rightarrow_{G}0
.

For finite sets
G,H\subseteq R
, let

GH:=\{gh:g\in G,h\in H\}

denote the set of products of polynomials
g\in G
and
h\in H
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Suppose
G,H\subseteq R
are two finite sets of polynomials.
Let
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
and
h_{1},h_{2}\in H
with the property that the leading monomials of
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are each relatively prime to the leading monomials of both
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
. If
S(g_{1},g_{2})\rightarrow_{G}0
and
S(h_{1},h_{2})\rightarrow_{H}0
, then
S(g_{1}h_{1},g_{2}h_{2})\rightarrow_{GH}0
.

Proof.

We calculate the
S
polynomial directly. Since
\mathrm{LM}(gh)=\mathrm{LM}(g)\mathrm{LM}(h)
for all
g\in G,h\in H
, (see
[
CLO
, Chap. 2, §2, Lemma 8]
), our assumptions imply
\mathrm{M}(g_{1}h_{1},g_{2}h_{2})=\mathrm{M}(g_{1},g_{2})\mathrm{M}(h_{1},h_{2})
.
For the rest of this proof, let
\mathrm{M}_{g}=\mathrm{M}(g_{1},g_{2})
and
\mathrm{M}_{h}=\mathrm{M}(h_{1},h_{2})
. A straightforward calculation shows

\displaystyle S(g_{1}h_{1},g_{2}h_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{M}_{g}\mathrm{M}_{h}}{\mathrm{LT}(g_{1})\mathrm{LT}(h_{1})}g_{1}h_{1}-\frac{\mathrm{M}_{g}\mathrm{M}_{h}}{\mathrm{LT}(g_{2})\mathrm{LT}(h_{2})}g_{2}h_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{M}_{g}\mathrm{M}_{h}}{\mathrm{LT}(g_{1})\mathrm{LT}(h_{1})}g_{1}h_{1}-\frac{\mathrm{M}_{g}\mathrm{M}_{h}}{\mathrm{LT}(g_{1})\mathrm{LT}(h_{2})}g_{1}h_{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{=\frac{\mathrm{M}_{g}\mathrm{M}_{h}}{\mathrm{LT}(g_{1})\mathrm{LT}(h_{1})}g_{1}h_{1}\ }+\frac{\mathrm{M}_{g}\mathrm{M}_{h}}{\mathrm{LT}(g_{1})\mathrm{LT}(h_{2})}g_{1}h_{2}-\frac{\mathrm{M}_{g}\mathrm{M}_{h}}{\mathrm{LT}(g_{2})\mathrm{LT}(h_{2})}g_{2}h_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{M}_{g}}{\mathrm{LT}(g_{1})}g_{1}\left(\frac{\mathrm{M}_{h}}{\mathrm{LT}(h_{1})}h_{1}-\frac{\mathrm{M}_{h}}{\mathrm{LT}(h_{2})}h_{2}\right)+\frac{\mathrm{M}_{h}}{\mathrm{LT}(h_{2})}h_{2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{M}_{g}}{\mathrm{LT}(g_{1})}g_{1}-\frac{\mathrm{M}_{g}}{\mathrm{LT}(g_{2})}g_{2}\right)

(5.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{M}_{g}}{\mathrm{LT}(g_{1})}g_{1}S(h_{1},h_{2})+\frac{\mathrm{M}_{h}}{\mathrm{LT}(h_{2})}h_{2}S(g_{1},g_{2}).

If
S(g_{1}h_{1},g_{2}h_{2})\neq 0
then together with
[
CLO
, Chap. 2, §2 Lemma 8]
, this equation implies

(5.5)

\mathrm{LM}(S(g_{1}h_{1},g_{2}h_{2}))\preceq\max\left\{\mathrm{M}_{g}\mathrm{LM}(S(h_{1},h_{2})),\mathrm{M}_{h}\mathrm{LM}(S(g_{1},g_{2}))\right\}

with equality if and only if
\mathrm{M}_{g}\mathrm{LM}(S(h_{1},h_{2}))\neq\mathrm{M}_{h}\mathrm{LM}(S(g_{1},g_{2}))

Proposition 5.7

(The Elimination Property)
.

Let

be an ideal and let

be a Gröbner basis of
\mathcal{I}
