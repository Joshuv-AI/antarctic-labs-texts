Analysis of the risk of metachronous colorectal cancer and long-term prognosis in patients following gastrectomy for gastric cancer
Authors: Jun He, Bixian Luo, Hongming Liu, Dong Cao, Weihua Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The impact of gastrectomy on the risk of developing second colorectal cancer (SCRC) in gastric cancer patients remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the association between gastrectomy and SCRC in gastric cancer patients. A total of 17,606 patients with gastric cancer were identified from the SEER database and categorized into gastrectomy (GT) and non-gastrectomy (NGT) groups. Fine-Gray competing risk analysis was employed to calculate the cumulative incidence of SCRC in different groups. Competing risk analysis and Poisson regression were used to assess the association between gastrectomy and SCRC risk. Standardized incidence ratios (SIRs) were calculated to compare CRC incidence in the GT and NGT groups with that in the general US population. A total of 128 patients in the GT group (0.8%) and 9 patients in the NGT group (0.4%) developed SCRC. Gastrectomy was identified as a significant adverse factor in both univariate and multivariate Poisson regression analyses. SIRs revealed a higher CRC incidence in the GT group compared to the general population, while the NGT group showed no significant difference. Gastrectomy is associated with an increased risk of SCRC in gastric cancer patients and significantly improves survival in those who develop SCRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25346-0.

Introduction

Despite significant advancements in gastric cancer treatment, gastrectomy remains the primary curative option1. The adverse effects of gastrectomy are mainly associated with reflux and impaired digestive function2. Some studies have reported a higher incidence of synchronous and metachronous colorectal cancer (CRC) in gastric cancer patients3–5. However, it is unclear whether the increased CRC incidence in gastric cancer patients is due to the cancer itself or its treatment.

For obese patients, the effect of bariatric surgery on postoperative CRC risk is controversial. Some studies indicate that bariatric surgery might elevate the risk of CRC, possibly due to structural alterations in the gastrointestinal tract caused by surgical reconstruction6,7. Conversely, other studies have reported a reduction in CRC risk following bariatric surgery, attributing this to systemic physiological changes, such as weight loss and improved glycemic control8–10. Similarly, gastrectomy in gastric cancer patients impacts both the digestive system and metabolism. However, the effect of gastrectomy on CRC risk in these patients remains uncertain.

Although some studies have investigated the probability of developing second primary tumors in gastric cancer patients, these studies primarily focus on the incidence of various cancers and do not examine the specific impact of gastrectomy on postoperative CRC risk11. Therefore, we utilized the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program (SEER) database to analyze data from gastric cancer patients. Our aim was to determine whether gastrectomy increases the subsequent risk of CRC in these patients. Additionally, we evaluated the survival outcomes of gastric cancer patients who developed CRC, comparing those who underwent gastrectomy with those who did not.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study utilized the Incidence—SEER Research Data, 17 Registries, Nov 2023 Sub (2000–2021) dataset from the SEER database, selecting patients diagnosed with gastric cancer between 2000 and 2015.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pathologically diagnosed gastric cancer, with the Third Edition of the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O-3) coded as C16.0–C16.9, (2) age of at least 18 years, (3) localized or regional stage (Supplementary Table 1), and (4) first primary gastric cancer. The exclusion criteria included unknown race, survival time of less than 3 years, distant stage, unstaged, or in situ, and unknown surgical procedure. Grouping and definition of second primary colorectal cancer

Gastric cancer patients were divided into two groups based on whether they underwent gastrectomy: the GT group (gastrectomy treatment group) and the NGT group (non-gastrectomy treatment group). The NGT group is defined as patients who did not undergo gastrectomy but received endoscopic treatments, including endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) and endoscopic laser ablation. The primary outcome of this study was the occurrence of second colorectal cancer (SCRC). The diagnosis of CRC was based on ICD-O-3 codes: C18.0–C18.9, C19.9, C20.9, and C26.0. SCRC was defined as colorectal cancer occurring at least three years after gastrectomy and meeting the criteria for a second primary malignancy.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages (n, %). Continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation) for normally distributed data or median (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Differences between categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fishers exact test. Continuous variables were analyzed using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, depending on the normality of their distributions.

Fine-Gray competing risk regression analysis was employed to calculate the cumulative incidence of SCRC. In the competing risk analysis, death without SCRC occurrence was considered a competing event. Variables with statistical significance in univariate competing risk analysis were included in the multivariate analysis, which was used to identify factors influencing SCRC occurrence. Poisson regression analysis was used to estimate the gastrectomy-associated risk (RR), calculating the relative risk and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for SCRC occurrence in gastric cancer patients who underwent gastrectomy compared to those who did not. The standardized incidence ratio (SIR) was defined as the ratio of the observed incidence of SCRC among gastric cancer survivors to the incidence of CRC in the general population of the United States. The SIRs were computed using SEER*Stat version 8.4.4.

The survival of gastric cancer patients with SCRC in the GT and NGT groups was compared using the Kaplan–Meier (KM) method and log-rank test. To balance confounding factors between the two groups, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed. All data analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance level set at p < 0.05.

Ethics statement

Since the SEER database is a publicly accessible database and patient information is anonymized, informed consent and ethical approval were not required. This study strictly adhered to the relevant guidelines.

Results

Patient characteristics

Initially, 98,877 gastric cancer patients were identified, and after rigorous screening, 17,606 patients with localized or regional stage were included. The majority of these patients were male (62.9%) and White (65.6%) (Table 1). The GT group consisted of 15,169 patients, while the NGT group included 2,437 patients. The median follow-up time were 90 months for the GT group and 66 months for the NGT group. A total of 128 patients in the GT group (0.8%) and 9 patients in the NGT group (0.4%) developed SCRC. The impact of gastrectomy on the risk of SCRC

The cumulative incidence of SCRC among all patients was 0.8%. According to the Fine-Gray competing risk analysis, the cumulative incidence curves of SCRC for the GT and NGT groups are shown in Fig. 2. Although the cumulative incidence curve for the GT group was higher than that of the NGT group, the difference did not reach statistical significance. In univariate competing risk analysis, only tumor stage (HR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.01–1.98, P = 0.044) and gastrectomy (HR = 2.13, 95% CI: 0.66–6.93, P = 0.038) were identified as significant factors influencing SCRC development (Table 2). However, when both variables were included in the multivariate analysis, neither was found to be a statistically significant factor. Poisson regression analysis was performed to calculate the relative risk (RR) for each variable. In univariate Poisson regression analysis, similar to the competing risk analysis, only tumor stage (RR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.01–1.97, P = 0.048) and gastrectomy (RR = 2.29, 95% CI: 1.23–4.84, P = 0.017) were significant factors (Table 3). Survival outcomes of gastric cancer patients with SCRC

We compared the overall survival (OS) of gastric cancer patients who developed SCRC in the GT and NGT groups. The median OS for gastric cancer patients with SCRC after gastrectomy was 152 months, compared to 80 months for those without gastrectomy (Fig. 3A). After PSM, the OS of gastric cancer patients with SCRC was reanalyzed. The median OS was 170 months for patients in the GT group and 80 months for those in the NGT group (Fig. 3B). Discussion

Gastric cancer is a prevalent malignancy of the digestive system, with the majority of cases diagnosed at a locally advanced stage12,13. Perioperative chemotherapy combined with surgery is the standard treatment for these patients14,15. Although gastrectomy remains the primary curative approach for gastric cancer, its potential adverse effects warrant attention. Gastrectomy can result in complications such as reflux and impaired digestive function, and gastrointestinal reconstruction may also disrupt the gut microbiota. With advancements in treatment strategies and efficacy, the survival of gastric cancer patients has improved. However, this extended survival has also led to an increased risk of developing second primary malignancies16. Both the tumor itself and its treatment are potential factors influencing the occurrence of subsequent malignancies17.

Previous studies have demonstrated that approximately 5% of gastric cancer patients develop second primary malignancies, with CRC being the most common18–20. Some research has reported that gastric cancer patients who undergo gastrectomy have a significantly higher likelihood of developing colorectal neoplasms compared to the general population. Many studies highlight the necessity of colonoscopy for CRC detection in postoperative gastric cancer patients, although the factors influencing the development of metachronous CRC in these patients remain unclear21,22. A retrospective study in Korea found that the incidence of synchronous and metachronous CRC in gastric cancer patients who underwent surgery (gastrectomy or endoscopic resection) was 4.4% and 2.0%, respectively23. Older age (≥ 63 years) and male were identified as risk factors for developing synchronous and metachronous CRC in these patients. However, this study was limited by a small sample size and the exclusion of gastric cancer patients who did not undergo surgical treatment, leaving the impact of gastrectomy on CRC development unclear.

The development of CRC is associated with multiple factors, including genetics, diet, age, and obesity24. In our study, although various methods were employed to reduce the influence of confounding factors, it is impossible to balance all factors completely. A systematic review suggested that structural changes in the gastrointestinal tract following gastrectomy may alter the composition of the gut microbiota, and this dysbiosis could increase the risk of postoperative CRC25. Additionally, the higher risk of developing SCRC in the GT group may be partly related to their longer survival, as patients with longer survival have a greater cumulative probability of developing secondary malignancies. Currently, there is a lack of mechanistic studies exploring the impact of gastrointestinal reconstruction on CRC development.

This study included a large sample of gastric cancer patients and is the first to investigate the impact of gastrectomy on the risk of developing SCRC. We found that the incidence of SCRC was significantly higher in gastric cancer patients who underwent gastrectomy compared to those who did not. Although the cumulative incidence of SCRC in the GT group was consistently higher than that in the NGT group in Fig. 2, the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.058), which may be related to the smaller sample size in the NGT group. In the competing risk analysis, gastrectomy was a significant factor only in the univariate analysis. In contrast, Poisson regression analysis revealed that gastrectomy was a significant factor in both univariate and multivariate analyses. Moreover, gastrectomy was identified as the sole significant factor influencing the risk of SCRC. The discrepancy between the results of these two statistical methods may be attributed to the consideration of competing events in the competing risk analysis. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the effect of gastrectomy on SCRC risk, we compared the CRC incidence of the gastrectomy and non-gastrectomy groups with that of the general US population. We found that gastric cancer patients who underwent gastrectomy had a higher CRC incidence than the general US population, whereas those who did not undergo gastrectomy showed no significant difference in CRC incidence compared to the general population. Colorectal cancer (CRC) screening for gastric cancer patients who have undergone gastrectomy may enable early detection of SCRC, which could lead to improved prognosis.

In our study, we also compared the survival of gastric cancer patients with SCRC who underwent gastrectomy to those who did not. Both before and after PSM, the survival of gastric cancer patients with SCRC who underwent gastrectomy was significantly better than that of those who did not undergo gastrectomy. This suggests that gastrectomy may have an important impact on the survival of gastric cancer patients who develop SCRC.

In summary, this study utilized multiple methods to analyze the impact of gastrectomy on the development of SCRC in gastric cancer patients. Fine–Gray competing risk analysis was used to account for competing risks. In this analysis, death without SCRC occurrence was considered a competing event. Poisson regression analysis was applied to estimate the relative risk of SCRC in patients undergoing gastrectomy. Standardized incidence ratios were used to compare the incidence of colorectal cancer in gastric cancer patients (both GT and NGT groups) with that in the U.S. general population. The use of these three approaches enhances the rigor of the study. Additionally, propensity-score matching was used to reduce the impact of baseline differences and sample size imbalance between the groups. Considering baseline balance and the difficulty in matching a sufficient number of patients in the GT group, a 1:2 matching ratio was chosen.

It is a large population-based study with a long follow-up period. However, there are several limitations. First, as a retrospective study, it is subject to unavoidable confounding biases. Second, the gastrointestinal reconstruction methods following gastrectomy were unknown, and these methods may influence the risk of developing CRC. Third, the study population was entirely limited to the United States, which may affect generalizability.

Conclusion

In summary, we utilized multiple approaches to investigate whether gastrectomy increases the risk of SCRC in gastric cancer patients. Without accounting for competing events, gastrectomy was associated with an increased risk of SCRC. Additionally, gastric cancer patients who underwent gastrectomy exhibited a higher incidence of CRC compared to the general population. Therefore, CRC screening should be emphasized for gastric cancer patients following gastrectomy.
