Flash droughts threaten global managed forests
Authors: Jianzhuang Pang, Hang Xu, Yang Xu, Yifan Zhang, Xiaoyun Wu, Kexin Li, Zhiqiang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFlash droughts, characterized by rapid onset and increasing frequency, pose significant threats to ecosystem stability and function. However, there remains no global consensus regarding forest responses to flash droughts. Here, using a reconstructed global high spatiotemporal resolution Standardized Precipitation-Evapotranspiration Index dataset and an interpretable machine learning framework, we find that global forests have experienced increasingly rapid, intense, and prolonged flash droughts over the past four decades. Managed forests are more prone to browning from flash droughts than intact forests due to their limited capacity to acclimate to rapid drought stress driven by extreme heat. Notably, our meta-analysis confirms that current forest management practices, designed to maximize ecosystem services, exacerbate the vulnerability of managed forests to flash droughts globally. Our findings highlight the escalating risks posed by increasingly frequent and prolonged flash droughts to managed forests, underscoring the urgent need to integrate resistance and resilience to extreme climatic events into forest management strategies.Subject terms: Natural hazards, Environmental impact

Introduction

Flash droughts, defined as droughts rapidly escalating into water stress within weeks1, are increasing in frequency and intensity with rising global temperatures2. Rapid shifts in water availability induced by flash droughts increase forest vulnerability, as trees typically struggle to adjust physiological processes to such abrupt changes3. Additionally, flash droughts are associated with compound hot-dry conditions4, further intensified by land-atmosphere feedback, thereby exacerbating stress in forests5 and potentially causing significant adverse impacts6. Indeed, the global response of forests to flash droughts remains a topic of ongoing debate. For instance, a recent study found rapid declines in the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) of forests during flash droughts in Northeast China7. In contrast, research reported that forests exhibit strong resistance to flash droughts, experiencing NDVI declines only after prolonged water stress8. Furthermore, studies from the Americas have documented a counterintuitive increase in NDVI during flash droughts in humid regions9,10. Therefore, understanding global forest responses to flash droughts is essential for identifying forests at potential risk and informing appropriate management strategies.

Accurate assessment of flash droughts impacts on global forests requires indicators capable of monitoring drought intensification at sub-weekly intervals1. Soil moisture is a key indicator for identifying flash droughts due to its direct relation to water availability11. However, existing soil moisture products suffer limitations due to short observational records and shallow detection depths constrained by sensor technology. This limitation is particularly problematic in forested areas, where high vegetation optical depth obstructs accurate measurements of deep soil moisture12, thereby introducing substantial uncertainty in flash drought identification. Although the evaporative stress ratio, which quantifies forest evapotranspiration responses relative to anomalous evaporative demand, is recognized as an indirect indicator of water limitation13, the high uncertainty of evapotranspiration measurements from remote sensing and land surface models14 hinders the accurate identification and prediction of flash droughts. By contrast, precipitation and air temperature data provide long-term, higher-density records with relatively high accuracy and predictive reliability, highlighting their potential for early flash drought warning15. Consequently, the Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI), integrating water availability and evaporative demand, is a widely adopted metric for identifying flash droughts and demonstrates strong consistency with soil moisture and evapotranspiration dynamics16. However, most SPEI-based flash drought studies typically focus on local scales16,17. Research is further constrained by the coarse spatiotemporal resolution (i.e., monthly or 0.25°) of available SPEI products at a global scale18. Thus, understanding global forest responses to flash droughts requires developing a long-term, high spatiotemporal resolution SPEI dataset for identifying and characterizing flash droughts precisely.

Forest responses to flash droughts arise from the interactive effects among multiple regulating factors. Many studies confirm that meteorological anomalies during flash droughts and regional background climate significantly mediate forest responses7,19. Specifically, meteorological anomalies impact tree transpiration and photosynthesis by directly altering evaporative demand and water availability4. In addition, background climate influences the direction and magnitude of forest responses to meteorological anomalies, thereby regulating how forests respond to flash droughts. Moreover, forest responses vary according to flash drought characteristics. Increased drought intensity and duration generally exacerbate NDVI declines20. Fundamentally, the insufficient quantification of flash drought characteristics—such as onset rate, peak stress, and stress duration—continues to impede insights into how these attributes mediate forest responses. Studies quantifying the impact of regulating factors on forest responses21, usually focus on specific regions or isolated factors, inadequately addressing the spatial variability and their complex interactive effects. These limitations—insufficient quantification of flash drought characteristics and narrow regional or isolated-factor approaches—have led to inconsistent identification of key influencing factors across studies19,22. Consequently, a global reassessment of various regulating factors and their interactive effects on forest responses to flash drought is essential for accurately identifying forests at potential risk.

Managed forests cover over 2 billion hectares (~54% of the global forest area), reflecting worldwide efforts to reduce forest loss and provide multiple ecosystem services, such as soil and water conservation, climate change mitigation, and socio-economic benefits23. However, recent studies raise concerns regarding the sustainability of these services, primarily because managed forests exhibit limited acclimatization to extreme climates24,25. Forest management practices (e.g., planting, logging, and fertilization) may compromise forest resilience and acclimatization capacity to water stress by altering characteristics such as age structure, canopy architecture, stand density, and root systems26. For instance, a recent study reported significantly greater reductions in basal area increments in Central Europe managed forests compared to intact forests under slowly developing drought conditions27. However, whether managed forests and intact forests differ in response to flash droughts remains unclear. Furthermore, the influence of forest management practices on forest responses to flash droughts under varying environmental conditions remains a critical knowledge gap. This gap may hinder forest managers and natural resource policymakers in addressing future natural disturbances, threatening the sustainability of forests and their associated benefits.

In this study, we quantify forest responses to flash droughts and analyze the effects arising from multiple regulating factors over the period 1979–2018. First, we identify global flash droughts and analyze the spatiotemporal patterns of flash drought characteristics using a global high spatiotemporal resolution SPEI dataset (gSPEI-HR, 0.1°, daily). This dataset, achieving unprecedented spatiotemporal resolution, is derived from standardized 5-day (i.e., pentad) moving-average water deficits (precipitation minus potential evapotranspiration) based on the land component of the fifth generation of European ReAnalysis dataset. Forest response is quantified using standardized NDVI anomalies (ΔNDVI) during flash droughts, obtained from satellite-based reanalysis datasets. Subsequently, an interpretable machine learning framework is applied to identify key factors that regulate the impacts of flash droughts on global forests across various background climates, meteorological anomalies, forest traits, and flash drought characteristics. Finally, we address the knowledge gap regarding the complex influence of forest management by examining differences in forest responses under management practices and exploring interactive effects between forest management and other regulating factors. We find that current forest management practices inadvertently exacerbate the global vulnerability of managed forests to flash droughts, amplifying their susceptibility to widespread browning under changing climate.

Results and discussion

Spatiotemporal patterns of flash drought characteristics from 1982 to 2022

Over the past four decades, global forests have experienced flash droughts (Fig. 1), characterized by accelerating onset, prolonged stress duration, and increasing intensities (Supplementary Fig. 1). These flash drought characteristics show a significant spatial heterogeneity across latitudes. Low-latitude forests experience flash droughts more frequently, with faster onset (onset rate = −0.89 pentad−1), longer duration (stress duration = 4.82 pentads), and greater intensity (peak stress = −1.89) compared to forests at other latitudes. Moderate frequency flash droughts observed in high-latitude forests exhibit onset rates comparable to those in low-latitude forests, though with relatively weaker intensity (peak stress = −1.88) and shorter durations (stress duration = 4.52 pentads). Mid-latitude forests experience substantially lower flash drought frequency than both low- and high-latitude forests, featuring slower onset (onset rate = −0.85 pentad−1), shorter duration (stress duration = 4.35 pentads), and lower intensity (peak stress = −1.82).

The Sahelian Belt, exhibiting high sensitivity to precipitation variability28, is identified as the region frequently experiencing precipitation-driven flash droughts (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 2). Prolonged dry spells (Supplementary Fig. 1c) exacerbate soil moisture depletion in this semi-arid region29, leading to increased sensible heat flux from the drying surface, elevated air temperatures, and potentially further amplifying the severity of flash droughts30. The Amazon Basin experiences the highest severity of flash droughts with onset rate <−1 pentad−1, stress duration >5 pentads, and peak stress <−2 (Fig. 1). Flash droughts in this region are generally induced by the substantial rises in air temperature and the declines in precipitation (52.70%, n = 27903 of 52945; Supplementary Fig. 2), which further exacerbate compound hot-dry conditions through the land-atmosphere feedback31. Furthermore, increases in temperatures and precipitation variability under high-emission scenarios in the future may further intensify compound hot-dry conditions32. In the Central Siberian Plateau, the substantial rise in temperature leads to a marked increase in atmospheric dryness and evaporative demand33, making flash droughts to enter the stress stage (SPEI ≤ −1.28, see “Methods”) rapidly (Fig. 1b). Notably, both the onset rate and intensity are intensifying more rapidly than those observed in low-latitude forests (Supplementary Fig. 1b, d).

Although regional discrepancies exist, particularly in ecologically diverse areas such as the Amazon Basin, Southern Africa, and Southeast Asia, the broad spatial patterns of flash drought frequency derived from SPEI are generally consistent with those derived from soil moisture-based indicator (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Such regional differences are consistent with previous studies employing various drought indicators4,11,28,34–37. For example, our SPEI-based identification of flash drought frequency aligns closely with ecological indicator-based results (i.e., standardized evaporative stress rate)28,35,37 (Supplementary Fig. 4), while soil moisture-based indicators show higher flash drought frequencies in these regions4,36. Differences among datasets, spatial resolutions, and study periods may further explain the regional discrepancies between our findings and those of previous studies16,34. Nevertheless, we emphasize that despite these variations in flash drought frequency, the overall spatial patterns of flash drought characteristics remain robust and are largely insensitive to the choice of drought indicators (Supplementary Fig. 3b–d).

Impacts of flash droughts on global forests

Flash droughts cause browning in 42.73% of global forests (ΔNDVI < 0), primarily occur along the east coast of the United States, the Brazilian highlands, the Sahelian Belt, and southeast Asia (Fig. 2a). The structure (i.e., leaf area index (LAI)) and function (i.e., solar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF)) of these forests also are impaired due to flash droughts, with consistency relative to NDVI changes being 63.19%and 63.11%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5). Interestingly, the spatial patterns of NDVI response during soil moisture-based flash droughts closely resemble those from SPEI-based flash droughts yet exhibit smaller response magnitude (Supplementary Fig. 6). Shallow soil moisture data fail to adequately represent the capacity of deep-rooted trees to utilize deeper water storage during droughts38. Moreover, SPEI integrates rapid moisture depletion identified by precipitation and changes in evaporative demand, thus offering more comprehensive characterization, better predictability, and earlier warnings of forest stress under flash droughts than soil moisture alone37.

The variability of forest responses to flash droughts is mediated by multiple interacting regulatory factors. Machine learning based importance analysis identifies forest management (intact and managed forest; 18.37%) as the primary factor influencing spatial variations in ΔNDVI patterns, followed by mean annual temperature (MAT; 12.07%), temperature anomalies (10.30%), and stress duration (9.98%) during flash droughts (Table 1). Managed forests typically exhibit lower NDVI than intact forests and show greater susceptibility to browning (mean ΔNDVI = −0.11; Fig. 2b), a pattern consistently observed across diverse forest types (Supplementary Fig. 7). While the total influence is limited, variations in age and canopy height within specific ranges can significantly affect forest responses. Specifically, forest resilience to flash droughts is enhanced with increasing age (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Expanded root system depth and extent enable forests to access more water to meet rising evaporative demand during flash droughts39. However, intensified competition for resources such as light, water, and nutrients in closed-canopy mature stands may offset the advantage conferred by stronger individual drought resistance40. As canopy height increases, the risk of browning decreases (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Taller trees possess a greater capacity for light acquisition, making them benefit more from increased radiation and favorable temperature conditions during flash droughts41. Nevertheless, additional increases in canopy height elevate vulnerability because the longer water transport distances amplify hydraulic failure risks42.

The impacts of flash droughts on forests vary depending on the background climatic conditions. NDVI declines are more common in forests located in regions with higher MAT and lower mean annual precipitation (MAP; Fig. 2c). In regions with higher MAT, forest photosynthesis is limited by increasing temperature, thus resulting in browning during flash droughts (Supplementary Fig. 8g). In regions with lower MAP, forests exhibit increased susceptibility to browning during flash droughts owing to water deficit (Supplementary Fig. 8h). In contrast, forests in colder (MAT < 5 °C) or wetter (MAP > 1500 mm) regions tend to show neutral or beneficial responses to flash droughts, potentially altering managed forests responses to flash droughts. For instance, managed forests in the Eastern European Plain (MAT ≈ 0 °C, MAP > 800 mm) exhibit greening during flash droughts. This greening response occurs because rising temperatures enhance the activity of previously subdued photosynthetic enzymes19, allowing forests to utilize abundant available soil water to sustain photosynthesis.

Furthermore, prolonged flash droughts significantly exacerbate forest browning (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 8). During the onset stage of flash drought, defined as the period of rapidly intensifying water deficit preceding the stress stage (see “Methods”), forests NDVI increase due to elevated photosynthetic capacity (Supplementary Fig. 5c) associated with higher temperatures and solar radiation4. As flash drought persists, soil moisture declines rapidly due to increased transpiration exceeding replenishment from precipitation (precipitation anomalies <−0.6; Supplementary Fig. 8f). Low soil water available consequently limits photosynthesis, offsetting the initial benefits during the onset stage and resulting in forest browning (stress duration > 5 pentads, Supplementary Fig. 8j). The beneficial effects observed during the onset stage are typically associated only with moderate temperature increases (0.5 <temperature anomalies ≤ 1.5). Heat stress constrains photosynthesis through reduced enzyme activity43 or stomatal closure20, leading to forest reductions in NDVI (Supplementary Fig. 8e). Interestingly, when flash droughts persist beyond 11 pentads, NDVI declines slows, indicating that forests gradually acclimate to the disturbances caused by flash droughts.

Role of forest management in modulating flash droughts impact

Forest management practices can significantly increase forest vulnerability to flash droughts (Fig. 3). Specifically, intact forests and managed forests exhibit contrasting ΔNDVI responses to changes in temperature anomalies (Fig. 3a). Analysis results of interactive effects indicate that intact forests can generally offset NDVI loss caused by rising temperature anomalies, whereas more significant NDVI declines are observed in managed forests. Moreover, ΔNDVI responses to flash droughts stress duration differs substantially between intact forests and managed forests (Fig. 3k). Intact forests show resilience during the later stages of drought, whereas managed forests experience more NDVI declines during prolonged flash droughts, particularly when stress duration exceeds eight pentads. Nonetheless, prolonged drought gradually exhausts available soil moisture, ultimately causing growth reductions in intact forests. Additionally, intact forests and managed forests exhibit divergent ΔNDVI responses to precipitation anomalies (ΔP ≤ −0.66; Fig. 3b) and peak stress (PS <−1.80; Fig. 3j) during severe drought conditions. When canopy height exceeds approximately 23 m, intact forests resistance to flash droughts weakens, increasing the browning risk (Fig. 3f).

During flash droughts, different types of forest management result in NDVI decreases in managed forests, albeit with considerable variation in sensitivity (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Naturally regenerating forests with management interventions exhibit the smallest NDVI decline (ΔNDVI = −0.05), whereas agroforestry suffers the largest reductions (ΔNDVI = −0.29). Planted forests respond more sensitively to flash droughts than naturally regenerating forests, indicated by a larger NDVI decrease (ΔNDVI = −0.08). Additionally, rotation practices slightly exacerbate the decline in NDVI in planted forests during flash droughts (ΔNDVI = −0.10). We further analyzed the impact of specific forest management practices on forest responses to flash droughts through meta-analysis. Except for thinning, forest management practices lead to negative impacts on forests during flash droughts (Supplementary Fig. 9b).

Within naturally regenerating forests, logging substantially increases vulnerability to flash droughts. Clear-cuts, which remove all stand overstory in a single harvest44, substantially reduce forests resistance to flash droughts. On the one hand, clear-cuts reduces canopy cover and live root biomass, decreasing light use efficiency41, which lowers photosynthetic capacity and diminishes NDVI during flash droughts (Supplementary Fig. 8b). On the other hand, clear-cuts may accelerate soil erosion and water loss, reducing soil water availability and nutrient supply45, further increasing forest vulnerability to flash droughts and probably causing degradation. Selective logging typically removes only older, high-biomass trees44, minimizing adverse effects on residual stands (Supplementary Fig. 8a).

Planted forests with simplified structure and poorly adapted species exhibit heightened sensitivity to flash droughts disturbances (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Afforestation or reforestation frequently involves planting non-native species23 at high stand density with homogeneous age structures26, reducing forest growth potential and resilience under environmental stress. Timber-oriented rotation practices (e.g., clear-cuts and selective logging) exacerbate these vulnerabilities by favoring younger stands. Young forests typically adopt a permissive water-use strategy, prioritizing rapid growth but having lower water-use efficiency, resulting in higher water consumption than mature stands46 and making them more prone to degradation or mortality under water stress (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Similarly, fertilization applied in some planted forests (e.g., fuelwood/pulp plantations) to accelerate growth44 also heightens their vulnerability to flash droughts. As a result, managed forests are particularly susceptible to flash droughts and experience substantial growth losses as water stress intensifies and prolongs.

Effective forest management is essential for mitigating managed forest vulnerability to flash droughts, which disrupt ecosystem services and trigger feedback loops potentially exacerbating climate change11. This issue is particularly critical in regions such as the Sahelian Belt and the Brazilian Highlands, where extreme compound drought-heat conditions significantly enhance forest browning risk (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 2, and Fig. 3). Therefore, adaptive forest management is crucial for enhancing forest resilience and maintaining ecosystem stability under rapid environmental changes. This study underscores the necessity of transitioning from conventional, productivity-centric forestry practices to comprehensive management strategies emphasizing biodiversity enhancement, native species prioritization, optimized stand density, and diversified stand structures. Furthermore, sustainable logging practices—including selective logging, retention of forest residues, and rational thinning—are essential to mitigate flash drought impacts. Finally, developing robust early-warning systems for flash drought detection and impact prediction is imperative, providing forest managers with critical intervention windows to proactively minimize ecological and economic losses.

Methods

Environmental factors

We obtained precipitation, temperature, and soil moisture data from the land component of the fifth generation of European ReAnalysis (ERA5-Land) dataset, which was the latest generation developed by the Copernicus Climate Change Service. This dataset provides a spatial resolution of 0.1° and has captured terrestrial parameter variability since 1982 by integrating global model outputs with observational data through physically based land surface processes, effectively representing the spatiotemporal dynamics of precipitation47. In addition, we utilized MAT and mean annual precipitation data from the ERA5-Land dataset as background climate factors to perform an attribution analysis of forest responses to flash droughts.

However, the potential evapotranspiration data provided by the ERA5-Land dataset, labeled as Potential Evaporation, was identified to have significant errors48. To ensure data consistency, we utilized the hourly potential evapotranspiration (hPET) dataset calculated using the Penman-Monteith method49. This dataset integrates climatic variables from ERA5-Land, including temperature, solar radiation, humidity, and wind speed, while also accounting for critical physical processes such as energy balance and aerodynamic effects49. It has been demonstrated to accurately estimate potential evapotranspiration across diverse climatic conditions and geographic locations, showing high consistency with observational data products in temporal, spatial, and seasonal distributions49.

Biophysical parameters

The NDVI, LAI, and SIF have been extensively used to assess vegetation growth dynamics4,7,50. In this study, we utilized the bi-monthly PKU GIMMS NDVI product from 1982 to 2022, provided by Li et al.51, as the primary research data (Supplementary Table 1). Additionally, we conducted supplementary analyses using GLOBMAP LAI data provided by Liu et al. from 1982 to 201952 and SIF data provided by the Global Observing Satellite Initiative for Fluorescence (GOSIF) from 2000 to 202253. The NDVI dataset was a calibrated product derived from the MODIS NDVI and GIMMS NDVI through a machine learning framework. This calibration effectively eliminated the noticeable effects of orbital drift and sensor degradation in tropical regions51. The LAI product was obtained through data fusion and inversion of AVHRR and MODIS data52. Meanwhile, GOSIF was derived from a machine learning model trained on discrete OCO-2 SIF observations, MODIS remote sensing data, and meteorological reanalysis data53. To exclude the interference of non-growing seasons, we utilized the MODIS Land Cover Dynamics product (MCD12Q2) provided by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration to determine the global distribution of growing seasons. The growing season was defined as the period from the annual Greenup date to the Dormancy date, and we used the multi-year average from 2002 to 2022 to minimize the influence of interannual fluctuations. The original spatial resolutions of the PKU GIMMS NDVI, GLOBMAP LAI, GOSIF, and MCD12Q2 products were 1/12°, 500 m, 0.05°, and 500 m, respectively. To align with the SPEI data used in our research, we resampled these data from their original resolutions to a spatial resolution of 0.1° using the nearest neighbor method.

Forest traits

The 2015 global fine-scale forest composition map, with a spatial resolution of 100 m, was obtained from Lesiv et al.54. This map categorizes global forests based on signs of forest management practices into intact forests and managed forests. Managed forests were further classified into four forest management types: naturally regenerating forests with signs of management, which showed evidence of activities such as logging or clear-cuts; planted forests; plantation forests with rotation periods of up to 15 years; and agroforestry (Supplementary Fig. 10). To minimize interference from other land-cover types, we retained only 0.1° grid cells (aligned with the SPEI data) that had a single forest management covering more than 50% of the grid area. Additionally, the Global 30 m Land-Cover Dynamic Monitoring Product with a Fine Classification System (GLC_FCS30D), a dataset at 30 m resolution covering the period from 1985 to 202255, was used to exclude the effects of forest expansion or contraction during the study period. Specifically, any 0.1° grid cells with more than 50% change in forest area were excluded from this study. Moreover, forest types were classified as evergreen needle-leaved forest, evergreen broad-leaved forest, deciduous needle-leaved forest, and deciduous broad-leaved forest using forest grids form GLC_FCS30D. Canopy height data were obtained from Simard et al.56, maximum rooting depth from the Earth2Observe dataset57, tree density from Crowther et al.58, and forest age from the Max Planck Institute portal59. Canopy height, maximum rooting depth, tree density, and forest age datasets featured global coverage at 1 km resolution. To ensure compatibility with other datasets, the aforementioned datasets were resampled to 0.1° using the maximum area method, retaining only grids matching the forest management data.

To investigate documented forest management practices, we conducted a systematic meta-analysis of the scientific literature from the Science Citation Index Expanded. The search included articles or review articles written in English up to December 31, 2023. And the search keywords were: (“forest management practices” OR “afforestation” OR “reforestation” OR (“forest” AND “clear cutting”) OR (“forest” AND “fertilization”) OR (“forest” AND “thinning”) OR (“forest” AND “logging”)). A total of 10,028 peer-reviewed articles were identified through this process. Additionally, we included 87 articles from previous meta-analyses on forest management practices44. We initially screened all articles based on the following selection criteria: explicit documentation of specific forest management practices (including afforestation/reforestation, clear-cutting, selective logging, fertilization, and thinning) without indicating the implementation of multiple forest management practices, and the recording of geographic coordinates of the forests. After further excluding studies which forests were not recorded in flash drought and growth dynamics anomalies dataset (n = 9), 288 articles were included in the analyses (Supplementary Fig. 11). The metadata from those articles were independently extracted by two authors, resulting in 429 consistent georeferenced records of forest management practices (Supplementary Data 1). These spatially defined forest management practices records were subsequently paired with corresponding forest response data derived from our study to analyze the impact of different management interventions on forest responses to flash droughts.

Flash drought identification

Flash droughts were identified by meeting two criteria: a rapid decline in water deficit (precipitation minus potential evapotranspiration) and sustained water stress over time1. To reduce noise from daily variations, the water deficit was first smoothed into 5-day averages (pentads), referred to as the pentad-mean water deficit index. Subsequently, a log-logistic distribution was fitted for each pentad across all years in the historical record using the maximum likelihood method. The cumulative probability of the observed water deficit for a specific pentad in a given year within the historical climate record was calculated using the log-logistic distribution function. Finally, this cumulative probability was transformed into a dimensionless index expressed in standard deviation units—the pentad-scale SPEI—by applying the inverse of the standard normal distribution function18. This approach eliminated the influence of cumulative past climate conditions, ensuring spatial and temporal comparability18.

As shown in Fig. 4, a flash drought was divided into two stages: the onset stage and the stress stage. The transition between these stages was marked as the endpoint (te), indicating that the SPEI value at this point (SPEI(te)) was sufficiently low to constitute water stress. According to Noguera et al.16, the water stress threshold was set at −1.28, corresponding to the 10-year return period of SPEI, which follows a standard normal distribution. To characterize the rapid decline of SPEI during the onset stage, the onset point (to) of this stage was defined as the closest pentad before te where the intensification (calculated from SPEI(te) – SPEI(to)) was ≤ −2 (Eq. (1)). Upon entering the stress stage, SPEI had to stay ≤ −1.28 for at least three pentads to reflect prolonged water stress. As water conditions recovered, SPEI gradually increased and eventually exceeded –1.28 for at least two consecutive pentads, marking the end of the flash drought, which was denoted as past point (tp) (Eq. (2)). To distinguish flash droughts from conventional or long-term droughts and exclude insignificant short-term events, flash droughts were defined as having a duration of 6–18 pentads4. Notably, this study smoothed daily water deficit with a pentad window rather than the traditional segmentation into 73 fixed windows per year for SPEI calculation9,16,22. This methodology aimed to accurately capture the entire sequence of flash droughts, acknowledging that the interval between two events might not strictly adhere to multiples of pentads.

Schematic illustration of the method used to identify a flash drought.

A flash drought begins when the Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI) drops below −1.28 and decreases by less than −2 within no more than five pentads. The event must persist with SPEI ≤ −1.28 for at least three pentads. The end of the flash drought is defined when SPEI increases above the threshold of −1.28 for two consecutive pentads.

Growth dynamics anomalies

A linear regression curve for the same day of the year was fitted for NDVI (as well as for LAI and SIF) against the year index to represent the normal vegetation growth trend during the study period. Predicted values from this regression were subtracted from the original daily data to remove natural growth trends and intra-annual variations, enabling detection of flash droughts induced anomalies in forest dynamics (NDVIe). Furthermore, this study employed the Z-score method to obtain standardized ΔNDVI, ensuring its comparability across different global pixels. To distinguish the effects of logging and flash droughts on forest dynamics, flash droughts with at least five consecutive pentads where ΔNDVI <−1 before to were excluded when analyzing the responses of naturally regenerating forests with signs of management and plantation forests with rotation. Analysis of influencing factors of ΔNDVI

The extreme gradient boosting model has been widely employed in ecological studies for nonlinear modeling due to its high parallel computing capabilities and interpretability60. To address potential biases in modeling and importance assessments caused by collinear predictors61, we employed the Variance Inflation Factor to remove predictors exhibiting high collinearity with others, using a threshold value greater than 5. Our model established ΔNDVI as the response variable, with mean annual precipitation, MAT, precipitation anomalies, temperature anomalies, onset rate, stress duration, peak stress, forest management, forest age, canopy height, maximum rooting depth, and tree density constituting the regulating factors (Supplementary Table 2). To validate the spatial transferability of the model, global forest response samples at the event scale were randomly partitioned into spatial grids to create a training dataset (60%) and a validation dataset (40%). The training dataset was used for model calibration, while the validation dataset was employed for optimal hyperparameter tuning and unbiased performance evaluation. The F1 Score, coefficient of determination, and root mean square error were used as metrics for model optimization. The F1 score was a metric that measures the classification accuracy of a model (ranging from 0 to 1). With repeated optimization, the following parameters were used in the extreme gradient boosting model: subsample ratio of columns when constructing each tree = 0.8, Boosting learning rate = 0.01, maximum tree depth = 20, number of gradient boosted trees = 100, subsample ratio of the training instance = 0.9.
