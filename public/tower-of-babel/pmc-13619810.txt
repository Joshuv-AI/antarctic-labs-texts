Privacy-aware learning analytics for exploratory behavior through federated and explainable AI
Authors: Rommel Gutierrez, Aracely Mera-Navarrete, Ana Cristina Villegas, Vanessa Guevara, William Villegas-Ch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Learning analytics platforms increasingly use behavioral traces to model engagement, navigation, and self-directed learning dynamics in virtual learning environments (VLEs). However, most current approaches prioritize predictive accuracy, centralize sensitive student records, and rarely analyze the stability of exploratory representations under distributed configurations. This study proposes a privacy-aware framework integrating behavioral learning analytics, federated learning (FedAvg), and Explainable AI to reconstruct informal and exploratory patterns from VLE traces while reducing direct exposure to raw data. Using OULAD as the primary dataset, the framework reconstructed 29,228 student–module–presentation units from 10,900,970 interactions and generated a behavioral proxy based on resource diversity, revisits, temporal dispersion, and non-evaluative activity. In the operational proxy-recoverability configuration, Logistic Regression achieved F1 = 0.977 and AUC-ROC = 1.000; this near-perfect value is interpreted as an upper-bound recovery of a transparent behavioral proxy rather than as external prediction of an independently observed construct. Importantly, the reduced scenario maintained F1 = 0.905 and AUC-ROC = 0.991 after excluding direct proxy components. Under distributed training, FedAvg preserved utility comparable to that of the centralized MLP (F1 = 0.671; AUC-ROC = 0.886), maintaining Overlap@10 = 1.000 and a Spearman correlation of ρ = 0.985 between centralized and federated explanatory rankings. The results suggest that exploratory representations can preserve analytical utility and explanatory stability in lightweight federated educational scenarios.

Introduction

Virtual learning environments (VLE) record a layer of student activity that is rarely limited to assessment submission or linear content consumption. Within a single learning trajectory, repeated access to resources, participation in forums, navigation between subpages, out-of-sequence queries, revisits, and temporally distributed activity can coexist. These signals are relevant because they describe how students explore the educational environment rather than only whether they pass or fail, particularly in scenarios associated with exploratory behavior, self-directed learning, and autonomous navigation on digital platforms (Cucuiat, 2018; Lin et al., 2024; Vaslin and Prié, 2025). However, when learning analytics is primarily oriented toward predicting grades, dropout, or final performance, this exploratory dimension becomes absorbed by performance-oriented variables and progressively loses value as an analytical object, despite evidence showing that distributed behavioral trajectories contain relevant information about participation and educational self-regulation (Li et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026).

The problem addressed in this work emerges from this tension. Analyzing informal or exploratory patterns requires observing subtle behavioral traces, but these traces also increase the exposure of student data when centralized for model training. Consequently, the detection of informal learning is not presented as a definitive pedagogical classification, but rather as an operational representation derived from observable signals in VLE logs. The central question is not whether educational logs “prove” informal learning, but whether they allow reconstruction of behavioral patterns compatible with autonomous exploration and whether this representation can preserve analytical utility and interpretability under decentralized training conditions. This issue becomes increasingly relevant in privacy-aware educational artificial intelligence (AI) scenarios, where reducing direct exposure of behavioral logs and preserving explanatory consistency are becoming methodological constraints comparable in importance to predictive utility.

To address this problem, a framework integrating behavioral learning analytics, federated learning (FL), and explainable AI was developed (Ayeelyan et al., 2025). The study audited OULAD and EdNet, selecting OULAD as the primary corpus because it preserves explicit relationships among students, modules, presentations, VLE resources, activity types, and their relative temporal order. EdNet was used as a structural reference; however, its local partitions are primarily oriented toward question-solving sequences and do not provide a direct equivalent to non-evaluative navigation within the VLE environment. Using OULAD, 29,228 student–module–presentation analytical units were reconstructed from 10,900,970 interaction records, generating a behavioral layer based on resource diversity, revisits, temporal dispersion, navigation irregularity, and the proportion of non-evaluative activity.

The resulting research gap is methodological rather than purely algorithmic: existing studies rarely evaluate whether behaviorally interpretable exploratory representations can simultaneously retain utility, reduce direct exposure to raw data, and preserve explanatory structure under federated training. The methodological contribution of this study, therefore, consists in jointly evaluating three dimensions that are commonly analyzed separately: the stability of an exploratory behavioral proxy, its analytical utility under centralized and FL configurations, and the preservation of explanatory consistency. The informal/exploratory proxy was defined using a normalized score and a binary label based on the 75th percentile, avoiding reliance on final grades or academic performance as the primary target. Subsequently, Logistic Regression (LR) and Random Forest (RF) were evaluated as centralized models, while a compact MLP was trained under both centralized and FedAvg-based federated configurations. The federated simulation considered academic-module partitions, balanced random partitions, and a variant incorporating light Gaussian noise in the parameter updates (Chauhan et al., 2024). Explainability was evaluated through permutation importance, Overlap@10, and Spearman correlation between feature-importance rankings.

The study contributes a reproducible operationalization of informal/exploratory learning based on interpretable VLE trace indicators, evaluates its recoverability under centralized and federated configurations, quantifies reduced direct raw-data exposure and communication overhead under simulated FedAvg without claiming formal differential privacy, and examines whether explanatory rankings remain stable when model training is decentralized.

The results suggest that the reconstructed representation does not depend on an arbitrarily fragile decision boundary. The p70, p75, and p80 variants maintained agreement values close to 0.95 and Jaccard similarity values between approximately 0.80 and 0.83, indicating structural stability under moderate threshold variations. In the centralized scenario, LR achieved F1 = 0.977 and AUC-ROC = 1.000 in the operational configuration, while the reduced control maintained F1 = 0.905 and AUC-ROC = 0.991 after excluding the direct proxy components. This behavior suggests that the exploratory signal is distributed across the broader behavioral structure rather than being exclusively tied to the operational proxy formulation.

Under federated configurations, FedAvg by module achieved F1 = 0.671 and AUC-ROC = 0.886, values practically equivalent to those obtained by the centralized MLP. Simultaneously, the federated scenarios shared no raw interaction records under the exposure definition adopted in this study, although no formal differential privacy guarantees were claimed. The most relevant result was that decentralization did not substantially alter the dominant explanatory structure: the centralized MLP and FedAvg shared Overlap@10 = 1.000 and a Spearman correlation close to ρ = 0.985.

Literature review

Learning analytics has evolved from approaches centered primarily on performance monitoring toward frameworks capable of reconstructing behavioral patterns associated with participation dynamics, interaction continuity, and autonomous learning in virtual educational environments. Previous studies have shown that traces generated within VLE platforms contain structural information related to navigation, temporality, and collaborative interaction, enabling the identification of latent behavioral patterns beyond grades or direct evaluative outcomes (D'Aquin et al., 2018; Dawson et al., 2019). More recent research has expanded this perspective toward informal and exploratory learning processes, suggesting that distributed interaction with educational resources can reflect autonomous trajectories that remain partially invisible through conventional academic indicators (Cucuiat, 2018; Vaslin and Prié, 2025). Nevertheless, most existing approaches continue prioritizing predictive accuracy or engagement classification over the interpretable operationalization of exploratory behavioral patterns derived directly from educational traces.

In parallel, the increasing integration of AI into learning analytics systems has intensified concerns regarding privacy, student-data exposure, and the centralization of behavioral records (Agudo-Peregrina et al., 2012). Recent studies warn that analytics-based educational systems can reconstruct learning habits, temporal routines, and interaction preferences from seemingly innocuous traces (Joseph-Richard and Uhomoibhi, 2024; Khalil et al., 2025). This issue becomes especially relevant in AI-driven educational platforms, where behavioral records are continuously used for personalization, prediction, and pedagogical adaptation. In response, privacy-preserving learning analytics approaches have increasingly incorporated distributed learning mechanisms designed to reduce the direct transfer of sensitive educational data.

In this direction, FL has gained prominence as an alternative collaborative-training paradigm that does not require centralized raw data. The foundational work of Brendan McMahan et al. (2017) demonstrated that multiple clients can jointly train models by sharing parameter updates instead of the original data. More recent reviews have highlighted the expansion of FL into sensitive domains such as healthcare, IoT, cybersecurity, and education because of its capacity to reduce direct exposure of private information (Chaudhary et al., 2025; Yurdem et al., 2024).

Nevertheless, educational studies based on FL still present important limitations. First, most approaches focus almost exclusively on predictive performance metrics, such as accuracy or recommendation quality, without evaluating whether interpretable behavioral signals remain stable after distributed aggregation. Second, the non-IID heterogeneity characteristic of educational environments is frequently treated only as a convergence problem, ignoring that differences across courses, academic modules, and interaction patterns can substantially alter the explanatory structure of the resulting models.

At the same time, recent literature on educational AI has emphasized the importance of incorporating explainability and transparency into analytical systems applied to learning processes. Explainable AI (XAI) has been used to improve trust, pedagogical interpretability, and understanding of automated decisions in educational platforms (Yang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2021). However, most XAI-oriented studies remain centered on fully centralized scenarios and evaluate interpretability independently of privacy constraints or decentralization mechanisms. This separation constitutes a relevant methodological limitation because preserving predictive utility under FL does not necessarily imply preserving explanatory consistency (Tao et al., 2025). Federated aggregation can modify feature-importance rankings, redistribute behavioral signals across clients, or alter dominant explanatory variables even when global predictive metrics remain relatively stable.

Another important limitation concerns the operationalization of informal or exploratory learning. Although numerous studies address autonomous learning, self-directed learning, and exploratory behavior from conceptual perspectives, relatively few define reproducible behavioral proxies derived directly from VLE traces (Lin et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026). Many existing approaches rely on surveys, institutional labels, or variables that are difficult to generalize across educational platforms. Consequently, the relationship among exploratory behavioral patterns, educational privacy, and distributed learning architectures remains insufficiently formalized within the learning analytics literature.

Given these limitations, the present study integrates behavioral learning analytics, FL, and XAI into a unified analytical framework for operationalizing informal and exploratory behavioral patterns. Unlike previous approaches focused primarily on predictive accuracy, this framework simultaneously evaluates preservation of analytical utility, reduction of raw-data exposure, and explanatory consistency under both centralized and federated configurations.

Materials and methods

Dataset audit and behavioral representation

The study was structured around reconstructing behavioral patterns from traces recorded in VLEs. Initially, two large-scale educational datasets, OULAD and EdNet, were audited. Although both datasets contain extensive records of student interactions, substantial structural differences emerged during the audit regarding event organization, temporal granularity, and the ability to reconstruct distributed behavioral trajectories. OULAD preserves explicit relationships among students, modules, academic presentations, VLE resources, and heterogeneous activity types within an integrated tabular schema (Choi et al., 2025). In contrast, EdNet is primarily organized around question-solving sequences distributed across fragmented user-specific files, prioritizing evaluative events over broader educational navigation patterns.

The audit evaluated structural coverage, identifier consistency, temporal availability, activity diversity, and the capacity to reconstruct longitudinal behavioral traces. OULAD simultaneously preserved activities associated with navigation (subpage, homepage), collaborative interaction (forumng), content interaction (oucontent), and resource access (resource, url), enabling reconstruction of behavioral trajectories extending beyond strictly evaluative sequences. Although EdNet maintained a substantially larger volume of records, most events were concentrated around response and evaluation activities, limiting the consistent recovery of exploratory behavioral patterns distributed throughout the VLE environment.

Informal learning proxy construction

The target variable of the study was defined as a behavioral proxy representing informal and exploratory behavior derived from observable regularities in VLE traces. The formulation deliberately avoids using final grades, assessment scores, or academic outcomes as predictive targets. Instead, the proxy was designed to represent trajectories characterized by relatively higher levels of autonomous exploration, non-evaluative activity, navigation diversity, revisits, and temporally distributed interaction patterns.

For each student–module–presentation analytical unit, five primary behavioral components were extracted: proportion of non-evaluative activity, resource diversity, revisit rate, temporal dispersion, and navigation irregularity. These components were selected because they capture complementary dimensions of exploratory behavior, including interaction beyond evaluative activities, distributed consultation of educational materials, return to previously visited resources, continuity of participation throughout the course, and variability in navigation paths. Conceptually, the proxy treats exploratory behavior as a trace-based pattern of autonomous engagement rather than as a latent psychological state: non-evaluative activity indicates interaction not directly driven by assessment completion, resource diversity captures breadth of material consultation, revisits indicate iterative return to prior resources, temporal dispersion reflects sustained engagement across the presentation, and navigation irregularity captures non-linear movement across VLE activity categories.

Centralized and federated learning setup

The comparative analysis was structured around two complementary scenarios: centralized learning and simulated FL. The centralized configuration established a benchmark for analytical utility under full access to the aggregated behavioral layer. In contrast, the federated configuration evaluated preservation of analytical utility and explanatory consistency under local data separation across educational clients.

LR and RF were adopted as centralized baseline models focused on interpretability and recovery of the informal/exploratory proxy from aggregated behavioral signals. LR was selected because of its ability to model transparent linear relationships between behavioral variables and the proxy label, whereas RF enabled capture of secondary nonlinear relationships without introducing large-scale deep architectures.

The federated scenario was constructed using a compact multilayer perceptron (MLP) with two hidden layers to preserve comparability between centralized training and FedAvg-based distributed training. Privacy, explainability, and consistency evaluation

The evaluation framework was structured around three complementary dimensions: reduction of direct raw-data exposure, preservation of analytical utility, and explanatory consistency between centralized and federated configurations. The objective was not to model formal differential privacy or cryptographic anonymization mechanisms, but rather to quantify the extent to which local data separation preserves predictive utility and interpretive stability during distributed training.

Reduction of direct raw-data exposure was estimated using a privacy-aware proxy defined as the proportion of raw records that remained locally stored during federated training. Analytical utility was evaluated using Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, and AUC–ROC over stratified test partitions. In addition to global metrics, local per-client metrics were computed to characterize variability associated with non-IID heterogeneity under both academic-module and balanced-random partitioning strategies.

Explanatory consistency between centralized and federated models was estimated through permutation-based feature importance computed within the same behavioral feature space. This procedure enabled direct comparison of importance rankings across equivalent architectures without dependence on client-specific interpretation mechanisms. The explanatory comparison was conducted primarily between the centralized MLP and the FedAvg configuration partitioned by academic module, since both models share the same architecture and differ only in their training procedures. This procedure enabled isolation of decentralization-associated effects from variations introduced by architectural differences between models. Simultaneously, local per-client metrics enabled characterization of federated stability under heterogeneous distributions of educational behavior.

Reproducibility and computational configuration

The experimental implementation was developed in Python 3.11 (Python Software Foundation, Beaverton, OR, USA) within a reproducible computational environment using NumPy, Pandas, Scikit-learn, and SciPy. All experiments were executed on a workstation equipped with a multi-core CPU, more than 32 GB of RAM, and optional GPU acceleration for MLP training. The computational configuration was intentionally designed to remain lightweight, prioritizing methodological stability and behavioral traceability over intensive optimization or deep over-parameterization.

Reproducibility was preserved by explicitly fixing pseudo-random seeds at all critical stages of the analytical pipeline, including stratified partitioning, model initialization, federated training, and Gaussian perturbation of local updates. Training and testing partitions were maintained constant across experimental runs to avoid variability between successive executions. Similarly, federated partitions by academic module and balanced random partitions were serialized before training to preserve consistency across the compared 'scenarios.

The framework was implemented following a modular architecture that separated dataset auditing, behavioral extraction, proxy construction, centralized training, federated simulation, and explainability analysis into independent components. This modular design enabled reuse of the same behavioral representation across all experimental scenarios without modifying the transformations applied to the original VLE traces. Furthermore, separation between processing modules facilitated comparison between centralized and federated configurations while preserving equivalence across the feature space, evaluation metrics, and analytical procedures.

The approximate computational complexity of federated training was dominated by the number of aggregation rounds, participating clients, local epochs, local sample counts, and trainable parameters exchanged during the FedAvg process. The structural audit revealed substantial differences between OULAD and EdNet partitions in terms of organization, granularity, and observable behavioral density. Although EdNet-KT1 exhibited the largest estimated record volume, with approximately 7.03 × 107 rows distributed across 748,230 CSV files, most interactions were concentrated around question-solving sequences and response-associated events. In contrast, OULAD consolidated 10,900,970 interaction records into seven integrated tabular files while preserving explicit relationships among students, VLE resources, temporal activity, and interaction types. The distribution of activity types observed in OULAD showed that educational interaction was not restricted to evaluative components. As illustrated in Figure 2B, the categories forumng, oucontent, subpage, and homepage collectively accounted for a substantial proportion of interactions and broad student coverage. The forumng category represented approximately 22.6 % of the recorded interactions, while oucontent exhibited the highest relative click volume among content-oriented activities. These results indicate that a considerable fraction of student activity was distributed across material consultation, resource navigation, and participation in interactive educational spaces beyond direct assessment completion.

Activities associated with educational resources (resource, url) exhibited comparatively lower click density but broad population coverage, suggesting cross-sectional consultation patterns distributed across the student population. In contrast, categories such as externalquiz and questionnaire simultaneously showed low interaction frequency and limited population reach, indicating more localized participation patterns within the VLE structure. This asymmetry across activity types suggests that the observed behavioral footprint did not depend on a single dominant interaction component, but rather on a heterogeneous combination of navigation, content interaction, and transitions among educational resources.

The consolidated behavioral layer reconstructed at the student–module–presentation level also exhibited substantial dispersion in both interaction intensity and structural organization. The average number of interactions per analytical unit reached 364.56, with certain trajectories exceeding 3,000 interactions in specific academic modules. However, interaction intensity did not behave equivalently to behavioral exploration. Some trajectories characterized by high click volume remained concentrated within relatively small subsets of resources, whereas other profiles with lower overall activity exhibited substantially greater diversity in navigation and transitions across educational content. This difference suggests that exploratory behavior emerges primarily as a structural interaction configuration rather than solely as an increase in activity frequency within the VLE.

Proxy construction and methodological sensitivity

The quantitative sensitivity of the proxy remained stable under moderate variations in the percentile threshold. As presented in Table 3, the configurations based on the 70th, 75th, and 80th percentiles maintained agreement values close to 0.95 relative to the baseline 75th-percentile configuration. Simultaneously, the overlap of the high-intensity exploratory class remained between 0.800 and 0.833 according to the Jaccard similarity metric. The progressive reduction in the number of trajectories classified as exploratory, decreasing from 8,769 cases at the 70th percentile to 5,846 at the 80th percentile, did not produce abrupt structural reorganizations in the composition of the high-intensity class. This behavior suggests that trajectories located within the upper region of the exploratory-score distribution remained comparatively stable under moderate variations in the cutoff threshold.

Figure 3B complements these results by showing that configurations based exclusively on percentile modifications preserved high levels of agreement and structural similarity relative to the baseline configuration. In contrast, variants associated with expansion of the behavioral components progressively reduced both overall agreement and overlap with the high-intensity exploratory class. The most pronounced degradation was observed in the expanded seven-component configuration, where similarity relative to the baseline decreased below 0.50 according to the Jaccard similarity metric. This reduction indicates that simultaneous incorporation of additional temporal and social dimensions substantially reorganized the structure of the trajectories classified as exploratory.

The stability observed across the p70–p80 configurations additionally revealed that the behavioral core of the proxy remained dominated by persistent regularities associated with navigation and interaction continuity. Trajectories consistently classified as exploratory across percentile configurations simultaneously maintained high resource diversity, frequent revisits, and substantial temporal dispersion. In contrast, the expanded configurations incorporated profiles characterized by more specific temporal or social patterns, altering the original composition of the exploratory class without necessarily modifying the overall interaction intensity within the VLE environment.

Centralized analytical utility

Centralized proxy retrieval demonstrated high analytical consistency even under reduced-variable configurations. As presented in Table 4, LR achieved the highest performance on the complete operational dataset, reaching Accuracy = 0.988, F1 = 0.977, and AUC-ROC = 1.000. This near-perfect result should be interpreted as an operational upper-bound for recovering a transparent additive proxy from behaviorally related variables, rather than as evidence of externally validated prediction of informal learning. RF also maintained strong analytical performance, although lower than that achieved by the linear model, with F1 = 0.920 and AUC-ROC = 0.993. The performance difference observed between the two models suggests that the principal structure of the proxy can be reconstructed from comparatively stable relationships among the behavioral variables included in its operational formulation.

This behavior suggests that the exploratory structure remained partially distributed across indirect variables associated with temporal continuity, resource transitions, interaction intensity, and broader navigation patterns within the VLE environment.

The relative performance reduction additionally revealed differences between the evaluated model families. LR exhibited an approximate 7.4% reduction in F1 between the operational and reduced configurations, whereas RF showed an approximate 5.1% decrease. Although RF degraded proportionally less, its absolute performance remained lower in both evaluated scenarios. This pattern is consistent with the construction of the proxy itself, which combines normalized behavioral components through an additive score. LR therefore aligns closely with the geometry of the operational label, whereas RF captures complementary nonlinear interactions that are informative but not dominant for this specific recovery task.

Discussion

The results suggest that informal and exploratory behavioral patterns can be operationalized stably from VLE traces without relying on explicit pedagogical labels or traditional academic indicators. The stability observed across the p70, p75, and p80 configurations, together with agreement values close to 0.95 and Jaccard similarity values between approximately 0.80 and 0.83, indicates that the behavioral representation does not depend on an arbitrarily fragile decision boundary but rather on persistent regularities associated with navigation diversity, temporal continuity, and non-evaluative activity. This behavior is consistent with recent studies on exploratory learning and self-directed interaction in educational platforms, where distributed interaction trajectories reflect learning dynamics partially independent of formal evaluative performance (Lin et al., 2024; Vaslin and Prié, 2025). At the same time, the partial stability preserved across the expanded proxy configurations suggests that the exploratory signal remains embedded within the broader interaction structure of the VLE rather than being exclusively tied to a specific operational formulation of the proxy.

Centralized proxy retrieval also produced particularly relevant results. LR achieved F1 = 0.977 and AUC-ROC = 1.000 under the complete operational configuration; however, this result must be read as high recoverability of an operationally defined proxy whose components are explicitly trace-based and partly additive. The reduced-control configuration is therefore central to interpretation: it maintained F1 = 0.905 and AUC-ROC = 0.991 even after exclusion of the direct proxy components. This behavior suggests that the reconstructed exploratory pattern retains a partially linear and coherent structure within the aggregated behavioral space, while also indicating that the signal is not limited to a direct mechanical reconstruction of the proxy formula.

This finding contrasts with recent literature in educational AI, where increasing architectural complexity is frequently prioritized over interpretability and behavioral traceability (Liu et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2023). In the present study, comparatively lightweight models maintained high retrieval capacity for exploratory behavior without requiring large-scale deep architectures.

The federated configurations produced one of the most relevant findings of the study: FedAvg preserved virtually the same analytical utility as the centralized MLP despite local separation of educational traces and non-IID heterogeneity. Both the centralized scenario and the FedAvg module configuration achieved approximately F1 = 0.671 and AUC-ROC = 0.886, while the noisy variant exhibited a marginal improvement in F1. This result is particularly relevant because it demonstrates that local separation of raw behavioral traces did not substantially reduce analytical utility under the evaluated configurations. However, the results also showed that global stability coexisted with considerable dispersion among individual clients, especially under academic-module partitioning. This behavior aligns with recent reviews on FL, which indicate that federated systems can preserve aggregate convergence while maintaining substantial structural differences among heterogeneous clients (Khalil et al., 2025; Yurdem et al., 2024). In the present study, heterogeneity manifested not only as statistical variability but also as differences in the recoverability of behavioral patterns associated with specific educational modules.

The explanatory consistency observed between the centralized MLP and FedAvg constitutes another central finding of the study. Permutation importance was used consistently across centralized and federated scenarios because it provides a model-agnostic basis for comparing feature-rank stability under the same behavioral feature space. The results showed Overlap@10 = 1.000 and Spearman correlations close to 0.985 between feature-importance rankings, indicating that decentralization did not substantially alter the dominant interpretive signals. Variables associated with temporal dispersion, transitions between resources, collaborative activity, and interaction with educational content consistently remained among the most relevant explanatory dimensions in both centralized and federated configurations. This result is particularly important because a large portion of the educational federated-learning literature continues to evaluate performance primarily through global predictive metrics without analyzing whether distributed aggregation preserves explanatory coherence between local clients and centralized models. Consequently, the findings suggest that joint evaluation of analytical utility, local trace separation, and interpretive stability can provide a more robust characterization of privacy-aware educational systems than predictive performance metrics alone.

Limitations and future work

The study also presents important limitations. First, the informal/exploratory proxy remains an operational representation derived from behavioral traces rather than a direct pedagogical observation of informal learning. Second, the reduction in direct data exposure achieved through FL should not be interpreted as a formal guarantee of differential privacy, since the framework did not incorporate cryptographic protection, secure aggregation, or formal privacy-preserving mechanisms. Third, federated heterogeneity was simulated through structured partitions within a single educational environment rather than across multiple independent institutions. Fourth, the analysis was intentionally restricted to compact model architectures and model-agnostic explanation methods to preserve reproducibility and interpretability. Consequently, the results should be interpreted as a controlled approximation of non-IID behavior under distributed educational settings. Future work should evaluate the framework in real multi-institutional deployments, incorporate formal differential privacy or secure aggregation, and examine whether dynamic temporal or graph-based representations preserve the same utility–exposure–explainability balance.

Conclusions

The work presented an integrated framework for analyzing informal and exploratory behavioral patterns in VLE through behavioral learning analytics, FL, and explainability based on behavioral signals. Unlike approaches focused exclusively on academic prediction or predictive performance, the proposed framework aimed to reconstruct interpretable learning trajectories from distributed interaction with VLE resources while simultaneously preserving local separation of educational traces and maintaining explanatory stability under decentralized configurations.

The results suggest that exploratory behavioral patterns can be represented through reproducible proxies derived from navigation diversity, temporal distribution, revisits, and non-evaluative activity. Furthermore, the study demonstrated that analytical utility can be preserved under federated configurations without requiring centralization of raw student traces. More importantly, the stability observed across the explanatory rankings indicates that dominant behavioral signals remained comparatively consistent even after distributed aggregation processes. This behavior is particularly relevant for privacy-aware learning analytics, where preservation of interpretability constitutes an additional challenge beyond maintaining global predictive metrics.

From a methodological perspective, the study additionally showed that comparatively compact models and lightweight federated configurations can capture relevant behavioral regularities without relying on large-scale deep architectures or highly complex analytical pipelines. Consequently, the proposal is positioned primarily as an analytical framework oriented toward interpretable behavioral representation rather than as a system focused exclusively on algorithmic optimization. This orientation enables integration of learning analytics, educational privacy, and XAI within a coherent and reproducible experimental framework.

The work can be extended in several future directions. First, evaluation should be expanded toward real-world multi-institutional scenarios to analyze heterogeneity across independent educational platforms and capture more diverse behavioral distributions. Second, incorporation of formal differential privacy and secure aggregation mechanisms would enable evaluation of the impact of cryptographic constraints on explanatory stability and analytical utility. Finally, future research could explore dynamic representations of exploratory behavior through longer temporal sequences, educational interaction graphs, and adaptive models capable of capturing progressive changes in autonomous learning trajectories within distributed educational ecosystems.
