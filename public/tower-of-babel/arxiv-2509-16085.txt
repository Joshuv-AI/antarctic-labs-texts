A more efficient method for large-sample model-free feature screening via multi-armed bandits
Xiaxue Ouyang, Xinlai Kang, Mengyu Li, Zhenxing Dou, Jun Yu, Cheng Meng

Abstract
We consider the model-free feature screening in large-scale ultrahigh-dimensional data analysis. Existing feature screening methods often face substantial computational challenges when dealing with large sample sizes. To alleviate the computational burden, we propose a rank-based model-free sure independence screening method (CR-SIS) and its efficient variant, BanditCR-SIS. The CR-SIS method, based on Chatterjee’s rank correlation, is as straightforward to implement as the sure independence screening (SIS) method based on Pearson correlation introduced by Fan and Lv 2008 , but it is significantly more powerful in detecting nonlinear relationships between variables. Motivated by the multi-armed bandit (MAB) problem, we reformulate the feature screening procedure to significantly reduce the computational complexity of CR-SIS. For a predictor matrix of size n × p n\times p , the computational cost of CR-SIS is O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ ( n ) ​ p ) O(n\log(n)p) , while BanditCR-SIS reduces this to O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ ( n ) ​ p + n ​ log ⁡ ( n ) ) O(\sqrt{n}\log(n)p+n\log(n)) . Theoretically, we establish the sure screening property for both CR-SIS and BanditCR-SIS under mild regularity conditions. Furthermore, we demonstrate the effectiveness of our methods through extensive experimental studies on both synthetic and real-world datasets. The results highlight their superior performance compared to classical screening methods, requiring significantly less computational time.

Big data; Variable selection; Sure independence screening; Chatterjee’s Rank

Introduction

In recent years, rapid advances in science and technology have led to explosive growth in high-dimensional data, which has become increasingly common in various academic and industrial domains.
Some fields have pushed this trend even further, giving rise to ultrahigh-dimensional data, particularly in areas such as bioinformatics, medical imaging, computer vision, and social network analysis
(
Fan and Ren 2006
;
Weinberger et al. 2009
;
Pang et al. 2018
;
Ghorbani et al. 2020
)
.
In such datasets, the number of features often far exceeds the available sample size, with dimensionality reaching thousands or even millions-several magnitudes larger.
For example, thousands of gene expression profiles are used for disease classification
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
, while millions of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP) are analyzed in genome-wide association studies to explore the relationship between genotypes and phenotypes (dbGaP Study Accession:
phs000368.v1.p1
).
While these ultrahigh-dimensional datasets offer unprecedented opportunities for discovery, they also present significant challenges for traditional statistical and machine learning algorithms.
Analyzing and modeling functional relationships in the presence of redundant and noisy features can be quite challenging due to computational burden, memory cost, statistical accuracy, and algorithmic stability
(
Fan et al. 2009
;
Hall and Miller 2009
;
Lv and Liu 2014
)
.

To address the challenges posed by ultrahigh dimensionality,
Fan and Lv 2008
highlighted the importance of feature screening and introduced a widely adopted method known as sure independence screening (SIS) within the framework of the linear regression model.
Feature screening aims to use some marginal utility measures to efficiently reduce the dimensionality of data, scaling it down from an extremely high dimension to a moderate size.
For example, the SIS method eliminates redundant features in the linear regression model by ranking the absolute values of marginal Pearson correlations with the response and attempts to retain all the important features at the same time.
This is referred to as the sure screening property, which states that the selected features will contain all the informative ones with probability approaching one when the sample size goes to infinity.

SIS has demonstrated a significant impact in the study of ultrahigh-dimensional data. However, it relies on the linear regression model, which poses substantial limitations.
To address these, SIS has been extended beyond the simple linear model to more diverse model settings, including generalized linear models
(
Fan and Song 2010
)
, nonparametric regression models such as additive models
(
Fan et al. 2011
)
, and varying coefficient models
(
Fan et al. 2014
)
, as well as applications to the categorical response
(
Fan and Fan 2008
)
.
Although these model-based methods are effective under the assumption that the specified model closely approximates the true underlying relationship, they can perform poorly when this assumption is violated, especially in cases where the relationship between variables is complex or unknown.
These limitations have prompted researchers to explore model-free approaches. Examples include sure independent ranking and screening (SIRS)
(
Zhu et al. 2011
)
, distance correlation-based screening (DC-SIS)
(
Li et al. 2012
)
, and projection correlation-based screening (PC-SIS)
(
Xu et al. 2020
)
, among others. In situations where the number of features is large and little prior information is available about the true model, model-free methods become particularly valuable.

Although these model-free methods have already shown great performance and enjoy strong theoretical properties, they may suffer from huge computational costs in practice.
For a predictor matrix of size
n\times p
, the computational cost of SIRS is at the order of
O(n\log(n)p)
.
The computational costs of the other two methods, DC-SIS and PC-SIS, are even higher, with complexities of
O(n^{2}p)
and
O(n^{3}p)
, respectively.
Such high computational costs limit the practicality of model-free methods for large-scale datasets, when both the sample size
n
and the number of features
p
are considerable, especially in the case where
n
has several millions.

In this paper, we propose a new model-free feature screening framework based on Chatterjee’s rank correlation
(
Chatterjee 2021
)
, a recently proposed rank-based marginal utility measure that has attracted great attention.
Like distance correlation, Chatterjee’s rank correlation measures the dependence between the predictor and the response, taking a value of zero if and only if the variables are independent. However, applying Chatterjee’s rank correlation for feature screening also comes with a computational cost of
O(n\log(n)p)
, which can be burdensome for large datasets.
To alleviate the computational burden mentioned above, we propose a more efficient method based on CR-SIS for a large-sample case. Inspired by the multi-arm bandit problem
(
Even-Dar et al. 2006
;
Bubeck et al. 2009
;
Audibert and Bubeck 2010
;
Jamieson and Nowak 2014
)
, we reformulate the feature screening process as a best-arm identification task.
In the standard best-arm identification problem, a multi-arm bandit machine is equipped with
p
arms, where pulling a given arm yields a reward drawn from an unknown corresponding distribution.
One goal is to identify the arm with the highest expected reward within a specified accuracy and probability.
Analogously, in our framework, the
p
features are treated as
p
arms, the marginal Chatterjee’s rank correlation serves as the rewards, and the task is to identify the top
d
most important features (the best
d
arms).
This approach aims to efficiently eliminate features with the smallest marginal utilities while retaining the most significant ones with an overwhelming probability. Using this bandit-inspired methodology, our proposed method significantly reduces computational overhead, making it suitable for large-scale feature screening tasks.

Our major contributions are summarized as follows. First, we introduce a novel rank-based marginal utility measure for feature screening (CR-SIS) that operates without relying on any specific regression model or the distributions of the variables.
To address the challenge of computational efficiency, we develop a randomized method called BanditCR-SIS, which significantly accelerates CR-SIS by reducing computational complexity from
O(n\log(n)p)
to
O(\sqrt{n}\log(n)p+n\log(n))
.
From a theoretical perspective, we demonstrate that both CR-SIS and BanditCR-SIS satisfy the well-known sure screening property under some mild regularity conditions.
To assess the empirical performance of our methods, we conduct extensive simulation studies and a real data analysis, comparing them against two mainstream competitors. The results highlight that our methods yield comparable results while substantially reducing CPU time.

Preliminaries

2.1
Chatterjee’s Rank Correlation

Chatterjee 2021
proposed a novel rank-based correlation coefficient to measure the dependence between two random variables.
Concretely, consider a pair of random variables
(X,Y)
, where
Y
is not a constant. The new correlation coefficient between
X
and
Y
is defined as

\mathrm{CR}(X,Y):=\frac{\int\text{var}(\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{I}_{\{Y\geq t\}}|X))d\mu(t)}{\int\text{var}(\mathbb{I}_{\{Y\geq t\}})d\mu(t)},

(1)

where
\mu
represents the distribution function of
Y
, defined as
\mu(t)=\mathbb{P}(Y\leq t)
. We can note that the value of
\mathrm{CR}(X,Y)
lies in the interval
[0,1]
.

Chatterjee 2021
also provided the corresponding empirical estimator.
Let
(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{n},y_{n})
be
n
i.i.d. pairs with the same distribution as
(X,Y)
.
First, rearrange the data as
(x_{(1)},y_{(1)}),\ldots,(x_{(n)},y_{(n)})
so that
x_{(1)}\leq\ldots\leq x_{(n)}
.
If the
X_{i}
’s do not have ties, this rearrangement is unique; otherwise, the ties are broken uniformly at random to produce a strictly increasing order.
Let
r_{i}
denote the rank of
y_{(i)}
, defined as the number of
k
such that
y_{(k)}\leq y_{(i)}
. Additionally, define
l_{i}
as the number of
k
such that
y_{(k)}\geq y_{(i)}
. The empirical estimator of Chatterjee’s rank correlation is given by

\widehat{\mathrm{CR}}(X,Y):=1-\frac{n\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|r_{i+1}-r_{i}|}{2\sum_{i=1}^{n}l_{i}(n-l_{i})}.

(2)

Chatterjee’s rank correlation offers a simple formulation as the most widely used statistical measure currently and excels in detecting the strength of relationships between variables, even when the relationships are not monotonic or in the presence of noise. In the following, we provide a brief overview of its properties
(
Chatterjee 2021
)
.

(1)

The value of
\mathrm{CR}(X,Y)
belongs to the interval
[0,1]
since
\text{var}(\mathbb{I}_{\{Y\geq t\}})\geq\text{var}(\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{I}_{\{Y\geq t\}}|X))
for any
t
. If
X
and
Y
are independent, then
\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{I}_{\{Y\geq t\}}|X)=\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{I}_{\{Y\geq t\}})
, which is a constant. In this case,
\mathrm{CR}(X,Y)=0
. Conversely, if
Y
is a measurable function of
X
, we have
\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{I}_{\{Y\geq t\}}|X)=\mathbb{I}_{\{Y\geq t\}}
, which leads to
\mathrm{CR}(X,Y)=1
.

(2)

By the definition of
\mathrm{CR}(X,Y)
, it is not symmetric with respect to
X
and
Y
. This asymmetry is intentional, as the primary objective is to assess whether
Y
is a measurable function of
X
, rather than simply to determine whether one variable is a function of the other. However, if symmetry is required, one can consider the maximum of
\mathrm{CR}(X,Y)
and
\mathrm{CR}(Y,X)
.
This combined measure equals zero if and only if
X
and
Y
are independent and equals one if and only if at least one of
X
or
Y
is a measurable function of the other.

(3)

If
Y
is not almost surely a constant, then
\widehat{\mathrm{CR}}(X,Y)
converges to
\mathrm{CR}(X,Y)
almost surely when
n
goes to infinity. The convergence of
\widehat{\mathrm{CR}}(X,Y)
does not depend on the specific distribution of
(X,Y)
, apart from the requirement that
Y
is not a constant. This correlation measure remains robust and effective regardless of whether
X
and
Y
are discrete, continuous, light-tailed, or heavy-tailed.

(4)

The coefficient
\widehat{\mathrm{CR}}(X,Y)
is invariant under strictly increasing transformations of
X
and
Y
because it is rank-based. It is robust and applicable to data that exhibit oscillatory patterns.

(5)

The computational complexity of the coefficient
\widehat{\mathrm{CR}}(X,Y)
is
O(n\log(n))
.

2.2
Multi-Arm Bandit Problem

The multi-arm bandit (MAB) problem is a classic framework for sequential decision-making.
It captures the challenge of balancing exploration (gathering information about the environment to discover potentially profitable actions) and exploitation (maximizing immediate rewards by choosing the empirically best action based on current knowledge).
The MAB problem has broad application in areas such as clinical trials
(
Durand et al. 2018
)
, recommender systems in temporal settings
(
Mary et al. 2015
)
, and anomaly detection
(
Ding et al. 2019
)
.

Consider an MAB with
p
arms, each associated with an unknown distribution of rewards.
When an arm is pulled, it generates a reward that is independently and randomly sampled from its corresponding distribution.
As discussed in
Chen et al. 2014
and
Kaufmann et al. 2016
, the MAB problem typically focuses on one of two distinct objectives: either maximizing the cumulative reward over a limited number of pulls or determining the arm with the highest expected reward (the best arm) with high confidence using the fewest possible pulls required.
This paper is concerned with the latter goal, specifically focusing on the best arm identification problem, which generally has two different settings.

•

Fixed budget setting
. In the fixed budget setting, the total number of pulls allocated is pre-determined. The goal is to identify the best arm within this limited budget while maximizing the confidence level
\delta
.
Algorithms such as Successive Halving
(
Karnin et al. 2013
;
Jamieson and Talwalkar 2016
)
and Successive Rejects
(
Audibert and Bubeck 2010
)
operate by dynamically allocating the budget across arms.
They progressively eliminate suboptimal arms round by round until only one arm remains. These methods are designed to fully utilize the available budget to ensure that the selected arm approximates the true best arm as closely as possible.

•

Fixed confidence setting
. The fixed confidence setting aims to devise a sampling procedure that, for a given confidence level
\delta
, identifies the best arm with a probability of at least
1-\delta
, regardless of the reward distributions.
In this setting, an algorithm must dynamically adapt its sampling strategy to account for cases where the reward expectations of the best and second-best arms are arbitrarily close, ensuring reliable identification under challenging situations. These algorithms include Successive Elimination
(
Even-Dar et al. 2006
)
, Exponential Gap Elimination
(
Karnin et al. 2013
)
, Lower Upper Confidence Bound algorithms
(
Kalyanakrishnan et al. 2012
)
.

Motivated by the MAB problem, we treat the candidate features as the arms and the informative features as the best arms.
Each pull of an arm corresponds to randomly drawing subsamples associated with a specific feature to compute its correlation coefficient. The reward returned from this interaction is represented by the computed correlation coefficient based on the subsamples drawn currently. Using the intermediate results observed so far, we must dynamically determine how many additional calculations to allocate to each feature to refine the correlation estimates.
The second setting aligns with our objective: to minimize sample complexity compared to the original screening procedure while ensuring that the returned results are within a pre-specified proximity of the optimal set. In this way, we can accelerate the original screening method.
In the following section, we provide a detailed description of our proposed method.

3
Method

In this section, we first integrate the Chatterjee’s rank correlation into feature screening. This rank-based screening procedure is fully model-free, as it allows for an arbitrary regression relationship between the response and the features, whether linear or nonlinear. To address computational time limitations, we accelerate this method by drawing inspiration from the MAB problem. As a result, we successfully reduce the computational cost from
O(n\log(n)p)
to
O(\sqrt{n}\log(n)p+n\log(n))
, while preserving the well-known sure screening property.

3.1
Rank-Based Feature Screening

Let
Y=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n})^{\top}
be the response vector and
\bm{X}=(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n})^{\top}=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{p})
be an
n\times p
random design matrix, where
\bm{x_{i}}=(x_{i1},x_{i2},\ldots,x_{ip})^{\top}
. We focus on the ultrahigh-dimensional setting where the dimensionality
p
greatly exceeds the sample size
n
. In such cases, it is natural to assume that only a small subset of features contributes to the response. Following the notations in the literature by
Li et al. 2012
and
Zhu et al. 2011
, without specifying a regression model, we define the index set of the active features as

\mathcal{M}:=\{j:F(Y|\bm{X})\text{ functionally depends on }X_{j},1\leq j\leq n\},

(3)

where
F(Y|\bm{X})
is the conditional distribution function of
Y
given
\bm{X}
. This means that when
j\in\mathcal{M}^{c}
,
X_{j}
is statistically independent of
Y
given all active features
X_{\mathcal{M}}
. This setup abstracts a large number of sparse regression problems, including both linear and nonlinear models.
For example,
Li et al. 2012
considered a case where
F(Y|\bm{X})
depends on
\bm{X}
only through
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{\mathcal{M}}
, that is,
F(Y|\bm{X})=F_{0}(Y|\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{\mathcal{M}})
, where
F_{0}(\cdot|\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{\mathcal{M}})
is an unknown distribution function for a given
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{\mathcal{M}}
. This framework is common in a large variety of parametric and semiparametric models where the response
Y
depends on the predictors
\bm{X}
through linear combinations of the active set
\bm{X}_{\mathcal{M}}
. Examples of such models include the partially linear model
(
Härdle et al. 2000
)
, the proportional hazards model
(
Cox 1972
)
, and the generalized partially linear single-index model
(
Carroll et al. 1997
)
. Now we gives the general model framework illustrate by (
3
) as follows,

F(Y|\bm{X})=g(\bm{X}_{\mathcal{M}}),

where
g(\cdot)
is an unknown measurable function.

Recall that the Chatterjee’s rank correlation
\mathrm{CR}(X_{j},Y)
equals zero if and only if
X_{j}
and
Y
are independent and equals one if and only if
Y
is a measurable function of
X_{j}
. This motivates us to screen out features
X_{j}
with relatively small values of
\mathrm{CR}(X_{j},Y)
, which selects those with large
\mathrm{CR}(X_{j},Y)
as active features.
We observe that the denominator of the correlation
\mathrm{CR}(X_{j},Y)
does not depend on the predictor
X_{j}
. Consequently, ranking each feature by this correlation is equivalent to ranking the corresponding numerator:

\omega_{j}=\int\text{var}(\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{I}_{\{Y\geq t\}}|X_{j}))d\mu(t).

Chatterjee 2021
derived a rank-based consistent estimator of
\omega_{j}
. To be precise, first rearrange the data pairs
(x_{1j},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{nj},y_{n})
into
(x_{(1)j},y_{(1)}),\ldots,(x_{(n)j},y_{(n)})
such that
x_{(1)j}\leq\ldots\leq x_{(n)j}
.
The sample estimator of
\omega_{j}
is given by

\widehat{\omega}_{j}=\frac{1}{n^{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}l_{i}(n-l_{i})-\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|r_{i+1}-r_{i}|+\frac{r_{n}-r_{1}}{2n^{2}},

(4)

where
r_{i}=\#\{1\leq k\leq n:y_{(k)}\leq y_{(i)}\}
, and
l_{i}=\#\{1\leq k\leq n:y_{(k)}\geq y_{(i)}\}
.
This modified correlation serves as an adaptation of Chatterjee’s rank correlation estimator, as defined in Equation (
2
), optimized for theoretical simplicity when screening.
Empirically, we propose to estimate the active set
\mathcal{M}
as

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}:=\big\{j:\widehat{\omega}_{j}\geq cn^{-\kappa},1\leq j\leq p\big\},

where
c
and
\kappa
are prespecified threshold values, to be defined in Condition (2) in the subsequent Section
4
. This screening procedure is called Chatterjee’s rank-based screening (CR-SIS), summarized in Algorithm

3.2
A More Efficient Screening Method

We now present a more efficient screening method for the large-sample CR-SIS method. Just as existing MAB algorithms do, our method iteratively discards non-informative features and samples batches of observations to update current correlation coefficients.
Instead of computing the correlation coefficient for each feature
X_{j}
using all available observations, our method estimates the coefficients using a subset of subsamples. Since a larger sample size gives higher estimation accuracy, our method adaptively allocates more samples to the more promising features, allowing it to better identify the most informative features.

Denote the number of remaining features in the
l
th round as
p_{l}
and the size of the selected model as
d
. We define a shrinking parameter
0<\delta_{l}<1
such that
p_{l}=\lfloor\delta_{l}p_{l-1}\rfloor
, which controls the number of features left in each round.
To determine the value of
\delta_{l}
, we apply the classic median elimination strategy in the best arm identification problem
(
Even-Dar et al. 2002
;
Liu et al. 2019
)
.
The basic idea behind the median elimination strategy is to discard the least performing half of the candidates in each iteration. By quickly eliminating poorly performing candidates, the algorithm focuses more resources on the promising ones, thus avoiding unnecessary exploration and improving overall efficiency.
Adapted to our setting, we extend this strategy to obtain the top
d
features with the largest correlation coefficients, rather than just the best one.
Specifically, at the end of the
l
th iteration, we remove the
\lceil(p_{l-1}-d)/2\rceil
features with the lowest correlation values
\widehat{\omega}_{j}
, retaining the top
\lfloor(p_{l-1}+d)/2\rfloor
features in the current set.
Equivalently, this can be expressed as the setting
\delta_{l}=(p_{l-1}+d)/(2p_{l-1})
.

Next, we discuss the process of determining the number of selected subsamples
n_{l}
in the
l
th round.
Our objective is to develop an efficient variant of the CR-SIS method that maintains the sure screening property while incorporating the median elimination strategy.
To achieve this, we propose a subsample function, defined as follows:

t(n,\alpha):=\frac{n(\alpha^{2}+1)}{\alpha^{2}\sqrt{n}+1}.

In the
l
th round, the total number of selected subsamples
n_{l}
is determined by the subsample function
t(n,\alpha_{l-1})
, where
\alpha_{l}
=
\alpha_{0}/\eta^{l-1}
for
l=1,2,\ldots,k
, with

k
representing the total number of iterations. The hyper-parameter
\alpha_{0}
balances between screening efficiency and accuracy, while
\eta
regulates the rate at which
\alpha_{0}
is scaled down in each round. Based on numerical experiments, we empirically set
\eta=1.1
, achieving an appropriate growth rate for subsamples under the median elimination strategy and the chosen subsample function.
This approach not only provides insights into the theoretical behavior of our method, but also offers a flexible mechanism for balancing error and computational efficiency in practice. Specifically, it helps us understand how the accuracy of the solution improves as more computational resources are allocated.
This approach aligns with the fixed confidence setting. The proposed functional form
t(n,\alpha)
offers the following properties: (1) it is compatible with the median elimination strategy, (2) it preserves the sure screening property, and (3) it significantly lowers computational complexity compared to the original screening procedure.
These properties facilitate both the practical execution of our algorithm and the theoretical analysis. We will demonstrate these results in the next section.

Intuitively, the efficient screening algorithm, referred to as BanditCR-SIS, can be summarized in the following steps:

•

We start by initializing
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
as the full model
\{1,2,\ldots,p\}
, and setting the initial model size
p_{0}=p
.

•

First, we compute the Chatterjee’s rank correlation coefficients using
n_{1}
randomly selected observations for each feature
X_{j}
, where
j\in\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
, and obtain a reduced submodel
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
with size
p_{1}=\lfloor\delta_{1}p_{0}\rfloor
.

•

We then apply a similar procedure to the model
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
using
n_{2}
randomly selected observations, resulting in a submodel
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
with size
p_{2}=\lfloor\delta_{2}p_{1}\rfloor
.

•

These process is repeated iteratively until the
k
th round, when the model size reaches the target size
d
, i.e.,
|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{k-1}|=p_{k-1}>d
, and
|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{k}|=p_{k}=d
.

Each
n_{l}
is determined by the subsample function in Equation (
3.2
).
For practical implementation, rather than randomly selecting subsamples in each round, we begin by shuffling the entire dataset
\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}^{n}_{i=1}
, which includes the response and the corresponding predictors. Subsamples are then sequentially selected in each round from this shuffled dataset.

Figure 1:
An illustration of the proposed BanditCR-SIS algorithm. (a) Randomly shuffle the entire dataset, including the response the corresponding predictors. (b) The light red cells indicate the subsamples (rows) chosen for the current iteration.
Within the predictor matrix, selected entries are marked in blue, with deeper shades of blue signifying higher values of Chatterjee’s rank correlation.
The shaded areas denote predictor features (columns) that have been excluded in both the current and earlier rounds.
The proposed algorithm alternates between discarding features with low Chatterjee’s rank correlation and updating the correlation with selected observations in each round.

The overall computational complexity of BanditCR-SIS is given by
O(\sqrt{n}\log(n)p+n\log(n))
.
As illustrated in Fig.
1
, the complexity of the first step, which involves shuffling the full sample (including the response and corresponding predictor features), has a complexity of
O(n\log{n})
.
The second step alternates between discarding features and updating the correlation coefficients based on the selected subsamples in each iteration, with a complexity of
O(\sqrt{n}\log(n)p)
.
Compared to the original CR-SIS method, BanditCR-SIS optimizes computational efficiency by iteratively eliminating less informative features, focusing calculations on more relevant coefficients, and avoiding redundant operations.
In addition, although our algorithm alternates between discarding features and updating coefficients, we demonstrate that, with the median elimination strategy, the overall complexity remains independent of the total number of iterations
k
, which is of the order of
\log(p)
.
Detailed proof of this computational complexity is available in the Supplementary Material.

4
Theoretical Analysis

We now examine the theoretical properties of the proposed methods.
Under mild regularity conditions, we demonstrate that the CR-SIS method possesses both the sure screening property
(
Fan and Lv 2008
;
Li et al. 2012
)
and the consistency in ranking property
(
Zhu et al. 2011
)
.
The sure screening property ensures that, with asymptotic probability one, all active features will be included in the selected feature set.
The ranking consistency property guarantees that the method selects all active predictors and excludes all inactive ones with high probability. Furthermore, the proposed efficient version preserves the sure screening property within our framework, yielding favorable results.
To facilitate the technical proofs, we impose the following conditions, although they may not be the weakest possible.

Condition
.

p>n
and
\log(p)=O(n^{\xi})
for
\xi\in(0,1/2-2\kappa)
, where
\kappa
is given by Condition (2).

The minimum
\omega_{j}
of active features satisfies

\min\limits_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\omega_{j}\geq 2cn^{-\kappa}

for some constants
c>0
and
\kappa>0
.

For some constant
c_{0}>0
,

\liminf_{p\rightarrow\infty}\big\{\min_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\omega_{j}-\max_{j\in\mathcal{M}^{c}}\omega_{j}\big\}\geq 2c_{0}.

In each round
l
,

\big\{j:\widehat{\omega}_{j}^{n_{l}}\geq cn^{-\kappa},j\in\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{l-1}\big\}\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{l},

where
\widehat{\omega}_{j}^{n_{l}}
denotes the correlation coefficient
\widehat{\omega}_{j}
calculated using
n_{l}
samples.

These conditions are milder than those for the SIS
(
Fan and Lv 2008
)
and DC-SIS
(
Li et al. 2012
)
, because we do not require the regression function of
Y
onto
X
to be linear and it needs little assumption on the moments of predictors.
Condition (1) corresponds to Condition 1 of
Fan and Lv 2008
, which indicates that we focus on the NP-dimensionality case where the dimension
p
grows exponentially with the sample size
n
.
Condition (2) is commonly encountered in the marginal screening literature
(
Fan and Lv 2008
;
Li et al. 2012
)
. Intuitively, it requires that the dependency between the active predictors and the response dose not decay too quickly to zero as
n
increases.
Condition (3) is similar to Condition (C3) in
Cui et al. 2015
, which ensures a clear signal separation between the active and inactive features.
Condition (4) is typically introduced for the technical analysis of the BanditCR-SIS method.
It implies that in each round, the number of retained features,
p_{l}
, is larger than what would be selected using the soft thresholding rule.
For example, in the final round
k
, the model size
d=|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{k}|=|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{B}|>|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}|
.

Theorem
1
below gives the sure screening property of the CR-SIS method. That is, all active predictors can be included in the reduced model
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}
with the probability approaching one at an exponential rate as
n
approaches infinity.

Theorem 1
.

For any
\delta>0
, threshold values
c>0
and
1/4>\kappa>0
, there exits a constant
K>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\Big\{\max\limits_{1\leq j\leq p}|\widehat{\omega}_{j}-\omega_{j}|>cn^{-\kappa}\Big\}\leq O\left(p\exp\left\{-Kc^{2}n^{1-2\kappa}\right\}\right).

Under Condition (2), we have that

\mathbb{P}\big\{\mathcal{M}\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{M}}\big\}\geq 1-|\mathcal{M}|O\left(\exp\left\{-Kc^{2}n^{1-2\kappa}\right\}\right).

Corollary 1

(Sure screening)
.

Under Conditions (1) and (2), we have the sure screening property

\mathbb{P}\big\{\mathcal{M}\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{M}}\big\}\rightarrow 1\quad\text{as }n\rightarrow\infty.

Another interesting property for independence screening is ranking consistency property
(
Zhu et al. 2011
)
. Theorem
2
below demonstrates a stronger theoretical result than sure screening property.
It tells us that, almost surely, the sample correlation values
\widehat{\omega}_{j}
rank active predictors above the inactive ones with high probability, guaranteeing a clear separation between active and inactive predictors.

Theorem 2

(Ranking consistency)
.

Under Conditions (1) and (3), we have that almost surely

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Big(\min_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\widehat{\omega}_{j}-\min_{j\in\mathcal{M}^{c}}\widehat{\omega}_{j}\Big)>0.

We now provide our main theorem that the proposed efficient version of CR-SIS method preserves the sure screening property under our settings.

Theorem 3
.

Under Conditions (2) and (4), there exists a constant
K>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\{\mathcal{M}\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{B}\}\geq\prod\limits_{l=1}^{k}\Big[1-|\mathcal{M}|O\big(\exp\{-Kc^{2}n_{l}n^{-2\kappa}\}\big)\Big].

where
n_{l}
is given by the subsample function (
3.2
) for
l=1,2,\ldots k
.

Corollary 2

(Sure screening)
.

Under Conditions (1), (2) and (4), we have the sure screening property

\mathbb{P}\{\mathcal{M}\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{B}\}\rightarrow 1\quad\text{as }n\rightarrow\infty.

Theorem
3
highlights two key points. Firstly, the probability that the selected set
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{B}
contains all active features is closely related to the choice of
n_{l}
. Clearly, a larger value of
n_{l}
leads to a higher screening accuracy.
When
n_{l}
is chosen to be the full sample size
n
, the BanditCR-SIS method reduces to the original screening method, and so does the screening accuracy converge towards that of the original method as
n_{l}
increases to
n
.
Recall that
n_{l}
is determined by the tuning parameter
\alpha_{0}
. Thus, by varying
\alpha_{0}
, we can control the accuracy of the results, depending on the available computational budget.
Secondly, the overall probability is the product of the probabilities that the active features set
\mathcal{M}
is contained in
\mathcal{M}_{l}
during each round.
In particular, the median elimination strategy implies that
k=O(\log(p))=O(n^{\xi})
, leading to
k
approaching infinity as
n
diverges.
Corollary
2
shows that the increase in
k
is negligible compared to the exponential convergence of the total probability towards one, since
n_{l}
also increases with
n
. A more detailed discussion is provided in the Supplementary Material.

The simulation studies were implemented using Python. We further demonstrate the effectiveness of these screening procedures with an empirical analysis of a real-world dataset. The performance of the proposed methods is compared with that of classic sure independence screening
(
Fan and Lv 2008
)
and distance correlation-based screening
(
Li et al. 2012
)
.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce a rank-based correlation, named Chatterjee’s rank, into the screening procedure, that is, CR-SIS.
To alleviate the computational burden, we further develop a more efficient variant named BanditCR-SIS, inspired by multi-armed bandit algorithms. We establish the sure screening property for both methods when the number of predictors grows exponentially with the sample size.
The proposed CR-SIS method is particularly effective in detecting informative features, especially when the variables exhibit complex nonlinear relationships.
Additionally, we accelerate the computational complexity of the CR-SIS method from
O(n\log(n)p)
to
O(\sqrt{n}\log(n)p+n\log(n))
, which is a sublinear order with respect to sample size
n
. This improvement makes BanditCR-SIS significantly more efficient for large-scale datasets compared to existing model-free screening methods.
Our numerical studies, conducted on both synthetic and real datasets, demonstrate the superior performance of the proposed methods in terms of both computational efficiency and screening accuracy.

Like most independence screening methods, CR-SIS also struggles to identify truly important predictors that are marginally independent of, but jointly correlated with, the response variable. To overcome this issue, some iterative screening methods have been proposed, such as iterative SIS
(
Fan and Lv 2008
)
and iterative DC-SIS
(
Zhong and Zhu 2015
)
. However, these methods often suffer from significantly higher computational costs.
The iterative nature of these procedures aligns well with the BanditCR-SIS algorithm, as both iteratively eliminate noninformative features in batches. Therefore, developing more efficient methods to identify important predictors that are marginally independent of the response, while also addressing the computational burden, is a critical topic for our future research.
