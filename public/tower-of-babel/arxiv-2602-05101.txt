Painlevé Universality classes for the maximal amplitude solution of the Focusing Nonlinear Schrödinger Equation with randomness
Aikaterini Gkogkou, Guido Mazzuca, Kenneth D. T-R McLaughlin

Abstract
We establish universality for extremal solutions of the focusing nonlinear Schrödinger equation. Extremal solutions are N N -soliton solutions that achieve the theoretical maximal amplitude and diverge as N → ∞ N\to\infty . We consider extremal solutions with the discrete eigenvalues randomly drawn from sub-exponential distributions, and identify two distinct universality classes, determined by the macroscopic structure of the spectrum: the Painlevé–III rogue-wave solution, where the eigenvalues take the form λ j = v j + i ​ μ j \lambda_{j}=v_{j}+i\mu_{j} , and the Painlevé–V rogue wave solution, where λ j = − ζ ​ j + v j + i ​ μ j \lambda_{j}=-\zeta\,j+v_{j}+i\mu_{j} , with 0 < ζ < 1 0<\zeta<1 . (In both cases, μ j \mu_{j} and v j v_{j} are subexponential random variables.) Universality can then be summarized as follows: independently of the specific distribution of the eigenvalues, the rescaled solutions converge locally to a deterministic profile governed by the Painlevé-III equation in the first regime, and the Painlevé-V equation in the second. These results demonstrate that the formation of Painlevé-type rogue waves is a universal phenomenon robust to randomness.

Introduction

Rogue waves, often referred to as
freak waves
or
monster waves
, are exceptionally large and spontaneous ocean waves with their average height being at least three times the height of the surrounding waves. They are very unpredictable, quite unexpected and mysterious. As Akhmediev describes
[
2
,
3
]
, they are waves “which come from nowhere and disappear with no trace.” From a practical standpoint, their unpredictability and extreme amplitude, pose serious hazards to ships and offshore platforms, making the study of their formation and dynamics crucial.

Historically, rogue waves were dismissed as exaggerated sailor reports. Today, they are recognized as statistically significant phenomena, representing extreme, low-probability events. Their rarity makes statistical approaches and probabilistic models fundamental to understand the mechanisms underlying their formation. For instance, Dematteis et al. in
[
16
]
, used large-deviation theory to identify the most likely precursor wave configurations and estimate the probability of extreme rogue waves in random deep-sea conditions. Beyond the ocean, rogue waves are also observed in optical fibers
[
31
,
22
,
23
,
18
]
, and in plasmas
[
26
,
6
]
, highlighting their widespread presence in diverse nonlinear systems. For a comprehensive overview of rogue wave phenomena and their physical applications, we refer the reader to the survey by Onorato et al.
[
28
]
. Recently, some experimental evidence of the existence of rogue waves has emerged. For instance, Chabchoub et al.
[
13
]
experimentally generated and reported the observation of a
Peregrine breather
, the prototypical model of rogue waves, in a controlled water
-
tank setup. Additionally, Dematteis et al.
[
15
]
, generated random waves in a long water flume and demonstrated that rogue waves correspond to rare hydrodynamic
instanton
configurations predicted by stochastic statistical models.

Water waves can be effectively described by a variety of nonlinear wave equations. In particular, the focusing nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation

i\partial_{t}\psi=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\psi-|\psi|^{2}\psi\,,\quad(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},

(1.1)

is a canonical model for the slow modulation of weakly nonlinear, quasi-monochromatic wave packets in deep water, see
[
34
]
. The Peregrine breather, given as follows

\psi(x,t)=\left[1-\frac{4(1+2it)}{1+4x^{2}+4t^{2}}\right]e^{it}

(1.2)

has been suggested as a possible model for rogue-wave phenomena, since it is localized in both space and time (albeit slowly decaying). A particularly notable feature of the Peregrine breather is that its peak amplitude reaches three times that of the background wave,
\psi_{\mathrm{bg}}(x,t)=e^{it}
. Originally derived by Peregrine
[
29
]
to model extreme water-wave events on a constant-amplitude background, it was later experimentally generated and observed for the first time in
[
13
]
. Beyond the Peregrine breather, the
Akhmediev breather
[
4
,
5
]
, and the Kuznetsov–Ma breather
[
24
,
25
]
are relatively simple exact solutions of the focusing NLS equation that are often considered models of rogue waves.

From a mathematical perspective, certain types of rogue waves have been extensively studied using a Riemann–Hilbert approach. For example, in
[
9
,
8
]
, Bilman, Ling, and Miller consider a Riemann-Hilbert problem (RHP) with
k
simple poles, and they rescale the pole locations so that all poles coalesce to a single point, forming a pole of order
k
. In the limit as
k\to\infty
, they establish the existence of a large-
k
limiting profile, referred to as the
rogue wave of infinite order
, which is described in terms of a member of the Painlevé–III hierarchy
[
8
]
. Other studies have used Riemann–Hilbert analysis to generate rogue waves from high-order breather solutions
[
10
]
, or high-order soliton solutions
[
7
]
, or high-order solutions belonging to a one-parameter family which includes the previous two classes
[
11
]
.

The focusing NLS equation (
1.1
) is an integrable system as shown by Zakharov and Shabat in
[
33
]
. Its Lax pair formulation is given by:

\partial_{x}\Phi=\begin{pmatrix}-iz&\psi\\
-{\overline{\psi}}&iz\end{pmatrix}\Phi,\quad\partial_{t}\Phi=\begin{pmatrix}-iz^{2}+\frac{i}{2}|\psi|^{2}&z\psi+\frac{i}{2}\psi_{x}\\
\\[0.1pt]
-z{\overline{\psi}}+\frac{i}{2}{\overline{\psi}}_{x}&iz^{2}-\frac{i}{2}|\psi|^{2}\end{pmatrix}\Phi,

(1.3)

where
z\in\mathbb{C}
is the spectral parameter, and
\Phi=\Phi(z;x,t)
are simultaneous solutions of (
1.3
). The NLS equation admits a broad collection of fundamental solutions. The most famous being the one-soliton solution

\displaystyle\psi_{s}(x,t)=2\eta\ \mbox{sech}\left(2\eta(x+2\xi t-x_{0})\right)e^{-2i\left[\xi x+\left(\xi^{2}-\eta^{2}\right)t\right]}e^{-i\phi_{0}}

(1.4)

parametrized by four parameters:
\xi,\eta,\phi_{0}
, and
x_{0}
. In addition to solitons, the NLS equation supports a rich variety of other solutions, including periodic and quasi-periodic waves, dispersive shock waves, breather solutions, rogue wave solutions, and multi-soliton solutions. In this work, we focus on multi-soliton solutions, which we refer to as
N
-soliton solutions, where
N
denotes the number of solitons. Our interest lies in the setting in which these solutions attain their maximal amplitude, corresponding to the formation of a rogue-wave profile.

Since it is integrable, the NLS equation can be solved through the inverse scattering transform (IST), which consists of three components: the direct scattering problem, the time evolution, and the inverse problem. We refer the reader to
[
1
,
12
]
for a detailed overview of the scattering and inverse scattering theory of the focusing NLS equation among other nonlinear partial differential equations. In the direct problem, one extracts the scattering data, which consists of the reflection coefficient
r(z)
, the discrete eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
(each associated with one soliton), and the normalization constants
\{c_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
. For the rest of the paper, we denote the scattering data by:

\mathcal{D}_{N}=\{r(z),\{\lambda_{n},c_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}\}.

The time evolution of the scattering data is straightforward: the eigenvalues do not change in time, while the reflection coefficient and normalization constants explicitly evolve as:

\mathcal{D}_{N}(t)=\left\{r(z,t),\{\lambda_{n},c_{n}(t)\}_{n=1}^{N}\right\}=\left\{r(z)e^{2itz^{2}},\{\lambda_{n},c_{n}e^{2it\lambda_{n}^{2}}\}_{n=1}^{N}\right\}.

(1.5)

The inverse problem can be formulated as the following RHP (see
[
12
]
for more details):

RHP 1.1
.

Given spectral data
\mathcal{D}_{N}(t)
, find a
2\times 2
matrix-valued function
M(z)
such that:

•

\ {M}(z)
is analytic in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
, except for simple poles located at the points
\{\lambda_{n},\overline{\lambda_{n}}\}_{n=1}^{N}
.

•

For
z\in\mathbb{R}
,
M
has boundary values
M_{+}(z)
and
M_{-}(z)
, which satisfy the jump relation

M_{+}(z)=\ M_{-}(z)\ J_{M}(z),\quad J_{M}=\begin{pmatrix}1+|r(z)|^{2}&{\overline{r(z)}}e^{-2i\theta(z;x,t)}\\
r(z)e^{2i\theta(z;x,t)}&1\end{pmatrix},

(1.6)

where
\theta(z;x,t)=zx+z^{2}t
.

•

At each of the poles
\lambda_{n}
in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
,
\ {M}(z)
satisfies the residue condition

\Res_{\lambda_{n}}M(z)=\lim_{z\to\lambda_{n}}\left[M(z)\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
c_{n}e^{2i\theta(\lambda_{n};x,t)}&0\end{pmatrix}\right]\,,

(1.7)

and at each pole
\overline{\lambda_{n}}
in
\mathbb{C}^{-}
:

\Res_{{\overline{\lambda_{n}}}}M(z)=\lim_{z\to{\overline{\lambda_{n}}}}\left[M(z)\begin{pmatrix}0&-{\overline{c_{n}}}e^{-2i\theta({\overline{\lambda_{n}}};x,t)}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right].

(1.8)

•

\ {M}(z)=\ I+{\mathcal{O}}(z^{-1})
as
z\to\infty
.

Since we are interested in pure solitonic (i.e.,
reflectionless
) solutions, we set
r(z)\equiv 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, in which case the jump condition on
\mathbb{R}
disappears. Therefore, RHP
1.1
reduces to finding a meromorphic function with
N
simple poles in the upper half plane and their complex conjugates in the lower half plane, and prescribed asymptotic behavior for large-
z
given by the last condition in RHP
1.1
.

While the IST and RHP analysis provide a powerful, analytic framework for constructing soliton solutions, there exists another equivalent way of obtaining the pure solitonic solution of the NLS equation: the Darboux method, also known as the
dressing method
. This method iteratively generates multi-soliton solutions by “dressing” a simpler seed solution, typically the trivial zero solution. This method will be explained in Section
3
below.

One important application of this method is a characterization of
extremal multi-soliton solutions.
First, it is well known (see, for example,
[
21
]
) that an
N
-soliton solution satisfies the inequality

|\psi_{N}(x,t)|\leq 2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Im\left(\lambda_{n}\right),\ \mbox{ for all }x,t.

(1.9)

It was also shown in
[
21
]
that this upper bound is sharp. Indeed, as we will see, for any collection of
N
eigenvalues, there is a one-parameter family of normalization constants
\{c_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
so that
\psi_{N}(0,0)
achieves this upper bound. We will consider sequences of these
N
-soliton extremal solutions, and show that as
N\to\infty
, new universal behaviors emerge.

As
N\to\infty
, we establish two different universality results: the (suitably rescaled) solutions converge to a new fundamental solution satisfying a Painlevé equation independently of the distribution of the (random) discrete eigenvalues. We identify two distinct universality classes, distinguished by the structure of the discrete eigenvalues: the
Painlevé–III
and
Painlevé–V
rogue-wave solutions. In the Painlevé–III case, the discrete eigenvalues are

\lambda_{j}=v_{j}+i\mu_{j},

whereas for Painlevé–V they are

\lambda_{j}=-\zeta\,j+v_{j}+i\mu_{j},\qquad 0<\zeta<1.

In both cases,
v_{j}
and
\mu_{j}
are sub-exponential random variables. Universality can then be summarized as follows: regardless of the specific realizations of the amplitudes and velocities, provided they are sub-exponential random variables and with normalization constants chosen to maximize the
N
-soliton solution, the resulting maximal peak always corresponds to either a Painlevé–III or Painlevé–V rogue-wave profile, see Figure
1
.

2
presents the main results. The analysis establishing these results is developed in Sections
4
and
5
. We defer the more technical proofs to the Appendices.

2
Statement of the results

In this section, we precisely state our results. First, we recall the definition of
sub-exponential
random variables.

Definition 2.1

(See
[
32
]
Proposition 2.6)
.

Let
{\mathfrak{X}}
be a random variable, we say that
{\mathfrak{X}}
is a sub-exponential random variable if there exists a
K>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(|{\mathfrak{X}}|>t)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{t}{K}\right)\,,\qquad\forall\,t\geq 0\,.

(2.1)

A classical example of sub-exponential random variable is the
\chi^{2}(\alpha)
-distribution,
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
, which is the random variable with probability density function

f(x;\alpha)=\begin{cases}\frac{x^{\alpha/2-1}e^{-\frac{x}{2}}}{2^{\alpha/2}\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)}\qquad&x\geq 0\\
\\
0\qquad&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(2.2)

where
\Gamma(x)
is the Gamma-function
[
27
, Ch. 5]
.
We now introduce the scattering data and state the first set of assumptions used throughout this work.

Assumptions 1

(Painlevé-III Rogue Wave)
.

For each fixed
N>0
, we generate the scattering data

\mathcal{D}_{N,III}=\left\{r\equiv 0,\left\{\lambda_{n},c_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{N}\right\}

(2.3)

as follows. Let the poles
\lambda_{n}=v_{n}+i\mu_{n}
, and in our probabilistic calculations, we assume the following:

1.

The amplitudes
\mu_{n}
are independent identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables

\mu_{n}\ \sim\ \mathcal{D}_{1},

(2.4)

where
\mathcal{D}_{1}
is sub-exponential random variable, with positive support and mean
\mu_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}
;

2.

The velocities
v_{n}
are independent identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables

v_{n}\ \sim\ \mathcal{D}_{2},

(2.5)

where
\mathcal{D}_{2}
is a sub-exponential distribution with mean
v_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}
;

3.

The normalization constants
c_{n}
are random variables chosen to satisfy

c_{n}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{N}(\lambda_{n}-{\overline{\lambda_{\ell}}})\prod_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell=1}{\ell\neq n}}^{N}\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{\ell}}.

(2.6)

We will refer to this set of assumptions as “Painlevé-III Rogue Wave assumptions”. The importance of the choice of normalization constants (
2.6
) is explained in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose
\{\lambda_{n},\}_{n=1}^{N}
is an arbitrary collection of eigenvalues in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
, and that the normalization constants are given by (
2.6
). Then the associated
N
-soliton solution is extremal, and satisfies

\left|\psi_{N}(0,0)\right|=2\Im\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\lambda_{n}\right).

(2.7)

This result is a direct consequence of Theorem
3.1
, and its proof is presented in Section
3
.
Hence, for each positive integer
N
, under the Painlevé-III Rogue Wave assumptions, any realization of the scattering data corresponds to an extremal
N
-soliton solution to the NLS equation, which we will denote by

\displaystyle\psi_{N,III}(x,t)=\psi(x,t;\mathcal{D}_{N,III}).

(2.8)

The following theorem is our first universality result.

Theorem 2.2
.

Under the Painlevé-III Rogue Wave assumptions, for all
(X,T)
in a compact set
{\mathfrak{K}}
, there exists an
N_{0}>0
, a constant
C\equiv C(N_{0},X,T)>0
and
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
N>N_{0}
the following holds

\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|\frac{2}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}\psi_{N,III}\left(\frac{2X}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}},\frac{4T}{N^{2}\mu^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}\right)-\Psi_{III}(X,T)\right|}\right]\leq CN^{-\varepsilon}\,,

(2.9)

where the mean value is taken with respect to the product measure induced by the distribution of
\{\mu_{n},v_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
. The quantity
\Psi_{III}(X,T)
is a relatively new fundamental solution of the NLS equation, discovered by Bilman, Ling, and Miller
[
8
]
, described below. Furthermore, for all
N>0
, the solution satisfies
|\psi_{N,III}(0,0)|=2\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu_{j}
, so that
\psi_{N,III}(x,t)
is an extremal solution.

In
[
8
]
, the authors considered a sequence of solutions of the NLS equation characterized by a meromorphic Riemann-Hilbert problem with a single pole of large order (and its complex conjugate). Under suitable rescaling and transformations as the order of the pole diverges to
\infty
, their work yielded a new limiting Riemann-Hilbert problem, and their analysis of that problem led to their discovery of
\Psi_{III}(X,T)
. They established many fundamental properties of this solution, which we have collected and summarized in Theorem
4.7
. In particular, they proved that
\Psi_{III}(X,T)
solves the focusing NLS equation (
1.1
), and forged an explicit connection to the Painlevé-III equation at
T=0
via the relation

\Psi_{III}\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{8},0\right)=\frac{\kappa}{x^{2}}\,\mathrm{exp}\left(\int_{1}^{x}\frac{2}{u(s)}\,ds\right)

(2.10)

where
u(x)
is a solution of the Painlevé-III equation

\frac{d^{2}u}{dx^{2}}=\frac{1}{u}\left(\frac{du}{dx}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{x}\frac{du}{dx}+\frac{4}{x}+4u^{3}-\frac{4}{u}

(2.11)

and the constant
\kappa
in equation (
2.10
) is fixed to satisfy
\left|\Psi_{III}(0,0)\right|=4
. Note that
u
is also characterized via a Riemann-Hilbert problem (rather than specific initial or boundary conditions).

Remark 2.1
.

In (
2.10
), the choice of the endpoint
1
is arbitrary, one can consider any point on the real axis except zero.

We prove Theorem
2.2
in Section
4
.

Remark 2.2
.

The previous result is universal in the following sense. Independent of the specific distribution of the amplitudes and the velocities,
\psi_{N,III}(x,t)
is an extremal solution and its peak is described in terms of a solution of the Painlevé-III equation.

Remark 2.3
.

We notice that the choice of the point
(0,0)
is completely arbitrary. Indeed one can use the translation invariance property of the NLS equation to move the point where the function
|\psi_{N,III}(x,t)|
reaches its maxima from
(0,0)
to any point
(x_{0},t_{0})
.

Remark 2.4
.

This result is consistent with
[
8
]
, where the same function
u(x)
is obtained.

The second set of assumptions that we consider is the following.

Assumptions 2

(Painlevé-V Rogue Wave)
.

For each fixed

, we generate the scattering data

\mathcal{D}_{N,V}=\left\{r\equiv 0,\left\{\lambda_{n},c_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{N}\right\}

(2.12)

as follows. Fix
0<\zeta<1
, let the poles
\lambda_{n}=-\zeta\,n+v_{n}+i\mu_{n}
, and in our probabilistic calculations we assume the following:

1.

The amplitudes
\mu_{n}
are independent identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables

\mu_{n}\ \sim\ \mathcal{D}_{1},

(2.13)

where
\mathcal{D}_{1}
is sub-exponential random variable, with positive support and mean
\mu_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}
;

2.

The velocities
v_{n}
are independent identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables

v_{n}\ \sim\ \mathcal{D}_{2},

(2.14)

where
\mathcal{D}_{2}
is a sub-exponential distribution with mean

;

3.

The normalization constants

are random variables chosen to satisfy

c_{n}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{N}(\lambda_{n}-{\overline{\lambda_{\ell}}})\prod_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell=1}{\ell\neq n}}^{N}\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{\ell}}.

(2.15)

Hence, for each positive integer

, these scattering data correspond to an extremal

-soliton solution to the NLS equation (again achieving the theoretical maximum at

), which we will denote by

\displaystyle\psi_{N,V}(x,t)=\psi(x,t;\mathcal{D}_{N,V}).

We will refer to this set of assumptions as “Painlevé-V Rogue Wave assumptions”. Under this assumption, we prove our second universality result.

.

Under the Painlevé-V Rogue Wave assumptions, for all
(X,T)
in a compact set
{\mathfrak{K}}
, there exists an
N_{0}>0
, a constant
C\equiv C(N_{0},X,T)>0
and

such that for all

\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|\frac{1}{N}\psi_{N,V}\left(\frac{X}{N},\frac{T}{N^{2}}\right)-\Psi_{V}(X,T)\right|}\right]\leq CN^{-\varepsilon}\,,

(2.17)

where the mean value is taken with respect to the product measure induced by the distribution of
\{\mu_{n},v_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}

,

, so that

The quantity
\Psi_{V}(X,T)
is a solution of the focusing NLS equation

characterized by Riemann-Hilbert problem
5.5
below. For

,

\Psi_{V}(X,0)=\frac{\kappa}{X}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2i\zeta}\int_{1}^{2i\zeta X}\frac{u(s)}{1-u(s)}ds\right)

where

satisfies the Painlevé-V equation

\frac{d^{2}u}{dx^{2}}=\left(\frac{1}{2u}\pm\frac{1}{u-1}\right)\left(\frac{du}{dx}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{x}\frac{du}{dx}-2\frac{\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}^{2}}{\zeta^{2}}\frac{(u-1)^{2}}{x^{2}}\left(u-\frac{1}{u}\right)+\frac{u}{x}-\frac{u(u+1)}{2(u-1)}

.

.

The choice of the endpoint

in (

) is arbitrary, one can consider any point on the real axis except zero.

Remark 2.6
.

Riemann-Hilbert problem

.

3
The Darboux method and extremal soliton solutions

In this section, we briefly describe the Darboux method for constructing pure solitonic solutions of the focusing nonlinear Schrödinger equation and explain its relevance for the characterization of extremal multi-soliton solutions. For a detailed overview of this method, we refer the reader to
[
19
,
20
]
.

The parameters used to characterize a pure solitonic solution of the NLS equation in the Darboux framework are different than (but equivalent to) the IST scattering data (
1.5
). We will use the following slightly different notation for the Darboux framework’s scattering data:

\mathfrak{D}_{N}(t)=\left\{r(z,t)\equiv 0,\{\lambda_{n},p_{n}(t)\}_{n=1}^{N}\right\}=\left\{r(z,t)\equiv 0,\{\lambda_{n},p_{n}e^{-2it\lambda_{n}^{2}}\}_{n=1}^{N}\right\}\,.

(3.1)

Here,
p_{n}
are the Darboux parameters associated with each soliton, playing a role analogous to the normalization constants
c_{n}
in the RHP formulation. The procedure is summarized as follows. Let
\mathfrak{D}_{n}(0)
denote the spectral data corresponding to the first
n
eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\}
and associated normalization constants
\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}
. The iterative procedure starts from the trivial solution

\psi_{0}(x,0)=0,

(3.2)

with the corresponding solution of the Zakharov–Shabat (ZS) system (
1.3
)

\Phi_{0}(x,z)=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-izx}&0\\
0&e^{izx}\end{pmatrix}.

(3.3)

Suppose that, after
n-1
steps, we have constructed the
(n-1)
-soliton solution
\psi_{n-1}(x,0)
with spectral data
\mathfrak{D}_{n-1}(0)
, which consists of the first
n-1
eigenvalues and their associated normalization constants. The corresponding solution of the ZS system is denoted by
\Phi_{n-1}(x,z)
. The spectral data
\mathfrak{D}_{n}(0)
of the
n
-soliton solution is obtained by adding an additional eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}
and its normalization constant
p_{n}
to
\mathfrak{D}_{n-1}(0)
. In this sense,
\mathfrak{D}_{n-1}(0)
is taken directly from
\mathfrak{D}_{n}(0)
by removing the
n
-th eigenvalue and normalization constant. The
n
-soliton solution at time
t=0
is then constructed from
\psi_{n-1}(x,0)
via

\psi_{n}(x,0)=\psi_{n-1}(x,0)+2i(\lambda_{n}-\overline{\lambda_{n}})\frac{\overline{q_{n1}}q_{n2}}{|\mathbf{q}_{n}|^{2}},

(3.4)

where the vector
\mathbf{q}_{n}=(q_{n1},q_{n2})^{\intercal}
is determined by
\Phi_{n-1}(x,z)
and the spectral data
\mathfrak{D}_{n}(0)
via the relation:

\mathbf{q}_{n}(x)=\overline{\Phi_{n-1}(x,\overline{\lambda_{n}})}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
p_{n}\end{pmatrix},

(3.5)

where
\Phi_{n-1}(x,z)
is the solution of the ZS system corresponding to
\psi_{n-1}(x,0)
. The corresponding ZS solution for the
n
-th soliton,
\Phi_{n}(x,z)
, is given in terms of
\Phi_{n-1}(x,z)
by the following relation

\Phi_{n}(x,z)=\chi_{n}(x,z)\cdot\Phi_{n-1}(x,z),\quad(\chi_{n})_{m\ell}=\delta_{m\ell}+\frac{\lambda_{n}-\overline{\lambda_{n}}}{z-\lambda_{n}}\frac{\overline{q_{nm}}q_{n\ell}}{|\mathbf{q}_{n}|^{2}},\quad m,\ell=1,2,

(3.6)

where
\delta_{m\ell}
is the Kronecker delta.
By iterating this procedure, starting from
\psi_{0}
and
\Phi_{0}
, one can construct a multi-soliton solution
\psi_{N}(x,0)
of the NLS equation by adding one soliton at a time. This iterative dressing procedure and its properties were analyzed in
[
21
]
, where the following result was established.

Let
\psi_{N}(x,t)
denote the multi-soliton solution constructed via the Darboux method with spectral data
\mathfrak{D}_{N}(t)
. Then the solution satisfies

\max_{x,t\in\mathbb{R}}|\psi_{N}(x,t)|\leq 2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Im(\lambda_{n}),

(3.7)

and choosing
p_{n}=1
, for all
n=1,\dots,N
, then

\max_{x,t\in\mathbb{R}}|\psi_{N}(x,t)|=|\psi_{N}(0,0)|=2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Im(\lambda_{n}).

In essence, when
\psi_{N}(x,t)
is constructed via the Darboux method, there exists a one-parameter family for
p_{n}
, namely
p_{n}=e^{i\eta}
, for which the solution attains its maximal amplitude. The special choice
\eta=0
recovers the equality above, see also Proposition 1.1 in
[
21
]
.

Since the Darboux normalization parameters provide the natural characterization of extremal multi-soliton solutions, it is essential to establish a precise correspondence between the Darboux parameters and the IST normalization constants, to facilitate the Riemann-Hilbert analysis. The following theorem provides this dictionary.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider the spectral data
D_{N}(t)
associated with RHP
1.1
, and
\mathfrak{D}_{N}(t)
associated with the Darboux method, defined in Eqs. (
1.5
)–(
3.1
), respectively. Suppose they are related via:

c_{n}=\frac{1}{p_{n}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{N}(\lambda_{n}-{\overline{\lambda_{\ell}}})\prod_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell=1}{\ell\neq n}}^{N}\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{\ell}}\,,

(3.8)

then the NLS solution arising from the two methods is the same.

The proof of this theorem is presented in Appendix
B
.

Remark 3.1
.

In this work, we choose
p_{n}=e^{i\eta}
with
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, so that the resulting solution achieves its maximal peak at
(x,t)=(0,0)
. This observation completes the proof of Lemma
2.1
.

4
The Painlevé–III Rogue Wave

In this section, we prove Theorem
2.2
. First, we recall some properties of sub-exponential random variables; then, we show that Theorem
2.2
holds
deterministically
in a subset
{\mathfrak{Q}}_{N}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}_{+}
, and finally, by leveraging the previously mentioned properties of sub-exponential random variables, we complete the proof.

4.1
Probabilistic Background

Here we recall some properties of sub-exponential random variables that we use to prove our main theorem. Experts in probability can skip this part since it only contains standard results.

Sub-exponential random variables are naturally equipped with the following norm
[
32
]
; let
{\mathfrak{X}}
be a sub-exponential random variable, then one can define the following norm

\|{\mathfrak{X}}\|_{\text{sub}}=\inf\{K>0\;|\;\mathbb{E}\left[{\exp({\mathfrak{X}}/K)}\right]\leq 2\}\,.

(4.1)

Using this definition, we can state the following lemma that we use later in the proof
[
32
, Corollary 2.9.2]
.

Lemma 4.1

(Sub-exponential Bernstein inequality)
.

Let
{\mathfrak{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathfrak{X}}_{N}
be independent, mean zero, sub-exponential random variables, and
{\bf a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. Then, there exists a
c>0
such that for every
t>0
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}{\mathfrak{X}}_{j}\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-c\min\left(\frac{t}{\max_{j}||{\mathfrak{X}}_{j}||_{\text{sub}}\max_{j}|a_{j}|};\frac{t^{2}}{\max_{j}||{\mathfrak{X}}_{j}||_{\text{sub}}^{2}||{\bf a}||_{2}^{2}}\right)\right)\,,

(4.2)

The following Lemma is a straightforward application of the definition of sub-exponential random variables.

Lemma 4.2
.

Under Assumption
1
or
2
, there exists a constant
\alpha>0
independent of
N
, such that for all
t>0

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{j=1,\ldots,N}\mu_{j}\geq\alpha\left(\ln(N)+t\right)+\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}\right)\leq 2e^{-t}\,.

An analogous statement applies to the
v_{j}
.

Proof.

Let
s>0
, then, by applying standard union bounds and the definition of sub-exponential random variable, there exists a constant
\alpha>0
independent of
N
such that the following holds

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{j=1,\ldots,N}\mu_{j}>s\right)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}(\mu_{j}-\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}>s-\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}})\leq 2N\exp\left(-\frac{s-\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}{\alpha}\right)\,,

(4.3)

setting
s=\alpha(\ln(N)+t)+\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}
we conclude.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

Notice that in the previous two lemmas
t
can depend on
N
. This implies in particular that in probability

\mu_{j}\ll N^{\varepsilon},\quad v_{j}\ll N^{\varepsilon}\,,\quad j=1,\ldots,N

(4.4)

for any
\varepsilon>0
.

4.2
Reduction to the model problem for Painlevé-III

We now use the previous results to define the set

, where we show that a deterministic version of Theorem
2.2
holds.
We fix

(4.5)

V_{2\delta}=\left\{(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}\Big|\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu_{j}-N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}\Big|\leq N^{2\,\delta}\right\}\,.

(4.6)

Remark 4.2
.

We notice that in view of Lemma

we know that there exists a constant

where obviously the term

The aim of this subsection is to prove the following

Theorem 4.3
.

Fix

\left|\frac{2}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}\psi_{N,III}\left(\frac{2X}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}},\frac{4T}{N^{2}\mu^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}\right)-\Psi_{III}(X,T)\right|\leq CN^{-\varepsilon}\,,

(4.9)

where

is as in Theorem
2.2

4.2.1
Proof of the Deterministic Result

To prove the previous theorem, we use the RHP formulation of the inverse scattering of the NLS equations. Therefore, we consider RHP
1.1
which under our assumptions reads as follows:

RHP 4.4
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
4.3
, find a
2\times 2
matrix-valued function
M(z)
such that:

•

\ {M}(z)
is meromorphic in
{\mathbb{C}}
, with simple poles at the points
\{\lambda_{j},\overline{\lambda_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{N}
.

•

At each of the poles
\lambda_{j}
in the upper half-plane,
{M}(z)
satisfies the residue condition

\Res_{\lambda_{j}}M(z)=\lim_{z\to\lambda_{j}}\left[M(z)\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\

(4.10)

where

\Res_{{\overline{\lambda_{j}}}}M(z)=\lim_{z\to{\overline{\lambda}}_{j}}\left[M(z)\begin{pmatrix}0&-{\overline{c}}_{j}e^{-2i\theta({\overline{\lambda}}_{j};x,t)}\\

(4.11)

•

As

M(z;x,t)=I+\frac{1}{2iz}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-m_{N,III}(x,t)&\psi_{N,III}(x,t)\\

Since

\Gamma

B(z)=\begin{cases}M(z)\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&a(z)e^{-2i\theta(z)}\\

\\[2.0pt]
M(z),&\quad z\,\,\text{outside}\,\,\Gamma\end{cases}

(4.14)

By direct calculation, one can prove that the matrix
B(z)
satisfies the following RHP.

RHP 4.5
.

Find a
2\times 2
matrix-valued function
B(z)
such that:

•

(4.15)

Consider now the following scaling

In this regime, one immediately gets that for

\displaystyle\ln(a(z))

\displaystyle=\ln(a\left(NZ\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}/2\right))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\ln\left(\frac{NZ\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}{2}-v_{j}-i\mu_{j}\right)-\ln\left(\frac{NZ\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}{2}-v_{j}+i\mu_{j}\right)

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&a(z)e^{-2i\theta(z)}\\

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\int_{V_{2\delta}^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left|\frac{2}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}\psi_{N,III}\left(\frac{2X}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}},\frac{4T}{\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}^{2}N^{2}}\right)-\Psi_{III}(X,T)\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{v}\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}

\displaystyle\leq CN^{2\delta-1}+\int_{\Omega_{\delta}^{c}}\left|\frac{2}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}\psi_{N,III}\left(\frac{2X}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}},\frac{4T}{\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}^{2}N^{2}}\right)\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{v}\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}

(4.37)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega_{\delta}^{c}}\left|\Psi_{III}(X,T)\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{v}\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\int_{V_{2\delta}^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left|\frac{2}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}\psi_{N,III}\left(\frac{2X}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}},\frac{4T}{\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}^{2}N^{2}}\right)\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{v}

(4.39)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{V_{2\delta}^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left|\Psi_{III}(X,T)\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{v}\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}.

(4.40)

Since
\Psi_{III}(X,T)
is a continuous function and we are considering
(X,T)
in a compact set, by Heine-Borel theorem there exists a constant
C_{1}
such that
|\Psi_{III}(X,T)|\leq C_{1}
, and there exists a constant
C_{2}
,
\varepsilon>0

\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{\delta}^{c})+\mathbb{P}(V_{2\delta}^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{N})\leq\exp(-C_{2}N^{\varepsilon})\,,

(4.41)

therefore we deduce that there exist two constants
c,\epsilon>0
such that

\int_{V_{2\delta}^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left|\Psi_{III}(X,T)\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{v}\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\int_{\Omega_{\delta}^{c}}\left|\Psi_{III}(X,T)\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{v}\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}\leq cN^{-\epsilon}\,.

(4.42)

From (
3.7
), we deduce that

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega_{\delta}^{c}}\left|\frac{2}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}\psi_{N,III}\left(\frac{2X}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}},\frac{4T}{\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}^{2}N^{2}}\right)\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{v}\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}\leq\frac{4}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}\int_{\Omega_{\delta,\mu}^{c}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu_{j}{\rm d}\boldsymbol{v}\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}\leq\frac{4}{\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}\int_{\Omega_{\delta,\mu}^{c}}\max_{j}\mu_{j}{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}.\end{split}

(4.43)

We introduce the notation
Y\sim\max_{j}\mu_{j}
and
\sigma(Y)=\mathbb{P}(Y>y)
, then we have that:

\int_{\Omega_{\delta}^{c}}\left|\frac{2}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}\psi_{N,III}\left(\frac{2X}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}},\frac{4T}{\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}^{2}N^{2}}\right)\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{v}\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}\leq\frac{4}{\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}\int_{N^{\delta}}^{\infty}Y\,{\rm d}\sigma(Y).

(4.44)

Integrating by parts and using the definition of the improper integral, we get

\int_{N^{\delta}}^{\infty}Y\,{\rm d}\sigma(Y)=-\lim_{t\to\infty}t\,\mathbb{P}(Y>t)+N^{\delta}\mathbb{P}(Y>N^{\delta})+\int_{N^{\delta}}^{\infty}\sigma(Y){\rm d}Y\,.

(4.45)

By Lemma
4.2
, we conclude that there exist two constants
c,\epsilon>0
, such that

\int_{\Omega_{\delta}^{c}}\left|\frac{2}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}\psi_{N,III}\left(\frac{2X}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}},\frac{4T}{\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}^{2}N^{2}}\right)\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{v}\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}\leq cN^{-\epsilon}\,.

(4.46)

Regarding the last term in (
4.39
), we notice that it can be estimated as

\displaystyle\int_{V_{2\delta}^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left|\frac{2}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}\psi_{N,III}\left(\frac{2X}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}},\frac{4T}{\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}^{2}N^{2}}\right)\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\int_{V_{2\delta}^{c}}\left|\frac{2}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}\psi_{N,III}\left(\frac{2X}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}},\frac{4T}{\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}^{2}N^{2}}\right)\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}\leq\frac{4}{N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}}\left(\int_{V_{2\delta}^{c}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu_{j}-N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}+N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}\int_{V_{2\delta}^{c}}{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}\right)\,.

Let
Y=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu_{j}-N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}
(note we are reusing
Y
, hopefully without confusion) and let
\sigma(Y)
be its cumulative distribution (so
{\rm d}\sigma(Y)
would be its p.d.f.). As before, integrating by parts we get

\begin{split}\int_{V_{2\delta}^{c}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu_{j}-N\mu_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}=&\int_{N^{2\delta}}^{\infty}Y{\rm d}\sigma(Y)\\
=&-\lim_{t\to\infty}t\,\mathbb{P}(Y>t)+N^{2\delta}\mathbb{P}\left(Y>N^{2\delta}\right)+\int_{N^{2\delta}}^{\infty}\sigma(Y){\rm d}Y.\end{split}

(4.47)

By Lemma
4.1
, we deduce that the right hand side of the previous equation goes to
0
as
N
grows and by (
4.8
)
\mathbb{P}(V_{2\delta}^{c})\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}0
. This completes the proof of Theorem
2.2
.
∎

Proof of the small-norm argument

In this section, we prove Lemma

and Lemma

. Specifically, we prove a more general result from which the two Lemmas follow. To do that, we need some definition. First, we define the following scalar product for matrix valued functions.

Definition A.1
.

Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\Sigma\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}
be a rectifiable curve and
M,Q\,:\,\Sigma\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}

for such matrix valued functions as

(A.1)

where
|{\rm d}z|
is the arc-length parameter and
M^{\star}
is the conjugate-transpose of the matrix
M

The previous scalar product naturally induces a norm as

\|M\|_{2}^{2}=\langle M,M\rangle\,.

(A.2)

Another norm for matrix-valued function
M:\,\Sigma\,\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
of interest is the
\infty
-norm defined as

\|M\|_{\infty}=n\essesup_{z\in\Sigma}\max_{i,j}|M_{i,j}(z)|\,.

(A.3)

We notice that for two matrix-valued functions
M,Q:\,\Sigma\,\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
the following holds.

\|MQ\|_{2}\leq\|M\|_{\infty}\|Q\|_{2}\,.

(A.4)

Naturally, the
\|\cdot\|_{2}
norm induces an operator norm as follows.

Definition A.2
.

Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\Sigma\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}
be a rectifiable curve. Let
C\,:\text{Mat}(n,\Sigma)\to\text{Mat}(n,\Sigma)
, where

\text{Mat}(n,\Sigma)\coloneqq\{M:\,\Sigma\,\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}|\|M\|_{2}<\infty\}\,.

(A.5)

We define the operator norm
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|C\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{2}
as

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|C\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{2}\coloneqq\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{M\in\text{Mat}(n,\Sigma)}{ ||M||_2=1}}\|C(M)\|_{2}\,.

C_{+},C_{-}\,:\text{Mat}(2,\Sigma)\to\text{Mat}(2,\Sigma)

C_{+}(h)(z)\coloneqq\lim_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\zeta\to z}{\text{ from inside of }\Sigma}}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{h(s)}{s-\zeta}{\rm d}s\,,\qquad C_{-}(h)(z)\coloneqq\lim_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\zeta\to z}{\text{ from outside of }\Sigma}}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{h(s)}{s-\zeta}{\rm d}s\,,

(A.7)

and, for any matrix valued function

C_{M}(h(z))(z)\coloneqq C_{-}(h(z)M(z))\,.

(A.8)

We notice that
\mathbbm{1}=C_{+}-C_{-}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{\overrightarrow{n-1}}\left(I_{2}+\frac{\overline{\lambda_{j}}-\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{n}-\overline{\lambda_{j}}}\frac{1}{||q_{j}(x)||^{2}}q_{j}(x)\cdot q_{j}^{\dagger}(x)\right)\,\Phi_{0}(x,\overline{\lambda_{n}})\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\
p_{n}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{\overrightarrow{n-1}}\left(I_{2}+\frac{\overline{\lambda_{j}}-\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{n}-\overline{\lambda_{j}}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(e^{-c|x|})\right)\right)\Phi_{0}(x,\overline{\lambda_{n}})\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\
p_{n}\end{pmatrix}

which entry-wise gives

\displaystyle q_{n1}(x)=e^{i\lambda_{n}x}\left(1+\mathcal{O}(e^{-c|x|})\right),\quad q_{n2}(x)=e^{-i\lambda_{n}x}p_{n}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{{\overline{\lambda_{j}}}-\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{n}-\overline{\lambda_{j}}}+\mathcal{O}(e^{-c|x|})\right),\quad\quad x\to+\infty.

The last equality implies that

||q_{n}(x)||^{2}\coloneqq|q_{n1}(x)|^{2}+|q_{n2}(x)|^{2}=e^{i(\lambda_{n}-\overline{\lambda_{n}})x}\left(1+\mathcal{O}(e^{-c|x|})\right)+e^{-i(\lambda_{n}-\overline{\lambda_{n}})x}|p_{n}|^{2}\left(\left|\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{{\overline{\lambda_{j}}}-\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{n}-\overline{\lambda_{j}}}\right|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(e^{-c|x|})\right)

or alternatively

||q_{n}(x)||^{2}\sim|q_{n2}(x)|^{2},\quad x\to+\infty

which implies that for
j=n
we get the desired asymptotics:

\frac{q_{n}(x)\cdot q_{n}^{\dagger}(x)}{||q_{n}(x)||^{2}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(e^{-c|x|}),\quad x\to+\infty

which concludes the induction, and therefore the proof.
∎

Appendix C
Proof of Lemma
5.1

In this section we prove Lemma
5.1
using the so called
\epsilon
-net argument. For the sake of the reader, we report the statement of the lemma

Lemma C.1
.

Fix
\zeta\in(0,1)
, and
\frac{1}{4}<\delta
. Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
be positive i.i.d. sub-exponential random variable with mean
\mu
. Then, there exists an
N_{0}>0
and two positive constants
c,\gamma>0
independent of
N
such that

\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{U}}^{c})\leq e^{-cN^{\gamma}}\,,

(C.1)

where

{\mathcal{U}}=\left\{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}\Big|\,\,\forall Z:|Z|=1,\Bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{X_{j}-\mu}{Z+\zeta\frac{j}{N}}\Bigg|\leq N^{2\,\delta}\right\}\,.

(C.2)

Proof.

We notice that

{\mathcal{U}}^{c}=\left\{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}\Big|\,\,\exists\,Z:|Z|=1,\Bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{X_{j}-\mu}{Z+\zeta\frac{j}{N}}\Bigg|>N^{2\,\delta}\right\}\,.

(C.3)

Define the following points and sets

Z_{k}=e^{2\pi i\frac{k}{N}}\,,\qquad{\mathcal{B}}_{k}=\left\{Z\in{\mathbb{C}}\;\Big||Z|=1,\,2\pi\frac{2k-1}{2N}\leq\arg\left(Z\right)<2\pi\frac{2k+1}{2N}\right\}\,,

(C.4)

then the following holds

\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{U}}^{c})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}\Big|\,\,\exists\,Z:Z\in{\mathcal{B}}_{k},\Bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{X_{j}-\mu}{Z+\zeta\frac{j}{N}}\Bigg|>N^{2\,\delta}\right\}\right)\,.

(C.5)

Since
0<\zeta<1
, there exists a constant
\nu>0
such that

\left|Z+\zeta\frac{j}{N}\right|>\nu\quad\forall\;Z\,:\;|Z|=1\,,

(C.6)

defining
S(Z)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{X_{j}-\mu}{Z+\zeta\frac{j}{N}}
, using Lipschitz inequality, one can show that for all
Z\in{\mathcal{B}}_{k}

\left|S(Z)-S(Z_{k})\right|\leq|Z-Z_{k}|\sup_{Z\in{\mathcal{B}}_{k}}|S^{\prime}(Z)|\leq 2\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}|X_{j}-\mu|}{N\nu^{2}}\leq 2\frac{\max_{j=1,\ldots,N}(|X_{j}-\mu|)}{\nu^{2}}\,.

(C.7)

Finally, by the previous proof and the Bernstein inequality (adapted to the of complex coefficients) for
|S(Z_{k})|
we conclude that

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\Bigg\{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}\Big|\,&\,\exists\,Z:\in{\mathcal{B}}_{k},\Bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{X_{j}-\mu}{Z+\zeta\frac{j}{N}}\Bigg|>N^{2\,\delta}\Bigg\}\Bigg)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}\Big|\,\,|S(Z_{k})|>\frac{N^{2\,\delta}}{2}\right\}\right)\\
&\qquad+\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}\Big|\,\,\exists\,Z:|Z|\in{\mathcal{B}}_{k}\,,\Bigg|S(Z_{k})-S(Z)\Bigg|>\frac{N^{2\,\delta}}{2}\right\}\right)\\
&\leq\frac{e^{-cN^{2\varepsilon}}}{2}+\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}\Big|\,\,\max(|X_{j}-\mu|)>\frac{\nu N^{2\,\delta}}{4}\right\}\right)\\
&\leq e^{-cN^{2\varepsilon}}\end{split}

(C.8)

where in the last inequality can be deduced by arguing as in Lemma
4.2
. Therefore, by using the previous estimate in (
C.5
) we conclude.
∎
