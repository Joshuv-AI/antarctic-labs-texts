Rare “double tragedy” and successful salvage strategy after failed bilateral humeral fracture fixation in a resource-constrained setting: a case report
Authors: Stephen Adesope Adesina, Isaac Olusayo Amole, Adepeju Olatayo Adegoke, Oluwafemi Oyewole Oyewusi, Chidiebube Enyeremchi Ukejianya, Ibukun Adewumi Okunade, Imri Goodness Adefokun, Adewumi Ojeniyi Durodola, Simeon Ayorinde Ojo, Innocent Chiedu Ikem, Samuel Uwale Eyesan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBilateral humeral shaft fractures are rare injuries that present significant management challenges, particularly in resource-constrained settings. This case report highlights the complexities of treating bilateral humeral shaft fractures in a 40-year-old male patient from southwestern Nigeria, whose treatment was initially complicated by failed plate osteosyntheses. The objectives of this report are (1) to illustrate the unusual injury patterns that result from inadequate transportation infrastructure in developing countries, (2) to highlight the challenges patients and surgeons face in managing fractures in resource-constrained environments, and (3) to emphasize the critical role humanitarian organizations play in enhancing orthopedic care and achieving positive patient outcomes in these settings.Case presentationA 40-year-old male patient, the primary earner for his family, presented to our facility three months after a road traffic crash with bilateral upper limb dysfunction. While driving with his seatbelt fastened, a trailer truck struck his car on the driver’s side, resulting in bilateral closed humeral shaft fractures, a mild traumatic brain injury, and minor skin abrasions. Initial plate osteosynthesis at a private peripheral hospital failed, resulting in bilateral nonunions. Upon presentation at our facility, the patient had normal vital signs and preserved neurovascular function, but limited upper-limb mobility. He underwent staged treatment, which involved removal of the plates, Surgical Implant Generation Network (SIGN) nail (Richland, WA, USA) fixation, and cancellous autografting. Despite financial constraints and infection complications, both fractures achieved union and functional recovery after two years.ConclusionsThis case underscores the challenges of managing complex orthopaedic trauma in low-resource settings, where inadequate healthcare infrastructure, limited access to suitable implants, and insufficient health insurance coverage exacerbate treatment difficulties. The successful outcome in this case was achieved through staged treatment, collaboration with the patient, and the use of donated SIGN nails. The report emphasizes the necessity for innovative solutions, collaborative efforts, and policy reforms to enhance fracture management outcomes and prevent long-term disabilities in resource-constrained settings. The role of humanitarian organizations in bridging the gap in orthopaedic care is also highlighted, demonstrating the potential for positive outcomes despite significant challenges.

Background

Bilateral humeral shaft fractures represent a rare occurrence in healthy adults, with very few documented cases [1–4]. This injury pattern becomes even more exceptional when complicated by bilateral failure of osteosynthesis and non-union. A comprehensive review of the peer-reviewed literature found no previously reported cases with similar complications in well-established, indexed journals. Traditionally, humeral shaft fractures are treated non-surgically with braces. However, there is a growing trend towards operative management, including open reduction internal fixation (ORIF) with plate and screws, minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis, and intramedullary nailing [5, 6]. This shift is attributed to the high nonunion rates associated with nonsurgical treatment [6–8] and the need to transition to surgical treatment in cases of nonunion, delayed union, or inability to tolerate or accept the brace [5]. Specific indications for surgical treatment include concomitant forearm or upper extremity fractures, polytrauma, open fractures, and bilateral fractures [5, 9].

Surgical intervention for acute humeral shaft fractures has nonunion rates ranging from 4% to 10% [5, 10]. Humeral shaft nonunion following a previous surgery can be categorised as either aseptic or septic/possibly septic, with causes including biological, metabolic, and mechanical factors, as well as infections [5, 11]. Treating humeral shaft nonunion involves various surgical techniques, but no consensus exists on a single optimal approach for all cases. Common methods include ORIF with plates, intramedullary fixation, cortical strut augmentation, and external fixation. However, ORIF with plates and autologous bone grafting is the most widely recommended treatment approach among these options [5, 12]. Nevertheless, recent literature questions the value of bone grafting for aseptic humeral shaft nonunions [13], with some authors raising concerns about donor site morbidity [14]. This has driven the development of modern alternatives, such as the Reamer-Irrigator-Aspirator (RIA) system. This system has shown promise as a safe and effective source of autologous bone graft [15].

Regardless of the cause, humeral shaft nonunion is a debilitating condition characterized by persistent pain, prolonged functional recovery, and diminished quality of life [5, 16]. The presence of humeral shaft nonunion significantly impairs daily functioning, leading to substantial disability and loss of autonomy. Therefore, prompt diagnosis and timely intervention are essential to mitigate these consequences [5]. Nevertheless, a thorough diagnostic workup is necessary to identify the underlying causes of nonunion, allowing for targeted optimization of modifiable factors before surgical intervention [5, 8]. This approach enhances the likelihood of successful union, minimizing complications and improving patient outcomes [5].

We report a case of a 40-year-old male presenting with bilateral humeral shaft fractures complicated by failed plate osteosyntheses following initial surgical intervention at an external facility. This case study aims to: (i) illustrate the unusual injury patterns associated with inadequate transportation infrastructure in developing nations, (ii) highlight the fracture management challenges faced by patients and surgeons in resource-constrained environments, and (iii) emphasize the critical role and positive outcomes of humanitarian organizations in bridging these gaps in orthopedic care. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient to publish this case report and accompanying images.

Our hospital is a mission teaching facility in a semi-urban city in southwestern Nigeria. It offers subsidized surgical and non-surgical treatments to a large number of trauma patients with varying injuries daily. As a referral center it serves patients from nearby health facilities, villages, and towns. Most of our patients are low- to middle-income individuals, such as artisans, transportation workers, government employees, small-scale agricultural workers, and traders. Most of them do not have health insurance coverage, except for some civil servants with limited coverage for specific surgical conditions. Except for the donated Surgical Implant Generation Network (SIGN) implants (Richland, WA, USA) and occasional operating room supplies and equipment from international donors, our hospital does not receive external financial support or grants to offset patient care expenses. As a result, patients are responsible for settling hospital bills, including medication, non-donated surgical supplies, bed fees, and subsidized surgical and rehabilitative procedure charges. Emergency packs are available for resuscitation and acute injury management, but partial payment of bills is required before elective surgical procedures.

Case presentation

A 40-year-old right-hand dominant male presented to our facility with bilateral upper limb dysfunction following a road traffic crash three months prior. The patient was driving a car with his seat belt fastened when a trailer truck struck it on the driver’s side. The collision resulted in bilateral closed humeral shaft fractures, a mild traumatic brain injury, and minor skin abrasions. The sole passenger, who was seated beside the patient, suffered a non-fatal blunt chest injury. Four days post-injury, the patient underwent surgical fixation with a T-buttress plate on the right and a narrow direct compression plate (DCP) on the left at a private peripheral hospital. Four weeks post-operatively, he started to experience pain in both arms, which worsened over time, and pus began to drain from the left arm. When he complained at the hospital, he was told that the surgeon had come on invitation from a government hospital only to operate, and he was given oral antibiotics and painkillers.

Following a neighbor’s advice, the patient came to our facility with pain, deformity, and limited mobility in both arms. Physical examination revealed normal vital signs, preserved neurovascular function, and healed longitudinal scars on both arms. Muscle power was 3/5 in the left arm and 4/5 in the right arm. The right arm had no wounds, discharging sinuses or differential warmth but was angulated. The left arm was swollen, angulated, and had a discharging sinus at the proximal end of the scar. Plain radiographs demonstrated a broken T-buttress plate on the right over an intact wedge diaphyseal fracture (AO/OTA 12B2) at the distal third (Fig. 1). A failed DCP was observed over a simple oblique diaphyseal fracture (AO/OTA 12A2) at the middle third on the left (Fig. 2). Laboratory results indicated elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) (99 mm/hour), white cell count of 8.1 × 10^9/L and fasting blood glucose of 84 mg/dl.

The patient had the infected plate on his left side removed. He was pleased to find that the sinus had healed. Four months later, he returned and requested that the left humeral shaft nonunion be repaired, despite being cautioned about the high risk of infection. We proceeded with antegrade SIGN nailing, but it became infected, necessitating nail removal. Due to financial constraints resulting from the inability to work, depletion of savings, and lack of health insurance, he could not return for further treatment until 12 months later. He then underwent one-stage removal of the broken right plate, fixation with retrograde SIGN fin nail, and cancellous autologous bone grafting. For retrograde nailing of the right humerus, a longitudinal incision was made from the tip of the olecranon to a point 8 cm proximal, granting access to the distal humerus. The nail entry point was located at the superior border of the olecranon fossa, where an elliptical entry portal was created. This was accomplished by making several small drill holes perpendicular to the cortical surface, allowing for a controlled entry. A hand reamer was then used to access the medullary canal, minimizing any impact on bone strength while avoiding entry into the elbow joint to prevent periarticular scarring. The fracture progressed to union, and functional recovery was achieved within three months (Fig. 1). Two years later, the patient returned for treatment of the left humeral shaft nonunion. The discharge from his left arm had stopped, and the infection markers were within normal limits. SIGN IM nailing and autologous bone grafting resulted in radiographic union and excellent functional recovery (Fig. 2). The third-month University of California-Los Angeles (UCLA) scores of 31 and 29, respectively, for the right and left limbs improved to 35 for both limbs at subsequent follow-ups.

Discussion

In Nigeria, it is common for patients to undergo surgery in small private peripheral hospitals with limited resources, performed by surgeons who also work in larger government hospitals. The subsequent post-operative and follow-up care is then left to the often non-surgeon hospital owners who may lack the skills to manage complications. Factors driving this practice include patients’ reluctance to wait on long waiting lists in government hospitals, the opportunity to negotiate care costs directly with the surgeon (which is not possible in government facilities), the desire for privacy not available in government hospitals, the potential for surgeons to earn supplemental income, and more.

Multiple challenges hinder the successful surgical treatment of fractures in low-resource settings, even when there are clear indications for surgery. These challenges include:

Inadequate healthcare infrastructure and safe surgical facilities [17], necessitating extensive travel for basic diagnostic procedures (e.g., C-reactive protein test and CT scans) and increasing infection rates.Limited access to suitable implants, compelling off-label use of available ones [18] (e.g., T-buttress plating and retrograde SIGN nailing for humeral shaft fracture).Bureaucratic delays in government-run facilities, prompting patients and surgeons to transfer care to poorly equipped private centers where timely treatment is prioritized over optimal resources [19, 20].Insufficient comprehensive health insurance and social safety nets, resulting in catastrophic out-of-pocket healthcare expenditures, exacerbating poverty [21].

However, the increasing dissatisfaction among patients with non-operative treatment and their desire for a quick return to function and work to alleviate poverty has led to a focus on surgically fixing fractures in active working-age individuals in developing countries [22, 23]. Healthcare providers in these settings face the challenge of addressing the patient’s goals for early recovery while minimizing the complications associated with surgical intervention. To support this cause, certain Western organizations have intervened to train doctors in trauma care, develop health infrastructure, and provide implants and equipment to numerous centers in developing countries [24–28]. Our center, for instance, relies on charitable donations of SIGN nails from SIGN Fracture Care International (Richland, WA, USA) to treat long-bone fractures. These nails enable effective treatment without requiring advanced equipment and are the only modern implants available to patients at no cost [29]. Patients needing different implants must purchase them from distant locations at significant expense.

Our 40-year-old male patient was the primary earner for his family, working as an automobile mechanic and earning a daily income from manual labour. He was involved in a high-energy collision when a trailer truck hit his car, resulting in direct impact and subsequent force that likely contributed to the bilateral humeral shaft fractures. While his bilateral humeral shaft fractures were a clear indication for surgical intervention [5, 9], resource constraints led to the failure of the initial plate osteosyntheses. Pressed by the need to return to work and support his family, the patient insisted on a re-operation on the left humeral shaft nonunion four months after the infected plate was removed. At that time, there was no convincing evidence of complete resolution of the infection, as he only had a normal complete blood count (CBC) and ESR, but no C-reactive protein (CRP) or culture. The surgery was performed without adequate access to effective anti-infective treatment, such as local antibiotics delivery through polymethylmethacrylate beads [8]. Additionally, the shortest SIGN nail available at our center at the time was too long, which caused distraction at the fracture site and ultimately led to fixation failure. This experience prompted us to use a shorter SIGN nail in an off-label, retrograde orientation to stabilize the right humeral shaft fracture successfully. (SIGN nails are approved for antegrade fixation of humeral shaft fractures.) Off-label use of implants is common in resource-limited settings [18].

In cases of nonunion or delayed union, it is recommended to conduct a thorough investigation to identify the causes so that these factors can be optimized before surgical intervention. In patients like ours, whose fractures were initially managed surgically, an underlying infection is a major concern. Recommended investigations include a CBC, ESR, CRP and cultures to guide the surgical technique [5, 30]. While diagnostic tests are suitable, they may not always be accessible or affordable in low-resource settings. For example, only CBC and ESR were available in our city, and patients had to travel for CRP testing. Moreover, our patient initially had his comminuted right HSF treated with a T-buttress plate. Fracture comminution, open fracture, and inadequate fixation (e.g., fracture not fixed using a standard 4.5 mm DCP) were identified by Maresca et al. as common features among humeral shaft nonunions that were originally treated with ORIF [31].

A single-stage surgery that includes hardware removal, debridement of the nonunion site, and revision ORIF with autologous bone grafting is the common approach for treating aseptic nonunion. In contrast, staged reconstruction is recommended for septic nonunion. This method involves hardware removal, multiple debridements, thorough irrigation of the nonunion site, tissue sampling for culture, placement of an antibiotic cement spacer, and the administration of intravenous antibiotics. The definitive fixation is then performed after the infection has resolved [5, 8, 30]. However, the index patient’s case demonstrates that managing injuries or complications that require staged or multiple surgeries can be challenging, especially for young working-age patients in our setting. This is due to limited resources, cultural barriers, and the desire to return to work early. Healthcare providers in resource-limited settings must carefully consider patient expectations and the risks of complications when deciding whether to proceed with surgical intervention [5].

The use of autogenous bone graft for humeral nonunions is debated, with recent literature suggesting that routine use may be unnecessary for many cases, particularly aseptic nonunions without significant bone loss, nonunions following primary non-operative management, and nonunion sites with good biological healing potential, where compression plating alone can yield comparable union rates [13, 14]. Given our patient’s initial operative treatment and subsequent development of septic nonunion, autografting from the proximal tibia was performed, and the donor site healed uneventfully [32]. Bone grafting remains valuable for specific situations, including significant bone loss and septic nonunions [33]. While modern technologies for collecting large volumes of bone graft with minimal complications are being developed, they are not yet routinely available in low-resource settings [15].

Conclusions

The case highlights the complexities of managing bilateral humeral shaft fractures in a low-resource setting, where the patient’s initial plate osteosyntheses failed due to resource constraints, and subsequent re-operation was complicated by inadequate access to effective anti-infective treatment and implant limitations. Nonetheless, the innovative use of available implants, staged treatment, and patient-centered care ultimately led to successful outcomes. The main takeaway message from this case report is that managing complex orthopedic trauma in resource-limited settings requires creative solutions and collaboration among healthcare providers, policymakers, and international organizations. Healthcare providers in these environments must balance patient expectations with the risks of complications while prioritizing strategies that optimize resource use and improve fracture management outcomes. By doing so, we can address the gaps in orthopedic care in low-resource settings, reduce long-term disability, and enhance the quality of life for patients with complex orthopedic injuries.
