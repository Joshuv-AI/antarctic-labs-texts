Enhancing spatial domain detection in spatial transcriptomics with EnSDD
Authors: Hui-Sheng Li, Yu-Ting Tan, Xiao-Fei Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvancements in spatial transcriptomics have transformed our understanding of organ function and tissue microenvironment. However, accurately identifying spatial domains to depict genome heterogeneity and cellular interactions remains a challenge. In this study, we propose EnSDD (Ensemble-learning for Spatial Domain Detection), a method that ingeniously integrates eight state-of-the-art spatial domain detection methods to automatically identify spatial domains. A key innovation of EnSDD is its dynamic weighting mechanism within the ensemble learning process, which optimizes the contribution of each base model and provides a performance evaluation metric without the need for ground truth data. By leveraging the spatial domains identified through EnSDD, we incorporate the detection of domain-specific spatially variable genes and the spatial distribution of cell types, thereby providing deeper insights into tissue heterogeneity. We validate EnSDD across diverse spatial transcriptomics datasets from various tissue organizational structures. Our results demonstrate that EnSDD significantly enhances spatial domain identification accuracy, identifies genes with spatial expression patterns, and reveals domain-specific cell type enrichment patterns, offering invaluable insights into tissue spatial heterogeneity and regionalization.Subject terms: Computational models, Statistical methods

Introduction

The intricate spatial organization of diverse cell types within tissues is crucial for understanding their biological functions and pathological states. Recent advancements in spatially resolved transcriptomics (SRT), such as 10x Visium, have significantly enhanced our ability to map tissue architecture and cellular interactions at the transcriptomic level through in situ sequencing1–4. A critical aspect of analyzing spatial transcriptomics data is accurately assigning spots to spatial domains characterized by coherence in gene expression and histology. This precise delineation of regions within complex tissues is vital for downstream analyses, including the identification of spatially variable genes (SVGs)5, the dissection of tissue heterogeneity6, and the exploration of cell-cell interactions7.

Current methods for spatial domain detection (SDD) can be broadly classified into non-spatial and spatial clustering approaches. Traditional non-spatial clustering algorithms, such as K-means and Louvain8, use gene expression data alone, often resulting in clusters that lack spatial continuity. In contrast, spatial clustering methods, including BayesSpace9, DR-SC10, GraphST11, and STAGATE12, incorporate spatial coordinates from SRT data to account for the spatial dependencies of gene expression. These methods cluster neighboring spots based on both gene expression and spatial coordinates, leveraging various strategies for spatial neighbor similarity. While these spatial clustering approaches have demonstrated significant improvements in identifying spatial domains in brain and cancer tissues12, they often overlook the morphological information provided by histology images, which can lead to spatial domains that do not align well with histological features13. To address this limitation, methods such as SpaGCN13, stLearn14, SiGra15, and spaVAE16 integrate gene expression data, spatial coordinates, and morphological images to learn the spatial dependencies between adjacent spots and detect contiguous spatial domains in both gene expression and histology.

The diversity of spatial clustering methods, each with unique assumptions and data requirements, results in varied outcomes when delineating spatial domains17. The effectiveness of these methods is influenced by the complexity of the tissue under investigation18, making it challenging to select the most appropriate method for new SRT datasets. One strategy to address this challenge is the development of ensemble methods that integrate results from multiple base methods19. However, ensemble methods face two main challenges: (i) differing base methods may produce varying numbers and types of spatial domains, complicating the integration of their results, and (ii) the performance of methods can vary across datasets, making it difficult to automatically assess and incorporate method performance into the ensemble process.

To address the challenges associated with selecting the most appropriate method for new SRT data and seamlessly integrating results from multiple base methods, we introduce EnSDD (Ensemble-learning for Spatial Domain Detection). EnSDD offers several advantages: (i) EnSDD adaptively assigns weights to similarity matrices generated by base methods, learning the complete similarity between spots; (ii) the weights assigned by EnSDD provide a reliable metric for evaluating the performance of base SDD methods without requiring ground truth; (iii) EnSDD offers a comprehensive framework for exploring tissue spatial organization, including the identification of spatial domains, determination of domain-specific SVGs, and inference of cell type distributions; (iv) it includes well-documented tutorials and an interactive platform to facilitate implementation and visualization.

We extensively evaluate EnSDD on various human and mouse tissue datasets generated by 10x Visium, including the human brain, mouse brain, human breast cancer, human prostate cancer, and human ovarian cancer. Our results demonstrate that EnSDD outperforms base methods in identifying spatial domains in brain tissues and effectively delineates the intratumoral and peritumoral microenvironments in cancer tissues. By leveraging spatial domains identified by EnSDD, we investigate the tumor-immune microenvironment in invasive ductal carcinoma, explore intratumoral heterogeneity in human breast cancer, and identify tumor ecotones—regions of increased tumor-immune diversity at tumor-normal boundaries—in human prostate and ovarian tissues. This capability enables detailed exploration of the peritumoral microenvironment and may guide novel cancer treatment strategies.

Results

Overview of EnSDD with the architecture analyses

An overview of the EnSDD method is depicted in Fig. 1. EnSDD leverages a comprehensive approach to SDD by integrating multiple advanced SDD methods. The input data for EnSDD includes gene expression profiles, spatial coordinates of spots, and paired morphology images (Fig. 1A). The core of EnSDD’s approach is the fusion of eight cutting-edge SDD methods: BayesSpace9, DR-SC10, GraphST11, STAGATE12, SpaGCN13, stLearn14, SiGra15, and spaVAE16 (Fig. 1B). EnSDD first executes these eight methods to obtain multiple clustering results. Subsequently, binary similarity matrices are constructed for each clustering result, representing pairs of spots within the same cluster. Leveraging these matrices, EnSDD employs an optimization model to derive a consensus similarity matrix for spots. This model simultaneously determines the final spatial similarity matrix between spots and the adaptive weights assigned to each base method. Notably, EnSDD adaptively assigns weights to each base method, and these adaptive weights could be used as a metric to quantify the performance of base methods when applied to a specific dataset. Finally, EnSDD applies the Louvain algorithm with adaptive resolution to identify spatial domains based on the consensus similarity matrix of spots.

While the primary goal of EnSDD is to detect spatial domains, it also advances the exploration of tissue spatial heterogeneity by integrating various existing methods (Fig. 1C). To comprehend how tissue regions form and function, it is crucial to investigate the SVGs that are enriched in specific spatial domains, as those links molecular functions with regional phenotypes. EnSDD offers two approaches for identifying SVGs: one involves detecting domain-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs), while the other identifies genes with expression patterns correlated to spatial locations. In addition, EnSDD incorporates cell type composition inference into its framework, providing a more detailed and accurate depiction of tissue complexity and heterogeneity. For easier implementation and visualization, EnSDD provides an R package with well-documented tutorials and a user-friendly interactive platform (Fig. 1D).

Application to DLPFC 10x Visium data

To assess the spatial clustering performance of EnSDD comprehensively, we initially applied it to the DLPFC dataset comprising 12 human DLPFC sections. These sections have been manually annotated by Maynard et al.20, providing ground truth labels for DLPFC layers and white matter (WM) based on morphological features and gene markers. We compare the clustering accuracy of EnSDD with the eight base spatial clustering methods in terms of the adjusted Rand Index (ARI)21 and purity score (Pur)22. Each base method is applied with its default parameter settings. EnSDD is then applied to obtain ensemble clustering results. In this application, we set the number of domains to match the annotated tissue slices.

The results demonstrate that EnSDD achieves top performance across most DLPFC slices. For instance, in slice 151,674 (Fig. 2A), EnSDD accurately delineates layer borders and achieves the highest clustering accuracy (ARI = 0.637, Pur = 0.753). The visual comparison reveals that some base methods, particularly DR-SC, fail to recover distinct layer clusters, with clusters mixed among different layers. While certain methods like SpaGCN, spaVAE, and stLearn exhibit partial success in identifying specific layers, they also struggle with accuracy. STAGATE generates layers that more closely match the annotation, but the layer thickness is incorrect, particularly in the WM layer (Fig. 2C). In contrast, EnSDD consistently outperforms individual SDD methods across various domains, aligning better with known annotation (Fig. 2A). Additionally, EnSDD assigns higher weights to more accurate methods, with a strong positive correlation between weights and ARI values (Fig. 2B), demonstrating its ability to effectively integrate and optimize base models.

However, a discrepancy is observed between EnSDD results and manual annotation in slice 151,669 (ARI = 0.273, Pur = 0.501). EnSDD tends to separate Layer 3 into subregions 1 and 4 (Fig. 2D, left and middle), a distinction supported by morphological differences within Layer 3 (Fig. 2D, right). Other methods like SiGra, SpaGCN, spaVAE, and stLearn, which leverage histology information, also divide Layer 3 into two regions (Supplementary Fig. S1A). This suggests the presence of multiple subregions within Layer 3. Gene expression analysis further confirms this, revealing distinct expression patterns between subregions 1 and 4 (Fig. 2E). Additionally, differential expression analysis identifies 129 DEGs between subregions 1 and 4, which are enriched for functions related to location maintenance, regulation of lipid localization, and the metabolic processes of triglycerides, neutral lipids, and acylglycerols (Supplementary Fig. S1B and C). Notably, several DEGs have reported roles in neural functions and thyroid carcinomas23,24, underscoring their potential significance. Local spatial autocorrelation analysis (Fig. 2F) further supports spatial segregation, demonstrating EnSDD’s ability to detect finer spatial variations within layers.

Considering the overall performance across all DLPFC slices except for slice 151,669, EnSDD achieves the highest median ARI (0.555) and purity score (0.704), outperforming STAGATE (ARI = 0.552, Pur = 0.678) and spaVAE (ARI = 0.553, Pur = 0.672) (Fig. 2G and Supplementary Fig. S2). STAGATE, however, shows significant variability across slices, as reflected in the wide range of ARI scores, whereas EnSDD exhibits more consistent performance. Although spaVAE’s ARI values are comparable to EnSDD, it has a lower purity score. These findings suggest EnSDD’s robustness and superior performance in detecting spatial domains within the DLPFC dataset. Furthermore, we observe a strong positive correlation between the weights assigned to the base SDD methods and their ARI values across different slices (Supplementary Figs. S3–S5). Considering all slices collectively, the strong positive correlation between ARI scores and assigned weights (R = 0.618, P value = 2.01e−11) (Supplementary Fig. S5D) underscores the effectiveness of EnSDD’s dynamic weighting mechanism as a reliable metric for evaluating base methods in the absence of ground truth.

Application to mouse brain data

To assess the effectiveness of EnSDD in identifying spatial domains within tissues with intricate structures, we apply it to mouse brain datasets characterized by complex spatial arrangements. Given the absence of a definitive gold standard annotation for these datasets, we compared EnSDD with base SDD methods by generating domain segmentations with varying numbers of clusters. For a fair comparison, we cluster the mouse coronal slice into 10, 16, and 17 clusters; the sagittal anterior slice into 10, 13, and 15 clusters; and the sagittal posterior slice into 10, 17, and 20 clusters. The Allen Brain Atlas serves as a silver standard for reference (Fig. 3A, B)25.

We first assess EnSDD’s performance using the mouse brain coronal slice, comparing the identified spatial domains with the anatomical reference from the Allen Brain Atlas (Fig. 3A). This section primarily includes key regions such as the cerebral cortex (CTX), hippocampus (HPF), cerebellum (CB), thalamus (TH), and brainstem (BS). Within the CTX, the isocortex displays a consistent six-layered structure, while the HPF features two prominent structures: the pyramidal layer of cornu ammonis (CA) and the granule cell layer of the dentate gyrus (DG). EnSDD successfully detects the CA and DG sections of the HPF with 10 clusters (Fig. 3C). As the clustering number increases, EnSDD identifies new spatial domains while maintaining the integrity of existing ones (Supplementary Fig. S6). For instance, domain 6, initially identified with 10 clusters, subdivides into domains 8 and 14 when using 16 clusters, delineating two slice layers within the isocortex of the CTX. Notably, the spatial domain structure remains consistent as the number of clusters increases from 16 to 17, highlighting EnSDD’s stability in handling complex tissue structures. In contrast, base clustering methods such as BayesSpace, spaVAE, and stLearn struggle to consistently recognize the DG sections across various clustering settings. Although methods like DR-SC, GraphST, SiGra, SpaGCN, and STAGATE maintain regional continuity, they fail to accurately capture boundary layers within the isocortex (Supplementary Fig. S7). EnSDD, however, effectively delineates these boundaries (Fig. 3C).

Next, we apply EnSDD to the sagittal anterior tissue of the mouse brain, where the somatosensory areas (SS) within the CTX exhibit a discernible six-layered structure (Fig. 3B). With a clustering number of 10, EnSDD initially identifies SS as spatial domains 3, 7, and 9. Increasing the number of clusters to 13 and 15 allowed EnSDD to further differentiate specific layers, including Layer 2/3, Layer 5, Layer 6a, and Layer 6b (Fig. 3D). Additionally, domain 6 (with 10 clusters) is subdivided into domains 7 and 8 (with 13 clusters), corresponding to the nucleus accumbens (ACB) and caudoputamen (CP). These findings align with the high expression of known domain-specific marker genes (Cux2, Lamp5, Pde1a, Nptx1, and Nxph3) (Fig. 3F)26. However, base methods like BayesSpace and stLearn exhibit limitations: BayesSpace overly subdivides Layer 2/3, while stLearn produces excessively smooth cluster boundaries, diminishing its ability to capture subtle cortical structures. BayesSpace and SpaGCN also generate disjointed spatial domains at certain resolutions (13/15 clusters) (Supplementary Fig. S9). In contrast, methods like SiGra, spaVAE, and EnSDD (with 15 clusters) clearly delineate the six-layered structure within the CTX.

In the mouse sagittal posterior dataset, EnSDD successfully delineates the boundaries of HPF, CTX and midbrain (MB) with 10 clusters. As the number of clusters increases, spatial domain 8 (with 10 clusters) is subdivided into domains 10 and 13 (with 17 clusters), where domain 10 corresponds to visual areas (VIS) in the sagittal posterior slice (Fig. 3B, E and Supplementary Fig. S10). EnSDD also identifies the CA1 and DG areas in HPF with 17 and 20 clusters. The expression patterns of several specific marker genes further validate these cluster partitions (Fig. 3G). However, EnSDD tends to include more spots in the Purkinje cell layer rather than defining it as a single spot layer within the cerebellar cortex (Fig. 3E), which might be due to the limitations of current methods in distinguishing Purkinje cells. Among the base methods, BayesSpace and stLearn fail to generate contiguous regions across different clustering settings. DR-SC and SiGra subdivide the dentate gyrus area into more subgroups at higher cluster numbers, while SpaGCN fails to accurately depict the VIS boundary with 10 and 17 clusters (Supplementary Fig. S11).

Overall, the spatial visualizations obtained from EnSDD and the eight base SDD methods confirm the effectiveness of EnSDD in decoding complex spatial domains in mouse brain samples. EnSDD consistently demonstrates superior performance in accurately delineating key brain structures, particularly in regions with intricate spatial arrangements.

Application to breast cancer tissue data

Intratumoral heterogeneity in cancer poses significant challenges for effective treatment and is associated with poor survival outcomes27. Spatial transcriptomics provides valuable insights into tumor complexity and the interactions between tumor and immune responses28. To demonstrate the generalizability of EnSDD to cancer tissues, we apply it to a human breast cancer dataset. The data is manually annotated by a pathologist based on the H&E image and the spatial expression of reported breast cancer marker genes29. The H&E image is segmented into four main morphotypes: ductal carcinoma in situ/lobular carcinoma in situ (DCIS/LCIS), healthy tissue (Healthy), invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), and tumor surrounding regions with low features of malignancy (Tumor edge) and the four main morphotypes are further divided into 20 subregions (Fig. 4A, right). Unlike the well-defined boundaries in DLPFC and mouse brain tissues, human breast cancer tissues are highly heterogeneous with complex microenvironments. Although manual annotations are provided, due to the heterogeneity of tumor tissue, we cluster the data with multiple clusters (10, 16, 17, 20, and 25) and compared spatial domains identified by various SDD methods at different clustering resolutions.

Visually, EnSDD effectively distinguishes DCIS/LCIS, healthy tissue, IDC, and tumor edge regions with cluster number 10 (Fig. 4A, B). Specifically, domain 1 encompasses tumor edges 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6, while domain 6 includes DCIS/LCIS 1, 2, and 5. Similarly, domains 2, 4, 5, and 7 correspond to IDC 4, 2, 8, and 5. As the cluster numbers increase, for example, from 10 to 16 clusters, domain 1 is further subdivided into domains 1 and 6, corresponding to tumor edges 6 and 2, respectively. EnSDD demonstrates an improved ability to reveal intricate details of intratumoral heterogeneity with increasing cluster numbers, as demonstrated by the identification of specific domains 13, 15, and 17 with 25 clusters, which corresponded to IDC_5 in manual annotation (Fig. 4B, C). Compared to base SDD methods, EnSDD identifies regions with greater regional continuity and reduced noise upon visual inspection (Fig. 4B). Notably, EnSDD demonstrates an ability to identify additional sub-clusters within tumor regions, whereas other methods tend to subdivide Healthy_1 region into sub-clusters, despite all methods generating the same number of clusters (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. S12). For a quantitative assessment, we compute the ARI and purity scores based on 20-cluster results compared to manual annotation. EnSDD achieves the highest ARI of 0.667 and a purity score of 0.668, surpassing other methods (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S12D).

To delve deeper into the intratumoral heterogeneity of human breast cancer, we focus on EnSDD domains 13, 15, and 17 in the IDC_5 region with 25 clusters (Fig. 4D). Differential expression analysis using the Wilcoxon test identifies 311 DEGs in subregion 13, 192 DEGs in subregion 15, and 270 DEGs in subregion 17 (P value ≤ 0.05; adjusted P value ≤ 0.1), compared to other regions. Among the top DEGs with smaller adjusted P values in different subregions, we observe that the DEGs expression patterns are consistent with the boundaries of subregions 13, 15, and 17 (Fig. 4E). Utilizing Local Getis and Ord’s Gi, the following significant local spatial autocorrelation (SA) patterns are observed: CSTA, FAM234B, and HEBP1 exhibit significant positive local SA in subregion 13; CLDN3, GPX4, and MGP show significant positive local SA in subregion 15; GRB14, MAOB, and TEX14 display significant negative local SA in subregion 17 (Supplementary Fig. S13). Notably, these DEGs are linked to IDC progression, with genes such as HEBP1 (adjusted P value = 1.64e−46) being reported to be associated with the development of the ERα + breast tumors30; FAM234B (adjusted P value = 1.89e−43) is linked to poor prognosis in patients with Luminal breast cancer31; CSTA (adjusted P value = 5.71e−38) is involved in regulating the progression of ductal carcinoma in situ to invasive breast cancer32. In addition, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) reveals that the DEGs are most enriched in biological functions associated with immune activation and response, highlighting the complexity of tumor-immune microenvironment in invasive ductal carcinoma of human breast cancer (Supplementary Fig. S14)28,33.

To further explore the tumor-immune microenvironment of IDC, we employ RCTD34 to predict the cell type composition of spots in subregions 13, 15, and 17 of the IDC_5 region using scRNA-seq human breast tissue as a reference (Fig. 4F)28. The predicted cell type proportion distributions reveal significant differences among subregions 13, 15, and 17 for cancer epithelial cells, macrophage, and dendritic cells (DCs) (Fig. 4G). To be specific, the proportion of cancer epithelial cells is higher in subregion 13 (central tumor) than in subregions 15 and 17 (surrounding the central tumor), while the proportions of macrophage and DCs in subregion 15 and 17 are significantly higher than those in subregion 13. The elevated proportion of macrophages and DCs in the peritumoral area suggests a potential role in cancer cell migration and invasion35,36. Additionally, CD4+ T cells, crucial for regulating immune responses and influencing breast cancer survival outcomes37,38, show slight differences in abundance between subregions 13 and 15 (Fig. 4G). The increased CD4+ T cell presence at the tumor periphery may be associated with a more favorable prognosis due to its role in anti-tumor immunity.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that EnSDD can identify finer regions with distinct biological functions, uncovering intratumoral heterogeneity within visually homogeneous tumor regions and providing insights into the tumor-immune microenvironment.

Applications to prostate and ovarian cancer tissue data

The edge effect in cancer, characterized by increased diversity at the boundaries between tumor and normal tissues—known as ecotones—plays a crucial role in tumor progression and treatment response39. Spatial transcriptomics allows for detailed mapping of these cancer ecotones, offering insights into the peritumoral microenvironment and aiding in the development of innovative treatment strategies. To demonstrate the effectiveness of EnSDD in delineating cancer ecotones, we apply the method to human prostate and ovarian cancer datasets. These datasets are initially categorized by pathologists into tumor and stroma regions40, necessitating the use of both EnSDD and base SDD methods to identify spatial domains at various cluster resolutions. For a fair comparison, we cluster human prostate cancer tissue into 3, 5, and 8 clusters, and human ovarian cancer tissue into 2, 5, and 8 clusters.

We first evaluate EnSDD’s performance on human prostate cancer tissue by comparing its identified spatial domains with manual annotation (Fig. 5A). EnSDD accurately maps tumor regions across different cluster numbers. As the number of clusters increases, EnSDD refines domain 3 (initially with 3 clusters) into domains 4 and 5 (now with 5 clusters), where domain 4 corresponds to stroma and partially atrophic changes, and domain 5 aligns with the stroma region identified in the manual annotation. When increasing to 8 clusters, domain 2 is further divided into domains 3 and 5 (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S15). EnSDD effectively distinguishes between the tumor and surrounding regions, setting 8 clusters, where spatial domains 1, 2, 4, 5, and 7 correspond to the tumor, and domain 3 represents the tumor’s edge. In contrast, base SDD methods, including GraphST, SiGra, SpaGCN, and STAGATE, fail to maintain contiguous and stable domains with increasing clusters, while SpaVAE suffers from over-smoothing (Supplementary Fig. S16).

To further explore differences in gene expression and cell type distribution between tumor and surrounding regions, we analyze spots from domains 1, 2, 4, 5, and 7 (within the tumor) and from domain 3 (the tumor edge), as identified by EnSDD with 8 clusters (Fig. 5D, left). Wilcoxon test is introduced to identify domain-specific SVGs and characterize transcriptional differences between these regions. Subsequently, RCTD is applied to predict cell type abundance for each spot and investigate variations in cell type distributions between the tumor and its edge. Gene expression and cell type distribution analyses reveal significant differences between tumors and surrounding regions. Differential gene expression analysis using the Wilcoxon test identifies total of 4170 genes that are defined as DEGs (P value ≤ 0.05; adjusted P value ≤ 0.1). The top DEGs with smaller adjusted P values show expression patterns that align with the boundaries of the tumor and tumor edges (Fig. 5C). Local spatial autocorrelation analysis using Local Getis and Ord’s Gi reveals significant local SA for KLK3, ATG9B, and NPRL2 in the tumor region, and FLNA, TP63, and TAGLN in the tumor edge region (Supplementary Fig. S17). Notably, GSEA shows the top DEGs, such as KLK3, ATG9B, TP63, are strongly associated with human prostate cancer development41,42. Cell-type distribution analysis, using RCTD, shows significant differences between the tumor and tumor edge regions, particularly for LE-KLK3 (luminal epithelial cells marked by the tumor gene KLK3), BE (basal cells), MNP (mononuclear phagocytes), and B cells. (Fig. 5D, right). Specifically, LE-KLK3 cells are more prevalent in the tumor region, indicating the complex cellular structure and glandular changes within the tumor. Conversely, BE cells and immune cells, including MNP and B cells, are more prevalent at the tumor edge, suggesting a complex immune microenvironment and potential mechanisms for tumor-immune escape (Fig. 5E)43.

EnSDD also effectively delineates comprehensive cancer ecotones in human ovarian tumor tissue (Fig. 5F), distinguishing between tumor and stroma regions with 5 and 8 clusters. With increased clustering number, domain 2 (with 2 clusters) refines into domains 2, 3, and 4 (with 5 clusters), and domains 1 and 2 (with 5 clusters) are further split into domains 1, 6 and 2, 5, 7 (with 8 clusters) (Fig. 5H and Supplementary Fig. S18). Specifically, EnSDD identifies domains 1, 3, and 6 (with 8 clusters), where domain 1 corresponds to the stroma region, domain 3 to the tumor region, and domain 6 to the interface between the tumor and stroma, aligning with manual annotation. In comparison, base SDD methods like GraphST and SpaGCN struggle with consistent domain identification as the number of clusters increases, while methods such as SiGra, DR-SC, and BayesSpace tend to overly subdivide the stroma region, and SpaVAE suffers from over-smoothing, resulting in poorly aligned domain boundaries (Supplementary Fig. S19).

We further explore gene expression and cell type distribution among tumor, stroma, and ecotone regions by analyzing spots from domain 1 (stroma), domain 3 (tumor), and domain 6 (ecotone) identified by EnSDD with 8 clusters. The Wilcoxon test identifies 6815 DEGs in domain 1, 8014 in domain 3, and 1489 in domain 6 (P value ≤ 0.05; adjusted P value ≤ 0.1). The expression patterns of top DEGs with smaller adjusted P values align with the boundaries of these regions (Fig. 5G). Local spatial autocorrelation analysis using Local Getis and Ord’s Gi reveals significant spatial patterns (Supplementary Fig. S20). Notably, marker genes of subregion 6, such as SFRP4, IGKN, THBS1 are strongly linked to Wnt signaling, immune function, and those gene expression changes may promote human ovarian tumor growth and invasiveness44–46. The predicted cell type proportions reveal significant differences among domains 1 (stroma), 3 (tumor), and 6 (ecotone) for epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) cells, epithelial cells, mesothelial cells, and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) (Fig. 5I, right). EOC cells are more abundant in the tumor region (domain 3), consistent with manual annotation, while epithelial cells, mesothelial cells, and CAFs are more prevalent in the ecotone (domain 6), reflecting tumor-induced changes in the microenvironment and providing insights into tumor-immune interactions and their implications for treatment development47 (Fig. 5J).

In summary, our results demonstrate that EnSDD can accurately identify detailed regions within the tumor microenvironment, revealing distinct cancer ecotones and providing new insights into potential treatment strategies.

Discussion

In this study, we present EnSDD, a weighted ensemble-learning method designed to integrate results from eight advanced spatial clustering methods. This approach captures spatial similarities in SRT data with high precision. The adaptive resolution Louvain algorithm employed by EnSDD effectively delineates spatial domains, as evidenced by its superior performance on the DLPFC dataset. Our analysis shows that the weights assigned to base methods are positively correlated with their performance, indicating that these weights are a reliable metric for evaluating SDD methods in the absence of ground truth. Furthermore, EnSDD successfully identifies spatial domains in complex mouse brain tissues and provides valuable insights into tumor microenvironments in human tissues. In human breast cancer, EnSDD aligns with manual annotations and maps cell types from scRNA-seq data onto SRT data, offering insights into the tumor-immune microenvironment. In prostate and ovarian tissues, EnSDD delineates peritumoral ecotones and identifies unique features such as increased immune diversity and specific molecular markers, which are crucial for understanding tumor behavior and identifying potential therapeutic targets. These comprehensive analyses affirm EnSDD’s capability to identify continuous and biologically meaningful spatial domains in SRT data.

Determining the optimal number of clusters is crucial in single-cell analysis48, as well as for accurate SRT data analysis. Traditional clustering evaluation methods, such as silhouette scores and stability analysis49,50, may not fully capture the complexities of SRT data, which often involve intricate spatial structures. Our study demonstrates the limitations of fixed cluster numbers. For example, EnSDD identifies new subregions within the manually annotated Layer 3 in the human DLPFC dataset (Fig. 2D), highlighting the shortcomings of static clustering resolutions. Similarly, increasing the cluster number from 10 to 25 in the breast cancer dataset unveiled additional subregions (Fig. 4B, C), emphasizing the need for flexible clustering adjustments. We recommend determining cluster numbers based on tissue annotations when available, or, in their absence, using histology H&E images for guidance. Additionally, visual tools such as Sankey diagrams can facilitate the exploration of hierarchical structures and aid in selecting optimal clustering resolutions. These approaches improve SDD accuracy and offer a deeper understanding of tissue complexity. In summary, a dynamic and context-sensitive approach to clustering is essential for capturing nuanced spatial relationships in SRT data, ultimately leading to more refined analyses and interpretations. To assist users of EnSDD, we have incorporated features in our R package that support multiple cluster settings and include Sankey diagram plotting to visualize spatial domain structures effectively.

In addition to EnSDD, ensemble learning has been previously utilized to generate consensus matrices for clustering analyses of both scRNA-seq and SRT data. For example, SC3 combines multiple binary similarity matrices from different clustering results and uses hierarchical clustering to assign cell labels in scRNA-seq data51. Similarly, STAMarker computes the proportion of two spots belonging to the same spatial domain across various clustering results to create a connectivity matrix, which is then used with hierarchical clustering or the Louvain algorithm for label assignment52. These studies illustrate the effectiveness of ensemble methods in capturing sample similarities. In contrast to SC3 and STAMarker, EnSDD uniquely adapts the weights assigned to base similarities, reducing the impact of poorly performing methods and ensuring a more robust consensus matrix. Furthermore, EnSDD employs a heterogeneous ensemble of eight distinct methods, providing a more comprehensive and nuanced approach to SDD compared to STAMarker’s homogeneous reliance on STAGATE. Future research should explore the effectiveness of heterogeneous versus homogeneous ensembles to gain deeper insights into their optimal applications.

The performance of EnSDD may be influenced by the selection and number of base methods integrated into the ensemble. To explore this, we conduct experiments using DLPFC slices 151671 and 151672, examining how varying the number of base methods affects EnSDD’s performance. We systematically test all possible combinations of eight base methods—BayesSpace, DR-SC, GraphST, STAGATE, SpaGCN, stLearn, SiGra, and spaVAE—while varying the number of methods at each integration level. The results show that increasing the number of integrated base methods generally improves performance, but this enhancement tends to plateau when the number of methods exceeds a certain threshold (e.g., more than five) (Supplementary Fig. S21). Additionally, EnSDD demonstrates robustness, as removing any one of the base methods does not significantly degrade performance (when comparing scenarios with seven versus eight methods). These findings suggest that once a sufficient diversity of methods is included, adding more methods may yield diminishing returns. Therefore, future work should focus on developing novel ensemble strategies or identifying new base methods that offer distinct or complementary insights to further enhance clustering outcomes.

EnSDD presents two key avenues for future advancement. First, the current methodologies employed in EnSDD are designed for individual tissue slices, which limits its analytical capabilities to single slices. Expanding EnSDD to incorporate the analysis of multiple tissue slices has the potential to markedly improve detection accuracy and yield insights into both shared and distinct tissue structures across various slices. This enhancement would facilitate the identification of disease-related substructures and the dynamic changes in domain structures across different slices53. Future research will prioritize the development of techniques for integrating multi-slice analysis into EnSDD. Second, the integration of external reference data has been demonstrated to enhance the accuracy of SDD in spatial transcriptomics studies54,55. Future investigations will focus on incorporating these reference-based methods into the EnSDD framework, aiming to further improve its performance and robustness in identifying spatial domains.

Methods

EnSDD model

Implementation of the individual spatial clustering methods

EnSDD incorporates eight state-of-the-art SDD methods, including BayesSpace9, DR-SC10, GraphST11, STAGATE12, SpaGCN13, stLearn14, SiGra15 and spaVAE16. Each method’s features are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. We utilize the packages provided by the authors and follow their guidelines for running these spatial clustering methods. Further details are available in Supplementary Section S3.1.

Ensemble combination rule

After obtaining individual clustering results from base models, binary similarity matrices are constructed from the corresponding spot labels: if two spots belong to the same cluster, their similarity is set to 1; otherwise, it is 0. We assume that S(m)∈RN×N represents the binary similarity matrix from base method m (m = 1, …, M; M = 8 by default), where N represents the number of spots. Our goal here is to learn a consensus similarity matrix S by integrating the multiple binary similarity matrices generated by the eight base models.

As each binary matrix summarizes pairs of spots located in the same cluster, integrating multiple binary similarity matrices into a consensus matrix may enhance the learning of a more accurate similarity matrix between spots51. While a straightforward approach would be to consider each binary similarity matrix equally and use their average to construct the consensus matrix, this may lead to a biased consensus similarity matrix, especially if some base models perform poorly. Instead, we propose an optimization model to integrate these base results:

Here, the first term measures the total weighted Euclidean distance between the consensus similarity matrix S and the base binary similarity matrix S(m), where ⋅F2 represents the square of the Frobenius norm for matrices. ωm represents the weight assigned to the m-th base binary matrix. Minimizing this term with respect to S yields the consensus spot similarity matrix. The second term is a negative entropy regularization of weights, preventing overfitting of the ensemble similarity matrix to any specific base similarity matrix. Minimizing the first term and the regularization term with respect to ωm allows us to learn weights assigned to the base similarity matrices. Large weight ωm encourages the consensus similarity matrix to align with the corresponding base similarity matrix S(m), while smaller weight indicates deviation from this similarity matrix. After obtaining the estimated model parameters, EnSDD generates a consensus spot similarity matrix S by assigning appropriate weights ωm to different base similarity matrices S(m). We employ a coordinate descent algorithm to solve the optimization problem. For details, please refer to Supplementary Section S3.2. Additionally, we explore the sensitivity of the tuning parameter λ, which controls the strength of regularization, as discussed in Supplementary Section S3.3.

Spatial domain assignment

After solving the optimization model, the consensus spot similarity matrix S is employed in conjunction with the Louvain algorithm8 to partition spots into distinct spatial domains. The resolution parameter in the Louvain algorithm plays a critical role in determining the number of spatial domains: a higher resolution parameter leads to the identification of more spatial domains, while a lower value results in fewer domains being detected. With a specific number of spatial domains given by the users, it becomes essential to adaptively determine the resolution parameter in Louvain algorithm to obtain the spatial domains with a predetermined number of clusters. Inspired by refs. 56,57, we introduce binary search for the determination of resolution in Lovain algorithm. For details, please refer to Supplementary Section S3.3.

Identifying spatial domain-specific SVGs

EnSDD provides two ways to identify genes with domain-specific spatial expression patterns. In the first approach, EnSDD employs the Wilcoxon test to identify domain-specific DEGs, whose expression levels show significant differences between the selected domains and other spatial domains.

For the second approach, we investigate the presence of spatial autocorrelation (SA) in gene expressions across different locations5. Spatial autocorrelation refers to the interdependence of gene expressions at nearby locations. To assess whether the identified genes exhibit a structured spatial expression pattern within various spatial domains, we employ Local Getis and Ord’s Gi58, a univariate measure of local SA. This metric evaluates SA for each spot, providing insight into the spatial autocorrelation of gene expression around specific spots for a chosen gene5. Inferring the spatial domain-specific cell type distribution

EnSDD utilizes RCTD34 to infer cell-type compositions for SRT data. Leveraging the identified spatial domains and cell type compositions within spots, EnSDD examines the variations in cell type abundances across different detected spatial domains, further elucidating the cell type heterogeneity within the tissue. We employ the run.RCTD function available in the spacexr R package for inferring cell type abundances in the SRT data.

Interactive platform

Taking advantage of the Shiny R package, we have developed an interactive platform for the streamlined application of EnSDD. The interface of the Shiny application is depicted in Fig. 1D (right). In the left panel, users can easily upload spatial-gene expression data, spatial coordinates, and H&E images via the designated data input box. The parameters required for running EnSDD are organized into three main components: executing base SDD methods, constructing similarity matrices, and spatial domain assignment. These essential parameters are prominently displayed at the top of the interactive interface for user convenience. Once the tuning parameters are set, users can initiate the EnSDD process by clicking the “Run EnSDD” button. Upon completion, the middle panel of the interface presents scatter plots illustrating the clustering results obtained from both the base methods and EnSDD. Additionally, the elapsed time for running the base methods and EnSDD is provided for user reference. Furthermore, a table is available in the panel, where rows represent spots and columns represent spatial domains. Users can easily download this table using the “Download” button located in the upper-right panel of the interface.

Evaluating performance using SRT data

Collecting SRT data

To assess the performance of various spatial clustering methods, we utilize five spatial transcriptomic datasets and three scRNA-seq dataset. A summary of these datasets is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Human DLPFC data

The human dorsolateral pre-frontal cortex (DLPFC) spatial transcriptomics data20 generated with the 10x Visium platform serve as a common benchmark for evaluating SDD methods. This dataset comprises 12 human postmortem DLPFC tissue sections from three independent neurotypical adult donors. Each section features between 3460 and 4789 spots, capturing expression data for 33,538 genes. Manual annotation of these sections delineates five to seven regions, including the DLPFC layers (Layers 1–6) and WM. The manual annotations serve as a benchmark for assessing SDD methods applied to this dataset. The processed data is publicly available from the spatialLIBD project repository at https://github.com/LieberInstitute/spatialLIBD.

Mouse brain tissue data

To assess spatial clustering methods in datasets with more complex spatial structures, we utilize spatial transcriptomics data from mouse brain tissues, including coronal59, sagittal anterior60, and sagittal posterior61 slices generated with the 10x Visium platform. The coronal section contains 2698 spots with expression data for 31,053 genes. The sagittal posterior section includes 2825 spots with 32,285 genes, while the sagittal anterior section features 3353 spots with 31,053 genes. Brain anatomical reference annotations from the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas25 (https://mouse.brain-map.org/static/atlas) serve as the silver standard for evaluating spatial clustering methods.

Human breast cancer data

Spatial transcriptomics data for human breast cancer is generated using the 10x Visium platform, consisting of 3798 spots and expression data for 36,601 genes62. This dataset represents complex microenvironments and high heterogeneity characteristic of breast cancer. The data has been manually annotated by a pathologist using H&E images and the spatial expression profiles of known breast cancer marker genes29. This annotation serves as the silver standard in our application and is available at https://github.com/JinmiaoChenLab/SEDR_analyses/blob/master/data/BRCA1/metadata.tsv.

Human prostate cancer data

The human prostate cancer spatial transcriptomics data, also generated with the 10x Visium platform, includes 4371 spots and 33,517 genes63. This dataset has been partially annotated by a pathologist based on H&E images and the spatial expression of prostate cancer marker genes40, providing a gold standard for defining tumor and tumor edge regions used in our experiments.

Human ovarian cancer data

The human ovarian cancer data is generated using the 10x Visium platform, comprising 3455 spots and 33,862 genes64. The data have been partially annotated by a pathologist based on the H&E image and the spatial expression of reported breast cancer marker genes40, which serves as the golden standard to match the predicted spatial domain and define the tumor, stroma and ecoton regions in our experiment.

Human breast cancer scRNA-seq data

For investigating the spatial distribution of cell types in human breast cancer, we rely on corresponding scRNA-seq data sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, GSE176078)28 for cell type deconvolution. The original dataset contains 100,064 cells with 29,733 genes and includes annotation for nine major cell types, 29 minor cell types, and 49 subset cell types. We focused on minor cell types and identified 5000 highly variable genes (HVGs) using the Seurat pipeline65. The processed dataset consists of 100,064 cells and 5000 genes. The supplementary Table S3 summarizes the identified cell types.

Human prostate cancer scRNA-seq data

For cell type deconvolution in human prostate cancer, we use corresponding scRNA-seq data from43. The initial dataset includes 51,786 cells with 32,847 genes and annotation for 13 cell types. We identify 5000 HVGs using the Seurat pipeline65. The processed dataset contains 17,108 cells and 5000 genes. Supplementary Table S4 provides a summary of the identified cell types.

Human ovarian cancer scRNA-seq data

To explore the spatial distribution of cell types in human ovarian cancer, we use scRNA-seq data from the GEO (GSE165897)66. The original dataset includes 17,108 cells with 33,538 genes and annotation for three major cell types and 27 minor cell types. We identify 5000 HVGs using the Seurat pipeline65. The processed dataset comprises 51,786 cells and 5000 genes. Supplementary Table S5 provides a summary of the identified cell types.

Evaluation metrics

To assess the spatial clustering performance of different methods, we utilize the ARI and Pur. ARI21 serves as a refined version of the Rand index (RI)67, quantifying the similarity between two distinct partitions and ranging between −1 and 1. A higher ARI value indicates greater similarity between the partitions. Specifically, when two partitions are equivalent to a permutation, ARI yields a value of 1. We calculate the ARI value using the adjustedRandIndex function within the R package mclust.

Pur ∈ [0, 1] and a larger purity score indicates a higher similarity between two classes. We use the purity function in the R package funtimes for the calculation of purity score.

Apart from domain identification, EnSDD also gives weights for base models. These weights are expected to serve as an assessment metric for base models when ground truth is missing. To measure the rationality of the weights learned by EnSDD, the Pearson correlation coefficient (R) between the learned weights and ARI of base methods is calculated.
