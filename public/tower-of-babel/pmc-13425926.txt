Metabolic disorder-MASLD-cardiovascular disease-kidney disease (MMCK) syndrome: an expansion of the Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) framework
Authors: Yan Guo, Ying Zhang, Suxuan Liu, Dajin Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome framework acknowledges metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) as a major manifestation, yet hepatic involvement remains insufficiently incorporated into risk stratification. This review proposes the metabolic disorder-MASLD-cardiovascular disease-kidney disease (MMCK) syndrome as an operational extension that integrates hepatic fibrosis assessment into cardiometabolic-renal care.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive narrative review across PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Scopus through March 2026. We synthesized evidence on pathophysiological crosstalk, proposed the Multi-Organ Risk Score (MORS)-enabled MMCK staging criteria (Stages 0–4), and developed stage-specific management algorithms.ResultsThe MMCK framework introduces four key operational advances: (i) quantitative multi-organ risk stratification through the MORS (L + C + B + K, range 0–12) with mandatory FIB-4/VCTE-based hepatic fibrosis assessment as the L-Score; (ii) stage-specific therapeutic thresholds linked to objective clinical cutoffs; (iii) a structured multidisciplinary team governance model with MORS-based electronic health record triggers; and (iv) a three-tiered endpoint framework for quality improvement and prospective validation. Observational evidence indicates that incorporating MASLD fibrosis into cardiometabolic staging improves coronary artery disease risk prediction. The MMCK framework positions MASLD as a cardiovascular risk-modifying axis within established cardiometabolic-renal care.ConclusionsThe MMCK syndrome operationalizes hepatic integration within established cardiometabolic-renal care by providing a quantitative, stage-specific framework for multi-organ risk assessment and management. Future research should prospectively validate MORS-based staging in diverse cohorts and evaluate whether MMCK-guided management improves clinical outcomes compared with CKM-guided care alone.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40842-026-00317-0.

Introduction

CKM syndrome is a recently established clinical framework that integrates the pathophysiological interconnections among cardiovascular diseases (CVD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and metabolic dysfunction. It originates from traditional metabolic risk factors—such as obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension, and dyslipidemias—which collectively drive progressive damage to the heart, kidneys, liver, and other organ systems. The American Heart Association (AHA) emphasized the multidirectional interactions among these organ systems and the necessity for a holistic, multidisciplinary approach to prevention, diagnosis, and management when defining CKM syndrome [1], ].The AHA Presidential Advisory explicitly acknowledges MASLD as a major organ manifestation of CKM syndrome and recommends screening for advanced liver fibrosis using the FIB-4 index every 1–2 years in individuals with metabolic risk factors [2]. This interconnectedness underscores the importance of early risk identification and stratification for implementing effective therapeutic strategies.

MASLD, formerly known as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is a highly prevalent condition that intersects with CKM components. MASLD affects about 25–30% of the general adult population, with prevalence rising to 70% among T2DM patients, underscoring its strong association with metabolic derangements and insulin resistance [3]. Beyond hepatic complications, MASLD is linked to extrahepatic manifestations, including CVD, CKD, and T2DM—all hallmarks of CKM syndrome [2]. Its pathophysiology involves systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and dysregulated lipid metabolism, which contribute to endothelial dysfunction and accelerated atherosclerosis, thereby increasing cardiovascular risk [4].

The AHA CKM framework clearly identifies MASLD as a major organ manifestation and recommends regular screening. However, CKM does not explicitly include the liver in its naming. This may lead clinicians to underappreciate the central role of the liver. Rather than proposing an entirely new conceptual framework, we introduce the metabolic disorder-MASLD-cardiovascular disease-kidney disease (MMCK) syndrome as an operational tool to explicitly highlight the hepatic contribution within the existing CKM syndrome continuum. This syndrome aims to remind clinicians that MASLD is an integral component of the metabolic-cardiovascular-renal axis, particularly regarding fibrosis staging and targeted hepatoprotective interventions. Recent studies have demonstrated that incorporating MASLD fibrosis stages into CKM risk stratification improves prediction of cardiovascular outcomes [5–7], underscoring the clinical value of explicit hepatic assessment within the established CKM framework.

Recent publications have substantially enriched the evidence supporting expanded metabolic assessment within CKM frameworks. Jankauskas et al. [8] provided a comprehensive review of CKM pathophysiology, while Liu and Dai [9] demonstrated that hepatic metabolic burden predicts mortality across glucose metabolism statuses in CKM populations. Complementary analyses have established the incremental prognostic value of integrated metabolic indices [10], identified sex-specific psychosocial modifiers [11], and highlighted the need for expandable multi-organ risk frameworks incorporating non-cardiometabolic comorbidities [12]. Against this backdrop, we present the MMCK syndrome as a hypothesis-generating conceptual model that operationalizes hepatic integration within established cardiorenal care, acknowledging that the proposed staging and scoring systems require prospective empirical validation before clinical adoption.

Definition and importance of MMCK

Definition

The MMCK syndrome is an emerging integrative framework that emphasizes the complex interplay among metabolic, hepatic, cardiovascular, and renal dysfunctions. MMCK differs from the CKM syndrome by explicitly including liver pathology, particularly metabolic liver disease, as a core component. While CKM focuses on the triad of CVD, CKD, and metabolic disorders like T2DM and obesity, MMCK expands this scope to incorporate the liver’s pivotal role in systemic metabolic regulation and organ crosstalk. This distinction is critical, as MASLD are increasingly recognized as both consequences and drivers of cardiovascular and renal impairments. For example, MASLD is associated with insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, and dyslipidemia, which in turn exacerbate cardiac and renal dysfunction, creating a vicious cycle of multi-organ damage [13, 14].

Clinical importance

The clinical significance of MMCK lies in its role as a multifaceted disorder characterized by the interplay and progressive dysfunction of the heart, liver, kidneys, and metabolic systems. MMCK represents a paradigm shift from viewing these organ systems in isolation to recognizing their interconnected pathophysiology, which can culminate in multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS). This integrated perspective underscores the complex crosstalk among metabolic derangements, systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction that collectively drive organ impairment [15, 16]. This systemic involvement necessitates a multidisciplinary approach for diagnosis and management, focusing on early identification of metabolic risk factors and their impact on organ function.

MMCK significantly affects cardiovascular events and hepatic-renal failure, major causes of global morbidity and mortality. Cardiovascular issues in MMCK stem from metabolic abnormalities and organ dysfunction. Patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) face higher risks of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), like myocardial infarction and stroke, regardless of traditional risk factors [17]. Progressing renal dysfunction worsens cardiovascular risk through volume overload and hypertension [18]. Hepatic decompensation, including portal hypertension, can lead to acute kidney injury and worsen cardiovascular status [19, 20]. Overall, MMCK highlights the intersection of metabolic, cardiovascular, hepatic, and renal dysfunctions, necessitating early risk assessment and comprehensive management to improve outcomes.

Distinction from related frameworks

We acknowledge the Cardiovascular-Renal-Hepatic-Metabolic (CRHM) syndrome [13] as an important conceptual advance recognizing hepatic-metabolic-cardiovascular-renal interconnections. However, MMCK differs fundamentally from CKM by establishing MASLD as an independent pathophysiological axis rather than a metabolic risk factor consequence, delivering tangible clinical value through enhanced cardiovascular risk stratification (MASLD is associated with MACE risk beyond metabolic syndrome components; HR 1.45, 95% CI 1.31–1.60), therapeutic specificity (MASLD-directed interventions such as GLP-1 RAs and SGLT2 inhibitors provide distinct cardiorenal benefits), and mandatory fibrosis screening (FIB-4/VCTE in Stage 2–3) that identifies high-risk subpopulations missed by CKM staging.

From CRHM, MMCK offers distinct temporal, functional, and practical advantages. It builds upon 2024–2025 evidence demonstrating that MASLD fibrosis stage specifically modifies cardiovascular risk beyond traditional CKM staging, positions MASLD specifically as a cardiovascular risk modifier requiring stage-specific management integration rather than one of several parallel organ manifestations, and provides an actionable framework for primary care and cardiology integration rather than a specialist-oriented conceptual model (Table S1).

Pathophysiological mechanisms of MMCK

Metabolic dysregulation-driven inflammation: the pivotal mechanism in MMCK syndrome

Metabolic dysregulation initiates systemic low-grade inflammation—the central MMCK mechanism—via nutrient excess and adiposity that disrupt multi-organ homeostasis (Fig. 1). Obesity-driven adipose expansion activates macrophage infiltration, secreting IL-1β and TNF-α that perpetuate insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction [21, 22]. In human primary adipocytes, macrophage-conditioned medium reduced IRS-1, PI3K p85α, and GLUT4 expression, reversed by IL-1β neutralization, establishing IL-1β as a potential contributor of macrophage-induced insulin resistance [23]. This inflammatory state extends systemically, affecting the heart, kidneys, and liver, driving MMCK’s multisystemic pathology.

Hypothalamic metabolic stress triggers glial cell activation and immune cell recruitment, sustaining chronic low-grade inflammation. High-fat diet feeding in mice induces hypothalamic inflammation within 24–72 hours (preceding obesity), elevating TNF-α, MCP-1, IL-6, and microglial markers (Iba-1, CD11b) [24]. This neuroinflammation impairs neuronal signaling related to feeding behavior, thermogenesis, and hormonal regulation, perpetuating metabolic disorders like obesity and T2DM [25, 26]. Oxidative stress, a metabolic byproduct, exacerbates tissue damage and inflammatory signaling in cardiovascular tissues, contributing to endothelial dysfunction, hypertension, and myocardial injury [22]. Dysregulated complement systems and reactive oxygen species amplify immune cell activation, fostering a vicious cycle of inflammation and metabolic derangement.

Hepatokines—proteins secreted by hepatocytes—represent critical endocrine mediators linking hepatic function to cardiovascular and renal pathology. Fetuin-A promotes insulin resistance and vascular calcification, accelerating atherosclerosis and CKD progression [27]. Fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) exerts pleiotropic metabolic effects, improving insulin sensitivity and lipid profiles while conferring cardiovascular protection [27]. FGF21 knockout HFpEF mice exhibited worsened diastolic dysfunction (E/A ratio: 1.08 ± 0.02 vs. 1.26 ± 0.02, p < 0.05; E/E’ ratio: 28.06 ± 0.73 vs. 24.20 ± 0.55, p < 0.01), increased oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction [28]. In vitro experiments confirmed that recombinant FGF21 restored glucose uptake and reduced lipid accumulation in neonatal rat ventricular myocytes [28]. Angiopoietin-like protein 3 (ANGPTL3) regulates lipid metabolism by inhibiting lipoprotein lipase, with elevated levels associated with dyslipidemia and increased cardiovascular risk [29]. ANGPTL3-targeted vaccines in familial hypercholesterolemia mice decreased LDL-C and triglycerides while reducing atherosclerotic lesions [30]. Hepassocin promotes hepatocyte proliferation and survival, while also modulating systemic glucose homeostasis [31].

Emerging evidence highlights specific molecular mediators linking metabolic dysregulation to inflammation. The ETS (E-twenty-six) transcription factor family modulates inflammatory and metabolic pathways, with ETS1 promoting inflammation and hepatocyte apoptosis, exacerbating metabolic liver disease [32]. Additionally, angiogenin-mediated biogenesis of 5’-tRNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs) modulates NLRP3 inflammasome activation, influencing inflammation and metabolic disorders [33, 34].

Gut microbiota alterations contribute to systemic inflammation by modulating immune responses and metabolic functions, with beneficial bacteria such as Akkermansia muciniphila attenuating inflammation and improving metabolic health [35, 36]. A. muciniphila supplementation in high-fat diet-fed zebrafish reduced hepatic steatosis, improved intestinal barrier function, downregulated IL-1β, and upregulated TGFβ [37]. A randomized trial (NCT02637115) demonstrated that 3-month oral A. muciniphila in obese patients decreased body weight and improved liver dysfunction markers [38]. Disruption of the lung-gut axis by environmental pollutants also exacerbates inflammation and metabolic disorders, highlighting the multifactorial nature of inflammation in MMCK syndrome [39].

Hyperuricemia represents a critical but often overlooked metabolic factor in MMCK pathophysiology. Elevated serum uric acid levels (>420 μmol/L in men and postmenopausal women, >360 μmol/L in premenopausal women) promote endothelial dysfunction through reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, increased oxidative stress, and induction of inflammatory cytokines [40]. Uric acid exposure in vascular smooth muscle cells increased protein carbonylation and superoxide anion levels while decreasing nitric oxide production [41]. In the kidney, uric acid induces afferent arteriolopathy, glomerular hypertension, and tubulointerstitial fibrosis, accelerating CKD progression. Intrahepatically, uric acid exacerbates steatosis and inflammation by promoting de novo lipogenesis and activating pro-inflammatory signaling pathways [42]. Furthermore, hyperuricemia is associated with insulin resistance and contributes to the development of hypertension, creating a vicious cycle that amplifies cardiovascular risk [43].

In summary, metabolic dysregulation is associated with chronic inflammation in MMCK syndrome, affecting the heart, kidneys, and liver through immune and metabolic interactions.To address the validation of proposed inflammatory mediators, we incorporated a tiered biomarker strategy translating the aforementioned mechanisms—adipose inflammation, hepatokine signaling, gut barrier dysfunction, and oxidative stress—into quantifiable clinical parameters (Table S2).

Liver-heart-kidney interactions: pathophysiological basis of MMCK syndrome

The cardiorenal axis

The cardiorenal axis operates through hemodynamic, neurohumoral, and cellular pathways, forming a tightly linked system [44]. Chronic heart failure, especially HFpEF, involves metabolic abnormalities, inflammation, and oxidative stress affecting both organs [45]. Pathophysiological dialogue operates at systemic, organ, cellular, and molecular levels [46].

At the systemic level, the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and sympathetic nervous system (SNS) are activated in both cardiac and renal dysfunction. Reduced cardiac output decreases renal perfusion, stimulating renin release and activating RAAS [47]. This generates angiotensin II (Ang II) and aldosterone, promoting vasoconstriction, sodium retention, and cellular toxicity through AT1 receptors [48]. Ang II stimulates NADPH oxidase, producing reactive oxygen species (ROS) [49]. Local RAAS components exacerbate oxidative stress, inflammation, and fibrosis [44].

SNS overexcitation results from impaired cardiorenal reflexes [50]. Cardiac baroreceptor dysfunction and renal ischemia increase central sympathetic outflow [51]. Norepinephrine damages cardiomyocytes and renal tubular cells, while SNS activation stimulates renin release, creating a feed-forward loop [52].

At the inflammatory level, damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) activate Toll-like receptors (TLRs), particularly TLR4, triggering NF-κB activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) [53]. These cytokines induce cardiomyocyte apoptosis, podocyte damage, and renal interstitial fibrosis [54]. Acute heart failure patients with elevated cystatin C and NT-proBNP showed 52.8% vs. 3.2% 12-month mortality (highest vs. lowest tertiles) [55].

Fibrotic remodeling is orchestrated by the TGF-β/Smad axis [56]. Dysregulated RAAS and inflammatory cytokines upregulate TGF-β, which initiates Smad-dependent pathways regulating extracellular matrix production [57]. Non-Smad pathways (MAPK, PI3K/Akt) amplify fibrotic responses [56]. HFpEF mouse kidneys exhibited 1.9-fold increased interstitial fibrosis (p < 0.05) with elevated endothelial-mesenchymal transition markers [58].

Metabolic and mitochondrial dysfunction constitutes another critical pillar [59]. The myocardium switches to inefficient glucose metabolism, while renal tubular cells experience mitochondrial dysfunction and ATP depletion [60]. Systemic insulin resistance promotes advanced glycation end product (AGE) formation, triggering inflammation through RAGE activation [59]. Mitochondrial quality control is compromised through disrupted mitophagy, leading to ROS accumulation [60].

EMPEROR-Preserved and DELIVER trials: SGLT2 inhibitors reduced CV death/HF hospitalization by 20% (HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.78–0.87; p < 0.001) and HF hospitalizations by 26% (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.67–0.83; p < 0.001) [61]. Meta-analysis of 15 RCTs (n = 90,000): SGLT2 inhibitors reduced kidney disease progression risk by 37% vs placebo [62]. DAPA-CKD: Dapagliflozin reduced CV death and HF hospitalization by 29% regardless of baseline HF status [63].

Collectively, these mechanisms create a vicious cycle where cardiac dysfunction impairs renal perfusion, while renal impairment exacerbates cardiac stress through electrolyte imbalances and uremic toxin accumulation, perpetuating cardiorenal syndrome progression [46, 52]. The liver-kidney axis

The liver-kidney axis demonstrates metabolic synergy. MASH and NAFLD are closely linked with insulin resistance and obesity, conditions that also drive CKD progression [17]. Lean patients with MASH exhibit higher risks of cardiovascular events, renal decompensation, and hepatic decompensation compared to non-lean counterparts, suggesting that hepatic metabolic dysfunction independently contributes to renal and cardiovascular pathology [17]. In 1,525 CKD patients followed 6.5 years, NAFLD associated with −0.79% annual eGFR decline vs +0.30% without (p = 0.002); advanced fibrosis showed −2.12% decline [64].

The bidirectional interaction between the liver and the cardiovascular system

Hepatic steatosis has been associated with left ventricular hypertrophy and diastolic dysfunction in hypertensive patients, indicating that liver pathology influences cardiac remodeling through mechanisms beyond insulin resistance [65]. Renal transplant recipients with metabolic syndrome demonstrate increased cardiovascular events and graft loss, emphasizing the impact of metabolic derangements on renal outcomes [66]. Metabolic syndrome components such as dyslipidemia, hypertension, and diabetes predict both cardiovascular and renal endpoints in CKD populations, including IgA nephropathy patients, highlighting the prognostic significance of metabolic factors in hepatorenal and cardiorenal interactions [67]. The hepatorenal axis is further influenced by systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, which exacerbate endothelial dysfunction and organ fibrosis, accelerating MMCK syndrome progression [15].

The liver and cardiovascular system engage in a dynamic bidirectional relationship mediated by metabolic, inflammatory, and neurohumoral factors central to MMCK pathogenesis. Metabolic syndrome and obesity induce hepatic steatosis and systemic inflammation, promoting atherosclerosis, cardiac remodeling, and heart failure [17, 68]. NAFLD is associated with increased left ventricular mass and diastolic dysfunction, independently of traditional risk factors like insulin resistance, underscoring the liver’s direct influence on cardiac structure and function [65]. Conversely, cardiac dysfunction can lead to hepatic congestion and ischemia, contributing to cardiac cirrhosis and further metabolic derangements [69].

Therapeutic advances targeting this axis include SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists, which confer cardio-renal-metabolic protection by improving glycemic control, reducing inflammation, and attenuating fibrosis across multiple organs [70, 71]. In 12,486 MASLD/MASH patients, GLP-1 agonists reduced MACE, portal hypertension, new-onset HF, and mortality over 7 years [72]. STEP-HFpEF: Semaglutide 2.4 mg achieved 15% greater NT-proBNP reduction and 43% greater CRP reduction vs placebo [73]. Pooled analysis (n = 3,743): Semaglutide reduced CV death/worsening HF by 31% (HR 0.69) [74]. Tirzepatide reduced CV death or worsening HF by 38% (HR 0.62) [75].

Novel agents like finerenone demonstrate efficacy in reducing cardiovascular and renal outcomes [76]. FIDELIO-DKD: Finerenone reduced primary kidney outcome by 18% (ARR 3.3%) and CV outcome by 14% (ARR 1.8%) [77]. FIGARO-DKD: Extended benefits to earlier-stage CKD, reducing CV events via decreased HF hospitalizations [78]. FINEARTS-HF (n = 6,001): Finerenone reduced unplanned HF hospitalization/urgent visit by 18% (RR 0.82) [79].

Interaction of genetic susceptibility and environmental factors

Genetic susceptibility to MMCK syndrome operates through polygenic risk scores that modulate individual progression through the staging continuum. PNPLA3 rs738409 (I148M) showed OR = 1.91 for hepatic steatosis and OR = 2.54 for NASH; TM6SF2 rs58542926 OR = 1.82; MBOAT7 rs641738 OR = 1.42 (>100,000 individuals). APOL1 high-risk genotypes conferred HR = 1.49 for incident CKD and HR = 2.43 for ESRD progression (n = 3,067) [80].

Gene-environment interactions substantially modify genetic risk. In 200 overweight adolescents, each additional PNPLA3 148 M allele increased steatosis severity (estimate +4.32, p < 0.0001), while weekly physical exercise hours showed independent negative association (estimate −1.78, p = 0.003) [81]. Notably, the PNPLA3 genotype interacts with physical activity such that lifestyle effects on hepatic steatosis are up to 6.3-fold stronger in PNPLA3 rs738409-GG versus -CC individuals [82].

Risk assessment and strategies of MMCK

MMCK syndrome risk assessment requires a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach integrating risk factor screening, evaluation of metabolic abnormalities, and detailed organ function assessments to identify high-risk patients (Fig. 2). To operationalize this multidimensional risk evaluation, we introduce the Multi-Organ Risk Score (MORS), a quantitative scoring system that standardizes the assessment of four organ systems—hepatic (L), cardiovascular (C), cerebrovascular (B), and renal (K)—each scored from 0 (normal) to 3 (severe). The MORS serves as a structured implementation tool that translates the conceptual MMCK risk assessment strategy into a clinically actionable algorithm.

Conducting risk factors screening

Risk factor screening forms the foundation across six domains: family history of metabolic, cardiovascular, renal, or hepatic disease in first-degree relatives [83]; dietary structure (caloric intake, carbohydrate and fat composition, fiber intake); physical activity frequency and intensity; sleep quality and duration, with OSA screening in obese patients [25, 26]; smoking and alcohol consumption; and comorbid conditions (autoimmune or inflammatory diseases, gestational diabetes/hypertension history, drug-induced metabolic dysfunction) [84]. Genetic polymorphisms such as PNPLA3 rs738409 confer OR = 1.91 for hepatic steatosis and OR = 2.54 for NASH, with gene-environment interactions modifying metabolic risk [81, 85]. Individuals with ≥2 positive domains proceed to Step 2.

Evaluating liver, cardiovascular-cerebral, and renal structure and function

Comprehensive multi-organ evaluation is critical. Within the MMCK framework, each organ system is scored 0–3 based on established clinical, imaging, and laboratory parameters. The MORS ensures metabolic derangement remains the central driver, with all four target organ systems evaluated as equally important manifestations of systemic metabolic disease [2, 13, 14].

MORS integration and MMCK Stage assignment

The Multi-Organ Risk Score is calculated as MORS = L + C + B + K (range 0–12), with derived parameters Max = max (L,C,B,K) and N = number of organs with score ≥ 2. The MMCK stage is assigned based on MORS and its derived parameters (Table 6), directly corresponding to the established MMCK staging system. Special rules: any individual organ score of 3 assigns minimum Stage 3; ≥2 organs with scores ≥ 2 assigns minimum Stage 3; ≥2 organs with score = 3 assigns Stage 4. The MASLD fibrosis stage contributes one-quarter of the MORS via the L-Score, reflecting its importance as a component—but not the sole determinant—of MMCK risk [2, 13, 14].

Integrated management strategies of MMCK

Integrated MMCK management requires multidisciplinary collaboration that extends beyond single-specialty care. The multidisciplinary team (MDT) comprises core members—endocrinologists or metabolic specialists (serving as coordinators), cardiologists, nephrologists, and hepatologists—supported by nutritionists, primary care physicians, radiologists, and clinical pharmacists. MDT meeting frequency is stratified by MMCK stage: more frequent for Stage 3–4, periodic for Stage 2, and systematic registry-based review for Stage 0–1. A designated MDT coordinator ensures care continuity across transitions, with referral triggers linked to MORS thresholds embedded in the electronic health record. To ensure evaluability, MMCK management incorporates two complementary pillars: (1) stage-specific, evidence-based therapeutic decision thresholds that standardize pharmacological initiation and escalation, detailed in the sections below; and (2) a three-tiered endpoint framework encompassing process measures (e.g., screening and prescribing fidelity), intermediate outcomes (MORS trajectory, metabolic target attainment, fibrosis progression, quality of life), and clinical endpoints (MACE, kidney and liver events, all-cause mortality). Quality improvement is supported by periodic MORS recalculation, clinical audits, and a dedicated MMCK registry. The operational feasibility of integrated cardiorenal clinics has been demonstrated in recent reports [103–105], supporting the translational potential of the MDT model described here (Fig. 4).

Abbreviations: SGLT (sodium-glucose Co-Transporter); GLP-1 (Glucagon-like peptide-1); MRA (mineralocorticoid receptor Antagonist); GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic Polypeptide); ESRD (End-Stage renal disease)

Stage 0 to Stage 1: At earliest stages, a collaborative multidisciplinary co-management model including the specific six-specialty configuration (cardiologists, nephrologists, endocrinologists, hepatologists, nutritionists, and primary care physicians) represents an operationalization adaptable to local healthcare resources; the core principle of cross-disciplinary collaboration remains evidence-based [103–105].

The decision threshold for initiating structured lifestyle modification is ≥ 2 of the “Five Highs” (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, fasting glucose ≥ 5.6 mmol/L, BP ≥ 130/80 mmHg, dyslipidemia, or hyperuricemia) or MORS 1–2. Interventions include individualized nutrition counseling (5–7% weight loss for overweight individuals), ≥150 min/week moderate-intensity aerobic exercise, and sleep apnea screening when indicated. Pharmacotherapy is reserved for persistent abnormalities despite lifestyle adherence: metformin for prediabetes with HOMA-IR ≥ 2.5; statins when LDL-C > 2.6 mmol/L (intermediate risk) or >1.8 mmol/L (high risk) [13, 15]. Annual MORS reassessment monitors early cardiac, renal, and hepatic changes.

Stages 2 and 3: Management shifts toward preventing further progression through pharmacotherapy guided by explicit thresholds. ACEi/ARBs are initiated when BP > 130/80 mmHg or UACR ≥ 30 mg/g, uptitrated to maximum tolerated doses [70, 106]. SGLT2i are added at eGFR ≥ 20 mL/min/1.73 m2 with UACR ≥ 30 mg/g, or in any heart failure regardless of EF or glycemic status [70, 106]. Finerenone is indicated at UACR ≥ 30 mg/g and eGFR ≥ 25 mL/min/1.73 m2 if K+ < 5.0 mmol/L and on maximally tolerated ACEi/ARB [76]. GLP-1 RAs are initiated at BMI ≥ 27 kg/m2 with metabolic dysfunction or established ASCVD; for MASLD with F2+ fibrosis (L-Score ≥ 2), they also confer hepatic histological benefit [70, 106]. Dual GIP/GLP-1 RAs are considered when HbA1c targets are unmet despite GLP-1 RA monotherapy or ≥15% weight loss is indicated [70, 107]. Hepatology referral is triggered by FIB-4 > 2.67 or VCTE > 12.5 kPa. Comprehensive risk factor control targets: BP < 130/80 mmHg, uric acid < 360 μmol/L (women)/<420 μmol/L (men), with sustained lifestyle interventions [70, 106].

Stage 4: In advanced organ dysfunction with decompensated multi-organ involvement (MORS ≥ 8 or ≥ 2 organs scoring 3), an intensified, multidisciplinary approach is essential to optimize outcomes and quality of life. The therapeutic threshold for Stage 4 management is the presence of any two or more of the following: cirrhosis with portal hypertension or decompensation; ASCVD with prior myocardial infarction or revascularization; heart failure requiring hospitalization or ejection fraction < 40%; and chronic kidney disease stages 4–5 or dialysis requirement. Care coordination expands to include interventional cardiology, interventional radiology, neurology, and palliative care alongside the core MDT. For refractory heart failure, device therapy including implantable cardioverter-defibrillators and cardiac resynchronization should be evaluated per established cardiovascular guidelines. Renal replacement therapy modalities should be planned proactively when eGFR declines below 20 mL/min/1.73 m2, with timely vascular access creation and transplant evaluation when appropriate. For portal hypertension complications, endoscopic surveillance and transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt procedures require hepatology-interventional radiology collaboration.

A report on the one-year experience of a dedicated Kidney-Heart outpatient service demonstrated the feasibility and patient-centered value of integrated ambulatory care for this population [104]. Carollo et al. proposed a transitional care model for cardiorenal patients emphasizing the integration of cardiology and nephrology services to ensure continuity of care during critical transitions [108]. This model incorporates structured follow-up protocols, patient education, and coordinated medication management. de Wolski et al. reported outcomes from a dedicated cardio-nephrology inpatient service at the University of Washington, demonstrating that patients managed by a specialized Kidney-Heart service had lower mortality rates (16.5% vs. 25%, p < 0.01) and reduced need for dialysis (36.7% vs. 42%, p = 0.05) compared to standard nephrology consultation [103]. Early recognition and management of cachexia and wasting syndromes, common in advanced MMCK, require integrated nutritional and rehabilitative strategies [109].

Central to MMCK management success is multidisciplinary collaboration. MMCK’s multifactorial nature, involving intertwined mechanisms like systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and metabolic abnormalities, demands coordinated specialty efforts to ensure comprehensive assessment and treatment. Multidisciplinary teams enable holistic patient evaluation, shared decision-making, adherence to evidence-based guidelines, and attention to social determinants of health influencing progression and treatment access [15, 110], ultimately enhancing clinical outcomes and supporting precision medicine tailored to individual needs. Integrated MMCK management represents a dynamic, multidisciplinary approach aimed at reversing progression and improving patient outcomes.

Causality considerations in the MASLD-cardiometabolic axis

The association between MASLD and cardiorenal outcomes derives primarily from cohort studies, with persistent concerns about confounding and reverse causation (e.g., heart failure raising FIB-4, uremia affecting liver tests). Applying the Bradford Hill criteria: consistency and biological plausibility are reasonable, but specificity is poor (MASLD shares risk factors with nearly all cardiometabolic outcomes) and experimental evidence remains limited to metabolic interventions not designed to test MASLD-specific mediation. Mendelian randomization provides some support but is constrained by genetic pleiotropy. We therefore characterize the MASLD-CVD-CKD axis as an observationally consistent and, biologically plausible association awaiting definitive causal confirmation, and emphasize that the MMCK framework operationalizes this association into a testable structure without implying causal validation.

Rationale and operational philosophy of the multi-organ risk Score (MORS)

MORS assigns equal organ weights (L = C=B=K=3) as a deliberate operational simplification, analogous to staging systems such as the Gleason score and the Child-Pugh classification, where equivalently graded parameters are additively combined, not assuming equal statistical contributions. Thresholds follow EASL/AASLD guidance and MASLD meta-analyses, though population-specific calibration may be needed. MORS is not a replacement for validated tools (ASCVD, DIAL, KDIGO) but serves as a cross-organ integration heuristic that situates hepatic fibrosis within a unified cardiorenal staging architecture. We explicitly provide a five-step validation roadmap (prospective derivation, discrimination/calibration, IDI/NRI testing, external validation, reproducibility assessment) and label MORS a proof-of-concept hypothesis (Table S8).

Operational complexity and incremental value of MMCK staging

Adding hepatic assessment to CKM produces two distinct effects on risk prediction. First, and clinically more important, it dramatically stabilizes the Stage 3 risk estimate: in the Kawaharata cohort [92] (n = 18,358), the modified staging transformed HR from an unstable 2.41 (95% CI 0.34–17.4, p = 0.381) to a robust 2.56 (95% CI 1.66–3.95, p < 0.001), enabling reliable identification of a high-risk subgroup previously ungradable by CKM alone. Second, the integrated discrimination index increment was modest (0.001, p = 0.005) and below the conventional 0.01 threshold for clinical meaningfulness, though companion studies demonstrate that incorporating fibrosis markers into established risk scores yields larger net reclassification improvements. In a large prospective cohort [111], FIB-4 combined with CHA2DS2-VASc achieved an IDI of 0.010 (95% CI 0.005–0.015, p < 0.001) and an NRI of 0.32 (95% CI 0.19–0.45, p < 0.001); similarly, the MASLD-FLI + SCORE2 model [112]achieved an NRI of 0.22 (95% CI 0.17–0.27, p < 0.0001). See also Tables S6 and S7. Hepatic metabolic burden predicts mortality in CKM populations [9]. However, no randomized study has demonstrated that MMCK staging improves clinical decision-making or hard outcomes over CKM alone. We explicitly acknowledge this evidentiary gap and present MMCK staging as a conceptually integrated, operationally explicit hypothesis that addresses a recognized organ-system gap, not a validated clinical upgrade.

Heterogeneity, generalizability, and population-specific adaptation of the MMCK framework

FIB-4 performance varies by ethnicity (Asian populations may require adjusted cutoffs; African-descent platelet confounders). In adults > 75 years, frailty assessment is recommended. For BMI > 35, VCTE failure rates necessitate XL-probe or MRI-PDFF; South Asian populations may need waist circumference supplementation. We propose a tiered implementation: Tier 1 (FIB-4, universal), Tier 2 (VCTE for intermediate-risk), Tier 3 (specialist review). Beyond the core axis, modular expansion is supported by emerging evidence (depression [11], COPD [12]), underscoring that MMCK represents an initial operational scaffold designed for iterative refinement as new evidence emerges.

Limitations and validation agenda

The MMCK framework is hypothesis-generating, lacking prospective validation that adding MASLD fibrosis improves risk prediction (C-statistic, IDI, NRI) beyond traditional factors. Optimal thresholds require population-specific calibration. To move from concept to evidence, we outline a phased agenda: Phase 1—retrospective application to NHANES, UK Biobank for discrimination/calibration and threshold derivation; Phase 2—prospective integration into clinical workflows with ≥5-year follow-up; Phase 3—cluster-randomized trials comparing MMCK-guided vs. CKM-guided management on hard endpoints (MACE, CKD progression, liver events, all-cause mortality). This roadmap provides a clear translational path from the current conceptual framework to evidence-based clinical adoption.

Recent evidence supporting the MMCK Framework

A growing body of recent evidence further contextualizes the biological and clinical rationale of the MMCK framework. This includes prospective data demonstrating the utility of TyG-related indices for cardiovascular risk prediction within CKM populations, large-scale cohort studies confirming a graded association between advancing CKM stages and all-cause mortality [7, 113], developmental evidence linking maternal nutrition to offspring CKM health [114, 115], epidemiological shifts observed during the COVID-19 pandemic [116], population-based data on adiposity phenotypes [117], and preclinical proof-of-concept for adjunctive nutritional strategies [118] (Table S5). Recent publications have further enriched this landscape: Jankauskas et al. [8] and Liu and Dai [9] (cited above) consolidated the CKM evidence base; Fu et al. [10]established the incremental prognostic value of combining metabolic and anthropometric indices within CKM staging through significant IDI and NRI metrics; Gao et al. [11] identified sex-specific associations between depression and CKM progression, highlighting psychosocial modifiers; and Ioannides et al. [12] showed that COPD and inhaled corticosteroids modify MACE risk in CKM populations, illustrating the framework’s potential for modular expansion beyond the core liver-cardiorenal axis.

Conclusion

The conceptual evolution from CKM to MMCK syndrome marks a conceptual extension in understanding metabolic-related multi-organ diseases. Rather than replacing the established CKM framework, the MMCK syndrome represents an operational extension that explicitly incorporates hepatic dysfunction into the existing cardiometabolic-renal continuum. By positioning endocrine metabolic dysregulation at the core, the MMCK concept aims to more comprehensively capture the intricate interplay among metabolic disturbances, liver pathology, cardiovascular complications, and renal dysfunction. This holistic perspective acknowledges the complexity of these interrelated systems and underscores the necessity of integrated diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Future research should integrate multidisciplinary resources, focusing on precision medicine for MMCK through collaboration on diagnostics, biomarkers, and treatment. Public health strategies can reduce the global burden of this syndrome. The MMCK concept offers an integrated perspective on understanding metabolic diseases, promoting comprehensive management to enhance patient outcomes. Commitment to integrative research and innovation is crucial for addressing MMCK challenges.

It is important to acknowledge that the MASLD-CVD-CKD association remains predominantly observational, with residual confounding and reverse causation concerns persisting. The MMCK framework operationalizes this association as a testable structure rather than implying causal validation. The Multi-Organ Risk Score is deliberately proposed as a proof-of-concept heuristic-analogous to established composite scores such as Child-Pugh-rather than a substitute for externally validated risk tools. Prospective studies are required to confirm whether MORS-based staging delivers incremental predictive value beyond existing instruments, and whether population-specific thresholds (accounting for age, ethnicity, and body mass index) are needed.
