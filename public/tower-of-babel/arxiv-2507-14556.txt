On a dense set of functions determined by sampled Gabor magnitude
Matthias Wellershoff

Abstract
We study the problem of recovering a function from the magnitude of its Gabor transform sampled on a discrete set. While it is known that uniqueness fails for general square integrable functions, we show that phase retrieval is possible for a dense class of signals: specifically, those whose Bargmann transforms are entire functions of exponential type. Our main result characterises when such functions can be uniquely recovered (up to a global phase) from magnitude only data sampled on uniformly discrete sets of sufficient lower Beurling density. In particular, we prove that every entire function of exponential type is uniquely determined (up to a global phase) among all second order entire functions by its modulus on a sufficiently dense shifted lattice.



1
Introduction

We consider the problem of recovering an entire function
f
of second order from its modulus
\lvert f\rvert
known only on a discrete set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}
. This is known as the
phase retrieval problem for entire functions
and has been investigated in
[
Lie24
,
Mc 04
,
Wel24b
]
.

Our interest in this problem comes from
Gabor phase retrieval
, where one seeks to recover a square integrable function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
from the modulus of its Gabor transform,

\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega):=2^{1/4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\pi(t-x)^{2}-2\pi\mathrm{i}t\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad(x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(1)

Through a unitary mapping (the Bargmann transform) from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
onto a space of entire functions (the Fock space), this problem becomes equivalent to recovering an entire function from its modulus. We elaborate on this connection in Section
1.3
.

A fundamental feature of any phase retrieval problem is that the function
f
and any scalar multiple
\tau f
, with
\tau\in C_{1}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,\lvert z\rvert=1\}
, yield the same measurements
\lvert f\rvert
(or
\lvert\mathcal{G}f\rvert
). Thus, it is impossible to distinguish them from magnitude only data. Therefore, recovery is typically understood
up to a global phase
; that is, with respect to the equivalence relation

f\sim g:\iff\exists\tau\in C_{1}:f=\tau g.

1.1
Overview of the literature on Gabor phase retrieval

The Gabor phase retrieval problem has attracted a lot of attention in recent years starting with the development of uniqueness results for compactly supported functions
[
AW21
,
GLS25
,
GL23
,
IPSV23
,
Wel23
,
Wel24a
]
. These works showed that, under suitable sampling conditions, a compactly supported function can be uniquely recovered (up to a global phase) from the modulus of its Gabor transform.

However, when extending the problem to the full space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, uniqueness fails: it has been shown
[
AW22
]
that, for any set
\Lambda
consisting of infinitely many equidistant parallel lines in the time-frequency plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, there exist functions
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
f\not\sim g
yet
\lvert\mathcal{G}f\rvert=\lvert\mathcal{G}g\rvert
on
\Lambda
.

1

In fact, there exist countably many such sets
(\Lambda_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, each consisting of infinitely many parallel lines, and a single function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
which cannot be recovered from the entire collection of measurements
(\lvert\mathcal{G}f\rvert|_{\Lambda_{n}})_{n=1}^{\infty}

[
Wel24b
]
.
Moreover,
[
ABSW24
]
showed that the set of such
counterexamples
,

\mathfrak{C}(\Lambda):=\left\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\,\middle|\,\exists g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}):f\not\sim g\text{ and }\lvert\mathcal{G}f\rvert|_{\Lambda}=\lvert\mathcal{G}g\rvert|_{\Lambda}\right\},

is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
regardless of the choice of infinitely many equidistant parallel lines
\Lambda
. At the same time, the Gaussian
\smash{\phi(t)=2^{1/4}\mathrm{e}^{-\pi t^{2}}}
can be recovered from samples of
\lvert\mathcal{G}\phi\rvert
on a sufficiently dense square lattice
a\mathbb{Z}^{2}
for
a<1
.

These results provide the background for the following questions, formulated in
[
AAC+25
]
: define the set of
examples

\mathfrak{E}(\Lambda):=\left\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\,\middle|\,\forall g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}):\lvert\mathcal{G}f\rvert|_{\Lambda}=\lvert\mathcal{G}g\rvert|_{\Lambda}\Rightarrow f\sim g\right\}.

Is
\mathfrak{E}(\Lambda)
dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
? And if so, which of the two sets,
\mathfrak{E}(\Lambda)
or
\mathfrak{C}(\Lambda)
, is “larger” in an appropriate sense?

In this paper, we answer the first of these questions affirmatively: we show that
\mathfrak{E}(\Lambda)
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
provided that
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a uniformly discrete set (i.e., the points are separated by a minimal distance) with sufficiently large lower Beurling density (see Section
1.5
) and suitable structure. This gives a foundation for a possible answer to the second question as well. Our approach builds on the ideas developed in
[
ABSW24
]
.

Remark 1

(Why results for compactly supported functions do
not
settle the question)
.

At first glance, one might expect the density of
\mathfrak{E}(\Lambda)
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
to follow from uniqueness results for compactly supported functions since all such functions are examples. However, this is not the case: the required sampling density in frequency generally depends on the size of the time support. For instance,
[
Wel23
]
shows that all
f\in L^{2}([-A,A])
can be recovered (up to a global phase) from
\lvert\mathcal{G}f\rvert
sampled on
\{0,1\}\times\mathbb{Z}/4A
; note that the sampling density grows as
A
increases.

1.2
Main results

Our main result establishes that entire functions of exponential type can be uniquely distinguished (up to a global phase) from all other second order entire functions based solely on knowledge of their modulus on uniformly discrete sets
\Lambda
with sufficient lower Beurling density and suitable geometric structure. We present it here in simplified form. For the terminology used, please consult Section
1.5
.

Theorem 1

(Main theorem; cf. Theorem
5
)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
be a second order entire function of type
\tau<\infty
and let
g\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
be an entire function of exponential type
\kappa<\infty
. Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}
be a uniformly discrete set with lower Beurling density
D^{-}(\Lambda)>2\tau/\pi
. Suppose that
\Lambda
contains a pair of infinite subsets lying on two parallel lines with distance strictly less than
\pi/2\kappa
; each forming an arithmetic progression with spacing strictly less than
\pi/2\kappa
. Then,

f\sim g\iff\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert\text{ on }\Lambda.

In particular, the result applies when
\Lambda
is a so called
shifted lattice
, i.e.,

\Lambda=z_{0}+\omega_{1}\mathbb{Z}+\omega_{2}\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{C},

where
z_{0},\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\omega_{1}/\omega_{2}\not\in\mathbb{R}
, provided that the lattice is sufficiently dense (i.e., its covolume is strictly less than
\pi/2\tau
) and that the geometric conditions

\lvert\omega_{2}\rvert<\frac{\pi}{2\kappa},\qquad\frac{\lvert\operatorname{Im}(\omega_{1}\overline{\omega_{2}})\rvert}{\lvert\omega_{2}\rvert}<\frac{\pi}{2\kappa}

are satisfied (cf. Corollary
2
). In view of the counterexamples from
[
AW22
]
, these geometric constraints are sharp (cf. Section
2.4
).

Example 1

(Polynomials; cf. Section
2.3
)
.

Let
g\in\mathbb{C}[z]
be a polynomial. Then our main theorem (in stronger form) implies that, for any second order entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
and any set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}
as above, we have

f\sim g\iff\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert\text{ on }\Lambda.

Using the Bargmann transform (which will be introduced in the next section), this yields a result for Gabor phase retrieval: let
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and let
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
be a finite linear combination of Hermite functions (the preimages of monomials under the Bargmann transform). Suppose
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a uniformly discrete set with lower Beurling density
D^{-}(\Lambda)>1
and contains two infinite subsets lying on parallel lines. Then,

f\sim g\iff\lvert\mathcal{G}f\rvert=\lvert\mathcal{G}g\rvert\text{ on }\Lambda,

which implies that
\mathfrak{E}(\Lambda)
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
(as explained in the following section).

Finally, we note that the proof of our main result relies on a striking property of second order entire functions: namely, their growth can be determined from their behaviour on uniformly discrete sets with sufficiently large lower Beurling density. This extends an earlier result of Earl
[
Ear66
]
.

1.3
Some preliminaries on the Fock space

The Gabor transform, as defined in equation (
1
), provides information about the frequencies
\omega
present at specific times
x
in a signal
f
. A closely related transform is the
Bargmann transform
, defined by

\mathcal{B}f(z):=2^{1/4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi tz-\pi t^{2}-\tfrac{\pi}{2}z^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

The precise relationship between the Gabor and Bargmann transforms is given by
[
Grö01
, Proposition 3.4.1(a) on p. 54]
:

\mathcal{G}f(x,-\omega)=\mathrm{e}^{\pi\mathrm{i}x\omega}\cdot\mathcal{B}f(x+\mathrm{i}\omega)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\tfrac{\pi}{2}(x^{2}+\omega^{2})},\qquad x,\omega\in\mathbb{R}.

(2)

This identity is immensely useful because the Bargmann transform defines a
unitary
operator from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
onto the
Fock space

\mathcal{F}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
of entire functions
F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
for which the norm

\lVert F\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}:=\left(\int_{\mathbb{C}}\lvert F(z)\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\pi\lvert z\rvert^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}z\right)^{1/2}

is finite
[
Grö01
, Theorem 3.4.3 on p. 56]
.

As a consequence, the problem of recovering a function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
from the magnitude of its Gabor transform
\lvert\mathcal{G}f\rvert
sampled on a discrete set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is equivalent to recovering the entire function
\mathcal{B}f
from its modulus
\lvert\mathcal{B}f\rvert
sampled on the reflected set
\smash{\overline{\Lambda}:=\{\overline{\lambda}\,|\,\lambda\in\Lambda\}}
, due to equation (
2
) and the unitarity of
\mathcal{B}
.

The Bargmann transform maps every
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
to an entire function of order at most two and type at most
\pi/2
. More precisely,
[
Grö01
, Proposition 3.4.1(b) and Theorem 3.4.2(b) on p. 54]
:

\lvert\mathcal{B}f(z)\rvert\leq\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{\tfrac{\pi}{2}\lvert z\rvert^{2}},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

(3)

Finally, the Fock space
\mathcal{F}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
has an orthonormal basis consisting of monomials
[
Grö01
, Theorem 3.4.2(a) on p. 54]
:

e_{n}(z):=\left(\frac{\pi^{n}}{n!}\right)^{1/2}z^{n},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C},~n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Since
\mathcal{B}
is unitary, the preimages of these monomials under
\mathcal{B}
form an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. We denote them by
h_{n}:=\mathcal{B}^{-1}e_{n}
and refer to them as the
Hermite functions
as is usual.

1.4
Auxiliary results on holomorphic functions

In the proof of our main result, we will need two classical results about holomorphic and entire functions. For the convenience of the reader, we summarise them here.

The first is Carlson’s theorem, which gives a uniqueness criterion for functions of exponential type based on the density of their zeros along a single line.

Theorem 2

(Carlson’s theorem; cf. e.g.
[
Tit39
, Section 5.81 on p. 186]
)
.

Let
f
be holomorphic on the right half-plane
\mathbb{H}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,\operatorname{Re}z>0\}
and suppose that
f
satisfies the following growth condition: there exist
\kappa\in[0,\pi)
and
c,r>0
such that
\lvert f(z)\rvert\leq c\mathrm{e}^{\kappa\lvert z\rvert}
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
with
\lvert z\rvert>r
. If
f(n)=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then
f=0
identically.

The second is Hadamard’s factorisation theorem, which describes how to write entire functions of finite order in terms of their zeroes, similarly to how polynomials factor over their roots.

Theorem 3

(Hadamard’s factorisation theorem; cf. e.g.
[
Tit39
, Section 8.24 on p. 250]
)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
be a nonzero entire function of finite order
\rho\geq 0
with a zero of order
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
at the origin and nonzero zeroes
(z_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
listed with multiplicity. Then,
f
factors as

f(z)=z^{k}\mathrm{e}^{P(z)}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}E\left(\frac{z}{z_{j}};p\right),

where
P\in\mathbb{C}[z]
is a polynomial of degree
q\leq\rho
,
E(u;p):=(1-u)\exp(u+u^{2}/2+\dots+u^{p}/p)
is the Weierstrass primary factor of genus
p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and
p\leq\rho
is the smallest natural number such that
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lvert z_{j}\rvert^{-p-1}<\infty
.

1.5
Notation and terminology

We use the convention
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\dots\}
for the natural numbers and denote
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. For a finite set
S
, we denote its cardinality by
\lvert S\rvert
. The open, complex ball of radius
r>0
around
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
is denoted by
B_{r}(z_{0}):=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,\lvert z-z_{0}\rvert<r\}
. The complex circle of radius
r>0
around the origin is denoted by
C_{r}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,\lvert z\rvert=r\}
.

A discrete set of complex numbers
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{C}
is
uniformly discrete
if

\delta(\Lambda):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k\in\mathbb{N}\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}\lvert\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}\rvert>0.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{C}
be a compact set of unit measure. Then, the
lower Beurling density
(or
lower uniform density
) of
\Lambda
is

D^{-}(\Lambda):=\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{\inf_{z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}}\lvert\Lambda\cap(rK+z_{0})\rvert}{r^{2}}.

This definition is independent of the particular choice of
K
.

The polynomials with complex coefficients (and complex argument) are denoted by
\mathbb{C}[z]
. The space of entire functions is denoted by
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
. We say that an entire function
f
is of
order

\rho\in[0,\infty]
if

\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\log\max_{z\in C_{r}}\lvert f(z)\rvert}{\log r}=\rho.

Functions of order
\rho=1
are said to be of
exponential type
. If
\rho
is finite and non-zero, we say that the entire function
f
is of
type

\tau\in[0,\infty]
if

\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\max_{z\in C_{r}}\lvert f(z)\rvert}{r^{\rho}}=\tau.

Finally, if
f(x)
,
g(x)
are two families of objects parametrised by
x\in X
, where
X
is some set, then we write
f\lesssim g
if there exists a constant
c>0
such that, for all
x\in X
,
f(x)\leq cg(x)
. Similarly, we write
f\gtrsim g
if
g\lesssim f
as well as
f\eqsim g
if
f\lesssim g
and
f\gtrsim g
are true at the same time. We use
\lesssim_{Q}
,
\gtrsim_{Q}
and
\eqsim_{Q}
if the aforementioned constant
c
only depends on some quantity
Q
.

2
Main results

Throughout this section, we consider entire functions
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
for which the following growth condition holds:

\underline{\tau}:=\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\max_{z\in C_{r}}\lvert f(z)\rvert}{r^{2}}<\infty.

(4)

This condition is slightly weaker than requiring that
f
is of order at most two and type at most
\tau\in[0,\infty)
but suffices for all our main results. We highlight it here since it will be used repeatedly in what follows.

2.1
Determining the growth of entire functions from uniformly discrete samples

We begin with a cornerstone result of this paper: a modification of
[
Ear66
, Theorem 2 on p. 724]
, which itself is a generalisation of earlier work in
[
Car38
,
Pfl37
]
. Our version shows that the growth of entire functions of second order can be determined from their behaviour on uniformly discrete sets with sufficiently large lower Beurling density.

The proof draws inspiration from both the original result as well as ideas in sampling theory
[
CMNW89
,
Sei92
,
SW92
]
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
be an entire function satisfying condition (
4
). Let
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{C}
be a uniformly discrete set with lower Beurling density
D^{-}(\Lambda)>2\underline{\tau}/\pi
such that

\kappa:=\limsup_{j\to\infty}\frac{\log\lvert f(\lambda_{j})\rvert}{H(\lvert\lambda_{j}\rvert)}<\infty,

where
H:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is non-decreasing and unbounded at infinity such that
r^{-2}H(r)
is non-increasing and tends to zero as
r\to\infty
. Then,

\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\max_{z\in C_{r}}\lvert f(z)\rvert}{H(r)}\leq\kappa.

Remark 2
.

As noted in
[
Ear66
]
, the statement of the above theorem is nicely completed by
[
Pfl45
, Satz 4 on p. 202–203]
, which shows that the result remains true when
H
is bounded, as well as
[
Nob51
, Corollary 3 on p. 716]
, which shows that the result remains true when
r^{-2}H(r)
tends to
c\in(0,\kappa^{-1}\underline{\tau})
.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Assume, without loss of generality, that
\underline{\tau}<\pi/2
and
D^{-}(\Lambda)>1
by applying the linear transform

z\mapsto(D^{-}(\Lambda)/2+\underline{\tau}/\pi)^{1/2}z

if necessary. In this proof, we will make use of the well-known notion of two uniformly discrete sets
\Xi=\{\xi_{m,n}\}_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
\Xi^{\prime}=\{\xi^{\prime}_{m,n}\}_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}
being
uniformly close
if

\Delta_{2}(\Xi,\Xi^{\prime}):=\sup_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}\lvert\xi_{m,n}-\xi^{\prime}_{m,n}\rvert<\infty;

or, equivalently,

\Delta_{\infty}(\Xi,\Xi^{\prime}):=\sup_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}\max\{\lvert\operatorname{Re}\xi_{m,n}-\operatorname{Re}\xi^{\prime}_{m,n}\rvert,\lvert\operatorname{Im}\xi_{m,n}-\operatorname{Im}\xi^{\prime}_{m,n}\rvert\}<\infty.

Due to a standard argument by Beurling, we can assume that
\Lambda
is uniformly close to the square lattice
\mathbb{Z}+\mathrm{i}\mathbb{Z}
: indeed, since
D^{-}(\Lambda)>1
, for all
r>0
sufficiently large and all
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
,

\lvert\Lambda\cap(rK+z_{0})\rvert>r^{2},

where
K=[-1/2,1/2]+\mathrm{i}[-1/2,1/2]
is the unit square centered at the origin. So, pick the smallest
k\in\mathbb{N}
that is sufficiently large in the above sense and tile the complex plane by squares with side length
k
which contain exactly
k^{2}
elements of
\Lambda
. We denote those squares by
\{S_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{\infty}
and note that
\lvert\Lambda\cap S_{\ell}\rvert>k^{2}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. We can, therefore, pick exactly
k^{2}
elements from
\Lambda\cap S_{\ell}
, for each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, to create a subset
\Lambda_{0}\subset\Lambda
. Now, in each
S_{\ell}
,
\Lambda_{0}
and
\mathbb{Z}+\mathrm{i}\mathbb{Z}
have the same number of elements and, so, we may index
\Lambda_{0}=\{\lambda_{m,n}\}
in such a way that
\lambda_{m,n}\in S_{\ell}\iff m+\mathrm{i}n\in S_{\ell}
. Therefore,
\Lambda_{0}
and
\mathbb{Z}+\mathrm{i}\mathbb{Z}
are uniformly close because

\Delta_{\infty}(\Lambda_{0},\mathbb{Z}+\mathrm{i}\mathbb{Z})\leq k<\infty.

Next, let
M,N\in\mathbb{Z}
be arbitrary but fixed and introduce the entire function

g_{M,N}(z):=(z-\lambda_{M,N})\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha_{M,N}(z-\lambda_{M,N})^{2}/2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\\
(m,n)\neq(M,N)\end{subarray}}E\left(\frac{z-\lambda_{M,N}}{\lambda_{m,n}-\lambda_{M,N}},2\right),

where

\alpha_{M,N}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\\
(m,n)\neq(M,N)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{m,n}-\lambda_{M,N})^{2}}.

Denote
\delta:=\delta(\Lambda)
,
\Delta:=\Delta_{2}(\Lambda,\mathbb{Z}+\mathrm{i}\mathbb{Z})
, let
\eta<\max\{1,\delta\}/4
be arbitrary but fixed and set

\mathcal{B}_{\eta}:=\bigcup_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}B_{\eta}(\lambda_{m,n}).

Then, according to
[
Ear66
, Theorem 5 on p. 725–726]
,
g_{M,N}
is well-defined (i.e.,
\alpha_{M,N}
is finite) and has the following properties:

1.

g_{M,N}
has simple zeroes at
\Lambda
and no others;

2.

for all
\epsilon>0
, there exists
r_{0}>0
depending only on
\epsilon
,
\eta
,
\delta
and
\Delta
such that,

(a)

for all
z\not\in B_{r_{0}}(\lambda_{M,N})
,
\lvert g_{M,N}(z)\rvert\leq\mathrm{e}^{(\pi/2+\epsilon)\lvert z-\lambda_{M,N}\rvert^{2}}
,

(b)

for all
z\not\in B_{r_{0}}(\lambda_{M,N})\cup\mathcal{B}_{\eta}
,
\smash{\lvert g_{M,N}(z)\rvert\geq\mathrm{e}^{(\pi/2-\epsilon)\lvert z-\lambda_{M,N}\rvert^{2}}}
,

(c)

for all
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\lambda_{m,n}\not\in B_{r_{0}}(\lambda_{M,N})\implies\lvert g^{\prime}_{M,N}(\lambda_{m,n})\rvert\geq\mathrm{e}^{(\pi/2-\epsilon)\lvert\lambda_{m,n}-\lambda_{M,N}\rvert^{2}};

3.

there exists
C>0
depending only on
\eta
,
\delta
and
\Delta
such that,

(a)

for all
z\not\in\mathcal{B}_{\eta}
,
\lvert g_{M,N}(z)\rvert\geq C
,

(b)

for all
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\lvert g^{\prime}_{M,N}(\lambda_{m,n})\rvert\geq C
.

Next, introduce the function

\phi_{M,N}(z):=g_{M,N}(z)\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{f(\lambda_{m,n})}{(z-\lambda_{m,n})g^{\prime}_{M,N}(\lambda_{m,n})}

and note that
\lim_{z\to\lambda_{m,n}}\phi_{M,N}(z)=f(\lambda_{m,n})
, for all
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore, the function

\Phi_{M,N}(z):=\frac{f(z)-\phi_{M,N}(z)}{g_{M,N}(z)}

has removable singularities at
\lambda_{m,n}
and extends to an entire function.

Finally, let
\epsilon>0
be arbitrary but fixed and note that condition (
4
) implies that there exists an increasing, diverging sequence
(r_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}\in(1,\infty)
such that

\lvert f(z)\rvert\leq\mathrm{e}^{(\underline{\tau}+\epsilon)r_{k}^{2}},\qquad z\in C_{r_{k}},

for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, let
\gamma_{k}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{C}
be an arbitrary closed curve inside the annulus
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,r_{k}-1\leq\lvert z\rvert\leq r_{k}\}
, with winding number one around the origin, which has shortest distance to any lattice point at least
\eta
.

In the remainder of the proof, we will bound
\Phi_{M,N}
and
f
. For the former, we apply the triangle inequality to obtain

\lvert\Phi_{M,N}\rvert\leq\frac{\lvert f\rvert+\lvert\phi_{M,N}\rvert}{\lvert g_{M,N}\rvert}.

The first summand is bounded just like in
[
Ear66
, equations (5.5)–(5.7) on p. 732]
:

\frac{\lvert f(z)\rvert}{\lvert g_{M,N}(z)\rvert}\lesssim_{\eta,\delta,\Delta}\mathrm{e}^{-(\pi/2-\underline{\tau})r_{k}^{2}/4},\qquad z\in\gamma_{k}([0,1]),

for
k
sufficiently large (depending on
M
and
N
).
The second summand is bounded as in
[

2.3
The polynomial case and applications to Gabor phase retrieval

Our main result, Theorem
5
, holds, in particular, when
g\in\mathbb{C}[z]
is a polynomial. In this specific case, we can, however, derive a slightly stronger result.

Proposition 1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
be an entire function satisfying condition (
4
) and let
g\in\mathbb{C}[z]
be a polynomial of degree
q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}
be a uniformly discrete set with lower Beurling density
D^{-}(\Lambda)>2\underline{\tau}/\pi
.

1.

If
\lvert f\rvert\lesssim\lvert g\rvert
on
\Lambda
, then
f\in\mathbb{C}[z]
is a polynomial of degree
q
.

2.

If
\Lambda
contains a pair of infinite subsets lying on two parallel lines, then
f\sim g
if and only if
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
\Lambda
.

Proof.

1.

Since
\lvert f\rvert\lesssim\lvert g\rvert
on
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{C}
and
g
is a polynomial of degree
q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have

\limsup_{j\to\infty}\frac{\log\lvert f(\lambda_{j})\rvert}{\log(1+\lvert\lambda_{j}\rvert)}\leq\limsup_{j\to\infty}\frac{\log\lvert g(\lambda_{j})\rvert}{\log(1+\lvert\lambda_{j}\rvert)}=q.

Therefore, Theorem
4

\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\max_{z\in C_{r}}\lvert f(z)\rvert}{\log(1+r)}\leq q.

In particular,

\lvert f(z)\rvert\lesssim(1+\lvert z\rvert)^{q+1/2},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

We can now conclude by a standard argument

f
is an entire function and is therefore given by the convergent power series

f(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(j)}(0)}{j!}z^{j}.

Additionally, our estimate above together with Cauchy’s estimate says that

\lvert f^{(j)}(0)\rvert\lesssim\frac{j!}{r^{j}}\cdot(1+r)^{q+1/2},\qquad j\in\mathbb{N}_{0},~r>0.

Letting

f\in\mathbb{C}[z]
is a polynomial of degree

2.

It is clear that
f\sim g
implies that

\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
\Lambda

is a polynomial of degree

2.4
On the counterexamples and the necessity of certain constraints in our main results

As discussed in the introduction, counterexamples for the lines
a\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}
already appeared in
[
AW22
, cf. Remark 3 on p. 6]
. These examples have Gabor transforms of the form

\mathrm{e}^{-\pi\mathrm{i}x\omega}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\pi}{2}\left(x^{2}+\omega^{2}\right)}\left(\cosh\left(\frac{\pi}{2a}\left(\omega+\mathrm{i}x\right)\right)\mp\mathrm{i}\sinh\left(\frac{\pi}{2a}\left(\omega+\mathrm{i}x\right)\right)\right),\qquad x,\omega\in\mathbb{R},

up to a real multiplicative constant. After applying the Bargmann transform, one obtains (again up to a constant) the entire functions

\cosh\left(\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2a}z\right)\mp\mathrm{i}\sinh\left(\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2a}z\right),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C},

which can be written more compactly as

\cos\left(\frac{\pi z}{2a}\right)\pm\sin\left(\frac{\pi z}{2a}\right)\eqsim\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\pm\frac{\pi z}{2a}\right)=:f_{a,\pm}(z),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

These are entire functions of exponential type
\pi/2a
whose moduli agree on
a\mathbb{Z}+\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
but which differ by more than a global phase.

The counterexamples demonstrate that our recurring assumption — namely, that
\Lambda
contains a pair of infinite subsets lying on two parallel lines with distance strictly less than
\pi/2\kappa
, each forming an arithmetic progression with spacing strictly less than
\pi/2\kappa
— is essentially tight. To illustrate this, consider a shifted lattice
\Lambda=z_{0}+\omega_{1}\mathbb{Z}+\omega_{2}\mathbb{Z}
. Without loss of generality (by applying a rigid motion and exchanging
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
if necessary), we may assume that
z_{0}=0
,
\omega_{2}>0
, and
\omega_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
lies in the upper half-plane. Then,
\lvert\omega_{2}\rvert=\omega_{2}
, and

\frac{\lvert\operatorname{Im}(\omega_{1}\overline{\omega_{2}})\rvert}{\lvert\omega_{2}\rvert}=\operatorname{Im}(\omega_{1}).

If
\omega_{2}\geq\pi/2\kappa
, then one can rotate the functions
f_{\omega_{2},\pm}
by the angle between
\omega_{1}
and the imaginary axis to produce two entire functions of exponential type at most
\kappa
whose moduli agree on
\Lambda
but which differ by more than a global phase. Similarly, if
\operatorname{Im}\omega_{1}\geq\pi/2\kappa
, then rotating
f_{\operatorname{Im}\omega_{1},\pm}
by
\pi/2
gives another such pair. Thus, both conditions

\lvert\omega_{2}\rvert<\frac{\pi}{2\kappa},\qquad\frac{\lvert\operatorname{Im}(\omega_{1}\overline{\omega_{2}})\rvert}{\lvert\omega_{2}\rvert}<\frac{\pi}{2\kappa},

are necessary for uniqueness up to a global phase.

Corollary 4
.

Let
\kappa>0
and suppose that
\Lambda=z_{0}+\omega_{1}\mathbb{Z}+\omega_{2}\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{C}
is a shifted lattice such that either of the two conditions

\lvert\omega_{2}\rvert<\frac{\pi}{2\kappa},\qquad\frac{\lvert\operatorname{Im}(\omega_{1}\overline{\omega_{2}})\rvert}{\lvert\omega_{2}\rvert}<\frac{\pi}{2\kappa},

is violated. Then, there exist functions
f,g\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
of exponential type
\kappa
such that
f\not\sim g
and
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
\Lambda
.

Remark 6
.

One might hope to replace these geometric conditions with a stronger constraint on the density or covolume of
\Lambda
. However, this is not possible: for any
\kappa>0
and any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a shifted lattice
\Lambda
of covolume
\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)<\epsilon
that violates at least one of the two necessary conditions above. For instance, taking
\omega_{2}=\pi/2\kappa
and
\omega_{1}=\mathrm{i}2\kappa\epsilon/\pi
yields such a lattice. Hence, no smallness assumption on the covolume or largeness assumption on the lower Beurling density alone, regardless of strength, can ensure the conclusion of Corollary
2
or Theorem
5
.
