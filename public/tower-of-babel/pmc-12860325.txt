Case of posterior atlantoaxial dislocation with backward displacement of type II odontoid fracture
Authors: Abolfazl Rahimizadeh, Housain Soufiani, Mahan Amirzadeh, Shahrzad Rahimizadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Type II odontoid fracture with backward displacement and ptosis secondary to posterior atlantoaxial dislocation (AAD) due to hyperextension injuries are extremely rare.Case Description: A 72-year-old male with a history of cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) presented quadriparetic after a fall. The cervical magnetic resonance imaging revealed CSM plus posterior AAD associated with marked backward displacement of an odontoid type II fracture. After reduction of the odontoid process, C1 to T2 screw-hook-rod fixation and a subaxial cervical laminectomy/multilevel fusion resulted in marked neurological recovery.Conclusion: Here, a 72-year-old male with underlying CSM presented with the acute onset of quadriparesis following a fall. Having sustained backward displacement/ptosis secondary to posterior AAD from the hyperextension injury, the patient successfully underwent a combined closed/open reduction of the displacement combined with subaxial decompression and long segment fixation.

INTRODUCTION

The combination of posterior atlantoaxial dislocation (AAD) and type II odontoid fractures resulting in marked backward displacement/ptosis of the upper cervical spine is extremely rare.[1-4] We were able to identify 24 similar cases in the literature [Table 1].

However, here, we present the unique combination of both chronic underlying cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) plus the trauma-induced posterior AAD/type II odontoid fractures requiring closed reduction, C1 to T2 instrumentation, and subaxial cervical laminectomy/fusion to achieve adequate stabilization and good recovery of neurological function.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 72-year-old male with an 8-month history of unsteady gait/upper extremity paresthesias sustained a fall 4 days before presenting in a cervical collar and with severe quadriparesis (ASIA C). X-ray, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance images documented chronic cervical spondylotic changes with C5-C6 myelomalacia and the new acute posterior AAD/odontoid type II fracture [Figures 1 and 2]. Close reduction and appropriate odontoid realignment were achieved with skull traction using 15 kg and 10° head flexion. The next day, surgery includes the placement of bilateral C2 pedicle screws followed by supralaminar atlas hooks, a C3-C6 laminectomy, and C3 to T2 pedicle screw insertion/fusion. Then two rods were placed into the tulips of C1 laminar hooks to the tulips of T2 screws [Figure 3]. The postoperative course was uneventful and plain radiographs at the 5th postoperative day showed appropriate odontoid alignment and good cervicothoracic stabilization [Figure 4]. He was discharged a few days later. Ten weeks postoperatively, the patient was able to ambulate with a walker. At 18-month follow-up, his clinical status was intact (ASIA E.), and plain radiographs plus CT studies confirmed good approximation/fusion of odontoid to the axis and proper C1 hook to T2 screw-rod fixation [Figure 5].

DISCUSSION

Odontoid type II fractures are a relatively common type of traumatic cervical spine injury. Most are associated with anterior vs. posterior AAD. Our review of the literature yielded 24 similar cases of posterior AAD with odontoid fractures complicated by its marked posterior displacement and ptosis.[1-10] Interestingly, this is the first example of posterior displacement/ptosis in a patient with CSM. The median age at presentation was 47.9% years (ranging from 15 to 82 with male predominance of 79.1%).[1,3-10] Neck pain and stiffness were the cardinal presenting features, and the majority sustaining this kind of traumatic injury were neurologically asymptomatic (70.8%).[1,3,4,6-8,10] As anticipated, falls were the most common cause of this traumatic event in 17 cases[1,4,7-10] followed by motor vehicle accidents in 6 cases[2,3,5] and face hit by a brick in one case;[6] all, notably, were caused by cervical hyperextension injuries.[1-10]

Treatment

The treatment in these causes requires closed reduction with realignment of the odontoid typically by first attempting axial traction. Subsequently, the best surgical fixation includes both C1-C2 screw rod and transarticular C2-C1 fixations.[1,2,5,7,9,10] Anterior odontoid screw fixation is not recommended because of associated ligamentous affection and should be associated with C1-C2 fixation.[3,8,10] Irreducible cases require approximation of the odontoid to the axis with atlantoaxial reduction maneuvers after posterior transfacet mobilization. This can be achieved utilizing the appropriate size osteotome inside the C1-C2 facet joint and rotating/reducing it (i.e., using the Goel technique). Additional different maneuvers including levering techniques with application of L or T shape tools or C1-C1 C2-C2 transverse rod technique may be utilized. Notably, the C1-C1 transverse rod complex should be pushed anterocranially and C2-C2 transverse rod complex should be pulled back and caudally. After approximation of the odontoid process to the axis, bony fusion usually appears in <6 months.

CONCLUSION

In patients with preexisting mild CSM, a hyperextension traumatic event causing an odontoid fracture with posterior AAD may cause quadriparesis. In such instances, surgery requires reduction of the odontoid process, C1 to T2 screw-hook-rod fixation, and a subaxial cervical laminectomy combined with fusion.
