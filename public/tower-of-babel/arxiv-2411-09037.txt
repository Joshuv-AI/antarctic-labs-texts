A Transformer-Based Visual Piano Transcription Algorithm
Uros Zivanovic, Carlos Eduardo Cancino-Chacón

Abstract
Automatic music transcription (AMT) for musical performances is a long standing problem in the field of Music Information Retrieval (MIR). Visual piano transcription (VPT) is a multimodal subproblem of AMT which focuses on extracting a symbolic representation of a piano performance from visual information only (e.g., from a top-down video of the piano keyboard). Inspired by the success of Transformers for audio-based AMT, as well as their recent successes in other computer vision tasks, in this paper we present a Transformer based architecture for VPT. The proposed VPT system combines a piano bounding box detection model with an onset and pitch detection model, allowing our system to perform well in more naturalistic conditions like imperfect image crops around the piano and slightly tilted images.

Introduction

Automatic music transcription (AMT) aims to generate a symbolic music representation given some input, most commonly an audio signal
[
4
]
.
The generated symbolic music representation is often inspired by MIDI, with notes being represented by their start time (onset), release time (offset), pitch, and loudness (velocity).
An AMT algorithm therefore aims to predict one or more of these features from the given input.
Various MIR tasks such as music search (indexing, recommendation), automatic music accompaniment, and music generation benefit from the symbolic music representation given by an AMT algorithm
[
4
,
23
]
.
High-quality AMT systems also enable the creation of new datasets
[
29
,
16
]
which can be used for a variety of MIR tasks.

Although audio based methods have been the primary focus throughout past research into AMT, video based visual transcription methods have also been considered.
Especially in the case of automatic piano transcription, a top-down video can provide a significant amount of information on the musical performance, with the ability to see note onset, offset and pitch directly from the key being pressed and released.
Videos with this setup are a popular way to showcase piano related content on platforms such as YouTube.

Related Work

Video based piano AMT models have been attempted using various methods in the past.
Because of the identifiable black and white keys of the piano, traditional computer vision tools are able to locate a piano in an image and even separate each key.
This enables traditional computer vision algorithms to be able to detect onsets on the piano with a high degree of accuracy
[
25
,
1
]
.
Such an approach is very difficult to apply to various lighting and environmental conditions however.
It also struggles in cases where the piano keys may be lightly obstructed, or when the piano is at an angle in the image.
Additionally, traditional computer vision tools are unable to capture and reason about complex movements across multiple frames in a video.

More recently, CNN’s have been shown to work well for visual piano transcription
[
2
,
15
,
24
]
.
By utilizing a modified ResNet model
[
12
]
, multiple works
[
15
,
24
]
have been able to make onset and pitch predictions directly on a cropped image of the piano.
Koepke et al.
[
15
]
have also shown that their vision based model can compensate for poor quality audio, helping an audio based AMT algorithm perform better.
There are also systems that are explicitly designed around using both video and audio simultaneously.
Such an audio-visual model is presented by
[
28
]
.
In their approach, they use the video model primarily for eliminating harmonic errors.
This is done by tracking the pianists hands and eliminating any predicted notes that would be impossible to play from that position.
Although Transformers have not been used for the task of VPT yet, they have been used for audio based transcription.

They have been shown to work well for both multi-track transcription
[
8
]
and single-track piano transcription
[
11
]
.
By utilizing end-to-end machine learning architectures, these works move the problem from hand designing a system for AMT to creating a high quality dataset and fitting a model to it.
Because of this, despite the difference in domain (audio vs video), the underlying Transformer architecture between these audio based methods and ours is similar, the notable difference being the lack of a Transformer Decoder in our model.

Since the Vision Transformer
[
6
]
showed that Transformers can compete with CNN’s at scale in the ImageNet
[
22
]
benchmark there has been an increased interest in Transformers for computer vision.
When adapting Vision Transformers to video, there have been numerous architectures proposed, although most focus on classification.
SwinBERT
[
18
]
is a multi-modal architecture which is able to take a video clip and generate a text caption describing it.
While SwinBERT uses both a Swin Transformer
[
19
]
and a Multimodal Transformer Encoder to generate the caption text, we are able to avoid needing a second model thanks to the simplicity of piano transcription compared to language.

3
Visual Transcription Architecture

In this section we showcase the details of our implementation.
For a visual overview of the system, see Fig.
1
.
In summary, we take in a video and perform bounding box detection looking for the piano keys.
Once identified, this region of the video is cropped out and given to the Transformer-based onset and pitch detector.
The onset and pitch detector iterates over the entire cropped video, predicting for each note whether there is an onset or not in the given time-window.
This gives us a likelihood for each piano key indicating the presence of a note onset in a given frame of the video.
We then perform post-processing to convert these likelihoods into actual note onset times.

Figure 1:
Overview of the model architecture.

3.1
Bounding Box Detection

We fine-tune a pre-trained YOLOv8
[
13
]
model on the task of piano bounding box detection in images.
The model accepts a
640\times 640
image and gives a set of bounding boxes with varying confidences.
In all datasets used for this work the location of the piano is static throughout each individual video.
Therefore, we make the assumption that the bounding box within a single video does not change over time.
Inference is performed by iterating through the frames at the start of the video and picking the bounding box with the highest predicted confidence from these.
In contrast to past CNN-based approaches
[
15
,
24
]
, we do not make any assumptions on the exact aspect ratio and shape of the bounding box.
This is an important detail, as it means that the aspect ratio of the subsequent crop changes based on the bounding box that was detected.

3.2
Onset and Pitch Detection

For the onset and pitch detector we use a pre-trained VideoMAE-Base model
[
26
]
which has been pre-trained on the Kinetics-400
[
14
]
dataset for 1600 epochs.
The need for pre-trained weights arises out of the limited amount of inductive biases present in Vision Transformer models and is often a key element to achieving good performance with them
[
6
,
3
]
.
Each sample consists of a 16 frame video with a resolution of
224\times 224
.
We use a temporal resolution of 30 frames per second (fps), which is the smallest frame-rate in the R3s dataset (described in Sec.
4.1
).
This temporal resolution is different from the temporal resolution the model was pre-trained on and is one of the key elements in the domain shift between pre-training and fine-tuning stages.
Therefore, each input into the model consists of
16\times\frac{1}{30}=0.5\bar{3}
seconds of video and has a shape of
T\times W\times H=16\times 224\times 224
.
Compared to previously mentioned research, our model has a much smaller frame resolution while taking in a much larger temporal window.
Although the low resolution allows for a relatively large number of frames as input, it also makes the model highly dependant on a sufficiently good crop around the piano keys in order to have a high number of pixels per key.
We additionally assume that all data follows the same setup as is found in R3s, specifically, that the pianist is above the piano in the image (facing downwards), and that the keys go from C7 to A1 as you go from left to right in the image.

Following along with the fine-tuning procedure outlined in
[
26
]
, we remove the Transformer decoder from the pre-trained model and average over all the outputs from the last layer.
Then, similarly to the approach taken in
[
15
]
, we place 88 linear layer heads, one for each key on the piano, on top of the last layer average.
Lastly, we add a sigmoid to the output of all heads in order to predict the likelihood.

Inference
:
When performing inference over an entire video, we iterate over all frames and then concatenate the predictions to produce an array of shape
88\times(\verb|number_of_frames|-16)
.
Sixteen frames are missing because we start predictions on the 8th frame and end 8 frames early due to needing 16 frames for a full sample.
An alternative to this approach could be to pad the start and end of the video.
With a temporal resolution of
30\text{\,}\mathrm{fps}
, all predicted notes get assigned to their nearest frame, leading to a potential error of
15\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
even given perfect predictions.

3.3
Post-Processing

After iterating through the video, the resulting array contains significant amounts of noise.
To remove this we apply a Gaussian filter (similarly to
[
15
]
) along the time axis with
\sigma=1
and a kernel radius of 16.
We then threshold the smoothed array using a value of 0.5.
Lastly, we find regions where a note was detected over multiple frames sequentially and assign the onset for that note to the middle of that detected region.
At this point we have an onset-only piano roll, which can be converted directly into MIDI.

4.2
Video Pre-Processing

In order to make data loading easier, videos are first transcoded from their native frame-rate to a frame-rate of 30 using FFMPEG.
2
2

2

https://ffmpeg.org/

We then crop out the piano using either the crops provided with the dataset (in the case of PianoYT), or using the bounding boxes detected by our YOLOv8 model (in the case of R3s).
We additionally make sure that the image is in the correct orientation and is mirrored correctly.
Specifically, this requires rotating all PianoYT images 180 degrees.

Because our onset and pitch detection model is pre-trained on a square
224\times 224
image, we experiment with a variety of transforms for modifying the rectangular crop so that it fits the desired resolution.
These are visualized in Fig.
2
.

Crop Square Transformations
:

1.

Stretch to fit
is the simplest transformation where we take the crop and simply resize the image to the desired resolution.

2.

Modify aspect ratio
involves resizing only the height of the cropped image by a set value.
During training we used a value of 4.3 which results in a transformation from an aspect ratio of 16:9 to 5:12.
The motivation behind this transformation is that by maintaining a constant aspect ratio we allow the model to make more accurate assumptions about the distance between two points in the image.

3.

Split crop in two
involves cutting the crop in half and then stacking the two halves on top of one-another.
This effectively increases the horizontal resolution of the processed image, making each individual key much easier to distinguish.
Because the resulting image is close to the desired square aspect ratio, we can avoid having to use the stretch transform.

4.

Split crop in two and stretch to fit
is the same as
split crop in two
, except that the resulting stacked image is stretched to fully fill the square aspect ratio.
Similarly to the
stretch to fit
transform, this results in a final aspect ratio that depends on the size of the crop.

When training in color, we normalize all images using the same values used during pre-training.
Specifically the mean and standard deviation are derived from the ImageNet
[
5
]
dataset.

Figure 2:
An illustration of different image pre-processing techniques aimed at fitting the piano crop into a square resolution.

Video Augmentations
:
We experiment with various data augmentations including spatial jittering, adding Gaussian noise, as well as color jitter (changing image hue, brightness, and sharpness).
Spatial jitter is done by moving any side of the crop by a random number of pixels toward or away from the center of the image.
We also experiment with a grayscale transformation with the aim of removing potentially unimportant information from the image.
When using grayscale we do not normalize the images as we would with color.

Bounding Box Model
:
Training for the piano bounding box model follows a different set of pre-processing steps which are taken primarily from
[
13
]
.
Images are first normalized using mean 0 and standard deviation 1.
We then apply a random horizontal flip with a probabality of 0.5 as well as jittering the hue, saturation, brightness, and scale of each image.
Lastly, we also use the mosaic transform to combine multiple images together into new samples.

4.3
MIDI Pre-Processing

We first assign each note onset to the nearest frame in the video.
Then we additionally assign positive values to the next and previous frames.
This is done in order to increase the number of positive values in the dataset and also to deal with any possible differences in bias in the datasets we train on.
Because we use 16 frames as input to the model, when the 9th frame is positive but 8th negative, we assign a label of 0.5 as opposed to 1 to that note.
This effectively means that each note stretches across 4 frames, of which 2 have a label of 1, while 2 have a label of 0.5.
While this action does risk reducing the temporal accuracy of the model, we are in turn increasing the number of positive samples in the dataset.
In order to further increase the ratio of positive samples to negative, we also cut out 95% of samples with no notes present.

4.4
Experimental Setup

When training we utilize 2 Tesla-V100-32GB GPUs with a total batch size of 16.
All experiments are done using the AdamW
[
20
]
optimizer with a weight decay of 0.05.
The learning rate follows a cosine decay function with a warmup length of either 5% or 10% of total steps depending on the experiment.
All reported learning rates are the values given after applying the linear scaling rule
[
9
]
.
For our onset and pitch detector we use binary cross-entropy as the loss.
We also experiment with class weights between 1 and 4 to balance between model precision and recall for the positive (e.g. note on) class.
Class weights are applied directly to the output of the loss function and give the ability to control the weight of the positive samples in the dataset.
In the case of the piano, if we wanted equal weights for a sample where only 1 note is active, we would use a weight of 88 to set the loss for that one (positive) sample equal to the loss of all other (negative) samples.
After all pre-processing, R3s and PianoYT combined hold enough samples for 96,367 steps at a batch size of 16.

Bounding Box Training Setup
:
We train the bounding box model using a single GeForce GTX 1080 Ti with a batch size of 16.
We use a learning rate of 0.01 with the AdamW optimizer for the first 10k steps, and then SGD for the rest.
A weight decay of
5\times 10^{-4}
is used with AdamW and a momentum of 0.937 with SGD.

4.5
Evaluation

We evaluate model performance using the transcription evaluation utilities provided by
mir_eval
[
21
]
.

mir_eval
matches as many notes as possible by checking if the distance between predicted note onset/pitch and reference note onset/pitch is less than set tolerance values.
MIR evaluation metrics such as precision, recall, and F-measure are then calculated using this matching.
All settings and tolerances are kept at their defaults except the onset tolerance, which we increase from
50\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
to
\sim
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
.
This corresponds to an increase from an onset tolerance of
\sim\pm 1
to
\pm 3
frames, meaning that a predicted onset up to 3 frames away from the true onset would now be considered correct.
When comparing to the tolerance used during training (described in Sec.
4.3
), this is 1 frame larger.
For an onset that is 3 frames (
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
) early, this is on the limit of being visually imperceptible
[
7
]
.
Therefore, for onsets that are 3 or less frames early when compared to the ground truth, the miss-timing would not be noticeable when viewing the video.

5
Experiments

5.1
Bounding Box Model

In this section we showcase the training results for the bounding box model.
We train the YOLOv8 model for 100 epochs and use the weights with the highest mAP@50 score on the test set as the final weights.
mAP@50 is a statistic used for bounding box detection models which measures the area under the precision-recall curve at a model confidence of 50.
The final model achieves a mAP@50 of 70.4, precision of 73.8, recall of 65.4, and F-measure of 69.3 over the test set.
Although training was over 100 epochs, the final (best performing) weights are taken from the end of the 55th epoch.
In fact, we find that the loss on the test set only increases after this point, indicating overfitting.
While the model could likely benefit from more data, our results in Sec.
5.3
indicate that the provided crops on the R3s dataset are of sufficient quality to be used as inputs to the onset and pitch prediction model.

6
Discussion

6.1
Difference in Metrics Between Datasets

The results shown in Tab.
3
illustrate a large difference in performance between the PianoYT and R3s datasets.
Specifically, the F-measure on R3s is
\sim 15.7\%
better than on PianoYT despite PianoYT having a higher quality crop around the piano.
We identify a few reasons for this.
Because R3s originally comes from only 27 different practice sessions, this limits the amount of variety in the dataset, which allows the model to recognize what pieces were practiced in each session and make more informed decisions based on this.
Another reason could be the difference between the true ground truth in R3s and the transcribed quasi ground truth in PianoYT.
Tong et al.
[
26
]
found that dataset quality can have a large impact on the performance of downstream fine-tuning tasks when using a VideoMAE model.

6.2
Image Resolution and Pre-Processing

We have presented 4 different methods for fitting the crop of the piano into the desired resolution.
Our best performing model, shown in Tab.
3
, utilizes the transforms visualized in Fig.
2
Image 3.
Compared to the top F-measure in Tab.
2
, which utilizes the transforms corresponding to Fig.
2
Image 1, our best performing model performs
\sim 3.9\%
better on R3s.
These results indicate that the model benefits from grayscale as well as having a higher horizontal resolution.
When comparing our model inputs to the VPT systems presented by
[
15
,
24
]
, our onset and pitch detection model takes a much smaller resolution image as input.
Our results therefore show that a high resolution input is not required in order to perform VPT successfully.

7
Conclusions

We identify various directions that could be worth investigating from the results presented here.
Training on a higher frame rate or with a smaller window size could help improve the low temporal accuracy of the model.
Our method is also highly influenced by the model settings used during pre-training.
One solution to this could be to do pre-training specifically for the task of VPT.
Alternatively, one could modify the resolution of the model by scaling the positional embeddings as is done in
[
6
]
.
A Transformer Encoder + Decoder could also be used to predict MIDI-like tokens directly from the provided video clip.
In this case, one could use an even larger temporal size such as 32 frames and process approximately a second of video at a time.
This could function similarly to
[
11
]
and would remove the need for a hand made post-processing pipeline.
Finally, some improvements can likely be had by simply training the model we present here for longer or with more data.

We have shown that a Transformer based VPT system can compete with and even outperform CNN based ones.
In order to achieve this, we have utilized pre-trained model weights along with image pre-processing methods to minimize the domain shift between the pre-training and fine-tuning model training stages.
As a part of our system we have also presented a piano bounding box model, which allows us to automatically create crops on which to perform model inference and training.
The effect of positive class weights on model performance has also been investigated, highlighting the importance and impact class weights have on the final model.
Although our implementation follows a similar architecture to past CNN based ones, it showcases the potential that Transformer based models have and illustrates an exciting future for Transformer based multi-modal MIR architectures.
