Risk factors for second primary cancer in patients who survive breast cancer
Authors: Phillip Park, Keunkyun Kim, Dong-woo Choi, Heejung Chae, So-Youn Jung, Hyun-Jin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer survivors are living longer. However, the risk of second primary cancer (SPC) remains a concern. The aim of this study was to identify risk factors for synchronous SPC (sSPC) and metachronous SPC (mSPC) among patients who survive breast cancer and evaluate causes of death. This population-based cohort study used data from the South Korean Cancer Public Library Database, which integrates the national cancer registry with insurance claim and health screening data. Female patients diagnosed with primary breast cancer between 2012 and 2019 were included in the study. SPCs were defined as new histologically distinct malignancies occurring six months or more (mSPC) or less than six months (sSPC) after breast cancer diagnosis. Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify risk factors for SPC. Cumulative incidence and causes of death were examined by cancer stage and age. Among 15,430 women with breast cancer who met the eligibility criteria, 497 developed sSPC and 812 developed mSPC. In multivariable models, an age ≥70 vs <40 years was associated with a higher risk of both sSPC (hazard ratio [HR], 3.58; 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.01–6.36; P < 0.001) and mSPC (HR, 3.18; 95% CI, 2.06–4.91; P < 0.001). Fasting blood sugar ≥140 mg/dL was associated with sSPC (HR, 1.61; 95% CI, 1.06–2.45; P = 0.02), but not with mSPC. Cumulative-incidence curves showed a higher prevalence of thoracic and gastrointestinal mSPC in older and lower-income strata, consistent with Cox results. Although smoking was more prevalent in low-income groups, it was not an independent predictor. Cause-of-death analyses indicated that SPCs more often accounted for death in older survivors and in those with early-stage index breast cancer. The results of this study identify metabolic and socioeconomic factors as key predictors of SPC in patients who survive breast cancer. Tailored survivorship strategies are needed for high-risk subgroups, including older patients and those with low incomes or poor metabolic profiles.Subject terms: Cancer, Oncology, Risk factors

Introduction

Cancer is a leading cause of death worldwide. In 2022, ~2.3 million individuals were diagnosed with cancer globally. Breast cancer was the most frequently diagnosed malignancy and the leading cause of cancer death in women, accounting for 665,684 deaths1. Breast cancer was also the most common cancer in women in South Korea in 2022, with 28,861 new cases and 2744 deaths2. Advances in early detection and therapeutic management have contributed to the increased survival of patients with breast cancer. This prolonged life expectancy is accompanied by a heightened risk of developing second primary cancer (SPC), defined as a distinct malignant tumor with a pathological morphology differing from that of the initial primary cancer.

Previous studies have demonstrated that an SPC can develop as a late complication of breast cancer treatment, which can include radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and hormone therapy3,4. A study of U.S. SEER-Medicare data found that approximately 13% of older patients who survived breast cancer developed an SPC during a median follow-up period of 44 months, with lung, colorectal, and urinary cancers the most common non-breast SPCs5. In a Swiss cohort, female survivors of cancer exhibited an overall standardized incidence ratio of ~1.2 for SPC compared to the general population, with higher risks for sites such as the lung and digestive organs6. A recent Korean population-based study showed that ~5.9% of patients who survived breast cancer developed a non-breast SPC within 10 years; the risk of an SPC was significantly higher in older patients and those who received combined systemic therapy7. Other studies have identified modifiable risk factors such as obesity, smoking, and hypertension as significant predictors of SPC development4,5,8–10. Collectively, these findings underscore the multifactorial nature of SPC risk among patients who survive breast cancer and highlight the need for tailored long-term surveillance strategies11,12.

Most previous studies investigating SPC have been limited by small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, or reliance on single-center data. Differing SPC definitions and a lack of detailed clinical information have further restricted the generation of representative or generalizable insights13,14. Studies using large, representative, and multicenter datasets that integrate population-based cancer registries with insurance claim and health screening data are required to overcome these issues10.

In this study, we used an integrated nationwide dataset to provide a more comprehensive and accurate assessment of SPC risk factors. The nationwide coverage and detailed clinical information enabled a nuanced analysis of patient characteristics, treatment, mortality, and long-term outcomes, offering a stronger foundation for the evidence of SPC risk in patients who survive breast cancer.

Results

Patient characteristics

In total, 14,121 patients had primary breast cancer without a second primary cancer during follow-up (PBC group), 497 developed sSPC, and 812 developed mSPC. During a median follow-up of 5.24 years (interquartile range, 3.37–7.33 years), the cohort contributed a total of 86,731 person-years of observation. Baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. Age distribution differed significantly among the groups (P < 0.001), with younger patients ( < 40 years) more common in the PBC group, whereas patients aged ≥70 years were more frequent in the sSPC and mSPC groups.

Tumor stage also differed significantly (P < 0.001), with stage IV tumors more frequent in the sSPC group and stage III tumors more frequent in the mSPC group compared with the PBC group. Risk factors for SPC

The results of the univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models are presented in Table 2. Older age was consistently associated with a significantly increased risk of developing both sSPC and mSPC compared with that of patients aged under 40 years. In the multivariable model, patients aged ≥70 years showed the highest risk of sSPC (hazard ratio [HR], 3.58; 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.01–6.36; P < 0.001) and mSPC (HR, 3.18; CI 2.06–4.91; P < 0.001), with a clear dose–response trend across age groups. Regarding tumor characteristics, a higher American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage at initial diagnosis was an independent predictor of increased risk; patients with stage IV disease at diagnosis had a significantly higher risk of sSPC (HR, 2.48; 95% CI 1.63–3.79; P < 0.001) and mSPC (HR, 1.95; 95% CI, 1.22–3.14; P = 0.006) than those with stage I disease. Among metabolic factors, patients with FBS levels ≥140 mg/dL had a significantly increased risk of sSPC (HR, 1.61; 95% CI, 1.06–2.45; P = 0.024) but not mSPC. BMI, WSTC, and smoking status were not significantly associated with SPC risk after adjustment. Breast cancer subtype showed no association with sSPC. Alcohol consumption appeared to be protective in univariable models but the association did not remain significant after adjustment. No significant association was found between household income and sSPC risk. However, patients enrolled in the Medical Aid Program (MAP) showed a higher risk of mSPC in univariable analysis (HR, 1.53; 95% CI, 1.03–2.27; P = 0.035); this association was not significant after adjustment (HR, 1.48; 95% CI, 0.82–2.66; P = 0.190) when compared with the top 1–10% income group. Older age and advanced tumor stage were independent predictors of both sSPC and mSPC, and a high FBS level independently predicted sSPC; by contrast, MAP was not independently associated with mSPC after adjustment.

SPC risk across cancer types

Among the 497 synchronous SPC (sSPC) cases, gastrointestinal cancers were the most frequent (n = 169, 34.0%), followed by gynecological cancers (n = 97, 19.5%) and thoracic cancers (n = 64, 12.9%). Other cancer types occurred less frequently, including skin (n = 56), lymphoid and hematopoietic malignancies (n = 16), head and neck cancers (n = 11), bone and soft tissue cancers (n = 18), brain tumors (n = 7), urologic cancers (n = 20), and other sites (n = 39).

Among the 812 metachronous SPC (mSPC) cases, gastrointestinal cancers were also the most common (n = 237, 29.2%), followed by gynecological cancers (n = 126, 15.5%) and thoracic cancers (n = 104, 12.8%). Additional mSPC cases included skin (n = 95), lymphoid and hematopoietic malignancies (n = 55), head and neck cancers (n = 18), bone and soft tissue cancers (n = 36), brain tumors (n = 41), urologic cancers (n = 30), and other sites (n = 70).

These distributions provide context for the overall Cox model results presented in Table 2, as the hazard ratios reflect the aggregated risk across these predominant cancer types. For less frequent SPC categories, the limited number of events may have reduced statistical power for subtype-specific analyses.

Cumulative incidence analyses for mSPC were stratified by age group, FBS level, and household income level across different cancer types and are presented in Supplementary Figs. 2–4 in the Supplement, with significant findings presented in Figs. 1–3.

Stratification by age group revealed significant differences in cumulative SPC incidence for gastrointestinal (P < 0.001), thoracic (P < 0.001), urologic (P < 0.001), and skin (P < 0.017) cancers. Patients aged ≥70 years showed consistently higher cumulative incidence rates across several types of cancer (Fig. 1).

Stratification by FBS level initially showed a significant difference in the cumulative incidence of gastrointestinal cancer (P < 0.01), with the highest incidence observed in patients with levels ≥140 mg/dL (Fig. 2). However, when gastrointestinal cancer was further subdivided into upper gastrointestinal tract, colorectal, pancreatic, and biliary cancers, no significant differences were observed.

When stratified by household income and smoking status, significant disparities in the cumulative incidence of thoracic cancer were observed (P < 0.001), with incidence notably higher among patients enrolled in the MAP (Fig. 3). Further stratification of patients enrolled in the MAP by smoking status revealed no significant differences in the cumulative incidence, suggesting that the elevated risk in this population is unlikely to be explained solely by smoking.

These findings indicate that age, metabolic status, and socioeconomic factors may contribute to the heterogeneity of SPC risk across cancer types, with gastrointestinal and thoracic cancers showing the clearest stratification patterns.

Index breast cancer and SPC as causes of death

Cause of death was analyzed in deceased patients with SPC; the results are presented in Supplementary Table 2. Among patients with mSPC, the proportion of deaths due to the index breast cancer increased along with the AJCC stage, from 22.6% in stage I to 93.1% in stage IV. Conversely, the proportion of deaths attributed to the mSPC decreased as the AJCC stage increased, from 51.6% in stage I to 6.9% in stage IV. Breast cancer remains the predominant cause of death in patients with advanced-stage primary tumors. A similar pattern was observed when the sSPC and mSPC groups were combined. In this analysis, the index breast cancer accounted for 20.7% of deaths in stage I, increasing to 88.9% in stage IV, whereas SPC-related mortality decreased from 58.6% in stage I to 9.3% in stage IV.

When stratified by age, the reverse trend was observed. Among patients aged <40 years with mSPC, the index breast cancer was the leading cause of death (80.0%), whereas in patients aged ≥70 years, the mSPC was a more common cause of death (46.4%) than the index breast cancer (39.3%). A similar trend was observed when the sSPC and mSPC groups were combined, with the proportion of SPC-related deaths rising from 8.3% in patients <40 years to 41.3% in those aged ≥70 years.

Discussion

In this population-based study, we used the Korean CPLD to investigate the risk factors associated with the development of SPC among patients who survive breast cancer. Our findings revealed that older age was the strongest and most consistent predictor of an SPC, with threefold higher HRs for sSPC and mSPC in patients aged ≥70 years than in those aged <40 years. A higher AJCC stage at initial diagnosis was also independently associated with SPC risk, particularly stage IV disease, which showed the greatest HR. Among metabolic indicators, an FBS level ≥140 mg/dL significantly increased the risk of sSPC, especially gastrointestinal cancer, but not mSPC. Furthermore, patients enrolled in the MAP, representing the lowest income group, experienced a higher incidence of mSPC than those in the higher income groups, suggesting that socioeconomic disparities can contribute to SPC risk. These findings identify age, pathological stage, metabolic indicators, and socioeconomic status as key predictors of SPC risk, with patterns differing between sSPC and mSPC.

The emergence of older age as a strong and independent risk factor for SPC is consistent with the findings of previous studies15–17. In the present study, patients aged ≥70 years showed a markedly higher SPC incidence across several cancer types, including gastrointestinal, thoracic, and urologic cancers. Age-related immunosenescence, prolonged latency periods, and cumulative exposure to genotoxic treatments may explain this increased vulnerability to SPC18.

A higher tumor stage at initial breast cancer diagnosis was also significantly associated with SPC risk, suggesting that aggressive primary disease or the intensive therapy required to treat it may influence SPC risk. Although previous studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding the association between cancer stage and SPC risk, our findings align with the data from large-scale cohort studies, which indicated a stage-dependent gradient in SPC development5.

An elevated FBS level was independently associated with a higher risk of SPC, particularly gastrointestinal cancer. This finding is consistent with the findings of previous studies, which linked hyperglycemia and metabolic dysfunction to carcinogenic pathways such as chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and enhanced cellular proliferation19–23. Although metabolic syndrome has been studied extensively in relation to primary cancers, its role in SPCs warrants further investigation. A population-based cohort study in Korea found that women with metabolic syndrome had significantly increased risks of colon and rectal cancers, with HRs of 1.20 and 1.15, respectively24. Metabolic syndrome has also been independently associated with an increased risk of kidney cancer, especially among younger individuals25.

Although the higher prevalence of smoking among patients enrolled in the MAP may have contributed to the observed increase in thoracic SPC, current smoking status was not a strong risk factor. This suggests that environmental exposure to secondhand smoke, rather than active smoking alone, may play a more substantial role in the development of thoracic cancer in patients with low incomes. Low socioeconomic status has been associated with an increased risk of lung and thoracic cancers by previous studies that emphasized the independent effects of income-related disparities on cancer risk beyond smoking behavior. In the present study, cumulative incidence analyses further demonstrated the heterogeneity in SPC risk across cancer types and subgroups. Thoracic and gastrointestinal cancers showed consistent stratification by age, metabolic status, and income level, suggesting that tailored surveillance may be particularly relevant for these cancers26,27.

The present study also showed that older patients were more likely to die of the SPC than of the index breast cancer. These findings highlight the importance of strengthening SPC surveillance strategies in older patients who survive breast cancer, who may otherwise be overlooked during routine follow-up28.

The strengths of this study lie in its use of a large population-based cohort linking the national cancer registry with insurance claim, health screening, and mortality data, enhancing representativeness and outcome ascertainment. Tumor markers and stages at diagnosis were also considered, as were causes of death.

This study also has some limitations. First, analyses were conducted on a 10% stratified sample of all incident cancers in Korea rather than the full national cohort. Consequently, several subgroup cells were small, which might have inflated the variance and rendered some estimates unstable. The apparent elevation of SPC risk among patients with stage IV disease could, in part, reflect small-number effects and surveillance bias rather than a purely biological gradient29,30. Second, despite linkage to health screening and insurance claim data, several potentially important confounders were unavailable or incomplete, including radiotherapy fields and dose, detailed systemic therapy regimens and duration, hereditary susceptibility and family history, reproductive factors, environmental or occupational exposures, and time-varying socioeconomic circumstances31. Finally, lifestyle variables contained missing values, and although we used complete-case approaches in stratified analyses, selection bias cannot be excluded32. Overall, these limitations suggest that some subgroup findings should be interpreted with caution and validated in future full-cohort analyses.

Our results identified older age, advanced-stage disease, hyperglycemia, and lower socioeconomic status as important predictors of SPC development in patients who survive breast cancer. These findings support the need for individualized survivorship strategies that account for the long-term SPC risk, particularly in vulnerable subgroups.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective, population-based cohort study analyzed data from the Cancer Public Library Database (CPLD), which is linked to national insurance claim and health screening data. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Cancer Center (NCC2024-0070), which waived the requirement for informed consent. All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki. The manuscript was prepared in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

In 2022, the Korean Ministry of Health and Welfare launched the Korea-Clinical data Utilization network for Research Excellence (K-CURE) project10, with the primary goal of establishing a database linking the Korea Central Cancer Registry with National Health Insurance Service (NHIS), Health Insurance Review & Assessment Service, and Statistics Korea data. This integration enables access to a wide range of information, including cancer registries, screening records, insurance claims, and mortality data. Follow-up time was calculated from the date of primary breast cancer diagnosis to the date of SPC diagnosis, death, or the administrative censoring date.

The Collaborative Stage (CS) data collection system involves a retrospective national cohort of patients with cancer33. These patient samples are stratified random samples from the cancer registry data of the Korea Central Cancer Registry. Breast cancer CS data include information about tumor size, regional lymph node status, and expression of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2). This study analyzed data from patients diagnosed with breast cancer between 2012 and 2019.

As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, the study population included patients with breast cancer who survived for a minimum of one-year post-diagnosis. Male patients, patients in which the breast cancer stage was unknown, patients aged <30 years, and those aged >80 years were excluded from the study. In addition, patients without a special calculation system were excluded to reduce misclassification of incident breast cancers and ensure complete capture of cancer-related care in claims data. Patients with thyroid cancer as the SPC were also excluded. Finally, 15,430 patients were included in the analysis.

Information on breast cancer diagnosis and tumor characteristics was extracted from the K-CURE CS dataset. Breast tumor characteristics included AJCC stage, histologic type, and ER, PR, and HER2 statuses. Based on this information, breast cancer was classified as HER2-enriched, luminal A, luminal B, or triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Health examination variables, including fasting blood sugar, body mass index, waist circumference, smoking status, and alcohol consumption, were obtained from the NHIS health screening database. For each participant, the health screening measurement obtained closest to the date of breast cancer diagnosis was used to define baseline covariates. Repeated measurements during follow-up were not incorporated into the analysis. Smoking status and alcohol consumption were assessed through self-reported questionnaires administered during the health examination. Smoking was categorized as current smoker or non-smoker, and alcohol consumption was classified as current drinker or non-drinker. Fasting blood samples were collected after an overnight fast, and serum glucose levels were measured using standardized laboratory procedures. Information on patient lifestyle and health-screening data were collected from the NHIS.

Although the K-CURE project was launched in 2022, it integrates retrospectively collected registry and administrative data from prior years. We selected 2012–2019 to ensure a minimum potential follow-up period and stable registry completeness across linked databases.

SPC definition

SPC was identified using the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3) and defined as a subsequent primary cancer that was histologically and anatomically distinct from the initial breast cancer. SPCs were categorized as synchronous SPCs (sSPCs), diagnosed within six months of the index breast cancer diagnosis, or metachronous SPCs (mSPCs), diagnosed more than six months after the initial diagnosis. All SPCs included in the analysis met these definitions and were classified accordingly. The detailed classification of SPCs by cancer type and the corresponding ICD-O-3 codes are provided in Supplementary Table 1 in the Supplement.

Statistical analysis

Chi-squared tests were performed to evaluate differences in the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of patients with and without SPCs and to determine the association between medication types and SPC development. To analyze the cumulative incidence of SPC, patients were stratified by age, fasting blood sugar (FBS) level, cancer stage, and household income. The cumulative incidence of SPC was estimated using the cumulative incidence function, with death treated as a competing risk; group differences were assessed using Gray’s test.

To evaluate the effects of covariates on SPC incidence, a cause-specific hazard model was used that accounted for the competing risk of death. Separate univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were constructed for sSPC and mSPC. The covariates included age, breast cancer subtype, BMI, WSTC, FBS level, AJCC stage, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and household income. FBS was included as a metabolic indicator because it showed significant differences between the PBC and SPC groups at baseline and has established biological relevance to carcinogenesis. Other metabolic markers available in the health screening data, such as blood lipid measures, were evaluated in preliminary analyses but did not demonstrate significant between-group differences and were therefore not included in the final multivariable models. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.1.0 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), SAS Studio software version 5.2 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), and Python software version 3.7.10 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA). The significance level for all statistical analyses was set at P < 0.05.
