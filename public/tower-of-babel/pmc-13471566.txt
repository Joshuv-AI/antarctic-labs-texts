Selenium nanoparticle-loaded microneedle patches promote diabetic oral ulcer healing by regulating the inflammatory microenvironment through upregulation of SELENBP1-mediated mitophagy
Authors: Xia Wang, Haiyin Wan, Xujie Cui, Bo Tan, Haofeng Wang, Jing He, Zichen Yang, Xiaoxuan Han, Jingang Xiao, Gang Tao, Yun He, Rui Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes mellitus (DM) is frequently complicated by refractory oral ulcers, which are characterized by persistent inflammation and impaired healing. We first identified that diabetic oral ulcer tissues exhibit significant selenium deficiency, downregulated SELENBP1 expression, and substantial mitochondrial damage – key factors contributing to the pathogenesis. To address this pathological condition, we developed a sericin-selenium nanoparticle-loaded microneedle patch (Ser-SeNPs@MN) for efficient selenium delivery and targeted therapy of diabetic oral ulcers. The Ser-SeNPs were synthesized using a green sericin-based method, demonstrating enhanced stability and biosafety. The Ser-SeNPs@MN enabled mucosal adhesion and sustained release of Ser-SeNPs, significantly improving selenium bioavailability. In vitro experiments revealed that Ser-SeNPs@MN promoted the expression of SELENBP1 and key selenoproteins (GPX1, GPX2, TXNRD1, TXNRD2), activated PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, and restored mitochondrial function. Consequently, it suppressed pro-inflammatory responses and facilitated M2 macrophage polarization. In both diabetic oral ulcer and skin wound rat models, Ser-SeNPs@MN accelerated wound closure, enhanced collagen deposition, and promoted angiogenesis. Introduction

Diabetic oral ulcers are highly prevalent, affecting over 80% of diabetic individuals, and pose a significant clinical challenge due to their recurrence, severe pain, and impact on eating and speaking [1, 2]. The pathogenesis of these ulcers is intricately linked to the hyperglycemic microenvironment, which disrupts local tissue homeostasis and drives chronic inflammation [3–5]. Prolonged high glucose levels promote the accumulation of advanced glycation end products and induce oxidative stress, leading to excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) from mitochondria [6, 7]. This mitochondrial ROS (mROS) overload not only fuels inflammatory signaling but also impairs mitophagy — the essential quality control mechanism that clears damaged mitochondria [8–10]. The resultant accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria exacerbates oxidative damage and inflammatory responses, ultimately compromising the integrity of the oral mucosal barrier and creating a vicious cycle that impedes healing [11].

Mitochondrial integrity is critically dependent on selenoproteins, a family of enzymes central to antioxidant defense and redox homeostasis [12]. Selenium, an essential trace element, is a key component of selenoproteins such as glutathione peroxidases (GPXs) and thioredoxin reductases (TXNRDs), which directly mitigate mROS and maintain mitochondrial electron transport chain function [13, 14]. Selenium-binding protein 1 (SELENBP1) binds exogenous selenium and participates in its intracellular transport and distribution, providing raw material for selenoprotein synthesis [15]. Selenium deficiency or dysregulation of SELENBP1 compromises selenoprotein synthesis, abrogating their protective effects and leading to unchecked oxidative stress, impaired mitophagy, and exacerbated inflammation [16, 17]. This mechanistic link establishes selenium homeostasis as a critical determinant in the pathogenesis of diabetic oral ulcers through the regulation of mitochondrial function and inflammatory responses. Consequently, restoring selenoprotein function via targeted selenium delivery may represent a promising therapeutic strategy.

Conventional selenium supplements suffer from poor stability and high toxicity [18]. Moreover, orally administered selenium is prone to metabolic degradation in the gastrointestinal tract, resulting in low bioavailability and limited therapeutic efficacy at ulcer sites [19]. To address these two major challenges, we designed a novel therapeutic system. Given that proteins have been shown to improve nanoparticle stability and reduce biotoxicity through their polar functional groups, a green synthesis method was developed using sericin, a natural silk-derived protein, as a biological template to form stable selenium nanoparticles (Ser-SeNPs) [20, 21]. Meanwhile, as microneedle (MN) patches have been reported as an effective drug delivery platform enabling direct and efficient release of therapeutic agents to target sites, Ser-SeNPs were integrated into a microneedle patch (Ser-SeNPs@MN) to achieve localized and sustained delivery [22]. Specifically, the microneedle tips were fabricated using rapidly dissolving hyaluronic acid (HA) to facilitate prompt release of SeNPs upon mucosal penetration. Considering the dynamic oral environment characterized by speech, mastication, and salivary flow, the backing layer was composed of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) to provide wet-adhesive properties, ensuring prolonged retention of the patch and sustained release of SeNPs from the tips [23–25] (Fig. 1A). Collectively, this Ser-SeNPs@MN system was designed to offer a targeted, stable, and highly bioavailable platform that overcomes the inherent limitations of systemic selenium supplementation.

B The Ser-SeNPs@MN patch is applied topically onto the diabetic oral ulcer or skin wound. Upon application, the microneedle tips penetrate the mucosal or epidermal barrier, dissolving rapidly to release Ser-SeNPs locally. This leads to upregulation of SELENBP1, enhanced selenoprotein production, activation of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, promotion of M2 macrophage polarization, and accelerated wound healing

In this study, we demonstrate that the Ser-SeNPs@MN patch effectively treats diabetic oral ulcers by restoring local selenoprotein function and mitochondrial homeostasis. Transcriptomic analysis of diabetic ulcer tissues identified downregulation of SELENBP1 and impaired mitophagy as key pathological features. In vitro mechanistic studies employing Selenbp1 knockdown and mitophagy inhibition revealed that Ser-SeNPs act by upregulating SELENBP1, thereby enhancing selenoprotein expression, restoring mitophagy, and promoting M2 macrophage polarization to relieve inflammation (Fig. 1B). The translational efficacy of this system was validated in both diabetic oral ulcer and skin wound models, where the Ser-SeNPs@MN patch significantly accelerated healing. By integrating green nanotechnology with a wet-adhesive microneedle platform, this work presents a targeted, efficient, and user-friendly strategy that directly addresses the selenium deficiency and mitochondrial dysfunction underlying diabetic oral ulcers, offering a promising avenue for clinical management.

Materials and methods

RNA sequencing and analysis of normal oral mucosal tissues and diabetic oral ulcer tissues

Total RNA was extracted from normal oral mucosal tissues and diabetic oral ulcer tissues of rats using TRIzol® Reagent. RNA quality was assessed using a 5300 Bioanalyzer (Agilent). High-quality RNA samples (OD260/280 = 1.8–2.2, OD260/230 ≥ 2.0, RIN ≥ 6.5, 28S:18S ≥ 1.0, total amount > 1 μg) were used for library construction. mRNA was enriched using Oligo(dT) magnetic beads and fragmented. Double-stranded cDNA was synthesized, followed by end repair, adenylation, and adapter ligation. The RNA-seq transcriptome library was prepared using the Illumina® Stranded mRNA Prep, Ligation Kit (San Diego, CA). Sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq X Plus platform (PE150) using a NovaSeq Reagent Kit. Raw data underwent quality control (QC) filtering to obtain high-quality clean reads. Clean reads were aligned to the rat reference genome (mRatBN7.2) using HISAT2 software. The mapped reads for each sample were assembled with StringTie. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 with the following criteria: |log2FoldChange| > 1 and p value < 0.05. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis. The clusterProfiler software was used to visualize significantly enriched biological processes and signaling pathways (Bonferroni-corrected p value < 0.05).

Observation of mitochondrial morphological changes in the diabetic oral ulcer tissues by transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Ultrastructural changes of mitochondria in normal oral mucosal tissues and diabetic oral ulcer tissues of rats were examined using TEM. Immediately after collection, tissues were trimmed into 1 mm3 pieces and fixed in pre-cooled 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 4 h. After washing three times with phosphate buffer, the samples were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4) for 2 h in the dark. Subsequently, the tissues were dehydrated through a graded acetone series (50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), infiltrated with epoxy resin Epon 812, and polymerized at 60 °C for 48 h. Ultrathin sections were prepared and double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Images were acquired using a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi HT7800, Japan) with emphasis on mitochondrial morphological features.

Measurement of selenium concentration in oral mucosal tissues

The selenium ion concentration in rat oral mucosal tissues was quantified using Selenium Colorimetric Assay Kit (Elabscience, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, fresh oral mucosal tissues from each group were weighed, homogenized in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.01 M, pH 7.4), and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected for total selenium content measurement.

A standard curve was prepared using serial dilutions of the standard solution provided in the kit. Then, 60 μL of each standard, blank control (PBS), and tissue supernatant were added into a 96-well plate. Subsequently, 200 μL reaction working solution was added to each well. The plate was further incubated at room temperature for another 30 min. The absorbance of each well was measured at 420 nm using a microplate reader (TECAN Infinite M200 Pro, Switzerland). The selenium concentration in each sample was calculated based on the standard curve and normalized to the wet tissue weight (μg/g tissue).

Measurement of SELENBP1 protein concentration by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentration of SELENBP1 in rat oral mucosal tissues was quantified using Rat Selenium-binding protein 1 (SELENBP1) ELISA Kit (Jingmei Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, tissue homogenates were prepared in ice-cold PBS with protease inhibitors and centrifuged to collect supernatants. Standards and samples were added in duplicate to the pre-coated plate and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After washing, biotinylated detection antibody was added and incubated for 1 h, followed by streptavidin-HRP conjugate for 30 min. Color was developed with TMB substrate, and the reaction stopped with sulfuric acid. Absorbance was read at 450 nm, and SELENBP1 concentrations were calculated from a standard curve and expressed as ng/mL total protein.

Synthesis and characterization of Ser-SeNPs

Sericin protein was extracted from Bombyx mori silk via a high-temperature and high-pressure method, followed by lyophilization to obtain sericin powder. The powder was dissolved in ultrapure water to prepare a 5% (w/v) sericin solution. Fresh solutions of sodium selenite (100 mM) and Vitamin C (VC, 100 mM) were also prepared immediately before use. A 500 mL aliquot of the 5% sericin solution was continuously stirred at 500 rpm using a magnetic stirrer at room temperature. Then, 10 mL of freshly prepared sodium selenite solution was added, and stirring was continued for 30 min to allow sufficient binding between selenite ions (SeO32−) and sericin. Subsequently, 40 mL of VC solution (molar ratio of VC to Na2SeO3 ≈ 4:1) was added dropwise slowly to the mixture. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 24 h in the dark. Throughout the reaction, the solution color transitioned from colorless to orange-red and finally to a stable brick red. After the reaction, the mixture was transferred into dialysis tubing with a molecular weight cutoff of 3,500 Da and dialyzed against ultrapure water for 48 h, with the external dialysate being replaced every 6–8 h to remove unreacted compounds. After dialysis, the red suspension inside the tubing was centrifuged (10,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C) to collect the precipitate. The final product was lyophilized to obtain deep-red Ser-SeNPs powder, which was stored protected from light at −20 °C for further use.

The morphology and microstructure of the Ser-SeNPs were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS Sigma 300, Germany). Elemental composition and distribution were analyzed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, Oxford Instruments Xplore 15/30, UK). Chemical composition and elemental valence states were characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, USA). Spectral analysis was performed using UV–Vis spectrophotometer (TU-1810, China), and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was carried out on an FTIR spectrometer (WQF-530, China).

Preparation and characterization of Ser-SeNPs@MN

Composite microneedles loaded with sericin-selenium nanoparticles (Ser-SeNPs@MN) were fabricated using a vacuum casting method [26]. First, Ser-SeNPs were mixed with a 10% (w/v) hyaluronic acid (HA) solution to prepare a needle tip solution. This mixture was injected into the cavities of a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) microneedle mold. To ensure complete filling of the fine needle structures, the mold was placed in a vacuum desiccator and subjected to a vacuum of −0.085 MPa for 5 min to remove trapped air bubbles. After releasing the vacuum, a 10% (w/v) polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) aqueous solution was slowly poured into the mold as a backing layer. The mold was then dried at room temperature for 24 h to allow complete solidification of the PVA base, after which the fully structured drug-loaded microneedle array was demolded. The morphology and microstructure of Ser-SeNPs were characterized using SEM. Elemental composition and distribution were analyzed by EDS.

To systematically evaluate the adhesion properties of the Ser-SeNPs@MN system, adhesion tests were conducted under both dry and wet conditions. For dry adhesion testing, a complete microneedle array was firmly attached to a rigid metal plate via its base. Biological and non-biological tissue samples were brought into contact with the needle tips under gentle preloading (approximately 5 N for 1 min) to ensure sufficient engagement. The metal plate was then pulled vertically away from the tissue surface to assess whether separation occurred. Wet adhesion testing was designed to mimic the oral physiological environment: excised tissue samples were soaked in phosphate-buffered saline to fully moisten the surface, and the same attachment, preloading, and vertical detachment steps were performed underwater.

To visually evaluate the dissolution behavior and drug release potential of the microneedle array in a simulated biological environment, a dissolution test was performed using agarose gel as a tissue-mimicking substrate. A 20% (w/v) agarose solution was prepared, heated until fully dissolved, and poured into a petri dish to form a uniform gel layer. After solidification, the complete Ser-SeNPs@MN array was pressed vertically into the gel surface with applied pressure (approximately 5 N) and maintained in fixed contact. The petri dish was incubated at 37 °C in a constant-temperature chamber. The microneedle array was retrieved every 1 min, and the morphological changes and dissolution extent of the tips were observed and recorded using a stereo microscope (OLYMPUS SZX7, Japan).

To determine the drug release profile, Ser-SeNPs@MN patches were respectively immersed in 6 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4, 37 °C) and incubated in a constant-temperature shaker at 100 rpm. At predetermined time points (0, 1, 3, 6, 10, 20 and 30 min), 5 mL of the PBS solution were withdrawn as the test sample. The selenium content in each collected sample was measured using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Agilent 5110, USA). The cumulative release curve was then plotted.

Cytocompatibility assessment of Ser-SeNPs and Ser-SeNPs@MN

The murine macrophage cell line RAW 264.7 and murine fibroblast cell line L929 were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The cytocompatibility of Ser-SeNPs and Ser-SeNPs@MN was evaluated using the CCK-8 Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (‌Solarbio, China) and a Live & Dead Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (UElandy, China). L929 and RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h, then treated with culture medium containing different concentrations of Ser-SeNPs (0–0.2 μg/mL) or microneedle extracts for 1, 3, and 5 days. For the CCK-8 assay, 10% CCK-8 reagent in medium was added to each well. After 2 h of incubation, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and cell viability was calculated. For live/dead staining, treated cells were co-incubated with Calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI) working solutions for 20 min, washed with PBS, and immediately observed under a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMI8, Germany). Cell viability was assessed based on fluorescence intensity and morphological changes, and survival rates were calculated.

A hemolysis assay was performed to evaluate the blood compatibility of Ser-SeNPs and Ser-SeNPs@MN. Fresh rabbit blood anticoagulated with sodium citrate was centrifuged, and red blood cells (RBCs) were collected and washed three times with saline to prepare a 2% RBC suspension. The experiment included three groups: Negative control (PBS), Positive control (1% Triton X-100), and Test groups (Ser-SeNPs solution or microneedle extracts). Each test sample was mixed with an equal volume of the RBC suspension and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After centrifugation, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 540 nm. The hemolysis rate was calculated as follows: Hemolysis (%) = [(ODTest − ODNegative) / (ODPositive − ODNegative)] × 100%.

Internalization experiment

Ser-SeNPs were labeled with NHS-rhodamine B (NHS-RhB, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Raw 264.7 macrophages were incubated with NHS-RhB labeled Ser-SeNPs for 0, 6, 12, and 24 h. After incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, China). Subsequently, the cytoskeleton was stained with FITC (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and the nuclei were stained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at room temperature. Finally, the cells were visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Detection of intracellular ROS and mROS

The fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (Solarbio, China) was used to detect total intracellular ROS. RAW 264.7 cells were seeded into culture plates. An oxidative stress environment was induced by stimulation with H2O2 (100 μM) for 2 h. The cells were then treated with microneedle extracts for 24 h, after which the culture medium was removed. Serum-free medium containing 10 μM DCFH-DA was added, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 20 min. Intracellular ROS levels were visualized using fluorescence microscopy.

Mitochondrial superoxide levels were detected using the MitoSOX Red fluorescent probe (Solarbio, China). RAW 264.7 cells were seeded into culture plates. A diabetic cell model was established by induction with high glucose (30 mM) and LPS (1 μg/mL) in complete medium for 48 h. The cells were then treated with microneedle extracts for another 24 h. After removing the medium, the cells were incubated with 5 μM MitoSOX Red working solution at 37 °C in the dark for exactly 10 min. Following dye incubation and washing, fluorescence images were immediately captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope. DCFH-DA fluorescence was detected at excitation/emission wavelengths of approximately 488/525 nm (green), and MitoSOX Red was detected at 510/580 nm (red). Semi-quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ software to evaluate changes in ROS levels.

Detection of mitochondrial membrane potential

Changes in mitochondrial membrane potential were assessed using the JC-1 fluorescent probe (Yeasen, China). After treatment, cells were incubated with a JC-1 working solution (5 μg/mL) at 37 °C in the dark for 20 min. The working solution was then aspirated, and the cells were washed twice with pre-chilled JC-1 staining buffer (1 ×) to thoroughly remove unincorporated probe. Imaging was immediately performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope. In healthy cells with normal mitochondrial membrane potential, JC-1 forms aggregates within mitochondria that emit red fluorescence (excitation/emission ≈ 585/590 nm). In cells with depolarized mitochondria, the probe remains in monomeric form, producing green fluorescence (excitation/emission ≈ 514/529 nm). The degree of mitochondrial membrane potential depolarization was quantitatively evaluated by calculating the ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity.

Detection of mitophagy by co-staining with MitoTracker and LysoTracker

Mitochondria and lysosomes were simultaneously labeled and analyzed for co-localization using MitoTracker Red (Yeasen, China) and LysoTracker Green (Yeasen, China). After treatment, the culture medium was removed. Cells were then incubated with a serum-free working solution containing MitoTracker Green (final concentration: 100 nM) and LysoTracker Red (final concentration: 50 nM) at 37 °C in the dark for 15–30 min. After incubation, the staining solution was aspirated, and the cells were immediately imaged under a confocal laser scanning microscope. MitoTracker Red labels mitochondria (excitation/emission ≈ 577/590 nm, red fluorescence), while LysoTracker Green stains lysosomes (excitation/emission ≈ 490/516 nm, green fluorescencee). Co-localization of mitochondria and lysosomes was assessed by analyzing yellow fluorescence in overlapping regions.

Monitoring mitophagy via mtKeima reporting system

To monitor mitophagy, RAW 264.7 cells were transfected with an mtKeima lentiviral vector. Cells were seeded into culture plates and pre-treated with high glucose and LPS upon reaching 40–50% confluence. Subsequently, the cells were treated with microneedle extracts for 24 h. Then, the cells were incubated with mtKeima lentiviral particles (titer ≥ 1 × 108 TU/mL) in enhancer-containing transduction medium (OBiO Technology, China) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20. After 12 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium, and the cells were cultured for an additional 48–72 h. Fluorescence signals of mtKeima were visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope. The probe exhibits excitation/emission at 440/590 nm under neutral pH conditions and at 586/620 nm under acidic pH.

Selenbp1 siRNA knockdown experiment

Gene expression of Selenbp1 was knocked down using siRNA interference technology. Specific siRNA sequences targeting the Selenbp1 gene, along with a negative control siRNA (Hippo Biotechnology, China), were selected. RAW 264.7 cells were seeded into culture plates. The siRNA and transfection reagent were mixed at a 2:1 ratio to form complexes, which were then applied to the cells in serum-free medium. After 8 h of transfection, the medium was replaced with complete medium. At 48 h post-transfection, the knockdown efficiency was evaluated by quantifying Selenbp1 mRNA and protein expression levels using qRT-PCR and WB analyses, respectively. Subsequent functional assays were performed following successful knockdown.

Western blotting (WB) analysis

Protein expression levels of specific targets were detected by WB. Treated cells were collected and lysed on ice using pre-chilled RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1% PMSF and protease inhibitors for 30 min. The lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected as the total protein extract. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit (Solarbio, China), and samples were adjusted to equal concentrations. Equal amounts of protein were mixed with 5 × loading buffer and denatured by boiling at 100 °C for 10 min. The proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred to a PVDF membrane using a wet transfer method.

After transfer, the membrane was blocked with blocking buffer at room temperature for 1 h. It was then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-Selenbp1 (1:1000, Abmart, China), anti-LC3 (1:1000, Abmart, China), anti-P62 (1:1000, Proteintech, China), anti-PINK1 (1:1000, Proteintech, China), anti-Parkin (1:1000, Abiowell, China), and anti-CD86 (1:1000, Proteintech, China). The following day, the membrane was washed three times with TBST and incubated with a secondary antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (1:1000, Proteintech, China) at room temperature for 1 h. After thorough washing with TBST, protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence kit and imaged with a chemiluminescence detection system. The gray values of the target bands were quantified using ImageJ software, with GAPDH serving as the internal control for normalizing relative protein expression levels.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Gene expression levels were determined by RT-qPCR. After treatment, cells were collected, and total RNA was extracted using an RNA Extraction Kit (Accurate Biology, China). The concentration and purity of the RNA were measured, and cDNA was synthesized according to the instructions provided with the Reverse Transcription Kit (Accurate Biology, China). The resulting cDNA was used as a template for qPCR amplification with SYBR Green Premix. The reaction mixture contained SYBR Green Master Mix, forward and reverse primers (each at a final concentration of 0.2 μM), and cDNA template. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. GAPDH was used as the reference gene, and the relative mRNA expression of the target genes was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis

The expression and localization of LC3, CD206, and iNOS proteins were detected using immunofluorescence staining. Treated cells grown on glass slides were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% goat serum for 1 h. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-LC3 (1:100, Abmart, China), anti-CD206 (1:100, Proteintech, China), and anti-iNOS (1:100, Proteintech, China). After washing with PBS, the cells were incubated with species-specific Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:100, Proteintech, China) for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 1 µg/mL DAPI (Solarbio, China) for 5 min. Finally, the slides were mounted and imaged under a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Flow cytometric analysis of macrophage polarization

Macrophage polarization into M1 or M2 phenotypes was assessed by detecting specific surface markers using flow cytometry. After treatment, cells were collected and resuspended in PBS. They were then incubated with anti-iNOS antibody (1:200, BioLegend, USA) and anti-CD206 antibody (1:200, BioLegend, USA) for 30 min in the dark. Data were acquired on a flow cytometer (ACEA NovoCyte 2070R, USA), and the percentages of iNOS-positive (M1) and CD206-positive (M2) cells were analyzed to evaluate M1/M2 polarization status.

Establishment of oral ulcer and skin wound models in diabetic rats

Four-week-old male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats weighing approximately 180–220 g were used in this study. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Southwest Medical University (Approval No: 20241108–008) and conducted in accordance with the committee’s guidelines. A type 2 diabetes mellitus rat model was established using a high-fat and high-sugar diet combined with streptozotocin (STZ) injection. Four-week-old SD rats were first fed a high-sugar and high-fat diet for 3 weeks to induce insulin resistance. After fasting for 12 h, the rats were intraperitoneally injected with STZ (35 mg/kg) for three consecutive days. Fasting blood glucose was measured starting on the third day after the last injection and then every two days thereafter. Rats with three consecutive random blood glucose measurements ≥ 16.7 mmol/L were considered successfully diabetic.

After stabilizing the diabetic condition for 1 weeks, oral ulcer and skin wound models were established. For the oral ulcer model, a standardized chemical burn method was applied using 40% acetic acid. Under inhalation anesthesia, a round filter paper disc (about 6 mm in diameter) soaked in acetic acid was applied to the labial gingival surface for 60 s to induce a uniform ulcer. For the skin wound model, a full-thickness excisional wound was created using a biopsy punch. After anesthesia and shaving of the dorsal hair, a circular skin defect about 10 mm in diameter was generated.

To evaluate the in vivo therapeutic effect of Ser-SeNPs@MN on wounds in diabetic rats, the animals were randomly divided into four groups: (1) control group, (2) diabetes mellitus (DM) group, (3) wound treated with blank microneedles (Blank MN) group, (4) wound treated with Ser-SeNPs@MN microneedles (Ser-SeNPs@MN) group. Starting from day 1 after modeling, microneedle arrays were applied vertically to the ulcer or wound area in the Blank MN and Ser-SeNPs@MN groups, and replaced every 48 h. During the treatment period, wound morphology was recorded daily with a digital camera, and wound area was quantified using ImageJ software. The initial wound area on the day of modeling was set as 100%, and the percentage of remaining wound area at each time point was calculated to evaluate the healing rate. On days 3, 7, and 15 post-treatments, rats were euthanized under anesthesia, and wound tissue samples were collected for subsequent histological analysis.

RNA sequencing and analysis of Ser-SeNPs@MN-treated oral mucosal tissues and diabetic oral ulcer tissues

RNA sequencing and analysis were performed on oral mucosal tissues from diabetic ulcer rats and from Ser-SeNPs@MN-treated rats, following the methodology described in Sect. "RNA sequencing and analysis of normal oral mucosal tissues and diabetic oral ulcer tissues".

Observation of mitochondrial morphological changes in the Ser-SeNPs@MN-treated oral mucosal tissues by TEM

Mitochondrial ultrastructural changes were examined in diabetic oral ulcer tissues and Ser-SeNPs@MN-treated oral mucosal tissues of rats using TEM, following the methodology described in Sect. "Observation of mitochondrial morphological changes in the diabetic oral ulcer tissues by transmission electron microscopy (TEM)".

Histological analysis

Histological examination was performed to further evaluate wound healing progression. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was used to observe tissue morphology and inflammatory cell infiltration. Collagen deposition and organization were assessed using Masson’s trichrome staining. The expression of key proteins, including CD86, CD206, TNF-α, CD31, SELENBP1, and LC3, was detected via immunohistochemistry (IHC) and IF staining.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.0. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student’s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA). A significance level of α = 0.05 was applied, with *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, indicating statistically significant differences. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

Results and discussions

Selenium deficiency induces mitochondrial damage in diabetic oral ulcer tissues via downregulation of SELENBP1

Substantial clinical evidence indicates a significant negative correlation of diabetes incidence and severity with systemic selenium levels [27, 28]. Selenium deficiency may contribute to the impairment of oral mucosal integrity, a common diabetic complication [29]. Thus, dysregulated selenium metabolism may represent a key trigger in the disruption of the diabetic oral mucosal barrier [30]. Mitochondria, as cellular powerhouses, play a critical role in disease progression when damaged [9]. To elucidate the molecular pathology underlying diabetic oral ulcer development, this study integrated transcriptomic profiling, ultrastructural observation, and molecular functional validation to systematically demonstrate the close association between diabetic oral ulcers and selenium deficiency with mitochondrial dysfunction.

Transcriptomic sequencing revealed a clear separation between control and DM groups via principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 2A). A Venn diagram further identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two groups (Fig. 2B). Volcano plot analysis confirmed 921 DEGs in the DM group (120 up-regulated and 801 down-regulated), including significant down-regulation of Selenbp1 and Gpx2 — key genes involved in selenoprotein synthesis (Fig. 2C). Both Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses indicated significant enrichment of pathways related to inflammatory response and oxidative stress in the DM group (Fig. 2D–E). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further revealed significant suppression of oxidative phosphorylation in the DM group (Fig. 2F), suggesting impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism and increased ROS production.

These molecular findings were corroborated by ultrastructural evidence from transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which showed intact mitochondrial structures in the control group, including continuous inner and outer membranes with clearly defined, dense cristae. In contrast, the DM group exhibited typical damage features: mitochondrial matrix swelling, disrupted and vanished cristae, and ruptured mitochondrial membranes (Fig. 2G). Molecular profiling confirmed reduced selenium content in diabetic mucosal tissues (Fig. 2H), accompanied by decreased SELENBP1 expression at both protein and RNA levels (Fig. 2I–J), along with transcriptional down-regulation of key selenoprotein genes, including glutathione peroxidase family members Gpx1/2 and thioredoxin reductase family members Txnrd1/2 (Fig. 2J). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining of animal tissues showed disrupted mucosal architecture, reduced cellularity, and markedly weakened SELENBP1 fluorescence intensity in the DM group (Fig. 2K).

These results reveal for the first time that selenium deficiency in diabetic oral mucosa leads to downregulation of SELENBP1, impairing functional selenoprotein synthesis, which subsequently induces mitochondrial dysfunction and ultimately drives oral ulcer. Taken together, these results provides support for the further investigation of selenium supplementation as a targeted therapy for diabetic oral ulcers.

Synthesis and characterization of Ser-SeNPs and Ser-SeNPs@MN

Conventionally synthesized selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) are limited by poor stability and high toxicity, which restrict their practical application. To address these limitations, this study introduced sericin as a surface modifier. A functionalized composite system, sericin-selenium nanoparticles (Ser-SeNPs), was developed through a self-assembly reaction between sericin and sodium selenite. To further develop a Ser-SeNPs delivery system suitable for oral mucosal application, we subsequently fabricated adhesive microneedle patches (Ser-SeNPs@MN) using a vacuum-assisted molding process. The fabrication involved a two-step casting procedure: a 10% hyaluronic acid (HA) solution containing Ser-SeNPs (0.15 μg/mL) was first injected into the tip cavities of a PDMS mold and placed under vacuum for 5 min to ensure complete filling, followed by the addition of a 10% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) backing solution, after which the intact MNs were demolded upon solidification (Fig. 3A).

Synthesis and characterization of Ser-SeNPs and Ser-SeNPs@MN. A Schematic illustration of Ser-SeNPs@MN synthesis. B Representative SEM and EDS elemental mapping images of Ser-SeNPs. C Zeta potential distribution of Ser-SeNPs. D UV–Vis absorption spectra of sericin, SeNPs, and Ser-SeNPs. E FTIR spectra of sericin, SeNPs, and Ser-SeNPs. F XPS spectrum and corresponding peak deconvolution of Ser-SeNPs. G Fluorescence image of RhB-labeled Ser-SeNPs@MN. H Representative SEM image of Ser-SeNPs@MN. I Representative EDS elemental mapping image of Ser-SeNPs@MN. J Adhesion of Ser-SeNPs@MN to biological and non-biological substrates under wet conditions. K Representative image showing the degradation of Ser-SeNPs@MN after insertion into 20% agarose gel. L In vitro release profiles of Ser-SeNPs from MN

Macroscopic stability analysis revealed that unmodified SeNPs aggregated and precipitated within 1 h, whereas Ser-SeNPs remained uniformly dispersed, confirming that sericin significantly enhanced the stability of SeNPs (Figure S1). Further morphological and structural characterization confirmed the successful synthesis of Ser-SeNPs. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping showed that Ser-SeNPs were monodispersed and spherical with an average diameter of approximately 55–65 nm, with a distinct low-electron-density protein corona surrounding the particles indicating successful coating with sericin (Fig. 3B) [31]. Zeta potential analysis showed a surface charge of −32 mV (Fig. 3C), consistent with the negative charge of sericin, further confirming successful surface functionalization [21]. Ultraviolet–visible spectroscopy (UV–Vis) revealed a blue shift in the maximum absorption peak from 285 nm (unmodified SeNPs) to 275 nm (Fig. 3D), which may be attributable to interfacial interactions between sericin and SeNPs. This shift aligns with previously reported optical changes in surface-modified SeNPs [32]. Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR) analysis indicated that Ser-SeNPs retained characteristic amide peaks of sericin (1625 cm−1, 1521 cm−1, and 1236 cm−1) [33], with spectra similar to those of sericin and SeNPs (Fig. 3E), suggesting that sericin and SeNPs are components of Ser-SeNPs with a sary structure similar [34]. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis provided further evidence of chemical bonding, with Se 3d peaks at 55.88 eV and 55.08 eV, and O 1 s peaks at 532.38 eV and 531.28 eV, assigned to Se-O bonds (Fig. 3F) [21].

The Ser-SeNPs@MN patches exhibited orange-colored tips and a transparent white backing (Figure S2A). Confocal fluorescence imaging of rhodamine B (RhB)-labeled Ser-SeNPs@MN revealed the complete microneedle structure (Fig. 3G), while SEM characterization showed well-defined conical structures of microneedle tips (Fig. 3H). EDS elemental mapping further confirmed that SeNPs was localized in the microneedle tips (Fig. 3I). The adhesive properties were systematically evaluated: under both wet and dry conditions, the patches exhibited strong adhesion to various biological tissues (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) and non-biological substrates (wood, plastic, metal, glass, rubber) (Figs. 3J and S3B). When applied to ex vivo rat skin under both dry and wet conditions, Ser-SeNPs@MN remained attached despite 180° twisting, 90° bending, and rinsing under flowing water without detachment (Figure S3A). Furthermore, degradation behavior assessed in a simulated oral mucosal environment showed that needle tips began to dissolve within 1 min, exhibited significant degradation by 3 min, and almost completely disintegrated within 12 min, demonstrating progressive drug release kinetics (Fig. 3K). Consistent with this degradation behavior, the in vitro release curve showed that Ser-SeNPs were rapidly released from the microneedles, with more than 80% of the loaded Ser-SeNPs released within the first 10 min and nearly 100% within approximately 30 min, indicating a fast and efficient delivery profile suitable for prompt local action (Fig. 3L).

In summary, comprehensive multi-technique characterization consistently demonstrates that sericin forms a stable coating on SeNPs through covalent interactions, markedly improving their dispersity and stability. The Ser-SeNPs@MN system combines fast and precise drug release with wet-adhesion capability, providing an ideal platform for targeted treatment of oral ulcers.

Biocompatibility evaluation of Ser-SeNPs and Ser-SeNPs@MN

Selecting an appropriate concentration is critical to avoid the toxic side effects associated with high concentrations of SeNPs. To evaluate the clinical potential of Ser-SeNPs and determine a safe application concentration, we systematically investigated key biocompatibility parameters, including hemocompatibility and cytotoxicity. As shown in Fig. 4A, Ser-SeNPs solutions (0.05–0.2 μg/mL) exhibited a noticeable concentration-dependent color change—from light amber-orange at 0.05 μg/mL to deep red at 0.2 μg/mL. Hemolysis assays indicated that the hemolysis rates in all concentration groups were below 3%, with no statistically significant differences among experimental groups (Fig. 4B). Morphological observation of red blood cells further confirmed that even after 4 h of incubation with 0.2 μg/mL Ser-SeNPs, the erythrocytes retained their intact biconcave disk structure, similar to the PBS group (Fig. 4C). Cytotoxicity was evaluated primarily in RAW 264.7 macrophages. Live/dead staining of RAW 264.7 cells revealed that after treatment with Ser-SeNPs at concentrations up to 0.15 μg/mL for 1, 3, and 5 days, the proportion of dead (red) cells remained minimal. In contrast, the 0.2 μg/mL group exhibited significantly intensified red fluorescence as early at day 1, with the dead cell proportion reaching 30.2% by day 5 (Fig. 4D–E). CCK-8 assays further confirmed these findings: cell viability in the 0.2 μg/mL group decreased to 68.4% by day 5, significantly lower than that in the 0.05, 0.1, and 0.15 μg/mL groups (Fig. 4F). Similar trends were observed in L929 fibroblasts, where Ser-SeNPs at concentrations ≤ 0.15 μg/mL maintained high cell viability, whereas the 0.2 μg/mL group showed a marked reduction in viability (Figure S4). Therefore, 0.15 μg/mL was selected as the safe concentration threshold for subsequent applications.

B Hemolysis rates of Ser-SeNPs at different concentrations. C Representative macroscopic and microscopic images of red blood cells after incubation with Ser-SeNPs at various concentrations. D Representative live/dead staining images of RAW 264.7 cells after treatment with Ser-SeNPs for 1, 3, and 5 days. E Quantitative analysis of live and dead cells in RAW 264.7 cultures. I Representative macroscopic and microscopic images of red blood cells after incubation with different MN. *p < 0.05, ****p < 0.0001

Based on this concentration, we further evaluated the biosafety of Ser-SeNPs@MN. After treatment with either blank microneedle carriers (Blank MN group) or drug-loaded MNs (Ser-SeNPs@MN group) for 1, 3, and 5 days, RAW 264.7 cells exhibited sustained high-density green fluorescence in live/dead staining, with minimal red dead cell signals that showed no difference from the control group (Fig. 4G). CCK-8 proliferation assays further quantitatively confirmed these results: the relative proliferation rates of RAW 264.7 cells treated with blank MNs or Ser-SeNPs@MN showed no statistically significant differences compared with the control group (Fig. 4H). Similar cytocompatibility was observed in L929 fibroblasts (Figure S5A-B), confirming the safety of the microneedle system across cell types. Hemocompatibility analysis demonstrated that after incubation with Ser-SeNPs@MN for 4 h, red blood cells maintained intact biconcave disc morphology under microscopy (Fig. 4I), and the hemolysis rates were all below 3% (Figure S5C), significantly lower than the safety threshold of 5%.

In summary, Ser-SeNPs exhibit excellent biocompatibility at concentrations ≤ 0.15 μg/mL, with a hemolysis rate below 3% and no significant cytotoxicity. The Ser-SeNPs@MN system, fabricated using this safe concentration, further demonstrates favorable cytocompatibility and hemocompatibility, as neither the carrier material nor the drug-loaded system adversely affects cell viability, proliferation, or erythrocyte integrity. These results support the safe application of Ser-SeNPs@MN for further in vitro studies.

Ser-SeNPs@MN upregulates SELENBP1, thereby enhancing selenoprotein expression and reversing mitochondrial oxidative damage under a diabetic microenvironment in vitro

In Sect. "Selenium deficiency induces mitochondrial damage in diabetic oral ulcer tissues via downregulation of SELENBP1", we identified SELENBP1 downregulation and mitochondrial dysfunction as key pathological features in diabetic oral ulcer tissues. To further dissect the underlying molecular mechanisms and determine whether Ser-SeNPs@MN could reverse these defects, we established an in vitro diabetic microenvironment by culturing RAW 264.7 macrophages with 30 mM glucose and 1 μg/mL LPS for 48 h.

Before evaluating the protective effect of Ser-SeNPs@MN against mitochondrial oxidative damage, we first verified whether Ser-SeNPs could be effectively taken up by cells. As shown in Figure S6, RhB-labeled Ser-SeNPs exhibited time-dependent intracellular accumulation. Negligible fluorescence was detected at 0 h, whereas fluorescent signals became observable at 6 h, markedly increased at 12 h, and reached the highest level at 24 h, indicating efficient cellular internalization of Ser-SeNPs. A high-ROS microenvironment was established by exposing cells to 100 μM H2O2 for 2 h. Treatment with Ser-SeNPs@MN significantly reduced ROS levels in both RAW 264.7 and L929 cells (Fig. 5A–B). Mitochondrial functional analysis revealed that the DM group exhibited elevated mROS levels and significantly impaired membrane potential (decreased JC-1 red/green fluorescence ratio). Following Blank MN intervention, no significant changes were observed in mROS levels or membrane potential. In contrast, treatment with Ser-SeNPs@MN restored mROS levels to near-normal values and returned membrane potential to levels comparable to those of the control group (Fig. 5C–E). Furthermore, WB analysis showed that Ser-SeNPs@MN significantly increased the expression of SELENBP1, which was downregulated in the DM group (Fig. 5F). RT-qPCR confirmed that Ser-SeNPs@MN markedly upregulated the expression of key selenoprotein genes, including glutathione peroxidases (Gpx1 and Gpx2), thioredoxin reductases (Txnrd1 and Txnrd2), and Selenbp1 (Fig. 5G).

F Representative WB images and quantitative analysis of SELENBP1 expression. These results demonstrate that Ser-SeNPs@MN upregulates SELENBP1-mediated selenium transport, enhances the expression of antioxidant selenoproteins, and thereby reverses diabetes-associated mitochondrial oxidative damage while preserving mitochondrial function.

Ser-SeNPs@MN activates mitophagy via SELENBP1 to ameliorate diabetic mitochondrial damage

Diabetic microenvironment induce excessive ROS production and impair mitophagy, leading to damaged mitochondrial accumulation and exacerbated cellular injury [35]. Previous studies have indicated that selenium supplementation can activate mitophagy and eliminate damaged mitochondria, thereby restoring mitochondrial function [36], yet the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Therefore, this study investigated the regulatory role of Ser-SeNPs@MN in mitophagy and its molecular connection with SELENBP1 to elucidate its potential mechanism in maintaining mitochondrial function.

The level of mitophagy was assessed using mtKeima reporting system. mtKeima is a pH-sensitive mitochondrial probe that exhibits green fluorescence in the neutral cytosolic environment and red fluorescence in acidic lysosomes [37]. The results showed that the red fluorescence intensity was significantly weaker in the DM group, indicating markedly reduced mitochondrial delivery to lysosomes. In contrast, Ser-SeNPs@MN intervention enhanced red fluorescence, suggesting increased signals of autophagic mitochondria within lysosomes (Fig. 6A). This finding was further supported by subcellular colocalization analysis: the colocalization level of LysoTracker (labeling lysosomes) and MitoTracker (labeling mitochondria) was reduced in the DM group but restored to near-normal levels after Ser-SeNPs@MN treatment, indicating increased mitochondrial delivery to lysosomes (Fig. 6B). Confocal IF imaging similarly confirmed that, compared to the DM group, Ser-SeNPs@MN treatment increased the colocalization area between the autophagosome marker LC3 and mitochondria, demonstrating enhanced encapsulation of damaged mitochondria by autophagosomes and significantly activated mitophagy (Fig. 6C).

Ser-SeNPs@MN activates mitophagy via SELENBP1 to repair diabetic mitochondrial damage. A Assessment of mitophagy in RAW 264.7 cells transfected with mtKeima adenovirus. B Mitophagy evaluated by LysoTracker and MitoTracker co-staining. C Mitophagy level detected by LC3 and MitoTracker co-staining. D-E Representative WB images and quantitative analysis of siSelenbp1 knockdown efficiency and expression of mitophagy-related proteins (LC3, p62, PINK1, Parkin). F RT-qPCR analysis of siSelenbp1 knockdown efficiency, selenoprotein (Gpx1, Gpx2), and mitophagy-related mRNA (p62, Pink1, Parkin) expression. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001

Our previous findings highlighted the important role of SELENBP1 and selenoproteins in maintaining mitochondrial function. To clarify the correlation between SELENBP1 and mitophagy in mitochondrial homeostasis, we transfected cells with siSelenbp1 to specifically knock down Selenbp1 expression, effectively inhibiting selenoprotein synthesis, and further examined mitophagy-related indicators. WB results revealed that, compared to the DM group, Ser-SeNPs@MN treatment increased SELENBP1 expression, elevated the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio, accelerated degradation of the autophagic substrate P62, and up-regulated the expression of the mitochondrial damage sensor PINK1 and the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin (Fig. 6D–E). However, siRNA-mediated knockdown of Selenbp1 abolished these effects. Both WB and RT-qPCR results confirmed successful transfection and significant suppression of Selenbp1 gene and SELENBP1 protein expression (Fig. 6D–F). Selenbp1 knockdown not only increased the expression of p62 and reduced the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio, but also suppressed the expression of PINK1 and Parkin (Fig. 6D–E). RT-qPCR further confirmed that the changes in Gpx1, Gpx2, Pink1, Parkin, and p62 mRNA levels were consistent with their protein expressions (Fig. 6F).

These results demonstrate that Ser-SeNPs@MN enhances SELENBP1-mediated selenium bioavailability, promotes selenoprotein generation, activates the PINK1-Parkin pathway, and drives ubiquitin-dependent mitophagy, ultimately restoring mitophagic flux in diabetic cells and maintaining mitochondrial functional homeostasis.

Ser-SeNPs@MN regulates M2 macrophage polarization and remodels the inflammatory microenvironment through activating mitophagy

Mitochondria are central organelles in cellular energy metabolism, and their functional homeostasis is closely linked to inflammatory responses [38]. Mitophagy helps maintain mitochondrial structural and functional integrity by selectively removing damaged mitochondria and suppressing the release of mROS and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [39, 40]. Impaired mitophagy leads to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria, which activate inflammatory signaling pathways and promote a pro-inflammatory microenvironment [41]. Macrophages are key regulators of inflammation, and their polarization phenotypes, M1 (pro-inflammatory) and M2 (anti-inflammatory), directly influence the state of the microenvironment [42]. DAMPs can induce M1 macrophage polarization and promote secretion of pro-inflammatory factors, while simultaneously inhibiting M2 polarization and anti-inflammatory cytokine production, thereby shaping the inflammatory landscape [43, 44].

Based on the activating effect of Ser-SeNPs@MN on mitophagy shown in Fig. 6, this study further evaluated its ability to regulate macrophage phenotypic switching by restoring mitochondrial homeostasis. IF analysis revealed that in the DM group, fluorescence intensity of the M1 marker iNOS was significantly stronger than in the control group, but weakened after Ser-SeNPs@MN intervention. Expression of the M2 marker CD206 decreased in the DM group and was restored to near-normal levels following treatment (Fig. 7A–B). Flow cytometry quantification further confirmed this phenotypic shift: the proportion of iNOS-positive cells increased from 35.7% in the control group to 74.1% in the DM group, while CD206-positive cells decreased from 42.7% to 0.2%. After Ser-SeNPs@MN treatment, the proportion of iNOS-positive cells decreased to 51.2%, and CD206-positive cells increased to 36.8% (Fig. 7C–D).

A Representative IF staining images of M1 macrophage marker (iNOS). B Representative IF staining images of M2 macrophage marker (CD206). C Flow cytometry analysis of M1 (iNOS) and M2 (CD206) macrophage-specific markers. D Semi-quantitative analysis of flow cytometry results. E WB detection of M1 and M2 macrophage-specific markers (CD86, CD206). F Quantitative analysis of WB results. G mRNA expression of M1 and M2 macrophage-specific markers (iNOS, CD206) detected by RT-qPCR. H Schematic diagram illustrating the mechanism by which Ser-SeNPs@MN regulates macrophage polarization and remodels the inflammatory microenvironment through mitophagy-dependency. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001

To investigate whether the regulation of macrophage polarization by Ser-SeNPs@MN is dependent on mitophagy activation, the autophagy inhibitor Bafilomycin A1 (Baf-A1) was employed to specifically block autophagosome-lysosome fusion. The specific experimental approach involved pretreatment with Baf-A1 followed by evaluation of macrophage polarization markers using both WB and RT-qPCR analysis. WB results showed that Baf-A1 effectively blocked the effect of Ser-SeNPs@MN, leading to increased expression of the M1 marker CD86 and reduced expression of the M2 marker CD206 back to DM group levels (Fig. 7E–F). RT-qPCR further confirmed that Baf-A1 treatment counteracted the effect of Ser-SeNPs@MN, resulting in elevated expression of the pro-inflammatory gene iNOS and reduced expression of the anti-inflammatory gene CD206 (Fig. 7G).

These results demonstrate that Ser-SeNPs@MN reduces the M1/M2 macrophage polarization ratio in the diabetic inflammatory microenvironment through a mitophagy-dependent mechanism, thereby promoting a shift from a pro-inflammatory to an anti-inflammatory state (Fig. 7H).

Ser-SeNPs@MN promotes the healing of soft tissue wound in diabetic rats

Based on the findings from the aforementioned in vitro experiments, this study further validated the therapeutic efficacy of Ser-SeNPs@MN in a diabetic rat oral ulcer model. The schematic diagram of the oral ulcer modeling process is shown in Fig. 8A. As shown in Fig. 8B–D, morphological observations on day 7 post-modeling revealed that the ulcer areas in the DM and Blank MN groups remained around 5 mm2 with limited wound contraction, whereas the Ser-SeNPs@MN intervention group showed significantly reduced ulcer areas (approximately 2 mm2) and marked contraction at the wound margins. Quantitative analysis of the healing rate further confirmed that the Ser-SeNPs@MN treatment group achieved a healing rate of 78.4% on day 7, significantly higher than that of the DM group (56.7%) and the Blank MN group (58.5%) (Fig. 8E).

To further evaluate the therapeutic effect of Ser-SeNPs@MN, a systematic histopathological assessment of tissue repair in the oral ulcers was performed. H&E staining was used to observe inflammatory infiltration and tissue regeneration, while Masson’s trichrome staining was employed to analyze collagen deposition and remodeling. In the early healing phase (day 3 post-modeling), H&E staining revealed extensive infiltration of neutrophils and monocytes in the ulcer areas of the DM and Blank MN groups. Masson’s staining indicated large-scale collagen fiber fragmentation with disordered arrangement and loss of continuity. In contrast, the Ser-SeNPs@MN group exhibited a significant increase in newly formed capillaries at the ulcer edge, reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, and markedly diminished collagen destruction (Fig. 8F). By the mid-to-late healing stage (day 7 post-modeling), the Ser-SeNPs@MN group displayed typical healing characteristics: significantly reduced ulcer area, extensive regeneration of the epithelial layer with migration toward the wound center, and notably decreased inflammatory cell infiltration in the ulcer bed. Masson’s staining demonstrated increased collagen deposition in the ulcer region, with dense and well-organized fibers arranged parallel to the epithelial surface (Fig. 8G).

To further validate the broad applicability of this therapeutic platform, the pro-healing effects of Ser-SeNPs@MN were also evaluated in a diabetic rat skin wound model. Consistent with the findings in oral ulcers, Ser-SeNPs@MN significantly accelerated wound closure, enhanced re-epithelialization, reduced inflammatory infiltration, and promoted collagen remodeling. Detailed results are presented in Figure S7.

Collectively, these morphological and histopathological results demonstrate that Ser-SeNPs@MN effectively promotes diabetic soft tissue wound healing, including both oral ulcers and skin wounds. The underlying mechanisms involve accelerating wound contraction, enhancing epithelial regeneration, reducing inflammatory infiltration, and improving collagen remodeling.

Transcriptomic analysis reveals the potential mechanism of Ser-SeNPs@MN in accelerating diabetic oral ulcer healing

To systematically elucidate the molecular mechanism by which Ser-SeNPs@MN accelerates the healing of diabetic oral ulcers, we performed a multi-dimensional validation combining transcriptomic and ultrastructural analyses. PCA analysis, Venn diagram and correlation analysis between samples showed clear separation between the Ser-SeNPs@MN group and the DM group (Fig. 9A–C). Volcano plot analysis confirmed 1585 significantly up-regulated and 1331 down-regulated genes in the Ser-SeNPs@MN group (Fig. 9D). A heatmap of key regulatory targets revealed significant up-regulation of genes related to selenium absorption, transport, and selenoprotein synthesis in the Ser-SeNPs@MN group, including SULT family genes, Selenbp1, Selenow, and Gpx2 (Fig. 9E) [15, 45, 46]. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses indicated significant enrichment of anti-inflammatory pathways associated with macrophage M2 polarization (Fig. 9F–G). GSEA demonstrated activation of autophagy and mitophagy pathways (Fig. 9H). Direct observation via TEM (Fig. 9I) provided mechanistic insights: the DM group exhibited typical mitochondrial damage, including swelling and cristae fragmentation, while the Ser-SeNPs@MN group showed mitochondria fusing with lysosomes to form autolysosomes, with internal mitochondrial structures being degraded, confirming the activation of mitophagy in vivo.

These results clarify the potential mechanism by which Ser-SeNPs@MN accelerates the repair of diabetic oral ulcers: Ser-SeNPs@MN synergistically upregulates the expression of genes such as Selenbp1 and Selenow, promotes the synthesis of selenoproteins including Gpx2, induces mitophagy. These actions collectively promote the polarization of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype, ultimately modulating the inflammatory microenvironment in diabetic oral ulcers.

Ser-SeNPs@MN accelerates diabetic soft tissue wound healing by upregulating SELENBP1 to promote mitophagy and suppress inflammation

To validate the in vivo mechanism of Ser-SeNPs@MN in regulating inflammation and promoting mucosal regeneration through the SELENBP1-mitophagy axis, we performed multi-target IF staining analysis on diabetic oral ulcer tissues. The results showed that on day 3, both the DM and Blank MN groups exhibited a significant inflammatory phenotype: fluorescence intensity of the M1 macrophage marker CD86 was enhanced, expression of the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α was elevated, and MPO immunohistochemical staining indicated neutrophil infiltration. Concurrently, expression of the neovascularization marker CD31 was reduced. In contrast, the Ser-SeNPs@MN group showed decreased expression of CD86 and TNF-α, significantly enhanced fluorescence intensity of the anti-inflammatory and reparative M2 macrophage marker CD206, and a marked increase in CD31 + vascular density (Fig. 10A).

Ser-SeNPs@MN upregulates SELENBP1 to promote mitophagy, thereby inhibiting inflammation and accelerating diabetic oral ulcer healing in rats. A IF staining of CD86, CD206, CD31, and TNF-α, SELENBP1, and IHC staining of MPO in oral mucosal tissues. B IF co-staining of LC3 and HSP60 in oral mucosal tissues

To define the core mechanism behind the accelerated healing of diabetic oral ulcers by Ser-SeNPs@MN, we analyzed the expression of SELENBP1 and the activation status of mitophagy. As shown in Fig. 10A, IF staining analysis revealed significantly weakened SELENBP1 intensity in the DM group, while Ser-SeNPs@MN intervention restored its expression to near-normal levels, which aligns with our previous transcriptomic and biochemical findings from animal models. Additionally, fluorescence intensity of mitochondrial heat shock protein 60 (HSP60), reflecting mitochondrial morphology and function, was reduced in the DM group, indicating impaired mitochondrial function. Colocalization of LC3 and HSP60 was also significantly decreased in the DM group and the Blank group. In the Ser-SeNPs@MN group, HSP60 fluorescence intensity was restored, and LC3-HSP60 colocalization returned to near-normal levels, indicating activation of mitophagy (Fig. 10B).

To further demonstrate the broad applicability of this mechanism, the regulatory effects of Ser-SeNPs@MN on the SELENBP1-mitophagy-inflammation axis were also validated in a diabetic rat skin wound model. Consistent with the findings in oral ulcers, Ser-SeNPs@MN upregulated SELENBP1 expression, restored mitophagy, reduced M1 macrophage polarization and TNF-α expression, and enhanced M2 macrophage infiltration and neovascularization. Detailed results are presented in Figure S8.

In summary, these results confirm that Ser-SeNPs@MN activates the SELENBP1-mitophagy axis to suppress inflammation in diabetic soft tissues, including both oral mucosa and skin. This establishes the mechanistic basis for its therapeutic efficacy in accelerating diabetic soft tissue wound healing.

Conclusion

Based on RNA sequencing and ELISA analyses, we identified selenium deficiency and downregulation of SELENBP1 as key pathological features in diabetic oral mucosa, which are closely linked to mitochondrial dysfunction and ulcer formation. To address this pathological mechanism, we developed Ser-SeNPs@MN for targeted selenium supplementation. Mechanistically, Ser-SeNPs@MN acts through multiple coordinated pathways: upregulating SELENBP1 expression, activating PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy to restore mitochondrial function, and promoting M2 macrophage polarization to alleviate inflammation. This multifaceted mechanism significantly accelerates healing in both diabetic oral ulcers and skin wounds, with synergistic effects that promote tissue regeneration. Notably, the novelty of our approach is twofold: first, we identify SELENBP1 as a previously unrecognized therapeutic target in diabetic oral ulcers; second, we integrate green-synthesized selenium nanoparticles with a microneedle patch to achieve targeted selenoprotein synthesis, mitophagy activation, and mitochondrial homeostasis restoration. Beyond preclinical efficacy, the Ser-SeNPs@MN system shows strong clinical potential. Its biocompatible components (sericin, HA, and PVA) are widely used in approved medical products, and the green synthesis of Ser-SeNPs further enhances safety. Collectively, these features support Ser-SeNPs@MN as a minimally invasive, self-administrable patch with significant translational promise for diabetic oral ulcer and skin wound therapy.
