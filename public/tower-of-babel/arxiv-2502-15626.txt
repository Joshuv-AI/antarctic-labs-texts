A Note on Weak Saturation Number of Trees
Wenchong Chen, Xiao-Chuan Liu, Xu Yang

Abstract
. In this paper, we estimate the weak saturation numbers of trees. As a case study, we examine caterpillars and obtain several tight estimates. In particular, this implies that for any α ∈ [ 1 , 2 ] \alpha\in[1,2] , there exist caterpillars with k k vertices whose weak saturation numbers are of order k α k^{\alpha} . We call a tree good if its weak saturation number is exactly its edge number minus one. We provide a sufficient condition for a tree to be a good tree. With the additional property that all leaves are at even distances from each other, this condition fully characterizes good trees. The latter result also provides counterexamples, demonstrating that Theorem 8 of a paper by Faudree, Gould and Jacobson (R. J. Faudree, R. J. Gould, and M. S. Jacobson. Weak saturation numbers for sparse graphs. Discussiones Mathematicae Graph Theory , 33(4): 677-693, 2013.) is incorrect.



1.
Introduction

We follow
[
10
]
for graph theory notation. Throughout, we consider only simple, finite, and undirected graphs.
Given a graph
F
, a graph
G
is said to be weakly

F
-saturated if its non-edges can be added one by one in a specific order, such that each newly added edge forms a new copy of
F
.
For any integer
n
, the weak saturation number of
F
, denoted
\text{w-sat}(n,F)
,
is the minimum size of an
n
-vertex weakly
F
-saturated graph.
This process produces a sequence of graphs, each with one additional edge, culminating in the complete graph
K_{n}
. This process is also referred to as an
F
-bootstrap percolation process. Bollobás
[
2
]
introduced both notions in 1968 and conjectured that

\text{w-sat}(n,K_{k})={k\choose 2}-1+(n-k)(k-2)
for any
n\geq k
, a result later proved by Lovász in
[
9
]
.
Lovász’s proof involves flats in matroids representable over fields and is somewhat mysterious, given that this is a purely combinatorial problem.
Consequently, several researchers have studied these problems, offering various proofs, including those by Frankl
[
6
]
, Kalai
[
7
]
, Alon
[
1
]
, and Yu
[
12
]
. It remains an open question whether a truly elementary combinatorial proof exists, as none of the above qualify. Determining the exact values of weak saturation numbers is generally very difficult.
Aside from the complete graphs discussed above,
a recent result states that
\text{w-sat}(n,K_{t,t})={t\choose 2}+(t-1)(n-2t+1)
, which was proved in
[
8
]

using the elegant linear algebra method.
We also highlight a recent combinatorial approach by Terekhov and Zhukovskii
[
11
]
,
which provides precise weak saturation numbers for certain graphs.

Turning our attention to a more relaxed problem, we can ask
for the correct order and leading coefficients for the weak saturation numbers of some fixed graph

F
with
k
vertices and with minimum degree
\delta
.
A straightforward upper bound
arises from constructing a copy of
K_{k}\backslash\{e\}
along with
n-k
additional vertices,
each joining to
\delta-1
vertices of that copy.
This immediately implies that for any
n\geq v(F)
,

\text{w-sat}(n,F)\leq{k\choose 2}-1+(\delta-1)(n-k)=(\delta-1)n+O(1)
. However, there are no matching lower bounds in general.
Faudree, Gould and Jacobson originally claimed the estimate
\text{w-sat}(n,F)\geq(\frac{\delta}{2}-\frac{1}{\delta+1})n+o(n)
in
[
5
]
, though their proof contained flaws. The result was later corrected by Terekhov and Zhukovskii in
[
11
]
.
In this paper, we mainly discuss weak saturation number of trees.
Likely because their weak saturation numbers are
O(1)
, trees, despite being the simplest and most fundamental types of graphs, have not been fully understood and were somewhat overlooked, with only a few results available, as seen in
[
4
,
5
]
.
First, we note that the number

\text{w-sat}(n,T)
is always non-increasing. The proof will be provided in Section
2
.

Proposition 1.1
.

For any graph
F
with minimum degree
1
,
\text{w-sat}(n,F)
is
non-increasing with respect to
n
for
n\geq v(F)
.

Therefore, for a tree
T
,
\text{w-sat}(n,T)

remains constant for all sufficiently large
n
.
We denote by
\text{w-wat}(T)
the limit number of

\text{w-wat}(n,T)
as
n
tends to infinity.
In extremal graph theory,
a famous rational exponent conjecture (Erdős and Simonovits
[
3
]
)
states that,
for every rational number
r\in(1,2)
, there exists a graph whose Turán exponent equals
r
.
However, for trees, the weak saturation number depends only on their size
k
and not on
n
.
In this paper, we prove
that all rational exponents between
1
and
2

can be realized by weak saturation numbers of trees
in terms of their sizes.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any
\alpha\in[1,2]
,
there exists a family
\{T_{k}\}_{k\geq 5}
of trees such that, where each
T_{k}
has
k
vertices, and

\text{w-sat}(T_{k})=\Theta(k^{\alpha})
.

A nondegenerate caterpillar

C_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{\ell}}
, where
a_{t}>0
,
t=1,2,\cdots,\ell
, is formed by adding
a_{t}

pendant edges to each
v_{t}
of a path

P_{\ell}=(v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{\ell})
, for
every
t=1,\cdots,\ell
.
Our next result gives a precise characterization of the weak saturation numbers for nondegenerate caterpillars.

Theorem 1.3
.

Consider a nondegenerate
caterpillar
T=C_{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{\ell}}
on
k+1
vertices,
and write
a=\min\{a_{t}\,\big|\ 1\leq t\leq\ell\}
. Then we have

(1)

\text{w-sat}(T)=k-1
if and only if
a\leq 2
.

(2)

If
a>2
, then
\text{w-sat}(T)=k-1+{a-1\choose 2}
.

A tree
T
is called a
good tree

if
\text{w-sat}(T)=e(T)-1
.
It is natural to explore the structure of good trees, and we prove the next theorem for this purpose.

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose a tree
T
satisfies that the distances between any two leaves are even.
Then
T
is a good tree
if and only if there exists a leaf that is adjacent to a vertex of degree 2.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we will present some general estimates for trees and more generally graphs with at least one pendant edge. In
Section
3
, we focus on caterpillars and prove Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
.
In Section
4
we prove Theorem
1.4
.

2.
General Estimates

In this section, we present several general estimates for weak saturation numbers as well as the
proof of Proposition
1.1
.
We will use the following definition introduced in
[
5
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a graph
F
, we define an induced subgraph
S=K_{1,s}
to be an end-star of
F
,
if the center of
S
has degree
s+1
in
F
and
s
of the other vertices of
S
have degree 1 in
F
.
If
F
contains at least an end-star,
the minimum end-degree of
F
, denoted by
\delta_{e}(F)
,
is the minimum degree of the center of an end-star of
F
.

The following simple lemma turns out to be crucial.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
F
be a graph on
k+1
vertices with minimum degree
1
and maximum degree
\Delta=\Delta(F)
.
Suppose a graph
G
with
v(G)\geq k+1

admits
an induced subgraph
H
on
k
vertices such that

\delta(H)\geq\Delta
.
Then
G
is weakly
F
-saturated.

Proof.

It suffices to show we can iteratively add edges to the graph
G
,
until it becomes the complete graph
K_{n}
.

(a)

For any

v\in V(H)
and
v^{\prime}\in V(G)-V(H)
,
we can add the edge
vv^{\prime}
. A new copy of
F
is created such that
a pendant edge is mapped to
vv^{\prime}
.

(b)

Now for any

v^{\prime}\in V(G)-V(H)
,
V(H)\subseteq N(v^{\prime})
, which implies

d(v^{\prime})\geq k\geq\Delta+1
.
Then we add all the remaining edges one at a time,
each of which will create a copy
while mapping a pendant edge to the newly added edge.

∎

Proposition
1.1
follows directly from Lemma
2.2
.

Proof of Proposition
1.1
.

Fix
n>m>k
, and let
G
be an
m
-ordered
F
-weakly saturated graph.
Define
H=G+\overline{K}_{n-m}
so that

H
is also weakly
F
-saturated.
Indeed, one can add edges to
G
until it forms
K_{m}
.
Then, by Lemma
2.2
, we can continue adding edges until we reaching
K_{n}
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

(Theorem 9 in
[
5
]
)
Let
F
be a connected graph with
k+1
vertices, containing
at least one end-star.
For any
n
sufficiently large,

(2.1)

\text{w-sat}(F)\leq k-1+{{\delta_{e}(F)}\choose 2}.

We obtain an upper bound estimate that, in certain cases, improves upon the result of the above lemma.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
F
be a graph on
k+1
vertices containing at least an end-star. Let
u
and
w
be adjacent non-pendent vertices in
F
. Let
S
be the union of the neighbors of
u
and
w
that are neither adjacent to any leaf nor equal to
u
or
w
. Then, we have
\text{w-sat}(F)\leq k-1+|S|\delta_{e}
.

Proof.

Let
U=\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{\delta_{e}}\}
be a set of
\delta_{e}
vertices, and let
K_{US}
be a complete bipartite graph with vertex set
U\cup S
. Let
e
be any edge in
F
, and define
G_{1}=F\backslash\{e\}
. We construct the graph
G_{1}
as the union of
F\backslash e
and
K_{US}
. Then let
G=G_{1}+\overline{K}_{n-k-\delta_{e}-1}
, where
n\geq 3k+\delta_{e}+1
. We will prove that
G
is
F
-weakly saturated by adding edges to
G
in the following order:

(a)

Add
e
.

(b)

For any neighbor of
u
or
w
that is adjacent to a leaf, namely
v
, add
vu_{k}
for every
1\leq k\leq\delta_{e}
to replace the leaf.

(c)

Now, for every
1\leq k\leq\delta_{e}
,
u_{k}
is adjacent to all the neighbors of
u
and
w
(except
u
and
w
). We can add
u_{i}u_{j}
to replace
uw
for each
1\leq i<j\leq\delta_{e}
.

(d)

Let
v_{1}
be the isomorphic image of the center of the smallest end-star of
F
. We can add
v_{1}u_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq\delta_{e}
to replace a pendent edge at
v_{1}
.

(e)

Let
v_{2}
be the only initially non-leaf neighbor of
v_{1}
. We can add
v_{2}u_{i}
for any
1\leq i\leq\delta_{e}
to replace
v_{1}
by
u_{i}
, and the pendent vertices at
v_{1}
by
v_{1}
and
u_{j}
(
j\neq i
).

(f)

For any initially isolated vertex
p\in V(\overline{K}_{n-k-\delta_{e}-1})
, add
pu_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq\delta_{e}
to replace
v_{1}
by
u_{i}
, and the pendent vertices at
v_{1}
by
p
and
u_{j}
(
j\neq i
).

(g)

For any
p_{1},p_{2}\in V(\overline{K}_{n-k-\delta_{e}-1})
, add
p_{1}p_{2}
to replace
v_{1}
by
p_{1}
, and the pendent vertices at
v_{1}
by
u_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq\delta_{e}
).

(h)

Now we have obtained a sufficiently large complete graph. By Lemma
2.2
, we can continue adding edges until we obtain
K_{n}
.

∎

Remark 2.5
.

Steps (d) to (h) clarify the proof of Lemma

3.
A Case Study: Caterpillars

Fix nonnegative integers
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{\ell}
,
and define a caterpillar
C_{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{\ell}}
as
the tree obtained by adding
a_{t}
pendant edges to the
t
-th vertex of a path
P_{\ell}
, where
1\leq t\leq\ell
.
In this section, we estimate weak saturation numbers for
caterpillars, providing tight results in many situations. As an application, we also prove Theorem
1.2
.
We begin with a useful lower bound estimate.

Proposition 3.1
.

Consider a caterpillar
T=C_{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{\ell}}
on
k+1
vertices.
Suppose that
a_{k}+a_{k+1}>0
for all
1\leq k\leq\ell-1
.
Define
a=\min\{a_{t}\big|1\leq t\leq\ell\text{ and }a_{t}\neq 0\}
.
Then,

\text{w-sat}(T)\geq k-1+{{a-1}\choose 2}.

Proof.

By convention,
{0\choose 2}={1\choose 2}=0
, making the cases
a=1
and

a=2
trivial.
Thus, we assume
a\geq 2
.
List all non-leaf vertices of
T
, namely
\{v_{1},\cdots,v_{\ell}\}
.
Define
Q=\{v_{t}|a_{t}>0\}
and let
q=|Q|
.
Consider any weakly
T
-saturated graph
G
.
Fix an isomorphic copy of
T\backslash\{e\}
in
G
, and
define
V_{1}
as the image of
Q
, while
V_{2}=V(G)-V_{1}
.

Claim
.
There exists a vertex
u_{1}\in V_{2}
such that
d_{G[V_{2}]}(u_{1})\geq a-2
.

Proof of Claim.

Consider the
T
-bootstrap percolation process starting from
G
.
Let
e^{\prime}
be the first edge added with both endpoints in
V_{2}
, creating a copy of
T
.
Since
a_{k}+a_{k+1}>0
holds for all
1\leq k\leq l-1
, we deduce that each edge of
T
has at least one endpoint in
Q
. Therefore, when creating the new copy, at least one element of
Q
, as an endpoint of
e^{\prime}
, is mapped to
V_{2}
.
Further, we assume that exactly
q_{1}>0

elements of
Q
are mapped to
V_{2}
, and that
these vertices are adjacent to a total of
s
leaves in the copy.
The remaining
q-q_{1}
elements of
Q

are mapped to
V_{1}
. Since
|V_{1}|=q
, at most
q_{1}
vertices in the copy can be leaves. Thus, at least
s-q_{1}
leaves are in
V_{2}
, implying that in
G[V_{2}]
, there exist
q_{1}
vertices collectively adjacent to at least
s-q_{1}
edges. Therefore, some vertex
u_{1}\in V_{2}

satisfies:

d_{G[V_{2}]}(u_{1})\geq\frac{s-q_{1}}{q_{1}}\geq\frac{q_{1}a-q_{1}}{q_{1}}=a-1.

Before adding
e^{\prime}
, we have
d_{G[V_{2}]}(u_{1})\geq a-2
, proving the claim.
∎

Continuing with the proof, we iteratively define vertices

u_{k}
and
V_{2,k}=V_{2,k-1}-\{u_{k}\}
such that
d_{G[V_{2,k-1}]}(u_{k})\geq a-1-k
,
where
k=1,2,\ldots,a-2
and
V_{2,0}=V_{2}
. Consequently, the number of edges in
G[V_{2}]

is at least

e(G[V_{2}])\geq(a-2)+(a-3)+\cdots+1={{a-1}\choose 2}.

Since
a_{k}+a_{k+1}>0
for every
k=1,\cdots,\ell-1
,
every edge in
T
has at least one endpoint in
Q
.
It follows that

e(G[V_{1}])+e(G[V_{1},V_{2}])\geq e(T)-1=k-1
, leading to the total edge count:

e(G[V_{1}])+e(G[V_{1},V_{2}])+e(G[V_{2}])\geq k-1+{{a-1}\choose 2}.

∎

Suppose that in the

-bootstrap percolation process,
the first edge added to

is

, which creates a copy of

.
In that copy, at least one of these two ends of

, say

,
has degree at least

. It follows that

, and all vertices in the initial

in the copy.
Nevertheless, any vertex

, no vertex can serve as the other vertex whose degree is at least

Following the claim, we obtain a vertex

with

. Using similar arguments, we show that there exists a vertex

∎

The following is an immediate corollary.

Corollary 3.6
.

For every tree

with diameter at most

\text{w-sat}(T)
is determined.

Proof.

If

vertices for some

If

, such that for any

.
∎

Corollary 3.7
.

Let

(a)

Let

4.
Local Structures of Good trees

Recall that a tree
T
is a
good tree
if
\text{w-sat}(T)=e(T)-1
.
In this section, we will discuss the structure of a good tree.
The following sufficient condition is a direct consequence of Lemma
2.3
, so we omit the proof.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
T
be a tree with
\delta_{e}(T)=1
, that is, it contains a leaf whose neighbor has degree
2
. Then,
T
is a good tree.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
T
be a tree on
k+1
vertices, where
k\geq 5
. Specify six vertices, namely
v_{1},\ldots,v_{6}
and assume that
T
has the following local structure:

d(v_{1})=d(v_{2})=1
,
d(v_{3})=d(v_{4})=3
, and
N(v_{3})=\{v_{1},v_{4},v_{5}\}

and
N(v_{4})=\{v_{2},v_{3},v_{6}\}
. Then
T
is a good tree.

Proof.

Let
H
be a copy of
T
, and we denote by
u_{i}
the embedding image of
v_{i}
, for
i=1,\cdots,6
. Suppose
n\geq 2k+1
, and
define
G=H\backslash e+\overline{K}_{n-k-1}
, where
e
is any edge of
T
.
We add edges to
G
in the following order:

(a)

Add
e
.

(b)

For any
u\in V(G)-V(H)
,
add
uu_{3}
to
G
;
T
can be embedded such that the edge
v_{1}v_{3}
is sent to
uu_{3}
. Similarly, we can add
uu_{4}
to
G
.

(c)

For any
u\in V(G)-V(H)
, add
uu_{5}
to
G
;
T
can be embedded such that the edges
v_{3}v_{5}
,
v_{3}v_{4}
,
v_{3}v_{1}
are sent to
uu_{5}
,
uu_{3}
,
uu_{4}
, respectively. Similarly, we can add
uu_{6}
to
G
.

(d)

For any two distinct vertices
u^{\prime}
,
u"\in(V(G)-V(H))
,
add
u^{\prime}u"
to
G
;
T
can be embedded such that
the vertices
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
,
v_{3}
,
v_{4}
,
v_{5}
,
v_{6}
,
are sent to
u_{3}
,
u_{4}
,
u^{\prime}
,
u^{\prime\prime}
,
u_{5}
,
u_{6}
, respectively.

(e)

Note that we obtain a complete graph of size at least
n-k
, which, by Lemma
2.2
, is weakly
T
-saturated, and the remaining edges can be added.

∎

The next proposition is a generalization of Theorem 20, item (1) of
[

]
, and the proof is also similar.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
T
be a tree with an induced path
v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{2k}
, where
k\geq 2
. If

satisfies
d(v_{2})=d(v_{3})=\cdots=d(v_{2k-1})=2
, and
v_{1}
and
v_{2k}
are adjacent to leaves, then

is a good tree.

Proof.

Let
H
be a copy of
T
, and denote by
u_{i}
the embedding image of
v_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,2k
. Suppose
n\geq 2v(T)+1
, and
define
G=H\backslash e+\overline{K}_{n-v(T)-1}
, where
e
is any edge of
T
.
We add edges to
G
in the following order:

(a)

Add
e
.

(b)

Add
wv_{1}
for each vertex
w\in V(\overline{K}_{n-v(T)-1})
to replace a pendent edge at

.

(c)

If

, add
wv_{3}
for any vertex

to replace

with

. Similarly, we can add

for all

(d)

For any vertices

and

in

to replace the path

(e)

Note that we obtain a complete graph of size at least

, is weakly

∎

We now prove Theorem
1.4
, which states the sufficient condition given in Lemma
4.1
becomes necessary for certain families of graphs.

Theorem 4.4

(Theorem
1.4
restated)
.

Suppose
T
is a tree on
k+1
vertices, where
k\geq 3
, and the distances between any two leaves are even.
Then,
T
is a good tree
if and only if there exists a leaf adjacent to a vertex of degree 2.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.1
, if there exists a vertex of degree 2 adjacent to a leaf, then
\text{w-sat}(n,T_{k+1})=k-1
.

Now, we only need to show that if
T
satisfies the following two properties, (P1) and (P2),
then
T
is not a good tree.

(P1).
The distances between any two leaves are all even.

(P2).
There is no leaf adjacent to a vertex of degree 2.

For any tree satisfying (P1), we properly color
V(T)
red and blue, assuming that the leaves are always red. Let
R(T)
be the set of red vertices in
V(T)
, and
B(T)
be the set of blue vertices. First, we have the following claim.

Claim 1.
If
T
satisfies (P1), then
|R(T)|>|B(T)|
.

Proof of Claim 1.

Take a non-leaf vertex as an ancestor, namely
v_{0}
. Let

L_{k}=\{v|d(v,v_{0})=k\}.

If the distances between
v_{0}
and the leaves are odd, note that the vertices in
L_{2t}
are non-leaves, and each of them has at least one child in
L_{2t+1}
; hence
|L_{2t}|\leq|L_{2t+1}|
. Also, note that
|L_{0}|<|L_{1}|
since
v_{0}
is non-leaf. Summing these inequalities yields Claim 1.

If the distances between
v_{0}
and the leaves are even, similarly we have
|L_{2t-1}|\leq|L_{2t}|
and
1\leq|L_{0}|
. Summing these inequalities yields Claim 1.
∎

Furthermore, if
T
satisfies (P1) and (P2), we have the following claim. Note that (P2) can also be restated as follows: (P2’) if a non-leaf vertex
v
has at most one non-leaf neighbor, then
d(v)>2
.

Claim 2.
For any tree
T
that satisfies both (P1) and (P2’) and is not
S_{3}
or
S_{4}
, we have
R(T)\geq B(T)+3
.

Proof of Claim 2.

If
T=S_{k+1}
with
k\geq 4
, then
R(T)=B(T)+k-1\geq B(T)+3
.
If
T
is not a star, then there are at least two non-leaves
v_{1},v_{2}\in B(T)
satisfying property (P2’). That is,
each of
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
has exactly one non-leaf neighbor.
By (P2’), we can delete a leaf adjacent to each of
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
to obtain a new tree
T^{\prime}
, which still satisfies (P1). Hence,
R(T^{\prime})\geq B(T^{\prime})+1
, and
R(T)=R(T^{\prime})+2\geq B(T^{\prime})+3=B(T)+3
.
In both cases, we conclude that
R(T)\geq B(T)+3
.
∎

Fix a large integer
n
and let
H
be an isomorphic copy of
T
.
Define
G=H+\overline{K}_{n-k-1}
.
We regard
G
as a bipartite graph with a vertex bipartition

V(G)=V_{1}\cup V_{2}
,
where

V_{1}
represents the isomorphic image of the blue vertices of
T
, and

V_{2}
consists of all the remaining vertices.
By definition,
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are independent sets initially.

Assume, to the contrary, that
T
is a good tree. Then we can continue adding new edges to
G
, creating new copies of
T
at each step, and eventually arrive at
K_{n}
.
Let
e=uv
be the first edge added inside one of the parts
V_{1}
or
V_{2}
, which creates a new copy
T^{\prime}
of
T
.
Thus,
T^{\prime}\backslash\{e\}
is a union of two trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
.
We can define a vertex bipartition

X\cup Y
for the graph
T_{1}+T_{2}

such that

u,v\in X
, where
X
is a subset of
V_{1}
or
V_{2}
, and
Y
is a subset of the other.

Claim 3.

(4.1)

\min\{|X|,|Y|\}>|B(T)|=|V_{1}|.

Proof of Claim 3.

First, by Claim 2,
|R(T)|\geq|B(T)|+2
.

If
e
is a pendant edge of
T^{\prime}
, then clearly
|X|=|B(T)|+1
,
|Y|=|R(T)|-1
, and the conclusion
follows.
So assume
e
is not a pendant edge. Without loss of generality, assume that in
T^{\prime}
,
u\in V(T_{1})
is at even distances to all leaves
and
v\in V(T_{2})
is at odd distances to all the leaves. Hence
u
is red and
v
is blue.
We obtain a bipartition
V(T_{1})=B_{1}\cup R_{1}
, such that
u\in R_{1}
, then
R_{1}\subset X
,
B_{1}\subset Y
, all the vertices in
R_{1}
are coloured red, and those in
B_{1}
are coloured blue.
Note that
T_{1}
contains at least
3
vertices
and satisfies property (P1).
It follows that
|R_{1}|\geq|B_{1}|+1
by Claim 1.
Similarly, we obtain the vertex bipartition
V(T_{2})=B_{2}\cup R_{2}
,
such that
v\in R_{2}
, and we have
R_{2}\subset X
,
B_{2}\subset Y
, vertices in
R_{2}
and
B_{2}
are coloured blue and red respectively.
If
v
is a leaf in
T_{2}
, then

T_{2}
cannot be a single edge,
because otherwise,
T^{\prime}
does not satisfy (P2).
By adding two more leaves to
T_{2}
, we obtain

T_{2}^{\prime}=T_{2}\cup\{vu_{1},vu_{2}\}
, which is not a copy of

S_{4}
and
satisfies both properties (P1)
and (P2).
The pair
B_{2}\cup\{u_{1},u_{2}\}
and
R_{2}

can be regarded as a bipartition for the tree
T_{2}^{\prime}
. Note that
B_{2}\cup\{u_{1},u_{2}\}
is a set including all the leaves of
T_{2}^{\prime}
. By Claim 2, it follows that

|B_{2}|+2\geq|R_{2}|+3
, which means that
|B_{2}|\geq|R_{2}|+1
.
If
v
is not a leaf in
T_{2}
, then
T_{2}
satisfies (P1) and we conclude again

|B_{2}|\geq|R_{2}|+1
.

By the definitions of
X
and
Y
, we have that
Y=B_{1}\cup B_{2}
and
X=R_{1}\cup R_{2}

and that
B=B_{1}\cup R_{2}
and
R=R_{1}\cup B_{2}
.
Moreover,
|Y|=|B_{1}|+|B_{2}|\geq|B_{1}|+|R_{2}|+1=|B(T)|+1
and
|X|=|R_{1}|+|R_{2}|\geq|B_{1}|+|R_{2}|+1=|B(T)|+1
, which implies Claim 3.
∎

Now note that Claim 3 contradicts with the fact that one of
X
and
Y
is a subset of
V_{1}
, and we conclude that
T
is not a good tree.
∎

We now construct a counterexample of Theorem 8 in
[
5
]
. Consider a tree
T
on vertex set

with edge set
\{v_{1}v_{2},v_{1}v_{3},v_{2}v_{4},v_{2}v_{5},v_{3}v_{6},v_{3}v_{7}\}

, and no

[

]
.

One may note that, each of the local structures given above contains a non-pendent vertex with small degree. However, we will show that this is not necessary for a good tree.

Proposition 4.5
.

For any positive integer

Proof.

We can construct
