GPR161 contributes to macrophage glycolytic reprogramming via targeting C5aR1 in acute lung injury
Authors: Yu-Huan Li, Xue Yang, Ying Chen, Miao Wang, Han Shu, Ke Wan, Dan-Tong Sun, Ning Yao, Ying-Li Yang, Fei Zhao, Bo-Bo Han, Chao Yao, Biao Song, Jing Bao, Geng-Yun Sun, Jun Li, Xiao-Feng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute lung injury (ALI) and its more severe form, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), are severe respiratory disorders characterized by a dysregulated and excessive inflammatory response within the pulmonary system. Recent studies have underscored the pivotal role of macrophage activation in driving inflammatory processes, with glycolytic reprogramming emerging as a critical regulator of macrophage function. In this study, we observed significantly elevated expression levels of G protein-coupled receptor 161 (GPR161) in peripheral circulating monocytes from patients with ARDS, with GPR161 expression positively correlating with disease severity. Utilizing genetically engineered mouse models, including global and macrophage-specific conditional knockout mice, we demonstrated that GPR161 deficiency attenuated pulmonary inflammatory damage in lipopolysaccharide-induced and sepsis-associated ALI mice. In vitro experiments further elucidated the essential role of GPR161 in macrophage activation and glycolytic reprogramming. Mechanistic investigations, integrating RNA sequencing with co-immunoprecipitation and surface plasmon resonance assays, identified complement component 5a receptor 1 (C5aR1) as a downstream target of GPR161 and showed that GPR161 promotes glycolytic reprogramming in macrophages by suppressing C5aR1 expression. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GPR161 enhances macrophage activation and glycolytic reprogramming in ALI/ARDS through a C5aR1-dependent mechanism. These results establish macrophage GPR161 as a promising therapeutic target for the treatment of ALI/ARDS.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-026-00955-3.

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI)/acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) are severe clinical conditions marked by a swift onset of extensive inflammation, hypoxemia, and respiratory failure [1]. Despite advances in areas such as mechanical ventilation and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, the mortality rate associated with this condition remains persistently high (up to 40%) [2]. Generally, uncontrolled inflammatory responses are considered to play a central role in perpetuating lung injury [3, 4].

The pulmonary innate immune system represents the foremost protective mechanism against exogenous pathogenic insults. Resident innate immune cells within the pulmonary epithelium are fundamentally involved in the regulation of pulmonary homeostasis, among which alveolar macrophages have garnered significant research attention. These specialized immune regulators, strategically positioned within the pulmonary microenvironment, are critically implicated in orchestrating the initial inflammatory cascade associated with ALI/ARDS [5, 6]. Activated macrophages serve as crucial pathological indicators in inflammatory processes [7–9]. These cells orchestrate inflammatory processes by secreting significant amounts of key inflammatory mediators, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNFα), interleukin-6 (IL6), and interleukin-1β (IL1β) [10]. The excessive production of these cytokines not only amplifies pulmonary inflammation but also facilitates the recruitment of additional immune cells, particularly neutrophils and inflammatory monocytes/macrophages, thereby exacerbating pulmonary tissue damage [11]. Consequently, the modulation of macrophage activation has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for the management of ALI/ARDS.

Recent investigations have established glycolytic reprogramming as a fundamental mechanism underlying macrophage activation [12]. In usual circumstances, macrophages primarily utilize oxidative phosphorylation for generating adenosine triphosphate [13, 14]. However, during acute inflammatory responses, these cells undergo a metabolic shift toward glycolysis to meet their heightened energetic and biosynthetic requirements, a phenomenon termed glycolytic reprogramming [15]. This metabolic transition represents a critical regulatory mechanism in inflammatory processes, as macrophage activation is intrinsically linked to metabolic reprogramming [16]. Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting macrophage metabolic pathways hold significant potential for the prevention and management of ALI/ARDS [17]. However, to our knowledge, clinical translation of metabolic reprogramming-based interventions for ALI/ARDS treatment remains unexplored.

G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) are key sensors of the cellular microenvironment and master regulators of diverse physiological processes, including metabolism and inflammation [18]. Pharmacological modulation of GPCRs has enabled their widespread clinical application, and drugs targeting this receptor family account for nearly 30% of all therapeutics currently in use [19]. In this study, we sought to delineate the differential expression patterns of GPCRs in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from patients with ARDS and investigated their potential mechanistic contributions to ALI/ARDS pathogenesis. Comparative profiling of inflammation-related GPCRs in PBMCs revealed that G protein-coupled receptor 161 (GPR161) exhibited the most pronounced dysregulation in patients with ARDS compared with healthy controls. This observation prompted us to investigate its potential role in inflammatory lung injury. GPR161 is well known as a negative regulator of the Hedgehog signaling pathway and has been identified as a novel genetic predisposition factor for pediatric brain tumors [20, 21]. Clinically, GPR161 mutations are associated with developmental abnormalities, including pituitary stalk interruption syndrome. The embryonic lethality observed in GPR161-knockout mice further underscores its essential role in development [22]. Notably, GPR161 overexpression has been implicated in the pathogenesis of triple-negative breast cancer [23]. Emerging evidence from comprehensive genomic analyses has revealed significant associations between GPR161 alterations and various inflammatory disorders, including mastitis [24] and enteritis [25]. Nonetheless, the effects of GPR161 in ALI/ARDS remain unclear and its underlying mechanisms have yet to be fully determined.

In this study, we elucidated the functional role of GPR161 in ALI/ARDS pathogenesis and macrophage activation. Mechanistic investigations revealed that GPR161 mediates macrophage activation by suppressing the expression of complement component 5a receptor 1 (C5aR1), a widely expressed receptor on diverse immune cells, including macrophages, neutrophils, monocytes, and dendritic cells [26]. Interestingly, recent evidence has highlighted context-dependent protective functions of C5aR1, including the mitigation of acute inflammation and maintenance of mitochondrial metabolic homeostasis [27]. Our findings further establish that C5aR1 facilitates glucose metabolic reprogramming in activated macrophages through a pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2)-dependent mechanism. These results collectively identify GPR161 as both a critical regulator of macrophage activation and a potential therapeutic target for ALI/ARDS.

Materials and methods

Study population and sample collection

Patients with ARDS resulting from direct lung injury (pneumonia or aspiration) were enrolled from the intensive care unit at Anhui Chest Hospital (Hefei, China). Blood and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples were collected from these patients. Control blood samples were obtained from healthy donors recruited through the hospital’s health examination center, and control BALF samples were collected from patients whose final diagnoses ruled out malignancies and acute inflammation (e.g., hamartoma, nonorganic chronic cough). PBMCs were isolated from both patients with ARDS and healthy controls using a commercially available Human Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cell Isolation Kit (Tianjin Hao Yang Biological, China). BALF samples were centrifuged at 4 °C to separate cell pellets from supernatant. This study protocol was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Anhui Chest Hospital (approval no. KJ2024-064) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided documented consent for the use of their clinical specimens in biomedical research.

Mice

GPR161 conditional knockout mice (Gpr161F/F) and male C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from Gempharmatech (Nanjing, China). R26-CreERT2 and Lyz-Cre tool mice were acquired from Shanghai Model Organisms. According to the supplier’s information, the Gpr161 floxed allele was generated using CRISPR/Cas9 with the following single-guide RNA (sgRNA) sequences: 5′-AGCCTATTCGCTAAGTAGAG-3′ and 5′-GAGTCAGCCTATCTTATTAC-3′. To generate the experimental mice, we crossed Gpr161F/F mice with R26-CreERT2 or Lyz-Cre mice. The double-heterozygous offspring were then intercrossed to establish and maintain two stable lines: tamoxifen-inducible systemic knockout mice (Gpr161F/F, R26-CreERT2) and macrophage-specific GPR161-deficient mice (Gpr161F/F, Lyz-Cre) for subsequent experiments. For systemic deletion of GPR161, Gpr161F/F, R26-CreERT2 mice received intraperitoneal injections of tamoxifen for five consecutive days prior to experiments, while macrophage-specific knockout mice exhibited constitutive GPR161 deficiency in macrophages without additional induction. All animals were kept in a pathogen-free environment at the Laboratory Animal Center of Anhui Medical University, maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Experimental procedures were conducted using 8–10-week-old mice and were performed in compliance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Anhui Medical University (approval no. 83243424).

Genotyping of transgenic mice

Tail biopsies (3-5 mm) were obtained using sterile ophthalmic forceps with cauterization and immediately transferred to sterile 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tubes. Genomic DNA was extracted by incubating samples in 300 μL of tail lysis buffer containing 5 μL proteinase K (20 mg/mL) at 55 °C for 6-8 h with constant agitation. Following complete tissue digestion, samples were vortexed vigorously for 15 s to ensure homogeneous lysate formation. Cellular debris was pelleted by centrifugation at 12,000g for 5 min at 4 °C. The clarified supernatant was transferred to a fresh tube, and genomic DNA was precipitated by adding an equal volume of ice-cold isopropanol with gentle inversion mixing. After centrifugation at 12,000g for 5 min at 4 °C, the DNA pellet was washed with 70% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in 100 μL of nuclease-free water. Genomic DNA samples were subjected to polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification using allele-specific primers for the loci. The PCR products were separated by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the electrophoresis was stopped when the bromophenol blue dye migrated to one-third of the gel length from the bottom. The gel was then visualized and analyzed using a gel imaging system. The complete sequences of all mouse-tail genotyping primers (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced ALI model

Following anesthesia induction, mice received intratracheal administration of LPS (Sigma-Aldrich, L2630, USA) at 5 mg/kg in 50 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Control animals were administered an equivalent volume of sterile PBS alone. At 24 h post LPS challenge, animals were humanely euthanized, and samples of lung tissue along with BALF were collected for subsequent analytical procedures.

Sepsis-associated ALI model (s-ALI)

Following anesthesia induction, mice underwent cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) to induce s-ALI. A midline incision was made to exteriorize the cecum, which was ligated and punctured with a 21-gauge needle. Sham-operated mice served as controls. All mice received a subcutaneous bolus of resuscitative fluid post-surgery. At 24 h post CLP, the animals were humanely euthanized and samples of lung tissue along with BALF were collected for subsequent analytical procedures.

Histopathology

The inferior lobe of the right lung from mice was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Sectioned lung tissues were then processed for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, immunofluorescence (IF) analysis, and immunohistochemistry (IHC). Stained images were captured by Case-Viewer software (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary).

BALF supernatant collection

Following anesthesia induction, BALF was collected through sequential intratracheal instillation of 800 µL ice-cold PBS, repeated three times. The lavage fluid was carefully aspirated after each instillation. The pooled BALF samples were centrifuged at 1000g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove cellular components, and the resulting cell-free supernatants were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C for subsequent biochemical analyses.

Isolation of alveolar macrophages from BALF

BALF was centrifuged at 250-300g for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended in 1 mL red blood cell lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), incubated for 1-2 min at room temperature, neutralized with ice-cold PBS, and centrifuged again. Cells were resuspended in 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) medium, counted, and seeded into culture plates. After 1.5-2.5 h incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, nonadherent cells were washed off with warm medium. Adherent cells were used as alveolar macrophages.

Cell culture and transfection

The MH-S murine alveolar macrophage cell line (catalog no. iCell-m078; iCell Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (PAN-Biotech GmbH, Aidenbach, Germany) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Cells were stimulated with 100 ng/mL LPS (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; catalog no. L2630) for 24 h. For genetic manipulation experiments, GPR161 knockdown was achieved through lentiviral transduction using short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) cloned into the pAdEasy-U6-CMV-EGFP vector (Hanheng Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), with two independent constructs generated (Gpr161-sh1 and Gpr161-sh2). GPR161 overexpression was accomplished by cloning the full-length GPR161-AS2 sequence (OBiO Technology, Shanghai, China) into the GL2003pcADV-EF1-mNeonGreen-CMV-MCS-3xFLAG plasmid (Ad-Gpr161). C5aR1 and GPR161 silencing was performed using specific small interfering RNAs (siRNAs; GenePharma, Shanghai, China), which were transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The complete sequences of all shRNA and siRNA constructs are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL1β, IL6, and TNFα, in murine BALF were quantitatively determined using commercially available ELISA kits (Elabscience Biotechnology, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s standardized protocols.

Pyruvate kinase activity assay

A pyruvate kinase activity assay kit (Elabscience Biotechnology, Wuhan, China) was employed to determine enzyme activity, in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Lung wet/dry ratio (W/D)

Following careful removal of surface blood contamination using sterile filter paper, the left lung lobe was taken out and weighed immediately to determine its wet weight. The tissue was then dehydrated in a constant-temperature oven at 60 °C for 48 h to obtain its dry weight. The W/D weight ratio was calculated and used as a quantitative indicator to evaluate the extent of pulmonary edema formation.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT–qPCR)

For RT-qPCR analysis, total RNA was extracted from MH-S cells and lung tissues using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Subsequently, RNA samples were converted into complementary DNA (cDNA) with the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara, Kyoto, Japan). Quantitative PCR amplification was performed using the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (AG Biotechnology, Changsha, China) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The specific primer sequences used for RT-qPCR analysis are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Western blotting (WB)

Total proteins were extracted using a radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer supplemented with phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Protein samples were resolved by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with protein-free rapid blocking buffer (Epizyme Biomedical Technology, Shanghai, China) for 15 min at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted 1:1000. Primary antibodies against GPR161, C5aR1, PKM2, and hexokinase 2 (HK2) were obtained from Proteintech Group (Wuhan, China), while antibodies targeting IL6, IL1β, TNFα, and β-Actin were purchased from Bioworld Technology (Shanghai, China). Following primary antibody incubation, membranes were treated with suitable horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 60 min at room temperature. The visualization of protein bands was achieved through the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

Molecular docking

The protein structures were retrieved from the UniProt database and subsequently imported into PyMol 2.3.0 for preprocessing to remove water molecules, ions, and other solvent molecules. The interaction mode between the target proteins was then investigated using the HDOCK server, a comprehensive and integrated platform supporting homology search, template-based modeling, structure prediction, macromolecular docking, bioinformation integration, and job management. By inputting the receptor and ligand information-provided as amino acid sequences or Protein Data Bank (PDB) structures-the server automatically predicts molecular interactions through a hybrid algorithm combining both template-based and template-free docking approaches. Finally, the resulting docking models were imported back into PyMol 2.3.0 for in-depth analysis of the interaction mode.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

SPR was employed to quantitatively determine the binding affinities between GPR161 and C5aR1, as well as between C5aR1 and PKM2. For each interaction, recombinant proteins were immobilized on the sensor chip, followed by the injection of a concentration series of the corresponding analyte. The equilibrium dissociation constants (KD) were subsequently calculated by fitting the steady-state binding responses to a 1:1 Langmuir binding model. Recombinant human GPR161 protein was obtained from FineTest (Wuhan, China), recombinant human C5aR1 from CUSABIO (Wuhan, China), and recombinant human PKM2 from Novoprotein (Suzhou, China). All SPR experiments were performed at Prometheus Biotechnology (Beijing, China).

Glucose consumption, lactate production, and seahorse assays

The Glucose Uptake-Glo™ Assay (Promega) and Lactate-Glo™ Assay (Promega) were employed to quantitatively assess intracellular glucose levels and lactate production, respectively. Cellular metabolic activity was further evaluated using the Seahorse XF24 extracellular flux analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience) to measure extracellular acidification rates (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rates (OCR). Briefly, MH-S cells that received various experimental treatments were seeded into a 24-well cell-culture microplate. The sensor cartridge was immersed in calibration solution (Seahorse Bioscience, USA) and equilibrated overnight in a CO₂-free incubator. Following calibration, mitochondrial respiration was assessed using the XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit by sequential injection of pharmacological agents, including oligomycin, carbonyl cyanide-p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP), and a mix of antimycin A and rotenone (Rot/AA). OCR measurements were recorded under basal conditions and following each inhibitor treatment. Glycolytic activity was determined using the XF Glycolysis Stress Test Kit, with ECAR measurements taken following sequential administration of glucose, oligomycin, and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG). This comprehensive approach enabled simultaneous evaluation of mitochondrial function and glycolytic flux in the experimental cell system.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

Co-IP experiments were conducted following the manufacturer’s protocol (Wanlei Bio, Shenyang, China). Briefly, MH-S cells were lysed on ice for 40 min using RIPA lysis buffer, and the resulting lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000g for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. The supernatant was then incubated with the appropriate primary antibody under constant agitation at 4 °C overnight to facilitate the formation of antigen-antibody complexes. The following day, prewashed protein A/G magnetic beads were added to the complex mixture and incubated for an additional 2 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation to allow for the capture of the immune complexes. After extensive washing to remove nonspecific binding, the immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted and processed with SDS-PAGE, followed by immunoblotting analysis to detect the target proteins. This protocol ensured the specific isolation and identification of protein-protein interactions within the MH-S cell system.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

After infection with Gpr161-Sh1, RNA-seq analysis of MH-S cells was conducted at LC-Bio Technologie (Hangzhou, China).

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPad Prism 9.5 was used for all statistical analyses. Two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for two-group comparisons. For multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post hoc test was applied when variances were homogeneous (Levene’s test, P > 0.05); otherwise, Welch’s ANOVA with Dunnett’s T3 post hoc test was used. Adjusted P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

GPR161 expression is upregulated in PBMCs of patients with ARDS

To investigate the potential role of GPR161 in ARDS, PBMCs were isolated from 15 patients with ARDS and 15 healthy controls. Detailed clinical characteristics of the patients with ARDS are presented in Supplementary Table S4. Following data preprocessing, comparative analysis revealed a significant upregulation of GPR161 expression in PBMCs from patients with ARDS compared with those from healthy individuals (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1A). To validate these experimental observations, we performed an integrated bioinformatics analysis utilizing two independent, publicly available ARDS-related microarray datasets (GSE243066 and GSE172114) obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Notably, the bioinformatic interrogation of these datasets confirmed our initial findings, demonstrating consistent upregulation of GPR161 expression in ARDS samples compared with controls (Fig. 1B). To further explore the clinical relevance of GPR161, Pearson correlation analysis was performed, demonstrating that GPR161 mRNA levels exhibited a significant negative correlation with the PaO2/FiO2 ratio (P < 0.05) and a positive correlation with levels of procalcitonin (PCT) and C-reactive protein (CRP) in patients with ARDS (Fig. 1C). Collectively, the significant correlations reveal that elevated GPR161 expression is associated with both worse lung function and a more intense inflammatory state. Notably, GPR161 mRNA expression also showed a strong positive correlation with the levels of key proinflammatory cytokines and chemotactic chemokines, including IL1β, IL6, TNFα, CCL2, CCL3, and CCL4 (Fig. 1D and E). These findings suggest that GPR161 may play a functional role in the pathogenesis of ALI/ARDS.

As anticipated, GPR161 protein expression was significantly downregulated in the lung tissue of Gpr161-knockout (KO) mice (Fig. 3B). GPR161 regulates glycolytic reprogramming in activated macrophages

In vivo experiments demonstrated that blocking macrophage GPR161 attenuates alveolar inflammation. To further elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we investigated the role of GPR161 in metabolic regulation using in vitro models. GPR161 expression was modulated in MH-S cells using shRNA-mediated knockdown (sh-Gpr161) or adenovirus-mediated overexpression (Ad-Gpr161). The efficacy of viral transfection was validated by RT-qPCR (Supplementary Fig. S5A). The results revealed that ECAR, an indicator of glycolytic activity, was significantly reduced in sh-Gpr161-transfected cells compared with the control group, while OCR, a measure of mitochondrial respiration, was significantly increased in the sh-Gpr161 group (Fig. 5A). Conversely, overexpression of GPR161 using an Ad-Gpr161 resulted in elevated ECAR and reduced OCR compared with the negative control (Ad-Nc) (Fig. 5B). Consistent with these findings, lactate production and glucose uptake assays demonstrated that GPR161 knockdown suppressed glucose uptake and lactate production in macrophages, whereas GPR161 overexpression enhanced these metabolic activities (Fig. 5C). This metabolic reprogramming is closely associated with alveolar inflammation. Thus, GPR161 represents a promising therapeutic target for modulating macrophage metabolism and inflammation in ALI.

Among these, C5aR1 emerged as the most prominently altered gene, showing a striking inverse correlation with GPR161 expression levels (Fig. 6C). WB and RT-qPCR analyses revealed that GPR161 knockdown upregulated C5aR1 expression, while GPR161 overexpression downregulated C5aR1 levels in LPS-induced macrophages (Fig. 6D and E). To investigate potential interactions between C5aR1 and GPR161, we conducted structural modeling and molecular docking analyses. The docking results demonstrated a binding energy of −484.07 kcal/mol for the C5aR1-GPR161 complex, with hydrogen bonds forming at the protein-protein interaction interface. Specifically, SER-240, TRP-111, and LYS-108 of C5aR1 formed hydrogen bonds with GLY-502, GLN-200, and HIS-199 of GPR161, respectively (Fig. 6G). These computational predictions suggested a potential interaction between C5aR1 and GPR161, which we next sought to validate experimentally. Co-IP assays in macrophages confirmed the association between C5aR1 and GPR161 proteins (Fig. 6H). To further determine whether this association reflects a direct physical interaction, we performed SPR analysis using purified recombinant proteins. The SPR results demonstrated a dose-dependent binding response, with a calculated KD of 1.03 × 10−5 M, confirming a direct interaction between GPR161 and C5aR1 (Fig. 6I). These findings unequivocally demonstrate that GPR161 and C5aR1 are capable of direct physical binding.

To extend these findings to physiologically relevant contexts, we examined C5aR1 mRNA expression in alveolar macrophages from patients with ARDS and Gpr161 myeloid-specific knockout mice. Notably, C5aR1 levels were significantly reduced in alveolar macrophages isolated from BALF of patients with ARDS compared with healthy controls (Supplementary Fig. S6A), consistent with the elevated GPR161 expression observed under inflammatory conditions. Conversely, genetic ablation of Gpr161 in myeloid cells led to a marked upregulation of C5aR1 in alveolar macrophages (Supplementary Fig. S6B), corroborating the negative regulatory axis identified in vitro.

To further validate the functional relevance of this regulatory axis in human cells, we performed GPR161 knockdown using specific siRNA in primary alveolar macrophages isolated from patients with ARDS. Consistent with our observations in MH-S cells, GPR161 silencing resulted in significant upregulation of C5aR1 mRNA (Supplementary Fig. S6C), accompanied by downregulation of PKM2 and IL1β mRNA (Supplementary Fig. S5F and S5G). These findings demonstrate that the GPR161-C5aR1-PKM2 regulatory axis identified in MH-S cells is faithfully recapitulated in primary human alveolar macrophages, further supporting its pathophysiological relevance in ARDS.

Collectively, these findings establish C5aR1 as both a direct binding partner and a key transcriptional target negatively regulated by GPR161. This functional coupling provides a mechanistic basis for GPR161-mediated control of macrophage activation and offers new insights into the molecular underpinnings of inflammatory lung diseases such as ARDS.

WB and RT-qPCR analyses revealed that C5aR1 knockdown significantly upregulated PKM2 expression at both protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 7G, H). Consistent with enhanced glycolytic capacity, C5aR1 knockdown also upregulated the expression of inflammatory factors Il1β and Il6 (Fig. 7G, I). However, PKM2 enzymatic activity assays showed that C5aR1 silencing did not alter the specific activity of PKM2 (Fig. 7J), indicating that C5aR1 regulates PKM2 primarily through modulating its expression rather than its intrinsic enzymatic activity.

To further delineate the functional role of C5aR1 downstream of GPR161, we performed rescue experiments in alveolar macrophages. MH-S cells were transfected with sh-Gpr161 alone or in combination with C5aR1-siRNA. As expected, GPR161 knockdown significantly increased C5aR1 expression. Notably, co-transfection with C5aR1-siRNA effectively reversed this effect, leading to reduced C5aR1 levels compared with the sh-Gpr161-alone group (Fig. 7K and L). Importantly, this reduction in C5aR1 was accompanied by increased expression of PKM2 and IL1β, as demonstrated by both western blot and RT-qPCR analyses (Fig. 7K, M and N). These results indicate that C5aR1 mediates the effects of GPR161 on downstream glycolytic reprogramming and inflammatory cytokine production.

To extend these findings to in vivo settings, we utilized myeloid-specific GPR161-knockout mice in two models of ALI. In the LPS-induced ALI model, western blot analysis of lung tissues demonstrated that GPR161 deficiency significantly increased C5aR1 protein expression while decreasing PKM2 protein levels (Fig. S7D). RT-qPCR analysis of lung tissues confirmed corresponding upregulation of C5aR1 mRNA and downregulation of Pkm2 mRNA (Supplementary Fig. S7E and S7F). In the sepsis-associated ALI model, a similar pattern was observed in lung tissues: GPR161 knockout resulted in elevated C5aR1 and reduced PKM2 at both the protein and mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. S7G-S7I).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the GPR161-C5aR1 axis governs macrophage inflammatory responses by modulating PKM2-mediated glycolytic reprogramming. This regulatory mechanism is conserved across distinct inflammatory challenges in vivo and highlights a critical metabolic checkpoint in the pathogenesis of ALI.

Discussion

Alveolar macrophages (AMs), the predominant immune cell population in the alveolar space, serve as the first line of host defense against pulmonary pathogens and environmental insults [28, 29]. AMs can be polarized into two distinct activation states: the proinflammatory M1 phenotype and the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [30]. Precise regulation of macrophage activation is critical for maintaining pulmonary homeostasis and modulating inflammatory responses, particularly in the context of inflammatory diseases such as ALI/ARDS [31–33]. Our study identifies GPR161 as a novel regulator of this process, demonstrating that its genetic inhibition curtails proinflammatory cytokine release (e.g., IL6, TNFα, IL1β) and confers protection in LPS-induced and sepsis-associated ALI models, nominating it as a promising therapeutic target.

This inflammatory role of GPR161 appears functionally distinct from its well-established function as a negative regulator of Hedgehog (Hh) signaling in developmental contexts [34, 35]. While the latter has been extensively characterized, the role of GPR161 in inflammation has remained largely unexplored. Our findings uncover a noncanonical, Hh-independent function for GPR161, repositioning it as a pleiotropic signaling node in inflammation. Using macrophage-specific GPR161-knockout mice, we demonstrated that this inflammatory function is cell-intrinsic to macrophages. The protective phenotype observed in both LPS-induced and sepsis-associated ALI models underscores the broad relevance of this axis across different ALI etiologies, revealing an Hh-independent, inflammatory role in macrophages that is distinct from its established developmental function.

A key mechanism underlying GPR161-mediated inflammation involves metabolic reprogramming, a well-established driver of macrophage phenotypic polarization [36–39]. Mirroring the classic metabolic profile of proinflammatory M1 macrophages, which undergo a pronounced shift from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis to meet heightened energetic and biosynthetic demands [40], we found that GPR161 is a direct regulator of this process. This is particularly relevant in ARDS, a condition characterized by significant hypoxia that further forces macrophages to rely on glycolysis [41–44]. By modulating GPR161 expression in alveolar macrophages, we identified a strong correlation between GPR161 and glucose metabolism. Specifically, GPR161 knockdown suppressed glycolysis (reduced ECAR, glucose uptake, and lactate production) while enhancing mitochondrial respiration (increased OCR), effects that were reversed upon GPR161 overexpression. At the molecular level, Gpr161 mRNA levels were positively associated with the expression of key glycolytic enzymes Pkm2 and Hk2. The clinical relevance of this metabolic axis was further supported by the finding that PKM2 expression was significantly elevated in alveolar macrophages from patients with ARDS together with its marked reduction in macrophages from GPR161 myeloid-specific knockout mice.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which GPR161 orchestrates this metabolic reprogramming, we performed RNA sequencing. This analysis in GPR161-knockdown macrophages revealed significant transcriptomic alterations, with C5aR1 emerging as the most prominently altered gene, showing a striking inverse correlation with GPR161 expression levels. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis further highlighted a strong association between low GPR161 expression and C5aR1 signaling. This led us to hypothesize that C5aR1 might serve as a binding partner for GPR161. This notion was confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation assays and, importantly, by SPR analysis using purified recombinant proteins, which demonstrated a direct and specific interaction with a calculated KD of 1.03 × 10–5 M, providing definitive evidence for direct binding. Furthermore, siRNA-mediated knockdown of C5aR1 not only impaired mitochondrial respiration but also mimicked the metabolic effects of GPR161 manipulation. Rescue experiments further solidified this link: co-transfection with C5aR1-siRNA reversed the effects of GPR161 knockdown on PKM2 and IL1β expression. These findings indicate that C5aR1 acts downstream of GPR161 to mediate glycolytic reprogramming and inflammatory cytokine production.

We further dissected the downstream events linking C5aR1 to glycolysis. Co-IP and SPR assays confirmed a direct interaction between C5aR1 and PKM2. Interestingly, while C5aR1 knockdown significantly upregulated PKM2 expression at both protein and mRNA levels, it did not alter PKM2’s intrinsic enzymatic activity. This indicates that C5aR1 primarily governs PKM2 expression, not its enzymatic function, revealing a nuanced layer of metabolic control, potentially at the level of PKM2 stability, transcription, or translation. The in vivo relevance of this axis was confirmed in GPR161 myeloid-specific knockout mice, which showed increased C5aR1 expression alongside decreased PKM2 levels in lung tissues from both LPS-induced and sepsis-associated ALI models.

While this study establishes a crucial role for the GPR161-C5aR1-PKM2 axis in macrophage reprogramming during ALI, several key questions remain. First, the precise molecular events downstream of the C5aR1–PKM2 interaction require further elucidation. The mechanism by which C5aR1, upon binding to PKM2, ultimately leads to decreased PKM2 expression, whether through transcriptional regulation, reduced mRNA stability, or enhanced protein turnover, requires further investigation. Future studies employing mRNA stability assays and proteasome inhibition experiments will be essential to dissect these intermediate events and define the complete signaling cascade from GPR161 engagement to metabolic reprogramming. Second, while our data suggest that C5aR1 does not affect PKM2’s intrinsic enzymatic activity under our experimental conditions, the complex may still influence PKM2’s noncanonical functions, such as its nuclear translocation and co-transcriptional regulation of inflammatory genes [45, 46]. Third, the generalizability of our findings to other ALI/ARDS etiologies, such as viral pneumonia or aspiration, requires further study. Finally, the development of selective pharmacological tools to modulate GPR161 function, which are currently lacking, will be essential to explore its full therapeutic potential.

Collectively, our study provides compelling in vitro and in vivo evidence that GPR161 plays a pivotal role in ALI/ARDS pathogenesis by promoting macrophage activation and glycolytic reprogramming in a C5aR1-dependent manner. These findings deepen our understanding of the disease pathophysiology and identify GPR161 as a promising therapeutic target.
