Lung microbiota dysbiosis exacerbates influenza virus infection
Authors: Hai Chang Yin, Xin Yu Zhang, Song Liu, Xinjie Jiang, Tian Fei Yu, He Zhang, Di Liu, Chang You Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have shown that microbiota in the respiratory tract play a significant role in shaping host immunity and reducing the risk of respiratory tract infections. Influenza, an acute respiratory infectious disease caused by the influenza virus (IAV), necessitates to have an in-depth understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms of viral infection and to utilize these mechanisms to prevent and control influenza infection.ResultsIn this study, we established models of different disease severity levels in mice infected with IAV, the relationships between changes in pulmonary microbiota and host interactions during IAV infection were analyzed. We found that these relationships were characterized by the enrichment of opportunistic pathogenic taxonomic groups and were related to disease severity. Multi-omics analysis combined with animal experiments clarified that colonization of Bacillus cereus in the lungs aggravates IAV infection through xanthosine, thereby downregulating the expression of nt5e and reducing the viral phagocytic ability of alveolar macrophages. Importantly, targeted removal of B. cereus by bacteriophages can alleviate IAV infection and the resulting pulmonary lesions.ConclusionsHighlighting provides a novel approach for preventing and controlling the progression of IAV infection by targeting the lung microbiota.In briefLung dysbiosis is associated with severe influenza virus (IAV) infection. Bacillus cereus aggravates IAV infection via the xanthosine-nt5e axis in SPF mice, which can be rescued by bacteriophage targeting. These findings highlight the potential of modulating the lung microbiota as a strategy for prevention and controlling IAV infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40168-026-02450-5.Keywords: Lung microbiota, IAV infection, B. cereus, Xanthosine, Nt5e, Bacteriophages

Introduction

Influenza is an infectious respiratory disease. Influenza A virus (IAV) and Influenza B virus (IBV) are the main causes of seasonal influenza, and severe symptoms can lead to lung damage or death. Annually, approximately 250,000–500,000 people die from flu, posing a huge burden on public health systems [1]. Currently, the most commonly used anti-influenza drugs in clinical practice are oseltamivir and ribavirin. Severely ill patients require combined treatment with immunoglobulins and thymosin [2]. Anti-influenza virus drugs have gradually become ineffective owing to the evolution of the virus and the abuse of drugs, leading to the emergence of drug-resistant strains [2]. Currently, vaccination is the most effective way to prevent the occurrence and spread of influenza. However, because of the numerous subtypes and rapid mutations in the influenza virus, existing vaccines are insufficient to provide comprehensive protection, which further increases the cost of prevention and difficulty of control [3]. Therefore, considering the possibility of a major epidemic of new influenza strains, there is an urgent need to develop new, safe and efficient biological agents for improved prevention and control. Moreover, it is important to identify host factors that influence influenza susceptibility to formulate supplementary influenza prevention strategies. One potential preventive strategy is to manipulate the host symbiotic microbiota, including the microbiota of the gastrointestinal system, skin, urogenital tract, and respiratory tract (e.g., nose, pharynx, and lungs).

Changes in the respiratory microbiome of the influenza virus, a respiratory pathogen, can affect an individual’s susceptibility to influenza [4], and this susceptibility is somewhat related to age [5]. Ding et al. [4] found that acute respiratory tract infections caused by different influenza virus subtypes differed in their oropharyngeal microbiota. In patients with IAV, the main species are Atopobium and Prevotella, whereas in patients with IBV, Bergeyella and Prevotella are dominant [6]. Nevertheless, the mechanism through which the respiratory microbiota affects susceptibility to IAV remains unclear.

Lung microbiota is an important component of the respiratory tract microbiota. It is stable under normal conditions, but may change due to infection, inflammation, or chronic respiratory diseases [7]. Changes in the lung microbiota homeostasis are associated with the occurrence and development of respiratory diseases. Relevant studies mainly focus on chronic inflammatory diseases of the lungs, including asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and chronic pulmonary fibrosis [8–10]. Previous studies have found that viruses including SARS-CoV-2, HIV, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) can alter the host’s respiratory tract microbiota, and these alterations in lung microbiota play an important regulatory role in the disease process [11, 12]. Liming et al. [13] found that IAV infection could lead to changes in the abundance and diversity of the lower respiratory tract microbiota in mice, suggesting a potentially important role for the lung microbiota in influenza virus infections. Therefore, clarifying the mechanisms by which lung microbiota disorders during IAV infection and taking effective measures to control the pathogenesis of influenza are vital for the prevention and control of IAV.

In this study, we attempted to clarify the relationship between lung microbiota disorders and the progression of IAV infection. We first employed strain transplantation to reveal the regulatory effects of B. cereus on IAV infection. Then, we investigated the molecular mechanisms by which B. cereus bacteria or their metabolites inhibit host immunity and aggravate viral infection through the “lung microbiota disordered-immune axis.” Finally, we explored the preventive and therapeutic effects of bacteriophage modification of lung microbiota against IAV infection.

Results

Lung microbiota dysbiosis was associated with the severity of IAV infection

To explore whether lung microbiota dysbiosis was associated with the severity of IAV infection. Five-week-old C57BL/6J mice were inoculated with the IAV strain at a dose of 0.1 mL (106 TCID50/0.1 mL) by nasal drip infection, while control mice received serum-free Dulbecco’s modified Eagles medium (DMEM) via the same route. The survival of the mice was measured daily (Fig. S1A), and clinical scores were assessed determined by ruffled fur, activity and hunched back. At 7 days post infection (dpi), surviving mice were euthanized for analysis of lung histopathological and virus titer (Table S1). It is revealed a significant positive correlation between lung viral titer and histopathological injury score across all infected mice, indicating that higher viral burden was associated with more severe lung tissue damage. Based on these parameters, the k-means clustering algorithm was applied, the features of each sample were extracted for dimension reduction and clusters with consistent features were grouped into one cluster. The final disease score was calculated using the weighted average method. Disease severity in mice was divided into three groups: severe, moderate, and mild (Fig. 1A). As shown in Fig. S1B, the severe group exhibited the greatest body weight loss, the moderate group showed intermediate loss, and the mild group displayed only modest changes in body weight, consistent with the clinical severity classification. 16S rRNA sequencing was used to analyze the microbial composition of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) in different groups of mice. Compared with severe, moderate and mild show significantly different alpha (α) diversity (Fig. S2A, B). The genera c_Bacilli and g_Bacillus were enriched in the severe group, g_Pseudoxanthomonas, f_Xanthobacteraceae and g_Acinetobacter were enriched in the moderate group, and o_Cytophagales, c_Clostridia, g_Mesorhizobium f_Micrococcaceae, and g_Delftia were enriched in mild group (according to linear discriminant analysis (LDA) > 3.0, false discovery rate corrected P-value (FDR) < 0.1; Fig. 1B; Table S2). If the relative abundance of a genus was more than two reads, it was defined as existing to explain possible sequencing artifacts. The presence of Firmicutes, Bacilli, and Bacillus at baseline was associated with the severity of infection symptoms (FDR < 0.1; Fig. 1C, Fig. S2C, D). Furthermore, the complexity of the bacterial co-occurrence network in the respiratory tract gradually decreased (Fig. 1D), suggesting that IAV infection altered putative microbial associations and contributed to the destabilization of the respiratory tract microbiota.

The microbiota-host interaction is associated with the severity of disease after IAV infection

The integration of the lung microbiome and host response has a significant impact on pulmonary diseases [9, 14]. After evaluating the changes in the lung microbiome, we measured the metabolome of the BALF and the transcriptome of lung tissue in each group of infected mice. Metabolomic analysis of BALF revealed that the severe, moderate, and mild groups were specifically enriched in 7, 15, and 5 pathways, respectively (Wilcoxon, FDR < 0.1; Fig. 2A, Table S3). Transcriptomic of the lung tissue showed that the upregulated genes in each group were enriched in 105, 111, and 26 pathways, respectively (DESeq2, FDR < 0.05; Fig. 2B; Table S4). Considering the potential of Bacillus and Bacilli in indicating the severity of pulmonary diseases, we used species-level Bacillus taxa and established metabolite-host pairs to identify the interactions and directions of action. Seventeen interaction links were identified, including two microbial species, one metabolite, and eight host genes. The related links of the moderate and mild groups were 111 and 7, respectively (FDR < 0.1; Fig. 2C; Table S5). To evaluate the significance of each microbiota-metabolite-host gene in relation to disease severity after IAV infection, random forest regression was performed using omics characteristics related to each group to predict disease severity in all three groups of individuals. The link with the highest predictability for the severe group was Bacillus cereus_xanthosine_nt5e (R2 = 0.580; Table S5), followed by Bacillus cereus_xanthosine_nt5m (R2 = 0.571) and Bacillus cereus_xanthosine_ Nt5c3b (R2 = 0.564). For the moderate and mild groups, the top links were Acidovorax wautersii_Homo-L-arginine_ Nos3 and Klebsiella oxytoca-N1-Acetylspermidine-Sat1, respectively. To identify the amplicon sequence variations (ASVs) with key biological roles in the lung, we conducted correlation analyses between ASVs rich in Bacillus in the severe group (Table S6), the key metabolite xanthosine, and the transcriptional gene nt5e. The results indicated that ASV182 was significantly positively correlated with xanthosine and, significantly negatively correlated with nt5e (Fig. 2D) suggesting its potential role as a key microbe involved in IAV-induced lung disease (Fig. S3A). The possible positive role of B. cereus ASV182 in explaining disease occurrence caused by IAV was emphasized in the biological process by which B. cereus ASV182 affects host transcription of the nt5e gene by generating xanthosine.

To further validate the connection between B. cereus and xanthosine, we analyzed the expression levels of the GuaB gene encoded by Inosine-50-monophosphate dehydrogenase (IMPDH), which catalyzes xanthosine synthesis and has abundant bacterial DNA in the BALF of the three groups. We found that the expression level of GuaB in the BALF and activity of IMPDH, and xanthosine content in the BALF of mice in the severe group were significantly higher than those in the mice of the moderate and mild groups (Fig. 2E), confirming the positive correlation between B. cereus and xanthosine.

Bacillus cereus exacerbates IAV infection through xanthosine

The microbiota-metabolite-host association suggests a potential mechanism by which microbiotak-host interactions aggravate IAV infection. To evaluate the effect of bacterial taxa rich in the severe group, one species (Bacillus subtilis, Bacillus cereus, or Klebsiella) was isolated and screened from each of the three genera (c_Bacilli, g_Bacillus, and f_ Burkholderiaceae‌) abundant in BALF of mice infected with IAV in the severe group, and used Delftia acidovorans, a mild enriched bacterium, as a control. Mice were continuously inoculated with 5 × 104 colony-forming units [CFU] of each bacterium for over 5 days (Fig. 3A). At the end of the 5-day inoculation period and another 5-day irrigation period, the bacterial load in the lungs of mice that received the corresponding bacteria significantly increased, compared with controls (Fig. S4A), indicating that the bacteria successful lung colonization. Five days after bacterial inoculation and 7 days after IAV infection, all three bacterial strains, C. bacilli, B. cereus, and K. pneumoniae, led to aggravated IAV infection compared with the non-colonized control group and D. acidovorans-colonized groups. Among them, the influence of B. cereus was the most clear (Fig. 3B, S4B). After inoculation with B. cereus, xanthosine in the BALF of the in mice significantly increased (Fig. 3C). These results support the relative importance of B. cereus and xanthosine in the pathogenicity of IAV infections, which warrant further study. To evaluate whether B. cereus aggravates IAV infection through xanthosine, the B. cereus mutant strain △GuaB, was constructed. The expression level of the GuaB gene in the △GuaB mutant strain was significantly decreased, compared with the WT strain and complementary strains (Fig. S3C). Moreover, the mutation of guaB in B. cereus △GuaB significantly reduced the in vitro production of xanthosine (Fig. 3D), compared with the WT strain and complementary strains, confirming the loss of function. GuaB-complemented B. cereus further restored GuaB expression and xanthosine production (Fig. S4C, Fig. 3D). Inoculation of the B. cereus mutant in mice led to a reduction in xanthosine in the BALF (Fig. S4D) and a decrease in IAV infection (Fig. S4D). Further reversal was achieved by inoculation with the GuaB-supplemented B. cereus (Fig. S4D). 100 µL xanthosine (1 μg/mL) was administered by intranasal instillation to mice for 6 h before inoculation with AIV. The concentrations of xanthosine measured in BALF were comparable to those detected in mice colonized with B. cereus (Fig. S4E). Therefore, similar to the effect on B. cereus, xanthosine aggravated the increase in IAV infection (Fig. 3B, Fig. S4E). Compared to the control group, the immunomodulatory factor CD73 encoded by nt5e, was significantly downregulated in the lung tissue of mice infected with IAV (Fig. 3E). This is consistent with the result reported by Famke et al. [15] that the expression level of the nt5e gene in infected mice decreased after IAV infection, and also supports the xanthosine-nt5e axis. The main function of CD73 is to work synergistically with the exonucleoside triphosphodiphosphate hydrolase-1 in normal tissues to convert extracellular adenosine triphosphate to adenosine, thereby limiting excessive immune responses in many pathophysiological events. However, the bidirectional nature of CD73 as an emerging immune checkpoint in the pathogenesis of lung injury remains unknown [16]. To examine whether xanthosine secreted by B. cereus aggravated IAV infection by downregulating the nt5e gene, we delivered the nt5e adeno-associated virus into mice via nasal drip, leading to CD73 overexpression (Fig. 3F) and subsequently observed a reduction in IAV infection (Fig. S5A, B).

To determine whether xanthosine directly suppresses CD73 in AMs and whether this effect can be rescued by restoring CD73 overexpression, we performed ex vivo experiments using FACS-sorted AMs from naïve mice. AMs were treated with xanthosine (10 nm, 50 nm, 100 nm, 1 µm), 6 h or vehicle, and parallel groups were transduced with AAV9-CAG-nt5e to overexpress CD73 prior to xanthosine (1 µm) exposure. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that xanthosine significantly reduced nt5e mRNA levels, whereas AAV-mediated CD73 overexpression markedly increased nt5e transcript abundance and prevented the xanthosine-induced reduction (Fig. S3G). Consistently, Western blot analysis demonstrated that CD73 protein levels were decreased by xanthosine, elevated by AAV-nt5e transduction, and maintained near baseline levels when CD73-overexpressing AMs were treated with xanthosine (Fig. S3H). These results demonstrate that xanthosine directly downregulates CD73 in AMs, and that enforced CD73 expression is sufficient to counteract this suppression. In conclusion, these results indicate that B. cereus colonization in the lungs produces excessive xanthosine, which aggravates IAV infection by downregulating CD73.

Bacillus cereus-mediated exacerbation IAV infection is associated with reduced numbers of alveolar macrophages (AMs)

According to the transcriptome data, most of the genes induced by IAV in the severe group were associated with immunity and inflammation (Fig. 2B). Combined with the pathological analysis of lung tissue from mice infected with IAV after colonization with B. cereus, inflammatory cell infiltration in the local bronchioli and perivascular areas of the lungs was accompanied by inflammatory cell infiltration in the alveoli (Fig. 3B). This shows that B. cereus may have exacerbated the lung pathological damage caused by immune system-driven IAV infection.

Based on the types of immune cells that affect IAV infection, we used flow cytometry to detect the effect of B. cereus colonization on the immune cells of the infected mice. Consistent with previous reports, intranasal IAV challenge can induce significant consumption of AMs in basal cells, as well as an increase in neutrophils, T and B lymphocytes. B. cereus itself can significantly reduce the numbers of AMs, cause a slight increase in neutrophils, but have no significant effect on T and B lymphocytes, resulting in a more significant decrease in AMs caused by IAV (Fig. 4A and S6A–C). Compared with non-colonized control, mice with B. cereus-induced IAV infection were significantly reduced in lung macrophages compared to neutrophils and lymphocytes, suggesting that macrophages may be important immune cells affecting B. cereus and aggravating IAV infections. To test the potential role of AMs in B. cereus−mediated exacerbation of IAV infection, we used cell-deficient mouse models. In Csf2−/− mice, which completely lack AMs, the ability of B. cereus-mediated promotion of IAV infection was significantly reduced, indicating that alveolar macrophages are required for the full promotive effect of B. cereus (Fig. S6D). The role of AMs was investigated by consuming the cells with clodronate liposomes, which confirmed their effective removal (Fig. 4B). Under these conditions, the effect of B. cereus in aggravating IAV infection was abolished (Fig. 4C). These results suggest that the impact of B. cereus−mediated on AMs is related to the promotion of IAV infection.

We also performed a macrophage adoptive transfer experiment. APC-labeled AMs isolated from B. cereus+ and B. cereus− (WT) mice lungs were transferred to B. cereus− (WT) mice, thereby enabling the distinction between endogenous cells and transplanted cells (Fig. 4D). The engraftment levels of AM obtained from B. cereus+ and B. cereus− mice (hereinafter referred to as B. cereus+ and B. cereus− AM) after transplantation were similar, accounting for approximately 20% of the total in their new hosts (Fig. 4E). Notably, mice that received B. cereus+ AMs exhibited more severe IAV infection (Fig. 4F), further indicating that B. cereus−-mediated exacerbation IAV infection was associated with the reduced AMs in the lung.

Bacillus cereus exacerbates IAV infection by altering macrophages function rather than phenotype

To further explore the role of macrophages in the process of B. cereus exacerbating IAV infection, the differences between B. cereus+ and B. cereus−AM were characterized as shown in Fig. 5A. RNA-seq analysis under baseline conditions showed that out of 57,010 genomic features by AMs, 1413 were differentially expressed between B. cereus+ and B. cereus−AMs, with 428 upregulated and 985 downregulated differentially expressed genes [DEGs] (Fig. 5B). However, there were no significant changes in the expression of molecular markers related to M1 and M2 macrophage polarizations. On the 7th day post-IAV infection, flow cytometry analysis in B. cereus+ mice showed no significant changes in major histocompatibility complex Class II (MHCII) and CD86, which are typical markers of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, compared to B. cereus− mice (Fig. 5C). Similarly, the frequencies of MHCIIlow and CD206+, which are markers of regulatory M2 macrophages, did not change significantly (Fig. 5C). These data indicated that B. cereus had no significant effect on the phenotype of lung AMs during IAV infection.

One key function of AMs is the phagocytic clearance of pathogens. As B. cereus increased the viral titer in the lungs, the next step was to assess whether the clearance ability of the AMs involved in IAV phagocytosis had changed.

The IAV medium was incubated with B. cereus+/B. cereus−AMs for 45 min. AMs were then removed by centrifugation, and the infection titer and IAV genome levels in the supernatant were quantified. B. cereus−AMs reduced the titer of IAV infection by approximately 20-fold compared to the control at 37°C instead of 4°C, whereas the reduction mediated by B. cereus+ AMs was only about fivefold (Fig. 5D). In the supernatant of B. cereus+ AMs, the levels of IAV genomes quantifiable by PCR decreased by tenfold, whereas the reduction mediated by B. cereus+ AMs was only about threefold (Fig. 5E), indicating that B. cereus+ AMs phagocytic function may have been impaired. Consistent with this concept, the reduction in the IAV genome in the supernatant of the B. cereus+AM/IAV mixture was consistent with an increase in the IAV genome in the AM lysate (Fig. 5F). To directly investigate whether B. cereus colonization impacts the phagocytic capacity of AMs independent of viral replication, we performed an ex vivo uptake assay using FITC-labeled beads. Flow cytometric analysis of SiglecF+ AMs revealed that B. cereus+ colonization significantly reduced the percentage of AMs capable of internalizing beads compared to the B. cereus− group (Fig. S7A, B). To confirm that the observed FITC signal represented active phagocytosis rather than non-specific surface adherence, we performed the assay at 4 °C or in the presence of Chlorpromazine (CPZ), a pharmacological inhibitor of endocytosis. In both control conditions, bead uptake was almost entirely abolished, and the difference between the B. cereus+ and B. cereus− groups was eliminated (Fig. S7C). These results provide direct evidence that B. cereus exposure impairs the intrinsic phagocytic uptake machinery of AMs, which likely contributes to the higher residual viral titers observed in subsequent viral clearance assays. Together, these results indicate that B. cereus colonization in the lungs impairs the phagocytic function of AMs, reduces the protection of AMs to the host, and thereby exacerbates IAV infection.

Bacillus cereus phages can reduce IAV infection

The role of B. cereus in exacerbating disease symptoms in IAV-infected mice led us to explore the potential preventive strategies. For this purpose, we isolated a bacteriophage that induces B. cereus lysis and named it B. cereus bacteriophage P315 (Fig. 6A, B). This bacteriophage can lyse B. cereus isolates extracted from the BALF of mice with a severe IAV infection (Figs. S8A, S8B), while showing no activity against other major BALF microbiota (Fig. S8C). We repeated the chronic lung colonization of B. cereus and performed prophylactic vaccination by intranasal inoculation with bacteriophages one day before IAV infection (Fig. 6A). In infected mice colonized with B. cereus, prophylactic phage treatment led to a reduction in IAV infection, an increase in macrophage count, and a decrease in xanthosine (Fig. 6C-E). After phage treatment, a significant reduction of B. cereus was observed in the lungs of mice colonized with B. cereus (Fig. 6F), whereas there was no significant disturbance in the overall microbiota (Fig. 6G). A positive correlation was observed between the weight change in mice after infection and the load of B. cereus after phage treatment (Fig. S8D), indicating the partial efficacy of the phages. The safety assessment indicated no notable phage-induced organ damage (Fig. S8E). These results suggest that targeting B. cereus phages may be a potential strategy for preventing IAV infection.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the mechanism through which lung microbiota imbalance exacerbates IAV infection and evaluated the therapeutic potential of phage intervention. Using a cross-omics longitudinal analysis, we found that opportunistic pathogenic bacteria were associated with the severity of IAV infection symptoms. Clinically, lung microbiota dysbiosis is associated with infection symptoms in mice, including weight loss, fur wrinkles, and reduced appetite. Mechanistically, lung microbiota-host interactions are associated with the aggravation of IAV infection symptoms. Bacillus cereus regulates host immunity through xanthosine-nt5e to exacerbate IAV infection.

The relative abundance and species diversity of the lung microbiota have significant impacts on the occurrence and spread of pulmonary diseases [17]. In this study, the mice were infected with IAV and classified as severe, moderate, or mild by measuring the body weight change, determining the fur and appetite, observing the histopathology and counting the lung viral titer of mice. Analysis of the BALF microbiota in different groups of mice using 16S rRNA sequencing revealed that the abundance and diversity of enriched lung microbiota in mice with different degrees of disease were significantly different. The presence of Firmicutes, Bacilli, and Bacillus is associated with symptom severity after IAV infection. This conclusion is consistent with previous reports [17, 18], although the mechanism remains unclear. The composition of the microbiota species showed that Bacillus of the Firmicutes phylum had a significant dominant position in lung BALF, and B. cereus was identified as the key bacterial species in severe IAV infection. In addition, using microbial genetic information and established metabolite-host pairs, the association with the highest predictability for severe infections was B. cereus_xanthosine_nt5. This suggests that the lung microbiota of mice may regulate host immunity through the B. cereus_xanthosine_nt5e axis to exacerbate IAV infection.

B. cereus strain was isolated and identified from the BALF of mice with severe symptoms. B. cereus is a common foodborne pathogen in animals. Zhang et al. [19] found that B. cereus from contaminated feed aggravated pulmonary distress in chickens by inducing host immunosuppression after infection with avian influenza virus H9N2. Consistent with this, our study showed that SPF mice inoculation with B. cereus significantly exacerbated IAV infection.

To further analyze the effect of B. cereus_xanthosine_nt5e on IAV infection, we analyzed the significantly different metabolic pathways between the severe and moderate/mild groups. We found that in the metabolic pathways of mice infected with IAV, lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, cancer (cancer occurrence), and xanthosine were significantly enriched. Significantly enriched pathways were screened using MetaBase (Clarivate Analytics). Xanthosine, a derivative of purine metabolites and the first effective intermediate in caffeine biosynthesis in tea and coffee plants [20], has few reports on the correlation between xanthosine and viruses. Only CLEVir-X, the dialdehyde form of xanthosine, has been shown to exert antiviral effects against porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome viruses in vitro and in vivo [21]. In our study, inoculation with the B. cereus guaB mutant strain led to a marked decrease in BALF xanthosine levels. In addition, xanthosine significantly exacerbated the symptoms of IAV infection in mice, indicating its contribution to exacerbated IAV infection.

The immunomodulatory factor nt5e gene was significantly downregulated in the lung tissue of IAV-infected mice, which was consistent with the results of a study by Famke et al., who reported that the expression level of the nt5e gene decreased in mice after IAV infection [15], and also supported the association with the xanthosine-nt5e axis. Exo50 nucleotidase (CD73) plays a strategic role in calibrating the magnitude and chemical properties of purinergic signals delivered to immune cells; CD73 is involved not only in macrophage differentiation and polarization [22], but also in shaping macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses [23], antigen presentation [24], and immunosuppressive states across different disease settings [25]. However, its bidirectional nature as an emerging immune checkpoint in the pathogenesis of lung injury remains unknown [16]. In this study, mice overexpressing nt5e exhibited alleviated aggravation of IAV infection caused by xanthosine. In conclusion, B. cereus produces excessive xanthosine in the lungs, which aggravates IAV infection by downregulating nt5e.

The excessive activation of the host’s innate immune system is a key contributor to viral pneumonia [26, 27]. Fatal influenza infections activate the innate immune cells of the host, such as macrophages, dendritic cells, and neutrophils. The transformation of macrophage phenotypes between the classically activated M1 type (pro-inflammatory type) and the alternatively activated type (anti-inflammatory type) is regarded as a key factor in the occurrence, progression, and termination of many inflammatory diseases [28–30], especially influenza virus infection [31, 32].

In this study, B. cereus significantly reduced the number of macrophages infected with IAV but did not affect the activated types of IAV-infected macrophages (M1 or M2), indicating that it altered other characteristics of macrophages rather than their phenotypes. Macrophages are a type of phagocyte with multiple functions, including phagocytosis and killing of pathogens, elimination of apoptotic cells through cell burial, participation in tissue repair and regeneration of the body, and immune regulation [33].

Liang et al. found that intranasal administration of bacteriophages alleviated weight loss in mice, significantly reduced the colonization of Staphylococcus aureus in the respiratory tract without obvious interference with the overall lung microbiota, and effectively reduced lung tissue damage and neutrophil inflows, thereby alleviating the aggravation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease caused by the colonization of Staphylococcus aureus [9]. Currently, most research has focused on the application of bacteriophages as vectors for viral vaccines, and there have been no studies directly using bacteriophages as microbial preparations for the prevention and control of viral diseases. In the present study, we found that bacteriophages targeting B. cereus alleviated the symptoms of IAV-infected mice aggravated by B. cereus colonization without significantly interfering with the remaining components of the lung microbiota (Fig. 7). Thus, avoiding the common problem of microbiota imbalance in traditional antibiotic treatment and providing evidence for precisely eliminating key pathogens in the lung microbiota as a great strategy to prevent the progression of IAV infection. In addition to disease-enriched taxa, we observed several taxa that were depleted in severe disease, which may represent members of a more homeostatic microbiota and potentially contribute to host resilience; however, their functional roles require direct experimental validation. Although PR8 is a mouse-adapted IAV and does not fully recapitulate human infection, it remains a well-established model for studying influenza-induced lung immunopathology. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as a mechanistic framework for influenza-associated host responses in mice, and future studies using clinically relevant influenza isolates will be needed to assess broader translational relevance.

Schematic summary of IAV-induced microbiota dysbiosis impairs alveolar macrophage phagocytosis. (1) Influenza A virus (IAV) infection triggers (2) lung microbiota dysbiosis, promoting (3) Bacillus cereus enrichment. (4) Elevated xanthosine from B. cereus causes (5) nt5e/CD73 downregulation, which (6) suppresses alveolar macrophage phagocytosis and (7) reduces viral clearance. (8) Phage P315 treatment selectively (9) clears B. cereus, breaking the cycle and restoring antiviral defense

Methods

Materials availability

This study did not generate new unique reagents.

Virus and experimental animals

The A/PR8/34 H1N1 strain was donated by the Guangdong Provincial Laboratory Animal Monitoring Center. All SPF-grade male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Beijing Weitonglihua Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. Csf2−/− mice were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center. Unless otherwise stated, the mice were 5 or 6 weeks old at the time of infection. After arrival, the mice were allowed to acclimate for 7 days, and randomly assigned to the study group, and maintained under ABSL-2 or ABSL-3 conditions during IAV strain infection.

IAV-infected mouse model

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Protection and Use Committee of Qiqihar University. All mice were maintained under a 12-h/12-h light/dark cycle at a temperature of 20–25°C, and relative humidity of 50–70%. A standard chow diet (SPF Biotechnology, Beijing, China) and water were provided ad libitum. Five-week-old C57BL/6J mice were intranasally inoculated with 0.1 mL of A/PR8/34 strain at a dose of 106 TCID50/0.1 mL. Mice in the control group were inoculated with serum-free DMEM via the same route. The mice were weighed daily, clinical symptoms were observed, and the survival rate was determined.

Lung tissues were weighed and homogenized in 300 μL PBS using a bead, set to three cycles of 30 s each at 4°C, with 30 s intervals. Homogenates were cleared by centrifugation (20,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C), and cleared supernatants were stored at –80°C until virus titration. Viral titers are expressed as TCID50 units per gram of input tissue.

For histopathological analysis, lung tissue was fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin blocks, cut into 4 µm sections, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and then examined under a microscope to rate the tissue lesions. Lesion intensity was graded as follows: normal, 0; indeterminate, 1–2; mild, 3–4; moderate, 5–7; and severe, 8–10.

Mice that lost more than 25% of their initial weight or showed severe clinical disease symptoms were euthanized.

Clinical severity was assessed daily using a 0–3 system based on four parameters: hair wrinkles, drowsiness, breathing difficulties, and hunchback posture. The daily scores for each mouse were summarized and the average across the entire mouse group was calculated [34].

Isolation, identification and culture of various bacteria

The BALF precipitate of mice was mixed evenly by pipetting with 500 μL of sterile PBS. Samples were diluted in sequence (10–1, 10–2, 10–3, and 10–4), and spread onto blood agar plates. The culture dishes were incubated at 37°C for 14–16 h, and the colonies were counted. Bacillus subtilis, Bacillus cereus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Delftia acidovorans were identified by colony morphology, and the full-length 16S rRNA gene was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Under normal circumstances, all CFU from the lungs of colonized mice had the same morphology and were identified as the target strain; no CFU was observed in the lungs of uncolonized mice. Xanthosine in the BALF of mice was quantified by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (SCIEX 6500) and analyzed using MultiQuant™ software (v3.0.2).

Bacterial species inoculation

B.cereus, B. subtilis, and K. pneumoniae were isolated from the BALF of infected mice in the severe group. D. acidovorans was isolated from the enriched lung BALF of infected mice in the mild group and cultivated in the corresponding medium under the specific conditions as recommended. To achieve low-dose colonization, 5 × 104 CFU bacterial cells were re-suspended in 50 µL PBS and administered intranasally to mice daily for consecutive 5 days, followed by IAV challenge. Five days after colonization, the bacterial load of each bacterium was quantitatively determined using qPCR. The primers used in this study are listed in Table S8. The PCR cycling conditions were as follows: one cycle at 50°C for 2 min and at 95°C for 1 min (holding stage); 40 cycles at 95°C for 15 s, at 60°C for 30 s, and at 72°C for 45 s (PCR stage), and one cycle at 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 1 min and 95°C for 15 s (dissolution curve stage). The DNA template containing the cloned target sequence with a known copy number was continuously diluted to obtain the absolute copy number.

Construction of guaB mutations strains and complementary strains of B. cereus

Construction of the mutant strains was based on previous study [35]. Using the genomic DNA of B. cereus 892–1 as a template, the upstream and downstream homologous arms of guaB-up-F/guaB-up-R and guaB-down-F/guaB-down-R were amplified by primers (Table S7), and the PCR amplification system (35 μL): upstream and downstream primers, 1 μL (10 μmol/L), PrimeSTAR® Max DNA Polymerase, 17.5 μL; template DNA, 1 μL (50 ng); ddH2O, 14.5 μL. Reaction conditions were as follows: Pre-denaturation at 98 °C for 3 min; denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 55 °C for 15 s, elongation at 72 °C for 5 s for 32 cycles; and extension at 72 °C for 3 min. Double enzyme digestion of granular pHT304-Ts was performed using Sal I and EcoR I. Enzyme digestion system: 5 μL of Sal I fast enzyme, 5 μL of EcoR I fast enzyme, 20 μL of 10 × Quickcut Green Buffer, 3 μg of plasmid, and ddH2O to reach a total volume of 200 μL. Enzymatic digestion reaction at 37 °C for 1 h. The upstream and downstream homologous arm fragments and linearized vectors were ligated for 25 min at 50 °C in accordance with the instructions of the Hieff Clone® Plus Multi One Step Cloning Kit. The ligated vectors were thermally stimulated and transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells at 42 °C for 90 s. After adding SOC medium and restoring the culture for 1 h, plates containing ampicillin (100 μg/mL) were used for screening. Positive inverters were confirmed by PCR amplification using primers 304-F/304-R on the grown colonies. Inverters with bands that met these expectations were selected for sequencing, seed preservation, and plasmid extraction to obtain the mutant plasmids. The correctly sequenced knockout plasmid was transformed into competent cells of B. cereus 892-1 by electric shock. The guaB gene of B. cereus 892-1 was mutated using the principle of homologous recombination. The mutant strain was verified by PCR using the guaB-up-F/guaB-down-R primers. Positive inverters with bands that met expectations were amplified to extract DNA and sent to BGI Technology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou) for sequencing.

Xanthosine administration

To evaluate the effect of xanthosine on AMs function and viral susceptibility, mice were administered xanthosine (Sigma-Aldrich) via intranasal instillation. Xanthosine was dissolved in PBS and administered at a dose of 100 µL 0.5 µg/mL per mouse once daily for 5 consecutive days prior to IAV infection. Control mice received an equal volume of PBS according to the same schedule. The dose was selected based on previous pharmacological studies and our preliminary dose–response experiments.

Quantification of xanthosine in BALF

Xanthosine concentrations in the BALF were quantified using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. Briefly, BALF samples were collected by flushing the lungs with 1 mL of ice-cold PBS. Samples were then centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min, and the supernatants were collected and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Proteins were precipitated by adding three volumes of ice-cold methanol containing internal standards. After centrifugation, the supernatants were analyzed on an Agilent 1290 Infinity II LC system coupled to a 6470 Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer. The concentrations of xanthosine in exogenous treatment groups were compared with those in B. cereus-colonized and control mice to ensure the physiological and pathological relevance of the administered dose.

Measurement of IMPDH activity

IMPDH activity in erythrocytes was measured in the supernatant of bacteria cell lysates or mouse serum after the removal of cell fragments by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 2 min. We used applied identical incubation conditions and high-performance liquid chromatography as described previously [36]. Enzyme activity was normalized to the hemoglobin concentration of the respective hemolysate and expressed in pmol xanthosine-5′-monophosphate (XMP)/h/mg hemoglobin. Aliquots of cell extracts or mouse serum with known IMPDH activity were used as internal quality control samples and included in each analytical run.

Adeno-associated virus treatment

The purified adeno-associated virus (AAV9) plasmid encoding CD73 (nt5e) was provided by Universal Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). To enhance nt5e expression, mice received intranasal instillation of AAV9-CAG-nt5e at a concentration of 1 × 1010 PFU/mL, AAV9-CAG serving as a negative control. Each mouse was administered 100 µL as an effective infective dose, and the infection efficiency was verified in subsequent analyses according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining was performed using mouse anti-CD73 as the primary antibody (antibody dilution 1:1000, 4 °C 16–18 h). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled goat anti-mouse secondary antibody was used for immunohistochemical staining (Dako, LSAB2 system-HRP, K0675; Thermo Scientific) (antibody dilution 1:100, 37 °C, 2 h). The negative control consisted of sections incubated with non-specific primary antibodies.

Lung digestion and flow cytometry analysis/isolation of innate immune cells

Lungs were dissected from mice and finely minced with scissors before digestion with warm RPMI containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), DNase, and 1 mg/mL collagenase type IV (Sigma-Aldrich) for 40 min at 37 °C; lungs were further homogenized via swishing with a 3 mL syringe every 10 min during digestion. After digestion, the immune cells were purified by 40%/80% isotonic percoll gradient centrifugation. For flow cytometry analysis, the prepared cells were blocked with 1 mg/million cells with 2.4G2 (anti-CD16/anti-CD32) in 100 µL of PBS for 15 min at 4 °C, washed two times with PBS to remove 2.4G2 residue, and then stained with the following fluorescently conjugated antibodies: CD45 BV605, MHCII BV650, CD11b APC-Cy7, CD11c BV786, Ly6C PerCp-Cy5.5, Ly6G AF700, CD64 BV421, CD24 FITC, SiglecF PE CF594, Fcer1 APC. Multi-parameter analysis was performed on a CytoFlex (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed using the FlowJo software (Tree Star). Cell sorting was performed using a cell sorter (SH800Z; Sony).

Ex vivo alveolar macrophages culture, xanthosine treatment assay, and AAV-CD73 infection

Alveolar macrophages (AMs) were isolated from lung tissues of naïve C57BL/6 J mice. Briefly, after digestion and percoll gradient centrifugation, CD45⁺MHCII⁺CD11c⁺SiglecF⁺ cells were sorted by FACS (SH800Z, Sony). Purity was routinely > 95%. Isolated AMs were seeded into 24-well plates (2 × 105 cells/well) in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 50 µM 2-mercaptoethanol. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 2 h to allow adherence, then washed twice with PBS to remove non-adherent cells.

For xanthosine treatment assay, adherent AMs were incubated with xanthosine (Sigma-Aldrich, X3002) at indicated concentrations (10 nm, 50 nm, 100 nm, 1 µm) in complete medium for 6 h at 37 °C. In some experiments, AMs were pre-treated with 1 µg/mL xanthosine for 6 h, cells were collected for qRT-PCR or western blotting to confirm CD73 overexpression. Control cells received an equal volume of PBS.

For AAV-CD73 infection, adherent AMs were infected with AAV9-CAG-nt5e at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 × 105 viral genomes per cell. AAV9-CAG-empty vector was used as negative control. The virus was diluted in serum-free RPMI 1640 and added to cells for 4 h at 37 °C, after which the medium was replaced with complete medium. At 48 h post-transduction, cells were collected for qRT-PCR or western blotting to confirm CD73 overexpression.

SDS-PAGE and Western blots

Drug-treated and virus-infected cell protein in six-well culture plates was extracted using Radio Immunoprecipitation Assay lysis buffer (Beyotime) with protease inhibitor PMSF (Beyotime). Protein samples in 5 loading buffer were boiled for 10 min and analyzed by 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Membranes were blocked with 1% BSA (Sigma) for 2 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibody for 2 h at room temperature with mouse anti-CD73 (Sigma), mouse anti-β-actin (Sigma). Membranes were treated with IRDye 800 CW goat anti-mouse IgG (LI-COR Biosciences) as secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. For detection, an Odyssey Infrared Fluorescence Scanning System (LI-COR Biosciences) was used.

qRT-PCR analysis of nt5e expression

The PrimeScript™RT kit and gDNA Eraser were used to extract total RNA from tissue and reverse transcribed into cDNA, which was then used to detect the relative gene expression of nt5e and β-actin. The primer sequences used were as follows: nt5e forward primer, TCGGGGTTTTGAAATGGATAAAC, nt5e reverse primer: TCAGGAATGCTGCTGTTTAGAA; β-actin forward primer, CAAGCAGGAGTACGATGAGTC, β-actin reverse primer, AACGCAGCTCAGTAACAGTC.. β-actin was selected as the reference gene. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 PCR cycles (94 °C, 15 s; 60 °C, 30 s). The final concentration of each primer is 1 μM. qPCR was performed using a LightCycler 96 (Roche). The 2-△△CT method was used to calculate the fold change in gene expression.

Adoptive transfer of AMs

Lungs were harvested from B. cereus+ mice and digested with type IV collagenase. AMs (CD45+MHCII+CD11c+SiglecF+) were stained and sorted by FACS. A total of 5 × 105 AMs were immersed in 30 µL of PBS and administered intranasally to mice. One day after cell transplantation, some mice were euthanized to determine the transplantation efficiency of the transplanted cells. Some mice were inoculated with IAV and euthanized at 7 days post-infection (dpi).

AM phagocytosis assay

The AM bead uptake test was conducted using an ex vivo phagocytic assay kit (Cat#500290, Cayman Chemical). B. cereus− and B. cereus+AMs were sorted by FACS as previously described. After sorting, 1 × 105 cells were seeded into each well of a 96-well plate and cultured at 37 °C for 18 h. The cells were washed twice, IgG-FITC microspheres were added, and then incubated at 37°C for 2 h. After incubation, the cells were centrifuged, the supernatant was collected, and the FACS buffer was resuspended. The fluorescence level of the cells, which is an indicator of bead uptake, was detected using flow cytometry.

Phage preparation

Bacillus cereus phage P315 was isolated from sewage samples. Briefly, the samples were homogenized in SM buffer solution. The suspension was centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 10 min and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm syringe filter (Millipore, USA). The filtrate was incubated with 2 mL of Luria–Bertani (LB) culture medium at 37 °C for 6–8 h, and then centrifuged at 8000 × g for 10 min. This enrichment process was repeated two to three times. Then, triple plaque purification of the bacteriophages was performed using double-layer agar plates and they were stored in 20% glycerol at − 80 °C. A clinical-grade phage solution was prepared as previously described [37]. In this study, a high-titer phage reserve was obtained by amplifying a clinical strain of B. cereus in BALF of infected mice in LB liquid medium. Cell lysis can be observed as a reduction in turbidity in liquid culture. The lysate was then incubated with 2% chloroform under gentle shaking for 30 min. Bacterial cell debris was centrifuged at 3,500 × g for 15 min, then the supernatant of the precipitation bacteriophage was collected, and centrifuged at 7000 × g for 15 min overnight at 4 °C with polyethylene glycol (PEG) 8000 (final concentration 10% (w/v)). The pellets were re-suspended in TM buffer, and PEG 8000 was removed by adding the same volume of chloroform and centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min. The phages were then purified by centrifugation in a CsCl step gradient (1.33, 1.45, 1.50, and 1.70 g/cm3) in TM buffer in ultra-clear tubes at 120,000 × g for 4 h and dialyzed using a Spectra/Por 6 membrane. The phage was titrated using the double-layer method, and the endotoxin was evaluated using a terminal chromogenic endotoxin detection kit (Bioendo, Xiamen, China) at multiplicities of infection (MOI) of 0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, and 100. The growth inhibition curve of the bacteriophages was determined by measuring the OD600 of the bacterial cultures at different time points after they were added. The bacteriophages are sterilized through a 0.22 µm filter and stored at 4°C until use.

Propagation and purification of phages

Phage preparations were processed in two different ways. The first batch of phage preparations was purified using Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter units (MWCO 100 K Da; Merck Millipore, Massachusetts, USA). The second phage preparation was purified using a cesium chloride density gradient as previously described [9], and then dialyzed using a Spectra/Por RC membrane (MWCO 20 K Da, Spectrum, New Jersey, USA) in PBS to remove cesium chloride. Dialysis reduced the cesium concentration to < 210 ppb, as detected by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry. Phages were then sterilized through 0.22 µm filters. The phages were titrated, and endotoxins were evaluated using an End-point Chromogenic Endotoxin Test Kit (Bioendo, Xiamen, China). Phage preparations were subsequently stored at 4 °C until required. The resulting titers and endotoxin levels were 5 × 108 PFU/mL and 105 EU/mL for the first batch, and 5 × 1010 PFU/mL and 1.3 × 104 EU/mL for the second batch.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For TEM imaging, 20 µL of phage suspension was placed onto a copper grid with carbon film, left for 3–5 min, and the excess liquid was removed using filter paper. Staining was performed with 2% phosphotungstic acid for 1–2 min and cuprum grids were observed using TEM (HT7800, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Phage administration

In mouse experiments, purified bacteriophages were administered intranasally at a dose of 1 × 108 plaque-forming units (PFU)/kg body weight, followed by IAV infection one day later. In the control group, an equal volume of TM buffer was administered intranasally. The tissue viral titer, histopathological score, immune cell count, and qPCR of B. cereus were conducted as described in the above sections. Lung microbiome sequencing was performed to amplify the hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene V4 from mouse lung bacterial DNA, and blank sequencing of DNA extraction was performed using the Illumina MiSeq platform.

Microbiome, metabolome, transcriptome analyses

For IAV-infected mice, the standardized pipeline of QIIME 2.0 (2020.8) was used to process the V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene sequences [38]. The DADA2 algorithm was used to denoise demultiplexing sequencing reads to generate ASVs at a 100% similarity threshold [39]. A custom Naïve-Bayes classifier was trained on 99% of the operational taxonomic units of Greengenes 13_8 to assign classifications to each dataset. Full-length (v1-v9) 16S rRNA sequences were processed using the MCSMRT pipeline (−e = 2), and classified using the UTAX classifier in the customized 16S full-length database [40]. Alpha and beta diversity analyses were performed based on rarefied ASV table (rarefied to the minimum sequencing depth across samples) by using “rrarefy” function from the R package “vegan”. Alpha diversity was characterized by calculating ASV-level richness and diversity indices. Beta diversity was assessed using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distances. Statistical significance of beta diversity differences among the severe, moderate, and mild groups was evaluated using ANOSIM, Adonis, and MRPP analyses by using the R package “vegan”. LEfSe analysis was performed using the “trans_diff”function in the R package “microeco” [41], applying FDR correction for P-values. For microbial co-occurrence network analysis, ASVs with a relative abundance below 0.01% and detected in fewer than 20% of samples were removed to reduce data sparsity and minimize false positives. Subsequently, SparCC method was used to construct the co-occurrence networks, with thresholds set at R > 0.8 and P < 0.01. Topological features of the networks were extracted using the R package “igraph”. Co-occurrence networks were visualized with ggClusterNet [42]. Multiple group comparisons were performed using the least significant difference (LSD) test at a significance level of P < 0.05. The decontam package in R was used for quality control of the amplicon data to identify potential microbial contaminants [43].

For the host transcriptome data, the original readings of the host transcriptome were high-quality filtered using Cutadapt [44], and the mouse genome Mus_musculus GRCm39 (http://asia.ensembl.org/Mus_musculus/Info/Index) was compared using Hisat2 software (v2.1.0) [45]. A gene-counting matrix was obtained using RSEM software (v1.3.1) [46].

Transcriptomics data were normalized to transcripts per million (TPM) for downstream analyses. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used for dimensionality reduction and visualization of transcriptome gene profiles. Enriched genes between groups were identified using the R package “DEseq2” [47]. Functional pathway enrichment analysis was performed separately for the enriched genes in the severe, moderate, and mild. Significantly enriched pathways were detected using clusterProfiler (Clarivate Analytics, FDR < 0.05) [48].

Raw metabolome data were converted to mzXML format and ion features were extracted using Progenesis QI software (v2.2). Low-quality ions (ions missing in over 50% of the QC samples or over 80% of the test samples) or ions with relative standard deviations greater than 30% were filtered for downstream analysis [49]. Metabolites were identified by searching the HMDB [50], METLIN [51], and KEGG [52] databases. To estimate the proportion of lung immune cells, host transcriptomic data were subjected to cell subset deconvolution analysis using CIBERSORT [53]. Metabolomics data were subjected to missing value imputation and normalization prior to analysis. PCA of metabolites was performed using partial least square discriminant analysis (PLS-DA). Before PLS-DA analysis, data were mean-centered prior to analysis, and the PLS-DA model was constructed with a confidence level of 0.95. Model validity was assessed by 200 permutation tests. Student’s t-test was used to assess the significance of metabolite differences between groups. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed to maximize group separation, and model validity was evaluated using 200-permutation tests. Metabolites with P < 0.05 and variable importance in projection (VIP) scores > 1 were considered significantly enriched in severe, moderate, and mild.

The 16S rRNA sequencing, host transcriptomics, and metabolomics of all samples from the severe, moderate, and mild groups were conducted in a single batch (n = 6).

Multi-omic integration analyses

For IAV-infected mice in the severe, moderate and mild groups, microbial species, metabolites, and host genes enriched in each corresponding group (with an average abundance higher than that in the other two groups) were identified. For microbial species and metabolites, biological connections were established if microbial species encoded genes involved in the biosynthesis or consumption of metabolites in their genomes, based on the MetaCyc database (version 23.1) [54]. Metabolites and host genes were included if there was a known interaction between the metabolite and host gene, according to the STITCH database (version 5.0, confidence score > 0.8, mode of action as activation, inhibition, or binding) [55]. Biologically associated subsets of the microbial species, metabolites and host genes were retained. Total inverse correlation analysis was conducted between species and metabolites, as well as between metabolites and host genes, in the three groups of individuals using HAllA software (v0.8.17), a tool that enhances statistical power while controlling FDR through the use of novel hierarchical association tests [56, 57]. Microbiota-metabolite-host associations with directional conflicts between the correlation and biological signals were excluded. Random forest analysis used each set of relevant and significant host gene characteristics of microbial metabolites to predict the disease severity in all three groups. For the random forest analysis, a customized sliding-windows R script was developed to automate the process. The script automatically reads biologically linked data files from the directory, constructs random forest models using the function “randomForest” from the R package “randomForest”, and calculates the R-squared and P-value of each model using the function “rf.significance” from the R package “rfUtilities”, with the number of trees set to 500 and the random seed fixed at 1122. To prevent overfitting, the Out-Of-Bag (OOB) R-squared was extracted as an internal cross-validation metric to evaluate true model predictability. Upon completion of the sliding-window R script, all resulting random forest model outputs for the biological linkages were saved for downstream analysis. The links were ranked by predictability in terms of R2 values.

To further identify microbes potentially involved in host-associated interactions, Pearson correlation was performed between ASVs assigned to biologically relevant microbial taxa and the metabolites or host genes. The results were visualized using the R package “ComplexHeatmap”.

Statistical analyses for experimental data

For in vivo and in vitro experimental data, the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s test were conducted to examine data normality and homoscedasticity (homogeneity of variance). For all other comparisons, ordinary one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by correction of multiple comparisons using the by Holm-Sidak method for comparisons involving more than two groups. Student’s t-test was applied for comparisons involving only two groups. All analyses were performed in R environment (version 4.2.1).

Metabolomics data of mouse BALF have been deposited at the Genome Sequence Archive (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/) (accession PRJCA053856). The key analytical computer code was developed at https://github.com/mak3outhill/Pulmonary-microbiota-dysbiosis.
