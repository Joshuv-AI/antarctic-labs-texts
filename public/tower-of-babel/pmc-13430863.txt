A multifunctional nanotherapeutic strategy based on exosome-liposome hybrid nanoparticles for comprehensive periodontitis management
Authors: Xin Qiao, Kai Liu, Jie Tang, Junyuan Du, Qicheng Sun, Shijian Deng, Deqin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeriodontitis is a formidable chronic inflammatory disorder, marked by pathological oxidative stress, persistent inflammation, and alveolar bone resorption. The simultaneous management of these interrelated pathological processes poses significant challenges to conventional therapeutic modalities. In this study, we developed a multifunctional nanotherapy, EXO@Lip-Res, constructed from exosome-liposome hybrid nanoparticles. By synergistically integrating resveratrol-loaded liposomes with exosomes derived from dental pulp stem cells, this approach was designed to address the multifaceted nature of periodontitis. The synthesized EXO@Lip-Res exhibited favorable physicochemical properties and retained distinct exosomal markers, facilitating a reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive to trigger programmed drug release. In vitro evaluations demonstrated that EXO@Lip-Res exhibited superior antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities. Specifically, EXO@Lip-Res treatment significantly scavenged H2O2-induced intracellular ROS in both RAW264.7 cells and PDLSCs (p < 0.05). In the context of inflammation, the nanoplatform markedly downregulated the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated PDLSCs compared to the LPS-only group. Quantitative analysis revealed a 50% reduction in the mRNA expression of IL-6 and IL-1β, while the protein levels of TNF-α and IL-1β were decreased by approximately 40% (p < 0.05). Mechanistic studies elucidated that its potent anti-inflammatory effects were governed by the inhibition of pivotal signaling pathways, including the Janus kinase 2 (JAK2)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways. Furthermore, EXO@Lip-Res significantly facilitated the polarization of macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. In vivo experiments utilizing a rat model of experimental periodontitis demonstrated that EXO@Lip-Res treatment reduced alveolar bone loss by 30%, diminished inflammatory infiltrates, reduced osteoclast numbers. Consistent with the in vitro results, a significant shift in macrophage polarization from the M1 to M2 phenotype was observed within periodontal tissues in vivo. Collectively, this research delineates a comprehensive nanotherapeutic strategy targeting oxidative damage, inflammation, and bone remodeling, providing a potential avenue for the clinical management of periodontitis and related chronic inflammatory diseases.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04602-z.Keywords: Exosomes, Liposomes, Hybrid nanoparticles, Resveratrol, Periodontitis

Introduction

Periodontitis is defined as a chronic inflammatory disease that is primarily induced by dental plaque and is characterized by progressive destruction of periodontal tissues and alveolar bone, thereby constituting the leading cause of tooth loss in adult [1]. Although considerable advances have been achieved in conventional therapeutic approaches, complete regeneration of periodontal tissues remains a major clinical challenge [2, 3]. Stem cell-based therapies have garnered increasing attention in regenerative medicine owing to their robust self-renewal capacity and multilineage differentiation potential [4–6]. However, the clinical translation of these therapies remains constrained by several limitations, including low cell survival rates, immune rejection, limited control over differentiation, and poor integration with host tissues [7, 8].

Exosomes have emerged in recent years as promising candidates for regenerative medicine, primarily due to their function as key mediators of intercellular communication via extracellular vesicles [9–11]. Previous studies conducted by our group demonstrated that dental pulp stem cell–derived exosomes (DPSC-EXOs) significantly promote tissue repair and modulate inflammatory responses, thereby supporting their potential as a therapeutic strategy for periodontitis [12]. However, the clinical translation of exosomes remains limited by several challenges, including difficulties in large-scale production, batch-to-batch variability, and intrinsic stability issues [13, 14]. Consequently, current research efforts have increasingly focused on the optimization of exosome-based therapeutic strategies, with particular emphasis on exosome-mediated drug delivery systems because of their capacity to enhance therapeutic efficacy [15, 16].

Exosomes are naturally occurring nanocarriers secreted by a wide range of cell types, rendering them highly suitable for diverse drug delivery and therapeutic applications from a materials science perspective [16, 17]. The structural versatility of exosomes enables their native configuration to be modified through multiple strategies, including genetic engineering, chemical modification, physical techniques, and microfluidic technologies, thereby facilitating the incorporation of exogenous cargo for expanded biomedical applications [16]. The unique architecture of exosomes, which consists of a functionalized phospholipid bilayer enclosing an intrinsic parental matrix, has stimulated growing interest in their development as customizable hybrid nanocarriers. By integrating exosomes with liposomes, exosome–liposome hybrid nanoparticles (EXO@Lip) can be constructed, in which exogenous lipids supplement the exosomal lipid bilayer while preserving intrinsic cargo and biophysical and biochemical properties [18]. This hybrid system strategically integrates the formulation advantages of liposomes, including their ease of preparation, prolonged stability, and enhanced circulatory persistence, with the native biological merits of exosomes such as superior biocompatibility, inherent tissue tropism, and natural ligand presentation, thereby creating a unified and optimized drug delivery platform [18–21]. Accordingly, such hybrid nanoparticles have attracted increasing attention, as they provide opportunities for the rational customization of exosome-based formulations to meet specific therapeutic requirements. Previous studies have successfully utilized these hybrid nanoparticles to deliver CRISPR/Cas9 systems, miR-497, and short interfering RNA to bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stem cells, thereby effectively overcoming chemotherapy resistance in ovarian cancer models [19, 22–24].

In recent years, the exploration of nanotherapeutic strategies for periodontitis has witnessed flourishing development. Extensive research has been dedicated to exosome-based therapies, leveraging their innate bioactivity and low immunogenicity to promote tissue regeneration and modulate local inflammation. For instance, mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes have been integrated into adhesive microneedle-nanosheet composite membranes to achieve temporally orchestrated immunomodulation and bone regeneration in periodontal defects [25]. Fan reported that protocatechualdehyde-functionalized BMSC-derived exosomes orchestrate calcium signaling in the immunoinflammatory microenvironment to promote periodontal regeneration [26]. Similarly, liposomal delivery systems have been successfully utilized to enhance the localized delivery of therapeutic agents within periodontal pockets. Research has demonstrated that integrating antibiotic-loaded niosomes or solid lipid nanoparticles into thermosensitive gels can significantly prolong drug release and improve antibacterial efficacy against periodontal pathogens [27]. Furthermore, resveratrol-loaded liposomes have been specifically shown to promote the polarization of macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory M2-like phenotype and scavenge ROS, thereby effectively remodeling the immune microenvironment in periodontitis [28]. Despite these advancements, research into exosome-liposome hybrid nanoparticles remains in its nascent stages within the field of periodontics. This strategy seeks to synergize the biological identity of exosomes with the structural modularity of synthetic liposomes, representing a promising frontier for achieving a more balanced profile of biocompatibility and pharmacological payload. However, this synergistic potential has not been comprehensively addressed in current periodontal literature.

Resveratrol, a polyphenolic compound renowned for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, demonstrates significant therapeutic potential for various diseases including periodontitis [29–34]. However, its poor water solubility and low bioavailability substantially limit clinical applications [35]. Nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems, particularly lipid nanoparticles, may offer a viable solution to these challenges by enhancing drug stability, improving bioavailability, and enabling controlled release characteristics.

Given the complementary advantages of biological and synthetic vehicles, we hypothesized that the strategic hybridization of DPSC-derived exosomes with resveratrol-loaded liposomes could yield a synergistic therapeutic effect far superior to that of individual components. Accordingly, the primary objective of this study was to develop a ROS-responsive hybrid nanoplatform (EXO@Lip-Res) designed to simultaneously address the pathological oxidative stress and persistent inflammation characteristic of periodontitis (Scheme 1). We sought to systematically characterize the physicochemical properties and exosomal identity of these hybrid nanoparticles and to evaluate their capacity to modulate macrophage polarization and PDLSC function under inflammatory conditions. This research aimed to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, specifically focusing on the modulation of the JAK2/STAT3 and p38 MAPK signaling pathways. Ultimately, this work strives to provide a rational framework and experimental evidence for the application of exosome-based hybrid nanocarriers in the comprehensive management of chronic inflammatory diseases and regenerative medicine.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of EXO@Lip-Res

We successfully constructed exosome-liposome hybrid nanoparticles (EXO@Lip-Res) and verified their structural characteristics, drug-loading performance, and retention of exosomal markers through multiple characterization methods. The synthesis process of EXO@Lip-Res is shown in Fig. 1A. The isolated DPSCs-EXOs were further characterized by Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA) to determine their size distribution and particle concentration. As shown in Fig. 1B, the NTA results revealed that the exosomes exhibited a well-defined size distribution with a mean diameter of 80.1 ± 21.5 nm, which aligns with the characteristic size range of small extracellular vesicles. The particle concentration was quantified at 5.47 × 109 particles/mL. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observations revealed that EXOs exhibited spherical structures with diameters of approximately 80–150 nm, Lip-Res showed spherical structures of about 150 nm, while EXO@Lip-Res displayed spherical structures with diameters of 250–300 nm, indicating the formation of new membrane structures through fusion between EXOs and Lip-Res (Fig. 1C). Western blot analysis (Fig. 1D) demonstrated that both EXOs and EXO@Lip-Res expressed typical exosomal marker proteins (CD63, CD81 and CD9), while these proteins were not detected in Lip-Res, confirming that EXO@Lip-Res retained the key bioactive components of exosomes during its formation. This characteristic is crucial for maintaining the natural targeting ability and biocompatibility of exosomes and may facilitate subsequent in vivo delivery applications.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Fig. 1E) showed that the average particle sizes of EXOs, Lip-Res, and EXO@Lip-Res were 150.34 ± 6.15 nm, 163.38 ± 2.02 nm, and 330.1 ± 2.66 nm, respectively, consistent with TEM observations. The significant increase in particle size can be attributed to the membrane reorganization process between exosomes and liposomes, resulting in more complex vesicular structures. While smaller nanoparticles are often favored for systemic circulation, the 300 nm diameter of EXO@Lip-Res offers specific strategic advantages for localized periodontal application. In the dynamic environment of the periodontal pocket, characterized by the continuous flushing of gingival crevicular fluid (GCF), smaller and more diffusive particles are prone to rapid clearance. The moderately larger size and reduced diffusion coefficient of our hybrid vesicles may increase their passive retention in irregular periodontal topographies and extend local residence time. Moreover, compared to smaller vesicles with higher membrane curvature, the lower curvature of the 300 nm particles may affect phospholipid packing in a way that contributes to stabilizing Res molecules within the membrane and minimizing leakage. We acknowledge that a 300 nm particle size may encounter more resistance in deep interstitial tissue penetration compared to smaller counterparts. However, given that periodontitis is primarily a localized inflammatory disease within the pocket and surrounding superficial tissues, the benefits of enhanced retention and robust drug protection outweigh these diffusive limitations.

Notably, in the exosome group, this mean diameter obtained by NTA was slightly smaller than the hydrodynamic diameter measured by DLS. This difference is likely attributed to the high sensitivity of DLS to a small number of larger aggregates in the sample, whereas NTA provides a more accurate number-based distribution by tracking individual particles. Combined with the morphology observed via TEM and the expression of exosomal markers (CD9, CD63, and CD81), these data confirm the successful enrichment of high-purity exosomes. Furthermore, zeta potential analysis (Fig. 1F) revealed that the surface potentials of EXOs, Lip-Res, and EXO@Lip-Res were − 11.78 ± 0.64 mV, -16.6 ± 0.54 mV, and − 15.13 ± 0.37 mV, respectively, with EXO@Lip-Res’s potential being closer to that of Lip-Res, suggesting that the lipid membrane components dominate in the hybrid nanoparticles and further confirming the occurrence of membrane fusion. The PDI of EXO, Lip-Res, and EXO@Lip-Res was 0.101 ± 0.004, 0.086 ± 0.034, and 0.145 ± 0.016, respectively (Fig. 1G). All values were below 0.3, indicating a narrow size distribution and monodisperse nature of the nanoparticles, with EXO@Lip-Res maintaining acceptable uniformity despite its increased structural complexity. UV-Vis spectrophotometry (Fig. 1H) detected characteristic absorption peaks of resveratrol at 306 nm in both Lip-Res and EXO@Lip-Res, while no such peaks were observed in EXOs and blank liposomes (Lip), confirming the successful loading of resveratrol into both the liposomes and hybrid nanoparticles. Notably, the absorption peak intensity of EXO@Lip-Res was similar to that of Lip-Res, indicating that the exosome-liposome fusion process did not significantly affect the loading efficiency of resveratrol. The resveratrol standard curve (UV-Vis, 306 nm) showed a good linear relationship, with the regression equation y = 0.0171x + 0.2612 and R² = 1.000 (Figure S1). Quantitative reporting of the formulation parameters revealed that the EXO@Lip-Res system achieved an encapsulation efficiency (EE) of 80.18 ± 0.02% and a drug loading (DL) of 2.47 ± 0.45% (Table S1). These values indicate that the hybridization process effectively encapsulates resveratrol, ensuring a sufficient therapeutic payload for the subsequent biological evaluations.

The in vitro release kinetics of Res from EXO@Lip-Res exhibited a distinctive ROS-triggered pattern (Fig. 1I). Under physiological conditions (PBS, pH 7.4), the hybrid nanoplatform demonstrated excellent structural stability, with a cumulative release of only 2.51 ± 0.01% at 15 min and 12.54 ± 0.03% at the 210 min mark. In sharp contrast, the introduction of H2O2 significantly accelerated the liberation of the encapsulated Res. Within the first 60 min, the cumulative release reached 51.39 ± 0.29% and 52.94 ± 0.10% in the 0.25 mM and 1 mM H2O2 groups, respectively. By the end of the 210 min observation period, approximately 78.10 ± 0.09% (0.25 mM H2O2) and 77.92 ± 0.19% (1 mM H2O2) of the Res had been released. These explicit values quantify the high sensitivity of the EXO@Lip-Res system to oxidative stress, functioning as an efficient gated-release mechanism for localized periodontal therapy.

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was employed to investigate potential molecular interactions between resveratrol and the liposomal carrier (Fig. 1J). The free Res exhibited distinct absorption peaks at 1605 cm⁻¹ (aromatic C = C stretching) and 1580 cm⁻¹ (alkene C = C vibration). Following encapsulation, subtle yet reproducible modifications were observed: the aromatic C = C stretching shifted from 1605 cm⁻¹ to 1595 cm⁻¹, while the ester carbonyl (C = O) peak of blank liposomes at 1740 cm⁻¹ shifted to 1735 cm⁻¹ in Lip-Res. The observed 10 cm⁻¹ red shift in aromatic C = C stretching (1605→1595 cm⁻¹) may be associated with π-π stacking interactions between resveratrol’s phenolic rings and the hydrophobic domains of phospholipids. Concurrently, the 5 cm⁻¹ shift in ester carbonyl stretching (1740→1735 cm⁻¹) is consistent with the potential formation hydrogen bonding formation between resveratrol’s hydroxyl groups and the polar headgroups of phospholipids. Collectively, these spectroscopic changes suggest that resveratrol interacts with the liposomal bilayer, likely involving both the hydrophobic and hydrophilic domains of the carrier system.

Verification of membrane fusion and cellular uptake of EXO@Lip-Res

To validate the membrane fusion characteristics and cellular uptake behavior of EXO@Lip-Res, we performed fluorescence labeling experiments. As shown in Fig. 2A, PKH26-labeled EXOs (red fluorescence) and FITC-labeled liposomes (green fluorescence) exhibited nearly complete overlap of red and green signals after co-incubation. Quantitative colocalization analysis further confirmed the high hybridization efficiency of the EXO@Lip system. The mean Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated to be 0.9177 ± 0.0398 (n = 6 independent fields), indicating a strong and stable spatial overlap between the labeled exosomes and liposomes (Fig. 2B). When EXO@Lip was co-cultured with PDLSCs for 2 h, similar fluorescence colocalization was observed within cells (Fig. 2C), structurally verifying the stable formation of EXO@Lip-Res and establishing its foundation for cellular delivery functions.

Further investigation of EXO@Lip-Res cellular uptake revealed time-dependent internalization patterns in both PDLSCs and RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 2D and E). At 1 h post-incubation, only weak FITC fluorescence signals were detected in both cell types, indicating limited early-stage uptake. The fluorescence intensity increased significantly after 2 h, demonstrating progressive enhancement of nanoparticle internalization over time. By 4 h, abundant green fluorescence signals had accumulated intracellularly, confirming efficient cellular uptake of EXO@Lip-Res. Notably, both cell types showed similar uptake kinetics, suggesting the potential of this delivery system for broad-spectrum cellular targeting.

These results not only structurally confirm the successful construction of EXO@Lip-Res but also elucidate its intracellular delivery kinetics. The stable structure formed through membrane fusion ensures nanocarrier integrity, while the efficient time-dependent internalization provides crucial evidence for its applications in drug delivery and gene therapy. The consistent uptake patterns across different cell types highlight its potential as a versatile delivery platform.

Antioxidant capacity of EXO@Lip-Res

The antioxidant capacity of the EXO@Lip-Res nanosystem was systematically evaluated through a multidimensional experimental approach. Fluorescence microscopy imaging (Fig. 3A) revealed intense DCF fluorescence signals, reflecting elevated intracellular ROS levels, in both the control (CON) and exosome-only (EXO) groups in RAW264.7 cells, whereas Lip-Res treatment significantly reduced ROS-associated fluorescence, with EXO@Lip-Res exhibiting the most pronounced ROS-scavenging effect. This phenomenon was consistently reproduced in PDLSCs (Fig. 3D), confirming the cross-cell-type antioxidant efficacy of this nanosystem. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 3B and E) via a fluorescence microplate reader revealed that both Lip-Res and EXO@Lip-Res significantly reduced ROS fluorescence intensity in PDLSCs compared to the CON and EXO groups. Notably, the ROS scavenging efficacy was comparable between the Lip-Res and EXO@Lip-Res groups. This observation suggests that in a static 2D culture system, the antioxidant performance is predominantly driven by the intrinsic pharmacological activity of the released Res once the dosage is standardized. In such a controlled environment, the potent radical-scavenging capacity of Res may reach a functional plateau, potentially masking any incremental biological influences of the exosomal membrane components. While the hybridization process does not further enhance the immediate antioxidant kinetics in vitro, it successfully preserves the full therapeutic potency of Res. Flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 3C and F) further corroborated these findings, showing a significant reduction in ROS-positive cell populations in both the Lip-Res and EXO@Lip-Res treated groups. Consistent with the microplate reader results, both Res-loaded formulations demonstrated potent antioxidant capacities, with EXO@Lip-Res maintaining a comparable or slightly enhanced efficacy in modulating the intracellular redox state. These data confirm that the hybridization of exosomes and liposomes successfully delivers Res to both macrophages and PDLSCs to effectively eliminate excess ROS, while EXO alone exhibited negligible scavenging effects, reinforcing the central role of Res in this therapeutic process.

The chemical antioxidant assays (Fig. 3G) provided mechanistic insights at the molecular level to complement the cellular findings. Across ABTS and DPPH radical scavenging assays, FRAP, and H₂O₂ clearance experiments, unmodified exosomes (EXO) showed only marginal antioxidant activity at high concentrations, whereas Lip-Res exhibited significant efficacy even at low concentrations. Notably, EXO@Lip-Res not only maintained the potent antioxidant properties of Lip-Res but also significantly augmented the inherent antioxidant capacity of the exosomes (p < 0.0001). Specifically, the ABTS and DPPH assays quantified free radical neutralization capacity, the FRAP test measured electron-donating/reducing activity, while H₂O₂ scavenging evaluated peroxide elimination efficiency across concentration gradients [36]. Collectively, these data demonstrate that liposomal modification of exosomes with resveratrol significantly amplifies their antioxidant potential, supporting their therapeutic application for oxidative stress-related conditions.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS), as important components of oxidative stress, are highly reactive molecules derived from molecular oxygen [37]. In periodontitis, dental plaque bacteria stimulate host cells to release pro-inflammatory factors, triggering the recruitment of defensive cells such as macrophages and neutrophils to inflammatory sites. These cells produce large amounts of ROS to combat bacteria, but excessive ROS can easily lead to oxidative stress in periodontal tissues and exacerbate damage [38–40]. Studies have shown that increased oxidation of lipids, DNA and proteins in gingival crevicular fluid, saliva or blood of periodontitis patients is positively correlated with the severity of periodontitis [41]. This means oxidative stress is involved in the pathogenesis of periodontitis. Previous studies have demonstrated that resveratrol can prevent alveolar bone loss in experimental rat models of periodontitis and enhance bone formation by modulating inflammation in mouse periodontitis models [28, 33, 42, 43]. In this study, we confirmed the antioxidant capacity of hybrid nanoparticles through multiple experiments from various perspectives. The results showed that modifying exosomes through hybrid nanoparticles enhanced their antioxidant capacity, providing theoretical support for periodontitis treatment. Meanwhile, we found that unmodified exosomes (EXO) did not demonstrate significant ROS-scavenging effects in either chemical antioxidant assays or intracellular antioxidant tests. We speculate that although the antioxidant effects of exosomes have been reported [44], their efficacy varies due to differences in extraction conditions and cell types. In contrast, the hybrid nanoparticles preserved the excellent antioxidant capacity of resveratrol, making them more suitable for periodontitis treatment.

At the molecular level, the enhanced antioxidant performance of EXO@Lip-Res may be explained by a multi-level synergy among its components, which we propose as a working hypothesis. The hybrid lipid bilayer architecture could help stabilize the active phenolic hydroxyl groups of Res through hydrophobic interactions and π-π stacking, potentially shielding these active sites from premature oxidative inactivation. Furthermore, we speculate that interfacial hybridization between exosomes and liposomes may optimize the spatial orientation and bioaccessibility of the encapsulated Res within lipid microdomains. This possibility is supported by the rapid ROS-triggered release profile (Fig. 1J), which suggests that Res is preferentially partitioned in the hybrid membrane, remaining relatively accessible to external free radicals. Such a configuration, if confirmed, would help unleash the therapeutic potential of Res to counteract oxidative stress, consistent with the observations from our cellular assays. The differential antioxidant performance between EXO@Lip-Res and EXO alone (p < 0.0001) highlights the critical importance of resveratrol loading and hybrid structure optimization. Our findings establish EXO@Lip-Res as a promising platform for addressing oxidative stress in periodontal therapy while providing insights for the development of nanomedicine for other inflammatory diseases.

Modulation of inflammatory response and macrophage polarization by EXO@Lip-Res

In the LPS-induced PDLSCs inflammatory model (Fig. 4A), RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that EXO@Lip-Res treatment significantly suppressed the gene expression of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-8 and IL-1β while upregulating the transcription level of the anti-inflammatory factor TGF-β and IL-10, indicating its dual regulatory capacity in modulating inflammatory responses. Western blot results (Fig. 4B) further corroborated these findings at the protein level, showing markedly reduced expression of TNF-α and IL-1β in the EXO@Lip-Res-treated group compared to controls. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 4C) confirmed statistically significant differences (p < 0.05), demonstrating that this nanosystem effectively inhibits the activation of canonical inflammatory signaling pathways.

(B) Western blot analysis and (C) corresponding quantification of pro-inflammatory proteins (TNF-α and IL-1β). (D) Immunofluorescence staining of the M2 marker CD206 and (E) the M1 marker CD86 in RAW264.7 cells. (F, G) RT-qPCR analysis of macrophage polarization markers (CD86, iNOS, Arg1, CD206) in RAW264.7 cells, showing that EXO@Lip-Res promotes M1-to-M2 transition. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001

As shown in Fig. 4D-F, the treatment markedly shifted macrophage polarization from an M1 to an M2 phenotype. At the protein level, immunofluorescence showed decreased CD86 (red) and increased CD206 (green) intensity. This shift was corroborated at the transcriptional level by RT-qPCR, which revealed downregulated mRNA expression of CD86 and iNOS and upregulated mRNA expression of Arg1 and CD206. Macrophage polarization plays a pivotal role in host defense against pathogenic infections and tissue repair. Intracellular pathogen infection triggers macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype, which is characterized by elevated secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-12) and increased expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) for microbial eradication [45–47]. M1 macrophages produce substantial nitric oxide (NO) via iNOS activity, demonstrating potent microbicidal and antitumor capabilities while participating in Type I immune responses through inflammatory cytokine secretion [48]. Conversely, M2 macrophages are primarily involved in suppressing inflammatory responses and Type I adaptive immunity. They facilitate debris clearance, promote angiogenesis, and actively contribute to tissue remodeling and repair processes [49, 50]. The results of this study indicated that the dual regulatory action of EXO@Lip-Res shifts the polarization of macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, suggesting that EXO@Lip-Res may modulate inflammation by reshaping the immune microenvironment.

Modulation of PDLSCs inflammatory responses via JAK2/STAT3 and p38 MAPK signaling pathway inhibition by EXO@Lip-Res

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which EXO@Lip-Res exerts anti-inflammatory effects at the genomic level, we treated PDLSCs with EXO@Lip-Res and performed RNA-seq analysis on the cells. PCA showed clear separation between PBS-treated and EXO@Lip-Res-treated groups in LPS-stimulated PDLSCs. (Fig. 5A). A total of 180 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, with 139 genes upregulated and 41 genes downregulated in the EXO@Lip-Res group compared to the control group (|log2FC| > 1, p-value < 0.05) (Fig. 5B). As shown in the clustering heatmap in Fig. 5C, all DEGs exhibited distinct group-specific expression patterns. Sample clustering revealed that biological replicates within the EXO@Lip-Res group showed high consistency and were clearly separated from other treatment groups, indicating that this treatment induced a unique transcriptional signature. GO term enrichment analysis highlighted significant involvement in pro-inflammatory regulation, cell migration, and cellular stress response (Fig. 5D). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed on significantly downregulated differentially expressed genes (Fig. 5E). The results revealed that downregulated genes were significantly enriched in several specific pathways. Among these, the Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, ROS, and JAK-STAT signaling pathways showed pronounced enrichment trends. These pathways are generally closely associated with oxidative stress, inflammation regulation, and specific metabolic processes. Figure 5F showed a circle plot heatmap of differentially expressed genes, indicating that EXO@Lip-Res treatment induced significant gene expression reprogramming. A key finding was the specific upregulation of SOCS3 (suppressor of cytokine signaling 3) in the treated group. SOCS3 serves as a classic and pivotal negative feedback regulator in the JAK/STAT signaling pathway. Its primary function is to terminate pro-inflammatory signaling triggered by multiple cytokines in a precise and potent manner by inhibiting JAK kinase activity and targeting signaling proteins for degradation. Thus, the upregulation of SOCS3 suggests that EXO@Lip-Res treatment may activate intracellular negative feedback regulatory mechanisms, thereby actively and precisely suppressing the core pro-inflammatory signaling axis of JAK/STAT. This finding prompted us to validate the activation status of the JAK2/STAT3 axis by Western blot analysis. Under basal conditions, PDLSCs exhibited low expression levels of phosphorylated JAK2 (p-JAK2) and STAT3 (p-STAT3). LPS stimulation markedly enhanced the phosphorylation of both JAK2 and STAT3, indicating activation of this pathway during acute inflammatory responses (Fig. 5G-H). Notably, EXO@Lip-Res treatment significantly suppressed LPS-induced JAK2/STAT3 phosphorylation (p < 0.01). To further validate this mechanism, PDLSCs were treated with the JAK2-specific inhibitor TG101348 under LPS stimulation. Consistent with the EXO@Lip-Res effects, pharmacological inhibition of JAK2/STAT3 signaling downregulated the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α (Fig. 5I-J). These results suggest that EXO@Lip-Res attenuates inflammatory responses in PDLSCs, potentially through the modulation of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, although the exact degree of pathway dependency requires further investigation.

In parallel to the JAK2/STAT3 investigation, we examined the involvement of the p38 MAPK pathway, a key mediator of cellular stress and inflammation. Western blot analysis demonstrated that LPS stimulation robustly activated the p38 MAPK signaling cascade, as evidenced by increased phosphorylation of both p38 and its downstream substrate MAPKAPK-2 (MK2). Importantly, EXO@Lip-Res treatment significantly suppressed this LPS-induced phosphorylation (Fig. 5K-L), indicating a concurrent inhibitory effect on this pathway. To corroborate the functional role of p38 in LPS-triggered inflammation, we pretreated PDLSCs with the specific p38 inhibitor SB203580. Consistent with the effect of EXO@Lip-Res, pharmacological inhibition of p38 markedly reduced the production of key inflammatory mediators, including IL-1β and TNF-α (Fig. 5M-N). These data collectively identify the p38 MAPK pathway as another critical target for EXO@Lip-Res-mediated anti-inflammatory action.

The coordinated suppression of the JAK2/STAT3 and p38 MAPK signaling pathways by EXO@Lip-Res represents a key mechanistic feature of our hybrid platform. While RNA-seq initially guided us to the JAK2/STAT3 axis, subsequent functional validation revealed that EXO@Lip-Res concurrently inhibits the activation of the p38 MAPK pathway. The p38 MAPK cascade is a well-established regulator of stress and inflammatory responses, directly influencing the stability and translation of pro-inflammatory mRNAs through its downstream target MK2. According to recent research in periodontal models, the JAK-STAT axis can act as an upstream regulator of ROS production, which subsequently triggers the p38 MAPK cascade [51]. This JAK-STAT-ROS-p38 MAPK signaling axis suggests that the inhibition of JAK2/STAT3 by EXO@Lip-Res may inherently contribute to the dampening of p38 MAPK signaling by reducing the intracellular oxidative stress. Our observation that EXO@Lip-Res effectively eliminates ROS while simultaneously suppressing both pathways aligns with this hierarchical regulatory model. Furthermore, the anti-inflammatory efficacy of our hybrid platform may stem from its multi-target action on an integrated signaling network. Literature on other chronic inflammatory disorders, such as psoriasis and rheumatoid arthritis, indicates that the JAK/STAT and MAPK/ERK pathways do not function in isolation but interact synergistically with other core axes to amplify pathological inflammatory responses [52, 53].

Despite the mechanistic depth provided by RNA-seq and pharmacological inhibition, several limitations should be acknowledged. The use of chemical inhibitors (TG101348 and SB203580) indicates pathway involvement but does not fully establish absolute pathway dependency. Without reverse-direction genetic experiments, the causal necessity of these signaling events remains partially inferential. While the dual inhibition of JAK2/STAT3 and p38 MAPK is evident, the specific molecular nodes governing their cross-talk in PDLSCs require further investigation using targeted gene-silencing approaches.

Therapeutic efficacy of EXO@Lip-Res in a rat experimental periodontitis model

The therapeutic efficacy of EXO@Lip-Res was systematically evaluated in a rat experimental periodontitis model. The experimental design is illustrated in Fig. 6A. Micro-CT three-dimensional reconstruction and two-dimensional radiographic analysis (Fig. 6B) demonstrated that all treatment groups (EXO, Lip-Res, and EXO@Lip-Res) promoted alveolar bone defect repair compared to the control group. Quantitative analysis results (Fig. 6C-D) revealed that the bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) were significantly higher in the EXO, Lip-Res, and EXO@Lip-Res groups than in the control group (p < 0.05). Notably, although the BV/TV and Tb.Th values in the EXO@Lip-Res group were numerically higher than those in the EXO and Lip-Res groups, the intergroup differences did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05). Furthermore, the EXO@Lip-Res group exhibited a significant advantage in the ABC-CEJ distance (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6E), confirming that EXO@Lip-Res treatment effectively alleviated periodontitis-induced alveolar bone resorption and promoted bone repair. Several factors may explain the lack of statistical significance for some bone parameters among treatment groups. The 30-day treatment duration might not be long enough for full bone regeneration to manifest significant intergroup divergence, given that bone remodeling is a slower process. Biological variability in the rat ligature-induced periodontitis model, including differences in ligature placement and individual inflammatory responses, could have added noise to the bone morphometric measurements. Future studies with larger cohorts and extended observation periods are warranted to definitively evaluate the bone-regenerative potential of EXO@Lip-Res.

These histological characteristics were highly consistent with the micro-CT findings, demonstrating that EXO@Lip-Res not only inhibited alveolar bone resorption but also promoted the regeneration of functional periodontal tissues.

TRAP staining results (Fig. 7A) demonstrated a significant reduction in osteoclast numbers in the EXO@Lip-Res treatment group compared to controls (Fig. 7B), indicating the effective inhibition of osteoclast activation in the periodontitis microenvironment by this nanosystem. These findings correlate with the previously observed bone-protective effects in micro-CT analysis, confirming that EXO@Lip-Res alleviates alveolar bone resorption through modulation of bone metabolic homeostasis.

Immunohistochemical analysis further revealed its anti-inflammatory effects in vivo (Fig. 7C-F). EXO@Lip-Res significantly downregulated TNF-α and IL-1β expression in periodontal tissues, consistent with the observed reduction of pro-inflammatory factors in vitro. HO-1 expression analysis (Fig. 7G-H) showed that EXO@Lip-Res markedly upregulated this critical antioxidant enzyme, corresponding to the enhanced ROS scavenging capacity observed in vitro. These results suggest that this therapeutic strategy not only mitigates inflammatory responses but also strengthens tissue antioxidant defenses through HO-1 activation, establishing a synergistic “anti-inflammatory-antioxidant” protective mechanism that creates a favorable microenvironment for periodontal tissue regeneration.

EXO@Lip-Res attenuates periodontitis via macrophage phenotype switching

To evaluate the in vivo regulatory effect of EXO@Lip-Res on macrophage phenotypic polarization, we performed immunofluorescence staining analysis on tissue sections. Immunofluorescence analysis of tissue sections revealed a significant modulatory effect of EXO@Lip-Res on macrophage polarization. As shown in Fig. 8A, compared to the control group, EXO@Lip-Res treatment significantly altered the expression profile of local macrophage markers. Specifically, the fluorescence intensity of the pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage marker CD86 (red) was significantly reduced, while the fluorescence signal of the anti-inflammatory M2 marker CD206 (green) was simultaneously enhanced. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity further confirmed this phenotypic shift: the average fluorescence intensity of CD206 in the EXO@Lip-Res group increased approximately onefold compared to the control group (Fig. 8B), while the average fluorescence intensity of CD86 decreased approximately onefold (Fig. 8C). Collectively, these data demonstrate that EXO@Lip-Res effectively reprograms macrophage polarization in vivo by suppressing the proinflammatory M1 phenotype while promoting the reparative M2 phenotype.

In this study, the results of tissue immunofluorescence analysis provide crucial in vivo evidence for understanding the anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory mechanisms of EXO@Lip-Res. We observed that EXO@Lip-Res treatment synergistically downregulates the M1 marker CD86 and upregulates the M2 marker CD206, signifying a fundamental shift in the local immune microenvironment from a pro-inflammatory state to an anti-inflammatory/reparative state. The phenotypic shift of macrophages from M1 to M2 is a core component in the resolution of tissue inflammation and initiation of the repair process. The polarization pattern induced by EXO@Lip-Res is consistent with its anti-inflammatory effects demonstrated in previous experiments. This result is consistent with the molecular mechanisms we previously identified in PDLSCs, inhibition of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway and upregulation of SOCS3. The JAK/STAT signaling axis, particularly the balance of STAT1 and STAT3 activation, is known to be a key switch regulating M1/M2 polarization. EXO@Lip-Res may systematically drive macrophage phenotypic conversion at the transcriptional level by inhibiting pro-inflammatory STAT1/3 signaling associated with M1 polarization, while simultaneously creating conditions for M2 polarization pathways through upregulating negative feedback factors like SOCS3. Furthermore, the successful induction of M2 macrophage polarization indicates the immense potential of EXO@Lip-Res in promoting tissue regeneration, as M2 macrophages play irreplaceable roles in secreting reparative cytokines, clearing apoptotic cells, and promoting angiogenesis [54, 55].

In summary, our study confirms that EXO@Lip-Res not only directly neutralizes inflammatory factors but also achieves its therapeutic effect by reshaping the local immune microenvironment, specifically, by transforming macrophages from destructors (M1) to builders (M2). This provides new theoretical and experimental support for the development of immune-regulatory regenerative therapies based on exosome-drug co-delivery systems.

In vivo safety evaluation

To comprehensively evaluate the in vivo biosafety of EXO@Lip-Res nanoparticles, we conducted a systematic toxicological analysis on experimental rats. Histopathological analysis of major organs (Fig. 8D) revealed that throughout the experimental period, rats in the EXO@Lip-Res treatment group exhibited no significant pathological alterations in the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, or kidneys. Their tissue morphology and cellular structure remained essentially consistent with the control group, showing no signs of toxic damage such as inflammatory cell infiltration, edema, necrosis, or fibrosis. This indicates that EXO@Lip-Res exhibits good tissue compatibility in vivo and does not induce acute or subacute toxic reactions in major organs.

Peripheral blood routine test results (Fig. 8E) showed significantly higher white blood cell counts (WBC) in the periodontitis model group (CON group), consistent with the systemic inflammatory state induced by local periodontal infection. Notably, WBC counts were significantly reduced in all treatment groups (EXO, Lip-Res, and EXO@Lip-Res), with no statistically significant differences among these three groups (p > 0.05). Concurrently, other key hematological parameters—including red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin concentration (HGB), and platelet count (PLT)—remained within normal physiological ranges across all experimental groups, with no significant intergroup differences. These findings indicate that EXO@Lip-Res treatment effectively reverses the systemic inflammatory markers elevated by periodontitis, with a regulatory effect comparable to that of individual component treatment groups, without causing abnormalities in hematopoietic or coagulation functions.

Analysis of liver and kidney biochemical indicators (Fig. 8F) provided crucial evidence of metabolic safety. Compared to the control group, rats in the EXO@Lip-Res group showed no significant elevation in liver function markers or kidney function markers, indicating that this treatment did not cause detectable damage to the liver’s metabolic detoxification function or the kidneys’ excretory filtration function. Notably, the observation that elevated blood glucose levels in periodontitis rats were normalized after treatment is intriguing, but its interpretation is limited by the multifactorial nature of glucose regulation. Dedicated metabolic studies are needed to explore any potential link between this nanotherapy and glucose homeostasis.

Although no significant systemic toxicity or organ damage was observed during the 30-day experimental period in this study, we acknowledge that a one-month observation may not fully elucidate the long-term metabolic fate of hybrid nanoparticles. To further enhance the credibility for future clinical translation, longitudinal toxicity studies extending to 3–6 months are warranted to comprehensively evaluate the chronic biosafety, including potential cumulative effects, precise organ distribution, and complete clearance kinetics of these engineered vesicles in vivo. The 30-day, every-other-day injection regimen was based on previous studies [12]. Although this dosing frequency is relatively intensive, the present study was designed as a proof-of-concept investigation in a rodent model. For future clinical translation, alternative strategies to reduce dosing frequency could be developed. Notably, local administration into the periodontal pocket is already used in clinical practice, supporting the clinical feasibility of this route. Nonetheless, further formulation optimization and large-animal studies are required to advance translational applications.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that EXO@Lip-Res exhibits significant potential as a therapeutic agent for periodontitis through its integrated antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and bone-protective properties. The incorporation of resveratrol into exosome-liposome hybrid nanoparticles substantially enhanced antioxidant capacity while effectively modulating key inflammatory pathways (JAK2/STAT3 and p38 MAPK), resulting in both pro-inflammatory cytokine suppression and macrophage polarization. Furthermore, the dual capacity to inhibit osteoclast activity and promote bone regeneration highlights EXO@Lip-Res’s unique advantage in concurrently addressing inflammatory responses and tissue destruction in periodontitis. These findings robustly validate the superior efficacy of EXO@Lip-Res in targeting the multifaceted pathogenesis of periodontitis, offering a novel therapeutic paradigm for this chronic inflammatory disease.

Given these promising preclinical results, EXO@Lip-Res represents an innovative and multifunctional therapeutic strategy with potential applications extending to other chronic inflammatory conditions. Future studies should include comprehensive long-term in vivo evaluations and clinical trials to systematically assess its safety profile, therapeutic efficacy, and clinical translatability in human patients.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and identification

DPSCs and PDLSCs were isolated from exfoliated third molars of healthy donors at the Surgical Outpatient Department of Stomatological Hospital, Chongqing Medical University, with informed consent obtained. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee (CQHS-REC-2023 (LSNo.187)). Freshly extracted teeth were used for isolating pulp and periodontal tissues, which were then digested with collagenase type I (3 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) at 37 °C for 40 min. The cell suspension was plated in a 25 cm² culture flask and cultured in α-MEM (α-Minimum Essential Medium, HyClone, Logan, Utah, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Fetal Bovine Serum, Lonsera, Suzhou, China) and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (HyClone, Logan, Utah, USA) under 5% CO₂ at 37 °C.

RAW264.7 cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (SCSP-5036, Shanghai, China) and cultured in DMEM (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium, HyClone, Logan, Utah, USA) with 10% FBS (Lonsera, Suzhou, China) and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (HyClone, Logan, Utah, USA) at 37 °C in 5% CO₂.

Exosomes isolation and identification

DPSCs were cultured until reaching 60% to 80% confluence, at which point the standard medium was replaced with serum-free α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM, HyClone, Logan, Utah, USA) containing 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (HyClone, Logan, Utah, USA). Cells were then incubated under these conditions for 48 h. The collected conditioned medium was processed through a stepwise centrifugation protocol at 4 °C: an initial spin at 300×g for 10 min, followed by 2000×g for 10 min and 10,000×g for 30 min, using a refrigerated centrifuge (Beckman Microfuge22R, Beckman Coulter, California, USA), to eliminate dead cells and cellular debris. Ultracentrifugation was performed at 100,000×g for 70 min (Optima™ XPN, Beckman Coulter, California, USA), and the pellet was retained while the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in PBS, and the ultracentrifugation step was repeated to ensure purification. Exosome concentrations were quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The standard curve is shown in Figure S2. Finally, the DPSC-EXO preparations were stored at -80 °C for future use.

The morphology of DPSC-EXO was observed using transmission electron microscopy (H-800, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Their particle size distribution was assessed through DLS. Additionally, Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NanoFCM, N30, NanoFCM Inc., Xiamen, China) was employed to precisely quantify the particle size distribution and absolute concentration. Furthermore, western blotting was performed to detect surface marker proteins, with specific antibodies targeting CD9, CD63 and CD81 (1:500, Abcam, Cambridge, UK).

Synthesis and characterization of EXO@Lip-Res

EXO, Lip-Res, and EXO@Lip-Res were synthesized by self-assembly. PBA-COOH (4-Carboxyphenylboronic acid), EDC (N-(3-Dimethylaminopropyl)-N’-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride), and NHS (N-Hydroxysuccinimide) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The solvent DMSO (Dimethyl sulfoxide) was obtained from the same supplier. Lipids were prepared by reacting PBA-COOH, EDC, and NHS in DMSO, followed by the addition of DSPE-PEG2K-NH2 (1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[amino(polyethylene glycol)-2000], Ruixi Biological Technology Co., Ltd, Xi’an, Shaanxi, China) to form the lipid material. The strategic modification with PBA-COOH serves as a ROS-responsive switch. Specifically, the phenylborate ester structure is susceptible to oxidative cleavage or structural transformation in the presence of H2O2, which destabilizes the hybrid membrane and consequently accelerates the release of the encapsulated resveratrol. The Lip-Res was prepared by mixing the lipid with resveratrol (Res, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in DMSO, followed by triethylamine addition and overnight reaction. EXO@Lip-Res was obtained by incubating the Lip-Res solution with exosomes (EXO) for 12 h at 37 °C. Preliminary experiments were performed with EXO‑to‑Lip mass ratios. The 1:1 ratio yielded the most homogeneous particle size distribution and the highest fusion efficiency, and was therefore used for all subsequent preparations. After co-incubation, the hybrid nanoparticles were purified by density gradient ultracentrifugation. Briefly, the mixture was layered onto an iodixanol gradient (10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 50%) and centrifuged at 100,000×g for 3 h at 4 °C. The fraction corresponding to the hybrid nanoparticles (typically found at the interface between 20% and 30%) was collected, diluted with PBS, and centrifuged again at 100,000×g for 70 min to remove the gradient medium. Three independently prepared batches of EXO@Lip-Res were characterized by dynamic light scattering (DLS; Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern Panalytical, UK). The batch‑to‑batch variation in particle size was less than 1% (CV = 0.5%, mean size 296.5 ± 1.5 nm, n = 3), indicating good reproducibility.

The size distribution, polydispersity index (PDI) and zeta potential of EXO, Lip-Res, and EXO@Lip-Res were measured by DLS, and their morphology was characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-2100Plus, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). Western blot analysis was performed to confirm the presence of exosome markers CD63, CD81, and CD9 (1:500, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). The full-wavelength UV-Vis spectra of Res, EXO, Lip, Lip-Res, and EXO@Lip-Res were recorded. The characteristic absorption peak of resveratrol at 306 nm was monitored to confirm successful drug loading.

The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL) of Res within EXO@Lip-Res were quantified via UV-Vis spectrophotometry at 306 nm. Briefly, a linear calibration curve was established using Res standard solutions. The weight of unencapsulated Res (Wfree) was determined by analyzing the filtrate obtained after centrifuging the EXO@Lip-Res suspension through an Amicon Ultra-4 centrifugal filter (MWCO: 100 kDa). FT-IR spectra of free Res, Lip and Lip@Res NPs were measured in order to investigate the drug-material interaction and the drug encapsulation into carrier. Liposomes were labeled with FITC (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and EXO were labeled with PKH26 (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Fluorescence co-localization was performed to observe the fusion of the hybrid nanoparticles. The quantification of fluorescence co-localization was performed using ImageJ software. The release profile of Res from EXO@Lip-Res nanoparticles (NPs) under different oxidative conditions was investigated using a dialysis method. Briefly, 1.0 mL of EXO@Lip-Res NPs solution was loaded into dialysis bags (MWCO: 3.5 kDa). Each bag was then immersed in 49 mL of release medium (PBS containing 5% Tween-20) containing different concentrations of H₂O₂ (0 mM, 0.25 mM, or 1 mM) to simulate reactive oxygen species (ROS) conditions. The systems were incubated under constant shaking at 37 °C. At predetermined time intervals, 1 mL of the external release medium was withdrawn for analysis and replaced with an equal volume of fresh pre-warmed medium to maintain sink conditions. The concentration of released Res was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 306 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. The cumulative release percentage was calculated for three independent replicates (n = 3) and is presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

In vitro anti-oxidant assay

ABTS scavenging activity

The ABTS+ solution was prepared by combining 2.45 mM K2S2O8 with 2 mM ABTS in 25 mM phosphate buffer (PBS, pH 7.4), and stored in the dark for 12 h. The solution was then diluted to achieve an absorbance of 0.750 ± 0.025 at 734 nm. Aliquots of 0.1 mL of the ABTS+ solution, with concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 µg/mL, were added to free EXO, Lip-Res, or EXO@Lip-Res. After incubation, absorbance was measured, with distilled water used as the control.

DPPH scavenging activity

3 mL of 0.1 mol/L DPPH solution was mixed with 1 mL of Free EXO, Lip-Res, or EXO@Lip-Res solutions at concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 µg/mL. The mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Anhydrous ethanol was used as the control.

H2O2 scavenging activity

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was dissolved in 0.1 mmol/L PBS to prepare a 40 mmol/L H2O2 solution. 3.4 mL of Free EXO, Lip-Res, or EXO@Lip-Res solutions (0–100 µg/mL) were mixed with 0.6 mL of the H2O2 solution, and the absorbance was measured. Distilled water served as the control, and pure water was used as the blank.

Ferric-reducing antioxidant power ability

The FRAP working solution was prepared by mixing 25 mL of acetic acid-sodium acetate buffer, 2.5 mL of 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TPTZ), and 2.5 mL of FeCl3 solution (20 mmol/L). The absorbance of the FRAP working solution at 593 nm was measured before the reaction (AbsBefore). Then, 0.6 mL of the sample solution was added to 4.5 mL of the FRAP solution and incubated for 8 min. After incubation, the absorbance was measured again (AbsAfter). The FRAP value was calculated as the difference between AbsBefore and AbsAfter.

Cellular uptake of EXO@Lip-Res

To evaluate the cellular uptake of EXO@Lip-Res, Lip was labeled with FITC, and the hybrid nanoparticles were prepared. These nanoparticles were co-cultured with RAW264.7 cells and PDLSCs for 1, 2, and 4 h. After fixation, the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (Solarbio, Beijing, China), and fluorescence microscopy was used to observe the uptake.

Intracellular ROS scavenging

PDLSCs and RAW 264.7 cells were pre-treated with PBS, EXO, Lip-Res, and EXO@Lip-Res for 12 h, respectively. Then were treated with equal volume of 600 µM H2O2 to induce cell stress and incubated for 2 h. The cells were cultured with DCFH-DA (10 µM), and finally observed by fluorescence microscopy. Cells were collected for quantification by fluorescence microplate reader and flow cytometry analysis.

The experimental groups were as follows. CON group: The control group was treated with PBS; EXO group: EXO was used at a final concentration of 10 µg/mL (10 µL of a 1 mg/mL EXO solution was added per mL of culture medium to achieve a final concentration of 10 µg/mL in the cells, this concentration was chosen according to our previous work [12].); Lip-Res group: Lip-Res was used at a final concentration of 5 µg/mL (10 µL of a 500 µg/mL Lip-Res solution was added per mL of culture medium to achieve a final concentration of 5 µg/mL in the cells). This concentration was based on pre-experiment results, with CCK-8 assays indicating it is a safe concentration with no significant toxicity to the cells (Figure S3; EXO@Lip-Res group: EXO and Lip-Res solutions were mixed in a 1:1 volume ratio to obtain the hybrid formulation. 10 µL of the mixture was added per mL of culture medium.

Gene expression

Gene expression was assessed for IL-8, IL-1β, IL-10, TGF-β,CD86, iNOS, Arg1, CD206 by RT-qPCR. PDLSCs and RAW264.7 cells were pre-treated with LPS (10 µg/mL, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 12 h, then treated with PBS, EXO, Lip-Res, and EXO@Lip-Res for 24 h. The treatment procedure was as described previously. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and quantified using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Takara Bio Inc., Beijing, China). RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix on a real-time PCR system. The amplification conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Primer pair sequences are listed in Table S2 and S3 (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Protein expression

Protein expression was assessed for IL-1β, TNF-α, JAK2, p-JAK2, STAT3, p-STAT3 (1:1000, CST, Danvers, MA, USA) by western blot to assessment of the drug’s effect on cellular inflammation. PDLSCs were pre-treated with LPS (10 µg/mL) for 12 h, then treated with PBS, EXO, Lip-Res, and EXO@Lip-Res for 24 h. The treatment procedure was as described previously. Total protein was extracted from cells using RIPA buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), and protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated overnight with primary antibodies against target proteins. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection system (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), and densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Animal experiment

The animals utilized in this study were handled in compliance with the “Guideline for Ethical Review of Animal Welfare of Laboratory Animals” issued by the China National Standardization Management Committee (GB/T 35892 − 2018) and received approval from the Shanghai Jiao Tong University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee(IACUC) Ethics for Animal Protocols (NO. A2025266). A total of 24 healthy male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, aged 8 weeks and weighing between 200 and 250 g. Sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 software. The following parameters were used: one‑way ANOVA; expected effect size f = 0.5 (based on preliminary data for ABC‑CEJ distance); significance level α = 0.05; desired power (1‑β) = 0.70; and four experimental groups. The calculation yielded a total of 24 animals (n = 6 per group), which were included in the study. The rats were disease-free, fed a standard diet, and housed in the Experimental Animal Center under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions, with a controlled environment of 25 °C, 40% humidity, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. They had unrestricted access to food and water and were randomly assigned to groups for subsequent experiments.

A rat periodontitis model was established following previously reported methods [56]. In brief, rats were anesthetized in a sealed chamber with 4% (v/v) isoflurane until complete sedation was achieved. A stainless-steel wire, 0.20 mm in diameter, was secured around the bilateral maxillary first molars of the anesthetized rats. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) derived from Porphyromonas gingivalis (10 µg/mL) was administered into the periodontal pockets every other day for a total of five times. The ligatures were maintained in place for 30 days to induce periodontitis. Following this, the 24 rats were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 6 per group). Randomization was performed using a computer‑generated random number sequence (Excel RANDBETWEEN function). The investigator responsible for treatment administration was not involved in outcome assessments. For outcome measurements (micro‑CT, histology, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence), all analyses were conducted blinded to group allocation. Control group: After inducing periodontitis, stainless-steel ligatures were removed, and PBS was injected into the periodontal pockets every other day. EXO group: Following periodontitis induction and removal of the ligatures, EXO was injected into the periodontal pockets every other day. EXO were locally administered into the rat periodontal pockets at a concentration of 30 µg/mL in a total volume of 10 µL per site, delivering a total dose of 0.3 µg per injection. This dosage was extrapolated from the effective concentration established in our in vitro studies (10 µg/mL). Lip-Res group: Lip-Res was administered into the periodontal pockets every other day. To achieve a local drug concentration comparable to the effective in vitro dose (5 µg/mL), Lip-Res at a concentration of 15 µg/mL were locally administered into the periodontal pockets of rats. A total volume of 10 µL was delivered per site, resulting in a total drug dose of 0.15 µg per injection. EXO@Lip-Res group: Hybrid nanoparticles were prepared by combining 30 µg/mL EXO solution and 15 µg/mL Lip-Res solution at a 1:1 volume ratio, and this drug was injected into the periodontal pockets every other day.

Specifically, each tooth was injected with the drug at six sites: buccal mesial, buccal distal, palatal mesial, palatal distal, and the central buccal-palatal side, with a total of approximately 10 µL of drug administered per tooth. After 30 days, the experimental rats were euthanized, and their maxillary alveolar bones were carefully dissected and preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde for further analysis.

Microcomputed tomography (micro-CT) analysis

To assess bone regeneration, the maxillary bones with defects were scanned using a micro-CT scanner (vivaCT 40, SCANCO Medical AG, Zurich, Switzerland). The dissected rat maxillae were secured in foam plates for stabilization during imaging. Scanning was performed under the following conditions: 45 kVp, 177 mA, and a resolution of 10 μm. Three-dimensional (3D) reconstructions were generated and analyzed using the manufacturer’s software. A region of interest (ROI) was defined as an 18 × 18 pixel² area on the alveolar bone between the first and second molars, with a height of 50 slices. Volumetric analysis was conducted to determine the ratio of bone volume to tissue volume (BV/TV). Additionally, the distance from the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) to the alveolar bone crest (ABC) was measured for further evaluation. The ABC-CEJ distance was measured on sagittal micro-CT sections at a standardized site: the distal interdental septum between the first and second maxillary molars. Measurements were performed independently by two blinded observers using the manufacturer’s software. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for inter-observer reliability was 0.94 (95% confidence interval: 0.89–0.97), indicating excellent reproducibility.

Histological analysis

Maxillae were decalcified in 10% EDTA, dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned into 5 μm slices (LEICABM2135, Leica, Germany). HE and TRAP stainin were performed per manufacturer protocols. HE staining included dewaxing, hematoxylin (2 min), differentiation (60 s), eosin (1 min), dehydration, xylene clearing, and sealing. TRAP staining involved fixation, incubation (37 °C, 50 min), hematoxylin (3 min), dehydration, and sealing. TRAP-positive osteoclasts (≥ 3 nuclei) between the first and second molars were counted. Sections were analyzed using a slide scanner (VS200, Olympus, Japan), with five fields (500 × 500 μm²) per sample, averaged across ≥ 3 samples per group.

Immunostaining was performed on paraffin sections. After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval (citrate buffer, pH 6.0, 80 °C, 20 min), sections were blocked with 3% H₂O₂ (10 min) and goat serum (20 min). Primary antibodies (TNF-α, IL-1β, HO-1; 1:200, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) were applied overnight at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibody incubation (goat anti-rabbit, 1:100, 37 °C, 30 min). DAB staining was performed to detect positive signals (brownish yellow), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining, dehydration, and sealing. Stained sections were analyzed using a slide scanner (VS200, Olympus, Japan). Positive area (%) was quantified with ImageJ. For each group, ≥ 3 samples were analyzed, with five random fields (100 × 100 μm²) per sample, and group means were calculated.

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were subjected to standard deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval in citrate buffer. After blocking with 5% BSA, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD86 and CD206 (1:200, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The following day, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis for this experiment was performed using GraphPad Prism 6 software. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD) (n ≥ 3). Levene’s test was used to assess the homogeneity of variance among groups, with significance > 0.05 indicating homogeneity. For data meeting the homogeneity of variance assumption, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted for comparisons among multiple groups, with a significance level of 5%. For data not meeting the homogeneity assumption, a non-parametric rank-sum test was performed. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to evaluate intergroup differences, and if significance < 0.05, Bonferroni correction was applied for pairwise comparisons among groups, with a significance level of 5%.
