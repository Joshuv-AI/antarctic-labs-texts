Effects of Premolar Extraction on the Dimensions of the Airway Through Cephalometric Appraisal: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Farzzana Mehraj, Mohammad Abrar Bhat, Ayushi Mangulkar, Cilpa Varghese, Piyush Sharma, Aishwarya Padmanabhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:In recent years, there has been a great deal of interest in research because of the changes that the extraction of premolars has caused to the structures surrounding of the upper airway. This is because it’s thought that these therapies could have unforeseen detrimental effects on a patient’s ability to breathe or put them at risk for diseases like obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA). Nevertheless, no research has looked into how the upper airway’s dimensions are affected when a second premolar is extracted as part of orthodontic treatment.Materials and Methods:This retrospective study was carried out on the available Pre- and Post- orthodontic treatment records (Lateral cephalograms) in order to find the effect of upper and lower premolar extractions in orthodontic treatment, on the pharyngeal airway space. The statistical sample size was determined to be 80 patients whose radiographs were obtained from the archives of the Institution.Results:Our study showed a significant increase in airway space where there was a loss in anchor and no or minimal change with maximum anchorage.Conclusion:The findings in our study suggest that anchor loss after extraction significantly impacts PAS and skeletal parameters, potentially influencing airway dynamics and skeletal relationships. Further research is necessary to elucidate the medical implications of these changes and their long-term effects on patient outcomes in orthodontic treatment.

INTRODUCTION

Orthodontic treatment often involves the extraction of premolars to manage dental crowding, correct bite discrepancies, and improve facial aesthetics.[1] However, there has been growing concern regarding the potential adverse effects of these extractions on the upper airway dimensions. Changes in the pharyngeal airway space have implications for respiratory health, particularly in the context of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA).[23]

The upper airway is a complex anatomical structure influenced by various craniofacial and dental factors. Orthodontic procedures that alter the positions of teeth and surrounding structures can impact airway dimensions and airflow.[456] As such, it is crucial for orthodontists to assess both the dental and respiratory implications of their treatment plans.[78]

This study aims to evaluate the effects of first premolar extraction on the pharyngeal airway space and hyoid bone position through cephalometric analysis. By comparing airway measurements before and after extraction, this study seeks to contribute to the understanding of the relationship between orthodontic interventions and airway health.

Study design

A retrospective analysis was conducted using cephalometric records from the AAOF Legacy collection (2015). The study included patients who underwent orthodontic treatment involving the extraction of all four first premolars. Cephalometric tracings were manually performed to measure sagittal airway dimensions along five planes.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 23. Paired t-tests were used to compare pre- and post-extraction measurements. Chi-square tests were utilized to evaluate qualitative variables. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

The findings of this study indicate that premolar extractions can impact airway dimensions, particularly in cases with anchor loss.[9] Our results are consistent with previous studies, such as those by Mortezai et al.[1] and Germec-Cakan et al.,[10] which highlighted the relationship between orthodontic treatment, hyoid bone position, and upper airway dimensions.

The changes observed in hyoid bone position (H-SN and H-FH) suggest that orthodontic extractions influence the vertical position of the hyoid bone. These changes could have implications for airway patency and respiratory function.[11]

However, the study also revealed that some parameters, such as H-HB and H-ME, did not show significant changes post-extraction. This aligns with findings by Wang et al. (2012), who reported that the downward movement of the hyoid bone is an adaptive response to maintain airway patency rather than a cause of airway narrowing.[121314]

It is important to note that this study used two-dimensional cephalograms, which may not fully capture the three-dimensional nature of airway changes. The use of CBCT scans could provide more accurate assessments but is limited by cost and radiation exposure concerns.

CONCLUSION

Orthodontic treatment involving premolar extractions can significantly impact the upper airway dimensions and hyoid bone position. While moderate anchor loss appears to increase airway dimensions by creating more posterior tongue space, maximum anchorage can diminish airway space. These findings underscore the need for orthodontists to carefully consider airway health during treatment planning.

Future research should focus on three-dimensional assessments and long-term follow-up studies to better understand the impact of orthodontic extractions on airway health and to establish safe retraction thresholds.
