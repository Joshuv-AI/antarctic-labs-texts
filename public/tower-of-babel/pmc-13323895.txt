Ginsenoside RK3 attenuates isoproterenol-induced heart failure in mice through AMPK/Sirt1/PGC-1α pathway activation
Authors: Chongyang Zhang, Yuning chen, Wei Liu, Rong-chang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGinsenoside RK3 was first isolated from heat-processed ginseng. Our study aimed to explore the underlying mechanism of RK3 in combating isoproterenol (ISO)-induced heart failure (HF) in mice.MethodsHF was induced in mice by subcutaneous injection of ISO (80 mg/kg/d) 1 h after intraperitoneal administration of RK3 (10 and 20 mg/kg/d) for 28 days. Cardiac function and heart injury were assessed and comprehensive metabolic alterations in the serum were profiled. Western blot analysis was conducted. In vitro experiments, H9c2 cells were pretreated with RK3 before hypoxia exposure. Cell viability and LDH release were determined.ResultsIn vivo experiments demonstrated that RK3 treatment prevented ISO-induced myocardial damage. Additionally, RK3 reduced serum inflammatory factors and levels of myocardial fibrosis. Metabolomics data indicated that RK3 mitigated the ISO-induced increase in glycolytic intermediates, including 3-phenyllactic acid, D-glucose-6-phosphate, D-glucose-1-phosphate, and D-fructose-6-phosphate and the decrease in intermediates of the TCA cycle, such as citric acid. Treatment with RK3 also inhibited the expression of MCT4 while enhancing the expression of MPC2, PGC-1α, Sirt1, and p-AMPK in heart tissues. In vitro investigations revealed that the protective effect of RK3 on H9c2 cells was abolished by AMPK inhibition.ConclusionsOur study, for the first time, confirmed that RK3 protects against ISO-induced HF, which may be related to the restoration of balance in the pyruvate-lactate axis through the activation of the AMPK/Sirt1/PGC1 pathway.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a complex and chronic myocardial condition. The primary characteristic of HF is the impairment of cardiac contraction and/or relaxation. It represents the terminal stage of most myocardial diseases [1]. According to the American Heart Association, HF occurs when the heart muscle is unable to pump enough blood to meet the body's demands for oxygen and nutrients [2]. Due to the improvement of cardiovascular disease management, the overall incidence of HF remains stable or is declining. However, the prevalence of HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) may be on the rise, primarily due to the absence of effective treatment options. Furthermore, there are currently no specific therapeutic drugs for HF patients with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF), as there have been no substantial prospective randomized clinical trials specifically targeting this population [3,4]. As a significant global public health concern, further research is essential to identify new disease targets and treatment strategies for HF.

Metabolic remodeling is an important factor for the decrease of cardiac function during the progression of HF [5]. Under normal conditions, free fatty acid and glucose oxidation are the two main pathways for energy production in the heart, with fatty acid oxidation (FAO) being the primary source of energy production [6]. However, during HF, glucose becomes the main substrate for ATP production and glycolysis is upregulated. Due to hypoxia, the pyruvate produced by glycolysis cannot enter the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. On the contrary, it is converted into lactate in the cytoplasm, leading to an increase in lactate production [7]. This metabolic abnormality can be observed in both HF patients and animal models. A large amount of studies have been conducted to elucidate the mechanisms underlying changes in cardiac energy metabolism and its relationship to HF [[8], [9], [10]].

Lactate can serve as a substrate for energy production in myocardial cells, despite often being regarded as a waste product [11]. Researchers have discovered that lactate can be converted into pyruvate by the enzyme lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), after which it is taken up by mitochondria to participate in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, ultimately leading to ATP production [12]. The consumption of lactate, along with the concurrent production of lactate from glycolytic pyruvate, maintains balance in a healthy heart, a balance that is disrupted during HF [13]. In HF, the production of pyruvate via glycolysis increases, and it is primarily converted into lactate, while the utilization of lactate decreases [14]. The researchers named this balance as the pyruvate-lactate axis [15]. The mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC), which functions as a transporter for pyruvate, is composed of two subtypes: MPC1 and MPC2 [16]. As a product of glycolysis, pyruvate enters the mitochondria through the MPC complex, where it is fully oxidized by pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) and the TCA cycle [17]. The monocarboxylate transporter (MCT4) is a member of the MCT family and plays a vital role as a lactate exporter in cells, which has low expression in the heart. Both MPC and MCT4 play essential roles in maintaining the balance of the pyruvate-lactate axis, which is a critical component of cardiac homeostasis and health [15].

Panax ginseng C. A. Mey., commonly known as ginseng, is a highly valued traditional Chinese medicinal herb. In clinical practice, it demonstrates significant therapeutic effects against various cardiovascular diseases and is also widely utilized as a functional food [18]. Ginsenoside RK3 is a dammarane glycoside that was first isolated from heat-processed ginseng, which was first found in 2002 [19]. Studies have shown that RK3 possesses multiple pharmacological effects, including anti-tumor, Anti-anemia, anti-kidney injury, anti-thrombotic, anti-inflammatory and liver protective effects [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. Our previous research first found that RK3 prevents H9c2 cardiomyocytes apoptosis induced by hypoxia-reoxygenation [24]. The cardioprotective potential of RK3 in HF remains unexplored. In this study, we investigated the impact of RK3 on cardiac dysfunction in a murine HF model. Notably, we demonstrate for the first time that RK3 substantially ameliorates ISO-induced cardiac impairment and mitigates myocardial pathology in mice, potentially through AMPK-mediated restoration of pyruvate-lactate homeostasis. These findings highlight a novel mechanism of RK3 and suggest its therapeutic relevance for HF treatment.

Materials and methods

Materials

Ginsenoside RK3 (GC31817) with more than 98% purity was purchased from Glpbio Technology (USA). Trimetazidine (TMZ, T274859) was obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. in Shandong, China. Dorsomorphin (866405-64-3) was procured from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. (St Louis, MO, USA). Kits of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) were purchased from the Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute in Nanjing, China. Elisa kits for cardiac troponin I (cTnI), monocyte Chemotactic Protein 1 (MCP1), N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NTproBNP), Interleukin-18 (IL-18), tumor Necrosis Factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) were obtained from Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd. in Shanghai, China. Primar antibodies for α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), collagenase I (Col I), collagen III (Col III), phosphorylated AMP-activated protein kinase (p-AMPK), phosphorylated serine/threonine protein kinase (p-AKT), monocarboxylate transporter 4 (MCT4), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC1α), insulin receptor substrate 1 S307 (Sirt1), and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) were obtained from Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co., Ltd. in Wuhan, China.

Animals and experimental protocols

All animal experiment were conducted in accordance with the policies of the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of the Institute of Medicinal Plant Development, Peking Union Medical College (SLXD, 20230718). 6-8 weeks old male C57BL/6 mice weighing 18 to 22 g were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China), with a certification number of SCXK (Jing) 20230-0013. Room temperature was maintained at 22 °C-24 °C, and mice were housed on a 12 h light-dark cycle. As depicted in Fig. 1, 60 mice were randomly divided into five groups: sham (10 mL/kg saline), ISO-treated (80 mg/kg ISO), ISO and RK3 combination treated (10 or 20 mg/kg RK3 1 h before ISO administration), and ISO and TMZ combination treated (20 mg/kg TMZ 1 h before ISO administration). ISO was administered subcutaneously (s.c.) in the neck once daily for a duration of 28 days. The treatment groups were given RK3 (intraperitoneal injection, i.p.) and TMZ (intragastric gavage, i.g.) simultaneously. The sham group was given an equivalent volume of physiological saline.Fig. 1Schematic overview of the experimental protocol in vivo.

Schematic overview of the experimental protocol in vivo.

Echocardiographic measurements

Mice were anesthetized via inhalation of 1.5-2.0% isoflurane in oxygen and positioned supine on a temperature-regulated platform (37 °C) throughout the procedure. Heart rate and core body temperature were continuously monitored using electrocardiogram leads and a rectal probe, respectively. Cardiac imaging was conducted using a Vevo 1100 High-Resolution Imaging System equipped with a 30 MHz linear-array transducer (MS-400). The imaging depth was set to 12 mm with a frame rate >200 frames per second. Gain settings were optimized for clear endocardial border definition. Under two-dimensional B-mode guidance, the M-mode cursor was positioned perpendicular to the interventricular septum and left ventricular posterior wall at the papillary muscle level to obtain parasternal short-axis views. For each animal, measurements were averaged from three consecutive cardiac cycles selected from stable M-mode tracings. Respiratory artifacts were minimized by triggering acquisition at end-expiration, and gentle restraint was applied to limit motion. Left ventricular internal dimensions at end-systole and end-diastole (LVIDs, LVIDd) and wall thickness were determined using the leading-edge method. Fractional shortening (FS) was calculated as [(LVIDd − LVIDs)/LVIDd] × 100%; left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) was computed automatically by the Vevo LAB analysis software (version 5.6.0) using the Teichholz correction formula. All analyses were performed offline by an investigator blinded to group assignment. Intra-observer and inter-observer coefficients of variation were <5% for all reported parameters.

Detection of serum levels of CK-MB, MCP-1, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-18, NT-proBNP, and cTnI

Blood was collected from the eyeball. After 2 h of standing still, the blood was centrifuged for 15 min at 3000 g. The serum was extracted carefully and stored at −80 °C. The levels of serum CK-MB were detected using kit. The concentrations of cTnI, NT-proBNP, MCP-1, TNFα, IL-1β, and IL-18 in serum were detected using ELISA kits.

Histological and immunohistochemical analyses

The heart tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, routinely processed, and then embedded in paraffin. Subsequently, serial sections (5 μm) were placed on glass slides. Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining was conducted to assess pathological injury, Picro-Sirius Red (PSR), and Masson's trichrome were conducted to assess myocardial fibrosis, wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) was conducted to assess cardiomyocyte hypertrophy.

Protein expressions of COLIII, COLI, p-AMPK, α-SMA, p-AKT, and TGFβ1 in the heart were detected using immunohistochemical staining, which was conducted using the method previously described [25].

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining

Cardiomyocyte apoptosis was assessed using TUNEL staining, which was performed using the method previously described [26].

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The ultrastructural analysis of heart tissues was processed using TEM as previously described [25]. Randomly select three areas from each section for photography.

Targeted metabolomics analysis

Targeted metabolomic analyses were performed using mice sera from sham, ISO, and ISO + RK3 groups (n = 6). Sample processing and detection methods have been previously described [27]. the AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 LC-MS/MS platform was used to detect all metabolites by Met Ware (http://www.metware.cn/).

Metabolomics data and statistical analysis

Metabolomic data were analyzed using R software (version 4.2.2). Prior to unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA), data were scaled to unit variance. PCA was conducted using the prcomp function. Supervised orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed using the R package MetaboAnalystR (version 5.0), following log2-transformation and mean-centering of the data. To assess model validity and prevent overfitting, permutation testing (200 iterations) was conducted. Differentially abundant metabolites were identified based on a variable importance in projection (VIP) score >1.0 and an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) > 1.0. In order to avoid overfitting, a permutation test (200 permutations) was performed. The metabolites of interest were annotated against the KEGG Compound database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/), annotated metabolites were then mapped to KEGG Pathway database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html). Pathways with significantly regulated metabolites mapped to were then fed into MSEA (metabolite sets enrichment analysis), their significance was determined by hypergeometric test's P-Values.

Western blotting (WB)

We detected protein expression levels of PGC1α, Sirt1, GAPDH, AMPK, and p-AMPK using WB analysis, which was conducted as previously study [25]. GAPDH was used as the internal standard expression.

Cell culture and treatment

Rat embryonic cardiomyoblast-derived H9c2 cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Invitrogen, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. To simulate myocardial injury in vitro, cells were subjected to oxygen–glucose deprivation (OGD) by incubation in glucose-free DMEM under hypoxic conditions (95% N2 and 5% CO2) in an airtight chamber (Coy Laboratory, USA) at 37 °C for 12 h. For all experiments, cells were seeded at appropriate densities based on experimental design and used upon reaching 70-80% confluence. H9c2 cells were assigned to the following groups: sham group (no treatment), hypoxia group (H; OGD for 12 h), and hypoxia + RK3 groups, in which cells were pretreated with various concentrations of RK3 (2.5, 5, 10, and 20 μg/mL) for 6 h prior to OGD exposure. In a subsequent experiment aimed at elucidating the underlying mechanism, cells were pre-incubated with the AMPK inhibitor Dorsomorphin (10 μM) for 30 min before RK3 treatment, following determination of the optimal RK3 concentration.

Cell viability detection and LDH activity assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in 100 μL of culture medium. After 24 h of incubation, the cells were subjected to drug treatment and OGD. Following treatment, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels in the culture medium were measured using an LDH assay kit. Subsequently, 10 μL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Infinite M200 PRO, Switzerland).

Statistical analysis

Data are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). In the statistical analysis, D'Agostino and Pearson omnibus normality test was used to test the normality of the data. If the dataset does not show a normal distribution, Mann-Whitney test and Kruskal-Wallis test will be applied for comparisons of two groups and more than two groups, respectively. Comparison between two groups was performed by unpaired student's t-test. Multiple group comparison was performed by two-way ANOVA followed by Turkey procedure for comparison of means. Statistical analyses were subjected to SPSS (version 18.0). A value of p < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Effects of RK3 on cardiac function and myocardial injury in mice

Echocardiographic analysis revealed significant impairment of cardiac function following ISO challenge (Fig. 2A). Compared to the sham group, ISO-treated mice exhibited markedly reduced EF and FS (P < 0.01), both of which were substantially restored by administration of ginsenoside RK3 (20 mg/kg) (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2B and C). These results demonstrate that RK3 effectively enhances myocardial contractile performance in this murine model of HF. Consistent with the functional decline, serum levels of the established HF biomarker NT-proBNP were significantly elevated in the ISO group (P < 0.01). Furthermore, ISO challenge induced pronounced increases in the myocardial injury markers CK-MB and cTnI (P < 0.01). Treatment with RK3 significantly attenuated the ISO-induced elevations in NT-proBNP, CK-MB, and cTnI (P < 0.05, P < 0.01) (Fig. 2D), indicating a clear reduction in cardiomyocyte injury. Effects of RK3 on heart pathological injury, cell hypertrophy, and serum inflammatory factors in mice

Histopathological analysis by HE staining revealed that ISO exposure induced marked myocardial damage characterized by cardiac fiber disruption and inflammatory cell infiltration, which were substantially attenuated by RK3 treatment (Fig. 2G). Assessment of cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area via WGA staining showed a significant increase in cell size following ISO challenge (P < 0.01); this pathological hypertrophy was effectively suppressed by RK3 administration (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, RK3 treatment significantly inhibited the ISO-induced elevation in serum levels of key inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, MCP-1, IL-1β, and IL-18 (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2I).

Effects of RK3 on MCT4 and MPC2 expression in heart tissue

Under physiological conditions, MCT4 expression remains low in cardiomyocytes but is upregulated upon myocardial injury. Conversely, MPC2—a mitochondrial inner membrane protein responsible for pyruvate transport into the matrix—is abundantly expressed in healthy hearts to support TCA cycle flux. Compared to the sham group, ISO exposure increased MCT4 expression and decreased MPC2 expression (P < 0.01), both of which were significantly corrected by RK3 treatment (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5H–J).

AMPK inhibition abolished the protective effect of RK3 on hypoxia-induced injury in H9c2 cells

We next utilized the AMPK inhibitor (Dor) in vitro to further investigate the involvement of AMPK signaling in the cardioprotective effects of RK3. As shown in Fig. 6, Fig. 7D and E, hypoxia exposure in H9c2 cells resulted in decreased cell viability (P < 0.01) and increased LDH release (P < 0.01). Pretreatment with RK3 (20 μg/ml) significantly attenuated these hypoxia-induced injuries (P < 0.01). However, the protective effects of RK3 were partially abolished by co-treatment with Dor, suggesting that the AMPK pathway is likely involved in mediating the protective action of RK3 against HF.

Discussion

Our previous study first demonstrated that RK3 protects against hypoxia-reoxygenation-induced H9c2 cardiomyocytes apoptosis through activation of the AKT/MAPK signaling pathway [24]. However, it remains unclear whether RK3 exerts a protective effect in HF. This study systematically demonstrates that ginsenoside RK3 confers substantial cardioprotection in an ISO-induced murine model of HF. Beyond confirming its beneficial effects on cardiac function, structural integrity, and inflammatory response, our work reveals for the first time that RK3 orchestrates a metabolic reprogramming in the failing heart, primarily through AMPK-dependent restoration of pyruvate-lactate homeostasis. These results establish RK3 as a promising therapeutic agent that simultaneously addresses functional decline and metabolic dysregulation in HF.

Inflammatory response is one of the core drivers of the onset and progression of HF [28]. Sustained or excessive inflammation impairs cardiac structure and function through multiple mechanisms, ultimately leading to and exacerbating HF [29]. On one hand, inflammatory cells (such as neutrophils and macrophages) infiltrate the myocardium and release pro-inflammatory factors, which directly suppress myocardial contractility and induce cardiomyocyte apoptosis and hypertrophy. On the other hand, the activation of inflammatory signaling can upregulate the expression of adhesion molecules, promote oxidative stress and fibrosis, thereby accelerating ventricular remodeling and dysfunction [30,31]. This creates a vicious cycle between inflammation and cardiac functional decline, which is particularly prominent in pathological contexts such as [32]. Therefore, modulating the inflammatory response provides an important direction for developing novel therapeutic strategies targeting immunometabolism.

There exists a closely intertwined and mutually reinforcing vicious cycle between mitochondrial energy metabolism dysfunction in cardiomyocytes and inflammatory responses. On one hand, mitochondrial dysfunction leads to excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and insufficient ATP synthesis, which can directly activate inflammatory signaling pathways, promoting the release of pro-inflammatory factors [33,34]. On the other hand, sustained inflammatory responses further inhibit the activity of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes, disrupt fatty acid and glucose oxidative metabolism, and exacerbate energy metabolism disorders [35,36]. This bidirectional dysregulation of “metabolism-inflammation” not only directly impairs myocardial contractility but also accelerates cardiomyocyte apoptosis, fibrosis, and ventricular remodeling, collectively driving the progression of HF. In this study, a high degree of coupling between inflammatory microenvironment activation and mitochondrial metabolic collapse was observed in the ISO-induced HF mouse model. Serum levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, MCP-1, IL-1β, and IL-18, were significantly elevated, accompanied by severe ultrastructural damage to mitochondria, a marked reduction in the TCA cycle intermediate citric acid, and accumulation of glycolytic intermediates. RK3 restored pyruvate oxidative flux, shifting the metabolic balance from glycolysis back toward mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, while simultaneously suppressing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. By acting on both energy metabolic reprogramming and inflammatory resolution, RK3 exerted integrative regulation of the “inflammation–metabolism” axis, thereby reversing the dual drivers of energy deprivation and immune dysregulation in the progression of HF. This may represent the underlying mechanism of its cardioprotective effect.

As we know, because of oxygen deficiency, pyruvate, produced from the glycolysis, cannot enter the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. It is converted into lactate in the cytoplasm, which causing an increase in lactate levels in HF [37]. Researchers found that lactate can be converted into pyruvate via the enzyme lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and then is taken up by mitochondria to participate in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle to produce ATP [15]. The interconversion of pyruvate and lactate is named pyruvate-lactate axis, which keep balance in healthy heart and is disrupted during HF. In HF, the production of pyruvate by glycolysis increases and is mainly converted into lactate, but the utilization of lactate decreases [38]. The MPC2 is a pyruvate transporter, which promotes mitochondrial uptake of pyruvate and enters the TCA cycle. MPC and MCT4 are crucial for maintaining the balance of the pyruvate-lactate axis, which plays a key role in the steady state of cardiac energy metabolism [15]. In ISO-induced HF mouse myocardial tissue, MPC2 expression was significantly downregulated, whereas MCT4 expression was markedly upregulated. Combined with the targeted metabolomics findings—accumulation of glycolytic intermediates and reduction of the TCA cycle intermediate citric acid—these results indicate a profound remodeling of myocardial energy metabolism under HF conditions, characterized by impaired mitochondrial pyruvate oxidation and compensatory enhancement of glycolysis/lactate transport. RK3 partially restored the metabolic balance between mitochondrial oxidative substrate supply and glycolytic output by upregulating MPC2 and downregulating MCT4. Thus, modulation of MPC2/MCT4-mediated energy metabolic remodeling represents a key mechanism underlying the cardioprotective effects of RK3.

In HF, the AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α signaling axis functions as a core energy metabolism sensor and a key regulatory hub for mitochondrial biogenesis, and has been consistently shown to play a critical protective role [39,40]. During HF, the activity of this pathway is suppressed, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired oxidative phosphorylation, and deficient ATP production. Activation of this pathway can restore mitochondrial homeostasis and tricarboxylic acid cycle function, enhance respiratory chain complex activity and ATP production, and upregulate fatty acid oxidation and aerobic glucose oxidation, thereby reversing pathological metabolic remodeling [41]. In addition, activation of the AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α pathway can directly inhibit NF-κB nuclear translocation and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines [42,43]. In our study, ISO exposure reduced the expression of p-AMPK, Sirt1, and PGC1α—an effect that was reversed by RK3 treatment. These findings suggest that RK3 exerts cardioprotective effects by activating the AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting inflammation and improving mitochondrial metabolism.

This study provides the first systematic demonstration that ginsenoside RK3 exerts multifaceted cardioprotective effects-enhancing myocardial energy metabolism, suppressing inflammatory responses, and attenuating mitochondrial dysfunction-through activation of the AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α signaling axis. These findings establish a robust pharmacological and experimental foundation for positioning RK3 as a promising novel therapeutic candidate for HF.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The upstream kinases or direct binding targets of AMPK activated by RK3 were not elucidated. Furthermore, although RK3 altered MCT4 and MPC2 expression, suggesting involvement in metabolic coupling between mitochondria and cytosol, the causal relationship between AMPK activation and these metabolic regulators remains undefined—specifically, whether MCT4 and MPC2 are direct transcriptional or post-translational substrates of AMPK, indirect downstream effectors, or modulated via AMPK-independent mechanisms. These open questions warrant further investigation in future studies.
