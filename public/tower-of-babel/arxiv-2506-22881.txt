How Semantically Informative is an Image?: Measuring the Covariance-Weighted Norm of Contrastive Learning Embeddings
Fumiya Uchiyama, Rintaro Yanagi, Shohei Taniguchi, Shota Takashiro, Masahiro Suzuki, Hirokatsu Kataoka, Yusuke Iwasawa, Yutaka Matsuo

Abstract
Contrastive learning has the capacity to model multimodal probability distributions by embedding and aligning visual representations with semantics from captions. This approach enables the estimation of relational semantic similarity; however, it remains unclear whether it can also represent absolute semantic informativeness. In this work, we introduce a semantic informativeness metric for an image calculated from text samples via a contrastive learning model; similarly, the informativeness of a text is calculated from image samples. We propose a redefinition of the concept of Information Gain, a concept previously explored in natural language processing, extending its application to the domains of vision and language. Our metric quantifies how conditioning on an image distorts the distribution of associated texts, and vice versa for text conditioning on image distributions. In OpenCLIP’s empirical results, we observe that images with the lowest Information Gain scores often correspond to placeholder icons such as "image not found." Furthermore, we propose to measure a norm-based metric of the embedding to estimate the Information Gain, following the theoretical results for Skip-Gram with Negative Sampling (SGNS) word embedding. Information Gain can be measured using either CLIP or SigLIP, and the results demonstrate a strong correlation with a coefficient of determination ranging from 0.98 to 1.00. After obtaining the mean and the covariance of the sample embedding, the computational cost of this method is independent of the sample size, and it is compatible with publicly available, open-weight models.

Introduction

When humans observe an image, we intuitively recognize that certain parts carry more semantic weight than others. For example, in a photograph of a street, we might notice a person or an unusual object before the background details. We select salient features from various objects in the image, then determine how much and at what granularity we write about them as a caption. Consequently, an image-caption pair implicitly encodes which visual features are semantically important and to what degree they contribute to the overall meaning of the image. However, while this perception is natural for humans, it remains an open challenge to define and quantify such informativeness computationally in the vision and language field.

Natural Language Processing (NLP) community developed several metrics to quantify the informativeness of words in the context of applications to several tasks: extracting keywords from a document, discriminating superordinate words and subordinate words in word pairs, etc.
MATSUO
;
Wartena
;
herbelot-ganesalingam-2013-measuring
;
shwartz-etal-2017-hypernyms
. Classic methods like TF-IDF estimate word importance by considering how frequently a word appears in a document compared to its frequency in a larger corpus. This effectively captures how indicative a word is of specific content. However, such methods rely on the discrete nature of language and the availability of large, structured text corpora. These assumptions do not directly carry over to multimodal domains, where both images and texts are diverse and difficult to discretize.

Despite the recent success of VLMs, it remains unclear whether they encode not just relational similarity, but also semantic informativeness. That is, can these models distinguish between images or texts that are generic versus those that are highly informative? Unlike word statistics, estimating informativeness in the vision-and-language domain is inherently more difficult. Images and texts are high-dimensional and diverse, and there is no direct analogue to document frequency or term occurrence. This motivates our central research goal: to define and evaluate informativeness in the vision-and-language setting, and to examine how it can be estimated and utilized.

Related Work

2.1
Norm of Embeddings in NLP

The main design concept of word statistics, such as co-occurrence information and word embedding models, is based on distributional semantics
Harris1954DistributionalS
;
firth57synopsis
, insisting that words appearing in similar contexts have similar meanings. Since lots of current language models are trained based on co-occurring information (co-occurring words, contexts before the word, etc.), analyzing embeddings with word frequency is common in the NLP community.

A norm of word embeddings is also a notable element in NLP research. Proper nouns have longer embeddings than functional words
schakel2015measuringwordsignificanceusing
. As explained in Section
1
, this metric seems to represent word informativeness. However, not all word embedding models encode semantic informativeness in norms, and embeddings of some models can be corrected by centering and whitening. Especially, considering word frequency during these corrections improves correction, and the norms corrected embeddings represent word probability well since weighted average exists near frequent words, which are often function words
yokoi2024zipfian
. Research on sentence embedding models also found that sentence embedding models with contrastive learning implicitly weight words during their forward processes with their Information Gain
kurita-etal-2023-contrastive
.

2.2
Contrastive Learning for Vision and Language

Contrastive learning is a common solution to align visual representations with textual semantics. CLIP
radford2021learningtransferablevisualmodels
and SigLIP
10377550
encode images and texts to embeddings in a shared space, and predict an image corresponds to a text query by measuring the cosine similarity between the query text embedding and each embedding of the candidate images. Prediction of text corresponding to an image query is executed in the reverse process. To estimate Information Gain of an image (text), we use the similarity to estimate the text (image) prior distribution and its posterior distribution conditioned by the input image (text) in a sample batch to introduce Information Gain of an image (text).

2.3
Measuring Image and Text Semantics

In the context of making a small and high-quality dataset of multi-modal models such as CLIP that outperforms a large, noisy dataset in terms of training efficiency
datacomp
;
fang2024data
. Most of the heuristics applied to curate a large raw dataset into a smaller high-quality subset could be classified into the two categories: metadata-based and embedding-based. The former considers metadata such as language, image size, resolution, caption length, and url
datacomp
. Though these attributes are suitable and a very fast method to discriminate superficial goodness, it is hard to inspect the semantics of each sample through them. The latter utilizes CLIP embedding, filtering whether a text and image pair is semantically similar to some extent. We explore the use of embedding to measure each informativeness of images and texts, other than relational similarity between images and texts.

If the former holds, we can measure the Information Gain of an image or a text independently from the sample size once we get the average vector and a covariance matrix. If the latter holds, measuring a norm is sufficient to estimate Information Gain, which has a very low computational cost and is simple.

Why does the norm of the word embedding of SGNS encode the Information Gain of the word? We suppose that the reason exists in calculating sigmoid with un-normalized embeddings: while the inner-product of normalized embeddings encodes only angular information, the inner-product of un-normalized embeddings is directly affected by the norms of the two embeddings. Therefore, we hypothesized that the norm of un-normalized logit is optimized by the contrastive loss function, and better encodes Information Gain.

6
Conclusion

We have investigated qualitative and quantitative features of Information Gain. The lowest Information Gain gathers meaningless captions and images. We also found norm-based metrics, being able to approximate Information Gain in a memory-efficient way. The most valuable results are that Information Gain can be calculated on current powerful models pre-trained on billions of images, and norm-based metric correlates with Information Gain on those models. We hope that our work will contribute to reveal further potential of multi-modal models.
