Line Stretching in Random Flows
D. R. Lester, M. Dentz

Abstract
How finite-sized material lines stretch in chaotic (mono-scale) and turbulent (multi-scale) flows remains a central but unresolved problem that governs mixing, transport and reaction. We show elongation is controlled by a finite-sampling process balancing ensemble and temporal averaging that is mediated by particle dispersion. These results expose the rich dynamics of line stretching and compel reassessment of experimental data and models of fluid-borne phenomena.

Fluid Deformation in Random Flows.
We consider stretching of a fluid element along a pathline
\mathbf{x}_{0}(\mathbf{X},t)
with initial Lagrangian position
\mathbf{X}
advected by the
d
-dimensional random uniformly hyperbolic velocity field
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)
.
All such flows involve exponential growth of infinitesimal line elements
\delta\bm{\ell}(\mathbf{X},t)
. For simplicity we consider divergence-free flows, but note these results are easily extended to compressible flow.
\delta\bm{\ell}
evolves according to the deformation gradient tensor
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},t)\equiv\partial\mathbf{x}/\partial\mathbf{X}
as

\delta\bm{\ell}(\mathbf{X},t)=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},t)\,\delta\bm{\ell}(\mathbf{X},0),

(5)

and
\mathbf{F}
evolves via the Lagrangian velocity gradient tensor
\bm{\epsilon}(\mathbf{X},t)\equiv\nabla\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}_{0}(\mathbf{X},t),t)^{\top}

\dot{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{X},t)=\bm{\epsilon}(\mathbf{X},t)\,\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},t),\quad\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},0)=\mathbf{1},

(6)

where
\det\mathbf{F}=1
due to incompressibility. We denote the principal components of
\bm{\epsilon}
in a

\epsilon_{i}
with

, where
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\epsilon_{i}=0
and
\langle\epsilon_{i}\rangle\geqslant\langle\epsilon_{i+1}\rangle

\bm{\epsilon}(\mathbf{X},t)
with Lagrangian time

[

26

1

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}_{0}(\mathbf{X},t)+\mathbf{Q}^{\top}(\mathbf{X},t)\,\mathbf{x},

(7)

via the unitary rotation tensor
\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{X},t)
, where the Protean frame is defined
[

,
1

by the rotation

\bm{\epsilon}^{\prime}(\mathbf{X},t)

\mathbf{F}^{\prime}(\mathbf{X},t)=\mathbf{Q}^{\top}(\mathbf{X},t)\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},t)\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{X},0)

\bm{\epsilon}^{\prime}(\mathbf{X},t)=\mathbf{Q}\,\bm{\epsilon}(\mathbf{X},t)\,\mathbf{Q}^{\top}+\dot{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}

Line Stretching in Random Flows.
Equation (
10
) quantifies stretching of line elements in random flows. For steady flows (which must be 3D to be hyperbolic), the Lyapunov spectrum
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})

(\lambda_{\infty},0,-\lambda_{\infty})

[
28

(\lambda_{\infty},-\lambda_{\infty})

(\lambda_{\infty},\lambda_{2},-\lambda_{\infty}-\lambda_{2})

\lambda_{2}>0

[

\langle\epsilon_{1}\rangle

[

For these hyperbolic flows, line elements

\lambda_{\infty}

[

\Omega(t)

\mathcal{L}

\epsilon

\displaystyle\ell(t)=\ell_{0}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}d\mathbf{X}\,\rho(\mathbf{X})\exp\left(\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\epsilon\left[\mathbf{x}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{X}),t^{\prime}\right]\right),

Zero Net Dispersion.
For illustrative purposes we first consider the simplest case of mono-scale flow with zero net advective dispersion that arises in
isolated mixing regions
in chaotic flows
[
41

\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle\ell_{n}=\ell_{0}\prod\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\int d\mathbf{x}_{i}\exp[\tau_{v}\epsilon(\mathbf{x}_{i},t_{i})]g(\mathbf{x}_{i},t_{i}),

Furthermore, we assume for the present that over each time step this dispersion within

N_{0}

\displaystyle\ell_{n}=\ell_{0}\prod\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N_{0}}\exp(\tau_{v}\epsilon_{k,i}),

N_{0}

\ell_{n}=\frac{\ell_{0}\exp(n\tau_{v}\lambda_{\infty})}{\mathcal{N}_{n}}\sum\limits_{r=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{n}}\exp\left(\zeta_{r,n}\right),

\displaystyle\ell_{n}=\ell_{0}\exp(n\tau_{v}\lambda_{\infty})\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,p_{\zeta}(x,n)\exp(x)

\displaystyle p_{\zeta}(x,n)\equiv\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{n}}\sum\limits_{r=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{n}}\delta\left(z-\zeta_{r,n}\right).

Highly Correlated Stretching.
Above we considered the extreme case where the distributions of line segments at subsequent time steps are independent. We consider now the opposite case that the line segments remain within the same stretching region at all times, that is, their positions are strongly correlated. In this case, we obtain
[
23
]

\displaystyle\ell_{n}=\ell_{0}\exp(n\tau_{v}\lambda_{\infty})\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N_{0}}\exp\left(\zeta_{k,n}\right),

(25)

where
\zeta_{k,n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon^{\prime}_{k,n}
. Here, the
\zeta_{k,n}
are independent for different
k
and thus the number of independent replica is constant and exactly equal to
N_{0}
. Thus the FTTE evolves as discussed above. Cases involving partial dispersion over the support volume
\Omega_{0}
and/or correlation between subsequent time steps lie between these two extremes and so behave in the same way.

Finite Net Dispersion.
For material lines that undergo finite net dispersion, the line support
\Omega(t)
has evolving volume
V(t)
that grows algebraically with time, as does the number
N(t)\approx V(t)/\ell_{v}^{d}
of stretching variates. Again we assume that the distribution of stretchers at a given time step is independent from the distribution at the previous step, so the line elongation is given by (
14
). For finite dispersion, the number of independent stretching regions denoted by
m_{i}
, that is, the number of independently distributed
\epsilon_{k,i}
, increases at each time step. Thus, we now discretize (
14
) as

\displaystyle\ell_{n}=\ell_{0}\exp(n\tau_{v}\lambda_{\infty})\prod\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{m_{i}}\sum\limits_{k_{i}=1}^{m_{i}}\exp(\tau_{v}\epsilon^{\prime}_{k,i}),

(26)

We can rewrite this expression in the form of Eq. (
16
), where now
\mathcal{N}_{n}=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}m_{i}
.
It appears that the number of replica of
\zeta_{r,n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\tau_{v}\varepsilon^{\prime}_{r,i}
increases exponentially fast with the number
n
of time steps. However, the
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{r,i}
for a given
i
are not independent, and therefore the
\zeta_{r,n}
are correlated Gaussian random variables. The set of all independent
\epsilon^{\prime}_{k,i}
in Eq. (
26

n
steps has the size
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}m_{i}
. Thus, the maximum number of independent replica
\zeta_{r,n}
of length
n
that can be formed is
N_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}m_{i}/n

Multi-Scale Flows.
We now show that the results above also apply to multiscale flows such as homogeneous isotropic turbulence, which exhibit intermittency and scale-dependent statistics rather than the single scaling law predicted by classical Kolmogorov theory
[
24
,
44
]
. Such flows possess a broad spectrum of velocity scales and strongly non-Gaussian finite-time Lyapunov exponent (FTLE) statistics arising from intermittent vortex stretching
[
24
]
. Nevertheless, stretching of a continuous material line is governed by the smallest flow scales and controlled by the Kolmogorov length
\eta\sim(\nu^{3}/\varepsilon)^{1/4}
and time
\tau_{\eta}\sim\sqrt{\nu/\varepsilon}

[
44
,
37
]
. This range of velocity length scales implies that
\Omega(t)
partitions into stretching regions of varying volumes, which is captured by the above formulation, as discussed in
[
23
]
. While intermittency produces non-Gaussian FTLE statistics at intermediate times, the distribution converges to Gaussian for
t\gg\tau_{\eta}

[
45
,
40
]
. Intermittency also drives anomalous dispersion
\langle\Delta r^{2}(t)\rangle\sim t^{p}
with
1\leq p\leq 3
, implying
V(t)\sim t^{dp/2}
. Together with ubiquitous folding in random flows, this yields algebraic growth of
N(t)
such that
h(t)\to\lambda_{\infty}
, as shown in Fig.
1
b.

Closing Remarks.

These results uncover the mechanisms governing the stretching of material lines
\mathcal{L}
in mono- and multi-scale random flows, and more generally the deformation of finite-sized material elements. Dispersion controls the number
N(t)
of independent stretching variates sampled by
\mathcal{L}
, yielding a competition between convergence of the finite-time topological entropy
h(t)
to its ensemble (
4
) and temporal (
3
) averages.

The rate of convergence depends on time, flow spatio-temporal structure, and dispersion, through their influence on the growth of
N(t)
, ultimately leading to convergence of
h(t)\rightarrow h=\lambda_{\infty}
. We thereby explain the commonly observed short-time convergence to the ensemble average (
t<t_{1}
) and reveal a transition to temporal averaging at long times (
t>t_{1}
). These findings enable inference of stretching dynamics directly from
l(t)
data and call for a reassessment of experimental measurements and models of fluid deformation, with implications for mixing, colloidal deposition, and reactive transport in random flows.

II
Random walk stretching model

In this section, we discuss the modeling of the stretching time series as a random walk process. First, we discuss the implications of Ito versus Stratonovic interpretation of the stochastic process characterized by a multiplicative noise. Then, we discuss the modeling of the line elongation using an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, which is compared to the data obtained from stretching in the Kraichnan flow discussed in the previous section.

II.1
Ito versus Stratonovic interpretation

The elongation of an infinitesimal line element
\delta\ell(t,a)
can be described by the stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{d\delta\ell(t,a)}{dt}=\lambda_{\infty}\delta\ell(t,a)+\epsilon(t,a)\delta\ell(t,a).

(S.6)

We model
\epsilon(t,a)
as a Gaussian white noise with variance
\langle\epsilon(t,a)\epsilon(t^{\prime},a)\rangle=\sigma_{\infty}^{2}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.
We consider now the process
\xi(t,a)=\ln\delta\ell(t,a)
. Using the Ito interpretation, its evolution is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d\xi(t,a)}{dt}=\lambda_{\infty}-\frac{\sigma_{\infty}^{2}}{2}+\epsilon(t,a).

(S.7)

When we use the Stratonovic interpretation, it is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d\xi(t,a)}{dt}=\lambda_{\infty}+\epsilon(t,a),

(S.8)

Note that we use throughout the Ito calculus. In the first case, the distribution of
\xi(t)
is

\displaystyle p_{\zeta}(x,t)=\frac{\exp\left[-\frac{(x-\lambda_{\infty}t)^{2}}{2\sigma_{\infty}^{2}t}\right]}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{\infty}^{2}t}}

(S.9)

and in the second case by

\displaystyle p_{\xi}(x,t)=\frac{\exp\left[-\frac{\left(x-\lambda_{\infty}t+\frac{\sigma_{\infty}^{2}t}{2}\right)^{2}}{2\sigma_{\infty}^{2}t}\right]}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{\infty}^{2}t}}

(S.10)

Therefore, in the Ito interpretation, the topological entropy obtained from the ensemble average is
\lambda_{\infty}
, in the Stratonovic interpretation it is
\lambda_{\infty}+\sigma_{\infty}^{2}/2
. This can be seen by performing the ensemble average of
\delta\ell(t,a)

\displaystyle\langle\delta\ell(t,a)\rangle=\delta a\left\langle\exp\left[\xi(t,a)\right]\right\rangle.

(S.11)

However, the physically meaningfull interpretation is the Stratonovic interpretation because it acknowledges the fact the white noise approximation for
\epsilon(t,a)
is obtained as a limit process from a finite time correlation as explored in the next section.

II.2
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck model

The evolution of the logarithm
\xi(t)
of the line elongation is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d\xi(t)}{dt}=\epsilon(t).

(S.12)

We model the deformation rate time series
\epsilon(t)
as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process

\displaystyle\frac{d\epsilon(t)}{dt}=-\gamma[\epsilon(t)-\lambda_{\infty}]+\sqrt{2\kappa}\zeta(t),

(S.13)

where
\gamma
is the relaxation rate,
\zeta(t)
a Gaussian white noise and
\kappa
the fluctuation strength. The characteristic correlation scale is given by
\tau_{v}=\gamma^{-1}
. The mean deformation rate evolves as

\displaystyle\langle\epsilon(t)\rangle=(\langle\epsilon_{0}\rangle-\lambda_{\infty})\exp(-\gamma t)+\lambda_{\infty}

(S.14)

We assume stationary initial conditions and set
\langle\epsilon_{0}\rangle=0
and the initial variance equal to the stationary variance
\sigma_{\epsilon_{0}}^{2}=\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}
. Thus, the covariance function of the deformation fluctuation
\epsilon^{\prime}(t)=\epsilon(t)-\lambda_{\infty}
is given by

\displaystyle\langle\epsilon^{\prime}(t)\epsilon^{\prime}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\exp[-\gamma(t-t^{\prime})],

(S.15)

where
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}=\kappa\gamma^{-1}
and
t\geq t^{\prime}
. The log-elongation
\xi(t)
is obtained by integration of Eq. (
S.12
) as

\displaystyle\xi(t)=\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\epsilon(t).

(S.16)

Thus, we obtain for the mean of
\xi(t)

\displaystyle\langle\xi(t)\rangle=\lambda_{\infty}t-\frac{\lambda_{\infty}}{\gamma}\left[1-\exp(-\gamma t)\right],

(S.17)

and for the variance

\displaystyle\langle\xi^{\prime}(t)^{2}\rangle=\frac{2\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\left[\gamma t-1+\exp(-\gamma t)\right].

(S.18)

The long-time evolution for
\gamma t\gg 1
is given by

\displaystyle\langle\xi^{\prime}(t)^{2}\rangle=\frac{2\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}}{\gamma}t\equiv\sigma_{\infty}^{2}t.

(S.19)

shows that these results compare very well with numerical simulations of the 2D Kraichnan flow.

III
Finite sample statistics

The
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{r,i}
within a series
\{\varepsilon^{\prime}_{r,i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are mutually independent because for a given series the
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{r,i}=\epsilon^{\prime}_{k_{i},i}
with
k_{i}\in[1,N_{0}]
. Expression (
) indicates the the number of replica of
\zeta_{r,n}
increases exponentially fast with

\zeta_{r,n}
is equal to
nN_{0}/n=N_{0}
. The nominator is due to the fact that after
n
steps there is a total of
nN_{0}
independent
\epsilon^{\prime}_{k,i}
, from which the
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{r,i}
are sampled. The denominator comes from the fact that each
\zeta^{\prime}_{r,n}
is based on a series of
n
different
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{r,i}
, which need to be sampled without replacement from the set of
\epsilon^{\prime}_{k,i}
in order to have a set of independent
\zeta_{r,n}
. That is, out of the sample of
N_{0}^{n}
replica, we can form
N_{0}^{n}/N_{0}=N_{0}^{n-1}
subsamples that have the same (finite) statistics. Thus, we can reorganize the sum and group the replica into
N_{0}^{n-1}
families of independent replica such that

\displaystyle\ell_{n}=\ell_{0}\exp(n\tau_{v}\lambda_{\infty})\frac{1}{N_{0}^{n-1}}\sum\limits_{r=1}^{N_{0}^{n-1}}\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N_{0}}\exp\left({\zeta}^{(r)}_{k,n}\right)

(S.20)

Thus, we can write

\displaystyle\ell_{n}=\ell_{0}\exp(n\tau_{v}\lambda_{\infty})\frac{1}{N_{0}^{n-1}}\sum\limits_{r=1}^{N_{0}^{n-1}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dxp_{\zeta}^{(r)}(x,n)\exp(x),

(S.21)

where
p_{\zeta}^{(r)}(x,n)
is the distribution of
N_{0}
independent
\zeta^{(r)}_{k,n}
within a family,

\displaystyle p_{\zeta}^{(r)}(x,n)=\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N_{0}}\delta(x-\zeta^{(r}_{k,n}).

(S.22)

Note that all families
\{\zeta^{(r)}_{k,n}\}_{k=1}^{N_{0}}
share the same maximum because they are based on the same
\epsilon^{\prime}
-statistics. Thus, as the (finite) statistical content of each family is the same, we set
p_{\zeta}^{(r)}(x,n)=p_{\zeta}(x,n)
independent of
