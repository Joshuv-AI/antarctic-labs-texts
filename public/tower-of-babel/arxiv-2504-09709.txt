A permutation based approach to the q q -deformation of the Dynkin Operator
Darij Grinberg \addressmark 1, Ekaterina A. Vassilieva \addressmark 2

Abstract
Introduced by Solomon, the descent algebra is a significant subalgebra of the group algebra of the symmetric group 𝐤 ​ S n \mathbf{k}S_{n} related to many important algebraic and combinatorial topics. It contains all the classical Lie idempotents of 𝐤 ​ S n \mathbf{k}S_{n} , in particular the Dynkin operator, a fundamental tool for studying the free Lie algebra. We look at a q q -deformation of the Dynkin operator and study its action over the descent algebra with classical combinatorial tools like Solomon’s Mackey formula. This leads to elementary proofs that the operator is indeed an idempotent for q = 1 q=1 as well as interesting formulas and algebraic structures especially when q q is a root of unity.

1
Introduction

Given a base ring
\mathbf{k}
and a non-negative integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
, denote
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
,
S_{n}
the symmetric group on
[n]
and let
\mathbf{k}S_{n}
be the group algebra of the symmetric group.
The
descent algebra

\Sigma_{n}
, introduced by Solomon in
[
11
]
, is the subalgebra spanned by the elements of
\mathbf{k}S_{n}
such that permutations with the same descent set have the same coefficients.
This algebra
\Sigma_{n}
has two elementary bases indexed by subsets of
[n-1]
or, equivalently, by integer compositions of
n
: the descent class
\mathbf{D}
-basis and the subset
\mathbf{B}
-basis.
The structure constants of the
\mathbf{B}
-basis admit an elegant combinatorial interpretation known as Solomon’s Mackey formula.
The Dynkin operator
\mathbf{V}_{n}
is a remarkable element of
\Sigma_{n}
defined as an alternating sum of some descent classes (i.e. elements of the
\mathbf{D}
-basis) playing a fundamental rôle in the study of the free Lie algebra
[
8
, (8.4.2)]
.
The result that
\mathbf{V}_{n}
is indeed (quasi)idempotent follows easily from the fact that
\mathbf{V}_{n}\mathbf{B}_{I}=0
for all non-empty subsets
I\subseteq[n-1]
.
The latter fact appears in the literature with several proofs, but we provide the first combinatorial proof that derives it from Solomon’s Mackey formula.
We also study the
q
-deformation of
\mathbf{V}_{n}
introduced in
[
3
]
and compute its left action on the
\mathbf{B}
-basis
as well as the dimension of the right ideal it generates depending on
q
.

1.1
Permutations, integer compositions and subsets

A
composition

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{p})
of a non-negative integer
n
, denoted
\alpha\vDash n
, is a finite sequence of
\ell(\alpha):=p
positive integers such that
|\alpha|:=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=n
. Denote
\operatorname{Comp}_{n}
the set of compositions of
n
. There is a natural bijection between compositions of
n
and subsets of
[n-1]
.
Namely, for a composition
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{p})\in\operatorname{Comp}_{n}
, let
\operatorname{Set}(\alpha)
be the subset of
[n-1]
defined as
\operatorname{Set}(\alpha)=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\dots+\alpha_{p-1}\}
. Conversely, given a subset
I=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{p}\}\subseteq[n-1]
let
\operatorname{comp}(I)
be the composition
(i_{1},i_{2}-i_{1},i_{3}-i_{2},\dots,n-i_{p})
of
n
.
Thus,
|\operatorname{Comp}_{n}|=2^{n-1}
when
n\geq 1
.

Definition 1

(composition refinement)
.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{m})
and
\beta=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{p})
be two compositions of the same integer
n
. We say that
\alpha

refines

\beta
, and write
\alpha\preceq\beta
, if and only if
\alpha
can be split into
p
subsequences
\alpha^{i}=(\alpha^{i}_{1},\alpha^{i}_{2},\dots,\alpha^{i}_{m_{i}})
for
1\leq i\leq p
such that
\alpha^{i}\vDash\beta_{i}
.
Note that the sequence of compositions
(\alpha^{i})_{i=1\dots p}
is uniquely determined by
\alpha
and
\beta
.
Denote by
\alpha|\beta
this sequence, and by
\overline{\alpha|\beta}
the list of the last (rightmost) elements of each
\alpha^{i}
, that is,

\overline{\alpha|\beta}=(\alpha^{1}_{m_{1}},\alpha^{2}_{m_{2}},\dots,\alpha^{p}_{m_{p}}).

If
I
and
J
are two subsets of
[n-1]
, then
J\subseteq I
if and only if
\operatorname{comp}(I)\preceq\operatorname{comp}(J)
. We write
I|J
(resp.
\overline{I|J}
) instead of
\operatorname{comp}(I)|{\operatorname{comp}(J)}
(resp.
\overline{\operatorname{comp}(I)|{\operatorname{comp}(J)}}
) for brevity’s sake. If
J\nsubseteq I
, we set
\overline{I|J}=\emptyset
. Finally, if
U
is a sequence of numbers, we let
|U|
(resp.
\Sigma U
) denote the number (resp. the sum) of its elements, i.e., we set
\Sigma U:=\sum\limits_{u\in U}u
.

1.2
The descent algebra of the symmetric group

Given a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
, we consider its
descent set

\operatorname{Des}(\pi)
defined as
\operatorname{Des}(\pi)=\{1\leq i\leq n-1|\pi(i)>\pi(i+1)\}.

For
I\subseteq[n-1]
, let
\mathbf{D}_{I}
and
\mathbf{B}_{I}
be the elements of the group algebra
\mathbf{k}S_{n}
defined as

\mathbf{D}_{I}=\sum_{\pi\in S_{n};\;\operatorname{Des}(\pi)=I}\pi,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\mathbf{B}_{I}=\sum_{\pi\in S_{n};\;\operatorname{Des}(\pi)\subseteq I}\pi.

As shown by Solomon in
[
11
]
in the more general context of finite Coxeter groups, the
\mathbf{D}_{I}
’s (resp.
\mathbf{B}_{I}
’s) span the
descent algebra

\Sigma_{n}
, a subalgebra of
\mathbf{k}S_{n}
of dimension
2^{n-1}
. The families
(\mathbf{D}_{I})_{I\subseteq[n-1]}
and
(\mathbf{B}_{I})_{I\subseteq[n-1]}
are bases of
\Sigma_{n}
, known as the
descent class
or simply
\mathbf{D}
-basis, resp. the
subset
or
\mathbf{B}
-basis.
The two bases are obviously related through

\mathbf{B}_{I}=\sum_{J\subseteq I}\mathbf{D}_{J},\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\mathbf{D}_{I}=\sum_{J\subseteq I}(-1)^{|I\setminus J|}\mathbf{B}_{J}.

(1)

The structure constants of the
\mathbf{B}
-basis have a combinatorial description known as
Solomon’s Mackey formula

[
12
, Theorem 2]
,
[
2
, Prop. 4.3]
(note the different conventions for the order of multiplication in
S_{n}
).
To state it, for any
f\in\Sigma_{n}
and
K\subseteq[n-1]
, let
[\mathbf{B}_{K}]f
denote the coefficient of
\mathbf{B}_{K}
in the expansion of
f
in the
\mathbf{B}
-basis of
\Sigma_{n}
.
We abbreviate
[\mathbf{B}_{K}](fg)
as
[\mathbf{B}_{K}]fg
.
Furthermore, if
M
is a
p\times q
matrix, its
row sums vector
is the
p
-tuple of row sums, its
column sums vector
is the
q
-tuple of column sums, and its
reduced reading word
is the concatenation of the rows of
M
without zeroes.

Proposition 1

(Solomon’s Mackey formula)
.

Let
I,J,K\subseteq[n-1]
.
Let
\mathbb{N}^{I,J}_{K}
be the set of nonnegative integer matrices with row sums vector
\operatorname{comp}(I)
, column sums vector
\operatorname{comp}(J)
and reduced reading word
\operatorname{comp}(K)
.
Then,
[\mathbf{B}_{K}]\mathbf{B}_{J}\mathbf{B}_{I}=|\mathbb{N}^{I,J}_{K}|
.

Note that
\mathbb{N}^{I,J}_{K}
is empty unless
I\subseteq K
. Thus,
[\mathbf{B}_{K}]\mathbf{B}_{J}\mathbf{B}_{I}=0
unless
I\subseteq K
.
The non-zero entries of the
i
-th row in the matrix must give the
i
-th subsequence in
K|I
. Furthermore the sequence of the rightmost non-zero elements of each row in the matrix is exactly
\overline{K|I}
.

Example 1
.

Assume
n=5
,
I=\{3\}
,
J=K=\{1,3\}
. We have
\operatorname{comp}(I)=(3,2)
,
\operatorname{comp}(J)=\operatorname{comp}(K)=(1,2,2)
. We notice that
I\subseteq K
, thus
\operatorname{comp}(K)\preceq\operatorname{comp}(I)
. Finally
K|I=\left((1,2),(2)\right)
,
\overline{K|I}=(2,2)
and
|\mathbb{N}^{I,J}_{K}|=2
as there are exactly
2
suitable matrices, namely:

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&2\\
0&2&0\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\
0&0&2\end{pmatrix}.

1.3
The Dynkin operator

A permutation
w\in S_{n}
is said to be a

V-permutation
if there exists some
k\in\left[n\right]
such that

w\left(1\right)>w\left(2\right)>\cdots>w\left(k\right)<w\left(k+1\right)<w\left(k+2\right)<\cdots<w\left(n\right).

In this case, automatically
w\left(k\right)=1
.

Definition 2

(Dynkin operator)
.

The
Dynkin operator
is the element

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in S_{n}\text{ is a}\\
\text{V-permutation}\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{w^{-1}\left(1\right)-1}w\in\mathbf{k}S_{n}.

The Dynkin operator can also be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{n}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{2,1}\right)\left(1-\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{3,2,1}\right)\left(1-\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{4,3,2,1}\right)\cdots\left(1-\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{n,n-1,\ldots,1}\right),

(2)

where
\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{i,i-1,\dots,2,1}
is the cycle permutation that maps
i,i-1,\ldots,2,1
to
i-1,i-2,\ldots,1,i
, respectively, and fixes all the other elements of
[n]
.
See
[
4
, Lemma 1.1]
for a proof. The Dynkin operator satisfies

\mathbf{V}_{n}^{2}=n\mathbf{V}_{n}.

(3)

This is proved in
[
1
, §1]
and
[
4
, Remark 2.1]
using the free Lie algebra, in
[
2
, proof of (5)]
using custom-built combinatorics, and in
[
6
, Subsection 4.5.2]
using Hopf algebras.
The former two proofs rely on the fact that

\mathbf{V}_{n}\mathbf{D}_{I}=\left(-1\right)^{\left|I\right|}\mathbf{V}_{n}\qquad\text{ for any }I\subseteq\left[n-1\right].

(4)

Using Equation (
1
), this can be rewritten as

\mathbf{V}_{n}\mathbf{B}_{I}=0\qquad\text{ for any nonempty }I\subseteq\left[n-1\right].

(5)

This surprising connection with the
\mathbf{B}
-basis
suggests a link to Solomon’s Mackey formula.
Furthermore we felt that it is worth studying how the action of
\mathbf{V}_{n}
on the
\mathbf{B}
-basis changes (e.g. in terms of rank and kernel) when the coefficient
-1
in
(-1)^{w^{-1}\left(1\right)}
is replaced by an arbitrary element
-q
of the base ring
\mathbf{k}
, especially a root of unity. We thus look at the following
q
-deformation of the Dynkin operator.

Definition 3

(
q
-deformation of the Dynkin operator,
[
3
]
)
.

Fix
q
in the base ring
\mathbf{k}
and a non-negative integer
n
. The
q
-
deformation
of the Dynkin operator is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{n}^{(q)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in S_{n}\text{ is
a}\\
\text{V-permutation}\end{subarray}}(-q)^{w^{-1}\left(1\right)-1}w\in\mathbf{k}S_{n}.

(6)

Generalizing (
2
),
\mathbf{V}_{n}^{(q)}=\left(1-q\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{2,1}\right)\left(1-q\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{3,2,1}\right)\cdots\left(1-q\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{n,n-1,\ldots,1}\right).

In the work of Calderbank, Hanlon and Sundaram
[
3
]
,
\mathbf{V}_{n}^{(q)}
appears under the name
\eta_{n}(\alpha)
for
\alpha=q
in the context of higher Lie modules: The left ideal of
\mathbf{k}S_{n}
generated by
\mathbf{V}^{(q)}_{n}
is an
S_{n}
-representation
V_{n}(\alpha)
, which is (isomorphic to) the multilinear component of the “
\alpha
-deformed free Lie algebra” on
n
generators (which generalises both the free Lie algebra and the free Lie superalgebra). Our focus is to study
\mathbf{V}^{(q)}_{n}
using Solomon’s Mackey formula and elementary permutation combinatorics.
Three specializations of
\mathbf{V}^{(q)}_{n}
are worthy of note. As per the definition
\mathbf{V}^{(1)}_{n}=\mathbf{V}_{n}
is the standard Dynkin operator.
Next,
\mathbf{V}^{(0)}_{n}=\operatorname{id}\in\mathbf{k}S_{n}
. Finally,
\mathbf{V}^{(-1)}_{n}
is the sum of all V-permutations, i.e. the sum of all permutations with empty
peak set
.
\mathbf{V}^{(-1)}_{n}
is the main building block of the peak algebra analog to the Dynkin operator introduced in
[
10
]
.

In the next sections, we proceed with a general expansion of the action of
\mathbf{V}_{n}^{(q)}
on the
\mathbf{B}
-basis using Solomon’s Mackey formula.
Then we provide some consequences:
new proofs of (
5
), (
4
) and (
3
);
a description of the eigenvalues of the action of
\mathbf{V}^{(q)}_{n}
on
\Sigma_{n}
;
and a combinatorial formula for the dimension of its image
\mathbf{V}^{(q)}_{n}\Sigma_{n}
.

2
Action of
\mathbf{V}_{n}^{(q)}
on the
\mathbf{B}
-basis of the descent algebra

Given an integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a parameter
q\in\mathbf{k}
and a subset
I\subseteq[n-1]
, we look at the expansion of
\mathbf{V}_{n}^{(q)}\mathbf{B}_{I}
in the
\mathbf{B}
-basis of the descent algebra
\Sigma_{n}
. We first decompose
\mathbf{V}_{n}^{(q)}
in the
\mathbf{D}
-basis and solve the problem for these basis elements.

2.1
Decomposition according to descent classes

First, we decompose the
q
-deformed Dynkin operator in the
\mathbf{D}
-basis of
\Sigma_{n}
.

Proposition 2
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
q\in\mathbf{k}
, the
q
-deformed Dynkin operator
\mathbf{V}_{n}^{(q)}
expands as

\mathbf{V}_{n}^{(q)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-q)^{k-1}\mathbf{D}_{[k-1]}.

(7)

As a result,
\mathbf{V}_{n}^{(q)}\in\Sigma_{n}
.

Proof.

For each
k\in[n]
, we know that
\mathbf{D}_{\left[k-1\right]}
is the sum of all permutations
w\in S_{n}
with descent set
[k-1]
. But these are precisely the V-permutations
w\in S_{n}
with
w^{-1}\left(1\right)=k
.
Hence, the right hand side of (
7
) agrees with the right hand side of (
6
).
The equality (
7
) follows, and thus the proposition holds.
∎

Define
\mathbf{V}_{n,k}:=\mathbf{D}_{\left[k-1\right]}
for each
k\in\left[n\right]
. We expand
\mathbf{V}_{n,k}\mathbf{B}_{I}
for
I\subseteq[n-1]
in the
\mathbf{B}
-basis.

Theorem 1
.

Given two positive integers
k\leq n
and two sets
I,K\subseteq[n-1]
with
I\subseteq K
.
Then, the
\mathbf{B}_{K}
-coefficient in the expansion in the
\mathbf{B}
-basis of
\mathbf{V}_{n,k}\mathbf{B}_{I}
is

[\mathbf{B}_{K}]\mathbf{V}_{n,k}\mathbf{B}_{I}=(-1)^{k+|K|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\subseteq\overline{K|I};\\
\Sigma U>n-k\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|U|}.

(8)

(The sum runs over subsequences of
\overline{K|I}
, parameterized by their positions in
\overline{K|I}
; thus, equal subsequences at different positions lead to repeated addends.)

Proof.

We define

\mathbb{N}^{I}_{K}:=\bigcup_{J\subseteq[n-1]}\mathbb{N}^{I,J}_{K};

this is the set of all nonnegative integer matrices with row sums vector
\operatorname{comp}(I)
and reduced reading word
\operatorname{comp}(K)
that have no zero columns. Given
A\in\mathbb{N}^{I}_{K}
, we let
\operatorname{col}(A)
be the number of columns of
A
and
A^{\star}
the sequence of entries in the rightmost column of
A
. We have

\mathbf{V}_{n,k}=\mathbf{D}_{\left[k-1\right]}=\sum\limits_{J\subseteq\left[k-1\right]}\left(-1\right)^{\left(k-1\right)-\left|J\right|}\mathbf{B}_{J}

by Equation (
1
).
Hence,

\displaystyle[\mathbf{B}_{K}]\mathbf{V}_{n,k}\mathbf{B}_{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{J\subseteq\left[k-1\right]}\left(-1\right)^{\left(k-1\right)-\left|J\right|}[\mathbf{B}_{K}]\mathbf{B}_{J}\mathbf{B}_{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subseteq\left[k-1\right];\\
A\in\mathbb{N}^{I,J}_{K}\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\left(k-1\right)-\left|J\right|}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{by Proposition \ref{prop.solmac}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathbb{N}^{I}_{K};\\
\Sigma A^{\star}>n-k\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{k-\operatorname{col}(A)}

(9)

(since
\operatorname{col}(A)=|J|+1
and
\sum A^{\star}=n-\max J
for any
A\in\mathbb{N}^{I,J}_{K}
).

Now, we shall establish a sign-reversing involution on the set
\mathbb{N}^{I}_{K}
that makes most addends on the right hand side of (
9
) cancel.
Namely, call a column of a matrix
A\in\mathbb{N}^{I}_{K}

splittable

if it is not the last column and has at least two nonzero entries.
A splittable column
c
can be split into two by creating a new column

c^{\prime}
immediately to its left and moving the top nonzero entry of
c

to
c^{\prime}
.
(All other cells of
c^{\prime}
are filled with
0
, and so is the cell of

c
from which an entry was moved.)
Conversely, two adjacent columns
c^{\prime}
and
c
of
A

(with
c^{\prime}
left of
c
) will be called
mergeable
if

c
is not the last column of
A
and the column
c^{\prime}
has
only one nonzero entry and this entry lies further up than all nonzero
entries of
c
. A mergeable pair of columns
c^{\prime},c
can be merged
into one simply by adding them together.
Splitting and merging are mutually inverse operations.
For example:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0&1\\
0&1&3&2\end{pmatrix}\overset{\text{split column }2}{\longrightarrow}\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0&0&1\\
0&0&1&3&2\end{pmatrix}\overset{\text{merge columns }2,3}{\longrightarrow}\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0&1\\
0&1&3&2\end{pmatrix}

(note that the last column is not splittable by definition).

Thus, all matrices
A\in\mathbb{N}^{I}_{K}
that have a splittable column
or a mergeable pair of columns cancel out from (
9
)
(just pick the leftmost splittable-or-mergeable column, and
merge or split it, as in
[
9
, proof of Theorem 2.2.2]
).
What remains are the matrices
A
whose
columns, all except for the last one, contain exactly one nonzero entry each,
and whose nonzero entries (apart from those in the last column)
are arranged from bottom to top as you move right in
A
. For example,

A=\begin{pmatrix}&&&&p\\
&&&w&q\\
&&&&r\\
&y&z&&s\\
x&&&&t\end{pmatrix}

is such a matrix (where empty cells mean zeroes, but the entries
q,s,t
can also be zeroes). We call such matrices
survivor matrices
. It is easy to see that such a survivor matrix
A\in\mathbb{N}^{I}_{K}
is
entirely determined
by the knowledge of which of its rows end with a
0
.
(Indeed, this data determines which entries of
\operatorname{comp}(K)
go into the last column of
A
and which go into the other columns. The latter entries must then be arranged in distinct columns from left to right.)
If the
i
-th row of the matrix does not end with a zero, then it ends precisely with the
i
-th element of
\overline{K|I}
(we remind the reader that
\overline{K|I}
is the sequence of the rightmost elements of each row of
A
). We can therefore encode this data as a nonempty subsequence
U
of
\overline{K|I}
, which consists of those entries of
\overline{K|I}
that are in the last column of
A
. Besides, there is exactly one column in
A
for each element of
\operatorname{comp}(K)
that is not in the last column and there are exactly
|\operatorname{comp}(K)|-|U|=|K|+1-|U|
such elements. Add the last column to get:

\operatorname{col}(A)=2+|K|-|U|.

(10)

Example 2
.

Let
\operatorname{comp}(K)=(1,2,3,1)
and
\operatorname{comp}(I)=(3,4)
so that
\overline{K|I}=(2,1)
. Then, the survivor matrices
A
are

A_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1&2\\
3&1&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad A_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&2&0\\
3&0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad A_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&2\\
3&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Their respective corresponding subsequences
U
are
U_{1}=(2)
,
U_{2}=(1)
and
U_{3}=(2,1)
.

In Equation (
9
) replace
\operatorname{col}(A)
by the formula of Equation (
10
) and reindex the summation over subsequences of
\overline{K|I}
to get Theorem
1
.
∎

2.2
Expansion of
\mathbf{V}_{n}^{(q)}\mathbf{B}_{I}
in the
\mathbf{B}
-basis

We use Theorem
1
to explicit the action of the
q
-deformed Dynkin operator on the
\mathbf{B}
-basis of the descent algebra
\Sigma_{n}
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
n
be a positive integer and
q\in\mathbf{k}
be an invertible parameter.
For
I,K\subseteq[n-1]
, the coefficient in
\mathbf{B}_{K}
of
\mathbf{V}^{(q)}_{n}\mathbf{B}_{I}
is
0
if
I\nsubseteq K
and otherwise given by

[\mathbf{B}_{K}]\mathbf{V}^{(q)}_{n}\mathbf{B}_{I}=(-1)^{|K|}q^{n-1}(1-q^{-1})^{|I|}\prod_{v\in\overline{K|I}}[v]_{q^{-1}}.

(11)

Here, for any integer
v\in\mathbb{N}
and any
r\in\mathbf{k}
, we define the
r
-integer
[v]_{r}
to be
r^{0}+r^{1}+\cdots+r^{v-1}
. (Note that
[v]_{q^{-1}}=q^{-v+1}[v]_{q}
.)

Proof.

We WLOG assume that
I\subseteq K
, since otherwise both sides are
0
by the second sentence after Proposition
1
.
Now, using (
7
) and Theorem
1
, we get

\displaystyle[\mathbf{B}_{K}]\mathbf{V}^{(q)}_{n}\mathbf{B}_{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}(-q)^{k-1}[\mathbf{B}_{K}]\mathbf{V}_{n,k}\mathbf{B}_{I}=\sum_{k}(-1)^{|K|+1}q^{k-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\subseteq\overline{K|I};\\
\Sigma U>n-k\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|U|}.

Switching the summation order between

\displaystyle[\mathbf{B}_{K}]\mathbf{V}^{(q)}_{n}\mathbf{B}_{I}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|K|+1}\sum_{U\subseteq\overline{K|I}}(-1)^{|U|}\sum_{k\geq n-\Sigma U+1}q^{k-1}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|K|+1}\sum_{U\subseteq\overline{K|I}}(-1)^{|U|}\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{\Sigma U-1}q^{n-k^{\prime}-1}\ \ \ \ \left(\text{reindex with }k^{\prime}=n-k\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|K|+1}q^{n-1}\sum_{U\subseteq\overline{K|I}}(-1)^{|U|}\frac{1-q^{-\Sigma U}}{1-q^{-1}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|K|}\frac{q^{n-1}}{1-q^{-1}}\sum_{U\subseteq\overline{K|I}}(-1)^{|U|}q^{-\Sigma U},

where the last line uses the fact that
\sum_{U\subseteq\overline{K|I}}(-1)^{|U|}=0
(since the sequence
\overline{K|I}
is nonempty). To end the proof, note that for any sequence

and any parameter

\prod_{s\in S}(1-x^{s})=\sum_{T\subseteq S}(-1)^{|T|}x^{\Sigma T},

where the summation is over all subsequences
T
of
S
. As a consequence,

\displaystyle[\mathbf{B}_{K}]\mathbf{V}^{(q)}_{n}\mathbf{B}_{I}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|K|}\frac{q^{n-1}}{1-q^{-1}}\prod_{v\in\overline{K|I}}(1-q^{-v})=(-1)^{|K|}q^{n-1}(1-q^{-1})^{|\overline{K|I}|-1}\prod_{v\in\overline{K|I}}{[v]}_{q^{-1}}.

Finally, notice that
|\overline{K|I}|=|I|+1
to recover Theorem
2
.
∎

∎

3.3
Image space dimension

We proceed with a corollary and some comments regarding the dimension of
\mathbf{V}_{n}^{(q)}\Sigma_{n}
.

Corollary 4
.

Let
n>0
. Let
\mathbf{k}
be a field. The dimension of the subspace
\mathbf{V}_{n}^{(q)}\Sigma_{n}
of the descent algebra
\Sigma_{n}
is
2^{n-1}
when
q
is not a root of unity.
If
q
is a primitive
p
-th root of unity with

\dim\left(\mathbf{V}_{n}^{(q)}\Sigma_{n}\right)=s_{n}^{(p)},

(13)

where
s_{n}^{(p)}
is the
n

p
defined by
s_{0}^{(p)}=0
and
s_{n}^{(p)}=2^{n-1}
for all
1\leq n<p
and the recurrence relation

s^{(p)}_{n}=s^{(p)}_{n-1}+s^{(p)}_{n-2}+\cdots+s^{(p)}_{n-p}\qquad\text{for all }n\geq p.

Proof.

If
q
is not a root of unity,
then all the eigenvalues

of
\mathbf{V}^{(q)}_{n}
are nonzero (see Corollary
3
),
and thus
\mathbf{V}^{(q)}_{n}
is invertible, so that its image is the whole
\Sigma_{n}
. Next, assume that
q
is a primitive
p
-th root of unity with
p>1
.
We let
\operatorname{Comp}_{n}^{(p)}
denote the set of the compositions of
n
that do not contain a multiple of
p
.
We have
|\operatorname{Comp}_{n}^{(p)}|=s_{n}^{(p)}
easily
1
1

1

If
p>n
, then no composition of
n
contains a multiple of
p
, and thus
|\operatorname{Comp}_{n}^{(p)}|=2^{n-1}
.
When
n\geq p
, we split compositions in
\operatorname{Comp}_{n}^{(p)}
according to their last entry
m
.
If
m<p
, we get a bijection with the compositions in
\operatorname{Comp}_{n-m}^{(p)}
by removing the last entry.
If
m>p
, replace
m
by

to get a composition in
\operatorname{Comp}_{n-p}^{(p)}
.
This last construction is also bijective, and the desired recurrence relation follows.
and it remains to show that
\dim\left(\mathbf{V}_{n}^{(q)}\Sigma_{n}\right)=|\operatorname{Comp}_{n}^{(p)}|
.
To prove this, we denote

and argue in two steps.

Proof of
\dim\left(\mathbf{V}_{n}^{(q)}\Sigma_{n}\right)\geq|\operatorname{Comp}_{n}^{(p)}|

Recall that the operator

Since

Proof of

The next step is to show that
\dim\left(\mathbf{V}_{n}^{(q)}\Sigma_{n}\right)\leq|\operatorname{Comp}_{n}^{(p)}|

Let
