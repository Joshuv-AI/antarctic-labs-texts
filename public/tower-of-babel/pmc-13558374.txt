Pain, quality of life, and functional outcomes of total hip arthroplasty in female patients with crowe type III–IV developmental dysplasia of hip: retrospective cohort study
Authors: Nizamettin Güzel, Ahmet Serhat Genç, Nevin Esra Gümüş, Orhan Taşkın, Aybars Güzel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe aim of this study was to investigate physical and psychosocial recovery including pain and quality of life measures, as well as lower limb functional out-comes in patients with Crowe type III–IV developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) who underwent total hip arthroplasty (THA) with subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy.Materials and methodsThis study was conducted with a total of 45 female patients aged 32–65 with Crowe type III–IV DDH. Pre-op 30-second chair-stand Test (30-s CST), 6-minute walk test (6MWT) and Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) were assessed clinically, whereas Harris Hip Score (HHS), EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5 L) scale, Forgotten Joint Score-12 (FJS-12), Hip disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (HOOS), and SF-36 Quality of Life Scale (SF-36) scores were measured and recorded based on patient self reports. At least two years after the surgery, the same measurements were repeated in the patients. ResultsAt the mid-term follow-up, a significant increase was observed in the 30-s CST and 6MWT distance compared with the pre-op period (p < 0.01). Furthermore, statistically significant differences were observed in the VAS, HHS, EQ-5D-5 L, FJS-12, HOOS and SF-36 scores (p < 0.01).ConclusionsAn evaluation after THA with subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy in female patients revealed a significant improvement in functional performance and clinical scores. Also, it is an effective factor in improving joint function and quality of life in cases of severe DDH. Prospective studies are needed to understand the potential long-term effects of the procedure.Keywords: Hip, Arthroplasty, Developmental dysplasia of hip, Pain, Quality of life

Introduction

Women living with congenital hip dislocation of varying degrees hold a special and sensitive place in the medical literature due to the challenges they face from birth onward [1]. Even though they are young, they often lead a life of chronic pain caused by severe limping, leg length discrepancy, and structural deformities [1, 2]. Furthermore, high loads and abnormal sliding forces in the dysplastic joint lead to impaired hip function and early-onset degenerative joint disease [3]. This chronic condition not only results in severe physical limitations but also imposes a heavy psychosocial burden, often underlying issues such as anxiety and depression that profoundly restrict patients’ social lives [1, 2, 4].

For adult patients with developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) who have developed advanced degenerative arthritis, total hip arthroplasty (THA) is definitively considered the treatment of choice and the current standard of care [5]. While historical or joint-preserving options such as arthroscopy, corrective pelvic osteotomies, or hip fusion have been described, THA remains the most reliable primary treatment method for restoring function, particularly in severe cases involving high dislocation (Crowe type III and IV) [5, 6]. However, the complexity of THA in this patient population is significantly increased due to concomitant acetabular dysplasia, severe soft tissue contractures, and the high dislocation of the femoral head [5, 7, 8]. Currently, the most widely accepted reconstructive approach to safely reestablish the center of rotation within the true acetabulum and correct lower extremity length discrepancies involves THA combined with a femoral subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy [9–11]. Because this complex surgical reconstruction addresses both the biomechanical deficits and the long-standing physical deformities, it is crucial to evaluate its success using a comprehensive approach that includes both objective clinical metrics and patient-reported psychological assessments.

The primary aim of this study is to comprehensively evaluate the mid-term clinical, functional, and psychosocial outcomes—specifically focusing on pain, physical performance, and quality of life—in female patients following THA combined with subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy for Crowe Type III-IV DDH. The hypothesis of this study is that there will be a statistically significant difference between the preoperative and mid-term postoperative clinical, functional, and quality of life scores.

Materials and methods

Participants

The study included 45 female patients aged 32–65 who were diagnosed with Crowe Type III–IV DDH and underwent primary THA (subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy) performed by the same surgeon between 2021 and 2023. The inclusion criteria included having undergone primary THA for a diagnosis of Crowe Type III–IV DDH, having no previous surgical history involving the ipsilateral hip joint, at least two years having elapsed since the surgery, being literate and possessing the cognitive ability to independently complete the scales, and signing the informed consent form. The exclusion criteria comprised pre-existing neurological, orthopedic, or cardiovascular conditions that would prevent the patient from undergoing functional performance tests, as well as the development of major postoperative complications during the follow-up period (such as periprosthetic joint infection, dislocation, or revision surgery). Pre-existing conditions thus referred to baseline contraindications, whereas postoperative exclusions accounted for structural failures. Patients with any previous surgical history involving the ipsilateral hip joint (such as pediatric pelvic osteotomies, previous soft tissue releases, or hardware in situ) were also excluded from the study. All included participants were strictly primary cases with no prior surgical interventions on the affected hip. The optimal number of subjects to participate in the study was determined using the GPower 3.1.3 program. It was determined that at least 34 patients needed to be included. A total of 45 patients were included in the study, ensuring sufficient sample size. (Fig. 1). Both preoperative and postoperative clinical and functional assessments were carried out as part of our standard clinical follow-up protocol for patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty. The data analysed in this study were obtained retrospectively from existing records. Consequently, the written informed consent obtained from all participants prior to surgery constituted standard consent for the routine academic use of the surgical procedure and anonymised medical data.

Study design

This study employed a retrospective cohort design that included data obtained from archival records and clinical evaluations conducted during the post-op period, incorporating repeated measurements (pre and post-op). As part of our clinic’s standard pre-op evaluation protocol for patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty, all pre-op measurements and questionnaires were routinely collected. All surgical procedures were performed exclusively at Samsun Training and Research Hospital between 2021 and 2023 by a single senior surgeon (NG). Following the primary surgeon’s institutional relocation in 2024, the final postoperative assessments for a subset of patients were conducted at Dokuz Eylül University during their routine follow-up visits. Crucially, because the exact same surgeon performed all surgical interventions and personally administered all preoperative and postoperative clinical assessments across both locations, potential inter-observer and inter-center variability was completely eliminated, ensuring strict methodological standardization. This study was approved by the local institutional ethics committee (Approval number: XXXX) and conducted adhering to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were excluded from the study if they voluntarily chose to discontinue participation, developed a new lower extremity injury that prevented completion of the test procedures during post-op assessments, or were found to have a major complication requiring revision surgery during the follow up period. This study used a comprehensive set of outcome measures to evaluate four different domains of patient recovery after THA: objective performance-based physical function (30-second chair-stand test (30-s CST), 6-minute walk test (6MWT)), hip-specific symptomatology and joint mechanics (Harris Hip Score (HHS), Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (HOOS), Visual Analogue Scale (VAS)), overall health-related quality of life (SF-36, EQ-5D-5 L), and patient-reported joint awareness (Forgotten Joint Score-12 (FJS-12)). All assessments were performed twice, once before and once after the surgery. Tests for all patients were conducted at the same time each day (2:00–4:00 p.m.) to minimize the confounding effects of diurnal symptom variations, such as morning stiffness, and to ensure optimal temporal standardization for the physical performance assessments.

Surgical technique

In this study, the cohort exclusively comprised patients with unilateral involvement. Patients who will undergo spinal anesthesia were prepared for surgery in the supine position after appropriate surgical site preparation and draping. All patients received 2 g of cefazolin intravenously approximately 30 min before surgery as part of surgical prophylaxis. The hip joint was exposed by making a lateral longitudinal incision through the skin and subcutaneous tissue. The M. tensor fascia lata was incised longitudinally and retracted using retractors The proximal portions of the M. vastus lateralis and M. gluteus medius were detached from their insertion at the greater trochanter using electrocautery to expose the joint capsule. Meticulous soft tissue release was then performed by mobilizing the joint capsule adhering to the femoral metaphysis, followed by the release of the iliopsoas muscle from its insertion site at the lesser trochanter. Soft tissue retraction was achieved by placing a winged, pointed Hohmann retractor in the anterosuperior aspect of the acetabulum. The capsule was excised, the femoral neck was osteotomized at an appropriate level, and the femoral head was removed. Following extensive capsule excision, the true acetabulum was identified, and exposure at the inferior acetabular margin was maintained using a blunt Hohmann retractor, allowing full visualization. The thick posterior wall of the acetabulum was expanded by removing a round piece of cortical bone approximately half a centimetre wide using a gouge. Carving has begun using the appropriate chisel. A cup of the appropriate size (Fx, Çekiçoğlu, Izmir, Turkiye) was press-fitted using a sufficient number of screws at an inclination angle of 40 to 45 degrees and an anteversion angle of 10 to 20 degrees to ensure stability. An angled insert liner has been fitted. The leg was then positioned in the Fig. 4 position, and rasping of the femur began. In preparation for the osteotomy, two drill holes were created in the same plane, one proximal and one distal to the planned osteotomy line, to ensure precise rotational alignment. Subsequently, a block resection of the femur was performed using a simple transverse osteotomy 1 cm below the lesser trochanter and further distally (at an appropriate length determined by measurements, 3–6 cm). After verifying that the appropriate trial rasp was securely wedged into the medulla of the femoral shaft distal to the osteotomy line, an appropriately sized type II femoral stem according to the Mont group classification [12] (Fx, Çekiçoğlu, Izmir, Turkiye) was inserted. With the lower extremity in a figure-of-four position, the stem was impacted with controlled mallet strikes until a 90-degree angle was achieved between the tibia and the neck of the femoral component, while strictly maintaining the pre-drilled holes in the same plane to preserve rotational alignment. This controlled impaction technique aimed to ensure a secure distal wedge fit and stable fixation of the femoral stem. The system was reduced by placing a suitable sized cap on it. To mitigate the risk of fracture, one prophylactic cerclage cable (Fx, Çekiçoğlu, Izmir, Turkiye) was applied to both the proximal and distal ends of the osteotomy. The osteotomy line was definitively stabilized using an LC-DCP plate and 3.5 mm screws (Fx, Çekiçoğlu, Izmir, Turkiye). Since the patient was maintained in the supine position throughout the operation, intraoperative leg length equalization was easily assessed and verified by comparing the heel levels. The prosthesis was observed to be stable in all leg positions. Autografts obtained from the acetabular reaming process were routinely applied locally to the osteotomy site. One Hemovac drain was placed, and the anatomical structures were repaired appropriately. In all cases, a cementless femoral stem with a round section and a dual layer osteoconductive coating (titanium plasma spray and hydroxyapatite [Ti + HA]) designed to stimulate bone ingrowth (F3; Fx Ortopedi, Izmir, Turkiye) was used to maximize proximal femoral fixation and achieve primary stability (Fig. 2). Allprocedures were performed under standardized spinalanesthesia protocol, combined with multimodal postoperative analgesia. Two ampoules of Transamin were administered intra articularly via the Hemovac drain, which was then locked. The lock was released 4–6 h later. The aim was to reduce bleeding. 24 h after the operation, the drain was removed and the patient was mobilised with the aid of a walking frame. The drain was removed 24 h after surgery, and the patient was mobilized with the aid of a walking cane. Following the initial mobilization performed 24 to 26 h postoperatively, full weight-bearing was permitted immediately, to the extent the patient could tolerate it. Concurrently, a standard postoperative physical therapy program was initiated. This early rehabilitation protocol focused primarily on isometric muscle strengthening and active range-of-motion exercises for the lower extremities to support functional recovery (Fig. 3).

Procedures

30-Second Chair-Stand Test (30-s CST): This test, used to assess lower extremity muscle strength and endurance, involved asking patients to stand up and sit down as many times as possible from a standard chair within 30 s [13].

6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT): The 6MWT was measured by recording the total distance (in meters) that the patient could walk continuously at a comfortable pace for six minutes [14].

Visual Analogue Scale (VAS): It was used to measure the intensity of pain during daily activities. Patients were asked to rate their pain level during the activity on a scale ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain) [15].

EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5 L) Scale: General health-related quality of life was evaluated using this questionnaire, which assessed five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension was rated across five levels of severity, and the results were converted into a single index value to reflect the patient’s overall health status [16].

Harris Hip Score (HHS): The HHS was used to assess hip joint function according to pain, function, deformity, and range of motion [16].

Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (HOOS): This questionnaire was used to evaluate patient-reported symptoms, stiffness, pain, and physical function specific to the hip joint [17].

SF-36 Quality of Life Scale (SF-36): This scale, used as the gold standard in clinical trials, was utilized to measure health-related quality of life [18, 19]. The scale assessed eight sub-dimensions (physical functioning, physical and emotional role difficulties, pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, and mental health), which constituted the primary domains of physical and mental health.

Forgotten Joint Score (FJS-12): Patient-reported psychosocial joint awareness was measured using the FJS-12 to evaluate the patients’ ability to “forget” their artificial joint during activities of daily living [20].

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM SPSS Corp., Armonk, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as the mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values. The Shapiro Wilk test was performed to determine whether the data followed a normal distribution. A paired sample t-test was used for pairwise comparisons. All of our statistical tests were twotailed, and the threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The main findings of the study were that the scores on the 30-s CST and 6MWT, as well as the HHS, were higher in the post-op period compared to the pre op period. In assessments of quality of life and joint awareness, the EQ-5D-5 L and FJS-12, along with all sub-parameters of the HOOS and SF-36 scales, yielded the highest results at the short-term follow-up. The VAS score, which measures pain intensity, was significantly lower at the short-term follow-up (mean 2.52 ± 0.49 years), indicating that pain had decreased substantially after surgery. The study found that positive results were observed in all pre-op and post-op parameters across all clinical, functional, and quality of life tests evaluated. While the general clinical success of THA in this patient population is well-documented, the primary novelty of the present study lies in its multidimensional methodology. Relatively few studies have performed such a comprehensive assessment that combines objective performance metrics with in-depth psychosocial outcomes specifically in patients with severe dysplasia. In particular, the psychosocial recovery demonstrated in our analysis—highlighted by the patients’ ability to “forget” their artificial joint and achieve high mental well-being scores—represents the most distinctive contribution of this work, proving that the benefits of this complex reconstruction extend far beyond mere mechanical restoration. Furthermore, another notable methodological choice in this study was the exclusive inclusion of female patients. DDH exhibits a significantly higher prevalence in females, who also more frequently present with advanced degenerative changes (Crowe III-IV) requiring complex THA [1, 2]. By restricting the cohort strictly to female patients, we aimed to eliminate gender-based confounding factors—such as inherent differences in pelvic anatomy, baseline muscle mass, and gait biomechanics—that could influence physical performance tests and pain perception [3, 21]. This gender-specific homogenization significantly increases the internal validity and reliability of our functional outcome measurements.

In the specific context of Crowe Type III–IV developmental dysplasia requiring subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy, the anatomical and biomechanical complexities vastly differ from standard osteoarthritis cases [22]. Patients who grow up with visible deformities such as limping and leg length discrepancy from childhood onward, impose a heavy psychosocial burden that profoundly affects their social and emotional lives [23]. In our study, objective functional recovery was assessed using the 30-s CST and the 6MWT. Both tests are highly reliable and sensitive tools for evaluating lower extremity functional capacity, walking endurance, and dynamic performance in THA patients [24–26]. Our results demonstrated a significant positive increase in these parameters at the short-term follow-up. While studies evaluating severe DDH patients with these specific dynamic tests are limited, recent literature consistently reports significant improvements in walking distance and functional independence following THA [27, 28]. Although some studies note that gait biomechanics and metabolic energy expenditure may not immediately return to completely normal levels [21], our findings indicate that achieving leg length equality through subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy successfully restores pelvic-femoral alignment and prevents metabolic energy waste, rendering the procedure highly clinically significant.

Separate from the objective performance metrics, clinical joint function and symptomatology were evaluated using the Harris Hip Score (HHS) and Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). THA in Crowe IV DDH involves unique biomechanical challenges, including severe limb length discrepancy, abductor compromise, and traction-related neurological risks. Meticulous restoration of the true hip center and stable osteotomy healing are crucial for mitigating these challenges [29]. Our findings of significantly improved HHS and decreased pain scores align strongly with recent literature reporting that successful transverse shortening osteotomy in severe DDH effectively resolves leg length discrepancy and permanently eliminates pain [9, 14, 30, 31]. The profound reduction in pain observed in our cohort clearly demonstrates the success and durability of the anatomic reconstruction, which in turn drives the improvement in overall hip scores and walking ability. Furthermore, multimodal analgesia approaches and optimized perioperative pain management likely contributed to this clinical improvement by facilitating early mobilization [32].

When evaluating the psychosocial implications of improvements in physical function, Engesæter et al. (2011) demonstrated that the pre-op EQ-5D quality of life scores of 540 young patients who underwent THA due to DDH were severely low due to severe pain and deformity, and that successful surgery eliminated this psychosocial distress and restored quality of life [33]. Li et al. (2024) followed 44 patients (51 hips) who underwent THA for an average of 4.4 years and reported that patients improved both physically and psychosocially, with the HHS score rising from 43.8 to 85.7 and the EQ-5D-5 L index increasing from 0.38 to 0.87 during the post-op period [16]. Focusing on the durability of this improvement, Roidis et al. (2013) reported in their EQ-5D study, which followed 82 female patients (115 hips) who underwent THA due to DDH for a minimum of 12 years, that the improvement in quality of life achieved following surgery was sustained in the long-term [1]. In a 17 year follow-up study of patients with developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH), Håberg et al. (2023) used the EQ-5D-5 L and EQ-VAS scales to demonstrate that successful surgery provides patients with a high and sustained quality of life, based on pre-op and post-op outcomes [34]. Finally, Hüsken et al. (2024), who analyzed Dutch national registries, reported that successful fixation with osteotomy, based on the clinical outcomes of 7,465 patients who underwent THA, resulted in significant improvements in patients’ clinical scores and quality of life indices [35]. The results of our study demonstrated that subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy and anatomical reconstruction performed in cases of DDH not only correct pelvic biomechanics but also relieve patients of the chronic pain they experienced prior to surgery, thereby significantly improving their overall quality of life. In our cohort, the amount of limb lengthening ranged from 3 to 5 cm. Importantly, despite some cases exceeding the traditional 40 mm safety threshold, no postoperative neurological complications were observed.

Zora et al. (2025) examined the clinical and functional outcomes of 40 patients who underwent THA for Crowe Type III and IV DDH and demonstrated that successful surgery resulted in high scores on the FJS-12 survey [36]. Clark et al. (2026) examined clinical outcomes in a study involving 190 patients, including those who had undergone THA and those with primary osteoarthritis, and found that FJS-12 reached high levels, noting that the surgery provided such a high level of comfort that patients in this group could forget they had artificial joints, while also highlighting that the HOOS had significantly improved and that the surgery had permanently eliminated pain [37]. The data obtained from our study also showed that the surgical technique we applied to patients allowed them to forget about their artificial joints and adapt to their daily lives. Swarup et al. (2016) examined the long-term outcomes (average 13 years) of 61 young patients (75 hips) who underwent THA due to of the DDH and demonstrated that successful surgical reconstruction resulted in sustained improvement across all sub-dimensions of the HOOS score, with patients adapting to social life with full independence [38]. Wang et al., (2017) examined the 10-year long-term outcomes of 49 patients (56 hips) who underwent THA and concomitant subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy for Crowe type III and IV DDH, and reported that successful anatomical reconstruction resolved leg length discrepancy and resulted in a significant and lasting improvement in patients’ HOOS [39]. In their study, which prospectively examined 40 patients who underwent THA, Tellini et al. (2008) assessed pre- and post-operative quality of life using the SF-36 questionnaire and reported a significant improvement in patients’ scores for physical functioning, pain, and general health [40]. Studies have shown that successful surgery for deformities present since childhood not only provides mechanical correction but also significantly improves the patient’s overall quality of life and psychosocial well-being. When evaluating health-related quality of life via the SF-36, contextualizing outcomes with normative data provides clinical insight. Preoperatively, our patients exhibited mental and physical scores severely depressed below population norms. At the mid-term follow-up, the significant elevation across SF-36 subdimensions demonstrates that successful anatomical restoration enables patients to recover robustly, bringing their quality-of-life parameters close to general population norms. Although the clinical efficacy of total hip arthroplasty (THA) in developmental hip dysplasia is well established in the literature, this study contributes to the literature by evaluating both objective performance measures and patient-reported joint awareness in a standardised cohort comprising exclusively women. The findings indicate that THA performed with a subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy supports functional mobility, psychosocial well-being, and remarkably high patient satisfaction during the short-term follow-up. This study provides valuable clinical data that may assist orthopedic surgeons in the treatment of female patients with severe congenital hip dysplasia (DDH).

This study has some limitations. The first limitation is that the study has a retrospective design and lacks a control group, making it impossible to directly compare the surgical technique with other methods. Another limitation is the limited number of patients and the fact that only women were included restrict the generalisability of the results to men. Third, while the follow-up period reflects short-term results, mid-term and long-term (20 + year) implant survival has not yet been assessed. However, this timeframe is entirely sufficient for evaluating osteotomy-site healing, as solid radiographic union was successfully achieved in all patients without any non-union complications. Finally, another limitation is that the quality of life questionnaires used in the assessment are based on patients’ subjective perceptions. Furthermore, excluding patients with major complications introduces potential selection bias, which may lead to an overestimation of the overall clinical outcomes. These factors are external variables that may influence functional scores and joint awareness variability. Therefore, studies conducted with large sample sizes and long-term follow-up will enhance the generalizability of the results. Also, using prospective designs and selecting participants with a control group will allow for more specific interpretations of surgical outcomes. Furthermore, taking into account objective laboratory data such as biomechanical gait analysis, alongside subjective surveys will help demonstrate the relationship between the applied anatomical restoration and patient performance in a more reliable and valid manner.

Conclusions

In our study, while a significant increase was observed in both the 30-s CST and 6MWT parameters at the short-term follow-up, there was a marked decrease in severe pain scores compared to the preoperative period. In quality of life assessments (EQ-5D-5 L and SF-36), short-term evaluations yielded better health and function scores compared to preoperative evaluations. The results of the FJS-12 and HOOS demonstrated that subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy has a significant and positive effect on daily living comfort and joint awareness. In conclusion, the study demonstrated that the surgery resulted in significant improvements in clinical scores and lower extremity strength at the short-term follow-up, and that its beneficial impact on psychosocial status and overall quality of life was also substantial.
