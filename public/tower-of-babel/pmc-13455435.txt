Suboccipital transhorizontal fissure approach for a deep-seated pediatric cerebellar tumor: illustrative case

ABSTRACT

Surgical approaches to the deep cerebellar hemisphere, the medial aspect of the cerebellopontine angle, and the upper fourth ventricle are technically demanding because these regions are deeply situated and surrounded by critical neurovascular structures. For deep cerebellar lesions, the transcortical approach has classically been used. However, this route requires traversing the normal cerebellar cortex and white matter before reaching the lesion, which may cause postoperative functional impairment, including ataxia and higher-order cerebellar dysfunction.

Pilocytic astrocytoma is a common posterior fossa tumor in pediatric patients. However, depending on the location of the tumor, conventional approaches may cause unnecessary injury to the cerebellar parenchyma. We previously introduced the SOTHF approach as a novel technique utilizing the cerebellar horizontal fissure.3 This technique creates medial, intermediate, and lateral surgical corridors, enabling access to the upper fourth ventricle, deep cerebellar hemisphere, and medial aspect of the cerebellopontine angle. Among these, the intermediate SOTHF approach allows broad access to the deep cerebellar hemisphere near the dentate nucleus by dissecting the horizontal fissure while avoiding substantial removal of the cerebellar cortex and white matter.

In supratentorial surgery, the transsylvian fissure approach is widely used because of its versatility. In our view, the anatomical relationship among the superior cerebellar lobule, inferior cerebellar lobule, and dentate nucleus in the SOTHF approach can be conceptualized analogously to the relationship among the frontal lobe, temporal lobe, and basal ganglia in the transsylvian approach. In the present case, we selected the SOTHF approach for a tumor located deep within the cerebellar hemisphere. By separating the horizontal fissure between the superior and inferior cerebellar lobules, we successfully reached the lesion while minimizing injury to the cerebellar cortex and white matter, thereby achieving a minimally invasive route with favorable functional preservation.

To our knowledge, this is the first report describing the use of the intermediate SOTHF approach for diagnostic sampling of a deep cerebellar lesion in a pediatric patient. This case illustrates that the horizontal fissure can serve as a natural cerebellum-sparing corridor in children, allowing access to the deep cerebellar hemisphere near the fourth ventricle and dentate nucleus while preserving the option of additional resection depending on the intraoperative pathological diagnosis.

Illustrative Case

An 11-year-old boy presented with vertigo. His medical history included asthma and sinusitis. Approximately 1 year before referral, he had developed rotational vertigo. Six months before referral, he was diagnosed with Ménière‘s disease and orthostatic dysregulation and was prescribed midodrine. Subsequently, during an evaluation at a previous hospital for headache and fever, MRI revealed findings suspicious for a tumor in the cerebellar vermis, and he was referred to our institution.

On initial examination, the patient was alert with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15. Neurological examination revealed no obvious cranial nerve palsy or apparent cerebellar ataxia.

MRI demonstrated FLAIR hyperintensity in the superior cerebellar vermis. Within this FLAIR-hyperintense area, an approximately 7-mm enhancing lesion was identified in the cerebellar hemisphere near the fourth ventricle (Fig. 1A and B). The enhancing lesion was located approximately 25 mm beneath the cerebellar cortical surface. Based on the patient’s age and tumor location, pilocytic astrocytoma was considered the leading differential diagnosis. Given the patient’s pediatric age and the small but deep location of the lesion, we planned to obtain diagnostic tissue from the enhancing component while minimizing injury to the surrounding cerebellar parenchyma.

Infratentorial stereotactic biopsy was considered as an alternative diagnostic strategy. However, the enhancing lesion was small and located near the fourth ventricle, brainstem, and dentate nucleus region; therefore, a stereotactic trajectory would have required passage through normal cerebellar cortex and white matter. In addition, although pilocytic astrocytoma was considered the leading preoperative differential diagnosis, we planned to modify the surgical strategy according to the intraoperative frozen-section diagnosis. If ­frozen-section analysis had suggested high-grade glioma, additional resection of the enhancing component and, when safely feasible, the surrounding FLAIR-hyperintense area would have been considered through the same corridor, consistent with the concept of FLAIR-guided supramarginal resection for high-grade glioma.1 Therefore, open biopsy through the intermediate SOTHF corridor was selected to allow direct visualization of the fissural anatomy and target region while preserving the option of additional resection if indicated.

Operation

The tumor was located deep within the cerebellar hemisphere and was adjacent to the fourth ventricle and brainstem. Preoperative imaging suggested that the tumor could be reached through the horizontal fissure with minimal transgression of the cerebellar parenchyma (Fig. 1C). Therefore, we selected the intermediate SOTHF approach.

The patient was placed prone. His upper body was elevated by approximately 20°, his head was maintained in the midline, and the vertex was positioned as downward as possible. To perform a midline suboccipital craniotomy, an 11-cm skin incision was made from 3 cm above the inion to the C2 level. The left occipital bone was widely exposed. The exposure measured approximately 4.5 cm on the left side and 1.5 cm on the right side, and a suboccipital craniotomy was performed below the transverse sinus. The dura was opened in a U-shaped fashion based on the midline.

Immediately after the dura was opened, the horizontal fissure of the left cerebellar hemisphere was identified (Fig. 2A). The arachnoid membrane over the horizontal fissure was incised, and the fissural veins were mobilized superiorly to preserve venous drainage while opening the inferior aspect of the horizontal fissure as the working corridor. This maneuver helped maintain a direct trajectory toward the enhancing lesion and reduced the need for venous sacrifice or excessive traction on fissural veins. Approximately 1.5 cm of the horizontal fissure was dissected to establish the surgical corridor (Fig. 2B). After approximately 1.5 cm of deep dissection, the region around the dentate nucleus was reached. Neuronavigation confirmed that the operative trajectory had reached the area surrounding the enhancing lesion.

Following minimal aspiration of the deep white matter, grayish tissue suspicious for a tumor was immediately encountered (Fig. 2C). Diagnostic tissue was obtained from a region clearly distinct from the surrounding normal tissue. Frozen-section analysis did not show high-grade features. Therefore, after adequate diagnostic sampling and limited internal debulking of the abnormal grayish tissue, the surrounding normal tissue layer was identified (Fig. 2D), and the procedure was completed to avoid unnecessary injury to the surrounding cerebellar parenchyma and cerebellar output pathways (Fig. 2E). The key operative steps, including dissection of the horizontal fissure and tumor exposure through the intermediate SOTHF corridor, are demonstrated in Video 1. The operative time was 6 hours 57 minutes.

Postoperative Course

Immediate postoperative and day 1 CT scans showed no hemorrhage or other acute complications, and mobilization and gait training were initiated. MRI performed on postoperative day 2 confirmed that the enhancing component had been adequately sampled, with no evident tract of cerebellar cortical injury (Fig. 3A and B). Immediately after surgery, the patient experienced mild rotational vertigo and nausea, but these symptoms rapidly improved. His postoperative course was uneventful, and he was discharged home on postoperative day 12 with a modified Rankin Scale score of 0. The 12-day hospitalization was not due to a major surgical complication. Rather, it reflected our institutional practice for postoperative observation after pediatric posterior fossa surgery, including confirmation of stable imaging, serial neurological assessment, gait and balance evaluation, and family preparation for safe discharge and home care. Gait training consisted of early mobilization under the supervision of a physical therapist, including sitting, standing, balance assessment, and supervised ambulation to confirm safe independent walking before discharge.

B:Sagittal gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted images obtained before and after surgery, demonstrating no evident injury to the cerebellar cortical surface.

At the 6-month follow-up, the patient remained neurologically intact, with no recurrent vertigo, cerebellar ataxia, or cranial nerve deficits. Follow-up MRI demonstrated no new or progressive lesion, and the patient continues to be followed with serial imaging.

Histopathological examination demonstrated glial cell proliferation with mild atypia, mainly around areas of mucinous accumulation, suggesting a glial lesion. Immunohistochemical analysis showed MAP2 positivity and IDH1 R132H negativity. Based on the histological and radiological findings, pilocytic astrocytoma was considered the most likely diagnosis.

Discussion

Observations

In this case, the SOTHF approach enabled access to a suspected pediatric pilocytic astrocytoma located in the deep cerebellar hemisphere near the fourth ventricle while minimizing cerebellar cortical incision and vermian injury. Surgery for pediatric posterior fossa tumors requires balancing tumor control with the preservation of neurological function. This balance is especially critical for lesions located in the deep cerebellum, upper fourth ventricle, and dorsal brainstem, where the choice of surgical route can substantially influence postoperative outcomes.2–4

For small lesions located deep within the cerebellar hemisphere, a transcortical or transcerebellar route from the superficial cerebellar cortex may be considered. However, this route requires passage through the normal cerebellar cortex and white matter. When the lesion is close to deep cerebellar structures such as the dentate nucleus or superior cerebellar peduncle, functional injury caused by the approach itself becomes a major concern. Indeed, a previous study reported that a paravermal transcerebellar approach for posterior fossa tumors was associated with a higher frequency of hypertrophic olivary degeneration due to dentate nucleus injury compared with other approaches.5 Furthermore, long-term functional assessment after pediatric posterior fossa tumor surgery has shown that injury to the superior cerebellar peduncle and deep cerebellar nuclei is associated with motor and cognitive dysfunction.6

Anatomically, the dentate nucleus is a critical structure when selecting an approach to deep cerebellar lesions. The dentate nucleus is located only approximately 5 mm lateral to the vermis and is close to the superior and middle cerebellar peduncles. It has also been reported that the dentate nucleus is at high risk of injury during transvermian and supratonsillar approaches, whereas the risk is relatively lower with telovelar and subtonsillar approaches.7 Therefore, for a small lesion located in the deep cerebellar hemisphere near the fourth ventricle, as in the present case, it is important to select an approach that minimizes injury to the normal cerebellar cortex and white matter to preserve the dentate nucleus and its associated fiber pathways.

Approaches using dissection of the horizontal fissure were originally applied to cerebellopontine angle lesions, including microvascular decompression via the suprafloccular transhorizontal fissure approach, and to lesions of the middle cerebellar peduncle.8,9 These approaches use the lateral aspect of the horizontal fissure. In contrast, the theoretical advantage of our SOTHF approach is that it uses the horizontal fissure more broadly from the suboccipital surface, allowing access not only to the medial cerebellopontine angle but also to the deep cerebellar hemisphere and upper fourth ventricle while minimizing incision of the cerebellar cortex and white matter.2 In fact, we previously reported the successful application of the SOTHF approach for a deep cerebellar hemispheric metastatic brain tumor, deep cerebellar hemispheric hemorrhage, and intraventricular hematoma.3

Quantitative clinical data regarding the SOTHF approach remain limited, particularly in pediatric patients. However, previous reports of horizontal fissure–based approaches have suggested their potential safety and usefulness. Ohue et al. reported on 10 patients with brainstem cavernous malformations treated via a retrosigmoid suprafloccular transhorizontal fissure approach.10 Complete resection was achieved in 9 patients, with no mortality; early postoperative neurological deficits occurred in 4 patients but were transient in 3. At final follow-up, neurological status was improved in 4 patients, unchanged in 5, and worsened in 1. Kuzucu et al. reported a clinical series of the suprafloccular transhorizontal fissure approach to the cerebellopontine angle.8 Among 33 attempted cases, the approach was successfully performed in 26, whereas it could not be completed in 7 because of adhesions. In the successful cases, cerebellar retraction was not required and no complications were reported. Although these reports involved mainly adult patients and different target lesions, they support the concept that horizontal fissure–based corridors can reduce the need for cerebellar retraction and may provide safe access when the fissural anatomy is favorable.

Importantly, however, these previously reported approaches primarily used lateral suprafloccular or cerebellopontine angle corridors, whereas the present intermediate SOTHF corridor was directed more medially toward a deep cerebellar hemispheric target near the fourth ventricle and dentate nucleus region.

Unlike these series in adults, the present case involved a pediatric deep cerebellar lesion, and the surgical goal was diagnostic sampling rather than planned complete resection. The operative time in the present case was 6 hours 57 minutes. Because this is a single illustrative case, we cannot directly compare the operative time with that of a transcerebellar approach. The SOTHF approach may initially require additional time for careful fissural dissection and preservation of fissural veins. However, because the horizontal fissure is a visually identifiable anatomical fissure, increasing familiarity with the approach may allow efficient fissure opening, analogous in principle to splitting the sylvian fissure in supratentorial surgery. Nevertheless, the primary goal in this pediatric case was not to shorten the operative time, but to obtain diagnostic tissue while minimizing injury to the normal cerebellar cortex and white matter and preserving the option of additional resection if frozen-section analysis suggested high-grade glioma.

In the present case, postoperative MRI confirmed adequate sampling of the enhancing target lesion without apparent injury to the cerebellar cortex or white matter and without a new cerebellar parenchymal defect. In a small lesion located deep within the cerebellar hemisphere, the volume of the lesion itself is small; therefore, the relative impact of normal tissue injury caused by the surgical route can be disproportionately large. Avoiding cerebellar cortical incision and vermian splitting through the SOTHF approach was therefore crucial for obtaining diagnostic tissue with minimal functional invasiveness. Recently, another group also reported the resection of a cavernous malformation located in the deep medial cerebellar hemisphere using the SOTHF approach.11

Careful attention to the venous anatomy around the horizontal fissure is essential when performing the SOTHF approach. We previously evaluated the venous anatomy around the horizontal fissure using imaging and cadaveric studies and reported that veins running vertically or horizontally across the horizontal fissure are frequently present and may obstruct fissure opening.12 In particular, veins crossing the horizontal fissure were observed in 82% of hemispheres, and characteristic veins running along the horizontal fissure were observed in 24%. Therefore, for safe execution of the SOTHF approach, preoperative assessment of the venous anatomy using contrast-enhanced MRI or CT venography is important. Intraoperatively, the vein of the great horizontal fissure and bridging veins should be preserved whenever possible, and any venous branch sacrifice should be limited to the minimum necessary. In the present case, the veins within the fissure were mobilized superiorly as much as possible, and only the necessary small branches were managed. This strategy likely contributed to the avoidance of postoperative cerebellar edema and venous complications.

Infratentorial stereotactic biopsy is an established diagnostic option for deep posterior fossa lesions. Furtak et al. analyzed 190 infratentorial stereotactic biopsies of brainstem and cerebellar lesions and reported a nondiagnostic rate of 4.7%, minor complications in 4%, and major complications in 1.5%, including 1 death due to intracranial bleeding.13 In pediatric brainstem tumors, Hamisch et al. reported a diagnostic success rate of 96.1%, overall morbidity of 6.7%, permanent morbidity of 0.6%, and mortality of 0.6% in a meta-analysis of 735 cases.14 These data indicate that stereotactic biopsy is generally safe and diagnostically effective, but it still carries measurable risks. In the present case, the enhancing lesion was small and located near the fourth ventricle, brainstem, and dentate nucleus region. In addition, we aimed to preserve the option of additional resection if frozen-section analysis suggested high-grade glioma. Therefore, we selected open biopsy through the intermediate SOTHF corridor to obtain diagnostic tissue under direct visualization while minimizing transgression of the normal cerebellar cortex and white matter.

Pediatric-specific considerations are particularly important when selecting a surgical corridor for deep cerebellar lesions. Compared with adults, children have a longer expected survival period and may be more vulnerable to the long-term functional consequences of injury to cerebellar output pathways. Injury to the dentate nucleus region, superior cerebellar peduncle, and cerebellar white matter has been associated with postoperative motor, cognitive, and affective dysfunction in pediatric posterior fossa tumor surgery. Therefore, minimizing transgression of the cerebellar cortex, white matter, and cerebellar output pathways may be especially important in children. In the present case, these considerations supported the selection of a fissure-based SOTHF corridor and, after frozen-section analysis did not show high-grade features, a diagnostic sampling strategy rather than aggressive resection. Cerebellar mutism syndrome (CMS) is a serious complication associated with speech disturbance, emotional instability, motor impairment, and cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome. CMS often requires prolonged recovery and may leave long-term residual deficits. Injury to the proximal cerebellar efferent pathway, including the dentate nucleus, superior cerebellar peduncle, and its decussation, has been suggested as a key mechanism.15 In addition, because Purkinje cells are located in the cerebellar cortex and represent the major output system from the cerebellar cortex, avoiding direct injury to the cerebellar cortex has a strong theoretical rationale.

Lessons

In this case, the intermediate SOTHF approach allowed safe diagnostic sampling of a pediatric deep cerebellar hemispheric lesion while avoiding cerebellar cortical and vermian incision. This fissure-based corridor preserved the option of additional resection depending on the intraoperative pathological diagnosis while minimizing injury to the normal cerebellar parenchyma. The SOTHF approach may therefore be a useful, function-preserving surgical option for selected pediatric deep cerebellar lesions, especially those located near the dentate nucleus, upper fourth ventricle, or medial cerebellopontine angle.
