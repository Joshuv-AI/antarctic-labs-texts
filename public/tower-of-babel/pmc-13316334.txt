Interhospital variation in Medicare spending for infections after coronary artery bypass grafting and surgical aortic valve replacement
Authors: J'undra N Pegues, Matthew Kazaleh, Ahmad M Hider, Chiang-Hua Chang, Reem M Fawaz, Kinka M Kimfon, Raed Alnajjar, Shiwei Zhou, Robert B Hawkins, Alphonse DeLucia, Charles F Schwartz, Michael P Thompson, Thomas M Braun, Geoffrey D Barnes, Francis D Pagani, Donald S Likosky, Michigan Society of Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgeons Quality Collaborative and the Michigan Value Collaborative
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfections following cardiac surgery are common and associated with increased complications and spending. While 1 in 5 beneficiaries experience a postoperative infection within 180 days, rates vary by 40% in absolute terms across hospitals. Efforts to reduce healthcare expenditures would benefit from associating interhospital variability in infection rates with Medicare spending.MethodsA total of 6439 coronary artery bypass grafting and/or surgical aortic valve replacement procedures between 2017 and 2022 across 33 hospitals in Michigan were evaluated using a clinical and administrative dataset. The primary outcome was overall and component inflation-adjusted spending within 180 days after surgery. Spending was compared across risk-adjusted terciles of hospital infection rates and phase of care.ResultsThe mean age (SD) of the cohort was 71.2 (7.7) years, with 29.1% female patients and 4.8% black patients. The mean adjusted infection rates across the low versus high terciles were 13.2% (2.3%) and 26.7% (6.5%) (P < .0001). Beneficiaries at low versus high infection tercile hospitals had more chronic lung disease, were more likely to undergo combined procedures and experienced longer cardiopulmonary bypass times. Overall mean (SD) spending was $72,956 ($39,875). Spending was 6.0% ($4306) lower on average at low infection rate versus high infection rate hospitals. The postindex hospitalization phase of care accounted for 19.3% of total 180-day spending but varied across hospital terciles (low, 15.9%; high, 24.5%). Infections increased 180-day spending on average by $37,207 ($102,351 vs $65,144).ConclusionsSpending following cardiac surgery varies across hospital risk-adjusted infection rate terciles. Infection prevention interventions could enhance cardiac surgical value.Key Words: infection, spending, Medicare, cardiac surgery

180-day spending varies across hospitals, with the greatest variation following discharge.

Central Message

Postoperative spending following cardiac surgery varies, with higher risk-adjusted infection rates associated with increased costs. Infection prevention could enhance cardiac surgical value.

Perspective

Hospitals with lower versus higher adjusted infection rates had on average 6.0% ($4306) lower Medicare spending over the 180 days after cardiac surgery. Hospital variability in overall spending was greatest following discharge. Infections contributed an additional $37,207 in average spending. Efforts to reduce postoperative infections could enhance the value of cardiac surgical care.

Infections following cardiac surgery are common and strongly associated with increased major morbidity and mortality.1, 2, 3 One in 5 Medicare beneficiaries experiences an infection postoperatively,4 with pneumonia increasing 90-day episode spending by approximately 25% ($9000-$13,000 on average).5 Furthermore, recent work has documented wide-scale interhospital variability in infections occurring within 180 days of coronary and/or valve operations (range, 7.2%-46.7%).4

Several initiatives have been undertaken to address interhospital variability in clinical outcomes following cardiac surgery, including infection rates.6,7 Increased attention has focused on healthcare spending, given that national healthcare spending accounted for 17.6% of the gross domestic product in 2023.8 While traditional bundled payments focus on the 30-day and 90-day periods, cardiac surgery patients are at increased risk of developing infection through the 180-day postoperative period,4 including patients not routinely tracked by national registries (eg, urinary tract infections, gastrointestinal infections).9 Brescia and colleagues10 leveraged Medicare claims data that were merged with clinically informed institutional registry data to assess interhospital variability in 90-day episode value (ie, complication rates/expenditures) after cardiac surgery. The low-value (ie, high spending and high complication rates) hospitals had approximately 10% ($5983) higher mean per beneficiary total episode expenditures compared to high-value (ie, low spending and low complication rates) hospitals. Opportunities to inform policy and quality improvement interventions in the setting of cardiac surgery would benefit from a more thorough assessment of infection-related spending over a 180-day episode period.

In the present study, we leveraged statewide institutional Society of Thoracic Surgery (STS) Adult Cardiac Surgery Database (ACSD) data and Medicare fee-for-service claims from the Michigan Value Collaborative (MVC) to evaluate whether hospital-level infection rates were associated with variation in Medicare spending across Michigan hospitals within 180 days after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and/or surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR). The goal of the study was to evaluate hospital-level variation in postoperative infections and associated spending within 180 days of surgery to inform benchmarking and value-based quality improvement. Recognizing that not all infections are directly attributable to the procedure, we found that prior studies have demonstrated substantial interhospital variability, likely driven by modifiable differences in longitudinal care management beyond the traditional 30-day STS reporting window.

Materials and Methods

The University of Michigan Medical School's Institutional Review Board approved this study on August 24, 2022 (HUM00214501), with a waiver of informed consent. The study leveraged institutionally provided, clinically informed STS-ACSD data via the Michigan Society of Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgeons Quality Collaborative (MSTCVS-QC). The access and use of Medicare claims data from the MVC were approved through data use agreement 26142 between the University of Michigan and the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS).11 Agreements with the CMS require data suppression when cell sizes are <11 observations (noted with an asterisk when applicable). Existing data and participant agreements restrict the distribution of raw study-related data files; requests for summary statistics will be reviewed and may be approved by the study team.

Study Design, Patients, and Data Source

This study included clinical data from 33 nonfederal hospitals participating in the MSTCVS-QC that were merged with Medicare fee-for-service claims data of Michigan residents, as described previously.4 Medicare beneficiaries who underwent isolated CABG, isolated SAVR, or combined CABG + SAVR between July 1, 2017, and December 31, 2022, were included. Infections occurring during the index hospitalization were identified from either STS-ACSD clinical data or Medicare claims data, with infections beyond this time window identified from claims data. Infection types included urinary tract infection, pneumonia, sepsis, gastrointestinal infection, bloodstream infection, endocarditis, sternal wound infection, cellulitis, cannulation site infection, and conduit harvest site infection. Infections were analyzed as a composite outcome to reflect the overall hospital-level infection burden across terciles of risk-adjusted infection rates, recognizing that individual patients may experience multiple overlapping infections during the 180-day postoperative episode.

Risk Adjustment for Hospital-Level Infection Rates

Risk-adjusted odds of hospital infection were calculated using multivariable logistic regression models incorporating patient-level baseline, operative, and perioperative characteristics available within the STS-ACSD. Covariates included age, sex, race, body mass index, smoking status, left ventricular ejection fraction, diabetes mellitus, chronic lung disease, immunosuppression, peripheral arterial disease, cerebrovascular disease, renal failure, New York Heart Association functional class, elective operative status, procedure type (isolated CABG, isolated SAVR, or combined procedures), cardiopulmonary bypass duration, and year of surgery (Table 1). As described previously,4 observed and risk-adjusted odds of infection were calculated using logistic regression, with the latter modeled using baseline and operative characteristics as covariates described previously. All variables were used in the model, and no formal model selection procedures were used. Missing values for one categorical measure were treated as a category; no other measures had missing values.

Covariates included in the multivariable modeling

In addition, clinically relevant factors reflecting baseline infection risk, physiologic reserve, and post-acute care needs were incorporated. These included the presence of infective endocarditis (STS variable InfEndo), preoperative nutritional status as measured by total serum albumin (TotAlbumin), and frailty as assessed by the 5-meter walk test, using available individual walk times (FiveMWalkTest, FiveMWalk1, FiveMWalk2, FiveMWalk3). Discharge disposition following the index hospitalization was also included (DisLoctn), to account for variation in post-acute care pathways. These variables were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and availability within the STS-ACSD to enhance risk adjustment when estimating hospital-level postoperative infection rates.

Spending

Observed spending in the 180 days following cardiac surgery was obtained from the Medicare claims, including payments made by Medicare, beneficiaries, and non-Medicare primary payers. Claims sources included inpatient, carrier (individual providers), outpatient, skilled nursing home, home health services, and hospice files. Spending was first evaluated by index and postdischarge periods, then by each component, such as hospitalizations, clinicians (professional), facility services, inpatient rehab, skilled nursing home, and others. Spending was inflation-adjusted to 2023 to ensure equal weighting across years using chain-type price indices for gross domestic product.12 Spending data include services provided to Medicare beneficiaries who are residents of the state of Michigan, regardless of where (ie, within or outside of Michigan) the services were rendered. The primary outcome was overall and component spending.

Statistical Analysis

As published previously,4 observed and risk-adjusted odds of infection were calculated using logistic regression, with the latter modeled using baseline and operative characteristics as covariates described previously. All variables were used in the model, and no formal model selection procedures were used. Missing values for one categorical measure were treated as a category; no other measures had missing values. Hospitals were classified into terciles (with an equal number of hospitals) based on their low, intermediate, and high observed and risk-adjusted infection rates. The absolute difference in mean overall and component spending was compared across hospital observed and risk-adjusted infection terciles. Similar to approaches used to evaluate curbing national healthcare expenditures,13 variability in infection rates across hospitals was estimated using the coefficient of variation (CoV), calculated as the standard deviation divided by the mean. Furthermore, the percentage of the difference in overall spending across hospital infection terciles that was attributed to each spending component was calculated.

For continuous measures, differences in means were evaluated across hospital infection terciles using either analysis of variance and/or the rank-sum test, while differences in proportions for categorical measures were evaluated using the χ2 test of association. To further evaluate potential confounding by other postoperative complications, we conducted a secondary analysis examining the relationship between operative major morbidity and 180-day spending (Table E1). Operative major morbidity was defined according to STS-ACSD criteria as the occurrence of at least 1 of the following: reoperation for cardiac reasons, renal failure, deep sternal wound infection, prolonged ventilation, or stroke. Statistical significance was defined as a P value <.05. Statistical analyses were performed with SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute), and figures were generated using GraphPad Prism.

Results

The study sample included 6439 procedures performed between 2017 and 2022. The mean (SD) observed and risk-adjusted infection rates across hospitals were 22.7% (7.7%) and 19.4% (6.9%), respectively. The study cohort had a mean (SD) age of 71.2 (7.7) years, with 29.1% female and 4.8% Black patients (Table 2). The most frequently performed procedure was CABG (78.3%). The median (interquartile range [IQR]) duration of cardiopulmonary bypass of all procedures was 101 (55) minutes, and the median (IQR) index hospitalization length of stay was 9.0 (IQR, 6.0) days.

Hospital Differences in Total Spending

The mean (SD) overall Medicare 180-day spending for the study cohort was $72,956 ($39,875). The mean (SD) spending across hospitals and time are displayed in Figure 1. As shown, variation was greatest in the postindex period (total: $72,956 [$14,081], CoV 0.19; index hospitalization: $58,899 [$9969], CoV 0.17; post-index hospitalization: $14,057 [$6486], CoV 0.40). The majority (80.7%) of the overall spending was attributed to index spending. Inpatient spending accounted for 88% of this index spending. One-third (34%) of postindex spending was attributed to rehospitalization payments. Mean (SD) overall spending was 6.0% ($4306) lower among beneficiaries at the lowest compared to the highest hospital risk-adjusted infection tercile ($71,718 [$38,265] vs $76,025 [$43,566]) (Table 4). This difference in spending was accounted for by $2923 lower index hospitalization spending and $7229 higher postindex spending. Center Differences in Spending by Presence of Infection

Figure 2 documents spending in the index and postdischarge periods, as well as stratified by procedures associated with (or without) an infection. Overall, spending was $37,207 greater for procedures with an infection ($102,351 [95% confidence interval, $20,317-$225,018] vs $65,144 [95% confidence interval, $13,976-$116,311]), with $19,806 attributed to the index hospitalization and $17,401 to the postindex period. Postindex spending accounted for 14% ($9164/$65,872) of overall spending for procedures without infections at low risk-adjusted infection tercile hospitals, compared to 19% ($12,462/$64,898) at high risk-adjusted infection tercile hospitals (Table E3). The share of overall spending attributed to the postindex period increased for procedures involving infections, rising from 22% ($22,481/$101,105) in the low infection tertile hospitals to 33% ($33,849/$103,653) in the high infection tertile hospitals.

Secondary Analysis: Operative Major Morbidity and Mortality

In a secondary analysis, higher hospital-level observed rates of operative major morbidity and mortality were associated with increased 180-day Medicare spending (Table E1). Hospitals in the low, intermediate, and high terciles had operative major morbidity and mortality rates of 10.4%, 10.9%, and 13.9%, respectively. Corresponding mean (SD) 180-day spending increased stepwise across the terciles, from $71,718 ($38,265) to $72,267 ($38,934) to $76,025 ($43,566), respectively (P for trend < .0001).

Discussion

This study reports significant interhospital variability in spending associated with infections following CABG and/or SAVR. Leveraging a unique dataset of Medicare claims linked to institutional STS-ACSD data, this study identified (1) a $4306 absolute difference in overall 180-day Medicare spending across hospital risk-adjusted infection terciles; (2) the greatest interhospital variability during the phase of care following discharge from the index hospitalization; and (3) an overall average spending increase of $37,207 for procedures involving infections.

Prior statewide and national analyses have documented hospital-level differences in observed and risk-adjusted infection rates.4,14,15 One of the earliest studies leveraged statewide STS-ACSD data to evaluate observed versus predicted infection rates following isolated CABG surgery.14 In that study, infection rates varied by 18% in absolute magnitude across hospitals, although only 3% after accounting for baseline risk factors. Similar large-scale differences in pneumonia rates following isolated CABG16 and durable left ventricular assist device implantation15 across hospitals have been reported nationally. A recent study documented a 39.5% absolute difference in infections within 180 days of CABG and/or SAVR.4

Numerous studies have focused on evaluating the rate and clinical sequelae of postoperative infections,3,9,17, 18, 19, 20 with fewer evaluating associated expenditures.1,2,15 The present study analyzed overall and composite expenditures across terciles of hospital risk-adjusted infection rates. Using STS-ACSD data linked to cost-to-charge ratios, Mehaffey and colleagues20 estimated the incremental expenditures associated with major complications following isolated CABG surgery. In their analyses of 36,588 procedures performed between 2006 and 2015, the investigators estimated approximately $20,000 in incremental costs associated with deep sternal wound infections. Unlike previous evaluations, the present study used clinical and administrative data to identify a broader spectrum of infections that occurred within 180 days of surgery in the hospital setting. While beneficiaries undergoing cardiac surgery at lower risk-adjusted infection tercile hospitals had on average 6.0% less spending within 180 days of surgery compared to higher tercile hospitals, index spending was 4.8% higher, although 63.6% lower in the postindex period. The higher index hospitalization payments by low infection rate hospitals likely reflect greater operative complexity and upfront investment in perioperative care, with subsequent reductions in postdischarge utilization and overall episode spending.

The present study also extends prior work evaluating spending associated with postoperative infections. Thompson and colleagues5 estimated Medicare spending associated with postoperative pneumonia following CABG and valve surgery among nearly 115,000 Medicare beneficiaries. Pneumonia occurred in 6.4% of beneficiaries following CABG and in 6.6% following valve procedures. Pneumonia increased episode spending by approximately $9000 to $13,000 on average, including for more expensive diagnosis-related groups and outlier payments. In the present study, 180-day spending was on average $37,207 higher for procedures associated with infections. The 180-day follow-up period was chosen to capture the heterogeneous timing of postoperative infections following cardiac surgery, including later-occurring infections not routinely identified within traditional 30- or 90-day surveillance windows, as well as to assess their cumulative downstream impact on healthcare utilization and spending. Future work examining the timing of infections within the 180-day timeframe, including those occurring between 90 and 180 days, may further clarify their contribution to downstream healthcare utilization and spending.

Because postoperative infections frequently co-occur during the 180-day period, the observed increase in spending reflects the cumulative burden of infection rather than those attributable to a specific infection subtype. Future studies leveraging detailed cost-accounting data may allow for more precise attribution of expenditures to individual infection subtypes. The index period accounted for 53% of the difference in overall spending between procedures involving (or not) infections. Beneficiaries in the low hospital risk-adjusted infection rate tercile had on average $4306 less spending compared to those in the high infection rate tercile. Higher infection rate hospitals exhibited increased frequencies across multiple postoperative infection types rather than a single dominant driver. Infection-specific analyses at the individual-hospital level were limited by CMS cell size suppression requirements.

These findings have important implications for advancing value (ie, quality as a function of expenditure) in the setting of cardiac surgery. Despite the interest in and importance of advancing value-based care,21 existing approaches for data feedback and reporting in the setting of cardiac surgery are limited to mortality, major morbidity, processes of care (eg, internal mammary artery use, beta blocker use), and readmissions.22,23 The present study highlights the important differences in short- and mid-term Medicare spending attributed to risk-adjusted hospital postoperative infection rates. While prior work has documented the success of physician-led, cardiac surgery quality improvement initiatives,7,24, 25, 26, 27 these initiatives traditionally have lacked important financial outcome metrics. Regionally based quality improvement collaboratives are well positioned to report the financial impact associated with their primary intervention, namely by merging clinical and administrative datasets.

The findings of this study should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, although this observational study is subject to unmeasured confounding, risk adjustment was used to mitigate this risk when calculating hospital-level infection rates. Second, while appreciable differences may exist in the cost of delivering healthcare services across regions of Michigan, this study did not use price standardization. Third, even though this is a large statewide study, the findings may lack generalizability outside of the study cohort (eg, outside of Michigan, among commercial payers). Fourth, because of Medicare claims data use restrictions prohibiting the reporting of cell sizes <11, infection was modeled as a binary exposure rather than by subtype (precluding infection subtype-specific spending estimates) to preserve hospital-level comparability. Finally, although absolute spending amounts may differ for commercially insured populations owing to differences in negotiated payment rates and benefit design, the observed relationships among postoperative infection burden, downstream healthcare utilization, and episode spending are likely to persist across payer types.

Conclusions

This multicenter study of 6439 cardiac surgical procedures documented important differences in average 180-day spending across terciles of hospital risk-adjusted infection rates. Hospital variation in spending was greatest during the postindex period. Overall, spending was on average $37,207 greater for procedures involving an infection. Large-scale improvements in cardiac surgical value may be realized by evaluating and disseminating infection prevention best practices.
