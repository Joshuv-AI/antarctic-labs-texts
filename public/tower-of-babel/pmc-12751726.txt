The construction of the gelatin methacrylate microneedle -encapsulated newcastle disease virus and its antitumor effect in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Tong Li, Xiyu Liu, Jintong Na, Shixu Fang, Liping Zhong, Yongxiang Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is associated with one of the highest cancer-related mortality rates worldwide and remains therapeutically challenging. There is an urgent need for innovative treatment strategies. Although Newcastle disease virus (NDV) has demonstrated potent oncolytic activity in vitro and in animal models, free virions administered intravenously are rapidly neutralized by pre-existing antibodies and exhibit poor tumor tropism, significantly limiting their clinical translation (Shalhout et al., Nat Rev Clin Oncol 20(3):160-177, 2023).ObjectivesThis study aimed to develop porous gelatin methacrylate (GelMA) microneedles (MNs) for encapsulating Newcastle disease virus (NDV) and to evaluate their antitumor efficacy against HCC.MethodsGelMA microneedles were fabricated and screened across a concentration gradient for biocompatibility and degradability. The optimal NDV-loaded formulation was subsequently evaluated for safety and therapeutic efficacy in HCC models using in vitro assays and a subcutaneous HCC xenograft model in nude mice.ResultsMicroneedles prepared with 10% (w/v) GelMA exhibited excellent biocompatibility and degradability. 10% GelMA MNs loaded with an NDV–gelatin mixture sustained NDV release for over 24 h. Compared with the free virus, they induced significantly stronger inhibition of proliferation, migration, and invasion, and promoted greater apoptosis in HCC cells. In mice bearing subcutaneous HCC xenografts, NDV encapsulated in 10% GelMA MNs markedly suppressed tumor growth without significant changes in body weight or histological damage to major organs. Histological analysis further confirmed reduced proliferation and increased apoptosis in the treated group.ConclusionGelMA-encapsulated NDV microneedles represent a novel, promising and well-tolerated therapeutic strategy for HCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13036-025-00582-0.

Introduction

HCC is the most common primary liver malignancy and ranks among the leading causes of cancer-related death worldwide [1, 2]. Curative options, such as surgical resection, radiofrequency ablation, and liver transplantation, are restricted to early-stage disease [3]. Because early-stage HCC is usually asymptomatic, over 80% of patients present at an advanced stage and are no longer candidates for Radical therapy [4]. For the majority of cases, systemic treatment remains the only feasible strategy. Unfortunately, conventional chemotherapy provides only modest survival benefit and carries substantial toxicity, whereas molecular-targeted agents frequently elicit acquired resistance. Consequently, the overall prognosis remains dismal, with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 18% [5]. Novel, low-toxicity therapeutic modalities are therefore urgently needed.

The concept of exploiting microorganisms to treat cancer can be traced to mid-19th-century reports of spontaneous tumor regression following acute infection. These observations established the principle that bacteria or viruses can selectively replicate within malignant cells, sparing normal tissue and inducing oncolysis [6]. With the advent of modern virology in the 1950s, oncolytic virotherapy entered rigorous pre-clinical and clinical evaluation [7]. Compared with other immunotherapeutic strategies, oncolytic viruses offer potent tumoricidal activity, precise tumor tropism, limited off-target toxicity, multimodal mechanisms of action, the ability to circumvent drug resistance, and relatively low production costs [8–14]. Because human viruses may elicit significant host toxicity, non-pathogenic animal viruses have emerged as safer alternatives. Newcastle disease virus (NDV), an avian paramyxovirus with a single-stranded, negative-sense RNA genome, exhibits intrinsic oncolytic activity by preferentially infecting and lysing transformed cells while leaving normal hepatocytes largely unaffected [8]. Nevertheless, a single low-dose systemic injection is rarely sufficient for durable efficacy, whereas an excessive bolus can provoke intolerable side effects. These limitations underscore the need for controlled, tumor-targeted delivery systems that maintain viral bioactivity while minimizing systemic exposure.

Hydrogel-based sustained-release platforms can prolong antitumor activity and mitigate the side-effects associated with high-dose boluses. However, the intrinsic lability of NDV makes the integration of tissue-engineered delivery systems with oncolytic virotherapy an attractive strategy [15]. Microneedle (MN) patches improve delivery efficiency by enabling controlled systemic release and bypassing first-pass hepatic metabolism [16]. GelMA is a photopolymerizable hydrogel with minimal immunogenicity. Owing to its favorable mechanical and swelling properties, GelMA has been widely used in dentistry, orthopedics, and cutaneous wound repair [11, 17–19]. Compared with traditional bolus injections, MNs provide more uniform drug distribution, reduce the need for repeated dosing, lessen pain, and enhance both therapeutic efficacy and patient adherence. MN patches thus offer dose-sparing, an improved safety profile, and superior compliance, positioning them as a potentially valuable modality for cancer therapy [20]. Sustained release that maintains continuous drug exposure at the tumor site further amplifies antitumor potency.

In this study, we engineered a GelMA-based microneedle patch loaded with NDV and comprehensively evaluated its biocompatibility, biodegradability, release kinetics, and antitumor efficacy in pre-clinical models of HCC.

Materials and methods

Cell cultures

Mouse embryonic fibroblast (NIH 3T3), human hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG2), normal human hepatocyte (L-02), and mesocricetus auratus kidney (BSR-T7/5) cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All lines were maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (High-Glucose DMEM; Invitrogen, Nanjing Biotech Development Co., Ltd) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Meilunbio, Dalian, China) at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. The medium was replaced every other day, and cells were used within 20 passages.

Synthesis of GelMA and Preparation of microneedles

GelMA was synthesized as previously described [21, 22].Porcine skin gelatin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved at 10% (w/v) in PBS at 60 °C under magnetic stirring until completely clear. Methacrylic anhydride (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was then added dropwise (5% v/v, 0.5 mL min⁻¹) via a syringe pump and the mixture was allowed to react at 50 °C for 3 h. The reaction was quenched by adding four volumes of pre-warmed PBS (40 °C). Unreacted reagents were removed by dialysis (MWCO 14 kDa) against ultrapure water at 40 °C for 5 days with daily water changes, followed by centrifugation to eliminate insoluble residues. The purified solution was lyophilized, and the resulting GelMA sponge was stored at − 80 °C. Prior to use, the sponge was ground to a fine powder and its chemical structure verified by FT-IR spectroscopy (Tensor II, Bruker, USA).

For microneedle fabrication, the GelMA powder was redissolved in PBS, poured into a custom 15.8 × 15.8 × 10 mm microneedle mold, covered with a glass coverslip, and photopolymerized under UV light (≈ 405 nm) for the required duration. After UV exposure, the mold was frozen at − 20 °C overnight, lyophilized to remove residual water, and stored at − 80 °C until use. All characterizations were performed on lyophilized samples (n = 3 per condition).

NDV preparation, titration and identity confirmation

Preparation and titration of NDV

As previously described, the allantoic cavity of 10-day-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) embryonated eggs (Xinhua Dahua Poultry and Egg Enterprise Co., Ltd., China) was inoculated with NDV and incubated for 3 days. After overnight storage at 4 °C, the allantoic fluid was harvested at room temperature and clarified by centrifugation (4 000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C). Viral titers were examined by plaque-forming unit (PFU) assays and expressed as log₁₀ 50% tissue-culture infectious dose mL⁻¹ (TCID50). a second centrifugation step was included to remove any residual debris.

Haemagglutination (HA) assay

A 96-well V-bottom plate was pre-loaded with 50 µL PBS per well. Allantoic fluid (50 µL) was subjected to two-fold serial dilutions across the plate. An equal volume of 1% (v/v) SPF chicken erythrocyte suspension was then added to each well and the plate incubated at room temperature for 20 min. Agglutination was read after tilting the plate to 90°.

NDV identity confirmation

NDV identity was confirmed by Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) and indirect immunofluorescence. Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol (Vazyme, Nanjing, China); 1 µg was reverse-transcribed using a commercial cDNA synthesis kit (Vazyme). NDV-specific primers were used for PCR and amplicons were visualised on 1% agarose gels.

For immunofluorescence, BSR-T/5 cells were infected with NDV at various dilutions and cultured for 72 h. Indirect immunofluorescence was performed to visualize cytopathic effects and confirm stable NDV expression. After fixation, permeabilization, and blocking, cells were incubated with Anti-NDV antibody (Abcam, UK) at 4 °C, washed, then treated with Goat Anti-Chicken IgY H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488, Abcam, UK) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by DAPI (Solarbio, Beijing, China) staining. The PBS group served as the uninfected negative control, and all subsequent steps were identical. Fluorescence images were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Ts2R, Nikon, Japan).

Release study

NDV-loaded microneedles were fully immersed in collagenase-containing PBS at 37 °C. At each scheduled time point, the release medium was sampled and analysed by PCR (Mastercycler Nexus, Eppendorf, Germany), inverted fluorescence microscopy (Ts2R, Nikon, Japan) and agarose-gel electrophoresis (DYY-12C, Beijing Liuyi Instrument Factory, China) to monitor NDV release in a qualitative manner.

Effect of GelMA-encapsulated NDV on HepG2 proliferation, migration, invasion, apoptosis, and NDV expression

NDV expression

In vitro experiments were performed with NDV applied at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1. HepG2 cells were plated at 3 × 10⁵ cells per well (6-well format, n = 3) and allowed to attach. 2 mL of the respective solution was then added; after 72 h, cells were fixed and stained for viral antigen as detailed in Sect. “NDV identity confirmation”, with NDV expression documented by inverted fluorescence microscopy. L-02 normal hepatocytes were examined in parallel to assess NDV selectivity.

Proliferation

Logarithmic-phase HepG2 cells (~ 80% confluence) were detached, adjusted to 4 × 10⁴ cells/mL, and seeded at 100 µL/well in 96-well plates (triplicate wells per group). After 24 h for adhesion, 100 µL of the test formulations was added; viability was assessed at 24, 48 and 72 h with 10 µL CCK-8 (Meilunbio, Dalian, China) for 1–2 h at 37 °C, and absorbance read at 450 nm. Results are expressed relative to untreated controls.

Scratch wound assay

HepG2 cells (6 × 10⁵ cells/well, 6-well plate, triplicates) were grown to confluence overnight. A sterile 100 µL tip was used to create a straight scratch; wells were washed twice with PBS to remove debris and refilled with 2 mL of the respective treatment. Images were captured at 0 h and after 24, 48 and 72 h. Wound closure was quantified as: Mobility (%) = (A₀ – Aₙ)/A₀ × 100%, where A₀ is the initial wound area and Aₙ the remaining area at each time point.

Invasion assays

Matrigel (Corning, USA) was diluted 1:8 (v/v) with serum-free DMEM; 100 µL of the mixture was coated onto 8 μm Transwell inserts (Corning, USA) and allowed to polymerise overnight at 37 °C. HepG2 cells (2.5 × 10⁵ cells/mL, 200 µL serum-free DMEM) were seeded into the upper chamber, and 700 µL DMEM with 10% FBS was placed in the lower chamber (n = 3 per group). After 24 and 48 h, non-invading cells were removed; the invaded cells were first fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Representative images were captured under an inverted microscope (Ts2R, Nikon, Japan) in three random fields of view per insert to document invasion. Subsequently, cells in the same fields were counted using ImageJ, and the mean value was taken as the invasion index.

Apoptosis assay

HepG2 cells (3 × 10⁵ cells/2 mL, 6-well plate, triplicates) were allowed to attach overnight, then treated for 72 h. Cells were harvested, washed, and resuspended in 1× binding buffer (1 × 10⁶ cells/mL). Annexin V-FITC (5 µL) and propidium iodide (10 µL) (Liankebio, China) were added and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. After adding 400 µL binding buffer, samples were immediately analysed by flow cytometry (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter, USA).

Establishment, treatment and ethical monitoring of HepG2 xenograft model in BALB/c nude mice

Animals and housing

Male and female BALB/c nude mice (half male and half female; 4–6 weeks, 12–20 g; Beijing Weitong Lihua Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.) were acclimatised for 2 weeks under SPF conditions: 23–26 °C, 40–60% humidity, 10–15 air changes h⁻¹, HEPA filtration (≥ 99.9%, 0.3 μm particles), and a 10 h light / 14 h dark cycle.

Tumor induction

On day 0, 100 µL HepG2 cell suspension (5 × 10⁶ cells) was injected subcutaneously into the right flank without deep penetration. Tumors were measured daily with electronic calipers until they reached ~ 5 mm diameter (7–14 days).

Randomization and treatments

Mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6): PBS, Material, NDV-solution, and Porous-microneedle-NDV. Under 2% isoflurane anaesthesia, a 2-mm superficial incision was made over the tumour immediately before the first treatment. Treatments were applied as follows: the PBS group received 100 µL PBS intratumorally; the material group was given blank lyophilised porous microneedles; the Porous-microneedle-NDV group received lyophilised microneedles loaded with NDV (1 × 10⁷ TCID₅₀/mL, 100 µL per patch); both microneedle formulations were secured to the disinfected tumour surface. The NDV-solution group received 100 µL NDV solution (1 × 10⁷ TCID₅₀/mL) intratumorally.

Ethical monitoring and endpoint criteria

All animal procedures were approved by the Guangxi Medical University Animal Ethics Committee (approval No. 202310025). Tumour length (A) and width (B) were recorded every 2 days, and volume was calculated as A × B² × 0.5; body weight was measured simultaneously. Mice were euthanised when tumour diameter reached 15 mm or volume exceeded 1 500 mm³, endpoints pre-approved by the same committee. Anaesthesia was induced with sodium pentobarbital (150 mg kg⁻¹, i.p.); after loss of pedal reflex, cervical dislocation was performed. Death was confirmed by absence of heartbeat and respiration for ≥ 3 min and fixed dilated pupils. Any mouse showing tumour ulceration, > 20% body-weight loss, or abnormal behaviour was euthanised earlier using the same protocol.

Tissue collection

Tumours and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) were harvested, fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, and processed for histological analysis.

Histological and Immunofluorescence analysis

Routine histology

Samples were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, paraffin-embedded and sectioned at 3–5 μm. Haematoxylin & eosin (H&E; Solarbio, Beijing, China) staining was performed and slides examined with a Nikon Eclipse 80 i light microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

Sections were baked (65 °C, 30 min), blocked, then incubated with Ki-67 primary antibody (Beyotime, China; 37 °C, 1 h) followed by horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (Beyotime, 60 min). Immunoreactivity was developed with DAB (Beyotime, China), lightly counterstained with haematoxylin, mounted in neutral balsam and imaged.

TUNEL apoptosis assay

Following proteinase K digestion, sections were incubated with terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT)-mediated dUTP Nick-End labeling (TUNEL) reaction mixture (Beyotime) for 1 h at 37 °C, counterstained with DAPI (5 min, RT), mounted in anti-fade medium, and imaged under fluorescence microscopy (Cy3 channel: ex 550 nm / em 570 nm).

NDV antigen detection

Sections were probed with anti-NDV antibody (Abcam, UK) followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam) and examined by fluorescence microscopy.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least three times. Data are expressed as mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were evaluated using Student’s t-test, and differences among multiple groups were assessed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism 8.

Identification and virulence testing of NDV

The experimental workflow is summarised in Fig. 1A. To propagate NDV, the haemagglutination activity of the harvested allantoic fluid was first determined; material exhibiting a high titre was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C to serve as seed stock for subsequent passages. The pre-inoculum registered a haemagglutination titre of 2⁸ (Fig. 1B), whereas the fluid recovered after two serial passages maintained a stable titre of 2⁹ (Fig. 1C), demonstrating consistent viral yield and infectivity. Agarose-gel electrophoresis of RNA extracted from both pre- and post-passage fluids showed prominent bands between 100 and 250 bp, confirming the persistence of NDV genomic RNA throughout the amplification process (Fig. 1D).

Characterizations of GelMA-Gel microneedle

Infrared spectra (Fig. 3A) display a broad envelope at 3290 cm⁻¹ generated by the overlapping O–H and N–H stretching modes of an extensive hydrogen-bonded network. The intense feature at 1650 cm⁻¹ is assigned to the amide I (C = O stretching) vibration, while the amide II band (N–H bending coupled with C–N stretching) appears at 1500–1570 cm⁻¹. Symmetric and asymmetric C–H stretches of methylene and methyl groups give rise to additional peaks at 2930–2970 cm⁻¹ and 2880 cm⁻¹, respectively. All signatures coincide with the characteristic absorptions of GelMA and are in full agreement with previously published spectra of the unmodified material [11, 24, 25].

The methacrylation degree of substitution (DS) acts as the first gateway for GelMA’s journey from molecule to material and ultimately to product. It dictates whether cross-linking is feasible, how fast it proceeds, and how precisely the construct can be shaped, while also governing mechanical strength, degradation kinetics, and cellular compatibility. Hence, an accurate determination of DS is essential. The singlet at 3.0 ppm was assigned to the two protons on the methylene group adjacent to the ε-amino group of lysine. After baseline correction and integration, the NMR-H1 of control gelatin (Fig. 3B) showed no detectable signals in the 5.2–5.7 ppm region. In contrast, the NMR-H1 of GelMA (Fig. 3C) exhibited two distinct singlets in this range, corresponding to the newly introduced vinyl protons, confirming that methacrylate groups had been successfully grafted onto the gelatin backbone. the measured methacrylation DS of 37.91% enables gel formation under mild UV conditions, providing a solid foundation for downstream studies.

Microscopy (Fig. 3D, E) revealed a glass-clear microneedle array with 800 μm-long, 225 μm-wide needles spaced 650 μm apart on a 15.8 mm × 15.8 mm patch with a base thickness of 1.50 mm. Microneedle fidelity improves steadily with increasing GelMA concentration (Fig. 3F). Specifically, the fidelity for 10% GelMA was 56.66 ± 5.79%, for 20% GelMA it was 71.66 ± 3.39%, and for 30% GelMA it reached 84.33 ± 2.29%; the 30% group differed significantly from both the 10% and 20% groups (one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test, both P < 0.05). Consistent with this trend, compression testing shows that 30% GelMA yields the stiffest material, exhibiting the highest compressive modulus (Fig. 3G). SEM images (Fig. 3H) show uniformly tapered microneedles arranged in an ordered array. At higher magnification (Fig. 3I, J, and K), all three GelMA concentrations exhibit a sponge-like porous architecture; the 10% formulation displays the most regular and uniformly distributed pores. Statistical analysis further reveals that lowering GelMA concentration progressively increases porosity from 42.36 ± 4.1% to 55.34 ± 3.75% and then to 68.92 ± 1.43%, yielding a looser network and markedly higher drug-loading (10% vs. 20% and 30% groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test; both comparisons P < 0.05) (Fig. 3L). Equilibrium swelling was determined from the mass change of each scaffold after immersion in PBS. Figure 3M demonstrates that the swelling properties of three concentrations of microneedles are 321.97 ± 40.9, 224.25 ± 19.9, and 172.93 ± 33.8, respectively. Lower GelMA concentration yielded higher swelling, whereas higher concentration markedly reduced it (10% vs. 20% and 30% groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test; both comparisons P < 0.05). The results also reflected that the hydrogel with the lowest concentration had more pores to store water, which was consistent with the scanning electron microscopy results of the microneedles. Degradation profiles (Fig. 3N) revealed that 10% GelMA microneedles dissolved completely within 14 days, whereas 20% and 30% formulations persisted beyond this period (10% vs. 20% and 30% groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test; both comparisons P < 0.05), confirming the superior degradability of the lowest-concentration hydrogel. Extracts from 10% GelMA microneedles (without supplemental gelatin) were subsequently evaluated for cytocompatibility.

In Fig. 4A, at 72 h the 30% GelMA microneedle extract lowered the relative proliferation rate to 71.03 ± 2.97% of the PBS control, whereas the 20% and 10% extracts achieved 86.71 ± 2.25% and 92.79 ± 3.98%, respectively. The 10% group differed significantly from both the 20% and 30% groups (one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test, both P < 0.05). Thus, the 10% GelMA extract preserved the highest metabolic activity at this endpoint. Microscopy (Figure S1) confirmed that 3T3 fibroblasts retained normal morphology and continued to proliferate in all extracts. Across all three sampling intervals (Fig. 4B), unloaded 10% GelMA microneedles (with supplemental gelatin) maintained a relative proliferation rate exceeding 90% of the PBS control, with no statistically significant difference versus the PBS group (P > 0.05), underscoring excellent cytocompatibility equivalent to the blank control. At all three time points, live and dead staining fluorescence remained stable, indicating that viable cells in the 10% GelMA group continued to proliferate at levels indistinguishable from the control (Fig. 4C). After 72 h (Fig. 4D), the 10% GelMA microneedle group retained 97.75 ± 0.41% viability, while the control group recorded 98.66 ± 1.03%, with no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05).

In summary, the foregoing data establish 10% GelMA microneedles as the optimal formulation, which combines superior swelling, exceptional porosity, excellent biocompatibility and rapid degradation; this formulation was therefore adopted for all subsequent drug-delivery experiments.

Release study

Release kinetics were monitored simultaneously by fluorescence imaging and PCR. PBS blanks remained signal-negative, whereas NDV-loaded positive controls displayed intense green fluorescence. Eluates from microneedles exhibited a peak signal at 6 h that declined to background by 48 h; the maintenance of cytopathic effect (CPE) through 24 h confirmed that released virions retained infectivity (Fig. 4E). PCR (Fig. 4F) detected the 136-bp NDV amplicon at 6, 12 and 24 h, with the 12 h band surpassing that at 24 h in intensity. Collectively, the data demonstrate that porous 10% GelMA microneedles provide sustained release of infectious NDV for at least 24 h.

The antitumor effect of NDV-loaded microneedles in HepG2 cells

The oncolytic activity of NDV was first monitored by tracking HepG2-cell proliferation. Figure 5A shows the relative proliferation curves (normalized to PBS). At 24 h, all lines overlapped, confirming a negligible initial effect. By 48 h, clear separation emerged, and at 72 h, the relative proliferation rates were 92.65 ± 3.44% for the material group, 63.62 ± 8.22% for the NDV group, and 30.62 ± 1.64% for the GM10-Gel-NDV group (porous microneedles containing NDV in gelatin). The last difference represents a 69% suppression versus PBS, underscoring the potent, time-dependent inhibition conferred by microneedle-delivered NDV. GM10-Gel-NDV beat free-NDV and material at 48 & 72 h (P < 0.001, Tukey’s test). NDV’s inherent tumor-selective replication underpins its oncolytic utility. Figure 5B shows that PBS and blank-material controls remained fluorescence-negative, confirming negligible background. Under the same release conditions validated in Figs. 4E and F, neither free NDV nor GM10-Gel-NDV elicited any signal in L-02 normal hepatocytes, demonstrating that the virus cannot establish productive infection in non-malignant liver cells and corroborating its selective tropism [26]. In HepG2 tumors (Fig. 5C), PBS and material-only groups again lacked signal. In contrast, free-NDV and GM10-Gel-NDV groups exhibited robust green fluorescence, indicating stable NDV expression in tumor cells. Under identical fluorescence exposure conditions, the GM10-Gel-NDV group displayed stronger green fluorescence than the free-NDV, suggesting that the porous microneedle-loaded NDV group promoted extensive viral replication and increased viral expression levels in tumor cells. Figure 5D shows the 72 h apoptosis profiles obtained by flow cytometry. Apoptosis rates were lowest in the PBS (2.5 ± 0.28%) and material-only (2.18 ± 0.11%) groups. Both NDV-containing formulations markedly increased apoptosis; values reached 17.86 ± 0.50% with free NDV and 60.67 ± 0.26% with GM10-Gel-NDV The GM10-Gel-NDV group exhibited a significantly higher apoptosis level than all other groups (Fig. 5E; one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test, P < 0.001 vs. each group).

The migratory capacity of HepG2 cells was evaluated by wound-healing assay. Representative images in Fig. 6A show scratch widths at 0, 24, 48 and 72 h; quantitative analysis (Fig. 6B) revealed migration percentages of 69.95 ± 0.21% (PBS), 51.55 ± 0.50% (material), 35.20 ± 2.25% (free NDV) and 23.69 ± 0.79% (GM10-Gel-NDV vs. all other groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD, all P < 0.01). Antitumor efficacy of GM10-Gel-NDV in subcutaneous HepG2 xenografts

HepG2 cells were implanted subcutaneously in nude mice and treatments (PBS, material, free NDV, GM10-Gel-NDV) were applied for 22 days (Fig. 7A). Tumors in PBS and material groups grew rapidly, whereas free NDV slowed expansion and GM10-Gel-NDV induced marked regression (Fig. 7B). After an initial slow-growth phase (days 0–14), volumes in NDV and GM10-Gel-NDV groups declined, with the latter reaching the smallest size. Final average volumes (Fig. 7C) were: PBS 1245 ± 140 mm³, material 1185 ± 184 mm³, NDV 529 ± 78 mm³, and GM10-Gel-NDV 285 ± 64 mm³ (One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD: GM10-Gel-NDV vs. each group, P < 0.05).

Ki67 staining revealed profoundly reduced proliferation in NDV and GM10-Gel-NDV groups versus PBS and material controls, with the greatest suppression in the porous-microneedle drug-loaded cohort. (Fig. 7D). Proliferation indices (Fig. 7E) were PBS 92.0 ± 4.6, material 81.4 ± 6.4, NDV 24.2 ± 2.4, and GM10-Gel-NDV 7.1 ± 3.7 (One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD: GM10-Gel-NDV vs. each group, P < 0.05).

H&E-stained sections (Fig. 7F) showed PBS-derived tumors composed of densely packed cells with large, hyperchromatic nuclei, high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratios and negligible necrosis; the material group exhibited similar tight cell arrangement and high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratios, with limited necrosis. NDV-treated tumors displayed looser architecture, reduced cellularity and patchy necrosis, whereas GM10-Gel-NDV sections revealed widespread cell loss and extensive necrotic foci, corroborating the most severe histological damage.

Discussion

Microneedle (MN) patches have rapidly evolved into a versatile, minimally-invasive platform that can precisely deliver therapeutics directly into the tumor microenvironment, dramatically reducing systemic exposure and off-target toxicity [27, 28].Here, we encapsulated the century-old, tumor-selective oncolytic virus NDV in a gelatin-reinforced, porous GelMA matrix to create a biodegradable MN system specifically for HCC [29]. By seamlessly merging NDV’s oncolytic potency with the painless, self-administrable advantages of MNs, we offer an innovative yet clinically translatable strategy for HCC.

Earlier work has established the material basis of this approach. Yuan et al. co-loaded HUVEC-derived exosomes and tazarotene in GelMA/PEGDA MNs and demonstrated excellent cytocompatibility and sustained bioactivity [30], while Nurdin et al. systematically characterized the degradation kinetics of GelMA MNs [31], findings that align closely with our own. To improve scalability and geometric uniformity, we will adopt the low-cost 3D-print–shrink-moulding protocol described by Ghanbariamin et al. [32], which should yield high-resolution, reproducibly loaded MN arrays.

From a translational standpoint, Liu Y et al. integrated viral antigen capture with MN-based sampling [33] and Lee C et al. showed that MNs can deposit chemotherapeutics directly into tumors with minimal side-effects. These precedents mirror our rationale: intratumoral NDV delivery bypasses first-pass hepatic metabolism and gastrointestinal degradation, maximizing viral oncolysis while limiting systemic exposure. Although MN systems have been explored in breast cancer and melanoma [34–36], HCC remains an unmet need; the present study fills that gap.

Previous reports documented rapid attenuation of NDV infectivity when simply entrapped in GelMA [37]. In this study, Lyophilized porous GelMA scaffolds co-encapsulating NDV and gelatin were shown to release replication-competent virions for more than 24 h, eliciting sustained oncolysis in vitro and measurable tumor regression in vivo. Compared with an equivalent titer of free NDV, the microneedle formulation achieved superior tumor suppression without increasing systemic exposure. These results mirror two recent proofs of concept: Menon I et al. used a virus-like-particle–nanoparticle–microneedle construct to enhance therapeutic efficacy [38] and Li H et al. employed porous microneedles to enhance CAR-T infiltration into solid tumors [39]. Together, these studies underscore the clinical translatability of biodegradable microneedles for precise, dispersed delivery of active biologics. Our NDV-loaded GelMA microneedles extend this paradigm, providing sustained intratumoral virus release and pronounced oncolysis with minimal systemic burden. Yet the nude-mouse model precluded assessment of NDV-induced adaptive immunity; future work must therefore validate efficacy and safety in immunocompetent animals. Relative to earlier clinical trials that delivered NDV intratumorally or intravenously [40, 41], our patch offers painless, minimally invasive administration and fully exploits NDV’s excellent safety record, rapid humoral immunogenicity, and direct oncolytic activity.

Collectively, our data establish an NDV-based, microneedle-delivered regimen for HCC with clear clinical translational potential; however, several hurdles remain. Uniform, high-throughput manufacturing will require GMP-compliant micromoulding or roll-to-roll embossing in partnership with specialist pharmaceutical partners. Subcutaneous xenografts must give way to orthotopic or genetically engineered HCC models that faithfully replicate human tumor architecture and immune complexity. Immunodeficient-mouse readouts preclude assessment of NDV-driven adaptive immunity, necessitating validation in humanized or immunocompetent large-animal models. Long-term NDV stability within GelMA matrices, human hepatic pharmacokinetics, and regulatory-scale production protocols must also be rigorously defined. Addressing these challenges through advanced biofabrication and stringent pre-clinical safety testing will be critical to advance this platform toward first-in-human trials.

Conclusion

A biodegradable GelMA microneedle patch is described that provides sustained, intratumoral release of oncolytic NDV. The platform exhibits excellent biocompatibility, tunable degradation and retention of viral infectivity beyond 24 h, culminating in superior anti-hepatocellular-carcinoma activity versus free virus. These findings establish GelMA-NDV microneedles as a clinically translatable therapeutic strategy for HCC.
