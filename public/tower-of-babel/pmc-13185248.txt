The role of anxiety and depressive symptoms in the longitudinal association between body image satisfaction and disordered eating behaviors among students from two Tibetan universities: a serial mediation model
Authors: Ruipeng Wu, Yeen Huang, Xinya Yan, Yingting Wang, Minxia Zhu, Xiangjun Chen, Zixuan Guang, Qiong Niu, Ciyong Lu, Shaokang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBody image plays a significant role in emotional and behavioral health. This study examined the short-term longitudinal association between body image satisfaction and disordered eating behaviors (DEBs) among students at two Tibetan universities, considering theory-driven sequential indirect roles of anxiety and depressive symptoms.MethodsA longitudinal survey was conducted among 2,993 first- and second-year university students in Xizang, with a baseline assessment in September 2021 and a follow-up in December 2021. Participants completed standardized questionnaires assessing body image satisfaction, anxiety, depressive symptoms, and DEBs. Statistical analyses included t-tests, ANOVA, Pearson correlations, and structural equation modeling (SEM) using robust maximum likelihood estimation (MLR) with bootstrap confidence intervals (1,000 resamples).ResultsSEM analyses indicated that body image satisfaction was prospectively associated with lower DEBs (β = −0.050, 95% CI = − 0.099 to − 0.008). Depressive symptoms independently mediated this association (β = −0.010, 95% CI = − 0.019 to − 0.003), whereas the indirect pathway through anxiety alone was not significant (β = −0.005, 95% CI = − 0.014 to 0.006). A sequential indirect pathway involving anxiety and depressive symptoms was significant (β = −0.033, 95% CI = − 0.047 to − 0.021). The total indirect effect was also significant (β = −0.048, 95% CI = − 0.065 to − 0.033). Model fit was acceptable (CFI = 0.961, TLI = 0.911, RMSEA = 0.060, SRMR = 0.021).ConclusionsThese findings highlight the potential roles of anxiety and depressive symptoms in the association between body image satisfaction and DEBs, suggesting that interventions targeting body image and emotional health may help reduce the risk of DEBs.

Introduction

Disordered Eating Behaviors (DEBs) refer to a range of eating-related problems, including unhealthy weight control behaviors or binge eating, such as dieting, food restriction, and vomiting [1]. These behaviors increase the risk of clinically diagnosed eating disorders (EDs) and are associated with various physical and mental health problems, including metabolic complications, malnutrition, anxiety, and depression. A systematic review across 16 countries reported that approximately 22.0% of children and adolescents experience DEBs, with prevalence increasing with age [2]. DEBs are also common among university students across different countries and settings, suggesting that emerging adulthood may represent a particularly vulnerable developmental period [3–6]. As one of the mental disorders listed in the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) 2019, EDs, like other mental illnesses, result from the combined effects of multiple risk factors, including body image disturbance, emotional symptoms, and eating behaviors, with body image disturbance serving as a significant risk factor for disordered eating behavior and a critical bridge linking emotional symptoms to eating-related behavioral problems [7]. Given the high prevalence of DEBs and the multifactorial nature of EDs, identifying upstream psychological factors and their temporal interrelationships has become an important research priority.

Body image refers to individuals’ perceptions and attitudes toward their bodies, including levels of satisfaction, importance, and behavioral investment, and has been widely recognized as a key factor in emotional and behavioral health [8–11]. Body image is a multidimensional construct. In the present study, we focused on one evaluative aspect of body image-satisfaction with specific body areas-using the Satisfaction with Body Areas subscale of the Multidimensional Body-Self Relations Questionnaire (MBSRQ). This subscale was selected because satisfaction with specific body areas is closely relevant to DEBs in student populations; however, it captures only one dimension of body image rather than the construct in its entirety. In contemporary society, body image is shaped through various micro-level social interactions [12]. Among university students, body management is increasingly shaped by sociocultural ideals and external evaluations, with exposure to appearance-related information potentially heightening sensitivity to body image concerns [12]. Surveys suggest that up to 70% of college students engage in dieting to pursue an ideal body, which may be associated with increased psychological burden [12–14]. Negative body image can be closely associated with poor mental and physical health outcomes such as low self-esteem, anxiety, depression, and EDs [15, 16]. A distorted body image and body dissatisfaction have been associated with a higher likelihood of DEBs. For example, Karime et al. found that body dissatisfaction was strongly associated with subsequent DEBs over a six-year observation of university students in Mexico [5]. Other studies focusing on adolescents and young adults suggest that body image issues may be significant predictors of EDs [17, 18]. Given the high prevalence of DEBs among young people and the established association between body image evaluation and DEBs, the psychological pathways linking the two remain insufficiently understood and warrant further investigation.

Previous studies have shown that body dissatisfaction is associated not only with various DEBs but also with negative emotional experiences. On one hand, negative emotions such as anxiety have repeatedly been linked to EDs (including anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, and binge eating) and DEBs [19–21]. Depression has been identified as one of the psychological factors closely associated with DEBs [22]. Research suggests that dieting or binge eating behaviors triggered by depressive emotions may serve as a coping mechanism to alleviate negative emotions or escape negative self-perceptions [23]. On the other hand, body image dissatisfaction can directly or indirectly lead to the occurrence of negative emotions. Studies have shown that body image dissatisfaction may increase the risk of generalized anxiety, social anxiety, and appearance-related anxiety [16, 24, 25]. The cognitive model of depression posits that negative self-evaluation—including body-related self-evaluation—is central to depressive symptom development [26]. Studies in different populations show that lower body satisfaction is associated with more severe depressive symptoms [27–29].

Therefore, it is plausible that anxiety and depressive symptoms may independently and jointly mediate the relationship between body image satisfaction and DEBs. Previous studies have reported complex pathways involving self-esteem, smartphone addiction, and negative emotions in the association between body image-related constructs and DEBs [15, 30, 31]. However, most of this evidence is based on cross-sectional data and cannot clarify temporal ordering. Longitudinal studies are therefore needed to examine whether anxiety and depressive symptoms help explain the prospective association between satisfaction with specific body areas and DEBs.

In summary, the purpose of this study is to investigate the prospective association between body image satisfaction and DEBs using a short-term prospective design and to explore the possible serial mediating mechanisms underlying this relationship. We hypothesize that: (1) anxiety or depressive symptoms independently mediate the relationship between body image satisfaction and DEBs, 2) a sequence of anxiety and depressive symptoms mediates this relationship (Fig. 1).

Methods

Participants

This study was conducted in two universities in Xizang, with first- and second-year students as participants. The initial survey was conducted in September 2021 (Time 1, T1), with a follow-up survey in December (Time 2, T2) of the same year. Prior to the survey, student management personnel from both universities were contacted to convene student council members and class leaders for extensive recruitment and promotion. They, in turn, informed students in their respective classes about the purpose, significance, and procedures of the survey. The survey was conducted using a combination of centralized and individual approaches. Students were gathered in classrooms, where class leaders distributed pre-made questionnaire QR codes to the class group. All students completed the questionnaire by scanning the QR code, and those who were absent were later contacted by class leaders to complete the survey individually. To ensure voluntariness, questionnaire responses were collected anonymously through the online platform, and class leaders had no access to individual responses or submission status. Students were informed that participation or non-participation would not affect their academic standing, and research staff rather than class leaders addressed any questions during data collection. All participants were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time. Any questions during questionnaire completion could be directed to trained research staff present on-site. Because recruitment was organized at the class level across the two universities, the exact number of students initially invited was not systematically recorded at the time of data collection; therefore, a precise response rate could not be calculated retrospectively.

Data quality screening procedures were conducted prior to statistical analyses according to predefined criteria. Students younger than 18 years or older than 30 years were excluded to ensure consistency within the target university population. In addition, questionnaire responses with implausible baseline age values or excessively short completion times were removed [32]. A total of 289 questionnaires were excluded based on these criteria. The online survey platform required all items to be completed before submission; therefore, no item-level missing data were present in the final dataset and no imputation procedures were applied. After excluding invalid questionnaires, a total of 2,993 students completed both the initial and follow-up surveys.

Measures

Satisfaction with specific body areas

In this study, one evaluative aspect of body image—satisfaction with specific body areas—was assessed using the Satisfaction with Body Areas subscale of the Multidimensional Body-Self Relations Questionnaire (MBSRQ) [33]. Although the full MBSRQ includes 10 dimensions and 67 items, only this 9-item subscale was used in the present study because it specifically captures satisfaction with discrete body areas, an evaluative component of body image [34]. Example items include “How satisfied are you with your height?” and “How satisfied are you with your upper torso (chest or breasts, shoulders, and arms)?”. Each item is rated on a 5-point scale from 1 (very dissatisfied) to 5 (very satisfied), with higher summed scores indicating greater satisfaction with body areas. The Chinese version translated and validated by Wang et al. has shown good reliability and validity [35]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the T1 satisfaction with body areas subscale was 0.94.

Disordered eating behaviors

DEBs were measured using the Eating Attitude Test-26 (EAT-26) [36]. This scale consists of 26 items that reflect three dimensions: dieting, bulimia, and oral control. Each item is rated on a 6-point Likert scale, from 0 (never) to 5 (always), reflecting the frequency of the behavior. The scores are converted from a 6-point scale to a 4-point scale [37]. The total score is obtained by summing all item scores, with higher scores indicating more severe disordered eating. The Chinese version of the scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity [37, 38]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the EAT-26 at T1 and T2 was 0.91 and 0.93, respectively.

Anxiety symptoms

Anxiety symptoms were measured using the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder module (GAD-7) [39]. This scale consists of 7 items that reflect the core anxiety symptoms experienced by the participants in the past two weeks. Each item is rated on a 4-point scale (0–3), reflecting the frequency of the symptoms. This scale is widely used across various populations. The Chinese version has good reliability and validity [40]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the GAD-7 at T1 and T2 was 0.93 and 0.94, respectively.

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were measured using the widely used Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [41]. This scale consists of 9 items that reflect the core depressive symptoms experienced by the individual in the past two weeks. Each item is rated on a 4-point scale (0–3), with the total score ranging from 0 to 27. Higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. The questionnaire has demonstrated good reliability and validity in Chinese samples [42]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the PHQ-9 at T1 and T2 were both 0.92.

Covariates

Basic demographic information was collected, including age, sex (male/female), ethnicity (Han/Tibetan and other ethnic minorities), family economic status, and parental marital status. Family economic status was self-reported by the participants as excellent or very good / good / fair or poor. Parental marital status options included harmonious, occasional quarrels, frequent quarrels, separation, divorce, or other.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.0). Descriptive statistics for categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations (M ± SD). Group differences were examined using independent-samples t-tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. Pearson correlation analyses were performed to examine bivariate associations among study variables. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted using the sem function in the lavaan package (v.0.6–18) [43] to examine the prospective associations among body image satisfaction at T1, anxiety and depressive symptoms at T2, and DEBs at T2. A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was first performed to evaluate the measurement model of DEBs as a latent construct, followed by testing of the full structural model. Prior to SEM estimation, distributional properties of observed variables were evaluated using skewness and kurtosis indices. Body image satisfaction, anxiety, and depressive symptom variables showed approximately normal distributions, whereas DEBs indicators exhibited noticeable positive skewness. Therefore, SEM parameters were estimated using robust maximum likelihood estimation (MLR), which provides standard errors and fit statistics that are less sensitive to deviations from multivariate normality. Model fit was evaluated using the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA; with 90% confidence intervals), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Values of CFI and TLI ≥ 0.90 and RMSEA and SRMR ≤ 0.08 were considered indicative of acceptable model fit [44, 45]. To examine potential multicollinearity among predictors, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for observed independent variables included in the structural model. VIF values were inspected to ensure that collinearity did not substantially inflate parameter estimates [46]. Indirect effects were evaluated using bootstrap 95% confidence intervals based on 1,000 resamples. A baseline-adjusted prospective mediation model was specified to examine independent and sequential indirect pathways linking body image satisfaction at T1 with DEBs at T2 via anxiety and depressive symptoms at T2. All models statistically controlled for sex, age, ethnicity, HSS, parental marital status, baseline anxiety and depressive symptoms. Standardized path coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals, and explained variance (R²) for endogenous variables were reported to facilitate interpretation of effect sizes beyond statistical significance. Because no missing data were present, sensitivity analyses related to missing data handling were not conducted. Statistical significance was determined using two-tailed tests with p-values < 0.05.

Results

Descriptive analysis

A total of 2,993 participants completed both the initial and follow-up surveys, with an average age of 19.8 years (SD = 1.3). Among the participants, 34.3% were male, 43.2% were Han Chinese students. Regarding family economic status, 52.5% of the participants reported it as good, and 31.2% reported it as fair or poor. Regarding parental marital status, 57.5% of participants reported harmonious relationships, while 3.0% reported frequent quarrels. Significant differences in DEBs scores across sex (t = -2.41, p = 0.02), ethnicity (t = 6.36, p < 0.001), and parental marital status (F = 4.28, p < 0.001) were observed, as shown in Table 1. Post hoc comparisons indicated that students who reported “frequent quarrels” had significantly higher DEBs scores than those who reported a “harmonious relationship”.

Serial multiple mediating analysis

To ensure the stability of the serial multiple mediating analysis, variance inflation factors (VIFs) for predictors were examined and all values were below 1.05, indicating minimal multicollinearity. A CFA was conducted to assess the measurement model for the latent construct of DEBs. The model suggested excellent fit to the data: CFI = 1.000, TLI = 1.000, RMSEA = 0.000, and SRMR = 0.000. Standardized factor loadings for the three indicators ranged from 0.572 to 0.799, indicating adequate construct validity.

The MLR-estimated SEM demonstrated acceptable model fit (CFI = 0.961, TLI = 0.911, RMSEA = 0.060, SRMR = 0.021). The serial mediation model is presented in Fig. 2; Table 3. All models controlled for sex, age, ethnicity, family economic status, parental marital status, baseline anxiety (T1), and baseline depressive symptoms (T1). Body image satisfaction at T1 was prospectively associated with lower DEBs at T2 (β = −0.050, 95% CI = − 0.099 to − 0.008, p = 0.043). The model explained 26.0% of the variance in anxiety at T2 (R² = 0.260), 69.0% in depressive symptoms at T2 (R² = 0.690), and 23.5% in DEBs at T2 (R² = 0.235). The standardized direct association between body image satisfaction and DEBs was small in magnitude, whereas the sequential indirect effect through anxiety and depressive symptoms was relatively larger than either individual indirect pathway. The explained variance was substantial for depressive symptoms and modest for DEBs, suggesting that the model captured a meaningful, although not exhaustive, proportion of the outcome variance.

The serial multiple mediation model included three indirect pathways. Body image satisfaction at T1 was associated with lower anxiety symptoms at T2 (β = −0.124, 95% CI = − 0.160 to − 0.088, p < 0.001). However, anxiety symptoms were not significantly associated with DEBs (β = 0.039, 95% CI = − 0.043 to 0.112, p = 0.317). Accordingly, the indirect effect through anxiety alone was not significant (β = −0.005, 95% CI = − 0.014 to 0.006, p = 0.320).

Body image satisfaction showed a small negative association with depressive symptoms (β = −0.030, 95% CI = − 0.052 to − 0.007, p = 0.009), and depressive symptoms were positively associated with DEBs (β = 0.349, 95% CI = 0.260 to 0.443, p < 0.001). The indirect pathway through depressive symptoms was significant (β = −0.010, 95% CI = − 0.019 to − 0.003, p = 0.011).

Anxiety symptoms were strongly associated with depressive symptoms (β = 0.764, 95% CI = 0.737 to 0.790, p < 0.001). The sequential indirect effect from body image satisfaction to DEBs through anxiety and depressive symptoms was significant (β = −0.033, 95% CI = − 0.047 to − 0.021, p < 0.001).

Overall, the total indirect effect was significant (β = −0.048, 95% CI = − 0.065 to − 0.033, p < 0.001), suggesting that the association between body image satisfaction and DEBs was primarily accounted for by the depressive-symptoms pathway and the sequential pathway from anxiety to depressive symptoms, rather than by anxiety alone.

Discussion

The present study used short-term longitudinal data from university students in Xizang to examine the prospective association between body image satisfaction and DEBs, as well as the potential mediating roles of anxiety and depressive symptoms. Body image satisfaction was associated with DEBs, and serial multiple mediation analyses revealed multiple indirect pathways linking body image satisfaction to DEBs. Specifically, depressive symptoms independently mediated this association, whereas anxiety alone did not show a significant mediating effect. However, anxiety and depressive symptoms formed a significant sequential pathway, suggesting that emotional processes may be involved in the relationship between body image satisfaction and DEBs.

Body image satisfaction and DEBs

The current findings suggest that body image satisfaction was prospectively associated with DEBs among university students in Xizang, which is consistent with previous research conducted in other countries and ethnic groups [47, 48]. Prior studies have reported strong associations between negative body image and maladaptive eating behaviors, as well as between positive body image and adaptive eating behaviors, highlighting the close link between body image and DEBs [47]. A systematic review including 46 studies indicated that although causal evidence remains limited, body image disturbance may be related to the course of anorexia nervosa [48]. These findings underscore the need for longitudinal research to clarify the complex pathways linking body image and ED-related symptoms [48].

The pathways underlying the association between body image satisfaction and DEBs remain incompletely understood. Cognitive-Behavioral Theories of EDs proposed by Williamson et al. provide a potential explanatory framework [49]. According to this model, body-related stimuli may activate body self-schemas and cognitive biases, such as excessive attention to body size or shape, which may interact with negative emotions and maladaptive eating behaviors. From a more positive perspective, the Attuned Representational Model of Self suggests that fostering positive body image through mindful self-care and attunement may serve as a promising approach for promoting psychological flourishing and improving eating-related outcomes [50].

Independent mediating role of depressive symptoms

Previous studies have highlighted the mediating role of depression in the relationship between body image-related evaluation and DEBs. For example, research among Chinese university students showed that body dissatisfaction was associated with disordered eating behaviors, with depression and smartphone addiction acting as mediators [30]. Studies by Soledad and Brechan also confirmed the mediating role of depression between body dissatisfaction and disordered eating [15, 31]. Consistent with these findings, the present study suggests that depressive symptoms may represent an important emotional pathway linking body image satisfaction and DEBs.

Sequential emotional pathway: anxiety and depressive symptoms

Anxiety and depression, as common psychological outcomes reported in relation to body dissatisfaction in prior studies, may jointly contribute to the emotional processes underlying DEBs. Individuals experiencing anxiety are often at higher risk of developing depressive symptoms [51]. The current study extends previous research by examining a serial mediation model and suggests that anxiety and depressive symptoms may operate together within a compounded emotional pathway. This sequential pathway indicates that body image concerns may be associated with a cascade of emotional responses that relate to higher levels of DEBs.

Non-significant mediation of anxiety

Interestingly, although body image satisfaction was associated with anxiety symptoms, anxiety did not significantly predict DEBs in this study, resulting in a non-significant independent mediation effect. This finding is partially consistent with previous research. A longitudinal study by Anna Vannucci et al. found that body dissatisfaction predicted various anxiety-related outcomes, including generalized anxiety and social anxiety [52]. From a sociocultural perspective, societal appearance ideals and appearance-based comparisons may be internalized, leading to anxiety or avoidance behaviors among students dissatisfied with their bodies [53].

However, the absence of a significant anxiety–DEBs link differs from some studies reporting mediation through more specific anxiety constructs. For instance, appearance comparison has been linked to eating disorders through social body shape anxiety [54], and research in Turkey showed that social physique anxiety fully mediated the relationship between body image satisfaction and orthorexia nervosa [55]. Such inconsistencies may reflect differences in measurement tools, sample characteristics, or analytical approaches, highlighting the need for future longitudinal research.

Demographic differences and sociocultural considerations

Regarding demographic characteristics, Han students showed higher DEBs scores than Tibetan students. Any explanation in terms of urbanization, consumerism, traditional lifestyles, or other sociocultural influences remains hypothetical, because such mechanisms were not directly measured in the present study. These group differences should therefore be interpreted cautiously, and future research should include direct assessments of sociocultural exposure, acculturation, appearance ideals, and related contextual factors [56]. Additionally, students reporting frequent parental conflict exhibited higher DEBs scores than those reporting harmonious parental relationships, suggesting that family environment may play a role in dietary behaviors [57]. Consistent with prior research, female students reported higher DEBs levels than males [58].

Public health significance

Most research on EDs and body image has been conducted in Western contexts, and studies involving Chinese ethnic minorities remain limited. The present findings provide valuable insights for prevention and intervention efforts. First, educational campaigns promoting healthy nutrition among university students are needed. Initiatives such as China’s “National Nutrition Week” may serve as an important platform for promoting dietary awareness [59]. Second, schools and educational institutions should implement targeted programs aimed at fostering healthier body image perceptions and promoting balanced aesthetic standards. National initiatives such as the “Year of Weight Management” campaign launched in 2024 may provide supportive policy contexts for improving public awareness of healthy weight management and the risks of extreme dieting [60]. Third, intervention programs addressing body image concerns and eating-related problems may benefit from simultaneously targeting emotional factors such as anxiety and depression.

Limitations and future directions

This study is not without limitations. First, although a short-term prospective design was employed, the study included only two waves over a relatively brief follow-up period. Such a design allows the examination of temporal associations, but it does not support strong conclusions regarding directionality or causality, nor does it permit a more dynamic evaluation of reciprocal processes over time. In addition, because the exact number of students initially invited was not systematically recorded, the response rate could not be calculated, which limits a more formal assessment of potential selection bias. Future research should adopt multi-wave longitudinal designs with longer follow-up intervals (e.g., three or more waves) and consider advanced longitudinal modeling approaches (e.g., cross-lagged or random-intercept models) to better clarify directionality and potential causal pathways. Second, all variables were assessed using self-report questionnaires, which may introduce reporting bias, recall bias, or shared method variance. Although validated instruments were used, future studies are encouraged to incorporate multi-method assessments, such as clinical interviews, behavioral indicators, or physiological and ecological momentary assessment approaches, to enhance measurement validity. Third, body image satisfaction was operationalized using only the Satisfaction with Body Areas subscale of the Multidimensional Body-Self Relations Questionnaire. While this subscale demonstrated excellent reliability and captures an important aspect of body satisfaction, body image is inherently multidimensional. Broader assessments encompassing additional components, such as appearance evaluation and body-related investment, would provide a more comprehensive understanding of body image processes. Fourth, our research focused solely on university students. Extending this line of research to other age groups (e.g., adolescents) and specific populations (e.g., community and clinical samples) would help improve generalizability. Finally, this study did not include other potentially relevant variables that may shape the association between body image satisfaction and DEBs, such as internet use, self-esteem, and sociocultural influences. Future studies should incorporate these factors into more comprehensive theoretical models to provide a fuller understanding of individual differences in DEBs. In particular, direct measurement of sociocultural variables—such as media exposure, appearance-related social comparison, cultural identity, and internalization of body ideals—would help clarify the contextual mechanisms that may underlie group differences and disordered eating risk.

Conclusion

This study provides short-term longitudinal evidence suggesting that body image satisfaction is prospectively associated with DEBs among Chinese university students. The findings indicate that depressive symptoms may serve as an independent emotional pathway, while anxiety and depressive symptoms together form a sequential pathway linking body image satisfaction and DEBs, with the combined indirect effect exceeding individual mediation effects. These results highlight the potential value of interventions that simultaneously address body image perceptions and emotional health, particularly depressive symptoms. Future multi-wave longitudinal studies and more comprehensive assessments are warranted to further clarify the temporal dynamics and potential psychological pathways underlying the association between body image satisfaction and DEBs.
