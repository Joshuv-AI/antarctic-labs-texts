Associations Between Paternal Pre‐Conceptional Body Mass Index and Lifestyle Factors and Offspring Weight Development
Authors: Amanda Beske Almstrup, Christina Sonne Mogensen, Christian Mølgaard, Stine Schramm, Nina Rica Wium Geiker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEvidence suggests that pre‐conceptional paternal factors, including BMI and diet, may influence offspring development.ObjectivesWe examined the associations between paternal BMI, dietary protein intake, glycemic index (GI), smoking and alcohol consumption and offspring development during the first 5 years of life.MethodsThis secondary analysis of an RCT included 162 father‐child pairs from pregnancies among women with pre‐pregnancy overweight or obesity. Paternal characteristics were reported at gestational week 15, reflecting the preceding 3 months. Offspring anthropometry was measured at birth, 6 and 18 months, 3 and 5 years. Associations were examined using linear mixed models and linear regression models.ResultsNo consistent associations were found between paternal characteristics and offspring outcomes from birth to 3 years. At age 5, higher paternal BMI was associated with higher offspring BMI z‐score (β = 0.07 (CI: 0.03; 0.10)), fat mass index (β = 0.07 kg/m2 (CI: 0.02; 0.12)) and fat‐free mass index (β = 0.05 kg/m2 (CI: 0.01; 0.08)). Lower paternal protein intake was associated with higher offspring BMI z‐score (β = 0.54 (CI: 0.07; 1.01)) and fat‐free mass index (β = 0.60 kg/m2 (CI: 0.13; 1.07)), while moderately higher protein intake was associated with higher waist‐to‐height ratio (β = 0.03 (CI: 3.00 × 10−3; 0.05)). Higher paternal GI was associated with lower offspring BMI z‐score (β = −0.04 (CI: −0.08; −2.14−10−3)) at age 5. Smoking and alcohol were not associated with offspring outcomes.ConclusionPaternal BMI was associated with offspring outcomes at age 5 years, while findings for paternal dietary factors were less consistent.Keywords: offsprng obesity, paternal nutrition, paternal obesity, preconception

Introduction

The global prevalence of childhood obesity is a growing public health concern, affecting 93 million children aged 5–14 years and 78 million adolescents aged 15–24 years in 2021 [1]. In 2020, 19% of 7‐year‐old Danish children were classified with overweight or obesity [2]. This prevalence is alarming due to its association with increased risks of cardiovascular disease, diabetes and various cancers later in life [3].

Obesity is a multifactorial condition influenced by genetic, socioeconomic and cultural factors [4]. Parental obesity substantially increases the likelihood of childhood obesity, with children under 10 years facing an increased risk of adult obesity regardless of their current weight status [5]. Numerous maternal factors before and during pregnancy, including higher body mass index (BMI) before pregnancy, excessive gestational weight gain and smoking during pregnancy, have been linked to a higher risk of offspring developing overweight and obesity [6, 7, 8]. Additionally, higher maternal dietary protein intake in early pregnancy has been linked to higher offspring birth weight and BMI [9, 10, 11, 12, 13].

A growing body of evidence suggests that pre‐conceptional paternal factors may be associated with offspring health. Potential mechanisms include alterations in sperm DNA methylation, histone modifications and small non‐coding RNAs in response to paternal BMI, diet, smoking and alcohol consumption [14, 15, 16, 17]. Because sperm cells are continuously renewed, paternal lifestyle during the months preceding conception may represent a relevant exposure period [18].

Previous studies have reported associations between paternal BMI and offspring growth and adiposity, whereas evidence concerning paternal dietary intake, smoking and alcohol consumption remains limited and heterogeneous [14, 15, 16]. Much of the available evidence is based on animal models and relatively few human studies have examined repeated offspring growth or body composition across early childhood [14, 15, 16, 17].

Therefore, this study examined associations of paternal BMI, dietary protein intake, dietary glycemic index (GI), alcohol consumption and smoking status with offspring weight development during the first 5 years of life in a secondary analysis of the ‘An optimised programming of healthy children’ (APPROACH) study.

Methods

Study Design

The APPROACH study was a randomised controlled trial (RCT) conducted during pregnancy to investigate whether a high‐protein, low‐GI diet could limit gestational weight gain and improve maternal and offspring outcomes among women with pre‐pregnancy overweight or obesity. The trial was conducted between January 2014 and December 2017, with a subsequent long‐term follow‐up period from June 2014 to January 2023, following offspring from birth to age 5 years. The intervention and offspring assessments from birth, including the first half of the 3‐year‐olds, were conducted at Copenhagen University Hospital Herlev‐Gentofte; the second half of the 3‐year and all the 5‐year assessments were performed at the Department of Nutrition, Exercise and Sports, University of Copenhagen (Copenhagen, Denmark). Detailed study methodology has been described previously [19]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. The study was accepted by the Ethical Committee of the Capital Region of Denmark (H‐3‐2013‐119) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01894139). All procedures adhered to the principles of the Helsinki II Declaration.

Participants

Pregnant women were recruited during their trans‐nuchal scan (11 weeks +4 days to 13 weeks +6 days of gestation). Eligibility criteria included a pre‐pregnancy BMI of 28–45 kg/m2, age ≥ 18 years, singleton pregnancy, spoke and understood Danish and planned delivery at a Capital Region hospital. Exclusion criteria included weight fluctuations > 10 kg in the previous year, drinking more than 14 alcohol units per week, drug abuse, critical illness, eating disorders or gestational diabetes mellitus. No inclusion criteria were applied for paternal participation.

Intervention

The participants were organised into clusters of 4–8 individuals and randomised in a 1:1 ratio to either an intervention or a control diet. The intervention diet was a high‐protein‐low‐glycemic index diet with women consuming 25% of energy per day (E%) from protein and a glycemic index (GI) of 45, while the control diet was moderate‐protein‐moderate‐GI, providing 18 E% from protein and a GI of 54. Both diets provided approximately 30% E% from fat and participants were advised to eat ad libitum. The women received nine counselling sessions with registered clinical dietitians to support dietary adherence during pregnancy [19].

Paternal Dietary Intake

At gestational week 15, both parents provided details on their dietary habits. Fathers were instructed to report their habitual dietary intake during the 3 months preceding questionnaire completion, thereby approximating the pre‐conceptional period. Paternal dietary intake was assessed using a validated Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) designed to measure dietary intake of protein, GI and glycemic load in pregnant women with obesity [20]. Participants specified portion sizes, food products and consumption frequency, ranging from ‘never’ to ‘several times per day’. Nutrient intake was estimated using DankostPro software (Kraftvaerk FoodTech, Denmark). GI values for individual foods were assigned based on three descending levels of confidence: (1) published values for specific foods, (2) equivalent values for similar foods or (3) categorisation into low, medium or high GI ranges [21]. Carbohydrate‐containing foods were grouped by GI values [22] and an overall dietary GI was calculated for each participant as a weighted mean based on reported intake using an Excel‐based tool (Microsoft, USA).

Paternal Anthropometry and Demographic Characteristics

At gestational week 15, fathers self‐reported information on height, weight, age, educational level, weekly cigarette use, monthly alcohol intake, paternity confirmation, weight history, mental health conditions, chronic diseases, medication use and cohabitation with the mother. Fathers were instructed to report smoking use, alcohol consumption, medication use and living with the mother based on their habitual status during the 3 months preceding questionnaire completion, thereby approximating the pre‐conceptional period. Highest level of completed education was categorised as follows: primary or lower secondary education, vocational education, short higher education (< 3 years after primary or lower secondary education), intermediate higher education (3–4 years after primary or lower secondary education), long higher education (> 5 years after primary or lower secondary education) or other education.

Maternal Anthropometry

Maternal pre‐pregnancy weight was obtained from general practitioner records measured at gestational weeks 6 to 8. At gestational week 15, height was measured to the nearest 0.5 cm using a wall‐mounted stadiometer (Seca, Germany) and weight to the nearest 0.1 kg using a medical scale (Tanita, Illinois, USA). Gestational weight gain was calculated as the difference between the final recorded weight before birth and pre‐pregnancy weight. BMI was determined as weight in kg divided by the square of height. Parity was categorised as 0, 1 or ≥ 2.

Offspring Anthropometry

At birth, weight was measured by a midwife using a medical beam scale (Tanita; Illinois, USA) accurate to 10 g and length using a non‐elastic measuring tape to the nearest 0.5 cm at Copenhagen University Hospital Herlev‐Gentofte. At 6 months (±4 weeks) and 18 months (±4 weeks), offspring weight was also measured by a medical beam scale, while length was measured in the supine position using a standardised measuring mat (ADE MZ10027‐1) to the nearest 0.5 cm. At 3 years (±4 weeks) and 5 years (±4 weeks) of age, offspring height was measured to the nearest 0.5 cm using a wall‐mounted stadiometer (Seca, Germany) and weight was assessed using a bioelectrical impedance scale (InBody570, USA), accurate to 100 g.

At ages 3 and 5 years, offspring's waist circumference was measured to the nearest millimetre using a non‐elastic measuring tape. The child stood upright on a firm surface with arms hanging relaxed along the sides of the body and feet placed approximately hip width apart (10–12 cm). Measurements in triplicate were taken at the waist, defined as the level of the umbilicus. The tape was applied around the abdomen, ensuring it remained in position without compressing the skin.

Offspring waist‐to‐height ratio was calculated by dividing the waist measure in cm by the length/height of the child in cm.

At age 5, fat mass (FM) and fat‐free mass (FFM) were assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis (InBody570, USA). The FM index was calculated as FM in kg divided by height (m) squared and the FFM index as FFM in kg divided by height (m) squared.

Z‐scores for growth were computed using the WHO Child Growth standards (2006) [23] via the anthro package in R for offspring up to 60 months of age. For offspring older than 60 months, z‐scores were derived using the WHO 2007 References for School‐Age Children and Adolescents (5 to 19 years) via the anthroplus package in R [24, 25].

Gestational age was determined via first‐trimester ultrasound scan (11 weeks +2 days to 13 weeks +6 days of gestation) based on crown‐rump length measurements.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation, SD) for normally distributed data and median (25th percentile, P25, to 75th percentile, P75) for skewed data. The Shapiro–Wilk tests and histograms were used for distribution assessment. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages.

For this secondary analysis, intervention groups were pooled, as previous findings from the APPROACH study showed no maternal intervention effects on neonatal outcomes [19]. The maternal intervention group was included as a covariate to account for potential residual intervention effects.

Offspring BMI z‐score at 5 years was defined as the primary outcome, as it is an age‐ and sex‐standardised measure of child weight status that has been used in previous studies on paternal pre‐conceptional exposures and offspring growth or weight status and was available in a larger proportion of participants than body composition measures. FM index at 5 years was defined as a key secondary adiposity‐specific outcome. Remaining outcomes, including length/height z‐score, waist‐to‐height ratio, FFM index and body fat percentage, were considered exploratory and hypothesis‐generating.

Associations between paternal exposures and repeated offspring outcomes from birth to age 5 years were analysed using linear mixed models. Subject ID was included as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements within the same child. Timepoint was modelled as a categorical fixed effect without an intercept and paternal exposures were included as timepoint‐specific terms, allowing exposure‐outcome associations to be estimated separately at each age. Thus, the reported beta estimates represent age‐specific associations between each paternal exposure and offspring outcome.

Paternal BMI and dietary GI were modelled as continuous variables, while paternal protein intake, smoking and alcohol use were modelled as categorical variables. Protein intake was categorised into quartiles, with the second quartile (Q2) used as the reference group. This approach was chosen as Q2 represented the protein intake closest to the Danish recommended intake of 0.83 g/kg body weight per day [26], thereby allowing comparison of both lower and higher intake levels relative to recommended intake. FM index, FFM index and body fat percentage at age 5 years were analysed using linear regression models. Three models were applied. Model 1 was unadjusted, while Model 2 was adjusted for gestational age, offspring sex, maternal diet intervention with either 18 or 25 E% of protein per day, gestational weight gain, parity and maternal pre‐pregnancy BMI. Model 3 was further adjusted for the highest level of parental educational level and maternal and paternal age. Model 3 was considered the final model. No correlation was found between paternal and maternal pre‐conceptional BMI.

Dropout bias was assessed by comparing baseline paternal, maternal and offspring characteristics between participants included and excluded from the analytical sample (Table S1). Continuous variables were compared using Welch's t‐test, with normality of residuals assessed using QQ plots. Categorical variables were compared using chi‐squared tests.

Multiple sensitivity analyses were performed. First, paternal BMI was categorised using BMI cutoffs of ≥ 25 kg/m2 and BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 to assess potential threshold associations with offspring primary and secondary outcomes (Table S2). Second, because paternal BMI was self‐reported at gestational week 15 rather than measured directly during the pre‐conceptional period, paternal BMI was recalculated under two scenarios allowing for plausible short‐term variation of −0.5 kg/m2 and + 0.5 kg/m2, based on previous studies on adult BMI variation over time [27, 28]. Analyses using BMI categories of ≥ 25 kg/m2 and BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 were then repeated using these alternative BMI values. Third, to account for potential bias due to self‐reported height and weight, paternal BMI was corrected using the Connor Gorber correction equation for men: corrected BMI = −1.08 + 1.08 × self‐reported BMI [29]. These sensitivity analyses were conducted using the fully adjusted model (Table S3). To assess whether selection into the analytical sample influenced the findings, inverse probability weighting was used as an additional sensitivity analysis. Stabilised weights for inclusion in the analytical sample were estimated from baseline characteristics and normalised to have a mean of 1 among included participants. The fully adjusted models for the primary and secondary outcomes were then repeated using these estimates (Table S4).

Sex‐specific associations were examined using sex‐stratified models and sex‐by‐exposure interaction terms in the fully adjusted model (Table S9). Due to small group sizes for paternal protein intake, alcohol use and smoking status, these analyses were restricted to paternal BMI and dietary GI in relation to repeated offspring BMI z‐score, length/height z‐score and waist‐to‐height ratio.

Model assumptions were evaluated using QQ plots and residual plots. Where model assumptions were not adequately met, the outcome was natural log transformed. This applied to FM index in the analyses of paternal protein intake, dietary GI, alcohol consumption and smoking status. For models with log‐transformed outcomes, regression coefficients and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) were multiplied by 100 and interpreted as approximate percentage differences in the outcome. Estimates from all other models were presented on the original outcome scale. Results are presented as estimates with 95% CIs and p‐values, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) in RStudio (version 2026.05.1 + 225; Posit Software).

Results

Of the 279 pregnant women enrolled in the APPROACH study (Figure 1), 100 participants were excluded due to missing data on confounders, fathers or their offspring. Additionally, 17 father‐child pairs were removed due to pre‐term or post‐term births or non‐biological fathers. The final analysis included data from 162 father‐child pairs, with dropout rates of 9% at 18 months (N = 14), 21% at 3 years (N = 34) and 28% at 5 years (N = 45). Differences in maternal, offspring and most paternal baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants were generally small (Table S1). However, a higher proportion of fathers were current smokers in the excluded group compared with the participants used in the main analysis.

Paternal and Offspring Characteristics

At gestational week 15, the mean paternal age was 34 years and more than half of the fathers were classified with overweight or obesity (Table 1). The majority of the fathers lived together with their offspring's mother, were non‐smokers and drank below the recommended Danish alcohol recommendations of a maximum of 10 units per week [30]. Median protein intake was 0.99 g/kg body weight/day and mean dietary GI was 55. Their offspring were born in gestational week 40 with a mean birth weight of 3627 g (Table 1).

Additionally, higher paternal BMI was associated with greater offspring length/height z‐scores at 6 months, 18 months, 3 years and 5 years, whereas no clear association was observed at birth (Table 2). Paternal BMI was not clearly associated with offspring waist‐to‐height ratio at ages 3 and 5 years.

In sensitivity analyses using clinical BMI cut‐offs, offspring of fathers with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 had a 0.46‐SD higher BMI z‐score (95% CI: 0.10; 0.82) and a 0.57‐kg/m2 higher FM index (95% CI: 0.09; 1.05) at age 5 years compared with offspring of fathers with a BMI < 25 kg/m2 (Table S2).

For paternal BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2, estimates from the primary categorical analysis were less precise and provided no clear evidence of differences in offspring BMI z‐score or FM index. After adding 0.5 kg/m2 to the reported paternal BMI values, the estimates for BMI z‐score and FM index at age 5 years were larger than in the primary categorical analysis. After subtracting 0.5 kg/m2 from the reported paternal BMI values, estimates for the ≥ 30 kg/m2 category remained imprecise, with the CIs including the null at age 5 years (Table S2). Applying the Connor‐Gorber‐correction resulted in estimates similar to those from the original continuous BMI analyses (Table S3).

In sex‐stratified analyses, estimates for the associations of paternal BMI with offspring BMI z‐score and length/height z‐score appeared larger and more pronounced among girls than boys at some ages, although differences by sex were not consistent across ages (Table S9). For waist‐to‐height ratio, interaction tests indicated differences by offspring sex at ages 3 and 5 years. Among girls, each 10‐kg/m2 higher paternal BMI was associated with a 0.03‐unit higher waist‐to‐height ratio at age 3 years (95% CI: 5.76 × 10−3; 0.06) and a 0.04‐unit higher ratio at age 5 years (95% CI: 0.02; 0.06) (Table S9). Corresponding estimates among boys were close to zero.

Paternal Dietary Protein Intake and Offspring Anthropometry

Across paternal dietary protein‐intake quartiles, defined according to the distribution of paternal protein intake and illustrated in Figure S1, no clear differences were observed in offspring length/height z‐scores from birth to 5 years (Table 3). Likewise, no clear differences in offspring BMI z‐score were observed from birth to 3 years. At age 5 years, offspring of fathers in Q1 had a 0.54‐SD higher BMI z‐score than offspring of fathers in Q2 (95% CI: 0.07; 1.01) (Figure 2).

Discussion

The associations between paternal characteristics and offspring outcomes changed across early life. No consistent associations were observed from birth to 3 years, whereas several associations emerged at age 5 years. Most notably, higher paternal BMI was associated with higher offspring BMI z‐score and adiposity‐related measures at age 5 years. Findings for paternal dietary factors were less consistent, while no associations were observed for paternal smoking or alcohol intake. Given the exploratory nature of this secondary analysis, individual findings should be interpreted with caution.

Our findings suggest no association between paternal BMI and offspring birth weight, although approximately 69% of fathers had a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (N = 109). In contrast, a cohort study from the United States of 429 father‐offspring pairs reported that paternal BMI > 25 kg/m2 was associated with higher offspring birth weight [31], while another cohort study supported our findings [32]. Differences in study populations may partly explain these inconsistent findings. In the APPROACH study, mothers were included based on BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2 and maternal adiposity and related intrauterine factors may therefore have had a strong influence on fetal growth. This may have reduced the ability to detect an independent association between paternal BMI and birth weight. However, our study cannot disentangle paternal influences from maternal intrauterine factors or shared family‐level characteristics.

Although we did not observe a clear association between paternal BMI and offspring birth weight, higher paternal BMI was associated with higher offspring BMI z‐score, FM index, FFM index and body fat percentage at age 5 years, but not with waist‐to‐height ratio. This discrepancy may partly reflect differences between adiposity measures, as waist‐to‐height ratio captures central body fat distribution [33], whereas FM index and body fat percentage reflect more general adiposity. Thus, the findings may indicate associations with overall body size and adiposity‐related measures rather than central adiposity specifically.

Several epigenetic mechanisms have been proposed to link paternal obesity with offspring adiposity and metabolic development, including obesity‐related changes in sperm DNA methylation, histone modifications and small non‐coding RNAs [14]. A cohort study including 429 parent‐offspring triads found that higher paternal BMI was associated with increased DNA methylation near the transcription start site of ARFGAP3, which in turn was linked to both lower offspring birth weight and 3‐year BMI z‐score [31]. Furthermore, higher paternal BMI was associated with persistent hypomethylation at CpG 04763273 in offspring blood across all examined ages, suggesting a stable epigenetic signal independent of maternal BMI and other covariates [31]. However, our study did not include data on paternal or offspring epigenetic markers. Therefore, potential epigenetic mechanisms remain speculative and cannot be distinguished from shared familial, behavioural or postnatal environmental influences within this study design.

Similar findings have been reported in larger observational studies. In a Norwegian cohort of 29 216 parent‐offspring triads, offspring BMI increased by 0.13 (95% CI: 0.13;0.17) units at age 3 years per one‐unit increase in paternal BMI, after adjustment [34]. In our study, we observed a smaller but comparable link, as for every one‐unit increase in pre‐conceptional paternal BMI, offspring BMI z‐score at 5 years of age increased by 0.07 SD.

Sex‐stratified analyses suggested that associations between paternal BMI and some offspring anthropometric outcomes may differ by offspring sex. Estimates for the association between paternal BMI and offspring BMI z‐score appeared larger among girls than boys at some ages, although formal interaction tests did not provide clear evidence of sex differences. In contrast, for waist‐to‐height ratio, interaction tests indicated differences by offspring sex at ages 3 and 5 years, with higher paternal BMI associated with higher waist‐to‐height ratio among girls, while corresponding estimates among boys were close to zero. However, these analyses were based on relatively small samples and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Human evidence regarding sex‐specific associations between paternal pre‐conceptional BMI and offspring adiposity remains limited. In a Canadian cohort of 218 children, higher paternal pre‐conceptional BMI was associated with higher offspring BMI z‐score growth rates in boys, but not in girls [35]. Similarly, a human study examining BMI trajectories from birth to age 5 years reported sex‐specific associations between maternal pre‐pregnancy BMI and gestational weight gain and offspring BMI trajectories, although the patterns differed between boys and girls [36]. Taken together, current human evidence does not indicate a consistent direction of sex‐specific associations. Larger studies are needed to investigate potential sex‐specific associations between parental pre‐conceptional adiposity and offspring growth and body composition.

Evidence on paternal pre‐conceptional protein intake is largely limited to animal models [14]. Similar to our finding for lower paternal protein intake and higher offspring BMI z‐score at age 5 years, one rodent study reported that low paternal protein intake led to higher offspring weight and an altered hepatic expression of fat mass and obesity‐associated gene (Fto), together with impaired glucose tolerance, compared to a standard protein diet [37]. Other rodent studies found that a paternal low‐protein diet before conception resulted in higher fetal weight [38, 39] with larger skeletons and reduced mineral density [38], partially aligning with our observed association between lower paternal protein intake and higher offspring FFM index. One possible explanation is that low paternal protein intake may influence the developmental programming of offspring growth and body composition rather than adiposity specifically. This interpretation is consistent with our observation of a higher FFM index, whereas no clear associations were observed with FM index or body fat percentage. However, whether such mechanisms operate in humans remains unknown and low paternal protein intake may also reflect other unmeasured dietary or lifestyle characteristics. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution given the lack of consistent results across outcomes or sensitivity analyses. In contrast, experimental studies of maternal protein intake suggest different potential mechanisms, including the protein leverage hypothesis, whereby higher maternal protein consumption may promote higher offspring food consumption and subsequent weight gain [40].

Waist‐to‐height ratio is a marker of body fat distribution and central obesity and has been shown to predict hypertension [33]. A systematic review identified 0.49 as the optimal cut‐off value for detecting abdominal obesity in both children and adolescents aged 3 to 19 years [41]. In our study, offspring waist‐to‐height ratio at age 5 years was higher among offspring of fathers in protein intake Q3 compared with Q2, whereas BMI z‐score was higher among offspring of fathers in Q1 compared with Q2. Although these outcomes capture different aspects of offspring anthropometry, the inconsistent pattern across protein quartiles and the lack of support from related adiposity measures weaken the interpretation. Furthermore, no associations were observed when paternal protein intake was analysed continuously or according to recommendations. These findings may therefore reflect chance, residual confounding or instability from categorising a continuous exposure and should be considered exploratory.

Paternal pre‐conceptional dietary GI has, to our knowledge, not previously been investigated in relation to offspring growth or adiposity development. In our study, higher paternal GI was associated with a lower BMI z‐score at age 5 years. This finding was unexpected and in contrast with evidence from maternal studies, including a Danish cohort study including 68 471 mother‐offspring pairs reporting that higher maternal GI intake during pregnancy was associated with a tendency towards increased offspring overweight and obesity, most pronounced at age 7 years [42]. Importantly, the association in our study was not supported by the inverse probability weighted sensitivity analysis, suggesting that it may be sensitive to differences between included and excluded participants. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory.

We found no associations between pre‐conceptional paternal smoking status or alcohol use and offspring outcomes. In contrast, an Irish cohort (N = 284) reported that pre‐conceptional paternal smoking was linked to offspring overweight and obesity at age 5 years, but not at birth or at age 9 years [43]. Another study reported that paternal smoking before age 11 years was associated with higher BMI in male offspring aged 7 to 17 years, with the association becoming significant from age 13 years [44]. The absence of similar findings in our study may be partly explained by the small number of fathers who smoked (N = 24). The impact of pre‐conceptional paternal alcohol consumption on offspring weight development has not been investigated in human studies. However, rodent studies have reported mixed findings. One study observed decreased weight in male offspring compared with controls [45], whereas another found no differences in offspring weight but observed reduced activity levels among offspring of alcohol‐exposed fathers [46].

A major strength of this study is its prospective design, spanning from gestational week 15 to offspring age 5 years, allowing for the assessment of long‐term father‐offspring associations. The use of bioimpedance analysis for body composition assessment at age 5 years further strengthens the study. However, as this was a secondary analysis, causal associations cannot be established and the relatively small sample size limited subgroup analyses and further categorisation of exposures. In addition, the exclusion of participants with missing paternal, offspring or covariate information may have introduced selection bias. Inverse probability weighted sensitivity analyses generally yielded estimates similar to the main analyses. However, the association between paternal GI and offspring BMI z‐score at age 5 years was attenuated and no longer supported, suggesting that this finding may have been sensitive to selection into the analytical sample.

Additionally, this study relied on paternal self‐reported data, which may be subject to recall or reporting bias. Paternal BMI was reported at gestational week 15 and used as a proxy for pre‐conceptional BMI, which may have introduced exposure misclassification. However, sensitivity analyses using Connor Gorber corrected BMI did not materially change the results, suggesting that bias from self‐reported height and weight was limited. Analyses allowing for ±0.5 kg/m2 variation in paternal BMI supported the findings for paternal BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, while results using the ≥ 30 kg/m2 cut‐off were more sensitive to reclassification, likely reflecting the small number of fathers with obesity (N = 37). Although exposure misclassification cannot be ruled out, the main findings appeared reasonably robust across sensitivity analyses.

The lack of paternal inclusion or exclusion criteria enhances generalisability in relation to fathers. However, approximately 69% of fathers had overweight or obesity, which was higher than the prevalence reported among Danish men in the general population during a comparable period [47]. This may partly reflect the recruitment of families in which mothers had a pre‐pregnancy BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2 and should be considered when generalising the findings to the broader paternal population. Furthermore, the study included only families living in the greater Copenhagen area, which may further limit generalisability.

Importantly, the same set of covariates was applied across all offspring ages, although environmental, behavioural and social influences increasingly shape growth trajectories throughout childhood [4]. The models did not include postnatal factors such as offspring diet, physical activity, sedentary behaviour, parental feeding practices, sleep patterns or evolving paternal and maternal lifestyle behaviours. These factors are likely to be important determinants of offspring growth and may act as shared family‐level confounders. Consequently, the adjustment for maternal gestational weight gain, parity and parental sociodemographic factors may not sufficiently account for shared lifestyle influences within the family. This represents a key limitation of the study and suggests that residual confounding is probable. The emergence of several associations only at age 5 years may therefore reflect increasing influence of shared postnatal environmental factors rather than direct pre‐conceptional effects.

Finally, no data on paternal pre‐conceptional physical activity were available and thus could not be assessed, despite previous studies suggesting that exercise prior to conception may protect against the transmission of obesity‐related traits to offspring and ameliorate adverse metabolic effects [48, 49, 50]. Future research should incorporate repeated, age‐specific measurements of both paternal and offspring lifestyle factors, alongside more detailed paternal phenotyping, to help differentiate between biological inheritance and shared family environment. Extended follow‐up into later childhood and adolescence, as well as exploration of sex‐specific pathways, will be important for understanding the timing and mechanisms underlying paternal influences on offspring growth, weight status and adiposity‐related outcomes.

Conclusion

Taken together, this secondary analysis showed that paternal BMI, assessed as a proxy for pre‐conceptional status, was associated with offspring anthropometric outcomes at age 5 years, whereas findings for pre‐conceptional dietary factors were less consistent. Given the exploratory nature of the study, the observed associations should be interpreted with caution and may reflect shared postnatal environmental influences rather than direct biological effects. Further research is needed to elucidate these pathways and to assess the long‐term impact of paternal health factors on offspring beyond early childhood. Additionally, potential mechanisms require further investigation in longitudinal human studies extending beyond early childhood.
