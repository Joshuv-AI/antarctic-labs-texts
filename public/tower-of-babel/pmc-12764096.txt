Cohort profile: the nurses’ mental health study
Authors: Dan Zhang, Qiang Yu, Meng Ning, Jiaxin Yang, Xuting Li, Zengyu Chen, Chongmei Huang, Yiting Liu, Zhenhui Ren, Chunhui Bin, Jianghao Yuan, Yamin Li, Yusheng Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOwing to the special nature of work and practice environment, nurses face relatively higher mental health risks. The mental health problems of nurses in China’s tertiary hospitals are particularly prominent. However, nationally representative longitudinal data on nurses’ mental health in China are lacking. The present paper describes the design and initial findings of the Nurses’ Mental Health Study (NMHS).MethodsThe study population comprised registered nurses employed at 67 tertiary hospitals across 31 provinces in mainland China. Survey content covers sociodemographic information, work-related factors, health status, lifestyle habits, objective experiences, family and social relationships, current affairs opinions, overall evaluation, and identity information. Data are collected annually and have been collected twice to date, with an initial follow-up period of five years.ResultsThe baseline survey (December 2023-January 2024) collected 132,910 valid questionnaires (effective response rate 89.9%, 93.5% female, ages 18–65 years), and the first follow-up (December 2024-January 2025) collected 131,713 valid questionnaires (effective response rate 90.8%, 92.9% female, ages 18–66 years). A total of 84,252 questionnaires were successfully matched between baseline and the first follow-up (matching rate 63.4%). Among the participants, 55.2% of participants reported depressive symptoms, 39.3% reported anxiety symptoms, and 45.0% reported insomnia symptoms. It should be noted that severity bands (e.g., mild) reflect symptom burden rather than clinical diagnoses. Suicidal ideation and self-harm behaviors were reported by 3.1% and 1.2% of participants, respectively. In the past year, 6.9% of nurses sought help from psychiatric or psychological services, and 15.4% participated in mental health promotion activities.ConclusionOur findings suggest that a substantial proportion of NMHS participants reported experiencing mental and psychological symptoms. However, the mental health service use rate was low. These findings highlight the necessity of routine psychological screening and the optimization of mental health promotion services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04128-z.Keywords: Nurse, Mental health, Cohort profile

Introduction

Nurses are the largest group in the entire health care workforce, accounting for about 59% of health professionals [1]. However, due to their special nature of work and practice environment, such as shift work, needling and sharp instrument injuries, workplace violence and bullying, nurses face relatively higher mental health risks [2–5]. Since 1976, several countries, including the United States, Denmark, Japan and Australia, have carried out nurses’ health cohort studies to investigate the causes of diseases, develop prevention and intervention strategies, and enhance nurses’ mental health [6–9].

Recently, our team conducted the first scoping review of nurses’ mental health cohort studies, including 171 cohort studies from 16 countries [2]. The results showed that nurses face extensive and serious mental health challenges, including depression, anxiety, cognitive dysfunction, post-traumatic stress disorder, job burnout, sleep disorders [2]. These psychological problems not only endanger the physical and mental health, and the quality of life of nurses, but also reduce the quality of nursing services, jeopardize the safety of patients, increase the turnover rate, and exacerbate the global shortage of nursing human resources [10, 11]. Although a large number of cohort studies have revealed multi-dimensional modifiable risk factors related to nurses’ mental health (such as shift work, lifestyle, workplace violence, level of social support, and job demands and resources.), 95.3% of cohort studies come from high-income economies, and there is a lack of cohort evidence from low - and middle-income economies [2].

By 2024, there are about 5.84 million registered nurses in China, accounting for nearly one-fifth of the global nursing workforce [12]. However, the density of nurses in China (3.67 per 1000 population) is much lower than that of developed regions such as America (13.38 per 1000 population) [13]. Therefore, the workload of nurses in China is significantly overwhelming. Particularly, nurses often experience greater work pressure and more severe mental health challenges in tertiary hospitals, as they are responsible for high-level clinical care, teaching and scientific research tasks [14]. At present, most studies on Chinese nurses’ mental health study rely on cross-sectional data and focus on single psychological symptoms (such as anxiety, depression, and burnout) [15, 16]. According to the psychopathological network theory, mental disorder is a network system composed of dynamic causal interactions among multiple symptoms [17]. Thus, longitudinal cohort data, which enable the tracking of individual psychological trajectories over time, may help reveal interaction mechanism between symptoms.

To our knowledge, two Chinese nurses’ health cohorts have addressed mental health content (the TARGET study and the National Nurses’ Health Study) [18, 19]. However, these samples were limited to a single hospital or a single province, restricting their generalizability to the national level. To address this gap, we established the Nurses’ Mental Health Study (NMHS), which may represent the first large-scale longitudinal study on nurses’ mental health in China [20]. The main objectives of this study are: (1) to investigate the mental health status of nurses in tertiary hospitals in China, including its prevalence, severity, trajectory, and consequences, (2) To explore the influence of personal, occupational and social environmental factors on nurses’ mental health and their interaction, in order to provide scientific basis for formulating intervention strategies and health policies for nurses.

Methods

Study design and participants

The NMHS is a nationwide multicentre prospective cohort study and used cluster sampling to recruit participants. Firstly, 2–3 tertiary hospitals were selected from each of the 31 province-level administrative regions (hereafter, ‘provinces’) in mainland China, making up 67 primary sampling sites (two for each, except for Hunan, Sichuan, Jiangxi, Zhejiang and Guangdong, where three hospitals in each were included). Secondly, to ensure adequate sample size, we preferentially selected large hospitals (≥ 2000 beds) in most provinces (remote provinces were exempt from this bed-size requirement). Hospitals were recruited via the research team’s professional network. Thus, the inclusion criteria of hospitals are: (1) general tertiary hospitals; (2) ≥ 2000 beds (exempted for remote provinces); (3) capable of dealing with a range of disease types similar to other tertiary hospitals in the provinces; (4) willing to participate in the NMHS.

The target population for data collection was nurses working in 67 tertiary hospitals from 31 provinces of the Chinese mainland. The inclusion criteria were: (1) Age 18 years or older, (2) Full-time employment as a registered nurse in a tertiary hospital. The exclusion criteria were: (1) on sick or maternity leave, or retired; (2) nurses currently receiving professional training in the hospital.

Recruitment procedures

Prior to data collection, two trained researchers sent invitation letters to the nursing directors of the selected hospitals, introducing the research content and instructions on data usage. After obtaining hospital approval, the hospitals provided lists of eligible potential participants based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The two researchers then assigned each hospital a unique QR code linked to the online informed consent form and questionnaire. These QR codes were distributed to potential participants via WeChat (a popular social application in China).

Once nurses logged in, they were first presented with an online informed consent form stating the following: (1) this is a national multicentre cohort study concerning mental health and associated factors; (2) after baseline recruitment, there will be annual follow-ups over the next 5 years or longer; (3) the participation is voluntary and they could refuse or withdraw at any time; (4) data will only be used for research purposes and will be kept confidential; (5) personal information (e.g., email address, phone numbers) will be stored securely and used solely for follow-up invitations; (6) participating in the study and completion of questionnaires will not affect their life or career.

After providing consent, nurses were enrolled in the study. The online questionnaires then became accessible and typically required approximately 15–20 min to complete. If participants had any questions or encountered problems, they could contact the research team via email, telephone, or WeChat. Upon completion, participants received a digital mental-health self-care resource pack from the research team by email.

The baseline survey was conducted from December 2023 to January 2024, with questionnaires distributed to 147,832 nurses and 135,161 responses received (response rate: 91.4%). After excluding duplicate and low-quality questionnaires, 132,910 questionnaires were included in the baseline data (effective response rate: 89.9%). Of the respondents, 6.5% (n = 8666) were male, and 93.5% (n = 124,244) were female, with a mean age of 33.5 ± 7.3 years (range: 18–65 years). The first follow-up was conducted from December 2024 to January 2025. Questionnaires were distributed to 145,036 nurses, receiving 132,255 responses (response rate: 91.2%). After removing duplicates and low-quality submissions, 131,713 questionnaires were included in the follow-up dataset (effective response rate: 90.8%). Among them, 7.1% (n = 9339) were male, and 92.9% (n = 122,374) were female, with a mean age of 33.8 ± 7.3 years (range: 18–66 years).

To match baseline and follow-up data, a unique identification number was assigned to each participant, incorporating the year of birth, date of birth, telephone number, and the last four digits of the national identification number. Baseline and follow-up data were automatically matched against this identification number, and duplicate questionnaires were manually checked with the use of variables such as sex, department, years of employment, contact number, and e-mail. Finally, 84,252 pairs of valid matching records were obtained (matching rate was 63.4%). The participation rates are shown in Fig. 1.

Measures

We designed the questionnaire according to the research objectives and distributed it online via the Questionnaire Star platform. The questionnaire consisted of self-reported short screening scales and standardized questions. The collected information included sociodemographic information, work-related factors, health status, lifestyle habits, objective experiences, family and social relationships, current affairs opinions, overall evaluations, and identification information. To ensure data quality and validity, multiple criteria were considered in selecting the survey instruments. We prioritized screening instruments that were shorter, exhibited good psychometric properties, and had been validated for reliability and validity [21, 22]. In addition, preference was given to scales and questions previously employed in large cohort studies or published papers [23, 24], ensuring methodological rigor and comparability across studies. The English version of the questionnaire and the associated reference materials have been uploaded as supplementary files (see S1 and S2).

In the first follow-up survey, several modifications were made to the baseline questionnaire to accommodate emerging research needs. Specifically, we (1) introduced new variables of interest (e.g., hidden absenteeism), (2) removed items deemed irrelevant to our research aims (e.g., handedness), and (3) refined the wording and content of existing scales (e.g., replacing standardized questions with the scale of Insomnia Severity Index to assess insomnia).

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were measured by the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). It consists of 9 items. Each item is scored from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), resulting in a total score of 0–27. Higher score indicated more severe symptoms. Scores of 0–4 indicated no depression, 5–9 mild, 10–14 moderate, 15–19 moderately severe, and 20–27 severe depression. This scale has been used in Chinese nurse populations, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.96 in prior studies [25]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.90 at baseline and 0.90 at follow-up.

Anxiety symptoms

Anxiety symptoms were measured by the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7). It consists of 7 items. Each item is scored from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), resulting in a total score of 0–21. Higher scores indicate more severe anxiety symptoms: none (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14) and severe (>15). This scale has been used in Chinese nurse populations, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.97 in prior studies [25]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.94 at baseline and 0.94 at follow-up.

Perceived stress

Perceived stress was measured by the 4-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-4). It consists of 4 items. Each item is scored from 0 (never) to 4 (very often), resulting in a total score of 0–16. Higher scores indicated greater perceived stress. This scale has been used in Chinese nurse populations [26].

Insomnia symptoms

The baseline insomnia symptoms were determined by three standard questions, that had been used in previous studies [27]: DIS: Did you ever have difficulties in falling sleep in the past two weeks? DMS: Did you ever have difficulties in maintaining sleep in the past two weeks? and EMA: Did you ever wake up in the middle of the night or early morning and have difficulties in falling asleep again in the past two weeks. There were three options to reply to these questions: “No”, “Sometimes” and “Often”. If a participant answered “often” to any of the three questions, they were classified as having insomnia symptoms.

Insomnia symptoms

The follow-up insomnia symptoms were measured by the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI). It consists of 7 items. Each item is scored from 0 (no problem) to 4 (very severe), resulting in a total score of 0–28. Higher score indicated more severe symptoms. Insomnia severity can be divided into “none” (0–7), “mild” (8–14), “moderate” (15–21), and “severe” (22–28). This scale has been used in Chinese nurse populations, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.93 in prior studies [28]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.90 at baseline and 0.90 at follow-up.

Obsessive–compulsive symptoms

Obsessive–compulsive symptoms were measured by five items from the SCL-90 obsessive–compulsive subscale (items 3, 10, 38, 45, 65), that had been used in previous studies [29]. It consists of 5 items. Each item is scored from 0 (none) to 4 (severe), resulting in a total score of 0–20. Higher score indicated more severe symptoms.

Burnout indicator

Burnout was assessed with a single-item indicator: “In the past year, do you feel burnout?” Responses were on a 5-point Likert scale: 1 = not at all, I enjoy my job; 2 = no, I feel pressure and am not always as energetic as before; 3 = a little, I have fatigue symptoms such as emotional exhaustion; 4 = very, I always feel frustrated at work; 5 = extremely, I need help. This single-item measure has been used previously [30].

Suicidal ideation

We used the second item of Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C- SSRS): “In the past year, have you seriously considered committing suicide?” (Yes, no, prefer not to answer), which has been used previously [31].

Self-harm behaviour

We used a single-item screener adapted from the UK Biobank “Thoughts and Feelings” mental-health questionnaire (Harm module): “In the past year, have you intentionally harmed yourself?” (Yes / No / Prefer not to answer). The item and response options follow the UK Biobank format for self-harm/suicidality items [32].

Mental-health service use

We assessed mental-health service use using two single-item questions. Two items were directly adopted from Tian et al. [33] to capture service use: “Have you ever consulted psychiatry/psychology outpatient services?” (Yes/No/Prefer not to answer) and “If you had mental-health problems, would you seek professional help?” (Yes/No/Unsure).

Data management and usage

All collected data were uploaded and stored on an encrypted data platform by the data manager using a dual entry system. Two independent researchers performed data cleaning before the analyses, following three steps. First, the duplicated questionnaires were removed. During the baseline survey, questionnaires were deemed duplicates if the composite ID (comprising of the year of birth, date of birth, telephone number, and the last four digits of the national identification number) was an exact match. In such cases, the earliest submission was retained. During follow-up, the rule was refined as follows: when two questionnaires had identical composite IDs, we conducted manual verification using information such as sex, department, years of service, telephone number, and email address; if duplication was confirmed, the earliest submission was retained. Next, low quality data were removed. The low quality data were identified as follows: (1) data with obvious logical errors, such as the difference between the age and working years was smaller than 16 years old, being pregnant with a gestational week of zero, duration of religious belief longer than the actual age, or weekly working hours exceeding 168 h (7 days a week, 24 h a day); (2) outliers, defined as values outside the Mean ± 3SD, or values lower than P25 − 3IQR and higher than P75 + 3IQR [34]. Questionnaires with three or more such conflicts will be excluded.

Participant information was stored in the database and was accessible only to the team’s core researchers. To access the research data, researchers were required to submit a detailed plan to the NMHS management group for approval and to sign a confidentiality agreement.

Statistical analyses

We summarised categorical variables as n (%) and continuous variables as mean (SD) to describe the cohort profile. To assess between-group balance (matched vs. unmatched cohorts), we reported absolute standardised mean differences (|SMD|) for each variable. |SMD| < 0.10 was taken to indicate good balance. All analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 (Windows).

Handling of adverse events and emergencies

Risk assessment of suicide and self-harm behaviors: If the nurse fills out the questionnaire and assesses that they have the risk of self-harm and suicide, the page after completing the questionnaire will show access to professional mental health services (such as psychological assistance hotlines and recommendations from authoritative psychiatric hospitals). In addition, the research team will provide a professional self-care manual to them via email. For details, please refer to Supplementary S3.

Table 3 presents the main characteristics of the participants at baseline and at the first follow-up. Among the 132,910 baseline nurses surveyed, 93.5% were female. The participants were aged 18 to 65 years, with a mean age of 33.5 ± 7.3 years, and 50.0% were aged between 30 and 39 years. 92.8% were Han Chinese individuals. 68.7% were married and 93.5% reported no religious belief. The majority (87.1%) held a bachelor’s degree. The professional titles were chiefly junior (51.6%) and intermediate (43.6%), with 94.5% holding non-administrative positions. 93.9% of the participants were engaged in clinical nursing work, of which 26.0% were from internal medicine and 22.1% from surgery. A total of 88.2% had worked for 20 years or less. 72.2% undertaking night shifts. In terms of lifestyle, in the past year, 96.3% of the participants never smoked and 61.2% never drank alcohol. 59.1% drank milk tea less than once a week. 42.7% exercised once a week or less, with 41.3% reporting no physical activity. 67.2% had a normal-range body mass index.

Table 4 presents participants’ mental health status and utilization of mental health services at baseline and first follow-up. In terms of the prevalence of mental health problems, the results of the baseline survey indicate that 55.2% of the participants reported varying degrees of depressive symptoms, 39.3% reported varying degrees of anxiety symptoms, and 24.1% reported insomnia symptoms. At the first follow-up, the assessment of insomnia shifted from standardized questions to the Insomnia Severity Index. The results of the first follow-up indicated that 45.0% of the participants reported varying degrees of insomnia symptoms. Because of this, the baseline (24.1%) and follow-up (45.0%) insomnia prevalence shouldn’t be interpreted as temporal change. Suicidal ideation and self-harm behaviors were reported by 3.1% and 1.2%, respectively. A small percentage of participants had a physician-diagnosed mental disorders (depressive disorder, 3.4%, anxiety disorder, 4.8%, and obsessive–compulsive disorder, 1.7%). In addition, 10.8% scored “No burnout” on the burnout indicator.

Regarding the severity of mental health symptoms, 41.8% of nurses reported mild depressive symptoms, 8.9% moderate, 3.2% moderate to severe, and 1.3% severe. For anxiety symptoms, 33.4% of nurses reported mild symptoms, 4.2% moderate, and 1.7% severe. The results at the first follow-up were similar. Additionally, the first follow-up survey showed that 30.4% of nurses reported mild insomnia symptoms, 11.3% moderate, and 3.3% severe.

In the positive psychological domain, a significant proportion of participants were satisfied with specific life domains: friendships (95.4%), family (89.8%), job (86.3%), physical health (77.3%), and financial status (69.8%). Furthermore, 62.5% indicated that they were always or often able to adapt to life changes, and 57.9% reported that they could always or often able to adjust their mindset when facing illness or adversity.

In terms of mental-health service use and engagement, a total of 12.0% of participants reported using a hypnotic in the past two weeks (7.1% self-medicated and 4.8% prescribed by a physician). In addition, 60.4% of nurses stated that they would seek professional help when experiencing psychological problems, 6.9% consulted a psychiatric or psychological service in the past year, 57.3% worked in hospitals that provided dedicated mental health support for staff, and 15.4% participated in mental health promotion activities in the past year. In addition, we additionally summarized baseline exposures to workplace violence, bullying, and adverse clinical events. Descriptive distributions at baseline are reported in Supplementary S4.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, NMHS is the largest nurse mental health cohort in the country to date, which spans 67 tertiary hospitals across all 31 provinces of mainland China. Our findings contribute critical evidence for middle-income countries and regions. Understanding the long-term dynamic development of nurses’ psychological status is crucial for the formulation of relevant policies.

The findings indicated that a significant proportion of participants in the NMHS reported experiencing mental and psychological symptoms. Depressive symptom was the most common mental health problem in NMHS (55.2%). Despite differences in the psychological assessment tools used, our findings were highly similar to those reported during the COVID-19 pandemic [14, 35]. A national cross-sectional survey showed that during the COVID-19 period, the prevalence of depression among Chinese nurses was 55.5% (including 20.8% mild depression, 28.3% moderate depression, and 6.5% severe depression), anxiety was 41.8%. This was similar to the overall prevalence of anxiety and depression in our study. The difference was that an increase in mild depression and a decrease in severe depression among NMHS nurses. Overall, many nurses experienced mild mood symptoms, but relatively few experienced moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms and anxiety symptoms, underscoring the need for early detection and effective intervention to prevent more complex and persistent psychological disorders. It should be noted that reported percentages reflect screening-based symptom burden (severity bands such as ‘mild’), not clinician-diagnosed disorder prevalence; therefore, comparisons with interview-based population ‘prevalence’ are inappropriate. In addition, insomnia was assessed with different instruments across waves, so the 24.1% and 45.0% figures are not directly comparable and should not be interpreted as change over time.

It is worth noting that although a significant proportion of nurses reported psychological symptoms such as anxiety and depression, most participants maintained high satisfaction in key life domains such as friendships, work, and family. Prior studies suggest two explanations. First, nurses’ strong adaptability and psychological resilience may buffer adverse effects [36, 37]. Second, social support from friends, family, and the workplace plays a critical role [37, 38]. Therefore, life satisfaction of nurses can be improved by cultivating resources related to resilience and strengthening social support networks.

Regarding hypnotics, the results of the study found that the use of hypnotics by nurses was 12% in the past two weeks. This is higher than the 8.01% reported by psychiatric nurses in China [39]. and the 6.5% to 9.4% reported by health care workers in Spain [40], below the 14.1% in Norwegian nurses [41]. These rates depend largely on the definition used (e.g., prescription drugs only or over-the-counter drugs included), the time frame of assessment, and regional variations [39–41]. However, it is undeniable that the use of hypnotics is high among Chinese nurses. Based on previous studies, this may reflect shift-work related sleep disorders and the heavy burden of chronic occupational stress among nurses [42]. This is associated with nurses’ physical and mental health, quality of life, and daytime work performance [43]. In addition, more than half (7.1%) of the nurses who took medication did so by themselves. Studies have shown that differences in the dose, combination, and frequency of use of hypnotics may lead to different efficacy and consequences [43, 44]. Medications for insomnia require a comprehensive assessment of factors such as circadian rhythm and depression, especially chronic insomnia. Therefore, Sleep health should be included in routine occupational health assessment, shift system should be optimized, and non-pharmacological interventions such as sleep hygiene education and CBT-I should be promoted. At the same time, the prescription and use of hypnotic drugs should be standardized, and the medication monitoring and professional guidance should be strengthened to reduce the sleep disorders and drug dependence of nurses.

Regarding mental health services, the results found that the willingness of nurses to seek professional help was high (60.4%), consistent with the previous research findings that 64.7% of nurses reported a moderate to high need for psychological counseling [45]. Based on previous studies, this may be related to the higher level of knowledge and positive attitudes towards mental illness among nurses [46]. However, only 6.9% of the nurses had consulted a professional organization in the past year. Moreover, although 61% of the nurses reported that their hospital had a special psychological care organization, only 15.4% of the nurses had participated in mental health promotion activities. This indicates the mental health service use rate was low. This finding is consistent with the conclusions of previous studies [33, 40, 47]. Factors such as privacy concerns, stigma and humiliation, and time constraints may be involved [40, 47, 48]. This can lead to a potential vicious circle in which poor mental health and inadequate utilization of treatment may lead to worsening of mental illness and increase turnover intention among nurses [49], and the remaining nurses also face higher work stress and mental health burden. To address psychological challenges among nurses, we should enhance personal initiative and improve the institutional support system. By eliminating stigma, providing convenient and effective psychological support resources and activities, nurses should be encouraged to actively seek help and improve their mental health.

Maintaining a cohort with a high rate of follow-up and matching is challenging. In the cohort of NMHS, both the baseline and first follow-up achieved response rates above 90%, which exceeded some nursing and mental health cohort studies [50–52]. The hospital-based recruitment design and the support from the Chinese Nursing Association contributed to these high participation rates, further enhancing the cohort’s robustness. However, only 63.4% of baseline records were successfully matched to the first follow-up data, which may reduce analytical power. To improve matching rates and data completeness, the research team is actively engaging participants by sharing study findings and emphasizing the study’s significance. Additionally, another unique feature of NMHS is that it can dynamically adjust the survey items. Before each wave of data collection, the variables are reviewed and appropriately updated to adapt to new research needs.

Limitations

There are some limitations in this study. First, self-report bias. The study relies on self-administered questionnaires, which may introduce reporting biases and missing data. However, our high response rate compensated for this problem to a certain extent. In addition, as health professionals, nurses have a more comprehensive and profound understanding of physical and mental health and are able to understand and answer the questionnaire content more accurately, which improves the reliability of the data. To further ensure quality, additional safeguards have been implemented, such as rigorous quality control and statistical methods. In future, our team plans to collect nurses’ biospecimens for further analysis. Second, limited data-matching rate. Only 63.4% of baseline records were successfully matched to the first follow-up data, which may reduce analytical power. To improve matching rates and data completeness, the research team is actively engaging participants by sharing study findings and emphasizing the study’s significance. Third, generalizability constraints. Because participants were nurses from tertiary hospitals, the findings may not fully generalize to the national nursing workforce or to other countries. Nevertheless, we will continue to refine the study design to enhance the reliability and applicability of our results.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that a substantial proportion of NMHS participants reported experiencing mental and psychological symptoms. Nevertheless, the majority of nurses still regarded their live as satisfying. In addition, although nurses have a high willingness to seek help for mental health issues, the mental health service use rate was low. These findings highlight the necessity of routine psychological screening and the optimization of mental health promotion services. Therefore, policymakers should take appropriate measures and provide professional psychological support to ensure the health and well-being of nurses.
