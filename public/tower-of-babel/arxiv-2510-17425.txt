Quantifying Climate Policy Action and Its Links to Development Outcomes: A Cross-National Data-Driven Analysis
Aditi Dutta

Abstract
Addressing climate change effectively requires more than cataloguing the number of policies in place; it calls for tools that can reveal their thematic priorities and their tangible impacts on development outcomes. Existing assessments often rely on qualitative descriptions or composite indices, which can mask crucial differences between key domains such as mitigation, adaptation, disaster risk management, and loss and damage. To bridge this gap, we develop a quantitative indicator of climate policy orientation by applying a multilingual transformer-based language model to official national policy documents, achieving a classification accuracy of 0.90 (F1-score). Linking these indicators with World Bank development data in panel regressions reveals that mitigation policies are associated with higher GDP and GNI; disaster risk management correlates with greater GNI and debt but reduced foreign direct investment; adaptation and loss and damage show limited measurable effects. This integrated NLP–econometric framework enables comparable, theme-specific analysis of climate governance, offering a scalable method to monitor progress, evaluate trade-offs, and align policy emphasis with development goals. The code and datasets used in this study are publicly available at: https://github.com/booktrackerGirl/climate_change_policy_analysis .

2
Methodology

The methodology follows two steps: (1) Classification Task, and (2) Statistical Analysis.

Step 1: We develop a quantitative text-based indicator of national climate policy ambition using official policies from the Climate Change Laws of the World (CCLW) database
[
15
]
. The dataset contains climate law and policy summaries labeled with thematic tags—Adaptation, Mitigation, Disaster Risk Management, or Loss and Damage. Using supervised multi-label classification, a distilled multilingual BERT model
[
16
]
generates dense embeddings from policy texts to assign themes without relying on hand-crafted features or external metadata. This task is essential to systematically identify and quantify the thematic orientation of national climate policies, thereby enabling standardized and comparable measures across countries.

Step 2: We analyze how countries emphasize different climate policies and their relationship to development outcomes using World Bank World Development Indicators (WDI)
[
17
]
(onwards from 2015). Our three-stage analytical pipeline includes: 1) descriptive boxplots identifying country leaders per policy area; 2) correspondence analysis (CA) uncovering latent associations between countries and policy types; and 3) regression-based coefficient plots estimating the impact of each policy category on key development indicators.

Together, the classification and statistical analysis constitute a coherent framework where the former derives consistent, theme-specific indicators from unstructured policy text, and the latter empirically evaluates their associations with development outcomes to elucidate their real-world implications.

The precision-recall curve (Figure
1
) and the classification report (Table
1
) reveal several key insights about the multi-label climate policy classification model. The performance strongly correlates with class frequency: Mitigation, being the most frequent category (498 instances), achieves exceptional performance (AP=0.99, 95% precision, 97% recall), while Loss and Damage, with only 11 instances, shows the poorest performance (AP=0.47) despite perfect precision (100%), suffering from extremely low recall (36%). This suggests severe class imbalance effects where the model becomes overly conservative with rare categories, missing many relevant documents to avoid false positives. Adaptation demonstrates solid balanced performance (AP=0.88, 82% precision, 87% recall), while Disaster Risk Management shows moderate discrimination ability (AP=0.83) but struggles with recall (66%), indicating difficulty in identifying all relevant documents in this category. The overall metrics (90% micro/macro precision, recall, and F1-score) mask these individual category disparities, with the weighted average being inflated by the dominant Mitigation category. The model’s tendency toward high precision but lower recall for underrepresented categories suggests it has learned to be conservative when uncertain, prioritizing avoiding false positives over capturing all relevant instances.

We examined links between climate policy variables and development outcomes using two-way fixed effects panel regressions on country-year data from the CCLW and WDI datasets. Four policy variables—Mitigation, Adaptation, Disaster Risk Management, and Loss and Damage—were modeled simultaneously to reflect real-world overlap in climate actions.
Mitigation policies showed consistently strong and statistically significant positive effects on GDP (current USD), GNI (Atlas method and PPP), and external debt stocks, indicating that action in this domain may reinforce macroeconomic growth. This aligns with World Bank estimates that each 10% increase in GDP per capita can reduce the population at high climate risk by 100 million people
[
18
]
, and with evidence that wealthier nations can leverage mitigation for long-term co-benefits
[
19
]
. GNI and debt stocks may signal fiscal space for climate interventions, and while debt is often a constraint, instruments such as climate-oriented debt swaps can convert it into a source of climate finance
[
20
,
21
]
.
DRM policies were positively associated with GNI PPP and debt stocks, suggesting financial mobilization for preparedness, but negatively linked to Foreign Direct Investment (FDI), possibly due to perceived regulatory or infrastructural risks—consistent with findings that inadequate disaster management can deter FDI inflows
[
22
]
. Adaptation policies showed limited significance, with one robust association: a negative coefficient with electricity consumption, potentially indicating efficiency gains, though such effects may reverse if adaptation relies on carbon-intensive infrastructure

Two unexpected results emerged. First, mitigation policies were positively associated with adolescent fertility, diverging from research linking lower fertility to greater climate resilience and mitigation co-benefits via gender equity and education
[

, potentially reflecting reverse causality or spurious correlation. Second, secondary education enrollment showed a negative relationship with mitigation policy, echoing findings by
[

that while education ultimately supports climate engagement, short-term effects can coincide with higher energy demand unless aligned with sustainability transitions. Loss and Damage policies showed no statistically significant associations with any development indicator, likely due to their limited implementation globally, as also indicated by the correspondence analysis.

4
Conclusion

This data-driven bottom-up analysis reveals several significant (and at times unexpected) links between climate policies and development outcomes. Modeling all four policy types jointly reflects real-world bundling and improves policy relevance, highlighting both areas of strong alignment, such as the positive economic impacts of mitigation, and gaps, notably in loss and damage implementation. Beyond methodological contributions, the framework provides a pathway to climate impact by enabling systematic cross-country tracking, supporting evidence-based governance, and informing progress toward the Paris Agreement and Sustainable Development Goals. Future work should investigate interactions and non-linearities to better capture the complexity of climate–development relationships and further refine policy evaluation.
