Geometric Invariants of Quantum Metrology
Christopher Wilson, John Drew Wilson, Luke Coffman, Shah Saad Alam, Murray J. Holland

Abstract
We establish a previously unexplored conservation law for the Quantum Fisher Information Matrix (QFIM) expressed as follows; when the QFIM is constructed from a set of observables closed under commutation, i.e., a Lie algebra, the spectrum of the QFIM is invariant under unitary dynamics generated by these same operators. Each Lie algebra therefore endows any quantum state with a fixed “budget” of metrological sensitivity—an intrinsic resource that we show, like optical squeezing in interferometry, cannot be amplified by symmetry‑preserving operations. The Uhlmann curvature tensor (UCT) naturally inherits the same symmetry group, and so quantum incompatibility is similarly fixed. As a result, a metrological analog to Liouville’s theorem appears; statistical distances, volumes, and curvatures are invariant under the evolution generated by the Lie algebra. We discuss this as it relates to the quantum analogs of classical optimality criteria. This enables one to efficiently classify useful classes of quantum states at the level of Lie algebras through geometric invariants.

Formalism

We wish to characterize a probe state,
\rho

\mathcal{H}

\mathcal{F}[\rho]_{\mu\nu}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}}[\rho\{\hat{L}_{\mu},\hat{L}_{\nu}\}],

(1)

While

The SLD

(2)

(3)

-generated evolution.

II
Theorem

The eigenvalues of a quantum Fisher information matrix,
\mathcal{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]_{\mu\nu}
, constructed from an orthonormal basis of observables constituting a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, are invariant under unitary evolution generated by
\mathfrak{g}
—the Lie group,
\mathfrak{G}
.

Proof:
We want to compare the QFIM for the probe state
\rho
to the QFIM for the unitarily evolved state

\rho\mapsto\tilde{\rho}=\hat{U}\rho\hat{U}^{\dagger}

(6)

for unitary operator
\hat{U}
.
To do so, we can use the following intuition: if each SLD is identified by a derivative, and each derivative is given by a commutator of the state with a Hermitian observable, then the SLD transforms the same as these observables under
\hat{U}
.
As a result, the two QFIMs will be related by a simple transformation law which, we will show, doesn’t change the spectrum of the QFIM as long as
\hat{U}
is generated by the same set of Hermitian observables used to identify the SLDs.
The cyclicity of the trace in the definition of the QFIM gives;

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\tilde{\rho}]_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}}[\tilde{\rho}\{\tilde{L}_{\mu},\tilde{L}_{\nu}\}]

(7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}}[\rho\{\hat{U}^{\dagger}\tilde{L}_{\mu}\hat{U},\hat{U}^{\dagger}\tilde{L}_{\nu}\hat{U}\}],

To establish a transformation law between the
\{L_{\mu}\}
and
\{\tilde{L}_{\mu}\}
, we need three things.
First, the operators
\{\hat{G}_{\mu}\}
associated to the SLDs must form a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.
Second, the unitary
\hat{U}
must belong to the Lie group
\mathfrak{G}
generated by
\mathfrak{g}
.
Lastly, we require that our basis of
\mathfrak{g}
is normalized with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt norm such that
\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}}(\hat{G}_{\mu}\hat{G}_{\nu})=C\delta_{\mu\nu}
for the norm
C
and
\delta_{\mu\nu}
the Kronecker delta function.

These conditions cause the QFIM to inherit transformation behavior from the Lie group
\mathfrak{G}
:

\hat{U}^{\dagger}\tilde{L}_{\mu}U=\Lambda^{\alpha}_{\mu}\hat{L}_{\alpha},\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\mathcal{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\tilde{\rho}]_{\mu\nu}=\Lambda^{\alpha}_{\mu}\Lambda^{\beta}_{\nu}\mathcal{F}[\rho]_{\alpha\beta},

(8)

where
\Lambda^{\alpha}_{\mu}
are the matrix elements in the
\{G_{\mu}\}
basis of the linear map
\mathbf{\Lambda}
which implements the adjoint action
\mathrm{Ad_{U^{\dagger}}}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
.
Our orthonormality condition ensures
\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{T}=\mathbf{\Lambda}^{T}\mathbf{\Lambda}=\mathbf{1}
, forcing
\mathbf{\Lambda}\in\mathrm{SO}(\dim\mathfrak{g})
.

On the surface, this is similar to the re-parameterization law for the QFIM
Liu et al. 2019
.
The difference is that here the state
\rho
has evolved to
\tilde{\rho}
and the parameterization, i.e. the basis
\{\hat{G}_{\mu}\}
, has remained fixed.
Our assumptions guarantee
Eq.

6
preserves the structure of
\mathfrak{g}
and therefore is an isometry of
\mathcal{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}
.
In matrix form,
\mathbf{F}[\tilde{\rho}]=\mathbf{\Lambda}^{T}\mathbf{F}[\rho]\mathbf{\Lambda}
, and thus

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]\vec{V}=\lambda\vec{V},\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\tilde{\rho}]\left(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{T}\vec{V}\right)=

\displaystyle\lambda\left(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{T}\vec{V}\right).

(9)

This means
\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\tilde{\rho}]
and
\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]
share the same eigenvalues—the same spectrum.
Since
\hat{U}
was an arbitrary unitary operator in
\mathfrak{G}
, the eigenvalues of the QFIM are invariant to all of
\mathfrak{G}
.
\blacksquare

In Ref.
Wilson et al. 2025
we show the same invariance holds in a
\mathfrak{g}
-basis independent manner to formally make the result geometrically well phrased, and we extend this invariance to arbitrary monotone metrics

and to the normalizer of
\mathfrak{G}
in the full Unitary group over
\mathcal{H}
.
Additionally, we show that on infinite Hilbert spaces this invariance is significantly modified.
The mathematical machinery necessary for these results falls outside the scope of discussion here.

II.1
Lemma: The Uhlmann Curvature and Metrological Incompatability

As a lemma to our main theorem, we show that the UCT built from
\mathfrak{g}
inherits the same symmetry, where

;
Carollo et al. 2018

\mathbb{U}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]_{\mu\nu}\equiv-\frac{i}{2}\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}}\left[\rho[\hat{L}_{\mu},\hat{L}_{\nu}]\right].

Physically, the UCT computes the quantum incompatibility of simultaneous estimations due to the underlying non-commutativity,

Lemma:

The eigenvectors of the quantum Fisher information matrix constructed from the orthonormal basis of
\mathfrak{g}
have invariant mutual Uhlmann curvature under

Proof:
Consider
\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]
and
\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\tilde{\rho}]
, where again,
\tilde{\rho}=\hat{U}\rho\hat{U}^{\dagger}

and

By the cyclicity of the trace
\mathbf{U}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\tilde{\rho}]=\mathbf{\Lambda}^{T}\mathbf{U}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]\mathbf{\Lambda}

\displaystyle\vec{V}^{T}\mathbf{U}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]\vec{W}=\left(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{T}\vec{V}\right)^{T}\mathbf{U}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\tilde{\rho}]\left(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{T}\vec{W}\right)\quad\blacksquare

We can use this Lemma to establish that the metrological incompatibility is invariant as well, but first we provide some geometric intuition.
Every metric defines an inner product, and the QFIM is no different;

III
Equivalence Class Manifolds

Any set of closed observables naturally carries with it a foliation (i.e. a partition into manifolds) of the set of all density operators, denoted
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
.
Thus, any Lie algebra can be used to partition
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
into the equivalence classes given by the
\mathfrak{G}
-adjoint orbits of
\rho
:

\mathcal{M}_{\rho}=\left\{\tilde{\rho}\ \Big|\tilde{\rho}=\hat{U}\rho\hat{U}^{\dagger}\ \mathrm{for}\ \hat{U}\in\mathfrak{G}\right\}.

(15)

Our theorem states that, on these equivalence classes, the spectrum of
\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]
is fixed.
This leads to an immediate geometrically flavored insight: the manifold dimension of
\mathcal{M}_{\rho}
is the number of non-zero eigenvalues of
\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]
.
Moreover, this dimension is the maximum number of statistically independent parameters encoded by
\mathfrak{G}
one could hope to estimate from measurements of
\rho
. Lastly, in the case that
\gamma^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]=0
, then one may simultaneously estimate all such parameters optimally. Our proof of invariance extends this optimality to
any
state in
\mathcal{M}_{\rho}
.

An immediate consequence is that if one wishes to use a variational algorithm or similar method in order to optimize the QFIM with respect to a Lie algebra, the search space of states may be reduced by modding out by these equivalence classes. This can be phrased through the lens of quantum resource theories; every Lie algebra foliates
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
into equivalence classes of states with the same “resourcefulness”. Actions in
\mathfrak{G}
cannot change this metrological budget, and therefore need not be searched over.

to find the trace

\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho])=4\sum_{\mu}\langle\hat{G}_{\mu}^{2}\rangle_{\rho}-\langle\hat{G}_{\mu}\rangle_{\rho}^{2}.

(20)

We can identify

\mathrm{su}(2)

If

has one distinct eigenvalue on
\mathcal{H}
.
Whereas if

is a reducible representation, the optimal state will be in the subspace of

with the largest Casimir eigenvalue.
This is called the highest weight sub-representation. In both cases, we denote this largest eigenvalue by

\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho])=4\zeta-4\sum_{\mu}\langle\hat{G}_{\mu}\rangle_{\rho}^{2}.

(21)

This leads to a practical conclusion; to experimentally measure the A-optimality criteria of a pure state, one needs only

parameter sensing now satisfies

\rho_{\mathrm{A\text{-}Opt}}=\underset{\rho}{\mathrm{argmin}}\sum_{\mu}\langle\hat{G}_{\mu}\rangle_{\rho}^{2}

(22)

Intuitively, a state with a high average response must democratically minimize the expectation value of orthonormal operators that span
\mathfrak{g}
.

V
Metrological Liouville’s Theorem

When these results are all taken together, we find a metrological analog to Liouville’s theorem
Liouville 1838
;
Song et al. 2024
:

\mathcal{M}_{\rho}
is the phase space of all evolution unitarily generated by
\mathfrak{g}
starting from
\rho
.
When the QFIM and UCT are constructed from
\mathfrak{g}
,
\mathcal{M}_{\rho}
comprises a manifold whereupon the Lie group,
\mathfrak{G}
does not compress volume, given by
\mathrm{pdet}(\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho])
, nor stretch distances, given by the spectrum of
\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]
, nor curve space, given by
\mathbf{U}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]
.
These geometric quantities are invariant, and this invariance corresponds to the manifold symmetries of
\mathcal{M}_{\rho}
itself.
In the ad-nauseam limit that one includes every observable over Hilbert space, this invariance principle extends to all of unitary mechanics.

VI
Conclusion and outlook

We have proven a simple yet previously unexplored conservation law of the QFIM, and connected it to the underlying geometrical symmetries of quantum state space.
Furthermore, through these symmetries the UCT inherits this same invariance.
This conservation law arises when one constructs the SLDs which are used to calculate the QFIM and UCT from an underlying Hermitian Lie algebra.
This Hermitian Lie algebra naturally foliates the space of density matrices into equivalence class manifolds.
On these manifolds, a metrological analog to Liouville’s theorem plays out where distances, curvatures, and volumes as diagnosed by the QFIM and UCT are incompressible.

From this invariance, we identified the geometric descriptions of quantum analogs to the classical optimality criteria typically given by functionals of the CFIM
Kiefer 1959
.
Using this, we derived conditions based on the trace of the QFIM to formulate a common entanglement witness and connected the determinant of the QFIM to the quantum Jeffrey’s prior on quantum state manifolds.
We showed that in the absurd limit for which one constructs the QFIM from all possible observables in Hilbert space, one recovers a unitarily invariant volume form that is reminiscent of the Haar measure.

We explored the consequences of this in the context of polar spin-squeezing where we showed the interplay of three Lie algebras, each one containing the last, and each generating a different partition of the space of density matrices.
There we showed that the incompatibility can actually vanish in an (idealized) physical system, thereby leading to a pseudo-QMFS where one could simultaneously estimate every parameter for a given
\rho
.

The QFIM and UCT are the real and imaginary components of the Quantum Geometric Tensor (QGT), a core object to quantum state geometry.
Because both the QFIM and UCT are invariant, the QGT is as well.
We focused on the QFIM and UCT objects separately as means for quantum state characterization and deriving useful intuition, but an interesting question is the implications of the invariant QGT as a whole.
There might be opportunities to leverage this invariance in situ with quantum learning techniques
Beckey et al. 2022
;
Zhang and Tong 2025
to greatly simplify the characterization of quantum states.
One could explore this invariance in experimentally accessible quantities like the sub-quantum Fisher information
Cerezo et al. 2021
or the semi-classical QGT
Imai et al. 2025
.
Of particular interest in extending these results is the apparent connections between the Haar-measure, an important tool in quantum computing theory
Mele 2024
. The existence of conserved quantities within each equivalence class also may open the door to novel subspace based quantum computing benchmarking methods
Baldwin et al. 2020
.

Lastly, we presented these results in the context of finite dimensional Hilbert spaces.
They do not extend straightforwardly to infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces because one loses the straightforward orthogonality condition provided by the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product and instead must use the Killing form on the Lie algebra.
In Ref.
Wilson et al. 2025
we develop the mathematical formalism for
any
Riemannian metric and extend an analogous conservation law to the infinite dimensional case, where these same invariance principles are non-trivially modified.

Appendix A
The Hilbert Schmidt Inner Product

Throughout the main text, we make use of the fact that one can fix the normalization of operators using the Hilbert Schmidt inner product:

\langle\hat{A},\hat{B}\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}\equiv\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}}(\hat{A}^{\dagger}\hat{B}).

(30)

In the main text, we only consider this as an inner product with Hermitian operators, and so we drop the complex conjugate on the first term.
We use this to define the inner product for orthonormal basis elements of a Lie algebra:

\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}}(\hat{G}_{\mu}\hat{G}_{\nu})=C\delta_{\mu\nu}

(31)

where
C\in\mathbb{R}
and
\delta_{\mu\nu}
is the delta function.

As mentioned at in the paper, we use this inner product to raise an index on the QFIM in order to make it a well-defined inner product.
This means we define

g_{\mu\nu}\equiv\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}}(\hat{G}_{\mu}\hat{G}_{\nu}),

(32)

whereupon

F^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]^{\mu}_{\nu}=C\ g^{\mu\alpha}\ \mathcal{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]_{\alpha\nu}

(33)

provides the matrix elements of the matrix
\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]
.
Here,
g^{\mu\alpha}
is the inverse of
Eq.

32
such that

g^{\mu\alpha}g_{\alpha\nu}=\delta^{\mu}_{\nu},

(34)

for the delta function
\delta^{\mu}_{\nu}
.
This means that

g^{\mu\alpha}=\frac{1}{C}\delta^{\alpha\mu}

(35)

in the basis we chose.
As a result

\displaystyle F^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]^{\mu}_{\nu}=

\displaystyle C\ g^{\mu\alpha}\ \mathcal{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]_{\alpha\nu}

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta^{\mu\alpha}\ \mathcal{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]_{\alpha\nu}

which is exactly what we want, so that the eigenvalues match the Crámer-Rao Bound.

Appendix B
Construction of the various Lie Algebras

Appendix C
Trace and Determinant Expressions

In this section, we derive the expressions we gave for the trace and determinant of the coherent spin state, and of the Noon state in the main text.
We also derive the condition for the entanglement witness using the trace.
Throughout, we take
\mathfrak{g}
to be the
N
particle representation of
\mathrm{su}(d)
, given in

B.1
.
We also use

\mathcal{F}[\rho]_{\mu\nu}=2\langle\{\hat{G}_{\mu},\hat{G}_{\nu}\rangle_{\rho}-4\langle\hat{G}_{\mu}\rangle_{\rho}\langle\hat{G}_{\nu}\rangle_{\rho}

(57)

C.1
The Coherent Spin State

A generalized Coherent Spin State (CSS) is given by
\ket{\mathcal{C}}=\ket{\psi}^{\otimes N}
where
\ket{\psi}
is any single particle pure state
Nemoto 2000
.
We want to classify the trace and determinant of the QFIM of this state, to do so we will use the observation made in Ref.
Barnum et al. 2003
.
Namely, every
\ket{\mathcal{C}}
is separable and therefore it is the unique groundstate of the sum of single particle operators.
This is a long winded way to say, we might as well label our single particle states such that
\ket{\psi}=\ket{1}
, and so
\ket{\mathcal{C}}=\ket{1}^{\otimes N}
.
This let’s us turn the calculation of the trace and determinant into a counting problem.

First, we note that
\ket{\mathcal{C}}
is an eigenstate of
d-1
many diagonal operators in this basis.
Second, we note that this state is in the kernel of
(d-1)(d-2)
many non-diagonal operators–i.e. the x and y-like operators without an element acting on
\ket{1}
but acting on the
d-1
many remaining states.
This leaves us with
2(d-1)
many remaining operators in the basis of the form

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{(m)}_{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\left(\ket{1}_{j}\bra{m}_{j}+\mathrm{H.c.}\right)

(58)

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{(m)}_{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2i}\left(\ket{1}_{j}\bra{m}_{j}-\mathrm{H.c.}\right)

for
m=2,d
.
We also note that the offdiagonal QFIM between any of these operators is zero. This is to say:

\displaystyle 4\left(\left\langle\frac{\hat{A}^{(m)}_{\mu}\hat{A}^{(n)}_{\nu}+\hat{A}^{(n)}_{\nu}\hat{A}^{(m)}_{\mu}}{2}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}-\langle\hat{A}^{(m)}_{\mu}\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}\langle\hat{A}^{(n)}_{\nu}\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}\right)=0

(59)

for
\mu,\nu=x,y
and
n,m=2,d
but either
n\neq m
or
\mu\neq\nu
(i.e.
\hat{A}^{(n)}_{\nu}\neq\hat{A}^{(m)}_{\mu}
).
For which
\ket{\mathcal{C}}
has a QFI of
N
.
This yields
2(d-1)
many eigenvalues of the QFIM of
N
for a coherent spin state.
Therefore:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{su}(d)}(\mathbf{F}^{\mathrm{su}(d)}[\mathcal{C}])=

\displaystyle 2N(d-1),

(60)

\displaystyle\mathrm{pdet}(\mathbf{F}^{\mathrm{su}(d)}[\mathcal{C}])=

\displaystyle N^{2(d-1)}.

An alternative argument goes as follows.

\ket{C}
is the (non-zero) eigenstate of
d-1
many operators–which is the dimension of the maximal Cartan sub-algebra.
The Lie algebra
\mathrm{su}(d)
, for the same reason, has
d-1
many unique
\mathrm{su}(2)
sub-algebras.
This means
\ket{C}
sits at the “North pole” of
d-1
many collective Bloch spheres.
This gives
2(d-1)
many unique directions for
\ket{C}
to evolve in.
Furthermore, if we normalize everything properly, it will evolve in each direction at a rate
N
corresponding to a QFI of
N
.
Notably, for multiparameter estimation,
d-1
of these QFI’s could be simultaneously estimated with commuting measurements.

C.2
The Noon State

In the text, we give the definition for the generalzied Noon state, which we construct as the sum of two orthogonal CSS:

\displaystyle\ket{\mathcal{N}}=(\ket{\psi}^{\otimes N}+\ket{\perp}^{\otimes N})/\sqrt{2}

(61)

where
\ket{\psi}
is a single particle state, just like in
\ket{\mathcal{C}}

\ket{\perp}
is any perpendicular single particle state to
\ket{\psi}

We can use the same labeling argument as above to identify the state

and

These states are different eigenstates of the same

From the construction in

.
Furthermore, they have a QFI of

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\left(\ket{1}_{j}\bra{2}_{j}+\mathrm{H.c.}\right)

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2i}\left(\ket{1}_{j}\bra{2}_{j}-\mathrm{H.c.}\right).

many more elements to consider in the construction of the QFIM.
First, for

of these,

many remaining operators.
These operators are of the fallowing form:

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{(n,m)}_{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\left(\ket{n}_{j}\bra{m}_{j}+\mathrm{H.c.}\right)

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{(n,m)}_{y}

\displaystyle\lambda=

\displaystyle 4\left(\langle(\hat{A}^{(1,m)}_{x})^{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{N}}-\langle\hat{A}^{(1,m)}_{x}\rangle_{\mathcal{N}}^{2}\right)

(65)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\langle(\hat{A}_{x}^{(1,m)})^{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{N}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bra{\mathcal{N}}(\hat{a}\hat{c}^{\dagger}+\hat{c}\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2}\ket{\mathcal{N}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N}{2},

where the

\displaystyle\to 1\ \mathrm{eigenvalue\ of}\ N^{2}

\displaystyle\to 2\ \mathrm{eigenvalues\ of}\ N

\displaystyle\to 4(d-2)\ \mathrm{eigenvalues\ of}\ N/2

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{su}(d)}(\mathbf{F}^{\mathrm{su}(d)}[\mathcal{N}])

\displaystyle=N^{2}+2N(d-2),

(67)

\displaystyle\mathrm{pdet}(\mathbf{F}^{\mathrm{su}(d)}[\mathcal{N}])

\displaystyle=N^{4}\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)^{4(d-2)}.

We have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho])

\displaystyle=4\sum_{\mu}\langle\hat{G}_{\mu}^{2}\rangle_{\rho}-\langle\hat{G}_{\mu}\rangle_{\rho}^{2}

\displaystyle=4\langle\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\rangle_{\rho}-4\sum_{\mu}\langle\hat{G}_{\mu}\rangle_{\rho}^{2}

\hat{\mathcal{G}^{2}}
is the quadratic Casimir operator of
\mathrm{su}(d)
.
The maximal eigenvalue of
\hat{\mathcal{G}^{2}}
is on the permutationally symmetric irreducible representation–i.e. when all the qudits are permutationally symmetric.
Since
4\sum_{\mu}\langle\hat{G}_{\mu}\rangle_{\rho}^{2}
is strictly positive, we know that a coherent spin state of the form in the main text has the largest trace QFIM for pures states.

Furthermore, no mixed state can surpass this:
Let

\rho_{q}=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{n=1}^{q}\left(\ket{n}\!\bra{n}\right)^{\otimes N},\quad\mathrm{for}\ 1\leq q\leq d

(69)

where
\ket{n}
is a single particle state labeled in an arbitrary basis.
Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathbf{F}^{\mathrm{su}(d)}[\rho_{q}])=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}[\rho_{q}]

(70)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\left(\frac{1}{q}\sum_{n=1}^{q}\lambda_{j}\left[\left(\ket{n}\!\bra{n}\right)^{\otimes N}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{q}\sum_{n=1}^{q}\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\left(\ket{n}\!\bra{n}\right)^{\otimes N}])\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2N(d-1).

In order for the trace of the QFIM to surpass this scaling, one of the eigenvalues must scale faster than
N
.
This is an indication of entanglement
Tóth 2012
.
Therefore,

\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho])\geq 2N(d-1),

(71)

is an entanglement witness.

Appendix D
The Average Response as the Trace QFIM

In the main text we consider a small unitary applied to the probe state;
\hat{U}(\epsilon,\vec{n})=\mathrm{e}^{-i\epsilon\hat{G}_{\vec{n}}}
, where
\epsilon\ll 1
,
\vec{n}
is a
\dim(\mathfrak{g})
unit vector and
\hat{G}_{\vec{n}}=n^{\mu}\hat{G}_{\mu}\in\mathfrak{g}
.
We state that the set of all these actions that form the
\epsilon
-ball of actions near the identity in the Lie group
\mathfrak{G}
.
Formally speaking, we mean that the Lie group,
\mathfrak{G}
, is a manifold and we are considering the set

\mathcal{B}(\epsilon)=\{\hat{U}(\epsilon,\vec{n})\in\mathfrak{G}|\epsilon\ll 1,|\vec{n}|^{2}=1\}.

(72)

The distance on
\mathfrak{G}
is given by the geodesic distance, but for
\epsilon\ll 1
we can us the metric on the group–which is the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product in this case:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathfrak{G}}(\hat{U}(\epsilon,\vec{n}),\hat{1})^{2}\approx

\displaystyle n^{\mu}\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\hat{G}_{\mu},\hat{G}_{\nu})n^{\nu}\epsilon^{2}

(73)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Cn^{\mu}\delta_{\mu\nu}n^{\nu}\epsilon^{2}=C\epsilon^{2},

where we used that
n^{\mu}\delta_{\mu\nu}n^{\nu}=|\vec{n}|^{2}=1
.
So, on
\mathfrak{G}
the
\epsilon
-ball is isotropic and has radius
r_{\mathfrak{G}}=\epsilon\sqrt{C}
.

Now, in the main text we want to see how this ball deforms when it is included into
M_{\rho}
.
We can include this ball into the state space by the adjoint action:

\tilde{\rho}(\vec{n})=\hat{U}(\epsilon,\vec{n})\rho\hat{U}(-\epsilon,\vec{n}),

(74)

where
\tilde{\rho}(\vec{n})\in M_{\rho}
.
To see how it deforms, we derive the mean square distance from
\rho
to
\tilde{\rho}(\vec{n})
, which find to be

\overline{r_{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}}\equiv\epsilon^{2}\ \frac{\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho])}{\dim(\mathfrak{g})}\frac{1}{8}

(75)

which was found from the integral:

\displaystyle\overline{r_{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}}\equiv\frac{1}{A}\int_{S^{\dim(\mathfrak{g})}}\Delta^{2}dA,

(76)

where
A=(2\pi^{\dim(\mathfrak{g})/2})/\Gamma(\frac{\dim(\mathfrak{g})}{2})
is the surface area of the
\dim(\mathfrak{g})
-sphere,
\Gamma
is the gamma function, and

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}=

\displaystyle 1-\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}}\left(\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho}\tilde{\rho}(\epsilon,\vec{n})\sqrt{\rho}}\right)

(77)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\vec{n}^{T}\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]\vec{n}\ \frac{\epsilon^{2}}{8}.

Here, we’ll solve this integral explicitly.
It reduces to a well known integral quickly.
Let
D=\dim(\mathfrak{g})
for conciseness.
First, we use expansion of the Uhlmann distance according to small
\epsilon
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A}\int_{S^{D}}\Delta^{2}dA

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{8A}\int_{S^{D}}\vec{n}^{T}\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]\vec{n}dA

(78)

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{8A}\sum_{\mu,\nu}\mathcal{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]_{\mu\nu}\int_{S^{D}}n^{\mu}n^{\nu}dA.

So, we have to evaluate the integral

\frac{1}{A}\int_{S^{D}}n^{\mu}n^{\nu}dA,

(79)

which is a well known integral in probability and statistics.
To integrate it, we’ll use hyper spherical coordinates.
This is
D-1
many angles,
\theta^{j}
such that

\displaystyle n^{1}=

\displaystyle\cos(\theta^{1}),

(80)

\displaystyle n^{2}=

\displaystyle\sin(\theta^{1})\cos(\theta^{2}),

\displaystyle n^{D-1}=

\displaystyle\sin(\theta^{1})\sin(\theta^{2})\cdots\sin(\theta^{D-2})\cos(\theta^{D-1}),

\displaystyle n^{D}=

\displaystyle\sin(\theta^{1})\sin(\theta^{2})\cdots\sin(\theta^{D-2})\sin(\theta^{D-1})

where
\theta^{D-1}\in[0,2\pi]
and
\theta^{j}\in[0,\pi]
for
j=1,\dots,D-2
.
The hyper spherical area element is

dA=\prod_{j=1}^{D-1}\sin(\theta^{j})^{D-1-j}d\theta^{j}.

(81)

We can evaluate this by symmetry arguments.
On the
D
-sphere,
n^{\mu}
has a minimum value of
-1
and a maximum of
+1
, and is odd.
Therefore, differing coordinates
\mu\neq\nu
will go to zero.
Now, the integral is spherically symmetric, so we know that all coordinates must contribute the same real number, call it
B
.
Therefore,

\frac{1}{A}\int_{S^{D}}n^{\mu}n^{\nu}dA=B\delta^{\mu\nu},

(82)

where we have to determine
B
. We can do this by choosing the simplest coordinate:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos(\theta^{1})^{2}\sin(\theta^{1})^{D-2}d\theta^{1}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{D-1}{2}\right)}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{D+2}{2}\right)}.

(83)

Unless
D=2
, in which case this is integration around the unit circle and left as an exercise for the reader.
This leaves the
D-1
angle which integrates to
2\pi
, and
D-3
other angles of the form

\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin(\theta^{j})^{D-1-j}d\theta^{j}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{D-j}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{D-j+1}{2}\right)}

(84)

We can then directly calculate
B
where, luckily, many terms cancel

\displaystyle B=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{A}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{D-1}{2}\right)}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{D+2}{2}\right)}\prod_{j=2}^{D-2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{D-j}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{D-j+1}{2}\right)}\right)

(85)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{D}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{D+2}{2}\right)}=\frac{1}{D},

as stated in the main text.
Thus, all the steps summarized are

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A}\int_{S^{D}}\Delta^{2}dA

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{8A}\int_{S^{D}}\vec{n}^{T}\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]\vec{n}dA

(86)

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{8A}\sum_{\mu,\nu}\mathcal{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho]_{\mu\nu}\delta^{\mu\nu}\frac{1}{\dim(\mathfrak{g})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{8}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathbf{F}^{\mathfrak{g}}[\rho])}{\dim(\mathfrak{g})}.

Here, we provide a brief derivation of the physical model used in the example in the main text.
We consider
N

The first laser shines on the cavity mode, and the other two directly on the atoms.
The cavity and pump scheme is shown in

7
(a).

.
