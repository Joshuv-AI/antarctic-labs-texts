SGLT2 Inhibitors: Dual Effects on Erythropoiesis and Bone Metabolism
Authors: Aseel H. Mohammad, Shatha H. Mohammad, Mohammad H. Alsaaty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors have been used as a therapy for type 2 diabetes mellitus, heart failure, and chronic kidney disease (CKD); however, their effects on erythropoiesis, phosphate regulation, and bone turnover are incompletely clarified. Studies report a marked increase in hemoglobin and hematocrit during treatment, suggesting an erythropoietic response rather than simple hemoconcentration. Simultaneously, changes in mineral metabolism, including a modest increase in phosphate, fibroblast growth factor-23 (FGF-23), and parathyroid hormone (PTH), raise questions about the possible consequences for bone quality, mainly in individuals with diabetes or CKD who are at increased risk of fracture. Accordingly, this narrative review was structured through a literature search of PubMed, Scopus and Google Scholar for studies published in English up to November 2025. Keywords included SGLT2 inhibitors, erythropoiesis, bone metabolism, phosphate homeostasis, FGF-23, PTH, vitamin D, bone mineral density (BMD) and fracture. Randomized trials, mechanistic studies, animal experiments, observational cohorts, sub-analysis and reviews were included. Current evidence shows that SGLT2 inhibition promotes erythropoietin production, enhances iron mobilization and modifies the bone marrow environment, while also influencing the FGF-23-PTH-vitamin D axis through renal phosphate handling. Although canagliflozin has been associated with reduction in hip BMD and possible fracture risk, findings from the drug class remain inconsistent and are often confounded by baseline comorbidities. In conclusion, SGLT2 inhibitors trigger a connected hematologic and mineral changes, but their long-term skeletal impact remains uncertain. Further mechanistic and longitudinal studies are advised to clarify these outcomes.

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors have rapidly evolved from being solely anti-diabetic agents to main therapies in the management of cardiorenal-metabolic syndrome.[1] Initially they are approved to lower blood glucose in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) by enhanced urinary glucose excretion; however, large-scale clinical trials exposed unexpected advantages extending to heart failure (HF) and chronic kidney disease (CKD), even in individuals without diabetes.[2] These benefits may provide the physiological context for downstream changes in renal oxygenation and tubular handling of phosphate, which are directly relevant to erythropoiesis and bone metabolism.[3,4] However, despite these well-known effects, the mechanistic impact of SGLT2 inhibitors on hematologic and skeletal system are still not clearly explored. Hemoglobin and hematocrit levels are reported to increase in clinical studies, but it is unclear whether they are due mainly to hemoconcentration, production of erythropoietin (EPO) or iron handling. Similarly, changes in phosphate balance and hormone regulators like vitamin D, parathyroid hormone (PTH), and fibroblast growth factor-23 (FGF-23) point to potential effects on bone turnover; however, the available data on bone density and fracture risk are still conflicting. These inconsistencies suggest the necessity of a targeted investigation into the ways by which SGLT2 inhibitors affect bone homeostasis and blood formation, as well as defined pathways connecting these two systems.

METHODS

The purpose of this narrative review was to gather the most recent data regarding the hematologic and bone effects of SGLT2 inhibitors. Using databases like PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar, a literature search was conducted for publications issued up to November 2025. A variety of keywords relevant to SGLT2 inhibitors and their impact on erythropoiesis, bone metabolism, phosphate homeostasis, and associated regulators like FGF-23, PTH, and vitamin D were used in the strategy. SGLT2 inhibitors, erythropoiesis, bone metabolism, phosphate homeostasis, FGF-23, PTH, vitamin D, bone mineral density (BMD), and fracture risk were among the specific terms used. Randomized controlled trials, observational studies, mechanistic experimental studies, animal research, and earlier English-language reviews were among the study types we took into consideration. Studies were chosen according to the quality of the evidence they offered and how relevant they were to the subject. Hematologic outcomes, including changes in hemoglobin and hematocrit, effects on bone remodeling and BMD, and modulation-related biochemical markers, were the main focus of data extraction. No formal quantitative synthesis or meta-analysis was carried out because of the narrative nature of this review. Rather, the gathered research was assessed to offer a comprehensive viewpoint on connection between SGLT2 inhibition, erythropoiesis, mineral metabolism, and bone health.

OVERVIEW OF SGLT2 INHIBITION AND ITS SYSTEMIC EFFECTS

SGLT2 inhibition restores tubuloglomerular feedback and reduces glomerular hyperfiltration

In normal renal physiology, SGLT2 is a high-capacity, low-affinity protein expressed almost exclusively on the apical membrane of the S1 segment of the proximal convoluted tubule.[5] Its main role is to reclaim about 90% of the glucose filtered by the glomeruli via a 1:1 sodium–glucose cotransport process, which is driven by the basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase–generated electrochemical gradient.[6] This primary action dose not only lower plasma glucose in an insulin-independent manner, but also trigger a critical intrarenal hemodynamic cascade, by increasing the delivery of sodium chloride to the macula densa. Afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction, a decrease in intraglomerular pressure, and a reduction in glomerular hyperfiltration result from the restoration of tubuloglomerular feedback caused by SGLT2 inhibition.[7]

SGLT2 inhibitors increase hematocrit but have mixed evidence on bone outcome

One important multiple effect associated with SGLT2 inhibitors is a steady increase in hematocrit. This increase is sustained by an increase in erythropoietic activity rather than just being a temporary hemoconcentration from diuresis.[5] Mechanistically, SGLT2 inhibitors are thought to improve renal cortical oxygenation and modulate hypoxiainducible factor-2α (HIF-2α ), culminating in augmented EPO generation within renal tissues, with the liver potentially serving as an additional source.[6] In contrast, the evidence regarding bone health remains mixed and a subject of ongoing investigation. Initial concerns arose from the CANagliflozin cardioVascular Assessment Study (CANVAS) trial, which suggested an increased fracture risk with canagliflozin. While most subsequent large-scale trials and meta-analyses involving other agents like dapagliflozin and empagliflozin have not found a significant class-wide risk of fracture. Several investigations have noted slight but statistically significant increase in markers of bone turnover like PTH and C-terminal telopeptide, a marker for bone resorption, with a small reduction in BMD, mainly at the hip.[7,8] Therefore, the motivation for examining hematologic and skeletal outcomes specifically originates from two significant and separate lines of investigation on SGLT2 inhibitors. First, SGLT2 inhibitors consistently raise hematocrit, where trial mediation analyses suggest that this may contributes significantly to cardiorenal benefit, making it an important area of research. On the other hand, patients with T2DM and CKD already face an elevated baseline fracture risk, which rises the safety concerns in early clinical trials and emphasize the need to distinguish true class effects from drug-specific risks.

HIF SIGNALING AS AN UPSTREAM REGULATOR UNDER SGLT2 INHIBITION

In the diabetic kidney, the hyper reabsorption state of proximal tubules would obviously increase adenosine triphosphatase (ATP) demand to creates a state of pseudohypoxia that paradoxically impairs HIF-2α stability and suppresses EPO synthesis.[8] SGLT2 inhibition reverses this effect by blocking glucose absorption early in the nephron. These drugs significantly reduce the metabolic workload of the proximal tubules, which may restore oxygen levels in the tissues. When the performance of proximal tubule become less prominent, the load of fluid and salts shifts to the outer medulla. The outer medulla might effectively work harder, creating a state of localized hypoxia. This preferentially stimulates the kidney to produce more EPO, leading to increased hematocrit.[9] Such stabilization in HIF-2α firstly will stimulate endogenous production of EPO. Secondly, will suppresses hepcidin transcription directly and upregulates iron transport proteins, such as erythroferrone, and ensure the availability of iron for erythropoiesis.[10,11] In the context of bone, this pathway will be linked with increased phosphate reabsorption, compensatory increase in FGF-23 and PTH. However, while persistent elevation of FGF-23 is generally unfavorable for bone due to its suppressive effects on vitamin D and phosphate availability, modest or transient increases may represent an adaptive response without clear clinical consequences.[12] The proposed HIF-2α –EPO–iron axis linking SGLT2 inhibition to bone marrow and bone remodeling is expressed in Figure 1.

The effect of EPO receptors (EPORs) on osteoblasts and osteoclasts

EPO is a kidney-produced hormone that effectively stimulates erythropoiesis. EPO promote the formation of vessel networks, protects against myocardial infarction and ischemic brain injuries, and promotes neural cell proliferation and viability. Additionally, it contributes to improved energy homeostasis via effects on skeletal muscle cells, hepatocytes, and adipocytes. Another tissue significantly affected by EPO is the skeleton, particularly bone, as functional EPORs are expressed on bone-forming osteoprogenitors and osteoblasts, as well as on osteoclast precursors.[13–15] EPOR-mediated pathways in osteoblast-lineage cells regulate the receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL)/osteoprotegerin (OPG) balance, a critical regulator of bone remodeling. RANKL promotes bone breakdown and OPG inhibits it, thereby modulating osteoblast–osteoclast interactions, which are essential for bone and immune regulation.[16] In parallel with its actions on osteoclast precursors through activation of the EPOR, EPO has been shown to activate multiple intracellular signaling pathways in amyloid-β–treated PC12 neuronal cells, including the Janus kinase 2/signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (JAK2/STAT5) and mitogen-activated protein kinase/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (MAPK/ERK) pathways. These signaling cascades are well recognized mediators of the anti-apoptotic effects of EPO in erythroid cells and may reflect conserved cytoprotective mechanisms across different cell types. In addition, EPO may exert indirect anabolic effects on bone through stimulation of ephrinB2/EphB4 signaling, a pathway known to promote osteoblast differentiation and bone formation.[17,18]

Dual anabolic vs. catabolic role of EPO

EPO exerts context- and dose-dependent effects on the skeleton. At physiological levels, EPO/EPOR signaling can support osteogenic programs, angiogenesis and bone repair through actions on OPG and stromal cells anabolic effect.[19] In contrast, systemic high doses of EPO primarily stimulate osteoclastogenesis, enhance bone resorption, and suppress osteoblast-mediated bone formation, resulting in an overall catabolic effect on the skeleton.[20] There is a definite dose-response relationship in experimental research. Higher doses of EPO may increase osteoclast numbers and cause trabecular bone loss, whereas low exposure inhibits osteoblast differentiation and modifies marrow precursor populations.[13] At the molecular level, EPO-induced osteoclastogenesis has been associated with JAK2/ERK signaling activation and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPARγ) up-regulation in precursor cells, suggesting a direct signaling pathway that encourages the catabolic response.[21] Additionally, sex, hormonal background, and the marrow microenvironment, particularly its interaction with adipogenic and hematopoietic pathways, all influence the net skeletal response to EPO. These factors explain the opposing reports about the anabolic effects of systemic EPO administration on bone loss versus fracture healing.[22]

Bone marrow microenvironment and the role of EPO in osteoblast–hematopoietic pathway

The bone marrow microenvironment represents a critical interface between the skeletal and hematopoietic systems, in which osteoblast-lineage cells play a central regulatory role. Osteoblasts not only contribute to bone formation but also shape the hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) niche at the endosteal surface by supporting HSC retention, self-renewal, and lineage commitment. This regulation is mediated through the secretion of niche factors such as osteopontin, angiopoietin-1, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12, and stem cell factor, as well as through direct cell–cell interactions involving adhesion molecules including N-cadherin.[23,24] Within this microenvironment, EPO acts as an important modulatory signal linking erythropoiesis to skeletal remodeling.[25] Emerging evidence suggests that EPO signaling can influence osteoblast differentiation and function, thereby indirectly regulating the composition and activity of the hematopoietic niche. Moreover, EPO-mediated effects on osteoblast–osteoclast coupling and osteoblast–HSC communication highlight its role as a key mediator of bone–marrow crosstalk. Disruption of this finely tuned microenvironment, as observed during pathological bone remodeling or hematopoietic stress, may therefore alter both erythropoietic efficiency and skeletal homeostasis.[26]

Interconnection between hematologic and skeletal pathways

According to recent research, the EPO, FGF-23, and PTH axis form a complex regulatory network that connects erythropoiesis and bone metabolism.[27,28] EPO is thought to be the kidney-derived factor that drives the production of red blood cells. It also directly affects skeletal cells by stimulating osteoclastogenesis and then osteoblastogenesis, which links bone remodeling and hematopoietic development.[19] The hormone FGF-23, which is mostly produced by osteocytes, is essential for controlling phosphate metabolism and has a variety of effects on several physiological systems. FGF-23 affects bone remodeling, cardiovascular function, iron handling, immune regulation, metabolic processes linked to obesity, and erythropoiesis in addition to preserving phosphate balance. Anemia and decreased erythroid progenitor survival or proliferation are linked to elevated FGF-23 levels. According to research, EPO administration may boost the expression of FGF-23, especially in its cleaved forms, creating a feedback loop between the skeletal and erythropoietic systems.[29,30] The PTH is generally known for its role in calcium and phosphate balance. It also modulates hematopoietic activity, and high PTH levels were shown to be associated with EPO resistance and marrow fibrosis. FGF-23 inhibits PTH secretion via Klotho-dependent signaling, thereby linking mineral and hematopoietic pathways.[31,32]

Influence of renal oxygenation and mineral metabolism

Disruption of renal oxygen homeostasis may alter mineral metabolism and trigger skeletal and cardiovascular dysfunction via the kidney-bone axis. Due to the combination of high perfusion and low tissue oxygen levels, the kidney is inherently prone to chronic hypoxia.[33,34] Under hypoxic conditions, the dysregulation of tubular and interstitial function accelerates nephron loss, diminishes activation of vitamin D, and impairs the expression of Klotho, thereby disrupting phosphate and calcium homeostasis.[35,36] High circulating FGF-23 induces renal phosphate loss, hypophosphatemia, and reduced calcitriol levels, resulting in rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults. In CKD, FGF-23 show early increase, with phosphate accumulation and secondary hyperparathyroidism through inhibition of vitamin D production and stimulation of phosphaturia.[37–39] Moreover, hypoxia-induced signaling via HIFs and oxidative stress can further trouble tubular phosphate handling and drive vascular calcification, linking low renal oxygenation directly to BMD.[34,40]

Integrated model of blood–bone interaction under SGLT2 inhibition

SGLT2 inhibition initiates an organized series of renal-driven adaptations that integrate hematopoietic and skeletal physiology. By reducing proximal tubular capacity and enhancing renal tissue oxygenation, these agents promote an early rise in EPO production, followed by persistent increases in reticulopoiesis, hemoglobin, and hematocrit. Together, these hematologic responses represent a key mediator of the cardiorenal benefits observed across major outcome trials.[41] By influencing local oxygen tension, stromal-cell signaling, and iron utilization, enhanced erythropoiesis modifies the state of the bone marrow. These changes affect osteoblastic and stromal activity and rebalance systemic iron metabolism in ways that support redcell production and modulate important bone remodeling pathways.[42] Likewise, SGLT2 inhibitors induce proximal tubular adaptations that modestly increase serum phosphate, leading to secondary elevations in FGF-23 and PTH, along with a reduction in active vitamin D. These hormonal changes are known to alter bone remodeling dynamics by favoring osteoclastic bone resorption relative to osteoblastic bone formation, thereby increasing overall bone turnover.[43] Within this integrated framework, two converging pathways can be identified. The first is a hematopoietic axis, in which renal EPO stimulation enhances erythropoiesis, modifies the bone marrow microenvironment, and alters iron trafficking. The second is a mineral-hormone axis, whereby proximal tubular phosphate retention drives increase in FGF-23 and PTH, resulting in downstream changes in bone turnover (Fig. 2).

DIRECT AND INDIRECT EFFECTS OF SGLT2 INHIBITION ON BONE

BMD data from trials and preclinical models

In individuals with T2DM, BMD is often elevated despite a paradoxical increase in fracture risk. This apparent discrepancy may, in part, reflect the anabolic influence of chronic hyperinsulinemia on bone mass.[44] SGLT2 inhibitors appear to modulate bone physiology through both direct and indirect mechanisms. Direct effects are supported by animal and genetic studies showing that SGLT2 deletion or prolonged pharmacologic inhibition can reduce cortical BMD and alter bone microarchitecture. Indirect effects, in contrast, stem from drug-induced calciuria, alterations in phosphate homeostasis with secondary elevations in PTH and FGF-23, as well as weight loss and hemodynamic changes that may influence fall risk and mechanical loading on the skeleton.[45,46] Studies in canagliflozin-treated male and female mice indicate that its effects on bone metabolism are driven largely by induction of the glucose restriction–like state, which influences bone-cell proliferation and differentiation. Moreover, canagliflozin modifies bone turnover in a manner that is both sex-dependent and age-dependent.[47] Preclinical rodent studies reveal heterogeneous, drug- and model-dependent effects on the skeleton; canagliflozin has been linked to impaired trabecular architecture in several diabetic and nondiabetic models, whereas dapagliflozin and empagliflozin generally demonstrated mixed or neutral effects on bone strength across comparable experimental settings.[48,49] In randomized controlled trials, dapagliflozin did not produce significant changes in bone turnover markers or in dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA)-based BMD denotes the BMD values quantified using DXA, scan over a 50 week treatment period.[50] In contrast, the CANVAS program identified an increased fracture signal and a modest reduction in total-hip BMD with canagliflozin; an agent-specific observation that has not been consistently reproduced in other outcome trials.[51] Short-term studies are generally inadequate to detect meaningful changes in bone metabolism, as bone remodeling responds slowly to metabolic shifts. While long term studies that follow-up beyond 6–12 months is more likely to detect a real alteration in bone turnover. So, fracture risk monitoring and BMD assessment should be considered in high risk patients with bone fracture.[44,52]

Effects of SGLT2 inhibitors on calcium, phosphate, vitamin D, FGF-23, and PTH

SGLT2 inhibitors enhance renal phosphate reabsorption and produce modest increases in serum phosphate, thereby eliciting compensatory endocrine responses characterized by elevated circulating FGF-23 and PTH and a corresponding reduction in vitamin D.[53]

Differentiating endogenous physiology from pharmacologic dosing

A crucial differentiation must be made between the erythropoietic stimulation caused by SGLT2 inhibitors and that seen with exogenous erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs). High-dose ESAs leads to elevated EPO levels, adversely affecting adult bone homeostasis and increasing osteoclasts, while the molecular mechanisms remain unidentified. It has been indicated that EPO enhanced osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption in is mediated via elevated PPARγ expression and facilitated osteoclast development by targeting the JAK2/ERK signaling pathway.[21] SGLT2 inhibitors cause a low-grade, physiological increase in endogenous EPO. The increased EPO is usually temporary, manifesting within several weeks of starting treatment. The resultant elevation in hemoglobin is moderate and typically remains within physiological boundaries, rather than inducing pathological polycythemia, mimics normal adaptation to high level.[54] Instead of continuously stimulating EPO-producing peritubular fibroblasts through sustained hypoxia, SGLT2 inhibitors reduce metabolic stress in the renal cortex by decreasing Na+/K+ ATPase activity, improving oxygenation and allowing them to function properly. Due to feedback loops, SGLT2 inhibitors lower HIF-1α (related with inflammation and damage) and promote HIF-2α (which stimulates EPO). This suggests an adaptive, regulated reaction rather than an uncontrolled one, limiting chronic, high-titer exposure that causes os teoclastogenesis.[55]

Although certain studies indicate that elevated doses of specific SGLT2 inhibitors (e.g., canagliflozin) may elevate bone turnover markers in rodents, clinical evidence in people often reveals no substantial increase in fracture risk, with some research suggesting a potentially beneficial skeletal profile.[56] The simple fact that the EPO stimulation is controlled helps to explain why they don’t usually cause the massive and prolonged osteoclastogenesis that is seen with high-dose EPO supplements.[57]

Energy metabolism and osteoblast bioenergetics

Emerging evidence indicates that SGLT2 inhibitors, independent of their systemic mineral effects, reprogram osteoblast bioenergetics toward enhanced mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation and more efficient ATP generation, potentially supporting osteoblast differentiation under specific metabolic conditions.[58] Given that β-hydroxybutyrate has been demonstrated to improve osteoblast differentiation and stimulate histone acetylation, this metabolic change is partially linked to the increase in circulating ketone bodies seen during SGLT2 inhibition. The thrifty substrate hypothesis states that β-hydroxybutyrate produces more ATP per unit of oxygen than glucose or fatty acids, which may contribute to the upregulation of bone anabolic gene expression.[59] According to experimental research, empagliflozin can improve osteoblast viability in oxidative stress situations by directly enhancing mitochondrial function and reducing ROS through AMPK–Nrf2–dependent antioxidant pathways.[60] By changing the osteoblast–osteoclast association at the tissue level, SGLT2 inhibitors may influence bone remodeling, especially in situations involving metabolic stress or persistent high blood glucose. [61]

Iron beyond erythropoiesis: impact on bone cell

While iron’s role in hemoglobin synthesis established very well, iron plays a pivotal role in maintaining skeletal integrity. It is considered as an essential cofactor for mitochondrial respiratory complexes, mainly in highly metabolically active cells such as osteoblasts.[62] The redistribution of iron which is mediated by SGLT2 inhibition is not limited to the production of red blood cell but may also provide energetic support for the maturation of osteoblast cells and matrix formation; both of which require high energy consumption.[44] Osteoclasts require iron for normal function; however, whereas pathologic iron overload induces osteoporosis through oxidative stress and ferroptotic pathways. The regulated iron mobilization associated with SGLT2 inhibition therapy may support balanced bone remodeling without over activation of pathological osteoclastic, contributing to the bone protection effects of these agents.[63,64]

CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS AND CONSIDERATIONS

Beyond lowering blood sugar, SGLT2 inhibitors may improve oxygen delivery and skeletal health in patients with CKD. They alleviate tissue hypoxia by lowering tubular capacity and renal cortical oxygen consumption through decreased proximal tubular sodium–glucose reabsorption. Increased cortical oxygenation stimulates the production of EPO by peritubular fibroblasts, which leads to long-term increases in hemoglobin and hematocrit and, ultimately, increased systemic oxygen-carrying capacity to organs like the kidneys and heart.[65,66]

SGLT2 inhibitors slow the progression of renal disease, lower cardiorenal events, and lessen the uremic and inflammatory status that causes abnormal bone turnover in CKD, where anemia, tissue hypoxia, and inflammation accelerate organ injury and contribute to BMD. When these medications are used with suitable clinical monitoring, large randomized trials in CKD populations (EMPA-KIDNEY, DAPA-CKD) have demonstrated significant kidney-protective benefits without consistent evidence of increased fracture risk, suggesting a potential net protective effect on bone health.[67] SGLT2 inhibitors have raised questions about phosphate handling and bone metabolism despite their therapeutic advantages. These drugs may increase sodium-phosphate cotransporter activity by delivering more sodium and glucose to downstream nephron segments. This could lead to mild phosphate retention and subsequent increases in FGF-23 and PTH.[45] Increases in FGF-23 and PTH can suppress calcitriol synthesis and encourage increased bone resorption, a mechanism that is especially problematic in people with CKD, where mineral metabolism is largely dysregulated.[68] Particularly with canagliflozin, a number of studies have found slight decreases in BMD and modest increases in bone turnover markers; however, these results are inconsistent and often influenced by variables like age, comorbidities, baseline CKD severity, mineral and bone disorder status, diuretic use, and weight loss.[49,52,61,69]

A study that stated the need for individualized therapy based on a patient’s cardiorenal risk profile and preferences highlighted the advantages of SGLT2 inhibitors, particularly their ability to improve cardiovascular outcomes and slow the progression of CKD in older adults. Raised questions about the safety of these drugs in the elderly population, where risks like urinary tract infections, acute kidney injury, and fractures are generally predicted. Because of age-related BMD declines, fall risk, and the frequent coexistence of CKD and frailty, the use of SGLT2 inhibitors in older patients necessitates careful clinical judgment.[70] Additionally, volume depletion and orthostatic hypotension that are usually associated with SGLT2 inhibitors use may maximize fall risk, an important determinant of fracture susceptibility in elderly group.[71]

From a clinical perspective, the skeletal impact of SGLT2 inhibitors is likely to differ across patient populations, emphasizing the need for individualized assessment.[52] Patients with CKD and anemia may derive indirect benefit, as SGLT2 inhibitors improve renal oxygenation, enhance endogenous EPO production, and optimize iron utilization, leading to consistent increases in hemoglobin levels. These hematologic effects may support bone marrow function and skeletal homeostasis in uremic settings, although direct evidence for fracture reduction remains limited.[54,72] Similar mechanistic considerations apply to patients with HF and functional iron deficiency, in whom improved tissue oxygen delivery and iron handling may have favorable downstream effects on bone remodeling.[73]

Conversely, caution is warranted in patients with advanced osteoporosis, particularly those with low-turnover or adynamic bone disease, where SGLT2 inhibitor–induced alterations in phosphate handling, increases in FGF-23 and parathyroid hormone, and reductions in active vitamin D could potentially aggravate skeletal fragility.[74] Elderly individuals at high fracture risk also require careful monitoring due to age-related bone loss, sarcopenia, frailty, and the increased likelihood of falls associated with volume depletion or orthostatic hypotension. Additionally, patients with baseline vitamin D deficiency or phosphate imbalance, especially in CKD, may be more susceptible to secondary hyperparathyroidism, highlighting the importance of baseline evaluation and periodic monitoring of phosphate, parathyroid hormone, and vitamin D during therapy.[75,76]

FUTURE DIRECTIONS AND RESEARCH GAPS

Although clinical evidence supports the cardiovascular and renal benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors, important gaps remain to be clarified, especially their long-term effects on phosphate homeostasis, bone turnover dynamics, and structural bone integrity.[49] Most existing clinical trials were not designed to assess skeletal outcomes, and many mechanistic studies were short in duration, limited by small sample sizes, or have excluded individuals at high risk for bone fragility. Consequently, the relationship between canagliflozin use and fracture risk remains incompletely understood and requires further investigations.[61] that the potential of SGLT2 inhibition to modify renal phosphate handling and influence regulatory hormones of bone need further studying of the causal pathways that cause these changes.[77] Future research should focus on the mechanistic insights, mainly in case of CKD, increased age, and dysregulated baseline skeletal status, together with longitudinal trials that integrate assessments of BMD, bone turnover biomarkers, and fracture incidence. Such work is essential to determine whether the observed biochemical alterations translate into clinically meaningful effects across varied populations.

Establishment and validation of integrated biomarkers that can also identify skeletal metabolic alterations and hematologic reactions during SGLT2 inhibitor therapy is a crucial future direction. A variety of biomarkers are required to monitor these dynamic interactions and identify people who are more susceptible to bone fragility because these agents have an impact on erythropoiesis, phosphate homeostasis, and bone-regulatory hormones.[74] Nevertheless, there has been little validation of these biomarkers in a variety of populations, such as the elderly and CKD patients with bone disorders. Future research should look into prospective, biomarker-guided monitoring trials to explore the associated mechanisms, encourage individualized treatment plans, and reduce unfavorable skeletal outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

The majority of clinical trials have not shown that SGLT2 inhibitors significantly alter BMD or fracture risk. Although certain reports indicate changes in bone turnover markers, these results vary by factors like age, menopausal status, comorbidities, and length of study. Canagliflozin, in particular, has been associated with an elevated fracture risk in certain analyses, however this may be due to weight loss or patient-related susceptibilities rather than a direct effect. Overall, current evidence remains inconclusive, urging the need for long-term studies to clarify the potential skeletal consequences of SGLT2 inhibitor therapy.
