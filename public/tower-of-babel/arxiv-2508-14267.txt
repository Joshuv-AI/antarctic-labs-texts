On the number of conjugacy classes of subgroups of a finite group
Mihai-Silviu Lazorec, Marius Tărnăuceanu

Abstract
Let k ′ ​ ( G ) k^{\prime}(G) and L ⁡ ( G ) L(G) be the number of conjugacy classes of subgroups and the subgroup lattice of a finite group G G , respectively. Our objective is to study some aspects related to the ratios d ′ ​ ( G ) = k ′ ​ ( G ) | L ⁡ ( G ) | d^{\prime}(G)=\frac{k^{\prime}(G)}{|L(G)|} and d ∗ ​ ( G ) = min ⁡ { d ′ ​ ( S ) ∣ S ​ is a section of ​ G } d^{*}(G)=\min\{d^{\prime}(S)\mid S\text{ is a section of }G\} which measure how close is G G from being a Dedekind group. We prove that the set containing the values d ′ ​ ( G ) d^{\prime}(G) , as G G ranges over the class of nilpotent groups, is dense in [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] . A nilpotency criterion is obtained by proving that if d ∗ ​ ( G ) > 2 3 d^{*}(G)>\frac{2}{3} , then G G is nilpotent and information on its structure is given. We also show that if d ∗ ​ ( G ) > 4 5 d^{*}(G)>\frac{4}{5} , then G G is an Iwasawa group. Finally, we deduce a result which ensures that a p p -group of order p n p^{n} ( n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 ) is a Dedekind group. This last result can be extended to the class of nilpotent groups and it also highlights the second maximum values of d ′ d^{\prime} and d ∗ d^{*} on the class of p p -groups of order p n p^{n} .

1
Introduction

All groups considered in this paper are finite and
n\geq 1
denotes an integer. Let
G
be a group and
p
be a prime number. We denote by
k(G),k^{\prime}(G),\nu(G),N(G)
and
L(G)
the number of conjugacy classes, the number of conjugacy classes of subgroups, the number of conjugacy classes of non-normal subgroups, the normal subgroup lattice and the subgroup lattice of
G
, respectively. The cyclic group of order
n
is denoted by
C_{n}
, the dihedral group
D_{2n}
(
n\geq 3
) has the following structure

D_{2n}=\langle x,y\mid x^{n}=y^{2}=1,yx=x^{n-1}y\rangle,

M_{p^{n}}
(
n\geq 4
if
p=2
;
n\geq 3
if
p
is odd) is a modular
p
-group (i.e. a
p
-group whose subgroup lattice is modular) given by

M_{p^{n}}=\langle x,y\mid x^{p^{n-1}}=y^{p}=1,yx=x^{p^{n-2}+1}y\rangle,

while
He_{p}
is the Heisenberg group modulo
p
(
p
is odd) and its representation is

He_{p}=\langle x,y,z\ |\ x^{p}=y^{p}=z^{p}=1,[x,z]=[y,z]=1,[x,y]=z\rangle.

We also recall that an Iwasawa group is a modular nilpotent group.

A way to measure how close is
G
from being abelian is given by its commutativity degree

d(G)=\frac{|\{(x,y)\in G\times G\mid xy=yx\}|}{|G|^{2}}.

Theorem IV of
[
6
]
states that the numerator is equal to
k(G)|G|
, so the commutativity degree of
G
can be also expressed as

d(G)=\frac{k(G)}{|G|}.

In this paper, we study another ratio that is defined similarly, the main difference being that we switch from working with the elements of
G
to operating with its subgroups. Hence, we are going to investigate the ratio

d^{\prime}(G)=\frac{k^{\prime}(G)}{|L(G)|}.

This quantity measures how close is
G
from being a Dedekind group (i.e. all its subgroups are normal). It is clear that all abelian groups are Dedekind, while the structure of the non-abelian Dedekind groups, which are called Hamiltonian groups, is also known (see statement 5.3.7 of
[
13
]
).

Some properties of
d^{\prime}
are straightforward. For any group
G
, we have
0<d^{\prime}(G)\leq 1
, and the equality
d^{\prime}(G)=1
holds if and only if
G
is a Dedekind group. If
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are two groups such that
G_{1}\cong G_{2}
, then
d^{\prime}(G_{1})=d^{\prime}(G_{2})
. The converse does not hold even for non-Dedekind groups. For instance, if
G_{1}\cong C_{3}\rtimes C_{8}
(SmallGroup(24,1)) and
G_{2}\cong C_{3}\times D_{8}
(SmallGroup(24,10)), we have
d^{\prime}(G_{1})=d^{\prime}(G_{2})=\frac{4}{5}
and
G_{1}\not\cong G_{2}
. Finally, if
I
is a finite set with
|I|\geq 2
and
(G_{i})_{i\in I}
is a family of groups of coprime orders, then

d^{\prime}\bigg({{\mathrel{\mathop{{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\mbox{\Large$\times$}}\limits}}}\limits_{{i\in I}}}{}\!}{}\!}G_{i}\bigg)=\prod_{i\in I}d^{\prime}(G_{i}),

so
d^{\prime}
is multiplicative.

Our main results are listed below and their proofs are included in the following two sections. In Section 2, we explicitly compute
d^{\prime}(G)
with
G
belonging to specific classes of groups. Given an integer
a\geq 1
, we show that all ratios of the form
\frac{a}{a+1}
are attained by
d^{\prime}
. The main result of this section is the following one:

Theorem A.

Let
\mathscr{N}
be the class of nilpotent groups. Then the set

\{d^{\prime}(G)\mid G\in\mathscr{N}\}

is dense in
[0,1]
.

In Section 3, we justify that there is no constant
c\in(0,1)
such that if
d^{\prime}(G)>c
, then
G
is a nilpotent/Iwasawa/Dedekind group. However, a nilpotency criterion and a criterion for
G
to be an Iwasawa group can be obtained by replacing
d^{\prime}(G)
with
d^{*}(G)
, where

d^{*}(G)=\min\{d^{\prime}(S)\mid S\text{ is a section of }G\}.

More exactly, we justify that the following results hold:

Theorem B.

Let
G
be a group. If
d^{*}(G)>\frac{2}{3}=d^{*}(S_{3})=d^{\prime}(S_{3})
, then
G
is a nilpotent group. Moreover, if
2\nmid|G|
, then
G
is the direct product of some modular
p
-groups.

Theorem C.

Let
G
be a group. If
d^{*}(G)>\frac{4}{5}=d^{*}(D_{8})=d^{\prime}(D_{8})
, then
G
is an Iwasawa group.

By using the classification of minimal non-Dedekind
p
-groups, we also determine a criterion which guarantees that
p
-groups of fixed orders are Dedekind groups. This result can be extended to the class of nilpotent groups and it is stated below:

Theorem D.

Let
n\geq 3
be an integer and
G
be a
p
-group of order
p^{n}
.

i)

Assume that
p=2
and
n=3
.

a)

If
d^{*}(G)>\frac{4}{5}=d^{*}(D_{8})
, then
G
is a Dedekind group;

b)

If
d^{\prime}(G)>\frac{4}{5}=d^{\prime}(D_{8})
, then
G
is a Dedekind group.

ii)

Assume that
n\geq 4
if
p=2
, and
n\geq 3
if
p
is odd.

a)

If
d^{*}(G)>\frac{(n-2)(p+1)+4}{(n-1)(p+1)+2}=d^{*}(M_{p^{n}})
, then
G
is a Dedekind group;

b)

If
d^{\prime}(G)>\frac{(n-2)(p+1)+4}{(n-1)(p+1)+2}=d^{\prime}(M_{p^{n}})
, then
G
is a Dedekind group.

The paper concludes with a list of open problems.

2
On the values of
d’

As a preliminary result we recall the main theorem of
[
3
]
. It outlines the classification of groups having one conjugacy class of non-normal subgroups. These groups are close to being Dedekind groups and this aspect will be helpful in obtaining some of our results.

Lemma 2.1.

Let
G
be a group . Then
\nu(G)=1
if and only if
G
is isomorphic to one of the following groups:

i)

M_{p^{n}}
;

ii)

a non-abelian split extension
G_{p,q,n}=N\rtimes P
where
N\cong C_{p}
,
P\cong C_{q^{n-1}},[N,\Phi(P)]=1
,
p,q
are primes such that
q\mid p-1
and
n\geq 2
.

In what follows we obtain explicit formulas for computing
d^{\prime}(G)
when
G
is a group with
\nu(G)=1
, a dihedral 2-group or a non-abelian group
p
-group of order
p^{3}
and exponent
p
.

Proposition 2.2.

The following results hold:

i)

d^{\prime}(M_{p^{n}})=\frac{(n-2)(p+1)+4}{(n-1)(p+1)+2};

ii)

d^{\prime}(G_{p,q,n})=\frac{2n}{2n+p-1};

iii)

d^{\prime}(D_{2^{n}})=\frac{3n-1}{2^{n}+n-1};

iv)

d^{\prime}(He_{p})=\frac{2p+5}{p^{2}+2p+4}.

Moreover, the sequences
(d^{\prime}(M_{p^{n}}))_{n\geq 3}
(
p
is odd),
(d^{\prime}(M_{2^{n}}))_{n\geq 4}
,
(d^{\prime}(G_{p,q,n}))_{n\geq 2}
are strictly increasing, while the sequences
(d^{\prime}(D_{2^{n}}))_{n\geq 3}
,
(d^{\prime}(He_{p}))_{p\geq 3}
are strictly decreasing.

Proof.
Note that
k^{\prime}(G)=|N(G)|+\nu(G)
for any group
G
. According to Lemma 3.3 of
[
16
]
, we have

|L(M_{p^{n}})|=(n-1)(p+1)+2\text{ and }|L(D_{2^{n}})|=2^{n}+n-1.

i)
As stated in Lemma 3.5 of
[
16
]
, we have

|N(M_{p^{n}})|=(n-2)(p+1)+3.

Since
\nu(M_{p^{n}})=1
, the conclusion follows.

ii)
The subgroup
P\cong C_{q^{n-1}}
is the representative of the unique conjugacy class of non-normal subgroups of
G_{p,q,n}
. This class contains
p
conjugates. Besides
N
and its improper subgroups,
G_{p,q,n}
has one normal subgroup of order
pq^{i}
and one normal subgroup of order
q^{i}
for each
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n-2\}
. Therefore,

k^{\prime}(G_{p,q,n})=2n\text{ and }|L(G_{p,q,n})|=2n+p-1,

which leads to the desired result.

iii)
Theorem 3.3 of
[
5
]
outlines the conjugacy classes of subgroups of any dihedral group. According to this result,
D_{2^{n}}
has 3 conjugacy classes of subgroups of order
2^{i}
for each
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\}
. Hence,

k^{\prime}(D_{2^{n}})=3n-1,

and the conclusion follows.

iv)
The improper normal subgroups of
He_{p}
are
Z(He_{p})\cong C_{p}
and its
p+1
maximal subgroups isomorphic to
C_{p}^{2}
. There are also
p+1
non-trivial conjugacy classes of subgroups, each being formed of
p
conjugates isomorphic to
C_{p}
. We conclude that

k^{\prime}(He_{p})=2p+5\text{ and }|L(He_{p})|=p^{2}+2p+4,

so we easily obtain the value of
d^{\prime}(He_{p})
.

In what regard the second part of this proposition, we only justify the monotonicity of the first sequence. Let
p
be a fixed odd prime and
f:[3,\infty)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function given by

f(x)=\frac{(x-2)(p+1)+4}{(x-1)(p+1)+2},\ \forall\ x\in[3,\infty).

We have

f^{\prime}(x)=\frac{p^{2}-1}{[(x-1)(p+1)+2]^{2}}>0,\ \forall\ x\in(3,\infty),

so
f
is strictly increasing on
[3,\infty)
. Hence, the sequence
(f(n))_{n\geq 3}=(d^{\prime}(M_{p^{n}}))_{n\geq 3}
is strictly increasing.

The following result is a consequence of our previous proposition. It shows that
d^{\prime}(G)
can be made arbitrarily large or small as
|G|
increases.

Corollary 2.3.

The following results hold:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}d^{\prime}(M_{p^{n}})=\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}d^{\prime}(G_{p,q,n})=1\text{ and }\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}d^{\prime}(D_{2^{n}})=\displaystyle\lim_{p\to\infty}d^{\prime}(He_{p})=0.

It is clear that the values of
d^{\prime}
belong to
S=[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
. We proceed our study by showing that specific values from
S
are attained by
d^{\prime}
. Then, in the proof of Theorem A, we justify that each value of
S
is an adherent point of the set

\{d^{\prime}(G)\mid G\in\mathscr{N}\},

where
\mathscr{N}
is the class of nilpotent groups. The classification given by Lemma 2.1 and the numerical results outlined in Proposition 2.2 play a significant role for both purposes.

Corollary 2.4.

Let
a\geq 1
be an integer. Then there is a group
G
such that
d^{\prime}(G)=\frac{a}{a+1}.

Proof.
If
a=1
, we take
G=G_{2,5,2}\cong D_{10}
and we have
d^{\prime}(G)=\frac{1}{2}
. Suppose that
a\geq 2
. Then, we can choose
G=G_{3,2,a}
and we get
d^{\prime}(G)=\frac{a}{a+1}.

Proof of Theorem A.
Let
x\in S
. We aim to show that there is a sequence of groups
(G_{n})_{n\geq n_{0}}\subseteq\mathscr{N}
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}d^{\prime}(G_{n})=x.

If
x=0
or
x=1
, we are done by Corollary 2.3. Suppose that
x=\frac{a}{b}
where
1\leq a<b
. Also, let
(p_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be the strictly increasing sequence of odd prime numbers. We construct
b-a
strictly increasing and disjoint subsequences
(p_{1_{n}})_{n\geq 1},(p_{2_{n}})_{n\geq 1},\ldots,(p_{{(b-a)}_{n}})_{n\geq 1}
of
(p_{k})_{k\geq 1}
. Also, for each
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,b-a\}
, consider the sequence of groups
(G_{i_{n}})_{n\geq 1}
where

G_{i_{n}}=M_{{p_{i_{n}}}^{a+i+1}}.

Note that each of these
b-a
sequences of groups is well-defined since we work only with odd prime numbers and
a+i+1\geq 3
. Finally, we define the sequence
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subseteq\mathscr{N}
whose general term is

G_{n}={{\mathrel{\mathop{{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\mbox{\Large$\times$}}\limits^{{b-a}}}}}\limits_{{i=1}}}{}\!}{}\!}G_{i_{n}}.

The factors of the above direct product are of coprime orders since the sequences
(p_{i_{n}})_{n\geq 1}
are disjoint. Hence, we are able to apply the multiplicativity property of
d^{\prime}
as follows

d^{\prime}(G_{n})=d^{\prime}\bigg({{\mathrel{\mathop{{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\mbox{\Large$\times$}}\limits^{{b-a}}}}}\limits_{{i=1}}}{}\!}{}\!}G_{i_{n}}\bigg)=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{b-a}d^{\prime}(G_{i_{n}})=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{b-a}d^{\prime}(M_{{p_{i_{n}}}^{a+i+1}})=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{b-a}\frac{(a+i-1)(p_{i_{n}}+1)+4}{(a+i)(p_{i_{n}}+1)+2}.

As
n
tends to infinity, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}d^{\prime}(G_{n})=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{b-a}\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{(a+i-1)(p_{i_{n}}+1)+4}{(a+i)(p_{i_{n}}+1)+2}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{b-a}\frac{a+i-1}{a+i}=\frac{a}{b}=x.

Therefore, we conclude that each value of
S
is an adherent point of the set
\{d^{\prime}(G)\ |\ G\in\mathscr{N}\}
. This implies that

S\subseteq\overline{\{d^{\prime}(G)\mid G\in\mathscr{N}\}},

so

[0,1]=\overline{S}\subseteq\overline{\{d^{\prime}(G)\mid G\in\mathscr{N}\}}.

Since the reverse inclusion is obvious, the proof is complete.

Theorem A can be easily extended to any class of groups containing
\mathscr{N}
as follows.

Corollary 2.5.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a class of groups such that
\mathscr{N}\subseteq\mathscr{C}
. Then
\{d^{\prime}(G)\mid G\in\mathscr{C}\}
is dense in
[0,1].

3
Criteria ensuring that a finite group is nilpotent / Iwasawa / Dedekind

Let
G
be a group. Given a group theoretic property
P
,
G
is said to be a minimal non-
P
group if
G
does not satisfy
P
and all its proper subgroups do. The following lemma recalls the structure and some properties of the minimal non-nilpotent groups. These are also known as Schmidt groups. The listed results are outlined in
[
14
]
and
[
12
]
.

Lemma 3.1.

Let
G
be a Schmidt group. Then the following statements hold:

i)

G=P\rtimes Q
where
P
is a normal Sylow
p
-subgroup,
Q
is a cyclic Sylow
q
-subgroup of
G
and
p,q
are distinct primes;

ii)

Z(G)=\Phi(G)=\Phi(P)\times\Phi(Q)
;

iii)

\frac{P}{\Phi(P)}\cong C_{p}^{r}
, where
r
is the multiplicative order of
p
modulo
q
;

iv)

If
N
is a proper normal subgroup of
G
, then
N
does not contain
Q
and either
P\subseteq N
or
N\subseteq Z(G)
.

Let
c\in(0,1)
and
G
be a group. A condition such as
d^{\prime}(G)>c
cannot guarantee that
G
is a nilpotent/Dedekind group. Indeed, according to Corollary 2.3 for a sufficiently large value of
n
, say
n_{c}
, we would get
d^{\prime}(M_{p^{n_{c}}})>c
and
d^{\prime}(G_{p,q,n_{c}})>c
, but
M(p^{n_{c}})
is a non-Dedekind group, while
G_{p,q,n_{c}}
is non-nilpotent. Theorem B highlights a nilpotency criterion that is obtained by replacing the condition
d^{\prime}(G)>c
with
d^{*}(G)>c
, where

d^{*}(G)=\min\{d^{\prime}(S)\mid S\text{ is a section of }G\}.

A useful property of
d^{*}
is that it behaves like a decreasing function defined on
L(G)
, i.e. if
H,K\in L(G)
and
H\subseteq K
, then
d^{*}(H)\geq d^{*}(K).
Also,
d^{*}
is multiplicative. Indeed, if
I
is a finite set with
|I|\geq 2
and
(G_{i})_{i\in I}
is a family of groups of coprime orders, then all sections of
{{\mathrel{\mathop{{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\mbox{\Large$\times$}}\limits}}}\limits_{{i\in I}}}{}\!}{}\!}G_{i}
are of the form
{{\mathrel{\mathop{{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\mbox{\Large$\times$}}\limits}}}\limits_{{i\in I}}}{}\!}{}\!}S_{i}
, where
S_{i}
is a section of
G_{i}
for any
i\in I
. Then

d^{*}\bigg({{\mathrel{\mathop{{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\mbox{\Large$\times$}}\limits}}}\limits_{{i\in I}}}{}\!}{}\!}G_{i}\bigg)=\prod_{i\in I}d^{*}(G_{i}).

Before proving Theorem B, we outline and justify some modularity criteria for
p
-groups.

Proposition 3.2.

Let
G
be a
p
-group.

i)

If
d^{*}(G)>\frac{4}{5}=d^{*}(D_{8})=d^{\prime}(D_{8})
, then
G
is a modular
p
-group;

ii)

If
p
is odd and
d^{*}(G)>\frac{11}{19}=d^{*}(He_{3})=d^{\prime}(He_{3})
, then
G
is a modular
p
-group.

Proof.

i)
Suppose that
G
is a non-modular
p
-group. According to Lemma 2.3.3 of
[
15
]
,
G
has a section
S
isomorphic to:

–

D_{8}
, if
p=2
;

–

He_{p}
, if
p
is odd.

We also know that
d^{*}(G)\leq d^{\prime}(S)
. We use the results stated in Proposition 2.2. If
p=2
, we get

d^{*}(G)\leq\frac{4}{5}=d^{\prime}(D_{8}),

a contradiction. If
p
is odd, then

d^{*}(G)\leq d^{\prime}(He_{p})\leq\frac{11}{19}=d^{\prime}(He_{3})<\frac{4}{5},

a contradiction.

The above reasoning also justifies statement
ii)
, so the proof is complete.

We proceed by proving Theorem B.

Proof of Theorem B.
Suppose that
G
is a counterexample of minimal order. Then
G
is non-nilpotent. Let
H\in L(G)
such that
H\neq G
. Then

d^{*}(H)\geq d^{*}(G)>\frac{2}{3},

so
H
is nilpotent. Consequently,
G
is a Schmidt group.

We consider the section
S=\frac{G}{Z(G)}
of
G
. By using the information introduced in Lemma 3.1, we deduce that
|S|=p^{r}q
and, by the correspondence theorem,
S
has a normal subgroup

H=\frac{P\times\Phi(Q)}{Z(G)}\cong C_{p}^{r}.

On the other hand, a subgroup
K\cong C_{q}
of
S
cannot be normal. Indeed, if
K\in N(S)
, then
G
would have a proper normal subgroup
N
with
|N|=q\cdot|Z(G)|=|\Phi(P)|\cdot|Q|
. By Lemma 3.1
iv)
, we would also have that
|P|
divides
|N|
and this leads to a contradiction. Hence, we conclude that

S=H\rtimes K\cong C_{p}^{r}\rtimes C_{q}

and
K
acts faithfully on
H
.
Moreover, all proper subgroups of
S
are nilpotent, so
S
is also a Schmidt group.

Note that
S
cannot have a subgroup
L_{i}
of order
p^{i}q
with
1\leq i<r
. If such a subgroup would exist, then it would be nilpotent, so we can assume that there is a subgroup
H_{i}\cong C_{p}^{i}
of
H
such that
L_{i}=H_{i}\times K\cong C_{p}^{i}\times C_{q}
. Since
K
acts trivially on
H_{i}
and faithfully on
H
, we can assume that there is an injective group homomorphism
\varphi:C_{q}\longrightarrow Aut(C_{p}^{r-i})
. This leads to
q\mid|Aut(C_{p}^{r-i})|
. Since

|Aut(C_{p}^{r-i})|=(p^{r-i}-1)(p^{r-i}-p)\cdots(p^{r-i}-p^{r-i-1}),

(see, for instance, Exercise 2 from Section 6 of
[
8
]
), we contradict that
r
is the multiplicative order of
p
modulo
q
.

We denote
|L(C_{p}^{r})|
by
a_{p,r}
. Taking into account that
S
has
p^{r}
conjugate subgroups isomorphic to
C_{q}
and there are no subgroups of order
p^{i}q
with
1\leq i<r
, we obtain

|L(S)|=a_{p,r}+p^{r}+1.

Let
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,r-1\}
and let
H_{i}
be a subgroup of
S
with
|H_{i}|=p^{i}
. By our previous discussion on the non-existence of subgroups of order
p^{i}q
, we deduce that
H_{i}
is not normal in
S
. Also,
H
is an abelian maximal subgroup of
S
and
H_{i}\subset H
, so the size of the conjugacy class of
H_{i}
is

[S:N_{S}(H_{i})]=[S:H]=q.

It is known that the number of subgroups of
S
that are isomorphic to
H_{i}
is given by the Gaussian coefficient

\big[\begin{subarray}{c}r\\
i\end{subarray}\big]_{p}=\frac{(p^{r}-1)(p^{r-1}-1)\cdots(p^{r-i+1}-1)}{(p^{i}-1)(p^{i-1}-1)\cdots(p-1)}

(see, for example, Exercise 1.74 of
[
1
]
). Hence, these subgroups are partitioned into
\frac{1}{q}\big[\begin{subarray}{c}r\\
i\end{subarray}\big]_{p}
conjugacy classes. Besides them,
S
has 3 trivial conjugacy classes of subgroups whose representatives are
1
,
H
and
S
, respectively, and the conjugacy class of
K
. Therefore,

k^{\prime}(S)=\frac{1}{q}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r-1}\big[\begin{subarray}{c}r\\
i\end{subarray}\big]_{p}+4=\frac{a_{p,r}+4q-2}{q}.

Consequently, we have

d^{\prime}(S)=\frac{a_{p,r}+4q-2}{q(a_{p,r}+p^{r}+1)}.

The following step is to show that
d^{\prime}(S)\leq\frac{2}{3}
. This would contradict that

Open problems

We end our paper by enumerating some questions that can be further explored.

Problem 4.1.
Let
a,b
be integers such that
1\leq a<b
. In Section 2 we proved that any ratio of the form
\frac{a}{a+1}
can be achieved by computing

for specific non-nilpotent supersolvable groups having one conjugacy class of non-normal subgroups. We also showed that each ratio of the form
\frac{a}{b}

(d^{\prime}(G_{n}))_{n\geq 1}
where
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a sequence of nilpotent groups formed as direct products of some modular
p
-groups. Hence, we pose the following question:

Is it true that there is a group
G
such that
d^{\prime}(G)=\frac{a}{b}
?

To get an affirmative answer, it suffices to search for
b-a
groups
G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{b-a}
of coprime orders such that

d^{\prime}(G_{i})=\frac{a+i-1}{a+i},\ \forall\ i\in\{1,2,\ldots,b-a\}.

By taking
G={{\mathrel{\mathop{{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\mbox{\Large$\times$}}\limits^{{b-a}}}}}\limits_{{i=1}}}{}\!}{}\!}G_{i}
and making use of the multiplicativity of

, we would get a positive answer.
