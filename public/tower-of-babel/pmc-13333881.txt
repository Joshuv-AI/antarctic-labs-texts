Evolution of surface integrity and tribological characteristics of Incoloy 925 under sequential multi-pass end milling
Authors: Shravan Kumar Yadav, Walle Tilahun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multi-pass machining processes are essential to get the specified shape and dimensions of the product. The thermal softening and work hardening histories from prior cutting passes can substantially influence the performance of subsequent cutting passes. This research has two investigative stages. The initial phase of the investigation examined the sequential cutting strategies and the impact of multi-pass end milling of Incoloy 925 on cutting force, chip morphology, and surface integrity, specifically regarding surface roughness, micro-morphology/surface topography, micro-hardness, microstructure, and crystallographic texture of the machined surface layer. The third cutting pass shows the highest cutting force, being 32.48% and 12.28% greater than the first and second passes, respectively. The surface roughness in the third pass is also increased, measuring 36.6% and 12.81% higher than that of the first and second passes, respectively. Similarly, the micro-hardness observed in the third pass, across all depths from the machined surface, exceeds that of the earlier passes. These results clearly demonstrate the influence of preceding passes on the performance and outcomes of subsequent machining operations. Further, EBSD analysis indicates that the extent of grain refinement and the evolution of crystallographic texture in the machined layer have intensified with an increasing number of cutting passes. XRD examination, however, reveals the absence of phase transition in all cutting passes. The second part of the study examined the impact of thermo-mechanical loading history on the tribological behavior of machined components subjected to multi-pass cutting. The samples subject to the initial cutting passes have been found superior to subsequent passes in terms of tribological performance.

Introduction

Superalloys play a crucial role in industrial sectors because of their superior strength and corrosion resistance qualities. Incoloy 925, a nickel-based superalloy, is predominantly utilized in the oil and gas sector due to its exceptional resistance to stress-corrosion cracking (SCC) and sulfide stress cracking (SSC) under acidic conditions. It is used for surface gas-well components, valves, fasteners, high-strength piping systems, and marine applications1. Machining is required for any product’s functional performance and better service life2. However, due to their lower thermal conductivity, hot hardness, high sensitivity to work-hardening, abrasive intermetallic, and chemical reaction with the cutting tool, Ni-based superalloys come under the category of difficult-to-cut material3,4. High cutting forces, rapid tool wear, and high heat generally induce degradation in machined surfaces with metallurgical alteration. Existing conditions at the machined surface layer are related to the surface integrity. Surface topography and metallurgy of machined surface layers are the two main constituents of surface integrity. Surface roughness, waviness, texture, morphology, and surface defects are related to surface topography, while micro-hardness, deformed layer, grain refinement, phase transformation, and white layer generation are related to surface metallurgy5. A white layer is formed near the machined surface in which microstructure cannot be seen with an optical microscope owing to nano-size grains. It is formed due to either generation of high heat causes phase transformation or thermo-mechanical effect causes severe plastic deformation6. It depends on cutting speed and tool wear5. Brown et al.7 observed a white layer (20–30 μm) containing ultra-fine grains with higher hardness than bulk material after milling Ti-6Al-4 V. Du et al.8 have explained the deformation-phase coupling mechanism for forming the white layer during the machining of FGH95 Ni-based alloy.

Poorly machined surfaces and bad surface integrity lead to the early failure of the product9. So, surface quality is essential for improving the product’s service life. However, the quality of the surface is influenced by many factors, like process parameters, cutting force, extrusion, cutting temperature, friction between mating surfaces, cutting tool geometry, and machining environment. Fan et al.10 reported that the development of a built-up edge influenced the surface roughness of Inconel 718 at reduced cutting speeds, but at elevated cutting speeds, the surface quality deteriorated owing to tool wear. Post-machining, a hardened layer forms on the machined subsurface, resulting in excessive hardness within the sub-region of the machined surface; work-hardening intensifies with prolonged machining duration, elevated process parameters, and tool degradation.

Most of the research on surface quality is focused on single pass cutting. However, multi-pass cutting is also one of the effective techniques in manufacturing and processing. Iqbal et al.11 observed that turning in two passes has more advantages than turning in one pass in terms of forces, surface finish, and cutting energy. Multi-pass machining operation is a multi-loading-stress relieving process, and the quality of the final machined surface depends on the entire machining operation. Compared to unprocessed material, the properties of the machined surface layers are altered due to produced heat, cutting forces, and accumulated strain, which can further influence the later machining operations9. In the milling of hardened steel, SKD 11, Li et al.12 found that extrusion and friction between the machined surface and tool were more in the second cutting step of milling than in the first step, which increased the compressive residual stress. Through the simulation process, Liu and Guo13 investigated the sequential cuts of annealed 304 stainless steel. They found that the first step affected cutting force, chip thickness, cutting temperature, and residual strain for the next machined layer. Work-hardening and thermal softening have essential effects on the machining characteristics in any machining process. However, the product is practically finished by a multi-pass machining operation14. Most of the research studies on multi-pass machining focused on environmental protection and production costs. Lu et al.15 studied energy consumption and machining quality and established an optimization model for multi-pass turning. Multi-pass cutting is the best surrogate for high-depth machining since machined surface quality has improved16. Yue et al.17 reported that multi-pass cutting strategy was better than single pass cutting when the total depth of cut is fixed. However, work hardening and thermal softening effects of previous passes of cutting that change the existing machined surface and subsurface properties greatly influence the microhardness and residual stress of the workpiece after the next pass of cutting.

Wear of material is a highly costly failure process of utmost importance. Tribology contacts account for 23% of global energy usage; most energy is utilized to reduce friction, with the remaining portion going towards remanufacturing worn parts18. It is essential for improved tribological properties to improve the surface integrity of material by changing surface roughness, microstructure, and surface residual stresses using techniques like shot peening, nitriding, laser shot peening, thermal spraying, and laser cladding19. These techniques have been widely characterized and well-studied. However, machining (a well-known thermo-mechanical process), another important technique that highly influences surface integrity, which affects the tribological properties of a machined part, still needs to be explored. According to Hou and Li’s20 investigation on the effects of machining-induced microhardness variation on titanium alloy wear and friction, the sources of wear and friction in machined components were influenced by a soft-hard alternating local area resulting from a stress gradient brought on by tool vibration. Peng et al.21 stated that improved surface integrity, including surface roughness, microstructure, microhardness, and residual stress, decreased the wear rate of machined Inconel 718 treated by HUVC by 24.01%. According to Bertolini et al.22, increased surface integrity caused additively produced nitinol to show less wear following cryogenic finishing.

According to the best knowledge of the author, till now, no study has been done on the different cutting strategies and the effects of multi-pass end milling on the machinability and surface integrity of Incoloy 925. This study will help the industry choose an appropriate cutting approach by considering how multi-pass affects the product’s surface integrity which in turn product performance.

Kistler type of 9170 A, rotating four component cutting force dynamometer (RCD), signal conditioning type of 5238B, and Dyno-Ware software were used for recording the cutting force signals. Using a Micro Vickers Hardness Tester (HMV-G21, Shimadzu), micro-hardness was measured on the cross-section of the machined subsurface layer in the cutting direction at a load of 300 g and a holding time of 12 s. TSL OIM software was used to further analysis of EBSD mapping, whereas, before EBSD mapping, samples were cut into cross-sections of 15 mm x 5 mm x 3 mm, polished up to mirror image, and electropolished using prepared electrolyte mixing of methanol (80%) and perchloric acid (20%).

Figure 3 (a) shows that case 1, when the depth of cut is 0.3 mm, produced the highest cutting force than any other cases. When case 2 and case 3 are divided into two parts: 0.1 mm and 0.2 mm for case 2, 0.2 mm and 0.1 mm for case 3, it is found that the total cutting force of case 2 is higher than the total cutting force of case 3. In case 3, when the depth of the cut is 0.1 mm for the second cut, the additional change in cutting force due to the hardening effect of the previous cut is lower than that of case 2, when the depth of the cut is 0.2 mm for the second cut. In case 4, when the depth of cut is divided into three equal parts, 0.1 mm, 0.1 mm, and 0.1 mm for all three cuts (first, second, and third cuts), it is observed that the third cut produced the highest cutting force, followed by a second cut and first cut, and thus total cutting force produced by case 4 shows higher than case 2 and case 3. Hence, it may be concluded that additional cutting forces due to the hardening effects of previous cuts in case 4 are higher than in case 2 and case 3; however, the total cutting force produced by case 4 is lower than case 1, it may be because the required degree of deformation energy for chip formation in the low depth of cut is lower than the higher depth of cut23. Thus, sequential cutting, in any case, is better than a single cut.

In order to understand a detailed study of the effects of previous passes, a further additional analysis has been done at an equal depth of cut of 0.3 mm as first pass, 0.3 mm x 2 as second pass, and 0.3 mm x 3 as third pass. It is observed that in multi-pass cutting, later passes show higher resultant cutting force than the previous passes, as shown in Fig. 3 (b). It may be due to the surface being machined in the next pass, being affected by the work hardening caused by the previous pass. Previous passes cause a specific accumulation of stiffness degradation, residual stress, and plastic strain24. Due to these accumulations, the cutting force for the subsequent passes has been increased.

The size and shape of chips for multi-passes have been shown in Fig. 4. As the number of passes has increased, the size and shape of chips have changed due to the work hardening effect. The smallest size of chips occurs at the third pass. It may be due to the accumulation of higher strain in the form of a plastically deformed layer by the previous passes14, causing crack initiation and easy chip fracture. Thus, the sudden break of chips leads to variation in cutting force, affecting the product’s surface quality.

Figure 5 shows the free surface morphology of chips. For all machining, lamellar structures on the free surface of chips have been formed due to the formation of localized adiabatic shear bands in the primary shear zone. Rahman et al.25 have also observed lamellar structured chips after the machining of Inconel 625 and have given the reason for localized shear deformation. The width of the lamella decreases as the cutting pass increases due to higher strain localization. Due to the previous work hardening effect, the higher cutting force needed leads to higher heat generation and caused a highly localized adiabatic shear strain26. Figure 6 shows the back surface morphology of chips. It is observed that the back surface of chips formed smoother than the free surface due to the restriction of the rake face of the tool. It is also observed that as the number of passes increases, the tool friction marks increase and the surface gets rougher than the previous passes due to a higher coefficient of friction because of increased hardness caused by the earlier work hardening effect9,20.

Surface roughness and topography

Arithmetic means height deviation (Ra) was measured five times to analyze the machined surface characteristics. Figure 7 (a) shows the roughness after the final machining of each case in sequential cutting. It is found that case 1 offers the highest surface roughness, followed by case 2, case 4, and case 3. It may be due to the cutting force produced in the final cut of each sequential case, accordingly, as can be seen in Fig. 3 (a). Further effects of previous passes of cut on surface roughness can be observed in Fig. 7 (b). Surface roughness increases with the increase of cutting passes. It can be explained in such a way that the resultant cutting force increases with the increase of cutting passes, and a higher cutting force leads to a high coefficient of friction, causing surface variation. Surface roughness also depends on the heat generated during cutting. As the number of passes increases, temperature also increases, which leads to the coating of a highly plastically deformed layer on the workpiece surface27, and thus surface roughness increases.

Fig. 7(a) Surface Roughness for Sequential cuts, (b) Surface Roughness for Multi-passes.

(a) Surface Roughness for Sequential cuts, (b) Surface Roughness for Multi-passes.

The FESEM images of the machined surface with different types of defects such as feed marks, scratches, metal flow, debris, carbide cavity, micro-cavity, smearing, micro-cracks, groove, and tearing have been shown in Fig. 8. Feed marks and scratches are commonly formed in all machining operations. Metal flow and debris are generally formed due to the generation of high cutting temperatures in dry machining. Carbide particles (MC) are formed during the manufacturing of Incoloy 925 and distributed inhomogeneously in the alloy, as shown in Fig. 1 (a). During machining, when carbide particles are cut, they either will be detached or cracked. When the carbide particles break away from the machined surface, they form a carbide cavity and micro cracks, and under high stress and strain, they form smeared. Tearing is formed due to the dragging of loose carbide particles, while micro-cavity or cavity is formed due to accumulation of dislocation, causing micro-cracks near the nose of the tool. Liu et al.28 found similar defects after milling Inconel 718; the same reasons have been explained.

Figure 9 shows the 2D and 3D topography of the machined surface after each sequential case of machining. From the 2D topography of Fig. 9, it is found that the areal surface roughness (Sa) of the machined surface after the final cut of case 1 is highest as 1.093 μm, followed by case 2, case 4, and case 3 as 1.018 μm, 0.96 μm, and 0.8989 μm, respectively. It is also observed that surface burning and feed marks are less in case 3. It may be due to producing less cutting force, causing less friction and thus resulting in less heat generation. Further from the 3D topography of Fig. 9, it is observed that the highest peak is produced in case 1, followed by case 2, case 4, and case 3.

Figure 10 shows the 2D and 3D topography of machined surfaces for different machining passes. The 2D topography of Fig. 10 shows that the areal surface roughness (Sa) of the machined surface increases with the increase of cutting passes. It can be explained in such a way that with the increase of cutting passes, variation in microhardness caused by the previous pass leads to changes in cutting force and temperature. Higher microhardness requires a higher cutting force, leading to higher heat generation and causing higher plastic deformation. Thus, localized areas are intensively uplifted or squeezed, and surface topography worsens. Hou et al.9 have observed similar 3D topography during multi-step turning of Ti-6Al-4 V titanium alloy, and the same reasons have been given.

It may be due to the produced cutting forces, which caused plastic deformation accordingly and increased microhardness. Additionally, it is noted that in all machining scenarios, microhardness decreases as the depth from the machined surface to bulk material increases because of the diminished impact of plastic deformation. Micro-hardness increases as the number of cutting passes increases, as seen in Fig. 11(c). It may be due to previous work hardening effects. From the above study, it is clear that previous cuts play a significant role in the next cut; hence, further detailed analysis has been done only for multi-pass cutting to analyze the effects of previous cuts on microstructure, crystallographic texture, phase transformation, and its subsequent effect on tribological performance of machined products.

EBSD analysis for microstructure and crystallographic texture evolution

Electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) characterization of microstructure for different cutting passes has been shown in Figs. 12 and 13, while crystallographic texture is shown in Fig. 14. The crystal orientation map (IPF), grain boundary (GB) map, Kernel average Misorientation (KAM) map, and associated KAM distribution curve for unmachined and different cutting passes have been shown in Fig. 12, whereas Fig. 13 shows the grain size distribution for different samples. For the unmachined sample, Low angle grain boundary (LAGB), defined as a rotation angle less than 2º, appears on the surface in negligible amounts. Further, the average magnitude of KAM was found to be low. However, grains alteration, LAGB, and misorientation angle changed significantly after machining. After the first pass of machining, the average grain size is changed from 22.215 μm to 19.9182 μm, showing a reduction of 10.34% in the average grain size of the affected layer. However, the LAGB appearance on the machined surface layer is not significant. Further, the KAM map confirms some degree of plastic deformation by indicating the increase of the average magnitude of KAM by 18.75%. In the second pass of machining, a reduction in average grain size of 22.93% has been found. The appearance of LAGB on the machined surface is higher than unmachined and first pass of machining. Further increased amount of KAM with 55.36% confirms higher plastic deformation. In the third pass of machining, there is a reduction in average grain size of 38.45%. The appearance of LAGB on the machined surface layer is higher than the unmachined, first pass, and second pass. Further, the KAM map shows a high intensity of misorientation angle with an increased amount of 107.74%, confirming the highest plastic deformation in the machined surface layer. A high amount of LAGB indicates that the dislocation activities are motivated during the machining process, whereas high KAM confirms local plastic strain and deformation in the machined surface layer31. Fine grains generally enhance hardness, leading to higher cutting forces, while coarse grains may reduce cutting forces. It also impacts chip formation and tool wear, thereby affecting overall machining performance.

XRD analysis for phase and crystallinity

Figure 15 shows the diffraction patterns of Incoloy 925 for unmachined (bulk material) and machined at different cutting passes. Figure 15 (a) shows the diffraction pattern of all peaks for all samples, while Fig. 15 (b) shows the strongest peak for all samples. Phase analysis (of bulk material of Incoloy 925) through X-ray shows five peaks at 43.51°, 50.79°, 74.56°, 90.29°, and 95.86°, which corresponds to the FCC (ϒ) structure of Ni alloy at (111), (200), (220), (311), and (222) Miller’s indices, respectively (JCPDS/ICDD 47-1417). Any material’s phase and crystal structure are defined by the peak intensity of the diffraction pattern32. From Fig. 15 (a), it is observed that the sequence of intensity peaks for all the machined samples is similar to that of bulk material. So, it is proof that there is no phase change during the milling of Incoloy 925, irrespective of the number of cutting passes in this work. A similar explanation is given by Thakur et al.5 for no phase transformation after turning Incoloy 825 at different parameters. However, from Fig. 15 (b), it is observed that the intensity of peaks reduces with the increase of cutting passes and at the same time, the FWHM (width) of peaks increases with increase of cutting passes. The decrease in intensity shows that the crystallinity of the machined surface layer is becoming poor8 with the increase of cutting passes, and the increase in FWHM of peaks shows that grain refinement33 with the increase in cutting passes. The grains modification in the machining process can be explained by the thermo-mechanical effect causing severe plastic deformation.

Tribological performance of machined parts

Surface characteristics are crucial to a component’s tribological performance. To find out how multi-pass cutting history affects the tribological performance of machined components, a tribological test (ball on disk) is conducted. Figure 16 displays the coefficient of friction (COF) graph for several samples treated under various cutting passes. It is observed that fluctuation in COF with sliding time for the third sample processed with higher cutting passes is higher compared to samples processed with lower cutting passes, which may be possibly due to high variation in surface roughness and microhardness within the local area20. Average COF increases for the samples processed with the increase of cutting passes and 14.03% hgher COF is observed for the sample processed with a third pass of cutting compared to the sample processed with the first pass of cutting. It may be the result of increased surface roughness (see Fig. 7(b)), which concentrates stress on the material’s surface and lessens its capacity to resist friction34.

Fig. 16Coefficient of friction of machined component after different cutting passes.

Coefficient of friction of machined component after different cutting passes.

The 3D optical profile of wear tracks for the samples processed under different cutting passes is shown in Fig. 17 (a1, b2, and c1). From the 2D optical profile of wear tracks (see Fig. 17 (a2, b2, and c2)), it is observed that the width and depth of wear tracks increase for the samples processed with the increase of cutting passes, which indicates a high amount of material wear. Further, the increased width of wear tracks for the samples processed with higher cutting passes is confirmed from SEM images (see Fig. 17 (a3, b3, and c3)). The higher width and depth of wear tracks for the sample processed with higher cutting passes are possibly due to higher surface roughness than those processed with lower cutting passes (see Fig. 7 (b)). Hanief and Wani35 also reported high amount of wear for the material (sample) having higher surface roughness. It is important to note that although surface microhardness increases with the increase of cutting passes (see Fig. 11 (c)), however COF and material’s wear are higher for the samples processed with higher cutting passes, which possibly due to the domination effect of higher surface roughness as compared to microhardness on tribological performance36.

Conclusions

A thorough examination of the various cutting strategies and the impact of multi-pass end milling on the machinability and surface integrity of Incoloy 925 was carried out in the first part of the study. The important factors, such as surface roughness, surface defects, surface topography, microhardness, microstructural changes, crystallographic texture evolution, and phase transformation, have been examined in relation to cutting force, chip shape, and surface integrity. The impact of thermo-mechanical loading history on tribological behavior has been investigated in the second part of the study. The following are the main findings of this experimental study:
