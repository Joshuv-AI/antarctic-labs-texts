Characteristics and long‐term outcomes of children with confirmed focal cortical dysplasia type 1 after epilepsy surgery: A population‐based study
Authors: Noreen Bux
Published: 2026

To the Editors,

We read with great interest the recently published article, “Characteristics and long‐term outcomes of children with confirmed focal cortical dysplasia type 1 after epilepsy surgery: A population‐based study,” 1 which evaluated surgical outcomes in children with isolated focal cortical dysplasia type 1 (FCD1). The authors are to be congratulated on providing important real‐world evidence through long‐term follow‐up, meticulous histopathological re‐evaluation, and a complete evaluation of seizure, cognitive, radiological, and surgical outcomes. The finding of residual postoperative MRI lesions as a significant predictor of seizure recurrence is particularly noteworthy and represents an important consideration for surgical planning and postoperative evaluation.

Despite these strengths, several methodological aspects merit further discussion. First, the study used univariate analyses primarily to identify factors associated with postoperative seizure outcomes. Considering the complex interactions among age at epilepsy onset, epilepsy syndrome, extent of resection, residual MRI abnormalities, and presurgical investigations, multivariable regression modeling may have better identified independent predictors while accounting for potential confounding, as recommended for observational research. 2

Second, the inclusion of patients treated over nearly three decades (1994–2022) may have introduced temporal heterogeneity. Major advances in neuroimaging, invasive EEG techniques, surgical planning, and perioperative management have substantially changed epilepsy surgery practice during this period. Stratifying outcomes according to treatment era could have clarified the influence of evolving technologies on long‐term surgical outcomes. 3

Finally, although seizure outcomes were reported using the Engel classification, the durability of seizure freedom over time was not evaluated using survival analysis. The median follow‐up was long at 8.4 years, and several patients did have delayed relapse of seizures. Time‐to‐event analysis including Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazards regression would have been able to provide a more comprehensive assessment of long‐term seizure outcomes while accounting for variable follow‐up duration. These methods are highly recommended in research on epilepsy surgery outcomes, as they allow an estimation of the risk of relapse over time and comparison across surgical cohorts. 4 Future studies should also assess the cost‐effectiveness of early epilepsy surgery for FCD1, especially concerning reductions in recurrent hospitalizations, prolonged use of antiseizure medications, and long‐term healthcare utilization. 5

Furthermore, the development and external validation of prediction models incorporating clinical, electrophysiological, imaging, and pathological variables could potentially improve individualized surgical decision‐making in pediatric FCD1. 6

Overall, this important study provides valuable evidence supporting epilepsy surgery in children with isolated FCD1 and establishes a strong foundation for future research aimed at optimizing patient selection and long‐term outcomes.
