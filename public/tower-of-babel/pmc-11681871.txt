Real-life study on the effectiveness and safety of sofosbuvir/velpatasvir-based antiviral agents for hepatitis C eradication in Chinese patients
Authors: Jiayi Wang, Lingyao Du, Dongmei Zhang, Chen Zhou, Yilan Zeng, Miao Liu, Xing Cheng, Xiaona Song, Han Chen, Ning Han, Enqiang Chen, Hong Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatitis C virus (HCV) eradication with sofosbuvir/velpatasvir (SOF/VEL) represents a significant advancement, offering hope for eliminating the virus in diverse patient populations. But real-world data on its effectiveness and safety remains scarce for patients with chronic hepatitis C (CHC) in China, especially those with HCV GT3b, cirrhosis, hepato-cellular carcinoma (HCC), or HCV/hepatitis B (HBV), HCV/HIV, or HCV/HBV/HIV coinfection.MethodsIn this real-world prospective observational study, we recruited patients from the West China Hospital and Public Health Clinical Center of Chengdu in China. Patients included adults with with CHC and any genotype (GT), with or without cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), HCV/HBV, HCV/HIV, or HCV/HBV/HIV coinfection. Patients were administered SOF/VEL (400/100 mg) ± ribavirin (RBV) once daily for 12 weeks. The primary efficacy endpoint was sustained virological response at post-treatment week 12 (SVR12). Adverse events (AEs) were evaluated during treatment.ResultsThe study included 483 patients with HCV genotypes 1, 2, 3, 6 and uncertain ones. Among them, 35.4 % (171/483, ITT) and 36.7 % (166/452, mITT) received SOF/VEL + RBV. At the end of treatment , 99.2 % (ITT, 479/483) and 99.1 % (mITT, 448/452) of patients had undetectable HCV RNA. SVR12 rates were 92.8 % [intention to treat (ITT), 448/483] and 99.1 % [modified ITT (mITT), 448/452]. In the mITT analysis, SVR12 for patients with HCV GT3b, those with cirrhosis or HCC, and those coinfected with HBV/HIV was 99.2 % (130/131), 99.4 % (168/169), and 97.6 % (40/41), respectively. The albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) (−3.01 vs. −3.18 P < 0.001), Fibrosis-4 (FIB4) Index (2.53 vs. 1.88, P = 0.004) and AST to Platelet Ratio Index (APRI) (0.99 vs. 0.44, P < 0.001) scores showed a significant decrease from baseline to SVR12. No patients experienced grade 3–5 AEs.ConclusionsAlthough a high proportion of patients included in this study had HCV GT3b, cirrhosis, HCC, or HCV/HBV, HCV/HIV, or HCV/HBV/HIV coinfection, SOF/VEL ± RBV was highly effective and well tolerated in Chinese patients with CHC.Keywords: Hepatitis C, Sofosbuvir-velpatasvir (SOF/VEL), Ribavirin, Genotype, Cirrhosis, Coinfection

Introduction

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) remains a major public health concern, responsible for an estimated 290,000 deaths in 2019 (1). In China, while both the prevalence and overall mortality have seen notable declines, approximately 10 million people are living with the infection.2,3 Patients with chronic hepatitis C (CHC) are at a significantly higher risk of progressing to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (4). Effective antiviral treatment can reduce the risk of disease progression, liver and non-liver complications.5, 6, 7 Because of the introduction of direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) for HCV, 95 % of treated patients with CHC are expected to achieve a sustained virological response 12 weeks after the end of therapy (SVR12),8 which is associated with a 71–76 % reduction in HCC risk and a 74 % reduction in risk of death compared to those who do not achieved SVR12.9,10

Sofosbuvir/velpatasvir (SOF/VEL) is a once-daily, single-tablet, pan-genotypic regimen consisting of the HCV NS5B polymerase inhibitor sofosbuvir and HCV NS5A inhibitor velpatasvir.11,12 The use of SOF/VEL has recently been approved by the Chinese government as DAA regimen for chronic HCV genotype (GT) 1–6 infection treatment regardless of cirrhosis. Real-world data of SOF/VEL therapy for CHC has been reported extensively in Europe and North America, showing that SOF/VEL is a highly effective treatment in GT1-6 (SVR12 rates ranging from 83.3% to 99.0 %).13 However, there aisstill few real-world data in Chinese CHC population.14, 15, 16

Both race/origin and HCV GT can affect SVR12 in individuals with CHC.17 Caucasians have higher odds of reaching SVR12 compared to African-Americans,18 therefore further comparison is required between Asian patients and those from Western countries. Moreover, several studies have shown that individuals with the HCV GT3b are less likely to achieve SVR12 compared to those with other genotypes. The HCV GT1–6 have been found in China, and show significant regional difference. The HCV GT1b and GT2a are the two predominant subtypes in the North, Northwest, Northeast, East (except Jiangxi province) and Central China (except for Hunan province), while HCV GT3 in the Southwest of China, with a prevalence of 18.8 %, remains at a higher level than in other regions.19 The latest data on SOF/VEL effectiveness in Chinese patients with HCV GT3b, cirrhosis, HCC and those coinfected with HIV or HBV requires further investigation,13 with Southwest China having a higher proportion of HCV GT3b.

Therefore, the aim of our study was to assess the effectiveness and safety of SOF/VEL-based treatment for CHC patients in China, particularly for those with HCV GT3b, cirrhosis, HCC, or HCV/HBV, HCV/HIV, or HCV/HBV/HIV coinfection in a real-world setting.

Methods

Study design

This real-world prospective observational study was performed in the West China Hospital and Public Health Clinical Center of Chengdu from February 2019, through June 2022. This study was approved by the West China Hospital of Sichuan University Clinical Trial Ethics Committee (No. 2019–008) and conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants agreed to participate to the study with good adherence and provided written informed consent.

Patients

The diagnosis of CHC was based on China's guidelines for the prevention and treatment of HCV (2019) with HCV for 6 months or longer. Patients were 18 years of age or older and required to meet all of the following eligibility criteria1: positive HCV antibody and documented history of CHC for at least 6 months2; detectable serum HCV RNA (>15 IU/mL)3; treatment naïve or treatment experienced. Patients with cirrhosis or HCC, coinfection with HBV or HIV were also eligible for enrollment. The diagnosis of cirrhosis was determined by ultrasound imaging ( criteria included features of cirrhosis and portal hypertension, such as splenomegaly, and portal vein diameter ≥1.3 cm according to Chinese guidelines on the management of liver cirrhosis), the Fibrosis-4 (FIB4) Index for Liver Fibrosis (FIB4) >3.25, or AST to Platelet Ratio Index (APRI) >2. For compensated cirrhotic patients, at least one of these non-invasive parameters needed to be abnormal, while those with decompensated cirrhosis needed to have at least one complication including ascites, jaundice, variceal hemorrhage or hepatic encephalopathy.

Patients who met any of the following exclusion criteria were not included in this study1: taking drugs (not including proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)/H2 receptor antagonists) that have significant drug to drug interaction (DDI) evidence with SOF/VEL2; patients who were already taking antiviral therapy, therefore unable to track baseline characteristics3; patients who had failed previous generic DAA therapy.

Interventions

According to the clinical-development program of SOF/VEL (ASTRAL 1–5),20, 21, 22, 23 eligible patients received SOF (400 mg)/VEL (100 mg) (Epclusa, Gilead Sciences) single tablet, administered orally once daily for 12 weeks, with or without ribavirin (RBV) according to the clinician's judgement; factors considered by clinicians included HCV GT3, HIV or/and HBV coinfection, liver cirrhosis HCC. and coinfections RBV was administrated orally with food twice daily, with the dose determined according to the label recommendation (400–1000 mg daily for body weight＜75 kg, and 1200 mg daily for body weight ≥75 kg, initial dose for Child-Pugh B or C patients was 600 mg).

Study assessment

All participants completed screening, on-treatment, and post-treatment assessments.

The screening visit took place at Week 0 pre-treatment, and procedures were completed prior to enrollment. Demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, gender, concomitant medications, comorbidities and coinfections were obtained. Blood samples were collected for hematology and chemistry testing, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), HCV RNA, HCV genotype, HCV antibody, HIV antibody, and HBsAg. Liver ultrasound was also performed at Week 0.

Treatment assessments were completed at Week 4 and Week 12 (±3 days), while post-treatment assessments were performed 12 weeks (±3 days) after the date of the last administration of SOF/VEL. Physical examination, and adverse events (AEs) were completed at both treatment and post-treatment assessments. Blood samples were collected for hematology and chemistry tests, AFP, and HCV RNA at treatment Week 4, Week 12, and post-treatment Week 12, while liver ultrasound was performed at treatment Week 12 and post-treatment Week 12 visits.

During visits, virological breakthrough or relapse was assessed. Virological breakthrough/relapse was defined as patients achieving undetectable HCV RNA during treatment or at the end of treatment but failing to achieve HCV RNA <15 IU/mL at 12 weeks post-treatment.8

Study medication was discontinued in the following instances1: unacceptable toxicity or intolerable AEs2; pregnancy3; significant protocol violation4; participant request to discontinue for any reason. Following the last dose of study drug; all participants who had terminated treatment early also completed the post-treatment Week 12 visit and were included in the analysis.

Data collection

Hematology testing included hemoglobin (Hb), platelet count (PLT), red blood cell count (RBC), white blood cell count (WBC) with differential (absolute and percentage) including lymphocytes, monocytes, neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, reticulocyte count and mean corpuscular volume (mcv). Chemistry testing included alanine aminotransferase (ALT/SGPT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST/SGOT), albumin, alkaline phosphatase, creatine kinase, creatinine, total bilirubin (total with reflex ), glucose, lipase, potassium, and sodium.

Serum HCV RNA was measured using the COBAS® TaqMan HCV Test, with a lower limit of quantification of 15 IU/mL. For selected analyses of early time point data, HCV RNA data (IU/mL) were transformed to the logarithmic base 10 scale (log10 IU/mL). HCV genotypes/subtypes were determined using Sanger sequencing.

The albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) score was calculated for liver function assessment according to the following formula: (log10 bilirubin [μmol/L] × 0.66) + (albumin [g/L] × −0.0852).24,25 Non-invasive scores of liver fibrosis, FIB4, and Aspartate Aminotransferase-to-Platelet Ratio Index (APRI) were calculated according to the following formula: FIB4 = [Age (yr) × AST (U/L)]/[PLT (109/L) × square root of ALT (U/L)],26 and APRI = AST (/ULN)/PLT (109/L) × 100.27 Liver ultrasound was also used for liver fibrosis assessment.

Outcomes

The primary outcome for the effectiveness of the SOF/VEL regimen was the rate of SVR defined as HCV RNA <15 IU/mL at 12 weeks after the end of treatment in patients who had received at least one dose of SOF/VEL. Secondary effectiveness outcome included the change from baseline in the FIB4 and APRI scores at 12 weeks post-treatment. Pill counts were used to estimate treatment adherence.28,29

Safety outcomes for tSOF/VEL were the rate of AEs (Grade 3–5) and treatment discontinuations because of AEs in participants who had received at least one dose of SOF/VEL. AEs (Grade 3–5) were defined according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) v5.0.

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographics, clinical characteristics, effectiveness, and safety were analyzed. Virological response was assessed using an intention-to-treat (ITT) approach, which included all patients who had received at least one dose of the SOF/VEL regimen, regardless of treatment discontinuation or loss to follow-up. A modified ITT (mITT) analysis was also performed, excluding patients who had discontinued treatment for reasons unrelated to therapy or were lost to follow-up.30,31 Demographic and baseline characteristics were summarized using standard descriptive methods. Quantitative continuous variables were given as either means (± standard deviations [SD]) or medians (with inter-quartile ranges [IQR]) and compared using the unpaired Student's t-test or the Mann-Whitney test. Qualitative or categorical variables were compared with the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. P values ＜0.05 were considered significant. All analyses were performed with SPSS software for Windows (Version 19.0).

Results

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 483 patients diagnosed with CHC were included in this prospective observational cohort study for SVR evaluation and safety assessment. Following the exclusion of 87 ineligible individuals, no patients discontinued study medication.

Characteristics of the patients who received SOF/VEL-based therapy are described in Table 1, with an average age of 49.6 ± 11.4 years and 53.0 % (256/483) of males. Among the ITT population, genotype 1 (198/483, 41.0 %), 2 (37/483, 7.7 %), 3 (174/483, 36.0 %), and 6 (57/483, 11.8 %) were identified, and 17 patients (17/483, 3.5 %) had an uncertain genotype due to low viral loads for the HCV genotype assay (7/17), one not aligned with known genotypes (5/17), or unavailable data (5/17). A total of 42 patients (8.7 %) were coinfected with HBV, HIV or both HBV and HIV. As for liver comorbidities, 7 (1.4 %) patients had HCC, 9 (1.9 %) were diagnosed with decompensated cirrhosis and 162 (33.5 %) with compensated cirrhosis. The baseline ALBI score was calculated for liver function, while the FIB4 and APRI ones were used to assess baseline liver fibrosis, with a median of −3.02 (−3.26 to −2.69), 2.54 (1.36–4.31) and 0.99 (0.56–1.69), respectively. Moreover, the FIB4 score in 151 (31.3 %) patients was over 3.25, while the APRI one in 88 (18.2 %) patients was over 2.

Response assessment in different genotypes and comorbidities

Among the 483 patients included in the ITT population, 31 (6.4 %) were lost to follow-up for reasons outlined in Fig. 1 and excluded from the mITT analysis (N = 452). Of these, 35.4 % (171/483) in the ITT population and 36.7 % (166/452) in the mITT population received SOF/VEL + RBV therapy. The baseline characteristics of patients, stratified by RBV treatment, are detailed in Supplemental Table 1. Of the patients who had received SOF/VEL, 99.2 % (479/483) and 99.1 % (448/452) had undetectable HCV RNA at the end of treatment (EOT) in the ITT population and mITT populations, respectively.

For the primary effectiveness endpoint, the overall SVR12 rate was 92.8 % (448/483) according to the ITT analysis (Table 2). Patients receiving SOF/VEL alone exhibited a lower SVR12 rate compared to those treated with SOF/VEL + RBV (91.0 % vs. 95.9 %), a difference potentially influenced by the those who were lost to follow-up (Supplemental Table 2). The effectiveness of SOF/VEL was further assessed among subgroups based on genotypes. Rate of SVR12 in genotype 1a, 3a/3 but subtype not confirmed, and genotype uncertain achieved 100 % (2/2, 38/38, 17/17, respectively).

In the mITT analysis, the SVR12 rate was 99.1 % (448/452). There was no significant difference between patients treated with or without RBV (P = 0.580, Supplemental Table 2). Rate of SVR12 in genotype 1a, 2a, 3a/3 but subtype not confirmed, 6u, 6n, and genotype uncertain achieved 100 % (2/2, 33/33, 38/38, 1/1, 6/6, 17/17, respectively), while SVR12 was 98.9 % (178/180), 99.2 % (130/131), and 97.7 % (43/44) in genotype 1b, 3b, and 6a, respectively. There was no significant advantage of RBV usage in genotype 3b (rate of SVR12 in patients treated without/with RBV: 100.0 % vs. 99.1 %, P = 0.698) and 6a (rate of SVR12 in patients treated without/with RBV: 97.6 % vs. 100 %, P = 0.825), while in genotype 1b, rate of SVR12 in patients treated with SOF/VEL was higher than SOF/VEL + RBV (99.4 % vs. 94.1 %, P = 0.049).

As for patients with HCC and decompensated cirrhosis, the SVR12 rate reached 100 % (7/7, 9/9), irrespective of RBV administration in both ITT and mITT analyses. In the mITT analysis, the rate of SVR12 in compensated cirrhosis with SOF/VEL 100 %was (91/91), while having added RBV showed a lower SVR12 (98.4 %, 61/62), without significant difference observed (P = 0.224). The rate of SVR12 in the mITT population coinfected with HBV or HBV/HIV reached 100 % (18/18, 3/3), while for those coinfected with HIV, SVR12 with SOF/VEL was lower than with SOF/VEL + RBV (92.3 % vs. 100 %, P = 0.452), without a significant difference.

Assessment and variation of liver function before/after SOF/VEL ± RBV therapy

The ALBI score was used to evaluate liver function, with lower scores (Grade 1) indicating better liver function compared to higher scores (Grade 3), which represents more advanced liver dysfunction. As shown in Fig. 2A, the mean ALBI score in the mITT population significantly decreased from baseline to Week 12 (−3.01 vs. −3.15, P < 0.001) and to SVR12 (−3.01 vs. −3.18, P < 0.001). No significant decrease was observed from Week 12 to SVR12 (−3.15 vs. −3.18, P = 0.20). Nevertheless, Fig. 2B shows that in a subgroup of patients with cirrhosis, significant decrease of the ALBI score was also observed from baseline to Week 12 (−3.02 vs. −3.15, P = 0.030) and SVR12 (−3.02 vs. −3.18, P < 0.001). Assessment and variation of liver fibrosis before/after SOF/VEL ± RBV therapy

FIB-4 and APRI are non-invasive scoring estimates commonly used to assess liver fibrosis in individuals with chronic liver diseases, with higher values indicating more advanced liver disease. Both FIB4 and APRI scores decreased significantly from baseline to Week 12 and SVR12 (Fig. 3) in the mITT population. The mean FIB4 score was significantly decreased from baseline to Week 12 (2.53 vs. 1.85, P = 0.002) and to SVR12 (2.53 vs. 1.88, P = 0.004). Although the FIB4 score increased from Week 12 to SVR12 (1.85 vs. 1.88, P = 0.979), no significant difference was observed. A similar result was found in the APRI score (baseline vs. Week 12/SVR12: 0.99 vs. 0.43/0.44, both P < 0.001; Week 12 vs. SVR12: 0.43 vs. 0.44, P = 0.996). Non‐SVR patients

In the ITT analysis, the 31 patients who were lost to follow-up were classified as non-SVR patients. However, all of these individuals successfully completed the 12-week treatment regimen without discontinuation and achieved HCV RNA negativity at the end of treatment.

Table 3 shows the characteristics of the four patients who did not achieve SVR12 in the mITT analysis. Case 1, 2, and 4 completed the whole treatment process with full adherence to the prescribed treatment regimens, while case 3 refused to provide adherence information. The HCV RNA of the 4 patients remained detectable during study assessments (nonresponse) and all of them did not experience virological breakthrough/relapse. Among these 4 cases, 2 were diagnosed with genotype 1b (case 2 and 3) and 3 received RBV treatment (case 1, 2 and 4). Case 1 was coinfected with HIV before treatment and with a higher ALBI score (grade 2) and case 2 was diagnosed with cirrhosis based on both FIB4 and APRI indexes.

Safety assessment in the overall CHC population

Patients who received at least one dose of SOF/VEL were enrolled for a safety assessment. No premature discontinuations of the study drug occurred due to AEs or other reasons. Additionally, no patients reported any grade 3–5 AEs.

Discussion

This prospective observational cohort study enrolled patients with CHC infection of any genotype and showed that SOF/VEL with or without RBV treatment achieved high SVR12 rates (92.8 % in the ITT and 99.1 % in mITT analyses). The study included patients with characteristics historically associated with lower response to DAA therapies such as HCV GT3b, cirrhosis, HCC, and HBV/HIV coinfection. None of these factors appeared to be associated with virologic failure under SOF/VEL with or without RBV, which occurred in only 4 patients. Regarding safety, no treatment discontinuation occurred and all adverse events were mild. Therefore, it is considered that SOF/VEL-based treatment showed good effectiveness and acceptable safety in patients with HCV in Chinese patients.

The effectiveness results observed in this study are consistent with those observed in the global SOF/VEL development program. Phase 3 clinical trials (ASTRAL-1, 21, ASTRAL-2, 20, ASTRAL-3, 20) reported SVR12 rates of 98.1 % (1015/1035) among patients with CHC, with or without cirrhosis. Similarly, growing evidence supports the effectiveness of SOF/VEL-based treatment in Asian populations. A phase 3 trial conducted in Asia reported SVR12 of 97 % (362/375),11 and studies from Japan and Singapore demonstrated SVR12 rates exceeding 90 %.32, 33, 34 Furthermore, our findings are consistent with a large real-world study in Taiwan, which reported a 99.4 % SVR12 rate in 3480 patients treated with SOF/VEL ± RBV, regardless of baseline characteristics.30 Another real-world study across the North and Northeast China confirmed the safety and effectiveness of SOF/VEL + RBV. However, regional differences in HCV genotypes must be considered. While the HCV GT1 and GT2 were predominant in Northern China, Southern regions exhibit greater genetic diversity, with a notably higher prevalence of HCV GT3b in Southwest China.19 These findings, combined with prior investigations, underscore the importance of tailored approaches in these areas. Our results further indicate that SOF/VEL ± RBV regimens are safe and effective, reinforcing the suitability of SOF/VEL-based therapies for patients with CHC in China.

Although HCV GT3b infection, comorbidities of cirrhosis or HCC, and HCV/HBV, HCV/HIV, or HCV/HBV/HIV coinfection were considered poor prognostic features, our current study observed high SVR12 rates among those with these features, which may be attributed to the coadministration of RBV in a proportion of them. Our mITT analysis indicated an overall SVR12 of 99.1 % despite a recruitment of patients with 29.0 % (131/452) of them with HCV GT3b, 37.4 % (169/452) with liver cirrhosis or HCC, and 9.1 % (41/452) with either HBV, HIV, or HBV/HIV coinfection. Previous studies reported a lower efficacy in those with HCV GT3b (75 %, 30/40) and with cirrhosis (90 %, 60/67),11 and these patients had a higher risk of virologic relapse after completing treatment.34 However, our findings demonstrated higher SVR12 rates in HCV GT3b (95.6 % with ITT, and 99.2 % with mITT) and with cirrhosis or HCC (94.4 % with ITT, and 99.4 % with mITT). The improvement may result from the addition of RBV to SOF/VEL which has been observed in patients with cirrhosis who are infected with HCV GT323,35). In a Spanish study of 12-week SOF/VEL treatment in patients with compensated cirrhosis and GT3 HCV infection, 94 % of whom having GT3a, the addition of RBV decreased the relapse rate from 5 % to 2 %. Similarly, a retrospective study in China showed a 3.3 % increase in SVR12 among cirrhotic patients with HCV GT3 when RBV was added.15 A meta-analysis has further indicated that while RBV did not significantly improve SVR12 in HCV GT1 or GT2 infection, it significantly enhanced SVR12 in GT3 infection.13 In our study, we found that SOF/VEL + RBV was as efficient and safe as SOF/VEL in HCV GT3b, those with HCC or cirrhosis, and those coinfected with HBV or HIV. Furthermore, patients with severe symptom or out of range baseline parameters were more likely to be administered SOF/VEL + RBV. These findings highlight the need for specific methods or scoring systems to identify those who would benefit from the addition of RBV and to optimize cost-effective treatment strategies.

Improvements in liver function and alleviation of liver fibrosis were observed in all patients in this study, as evidenced by significant reductions in the ALBI, FIB4, and APRI scores from baseline to Week 12 and at SVR12. Our results also showed an improvement of liver function in CHC patients with cirrhosis. The findings were consistent with another report, which showed significant improvement in the FIB4 index post-treatment with SOF/VEL.36 A real-world study which included the Child-Pugh class assessment found that 84.4 % and 64.6 % had improved Child-Pugh and model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) scores among the 96 patients achieving SVR12.37 Thus, SOF/VEL-based treatment is effective in improving liver function and alleviating liver fibrosis in patients, regardless of cirrhosis.

There were limitations identified in this real-world cohort study. Physicians may extend the duration of SOF/VEL therapy to 24 weeks if patients failed to achieve virological suppression after 12 weeks of treatment according to Chinese guideline, which may impose a significant economic burden. To address this, RBV was added to 35.4 % (171/483) of patients based on clinical judgment, particularly for those who were considered less likely to achieve SVR12. We analyzed the baseline characteristics of the those treated with or without RBV, and found that HCV GT3, cirrhosis, AST, and direct bilirubin were conditions or laboratory tests considered by clinicians before treatment. A significant difference was observed in the APRI index between groups with and without RBV. Moreover, as a result of limited numbers of patients and the lower ratio of HCV GT4 and GT5 in China, the treatment outcomes for these may be lacking. Furthermore, 31 patients were lost to follow-up after completing treatment due to various reasons including COVID-19. Although all of them achieved HCV RNA negativity at Week 12 and reported no AEs during the treatment period, we are unable to confirm whether they would have achieved SVR12, which may introduce a potential bias in the assessment of treatment outcomes. Future studies would benefit from strategies to minimize loss to follow-up, ensuring more complete data collection and a more comprehensive understanding of treatment effectiveness and safety. Despite these limitations, the usual exclusion criteria were applied to the study population, which ensures that the study is a true reflection of the real-world management of patients with CHC in China.

In conclusion SOF/VEL with or without RBV for 12 weeks is an effective and safe treatment for Chinese patients with CHC infection, regardless of genotype, cirrhosis, HCC and HCV/HBV, HCV/HIV, or HCV/HBV/HIV coinfection. Baseline characteristics including HCV GT3, cirrhosis, AST, direct bilirubin, and APRI index are considered as factors for RBV coadministration.
