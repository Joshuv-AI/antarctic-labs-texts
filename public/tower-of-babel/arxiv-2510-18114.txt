Latent Discrete Diffusion Models
Dario Shariatian, Alain Durmus, Stefano Peluchetti

Abstract
We study discrete diffusion for language and other categorical data and focus on a common limitation of masked denoisers: reverse transitions typically factorize across positions, which can weaken joint structure and degrade quality in few-step generation. We propose Latent Discrete Diffusion Models (LDDMs), which couple a masked discrete diffusion over tokens with a continuous diffusion over latent embeddings. The latent channel provides a softer signal and carries cross-token dependencies that help resolve ambiguities. We present two instantiations: (i) FUJI-LDDMs, which perform fully joint denoising of tokens and latents, and (ii) SEQ-LDDMs, which sequentially resolve the latent and then the discrete chain conditionally on it. For both variants we derive ELBO-style objectives and discuss design choices to learn informative latents yet amenable to diffusoin modeling. In experiments, LDDMs yield improvements on unconditional generation metrics as compared to state-of-the-art masked discrete diffusion baselines, and are effective at lower sampling budgets, where unmasking many tokens per step is desirable.



1
Introduction

Diffusion models have become a standard tool for high-fidelity generation on continuous modalities such as images and audio
(
Esser et al. 2024
;
Ho et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2021
)
. For discrete data, diffusion offers coarse-to-fine generation with parallel token refinement, flexible conditioning/infilling via bidirectional attention, and opportunities for faster sampling through block updates.

Early approaches adapted continuous diffusion to categorical data by embedding tokens in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and running a continuous process in the embedding space
(
Dieleman et al. 2022
)
. Subsequent work emphasized staying in discrete state space and reported improved performance and scaling characteristics. Within discrete formulations, masked processes are particularly effective and scalable in high dimensions
(
Sahoo et al. 2024
;
Lou et al. 2024
;
Hoogeboom et al. 2021
;
Austin et al. 2021
)
.

However, discrete data, like text, remains challenging for discrete diffusion. Despite steady progress and large-model training
(
Nie et al. 2025
;
Song et al. 2025
)
, auto-regressive (AR) models remain strong baselines across model sizes, while discrete diffusion primarily differentiates itself by enabling multi-token unmasking per step enabling faster sampling speeds
(
Campbell et al. 2022
;
Lou et al. 2024
;
Hoogeboom et al. 2021
;
Austin et al. 2021
;
Zheng et al. 2025a
;
Ni et al. 2025
;
Kang et al. 2025
)
.

A key limitation is the factorized model: if unmasked, individual tokens are updated independently given a noise sequence, which discards joint structure. The effect is magnified in the few-step regime, where independent per-token decisions can combine into jointly inconsistent outputs
(
Liu et al. 2025a
)
. Predictor–corrector samplers and tailored unmasking schedules help but do not fully address this sensitivity
(
Wang et al. 2025
;
Gat et al. 2024a
;
Liu et al. 2025b
;
Shi et al. 2024
;
Pham et al. 2025
)
. In continuous diffusion, denoising is soft and reversible, allowing errors to amortize, whereas unveiling a token in masked discrete diffusion is a hard commitment. A softer auxiliary signal may mitigate this rigidity and stabilize training
(
Sahoo et al. 2025
)
.

Our approach.

We augment masked discrete diffusion with a continuous latent channel that carries cross-token information and supplies a softer learning and inference signal. Concretely, we introduce
Latent Discrete Diffusion Models
(LDDMs), which couple a masked discrete diffusion over tokens with a continuous diffusion over latent embeddings. Latents can be obtained from a pre-trained encoder or via a custom encoder trained to be highly informative for conditional reconstruction, improving coherence when many tokens are unmasked simultaneously.

We instantiate two core variants.

-LDDMs perform

J

nt denoising in both channels, co-evolving tokens and latents at each step.

-LDDMs perform

(i)

if
\mu\ll\nu
, and
\mathrm{KL}(\mu|\nu)=+\infty
otherwise. We denote by
\updelta_{x}
the Dirac mass at
x\in\mathsf{E}
. For any
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Sigma
a semi-definite positive matrix,
y\mapsto\mathrm{N}(y;\mu,\Sigma)
, the density of the Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu

\Sigma
.

2.1
Continuous Diffusion Models

In this first setting, we aim to sample from a continuous distribution on the state space
\mathsf{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, with density
q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{0}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure. The forward, or noising process,
\{Y_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
, evolves in
\mathsf{Y}
and is a Markovian process starting from
Y_{0}\sim q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{0}
, and driven for
t>0
by the following updates:

Y_{t}=\alpha_{t}Y_{t-1}+\sigma_{t}\varepsilon_{t}\;,

(2)

where
\{\varepsilon_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables, independent from
Y_{0}
, and
\{\alpha_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
,
\{\sigma_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
are two sequences of positive real number, referred to as the noise schedule. Then, given
Y_{0}
,
(Y_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
is a Gaussian process which is also Markov. We can decompose the joint density of
\{Y_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
as

q_{0:T}(y_{0:T})=q_{0}(y_{0})q_{T|0}(y_{T}|y_{0})\prod_{t=2}^{T}{q_{t-1|t,0}(y_{t-1}|y_{t},y_{0})}\;,

(3)

where
q_{t-1|t,0}
are Gaussian transition densities, such that for any
y_{0},y_{t}\in\mathsf{Y}
,
q_{t-1|t,0}(y_{t-1}|y_{t},y_{0})=\mathrm{N}(y_{t-1};\tilde{\mu}_{t-1}(y_{t},y_{0}),\tilde{\Sigma}_{t-1})
, where

\tilde{\mu}_{t-1}(y_{t},y_{0})=\frac{\alpha_{t}\,\bar{\sigma}_{t-1}^{2}}{\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\,y_{t}+\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\,\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\,y_{0},\qquad\tilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}=\frac{\bar{\sigma}_{t-1}^{2}\,\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\,\mathrm{I}_{d},

(4)

with
\bar{\alpha}_{t},\bar{\sigma}_{t}
defined recursively as
\bar{\alpha}_{t}/\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}=\alpha_{t}
, and
\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}=\alpha_{t}^{2}\bar{\sigma}_{t-1}^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}
, see
Section

B.1
for more details. Based on this decomposition, we consider the variational family associated with our generative process, parameterized by
\theta
defined for
y_{0:T}\in\mathsf{Y}^{T+1}
as

p^{\theta}_{0:T}(y_{0:T})=p^{\theta}_{T}(y_{T})\prod_{t=1}^{T}q_{t-1|t,0}(y_{t-1}|y_{t},y_{\theta}(y_{t},t))\;,

(5)

where
p^{\theta}_{T}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\bar{\sigma}_{T}^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d})
, and
y_{\theta}
is the
continuous denoiser
parameterized by a neural network with weights
\theta
. This family is then fitted by minimizing the negative Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) given by

\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{Y}}(\theta)=\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{Y}}_{T}+\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{Y}}_{0}+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=2}^{T}\ {\lambda}^{\mathsf{Y},\text{ELBO}}_{t}\ \|Y_{0}-y_{\theta}(Y_{t},t)\|^{2}\right]\;,

(6)

where

{\lambda}^{\mathsf{Y},\text{ELBO}}_{t}=\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}^{2}\,\sigma^{2}_{t}}{2\bar{\sigma}_{t-1}^{2}\,\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\;,

(7)

and
L_{T},L_{0}
are given in (
38
) but are typically neglected, see
Section

B.1
for more details. In practice, during optimization, the multiplicative factors
{\lambda}^{\mathsf{Y},\text{ELBO}}_{t}
can deviate from their true ELBO values
(
Karras et al. 2022
;
Dieleman 2024
)
. In addition, it holds that for any
t\in\{0,\ldots,T\}
,

q_{t|0}(y_{t}|y_{0})=\mathrm{N}(y_{t};\ \bar{\alpha}_{t}y_{0},\ \bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d})\;,

(8)

where
q_{t|0}
is the conditional distribution of
Y_{t}
given
Y_{0}
. Plugging (
8
) with stochastic optimization of (
6
) yields a
simulation-free training
procedure. We mention that we can use the trained denoiser network
y_{\theta}
with a deterministic generation procedure, as introduced in DDIM
(
Song et al. 2020
)
; see
Section

B.1
for more details.

2.2
Masked Discrete Diffusion Models

We now consider a target distribution on the discrete space
\mathsf{X}^{S}
of sequences of
S
tokens, where
\mathsf{X}=\{0,\ldots,K-1\}
and the mask state is
\mathbf{m}=K-1
, so
\mathsf{X}^{S}
has size
K^{S}
.
Let
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{K})
be the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{K}
and set
{m}=e_{K}
.
We identify each token
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{X}
with its one-hot vector
x\in\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{K}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{K}
.
We also identify categorical distributions on
\mathsf{X}
with points in the simplex
\bm{\delta}_{K}=\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{K}:\ p_{i}\geq 0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{i}=1\}
.
We follow
Sahoo et al. 2024
for the discrete-time masked process and do not pursue continuous-time variants
(
Campbell et al. 2022
;
Lou et al. 2024
;
Pham et al. 2025
)
.

Forward process.

For a single token, the forward Markov chain
\{X_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
on
\mathsf{X}
starts from
X_{0}\sim q_{0}
and evolves by

q_{t\mid t-1}(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1})=\begin{cases}{m}&\text{if }x_{t-1}={m}\\
\gamma_{t}x_{t-1}+(1-\gamma_{t}){m}&\text{else}\end{cases}\;,

(9)

with
\gamma_{t}\in[0,1]
and
\bar{\gamma}_{t}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\gamma_{s}
.
This yields the closed-form marginal

q_{t\mid 0}(x_{t}\mid x_{0})=\bar{\gamma}_{t}x_{0}+(1-\bar{\gamma}_{t}){m}\;,

(10)

and we require
\bar{\gamma}_{T}=0
so that the chain reaches the mask state at
t=T
. For sequences of length
S
, the forward process evolves component-wise on
\mathsf{X}^{S}
. Conditioned on
x_{0}
, the reverse bridge transitions factorize across positions,

q_{t-1\mid t,0}(x_{t-1}\mid x_{t},x_{0})=\prod_{i=1}^{S}q_{t-1\mid t,0}(x_{t-1}^{i}\mid x_{t}^{i},x_{0}^{i})\;,

(11)

with

q_{t-1\mid t,0}(x_{t-1}^{i}\mid x_{t}^{i},x_{0}^{i})=\begin{cases}x_{t}^{i}&\text{if }x_{t}^{i}\neq{m}\\
\displaystyle\frac{(1-\bar{\gamma}_{t-1}){m}+(\bar{\gamma}_{t-1}-\bar{\gamma}_{t})x_{0}^{i}}{1-\bar{\gamma}_{t}}&\text{if }x_{t}^{i}={m}\end{cases}\;.

(12)

Generative process.

We define the variational generative family
\{X_{t}^{\theta}\}_{t=0}^{T}
by

p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}(x_{0:T})=\Big[\prod_{i=1}^{S}\updelta_{{m}}(x_{T}^{i})\Big]\ \prod_{t=1}^{T}p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{t-1\mid t}(x_{t-1}\mid x_{t})\;,\qquad p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{t-1\mid t}(x_{t-1}\mid x_{t})=\prod_{i=1}^{S}p^{\mathsf{X},\theta,i}_{t-1\mid t}(x_{t-1}^{i}\mid x_{t})\;,

(13)

so each reverse transition factorizes across sequence positions while allowing the network to condition on the full
x_{t}
. Let
x_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\in\bm{\delta}_{K}^{S}
denote the per-position categorical predictions, with the mask coordinate set to zero for each position. We parameterize
p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{t-1|t}
as follows:

p^{\mathsf{X},\theta,i}_{t-1\mid t}(x_{t-1}^{i}\mid x_{t})=q_{t-1\mid t,0}\big(x_{t-1}^{i}\mid x_{t}^{i},\ x_{\theta}^{i}(x_{t},t)\big)=\begin{cases}x_{t}^{i}&\text{if }x_{t}^{i}\neq{m}\\
\displaystyle\frac{(1-\bar{\gamma}_{t-1}){m}+(\bar{\gamma}_{t-1}-\bar{\gamma}_{t})\,x_{\theta}^{i}(x_{t},t)}{1-\bar{\gamma}_{t}}&\text{if }x_{t}^{i}={m}\end{cases}\;.

(14)

Objective function.

The objective is the negative ELBO:

\displaystyle\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}_{T}+\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}_{0}+\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=2}^{T}{\lambda}^{\mathsf{X},\text{ELBO}}_{t}\ \log\langle x_{\theta}(X_{t},t),X_{0}\rangle\Big]\ \geq\ \mathbb{E}\big[-\log p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{0}(X_{0})\big]\;,

(15)

where the expectation is over
X_{0}\sim q_{0}
and
X_{t}\sim q_{t\mid 0}(\cdot\mid X_{0})
, and

{\lambda}^{\mathsf{X},\text{ELBO}}_{t}=\frac{\bar{\gamma}_{t}-\bar{\gamma}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\gamma}_{t}}\;,\qquad\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}_{T}=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{KL}\big(q_{T\mid 0}(\cdot\mid X_{0})\ \|\ p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{T}\big)\big]\;,\qquad\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}_{0}=-\mathbb{E}\big[\log\langle x_{\theta}(X_{1},1),X_{0}\rangle\big]\;.

(16)

\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}_{T},\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}_{0}
are typically neglected. By construction,
x_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\in\bm{\delta}_{K}^{S}
assigns zero mass to the mask coordinate at each position. Because the reverse transition in (
13
) factorizes across positions, the induced predictor learns a factorized approximation
p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{0\mid t}(x_{0}\mid x_{t})\approx\prod_{i=1}^{S}q^{i}_{0\mid t}(x_{0}^{i}\mid x_{t}^{i})
in
\mathrm{KL}
divergence.

2.3
Limits and Motivation

The objective in (
15
) trains a discrete denoiser that discards joint dependencies across tokens. The limitation is stark at
t=T
, where
q^{i}_{0\mid T}=q^{i}_{0}
: the model learns independent token marginals. An analogous limitation appears in continuous diffusion when Gaussian transitions are parameterized with diagonal covariance. The key difference is that continuous diffusion performs soft denoising steps: components are refined through small, reversible adjustments, allowing errors to amortize across steps or to be mitigated by finer solvers and adaptive schedulers
(
Karras et al. 2022
)
.

In masked discrete diffusion, unmasking is a hard commitment. When multiple tokens are unmasked simultaneously, independent per-token predictions can combine into sequences that are marginally plausible yet jointly inconsistent. Empirically, samplers that reveal multiple tokens per step tend to perform best when the unmasking schedule progressively increases the number of unmasked tokens, highlighting how early-stage ambiguity limits quality (e.g., constant vs. cosine schedules in
(
Pham et al. 2025
;
Shi et al. 2024
)
). Predictor–corrector variants that re-noise between steps soften this rigidity but cannot fully remove the bias introduced by simultaneous commitments, leading to mild mode collapse and entropy–quality trade-offs
(
Gat et al. 2024b
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
.

The following proposition quantifies this observation.

Proposition 1
.

(
Liu et al. 2025a
, Proposition 1)

Let
q^{\mathsf{X}}
be the path measure of the forward masked diffusion process on
\mathsf{X}^{S}
. Let
p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}
be any factorized Markovian reverse model initialized at the all-mask state. Then

\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}(\theta)\ \geq\ \sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\Big(q^{\mathsf{X}}_{t-1\mid t}(X_{t-1}\mid X_{t})\ \Big|\ \prod_{i=1}^{S}q^{\mathsf{X},i}_{t-1\mid t}(X_{t-1}^{i}\mid X_{t})\Big)\Big]\ +\ \mathrm{H}(q^{\mathsf{X}}_{0})\;,

(17)

where
\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}
is the negative ELBO in (
15
),
\mathrm{H}
is the Shannon entropy, and
q^{\mathsf{X},i}_{t-1\mid t}
denotes the
i
-th marginal of the true reverse
q^{\mathsf{X}}_{t-1\mid t}
.

Thus, the performance of any factorized model is bounded by how much the true reverse
q^{\mathsf{X}}_{t-1\mid t}
deviates from a product form. To overcome this limitation, one can reintroduce token dependencies into the reverse process via:
(i) autoregressive or Gibbs-style updates that sample one token at a time;
(ii) non-factorized updates with explicit couplings (e.g., copulas or energy-based corrections); or
(iii) latent variables that encode joint structure while keeping per-token updates simple.
We pursue the third option, as motivated next.

Proposition 2
.

Let
(X,Y)\sim q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{0}
be coupled random variables with marginals
q^{\mathsf{X}}_{0}
and
q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{0}
. Suppose we seek to model
X
and can sample
Y\sim q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{0}
. Consider a discrete diffusion model defined by

p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}(x_{0:T})=\int p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{|y}(x_{0:T}\mid y)q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{0}(\mathrm{d}y)\;,

(18)

where for each
y
the conditional process
p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{|y}(\cdot\mid y)
is of the form (
13
) but conditioned on
y
. Then

\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}(\theta)\ \geq\ \sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\Big(q^{\mathsf{X}}_{t-1\mid t,y}(X_{t-1}\mid X_{t},Y)\ \Big|\ \prod_{i=1}^{S}q^{\mathsf{X},i}_{t-1\mid t,y}(X_{t-1}^{i}\mid X_{t},Y)\Big)\Big]\ +\ \mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{H}(q^{\mathsf{X}}_{0\mid y}(\cdot\mid Y))\big]\;,

(19)

where
q^{\mathsf{X},i}_{t-1\mid t,y}
denotes the
i
-th marginal of the true conditional reverse.

See
Section

B.2
for a proof. If the conditioning variable
Y
is such that
X_{t}\mid Y
becomes closer to a factorizable distribution, the lower bound tightens, suggesting improved generative performance. In our setting,
Y
is not observed; instead, we construct
Y\sim\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}(\cdot|X)
via a dedicated encoder
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}
and model its distribution with a separate latent diffusion process. This introduces a trade-off between better reconstruction of
X
and the difficulty of correctly modeling the auxiliary latent
Y
, which we formalize next.

3
Latent Discrete Diffusion Models: LDDMs

As motivated above, we introduce a latent variable
y\in\mathsf{Y}
to condition the discrete denoiser. We take a continuous latent space
\mathsf{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{S^{\prime}d^{\prime}}
of total dimension
d=S^{\prime}d^{\prime}
, interpreted as a concatenation
y=(y^{1},\ldots,y^{S^{\prime}})
with
y^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
. Exploring discrete latents is left to future work.

3.1
General Framework for LDDMs

Our formulation follows a variational framework with three components: (i) a latent encoder
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}
mapping data
x_{0}
to latent embeddings
y_{0}
; (ii) a forward noising process applied independently to the data and latent modalities, yielding
\{(x_{t},y_{t})\}_{t=0}^{T}
; (iii) a denoising process
\{(x_{t}^{\theta},y_{t}^{\theta})\}_{t=0}^{T}
.

We temporarily make channel superscripts explicit. For the data channel we use
q^{\mathsf{X}}
and
p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}
, and for the latent channel we use
q^{\mathsf{Y}}
and
p^{\mathsf{Y},\theta}
. Joint path measures on
Z=(X,Y)
use
q^{\mathsf{Z}}
and
p^{\mathsf{Z},\theta}
. When unambiguous, we drop superscripts and write
q
and
p^{\theta}
for clarity.

We denote by
q^{\mathsf{X}}_{0}
the data distribution on
\mathsf{X}^{S}
. A trainable encoder with density
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}(\cdot\mid x_{0})
, parameterized by
\varphi
, stochastically maps discrete tokens
x_{0}
to continuous embeddings. Let
q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{0}
be the induced marginal latent distribution and
q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{0}=q^{\mathsf{X}}_{0}\,\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}
the joint at
t=0
. Let
T
be the time horizon and
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
the current timestep. We consider a diffusion process
\{Z_{t}=(X_{t},Y_{t})\}_{t=0}^{T}
starting from
Z_{0}\sim q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{0}
with forward path measure
q^{\mathsf{Z}}
; its marginals are
q^{\mathsf{X}}
and
q^{\mathsf{Y}}
. Dependence on
\varphi
is omitted for brevity.

Any sequence in
\mathsf{X}^{S}
is said to lie in the data channel, any sequence in
\mathsf{Y}
is said to lie in the latent channel. Data and latent are considered distinct modalities, as each channel requires tailored parameterization.

Forward process.

The forward process
q^{\mathsf{Z}}
noises each modality independently:

\displaystyle q^{\mathsf{Z}}(x_{0:T},y_{0:T})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{q^{\mathsf{X}}_{0}(x_{0})}_{\text{data}}\cdot\underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}(y_{0}|x_{0})}_{\text{latent encoder}}\times\underbrace{q^{\mathsf{X}}_{|0}(x_{1:T}|x_{0})\cdot q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{|0}(y_{1:T}|y_{0})}_{\text{multi-modal noising process}}\;,

(20)

where
q^{\mathsf{X}}_{|0}
is the masked discrete forward from
Section

2.2
with schedule
(\gamma_{t})
and
q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{|0}
is the continuous forward from
Section

2.1
with schedule
(\alpha_{t},\sigma_{t})
. Because
\mathsf{Y}
is continuous, we choose a Gaussian encoder:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}(y_{0}|x_{0})=\mathrm{N}(y_{0};\mu_{\varphi}(x_{0}),\sigma_{\varphi}(x_{0})^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d})\;.

(21)

Our generative models are path distributions of the general form
p^{\theta}(z_{0:T})=p^{\theta}_{T}(z_{T})p^{\theta}(z_{0:T-1}|z_{T})
on
\mathsf{Z}=\mathsf{X}^{S}\times\mathsf{Y}
. We now give two core variants.

FUJI-LDDM: FUlly JoInt denoising.

\displaystyle p^{\theta}(z_{0:T})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{T}(x_{T})\cdot p^{\mathsf{Y},\theta}_{T}(y_{T})}_{\text{initialization}}\cdot\prod_{t=1}^{T}\underbrace{p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{t-1|t}(x_{t-1}|z_{t})\cdot p^{\mathsf{Y},\theta}_{t-1|t}(y_{t-1}|z_{t})}_{\text{joint transitions}}\;,

(22)

Each transition factorizes across components within its channel. For initialization we take
p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{T}
to be the all-mask distribution on
\mathsf{X}^{S}
, and
p^{\mathsf{Y},\theta}_{T}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\bar{\sigma}_{T}^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d})
. These models involve a joint denoising process where, at each step, both modalities interact with each other. These joint interactions ease the task of data and latent reconstruction. See
Figure

1
.

SEQ-LDDM: SEQuential denoising.

Let
T_{X}
and
T_{Y}
be horizons on the data and latent channels. SEQ-LDDM first denoises the latent to
y_{0}
and then denoises the data conditioned on
y_{0}
:

p^{\theta}(z_{0:T})=\underbrace{p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{T_{X}}(x_{T_{X}})\cdot p^{\mathsf{Y},\theta}_{T_{Y}}(y_{T_{Y}})}_{\text{initialization}}\cdot\underbrace{\prod_{t=1}^{T_{Y}}p^{\mathsf{Y},\theta}_{t-1\mid t}(y_{t-1}\mid y_{t})}_{\text{1: latent channel}}\cdot\underbrace{\prod_{t=1}^{T_{X}}p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{t-1\mid t}(x_{t-1}\mid x_{t},y_{0})}_{\text{2: data channel}}\;,

(23)

where each transition factorizes across components within its channel. Initialization mirrors FUJI. SEQ exposes a clean latent
y_{0}
throughout data denoising, which can accelerate sampling while shifting modeling burden to the latent channel. The SEQ core model can be viewed as a special case of FUJI by choosing an appropriate noise schedule, noising data first and latents last. We leave the possible exploration of schedules interpolating between both core models to further work.

Fixed variance.

We fix the encoder variance
\sigma_{\varphi}
to a constant
\sigma_{\text{lat}}^{2}
. Larger
\sigma_{\text{lat}}
smooths the latent distribution but increases ambiguity during denoising. We do not explore this trade-off further and set
\sigma_{\text{lat}}^{2}=10^{-4}
in experiments.

Related Works

We review concurrent advances in discrete diffusion that address the limitations of factorized denoisers by either inducing cross-token dependencies or coupling the discrete path to a continuous representation. A more extensive review is provided in
Appendix

A
.

Non-factorized denoisers.

One line of work augments masked diffusion with inference-time, non-factorized predictions. Discrete Copula Diffusion (DCD)
(
Liu et al. 2025a
)
pairs per-token diffusion marginals with an auxiliary autoregressive copula to impose dependency structure, improving few-step quality at the cost of an AR loop. Self-speculative decoding
(
Campbell et al. 2025
)
modifies attention to draft and verify a joint proposal in a single forward pass, reducing evaluations and relaxing strict factorization. These approaches operate purely within the discrete diffusion process and primarily target the sampler.

Continuous latents.

A complementary direction introduces continuous variables to capture joint structure along the diffusion path. VADD
(
Xie et al. 2025
)
associates a continuous latent with each reverse transition and trains a VAE-style model to obtain non-factorized posteriors; the latent prior remains simple and decoupled across steps. Immediately concurrent to our work in continuous augmentations of the discrete path, CCDD
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
is a closely related FUJI-style formulation, but instantiated with latents sized to the token sequence (
S=S^{\prime}
) and without a learned encoder; CADD
(
Zheng et al. 2025b
)
adopts a token-wise augmentation in which each discrete variable carries a continuous latent derived from an encoder, and no separate network is used to parameterize the latent denoiser. While this simplifies optimization by emphasizing data reconstruction, it entangles channels and can induce quality–entropy trade-offs at inference.

6
Conclusion

We revisited masked discrete diffusion through the lens of the factorization bottleneck and introduced
Latent Discrete Diffusion Models
(LDDMs), which couple a masked discrete process with a continuous latent channel. We instantiated two variants,
FUJI
(fully joint denoising of tokens and latents) and
SEQ
(latent-first, then data), and derived corresponding ELBO-based objectives. This design preserves parallel token updates while supplying a softer, global signal that can carry cross-token information.
Empirically, LDDMs improve unconditional generation under few-step budgets. On the
binary sawtooth
task, SEQ resolves the conditionally factorized target with one or two data steps. On LM1B, FUJI yields lower generative perplexity across sampling budgets while maintaining entropy close to the data baseline. These observations are consistent with the view that an auxiliary continuous channel can reduce the brittleness of simultaneous token unmasking.

Appendix A
Related Works

We review recent directions in discrete diffusion that reintroduce cross-token dependencies and/or couple the diffusion path with a continuous representation to improve performance.

Non-factorized denoisers via copulas and speculative decoding.

Discrete Copula Diffusion (DCD)
[
Liu et al. 2025a
]
augments a discrete diffusion model at inference time with a separate copula model instantiated as a small autoregressive (AR) language model. At each step
t
, DCD combines (a) univariate marginals from causal and non-causal denoisers with (b) a dependency structure from the AR copula via a projection step. Sampling performs an AR pass over the currently masked subset followed by the diffusion update. Reported results show improved few-step quality with fewer calls to the diffusion model, at the cost of an auxiliary AR model and an additional AR pass whose compute scales with the masked span.

Self-speculative decoding
[
Campbell et al. 2025
]
modifies the attention mask to include a causal drafting block and validates the draft against the full (non-causal) network via speculative acceptance. This yields a non-factorized proposal over the masked subset in a single forward of the full model and can reduce the number of evaluations in their setup.

LDDMs pursue complementary aims by training a continuous latent that captures cross-token dependencies and using it to condition parallel denoising.

Continuous relaxations and consistency.

Sahoo et al. 2025
establish a duality between Gaussian diffusion and uniform-state discrete diffusion via an
argmax
map on continuous latents. They propose lower-variance training by feeding soft probabilities to the denoiser and adapt consistency-style distillation to discrete models.

Latent variables for dependency modeling.

Xie et al. 2025
introduce Variational Autoencoding Discrete Diffusion (VADD). They associate one continuous latent
y_{t}
per transition, obtained with an encoder
\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\varphi}
trained as part of a classical VAE. The generative process thus only involves sampling a Gaussian random variable at each transition. This yields non-factorized posteriors and improves few-step quality without external AR models. In contrast, our LDDMs learn a persistent continuous latent trajectory
Y_{0:T}
coupled to the discrete chain, enabling flexible denoising sequencing (FUJI joint denoising or SEQ latent-first denoising). Importantly, in LDDMs, the latent distribution is modeled by its own diffusion model, which enables encoding more complex information and relationships.

Joint continuous–discrete co-evolution.

Contemporaneous work
[
Zhou et al. 2025
]
proposes CCDD, which co-evolves continuous embeddings and discrete tokens within one network. In their experiments, continuous embeddings are derived from a large pretrained encoder (e.g., Qwen3-Embedding
[
Team 2025
]
), and cross-modal classifier-free guidance is explored. LDDMs share the co-evolution view but (i) allow a learned encoder to tailor latents to the objective (while we also evaluate a frozen encoder at scale), and (ii) derive an ELBO that balances data and latent terms. Their instantiation sizes latents to the token sequence
(S=S^{\prime})
in reported setups.

Token-wise continuous augmentation.

Contemporaneous work, Continuously Augmented Discrete Diffusion (CADD)
[
Zheng et al. 2025b
]
, attaches a continuous diffusion to each token position so that masked tokens carry soft hints instead of a hard
\mathbf{m}
token. Moreover, they do not use a separate network for the latent denoiser, which they obtain directly from the data denoiser
x_{\theta}
and the encoder
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}
, simplifying training (data reconstruction loss only) but inducing quality/entropy tradeoffs at inference. Our LDDMs target a complementary regime: instead of per-token embeddings, we use compact sentence-level latents to encode global joint structure, which is beneficial when many tokens must be unveiled simultaneously.

Appendix B
Additional remarks on continuous and discrete diffusion

B.1
Derivations for continuous diffusion

Forward process.

The forward process is designed to satisfy the marginals

y_{t}=\bar{\alpha}_{t}\,y_{0}+\bar{\sigma}_{t}\,\varepsilon\;,\qquad\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{d})\;,

(31)

with
\bar{\alpha}_{0}=1
and
\bar{\sigma}_{0}=0
. It suffices to specify
(\bar{\alpha}_{t},\bar{\sigma}_{t})
for
t\in\{0,\ldots,T\}
(or, equivalently, for a rescaled grid
t\in\{0,1/T,\ldots,1\}
). For two consecutive times
s=t-1/T<t
we set

\alpha_{t\mid s}=\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}{\bar{\alpha}_{s}}\;,\qquad\sigma_{t\mid s}^{2}=\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}-\alpha_{t\mid s}^{2}\,\bar{\sigma}_{s}^{2}\;,

(32)

which yields the Gaussian forward kernel

q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{t\mid s}(y_{t}\mid y_{s})=\mathcal{N}\!\big(\alpha_{t\mid s}\,y_{s},\ \sigma_{t\mid s}^{2}\,\mathrm{I}_{d}\big)\;.

(33)

Posterior distributions.

The bridge posterior
q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{s\mid t,0}(y_{s}\mid y_{t},y_{0})
is Gaussian with

\displaystyle q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{s\mid t,0}(y_{s}\mid y_{t},y_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\!\big(\,\tilde{\mu}_{s\mid t}(y_{t},y_{0}),\ \tilde{\sigma}_{s\mid t}^{2}\,\mathrm{I}_{d}\,\big)\;,

(34)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{s\mid t}(y_{t},y_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{t\mid s}\,\bar{\sigma}_{s}^{2}}{\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\,y_{t}+\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{s}\,\sigma_{t\mid s}^{2}}{\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\,y_{0}\;,

(35)

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{s\mid t}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{t\mid s}^{2}\,\bar{\sigma}_{s}^{2}}{\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\;.

(36)

Writing the noise at time
t
as
\varepsilon(y_{t},y_{0})=(y_{t}-\bar{\alpha}_{t}y_{0})/\bar{\sigma}_{t}
, the posterior mean can be equivalently expressed as

\tilde{\mu}_{s\mid t}(y_{t},y_{0})=\frac{1}{\alpha_{t\mid s}}\Big(y_{t}-\frac{\sigma_{t\mid s}^{2}}{\bar{\sigma}_{t}}\,\varepsilon(y_{t},y_{0})\Big)\;.

(37)

ELBO loss.

Let
p^{\mathsf{Y},\theta}_{T}(y_{T})=\mathcal{N}(0,\bar{\sigma}_{T}^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d})
and
p_{\theta}(y_{s}\mid y_{t})
be Gaussian with the same covariance as the bridge and mean obtained by replacing
y_{0}
with a predictor (e.g.,
y_{\theta}(y_{t},t)
or
\varepsilon_{\theta}(y_{t},t)
) in
\tilde{\mu}_{s\mid t}
. The negative ELBO decomposes as

\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{Y}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big(q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{T\mid 0}(\cdot\mid Y_{0})\,\|\,p^{\mathsf{Y},\theta}_{T}\big)}_{L_{T}}+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big(q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{t-1\mid t,0}(\cdot\mid Y_{t},Y_{0})\,\|\,p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid Y_{t})\big)}_{L_{t-1}}\;-\;\underbrace{\log p_{\theta}(Y_{0}\mid Y_{1})}_{L_{0}}\Big]\;,

(38)

where the expectation is over
Y_{0}\sim q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{0}
and
Y_{t}\sim q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{t\mid 0}(\cdot\mid Y_{0})
. With the usual parameterizations,
L_{t-1}
reduces to a weighted MSE, and the extremal terms
L_{T}
and
L_{0}
are typically small and often omitted in practice.

Loss weighting.

Different prediction targets lead to different weights:

\displaystyle\text{($\varepsilon$-prediction)}\qquad\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{Y}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{\sigma_{t\mid s}^{2}}{2\,\bar{\sigma}_{s}^{2}\,\alpha_{t\mid s}^{2}}\ \|\varepsilon_{\theta}(Y_{t},t)-\varepsilon(Y_{t},Y_{0})\|^{2}\Big]\;,

(39)

\displaystyle\text{($y_{0}$-prediction)}\qquad\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{Y}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{s}^{2}\,\sigma_{t\mid s}^{2}}{2\,\bar{\sigma}_{s}^{2}\,\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\ \|y_{\theta}(Y_{t},t)-Y_{0}\|^{2}\Big]\;,

(40)

so that in the
y_{0}
-prediction case the time weight is

{\lambda}^{\mathsf{Y},\text{ELBO}}_{t}=\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{s}^{2}\,\sigma_{t\mid s}^{2}}{2\,\bar{\sigma}_{s}^{2}\,\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\;.

(41)

These weights can be replaced by alternative choices (e.g., schedule- or SNR-based) without changing the model class.

Noise schedules.

The relation between
(\bar{\alpha}_{t},\bar{\sigma}_{t})
depends on the diffusion type. In the variance-preserving (VP) case,
\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}=1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}^{2}
. In the variance-exploding (VE) case,
\bar{\alpha}_{t}=1
and
\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}=\sigma^{2}(t)
with
\sigma^{2}(T)\gg 1
. We focus on VP and consider
\tau=t/T\in[0,1]
. Two common VP choices are listed in

.

Deterministic DDIM sampling.

A deterministic variant of the diffusion process, known as DDIM
[
Song et al. 2020
]
, can be obtained by defining the forward process directly through the posterior
q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{s|t,0}
and (
3
), but without stochasticity. We set:

q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{s|t}(y_{s}\mid y_{t},y_{0})=\mathrm{N}\left(y_{s};\tilde{\mu}_{s|t}^{\eta}(y_{t},y_{0}),\ \eta^{2}\tilde{\Sigma}^{2}_{s|t}\right)\;.

(42)

where
\eta\in[0,1]
controls the level of stochasticity in the sampling process,
\tilde{\Sigma}^{2}_{s|t}=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{t|s}\,\bar{\sigma}_{s}^{2}}{\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}
,

\tilde{\mu}^{\eta}_{s|t}(y_{t},y_{0})=\bar{\alpha}_{s}y_{0}+\sqrt{\bar{\sigma}_{s}^{2}-\eta^{2}\tilde{\Sigma}^{2}{s|t}}\ \varepsilon(y_{t},y_{0})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\varepsilon(y_{t},y_{0})=\frac{y_{t}-\bar{\alpha}_{t}y_{0}}{\bar{\sigma}_{t}}\;,

(43)

Setting
\eta=1
recovers the original Markovian diffusion process, while
\eta=0
yields a purely deterministic trajectory (DDIM) that preserves the same marginal
q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{t}
at every timestep. The loss function is the same up to a different multiplicative factor
\lambda^{\mathsf{Y}}
and in practice sampling the generative process consists in applying
q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{s|t}
with
\eta=0
and a trained denoiser
\epsilon_{\theta}
(or
y_{\theta}
), providing faster and smoother generation paths with minimal loss of sample quality. We leave the exploration of the DDIM sampler for LDDMs latent channel to further work.

\tilde{Z}_{t}=(X_{t},Y_{0})
where
X_{0},Y_{0}\sim q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{0}
and
X_{t}
is driven by a forward noising process as given in (
9
). Denote by
q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}
the path measure of
\{\tilde{Z}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
. We first use the data processing inequality on
\mathrm{KL}(q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{0}\mid p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{0})
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{0}\mid p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{KL}(q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{0|y}(\cdot|Y)\mid p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{0|y}(\cdot|Y))\right]

(44)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\log q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{0|y}(X_{0}|Y)-\log p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{0|y}(X_{0}|Y))\right]\;.

(45)

Finally, we bound
-\mathbb{E}\log p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{0|y}(X_{0}|Y)
with the classical ELBO bound involving the diffusion path
q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[-\log p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{0|y}(X_{0}|Y))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[-\log\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{|y}(X_{1:T}|Y)}{q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{1:T|0,y}(X_{1:T}|X_{0},Y)}\ \bigg|\ X_{0},Y\right]\right]

(46)

\displaystyle\leq-\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(\frac{p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{|y}(X_{1:T}|Y)}{q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{1:T|0,y}(X_{1:T}|X_{0},Y)}\right)\right]

(47)

\displaystyle\leq-\mathbb{E}\bigg[\log p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{T|y}(X_{T}|Y)+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\frac{p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{t-1|t,y}(X_{t-1}|X_{t},Y)}{q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{t|t-1,y}(X_{t}|X_{t-1},Y)}\bigg]\;,

(48)

But:

q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{t|t-1,y}(x_{t}|x_{t-1},y)=\frac{q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{t-1|t,y}(x_{t-1}|x_{t},y)q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{t|y}(x_{t}|y)}{q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{t-1,y}(x_{t-1}|y)}\;,

(49)

so:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[-\log p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{0|y}(X_{0}|Y))\right]\leq

(50)

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\bigg[\log\frac{p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{T|y}(X_{T}|Y)}{q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{T|y}(X_{T}|Y)}-\log q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{0|y}(X_{0}|Y)+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\frac{p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{t-1|t,y}(X_{t-1}|X_{t},Y)}{\log q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{t-1|t,y}(X_{t-1}|X_{t},Y)}\bigg]

(51)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{KL}\left(q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{T|y}(\cdot|Y)\mid p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{T|y}(\cdot|Y)\right)+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{H}(q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{0|y}(\cdot|Y))\right]+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{KL}(q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{t-1|t,y}(\cdot|X_{t},Y)\mid p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{t-1|t,y}(\cdot|X_{t},Y)\right]\;,

(52)

where
\mathrm{H}
maps a distribution to its entropy. Here,
\mathrm{KL}\left(q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{T|y}(\cdot|Y)\mid p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{T|y}(\cdot|Y)\right)=0
because both distributions are the Dirac mass at the mask state. The latter term is the ELBO term
\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}
:

\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{H}(q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{0|y}(\cdot|Y))\right]+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{KL}(q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{t-1|t,y}(\cdot|X_{t},Y)\mid p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{t-1|t,y}(\cdot|X_{t},Y)\right]\;.

(53)

We can also rewrite
\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}
by following the derivations leading to the closed-form discrete diffusion loss in (
15
). To do so, let us work with

q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{t-1|t,0,y}(x_{t-1}|x_{t},x_{0},y)=\frac{q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{t|t-1,y}(x_{t}|x_{t-1},y)q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{t-1|0,y}(x_{t-1}|x_{0},y)}{q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{t|0,y}(x_{t}|x_{0},y)}\;,

(54)

thus obtaining:

\displaystyle\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{KL}\left(q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{T|y}(\cdot|Y)\mid p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{T|y}(\cdot|Y)\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\log p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{0|1}(X_{0}|Y)\right]

(55)

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{KL}(q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{t-1|t,0,y}(\cdot|X_{t},X_{0},Y)\mid p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{t-1|t}(\cdot|X_{t}))\right]\;.

(56)

Since
q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{t-1|t,0,y}
factorizes, the minimum of
\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}(\theta)
is attained when
-\mathbb{E}\log p^{\mathsf{X},\theta,i}_{t-1|t,y}(X_{t-1}^{i}|X_{t},Y)
is minimized, i.e., when we recover the true distribution
q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}},i}_{t-1|t,y}
for all
i\in\{1,\cdots,S\}
.
Plugging into (
53
), the ELBO loss
\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}(\theta)
is thus lower bounded by:

\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}(\theta)\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{H}(q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{0|y}(\cdot|Y))\right]+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{KL}(q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}}}_{t-1|t,y}(\cdot|X_{t},Y)\ \Big|\ \prod_{i=1}^{S}q^{\tilde{\mathsf{Y}},i}_{t-1|t,y}(\cdot|X_{t},Y)\right]\;.

(57)

This ends the proof.
∎

Appendix C
LDDMs general framework

C.1
LDDMs ELBO Derivation

Recall the forward process of our LDDMs (
20
):

\displaystyle q^{\mathsf{Z}}(x_{0:T},y_{0:T})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{q^{\mathsf{X}}_{0}(x_{0})}_{\text{data}}\cdot\underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}(y_{0}|x_{0})}_{\text{latent encoder}}\times\underbrace{q^{\mathsf{X}}_{|0}(x_{1:T}|x_{0})\cdot q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{|0}(y_{1:T}|y_{0})}_{\text{multi-modal noising process}}\;,

(58)

In our study, the latent encoder is parameterized as a Gaussian distribution with constant variance:

\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}(y_{0}|x_{0})=\mathrm{N}(y_{0};\mu_{\varphi}(x_{0}),\sigma_{\text{lat}}^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d})\;.

(59)

FUJI-LDDMs

Recall that the FUJI-LDDMs admit the following form:

\displaystyle p^{\theta}(z_{0:T})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{T}(x_{T})\cdot p^{\mathsf{Y},\theta}_{T}(y_{T})}_{\text{initialization}}\times\prod_{t=1}^{T}\underbrace{p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{{t-1}|t}(x_{t-1}|z_{t})\cdot p^{\mathsf{Y},\theta}_{{t-1}|t}(y_{t-1}|z_{t})}_{\text{joint transitions}}\;,

(60)

where the transitions
p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{{t-1}|t}(x_{t-1}|z_{t})
and
p^{\mathsf{Y},\theta}_{{t-1}|t}(y_{t-1}|z_{t})
are the discrete and continuous transitions given in
section

2
, but conditioned on the couple
z_{t}=(x_{t},y_{t})
at each timestep
t
. We compute the negative ELBO as follows:

\displaystyle-\log p^{\theta}_{0}(x_{0})

\displaystyle=-\log\left(\int_{\mathsf{X}^{T}\times\mathsf{Y}^{T+1}}p^{\theta}(z_{0:T})\mathrm{d}y_{0}\mathrm{d}z_{1:T}\right)

(61)

\displaystyle=-\log\left(\int_{\mathsf{X}^{T}\times\mathsf{Y}^{T+1}}\frac{p^{\theta}(z_{0:T})}{q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{|0}(z_{1:T}|z_{0})\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}(y_{0}|x_{0})}q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{|0}(\mathrm{d}z_{1:T}|z_{0})\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}(\mathrm{d}y_{0}|x_{0})\right)

(62)

\displaystyle\leq-\int_{\mathsf{X}^{T}\times\mathsf{Y}^{T+1}}\log\left(\frac{p^{\theta}(z_{0:T})}{q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{|0}(z_{1:T}|z_{0})\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}(y_{0}|x_{0})}\right)q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{|0}(\mathrm{d}z_{1:T}|z_{0})\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}(\mathrm{d}y_{0}|x_{0})\;.

(63)

The forward process
q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{|0}
can be rewritten as:

q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{|0}(z_{1:T}|z_{0})=q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{T|0}(z_{T}|z_{0})q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{0|1}(z_{0}|z_{1})\prod_{i=2}^{T}q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{t-1|t,0}(z_{t-1}|z_{t},z_{0})\;,

(64)

where
q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{T|0},q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{0|1},q^{\mathsf{Z}}_{t-1|t,0}
factorize across modality. Then, regrouping each term:

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\left[\log p^{\theta}_{0}(x_{0})\right]

(65)

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\log\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}(Y_{0}|X_{0})\right]}_{L^{\text{NE}}_{\text{encoder}}}+\underbrace{-\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{0|1}(X_{0}|Z_{1})\right)\right]}_{\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}_{0}}\ \underbrace{-\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(p^{\mathsf{Y},\theta}_{0|1}(Y_{0}|Z_{1})\right)\right]}_{\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{Y}}_{0}}

(66)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{KL}(q^{\mathsf{X}}_{T|0}(\cdot|X_{0})\mid p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{T})\right]}_{\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}_{T}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{KL}(q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{T|0}(\cdot|Y_{0})\mid p^{\mathsf{Y},\theta}_{T})\right]}_{\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{Y}}_{T}}

(67)

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathrm{KL}\left(q^{\mathsf{X}}_{{t-1}|t,0}(\cdot|X_{t},X_{0})\mid p^{\mathsf{X},\theta}_{t-1|t}(\cdot|Z_{t})\right)\bigg]}_{\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}_{t-1}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathrm{KL}\left(q^{\mathsf{Y}}_{{t-1}|t,0}(\cdot|Y_{t},Y_{0})\mid p^{\mathsf{Y},\theta}_{{t-1}|t}(\cdot|Z_{t})\right)\bigg]}_{\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{Y}}_{t-1}}\;.

(68)

We recover conventional terms for each modality, except for the negative entropy term
L^{\text{NE}}_{\text{encoder}}
. First, we neglect extremal terms
\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}_{T},\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{Y}}_{T}
which are zero or near zero and depend only of the noise schedules. Second, as we outlined in
Section

2.2
, we neglect
\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}_{0}
.

Finally, we regroup
\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{Y}}_{0}
and
\mathrm{L}_{\text{encoder}}^{\text{NE}}
into a single term
L_{0}^{z}
. This results in computing a
\mathrm{KL}
divergence between two dependent Gaussian distributions, akin to classical diffusion terms. Let
(y_{\theta},\sigma_{\theta}^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d})
be the mean and covariance of the Gaussian distribution
p^{\mathsf{Y},\theta}_{0|1}
. Because we do not know how to characterize the distribution of
Y_{0}
given
X_{0},Y_{1}
straightforwardly, we resort to a full
\mathrm{KL}
computation:

\displaystyle\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{Z}}_{0}=\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{Y}}_{0}+\mathrm{L}_{\text{encoder}}^{\text{NE}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}(\cdot|x_{0})}{p^{\mathsf{Y},\theta}_{0|1}(\cdot|z_{1})}\right)\right]

(69)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[-\frac{\|Y_{0}-\mu_{\varphi}(X_{0})\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{\varphi}(X_{0})^{2}}+\frac{\|Y_{0}-y_{\theta}(Z_{1})\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{\theta}(Z_{1})^{2}}+d\log\left(\frac{\sigma_{\theta}(Z_{1})}{\sigma_{\varphi}(X_{0})}\right)\right]

(70)

\displaystyle=-\frac{d}{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|Y_{0}-y_{\theta}(Z_{1})\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{\theta}(Z_{1})^{2}}+d\log\left(\frac{\sigma_{\theta}(Z_{1})}{\sigma_{\varphi}(X_{0})}\right)\right]\;,

(71)

where
d
is the dimension of the continuous space
\mathsf{Y}
.

In the case of the
fixed variance
design choice
\sigma_{\varphi}^{2}=\sigma_{\theta}^{2}=\sigma_{\text{lat}}^{2}
, as we make in this work, this leads to:

\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{Z}}_{0}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|Y_{0}-y_{\theta}(Z_{1})\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{\text{lat}}^{2}}\right]-\frac{d}{2}\;.

(72)

This mirrors the existing denoising loss terms
\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{Y}}_{t-1}
. We have found this term negligible empirically, and leave additional exploration, e.g., when
\sigma_{\varphi}^{2},\sigma_{\theta}^{2}
are trainable, to further work. Using the formulas laid out in (
6
) and (
15
), we recover the final loss objective

\mathrm{L}^{\text{ELBO}}_{\text{FUJI}}(\theta,\varphi)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left(\lambda^{\mathsf{X}}_{t}\log\langle x_{\theta}(Z_{t},t),X_{0}\rangle+\lambda^{\mathsf{Y}}_{t}\|Y_{0}-y_{0}^{\theta}(Z_{t},t)\|^{2}\right)\right]\;.

(73)

SEQ-LDDMs.

Recall that SEQ-LDDMs admit the following form:
The same derivations done in (
65
) are straightforwardly adapted to SEQ-LDDMs and result in the following loss objective:

\mathrm{L}^{\text{ELBO}}_{\text{SEQ}}(\theta,\varphi)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left(\lambda^{\mathsf{X}}_{t}\log\langle x_{\theta}(\tilde{Z}_{t},t),X_{0}\rangle+\lambda^{\mathsf{Y}}_{t}\|Y_{0}-y_{0}^{\theta}(Y_{t},t)\|^{2}\right)\right]\;,

(74)

where
\tilde{Z}_{t}=(X_{t},Y_{0})
.

Appendix D
Neural network architectures

DiT (single–modality).

We use the Diffusion Transformer (DiT) blocks of
Peebles and Xie 2023
with bidirectional self-attention and timestep embeddings added to token features. This backbone is used (i) as the MDLM denoiser and (ii) as the latent denoiser in SEQ-LDDM.

MM-DiT (multi–modal, joint).

To evolve data and latent channels jointly, we adopt the multi-modal DiT of
Esser et al. 2024
. The input sequence is the concatenation of
S
data tokens and
S^{\prime}
latent tokens. A single self-attention layer attends across the full
(S+S^{\prime})
sequence, while feed-forward blocks (MLPs), input embeddings, layer norms, and timestep conditioning are
modality-specific
. Outputs are given by two heads, yielding both
x_{\theta}
(token distribution) and
y_{\theta}
(latent
y_{0}
prediction). We use MM-DiT both for the encoder
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}
and for the joint denoiser in FUJI-LDDM; in SEQ-LDDM, the data denoiser is MM-DiT conditioned on
Y_{0}
, while the latent denoiser is the single–modality DiT above.

Encoder
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}
.

When trained from scratch,
\mu_{\varphi}
the mean of the encoder is given by an MM-DiT that receives the
S
input tokens together with
S^{\prime}
learnable latent embeddings of the hidden dimension size, and returns
S^{\prime}
latent vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
as the last
S^{\prime}
tokens embeddings; the latent
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{S^{\prime}d^{\prime}}
is the concatenation of these vectors.

Off-the-shelf encoder.

For some LM1B runs, we replace
\mu_{\varphi}
with the frozen pretrained Qwen3-Embedding 0.6B model
[
Team 2025
]
. For our latents, we select the last layer embedding, yielding
S^{\prime}=S
and we clip and normalize each embedding from
1024
dimensions to the recommended minimum of
d^{\prime}=32
, yielding a total latent space of dimension
d=4096
. This choice eases our computational burden, but is not necessarily optimal for our objective; these sentence models produce semantic vector representation that enable information retrieval with cosine similarity, whereas we are trying to encode joint structure information in our latent vectors for a data denoising task.

E.3
Metrics

We adopt metrics used classically to measure the performance of generative model for categorical data
[
Sahoo et al. 2024
,
Zheng et al. 2025a
]
.

Validation perplexity (ELBO-based).

Given a validation set
\mathcal{D}=\{x_{0}^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^{M}
of length-
S
sequences, and a model
p_{\theta}(x_{0})
, we report

\mathrm{PPL}_{x}(\mathcal{D})=\exp\left(\frac{1}{MS}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\widehat{\mathrm{NLL}}_{\mathrm{ELBO},x}\left(x_{0}^{(m)}\right)\right)\;,

(76)

where
\widehat{\mathrm{NLL}}_{\mathrm{ELBO},x}(x_{0})
is the data-channel negative ELBO in LDDMs
\mathrm{L}^{\mathsf{X}}
.

Generative perplexity.

For model samples
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}=\{x_{1:S}^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^{M}
and a fixed autoregressive teacher
p_{\text{gen\_ppl}}
, we compute

\mathrm{GenPPL}_{T}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}})=\exp\left(\frac{1}{MS}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{s=1}^{S}-\log p_{\text{gen\_ppl}}\left(x_{s}^{(m)}\mid x_{<s}^{(m)}\right)\right)\;,

(77)

Special tokens are omitted when scoring. In our experiments we use GPT-2 Large as
p_{\text{gen\_ppl}}
.

Entropy.

For the same sample set
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
, we estimate the per-position marginal token distributions

\widehat{p}_{i}(k)\;=\;\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}\!\{x^{(m)}_{i}=k\}\,,\qquad k\in\mathsf{X},\;i\in\{1,\dots,S\}.

(78)

We report the mean positional entropy as:

\mathrm{Ent}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}})\;=\;\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S}H\!\big(\widehat{p}_{i}\big)\;=\;\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S}\bigg(-\sum_{k\in\mathsf{X}}\widehat{p}_{i}(k)\,\log\widehat{p}_{i}(k)\bigg)\,,

(79)

with the convention
0\log 0=0

Sliced Wasserstein distance (SWD).

For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
p\geq 1
, define

\mathbf{W}_{p}^{p}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{M}(\mu,\nu)}\int\left\|x-y\right\|^{p}\gamma\!\left(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y\right)\;,

(80)

and the sliced Wasserstein distance

\mathrm{SWD}_{p}(\mu,\nu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{W}_{p}\left(\langle u,\cdot\rangle_{\#}\mu,\ \langle u,\cdot\rangle_{\#}\nu\right)\mathrm{N}(\mathrm{d}u;0,\mathrm{I}_{d})\;,

(81)

where
\langle u,\cdot\rangle_{\#}\mu
is the pushforward of
\mu
by
x\mapsto\langle u,x\rangle
. In one dimension, for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
,

\mathbf{W}_{p}^{p}(\alpha,\beta)=\int_{0}^{1}\left|F_{\alpha}^{-1}(t)-F_{\beta}^{-1}(t)\right|^{p}\mathrm{d}t\;,

(82)

where
F^{-1}_{\alpha},F^{-1}_{\beta}
are the inverse CDFs of
\alpha,\beta
. Hence for empirical measures
\hat{\alpha}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}
and
\hat{\beta}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{y_{i}}
,

\mathbf{W}_{p}^{p}(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|x_{(i)}-y_{(i)}\right|^{p}\;,

(83)

with
x_{(i)},y_{(i)}
the sorted samples. We report
\mathrm{SWD}_{1}
estimated by Monte Carlo over directions
u\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{d})
averaging the corresponding one-dimensional
\mathbf{W}_{1}
values.
