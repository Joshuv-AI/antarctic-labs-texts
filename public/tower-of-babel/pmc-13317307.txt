Hyperactive basolateral amygdala mediates comorbid anxiety in chronic rhinosinusitis
Authors: Xiaojun Zhang, Wensi Wu, Haomiao Zhao, Yimeng Chang, Xinyi Ma, Xueyun Shi, Wenshuang Li, Binxiang Tang, Pin Wang, Chen Duan, Min Jin, Xin Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationaleAnxiety disorder is a common but often overlooked comorbidity in patients with chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS). However, it remains unclear which specific brain regions are involved in CRS-related anxiety-like behaviors, as well as how neural-immune interactions contribute to the progression of comorbid anxiety in CRS.MethodsAnxiety symptoms in CRS patients were evaluated using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). A papain-induced CRS mouse model was established to examine anxiety-like behaviors and associated neural and immunological alterations. Behavioral assays were combined with immunohistochemistry, in vivo fiber photometry, electrophysiology, viral tracing, and chemogenetic manipulation to identify brain regions and circuits. Peripheral inflammation, sympathetic activity, blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity and the effects of IL-33 neutralization on peripheral inflammation, anxiety-like behaviors, and neuronal excitability were also examined.ResultsPersistent anxiety was observed in CRS patients and mice, even after improvement of sinonasal inflammation. In CRS mice, anxiety-like behaviors were linked to selective hyperactivation of BLA CaMKIIα-positive neurons, with increased intrinsic excitability and excitatory synaptic input shifting the excitation-inhibition balance toward excitation. Chemogenetic inhibition of BLA neurons alleviated anxiety-like behaviors and reduced sympathetic nerve fiber density, norepinephrine release, and nasal eosinophilic inflammation, whereas activation produced the opposite effects. Functional analyses implicate a BLA-centered network involving the vHPC and premammillary nucleus (PMN) in regulating peripheral sympathetic and immune responses. Additionally, CRS mice exhibited impaired BBB integrity, and IL-33 neutralization mitigated peripheral inflammation, improved anxiety-like behaviors, and decreased BLA hyperexcitability.ConclusionsDysregulated excitatory activity in the BLA underlies anxiety comorbidity in CRS. Our findings support the involvement of a BLA-centered functional network encompassing the vHPC and PMN in the regulation of sympathetic activity and peripheral inflammation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03850-y.Keywords: Chronic rhinosinusitis, Anxiety comorbidity, Basolateral amygdala, Eosinophilic infiltration, Sympathetic nerve fibers

Introduction

Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) is a prevalent condition characterized by persistent mucosal inflammation of the paranasal sinuses, posing a risk for developing mental disorders [1]. Specifically, CRS patients are at a markedly increased risk of anxiety [2], with reported prevalence ranging from 25% to 38.95% [3, 4]. Moreover, the likelihood of CRS patients developing anxiety exceeds that of developing other mental disorder [5], suggesting that anxiety may be a more significant concern in this population. Given the substantial comorbidity of anxiety in CRS, elucidating the underlying mechanisms is crucial.

When anxiety is comorbid with other conditions, it exacerbates the severity of symptoms and complicates treatment, contributing to a more severe clinical presentation and an increased therapeutic burden [6–9]. A recent study in CRS patients has shown that comorbid anxiety may distort symptom perception, weakening the correlation between imaging and clinical severity [10]. Besides, anxiety in CRS patients remained unchanged after improvements in nasal symptoms post-surgery [11], suggesting that factors beyond nasal symptoms may contribute to the comorbid anxiety observed in CRS patients. However, the underlying neurobiological mechanisms remain poorly understood.

The nasal cavity’s anatomical proximity to the brain makes the latter potentially susceptible to inflammation from conditions such as CRS, which may contribute to neurological complications [12]. Although the blood-brain barrier (BBB) protects the brain from direct bacterial infection, compromised epithelial integrity allows inflammation to spread via the olfactory bulb and other nervous system pathways not fully protected by the BBB, as suggested by prior studies [13, 14]. This raises the possibility of an indirect pathway through which nasal inflammation could impact brain function. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging studies have shown altered brain activity and functional connectivity in CRS patients, which may offer insights into the pathophysiology of mood disturbances associated with the condition [15, 16]. Given the bidirectional relationship between CRS and anxiety, recent studies have explored the interaction between the brain’s emotional regions and peripheral immune responses, highlighting a potential neural-immune axis [6, 17, 18]. However, few neurobiological investigations have clarified how alterations in specific brain region activity and neural circuits integrate with peripheral immune signals, leaving the neural mechanisms underlying CRS comorbidity with anxiety largely unexplored.

In the present study, we investigated the neuroimmune mechanisms underlying the comorbidity of CRS and anxiety, identifying hyperactivity of the BLA as a key contributor to anxiety-like behaviors in CRS mice. This is supported by unbiased multi-brain-region c-Fos screening and electrophysiological validation, consistent with the BLA’s role as a predominantly excitatory integrative hub in anxiety associated chronic stress and inflammation [19–23]. Guided by these findings, we focused our mechanistic investigations on excitatory circuits within the BLA, revealing that CRS anxiety was associated with hyperactivity of BLA excitatory circuits, which were functionally associated with the vHPC and PMN and modulated peripheral sympathetic nerve signaling as well as eosinophilic inflammation. Collectively, these results advance our understanding of the neural-immune interactions underlying CRS-associated anxiety and provide a basis for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies for patients with CRS and comorbid anxiety.

Methods

Patients and patients-reported outcomes

This study ultimately recruited a total of 201 patients with CRS who received endoscopic sinus surgery at the Department of Otorhinolaryngology, Qilu Hospital of Shandong University after the screening process. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Qilu Hospital, Shandong University (approval number: KYLL-202206-006). All participants in the study provided written informed consent. The inclusion criteria for CRS patients were defined based on the EPOS 2020 guidelines [24]. Participants with immunodeficiency disorders, fungal sinusitis, cystic fibrosis, or nasal tumors were excluded. Furthermore, none of the participants received systemic corticosteroid treatment for at least four weeks before surgery. Demographic and clinical data for each patient were recorded and summarized in Supplementary Table 1. To assess anxiety symptoms, the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was administered. A score of 8 or higher on the HADS-A is considered indicative of anxiety [25]. The 22-item Sino-Nasal Outcome Test (SNOT-22) was utilized to evaluate health-related quality of life [26]. Both HADS and SNOT-22 were evaluated pre-operatively and at 3 and 6 months post-operatively. The flow diagram of participant enrollment was shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Animals

Male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old, Charles River, Beijing, CHN) were used for all experiments and were housed under pathogen-free conditions with ad libitum access to food and water. Separate cohorts of mice were utilized for testing behavioral performance and biochemical experiments. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Shandong University Cheeloo College of Medicine (approval number: 23087).

CRS mice model and intranasal interventions

The CRS mouse model was established as previously described [27, 28]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with 2.5% isoflurane for 3 min, followed by intranasal administration of papain (20 µg in 20 µL PBS; Sigma-Aldrich, P5306-25MG, USA) on days 1–3 and 8–11 to induce CRS. Mice in the control group received an equivalent volume and dose of heat-inactivated papain (95 °C for 30 min). Based on previous studies, dexmedetomidine (Dex) was administered intranasally at a dose of 5 µg/kg in a total volume of 20 µL per mouse, with the solution equally divided between both nostrils [29]. According to previously published protocols, mice received intranasal administration of a neutralizing anti-IL-33 antibody (20 µg per animal) in a total volume of 20 µL, evenly distributed between the two nostrils [30]. All treatments were administered once daily for three consecutive days following CRS induction. Mice were euthanized, and tissue samples were collected 24 h after the final administration for subsequent analyses.

Diff-quick staining

Nasal airway lavage fluid (NALF) was collected by trans-pharyngeal instillation of three consecutive volumes of 1 mL PBS. The lavage fluid was obtained from the nostrils, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected for further experiments. The cell pellet was resuspended in 100 µL PBS, and 10 µL of the suspension was used for total cell count determination. The remaining 90 µL was used to prepare cytospin slide. Slides were stained using a Diff-Quick staining kit (Solarbio, G1540, CHN). Briefly, the slides were fixed for 1 min, sequentially immersed in Dye I and Dye II for 2 min each, and then mounted with neutral resin. The stained slides were examined and captured using a Nikon Eclipse Ni-U epi-fluorescence microscope.

Hematoxylin plus eosin (HE) staining

Nasal tissues were initially fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4 °C to preserve cellular morphology and prevent tissue degradation. After fixation, the tissues were dehydrated through a series of graded ethanol solutions, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. The embedded tissues were sectioned into 5-µm-thick slices using a microtome. The tissue sections were deparaffinized by sequentially immersing them in xylene and rehydrated through a decreasing ethanol gradient. Following rehydration, the sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, followed by eosin staining for 3 min. After staining, the sections were dehydrated again through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with a neutral resin. Images were obtained by a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, VS200, JPN).

Behavioral tests

All behavioral assessments were performed during the dark phase of a reversed light/dark cycle, in a soundproof room to minimize external disturbances. To reduce stress, mice were handled for 2–3 min daily over a 3-day period prior to testing. Additionally, the mice were allowed to acclimate to the testing room for a minimum of 2 h before each experiment. To eliminate any potential olfactory cues, all testing apparatuses were cleaned thoroughly with 75% ethanol between trials involving different animals. The experimenter was blinded to the experimental groups during both the testing and scoring procedures. Detailed descriptions of each behavioral test are provided below.

Open field test (OFT)

The OFT was conducted as described in previous studies [31]. Mice were individually placed in a 50 cm x 50 cm open arena and allowed to explore the environment freely for 10 min. Locomotion was monitored and recorded using a video-tracking system (Topscan, CleverSys, Inc., USA). Parameters such as total distance traveled, distance traveled in the central area (defined as a 25 cm x 25 cm zone), time spent in the central area, and average velocity were analyzed using Topscan software.

Elevated plus maze (EPM)

The EPM test is a well-established method for assessing anxiety-like behavior in rodents [32]. The EPM apparatus consists of two open arms (30 cm x 6 cm), two enclosed arms (30 cm x 6 cm), and a central platform (6 cm x 6 cm), elevated 70 cm above the floor. Each mouse was placed in the center of the maze, facing an open arm, and allowed to explore the maze for 8 min. Locomotor activity was recorded using an overhead video-tracking system (Topscan, CleverSys, Inc., USA). The number of entries into the open and closed arms, as well as the time spent in the open arms, were measured and analyzed using Topscan software.

Light-dark box test (LDB)

The LDB test exploits the natural aversion of rodents to bright, open spaces [33]. Mice were gently placed in a two-chambered cage, one of which was illuminated and the other kept dark. A partition with an open door allowed free movement between the chambers for a duration of 5 min. The movement of the mice was tracked using video recording, and the time spent in each chamber, as well as the number of entries into the light or dark chamber, were analyzed using tracking software (Topscan, CleverSys, Inc., USA).

Sucrose preference test (SPT)

To assess depression-like behavior, mice were maintained in their home cages and provided free access to two identical 100-mL drinking bottles fitted with stainless-steel sipper tubes. One bottle contained tap water, and the other contained a 1% (w/v) sucrose solution. Bottle positions were alternated every 12 h to avoid side preference. Before testing, animals underwent 24 h of water deprivation, followed by a 48-h period in which baseline intake of water and sucrose was quantified. Sucrose preference was calculated as the proportion of sucrose intake relative to the total fluid consumption.

Tail suspension test (TST)

Depressive-like immobility was evaluated using the classical tail suspension paradigm. Each mouse was suspended by adhesive tape affixed approximately 4 centimeters from the tail tip and positioned in the center of a suspension frame, 30 centimeters above the floor. Sessions lasted 6 min, and the immobility time was quantified during the final 5 min. All trials were video-recorded, and behavioral scoring was performed offline using the SMART tracking system (SMART 3.0, Panlab, Spain).

Forced swim test (FST)

The FST was conducted in a transparent cylindrical chamber (45 centimeters height, 19 centimeters diameter) filled with water (23 ± 1 °C) to a depth of 23 centimeters. Mice were gently placed into the water and allowed to swim for 6 min. The final 4 min of each trial were used to quantify immobility, defined as the absence of active escape-directed behavior. Sessions were captured with an overhead camera, and immobility was analyzed using the SMART video-tracking software (SMART 3.0, Panlab, Spain).

Fear conditioning response (FCR) test

The fear conditioning procedure consisted of one acquisition session followed by two test sessions. Twenty-four hours after the last papain administration, each mouse was placed individually into the conditioning chamber (VFC-008, Med Associates Inc.,USA) and allowed to acclimate for 180 s. A pure auditory cue (1 kHz, 90 dB, 28 s) was then delivered and immediately followed by a 2-second foot shock (1.0 mA). This tone-shock pairing was repeated three times. After the final pairing, the mouse remained in the chamber for an additional 2 min before being returned to its home cage. Twenty-four hours later, contextual fear memory was assessed. Mice were reintroduced into the same chamber for 300 s without any tone or foot shock. One hour after the contextual test, cued fear memory was evaluated in an unrelated environment. Animals were placed into a neutral chamber and exposed to a neutral auditory stimulus (4 kHz, 80 dB, sine wave) for 180 s, with no shock presented. All behavioral sessions were monitored with an automated tracking system (SMART 3.0, Panlab, Spain). Freezing behavior was operationally defined as the absence of any detectable movement for ≥ 2 s.

Three-chamber social interaction test

The apparatus consisted of a rectangular arena partitioned into three compartments of equal dimensions, with two identical wire enclosures positioned in the side chambers. The test was carried out in three consecutive 10-minute phases. During the habituation phase, the subject mouse was allowed to freely explore all chambers containing two empty enclosures. For the sociability phase, the animal was briefly confined to the center compartment while an unfamiliar conspecific (stranger 1, S1; age- and sex-matched C57BL/6J) was placed inside one of the wire enclosures; the mouse was then released and permitted to explore the arena. For the social novelty phase, the subject was again restricted to the center compartment, and a second unfamiliar mouse (stranger 2, S2) was introduced into the previously empty enclosure. The mouse was subsequently allowed to explore all chambers. Behavioral variables-including time spent in each chamber and time spent in close contact with each enclosure (sniffing)-were quantified using tracking system (SMART 3.0, Panlab, Spain). Sociability and novelty indices were calculated as (S1 - E) / (S1 + E) and (S2 - S1) / (S1 + S2), respectively, where S1 is the time interacting with stranger 1, S2 with stranger 2, and E with the empty enclosure. The side assignment of S1 and the empty cage was counterbalanced across trials. Stranger mice were habituated to the wire enclosures prior to testing.

Intracranial adeno-associated virus (AAV) injection

Mice were anesthetized using 2% isoflurane and positioned on a stereotactic frame (RWD Life Science, CHN) to secure their heads during the surgery. A standard surgical procedure was employed to expose the brain regions overlying the BLA, ventral hippocampus (vHPC), hypothalamus premammillary nucleus (PMN, including premammillary nucleus ventral, PMV, and premammillary nucleus dorsal, PMD). The following AAV serotypes were used: AAV2/9-hSyn-hM3Dq-mCherry for neural chemogenetic activation (0.3 mg/kg CNO was intraperitoneally injected 30 min before the behavioral assays), AAV2/9-hSyn-hM4Di-mCherry for neural chemogenetic inhibition (3 mg/kg CNO was injected intraperitoneally 30 min prior to the behavioral assays) [34], AAV2/9-hSyn-mCherry were used as the control virus. AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-EGFP-Cre mixed with AAV2/9-flex-taCasp3-TEVp (1:1) for BLA CaMKIIα positive neuron ablation. AAV2/9-CaMKIIα/GAD67-GCaMP6s was bilaterally injected into the BLA. An optical fiber (FOC-W-1.25-200-0.37-3.0, Inper) was implanted 100 μm above the injection site and secured with dental cement. Calcium signals were recorded after sufficient viral expression and recovery from fiber implantation. For retrograde, projection-specific chemogenetic inhibition, AAV-retro-Cre-EGFP (serotype 2/retro) was injected into the target region, while AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry was injected into the upstream region. Pseudorabies virus (PRV)-GFP for retrograde trans-synaptic tracing (Brain case, China). The viral vectors used in this study were purchased from Obio Technology (Shanghai, China), and the final titer for AAV injections was 5 × 10¹² viral particles/mL. Using a glass pipette, 50 nL of the AAV solution was injected into the target region at a rate of 20 nL/min. The coordinates for the BLA were: bregma − 1.50 mm, lateral ± 2.8 mm, and depth − 4.65 mm. For vHPC, the coordinates were: bregma − 3.50 mm, lateral ± 3.2 mm, and depth − 4.0 mm. For PMD, the coordinates were: bregma − 2.50 mm, lateral ± 0.5 mm, and depth − 5.25 mm. For PMV, the coordinates were: bregma − 2.50 mm, lateral ± 0.5 mm, and depth − 5.50 mm.

Slice electrophysiology

Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were conducted in acute brain slices prepared from mice subjected to AAV injections. Slice preparation and electrophysiological recording procedures were adapted from previously established protocols [35, 36]. Mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with ice-cold, oxygenated cutting solution continuously bubbled with 95% O₂ and 5% CO₂. The cutting solution consisted of (in mM): 93 N-methyl-D-glucamine, 2.5 KCl, 1.2 NaH₂PO₄, 30 NaHCO₃, 20 HEPES, 25 glucose, 5 sodium ascorbate, 2 thiourea, 3 sodium pyruvate, 10 MgSO₄, 11.9 N-acetyl-L-cysteine, and 1 CaCl₂. The pH was adjusted to 7.3–7.4 with HCl, and the osmolarity was adjusted to 300–310 mOsm. Following decapitation, the brain was rapidly removed, and coronal Sect. (300 μm thick) containing the target region were cut using a vibratome (VT1000S, Leica) in ice-cold cutting solution. Slices were subsequently transferred to oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) composed of (in mM): 125 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 12.5 glucose, 1 MgCl₂, 2 CaCl₂, 1.25 NaH₂PO₄, and 25 NaHCO₃ (pH 7.35; osmolarity 280–300 mOsm). After sectioning, slices were allowed to recover at 31 °C for at least 1 h before recording. For electrophysiological recordings, slices were placed in a submerged recording chamber and continuously perfused with oxygenated ACSF at a flow rate of 2–3 ml/min while maintained at 32 ± 1 °C. Neurons were visualized using an upright microscope (BX51WI, Olympus) equipped with differential interference contrast optics and an optiMOS camera (QImaging, Teledyne Imaging Group). Patch electrodes (3–5 MΩ) were pulled from borosilicate glass capillaries using a micropipette puller (P-2000, Sutter Instrument). Whole-cell currents were recorded using an Axon 200B amplifier (Molecular Devices), digitized with a Digidata 1440 A interface, and acquired using pClamp 10.5 software. Series resistance and input resistance were continuously monitored throughout recordings, and data were excluded if series resistance varied by more than 20%.

Miniature and spontaneous synaptic current recordings

Miniature excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs and mIPSCs) and spontaneous excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs and sIPSCs) were recorded from BLA neurons in whole-cell voltage-clamp configuration. Patch electrodes (3–5 MΩ) were filled with internal solutions as described below. For spontaneous synaptic current recordings, sEPSCs were recorded at a holding potential of -70 mV, whereas sIPSCs were recorded at 10 mV to predominantly isolate inhibitory currents. A cesium methanesulfonate-based internal solution containing (in mM): 125 CsCH₃SO₃, 5 CsCl, 10 HEPES, 0.2 EGTA, 4 Mg-ATP, 0.3 Na-GTP, and 5 QX-314 was used. The pH was adjusted to 7. 3 with CsOH and osmolarity to 280–290 mOsm. mEPSCs were recorded using a potassium gluconate-based internal solution containing (in mM): 105 K-gluconate, 30 KCl, 10 HEPES, 0.3 EGTA, 4 Mg-ATP, 0.3 Na-GTP, and 10 Na₂-phosphocreatine. The pH was adjusted with KOH and osmolarity to 285–295 mOsm. Neurons were held at -70 mV. Tetrodotoxin (TTX, 1 µM) was included in the external solution to block action potentials, and bicuculline (20 µM) was added to block GABAA receptor-mediated inhibitory transmission. mIPSCs were recorded using a cesium chloride-based internal solution containing (in mM): 140 CsCl, 10 HEPES, 0.2 EGTA, 2 Mg-ATP, 0.3 Na-GTP, and 5 QX-314. The pH was adjusted to 7.2–7.3 with CsOH and osmolarity to 280–290 mOsm. Neurons were held at -70 mV. During recordings, TTX (1 µM) was included to block action potentials, and CNQX (20 µM) and AP5 (50 µM) were added to block glutamatergic transmission. Signals were acquired using an Axon 200B amplifier, digitized with a Digidata 1440 A interface, and recorded with pClamp 10.5 software. Miniature and spontaneous events were analyzed offline for frequency and amplitude using Clampfit or Mini Analysis software.

Evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents (eEPSCs) recordings

Following acute brain slice preparation, eEPSCs were recorded from neurons using whole-cell voltage-clamp at a holding potential of -70 mV. Patch electrodes were filled with a potassium gluconate-based internal solution containing. Bicuculline (20 µM) was included in the external solution to block GABAA receptor-mediated inhibitory transmission. EPSCs were evoked by local electrical stimulation delivered through a bipolar electrode placed in the vicinity of the recorded neurons. Comparisons of eEPSC amplitudes were performed between experimental groups treated with CNO or vehicle to assess the impact of chemogenetic inhibition on synaptic activity. All recordings were conducted under controlled temperature conditions, and data were analyzed using standard electrophysiological procedures.

Paired-pulse ratio (PPR)

Paired-pulse evoked EPSCs were recorded using whole-cell voltage-clamp configuration. Patch electrodes were filled with a potassium gluconate-based internal solution. Neurons were voltage-clamped at -70 mV. Bicuculline (20 µM) was included in the external solution to block GABAA receptor-mediated inhibitory transmission. Synaptic responses were elicited by local electrical stimulation delivered through a bipolar stimulating electrode positioned near the recorded neuron. Each paired stimulus consisted of two identical monophasic current pulses (0.1 ms duration) applied at a low frequency to ensure response stability. The stimulation intensity was adjusted to evoke EPSCs with amplitudes corresponding to approximately 30% of the maximal response. Paired-pulse responses were collected at inter-stimulus intervals of 20, 50, and 100 ms. The paired-pulse ratio was calculated as the amplitude of the second EPSC divided by that of the first (P2/P1) and averaged across repeated trials for each neuron.

AMPA/NMDA ratio

Evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) were elicited by local electrical stimulation delivered near the recorded neurons. Patch electrodes were filled with a cesium methanesulfonate-based internal solution, and bicuculline (20 µM) was included in the external solution to block GABAA receptor-mediated inhibitory transmission. AMPA receptor-mediated currents were recorded at -70 mV, whereas NMDA receptor-mediated currents were recorded at + 40 mV. NMDA currents were quantified as the mean current amplitude measured 100 ms after stimulus onset to minimize contamination from AMPA receptor–mediated components. The AMPA/NMDA ratio was calculated by dividing the peak AMPA current amplitude by the NMDA current amplitude for each neuron.

Action potential recordings

Depolarizing current injections of 500 ms duration were applied in a stepwise manner, increasing by 50 pA from the minimal current sufficient to elicit action potentials, up to a maximum of 300 pA. Evoked action potential traces were recorded, and firing frequency at each current step was quantified. Patch electrodes were filled with a potassium gluconate-based internal solution containing.

Following brain slice preparation, spontaneous neuronal firing was recorded using whole-cell patch-clamp recordings. Firing activity of the same neuron was recorded for 1 min to establish a stable baseline, followed by CNO (10 µM) application via bath perfusion to assess chemogenetic modulation of neuronal excitability. All experiments were conducted under controlled temperature, and data analysis was performed using standard electrophysiological protocols to ensure reproducibility.

Fiber photometry recording of calcium activity

Calcium dynamics in the target neuronal population were assessed using a fiber photometry approach. Prior to data acquisition, the implanted optical fiber was coupled to the recording apparatus via an external patch fiber. Fluorescence signals were collected using a fiber photometry system (Inper, C11946), with excitation provided by a 488-nm diode laser. Calcium-related fluorescence signals were continuously recorded using fiber photometry throughout the EPM test. Five entries into the open arms during the 8-min session were defined as individual events, and fluorescence signals were aligned to event onset. For each event, a 20-s segment (± 10 s) centered on open-arm entry was extracted, normalized, and averaged across events to capture the time course of a typical Ca²⁺ transient. Fluorescence intensity changes were quantified as ΔF/F, calculated using the formula ΔF/F = (F − F₀) / F₀, where F represents the instantaneous fluorescence signal and F₀ was defined as the average fluorescence signal during a baseline period. The photomultiplier tube output current was converted into voltage signals using an amplifier (C7319, Hamamatsu). These signals were subsequently processed through a low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 40 Hz (Brownlee 440). The filtered analog signals were digitized at a sampling rate of 100 Hz using a Power 1401 digitizer and acquired with Spike2 software (Cambridge Electronic Design, Cambridge, UK). Following completion of the recordings, the collected data were imported into calcium signal analysis software for further processing and analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining

Mice were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains and nasal tissues were harvested and post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight, then cryoprotected in sucrose solutions (10%, 20%, and 30%) for 24 h at 4 °C. Coronal sections (brain:35 μm thick, and nasal tissue:5 μm) were cut using a cryostat and incubated in blocking buffer (5% normal goat serum in PBS with 0.1% Triton X-100) for 2 h at room temperature. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including anti-c-FOS (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology, 2250s, USA), anti-tyrosine hydroxylase (TH, 1:200, Abcam, ab137869, UK), and anti-major basic protein (MBP, 1:200, Affinity, DF6992, CHN). Following primary antibody incubation, sections were washed three times in PBS containing 0.4% Triton X-100 (PBST) and incubated for 2 h at room temperature with secondary antibodies, including goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor Plus 647 (1:200, Proteintech, RGAR005, CHN). Sections were then mounted with fluorescence mounting medium containing DAPI (Servicebio, G1407, CHN) to prevent fluorescence quenching. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus VS200, Japan) and analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.52n, NIH, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Elisa)

The concentration of interleukin (IL)-4 (4ABio., CME0005, CHN), IL-5 (4ABio, CME0023, CHN), IL-13 (4ABio, CME0009, CHN), IL-33 (Boster, EK0930, CHN), Epinephrine (E, ArigoBio, ARG80440, CHN), dopamine (DA, ArigoBio, ARG80440, CHN), norepinephrine (NE, ArigoBio, ARG80440, CHN) were determined according manufacturer’s instruction and analyzed by multifunctional microplate reader (BioTek, Synergy H1, USA). The absorption was measured at 450 nm, and 630 nm as the reference wavelength.

Western blotting

Mouse brain tissues were collected and homogenized in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013B, China). Protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0010, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein (10 µg per lane) were loaded onto 10% SDS-PAGE gels under denaturing conditions for separation. The proteins were then transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Cat# IPVH00010, Burlington, MA) using electroblotting. After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-c-FOS (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 2250s, USA), anti-synaptophysin (SYP, 1:1000, Abcam, ab309493, UK), anti-postsynaptic density protein-95 (PSD95, 1:1000, Abcam, ab18258, UK), anti-ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba1, 1:1000, Abcam, ab178847, UK), anti-ZO-1 (1:10,000, Proteintech, 21773, China), and anti-β-actin (1:500, Santa Cruz, sc-47778, USA). Following primary antibody incubation, the membranes were washed three times with TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.2% Tween-20) and subsequently incubated with secondary antibodies: anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000, Cell Signaling Technology, 5470s, USA) and anti-mouse IgG (1:10,000, Cell Signaling Technology, 5366s, USA) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized and scanned using a two-color infrared laser imaging system (LI-COR, Odyssey, USA). The images were subsequently analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Band intensities of the target proteins were normalized to the corresponding β-actin signals, and the data were expressed as relative intensities compared to the control sample on the same gel.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± SEM and analyzed statistically using GraphPad Prism (version 8, GraphPad Software, USA) and IBM SPSS (version 27, IBM, USA) statistics software. For two-group comparisons, paired t-test or unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used. For multiple-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test and two-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test were used. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Surgery and steroid improve nasal symptoms but not anxiety in CRS patients

To investigate the comorbid anxiety in CRS, we recruited a cohort of 209 CRS patients (Supplementary Table 1), in which 44.5% of patients were found to have anxiety as evaluated by HADS-A (Fig. 1A). Preoperative correlation analysis revealed a significant positive relationship between anxiety scores and SNOT-22 scores, indicating that higher nasal symptom severity was associated with greater levels of anxiety (Fig. 1B). Nasal symptom severity was assessed at 3- and 6-months following surgery and topical steroid therapy using the SNOT-22. A significant reduction in symptom scores was already evident at 3 months post-treatment compared with baseline, with no further improvement observed at 6 months (Fig. 1C), indicating an early and sustained alleviation of nasal symptoms. The anxiety scores showed no significant changes at either 3- or 6-months post-treatment compared with baseline, nor were there differences between the two postoperative time points (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, no significant correlation was observed between postoperative anxiety scores and postoperative nasal symptom severity (Fig. 1E). Together, these results suggest that while routine CRS treatment may improve nasal symptoms, its impact on comorbid anxiety appears limited, highlighting the need for further investigation into potential mechanisms underlying anxiety in CRS.

CRS mice exhibit persistent anxiety-like behaviors despite amelioration of sinonasal inflammation

To investigate the potential mechanisms underlying the comorbid anxiety observed in CRS, a CRS mouse model was established using papain administration (Fig. 2A). Throughout the papain administration, no adverse health effects, including weight loss or alterations in food and water intake, were observed (supplementary Fig. 2). In the CRS, both NALF and nasal tissues exhibited a significant increase in eosinophils (Fig. 2B-E). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed markedly increased numbers of MBP-positive eosinophils in the nasal mucosa of CRS mice (Fig. 2K-L), suggesting enhanced eosinophil infiltration [28]. Besides, CRS mice demonstrated goblet cell hyperplasia and disrupted continuity of the ciliated epithelium (Fig. 2C and F). Inflammatory cytokines, including IL-33, IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, were markedly elevated in the NALF of CRS mice compared to controls (Fig. 2G-J). In addition to inflammatory alterations, changes in sympathetic nerve density of the nasal mucosa were observed in CRS mice. Immunofluorescence staining revealed altered density of TH-labeled fibers in the nasal mucosa, which was further quantified by TH-positive staining intensity (Fig. 2M). Moreover, norepinephrine (NE) concentrations in the NALF were significantly elevated in CRS mice compared with controls, while no significant changes observed in epinephrine (E) or dopamine (DA) levels (Fig. 2N). Collectively, these findings further characterized the CRS mouse model by demonstrating prominent sinonasal inflammation and alterations in local sympathetic features.

CRS pathology and anxiety levels were assessed three months post-papain administration. Chemogenetic inhibition of BLA neurons modulates neuronal activity and anxiety-like behaviors in CRS mice

To explore potential central mechanisms, we analyzed neuronal activation in brain regions implicated in emotion regulation. One day after the final papain administration, brain samples were collected, and immunostaining for c-FOS, a marker of neuronal activation, was performed. Among the 20 brain regions examined, the CRS group showed a significant increase in c-FOS+ cell density specifically in the BLA (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Table 2) compared to controls [37, 38]. Representative immunofluorescence images further confirmed enhanced c-FOS expression in the BLA of CRS mice (Fig. 4B). To examine whether the elevated neuronal activity was accompanied by intrinsic excitability changes, whole-cell current-clamp recordings were performed in BLA neurons. Representative traces of current-evoked action potentials (APs) revealed increased firing responses in neurons from CRS mice compared with controls, which was reflected by a significantly higher firing frequency across depolarizing current steps (Fig. 4C). Together with elevated c-FOS expression, the enhanced intrinsic excitability suggests a hyperactive state of the BLA circuit in CRS mice.

To further determine whether BLA hyperactivity contributes to anxiety-like behaviors in CRS mice, we employed chemogenetic inactivation of BLA neurons. CRS mice were administered papain eighteen days following chemogenetic manipulation of the BLA neurons, and their anxiety-like behaviors were assessed at specific time points as indicated in Fig. 4D. Mice were stereotaxically injected with AAV vectors expressing hM4Di and mCherry to selectively inhibit BLA neurons, while a control group received AAV expressing mCherry only (Fig. 4E). The efficacy of chemogenetic inactivation was validated by a reduction in c-FOS expression (Fig. 4F-G). Consistently, whole-cell current-clamp recordings showed that CNO application reduced the frequency of membrance potentials in BLA neurons, indicating effective suppression of neuronal excitability (Fig. 4H). Behavioral testing revealed that inactivation of BLA neurons led to a significant amelioration in anxiety-like behaviors in CRS mice. Specifically, total movement distance, central area movement distance, time spent in the central area, and average movement speed all increased in CRS mice with inactivated BLA neurons compared to control virus-injected mice in the OFT (Fig. 4I-K). In the EPM, CRS mice with reduced BLA activity displayed a higher number of open-arm entries and increased time spent in the open arms, while the number of closed-arm entries was significantly reduced (Fig. 4L-M). In the LDB test, the mice spent less time in the dark box and made more entries into the light box compared to the control group (Fig. 4N). Collectively, these results revealed increased neuronal activation and altered excitability in the BLA of CRS mice and demonstrated that chemogenetic inhibition of BLA neurons modulated neural activity and behavioral outcomes in CRS mice.

Representative whole-cell current-clamp recordings were conducted to quantify membrane potentials before and after CNO application, which revealed significantly increased firing frequency in BLA neurons upon activation (Fig. 5D). In the EPM, activation of BLA neurons led to decreased time spent in the open arms and fewer entries into the open arms, while increasing entries into the closed arms (Fig. 5G-I). In the LDB test, BLA activation resulted in increased time spent in the dark box and fewer entries into the light box (Fig. 5J-K).

We further assess the effects of chemogenetic inhibition and activation of BLA neurons on peripheral inflammation and sympathetic activity. TH-labeled sympathetic nerve fibers in the nasal mucosa were reduced in the neuronal inhibition group, whereas the neuronal activation group exhibited an increase in fiber density compared to the CRS group (Fig. 5L-M). Enhanced excitatory synaptic input and structural remodeling in BLA excitatory neurons of CRS mice

To further investigate the synaptic mechanisms underlying comorbid anxiety, we first differentiated excitatory and inhibitory neurons in the BLA using cell-type–specific viral labeling strategies. Whole-cell recordings revealed no significant differences in action potential firing of inhibitory neurons under a 200pA current injection between control and CRS mice (supplementary Fig. 7). We therefore focused on CaMKIIα-positive excitatory neurons in the BLA, recording both sEPSCs to assess excitatory input and sIPSCs to evaluate inhibitory input onto these neurons. Compared with control mice, CRS mice exhibited a significant increase in sEPSC frequency (Fig. 6B-C), indicating enhanced excitatory synaptic drive onto BLA excitatory neurons. In contrast, sEPSC amplitude was unchanged, suggesting that postsynaptic responsiveness at individual excitatory synapses remained stable. No significant differences were observed in either the frequency or amplitude of sIPSCs between CRS and control groups (Fig. 6D), indicating preserved inhibitory synaptic input. Consistent with these findings, the ratio of spontaneous excitatory to inhibitory synaptic currents (CsEPSC/CsIPSC) was significantly elevated in CRS mice (Fig. 6E), reflecting a shift in the excitation-inhibition balance toward increased excitation in BLA excitatory neurons.

To further delineate whether these alterations originated from presynaptic or postsynaptic mechanisms, miniature synaptic currents were recorded in the presence of tetrodotoxin. Analysis of miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) revealed a significant increase in event frequency in CRS mice, while mEPSC amplitude remained unchanged (Fig. 6F-G). These results suggest enhanced presynaptic excitatory input and/or an increased number of functional excitatory synapses, without detectable changes in postsynaptic AMPA receptor-mediated responsiveness. To further assess synaptic transmission properties, paired-pulse ratios (PPRs) of evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents were measured at multiple inter-stimulus intervals. Compared with control mice, CRS mice exhibited a significant reduction in PPR (Fig. 6J), suggesting an increased presynaptic release probability at excitatory synapses in the BLA. Additionally, AMPA receptor-mediated and NMDA receptor-mediated EPSCs were recorded to evaluate postsynaptic glutamatergic receptor function. Neither AMPA nor NMDA peak amplitudes differed significantly between CRS and control groups (Fig. 6K-L), suggesting preserved postsynaptic glutamate receptor function. Finally, Golgi staining revealed a significant increase in dendritic spine density in the BLA of CRS mice compared with controls (Fig. 6M), indicating structural remodeling that is consistent with enhanced excitatory synaptic connectivity in BLA excitatory neurons. Besides, VGLUT2 immunofluorescence staining further revealed an increase in excitatory synaptic inputs in the BLA (Supplementary Fig. 8). Additionally, a significant reduction in synaptic-related proteins, including SYP and PSD95 (Fig. 6N-O) were observed in the BLA of CRS mice. These molecular changes occurred in the context of enhanced excitatory synaptic drive and increased dendritic spine density, suggesting activity-dependent synaptic remodeling characterized by increased synaptic turnover and reduced stability of mature synaptic components under sustained excitatory drive.

Elevated activity of BLA CaMKIIα neurons in CRS mice and reduction of anxiety-like behaviors by selective ablation

Given that enhanced excitatory synaptic input onto CaMKIIα-positive neurons in the BLA was observed alongside an overall increase in dendritic spine density of CRS mice, we next examined whether these neurons exhibit altered activity during anxiety-related behavior. Fiber photometry recordings revealed a marked increase in Ca²⁺ signals in BLA excitatory neurons of CRS mice when transitioning from the closed arms to the open arms during the elevated plus maze test (Fig. 7A-F). To assess the functional contribution of CaMKIIα-positive neurons to CRS-related anxiety-like behavioral, we selectively ablated this neuronal population in the BLA and examined the resulting behavioral outcomes (Fig. 7G-H). Behavioral testing revealed that ablation of CaMKIIα-expressing neurons in the BLA significantly reduced anxiety-like behaviors in CRS mice. In the OFT, CRS mice injected with AAV-taCasp3 exhibited significantly increased total movement distance, central area movement, time spent in the central area, and average movement speed compared to control virus-injected mice (Fig. 7I). In the EPM, these mice displayed increased time spent in and more entries into the open arms, along with a reduced number of closed-arm entries (Fig. 7J). In the LDB test, ablation of CaMKIIα-expressing neurons resulted in less time spent in the dark box and more entries into the light box (Fig. 7K). Collectively, these findings suggest that heightened activity of CaMKIIα-positive neurons in the BLA is associated with anxiety-related behaviors in CRS mice, reducing the neuronal population attenuates anxiety-like behaviors.

Chemogenetic inhibition of BLA→vHPC CaMKIIα-positive neurons reduce anxiety-like behaviors in CRS mice

To map the circuits associated with BLA CaMKIIα-positive neurons in CRS mice, we performed retrograde trans-synaptic tracing by injecting pseudorabies virus (PRV) into the nasal cavities. PRV-labeled neurons were observed in the PMN (including the PMV and PMD), vHPC, and BLA (Fig. 8A), confirming connectivity among these regions [39, 40]. Recombinant AAV expressing mCherry was injected into the BLA of CRS mice, and the expression was detected. As shown in Fig. 8B, mCherry-positive axons were located near the vHPC. To examine the functional role of BLA CaMKIIα-positive neurons projecting to the vHPC, we employed a dual-virus chemogenetic strategy. AAV-retro-Cre was injected into the vHPC and AAV-DIO-CaMKIIα-hM4Di-mCherry was injected into the BLA (Fig. 8C-D), specifically targeting BLA→vHPC CaMKIIα-positive neurons for inhibitory DREADD expression. Whole-cell recordings from GFP+/mCherry+ BLA neurons showed that CRS increased action potential firing, which was attenuated by CNO, confirming effective chemogenetic inhibition of BLA→vHPC CaMKIIα-positive neurons (Fig. 8E-F). In the vHPC, CRS enhanced eEPSC amplitudes and sEPSC frequency in recorded neurons, both of which were reduced by CNO administration, suggesting that inhibition of BLA→vHPC CaMKIIα-positive neurons alters excitatory synaptic drive within the vHPC circuit (Fig. 8G-H, I-J). Following CNO administration, chemogenetic inhibition of these neurons significantly reduced anxiety-like behaviors compared with vehicle-treated controls. Specifically, in the OFT, CNO-treated CRS mice showed increased locomotor activity, greater exploration of the central area, and higher movement velocity (Fig. 8K-L). In the EPM, CNO administration resulted in prolonged time spent in the open arms and an increased number of open-arm entries, accompanied by fewer closed-arm entries (Fig. 8M-O). Similarly, in the LDB test, chemogenetic inhibition led to reduced occupancy of the dark compartment and increased transitions into the light compartment (Fig. 8P). Together, these results indicate that BLA→vHPC CaMKIIα-positive neurons are functionally involved in CRS-associated anxiety-like behaviors, and chemogenetic inhibition of this projection effectively attenuates anxiety-related behavioral phenotypes.

Sympathetic–IL-33 signaling links nasal inflammation to anxiety-like behaviors in CRS mice

Building on the observation that chemogenetic inhibition reduced sympathetic nerve density and attenuated eosinophilic inflammation, we next investigated the mechanisms linking sympathetic nerve fiber proliferation to eosinophil infiltration. Administration of Dex to inhibit NE release (Fig. 10A-B) effectively reduced IL-33 levels (Fig. 10C) and eosinophil infiltration in CRS mice (Fig. 10D-E). To further investigate whether inhibition of the inflammatory cytokine IL-33 could ameliorate CRS-associated comorbid anxiety, we first evaluated blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity by assessing the tight junction protein ZO-1 and BBB permeability using Evans blue extravasation. CRS mice displayed significant disruption of BBB tight junctions together with increased BBB permeability compared with control mice, indicating compromised BBB integrity under CRS conditions (Fig. 10F-G). Given the impaired BBB integrity in CRS mice, we next examined whether nasal neutralization of IL-33 could modulate inflammatory responses and behavioral outcomes. Treatment with an IL-33 neutralizing antibody in CRS mice (Fig. 10H) led to decreased levels of IL-5 (Fig. 10I) and IL-13 (Fig. 10J), accompanied by a reduction in eosinophil infiltration (Fig. 10K-L). Consistent with these anti-inflammatory effects, nasal IL-33 neutralization alleviated anxiety-like behaviors in CRS mice. In the OFT, IL-33 blockade increased total travel distance, the percentage of time spent in the center zone, distance traveled in the center, and mean movement speed (Fig. 10M-N). In the EPM, IL-33-neutralized mice spent more time in the open arms, showed increased open-arm entries, and displayed reduced closed-arm entries (Fig. 10O-R). Consistently, in the LDB test, IL-33 neutralization increased entries into the light compartment and decreased time spent in the dark compartment (Fig. 10S). Whole-cell recordings from BLA neurons revealed that CRS significantly increased action potential firing, an effect that was attenuated by nasal anti–IL-33 treatment, suggesting that neutralization of IL-33 in the nasal mucosa ameliorates CRS-induced hyperexcitability of BLA neurons (Fig. 10T). Together, these results show that nasal IL-33 neutralization is associated with reduced inflammation, improved anxiety-like behaviors, and attenuated BLA neuronal hyperexcitability in CRS mice, coinciding with compromised BBB integrity.

Discussion

Epidemiological studies have consistently shown that anxiety is highly prevalent among CRS patients [3, 43]. In line with these observations, our cohort analysis revealed that 44.5% of CRS patients exhibited comorbid anxiety prior to surgery. Although sinonasal symptoms improved postoperatively, anxiety persisted in a substantial proportion of patients at 6 months after surgery. These findings underscore the clinical persistence of anxiety symptoms in CRS. Importantly, CRS and anxiety are not merely co-occurring conditions but appear to mutually exacerbate disease severity and progression [5]. Consistent with our findings, a study of 18 individuals found that CRS-related anxiety remained unchanged approximately two years following endoscopic nasal surgery [11]. These findings suggested that comorbid anxiety in CRS was not merely a consequence of symptom severity or nasal inflammation, but may also involve central neural mechanisms and neuroimmune interactions. Jafari A. et al. demonstrated participants with sinonasal inflammation showed decreased brain connectivity within the frontoparietal network, a major functional hub [44]. Studies showed CRS mice exhibited elevated inflammatory markers in the brain and alterations in neural circuits associated with cognitive regulation [45, 46]. Thus, elucidating the neural circuitry that links CRS to anxiety may shed light on the development of safer and more durable therapeutic strategies targeting both CRS and its psychiatric comorbidities. In the present study, we selectively monitored and manipulated CaMKIIα-positive neurons, the principal glutamatergic neuronal population in the BLA, revealing their critical role in mediating CRS comorbid anxiety. Further, anatomical tracing revealed multisynaptic connectivity among the BLA, vHPC, and PMN, which was associated with alterations in peripheral nasal inflammatory responses. Functional manipulation of the BLA→vHPC projection demonstrated that this pathway contributes to anxiety-like behaviors and was accompanied by changes in PMN neuronal excitability and sinonasal immune indices. Collectively, these findings provide mechanistic insight into a central limbic-hypothalamic network through CRS comorbid anxiety.

Clinically, CRS and anxiety frequently coexist and reinforce each other [5], yet the neural circuit mechanisms underlying anxiety comorbidity in CRS remain largely unexplored. To address this gap, we established a behavioral paradigm to assess anxiety-like phenotypes associated with CRS and present multiple lines of evidence supporting a pivotal role for BLA neurons—particularly glutamatergic neurons—in CRS-anxiety comorbidity. Multimodal analyses revealed hyperactivation of BLA glutamatergic neurons in CRS mice, characterized by enhanced excitatory synaptic input and anxiety-related activity dynamics. Manipulating BLA activity bidirectionally regulated CRS-associated anxiety and inflammation, while targeted ablation of BLA glutamatergic neurons alleviated anxiety-like behaviors, identifying the BLA as a key circuit node linking CRS inflammation to anxiety.

To define how the BLA modulates sinonasal pathology in CRS mice with comorbid anxiety, we combined virus-mediated anatomical tracing, glutamatergic neuron-specific chemogenetic manipulations, and anxiety-related behavioral assays. This integrative approach revealed a multisynaptic circuit linking the BLA, vHPC, and PMN, through which activity was associated with peripheral nasal immune responses. Circuit-specific inhibition of glutamatergic neurons along this pathway alleviated anxiety-like behaviors in CRS mice and concomitantly reduced sympathetic hyperinnervation and inflammatory exacerbation in nasal tissues. These findings suggesting a causal circuit mechanism through which limbic activity can influence peripheral sinonasal pathology in CRS comorbid anxiety states. Despite these advances, the upstream pathways by which sinonasal inflammation engages the BLA remain unresolved. Given the anatomical proximity of the nasal cavity to the brain [12], CRS-associated disruption of the BBB and nasal epithelial integrity may permit inflammatory signals to access the CNS via the olfactory bulb and other barrier-deficient pathways, facilitating immune-neural communication. Our data are consistent with a contributory role for BBB compromise in linking CRS pathology to anxiety comorbidity; however, the specificity, functional relevance, and involvement of additional brain regions require further investigation. Defining parallel or convergent routes that convey sinonasal inflammatory signals to anxiety-related circuits represents an important direction for future studies.

Previous studies have shown that sympathetic adrenergic signaling can promote type 2 immune responses, including IL-33 release and eosinophil accumulation in allergic inflammation [47–50], and that eosinophils may in turn interact with the sympathetic nervous system [51, 52]. In addition, study showing increased neural expression and NALF levels of secretoneurin in allergic rhinitis [53]. In line with these findings, we observed altered immune–neural features in the nasal mucosa of CRS mice with comorbid anxiety, characterized by increased sympathetic fiber density, elevated norepinephrine levels, enhanced IL-33 expression, and augmented eosinophilic infiltration. Importantly, these peripheral changes were bidirectionally modulated by BLA neuronal activity. The activation of BLA neurons exacerbated, whereas suppression alleviated sympathetic and inflammatory markers in the sinonasal tissue. Consistently, inhibition of the BLA→vHPC glutamatergic pathway was associated with attenuation of these peripheral alterations. Notably, TH immunoreactivity was used here as an index of sympathetic innervation, and the observed effects are interpreted as downstream consequences of circuit modulation rather than evidence of direct central projections.

Studies have identified the proliferation of sympathetic nerve fibers in the CRS nasal mucosa, which contributes to disease progression [54]. Additionally, the sympathetic nervous system is believed to play an important role in monitoring and regulating immune homeostasis via adrenergic signaling [18]. It has been reported that norepinephrine promotes the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines by activating adrenergic receptors expressed on macrophages [55]. Our data demonstrated that Dex-mediated activation of β2-adrenergic receptors in CRS mice suppresses NE release and is associated with reduced IL-33 levels and decreased eosinophilic infiltration. These findings raise the possibility that Dex may exert its anti-inflammatory effects, at least in part, by limiting NE-mediated epithelial injury and subsequent alarmin release [56, 57]. Further investigation demonstrated that neutralization of IL-33 in CRS mice markedly reduced downstream IL-5 and IL-13 production and eosinophil infiltration, accompanied by attenuation of BLA hyperexcitability and anxiety-like behaviors. Given that BBB integrity is compromised in CRS, peripheral type 2 inflammatory signals may gain increased access to the central nervous system, thereby influencing BLA activity, dampening IL-33-driven inflammation may therefore ameliorate both peripheral immune responses and central neural dysfunction. Together, these findings support a model in which glutamatergic output from the BLA-associated circuit is linked to enhanced sympathetic activity in the nasal mucosa, accompanied by increased norepinephrine release, IL-33 induction, eosinophil recruitment, and exacerbation of sinonasal inflammation. BBB disruption observed in CRS may further facilitate the coupling between peripheral inflammatory signals and central anxiety-related circuits, although its precise contribution requires further investigation. Moreover, the precise cellular targets of NE and the molecular mechanisms linking sympathetic signaling to epithelial damage and alarmin release warrant further investigation.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, mechanistic experiments were performed exclusively in male mice to minimize variability associated with estrous cycle-related hormonal fluctuations and to facilitate mechanistic interpretation [58]. However, this male-only design may limit the generalizability of the findings given the sex heterogeneity of clinical CRS populations. Future studies incorporating both sexes will be necessary to assess potential sex-dependent circuit and immune mechanisms. Second, most mechanistic analyses relied on the papain-induced CRS model, which does not fully recapitulate all clinical subtypes of CRS, especially CRS with nasal polyps. Validation of the identified mechanisms in additional CRS models would strengthen the conclusions. Third, although BBB disruption was observed in CRS mice, its functional contribution to altered BLA activity remains unclear, and whether peripheral inflammatory mediators directly access central circuits through a compromised BBB requires further investigation. Finally, while chemogenetic inhibition targeted BLA glutamatergic projections to the vHPC, electrophysiological recordings were not pathway-specific, and thus likely reflect network-level effects rather than direct synaptic changes. Future studies employing projection-specific approaches will be required to resolve these issues.

Conclusions

In summary, this study identifies a BLA-centered central–peripheral neural network linking CRS to comorbid anxiety and sinonasal inflammation. Glutamatergic neurons in the BLA are associated with anxiety-like behaviors and peripheral sympathetic–immune remodeling involving the vHPC and PMN, characterized by enhanced sympathetic innervation, NE release, IL-33–dependent type 2 immune responses, and eosinophilic inflammation in the nasal mucosa. Chemogenetic inhibition of BLA→vHPC CaMKIIα-positive neurons attenuated excitatory input to downstream regions, improved anxiety-like behaviors, and reduced peripheral sympathetic and inflammatory responses. In parallel, neutralization of IL-33 in the nasal mucosa was associated with reduced inflammation, improved anxiety-like behaviors, and decreased BLA neuronal hyperexcitability in CRS mice with compromised BBB integrity. Together, these findings extend CRS beyond a purely peripheral inflammatory disorder and suggest brain–periphery interactions as potential therapeutic targets for CRS with comorbid anxiety.
