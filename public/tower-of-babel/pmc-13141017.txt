Psychosocial factors associated with non-suicidal self-injury in Iranian adolescents and young adults: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Rahele Dinparvar, Bahram-Ali Ghanbari Hashemabadi, Ali Taghipour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNon-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is defined as the deliberate harm to one’s body without suicidal intent. Although individuals engaging in NSSI do not intend to die, repetitive self-injury may increase the risk of future suicidal behaviour through reduced fear of death and increased pain tolerance. Global lifetime prevalence estimates of NSSI range from 4.2% in adults to 15.4% in adolescents, with an observed increase during the COVID-19 pandemic. In Iran, the reported prevalence of NSSI among adolescents and young adults is approximately 22%; however, comprehensive and culturally contextualised evidence remains limited. Psychosocial, interpersonal and ecological factors contribute to NSSI, yet no prior study has systematically mapped these determinants within an integrated, multilevel framework in the Iranian context.ObjectiveThis scoping review aims to identify and map intrapersonal, interpersonal and ecological psychosocial factors associated with NSSI among Iranian adolescents and young adults, summarising quantitative and qualitative evidence and highlighting gaps for future research and intervention planning.Methods and analysisFollowing Arksey and O’Malley’s six-stage framework, with enhancements from the Joanna Briggs Institute guidelines, a comprehensive search was conducted across international (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, PsycINFO, Embase) and national (SID, Magiran, Noormags) databases. Two reviewers will independently conduct screening, data extraction and thematic coding. A third reviewer will resolve any discrepancies. Findings will be synthesised using a directed narrative synthesis combined with integrative thematic analysis. Psychosocial factors will be mapped to an integrated framework combining Nock’s functional model and Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory. Methodological quality will be appraised using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool.Ethics and disseminationThis scoping review will be conducted based on published and publicly available literature; therefore, no individual informed consent is required. Ethical approval has been obtained from the Ethics Committee of Ferdowsi University of Mashhad (Approval code: IR.UM.REC.1404.282). The findings will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal, conference presentations and academic workshops to inform researchers, clinicians and policymakers about the factors associated with NSSI among Iranian adolescents and young adults.

Introduction

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is defined as the deliberate, damage to one’s body without suicidal intent.1 2 Menninger earlier described self-harm as a ‘self-destructive’ act and a ‘gesture against suicide’, suggesting it serves as a strategy to avoid death.3 Although individuals engaging in NSSI do not intend to die, repeated self-injury may increase the risk of future suicidal behaviour4 by reducing fear of death5 6 and increasing pain tolerance.7 The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-5-TR8 recognises NSSI as a distinct diagnostic entity.

Global lifetime prevalence estimates of NSSI are 4.2% in adults, 10.5% in young adults and 15.4% in adolescents.9 Adolescents are the most vulnerable group, with prevalence rising from 17% before the COVID-19 pandemic to 27% during the pandemic.10 In Iran, the prevalence of NSSI among adolescents and young adults is approximately 22%,11 although up-to-date national data are not available.

Several theoretical models have been proposed to explain NSSI. Emotion regulation and experiential avoidance models emphasise negative reinforcement, whereas functional models also highlight positive reinforcement, such as relief from distress or increased positive affect, in the repetition of the behaviour. Cognitive–emotional12 and benefits-and-barriers13 models explain the initiation of NSSI by considering self-efficacy beliefs and perceived initial obstacles. Empirical evidence indicates that emotion regulation plays a central role across all models.14

Risk factors for NSSI are observed at intrapersonal, interpersonal and structural levels. At the intrapersonal level, vulnerabilities include negative affect toward self-criticism, perfectionism, anger, emotion dysregulation and experiences of childhood trauma.1519 At the social and cultural level, the roles of cultural norms, socio-economic status, discrimination, social exclusion, educational systems and peer networks have been emphasised.2022 Meanwhile, structural factors such as gender-based violence disproportionately affect women.23 Iranian studies similarly highlight impulsivity and executive dysfunction,24 negative self-concept and trauma,25 and engagement in risky behaviours.26 We will categorise identified factors according to an adapted ecological model (intrapersonal, interpersonal and ecological/structural levels) to guide the synthesis and presentation of results.

Despite extensive research, interventions for NSSI show limited effectiveness. Systematic reviews suggest that the most effective treatments are those designed based on a deep understanding of the psychological and social risk factors underlying NSSI,27 28 and that individuals’ cultural and bioecological contexts play a key role in the manifestation of this behaviour.23 Previous reviews in Iran have focused only on some individual or social factors,29 and no study in the past 50 years has provided an integrated, multilevel framework.

This scoping review protocol aims to synthesise existing evidence, identify research gaps and provide a theoretical foundation for culturally informed preventive and therapeutic interventions for NSSI among adolescents and young adults in Iran.

Methods

Given the conceptual breadth and heterogeneity of studies on the factors associated with NSSI among Iranian adolescents and young adults, and since the aim is to identify and map the scope and characteristics of existing evidence rather than systematically synthesise and compare results,30 a scoping review methodology was chosen. This approach enables a comprehensive examination of diverse study types and the identification of knowledge gaps, and it provides a foundation for conducting a systematic review in a subsequent phase.31

The current scoping review will be conducted following the six-stage framework of Arksey and O’Malley,30 with consideration of the supplementary recommendations by Levac et al (2010)32 and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidance.33 The main stages of this framework include: (1) defining the research question, (2) identifying relevant studies, (3) selecting eligible studies, (4) charting (extracting) the data, (5) collating, summarising and reporting the results, and (6) (optional) stakeholder consultation.

To define the study scope, develop inclusion and exclusion criteria and design the search strategy, the Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework was used. Stage 2: identifying relevant studies

The search strategy was designed and executed in accordance with the JBI three-step approach.33

Step 1: An initial limited search was conducted in PubMed and Scopus to identify relevant text words from titles and abstracts, as well as controlled vocabulary/index terms (Medical Subject Headings/Emtree) related to NSSI and psychosocial factors. Based on this preliminary search, and following review by two adolescent mental health specialists and the supervisory team, the final set of search terms was refined using the PCC framework.

Step 2: Comprehensive searches will subsequently run in international databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, PsycINFO and Embase) and Iranian national databases (SID, Magiran, Noormags). Searches were completed on 15 October 2025. All retrieved records were exported and imported for screening without restrictions on study design. Boolean operators (AND/OR) and truncation (*) were applied as appropriate to maximise sensitivity and ensure reproducibility. In designing the search, we made a conceptual distinction between NSSI and suicidal behaviours; studies examining both were eligible only if NSSI-specific results were reported separately or could be extracted independently.

Step 3: To enhance completeness and identify evidence not consistently indexed in bibliographic databases, supplementary searching was undertaken with no date restrictions, consistent with the main inclusion criteria. This included searching the same international (Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed) and national (SID, Magiran, Noormags) databases for conference proceedings using document-type filters; searching IranDoc for Persian-language theses and dissertations; manually searching the websites of the Iranian Ministry of Health and Medical Education and the National Organisation for Youth for relevant reports; conducting a comprehensive search in Google Scholar using the same search terms with no restrictions on the number of records screened; backward citation tracking of reference lists from included studies and relevant reviews; and consulting experts in adolescent mental health and NSSI in Iran to identify ongoing or unpublished studies. All documents identified through these methods were subject to the same eligibility screening as database records and will be documented in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram.

An example of the Scopus search strategy is provided in box 1, and full search strategies for all sources are presented in online supplemental file 1.

Stage 3: study selection

The study selection process will be conducted in accordance with the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines30 and the JBI guidance for evidence synthesis.30 All records retrieved from the databases will be imported into EndNote; duplicates will be removed automatically and then checked manually.

Selection will occur in two stages. In the first stage, two reviewers will independently screen titles and abstracts of all identified records against prespecified eligibility criteria. Studies will be retained if they focus on NSSI among Iranian adolescents (aged 12–18 years) or young adults (aged 18–24 years) and examine psychosocial factors. In the second stage, the same two reviewers, using the same eligibility framework, will assess full texts of potentially eligible studies independently. A summary of the inclusion and exclusion criteria is presented in table 2. A pilot of 50 records will be conducted; Cohen’s kappa will be calculated and κ≥0.70 will be considered acceptable. Any disagreements between reviewers will be resolved through discussion and, if necessary, by consulting a third reviewer or subject-matter experts. In addition to database searches, supplementary studies will be identified through manual searching and a snowballing approach (screening reference lists of included studies). The entire study selection process will be documented using a PRISMA flow diagram, showing the numbers of records identified, screened, excluded and included at each stage. This systematic and transparent approach is designed to minimise selection bias and to enhance the rigour and reproducibility of the review.

Stage 4: data charting

Data charting will be conducted using a standardised extraction form,31 33 developed in alignment with the JBI guidance for evidence synthesis and the PRISMA-ScR checklist. The form will be pilot-tested on a small sample of included studies (5–10 studies) to ensure clarity, consistency and comprehensiveness and will be iteratively refined as needed throughout the review process. Two reviewers will independently extract data from all included studies, with any disagreements resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

The data charting form will capture both bibliographic information and detailed content from each study. The domains to be extracted are summarised in table 3. To ensure transparency and reproducibility, the charting process will remain iterative, and the extraction form will be updated if new concepts emerge during analysis. All changes will be recorded and documented. Extracted data will be managed using Microsoft Excel for tabulation, and qualitative findings will, if necessary, be imported into qualitative analysis software to facilitate thematic synthesis. This structured and systematic approach ensures that all relevant data are comprehensively extracted and consistently analysed across included studies. Theoretical integration:To comprehensively examine the interaction of multilevel factors that contributed to NSSI, we will adopt Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems framework as an organising heuristic to classify and map risk and protective factors for NSSI across nested levels of influence (individual, interpersonal, institutional and societal).34 To capture proximate mechanisms of self-injury, we integrate this multilevel framing with Nock’s functional model, which characterises NSSI in terms of intrapersonal and interpersonal functions.2 The combined approach enables identification of which functional processes operate at the individual level and which contextual features at higher ecological levels may precipitate, reinforce or buffer those processes. Importantly—consistent with recent reflections on applications of ecological theory in mental-health research—we use the ecological framework as a conceptual and classificatory tool for organising empirical evidence and designing multilevel interventions, not as a claim of causal proof; establishing causal links would require longitudinal and experimental designs beyond the scope of this protocol.35 A conceptual illustration of how the ecological levels and functional mechanisms may be combined is shown in table 4.

In this scoping review, the integration of quantitative and qualitative evidence will proceed as follows:

For each factor associated with NSSI, quantitative findings—including correlation coefficients, ORs, regression coefficients, sample sizes and direction of association (positive/negative/no effect)—will be extracted and reported descriptively. Meta-analysis will not be conducted; quantitative results will serve solely to illustrate general patterns of association between factors and NSSI.

Subsequently, qualitative findings (themes, researchers’ interpretations and participant quotations) will be analysed to explain how and why these factors influence behaviour and to elucidate lived experiences of adolescents and young adults.

During the integration step, quantitative patterns (association strength/direction) and qualitative insights (mechanistic explanations) will be combined to produce an integrated statement for each factor—that is, a concise explanation of how and under what conditions the factor relates to NSSI.

The strength of evidence for each factor will be rated as strong, moderate or weak based on the number of studies, study design types and coherence of quantitative and qualitative evidence.

To enhance credibility and reduce bias, two independent reviewers will conduct thematic analysis. Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion and consultation with a third reviewer. In addition, findings will be validated in a feedback session with two supervisors specialising in adolescent and youth mental health, and their input will be incorporated into the final synthesis.
