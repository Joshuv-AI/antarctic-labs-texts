Clinical spectrum of Talaromyces marneffei infections in HIV-negative patients: an 18-case series including a rare presentation with gastrointestinal onset, adrenal mass, and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis
Authors: Jiewei Liu, Jianqi Wu, Zheng Zheng, Jianjian Xue, Wei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe diverse manifestations of Talaromyces marneffei (TM) infection in HIV-negative patients complicate and often delay diagnosis. This study reviews 18 cases to better characterize the clinical spectrum of this disease, with the goal of improving recognition of its varied presentations.MethodsRetrospective analysis of 18 patients assessing underlying diseases, manifestations, co-infections, diagnostics, treatment, and outcomes. Diagnosis required TM positivity via culture, histopathology, or metagenomic next-generation sequencing(mNGS).ResultsThis cohort (14 males and 4 females; mean age 57.94 ± 11.12 years) exhibited diverse comorbidities, including renal transplantation (27.8%), tuberculosis (22.2%), cancer (16.7%), bronchiectasis (16.7%), and anti-interferon-γ autoantibody (AIGA) syndrome (11.1%). Common manifestations included respiratory symptoms (72.2%), fever (55.6%), and anemia (50.0%). Immunodeficiency was frequent, with an abnormal CD4/CD8 ratio in in 10/15 patients (66.7%). Notably, 94.4% of cases were initially misdiagnosed—often as tuberculosis (35.3%) or bacterial pneumonia (23.5%)—leading to a mean diagnostic delay of 8.97 ± 7.02 weeks. mNGS provided diagnosis in 77.8% of cases. Overall mortality was 22.2%, with two relapses linked to AIGA syndrome. We report the first documented TM triad in a patient with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) coinfection: concurrent gastrointestinal onset (manifesting as ileocolonic ulcers), adrenal mass, and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH).ConclusionsTM infection in HIV-negative hosts demonstrates broad clinical heterogeneity, often masked by comorbidities and leading to delayed diagnosis. mNGS is critical for early identification. The unprecedented triad of gastrointestinal onset, adrenal mass, and HLH underscores TM’s capacity for atypical dissemination. Introduction

Talaromyces marneffei (TM), a thermally dimorphic fungus endemic to Southeast Asia and Southern China (including Fujian Province), has emerged as a significant opportunistic pathogen in immunocompromised hosts [1]. Our hospital, located in Fuzhou, the capital of Fujian, serves a population within this endemic region, providing a critical vantage point to study the evolving epidemiology of this infection. While historically associated with advanced HIV infection, contemporary epidemiology reveals an expanding spectrum of at-risk populations [1, 2]. The past decade has witnessed a marked increase in HIV-negative TM cases, attributable to diverse immunodeficiency states including: anti-interferon-γ autoantibody (AIGA) syndromes [3], inborn errors of immunity (STAT1/STAT3 mutations) [4], iatrogenic immunosuppression (particularly post-transplantation) [5], and autoimmune diseases managed with biologic therapies [6].

This epidemiological shift presents unique diagnostic challenges. Unlike HIV-associated TM with characteristic disseminated disease, HIV-negative infections exhibit protean manifestations reflecting underlying immune dysfunction [7, 8]. Over 200 documented cases demonstrate site-specific presentations ranging from localized pulmonary nodules to fatal disseminated infection with multiorgan involvement - a clinical heterogeneity that frequently delays diagnosis. Particularly concerning are reports of atypical visceral involvement and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) [9, 10].

We present a single-center analysis of 18 HIV-negative TM cases, systematically characterizing: (a) demographic patterns and comorbidity spectra in this emerging population; (b) clinical-radiological correlates of disease severity; and (c) diagnostic pitfalls through an index case featuring the novel triad of gastrointestinal onset (manifesting as ileocolonic ulcers), adrenal mass, and HLH. Our findings establish critical phenotype-immunotype correlations in HIV-negative TM infection, which have critical implications for improving diagnosis, guiding therapeutic intervention, and ultimately enhancing patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study enrolled 18 HIV-negative patients diagnosed with TM infection at the 900th Hospital between January 2020 and June 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the 900th Hospital of the Joint Logistic Support Force, and written informed consent was obtained from the participants. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki principles.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used due to the small cohort size (n = 18). Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables given small sample size. Categorical variables (e.g., symptoms, diagnostic methods) were reported as counts (%). Normally distributed continuous variables (e.g., age) were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. For comparisons between groups (e.g., male vs. female), independent samples t-tests were applied for normally distributed data. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Incidence rates were not calculated given the limited sample size.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The study comprised 18 patients (male: female ratio 3.5:1) with a mean age of 57.94 ± 11.12 years (range 32–75), demonstrating significant age disparity between genders (male 61.64 ± 7.46 vs. female 45.00 ± 13.11 years, p = 0.004). All patients had significant comorbidities, including solid organ transplant recipients (n = 5, 27.8%), individuals with tuberculosis (TB, n = 4, 22.2%), patients with cancer (n = 3, 16.7%), and those with chronic bronchiectasis (n = 3, 16.7%). The cohort also included patients with anti-interferon-γ autoantibody (AIGA) syndrome (n = 2, 11.1%). Additional comorbidities included one case of SLE. Several patients also had other comorbid conditions—such as renal impairment, diabetes, hypertension, hepatitis C, or pneumothorax—with many exhibiting multiple comorbidities simultaneously.

Diagnostic approaches and misdiagnosis

Microbiological confirmation of TM infection was achieved through multiple diagnostic modalities, with mNGS serving as the primary diagnostic tool (n = 14, 77.8%). Specifically, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) mNGS identified pathogens in 55.6% of cases (n = 10), while blood mNGS (n = 2, 11.1%) and pleural effusion mNGS (n = 2, 11.1%) provided additional diagnostic yield. In comparison, conventional methods—including BALF culture (n = 1, 5.6%), urine culture (n = 1, 5.6%), sputum culture (n = 1, 5.6%), and histopathological examination of lung nodules (n = 1, 5.6%)—collectively contributed to 22.2% of diagnoses.

Notably, 94.4% of the cohort (17/18) experienced initial misdiagnosis or underrecognition. TB was the most common erroneous diagnosis (35.3%, 6/17), followed by community-acquired pneumonia (CAP, 23.5%, 4/17) and influenza virus infection (11.8%, 2/17). Other misdiagnoses included malignancies (11.8%, 2/17), musculoskeletal disorders such as gouty arthritis and rheumatoid arthritis (11.8%, 2/17), gastrointestinal disorders (11.8%, 2/17), and bacterial urinary tract infections (5.9%, 1/17). The aggregate percentage exceeds 100% due to multiple sequential misdiagnoses in some patients. These diagnostic errors and delays led to a substantial interval from symptom onset to definitive diagnosis, with a mean of 8.97 ± 7.02 weeks (range: 1–21 weeks), underscoring the ongoing challenges in timely recognition of TM infection in immunocompromised hosts.

Clinical manifestations and co-infections

The cohort exhibited a spectrum of clinical presentations, predominantly respiratory symptoms (n = 13, 72.2%) including productive cough and sore throat, followed by systemic manifestations such as fever (n = 10, 55.6%) and hematologic abnormalities (anemia n = 9, 50.0%). Musculoskeletal involvement (arthralgia n = 6, 33.3%) and lymphadenopathy (n = 4, 22.2%) were notable extrapulmonary features. Less common but significant findings included cutaneous lesions (n = 3, 16.7%), gastrointestinal symptoms (n = 2, 11.1%), and life-threatening hemoptysis (n = 2, 11.1%), and lower urinary tract symptoms (frequency and urgency, n = 1, 5.6%). One case (5.6%) progressed to HLH.

Concurrent infections were prevalent, with fungal coinfection by Aspergillus species (n = 4, 22.2%,) and mycobacterial infections (n = 5, 27.8%), combined tuberculosis(n = 3) and nontuberculous༈n = 2༉ mycobacteria being most clinically significant. Viral coinfections included Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), Cytomegalovirus (CMV), and varicella-zoster virus (VZV). It is important to note that for these herpesviruses, the detection of genomic material by mNGS may indicate latent infection and does not necessarily confirm active disease. Bacterial superinfections featured Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pneumoniae.

Laboratory and immunological findings

Laboratory findings on admission revealed systemic inflammatory activation, with elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) in 13/18 cases (72.2%) and increased procalcitonin (PCT) in 9/18 cases (50.0%). Cellular immunodeficiency was also observed, characterized by reduced CD3 T cell counts in 6/15 patients (40.0%), CD4 T cell depletion in 7/15 patients (46.7%), and CD8 T cell depletion in 7/15 patients (46.7%). An abnormal CD4/CD8 ratio was noted in 10/15 patients (66.7%). Organ dysfunction was frequently present, including anemia (9/18, 50.0%), renal impairment (6/18, 33.3%), and hepatic injury (2/18, 11.1%). Fungal biomarkers were positive in a notable proportion of cases: galactomannan (GM-test) was detected in 9/16 patients (56.25%), and β-D-glucan (G-test) was positive in 10/18 (55.6%) (Table 2). Furthermore, peripheral blood smear review identified neutrophil phagocytosis of fungal elements in one patient (P16, 5.6%), with morphological features consistent with TM (Fig. 1A).

Histopathological examination of seven biopsies revealed diverse tissue involvement. Three pulmonary samples predominantly showed fungal microabscesses containing characteristic yeast-like cell clusters. Definitive identification of TM was based on its characteristic morphological features observed on PASM staining: the yeast forms were predominantly oval to sausage-shaped, exhibited distinct transverse septa (fission), and lacked evidence of budding [1, 13]. These features are crucial for differentiating TM from other endemic dimorphic fungi, such as Histoplasma capsulatum, which is typically smaller, rounder, and reproduces by narrow-based budding without the formation of transverse septa [14]. Two lymph node biopsies revealed non-specific diffuse inflammatory infiltrates without definitive fungal elements. Histologically confirmed TM aggregates were identified in colonic ulcer biopsies from 1 of 2 patients with gastrointestinal involvement (50.0%)(Figs. 1D and 3A-D). In one patient (5.6%), bone marrow examination showed evidence of both hemophagocytosis, suggestive of HLH (Fig. 1F), and phagocytosed fungal elements (Fig. 1G).

Treatment outcomes and follow-up

All enrolled patients underwent systemic antifungal therapy, with initial treatment regimens comprising amphotericin B (n = 12, 66.7%), voriconazole (n = 4, 22.2%), or posaconazole (n = 2, 11.1%). Supplemental antimicrobial agents were administered based on identified coinfections. Following induction therapy, patients were maintained on oral voriconazole or itraconazole for a median of 14 weeks (range 12–16 weeks).

Therapeutic outcomes demonstrated an 88.9% (16/18) initial clinical success rate with complete recovery, while 11.1% (P2, P3) succumbed to acute respiratory failure during hospitalization. During the follow-up, two cases (11.1%) with confirmed AIGA experienced disease recurrence, both achieving sustained remission after range 14-week secondary antifungal therapy. Late mortality was documented in 2 patients (11.1%), including one lung cancer progression, and one COVID−19-associated mortality.

Case report: Disseminated TM infection complicating undiagnosed SLE

A 32-year-old female with no prior medical history presented to our institution on March 29, 2024, with persistent vomiting, abdominal pain, and high-grade fever (39.4 °C) that had been diagnosed as gastroenteritis for one month. The initial evaluation revealed hemodynamic instability (tachycardia 122 bpm, tachypnea 27/min, hypotension 100/83 mmHg). The abdomen was soft and distended, with diffuse abdominal tenderness and no rebound tenderness. She also exhibited spontaneous bleeding manifestations, including epistaxis and cutaneous ecchymoses distributed across the upper extremities and lumbosacral region(Fig. 3K, L).

Microbiological diagnosis

The diagnostic trajectory was accelerated by a critical blood smear finding: neutrophils actively phagocytizing yeast-like organisms morphologically consistent with TM (Fig. 1A). This rare phenomenon provided immediate presumptive evidence of disseminated infection, prompting expedited mNGS testing that confirmed TM within 48 h—contrasting with the cohort’s 8.97 weeks mean diagnostic delay. The mNGS demonstrated unequivocal evidence of TM infection with 67,822 reads achieving 11.56% genome coverage at 1.083X depth - significantly exceeding the > 5% threshold typically used to confirm disseminated infections(Fig. 3O). This molecular evidence was corroborated five days later by traditional blood culture isolation, completing the diagnostic triad(Fig. 1B, C). Concurrently, mNGS detected EBV at low coverage (13 reads, 0.38%) (Fig. 3P), with subsequent quantification revealing 1.79 × 10⁴ copies/mL. This finding was interpreted as likely reactivation given the low genomic coverage but detectable viral load. Other infections were excluded through comprehensive testing (negative HIV/syphilis serology, undetectable CMV, negative TB workup).

Immunological and laboratory findings

Supportive mycological evidence included strongly positive fungal biomarkers (β-D-glucan >600.0 pg/mL and galactomannan 4.336 S/CO). Serial hematologic monitoring demonstrated dynamic changes in complete blood counts and coagulation parameters throughout hospitalization (detailed in Table 3), with initial findings meeting criteria for disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) (ISTH-SSC score 8) characterized by severe coagulopathy [15].

This fulfill the 2019 EULAR/ACR classification criteria (score ≥ 10) [12]. Further evaluation showed elevated proinflammatory cytokines including IL-6 (84.64 pg/mL), IL-8 (76.20 pg/mL), IL-10 (27.68 pg/mL), and TNF-α (46.83 pg/mL). Serological testing yielded negative results for AIGA, antiphospholipid antibodies (anticardiolipin and anti-β2 glycoprotein I). All tumor markers (CEA, AFP, CA125 series) were unremarkable. Endocrine evaluation revealed normal aldosterone and ACTH levels. Collectively, the presence of prolonged high-grade fever, splenic lesions, bi-cytopenia, hyperferritinemia, coagulopathy indicative of hypofibrinogenemia, and the definitive finding of hemophagocytosis on bone marrow aspirate satisfied the diagnostic criteria for secondary HLH [16].

Radiologic and pathologic features

Imaging investigations demonstrated multi-organ involvement, characterized by a 4.8 cm × 3.2 cm mass in the left adrenal gland, splenic lesions(Fig. 3I, J), and intestinal ulcers—with the latter confirmed by endoscopy in the terminal ileum and colorectum(Fig. 3A-D). Histopathological examination of intestinal biopsies demonstrated characteristic PASM-positive yeast forms within foamy macrophages, which were consistent with the infectious lesions of TM(Fig. 1D, E). Additionally, bone marrow aspiration revealed histiocytes exhibiting active hemophagocytosis (Fig. 1F), a pathological hallmark of the HLH.

Therapeutic intervention and outcome

The patient was promptly initiated on a treatment regimen that included intravenous Amphotericin B Cholesterol Sulfate Complex for Injection (3.5 mg/kg/day), coupled with intensive supportive measures such as platelet transfusions (seven times, totaling 1400 ml), suspended red blood cells (twice, totaling 600 ml), and fresh frozen plasma (twice, totaling 770 ml) to improve anemia and manage DIC/coagulopathy. Considering the clinical suspicion of SLE and secondary HLH, immunomodulatory therapy with intravenous immunoglobulin (200 mg/kg/day for three consecutive days), low-dose dexamethasone (5 mg daily), and hydroxychloroquine sulfate (0.2 g twice daily) was concurrently administered. Additionally, supportive treatments included liver protection, gastroprotection, and maintaining electrolyte balance.

The clinical course was characterized by progressive improvement. Defervescence achieved within five days by April 2. Hematologic recovery became evident from April 11 onward, manifesting as a steady rise in platelet counts from 18 to 40 × 10⁹/L and hemoglobin levels from 58 to 76 g/L, although these values remained below normal ranges at the time of discharge on April 23. Subsequent outpatient monitoring confirmed complete normalization of peripheral blood parameters (detailed in Table 3). On April 23, the antifungal regimen was transitioned to oral voriconazole for a planned 12-week consolidation therapy, which was well tolerated.

Follow-up evaluations through June 2024 demonstrated significant radiologic improvement, with the adrenal mass reduced from an initial 4.8 × 3.2 cm to 1.0 × 0.9 cm on contrast-enhanced MRI (Fig. 3M, N). Concurrently, the patient’s multiple ulcers in the terminal ileum and colorectum showed marked resolution, with follow-up endoscopy revealing complete ulcer healing(Fig. 3E-H). This clinical progress occurred alongside sustained microbiologic remission (TM and EVB DNA negative through mNGS). The patient continues rheumatologic surveillance for underlying SLE without evidence of fungal recurrence one year after antifungal discontinuation.

Discussion

Epidemiological trends and diagnostic challenges

TM, once predominantly associated with HIV/AIDS, has exhibited shifting epidemiological patterns. With advances in HIV management, the incidence of TM in HIV-positive populations has declined, while HIV-negative cases are increasingly reported [17–20]. However, early diagnosis of TM in HIV-negative patients remains elusive due to nonspecific presentations and pervasive misdiagnosis. Our study uncovers an alarming 94.4% initial misdiagnosis rate—predominantly as TB—with erroneous diagnoses spanning respiratory infections (CAP/influenza), autoimmune disorders, malignancies, and gastrointestinal conditions. This diagnostic chaos culminated in sequential misdiagnoses (aggregate rate >100%) and a 8.97 weeks diagnostic odyssey, driven by a cascade of failures: the overreliance on nonspecific inflammatory markers (CRP/PCT, elevated in 72.2% and 50.0% of cases, respectively) which prompted initial bacterial/viral misclassification; progression through futile therapies for these misdiagnosed conditions, potentially exacerbated by the high prevalence of cellular immunodeficiency (e.g., abnormal CD4/CD8 ratio in 66.7%); and the critically delayed consideration of fungal pathogens in non-HIV hosts, despite the high positivity rates of fungal biomarkers (GM-test 56.2%, G-test 55.6%).

Clinical and imaging heterogeneity

HIV-negative TM patients typically present with fever, cough, anemia, hepatosplenomegaly, and arthralgia, consistent with prior reports [17]. However, our cohort also included rare manifestations such as abdominal pain and distension, urinary symptoms, and secondary HLH, complicating early diagnosis. Notably, we identified one case through imaging demonstrating a rare adrenal mass - the first such radiologically documented presentation in TM infection, which significantly expands the spectrum of possible imaging findings. Imaging features further contribute to diagnostic ambiguity. While some studies suggest that severe chest CT findings (e.g., patchy lesions, halo signs, the air crescent sign) are more common in HIV-negative patients [8], others report no significant differences between HIV-positive and negative groups [21, 22]. This variability, further complicated by newly recognized manifestations such as adrenal mass, underscores the necessity for nuanced interpretation of imaging findings in suspected TM infections.

Case report: Gastrointestinal-onset TM with adrenal mass and HLH in a SLE

We describe a diagnostically challenging case of HIV-negative disseminated TM infection with concurrent gastrointestinal, adrenal, and hematologic involvement - a unique triad not previously reported in literature. Notably, this occurred in a patient with underlying SLE, highlighting the critical role of host immunity in atypical TM manifestations. This case provides three critical insights:

Gastrointestinal TM as a diagnostic mimicker

Although TM-induced enterocolitis is reported in 5.9–12.6% of HIV-negative cases, our patient’s clinicopathological profile reveals critical diagnostic insights [6, 9]. Endoscopic examination demonstrated multiple well-circumscribed ulcers exclusively localized in the terminal ileum and colorectum—distinct from gastric involvement—with histopathology showing PASM-positive foamy histiocytes within ulcer beds. This topographic predilection for the lower gastrointestinal tract suggests potential microbial tropism, though further studies are needed to elucidate colonization mechanisms [23]. Crucially, these findings underscore that in immunosuppressed patients with unexplained gastrointestinal symptoms, suspicion for TM should prompt prioritized ileocolonoscopy with targeted biopsies over upper endoscopic approaches, as ileocolonic lesions may represent primary diagnostic targets.

Adrenal involvement: Radiologic-pathologic correlation

Despite a 4.8 × 3.2 cm adrenal mass (Fig. 2I, J), endocrine function remained normal. While biopsy was contraindicated due to thrombocytopenia (PLT < 20 × 10⁹/L), near-complete resolution (1.0 × 0.9 cm) post-antifungal therapy (Fig. 2M, N) confirmed its infectious nature, representing the first reported HIV-negative adrenal TM lesion. Notably, this contrasts with a 2025 lymphoma/TM co-infection case where adrenal masses enlarged during antifungal treatment, suggesting concurrent lymphoma infiltration [24]. These divergent responses highlight the need to consider both infectious and neoplastic etiologies for adrenal masses in immunocompromised hosts, with treatment response serving as a potential diagnostic indicator.

HLH pathogenesis in coinfection contexts

This SLE patient’s secondary HLH was precipitated by a high-risk triad of triggers: underlying SLE, combined with disseminated TM and active EBV infection. Although mNGS detection alone cannot definitively prove active viral replication, the presence of EBV DNA was considered clinically significant in the context of profound immunosuppression and systemic inflammation. This convergence aligns with established epidemiology where infections account for 49.9% of secondary HLH triggers, with autoimmune diseases constituting 12.1%. Critically, fungal-triggered HLH carries a 73.7% mortality rate, while EBV-associated HLH reaches 79.3% [25]. The co-occurrence of SLE, TM (fungal), and EBV—three independently lethal factors—explains the fulminant HLH presentation observed.

Management and mechanistic implications

Combined antifungal therapy (liposomal amphotericin B cholesterol sulfate complex for injection→ voriconazole) resolved mucocutaneous ulcers and reduced the adrenal mass, while HLH was controlled with transfusion intravenous immunoglobulin, low-dose dexamethasone, and hydroxychloroquine, achieving hematologic remission by day 12.

Disseminated TM in this SLE host arose from a triple-tiered immunodeficiency: complement collapse (C3 < 0.42 g/L, C4 < 0.07 g/L) impaired opsonophagocytosis [26], allowing tissue invasion, while IL−10-dominated suppression (IL−10 >25 pg/mL) hindered Th1/Th17-dependent macrophage fungicidal activity [27]. Concurrently, splenic hypofunction due to LA-associated microthrombosis (DRVVT ratio >1.2, splenic lesions) compromised blood filtration [28]. Critically, EBV co-infection amplified these cascades, creating an immunopermissive niche for TM proliferation.

The intestinal-adrenal-bone marrow dissemination reflects macrophage-facilitated trafficking, where intracellular yeasts sequentially colonize immunocompromised niches [29]. Following alveolar adhesionn and phagocytosis, impaired immunity permits phagosomal conidia-to-yeast conversion [30]. Yeasts escape into the cytoplasm, hijacking macrophages for systemic spread [31, 32]. This enables targeting of immune-privileged sites: gut-associated lymphoid tissue (entry), adrenal glands (steroid-rich), and bone marrow (myeloid reservoir), culminating in disseminated disease.

Risk factors and relapse mechanisms

Underlying conditions in our cohort included post-kidney transplantation (27.8%), TB (22.2%), malignancies (16.7%) and chronic bronchiectasis (16.7%). Other comorbidities (e.g., diabetes mellitus, hypertension, pneumothorax) were also observed but at lower frequencies. The high prevalence of transplant recipients aligns with our center’s extensive kidney transplant volume, where chronic immunosuppression creates a permissive environment for TM infection.

Both AIGA-positive patients (100%, 2/2) relapsed, whereas no recurrence occurred in others (0/16). This clinical pattern biologically mirrors murine models establishing IFN-γ’s critical role in macrophage-mediated TM clearance [33], explaining the refractory infections observed despite appropriate antifungal therapy. Both AIGA-positive patients developed co-infections with prolonged disease courses, consistent with literature documenting worse outcomes in this subgroup [34, 35]. While currently maintaining remission at last follow-up (median 20 months), their persistent immune dysfunction suggests high relapse risk, supporting emerging protocols that combine antifungals with targeted immunomodulation to address the root immune defect rather than merely control fungal proliferation [36–38].

Diagnostic advancements and clinical outcomes

While TM culture remains the gold standard, its slow turnaround (≥ 6 days) impedes timely intervention. In our cohort, the use of mNGS was associated with a reduced time-to-diagnosis to 2–3 days. However, this technical potential was often unrealized due to systemic diagnostic failures detailed in Section. “Epidemiological trends and diagnostic challenges”. Specifically, the 94.4% pre-mNGS misdiagnosis rate may reflect critical gaps in early suspicion of TM.

Thus, while mNGS demonstrates unparalleled rapidity at the confirmation stage, its clinical impact is contingent upon earlier deployment. We posit that a paradigm shift grounded in standard clinical timelines: for immunocompromised patients in endemic regions with persistent fever showing no response to empirical therapy-at the conventional 72-hour reassessment point, immediate mNGS testing (prioritizing BALF or blood) should supersede conventional diagnostics to truncate the diagnostic odyssey(Fig. 4). Further prospective studies are required for validation.

The observed mortality rate of 22.2% (with 2 in-hospital deaths and 2 during follow-up) is lower than the reported outcomes for HIV-negative TM infections (27.7–29.4%) [6, 9]. This risk likely reflects both diagnostic delays due to nonspecific presentations and the compounding impact of underlying immune dysfunction. These findings underscore the importance of rapid molecular diagnostics (mNGS) in high-risk patients, and aggressive immunomodulatory management when TM infection coexists with autoimmune or hematologic disorders.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, the modest cohort size (n = 18) may limit extrapolation of findings to broader populations. Second, exclusive focus on HIV-negative individuals precludes comparative assessment of diagnostic dynamics in HIV-positive patients—a key subgroup historically associated with TM infection. Third, partial unavailability of laboratory parameters may compromise analytical depth. Finally, the application and timing of mNGS were not standardized across all patients, being subject to clinician discretion, which could introduce selection bias. Despite these constraints, our findings expose critical systemic delays in TM diagnosis, demanding urgent intervention.

Conclusion

This study highlights the expanding clinical spectrum of HIV-negative TM infections, which can present with novel phenotypes beyond the classic disseminated disease, including gastrointestinal-onset manifestations, adrenal mass, and HLH. Our findings identify AIGA positivity and underlying immunosuppression as key drivers of mortality and relapse. To address the critical diagnostic delays uncovered here, we propose an integrated diagnostic algorithm that combines risk fators, targeted imaging, and the strategic use of rapid molecular testing like mNGS. Ultimately, improving outcomes for this serious infection will depend not only on technological advancements but also on heightened clinical awareness of its variable presentations to reduce misdiagnosis. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate our proposed diagnostic approach and elucidate the underlying mechanisms of these novel disease manifestations.
