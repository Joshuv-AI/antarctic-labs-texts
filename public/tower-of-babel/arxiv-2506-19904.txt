IBIS : Inverse BInomial sum Solver
Paul A.J.W. van Hoegaerden, Coenraad B. Marinissen, Wouter J. Waalewijn

Abstract
In higher-loop calculations, Mellin-Barnes representations are used to simplify the denominators encountered in Feynman parameter integrals. The contour integral of these representations yield sums over residues. We extend the classes of such sums that can be calculated, to include those involving inverse binomials. Our results are expressed in terms of so-called S S -sums, where the dependence on the upper limit of the sum is analytic. This is accomplished by deriving several new recursion relations, obtained from telescoping series and repeated synchronization. We make our results available through IBIS (“Inverse BInomial sum Solver”), a Form program to perform such inverse binomial sums. To illustrate the efficiency of our code: sums up to weight 6 can be carried out in less than a second. We show in an example how inverse binomial sums arise, though in many instances these are nested with binomial sums, beyond the cases studied here. Our work thus provides a starting point for studying such sums, that will open up new avenues in higher-loop calculations.

Definitions and properties of nested sums

As explained in the introduction,

Definition of
S
-sums and
Z
-sums

We define

-sums as follows
Moch:2001zr

S(n;p_{1},\dots,p_{k};x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\sum_{n\geq i_{1}\geq i_{2}\geq\dots\geq i_{k}\geq 1}\frac{(\text{sign}(p_{1}))^{i_{1}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}}{i_{1}^{\lvert p_{1}\rvert}}\dots\frac{(\text{sign}(p_{k}))^{i_{k}}x_{k}^{i_{k}}}{i_{k}^{\lvert p_{k}\rvert}}\,,

(4)

where

\displaystyle\text{sign}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\quad x\geq 0\,,\\
-1&\text{if}\quad x<0\,.\end{cases}

(5)

In a similar way,
Z
-sums can be defined by

Z(n;p_{1},\dots,p_{k};x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\sum_{n\geq i_{1}>i_{2}>\dots>i_{k}>0}\frac{(\text{sign}(p_{1}))^{i_{1}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}}{i_{1}^{\lvert p_{1}\rvert}}\dots\frac{(\text{sign}(p_{k}))^{i_{k}}x_{k}^{i_{k}}}{i_{k}^{\lvert p_{k}\rvert}}\,.

(6)

For both

-sums and

-sums we refer to

as the

and

as the
indices
of the sums.

Relation between
S
-sums and
Z
-sums

S
-sums are closely related to
Z
-sums, the difference being the upper summation boundary for the nested sums:

for

-sums,

for

An

-sums, by recursively using

\displaystyle S(n;p_{1},

\displaystyle\dots,p_{k};x_{1},\dots,x_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n}\frac{\text{sign}(p_{1})^{i_{1}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}}{i_{1}^{\lvert p_{1}\rvert}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{i_{1}-1}\frac{\text{sign}(p_{2})^{i_{2}}x_{2}^{i_{2}}}{i_{2}^{\lvert p_{2}\rvert}}S(i_{2};p_{3},\dots,p_{k};x_{3},\dots,x_{k})

\displaystyle\quad+S(n;\text{sign}(p_{1}\cdot p_{2})(\lvert p_{1}\rvert+\lvert p_{2}\rvert),p_{3},\dots,p_{k};x_{1}\cdot x_{2},x_{3},\dots,x_{k})\,.

Similarly, one can convert a

\displaystyle Z(n;p_{1},

\displaystyle\dots,p_{k};x_{1},\dots,x_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n}\frac{\text{sign}(p_{1})^{i_{1}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}}{i_{1}^{\lvert p_{1}\rvert}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{i_{1}}\frac{\text{sign}(p_{2})^{i_{2}}x_{2}^{i_{2}}}{i_{2}^{\lvert p_{2}\rvert}}Z(i_{2}-1;p_{3},\dots,p_{k};x_{3},\dots,x_{k})

Reducing products of

Furthermore,

-sums and

\displaystyle S(n;p_{1},\dots,p_{k};x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\cdot S(n;p_{1}^{\prime},\dots,p_{l}^{\prime};x_{1}^{\prime},\dots,x_{l}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n}\frac{\text{sign}(p_{1})^{i_{1}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}}{i_{1}^{\lvert p_{1}\rvert}}S(i_{1};p_{2},\dots,p_{k};x_{2},\dots,x_{k})S(i_{1};p_{1}^{\prime},\dots,p_{l}^{\prime};x_{1}^{\prime},\dots,x_{l}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n}\frac{\text{sign}(p_{1}^{\prime})^{i_{2}}x_{1}^{\prime i_{2}}}{i_{2}^{\lvert p_{1}^{\prime}\rvert}}S(i_{2};p_{1},\dots,p_{k};x_{1},\dots,x_{k})S(i_{2};p_{2}^{\prime},\dots,p_{l}^{\prime};x_{2}^{\prime},\dots,x_{l}^{\prime})

Weights

To perform checks on our results, we define a

The following notation will be used here,

\displaystyle G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,n)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{j!(n-j)!}{n!}\frac{1}{j^{k}}S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(n-j)\,,

\displaystyle G_{q_{1},...,q_{r}}(k,n)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{j!(n-j)!}{n!}\frac{1}{j^{k}}S_{q_{1},...,q_{r}}(j)\,,

(24)

where the initial exponent and the upper limit are the arguments of the function
G
.

3.1
Telescoping

The main concept that is used to derive the recursions is telescoping
Schneider:2013
.
Concretely, to find a recurrence relation for
G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,n)
, we set up the telescoping series as follows:

G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,n)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i)-G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i-1)\Bigr)\,,

(25)

where subsequent terms cancel in the sum on
i
, leaving only the left-hand side and
G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,0)
.
Since
G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,0)
is an empty sum and thus equal to zero, the equivalence becomes evident. We set up this telescoping series with respect to
n
, because the whole point of our work is to solve for symbolic
n
rather than restricting to specific numerical values. To proceed with this telescoping series, we need to derive an expression for
G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i)-G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i-1)
where either the number of indices or the initial exponent of the inverse binomial sum has been reduced, which then yields a recursive relation that reduces the complexity of the inverse binomial sum.

To find an expression for
G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i)-G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i-1)
of which all the terms have a lower complexity than
G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,n)
, we can rewrite the inverse binomial factor as follows:

\frac{1}{\binom{i}{j}}=\frac{j!(i-j-1)!}{(i-1)!}\biggl(1-\frac{j}{i}\biggr)=\frac{1}{\binom{i-1}{j}}\bigg(1-\frac{j}{i}\bigg)\,.

(26)

By doing so, we are able to rewrite the inverse binomial sum with
i
as its upper limit into the same inverse binomial sum, but now with
i-1
as its upper limit. For our example in eq. (
25
), this yields

\displaystyle G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{1}{\binom{i}{j}}\,\frac{S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(i-j)}{j^{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{1}{\binom{i-1}{j}}\bigg(1-\frac{j}{i}\bigg)\frac{1}{j^{k}}S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(i-j)\,.

(27)

However, this does not yet give a proper recursion as the arguments of
S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(i-j)
and
\frac{1}{\binom{i-1}{j}}
are not aligned:
S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(i-j)
contains
i
, while the binomial term contains
i-1
. As a result, we can’t directly express the right-hand side in terms of the
G
functions. We will discuss in sec.
3.2
how to remedy this by using
synchronization
.

We now comment on the reason for choosing to work with
S
-sums instead of
Z
-sums. The upper bound
n
of the sum on
i
in eq. (
25
) is equal to the argument
n
on the left-hand side, which naturally results in
S
-sums. A recursion that would result in
Z
-sums would use the following telescoping series instead:

G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,n)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\Bigl(G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i+1)-G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i)\Bigr)\,,

(28)

However, we found early on by studying some examples that using
S
-sums yielded shorter expressions for the recursions than with
Z
-sums.

3.2
Synchronization of the telescope

When the summand contains multiple terms, a common challenge is that their arguments may differ in such a way that we are not able to directly write the sum in terms of expressions that we know (i.e. the
G
functions). Here we discuss how to address this using synchronization, illustrating this first for a toy example, then a realistic example used in
IBIS
, followed by a discussion of the subtleties that appear in other cases.

Toy example

We start by looking at a simple example to show how a slight change in one of the terms in the summands can mean that the nested sums cannot be integrated in the
S
-sum framework:

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\frac{(\text{sign}(p_{1}))^{i_{1}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}}{i_{1}^{|p_{1}|}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{i_{1}}\frac{(\text{sign}(p_{2}))^{i_{2}+1}x_{2}^{i_{2}-2}}{i_{2}^{|p_{2}|}}\sum_{i_{3}=1}^{i_{2}}\frac{(\text{sign}(p_{3}))^{i_{3}}x_{3}^{i_{3}}}{i_{3}^{|p_{3}|}}S(i_{3};p_{4},p_{5};x_{4},x_{5})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n}\frac{(\text{sign}(p_{1}))^{i_{1}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}}{i_{1}^{|p_{1}|}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{i_{1}}\frac{(\text{sign}(p_{2}))^{i_{2}+1}x_{2}^{i_{2}-2}}{i_{2}^{|p_{2}|}}S(i_{2};p_{3},p_{4},p_{5};x_{3},x_{4},x_{5})\,.

(29)

Due to the exponents of
\text{sign}(p_{2})
and
x_{2}
in the numerator of the second sum not matching up with the summation variable
i_{2}
, only the third sum (over
i_{3}
) can directly be expressed as an
S
-sum. In the current form, the rest of the sums (over
i_{2}
and
i_{1}
) would have to be performed explicitly by
Form
using
Summer
. To avoid this, it is important to adjust the arguments of the objects in the summand so that they match, which is called synchronization.
In this example this can simply be achieved by writing eq. (
3.2
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\text{sign}(p_{2})}{x_{2}^{2}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n}\frac{(\text{sign}(p_{1}))^{i_{1}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}}{i_{1}^{|p_{1}|}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{i_{1}}\frac{(\text{sign}(p_{2}))^{i_{2}}x_{2}^{i_{2}}}{i_{2}^{|p_{2}|}}S(i_{2};p_{3},p_{4},p_{5};x_{3},x_{4},x_{5})

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{sign}(p_{2})}{x_{2}^{2}}S(n;p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},p_{4},p_{5};x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5})\,.

(30)

Realistic example

To see how we apply this concept of synchronization to find the recursions for
IBIS
, let us show how the terms for our example in eq. (
27
) can be synchronized. Suppose that
k>1
and
p_{1}\geq 0
, we get

\displaystyle G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{1}{\binom{i-1}{j}}\frac{1}{j^{k}}\bigg(S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(i-j-1)+\frac{S_{p_{2},...,p_{s}}(i-j)}{(i-j)^{p_{1}}}\bigg)

(31)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{j!(i-j-1)!}{i!}\frac{S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(i-j)}{j^{k-1}}

\displaystyle=G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i-1)+\frac{(i-1)!0!}{(i-1)!}\frac{1}{(i-1)^{k}}S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(0)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+i\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{j!(i-j)!}{i!}\frac{1}{j^{k}}\frac{S_{p_{2},...,p_{s}}(i-j)}{(i-j)^{p_{1}+1}}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{j!(i-j)!}{i!}\frac{S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(i-j)}{j^{k-1}(i-j)}\,,

where we used eq. (
11
) in the first step of this derivation and that
S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(0)=0
for all
p_{1},...,p_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}
. As shown, we have successfully synchronized the arguments of the binomial and
S
-sum, allowing us to identify
G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i-1)
along with other terms that still require synchronization.

Since we restrict inverse binomial sums to denominators of the form
\frac{1}{j^{k}}
rather than
\frac{1}{j^{k}(i-j)^{k^{\prime}}}
, the next step is to rewrite the remaining terms in eq. (
31
) in terms of
G
functions. Using the following general expression to split the fractions,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(i+x)^{m}(i+y)^{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{m}\binom{m+k-1-a}{k-1}\frac{(-1)^{m-a}}{(y-x)^{m+k-a}(i+x)^{a}}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\sum_{a=1}^{k}\binom{m+k-1-a}{m-1}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{(y-x)^{m+k-a}(i+y)^{a}}\,,

(32)

we obtain

\displaystyle G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i)

\displaystyle=G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i-1)+\sum_{a=1}^{k}\binom{k+p_{1}-a}{p_{1}}\frac{1}{i^{k+p_{1}-a}}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{j!(i-j)!}{i!}\frac{S_{p_{2},...,p_{s}}(i-j)}{j^{a}}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\sum_{a=1}^{p_{1}+1}\binom{k+p_{1}-a}{k-1}\frac{1}{i^{k+p_{1}-a}}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{j!(i-j)!}{i!}\frac{S_{p_{2},...,p_{s}}(i-j)}{(i-j)^{a}}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{i^{k-a}}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{j!(i-j)!}{i!}\frac{S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(i-j)}{j^{a}}-\frac{1}{i^{k-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{j!(i-j)!}{i!}\frac{S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(i-j)}{(i-j)}

\displaystyle=G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i-1)+\sum_{a=1}^{k}\binom{k+p_{1}-a}{p_{1}}\frac{1}{i^{k+p_{1}-a}}G^{p_{2},...,p_{s}}(a,i)-\frac{1}{i^{k-1}}G_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(1,i)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\sum_{a=1}^{p_{1}+1}\binom{k+p_{1}-a}{k-1}\frac{1}{i^{k+p_{1}-a}}G_{p_{2},...,p_{s}}(a,i)-\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{i^{k-a}}G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(a,i)\,.

(33)

In the last line, we performed a change of the summation variable, substituting
j\to i-j
for the third and fifth term on the right-hand side. This transformation caused the indices of the inverse binomial sums to shift from lower to upper indices. By synchronizing all elements in the summands, we rewrote the expressions in terms of recognizable components (i.e. inverse binomial sums like in eq. (
3
)). This approach minimizes the number of sums that need to be evaluated.

The final step involves setting up the telescoping series:

\displaystyle G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i)-G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i-1)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{k}\binom{k+p_{1}-a}{p_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{i^{k+p_{1}-a}}G^{p_{2},...,p_{s}}(a,i)-\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{i^{k-a}}G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(a,i)

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76228pt+\sum_{a=1}^{p_{1}+1}\binom{k+p_{1}-a}{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{i^{k+p_{1}-a}}G_{p_{2},...,p_{s}}(a,i)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{i^{k-1}}G_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(1,i)\,.

(34)

By establishing this telescoping series, we have derived a recursion expressed in terms of recognizable components with either upper or lower indices. The inverse binomial sums on the right-hand side have a lower complexity, meaning that they have either fewer indices or a reduced initial exponent, guaranteeing that the recursion will terminate.

Subtleties

We have carried out this process of setting up the telescoping series after finding an expression for the summand for every different type of inverse binomial sum listed in tab.
1
. The following subtleties are worth mentioning: First of all, during the synchronization process of these recursions, we encountered terms where the upper limit of an inverse binomial sum was not synchronized with its denominator. To synchronize these two elements, a different technique was required, which
is illustrated here for the following term:

\displaystyle\frac{n}{2^{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{2^{i}}{i}G_{p_{2},...,p_{s}}(p_{1},i-1)\,.

(35)

To synchronize the inverse binomial sum with the denominator, we need to find an expression for
G_{p_{2},...,p_{s}}(p_{1},i-1)
in terms of inverse binomial sums with
i
as the upper limit. While constructing the telescoping series, we derived an expression for
G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i)-G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i-1)
in terms of inverse binomial sums with
i
as the upper limit. By rewriting this expression, we can express
G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i-1)
in terms of inverse binomial sums with
i
as its upper limit, as desired. Thus, let us rewrite eq. (
33
) as follows:

\displaystyle G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i-1)

\displaystyle=G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,i)-\sum_{a=1}^{k}\binom{k+p_{1}-a}{p_{1}}\frac{1}{i^{k+p_{1}-a}}G^{p_{2},...,p_{s}}(a,i)+\frac{1}{i^{k-1}}G_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(1,i)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\sum_{a=1}^{p_{1}+1}\binom{k+p_{1}-a}{k-1}\frac{1}{i^{k+p_{1}-a}}G_{p_{2},...,p_{s}}(a,i)+\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{i^{k-a}}G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(a,i)\,.

(36)

This relation for inverse binominal sums is similar to the relation between
S
sums with shifted arguments in eq. (
11
). To fully synchronize the inverse binomial sum with the denominator, all that is left is to substitute eq. (
36
) into eq. (
35
). For the case of
p_{1}>1
and
p_{2}\geq 0
we therefore find that

\displaystyle\frac{n}{2^{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{2^{i}}{i}G_{p_{2},...,p_{s}}(p_{1},i-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2^{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{2^{i}}{i}G^{p_{2},...,p_{s}}(p_{1},i)-\sum_{a=1}^{p_{1}}\binom{p_{1}+p_{2}-a}{p_{2}}\frac{n}{2^{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{2^{i}}{i^{p_{1}+p_{2}+1-a}}G^{p_{3},...,p_{s}}(a,i)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\sum_{a=1}^{p_{2}+1}\binom{p_{1}+p_{2}-a}{p_{1}-1}\frac{n}{2^{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{2^{i}}{i^{p_{1}+p_{2}+1-a}}G_{p_{3},...,p_{s}}(a,i)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\sum_{a=1}^{p_{1}-1}\frac{n}{2^{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{2^{i}}{i^{p_{1}+1-a}}G^{p_{2},...,p_{s}}(a,i)+\frac{n}{2^{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{2^{i}}{i^{p_{1}}}G_{p_{2},...,p_{s}}(1,i)\,.

(37)

As one can see, the upper limits in the argument of the
G
functions representing the inverse binomial sums on the right-hand side are now synchronized with their corresponding denominators.

A second interesting subtlety is that we had to distinguish numerous cases. For example for eq. (
37
), the reader can see that specifying
p_{1}>1
and
p_{2}\geq 0
was required due to the appearance of the sign function in the definition of
S
-sums in eq. (
4
). In the case of inverse binomial sums containing two harmonic sums, this process ultimately required distinguishing 124 distinct cases, each with unique conditions on the indices
p_{i}
, and implementing them systematically in
Form
. This intensive case-by-case approach was essential for handling the diversity of possible inputs. For interested readers, we have all computations for these recursions available on request.

3.3
Synchronization of the denominator

Our package includes a feature that handles inverse binomial sums where the denominator is not synchronized with the rest of the summand, i.e. sums like

G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,n)_{c}\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{j!(n-j)!}{n!}\frac{1}{(j+c)^{k}}S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(n-j)\,,

(38)

with
c
an integer not equal to zero. These shifted denominators can for example arise when rewriting the arguments of the binomial by using identities like eq. (
26
).

To address these types of inverse binomial sums, we iteratively reduce the value of
c
to zero. This reduction is achieved through recursions obtained by changing the summation variable:

G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,n)_{c}=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{(j-1)!(n+1-j)!}{n!}\frac{1}{(j+c-1)^{k}}S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(n+1-j)\cdot\frac{j}{j}\,.

(39)

After this step, the remaining elements only need to be synchronized and there is no need to set up a telescoping series. We therefore focus solely on formulating a recursion for
G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,n)_{c}
that can iteratively reduce the value of
c
to zero, and is expressed in terms of recognizable components. This process is carried out as follows:

\displaystyle G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,n)_{c}

\displaystyle=(n+1)\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{j!(n+1-j)!}{(n+1)!}\frac{1}{j(j+c-1)^{k}}S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(n+1-j)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-(n+1)\frac{1!(n+1-1)!}{(n+1)!}\frac{1}{1(1+c-1)^{k}}S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(n+1-1)\,,

(40)

after which we can split the fractions

\displaystyle G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,n)_{c}

(41)

\displaystyle=(n+1)\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{j!(n+1-j)!}{(n+1)!}S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(n+1-j)\bigg(\frac{1}{(c-1)^{k}j}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\sum_{a=1}^{k}\frac{1}{(c-1)^{k+1-a}(j+c-1)^{a}}\bigg)-\frac{S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(n)}{c^{k}}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\sum_{a=1}^{k}\frac{n+1}{(c-1)^{k+1-a}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{j!(n+1-j)!}{(n+1)!}\frac{1}{(j+c-1)^{a}}S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(n+1-j)

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+1)}{(c-1)^{k}}G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(1,n+1)_{0}-\sum_{a=1}^{k}\frac{n+1}{(c-1)^{k+1-a}}G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(a,n+1)_{c-1}-\frac{S_{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(n)}{c^{k}}\,.

As one can see, we have successfully derived a recursion that rewrites the original inverse binomial sum into sums with a lower value of
c
. By applying this process iteratively, we can rewrite the original inverse binomial sums into sums where the denominator and the arguments of the harmonic sums are synchronized.

3.4
Validation

To ensure that the derived recursion terminates correctly, it is important to verify that all inverse binomial sums in the recursion have either fewer indices than the original sum or a lower initial exponent. In our example, we saw that this condition was satisfied.

Finally, to check for any potential errors in the construction of the algorithms we have performed three additional checks:

1.

For every recursion, the extended weight of the terms on the left-hand side must be equal to the extended weight of the terms on the right-hand side. In our example this means that the extended weight of the terms on the right-hand side of eq. (
34
) must all be equal to

W(G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,n))=k+\sum_{i=1}^{s}p_{i}\,,

(42)

which the reader can verify.

2.

For every recursion, the extended product weight of all the terms on the left-hand side must equal the extended product weight of all the terms on the right-hand side. Since we only allow the inverse binomial sums to contain harmonic sums, the extended product weight of the inverse binomial sums that we solve is always equal to 1 (i.e.
P(G^{p_{1},...,p_{s}}(k,n))=1
). This implies that all terms on the right-hand side of the recursion must also have an extended product weight of 1, which can be easily verified for eq. (
34
).

3.

The strongest check we used to verify the correctness of the recursions implemented in
IBIS
involved selecting a representative inverse binomial sum for each type listed in tab.
1
and for each special case (including the 124 cases for two
S
-sums with
k=0
mentioned in sec.
3.2
). By assigning numerical values to their upper limits, we obtained an exact answer in the form of fractions from
IBIS
. These fractions were then compared with the fractions obtained from a direct evaluation of the original sum. Their equality confirmed the validity of all recursions included in
IBIS
.

5
Speed of the program

To provide an indication of the program’s speed, we conducted several performance tests. We calculated every possible type of inverse binomial sum that the program can handle, from extended weight 1 through weight 6 for sums containing one harmonic sum, and from weight 2 through weight 6 for sums containing two harmonic sums. As one can see in all three figures, corresponding to different numbers of harmonic sums, the slowest time for solving one of the inverse binomial sums of a given extended weight increases with this weight, while the fastest time remains stable. That the fastest time is independent of the extended weight is likely due to a consistent overhead in
Form
’s processing that cannot be reduced. To investigate this, we ran a loop solving the same inverse binomial sum (containing one harmonic sum of weight 2) 100 times in the same
Form
program (previously we ran the
Form
program 100 times). Since the fastest time for these sums is around 0.03 seconds, we expected a minimum of 3 seconds for the test. However, the program completed the task in just 0.48 seconds, indicating that solving low-weight sums doesn’t require enough time to increase the fastest time substantially. The fastest time does start to depend (exponentially) on the extended weight if the weight is further increased.

The exponential increase in runtime can be attributed to the nature of the implemented recursions. Due to fraction splitting, the recursions involve sums that extend up to e.g. the initial exponent or the value of one of the indices of the inverse binomial sum, as seen in eq. (
34
). Thus, increasing the weight or indices produces a significantly higher number of terms for
Form

In sec.

41

c\to c-1
), generating numerous additional terms for
Form

6
Conclusion and outlook

In this paper, we presented
IBIS
, a practical tool designed to solve certain classes of inverse binomial sums that arise in multi-loop calculations. We have chosen
Form
for its implementation, because of its speed, efficiency and capability to handle large symbolic expressions.
IBIS
employs recursions to express inverse binomial sums in terms of
S
-sums, maintaining an analytic dependence on the upper limit. We explained in detail how these recursions are constructed using telescoping series. Furthermore, we addressed potential complications, such as the synchronization of the elements within the inverse binomial sums. To illustrate the relevance of inverse binomial sums, we provided an example of a loop calculation where these naturally appear in the appendix. Moreover, we demonstrated that the program is capable of solving inverse binomial sums of weights up to 6 relatively quickly. By offering this tool, we aim to broaden the scope of possible loop calculations, thereby contributing to advancements in high-order perturbative computations. The GitHub repository includes a detailed manual and a sample calculation alongside
IBIS
.

While
IBIS
successfully tackles the class of inverse binomial sums it was designed to solve, we mentioned in the introduction (sec.
1
) that addressing only these sums does not necessarily yield complete solutions. In many Feynman integral calculations, inverse binomial sums appear together with additional binomial terms, see e.g. eq. (
3
). Therefore, before computing the inverse binomial sums, we must first address these accompanying binomial sums. We envision three possible strategies to tackle this issue:

•

Modifying existing recursions

In this approach, one adapts the current recursion relations to incorporate the binomial factors. Although this leads to a more complex framework, it also provides a potentially more versatile tool. For example, we can rewrite the sum of residues in eq. (
3
) in the following way:

eq. (
3
)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k_{3}=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1+k_{3}}\sum_{k_{2}=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2+k_{2}+k_{3})(1+k_{2})}S_{2}(1+k_{2}+k_{3})

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{k_{3}-1}\frac{1}{\binom{k_{3}}{k_{1}}}\frac{(-1)^{k_{1}}}{1+k_{1}}\binom{k_{1}+k_{2}}{k_{1}}\,.

(52)

If we extend
IBIS
to handle combinations of inverse binomial and binomial sums, we could evaluate the innermost sum over
k_{1}
directly.

•

Separate evaluation of binomial sums

The example calculation in app.
A
resulted in the infinite sums in eq. (
64
). This can be restructered in terms of the nested sum shown in eq. (
3
), consisting of an infinite sum involving binomials, inside a finite sum with inverse binomial terms. Solving instead this infinite binomial sum first, would ernable the subsequent sum to be addressed using
IBIS
. However, this would likely require modifying the current recursion relations of
IBIS
to incorporate
S
-sums instead of just harmonic sums, as solving these infinite binomial sums might only be possible in terms of
S
-sums.

•

Reexamining the form of inverse binomial sums

In app.
A
, a substitution (
k_{3}\to k_{3}+k_{1}
) was used to rewrite the infinite inverse binomial sum into a finite inverse binomial sum. This was done to simplify the process of deriving the recursions. While effective, this method makes the upper limit of one sum depend on the summation variable of another, limiting flexibility. Addressing infinite inverse binomial sums directly, without such substitutions, would be significantly more complex but could yield a more versatile approach. For example, the sum of residues in eq. (
64
) can be rewritten as:

eq. (
64
)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k_{1}}}{1+k_{1}}\sum_{k_{2}=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1+k_{2}}\binom{k_{1}+k_{2}}{k_{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sum_{k_{3}=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\binom{k_{1}+k_{3}}{k_{3}}}\frac{S_{2}(1+k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3})}{(2+k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3})(1+k_{1}+k_{3})}\,.

(53)

If
IBIS
were extended to handle infinite inverse binomial sums, we could evaluate the innermost sum directly.

In conclusion, the development of
IBIS
marks a step forward in
solving inverse binomial sums that appear in multi-loop calculations. While the current implementation successfully addresses a specific class of sums, this approach needs to be extended to also include binomial structures that arise in realistic examples. The strategies outlined above suggest possible directions to achieve this.
By expanding these methods, we aim to enhance the analytical toolkit available for high-order perturbative calculations in quantum field theory.
