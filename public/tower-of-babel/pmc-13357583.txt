Methods for classifying physical activities using accelerometer data: a scoping review
Authors: Kiyan Sadeghi Janbahan, Osvaldo Espin-Garcia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate classification of physical activity from accelerometer data is essential for health research and population-scale studies, yet the wide range of computational approaches has created inconsistencies in implementation, validation, and reproducibility. This scoping review identified and categorised methods used to classify physical activities from accelerometer data, emphasising implementation, simplicity, validation, and feasibility for large datasets such as the All of Us Research Programme. We searched PubMed, Web of Science, and SPORTDiscus (2015–2025) for studies using accelerometer data to classify activities or activity levels with a reported validation strategy. Of 1851 records screened, 158 met the inclusion criteria. Machine learning was most common (n = 73), followed by deep learning (n = 38), hybrid models (n = 27), rule-based methods (n = 5), and unsupervised or other novel approaches (n = 3). Walking (n = 97), sitting (n = 79), and standing (n = 68) were most frequently studied. Most studies used lab-based protocols with k-fold or leave-one-subject-out validation. Only 16 studies provided public code, and just two examined seasonality, a limitation for generalisability. Substantial variation exists in classification methods and reporting practices. Open-source tools and real-world validation remain limited. Simpler, validated, and reproducible approaches are needed for population-scale datasets such as All of Us and the UK Biobank.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Accurately measuring physical activity is essential for population health surveillance, clinical monitoring, and epidemiological research1,2. Understanding the type, intensity, and context of human movement enables researchers to link lifestyle patterns to outcomes such as cardiovascular disease, musculoskeletal disorders, and all-cause mortality3,4. In recent years, accelerometers have become a widely adopted tool for measuring physical activity in a continuous and objective manner, offering an alternative to traditional self-report instruments, which are subject to recall and social desirability bias5,6.

However, translating raw accelerometer data into specific meaningful activity classifications, such as walking, sedentary time, or moderate-intensity exercise, requires additional processing and decision-making7,8. A wide range of computational methods has emerged to address this need, from rule-based cut-point algorithms to complex machine-learning and deep-learning models. This diversity of approaches has led to inconsistencies across studies, making it challenging to compare findings or select optimal methods for new applications9–11. Large-scale datasets like the All of Us Research Programme, which seek to integrate wearable data into precision health strategies, require scalable, transparent, and validated activity classification methods12.

Given the expanding landscape of accelerometer-based research, a systematic overview of activity classification methods is warranted. While systematic reviews have explored accelerometer validity or general wear protocols, few have focused specifically on how physical activities are classified from raw data, and even fewer have mapped the trade-offs between simplicity, performance, and real-world applicability1,13. A scoping review is well-suited to this aim, as it allows for the exploration of a broad methodological space and the identification of knowledge gaps.

This review focuses on methods that may be suitable for large, diverse, and longitudinal cohorts such as All of Us, which includes physical activity data from wrist-worn accelerometers alongside detailed sociodemographic and genomic information. Comparable biobanks, such as the UK Biobank, have successfully integrated accelerometry data at scale, and understanding how these efforts classify activity can inform best practices for other large datasets.

Classification model choice also has implications beyond activity recognition itself. In digital-twin frameworks and mHealth risk-prediction pipelines, inconsistently or inaccurately classified activity features can propagate error into cardiometabolic risk models, disease-progression tools, and personalised intervention systems3,4,14,15. Scalable, interpretable, and well-validated classification methods are therefore essential to ensure that accelerometer-derived features support reliable clinical and translational applications.

In this review, we use the term classical machine learning to refer to algorithms that rely on handcrafted feature extraction and traditional statistical learning models (e.g., decision trees, random forests, support vector machines, logistic regression). In contrast, deep learning refers to neural-network–based architectures that learn hierarchical features directly from the input data (e.g., convolutional or recurrent neural networks). Hybrid approaches were defined as modelling pipelines that combined classical feature-engineering–based methods with deep-learning components, or that integrated models from different methodological families within a single classification framework. This distinction is applied consistently throughout the manuscript.

Results

Study selection overview

After removal of duplicates, a total of 1851 studies were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 183 full-text articles were reviewed, and 158 studies met all eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis. Study characteristics

The 158 studies in this scoping review span publication years from 2015 (n = 6) through 2025 (n = 3). Geographic reporting was uneven; approximately 6% of studies (n = 9) did not specify location, while those that did encompass North America, Europe, Asia, and Australia, often as single-site laboratory or field investigations. Participant cohorts were diverse (Fig. 2). Just over half of the studies enroled healthy adult volunteers (n = 83, 52%), around one‑fifth drew on publicly available Human Activity Recognition (HAR) datasets such as the UCI Human Activity Recognition Using Smartphones Dataset or WISDM Human Activity Recognition Dataset16 (n = 34, 22%), while a small minority focused on disease‑specific clinical populations (n = 10, 6%)16,17. The remaining 20% of papers did not clearly describe participant characteristics.

Activities studied

Classification targets centred on locomotor and postural behaviours (Fig. 4): 97 studies addressed walking, 79 sitting, and 68 standing. Stair-climbing appeared in 48 studies, running in 43, jogging in 17 and cycling in 15. Lying down was tackled in 24 papers, while transitional movements such as sit-to-stand featured in 5. In this review, ‘running’ and ‘jogging’ reflect the terminology used in each primary study; most papers distinguished them by self-reported labels or protocol descriptions rather than speed thresholds, and therefore, the original study-defined categories were retained. An additional 22 studies focused on broader intensity categories (e.g., sedentary vs. moderate vs. vigorous). These categories are not mutually exclusive; many studies investigated multiple activities, so the counts across activity types exceed the total number of included studies.

Classification methods used

Classical machine-learning algorithms, notably decision trees, random forests, support-vector machines, and logistic regression, were applied in 73 studies (Fig. 5). Deep-learning models (e.g., convolutional or recurrent neural networks) appeared in 38 studies, while 27 papers adopted hybrid or ensemble pipelines that combined handcrafted features with neural networks. Purely rule-based or threshold heuristics were reported in 5 studies, and 3 investigations relied primarily on unsupervised or other novel statistical techniques.

Furthermore, among the 158 studies included, programming environments and software platforms were reported inconsistently. MATLAB was the most frequently mentioned (n = 14), followed by Python (n = 10). Twelve studies provided links to GitHub repositories, while four cited the use of Keras and four used TensorFlow. Six studies explicitly reported using R, and one used SPSS.

The majority of papers (approximately 66%) did not specify the computational environment used for analysis or model development.

Validation strategies

Ground‑truth procedures were described in 156 (128 + 28) of the 158 studies (Fig. 6). Twenty‑eight papers synchronised their wearable signals with video footage for precise time‑stamping, whereas the remainder relied on reference devices (e.g., ActiGraph), live observer coding, or participant logs. Sixty studies used a k‑fold or leave‑one‑subject‑out cross‑validation scheme, and 20 additionally reserved a separate hold‑out test set to report final model performance. Only three studies conducted true external validation using an independent dataset.

Reporting of accuracy metrics was highly heterogeneous across studies. While some articles provided explicit accuracy or F1-score values, for example, accuracies in the 80–95% range for selected activities, many others reported only qualitative performance descriptions or omitted metrics entirely. Because of the diversity of reported metrics, activity sets, datasets, and validation strategies, these values were not directly comparable and could not be synthesised quantitatively. However, where reported, deep-learning methods generally demonstrated high performance for complex or high-dimensional activity sets, while traditional machine-learning models achieved competitive accuracy when feature engineering was carefully applied.

Accelerometer use and protocols

Devices spanned from consumer wearables, smartphones (n = 66) and smartwatches (n = 9), to research‑grade sensors such as ActiGraph (n = 11), GENEActiv (n = 3) and Shimmer (n = 9); however, 101 of the 158 studies did not specify an exact make or model, referring only to a generic “smartphone accelerometer” or “IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit)”. Waist or hip placement was most common (n = 58), while wrist, pocket and thigh sites appeared in 30, 27, and 4 studies, respectively; device placement was unclear in six papers. Wear protocols ranged from short, scripted laboratory sessions (5–30 min) to multi‑day free‑living monitoring, yet explicit daily wear-time (e.g., “≥10 h per day”) was rarely reported.

Seasonality

Two studies explicitly considered seasonality in data collection: Christian et al.18 sampling across seasons in young children and Sandhu et al.19 stratifying by time of day to capture temporal variation.

Tool and code availability

Only 16 of the 158 studies provided public code or tools (12 via GitHub, 4 via institutional archives/Zenodo). Seventeen noted code availability upon request, 15 explicitly stated “not provided,” and the remaining 110 did not mention code or tool sharing. Discussion

This scoping review identified a diverse range of methods used to classify physical activities from accelerometer data. The majority of studies applied classical machine-learning techniques such as decision trees, random forests, and support vector machines10,20. Deep-learning approaches, particularly convolutional and recurrent neural networks, also appeared with increasing frequency, often in hybrid models combining handcrafted feature extraction and automated learning11,21,22.

Walking, sitting, and standing were the most frequently studied activities, reflecting their importance in both health surveillance and functional assessments7,8. However, more complex movements (e.g., transitional motions or non-exercise tasks) and activity intensities were less commonly addressed, indicating areas for future exploration. Validation practices varied, with k-fold and leave-one-subject-out schemes being the most common internal validation approaches, used in 60 studies (38%)23,24. Twenty studies used a dedicated hold‑out test set for final performance evaluation, and only three employed true external validation on an independent dataset. This lack of external validation highlights a critical limitation: while internal cross-validation can demonstrate model performance within a given sample, it does not ensure generalisability to new populations, devices, or real-world settings23,24. To strengthen future external validation, several established frameworks from the activity-recognition literature are particularly relevant for large, heterogeneous cohorts such as All of Us. Robust participant-level generalisation can be evaluated using leave-one-participant-out or leave-one-group-out validation, while cross-dataset testing, using publicly available HAR datasets such as UCI HAR, WISDM, PAMAP2, or RealWorld HAR, provides a means of assessing model transportability across populations, protocols, and environments16,25–27. In addition, cross-device validation can be used to evaluate robustness to differences in sensor hardware and wear location, and domain-adaptation strategies such as feature normalisation, transfer learning, or correlation-alignment approaches offer established pathways for mitigating device- and cohort-related distribution shifts. Together, these frameworks represent practical and widely used strategies for improving the external validity of accelerometer-based activity-classification models in future large-scale applications.

Because the included studies differed widely in device types, sampling frequencies, activity taxonomies, preprocessing pipelines, and validation schemes, performance metrics were not comparable across studies. As a scoping review, the aim was not to pool or contrast classification accuracy but to map methodological approaches. Consequently, the available evidence does not support drawing conclusions about whether deep-learning models outperform classical machine-learning approaches or whether any reported performance gains justify increased computational complexity. Standardised benchmarking datasets and harmonised evaluation protocols will be necessary for future work to rigorously assess such trade-offs.

Reporting was highly inconsistent: the make or model of the accelerometer was missing in 101 of 158 studies (64%), daily wear‑time criteria were absent in the majority of studies, and the study context was unclear in a further 17 papers (11%)6,28. Despite the growing importance of open science, only 16 studies provided public access to code or tools. These findings suggest a need for greater standardisation, transparency, and emphasis on real-world applicability in activity classification research21,29,30.

A clear strength in the current literature is the diversity of methodological approaches. The application of machine-learning and deep-learning models has enabled increasingly accurate and nuanced classification of physical activities31,32. Additionally, the availability of public datasets, such as UCI HAR and WISDM, has facilitated the development and benchmarking of algorithms across various studies.

However, key weaknesses remain. Validation strategies are often limited to internal cross-validation, reducing generalisability.

The choice of software environment has implications for transparency and reproducibility. Proprietary platforms such as MATLAB and SPSS, while powerful, limit accessibility and open validation. In contrast, open-source ecosystems like Python and R align more closely with open science practices, enabling reproducibility and broader adoption. The mixed use of these environments highlights both the increasing uptake of open-source tools and the continued reliance on proprietary software in the field.

For research, these findings highlight the need to prioritise validation in diverse populations and free-living settings. Future studies should adopt standardised reporting guidelines, including details about sensor brand, placement, sampling protocols, and wear time. Comparability across studies will also benefit from shared benchmark datasets and reproducible pipelines.

For practice, simpler and interpretable classification methods, such as rule-based systems or lightweight models, may be better suited to clinical, public health, or community interventions, especially when real-time feedback is needed or computational resources are limited20.

For policy, open science practices must be encouraged. Funding agencies and journals can promote transparency by requiring publicly available tools, datasets, and model architectures. This shift will support broader adoption and implementation of physical activity classification systems in healthcare and population health initiatives14,30,31.

All of Us is highlighted in this review because it is currently the largest ongoing U.S. cohort to collect high-volume, continuous, wrist-worn accelerometer data at scale through Fitbit devices, paired with deeply phenotyped electronic health records, genomic data, and longitudinal behavioural surveys12. The programme’s demographic diversity and multi-modal data structure make it a uniquely demanding environment for evaluating the scalability and reproducibility of physical-activity classification pipelines. However, several barriers exist. Many of the included studies used homogenous populations, often young and healthy, whereas All of Us encompasses a demographically and clinically diverse cohort.

To leverage All of Us data effectively, classification methods must be adaptable, transparent, and computationally scalable. Models should also be validated across demographic groups, devices, and real-world scenarios, something not yet commonly investigated in the existing literature.

These findings also have implications for the implementation of FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) data principles within large-scale cohorts such as All of Us. The substantial heterogeneity in feature engineering pipelines, model architectures, validation procedures, and reporting practices creates barriers to interoperability across wearable datasets, devices, and research groups. Standardised metadata schemas, transparent reporting of pre-processing steps, and the use of openly documented model architectures would improve cross-study comparability and facilitate integration of accelerometer-derived features into interoperable, multi-modal research environments. Strengthening adherence to FAIR principles would also enable downstream reuse of activity-classification outputs across digital-twin modelling, cardiometabolic risk prediction, and other precision-health applications.

Several established open-science initiatives demonstrate how transparent pipelines and openly shared datasets can accelerate progress in wearable-sensor research. Platforms such as PhysioNet, which provides openly curated physiological and wearable datasets with standardised annotation protocols, and mPower, which offers openly shared mobile-sensor data for Parkinson’s research, exemplify the reproducibility culture that could strengthen accelerometer-based activity-classification research33,34. Adoption of similar open-data and open-model practices, including public release of pre-processing scripts, feature-extraction code, and trained models, would meaningfully enhance reproducibility and support wider reuse of accelerometer-derived features across research communities.

This review has several limitations. First, the inclusion was restricted to peer-reviewed articles published in English, which may have excluded relevant studies from non-English journals or grey literature. Second, while the scoping review approach allows for a broad overview of the field, it does not involve formal quality appraisal or meta-analysis. A further limitation is that screening and full-text review were conducted by a single reviewer, which may introduce selection bias despite predefined eligibility criteria and consultation with the senior author for ambiguous cases. Additionally, our decision to restrict inclusion to studies published from 2015 onward may have excluded earlier foundational accelerometer research. However, because this review focuses on contemporary machine-learning and deep-learning approaches suitable for modern, large-scale datasets, this restriction is unlikely to affect the methodological conclusions drawn. Finally, some included studies lacked sufficient methodological detail, limiting our ability to assess certain features such as annotation protocols, validation rigour, or generalisability.

This scoping review synthesised current methods used to classify physical activities from accelerometer data across 158 studies published between 2015 and 2025. Classical machine-learning techniques, particularly random forests and support vector machines, were the most common approaches, while deep-learning and hybrid models have gained traction in recent years. Most studies focused on common movements like walking and sitting, with limited attention to less structured or context-specific behaviours.

Validation strategies were typically internal, and a sparse number of studies included real-world testing or held-out external datasets. Reporting on device specifications, wear protocols, and data annotation was often incomplete or lacking, and public availability of code and tools remained limited.

To advance the field, future studies should emphasise transparent reporting, reproducible pipelines, and validation across diverse populations and settings. Open-source methods and standardised benchmarks are especially important for ensuring that classification approaches can be translated into population-scale datasets such as All of Us. The findings of this review provide a foundation for guiding future research and implementation of physical activity classification systems in large epidemiological studies.

Methods

Review design

We conducted this scoping review following the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews) guidelines.32 This design was selected to systematically map the existing literature on methods used to classify physical activities using accelerometer data, identify knowledge gaps, and summarise approaches that may be feasible for implementation in large-scale datasets such as All of Us.

Grey literature and preprints were not included, as the search was limited to peer-reviewed journal articles indexed in the selected databases.

The 2015–2025 window was selected a priori because contemporary machine-learning and deep-learning activity-classification methods began to expand substantially after 2015, coinciding with the broader availability of raw, high-frequency accelerometer data and increased adoption of modern computational pipelines35. Earlier literature predominantly focused on cut-point approaches or early feature-engineered models that are less reflective of current practices relevant to large-scale cohorts such as All of Us.

Protocol and registration

No formal protocol for this scoping review was registered in an external repository (e.g., PROSPERO or OSF). All methods were defined a priori.

Critical appraisal

Critical appraisal of individual sources of evidence was not undertaken, consistent with JBI and PRISMA‑ScR guidance for scoping reviews whose aim is to map the breadth of literature rather than evaluate study quality32,36.

Information sources

We searched PubMed, Web of Science, and SPORTDiscus for relevant articles. The final search was conducted in March 2025 and was limited to studies published between 2015 and 2025. Search strategies were customised for each database, and all details are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Methods: Search Strategy).

Study selection

A total of 1851 records were identified after deduplication and screened at the title and abstract levels. Of these, 183 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. A total of 158 studies were ultimately included in the review. Full-text exclusions (n = 25) were due to reasons outlined in the PRISMA diagram, including non-English publications, non-human studies, wrong outcomes or interventions, ineligible study designs, or unavailable full texts.

During title and abstract screening which was conducted in Covidence, studies were excluded if they: (1) did not use accelerometer data for activity classification; (2) focused solely on subjective or self-reported physical activity; (3) did not include a classification or validation component; (4) used wearable sensors other than accelerometers without incorporating accelerometer data; (5) were reviews, commentaries, conference abstracts, or non–peer-reviewed sources; (6) or were not peer-reviewed, published in English, or within the predefined 2015–2025 timeframe.

Study selection was performed by a single reviewer due to resource constraints, which is consistent with established scoping-review methodology. To minimise potential selection bias, eligibility criteria were defined a priori and applied consistently throughout screening. Any unclear, borderline, or ambiguous records were discussed with the senior author to ensure accurate interpretation, and all such studies were re-reviewed during the data-extraction phase to confirm that inclusion decisions were applied consistently. While these procedures were implemented to improve transparency and reduce the risk of bias, this approach may limit comprehensiveness in rapidly evolving AI-driven fields.
