Rotational atherectomy with cutting balloon before stenting in severely calcified coronary lesions: a meta‐analysis
Authors: Obieda Altobaishat, Mohamed Abouzid, Mohammad Tanashat, Ahmed Mazen Amin, Mustafa Turkmani, Mohamed Abuelazm
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRotational atherectomy (RA) has been proven to treat coronary artery calcification (CAC) during percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).PurposeThis study evaluates the safety and efficacy of RA followed by cutting balloon angioplasty (ROTACUT) before stent placement in CAC.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials and observational studies was conducted. PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Cochrane were searched through January 2024. We used Stata version 17 to pool dichotomous data using risk ratio (RR) and continuous data using mean difference (MD), with a 95% confidence interval (CI).ResultsEight studies with 846 patients were included. There was no significant difference between ROTACUT and RA + bare balloons in major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) (RR:0.60; 95%CI [0.31,1.16], p = 0.13), cardiac death (RR:1.32; 95%CI [0.42,4.14], p = 0.64), target vessel revascularization (TVR) (RR:1.89; 95%CI [0.40,8.84], p = 0.42), target lesion revascularization (TLR) (RR:0.83; 95%CI [0.39,1.79], p = 0.64), procedural duration (MD:0.78; 95%CI [−4.68,6.24], p = 0.78), stent thrombosis (RR:0.81; 95%CI [0.22,2.95], p = 0.75), and any procedure-related complications (RR:0.86; 95%CI [0.42,1.75], p = 0.68).ConclusionROTACUT and RA + bare balloons demonstrated similar efficacy and safety profiles regarding MACE, cardiac death, TVR, TLR, procedural duration, stent thrombosis, and all safety outcomes.KEYWORDS: Interventional, atherectomy, percutaneous coronary interventions, severely calcified coronary lesions, cutting balloon

Introduction

Coronary artery calcification (CAC) during percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is associated with a significant increase in procedure complexity and periprocedural adverse outcomes. These outcomes include coronary dissection, stent thrombosis, target lesion revascularization (TLR), and mortality [1,2]. Approximately 20% of patients undergoing PCI exhibit moderate to severe calcification angiographically [3]. Notably, severely calcified coronary lesions pose challenges for interventional cardiologists during the PCI process. These lesions may lead to balloon dilation failure and incomplete and asymmetrical stent expansion [4].

Over the past three decades, rotational atherectomy (RA) has been the standard treatment for calcified coronary lesions in the United States. Developed by Boston Scientific in Natick, MA, USA, RA employs a rotating abrasive burr at high speeds to ablate and shatter superficial calcium [5]. Specifically, RA alters the physical characteristics of calcified plaque, facilitating balloon angioplasty and stent deployment.

Despite the use of RA, restenosis rates and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) have remained elevated following PCI for calcified lesions. It is also worth noting that Tomasiewicz et al. highlighted that the presence of an acute angulation on lesion and sequential lesion are independent determinants of clinical and procedural failure of RA [6]. Although RA has improved strategic success compared to procedures without RA [7], researchers have explored adjuvant therapies to enhance procedural outcomes. These therapies aim to modify calcified plaque, promoting coronary artery dilation and subsequent stenting [8]. Combining RA with other lesion preparation methods may hold promise for improving both acute procedure outcomes and long-term results.

Cutting balloons (CBs) are specially modified balloons (MB) equipped with either a nitinol spiral cage or cutting blades that facilitate the shattering of calcified plaques. The underlying mechanism of CB involves creating distinct incisions in the atherosclerotic plaque, allowing for further expansion while limiting uncontrolled dissection [9]. Notably, CB has primarily been investigated in patients with mild to moderate CAC.

Recently, the ROTACUT randomized controlled trial (RCT) explored the combined technique of RA and CB in patients with moderate to severe CAC [10]. As a result, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the safety and efficacy of performing RA followed by CB angioplasty prior to stent placement in cases of CAC. Our analysis considers the novel data from the ROTACUT trial, specifically on severe CAC.

Methodology

Protocol registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis were completed using the PRISMA statement Figure 1 [11] and the Cochrane Handbook for systematic reviews and meta-analyses [12]. Under the following ID: CRD42024505135, this review has been registered and published in PROSPERO.

PRISMA chart of the reported studies showing the search selection strategy and exclusion criteria.

Data sources & search strategy

Our search was conducted until 8 January 2024, on the following databases: PubMed (MEDLINE), Web of Science (WoS), SCOPUS, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). Following our modifications to each database’s search terms and keywords, the outcomes are shown in (Table S1).

Eligibility criteria and study selection

We included studies that followed the following PICOS criteria: population (patients with severely calcified chronic lesions in the coronary artery prior to stent implantation), intervention (rotational atherectomy with cutting balloon angioplasty (RA+CBA), comparison (RA followed by non-compliant balloon angioplasty (RA+NCBA) or semi-compliant balloon (SB) or modified balloon (MB) or rotational atherectomy (RA)), and outcomes: our primary outcome is (MACE). The secondary outcomes are (target vessel revascularization (TVR), stent thrombosis, stroke, cardiac death, myocardial infarction, TLR, in-stent restenosis, strategy success, procedural duration contrast amount, any adverse events, access site complication, slow flow/no flow, coronary dissection perforation, AV block). Studies included were RCT.

The following criteria were used to exclude papers: The following study types are not considered original: (1) book chapters, reviews, comments, letters to the editor, and guidelines; (2) any other study design other than randomized controlled trials; (3) studies with overlapping or duplicate datasets; (4) non-human and in vitro experiments; and (5) studies not published in English.

Study selection

The review was carried out using the Covidence online tool. After eliminating any duplicates, two authors (MT and OA) independently evaluated each of the records they had obtained. Two authors (MT and OA) reviewed the full texts of the records during the first full-text screening for eligibility criteria. Any differences were settled through discussion and agreement with a senior author.

Data extraction

To set up the data extraction sheet accurately, we performed a pilot extraction after obtaining the full texts of the relevant publications. The Excel (Microsoft, USA) structured data extraction sheet is divided into three sections. The first part included the summary characteristics of the included studies (study ID, country, study design, recruitment duration, number of centers, registry number, inclusion criteria of the RCT, intervention group, control (comparison group), sample size, stent type, MACE definition, primary outcome and follow-up period. The second part included the baseline information of the participants (number of patients in each group, age, gender, body mass index, LVEF, myocardial infarction type, lesion location, and comorbidities). Finally, the third part included outcomes data. Two reviewers (MT and OA) were responsible for data extraction. Any differences were settled by discussion and agreement with a senior author.

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

Two reviewers (MT and OA) evaluated the studies’ quality independently using the Cochrane RoB2 method Figure 2 [14]. They considered five domains, including the risk of bias associated with the randomization process, deviation from the intended intervention, missing outcome data, measuring the outcome, and choosing the reported results. Discussion with a senior author was the way to resolve any disagreements.

Risk of bias assessment of (a) RoB 2 tool (revised tool for risk of bias in randomized trials) and (b) ROBINS-I tool (risk of bias in non-randomized studies – of interventions), created using robvis [13].

Statistical analysis

We used StataMP version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) for the statistical analysis. Dichotomous data was reported using (RR), and continuous data was reported mean differences (MD) with a 95% confidence interval (CI). A fixed effect was used; however, the random-effects model was applied in cases with significant heterogeneity. We used the Chi-square and I-square tests to evaluate heterogeneity; the Chi-square test determines whether heterogeneity exists, and the I-square test determines the degree of heterogeneity. According to the Cochrane Handbook (chapter nine) [15], significant heterogeneity was indicated by an I-square of greater than 50%, while an alpha level of less than 0.1 for the Chi-square test was considered to indicate considerable heterogeneity.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

Three RCTs [10,16,17], three retrospective [18–20], and two prospective observational studies [21,22] were included in the meta-analysis with 846 patients with severely calcified coronary lesions. The follow-up duration in those studies ranged from six months to 24 months. These studies were conducted in four countries, mainly in China (four studies). Primary outcome: MACE

There was no significant difference between ROTACUT and RA + bare balloons (RR: 0.60 with 95% CI [0.31, 1.16], p = 0.13) (Figure 3). The pooled studies were homogenous (I2 = 0%, p = 0.81) (Figure 3).

Secondary efficacy outcomes

There was no significant difference between ROTACUT and RA + bare balloons in the incidence of cardiac death (RR: 1.32 with 95% CI [0.42, 4.14], p = 0.64) (Figure 4(a)), the incidence of myocardial infarction (RR: 1.11 with 95% CI [0.40, 3.12], p = 0.84) (Figure 4(b)), the incidence of TVR (RR: 1.89 with 95% CI [0.40, 8.84], p = 0.42) (Figure 4(c)), the incidence of TLR (RR: 0.83 with 95% CI [0.39, 1.79], p = 0.64) (Figure 4(d)), strategy success (RR: 1.00 with 95% CI [0.89, 1.13], p = 0.99) (Figure S1), procedural duration (MD: 0.78 with 95% CI [−4.68, 6.24], p = 0.78) (Figure S2), stent thrombosis (RR: 0.81 with 95% CI [0.22, 2.95], p = 0.75) (Figure S3), the incidence of in-stent restenosis (RR: 0.67 with 95% CI [0.09, 4.95], p = 0.69) (Figure S4).

Forest plot of secondary efficacy outcomes (a) Cardiac death, (b) Myocardial infarction, (c) Target vessel revascularization, and (d) Target lesion revascularization.

The pooled studies were homogenous in cardiac death (I2 = 0%, p = 0.97), the incidence of myocardial infarction (I2 = 0%, p = 0.98), the incidence of TVR (I2 = 0%, p = 0.95), the incidence of TLR (I2 = 20.76%, p = 0.28), the incidence of strategy success (I2 = 0%, p = 1.00), procedural duration (I2 = 0%, p = 0.60), and stent thrombosis (I2 = 0%, p = 0.89). However, pooled studies were heterogeneous in the incidence of in-stent restenosis (I2 = 70.65%, p = 0.03). Safety outcomes

There was no significant difference between ROTACUT and RA + bare balloons in the incidence of any-procedure-related complications (RR: 0.86 with 95% CI [0.42, 1.75], p = 0.68) (Figure 5(a)), the incidence of coronary dissection (RR: 1.39 with 95% CI [0.83, 2.33], p = 0.22) (Figure 5(b)), the incidence of perforation (RR: 0.99 with 95% CI [0.35, 2.78], p = 0.99) (Figure 5(c)), and the incidence of slow reflow/no-reflow (RR: 0.78 with 95% CI [0.36, 1.70], p = 0.54) (Figure 5(d)).

Forest plot of analyzed safety outcomes (a) Any-procedure associated complications, (b) Coronary dissection, (c) Perforation, and (d) Slow reflow/no flow.

The pooled studies were homogenous in any-procedure-related complications (I2 = 12.23%, p = 0.34), the incidence of coronary dissection (I2 = 30.60%, p = 0.22), the incidence of perforation (I2 = 0%, p = 1.00), and the incidence of slow reflow/no-reflow (I2 = 0%, p = 0.61).

Discussion

This meta-analysis provides a comprehensive overview of lesion preparation techniques, comparing rotational atherectomy (RA) followed by a cutting balloon (CB) [referred to as ROTACUT] with RA and a bare balloon [referred to as RA+ bare balloon] for the treatment of moderate to severe calcific coronary lesions prior to drug-eluting stent (DES) placement. It synthesizes the existing studies, offering valuable insights into these two methodologies.

We observed comparable outcomes concerning MACE and efficacy measures in the ROTACUT and RA + bare balloons groups. These include the incidence of cardiac death, myocardial infarction, TVR, TLR, procedural success rate, procedural duration, stent thrombosis, and in-stent restenosis. Furthermore, there were no significant disparities between the ROTACUT and RA + bare balloons groups regarding procedure-related complications such as coronary dissection, perforation, and slow reflow/no-reflow phenomena.

The previous meta-analysis by Patel et al. [23] included four studies published between 2012 and 2018, three of which were observational [18–20], and one was an open-label RCT [17]. Similar to the results of this study, no significant differences were observed in terms of MACE [OR: 0.74; 95% CI: 0.25–2.18], the phenomenon of slow flow/no‐reflow (OR: 0.71; 95% CI: 0.23–2.16), the risk of all‐cause mortality (OR: 2.02; 95% CI: 0.28–14.60), and device success rate (OR: 1.79; 95% CI: 0.28–11.18). Notably, the authors reported a trend toward less TLR in the ROTACUT group; however, this outcome was reported in only two studies (OR: 0.29; 95% CI: 0.08–0.99). This outcome was compared across five studies in our study, and no significant differences were observed (RR: 0.83, 95% CI [0.39, 1.79], p = 0.64).

RA before DES placement appears safe, as evidenced by retrospective studies [24]. It can serve as a primary method for lesion preparation or be employed after initial failed dilation attempts [25]. Primary RA is associated with shorter procedure times, less radiation exposure, and reduced contrast volume compared to provisional RA [25]. While RA improves stent delivery and angiographic success, it does not prevent restenosis as a stand‐alone device [26–28]. The ROTAXUS trial [7] noted increased late lumen loss at nine months with RA prior to paclitaxel DES placement, prompting the exploration of adjunctive measures like cutting balloon angioplasty. CB, proven effective in hemodialysis access stenosis, yields acute lumen gain without additional complications when used with RA [17]. Additionally, CB usage has demonstrated lower rates of in-stent restenosis compared to RA + bare balloons [19].

RA targets superficial calcium and moderately reduces plaque volume, often leaving a persistent calcium ring that hinders stent expansion. However, a study by Tang et al. [18] using intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) showed that ROTACUT fractured the calcium ring, leading to better stent expansion than RA + bare balloons [18]. The authors also found that more patients in the RA group required larger burr sizes (1.75) compared to the ROTACUT group, which was associated with higher complication rates. Smaller burr sizes and slower speeds are now recommended in DES implantation, potentially explaining the advantage of ROTACUT in lesion preparation. A recent RCT by Sharma et al. [10] highlighted that ROTACUT can be used in severe calcium lesions (even though the device was approved for mild to moderate calcium lesions), which could also be a cost-effective approach.

Despite the advantages associated with ROTACUT, concerns have been raised regarding the combined approach of ROTACUT prior to stent implantation for treating calcified lesions [24,29]. These studies consistently demonstrate an immediate acute improvement in luminal diameter post-ROTACUT, facilitating enhanced stent expansion, as shown in studies by Allali et al. [22] (1.92 ± 0.45 mm versus 1.70 ± 0.42, p < 0.001 for ROTACUT and RA, respectively) and Ai et al. [19] (0.87 [0.66–1.19] vs. 0.39 [0.19–0.72], p < 0.001 for ROTACUT and RA, respectively). However, a recent ROTA-CUT trial [10] revealed that RA combined with cutting balloon angioplasty (CBA, ROTACUT) compared with RA followed by non-compliant balloon angioplasty (NCBA) did not result in a greater minimum stent area or acute lumen gain. Nonetheless, there were no differences in MACE or all-cause mortality between the CB and conventional balloon groups. Both strategies have demonstrated comparable safety profiles and success rates in stent delivery. These findings may aid operators when RA alone fails to modify the desired plaque adequately.

This meta-analysis has several limitations. First, the sample sizes of the included studies varied significantly, ranging from as few as 25 participants to as many as 310, which may affect the statistical power of the analysis. The number of available studies was limited due to the complex nature of addressing coronary calcified lesions, and most included studies were published after 2018. The lack of data on the timing of procedures and operator experience limits our ability to assess its potential impact on our findings. Additionally, only three out of the eight studies were randomized, which may affect the overall robustness of our findings.

Moreover, although we found some heterogeneity in the incidence of in-stent restenosis, this was primarily mitigated by excluding the study by Allali et al. (2022). The remaining outcomes showed reasonable homogeneity. However, it is essential to note that the populations across the included studies were not entirely homogenous, with variability in participant characteristics. This variation could impact the comparability of the results and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

Our comprehensive analysis indicates that the ROTACUT procedure, which involves the use of RA followed by CB angioplasty before stent placement in CAC, is comparable to the use of RA + bare balloons in terms of MACE, cardiac death, TVR, TLR, procedural duration, stent thrombosis, and all safety outcomes. These findings suggest that both strategies can effectively manage CAC with no significant difference in patient outcomes. Further research is needed to confirm these results and explore the potential advantages of one method over the other in specific clinical scenarios or from the economic perspective.
