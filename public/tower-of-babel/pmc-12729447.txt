Effects of naturalistic housing conditions on amphibian growth and microbiome in captivity
Authors: Justin C. Touchon, Myra C. Hughey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnimals in captivity are inherently separated from their natural environments, which both exposes them to new heterospecific organisms as well as reduces contact with naturally occurring predators, prey or microbiota. The microbes that live on and in animals are increasingly recognized as having important impacts on animal health, development and behavior. We raised post-metamorphic treefrogs in 1) naturalistic containers in groups, 2) regularly sterilized containers in groups, or 3) regularly sterilized containers but solitary. Froglets were raised for over eight months; in addition to monitoring growth and development, we collected fecal samples on three occasions, gut samples on two occasions, and skin swab samples once. We compared the diversity of microbial communities across sample types and over time.ResultsFroglets raised in group housing, either naturalistic or regularly cleaned, had the fastest growth and sexual differentiation, but naturalistic housing also improved survival. Alpha diversity of bacteria on the skin or in the gut did not vary with rearing conditions, whereas diversity in the gut increased over time. Alpha diversity of feces did vary with rearing treatment and changed over time. Bacterial community composition (beta diversity) varied most strongly with sample type, but also with rearing conditions and over time. In addition, bacterial communities of feces were highly correlated with those of guts, indicating that feces can serve as an accurate and non-invasive biomarker of the gut microbiome. Lastly, transferring frogs from regularly sterilized environments to naturalistic vivaria improved bacterial community diversity. ConclusionsOur study suggests that naturalistic housing improves the overall health and development of captive amphibians and that these improvements may occur by facilitating a more stable and diverse microbiome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42523-025-00491-z.

Background

In recent decades, ex situ conservation programs (protecting plants or animals in captivity) have become increasingly common as a complementary approach to in situ conservation (protecting plants or animals in their native environments) or when in situ efforts are not a viable option [1, 2]. Although captivity is obviously preferable to extinction, living separated from nature has long been known to affect organisms negatively [3, 4]. Captive environments are often physiologically stressful and create novel selection pressures which animals may or may not adapt to effectively [5–7]. In addition, species in captivity are exposed to just a subset of the variety of heterospecific organisms they encounter in the wild, while simultaneously being exposed to organisms that they do not encounter in nature or quantities of organisms that differ from what would be expected. Such organisms may be macroscopic, such as predators, prey, and competitors, but also microscopic, such as fungi and bacteria [8–11].

One of the most critically endangered groups of animals are the amphibians, and biologists have observed precipitous declines in amphibian abundance across all levels of taxonomic diversity and on all continents [12–14]. These declines are the results of various factors including disease, habitat loss, and climate change [15–17]. The irreversibility of many of these threats has compelled conservation biologists worldwide to establish captive assurance colonies in order to preserve at-risk species before population extirpation or extinction [18–20]. For example, the Amphibian Ark alone documents captive rescue programs for 75 species of amphibians [21].

Amphibians are particularly known for being sensitive to environmental variation and often suffer developmental problems in captivity. Due to the breadth of ecological niches occupied by amphibians and the fact that the number of described species of amphibians has nearly doubled in the last 30 years [22, 23], we know very little about dietary and environmental requirements for most species. Most published recommendations for keeping amphibians in captivity have focused on the physical and chemical needs of their habitats [24], but developmental problems remain common. For example, ‘spindly leg syndrome’ is a condition wherein anurans have severe limb underdevelopment or deformities during metamorphosis, usually resulting in death [25–28]. Occasionally limbs are so underdeveloped that they are entirely absent [25]. The causes of spindly leg syndrome remain elusive, but research has pointed to roles of dissolved minerals in water and tadpole overfeeding [26–28]. Captive amphibians usually have access to a diet of relatively limited breadth, and so it is common to supplement the diet of adults with vitamin powders dusted on to feeder insects [24] in order to prevent malnutrition due to the lack of a key nutrients, such as vitamin A [29, 30]. However, overuse of vitamins can also lead to developmental problems, such as hypervitaminosis [31]. Lastly, although some research has endeavored to understand the role of particular nutrients on amphibian health in captivity, less attention has been paid to potentially important biological and symbiotic aspects of their habitats [32, 33].

The importance of the relationship between amphibians and their microbial symbionts—the microbiome—has become increasingly clear during the past decade or so [34]. For example, microbes living on the skin of amphibians have roles in immune function, helping to defend the host against pathogens [34, 35]. Microbes in the gut influence growth and development, responses to stressful conditions, and immunity [36–41]. These microbial symbiont communities are sensitive to extrinsic factors [reviewed by [42], including seasonal changes in host habitat usage [43–45] and the presence of conspecifics [46]. Similar effects are seen in tadpoles [47–50]. Importantly, the diversity and composition of both skin and gut microbiota are known to be negatively affected by time in captivity [51–53]. For example, Hughey et al. [54] and Longo and Zamudio [55] both found that skin microbiome diversity was diminished after as little as three days in captivity. This may be due to captive amphibians not having access to source pools of microbes found in nature, which may be necessary to regularly replenish the skin and gut bacterial communities. Loudon et al. [56] found that the presence of field-collected soil in captive amphibian enclosures helped individuals maintain skin bacterial diversity over the course of a four week experiment. It may thus be the case that the presence of natural substrates can help ameliorate the negative effects of captivity on the amphibian microbiome.

In an effort to improve amphibian health in captivity, the Amphibian Taxon Advisory Group of the Association of Zoos and Aquariums (AZA) has developed guidelines for amphibian husbandry which recommend, amongst other things, naturalistic housing conditions that may include running water, embedded plants, and naturalistic substrates [57]. However, animals used in research settings in the United States are subject to The Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals [58] which, amongst other things, advises that “enclosures and accessories … should be sanitized at least once every 2 weeks.” While leeway is given for species-specific housing if deemed appropriate by the local Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), such cleaning measures are seemingly at odds with enclosures that feature natural substrates, field-collected or otherwise.

Here, we aimed to quantify the effect of rearing environment on the growth and development of post-metamorphosis juvenile frogs. Using a laboratory colony of Neotropical pantless treefrogs (Dendropsophus ebraccatus), we raised froglets in three conditions: naturalistic vivaria in groups, “clean” vivaria in groups, or solitary “clean” environments. We weighed frogs to monitor growth and took periodic fecal and skin swab samples to assess the gut and skin microbiome. After 10 weeks in rearing environments, froglets from all three rearing treatments were transferred to naturalistic vivaria and their microbiomes were twice more assessed during the subsequent six months. At two points during the experiment, a subset of animals was euthanized and we assessed gonad and fat body development and sequenced bacteria from the gut itself. We aimed to understand how particular rearing environments would contribute to the animals’ microbiome and if any observable effects in bacterial community diversity and composition would translate into corresponding effects on host survival, growth and development. From a methodological standpoint, we also hoped to determine if non-invasive fecal sampling would provide a convenient way to assess the gut microbiome instead of sacrificing animals. This research contributes to our limited understanding of the role that microbiota may play in maintaining the health of animals in captivity [32].

Materials and methods

Animal rearing

On December 6, 2022 we bred five pairs of D. ebraccatus treefrogs from a captive research colony at Vassar College. Seven adult male and five adult female frogs were placed in a 50 × 50 × 90 cm rain chamber containing artificial pond water (reverse osmosis water plus Josh’s Frogs R/O Rx water conditioner; Josh’s Frogs, Owosso, MI, USA) and an assortment of bromeliad and philodendron plants. Frogs were “rained on” for approximately six hours to stimulate amplexus and were allowed to breed in the rain chamber overnight. All females laid eggs (as determined by lack of visible eggs in the body the next day). The following morning, eggs were gently transferred with forceps from oviposition sites (leaves of plants and the walls of the rain chamber) to mesh screening which allowed maximum oxygen exposure to all surfaces of the eggs. Eggs on mesh were suspended over a basin of artificial pond water and were misted four times daily to ensure sufficient hydration. Three days into development, the basin was flooded with artificial pond water to induce hypoxic stress in embryos and thus stimulate hatching. Tadpoles from all families mixed in the water and were allowed to further develop for another two days post-hatching before the start of experiments.

On December 14, 2022 we haphazardly assigned 120 tadpoles to 12 tanks of 10 tadpoles each. Each tank (25 cm × 15 cm × 15 cm) contained 4 liters of artificial pond water, an air stone for water oxygenation, a layer of calcium enriched gravel on the bottom, and an artificial plant for enrichment. Tadpoles were initially fed powdered Sera Micron tadpole food (Sera, Heinsberg, Germany) and were switched after the first week to a 50/50 mixture of Repashy Community Plus and Repashy Soylent Green (Repashy Specialty Pet Products, Oceanside, CA, USA), three times per week. Uneaten food was always removed before adding new food and 50% of the water was changed weekly.

As tadpoles approached metamorphosis (defined as elbows visible underneath the skin of the body), tanks were checked daily for metamorphs with newly emerged arms. Upon emergence of the arms, metamorphs were removed from tanks and placed into individual 473 ml clear lidded plastic cups with a moistened paper towel to complete metamorphosis. Pantless treefrog metamorphs need 5–6 days to fully resorb the tail into the body. Paper towels used throughout the experiment were always unbleached brown tri-fold paper towels.

At metamorphosis, froglets were assigned to one of three juvenile rearing treatments: naturalistic group housing (hereafter “natural”), group housing in regularly cleaned and sterilized tanks (hereafter “clean”), or solitary housing in regularly cleaned and sterilized cups (hereafter “solo”) (Fig. 1). AZAs Amphibian Husbandry Resource Guide [24, 57]. In brief, the substrate was a mixture of Josh’s Frogs “ABG mix” (charcoal, milled sphagnum moss, fir bark, tree fern, and peat), orchid bark, coconut husk fiber, and sphagnum moss, seeded with isopods and springtails (Colembolla) and topped with terrarium soil. Two plants (one Pothos sp. and one bromeliad) were planted into the substrate. Tanks were set up at least two weeks before froglets were added. Terraria were set up in 40 cm × 24 cm × 32 cm plastic containers with a plastic mesh lid (Lee’s XL Herp Haven, San Marcos, CA, USA). The inside of each lid was covered in small insect window screen to prevent food (fruit flies) from escaping. Clean tanks were the same size, but contained only a layer of new moistened paper towels, two clean one-inch diameter PVC tube couplings, and two 60 mm petri dishes half filled with artificial pond water (reverse osmosis water with Josh’s Frogs R/O Rx added; Josh’s Frogs, Owosso, MI, USA) and a crumpled-up paper towel. The petri dishes served as reservoirs of water in case the paper towels on the bottom of the tank dried out. Solo individuals were kept in clear lidded 473 ml cups with a moistened paper towel. Care was taken to ensure that paper towels in both the clean group tanks and the solitary cups were thoroughly moistened but without standing water on the bottom of the tanks. We set up both group- and individually-housed clean treatments because we and others have raised froglets individually in the past [e.g. [59–61]], and we were interested to know if there were any differences between these conditions.

Froglets were assigned to housing treatments on a rotating basis in order to ensure balance across the window of metamorphosis; thus, for example, the first tadpole to metamorphose was assigned to the natural treatment, the second to metamorphose was assigned to the clean treatment, the third to the solo treatment, the fourth to the natural treatment, and so on. Froglets were placed in their housing treatment one week after the emergence of their arms, at which point they had fully resorbed their tail and were ready to begin feeding. Of the 120 tadpoles that began the experiment, 111 survived to metamorphosis (92.5% survival). Of these, three died while resorbing their tails, leaving a final sample size of 108 froglets at the start of the rearing experiment.

Natural and clean tanks were allocated seven froglets each, except for the final natural tank that was allocated eight froglets and the final clean tank which had nine froglets. For the purposes of statistical comparison, solo housed froglets were considered to be in “pseudo-tanks” of seven individuals. The first tadpole metamorphosed on February 12, 2023 and the last on March 16, 2023, meaning that the entire cohort of tadpoles reached metamorphosis over a 32 day window. Thus, rearing tanks were established at slightly different times based on when tadpoles went through metamorphosis, but given the nature of how froglets were assigned to rearing tanks, this was balanced across treatments. For example, the average date of metamorphosis of the first natural tank was February 27, the first clean tank was February 28, and the first pseudo-tank of solo froglets was also February 28. Five tanks or pseudo-tanks were set up for each rearing treatment.

Metamorphs were not fed prior to being assigned to a housing treatment, as they do not eat during tail resorption. All frogs were fed twice per week with fruit flies (Drosophila hydei) dusted with vitamin powders alternating per feeding (Repashy Calcium Plus and Repashy Supervite). Fruit flies were bred in house from stocks originally obtained from Josh’s Frogs. Prior to each feeding, the clean and solo froglets had their environments thoroughly cleaned and sterilized. Froglets were caught with clean nitrile gloved hands and transferred to a previously sterilized cup. The tank or cup was then thoroughly sprayed out with RO water and lightly scrubbed to remove any feces and residue of dead flies, was sprayed with a food-grade bleach-free hydrogen peroxide disinfectant spray (Clorox, USA) which was allowed to sit on the surface for 60 seconds as per instructions to achieve surface sterilization, then thoroughly rinsed with RO water multiple times to remove any chemical residue. New paper towels moistened with RO water were added back to housing and then frogs were reintroduced. Nitrile gloves were changed between handling frogs. Natural tanks were not cleaned at all during the experiment. After tanks had been cleaned (or not, in the case of natural tanks) fruit flies were added to tanks. We attempted to add enough flies so that there would still be some left at the next feeding. Occasionally if it was observed that all flies had been consumed or had escaped from tanks, we added more in between the main feedings, but this was very infrequent.

During the first four weeks of the experiment, 15 froglets died and the distribution of these deaths was not even across the three rearing treatments (see Results). Two froglets died in natural tanks, one froglet died in a solo cup, and 12 froglets died in clean tanks. Another five froglets died between weeks four and eight (two in natural tanks, one in a solo cup, and two in clean tanks). This left a final sample size of 88 froglets that we obtained continuous data for from metamorphosis through the end of the first phase of the experiment.

We weighed all froglets four and eight weeks after the establishment of each tank or pseudo-tank (Fig. 1). Thus, these samples were collected on different dates but at approximately the same number of days since each froglet had reached metamorphosis. At each of these timepoints, we also collected a fecal sample. Fecal samples were obtained by placing each froglet overnight into a sterilized lidded 473 ml plastic cup with a paper towel moistened with RO water. The following morning, feces were removed from cups with autoclaved wooden toothpicks and placed into 1.5 ml microcentifuge vials, which were frozen at −80 °C immediately after collection. At eight weeks we also collected a skin swab sample of each froglet using a standard swabbing protocol [62]. In brief, we swabbed the ventral surface of each froglet 10 times, the inside of each thigh five times, and each hind foot five times. Swabs were stored in 1.5 ml microcentifuge vials and frozen at −80 °C immediately after collection. The first phase of the experiment ended on May 16, 2023, at which point we once again collected weight, feces, and skin swab samples. Since these data were collected on a single date, samples were collected 60–78 days after tanks had initially been set up. At the end of the first phase of the experiment, 58 froglets were randomly selected (N = 22 natural, 13 clean, and 23 solo) and were euthanized with 20% benzocaine (Orajel, USA) applied topically to the ventral and dorsal surfaces after collection of the swab sample. Following euthanasia, the body cavity was opened, the entire intestinal tract was removed and frozen at −80 °C and we recorded the presence of sex organs. Thus, for each of these 58 froglets we had skin swab samples, fecal samples, and gut samples.

The remaining 30 froglets (N = 10 per treatment) were all placed into six newly established natural tanks on May 17, 2023 (two tanks of five frogs per original treatment). Thus, each of the new tanks contained frogs from different tanks or pseudo-tanks from phase 1, and even frogs reared in natural tanks in phase 1 were placed into new natural tanks for phase 2. We collected fecal samples as described above from all 30 frogs 100 days later, on August 24, 2023. Froglets were returned to their tanks and were raised for another 70 days, until November 2, 2023, at which point all frogs were weighed, swabbed, and euthanized with 20% benzocaine applied topically to the ventral and dorsal surfaces. Prior to this final timepoint there appeared to be a disease outbreak in one tank of previously clean raised froglets and all five individuals in that tank died within a few days of one another, meaning that the sample size at this final timepoint was 25 froglets. Following euthanasia, froglets were dissected and their intestines were removed and frozen at −80 °C. We also removed and weighed any fat bodies present and recorded the presence of sex organs. Thus, for these 25 froglets we had data on weight at five time points, fecal samples at four timepoints, and skin swabs at three timepoints, in addition to sex, fat body and gut samples at approximately 36 weeks after metamorphosis.

Microbiome sample preparation

We used a subset of collected samples for microbiome sequencing to address two questions. First, we randomly selected seven individuals per treatment to compare the bacterial communities of skin swab, fecal samples, and gut samples for individual froglets, resulting in a total of 63 samples. Secondly, we wanted to conduct a longitudinal study of gut bacterial community dynamics over time in the 30 froglets that were raised past the end of the first phase of the experiment. We selected fecal samples collected at 4 weeks, at the end of the first phase, at 100 days, and the guts of the 25 froglets that survived until the end of the experiment. Notably, the first two of these timepoints are from the original rearing treatments and the second two were collected after all froglets had been placed into natural tanks. Three froglets did not produce feces at the end of the first phase of the experiment, resulting in a total of 112 samples.

We extracted DNA with DNeasy kits (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) using the standard protocol for purifying DNA from animal tissue, including a pretreatment for gram-negative bacteria. Gut and fecal samples were homogenized with individual autoclaved mortar and pestles after incubation in lysis buffer. We prepared samples for sequencing using the 515F and 806 R primer pair with barcodes on the forward primer to amplify the V4 region of the 16s rRNA gene [63–65]. Triplicate reactions for each sample were combined, product was verified by gel electrophoresis and then quantified using the high sensitivity assay kit and Qubit fluorometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Samples were pooled into a single library at 180 ng/sample. The pooled sample was cleaned using a Qiaquick PCR purification kit and sent to the Molecular Biology Core Facilities at the Dana Farber Cancer Institute, Harvard University, MA, for sequencing on an Illumina MiSeq using a single-end 250 bp strategy.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R v4.2.2 [66]. Froglets in either of the group-housed treatments (natural or clean) were not able to be distinguished from one another, and thus are all considered to have the same average values with their tank mates for age at metamorphosis. We analyzed variation in weight during phase 1 of the experiment with Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA), using log-transformed tank mean weight as the response variable and days since metamorphosis and rearing treatment as predictor variables. Froglet weight was log-transformed to improve normality. We separately tested for an effect of tank density on log-transformed froglet weight in the natural and clean tanks with ANCOVA. Survival to the end of phase 1 was analyzed with a binomial generalized linear model (GLM). We tested for differences in sexual differentiation at the end of phase 1 with a chi-square test. We tested for differences in log-transformed body mass and fat body mass (corrected for body mass) at the end of the experiment with a linear mixed-effects models (LMMs). Both models included froglet sex and phase 1 rearing treatment as fixed effects and phase 2 tank as a random effect, to account for the common rearing environment of co-housed individuals [67]. Significance of fixed effects was assessed with likelihood ratio tests.

To assess variation in bacterial community diversity (“alpha diversity”), we tested for differences in the effective number of species [68] using linear models. To assess variation in bacterial community composition and structure (“beta diversity”), we tested for differences in Bray-Curtis dissimilarity using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). For the skin, we tested for differences in alpha and beta diversity, respectively, across rearing treatments at the end of phase 1. The gut was sampled twice—at the end of phase 1 and the end of the experiment; thus, we tested for differences in alpha and beta diversity, respectively, across time, treatment, and their interaction. Fecal samples were taken three times—at 4 weeks into the experiment, at the end of phase 1, and 100 days after transfer to naturalistic tanks; thus, we again tested for differences in alpha and beta diversity, respectively, across time, treatment, and their interaction. Post-hoc comparisons were conducted when warranted by significant differences using the emmeans() function [69] for linear models, and pairwise.adonis2() function with fdr correction for PERMANOVA.

To assess changes in the specific taxa of microbes present on and in froglets, we used the LinDA technique as implemented in the MicrobiomeStat package [70]. We used the generate_taxa_test_single() function to assess differences in Amplicon Sequence Variant (ASV) relative abundance across 1) rearing treatments at the end of phase 1 for skin samples, 2) gut samples collected at the end of phase 1 and the end of the experiment, 3) fecal samples collected at the end of phase 1 and after 100 days in naturalistic tanks, and 4) amongst the three sample types collected per individual at the end of phase 1. For all LinDA analyses, bacterial taxa were filtered to a prevalence of at least 0.1 and abundance of at least 0.0001. We further limited our focus to taxa that were significant (p < 0.05) after FDR correction [71] and had a mean relative abundance of at least 0.02 across individuals.

Results

Rearing effects on development and survival

Froglet mass did not differ across the three treatments at the start of the experiment (ANCOVA, F2,12 = 1.45, p = 0.27). However, froglet masses quickly diverged and were influenced by time since metamorphosis, rearing treatment, and their interaction, with froglets in the clean tanks being the heaviest, followed by those in natural tanks, and solo housed froglets being the smallest (Fig. 2A; ANCOVA, time: F1,54 = 738.05, p < 0.0001, treatment: F2,54 = 18.54, p < 0.0001, time*treatment: F2,54 = 11.01, p < 0.0001). However, differences between froglets raised in clean and natural tanks were also explained by differences in density, due to higher mortality in the clean tanks (Fig. 2B& C). Survival to the end of phase 1 differed significantly by rearing treatment, with clean tanks having the lowest survival (Fig. 2B; binomial GLM, treatment: χ2 = 9.44, p = 0.009). When comparing only the clean and natural tanks, there was a significant effect of tank density on froglet weight, but no effect of rearing treatment or interaction between density and treatment (Fig. 2C; binomial GLM, density: F1,36 = 9.57, p = 0.004, treatment: F1,36 = 0.30, p = 0.57, density*treatment: F1,36 = 0.07, p = 0.80). Effects of rearing habitat on the skin microbiome

The skin microbiome was sampled via swabs at the end of phase 1 of the experiment. Rearing froglets in naturalistic tanks did not increase the diversity of their skin bacterial symbiont communities compared to rearing under more sterile conditions (Fig. 3A; LM, F2,17 = 0.3, p = 0.8). The diversity of bacterial species on froglet skin in natural tanks was, however, less variable across individuals, with a variance of 6.2 for natural tanks but 31.2 and 57.2 for clean and solo treatments, respectively (Bartlett test, K2 = 5.8, p = 0.05). Rearing conditions did affect skin bacterial community composition and structure, with froglets from each treatment having significantly different communities from one another (Fig. 3D; PERMANOVA pseudoF2,19 = 2.9, p = 0.001; pairwise comparisons all p ≤ 0.03). Differential abundance analysis identified five genera from the phyla Bacteroidota and Bacillota (synonym Firmicutes) that were associated with rearing environment (Supplementary Table 1). Specifically, frogs raised in natural tanks had less Paenibacillus and Sphingobacterium than those from clean tanks and more Chryseobacterium than frogs from solo cups. Frogs from natural and clean tanks both had less Flavobacterium than frogs from solo environments, and frogs from clean tanks had more Bacteroides than either solo or natural frogs. Effects of rearing habitat on the gut microbiome

The microbial community of froglet guts was sampled twice, at the end of phase 1 of the experiment and again at the end of the experiment. Since sampling gut bacteria required sacrificing froglets, data from the two timepoints were necessarily from different individuals. Bacterial alpha diversity in froglets was similar across rearing treatments but differed based on when samples were collected; diversity significantly increased after froglets had been in naturalistic housing conditions for approximately 5.5 months (Fig. 3B; LM, time, F1,36 = 26.4, p < 0.00001, treatment, F2,36 = 2.3, p = 0.11). Indeed, there was not a significant interaction between time and treatment, indicating that diversity increased across all treatments after the move to naturalistic housing, even for froglets that were in naturalistic housing during phase 1 (time*treatment, F2,36 = 1.4, p = 0.25). It is worth pointing out that there was a significant effect of rearing treatment when samples collected at the end of phase 1 were analyzed by themselves (F2,18 = 4.4, p = 0.023).

Bacterial community structure (beta diversity) differed based on rearing treatment, time of sampling, and the interaction between treatment and time (Fig. 3E; PERMANOVA, time pseudoF1,39 = 7.3, p = 0.001, treatment pseudoF2,39 = 2.2, p = 0.004; time*treatment pseudoF2,39 = 1.9, p = 0.02). Timewise, a large shift in the composition of bacterial communities occurred after the move to natural tanks across all treatments. Post-hoc comparisons revealed that the interaction was the result of communities differing between froglets in both natural and clean tanks compared to those in solo cups at the end of phase 1 (pairwise comparisons; N-S p = 0.04, N-C p = 0.08, C-S p = 0.03), but with communities converging by the end of the experiment leaving essentially no differences amongst treatments (pairwise comparisons; N-S p = 0.08, N-C p = 0.05, C-S p = 0.18).

LinDA analysis revealed that the differences among gut bacterial communities at the end of phase 1 were subtle and occurred amongst relatively low abundance genera (all mean relative abundance p < 0.003; Supplementary Table 1). The shift in gut bacterial communities after froglets were transferred to naturalistic housing was, however, more pronounced (Fig. 3G). In comparison to the end of the experiment, at the end of phase 1 frogs in clean tanks had significantly more Faecalitalea (Bacilli) and Lachnoclostridium (Clostridia), whereas Clostridium (Clostridia) had increased by the end of the experiment. That same Clostridium also increased in frogs originally reared in natural tanks, as did two species of Bacilli (Enterococcus and Lactococcus). Another genus from the Clostridia, Robinsoniella, decreased during that time. Frogs originally from solo cups had the fewest changes in bacterial abundance in their guts, although Faecalitalea (Bacilli) and Bacteroides (Bacteroidia) were both more abundant at the end of phase 1 than at the end of the experiment.

Effects of rearing habitat on the fecal microbiome

Fecal diversity, which we were able to non-invasively sample at three time points, was influenced by both rearing treatment and the time when samples were collected (Fig. 3C; LM, time, F2,78 = 7.1, p = 0.001, treatment F2,78 = 1.9, p = 0.16). There was also a significant interaction between sample time and treatment, indicating that changes in diversity across time differed among rearing treatments (LM, time*treatment, F4,78 = 3.1, p = 0.02). We used post-hoc comparisons to compare longitudinal diversity data for each rearing treatment. Bacterial diversity in the feces of froglets in clean tanks was similar throughout the experiment (all pairwise comparisons p > 0.2), as it was for froglets in natural tanks (all pairwise comparisons p ≥ 0.05). By contrast, for froglets in solo tanks, diversity increased after the transfer to natural tanks (4 weeks vs Ph1End p = 0.9, Ph1End vs 100 Days p = 0.003, 4 weeks vs 100 Days p = 0.001).

Fecal bacterial community composition and structure were also sensitive to rearing conditions and changed over time (Fig. 3F; PERMANOVA, time pseudoF2,78 = 18.8, p = 0.001, treatment pseudoF2,78 = 4.1, p = 0.001). Differences in the composition and structure of fecal bacterial communities due to rearing treatment were detectable by four weeks (pairwise comparisons all p ≤ 0.002) and remained so for the duration of the study (phase 1 end pairwise comparisons all p ≤ 0.003, 100 Days pairwise comparisons all p ≤ 0.03). Again, timewise, bacterial communities of all treatments shifted after the move to natural tanks. The interaction between time and rearing treatment was significant, indicating that differences between rearing treatments changed over time (PERMANOVA, time*treatment pseudoF4,78 = 2.8, p = 0.001). However, this may have been partially driven by changes in dispersion. The dispersion of bacterial communities in fecal samples differed across the three rearing treatments four weeks into the experiment (betadisper, F2,27 = 4.1, p = 0.03), but converged by the end of phase 1 and did not differ 100 days later (betadisper, phase 1 end, F2,45 = 1.1, p = 0.33, 100 days, F2,27 = 0.1, p = 0.99).

The bacterial communities of fecal samples changed considerably from the end of phase 1 to 100 days after all frogs were moved to naturalistic housing conditions, and these changes in relative abundance were generally consistent regardless of original rearing treatment (Fig. 3G, Supplementary Table 3). Most bacterial taxa increased in relative abundance with the change in housing; of 15 bacterial taxa that changed in abundance, 10 increased and five decreased. Frogs from all three original rearing environments experienced increases in Bacillus (Bacilli), Acinetobacter (Gammaproteobacteria), and two ASVs not classified to genus from the Gammaproteobacteria and Clostridia, as well as decreases in abundance of Providencia and Morganella (Gammaproteobacteria). The largest magnitude changes in abundance were in frogs originally raised in solo and clean environments.

Comparison of bacterial communities across skin, gut and feces

At the end of the first phase of the experiment we simultaneously sampled the bacterial communities of the skin, gut, and feces of 21 froglets (N = 7 per rearing treatment). Froglet feces were rich in bacteria, and, along with the gut, tended to harbor a greater assortment of bacteria than the skin (Fig. 4A; LMM, χ2 = 8.5, p = 0.011). Pairwise comparisons found that fecal samples had significantly more bacterial species than skin, but neither were different from gut samples (post-hoc comparisons: fecal vs swab – p = 0.013, all others p > 0.14). We also found that skin, guts and feces contained distinct communities of bacteria (Fig. 4B; PERMANOVA pseudoF2,59 = 26.25, p = 0.001, all post-hoc comparisons p = 0.001). Collectively, 272 ASVs were associated with these samples, 22% of which (N = 60) were found across all three sample types (Fig. 4C). Nonetheless, the bacterial communities of feces and guts were much more similar to one another than either was to skin. Of the 163 ASVs found in gut samples, 88% (N = 144) were also found in feces. There was more overlap in the ASVs shared across just the gut and feces (N = 84; 31%) than those shared only across feces and on the skin (N = 30; 11%) or only by the skin and gut (N = 5; 2%). Skin also harbored the most unique ASVs not found in other sample types (N = 47) as compared with feces and guts (N = 32 and 14, respectively). Moreover, gut and fecal diversity of individuals were significantly correlated (Fig. 4D; Pearson’s product moment correlation, gut vs fecal p = 0.003), whereas neither were correlated with diversity of the skin microbiota (Pearson’s product moment correlation, gut vs. skin p = 0.65; fecal vs. skin p = 0.54). The skin community was also more variable in composition and structure among individuals than the gut or feces (betadisper F2,59 = 15.4, p < 0.001, TukeyHSD skin-feces & skin-gut p ≤ 0.002, gut-feces p = 0.14).

Comparing the relative abundance of bacteria in the three sample types reinforced that the skin microbiome was considerably different from guts or feces (Fig. 4E), harboring significantly more Sphingobacterium (Bacteroidia), Chryseobacterium (Bacteroidia), Acinetobacter (Gammaproteobacteria), and Comomonas (Gammaproteobacteria), and significantly less Faecalitalea (Bacilli), Bacteroides (Bacteroidota), Lachnoclostridium (Clostridia), Crenobacter (Gammaproteobacteria), Morganella (Gammaproteobacteria) and Akkermansia (Verrucomicrobiae), along with an unknown species of Clostridia (Supplementary Table 4). As might be expected, some bacterial taxa were more abundant in feces compared to guts despite being present in both. Despite some differences in composition and structure, the microbial community of fecal samples proved to be highly representative of the gut samples from corresponding frogs (Figs. 4F and 4G).

Discussion

Animals raised in captivity are inherently removed from their natural environments and often suffer differences in growth and development relative to their wild counterparts. These differences may include altered physiology [72], obesity [73, 74], and abnormal behaviors [75]. Some differences between wild and captive environments are obvious and relatively well-understood; for example, depauperate social environments lead to observable declines in the health of captive rodents, and as such the law in the United States and Europe requires that rodents have access to environmental enrichment and should be housed in groups unless otherwise necessary for research [58]. Less obvious are aspects of the captive environment that are not easily observable with the naked eye, such as the community of microbiota living in and around captive animals. The microbiomes of captive animals often differ from those of their wild counterparts [33, 76–78], and recently there have been calls to consider the microbiome to improve animal welfare and conservation [32, 33, 79, 80]. However, little is known about the microbial needs of animals in captivity, particularly those that have not been studied as extensively as rats and mice or other vertebrate model organisms (e.g., zebrafish, Xenopus) [81, 82].

We found that housing froglets in groups was beneficial for overall health, as measured by size and rate of sexual maturation, and that in particular naturalistic housing conditions improved survival in addition to size. Specifically, froglets raised in groups in both the clean and natural environments were larger and had greater rates of sexual differentiation after approximately 10 weeks than froglets housed singly. In addition, froglets in clean tanks were larger than those in natural tanks. This latter effect appeared to be driven by greater mortality in clean tanks, as accounting for density due to differential survival across tanks removed treatment differences (Fig. 2C). The fact that froglets in solo tanks were smaller and less differentiated is likely at least partly due to the size of the enclosures. Our solo containers were 473 ml plastic cups, whereas our group enclosures were roughly 29,500 ml, or about 4,214 ml per froglet. The actual space available to froglets in natural tanks was considerably less, approximately 3,000 ml per froglet, assuming that approximately ¼ of the tank was taken up with soil and plants. Thus, animals in natural and clean tanks had roughly 6–9 times more space per froglet than individuals in solo cups. Relatively little is known about the effects of enclosure size in captive juvenile amphibians, but the size of field enclosures is positively correlated with growth, development, and survival in juvenile frogs and toads [83–85]. The optimal size of captive enclosures will reflect the practical constraints of individual setups. In our experiment, it was not feasible to house solo froglets in tanks that would provide the same space allocation as the natural and clean treatments.

Differences in body size had largely equilibrated by the end of the experiment, at which point frogs from the three original rearing conditions had been housed in naturalistic conditions for nearly six months. There did seem to be some lasting effects of the original rearing treatments, with males originally from clean tanks having the greatest accumulation of fat bodies, although the ramifications of this are unclear. Fat bodies in amphibians are found in association with the gonads in both males and females, increasing in size during the breeding season to provide nutrients to gonads [86] and removal of fat bodies in females reduces the size of ovulated eggs [87]. Harmon et al. [88] found that overfeeding amphibians in captivity led to obesity, which in turn appeared to negatively contribute to offspring survival, although this effect was seen primarily in females and a mechanism for this is not yet known. Considering growth development and survival together, naturalistic vivaria appear to be the best overall option and may also be for other species of amphibians [89, 90].

We did not parse the mechanisms by which naturalistic and group housing affected growth and survival. Prior studies have demonstrated that providing cover or shelter improves growth rates in captive amphibians, possibly by reducing stress or negative intraspecific interactions [reviewed by 91]. Environments that are naturalistic or simply larger could improve health by promoting the expression of normal behaviors (e.g., foraging) and activity levels [89]. However, the differences we found may have been related to the microbiome, as microbiota are increasingly recognized as mediators of host metabolism and physiological responses to stress [40, 91, 92]. Intriguingly, Michaels et al. [93] found that changes in the skin microbiota resulting from including a plant in the enclosure of red-eyed treefrogs (Agalychnis callidryas) not only enhanced bacterial diversity and abundance associated with the skin but also improved frog growth rates. Similarly, we found that rearing environment affected both the skin and gut bacterial communities of young pantless treefrogs from the three treatments. Further research is necessary, however, to determine if any of the bacteria we identified as differentially abundant across treatments are linked to frog health, in either positive or negative directions [32]. It may be that there are keystone bacterial species that are enriched in naturalistic environments that contribute positively to froglet growth and development, or detrimental bacterial species that are able to proliferate under “cleaner” conditions. These hypotheses could be tested through probiotic administration of cultured isolates, which has been shown to increase growth and immune function in other species of frogs [94–97] or assessment of functional changes in the microbiome via -omics approaches or direct quantification of microbial metabolites [98, 99].

Transferring frogs from highly simplified and routinely sterilized environments to natural tanks partway through the experiment increased bacterial community diversity and elicited homogenizing effects on the gut microbiota across rearing treatments, indicating that it is possible for the microbiome to be influenced by a change in the captive environment. Dispersal processes likely contributed to these changes. First, froglets likely acquired new microbes from the substrate in the naturalistic housing [44, 56, 100]. These may have been introduced into the gut through feeding, as juveniles ingested flies or soil and their respective microbial consortia. Future studies should investigate the effect of varying the diet on gut and fecal microbiomes. Second, froglets may have picked up additional microbes from other individuals they were housed with [46, 48, 49]. This helps explain why even individuals that were reared under naturalistic housing conditions in phase 1 of the experiment showed increases in diversity and shifts in composition, as they were combined with new tank mates into new tanks during the second phase. Overall, our results indicate that assembly dynamics within the gut microbiota are sensitive to social and environmental conditions in captivity; thus, it may be prudent to consider these factors alongside more typical factors such as diet, light, temperature, and moisture requirements in the design and implementation of captive husbandry [32, 33, 79, 101].

Relative to the skin, gut and fecal assemblages showed strong congruence in bacterial community diversity and structure, as well as responses to treatment. When samples were collected from a given individual, the alpha diversity of gut and fecal samples were highly correlated with one another but neither was with skin diversity. Furthermore, the taxonomic profiles of the gut and feces were much more similar than either were to the skin. Both types of samples harbored high relative abundances of several genera commonly associated with anaerobic fermentation in the gut, i.e., Bacteroides, Faecalitalea, Clostridium, and Morganella. Akkermansia—a mucin-degrading bacterium found in the mucus layer of the intestine—was also relatively abundant in these samples. Some structural differences did exist, however. As bacterial communities progressed from the internal environment of the gut to the external environment in feces, there were increases in the relative abundance of bacterial genera found on the skin (e.g., Sphingobacterium, Chryseobacterium, Acinetobacter, Comamonas), as well as aerobic genera (e.g., Crenobacter).

Nonetheless, fecal microbiota provided a good indication of the changes that were occurring in the gut in response to housing conditions: we saw (1) variation in both gut and fecal microbiota among housing conditions at the end of phase 1, (2) a substantial increase in the diversity of gut and fecal microbiota of froglets from the solo treatment after transfer to natural tanks, and (3) a shift in the structure of both the gut and fecal microbiota after the transfer to natural tanks. Only a few studies have compared lethal and non-lethal sampling methods to assess the gut microbiota in amphibians. A study of cane toads (Rhinella marina) found that cloacal swabs were a better approximation of the microbiota of the large intestine [102]; however, cloacal swabs are unlikely to be a feasible option when the host species is very small or immature. A second study, focused on Asiatic toad (Bufo gargarizans) tadpoles, found substantial overlap in the composition of the gut and fecal microbiota, but that similarity was influenced by DNA extraction method [103]. Considering all of these results together, we conclude that noninvasive sampling is informative for understanding changes in the gut microbiome, but that invasive sampling may be necessary when one seeks precise taxonomic or functional information regarding the gut microbiota [104].

Conclusions

Improving the health of captive animals has long been a priority for the businesses, institutions, and individuals that maintain them, and advances have certainly been made in understanding the needs of some animals [29, 90, 105]. While the focus of those overseeing animal research has understandably been the nutrition and abiotic conditions of captive animals [57, 106], researchers have recently begun to appreciate the importance of the microbes that live in and on animals for their health and normal functioning. However, we know unfortunately little about the microbial needs of most animals, or even what represents a “healthy” microbiome. Amphibians, with the diverse ecological niches they fill, are likely to have species-specific needs and it will be important for researchers to determine the needs of their own study organisms [107]. Our study suggests that naturalistic housing can improve the overall health and development of captive amphibians and that these improvements may occur by facilitating a more stable and diverse microbiome. Importantly, these housing conditions are likely to be at odds with more sterile or isolated housing options that might be recommended at many institutions [58]. Lastly, our study demonstrates that feces may be useful as a biomarker to understand and monitor gut/host health [108, 109], complementary to other non-invasive approaches [110, 111].
