Association of time of day at stroke onset with functional outcomes after reperfusion therapy in acute ischaemic stroke: a retrospective cohort study in China
Authors: Yiqing Wang, Shenghao Fan, Shicun Huang, Man Lu, Yi Yang, Haifeng Lu, Qi Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo investigate the association between the time of day of stroke onset and functional outcomes among patients with acute ischaemic stroke who received reperfusion therapy.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingPatients with acute ischaemic stroke who received reperfusion therapy (intravenous thrombolysis or combined endovascular therapy) between October 2018 and March 2022 in China were included.ParticipantsA total of 712 patients with acute ischaemic stroke receiving reperfusion therapy were included. Patients were categorised into two groups (day-onset (06:00–18:00) and night-onset (18:00–06:00)) and into six groups by 4-hour intervals.Outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the 3-month functional status, evaluated using the modified Rankin Scale, with scores of 0–2 classified as favourable and scores of 3–6 as unfavourable outcomes. Propensity score matching (PSM), inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and overlap weighting (OW) were used for analyses.Results148 out of 286 patients (51.7%) in the night-onset group and 182 out of 426 patients (42.7%) in the day-onset group experienced unfavourable outcomes at 3 months. Night-onset stroke was associated with a significantly higher risk of unfavourable outcome compared with day-onset stroke (OR 1.44 (95% CI 1.06 to 1.94), p=0.018). This association remained significant after adjustment using PSM (OR 1.45 (95% CI 1.01 to 2.08), p=0.044), and was further confirmed in analyses using IPTW (OR 1.42 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.96), p=0.036), OW (OR 1.42 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.96), p=0.032) and multivariable adjustment (adjusted OR 1.55 (95% CI 1.07 to 2.22), p=0.019). Night-onset stroke during 22:00–02:00 (adjusted OR 2.44 (95% CI 1.34 to 4.43), p=0.003) was significantly associated with a higher likelihood of unfavourable outcome in adjusted analysis.ConclusionsNight-onset stroke was associated with poorer functional outcomes at 3 months among patients receiving reperfusion therapy. Further multicentre prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings.Trial registration numberChinese Clinical Trial Registry Identifier: ChiCTR2500101844.

Introduction

Cerebrovascular and cardiovascular events such as stroke and myocardial infarction exhibit a well-recognised circadian pattern.1–4 Numerous physiological processes that influence stroke outcomes, including blood pressure, metabolic activity, platelet aggregation, endothelial function and immune mechanisms, are modulated by the circadian clock.5–8 In acute ischaemic stroke, growing evidence suggests that the timing of stroke onset may influence clinical outcomes by affecting the progression of the ischaemic core, susceptibility to reperfusion injury and thrombotic risk.9 10

Circadian rhythms may influence the efficacy of treatment in acute ischaemic stroke. An experimental study has demonstrated that neuroprotective agents are more effective during the daytime (the inactive phase for rodents) compared with the nighttime active period.11 However, whether the therapeutic efficacy of intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) or endovascular therapy (EVT), the standard interventions for acute ischaemic stroke, is modulated by circadian mechanisms—particularly in relation to long-term functional outcomes—remains inadequately understood. Recent studies have investigated the impact of stroke onset time on clinical outcomes following IVT or EVT, yielding inconsistent results.12–16

In this study, we aimed to assess the association between stroke onset time and 3-month neurological outcomes in patients treated with IVT alone or in combination with EVT. We hypothesised that stroke onset time would significantly influence mid-term neurological outcomes in this population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective, single-centre study included 712 consecutive patients diagnosed with acute ischaemic stroke at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University from October 2018 to March 2022.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) acute ischaemic stroke receiving IVT; (2) the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score before the onset of stroke was 0 or 1. The exclusion criteria were: (1) occlusion in the posterior circulation; (2) the exact time of onset was unclear and (3) missing clinical data. The use of IVT and bridging endovascular thrombectomy adhered to the 2018 Guidelines for the Early Management of Patients With Acute Ischaemic Stroke and the eligibility criteria of the DAWN trial.17 18

Clinical data collection

Two researchers independently reviewed the Health Information System to extract patient demographics, medical history and clinical characteristics. Demographic variables included age and sex. Medical history variables encompassed hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, peripheral vascular disease, atrial fibrillation, transient ischaemic attack (TIA), hyperglycaemia, prestroke antiplatelet use, prestroke statin use and current smoking status. Clinical characteristics included stroke subtype (large artery atherosclerosis (LAA), cardioembolism (CE), small vessel occlusion (SVO), other-determined and undetermined), onset-to-door time, door-to-needle time, the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, Alberta Stroke Programme Early CT Score, endovascular thrombectomy and mRS score.

Stroke onset time was determined by a neurologist via interviews with patients and/or witnesses who observed onset of the symptoms. For patients presenting with wake-up strokes, defined as those last seen normal at bedtime, the estimated onset time was calculated as the average time between the time last seen well and time of symptom recognition.12 19 The efficacy of IVT was evaluated based on early neurological improvement at 24 hours after treatment. Successful reperfusion was defined as a reduction of ≥4 points in the NIHSS score from baseline or an NIHSS score of 0.20 For patients undergoing bridging EVT, recanalisation was angiographically assessed on the final digital subtraction angiography run using the modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (mTICI) scale, with successful recanalisation prespecified as an mTICI grade of 2b or 3.18 Based on the time of symptom onset, all patients were categorised into a day-onset group (06:00–18:00) and a night-onset group (18:00–06:00). For a more detailed temporal analysis, patients were further stratified into 4-hour onset intervals. The 3-month mRS score was collected via structured follow-up by designated personnel who were blinded to patients’ time of onset and documented in the electronic medical records.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was the 3-month functional status, evaluated using the mRS, with scores of 0–2 classified as favourable and scores of 3–6 as unfavourable outcomes.

Exploratory outcomes included haemorrhagic transformation, the status of collateral circulation, the volume of the ischaemic core, mismatch ratio and the volume of the penumbra.

The collateral circulation status was evaluated using the regional leptomeningeal collateral (rLMC) score.21

CT perfusion data were postprocessed using MIstar software (Apollo Medical Imaging Technology, Melbourne, Australia). Perfusion parameters were computed using a deconvolution algorithm on a voxel-by-voxel basis. CT acquisition protocols and image reconstruction parameters are provided in online supplemental table S1. The volume of the ischaemic core was defined as the region of hypoperfusion where the relative cerebral blood flow (rCBF) was less than 30%.22 The mismatch ratio was calculated as the total hypoperfusion volume (delay time (DT) >3 s) divided by the ischaemic core volume (rCBF <30%).23 DT >3 s was used to delineate the hypoperfusion lesion. The penumbral volume was calculated by subtracting the ischaemic core volume from the total hypoperfused tissue volume.23

Statistical analysis

To account for baseline differences between groups, propensity scores were estimated using multivariable logistic regression incorporating patient and clinical characteristics.24 Three propensity score-based adjustment methods were applied: propensity score matching (PSM), inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and overlap weighting (OW).25 Treatment effects were evaluated in one-to-one matched cohorts using PSM with a greedy matching algorithm and a calliper width of 0.2 times the pooled SD. IPTW and OW were further employed to estimate the average treatment effect in the overall study population.26 For IPTW, patients with daytime onset were weighted by the inverse of their propensity score, while those with nighttime onset were weighted by the inverse of one minus the propensity score. In OW, patients with daytime onset were assigned a weight of one minus the propensity score, whereas those with nighttime onset were weighted by the propensity score. Covariate balance after adjustment was assessed using standardised mean differences (SMDs) and visualised through propensity score density plots.

Continuous variables are reported as mean (SD) or median (IQR), as appropriate, while categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. The primary and exploratory outcomes were analysed using univariate logistic regression or generalised linear models, with log10 transformation applied to variables exhibiting skewed distributions. A logistic regression model was used to investigate the association between stroke onset time and functional outcome at 3 months. Given that reperfusion success or failure may also influence 3-month functional outcomes, we additionally adjusted for reperfusion status alongside all covariates listed in table 1 using multivariable logistic regression. For the primary outcome, subgroup analysis was further conducted in terms of age (<60 vs ≥60 years), sex (male vs female), history of hypertension (yes vs no), diabetes mellitus (yes vs no), atrial fibrillation (yes vs no), TIA (yes vs no) and stroke subtype (LAA vs CE vs SVO vs other-determined vs undetermined) in the unmatched cohort. Sensitivity analyses were performed by (1) excluding patients with wake-up stroke; (2) separately analysing patients who received IVT alone and those who received IVT combined with EVT and (3) conducting ordinal logistic regression with the 3-month mRS (0–6) as the dependent variable, adjusting for all covariates listed in table 1.

A post hoc sample size assessment was performed based on the observed incidence of unfavourable functional outcomes, which was approximately 50% among patients with night-onset stroke. Assuming a 25% relative reduction among patients with day-onset stroke, corresponding to an absolute difference of 12.5 percentage points, a minimum of 246 patients per group (492 patients in total) was required to achieve 80% power at a two-sided α level of 0.05.

There were no missing data for the primary outcome. Covariates (coronary artery disease 1.5%, TIA 1.2%) exhibited less than 5% missing data, and multiple imputation was performed using the mice package in R. A two-tailed p value of <0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (V.4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Patient and public involvement

We recognise the importance of patient and public involvement in research. However, there was no patient or public involvement in this study due to the retrospective nature of the design.

Results

A total of 1061 patients presenting with acute ischaemic stroke and treated with recombinant alteplase (rt-PA) infusion were screened for eligibility (figure 1). Of these, 712 patients were finally included: 426 experienced symptom onset during daytime hours and 286 during nighttime hours. The median patient age was 70 years (IQR 58–76), and 466 patients (65.4%) were male. Among the included patients, 614 (86.2%) received IVT alone, while 98 (13.8%) received bridging EVT (IVT followed by EVT). Patient demographics, medical history and clinical characteristics were summarised in table 1. Several baseline characteristics, including age, history of hypertension, atrial fibrillation, prestroke antiplatelet use, onset-to-door time and endovascular thrombectomy, differed between groups (SMD >0.1). After adjustment using PSM, IPTW and OW methods, the baseline covariates were balanced between groups (table 1, online supplemental table S2), as further confirmed by the SMD distribution plot (online supplemental figure S1) and the propensity score distribution plot (online supplemental figure S2).

Study flow diagram. IPTW, inverse probability of treatment weighting; OW, overlap weighting; PSM, propensity score matching.

Primary outcome

In the unadjusted cohort, 148 out of 286 patients (51.7%) in the night-onset group and 182 out of 426 patients (42.7%) in the day-onset group experienced an unfavourable outcome at 3 months. Night-onset stroke was associated with a significantly higher risk of unfavourable outcome compared with day-onset stroke (OR 1.44 (95% CI 1.06 to 1.94), p=0.018) (figure 2). This association remained significant after adjustment using PSM (OR 1.45 (95% CI 1.01 to 2.08), p=0.044), and was further confirmed in analyses using IPTW (OR 1.42 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.96), p=0.036) and OW (OR 1.42 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.96), p=0.032). In addition, successful reperfusion was achieved in 407 patients (66.2%) treated with IVT alone and in 83 patients (84.6%) undergoing IVT combined with EVT. After multivariable adjustment, night-onset stroke remained associated with a significantly higher risk of unfavourable outcome (adjusted OR 1.55 (95% CI 1.07 to 2.22), p=0.019).

When stroke onset times were grouped by 4-hour intervals, 169 patients (23.7%) presented between 06:00 and 10:00, 118 (16.6%) between 10:00 and 14:00, 139 (19.5%) between 14:00 and 18:00, 167 (23.5%) between 18:00 and 22:00, 69 (9.7%) between 22:00 and 02:00, and 50 (7.0%) between 02:00 and 06:00 (table 2). Patients with symptom onset at night exhibited higher ischaemic core volume compared with those with daytime onset, both in the unadjusted and after adjustment using PSM, IPTW and OW method (table 3, online supplemental table S3). Sensitivity analysis

First, after excluding 132 patients with wake-up stroke (82 in the day-onset group and 50 in the night-onset group), nighttime onset remained significantly associated with unfavourable 3-month functional outcome compared with daytime onset (51.3% vs 42.4%; adjusted OR 1.53 (95% CI 1.02 to 2.30), p=0.039). Second, among patients treated with IVT alone, nighttime onset was also significantly associated with an unfavourable 3-month functional outcome (51.9% vs 42.7%; adjusted OR 1.61 (95% CI 1.07 to 2.38), p=0.021). However, among patients who received IVT combined with EVT, the between-group difference was not statistically significant (51.0% vs 42.9%; adjusted OR 1.18 (95% CI 0.40 to 3.45), p=0.769) (online supplemental table S4). Finally, when analysed using ordinal logistic regression with the 3-month mRS (0–6) as the dependent variable, nighttime onset remained significantly associated with worse 3-month functional outcome compared with daytime onset (adjusted OR 1.38 (95% CI 1.04 to 1.83), p=0.028) (online supplemental table S5).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of 712 patients with acute ischaemic stroke who underwent IVT, we found that night-onset stroke was significantly associated with an increased risk of unfavourable functional outcomes at 3 months compared with day-onset stroke. Further temporal stratification showed that symptom onset between 22:00 and 02:00 was associated with a lower likelihood of achieving a favourable functional outcome at 3 months. Night-onset stroke was also associated with larger ischaemic core volume than day-onset stroke. No significant intergroup differences were observed in terms of haemorrhagic transformation, collateral circulation, mismatch ratio or penumbra volume.

Emerging evidence suggests that circadian regulation is increasingly recognised as an important contributor to stroke progression. Fundamental stroke risk factors, including blood pressure and metabolic homeostasis, along with key mechanisms involved in stroke onset and injury progression—such as endothelial function, platelet aggregation and thrombus formation—are all modulated by circadian rhythms.8 10 27 28 Core clock genes regulate the expression of up to 40% of all genes, including those involved in inflammation, metabolism and cell death.10 29 An experimental study using mouse neurons subjected to oxygen and glucose deprivation demonstrated that the inactive phase (daytime in rodents) induced greater release of glutamate and reactive oxygen species, as well as enhanced activation of apoptotic pathways, compared with the active phase (nighttime in rodents).11 Lu30 demonstrated in a mouse stroke model that circadian rhythms modulate infarct volume via autophagy-dependent degradation of the GluA1 receptor, and that knockout of the core clock gene Per1 abolishes this rhythmic neuroprotection. Mergenthaler proposed that the brain is more susceptible to ischaemic damage and neurodegeneration during sleep.10

Many clinical studies have investigated the association between circadian rhythms and stroke outcomes.9 12 13 31–33 A recent cohort study demonstrated that strokes occurring at night, compared with those with daytime onset, are associated with greater neurological severity at presentation, a higher incidence of early neurological deterioration and poorer functional outcome at 3 months.13 However, as approximately 35% of patients in that study received revascularisation therapy, the findings may have been influenced by potential confounding factors. In our study, we specifically included patients who underwent IVT or combined endovascular thrombectomy therapy for revascularisation. In addition, we applied four distinct statistical methods to adjust for potential confounders, consistently confirming an association between night-onset strokes and worse outcomes. Another retrospective cohort study involving 1558 acute ischaemic stroke patients treated with EVT demonstrated that EVT procedures initiated in the morning (08:00–10:20) were associated with more favourable midterm functional outcomes, whereas those starting at the end of the working day (15:54–17:15) were associated with less favourable neurological results.31 In our study, the majority of patients received IVT alone, with only 13% undergoing additional EVT. Our findings revealed that stroke onset during the period of 22:00–02:00 was associated with a lower likelihood of achieving favourable functional outcomes at 3 months. Nevertheless, the evidence remains inconclusive. A retrospective cohort study conducted in China demonstrated that circadian rhythms had no significant impact on the clinical outcomes in acute ischaemic stroke patients treated with rt-PA, although nighttime onset was associated with more pronounced neurological deficits before thrombolysis.15 Consistent with that study, our cohort also showed short onset-to-door intervals and poor baseline neurological status for nighttime-onset stroke. Crucially, we tracked the functional outcomes at 3 months, whereas the earlier study evaluated only 7-day neurological improvement and mortality. Differences in initial stroke severity, reflected by the lower admission NIHSS scores in our population, may contribute to the divergent findings.

The ischaemic core, representing irreversibly infarcted brain tissue in acute ischaemic stroke, plays a pivotal role in guiding therapeutic decisions and predicting clinical outcomes. Chen et al demonstrated that larger baseline core volumes were significantly associated with poorer functional outcomes in patients receiving IVT alone.32 Specifically, patients with a baseline ischaemic core volume of <5 mL had a markedly higher probability of being disability-free at 90 days compared with those with core volumes >30 mL. A retrospective study further indicated that stroke symptom onset during nighttime was associated with larger ischaemic core volumes at admission relative to daytime onset, with the most extensive core volumes observed in patients whose symptoms began around 23:00.12 Consistently, another large cohort study based on a national database reported that nighttime stroke onset was associated with greater ischaemic core volumes and poorer collateral status relative to daytime onset, with the peak imaging typically obtained around the same time at night (03:00–04:00).33 In line with previous reports, our study also investigated the association between stroke onset time and ischaemic core volume, revealing larger core volumes in patients with nighttime stroke onset. Nevertheless, no significant between-group difference was found in collateral status. This divergence from earlier studies may stem from methodological differences in collateral assessment: we quantified collateral flow with the straightforward and clinically practical rLMC score rather than the hypoperfusion intensity ratio. In acute ischaemic stroke with large vessel occlusion, the core infarct volume grows more rapidly at night compared with during the day.34 A faster core infarct growth rate is associated with worse 90-day functional clinical outcomes in these patients.35 This suggests that stroke treatment workflows should be further expedited for patients with nighttime onset to minimise time to reperfusion therapy. It is recommended that patients with acute ischaemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion occurring at night be directly transferred to an endovascular-capable stroke centre.36

To further minimise potential confounding arising from differences in reperfusion success, we additionally included reperfusion status as a covariate in the multivariable regression models. After this additional adjustment, nighttime onset remained associated with an unfavourable outcome. The exact onset time of wake-up stroke cannot be reliably determined, potentially resulting in exposure misclassification. However, a previous study has shown that most wake-up strokes occur during the early morning hours,37 which may partly mitigate this potential bias. To address this concern, we repeated the analysis after excluding all 132 patients with wake-up stroke. The association between nighttime onset and an unfavourable functional outcome at 3 months remained statistically significant. In the smaller bridging therapy subgroup, the proportion of patients with an unfavourable outcome was numerically higher among those with nighttime onset than among those with daytime onset (51.0% vs 42.9%). However, the between-group difference was not statistically significant, most likely because the small sample size resulted in insufficient statistical power.

Our study has some limitations. First, despite the use of PSM, IPTW and OW to minimise selection bias between daytime- and nighttime-onset groups, unavailable measures (eg, door-to-imaging time, imaging-to-needle time and door-to-groin puncture time among patients undergoing EVT) and other limitations of the retrospective design may still introduce residual confounding. Nevertheless, two key treatment-process metrics, onset-to-door time and door-to-needle time, were comparable between groups after adjustment. Second, early neurological deterioration, which reflects infarct progression, was not assessed. However, we examined the penumbra volume, which may serve as an indirect indicator of the potential for infarct growth.38 Third, given the distinct pathophysiology and treatment response of posterior circulation strokes and the difficulty in determining their onset time, these cases (approximately 25% of the screened population) were excluded, which may limit the generalisability of our findings. Fourth, achieving greater temporal granularity requires a larger sample size. The relatively small numbers of patients in certain 4-hour intervals resulted in wider CIs; therefore, these interval-specific estimates should be interpreted with caution.

In conclusion, night-onset stroke was associated with poorer functional outcomes at 3 months among patients receiving reperfusion therapy. Further multicentre prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings.
