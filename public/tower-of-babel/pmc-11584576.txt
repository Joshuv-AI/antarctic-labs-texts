Rearing laying hens: Early environmental complexity and genetic strain have life-long effects on keel bone size and fractures☆
Authors: Ana K. Rentsch, Vickneswary Aingkaran, Erin Ross, Tina M. Widowski
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Keel bone damage (KBD) is a major welfare concern for laying hens. Environmental complexity during rearing is suggested to promote skeletal development and reduce KB fractures (KBF). We investigated the effect of rearing environment and genetic strain on KB development and health. Three consecutive replicate flocks of brown (B) and white-feathered (W) layer chicks (3000/ flock) were raised in three styles of rearing aviaries of Low,Mid, or High complexity and in conventional cages (Conv). Pullets from flocks 1 and 3 (1080/ flock) were subsequently housed in 24 furnished cages of two sizes (small: 30 vs large: 60 birds) for lay. Samples of pullets and hens were assessed as follows: Dissections at six, 11, and 16 WoA in flocks 1 and 2 for KB size, radiographs at 30, 50, and 70 WoA in flocks 1 and 3, for KBD and dissections at 70 WoA in flock 3 for KBD.Rearing complexity affected KB size of pullets (Conv < High < Mid, Low; P< 0.0001). W pullets had larger keels than B when adjusted for body weight throughout rearing (strain x age P= 0.005) and at 70 WoA (P< 0.001). KBF prevalence at end-of-lay was affected by rearing complexity (High < Low, Conv, P= 0.002). During lay, KBF severity was affected by rearing complexity (High < Low, Mid; P= 0.007), strain (W < B; P< 0.0001) and age (30 < 50 < 70 WoA; P< 0.0001). At 70 WoA, an interaction of rearing by strain affected KBF severity (most severe: B-Conv, least severe: B-Mid and W-High, P= 0.003). KBF severity scores from radiographs and dissections corresponded moderately (r= 0.547, P< 0.0001). The severity of KB deviations was affected by the size of the furnished cages (large > small, P= 0.049).In conclusion, rearing complexity and strain affected KB size and fracture severity and prevalence. These data confirm that complex rearing aviaries that are known to promote exercise reduce KBF severity and prevalence throughout lay.

Introduction

The high prevalence of hens with keel bone fractures (KBF) is a major welfare concern of the egg production industry (Widowski and Rentsch, 2022). Laying hens' susceptibility to KBF is largely attributed to the high demand for calcium for eggshell formation (Sinclair-Black et al., 2023) and opportunities for collisions with structural elements in commercial housing systems (Sandilands et al., 2009), such as elevated metal perches. However, KBF are found in all housing systems (Rufener and Makagon, 2020), even those without said furnishings, suggesting multiple etiologies (Thøfner et al., 2020). Commonly, higher prevalences of KBFs are reported in brown-feathered genetic strains compared to white strains (Rufener and Makagon, 2020). However, brown hens accumulate incrementally fewer fractures after ca. 50 weeks of age (WoA), while white hens continue accumulating KBF (Rufener and Makagon, 2020). Different genetic lines of commercial laying hens vary in KB characteristics, with brown-feathered genetic strains having proportionally smaller KBs compared to white-feathered strains at the end of rearing (Fawcett et al., 2020; Pufall et al., 2021) and white adult hens having lower keel bone density than brown hens (Gebhardt-Henrich et al., 2017). While genetic selection for stronger bones and calcium supplementation through nutrition have ameliorated osteoporosis (weak bones) in laying hens to a degree (Widowski and Rentsch, 2022), another promising avenue has been considered more recently: the use of housing design to promote skeletal development during the rearing period (Widowski and Torrey, 2018; Pufall et al., 2021; Vitienes et al., 2023).

A hen's skeleton develops mainly during growth, but the KB is not fully calcified until about 40 WoA (Buckner et al., 1949), several months after reaching sexual maturation and the onset of lay. During skeletal development, pullets (young hens) are particularly sensitive to environmental factors that facilitate bone-loading exercise (Regmi et al., 2015). Heightened plasticity during skeletal development is a phenomenon that is well understood in other species (Currey, 2003), such as humans (Vicente-Rodríguez, 2006) and mice (Gardinier et al., 2018). Pullets reared in multi-tier non-cage systems (aviaries), the most spatially complex housing type for laying hens, developed larger KBs than pullets reared in conventional cages (Casey-Trott et al., 2017b). More bone-loading exercise during skeletal development engenders bone formation (Frost, 1987), with opportunities for exercise leading to stronger bones at skeletal maturity in pullets (Pufall et al., 2021) and reduced KBF during lay (Casey-Trott et al., 2017a). In laying hens, pullet-rearing housing is greatly variable in terms of environmental complexity (Widowski and Torrey, 2018) and opportunities for exercise (Rentsch et al., 2023a).

The most striking difference between rearing aviary types is in the design of the brooding compartment (Rentsch et al., 2023a). Brooding compartments are areas where chicks are confined during their first few weeks of life, before gaining access to the whole of the aviary. The design of these brooding compartments varies in the number of perches, the inclusion of elevated platforms, and mostly their floor space and group sizes. Rearing aviaries can be categorized into three common design types based on the complexity of their brooding compartments: in the Low complexity aviaries brooding compartments have low perches, little vertical space and relatively low horizontal area, the brooding compartments in intermediate complexity aviaries (Mid) offer more vertical space with elevated perches and a platform, and the High complexity rearing aviaries are open concept with large spans of horizontal area as well as elevated perches and terraces (Rentsch et al., 2023a). On commercial rearing farms, chicks in High locomoted the most and white but not brown chicks in High performed more aerial behaviour (jumping and flying) (Pufall et al., 2021). We have previously shown that in a controlled lab setting, chicks performed more bone-loading exercises in High during brooding. However, white and brown pullets diverged in their behaviour after gaining access to the whole of the aviaries; white pullets from highly complex brooding compartments continued their higher performance of load-bearing exercises, while brown pullets did not (Rentsch et al., 2023a). Here, we examine whether the differences in exercise during rearing we previously reported resulted in improved bone strength and a lower KB fracture (KBF) prevalence later in life.

Thus, the objective of this study was to investigate whether the degree of early environmental complexity experienced in different styles of rearing aviaries and genetic strain affected KB development during rearing and KBF prevalence throughout lay. For this purpose, three consecutive replicate flocks (3000 chicks/ flock) of a brown and a white line were raised in three types of rearing aviaries (with Low, Mid, or High complexity) and in conventional cages (Conv). From the first two flocks, we assessed the KB size of pullets sampled at six, 11 and 16 WoA. Pullets from flocks 1 and 3 (1080/ flock) were subsequently housed in furnished colony cages of two sizes (small: 30 hens, large: 60 hens, 24 cages/ flock) at equal stocking density, until the end of lay, and their KB health was assessed in samples of hens radiographed at 30, 50, and 70 WoA. Additionally, KB size and KBD were determined by dissection from hens sampled at 70 WoA (only in flock 3). We predicted that 1) pullets from more complex brooding compartments would have larger KBs than those reared in lower complexity (High > Mid > Low > Conv); 2) hens raised in higher complexity aviaries would have fewer KBF throughout lay (High < Mid < Low < Conv), 3) white feathered pullets/ hens would have proportionally larger KBs than browns, and 4) brown hens would have more severe KBF than whites during lay with similar KBF at the end-of-lay.

Materials and methods

Animals and Management

Three consecutive flocks of Lohmann Selected Leghorn-lite (white feathered) and Lohmann Brown-lite (brown feathered) laying hens were hatched at a commercial hatchery (Archer's Poultry Farm Ltd., Ontario, Canada). The day-old chicks were transported to the research farm (Arkell Poultry Research Station, Guelph, ON, Canada), where they were raised in rearing housing for 17 weeks before being transferred into furnished cages for the laying period. The ethical use of animals and procedures in this study was considered and sanctioned by the University of Guelph Animal Care Committee (Animal Utilization Protocol #4127).

Housing

The housing treatment occurred during the rearing phase, where pullets were reared in one of four different commercially available rearing environments: barren conventional pullet cages (Conv), rearing aviaries of low (Low), intermediate (Mid) or high (High) early environmental complexity (see Fig 1). The two genetic strains were housed in separate rooms for High and the same room but visually separated in Conv, Low and Mid. The location of the two strains within each housing type were alternated between flocks to avoid any biases due to microclimates in the barn. In the rearing aviaries, chicks were confined to brooding compartments of different spatial complexity for the first six weeks before gaining access to a litter area, multiple tiers, perches, and ramps until 17 WoA (aviaries are described in detail in Rentsch et al., 2023a). The main difference between the rearing aviaries was the brooding compartments' design (size and complexity). There were three brooding compartments in Low per strain per flock with two perches 15 cm from the floor and raised with the drinker line (115 chicks/ compartment). Mid had three brooding compartments per strain per flock that offered three elevated perches (20 cm, variable on the drinker line and 70 cm high) and a 40 cm high platform (144 chicks/ compartment). The single brooding compartment in High spanned the whole length of the barn (1 compartment/ strain/ flock; 2.45 × 7.25 m) with six perches of varying heights and an elevated platform (600 chicks). Pullets in the Conv treatment were confined to barren cages throughout the entire rearing period. Conv birds started out with four cages per strain per flock (30 chicks/ cage) until six WoA and then eight cages per strain (15 pullets/ cage) until end of rearing. The floor and feeder space differed between the rearing aviaries (see Rentsch et al., 2023a for details), but all met the requirements of the Canadian Codes of Practice for Pullets and Laying Hens (NFACC, 2017). The temperature and light intensity in the barn were adjusted for the life stage of the birds (NFACC, 2017). Dissections

At six, 11, and 16 WoA (flocks 1 and 2), pullets were randomly selected from all locations (perches, platform, floor, ramps, and litter) within the rearing environments. From each rearing aviary (Low, Mid, and High), ten to 15 pullets were selected per age group and seven to eight from Conv. At 70 WoA (flock 3), ten hens were randomly selected from each cage and radiographed before being processed for dissections. At all timepoints, qualified personnel euthanized birds via carbon dioxide gassing. Birds were assigned a coded label, blinding experimenters to housing treatment in subsequent steps. Carcasses were refrigerated overnight and then frozen at -20°C. Before dissection, carcasses were thawed at room temperature for approximately 24 hours then weighed using a digital scale. Keel bones were collected using the methods described in Casey-Trott et al. (2017b).

Keel Area measurements were collected from photographs taken immediately after keel removal according to the methods of Fawcett et al (2020). Keels were placed on a white background next to a fixed ruler. A camera (Canon Powershot Elph 340Hs) was mounted on a monopod 23.8 cm above the background, and the left and right lateral views were photographed. Keels were traced using ImageJ software (1.46r, US National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA) on a drawing tablet (XP Pen Artist Pro 15.6). The keel area was normalized by body weight (mm2/kg body weight). All pullet keels were traced by the same observer, and a random subset of 30 keels was traced twice to calculate intra-observer reliability. Another observer traced the hen's keels, and 24 keels were re-traced for reliability assessment.

KB scoring – The dissected keels of the 70-week-old hens were scored for fractures and deviations using a categorical scale ranging from zero (undamaged) to three (severely damaged) (see Table 2 for detailed descriptions). Additionally, tip fractures were recorded binary as the presence or absence of a fracture in the last third of the keel length at the caudal end of the keel. Radiographs

At 30, 50, and 70 WoA (flocks 1 and 3), a subset of hens (20% from each cage) was crated (convenience sampling) and transported to a building equipped with a diagnostic X-ray machine (80kV, 2.5mAs, 650nm; model VET-20BT, POSKOM Co. Ltd., Gyeonggi-do, Korean; United Radiology Systems, Inc., Deerfield IL, USA). Hens were inverted, hung from shackles by their feet, and radiographed before being returned to their cages (see Casey-Trott et al., 2015). The radiographing procedure took less than one minute per bird. The radiographs were viewed using the Metron Imaging software (Metron-DVM 7.20, Metron Imaging, Creston CA, USA).

KB scoring - The radiographs were used to score the keel bone fracture severity using the tagged visual scale developed by Rufener et al. (2018). The scoring system consists of a continuous scale between zero (not fractured) and five (severely fractured). The radiographs were scored by two observers. After initial training using the online training tool provided by Rufener et al. (2018), 145 radiographs were scored by both observers for inter-observer reliability assessment. The radiographs from 70 WoA hens were used to score KBF prevalence (at least one KBF: yes or no). These data were collected by one observer and intra-rater reliability was assessed by re-scoring 100 radiographs. All observers were blinded to housing treatment and strain.

Correspondence between the KBF scores of dissected and radiographed keels was assessed by correlating the dissection scores for fractures with the radiograph scores for the subset of hens that were dissected after being radiographed at the end of flock 3 (210 hens).

Methodological limitations

This study was logistically challenging with its longitudinal design where flocks were replicated consecutively instead of concurrently. As resource availability varied across flocks (e.g., due to COVID-19 pandemic), data collection became fragmented. Additionally, pullet rearing was shorter than the lay period, and as a result, flock 1 hens were still occupying the adult housing when flock 2 matured. Consequently, some variables were assessed in only one or two out of three flocks. This segmentation reduced replication.

The different population sizes and total available floor space in the rearing housing treatments was another limitation. Population sizes were dictated by the most limited resource (usually feeder space) resulting in different population densities (Conv > Low > Mid > High, see Rentsch et al., (2023a)). Therefore, our experimental design does not allow for the dissection of different aspects of environmental complexity (spatial vs structural vs social). However, because we used commercially available systems, these differences in design and space allowance also occur in commercial practice, which increases the external validity of our findings.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were done in R and R Studio (R version 4.2.2 (2022-10-31) Copyright (C) 2022 The R Foundation for Statistical Computing (R Core Team, 2021)). Packages used included 'car' for modelling, 'DHARMA' to visualize model fits, 'emmeans' for post-hoc analyses, and 'effects' to call predicted values of fixed effects. Pearson's correlations were calculated to assess observer reliability (KB tracing and KBF scoring) and accuracy (dissection vs radiograph KBF scores). Data normality was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk test statistics. Generalized-/ Linear mixed effects models (G/LMM) were fitted to the following outcome variables: keel area by body weight, KBF scores, KBF prevalence, keel tip fracture presence, and KB deviation (KBD) scores. For the two categorical variables (KBF and KBD dissection scores), data were analyzed with a GLMM with Poisson distribution. While Poisson distributions are traditionally used for count data, our ordinal categorical data fulfilled the assumptions of a Poisson regression (each response was independent and a non-negative integer). KBF prevalence was fitted to a GLMM with a binary distribution (yes = 1, no = 0) to compare KBF prevalence as the probability of a hen obtaining at least one KBF. Normally distributed continuous data were analyzed by fitting LMMs. All models were evaluated by visually assessing homoscedasticity and normality of residuals. When analyzing pullet data, fixed effects were strain, housing treatment, age, and all their interactions with flock as a random effect. The significance of each fixed effect was assessed using Wald Chi-Square test statistics, where a larger chi-square [χ2] value indicates a higher contribution of the variable to the model. Post-hoc analyses assessed pairwise comparisons of statistically significant main effects with more than two levels or between groups of a statistically significant interaction. Test statistics of post-hoc analyses were corrected for multiple testing using the Tukey method. Effect sizes were calculated using the command ‘eff_size’ from the R package ‘emmeans’. While effect sizes are relative, they can be cautiously interpreted according to the rule of thumb for Cohen's D (small= 0.2, moderate= 0.5, large effect= 0.8, see Metsämuuronen, (2024)).

Results

Reliability: Keel tracings had a high intra-observer reliability with a Pearson's correlation coefficient of 0.999 for pullets and 0.991 for hens. The inter-observer reliability of the KBF severity scores based on radiographs was acceptable, with a Pearson's correlation coefficient of 0.788. KBF prevalence was scored with a moderate intra-rater agreement (Cohen's Kappa= 0.72, 95%-confident interval [CI]: 0.58, 0.86).

KBF score correspondence: KBF scores were normally distributed (radiograph: W= 0.941, <0.0001, dissection: W= 0.876, P < 0.0001). The two scores correlated with a Pearson's correlation coefficient of 0.547 (CI: 0.444, 0.635, P < 0.0001).

Pullets

Keel area [mm2/kg] was affected by genetic strain (χ2 = 326.98, df = 1, P < 0.0001), pullet housing treatment (χ2 = 54.16, df = 3, P < 0.0001, Fig 2A), age (χ2 = 83.52, df = 2, P < 0.0001), and by the interactive effect of strain and age χ2 = 10.58, df = 2, P =0.005, Fig 2B). White pullets had proportionally larger keels (estimated marginal mean [mean est] = 1586.11±11.2 mm2/kg) than brown pullets (mean est = 1372.91±11.2 mm2/kg, effect size [ES]= 1.64±0.42). Pullets from Mid and Low had proportionally larger keels than those from High (Mid vs High: t = 2.79, P = 0.028, ES= 0.34±1.14; Low vs High: t = 3.46, P = 0.003, ES= 0.42±0.16), and all aviary-reared pullets had proportionally larger keels than Conv reared pullets (High vs Conv: t = 3.56, P = 0.002, ES= 0.49±0.18; Mid vs Conv: t = 6.07, P < 0.0001, ES= 0.83±0.25; Low vs Conv: t = 6.67, P < 0.0001, ES= 0.91±0.27). All pullets had the greatest KB to body weight ratio at six WoA and lowest at 11 WoA (P < 0.0001 for all pairwise comparisons). Brown Conv hens had the highest KBF scores (most severe), statistically higher than Conv whites (t = 3.46, P = 0.004, ES= 0.93±0.30) and browns from Mid (t = 4.7, P = 0.002, ES= 1.23±0.32) or High (t = 3.10, P = 0.039, ES= 0.81±0.29). Browns from Low had higher KBF scores than browns in Mid (t = 3.34, P = 0.026, ES= 0.88±0.29). Hens in large cages tended to have more severe KBF than those in small cages (mean est ± SEM: small= 1.22±0.1, large= 1.47±0.01; χ2 = 3.78, P = 0.052, ES= 0.28±0.15). Tip fracture prevalences tended to be affected by rearing complexity (χ2 = 7.20, P = 0.066) but not strain (χ2 = 1.57, P = 0.210). Tip fractures were frequent in all treatment groups, with the highest prevalence in Conv browns (100%) and the lowest in High whites (66.67 ± 1.6%).

Deviations at the end-of-lay were affected by cage size (χ2 = 3.88, P = 0.049) but not rearing (χ2 = 5.52, P = 0.138) or strain (χ2 = 0.32, P = 0.571). Hens in large cages had higher deviation scores than those in small cages (mean ± SEM = 0.40±0.1 vs 0.15±0.1, ES= 0.25±0.13).

Lateral KB area at the end-of-lay was best explained by strain (χ2 = 11.51, P < 0.001) but not rearing complexity (χ2 = 1.69, P = 0.638) or cage size (χ2 = 0.26, P = 0.607). White hens had larger KBs in proportion to their body weight than brown hens (mean est ± SEM: 957.73±13.6 vs 889.52±13.6 mm2/kg, ES= 0.48±0.16).

Discussion

We studied the effect of early environmental complexity and genetic strain on KB development during rearing and KB health throughout lay. While, as predicted, pullets from Conv had proportionally smaller keels than aviary-reared pullets, aviary-reared pullets High did not have larger keel sizes compared to pullets reared in the less complex aviaries. Radiographs showed that hens from Mid and Low complexity brooding compartments had more severe KBF throughout lay than those from High. However, the KBF severity of Conv-reared hens did not differ statistically from aviary-reared hens. Yet, as expected, hens from High had the lowest KBF prevalence and were statistically less likely to have a KBF than hens from Conv or Low rearing complexity. In brown end-of-lay hens only, dissected keels of hens reared with less environmental complexity had incrementally more severe KBF, although brown High did not differ statistically from brown Mid or brown Low. As predicted, white pullets and hens had proportionally larger KBs than browns. As expected, brown hens had more severe KBF than whites during lay (radiographed keels) with no strain difference at the end-of-lay (dissected keels) in aviary-reared hens. Although, brown Conv hens had more severe KBF than Conv whites. Lastly, there were no rearing or strain effects on the severity of KB deviations at the end-of-lay. There was an unexpected effect of adult housing with larger cages leading to more severe deviations.

Strain effects. This study replicated findings on strain differences in KB size and KBF susceptibility. White pullets had larger keels in proportion to body weight than brown pullets, matching data reported by Pufall et al. (2021) and Fawcett et al. (2020). Furthermore, to the authors' knowledge, this is the first documented evidence that this relative strain difference in keel size is maintained throughout lay. The variation in keel size between white- and brown-feathered strains corresponds to the differences in the relative sizes of the pectoralis muscles observed in pullets (Fawcett et al., 2020; Pufall et al., 2021) and in differences in wing-loading (LeBlanc et al., 2018). Keel size differences are also related to functional differences in flight kinematics observed during tests for aerial descent (Hong et al., 2024). These morphological differences also support the findings that the strains differ in their behavioural tendencies and/or abilities to climb ramps using Wing Assisted Incline Running (LeBlanc et al., 2018) and to ascend to elevated structures (Ali et al., 2016; Rentsch et al., 2023b). White-feathered hens are generally more motivated to seek elevation and are more adept ‘flyers’ than brown-feathered strains. The strain differences in behaviour correspond with differences in their physiology, meaning the musculoskeletal traits of their flight apparatus.

Brown hens are reported to have a higher prevalence of KBF than white hens for most of the lay period (Heerkens et al., 2016; Eusemann et al., 2018; Fawcett et al., 2020), which agrees with the more severe KBF found in this study. The fact that the strain difference mostly disappeared by the end-of-lay is in concordance with the systematic review by Rufener and Makagon (2020). These authors reported that white hens have a more linear relationship between KBF prevalence and age while brown hens obtain fewer new fractures towards the end-of-lay.

Rearing complexity. It was proposed that offering more opportunities for load-bearing exercise during development would improve bone strength and keel size at the end of rearing and decrease bone fracture prevalence in adult laying hens (Janczak and Riber, 2015; Widowski and Torrey, 2018). Rearing effects of KB lateral area size disappeared by the end-of-lay, which could be a result of structural changes through KB deviations (Eusemann et al., 2018; Hildebrand et al., 2024). In our study, we provide evidence for the importance of structural complexity during early life (first six weeks) in improving bone strength. Our data suggests that even among different types of rearing aviaries, there are differences in the long-term effects on skeletal health, with higher early-life complexity reducing KBF severity and prevalence.

Interestingly, data collected from radiographs throughout lay did not show aviary-rearing improvement over conventional cages, contradicting pre-existing literature (Casey-Trott et al., 2017a). However, there were methodological differences between studies for scoring KBF in the live birds. Casey-Trott et al (2017a) used palpation with scoring for presence and absence of fractures (i.e. SKAP method as per Casey-Trott et al (2015)). Here, we used the scoring system developed by Rufener et al (2018) specifically for radiographs and which focuses on severity. Palpation for keel scoring has been shown to suffer from lack of sensitivity, characterized by false negatives, for tip fractures (Casey-Trott et al., 2015). Radiographs are reported as sensitive to fracture detection, including fractures at the dorsal side (Richards et al., 2011). In a recent study by Makagon and colleagues (Makagon et al., 2024) radiographs were used to assess KBF of hens raised in cage-free housing of varying complexity. Makagon et al. did not detect any rearing effects on KBF severity or prevalence, though their study ended at 30 WoA. In our study, dissections and radiographs at the end of the lay (70WoA) confirmed the positive effect of aviary rearing on long-term KB health (KBF severity and prevalence). Though the effect on KBF severity was only detected in brown hens. This particular strain effect seems counter-intuitive considering the behavioural display of laying hen pullets described in a previous publication by Rentsch et al. (2023a), which showed that complex rearing aviary design inspired behaviour assumed to be wing-loading in predominantly white pullets. An alternative explanation could be that instead of increased bone strength, differences in the behavioural development of pullets from the different rearing environments led to differences in collisions with structures which in turn affected KB health.

In this same population of birds, rearing aviary complexity influenced fear behaviour (Rentsch et al., 2024), which has been suggested to be a contributing factor to KBF prevalence (Harlander-Matauschek et al., 2015). The pattern seen in KBF from radiographs (Fig 3A) matches the emotionality in response to fear-inducing stimuli. In our previous study (Rentsch et al., 2024), we reported that 14-week-old pullets from Mid and Low responded in a more active (fleeing) way compared to High or Conv pullets which responded through inactivity (indifference or freezing respectively). It has been shown that aviary-reared pullets have a flighty fear response with potential negative welfare consequences (Brantsæter et al., 2016), such as panic-induced piling or collisions with structural elements. Our data only partly supports this concern, as hens reared in aviaries with low or intermediate complexity did have more severe KBF. Contrary, KBFs were least severe and least prevalent if the early life environment is highly complex. Additionally, hens from Conv rearing had the highest KBF prevalence.

A further point of consideration is that our hens were housed in furnished cages. Much of the discussion about collisions with structural elements focuses on multi-tier aviary systems (Stratmann et al., 2015). Yet, there is also evidence showing an increase in collisions in furnished cages when hens were reared in cages versus a rearing aviary of the 'Low' design (Pullin et al., 2020). A recent study demonstrated that the more collisions and aggressive altercations a hen experienced, the higher her risk of obtaining KBFs (Baker et al., 2024). Hence, there is a need for additional research on hens' risk of collision with perches in furnished cages and their respective impact dependent on fear behaviour.

KBF severity. KBFs scored in this study were likely a collection of old healed, and new, healing fractures with the majority being already healed (Baur et al., 2020). There is mixed evidence for KBF to induce pain once healed (Nasr et al., 2012, 2013, 2015). While KBF severity scores are unlikely to reflect a hen's welfare in that moment of time, it does represent the accumulation of negative experiences throughout her life. In addition, high KBF severity has been linked to indicators of depressive-like states in the brain (Armstrong et al., 2020). The two KB scoring systems used in this study differ in many ways, including their invasiveness and limitations, and while they were statistically correlated, the correlation was only moderate. The correlation is not strong enough to imply a highly predictable association and suggests that other factors might influence the variables. Scoring KBF severity via radiographs is a minimally invasive method that can be repeated over time (Rufener et al., 2018) while dissection scores, by their very nature, are a singular measure. The continuous measure of the visual analogue scale used to score the KBF severity from radiographs had a good inter-observer reliability though it is limited to a lateral view, which could explain discrepancies between scoring systems due to fractures that were missed. In terms of accuracy, dissections should be more accurate, as they are not limited to a lateral view and the bone can be inspected directly (Casey-Trott et al., 2015). However, reducing the information gained to an ordinal scale with only four categories might have diminished the reliability and sensitivity of the measure (Tuyttens et al., 2009). We suggest that an in-depth assessment of different severity scoring systems should be conducted, to assess the correlation between measures but also to evaluate their accuracy in describing the actual damage to the bone.

Collectively, the presented data from various methods used to quality KB fractures and deviations confirm the life-long effects of rearing environments on KB health (Casey-Trott et al., 2017a). We saw fewer and less severe KBF in aviary reared hens with the least damage experienced by those from most complex environments that promoted locomotion with wing-use (Pufall et al., 2021; Rentsch et al., 2023a). Our study supports recommendations to raise pullets in environments that provide chicks with diverse opportunities for exercise.

Conclusion

In this study, rearing complexity and strain affected KB size and fracture severity. Overall, more complex early life environments increased KB size and decreased KBF severity and prevalence. White birds had larger KBs at the end of rearing and at the end-of-lay, while brown hens had more severe fractures during lay. KBF severity scores from radiographs and dissected keels correlated moderately. We suggest validating different KBF severity scoring systems for their accuracy and correspondence. In summary, rearing environment can improve long-term KB health and improve hen welfare, though different genetic strains vary in their susceptibility of these beneficial effects.
