Skyrmions with multiple topological charges as orbital angular momentum encoders
Authors: Shuhua Guan, Xiaolong Zou, Wenhui Duan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe orbital angular momentum (OAM) of magnon, which is related to its spiral phase wavefront, shows great application potential benefiting from its attractive physical properties, such as strong robustness and orthogonal multiplexing. However, the generation of such magnons poses great challenges, especially in the nanoscale. We propose that skyrmions with multiple topological charges (Q) and nanosize can be used to generate magnons with programmable OAM in a layered frustrated antiferromagnetic film. This general mechanism is applicable to various layered materials with inversion symmetry, facilitating the practical application of magnonic OAM. Moreover, we reveal skyrmion dynamics in frustrated antiferromagnets, deepening the mechanistic understanding of dynamics of topological magnetic structures.

As topologically protected quasiparticles in spin texture, skyrmions have been extensively explored across various fields such as polariton superfluids (1), optics (2, 3), and magnetics (4–9). Different skyrmions can be characterized by an invariant known as the topological charge Q, determined by the polarity and vorticity of these structures (10, 11). While |Q| = 1 skyrmion has been widely investigated, only until recently high-order skyrmions with |Q| > 1 (12, 13) have received growing attention. Although high-order skyrmions cost additional energy when the short-range exchange coupling is dominant (13), they could be stabilized through different ways. For example, the dissipative skyrmions with |Q| ≥ 2 are formed by spin-transfer torque in chiral ferromagnet (14), and the |Q| = 2 skyrmion crystal is stabilized by the Fermi surface effect in itinerant magnet (13), while skyrmions with |Q| up to 10 are generated by dipolar interaction in Co/Ni multilayers at room temperature (12). Besides larger topological Hall effect (15) for incident electrons and more significant anisotropic magnetoresistive effect (16), these high-order skyrmions with richer structures might unveil new physics. In particular, the phase profiles of high-order skyrmions indicate that they have an inherent connection with the orbital angular momentum (OAM) of magnons, potentially offering a route to control the latter, which is a long-sought goal for magnonics.

Magnons, the quantized spin waves in magnetic systems, possess dual angular momentum characteristics analogous to photons, i.e., spin angular momentum (SAM) and OAM (17, 18). While circularly polarized magnons carrier SAM with ±ћ, originating from their boson properties, the OAM of a magnon is related to its spiral phase wavefront characterized by an azimuthal phase factor exp (ilφ), where l is the OAM quantum number, i.e., the topological charge of the magnon (19–21). Compared to the amplitude, frequency, and phase of magnons, their OAM might display stronger robustness (22, 23) and thus can be regarded as a superior information carrier for nonvolatile spintronics devices. The bound-state magnons carrying OAM are known as twisted magnons (20), while those scattering-state magnons are called multipolar spin waves (24, 25). Although magnon transport processes are extensively investigated, including magnon frequency combs (26–28), magnon valve effect (29, 30), and skin effect (31), its OAM-mediated transport phenomena are rarely explored. One of the key reasons is that generation of magnons with specific OAM poses great challenge. Pervious works have revealed that the twisted magnons can be produced by Aharonov–Casher effect (17), magnonic spiral phase plate (32), and SAM-to-OAM conservation (33), while the multipolar spin waves can be induced by optical vortex (24, 25). However, the above means require delicate experiment design and engineering, such as sophisticated nanofabrication of peculiar structures and breaking the diffraction limit of electromagnetic waves. A more practical way for the generation of such traveling magnons with programmable OAM is urgently required.

In this work, we propose a mechanism of generating scattering-state magnons carrying intrinsic OAM by antiferromagnetic (AFM) skyrmions with multiple topological charges. By constructing phase diagrams for a tetragonal lattice frustrated AFM film, we reveal the stability of multiple-Q skyrmions and the controllability of Q by uniaxial anisotropy. Under out-of-plane polarized current, spin–orbit torque (SOT) causes the ultrafast oscillation of skyrmion helicity, which can be used to generate multipolar spin waves, with OAM quantum numbers identical to the Q of skyrmions. Further, we explore the thermal stability of such skyrmions and reveal the stepwise annihilation behaviors for |Q| > 1 structures above the transition temperature, demonstrating that temperature is another control parameter for modulating Q. Moreover, we find that Hall motion emerges even for AFM skyrmion driven by in-plane polarized current. The Hall angle depends on the arbitrarily initialized but dynamically locked skyrmion helicity at zero temperature, which is demonstrated by Thiele’s theory. At finite temperature, besides thermal magnon-induced friction to the Thiele’s equation, the heat-induced helicity variation introduces a nonlinear correction term for skyrmion dynamics.

Results

Model and Simulations.

First, we propose our concept schematically in Fig. 1. In an AFM film, a |Q| = 2 skyrmion is stabilized, whose configuration is shown in Fig. 1A and its helicity η can be modulated periodically through current within time interval Δt ~ ps, corresponding to THz frequency. We consider a bilayer system with weak interlayer exchange coupling as illustrated in Fig. 1B, and the magnetocrystalline anisotropy (K) of the upper layer is set to be smaller than that of the lower layer. The other parameters of the two layers keep the same. An out-of-plane spin-polarized current is injected into the lower layer, where a skyrmion with |Q| = 2 is stabilized. An appropriate current density is chosen to control the helicity oscillation frequency in the THz range. The topological skyrmion acts as an OAM encoder, which generates multipolar spin waves in the upper layer, with their OAM quantum number identical to the |Q| of skyrmion. The spin waves cannot be excited in the lower layer because of its larger magnon gap originating from the larger magnetocrystalline anisotropy. Therefore, the skyrmion is a stable spin-wave exciter. This mechanism can be readily generalized to multilayer systems, where spin waves could propagate along the vertical direction, whose OAMs can be encoded as data carriers, analogous to using OAM states in optical and quantum communications (34–36).

Here, Si is the local spin at site i, J1, J2, and J3 > 0 are nearest-neighbor, next-nearest-neighbor, and next-next-nearest-neighbor AFM exchange coupling coefficients, respectively. K is the magnitude of magnetic anisotropy along z-axis, and J⊥ is the interlayer exchange coupling coefficient.

The time-dependent spin dynamics is investigated by solving the Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert (LLG) equation,[2]dSidt=-γSi×(Hieff+R)+αSi×dSidt+uSi×(p×Si),

where γ is gyromagnetic ratio, Hieff= −(1/μ0)∂H/∂Si is the effective field, and α is the Gilbert damping coefficient. The last term of Eq. 2 is the current-induced SOT with polarization direction p and coefficient u = jPћγ/2µ0eaMs with driving current density j, the spin Hall angle P, the vacuum magnetic permeability µ0, the elementary charge e, the lattice constant a, and the saturation magnetization Ms. The thermal fluctuation at finite temperature T is considered by an additional random field term R, satisfying ⟨Rμ(r, t)⟩ = 0, and ⟨Rμ(r, t)Rν(r’, t’)⟩ = 2αћkBTa2δμνδ(r−r’)δ(t−t’) (37–40) with μ, ν = x, y and the Boltzmann constant kB. Without losing the generality, we select J1 = 32 meV, J2/J1 = 0.32, J3/J1 = 0.1, K/J1 = 0.05, which are close to our DFT results for FeSe monolayer (J1 = 32 meV, J2 = 11 meV, J3 = 3.8 meV, see Materials and Methods). γ = 2.3 × 105 m/(A s), a = 0.4 nm, Ms= 80 kA/m, α = 0.1, j = 8.4 × 1011 A/m2, and P = 0.4 are chosen for dynamics exploration (41, 42).

Out-of-plane polarized current-induced skyrmion helicity oscillation frequency (~THz) is expected to exceed magnon gap frequency and excite spin waves. As illustrated in Fig. 1B, a bilayer system with weak interlayer AFM exchange coupling J⊥/ J1 = 0.01 is considered, where α is set to be 0.01, K/J1 = 0.01 and K/J1 = 0.05 for the upper layer and lower layer, respectively. The skyrmions with various |Q| are stabilized in the current-injected lower layer. The dispersion relation for magnon is given by[6]ωs=±γ2A0(Ak2-A3k4+K),

where the positive and negative frequencies indicate that there exist degenerate right-handed and left-handed magnon modes in the antiferromagnets, originating from the time-reversal invariant term A0−1n × (∂2n/∂t2×n) in Eq. 12 (Materials and Methods). Fig. 3B shows the analytical dispersion curves obtained from Eq. 6, revealing layer-dependent magnon gap frequencies, that is, 3.2 and 7.1 THz for the upper and lower layers, respectively. By applying out-of-plane polarized current to induce skyrmion helicity oscillation with frequency ω, the spin precession frequency of the spin waves in the upper layer and that of skyrmions in the lower layer are synchronized due to the interlayer exchange coupling, i.e., ωs = ω. If skyrmion helicity oscillation frequency ω is set to between 3.2 and 7.1 THz by controlling current density j, the multipolar spin waves can only be induced in the upper layer, and the skyrmions in the lower layer act as the stable exciters.

The dependence of ω on j for |Q| = 1 skyrmion is shown in Fig. 3C, where the open and solid points are simulation results for bilayer and monolayer systems, respectively, and the lines are analytical results obtained from Eq. 5. It can be seen that magnons with frequency ωs ∈ (3.2 THz, 7.1 THz) can be induced under j ∈ (4.6 × 1011 A/m2, 8.4 × 1011 A/m2). As the helicity oscillation frequency ω of skyrmions increases with increasing j (j < 4.6 × 1011 A/m2), the precession frequency of the z-polarized spins in the upper layer gradually approaches the AFM resonance frequency, causing the spins to tilt toward the xy-plane. Accordingly, the effective mass M of the bilayer system increases with increasing j, as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S2A and section S2), which suppresses the growth of ω (Eq. 5). This leads to a sublinear increase of ω at low j. When ω exceeds the gap frequency of the upper layer, scattering-state magnons begin to emerge. Beyond this threshold, the spin precession in the upper layer can be transferred to the lower layer. As j further increases, ω approaches the AFM resonance frequency of the lower layer. Then, the spin precession amplitude of the collinear AFM ground state in the lower layer is increased, which can affect the upper layer through interlayer coupling, increasing the magnon amplitude, as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S2B and section S2). Therefore, as j continues to increase, more energy is dissipated via magnon excitation, suppressing the increase of ω. This process continues until the magnons are excited in both layers. Accordingly, the helicity frequency is synchronized with the energy required for magnon excitation. When the current density exceeds j = 8.4 × 1011 A/m2, magnons can be induced in both layers. In this case, the skyrmions become unstable under magnon-skyrmion scattering. Meanwhile, the simultaneously excited magnons in two layers leads to the decrease of interlayer coupling energy, and accordingly, ω sharply increases.

The multipolar spin waves induced by skyrmions with |Q| = 1, 2, and 3 under current density j = 7 × 1011 A/m2 are shown in the top panels of Fig. 3 D–F, respectively. The OAM quantum numbers |l| of the generated spin waves are 1, 2, and 3, respectively, identical to |Q|. The demonstrated |Q|-OAM correspondence establishes skyrmions with multiple topological charges as OAM encoders. The bottom panels of Fig. 3 D–F display the corresponding reciprocal space images, which exhibit anisotropy along different wavevector directions. The reciprocal space images show higher-order ring-shape structures with the increase of |Q|, which indicates that the spin waves with |Q| > 1 exhibit multiple-k property, with nonmonochromaticity. The magnon amplitude, i.e., the magnon density (48), and the magnon frequency increase as |Q| increases, as shown in the bottom panels of Fig. 3 D–F and SI Appendix, Fig. S2C and section S2, respectively. Moreover, the SAM and thus the chirality of spin waves can be turned by the helicity oscillation direction of skyrmions. SI Appendix, Fig. S2D demonstrates that left- and right-handed spin waves can be generated by currents with p = z and p = −z, respectively (SI Appendix, section S2).

Notably, the SAM and OAM of magnons are intrinsically coupled in our model, as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S3 and section S3. To illustrate this, we simulate magnon generation under different current injection conditions. For example, injecting a current with p = −z into a skyrmion with |Q| = 1, magnons carrying OAM with l = 1 and SAM of −ћ are generated. On the contrary, magnons carrying OAM with l = −1 and SAM of ћ are induced by a current with p = z. Further, SI Appendix, Fig. S4 shows magnons with |l| = 3 and zero SAM can be generated by magnetic field with Laguerre-Gauss mode, which displays consistent behaviors with Fig. 3F (SI Appendix, section S3). In addition, introducing Rashba SOC does not influence the OAM of magnons, although the critical current density jc for magnon generation does increase, as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S5 and section S3. These results indicate the robustness of magnonic OAM against SOC effects.

To address the nonidealistic conditions in the samples, we simulated the case where several skyrmions with different |Q| are placed in arbitrary positions. Magnons with different |l| can be generated simultaneously by skyrmions with corresponding |Q|, as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S6 and section S4. Even if the rotational symmetry around z-axis of the system is broken through adding x-axis magnetic anisotropy, designing asymmetric geometry of the system and introducing random defects, the |l| of magnons maintains well, as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and section S5. Moreover, SI Appendix, Fig. S8 demonstrates that jc increases with increasing J⊥, and the OAM of magnons remains robust at finite temperature (SI Appendix, section S6).

Skyrmions Thermal Stability and Controlled Q.

The analytical profiles of skyrmions, i.e., nz versus r, obtained from Eq. 3 are shown in Fig. 4A, which demonstrate that the skyrmion radius increases while the domain wall narrows with the increase of |Q|. The stability of high-order skyrmions could be tuned by controlling the magnetic anisotropy and temperature. Fig. 4B shows the critical transition temperature Tc of the annihilation of skyrmions with different |Q|. For a small magnetic anisotropy K, the transition temperatures of different skyrmions are slightly different. However, the Tc of skyrmions with higher |Q| decreases more rapidly as K increases. This is because higher K leads to a smaller skyrmion radius, which increases the energy cost for stabilizing high-order skyrmions. Further, the skyrmions with different |Q| could be transformed by temperature, as shown by the variation of topological charges over time at T = 280 K in Fig. 4C. The stepwise annihilation processes for structures with |Q| > 1 are observed. For instance, the skyrmion with |Q| = 2 first degrades into the structure with |Q| = 1 before disappearing in the trivial AFM background. This phenomenon results from the thermal stability difference of skyrmions with different |Q|. The combined magnetic anisotropy and temperature offer a feasible knob to control the generation of high-order skyrmions. Besides, the diffusion coefficients for different α at T = 75 K are investigated, as shown in Fig. 4D, where the dots are simulation results and the lines are analytically obtained from D = kBT/γ2MA02α. The diffusion coefficient satisfies D ∝ 1/α well, and it decreases as |Q| increases, due to the increase of M.

Here, we investigate skyrmions dynamics under the application of current with p = −x. The current-induced forces in Eq. 4 derive as,[7]Fx=uπ sinη∫r(∂θ∂r+sinθ cosθr)dr|Q|=10Q=other integer valuesFy=-uQπ cosη∫r(∂θ∂r+sinθ cosθr)dr|Q|=10Q=other integer values.

Ignoring the second-order derivative term in Eq. 4, the skyrmion velocity can be expressed as vx/y=Fx/y/γA0Mα and the skyrmion Hall angle is given by θHall = arctan(vy/vx) = −Qcosη/sinη. Interestingly, in contrast to chiral AFM systems, skyrmion Hall effect is induced in frustrated antiferromagnets, and the Hall angle depends on skyrmion helicity. Different from continuously changing helicity in the current-driven FM skyrmions in frustrated magnets (10), the AFM skyrmion helicity is arbitrary but remains unchanged on average when it is driven by in-plane polarized current as shown in Fig. 5A. Consequently, skyrmions with different initial helicities move in different directions, as shown in Fig. 5B, where the data points and lines are simulation and analytical results, respectively. Further, Eq. 7 indicates that skyrmions with |Q| > 1 remain stationary under in-plane polarized current, which is demonstrated in our simulations, as evidenced by the orange and green points in Fig. 5C, consistent with previous work on FM systems (49). The skyrmion dynamics critically depends on the frustrated exchange interaction and magnetic anisotropy. SI Appendix, Fig. S9 shows that v increases with the increasing frustrated exchange coefficients J2 and J3, while larger magnetic anisotropy leads to reduced v (SI Appendix, section S7). It is noted that, although skyrmions with |Q| > 1 cannot be driven by SOT, they can be translated by spin-transfer torque without skyrmion Hall effect, as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S10 and section S8.

Interestingly, thermal fluctuation at finite temperature induces the variation of skyrmion helicity, which exerts critical influence on skyrmion dynamics. Fig. 5D shows the simulated trajectories of skyrmion with |Q| = 1 at different T, with the corresponding average velocities v under different current densities j plotted as points in Fig. 5C. The skyrmion velocity decreases significantly with increasing temperature, while the relationship v∝j persists. In order to describe the unique skyrmion dynamics at finite temperature accounting for the helicity variation, we revise Eq. 4 as,[8]M[q.+γA0(α+λT)q˙]=(1-χΔη2)F,

where two terms are introduced. The first term λT represents the dissipation originating from the thermal magnon-skyrmion scattering (37). The magnon-induced friction is proportional to temperature and dependent on α, with coefficient λ = 0.01 for skyrmions with |Q| = 1 determined through the comparison between the simulation results and analytical diffusion coefficient D = kBT/γ2MA02(α + λT) at low α from Fig. 4D. The analytical result considering only the dissipative term at T = 135 K (the red dashed line in Fig. 5C) deviates significantly from the simulation results (red points). Therefore, the second term, that is the heat-induced skyrmion helicity variation χ⟨Δη2⟩ is necessary to be considered. This term originates from the thermal-induced SOT fluctuation and skyrmion Hall angle oscillation. The Inset in Fig. 5C reveals ⟨Δη2⟩ at different T from our simulations, whereby the coefficient χ can be fitted as 2.6. Considering both the thermal magnon-induced friction and heat-induced skyrmion helicity variation at finite temperature by Eq. 8, analytical results match well with our simulations. In the present case, the heat-induced skyrmion helicity variation dominates the skyrmion dynamics, because the relatively large Gilbert damping coefficient α = 0.1 is chosen, weakening the role of the dissipation term.

Discussion

In this work, we propose a mechanism of generating magnons with target OAMs by antiferromagnetic skyrmions possessing multiple topological charges. This mechanism should also be applicable to other frustrated magnets with inversion symmetry such as Fe3Sn2 (50, 51), Fe3GeTe2 (52), and CrI3 (53) multilayers. The J3-J2 and K-J3 phase diagrams at T ~ 0 obtained by MC simulations reveal the stability of high-order skyrmions and the controllability of Q, which increases with the decrease of K. The strength of K can be modulated through voltage-controlled magnetic anisotropy engineering (54–56), facilitating experimental realization of high-order skyrmions in layered materials. Out-of-plane polarized current-induced helicity oscillation of skyrmions with frequency ~THz enables the generation of multipolar spin waves with OAM quantum number |l| = |Q|. Moreover, analysis of thermal stability of the skyrmions demonstrates a significant decrease in phase transition temperature with increasing |Q|. The stepwise annihilation of skyrmions with |Q| > 1 renders temperature as a viable control parameter for OAM manipulation. Remarkably, under in-plane polarized current, Hall effect is induced even for AFM skyrmion, due to its arbitrarily initialized but dynamically locked helicity. Heat-driven skyrmion helicity oscillation induces spin–orbit torque fluctuation, which makes it necessary to introduce a nonlinear correction team to describe skyrmion dynamics, in addition to thermal magnon-induced friction. This work establishes a paradigm for encoding OAM of THz magnon at the nanoscale through topological skyrmions, circumventing the diffraction limit inherent in optical vortex (25) and microwave-based (19, 20) approaches. The OAM of generated magnons is identical to the topological charge of skyrmion, which can be controlled by magnetic anisotropy and temperature, offering practical implementation. The OAM of magnons can carry and transmit information in multilayer systems, realizing robust magnonic computing and communications. Benefiting from the orthogonality of magnons with different OAMs, the magnonic OAM can also be used in mode division multiplexing communications (33, 35).

Materials and Methods

Density Functional Theory Calculations.

The exchange parameters of FeSe are calculated using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (57). A 500 eV plane wave cutoff energy, and a 18 Å vacuum layer are adopted. The lattice parameter is fixed as a = 3.85 Å, which is obtained from monolayer FeSe on Si (001) substrate (42). The generalized gradient approximation parameterized by Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (58) with an effective Hubbard U = 4.2 eV for the localized 3d electrons (59) of Fe ions is used. The spin–orbit coupling (SOC) is considered and Gaussian smearing with a width of 0.05 meV is used. The magnetic exchange parameters are obtained through four-state energy mapping method (6, 60). A 4 × 4 × 1 supercell and a 6 × 6 × 1 k-point mesh are chosen for J1, J2, and J3 calculations. The exchange parameters J1 = 32 meV, J2 = 11 meV and J3 = 3.8 meV are qualitatively consistent with previous works calculated for bulk and monolayer FeSe (44, 61).
