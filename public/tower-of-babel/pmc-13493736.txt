Corticosteroid-Associated Adverse Events in Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria: A US Claims Data Study
Authors: Gil Yosipovitch, Dhaval Patil, Jonathan Rodrigues, Merin Kuruvilla, Tara Raftery, Irina Pivneva, Jason Doran, Arthur Voegel, James Signorovitch, Marc A. Riedl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionChronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is a skin disorder for which short-course systemic corticosteroids are recommended only as rescue medication during acute exacerbations. Here, we describe real-world corticosteroid use, adverse events (AEs) occurring post-corticosteroid initiation, and health care resource utilization (HCRU) of patients with CSU in the USA.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used data from the US HealthVerity claims database of adults with diagnosed CSU (January 2016–March 2023). Only prescription and non-antihistamine claims were included in the analysis of treatment patterns. In the AE and HCRU analyses, patients were stratified into five cohorts on the basis of total days of systemic corticosteroid supply as a proxy for duration of use: ≤ 31 days, > 31 to ≤ 60 days, > 60 days, ≥ 90 days (subcohort of > 60 days), and non-corticosteroid users.ResultsOf 200,298 patients, corticosteroids were the most prescribed treatment (78.3% all; 68.2% systemic) following CSU diagnosis, excluding over-the-counter medication and antihistamines. The ten most common AEs occurring after systemic corticosteroid initiation (hypertension, lipid disorders, anxiety, obesity, fatigue, depression, diabetes, insomnia, cataracts, and gastritis) occurred in higher proportions of all corticosteroid user types versus non-users. The proportion of patients experiencing AEs generally increased with longer duration of corticosteroid use. More frequent HCRU was generally observed after the first systemic corticosteroid prescription and with increasing duration of corticosteroid use.ConclusionsPatients with CSU who were prescribed corticosteroids reported a higher proportion of AEs and more frequent HCRU than those who were not. These trends in AEs and HCRU were more prominent with increasing duration of corticosteroid use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-026-01776-0 users.

Introduction

Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is a disorder characterized by the sudden and unpredictable appearance of itchy wheals, angioedema, or both, which last for > 6 weeks [1]. Although the average duration of CSU is 1–4 years, some patients experience intermittent symptoms that last > 5 years, or even decades [2, 3]. The physical, emotional, and social impact of symptoms has a substantial effect on patients’ quality of life [4, 5]. CSU has an estimated global point prevalence of up to 3% [2]; real-world data have shown prevalence rates of 0.23–0.78% in the USA [6–8].

Global treatment recommendations for CSU are based on severity of symptoms and response to treatment. Second-generation nonsedating H1-antihistamines (sgH1-AHs) are recommended as first-line treatments for CSU; subsequent lines of treatment include sgH1-AHs at higher doses, omalizumab, dupilumab, remibrutinib, and/or cyclosporine. A short course (maximum of 10 days) of systemic (oral or injectable) corticosteroids may be considered for acute exacerbations. Topical corticosteroid (TCS) treatment is not recommended for patients with CSU [1].

Despite these recommendations, corticosteroids are frequently prescribed for patients with CSU [9, 10]. Most clinical trials prescribe systemic corticosteroids for up to 7 days [11]; however, real-world data on the duration of corticosteroid use in the management of CSU are limited. Real-world studies in the USA have reported a mean of 29.7–32.5 days of oral corticosteroid supply during the study year [9, 12]. Frequent and/or prolonged use of systemic corticosteroids, including in patients with CSU, can result in serious adverse events (AEs), including lipid disorders, osteoporosis, hypertension, diabetes, neuropsychiatric conditions, cataracts, and obesity [13, 14]. Additionally, the risk of AEs is associated with the duration and dosage of systemic corticosteroid treatment [14, 15]. A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials found that even short-term (3–12 days) add-on systemic corticosteroid use in patients with acute or chronic urticaria flares was likely to increase the risk of AEs, versus patients who did not receive corticosteroids [11].

Here, data from a large, nationally representative US health insurance claims database, as previously described [16], were used to determine the prevalence of corticosteroid use and the burden of systemic corticosteroid-associated AEs in patients with diagnosed CSU. Health care resource utilization (HCRU) in patients who had and had not received systemic corticosteroid treatment was also explored, in addition to the relationship between the duration of systemic corticosteroid usage and occurrence of associated AEs.

Methods

Study Design

In this descriptive, noninterventional, retrospective cohort study, medical and pharmacy data of patients with new records of CSU-related diagnosis codes from the US HealthVerity claims database were analyzed (January 2016–March 2023) [16] (Fig. 1). In the analysis of treatment patterns, only data on prescription medication were included; over-the-counter (OTC) medication and antihistamines were excluded owing to their incomplete representation in claims databases. The index date was the date of initial CSU diagnosis; the 1 year before the index date was defined as the baseline period (January 2016–February 2022); and the time from the index date until end of continuous insurance coverage or data end (January 2017–March 2023) was defined as the follow-up period.

Objectives

The main aims of this analysis were to determine utilization of corticosteroids along with the frequency of systemic (oral or injectable) corticosteroid user types (according to duration of use) and the frequency of AEs occurring after corticosteroid initiation by user type. Secondary aims were to describe the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of patients at baseline, describe prescription treatment patterns at baseline and follow-up, and explore HCRU in systemic corticosteroid users and non-corticosteroid users during the follow-up period.

Participants

Eligible patients were ≥ 18 years of age with a confirmed CSU diagnosis at the index date and had ≥ 1 year of continuous health insurance enrollment before the index date, allowing for gaps in continuous enrollment of < 30 days (Supplementary Fig. S1). A previously validated algorithm was used to identify patients with CSU, or events deemed CSU-related, using combinations of diagnosis codes from the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) [17] (Supplementary Table S1). Diagnosed CSU was defined as ≥ 2 diagnoses of idiopathic, other, or unspecified urticaria, or ≥ 1 diagnosis of idiopathic, other, or unspecified urticaria and ≥ 1 diagnosis of angioedema, separated by ≥ 6 weeks but ≤ 1 year, in either order.

Assessments

Treatments received at baseline and follow-up were categorized into the following drug classes: leukotriene receptor antagonists (LTRAs), biologics, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, and immunomodulators (Supplementary Table S2). These were informed by clinical expert input and real-world prescribing practice in the USA, reflecting agents commonly used in the management of CSU. Further corticosteroid analyses only included patients who received systemic formulations.

AEs were identified using ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes (Supplementary Table S3). The proportion of patients experiencing AEs occurring at 1 year after the first prescription of systemic corticosteroids in the follow-up period was assessed according to duration of corticosteroid use. Patients were stratified by the increasing duration of total systemic corticosteroid supply in the follow-up period for up to 1 year: ≤ 31 days’ supply (≤ 31D); > 31 to ≤ 60 days’ supply (> 31 to ≤ 60D); > 60 days’ supply (> 60D); ≥ 90 consecutive days’ supply (≥ 90Dc); and non-corticosteroid user (non-user). The ≥ 90Dc group is a subgroup of patients from the > 60D group. For non-users, AEs were assessed 1 year after a reference date (mean time to first corticosteroid prescription from the index CSU diagnosis for the ≤ 31D, > 31 to ≤ 60D, and > 60D groups). Patients for whom this reference date occurred after the end of the eligibility period were removed from the AE analysis.

HCRU was assessed 1 year pre- and post-first prescription of systemic corticosteroids in the follow-up period for all corticosteroid user types. For non-users, the reference date was as described earlier. Data on inpatient admissions and outpatient, emergency department (ED), and urgent care visits were included. AE-related HCRU was identified using medical claims with ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes for AEs occurring after systemic corticosteroid initiation.

The AE and HCRU analyses were conducted among a subgroup of patients who had ≥ 1 year of available data after their first corticosteroid prescription in the follow-up period (or ≥ 1 year of available follow-up data after the reference date for non-users).

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed using Statistical Analysis Software 7.15 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Continuous variables were summarized using mean, standard deviation (SD), and median, and categorical variables were summarized using frequency counts and percentages.

Ethical Approval

As this was a noninterventional study that used a de-identified administrative claims database, ethical approval and consent to participate were not applicable.

Results

A total of 200,298 participants were included in the analysis; baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Patients were mostly female (76.4%) and commercial insurance payers (66.3%), and had a mean age of 44.2 years at the index date. The mean (SD) Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was 0.58 (1.18); the most frequently reported CCI comorbidities were chronic pulmonary disease and diabetes. When all comorbidities were considered, hypertension and anxiety were the most frequently reported, affecting > 20% of participants (Supplementary Table S4). Participants were followed for a mean (SD) of 2.3 (1.5) years in the follow-up period. In patients receiving ≥ 1 treatment, excluding OTC medication and antihistamines, corticosteroids were the most prescribed treatment at baseline (52.7%) and follow-up (78.3%). Systemic corticosteroids were used by 43.0% of patients at baseline and 68.2% at follow-up. Of 136,530 patients who received systemic corticosteroids during follow-up, most required one or two episodes of use; 27.4% required > 2 episodes, and 8.5% reported ≥ 5 separate episodes. TCS use was reported in 22.7% of patients at baseline and 40.7% of patients at follow-up. Use of LTRAs and biologics increased from baseline (10.8% and 1.6%, respectively) to follow-up (27.3% and 11.0%, respectively). The number of immunosuppressant and immunomodulator prescriptions were twofold higher during follow-up (5.8% and 4.5%, respectively) versus baseline (2.5% and 2.0%, respectively).

Of the 131,358 patients who had ≥ 1 year of available follow-up data after the first corticosteroid prescription, 74.3% continued to receive corticosteroids. Most patients were in the ≤ 31D group (62.5%), 6.7% were in the > 31 to ≤ 60D group, 5.1% were in the > 60D group, and 1.2% were in the ≥ 90Dc group. In this cohort of corticosteroid users, 92.3% were prescribed an oral formulation and 53.5% were prescribed an injectable formulation. The proportions of patients prescribed oral and injectable formulations were consistent across user types (Supplementary Table S5).

AEs Occurring after Corticosteroid Initiation during Follow-Up

Of patients who received systemic corticosteroids in the follow-up period (n = 136,530), the top ten most common AEs included hypertension, lipid disorders, anxiety, obesity, fatigue, depression, diabetes, insomnia, cataracts, and gastritis (see full list in Supplementary Table S6).

When assessed according to corticosteroid user type, lipid disorders were the most common AE among patients in the ≤ 31D group (33.0%), and hypertension was the most frequent in the > 31 to ≤ 60D, > 60D, and ≥ 90Dc groups (38.0%, 44.0%, and 49.4%, respectively) (Supplementary Table S7). The eight most prevalent AEs occurred in higher proportions of all systemic corticosteroid user types versus non-users (Fig. 2). For most AEs, the proportion of patients affected appeared to be positively correlated with increasing duration of systemic corticosteroid use. HCRU in Patients with CSU Treated with Systemic Corticosteroids

The number of all-cause inpatient admissions and the number of days with outpatient, ED, and urgent care visits were generally higher in all corticosteroid user types versus non-users (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S8). The number of inpatient admissions and days with outpatient visits increased post-first prescription of corticosteroids in all user types. Days with ED and urgent care visits increased post-first prescription of corticosteroids in the ≤ 31D, > 31 to ≤ 60D, and > 60D groups. The number of AE-related inpatient admissions and the number of days with ED visits were numerically more frequent in all corticosteroid user types relative to non-users (Supplementary Table S9). Days with outpatient visits and the number of AE-related inpatient admissions appeared to increase with increasing duration of corticosteroid use. This trend was less apparent for other HCRU parameters.

Patients in the ≥ 90Dc group experienced the highest number and duration of inpatient admissions, and number of days with outpatient visits (both all-cause and AE-related) post-first prescription of corticosteroids.

Discussion

In this analysis of a large real-world patient population with CSU, we report extensive use of systemic corticosteroids in its management, and a higher proportion of patients with AEs after corticosteroid initiation with longer duration of use. Despite guideline recommendations for their use as rescue medication only and their well-documented risks, systemic corticosteroids were the predominant prescribed treatment both before and after CSU diagnosis, with 68.2% of patients treated with oral or injectable corticosteroids after diagnosis [1]. Potential reasons for the high levels of corticosteroid treatment seen here include lack of awareness regarding the cumulative burden of corticosteroid use, lack of clarity on treatment escalation, poor dissemination of management guidelines among CSU-treating physicians, or a high proportion of exacerbations in a patient’s disease course. Cost may also impact treatment decisions; for example, alternative advanced treatment options may not be covered by insurance [18]. A real-world survey of US physicians and their patients with CSU showed that dermatologists and primary care physicians prescribed oral corticosteroids more frequently versus allergists/immunologists [19]. Additionally, although TCS use is not supported by CSU treatment guidelines, 40.7% of patients were prescribed a TCS at follow-up [1]. TCSs were excluded from the AE and HCRU analyses owing to their likely use for other conditions and the reported low risk of associated AEs in patients with urticaria [20].

The prevalence of different AEs reported here was similar to findings from a previous retrospective cohort study that reported selected AEs of interest, including lipid disorders (37.0%), hypertension (34.1%), diabetes (11.7%), and skeletal conditions (6.8%) [14]. The same study also found an increased likelihood of developing AEs with increasing corticosteroid dosage. Therefore, in cases where prescribing corticosteroids is unavoidable, patients’ blood pressure, lipids, glucose, and bone health should also be monitored, and usage should be restricted to the guideline-recommended duration of up to 10 days.

Patients in this study experienced more frequent HCRU associated with corticosteroid use. This has also been reported in other therapeutic areas such as asthma and systemic lupus erythematosus [15, 21, 22]. More frequent HCRU may also have additional implications for health care costs, which are not discussed here. Statistically significant differences in these costs have been reported in patients with, versus without, corticosteroid-related AEs [14].

The overall findings suggest that there is a need for education and shared decision-making between patients and physicians in escalating treatment beyond antihistamines and minimizing AEs associated with corticosteroid use. Over 50% of patients with CSU do not achieve disease control using sgH1-AHs [10, 23, 24], and despite treatment guidelines on subsequent lines of therapy, only one-third received omalizumab in a real-world US study of 670 patients with CSU [1, 25]. Here, only 11% of patients reported receiving a biologic agent (including omalizumab) in the follow-up period, highlighting the limited treatment options for the management of CSU in those refractory to antihistamines. Recent advances in understanding the pathogenesis of CSU have led to the development of novel targeted therapies such as the highly selective oral Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibitor remibrutinib, which was recently approved in the USA for treatment of CSU in adults [26]. Additionally, dupilumab, a subcutaneous anti-interleukin-4 receptor alpha monoclonal antibody, is approved for the treatment of CSU in patients aged ≥ 12 years in the USA (April 2025) [27]. Other therapies in ongoing phase 3 clinical trials include the intravenous anti-KIT monoclonal antibody barzolvolimab [28–30] and the oral Janus kinase inhibitor TLL-018 [30, 31].

Despite the frequent use of corticosteroids in patients with CSU and the widely known risks, real-world data on this specific population are limited. Here, we studied AEs and HCRU in a large population of patients with CSU in the USA who were prescribed corticosteroids. However, there were several limitations. Treatment patterns were not fully captured owing to exclusion of OTC medication and antihistamines; it should also be noted that medication prescriptions do not necessarily reflect actual intake. Corticosteroids may have been prescribed for conditions other than CSU; therefore, the trend observed between increasing corticosteroid use and risk of AEs may not be CSU specific. An important limitation to highlight is that dosage, formulation, and potency of corticosteroids used were not considered in the AE analysis. It is possible that some of the AEs reported were chronic, and since they were not adjusted for patient baseline characteristics or comorbidities, the causality with respect to corticosteroid treatment could not be assessed, a common limitation of retrospective and nonrandomized real-world studies. Of note, immunosuppressive agents such as cyclosporine may be associated with similar AEs to corticosteroids; for example, patients with CSU who have taken cyclosporine have reported AEs such as hypertension, gastrointestinal symptoms, and hyperlipidemia [32, 33]. Although these patients were not excluded from analysis, immunosuppressive agents were only reported in a small proportion of the study population and therefore unlikely to have significantly affected the analysis. Furthermore, the corticosteroids included in this analysis reflect agents commonly used in the management of CSU in the USA only. The effect of corticosteroids on osteoporosis is likely to have been underreported, as changes in bone mineral density are not routinely monitored. Changes in AE-related HCRU were assessed as a whole; therefore, the weighted attribution of specific AEs could not be ascertained. Importantly, patients with severe CSU are more likely to receive corticosteroids and thus experience more AEs and more frequent HCRU. Since corticosteroid usage may not be CSU-specific, the observed HCRU trends may also not necessarily have been due to corticosteroid usage for CSU. Data were from insured patients, who may be more likely to seek care for non-life-threatening diseases versus those who are uninsured. There are no specific ICD-10-CM codes for CSU, and combinations of codes for urticaria were used as proxies; therefore, some patients may have been misclassified. Finally, the dataset contained a considerable proportion of patients with unknown ethnicity and may therefore not be representative of the US population.

Conclusions

This study provides evidence of corticosteroid overuse in the real-world management of CSU. Corticosteroid use, as well as increasing duration of use, was associated with higher proportions of patients experiencing AEs and more frequent HCRU. These findings suggest that corticosteroids should be used sparingly, in favor of alternate effective treatment approaches for CSU, to minimize the risks of AEs and reduce burden on health care systems.
