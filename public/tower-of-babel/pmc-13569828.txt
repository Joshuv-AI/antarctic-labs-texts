Experimental study on the sand discharge characteristics of a swirling sand dredging device in a reservoir area with multiple mud and sand rivers
Authors: ZheHui Wang, Yu Wang, HaiYang Xi, TianFeng Luo, Tian Miao, KaiQing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReservoir sedimentation is a globalized challenge. Existing methods for reservoir desilting have certain limitations and often fail to meet the requirements for sand discharge fully. To address this issue, a swirling sediment dredging device has been developed. Through hydraulic model testing, the characteristics of sand removal, erosion, and deposition patterns were examined under various conditions, including flow velocity, water depth, sand depth, and sand gradation. The experimental results demonstrated that the sand discharge efficiency of the swirling device varies significantly with sand deposition states and is influenced by flow velocity. The silt removal effect is optimal when a sand hopper forms, while the application range expands when a sand-washing corridor is created. When a sand flushing funnel forms, both the funnel radius and the sand discharge amount decrease as water depth and sand deposition depth decrease. The sand gradation after the addition of the swirl device closely resembles that of the pre-sand placement, with the funnel radius and sand discharge rate remaining unaffected by particle size. When a sand-washing corridor forms, both the corridor width and sand discharge increase with increasing flow velocity, water depth, and sand depth. Additionally, sand removal is more effective for particles smaller than 0.83 mm in the sand-flushing corridor, with the swirling-flow device having a more pronounced impact. This study clarifies the applicability of swirling sand removal impeller systems under various water and sand conditions, providing valuable insights for practical engineering applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-53151-w.Keywords: Sand discharge, Model testing, Sand funnel, Sand flushing corridor, Desilting efficiencySubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Hydrology

Introduction

Reservoirs play an indispensable role in mitigating spatiotemporal unevenness in water resources and alleviating the imbalance between water supply and demand1,2. However, sedimentation poses a major threat to reservoir sustainability by progressively reducing storage capacity and undermining their intended benefits. The accumulation of sand, particularly near dam structures, elevates riverbed levels in the backwater zone, decreases channel slope and flow velocity, and compromises drainage capacity. These morphological alterations negatively affect flood discharge, water supply, navigation, and shoreline utilization3,4. Furthermore, poorly managed sedimentation can lead to elevated groundwater tables along reservoir banks, clogging of sand release outlets, and accelerated abrasion of hydropower hydraulic components5.

In arid regions, the sediment challenge is particularly acute. Inland rivers in these areas typically originate on mountainous plateaus, traverse alluvial plains, and terminate in deserts6. Characterized by dry climates and sparse vegetation, these regions suffer from pronounced soil erosion7. A notable hydrological feature is the synchronization of water and sand peaks, resulting in relatively low flow volumes but extremely high sediment concentrations8. The Hexi inland river system in northwestern China exemplifies this challenge, where cascade reservoirs are particularly affected by severe sedimentation, aggravated by highly uneven spatiotemporal precipitation patterns9. In such water-scarce environments, conventional sand management approaches that require substantial water consumption become prohibitively difficult to implement.

Current strategies for managing reservoir sedimentation fall into two main categories: sand removal from the reservoir and sand discharge downstream10. Common techniques include dredging11, siphon sand removal12, and jet pump desilting13. Downstream sand transport methods include retrogressive erosion14, density current venting15, and self-suction sand discharge. Despite their utility, these approaches suffer from significant drawbacks: high water consumption, limited sand transport efficiency, narrow applicability, and substantial economic costs. These limitations underscore the urgent need to develop efficient, water-saving sand-removal technologies that maintain reservoir functionality in water-stressed regions.

Swirl technology offers a promising alternative. Recognized for its high energy concentration, strong flow field intensity, and substantial sand-carrying capacity16, this approach generates vortical flows that can fluidize deposited sand and convert bedload into suspended load for downstream transport. While swirl technology has been widely applied in sand management for channels, pipelines, and stormwater systems17–20, its application to reservoir sedimentation has remained limited. Recent developments, however, indicate growing research interest in this direction. Numerical simulations and hydraulic model studies have demonstrated the potential of swirling-flow devices for reservoir sediment management, with investigations examining flow-field characteristics, optimal impeller configurations, and sand-removal efficiency under varying operational conditions. Additionally, experimental studies on swirling sand discharge devices have examined their performance across varying flow velocities, water depths, sand depths, and grain-size distributions.

To address sediment deposition—particularly in water-scarce regions with high sediment loads—a cyclone sand removal device has been developed. This mechanical device combines principles from mechanical dredging and hydraulic engineering, building upon existing water conservancy sand-removal infrastructure. Powered externally, it generates a swirling flow that fluidizes deposited sediment, converting bedload into suspended load. The sand is then expelled from the reservoir via high-velocity flow through a dedicated sand discharge tunnel. Positioning the device upstream of the flushing outlet enhances sand entrainment and expands the erosion zone in front of the tunnel, thereby improving discharge efficiency. While prior research has established the operational rationale and feasibility of this device21, a systematic investigation of its performance under varying hydraulic and sedimentary conditions remains necessary. The present study investigates the performance and hydraulic characteristics of this cyclone sand removal device using a movable-bed hydraulic model. Specifically, the objectives are to: (1) quantify sand removal efficiency across a range of flow velocities (0–0.3 m/s) and water depths (50–400 mm) representative of field conditions; (2) evaluate the influence of sand characteristics, including deposition thickness (50–100 mm) and grain size distribution (0.05–1.7 mm), on device performance; (3) characterize the relationship between swirl flow parameters and the spatial extent of the erosion zone upstream of the discharge tunnel; and (4) identify optimal operating conditions for maximizing sand discharge efficiency while minimizing water consumption. By systematically linking these experimental parameters to device performance, this research aims to provide both a mechanistic understanding and practical guidance for implementing swirl technology in reservoir sedimentation management, particularly in water-scarce environments with high sand loads.

Methodology

Experimental setup

The experiment was performed in the laboratory of hydraulic constructions (LCH) at Lanzhou University of Technology (LUT, China), where a river-sand bed model test was conducted using the established hydraulic model test device.

Design principle of swirl sand discharge device in reservoir area

This study utilized the self-dynamic force of the discharge of water and the increased velocity of the swirling flow field beneath the sand drainage tunnel. During the tests, sand was pre-laid to simulate sand deposition in the reservoir area. The impeller in the swirl device was then electrically powered to rotate, generating a horizontal flow field that gradually increased the flow velocity in the reservoir area to the critical velocity required to mobilize the sand. Once the bed-load sand became suspended, it was carried away by the water flow through the sand drainage tunnel21, to enhance the volume of sand discharge, as shown in Fig. 1. The swirl sand discharge device is an effective means of silt removal, combining hydraulic machinery with flood discharge facilities.

Schematic diagram of the swirling sand removal device.

Studies have shown that when the water depth in the reservoir area is relatively deep, the water velocity in front of the tunnel decreases significantly, leading to erosion and sand deposition in a funnel-shaped pattern22. In contrast, when the water depth is shallow, particularly when the sand thickness in front of the tunnel is comparable to the water depth, the impact of water pressure is minimal. In this case, the scouring effect of upstream water flow creates a sand-washing corridor extending from the upstream area to the front of the sand-discharge tunnel23. The basic principle of the cyclone device is to use a swirling flow to convert bed-load sand into suspended particles, which the water flow can then carry away. When the sand flushing pattern is funnel-shaped, the swirl device initiates sand movement outside the funnel area, relying on the high-speed water flow generated by the flushing hole to extend the sand-flushing funnel’s range and increase the volume of sand discharged. In the case of a sand-washing corridor, the flow velocity in the center of the corridor is higher than at its edges, causing sand from the edges to be transferred toward the center. This sand is then discharged through the swirl device, thus broadening the sand-washing corridor and enhancing the sand discharge capacity.

Establishment of hydraulic model test in LCH

The prototype reservoir is a small to medium-sized reservoir in Dunhuang, Jiuquan City, primarily used for flood control, irrigation, and power generation. The maximum height of the dam is 40.3 m, with a crest length of 232 m and a crest width of 5 m. The highest water level is 32 m, and the maximum discharge capacity is 180m3/s. The tunnel body is a reinforced-concrete, city-gate-shaped structure, with a width of 4.0 m, a height of 6.0 m, and a length of 32 m. After a hydrological investigation of sand in the reservoir, it was found that its particle size ranges from 0.5 mm to 19.7 mm. Due to the significant changes in riverbed erosion and deposition in this study, a moving bed model was adopted to more effectively investigate the interaction between water flow and sand on the river bed and obtain more accurate riverbed evolution24. This model accounts for the similarity of gravity, resistance, and sand suspension across variations in riverbed erosion and deposition, while ignoring the changes in the riverbed during the simulation of riverbed deformation25,26. To better simulate the flow field and flow state in front of the sand removal tunnel, as well as the changes in the flow field after adding vortex sand removal, the model is designed as a standard model with horizontal and vertical scales of λl and λh, respectively. Measurement parameters and methods

Analyze the influence of different water and sand conditions on the sand discharge characteristics of the cyclone device in the reservoir area based on the changes in sand discharge volume and proportion of sand discharged. For the sand funnel and sand gallery, due to their clear boundaries and small range, a millimeter scale is used to measure their range. During the experiment, sand was pre-laid, the sand discharge tunnel was opened, and a vortex device was added when sand failed to discharge. At this time, the model sand was collected. A sedimentation tank and a filter screen were set up at the outlet and downstream of the sand discharge tunnel. After collecting the sand, it was dried in an oven, sieved with a standard sieve, and weighed on an electronic balance to obtain sand removal amounts for different particle sizes. The proportion of sand by particle size was calculated to evaluate the vortex device’s sand-removal effect in the storage area. The termination condition for marking the end of the experiment is that there is no longer sand-laden water flow in the sand discharge tunnel, and only clear water is discharged. The flow velocity at each point in the storage area and at the sand discharge hole is measured using a handheld portable flow meter (LS300-A, Shandong, China). After laying the predesigned sand with a predetermined particle size, the experiment begins. The sand discharge tunnel is opened, and the collected and discharged sand is recorded as W1 until it can no longer be discharged. After that, the cyclone device is added, and the sand discharge tunnel continues to discharge sand. At this point, the collected and discharged sand is W2. The collected model sand is dried, screened, and weighed, and compared with the particle size and mass of the laid sand. The sand discharge efficiency is calculated from (W2-W1)/W1.

Results

Sand removal characteristics of swirling devices at different incoming flow rates

To investigate the effects of inlet flow velocity on the scouring range and sand removal efficiency of the cyclone sand removal device, hydraulic model experiments were conducted at different inlet flow velocities. The experimental results show that the single-sand flushing process in the sand-flushing tunnel is essentially complete within half an hour. To facilitate the analysis of the universality of the influence of flow velocity across different operating conditions, the ratio of the incoming flow velocity to the critical flow velocity for sand initiation is introduced as a dimensionless parameter, P = V/V0 (where V is the incoming flow velocity and V0 is the critical flow velocity for sand initiation), When P ≥ 1, the incoming flow itself can initiate sediment, and the installed device has minimal effect; when P < 1, the incoming flow cannot initiate sediment, and the swirling device is the main factor affecting sediment discharge. Among them, the critical flow velocity for sand initiation is determined by calculating the experimental sand particle size and water density.

When a “scouring funnel” is formed (with a water depth of 100 mm and a sand depth of 50 mm), the increase in sand discharge after adding a swirl device is not significant under different flow velocity ratios. Independent-samples testing shows that the difference in sand discharge under each condition is not statistically significant (at a significance level greater than 0.05), indicating that the effect of flow velocity on the swirl device’s synergistic effect is relatively small in this form. When a “scouring corridor” is formed (both water depth and sand depth are 100 mm), the sand discharge rate shows a significant upward trend with the increase of the flow velocity ratio. There is a significant linear relationship between the two (coefficient of determination is 0.98, significance level is less than 0.01), indicating that in the form of a “scouring corridor”, the inflow velocity is the key factor affecting the sand discharge effect (Fig. 4a).

Particle size and sand discharge at different flow velocities: a change of sand discharge under different flow velocities; b particle size of sand discharge at different flow velocities.

Under the condition of a “scouring funnel” with a sand depth of 50 mm and a water depth of 100 mm, there was no significant change in the amount of sand discharged after adding a swirl device, so the particle size composition of the discharged sand was not analyzed. Under the condition of a “scouring corridor” with a water depth and sand depth of 100 mm, when the flow velocity is relatively low (corresponding to an incoming flow velocity of 0.104 m/s), the proportion of particles with a diameter greater than 0.83–1.7 mm in the sand discharge reaches 49%; Sand with particle sizes ranging from 0.15 mm to 0.83 mm are more fully disturbed by incoming flow and have better sand discharge effects. When the flow velocity is relatively high (corresponding to an incoming flow velocity of 0.302 m/s), the addition of a swirl device significantly increases the total sand discharge, with particles larger than 0.05 mm being the main sand discharge. Overall, fine particles with a particle size less than 0.83 mm always meet the starting flow velocity requirements in sand removal. In comparison, coarse particles with a particle size greater than 0.83 mm have significant differences in sand removal effects at different flow velocities, indicating that the sand removal effect of the cyclone device on coarse particles is greatly affected by the incoming flow velocity (Fig. 4b).

Sand removal characteristics of the swirl device with different water depth and sand depth

The water depth in the reservoir area is one of the key factors affecting the sand discharge capacity of the sand discharge tunnel. To analyze the coupling effect of water depth and sand depth, the relative water depth ratio Hw/Hs (where Hw is the water depth and Hs is the sand depth) is introduced as a dimensionless parameter. The experimental results show that under the same inflow conditions, when Hw/Hs>1, a “scouring funnel” is formed at the sand interface; When Hw/Hs=1 or slightly greater than 1, a “scouring corridor” is formed. The sand discharge increases with the increase of water depth and sand depth, and the synergistic effect of the cyclone device under the “scouring corridor” condition is significantly better than that under the “scouring funnel” condition (Fig. 5).

Under the condition of “scouring corridors”, the influence of the cyclone device on the particle size composition of sand discharge is more significant. When Hw/Hs=1 and the water depth increases from 75 mm to 400 mm, the proportion of particles with a diameter greater than 0.05–0.15 mm in sand discharge increases, while the proportion of particles with a diameter greater than 0.15–1.7 mm decreases; When Hw/Hs=0.25 (water depth 100 mm, sand depth 400 mm), the particle size composition of the sand is basically the same as the original gradation, indicating that the cyclone device has a weak effect on particle size sorting under this working condition.

The experimental results of adding a vortex sand removal device at different water and sand depths to discharge the proportion of various sand particles show that when the inflow velocity remains constant and the water and sand depth are both 75 mm and increased from 100 mm to 400 mm, the proportion of sand particles with a particle size greater than 0.05–0.15 mm in the discharged sand increases, and the proportion of sand particles with a particle size greater than 0.15–1.7 mm decreases; When the water depth is 100 mm and the sand depth is 400 mm, the proportion of discharged sand particle size remains unchanged as the depth of sedimentation decreases. When forming a flushing funnel, the cyclone flushing device has little effect on the flushing performance of the flushing tunnel. Only the flow velocity within the radius of the flushing funnel flow field under the action of water pressure reaches the starting flow velocity of sand, and the discharged sand is consistent with the original sand gradation; When a sand flushing corridor is formed, the addition of a vortex sand flushing device increases the amount of sand flushing significantly, improves the sand flushing performance of the sand flushing tunnel, and increases the proportion of sand > 0.05–0.15 mm in the discharged sand. Further analysis shows that under the “scouring funnel” condition, only within the radius of the scouring funnel can the flow velocity reach the sand initiation condition, and the particle size composition of the sand discharge is not significantly different from the original gradation. The improvement of sand discharge performance by the cyclone device is limited. Under the “scouring corridor” condition, the swirl device significantly increased the effective scouring width, resulting in a significant increase in the discharge ratio of fine particles (> 0.05–0.15 mm) (Fig. 6).

Sand removal characteristics of swirl sand removal devices under different sand gradations

There are significant differences in sand conditions among watersheds, mainly reflected in bulk density, particle size, and gradation distribution27. To analyze the adaptability of the cyclone sand removal device to different incoming sands, three types of pre-embedded sand with median particle sizes (0.83 mm, 0.425 mm, 0.15 mm) were tested, and the ratio of sand particle size to median particle size (d/d50) was used as a dimensionless parameter to normalize the particle size distribution under different gradations.

Under the condition of “scouring corridor” (with both water depth and sand depth of 100 mm), the addition of a swirl device increased the amount of sand discharged by 5.87%, 6.17%, and 6.59% respectively compared to the condition without the device; Under the “scouring funnel” condition (water depth of 400 mm, sand depth of 100 mm), the lifting amplitudes are 3.34%, 3.86%, and 4.82%, respectively. The increase in sand discharge is negatively correlated with the median particle size of pre-buried sand, indicating that a higher fine-particle content yields a more pronounced synergistic effect of the cyclone device. In addition, the sand removal efficiency under the “scouring corridor” condition was significantly higher than that under the “scouring funnel” condition. After paired sample testing, the difference between the two was statistically significant (significance level less than 0.01) (Fig. 7-c). Under the “scouring corridor” condition, when the median particle size of the original sand is 0.83 mm, the content of particles smaller than 0.15 mm in the sand discharge increases compared to the original gradation, while the content of particles larger than 0.15 mm decreases; When the median particle size is 0.425 mm, the content of particles with a particle size less than 0.425 mm increases and the content of particles with a particle size greater than 0.425 mm decreases in sand discharge; When the median particle size is 0.15 mm, the variation pattern of particle size composition is consistent with that when the median particle size is 0.83 mm (Fig. 7a). Under the “scouring funnel” condition (water depth of 400 mm, sand depth of 100 mm), the particle size composition of the sand is basically the same as the original gradation, indicating that the cyclone device has a weak effect on particle size sorting under this condition (Fig. 7b).

Discussion

Analysis of the morphological changes of sand erosion and deposition in front of the sand drainage tunnel

Upon dam construction, most of the sand transport toward the dam occurs in the form of suspended load. The combined effects of backwater and circulation in the forebay region induce a sharp reduction in flow velocity, prompting sand settlement and its subsequent transformation into bed load, which accumulates near the dam structure28. During the operation of the sand drainage tunnel, the mobilized sand exhibits two distinct deposition patterns: the sand washing funnel and the sand washing gallery, the formation of which is primarily governed by water depth29. However, due to geometric constraints and limited opening dimensions, the scouring and silting zones tend to stabilize, thereby limiting the complete expulsion of deposited sand from the forebay region11. Experimental results indicate that introducing the swirl sand removal device significantly expands both the sand-washing funnel and the gallery. While inflow velocity exerts a negligible influence on funnel dimensions, the width of the sand washing gallery increases proportionally with flow velocity30. Notably, the incipient motion of uniform sand particles is not governed solely by velocity; water pressure and flow direction also play critical roles31. An increase in water pressure alters the funnel slope, causing flow to align with it. In contrast, the flow direction within the sand washing gallery remains predominantly horizontal, resulting in a lower incipient velocity for particles of equivalent size within the funnel region.

The formation of the sand washing funnel and gallery is jointly influenced by water depth and sand depth. With increasing water depth, both the outlet velocity at the dredging orifice and the effective discharge range expand. Concurrently, sand discharge rate increases with greater sand depth. Post-installation of the swirl device, rising water pressure increases both near-field flow velocity and the pressure exerted on sand particles, thereby elevating the axial velocity gradient near the tunnel inlet. When water depth increases, both the slope and radial extent of the funnel increase; under constant water depth, the slope remains stable, but increased sand depth yields a larger funnel radius. In scenarios where water depth is equal to or slightly less than the sand depth, the upstream scouring effect promotes the development of a sand-washing corridor extending from upstream to the drainage inlet. Provided that the drainage inlet remains unobstructed, increases in both water and sand depth positively influence dredging efficiency. In the three tested particle size fractions, the funnel radius increased by 4.4% -48.2% after the operation of the vortex device. The effect of this device in expanding the funnel range is significant, although particle size has no significant impact on the funnel geometry. For the sand-flushing corridor, the width has increased by 88%- 90%. Therefore, compared to funnel-dominated configurations, swirl devices are more effective at increasing corridor width and achieving higher discharge efficiency. For non-uniform sand mixtures, coarser particles exhibit greater fluidity, while finer particles exhibit higher resistance to entrainment32. The cyclone device effectively expands the funnel and gallery range, thereby improving the reservoir’s baseline sand-removal capacity. Analysis of sand discharge characteristics

The swirl sand removal device operates by leveraging high-velocity flows in the drainage tunnel and upstream scouring effects, using rotational flow to suspend and transport sand. Flow field characteristics differ markedly between the sand-washing funnel and the gallery, resulting in variations in the incipient sand size. Experimental results demonstrate that variations in flow velocity exert minimal influence on sand discharge rates, with the particle-size distribution of the discharged sand remaining broadly consistent with that of the pre-deposited material. This behavior is attributed to the limited spatial extent of the sand-washing funnel, despite its locally high flow-field intensity.

Water depth, flow velocity, and sand depth collectively influence the extent of sedimentation zones but do not substantially alter sand discharge characteristics. The sand washing funnel enhances the removal of sand particles in the 0.15–1.7 mm range. Following swirl device activation within the sand washing corridor configuration, the proportion of discharged particles finer than 0.83 mm increases with flow velocity. Overall, the mean particle size of discharged sand decreases, while coarser fractions tend to remain in the reservoir. For sand with varying median diameters, the proportion of particles finer than 0.15 mm increases, while that of particles coarser than 0.425 mm decreases. In corridor-type configurations, the swirl device facilitates the removal of particles finer than 0.425 mm. Synthesizing these observations, the swirl device exhibits dual-mode behavior: at greater water depths, it discharges coarser sand but operates over a smaller dredging extent; at shallow water depths, it discharges finer sand over a larger area. These trends align with the overarching objectives of this study. Given the absence of prior full-scale applications of this novel technology, the experimental design adhered to the Geotechnical Test Method Standard GBT50123-201933, employing quartz sand fractions ranging from 0.05 mm to 1.7 mm. Hydraulic parameters were scaled according to prototype reservoir conditions. The proposed device integrates mechanical and hydraulic dredging principles, offering a low-cost, highly adaptable solution. Experimental outcomes delineate the operational envelope of the swirl device across varying water and sand depths, including dredging extent and ranges of removable particle sizes.

To facilitate practical deployment, the following application strategies are proposed: under high water depth conditions, where a sand washing funnel dominates, device installation is confined to areas above the funnel; under low water depth conditions, where a sand washing corridor forms, devices can be installed along both sides of the corridor to enhance its effective width. By adapting device deployment to fluctuations in reservoir water level, one or more units can be activated to optimize sand removal efficiency. This study clarifies the influence of key variables on device performance and provides a basis for adjusting operational parameters—such as water depth and flow velocity—to maximize sand discharge.

Comparative performance, applicability, and research limitations

Reservoir sedimentation not only diminishes active storage capacity and affects structural stability but also influences downstream ecological conditions through nutrient adsorption onto fine sand34. Existing sand removal methods are broadly classified as mechanical dredging, typically employed during dry seasons, and hydraulic flushing, utilized during high-flow periods. Mechanical dredging achieves high removal efficiency but incurs substantial economic costs and labor demands35,36. Hydraulic flushing is cost-effective but relies heavily on hydrological conditions and sand deposition patterns, often resulting in inconsistent performance37.

The swirl sand removal device proposed herein bridges these two paradigms by combining mechanical action with hydraulic energy. Experimental results confirm its operational viability across both dry and wet seasons, achieving favorable dredging efficiency with low associated costs. In high-water conditions, the device can be deployed via dredging vessels or fixed installations on the reservoir bed. In shallow-water conditions, it can be positioned along the margins of the sand washing corridor. To enable a more rigorous comparison with established technologies, performance metrics must be contextualized within a consistent framework. Existing methods exhibit a wide range of sand discharge rates: mechanical dredgers operate between 100 and 4500 m³/h but with substantial energy consumption38; siphon dredging achieves average sand concentrations of approximately 152 kg/m³39,40; jet pump dredging exceeds 100 m³/h, though performance is equipment-dependent41,42; tractive scouring can reach up to 1500 m³/s, contingent on inflow and sand characteristics43. In the Arda Magnano reservoir basin, natural tractive scouring during storm events generated bimodal sand hydrographs with peak suspended sand concentrations reaching 37.39 g/L44. Self-priming dredging systems achieve economic flow velocities of 5–6 m/s45.

In this study, the average model-scale sand discharge rate was 9 kg/h. When scaled to prototype conditions based on density and geometric similarity, this corresponds to a sand discharge rate exceeding 350 m³/h, positioning the proposed device within the higher tier of existing technologies. However, direct comparisons remain incomplete without systematic accounting for energy consumption, water usage, and operational costs. For instance, the reported 43–57% increase in funnel radius refers to configurations with the swirl device versus without it. In contrast, claims of “relatively high” discharge rates lack explicit benchmarking against specific technologies under equivalent hydraulic conditions. Future comparative studies should adopt standardized metrics such as sand discharge per unit energy input (kg/kWh) or per unit water volume to enable more equitable assessments.

Quantitative operational thresholds also require further refinement. Although the discussion distinguishes between “high” and “low” water depths, it provides no explicit depth thresholds. A more mechanistic criterion, such as the ratio of water depth to sand depth (h/H), could serve as a more robust discriminant for funnel- versus corridor-dominated regimes. Such parameterization would enhance the generalizability of findings across different reservoir contexts. From a structural standpoint, this section currently spans a wide array of topics—including device limitations, comparative performance, application scenarios, and future research directions—and could benefit from clearer segmentation. A dedicated subsection on “Comparative Analysis with Existing Technologies” would improve logical flow and thematic focus.

Finally, the study’s limitations warrant more comprehensive acknowledgment. While the discussion addresses certain design parameters (e.g., impeller configuration), it does not sufficiently consider scale effects inherent to physical modeling, differences between model and prototype sand, or the implications of using clear-water fixed-bed experiments to simulate prototype conditions characterized by turbid water and mobile beds. These factors may influence the extrapolation of results to field applications. Addressing these issues in future research, particularly through site-specific validation and sensitivity analysis, will be essential for establishing the practical reliability of the swirl sand removal device.

Conclusion

This study develops a swirl sand-removal device to enhance sand-discharge efficiency in reservoir sand-drainage tunnels. Through hydraulic model experiments, the effects of flow velocity, sand gradation, sand depth, and water depth on the sand removal characteristics of the swirl impeller assembly were systematically investigated. The main conclusions are as follows.

The swirl device significantly expands both the sand washing funnel and sand washing corridor. Compared with configurations without the device, the funnel radius increased by approximately 43–57%, and the sand-washing corridor width increased by approximately 90% under equivalent hydraulic conditions (Fig. 9). These expansions directly enhance the effective scouring range of the reservoir forebay.

The formation of a funnel versus a corridor is governed by water depth and sand depth, with the ratio of water depth to sand depth (h/H) serving as a potential discriminant. When water depth is high relative to sand depth, a funnel-dominated pattern emerges, characterized by limited scouring but locally high flow-field intensity. When water depth is low or slightly less than sand depth, a corridor-dominated pattern develops, extending upstream and enabling broader sand removal.

Sand discharge characteristics differ fundamentally between the two morphodynamical patterns. In funnel configurations, sand discharge rate remains largely unaffected by flow velocity, and the particle size distribution of discharged sand closely resembles that of pre-deposited material. In corridor configurations, higher flow velocities increase the proportion of discharged particles finer than 0.83 mm, while coarser fractions (>0.425 mm) tend to remain in the reservoir. Consequently, the swirl device exhibits dual-mode behavior depending on water depth: at greater water depths, it discharges coarser sand but operates over a smaller dredging extent (funnel mode); at shallower water depths, it discharges finer sand over a larger dredging extent (corridor mode). This dual-mode characteristic aligns with the overall objectives of adaptable reservoir sand management.
