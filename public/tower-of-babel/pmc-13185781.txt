Reconstructing protein conformations to activate follicular enzyme-nutrient reservoirs for reversing follicle aging
Authors: Shifeng Ling, Mingyue Liu, Chen Meng, Kaifeng Huo, Yida Wang, Yixiang Zhang, Yingying Wei, Lucia Zhou, Yawei Du, Wenguo Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hair regeneration depends on a reservoir of follicular enzymes that regulate the microenvironment and provide metabolic support. However, in degenerative alopecia, this enzymatic reservoir is disrupted, impairing microenvironmental homeostasis. Here, through bioinformatics and molecular dynamics analyses, we revealed that several Zn2+/Cu2+-dependent proteases (e.g., LOX and SOD1) were downregulated and exhibit aberrant conformations and activities in degenerative alopecia, further weakening microenvironmental support. To address this, we developed a Zn2+/Cu2+-doped decellularized extracellular matrix microneedle patch (dECMMAZn/Cu) to rapidly restore the follicular microenvironment and enzymatic reservoir function. In vitro, microneedle delivery of dECMMAZn/Cu significantly upregulated key Zn2+/Cu2+-dependent proteases. Restoration of the enzymatic reservoir reduced p16 expression in dermal papilla stem cells, inhibited cellular senescence, and promoted stem cell proliferation. In a classical degenerative alopecia animal model, dECMMAZn/Cu microneedle treatment resulted in denser and more continuous dermal collagen, a significant increase in hair follicle numbers, and promoted hair regeneration. In summary, this study establishes an innovative microneedle material that restores follicular function by remodeling protein conformation and activating the enzymatic reservoir.

Introduction

Hair regeneration relies on a reservoir of follicular enzymes that maintain the microenvironment and provide metabolic support [[1], [2], [3]]. This enzymatic reservoir is essential for regulating follicle aging by sustaining stem cell function and microenvironmental homeostasis. Among these enzymes, metalloproteases play a particularly critical role [4]. Their activity depends on metal ion-mediated conformational stability. However, aging or prolonged external stress reduces protein stability, leading to metalloprotease inactivation, disruption of catalytic centers and substrate recognition, and ultimately impaired extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and stem cell niche function [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. Current treatments, such as minoxidil and finasteride, mainly target hormonal pathways and have limited efficacy, as they do not address the underlying loss of enzymatic function caused by conformational destabilization [[9], [10], [11]]. Conventional protein delivery and trace-element supplementation fall short of fully reactivating the enzymatic reservoir. Furthermore, clinical application is challenged by poor transdermal delivery, uneven distribution, and limited tissue penetration. Therefore, innovative strategies are urgently needed to address both follicular microenvironmental aging and enzymatic reservoir dysfunction (see Scheme 1).Scheme 1Schematic illustration of the Zn2+/Cu2+-doped decellularized extracellular matrix microneedle system (dECMMAZn/Cu) to for reversing follicle aging. Enzyme activity depends on their conformational structure, especially for metalloproteases with metal ions at their active centers, where conformational stability is critical for catalytic function. Metalloproteases typically exist as inactive zymogens and require metal-induced conformational changes to transition into their active forms [12]. These ions coordinate with amino acid residues (e.g., histidine, cysteine) to alter the metalloprotease conformation, stabilize catalytic domains, and enhance substrate binding and catalytic efficiency. Importantly, Zn2+ and Cu2+ primarily regulate enzyme activity through coordination-mediated conformational stabilization rather than directly increasing enzyme expression, thereby restoring catalytic competence in metalloproteases. For example, zinc is a core cofactor in matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), especially MMP-2 and MMP-9, which are cyclically expressed in follicles to regulate ECM remodeling [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. MMP-9 is significantly upregulated during follicle regression and requires zinc to maintain its catalytic domain structure [17]. Similarly, copper is essential for lysyl oxidase (LOX) activity, facilitating collagen and elastin crosslinking, enhancing ECM mechanical stability, and supporting follicle reconstruction [18]. Importantly, the follicular enzymatic reservoir is highly sensitive to local ion concentrations. These metal ions not only serve as essential cofactors but also act as triggers for conformational activation. Their mechanisms can be summarized as: (1) structural stabilizers that promote folding or stabilize active states [19,20]; and (2) catalytic cofactors that enhance substrate-induced fit, boosting reaction specificity and efficiency [21,22]. Based on this mechanism, recent research has focused on ion-induced activation strategies for hair regeneration. Controlled local delivery of metal ions can restore the conformational and functional states of key proteases, enabling ECM renewal and follicle regeneration. Precisely regulating ion concentrations and their timing in the follicular microenvironment is thus crucial for reversing hair loss and offers new avenues for conformation-driven protein activation therapies.

There is a significant skin barrier that limits efficient follicular delivery. Recently, microneedle (MN) patches have emerged as a promising strategy for transdermal drug delivery and microenvironmental regulation [10,[23], [24], [25], [26]]. Their micron-scale needle tips can penetrate the stratum corneum to enable localized, sustained drug release, improving therapeutic efficacy and minimizing systemic side effects. Compared to topical or oral routes, MNs allow direct dermal delivery, enhancing local drug concentration, especially useful for inflammation, regeneration, and ECM repair [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Conventional MNs are typically made from biodegradable (e.g., PLA, PCL) or natural polymers (e.g., hyaluronic acid, gelatin), offering biocompatibility and fabrication flexibility. However, most current systems focus on single-drug delivery and cannot meet the complex requirements of the aging follicular stem cell niche. Hair follicle aging is accompanied by ECM degradation, signaling disruption, and stem cell dysfunction, necessitating materials with enhanced bioactivity and structural support [[31], [32], [33]]. Decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM) holds great potential due to its preserved bioactive components and native ECM signals [[34], [35], [36], [37], [38]]. Integrating dECM into MNs provides a biomimetic scaffold for stem cells, promoting adhesion, migration, and differentiation via endogenous growth factor release [39]. However, native dECM suffers from poor mechanical strength and rapid degradation. To address these limitations, methacryloyl modification (dECMMA) introduces photopolymerizable methacrylate groups, enabling the formation of a crosslinked network under UV light, enhancing mechanical stability and controlled degradation [40,41]. Furthermore, doping with ions allows dynamic coordination with dECM functional groups (e.g., carboxyl, hydroxyl), creating a reinforced network with improved mechanics [39]. Such dECMMA-based microneedles thus offer a targeted and sustained solution for stem cell niche rejuvenation and follicular regeneration [42].

In this study, bioinformatics and molecular dynamics analyses revealed that several Zn2+/Cu2+-dependent metalloproteases, including LOX and SOD1, are downregulated and exhibit abnormal conformations and activities in degenerative alopecia, further impairing the follicular microenvironment. To address this, we developed a Zn2+/Cu2+-doped dECMMA microneedle patch (dECMMAZn/Cu), designed to rapidly restore the follicular microenvironment and enzymatic reservoir function. UV-induced crosslinking enhanced the mechanical strength of the microneedles, enabling them to effectively penetrate the stratum corneum and deliver both dECM and essential ions directly to the target site. In vitro, dECMMAZn/Cu treatment reduced p16 expression in dermal papilla stem cells, inhibited cellular senescence, and promoted proliferation. It also enhanced angiogenesis in hUVECs and facilitated macrophage polarization from a pro-inflammatory to an anti-inflammatory phenotype. In vivo, in a classical androgenetic alopecia (AGA) model, dECMMAZn/Cu microneedles activated Zn2+/Cu2+-dependent metalloproteases and significantly promoted hair regeneration within 14 days post-treatment. In summary, this study achieved the synchronous delivery of metal ions and dECM into the follicular microenvironment via a microneedle system, enabling rapid reconstruction of ECM structure and restoration of protease activity conformations. Ultimately, this approach reversed follicular aging and significantly promoted hair regeneration.

Materials and methods

Decellularized matrix methacrylation hydrogel and microneedle preparation

Decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM) was prepared from mouse dorsal skin tissue. Briefly, skin tissues were harvested, and hair and subcutaneous fat were removed. The samples were treated with 1% SDS for 3-4 days with daily solution changes to remove cellular components, followed by isopropanol treatment to eliminate lipids. The tissues were extensively washed with deionized water to remove residual reagents, freeze-dried, and ground into powder for further use. dECM methacrylate (dECMMA) was synthesized according to a modified protocol based on previous reports. Briefly, purified dECM was dissolved in 0.5 M acetic acid at 4 °C overnight and adjusted to pH 8-9. Methacrylic anhydride was added dropwise under stirring and allowed to react for 48 h at 4 °C. The product was dialyzed against deionized water for 5-7 days, followed by freeze-drying to obtain dECMMA [[43], [44], [45]]. Metal ion-doped dECMMA precursor solutions were prepared by adding metal salts to the dECMMA solution. Specifically, CuSO4·5H2O (Sigma-Aldrich, MS) was added at 2% (w/v) for dECMMACu, ZnCl2 at 2% (w/v) for dECMMAZn, and CuSO4·5H2O plus ZnCl2 at 1% (w/v) each for dECMMAZn/Cu. The mixtures were stirred until homogeneous. For microneedle fabrication, 0.1% (w/v) LAP was added to the precursor solution, which was then cast into microneedle molds and exposed to UV light (365 nm, ∼8 mW/cm2) for 5 min to induce crosslinking. After curing, the microneedle patches were carefully removed. The resulting microneedle arrays consisted of a high-density circular arrangement of square pyramidal needles, with a height of approximately 850 μm, a base width of approximately 380 × 380 μm, and a tip diameter of approximately 15-20 μm. The center-to-center spacing between adjacent microneedles was approximately 600 μm. Each patch contained approximately 688 microneedles within an effective circular area of approximately 19 mm in diameter. Microneedle extracts were prepared by incubating fully crosslinked microneedles (≈0.07 g) in 100 mL of complete culture medium at 37 °C for 24 h, allowing swelling-induced degradation and release of loaded components.

Fourier infrared (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), thermal stability (TGA) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

The morphology of the prepared scaffolds was examined by digital camera (EOS 90D, Canon, Japan) and SEM (Nova NanoSEM 450, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Hillsboro, OR), and the elemental distribution in the scaffolds was examined by X-ray energy dispersive spectroscopy. Total reflection Fourier infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed on dECM with a scanning wavelength range of 600 ∼ 3600 cm−1 For XRD analysis, the crystalline phase of the samples was detected using a Bruker AXS D8 Discover X-ray diffractometer at 2θ between 10° and 60°. A mass of 5 mg of FC-support with different contents was taken and placed in an alumina crucible, which was then placed in the TGA instrument. The program was set as follows: temperature: 700 °C, heating rate: 20 °C/min, protected by nitrogen gas. A certain amount of the scaffold was taken and the elemental composition of the scaffold was tested by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS).

dECM ion release assay

In this study, we performed tests for the release of copper (Cu2+) and zinc (Zn2+) by incubating the samples at 37 °C in 0.9% saline. At predesigned time points, the incubation solution was collected for analysis and replaced with fresh saline. The concentrations of Cu2+ and Zn2+ (n = 3) were determined by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) (Prodigy Plus, Teledyne Leeman Labs, Hudson, NH).

Extraction and culture of DPSCs

The 4-week-old C57BL/6 mice were taken and killed by breaking the neck, the back hair trunk was shaved with a shaver, the dorsal skin of mice was sterilized with 75% ethanol, the whole dorsal skin was cut off, rinsed with PBS containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin for 3 times, and digested with 0.2% type I collagenase at 37 °C for 2 h, and the fat around the follicles and dermal tissues were removed. After rinsing 3 times with PBS, the dermis was scraped off the bulb of the hair follicle with scissors face upward, and then blown uniformly in PBS solution, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min to remove the supernatant, and then resuspended in whole culture (10% FBS + DMEM) and added to an equal amount of 9% Ficoll solution, and then centrifuged in a gradient of 200 rpm for 5 min to take the lower portion of the follicle, and then centrifuged at 1000 r/min for 5 min to take the sediment, and then added again to whole culture, and then mixed well. The whole culture was added again, mixed well and added to an equal amount of 9% Ficoll solution, centrifuged at 200 rpm gradient for 5 min, the upper part was taken, centrifuged at 800 rpm for 5 min, and the precipitate was taken for incubation. 48 h later, the solution was changed, and the cells were allowed to fuse to 90%, and then passaged culture was performed according to the 1:2.

Cell culture and rationale for selection

Dermal papilla stem cells (DPSCs), Raw264.7 macrophages, and HUVECs were used in this study. DPSCs were isolated from murine dorsal skin and maintained in DMEM (Life Sciences, Logan, UT) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Life Sciences, Logan, UT), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Raw 264.7 cells were cultured in DMEM (Life Sciences, Logan, UT) with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. HUVECs were cultured in DMEM (Life Sciences, Logan, UT) with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. The identity of HUVEC cells used in this study was authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling prior to experiments. The STR electropherogram and allele profiles are provided in Fig. S1 and Table S1.

Cytotoxicity test

After the cells were resuspended by trypsin digestion, they were inoculated into 24-well plates according to 20,000 cells/well, and after the cells were attached to the wall, 200 μL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) solution was added to each well after intervening under different conditions for 1, 4, and 7 days, and then incubated at 37 °C and protected from light for 2 h, and then the absorbance of the solution was measured using an enzyme counter at 450 nm.

Staining of live and dead cells

The cells were digested and resuspended with trypsin and then inoculated into 24-well plates at 20,000/well. After the cells were attached to the wall and intervened under different conditions for 1, 4 and 7 days, the cells were stained for live-dead cells using Calcein/PI Cell Activity and Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the instructions. Briefly, the inoculated cells were gently washed with PBS for 3 times, and then each well was incubated with an appropriate amount of Calcein/PI working solution for 10 min at 37 °C, protected from light, and the live cells were observed using a fluorescence microscope, and the number of live and dead cells were counted using Image J. The cells were then stained with the Calcein/PI working solution for 10 min at 37 °C, protected from light [46].

SA-β-gal staining

Cells were stained using Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the instructions. Briefly, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, washed three times with PBS, and incubated overnight at 37 °C protected from light by adding SA-β-gal staining working solution. The next day, the cells were observed under an ordinary light microscope and photographed for recording. The number of positive cells was counted using Image J [47].

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using FreeZol Reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the instructions, cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription using HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and qRT-PCR experiments were performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR The cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription using ChamQ Universal SYBR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was used to perform qRT-PCR experiments. The expression of target genes was normalized using GAPDH. The relative expression of the target genes was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. At least three biological replicates were performed for each target gene. The primers used in the experiments are shown in Table S2.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and then blocked with QuickBlock™ Blocking Buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 30 min, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies including: anti-p16 antibody (rabbit polyclonal, 10883-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), Anti-γ-H2AX (rabbit monoclonal, 83307-2-RR, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). On the next day, the cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488, A0423, Beyotime, China) or goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488, A0428, Beyotime, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, DAPI (P0131, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added to stain the nuclei. The fluorescence images of the cells were taken using a laser confocal microscope, and the average fluorescence intensity or the number of positive cells in each group was counted using Image J. The average fluorescence intensity or the number of positive cells in each group was calculated using Image J.

Total protein was extracted after lysis of cells using RIPA. Macrophage polarization assay

Raw 246.7 cells were wall-plated and 10 ng/mL of LPS was added to induce macrophage M1 polarization. dECMMA, dECMMAZn, dECMMACu, and dECMMAZn/Cu were added for treatment, and immunofluorescence detection or flow assay was performed after 12 h of co-culture.

HUVECs vascularization experiment

To observe the effect of materials on cell morphology and connectivity structure during the vascularization of HUVECs, 6-well confocal culture dishes were pre-laid with Matrigel (200 μL/well) and placed in 37 °C for 30 min of incubation for gelation. Subsequently, HUVECs were inoculated on the surface of Matrigel at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well, and the culture medium of different treatment groups was added, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 6 h. After incubation, the plates were washed gently with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized, closed, and then subjected to immunofluorescence staining for confocal laser microscopy to observe the vascular-like structure, cellular connection and skeleton remodeling and other indexes [48].

Bulk RNA sequencing

Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Kit (Vazyme Co., Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA quality was verified with an Agilent 4200 TapeStation (G2991A, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) and quantified using a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Cat#Q33216, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA)/NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). High-quality RNA was used to construct sequencing libraries with the VAHTS Stranded mRNA-seq Library Prep Kit (NR602, Vazyme Co., Nanjing, China). Library concentration and integrity were assessed using the Qubit system and TapeStation. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina platform using paired-end 150 bp reads (PE150). Illumina sequencing by synthesis (SBS) technology incorporates fluorescently labeled nucleotides during cluster amplification, with emission signals captured and translated into base calls. Data processing and statistical analyses were performed on a Linux-based computational platform using R software for transcriptomic analysis and visualization.

AGA model

The AGA model was established by subcutaneous injection of testosterone solution on the back of C57BL/6 mice (n = 3). During the experimental period (28 days), each animal was injected daily with 0.1 mL of 5 mg/mL testosterone solution. 2 × 3 cm2 of hair on the dorsal skin was scraped off on the 14th day after injection. Mice were randomized into groups. The skin of each animal was photographed every 2 days. Another healthy mouse without testosterone injection was used as a positive control group. At the end of the experiment, the area covered by new hairs in the treated skin was measured and the skin of each animal was collected for subsequent studies [49].

H&E staining and immunohistochemistry

Tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h, dehydrated in graded ethanol, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm (skin). Bone samples were decalcified in 10% EDTA solution (pH 7.4) at 4 °C for 21 days with solution changes every two days prior to embedding. Histopathological features were evaluated by H&E staining. For immunohistochemistry, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval in sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by blocking with goat serum. Sections were then incubated overnight with primary antibodies and subsequently with biotinylated secondary antibodies. H&E and IHC staining were performed using standard protocols. Quantification was conducted with ImageJ software: positive staining area or integrated optical density (IOD) was measured from at least five randomly selected fields per sample.

Masson's trichrome staining of skin sections

Dorsal skin tissues were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (4 μm) were cut using a microtome and mounted on glass slides. After deparaffinization and rehydration, the sections were mordanted in Bouin's solution at 56 °C for 1 h to enhance staining contrast, followed by washing in running tap water until the yellow color disappeared. Nuclear staining was performed using Weigert's iron hematoxylin for 10 min, followed by rinsing with distilled water. Cytoplasmic and muscle components were stained with Biebrich scarlet-acid fuchsin solution for 10 min, then differentiated with phosphomolybdic-phosphotungstic acid solution for 10 min. Collagen fibers were stained with aniline blue for 5 min, followed by a brief rinse with 1% acetic acid. Finally, sections were dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin. The collagen fibers appeared blue, cytoplasm and muscle red, and nuclei dark brown or black. Stained slides were imaged using a light microscope (3DHISTECH, Hungary).

Data analysis

Results

Impact of metalloproteases activity on aging of hair follicle stem cell niche

The follicular enzymatic reservoir is a key system for regulating hair follicle stem cell activity and ECM homeostasis, with its core components comprising various metal ion-dependent metalloproteases [50,51]. These enzymes typically exist as inactive zymogens and require conformational rearrangement induced by metal ions such as Zn2+ and Cu2+ to become active. Once activated, they mediate the degradation and remodeling of the ECM.

In this work, we first used bioinformatics analysis (NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). Dataset: GSE184501, n = 3.) to explore the relationship between Zn2+/Cu2+-dependent metalloproteases and ECM homeostasis during hair follicle niche aging. Volcanogram analysis (Fig. 1A) revealed that a large number of genes were significantly down- or up-regulated in the alopecia group. Further analysis revealed that the alopecia-associated senescence marker gene CDKN2A was significantly up-regulated in the alopecia group (Fig. 1B), validating the senescence signature of the alopecia samples. In the analysis of metal ion-related protease expression, we found that both the zinc ion-dependent MMPs family and the copper ion-dependent LOX family were significantly reduced in alopecia areata samples. Specifically, heat map analysis (Fig. 1D) showed that the expression of zinc-dependent proteases MMP-2, MMP-9, MMP-24, MMP-19, MMP-10, and MMP-14 was significantly down-regulated in the alopecia areata group, suggesting that zinc-ion-dependent remodeling of the ECM was significantly diminished, reduced expression of zinc finger protein families (ZFPs) indicates reduced zinc transport capacity (Fig. 1F) Meanwhile, the copper ion-dependent lysyl oxidase family (LOX, LOXL2, LOXL3) expression was also significantly reduced in the alopecia areata group (Fig. 1E). Quantitative analysis (Fig. 1C) further confirmed the significant decrease of LOX, LOXL2, and LOXL3. In addition, the superoxide dismutase family (SODs), which are key enzymes for oxidative stress, especially Sod1, Sod2, and Sod3, were also significantly down-regulated in the alopecia group. We identified cellular extracellular matrix orgnization (Fig. 1G), collagen-containing extracellular matrix (Fig. 1H), collagen binding (Fig. 1I) by GO analysis of 3425 genes with significantly decreased expression), extracellular matrix binding (Fig. 1I), and other extracellular matrix-associated biological processes with reduced biological activity. KEGG analysis revealed reduced activity of ECM-receptor interaction biological processes (Fig. 1J). Notably, calcium-related pathways were also altered in the transcriptomic analysis. In summary, the significant decrease in the expression of zinc and copper ion-dependent metalloprotease family revealed the molecular basis of the impaired function of follicular enzyme nutrient pools and the imbalance of ECM homeostasis during alopecia, which may be an important mechanism for the development of follicular aging and degenerative alopecia.

Activation of protease activity by metal ion-mediated conformational regulation

In a follow-up study, to facilitate the study of the roles of Cu2+/Zn2+-dependent proteases in the enzyme nutrient pool of the hair follicle, we selected the Cu2+-dependent protease LOX and the Cu2+/Zn2+-dependent protease SOD1. Previous studies have shown that LOX is involved in regulating hair follicle structure and the hair growth cycle, primarily by promoting ECM cross-linking, maintaining mechanical stability, activating hair follicle stem cells, and modulating regenerative signaling pathways. In contrast, SOD1 protects stem cell function by scavenging ROS and maintaining redox homeostasis.

To evaluated the binding properties and conformational stability of LOX and SOD1 proteins with metal ions (Cu2+ and Zn2+), molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed. The original model of LOX protein (Fig. 2A) and its binding model with Cu2+ (Fig. 2B), and the original model of SOD1 protein (Fig. 2D) and its binding model with Cu2+/Zn2+ (Fig. 2E) were constructed, respectively. The Cu2+ in the LOX protein mainly formed obvious ligand interactions with His297, while the Cu2+/Zn2+ in the SOD1 protein interacted with His46, His48, His63, His71, His120 and Asp84, respectively, and several amino acid residues. Preparation and characterization of dECMMAZn/Cu microneedles

Conventional drug delivery to hair follicles has been plagued with problems such as uneven drug distribution, insufficient penetration depth and obvious skin trauma. In order to effectively promote drug delivery, a dECMMAZn/Cu microneedle system was constructed in this study to achieve precise regulation of the local microenvironment of the hair follicle. The microneedle system can effectively penetrate the skin stratum corneum and release Zn2+ and Cu2+ into the microenvironment of the hair follicle cell niche in a sustained and stable manner to precisely activate Zn2+ and Cu2+-dependent proteases, thus realizing the long-lasting modulation of the activity of hair follicle ECM and stem cells.

First, dECMMA was prepared as a 5% hydrogel. Subsequently, either Zn2+ and Cu2+ were added to the dECMMA hydrogel to generate dECMMAZn/Cu hydrogels. And then, we observed the surface morphology of the dECMMA hydrogels using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and environmental scanning electron microscopy (ESEM). The results showed that all the samples showed porous structure on the surface. Meanwhile, through energy spectrum analysis and semi-quantitative detection, we found that the elements C and N elements were present in all hydrogels. In addition, the presentation of Zn2+ and Cu2+ in dECMMA was detected as well (Fig. 3A). As the dECMMA hydrogel gradually degraded, Zn2+ and Cu2+ were slowly released, ensuring continuous bioactivity throughout the therapeutic process and maximizing the effective utilization of the ions (Fig. 3B). Additionally, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis revealed characteristic absorption peaks of F127 at 2895 cm−1 (C-H stretching), 1347 cm−1 (C-H bending), 1466 cm−1 (C-C stretching), and 1105 cm−1 (C-O stretching). FTIR analysis also identified a collodionic amide B-band absorption peak, which is associated with C-N stretching and CH2 stretching vibrations. Furthermore, the characteristic amide III band absorption peaks observed at 1270 cm−1 and 1281 cm−1 in the corresponding collagen were attributed to C-N stretching and N-H bending vibrations, respectively (Fig. 3D). The presence of these characteristic peaks indicates the composition and role in dECMMA hydrogel. In the XRD patterns, the dECMMA hydrogels showed two major diffraction peaks of collagen at 2θ = 19.16° and 23.38°, and the diffraction intensities of all the samples were close to zero in the region of the high 2θ angle, which suggests that there is no significant crystalline structure at these angles (Fig. 3E). This implies that both Zn2+ and Cu2+ are present in dECMMA. To evaluate the thermal stability of these scaffolds, we employed thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). TGA data demonstrated the weight loss of dECMMA with increasing temperature, revealing that the incorporation of inorganic ions enhances the thermal stability of dECMMA (Fig. 3F). In the XPS survey spectrum, distinct peaks for Zn and Cu were observed, indicating their effective incorporation (Fig. 3G).

After establishing the preparation process for dECMMAZn/Cu hydrogel materials and the incorporation of metal ions, we further fabricated microneedle patches using a pre-made mold (Fig. 3H). We examined the different microneedle patches using SEM and EDS, and found that the tips of the microneedles exhibited a porous structure and contained Zn and Cu elements (Fig. 3I and K). In macroscopic images, the dECMMAZn/Cu microneedle patch also exhibited a highly regular structure and demonstrated decent hardness (Fig. 3J). H&E staining revealed clear microchannels penetrating the stratum corneum into the epidermis, confirming effective insertion of the microneedles into skin tissue (Fig. S2). The dECMMA microneedles exhibited time-dependent swelling, with gradual structural deformation observed at 8-16 h and near-complete loss of tip integrity by 24 h (Fig. S3), indicating rapid in situ degradation. The force-displacement curves show a continuous increase in force without abrupt failure, indicating good mechanical strength and structural integrity of the microneedles (Fig. S4). The above results indicate that the dECMMAZn/Cu microneedle patches were successfully prepared.

Discussion

The core of hair follicle regeneration and aging lies in the imbalance of the “follicular enzymes”. This system regulates the homeostasis of the follicular stem cell microenvironment and provides essential metabolic support. Its decline leads to disrupted matrix crosslinking, redox imbalance, and weakened signal transduction. In this study, bioinformatics and molecular dynamics simulations revealed that several Zn2+/Cu2+-dependent metalloproteases (such as LOX and SOD1) were significantly downregulated in degenerative alopecia, exhibiting conformational instability and loss of catalytic activity. This enzymatic decline reduces the regenerative potential of the follicular microenvironment, indicating that metal ion homeostasis and enzyme conformational stability play a decisive role in follicular aging. Mechanistically, LOX-mediated collagen crosslinking governs ECM stiffness and structural integrity, whereas SOD1 maintains redox homeostasis to prevent ROS-induced matrix degradation. The coordinated decline of these enzymes disrupts the balance between matrix assembly and degradation, leading to impaired mechanotransduction and dysfunction of the follicular stem cell niche.

To restore this system, we developed a Zn2+/Cu2+ dual-doped decellularized extracellular matrix microneedle (dECMMAZn/Cu), which reactivates the follicular enzymatic reservoir through ion-mediated protein conformational regulation. Unlike previous single-ion supplementation or topical drugs, this platform enables the synchronous delivery of ions and biomimetic matrix components, establishing a localized dynamic ionic microenvironment that restores enzyme conformational stability and catalytic activity at the molecular level. Material characterization confirmed that Zn2+ and Cu2+ form coordination crosslinks with functional groups in dECMMA, generating a thermally stable and mechanically enhanced network that ensures sustained ion release and bioactivity. Importantly, metal ion doping in this system is governed by coordination interactions between Zn2+/Cu2+ and functional groups within the dECMMA matrix. These reversible bonds regulate ion retention and release, maintaining a dynamic equilibrium between coordinated and free states, thereby enabling sustained and localized ion availability. This coordination-controlled reservoir underpins prolonged enzymatic activation and microenvironmental homeostasis.

At the cellular level, dECMMAZn/Cu significantly promoted the proliferation and migration of DPSCs by reconstructing the local microenvironment and effectively downregulated senescence markers p16 and p21, suggesting rejuvenation of cellular senescence. Molecular dynamics simulations showed that coordination between Cu2+ and His297 in LOX stabilized the catalytic conformation, while the synergistic binding of Zn2+/Cu2+ in SOD1 maintained redox balance, providing a structural explanation for ion-mediated protein reactivation. Furthermore, dECMMAZn/Cu enhanced VEGF secretion and tube formation in endothelial cells and induced macrophage polarization from the M1 to the M2 phenotype, supporting tissue repair through both angiogenic and immunoregulatory pathways. This effect is attributed to ion-mediated regulation, where Cu2+ enhances VEGF expression via HIF-1α stabilization, while Zn2+ supports redox balance. Concurrently, Zn2+/Cu2+ shift macrophages toward the M2 phenotype, collectively establishing a pro-angiogenic and immunoregulatory niche.

RNA sequencing further revealed that dECMMAZn/Cu significantly upregulated Lox, Loxl1-4, Sod1-3, and multiple Mmp family members, while activating key ECM homeostasis genes such as Adamts20, Eln, Col26a1, and Spp1. This transcriptional remodeling indicates a systematic reconstruction of the ion-enzyme-matrix network, restoring ECM plasticity and antioxidant capacity in aged follicles. ECM remodeling in this system is regulated rather than excessive, as supported by balanced transcriptional changes, enzyme-mediated matrix maturation, and anti-senescent cellular responses. In vivo, dECMMAZn/Cu microneedles effectively penetrated the stratum corneum and markedly restored follicular structural integrity and dermal collagen density in an androgenetic alopecia mouse model, thereby promoting robust hair regrowth. Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed re-expression of LOX and SOD1 around the follicles and within the dermal ECM, indicating reactivation of the Zn2+/Cu2+-dependent enzymatic network. Notably, although dECMMA alone showed a mild improvement compared with the AGA-treated Blank group, its effect was limited. The Sham and Blank groups represented physiological and pathological baselines, respectively. In contrast, dECMMAZn/Cu achieved markedly enhanced regeneration, indicating a synergistic effect of Zn2+/Cu2+ and dECM. Future studies will evaluate microneedle performance under varying skin mechanical conditions to better approximate clinical scalp environments.

One limitation of this study is the use of both mouse- and human-derived cell models in vitro. Although the core processes investigated-ECM remodeling, macrophage polarization, and metal ion-dependent enzymatic regulation-are evolutionarily conserved, future validation using fully human-derived cells would further strengthen the translational relevance of these findings. The key contribution of this study is the proposal of a continuous regulatory axis: metal ions-protein conformation-enzymatic activity-microenvironment. Compared with conventional therapies such as minoxidil and finasteride, which primarily target vascular or hormonal pathways, the present system regulates the follicular microenvironment through enzymatic and ECM remodeling, providing a distinct mechanistic strategy for hair regeneration. Zn2+ and Cu2+ act not only as cofactors but also as conformational triggers that drive protein folding and activation. By constructing a dECMMA microneedle patches with controllable ion release, this study achieved precise reconstruction of the follicular enzymatic reservoir, overcoming the limitations of conventional therapies that target only hormonal or signaling pathways.

Future research will focus on three aspects: (1) elucidating the dynamic regulation of ion homeostasis within hair follicles and identifying key transporters and metallochaperones; (2) employing multi-omics and spatial transcriptomics to characterize cell-type-specific responses to ionic signaling; and (3) optimizing ion release kinetics and dosage windows to achieve cyclic and controllable follicular regeneration. Alternative delivery strategies, including hydrogels, nanoparticles, and topical formulations, may also enable Zn2+/Cu2+ administration; however, they are often limited by insufficient penetration, rapid diffusion, or poor spatial control. In contrast, microneedle-based delivery provides localized and minimally invasive administration with enhanced retention. Nevertheless, microneedle patches may face limitations in coverage area for large-scale applications, which could be addressed through scalable design, repeated administration, or integration with complementary delivery systems. From a translational perspective, the ion-mediated regulation of enzymatic activity and microenvironmental homeostasis may be generalizable to other ECM-related degenerative conditions. Moreover, dECM-based microneedle systems can be manufactured using established micromolding techniques compatible with scalable and GMP-compliant production, although further optimization in standardization and regulatory validation is required.

In conclusion, the dECMMAZn/Cu microneedle patches reactivate the follicular enzymatic reservoir and ECM homeostasis through ion-mediated protein conformational activation, significantly delaying stem cell aging and promoting hair regeneration. By targeting cofactor homeostasis, this work establishes a new paradigm of conformational activation for tissue regeneration, offering a translational strategy for treating follicular aging and other ECM-related degenerative disorders.

Conclusion

This study successfully reconstructed the enzymatic reservoir function of the hair follicle by developing a dual-ion-doped (Zn2+/Cu2+) decellularised extracellular matrix microneedle (dECMMAZn/Cu), providing an innovative therapeutic strategy for hair follicle regeneration and aging. Through ion-mediated activation of protein conformation, the microneedles not only restored the catalytic activity of Zn2+/Cu2+-dependent metalloproteases (such as LOX and SOD1), but also regulated the local microenvironment. This inhibited the expression of senescence markers (p16/p21) and promoted the proliferation and migration of HFSC. Furthermore, the microneedle system played a pivotal role in the regenerative regulation of HUVECs and macrophages, thereby promoting angiogenesis and immune homeostasis to further support tissue repair. In vivo experiments revealed that dECMMAZn/Cu microneedles significantly facilitated hair follicle structure recovery and hair growth, thus validating the potential of this strategy for hair follicle regeneration. By precisely controlling ion concentrations and conformational activation, this study provides a new approach for the treatment of hair follicle ageing and other matrix-related degenerative diseases.
