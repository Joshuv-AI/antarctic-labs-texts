The Potency of Artificial Intelligence in Diagnosing and Evaluating the Severity of Vitiligo: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Maureen M. Stella, Arief Budiyanto, Agnes RP Sari, Shinta T. Dewi, Yohanes W. Wirohadidjojo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neural networks (NNs), a subset of artificial intelligence (AI), have the ability to assess the severity of vitiligo and improve diagnosis accuracy. In contrast to human physicians’ diagnosis, which is primarily based on subjective assessment, standardised and objective diagnostic methods are needed to provide an accurate diagnosis and grade the severity of vitiligo. The aim of this study is to analyse the sensitivity and specificity of AI in vitiligo diagnosis and severity assessment. The PRISMA 2020 protocol was followed to screen literature in several databases: Proquest, PubMed, Taylor and Francis, SAGE, JSTOR, and ScienceDirect. Human studies that investigated the potential of AI in vitiligo diagnosis were included in vitiligo diagnosis were included. Statistical analysis was carried out using RevMan 5.4 and RStudio, and risk of bias tools were used to assess study bias. Certainty of evidence was analysed using GRADE. The systematic review consisted of 15 studies, and seven studies were statistically analysed. The most studied NNs were Inception V3 (n = 2), visual geometry group (n = 2), and ResNet (n = 2). The overall sensitivity ranged from 72.5% to 99.7%, with a pooled sensitivity of 95.5%. Specificity ranged from 72% to 99.9%, with a pooled specificity of 97%. Among the evaluated models, Inception V3 showed the highest diagnostic performance. The summary receiver operating characteristics curve (SROC) from multiple analyses showed an area under the curve of 0.96. AI showed potential in diagnosing and evaluating the severity of vitiligo.

Introduction

Vitiligo is a chronic acquired depigmenting skin disorder that can be widespread or localised. As the most prevalent acquired skin depigmenting condition, vitiligo significantly lowers quality of life. The prevalence of vitiligo ranges from 0.2% to 1.8% globally.[123] Vitiligo’s chronicity and the absence of effective therapies can seriously impair patients’ quality of life and lead to significant psychological stress. Observable lesions can cause despair, anxiety, and embarrassment, particularly if they are on areas that are easily seen, such as the hands, neck, and face.[4] Dermatologists’ diagnosis largely depends on subjective visual assessment and clinical experience in this diagnostic procedure. For precise and timely detection, particularly in individuals with an unusual presentation, examination by experienced clinicians using Wood’s lamp is essential. Point-counting techniques and the Vitiligo Area Scoring Index (VASI) are examples of semi-objective approaches. A more objective method to diagnose and score the grade of vitiligo is needed.[567]

Artificial intelligence (AI) deep learning (DL) has been utilised in clinical settings and medical image analysis in recent years. DL, in contrast to earlier machine learning techniques, makes use of intricate techniques to provide data prediction, classification, large-scale pattern recognition, and decision-making assistance. In this case, multilayer artificial neural networks (ANNs) are utilised.[48] In contrast to human physicians’ diagnosis, which is primarily based on subjective assessment and is not always repeatable, standardised, and objective, DL techniques were considered as a viable support system capable of providing accurate skin lesion diagnosis and grading the severity of the disease. One of the most often used DL models is the convolutional neural network. They have shown expert-level performance in the classification of skin conditions on medical photographs in recent years.[59]

In recent years, a few studies have been reported on how AI could enhance the diagnosis of vitiligo. A computer-based automatic scoring technique would be more objective, simpler to use, save time, and provide a more accurate diagnosis and grading compared to human analysis.[10] The primary goal of treatments is to first halt the progression and severity of vitiligo and then repigment the afflicted skin area to restore it. By analysing the skin and determining variables such as colour changes and the size of the affected area, the efficacy of the treatment is assessed.[11]

A thorough evaluation and meta-analysis evaluating the potential of AI in diagnosing and grading the severity of vitiligo has not yet been published. This article aimed to analyse the efficacy of AI neural networks (NNs) in vitiligo diagnosis and severity grading. Early intervention can be initiated promptly to reduce disease progression and morbidity in patients with vitiligo.

Methods

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines were followed in this study, as displayed in Figure 1. The registry number CRD420251048662 is associated with this systematic review’s PROSPERO registration.

PRISMA flowchart 2020

Literature search

To determine the potential of AI in identifying and assessing the severity of vitiligo, we performed a thorough literature search across multiple databases: PubMed, Proquest, Taylor and Francis, SAGE, ScienceDirect, and JSTOR. The articles were retrieved with no time limitation (until 20 February 2025). The search terms were as follows: (“machine learning” OR “artificial intelligence” OR “neural network”) AND (“vitiligo”). Furthermore, we searched for other potential studies to provide as references.

Study selection

Literature search was conducted using inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) full-text article available in English, (2) investigated the potency of AI in diagnosing and detecting the severity of vitiligo, and (3) human studies. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) review articles, (2) letters to the editor and commentary, (3) repeated publications, and (4) lack of data.

Data extraction and synthesis

Four authors independently extracted additional information on the included studies. The following were the extracted data: (1) author and publication year, (2) country of origin, (3) subjects (number of vitiligo, number of non-vitiligo), (4) data sources, (5) methods (devices and NN family), and (6) outcomes (sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, area under the curve (AUC), precision, and F1-scores).

Risk of bias assessment

Using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) risk of bias (RoB) tools, four independent reviewers evaluated the bias risk. The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) was used to assess the degree of certainty in the evidence. The fifth author discussed and settled any issues.

Statistical analysis outcome measurement

The outcomes included an analysis and evaluation of the F1-score, precision, area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy. Every study was carried out in accordance with the ethical guidelines mentioned. RStudio and RevMan 5.4 were used for statistical analysis. Statistical analysis was performed using RevMan 5.4 and RStudio. A diagnostic meta-analysis was conducted by pooling sensitivity and specificity across eligible studies. A random-effects model was applied to account for between-study variability. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the I2 statistic, with values >50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses were performed based on different neural network (NN) families (e.g., Inception V3, VGG, ResNet, and others). Summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curves were generated to evaluate the overall diagnostic performance.

Results

Database literature searches yielded a total of 943 articles. Following screening and the elimination of duplicates (n = 185), 15 studies were included in the systematic review, and seven studies were analysed in the meta-analysis. The majority of studies were determined to have a low risk of bias [Figures 2 and 3].

From the systematic review of 15 studies, it was found that the sensitivity and specificity ranged from 72.41% to 100% and 73.2% to 100%, respectively. Accuracy ranged from 59% to 99.9%. Precision and F1 score ranged from 99.5% to 99.9% and 90.46% to 100%, respectively. NNs such as Inception V3 and ResNet showed high efficacy in sensitivity and specificity. The majority of the studies focused on diagnostic performance rather than explicitly grading the severity of vitiligo.

Seven studies (Liu et al. (2020),[14] Sharma et al. (2023),[17] Zhang et al. (2021),[5] Guo et al. (2022),[4] Jalaboi et al. (2023),[20] Marri et al. (2024),[18] and Zhong et al. (2024)[12]) out of 15 studies were eligible for statistical analysis in the diagnostic meta-analysis [Figure 4]. These included studies reported sensitivity, specificity, number of total subjects, and vitiligo cases explicitly as the key information to be input and pooled in statistical analysis. The 15 studies were previously included in the data synthesis to describe their findings qualitatively. In Figure 4, subgroup analysis was performed and visualised in forest plots, categorised based on the type of NNs (Inception V3, VGG, ResNet, and others). Some studies (Liu et al. (2020),[14] Sharma et al. (2023),[17] Zhang et al. (2021),[5] and Guo et al. (2022)[4]) reported more than one NN and thus appear in multiple subgroups. The use of letters a, b, and c following the publication year (e.g., Sharma et al. 2023a, 2023b)[17] was necessary due to limitations in meta-analysis programmes, which do not allow identical author-year citations during statistical analysis, as they conduct several assessments in the same study.

Overall, the NNs showed high sensitivity and specificity. In the multiple analysis, each of the NN families was analysed. For Inception V3 (n = 4),[1417] sensitivity and specificity ranged from 88% to 100% and 75% to 100%, respectively. In VGG (n = 2),[514] the sensitivity ranged from 90% to 97%, and its specificity ranged from 73% to 96%. For ResNet (n = 2),[514] the sensitivity and specificity ranged from 90% to 95% and 78% to 96%, respectively. Various other NN families (Swin Transformer networks, Aysa AI App, Xception, ANN, YOLO v3 architecture, and UNet++ architecture) showed both sensitivity and specificity from 72% to 100%.[4512141820]

The plot’s upper left section contained the majority of the machine learning models, suggesting relatively high sensitivity (>0.72) and high specificity (>0.72). Based on the affected region, these models aided in assessing the severity of the disease and were quite successful in differentiating between vitiligo and other conditions. Plotting the data into the summary receiver operating characteristics (SROC) curve revealed that, as Figure 5 illustrates, all seven experiments were over the dotted line.

Summary receiver operating characteristic curve of the studies

This forest plot in Figures 6 and 7 summarises the sensitivity of diagnostic tools in assessing vitiligo severity across 15 studies. A great diagnostic performance was shown in high pooled sensitivity 0.955 (95% CI: 0.916–0.976) with the random-effects model. The majority of the studies reported a sensitivity of more than 90%. Large heterogeneity between studies was found (I² =95.7%, P < 0.001). The prediction interval was between 0.572 and 0.997, indicating that despite the good diagnostic performance, the results may be varied across settings.

The forest plot in Figure 6 shows the specificity of AI in diagnosing vitiligo. Using a random-effects model, the pooled specificity was 97% (95% CI: 0.905–0.991), suggesting the ability to distinguish non-severe cases. The majority of the studies showed high specificity, nearly 100%. However, due to variability across study designs or tools, a significant heterogeneity was found (I² =98.1%, P < 0.0001). In the future diagnostic performance settings, the results may be different, as shown in a wide prediction interval, ranging from 0.149 to 1.000.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis highlighted the direct comparison of several NNs in the diagnosis of vitiligo. We performed a statistical analysis to draw a conclusion in order to improve the quality of the systematic review. The included research demonstrated how AI might be used to define the severity of vitiligo and draw the lesion margin.

Most studies reporting on AI have focused on pigmented skin neoplasms, despite the fact that DL has been increasingly popular in dermatology in recent years. There is little information on depigmented skin disorders such as vitiligo.[4] We analysed the outcomes based on several parameters (specificity, sensitivity, accuracy, AUC, precision, and F1-Score) to have a global vision of their performance. The overall outcome of using NNs in machine learning was great in diagnosing and evaluating the severity of vitiligo in patients. Because the visual perception of various skin tones is subjective and can vary greatly from person to person, pigmentation evaluation is difficult. There is currently no widely used measurement to evaluate the pigmentation of the skin in vitiligo studies. Lesions can be classified based on the proportion of pigment loss. Another validated scoring method that offers more accurate pigmentation assessments is VASI.[232425]

To achieve some degree of repigmentation, vitiligo typically takes a lengthy course of treatment; thus, early detection and diagnosis confirmation are needed. There are clear benefits to managing vitiligo patients when a prediction rule for clinical response is available. To the best of our knowledge, no formal attempt has been made to construct a predictive rule, and there are very few studies that evaluated characteristics linked to clinical response in vitiligo.[16]

A study by Lu et al. (2024)[22] stated that CRFF-OCT with AI could evaluate vitiligo stability and differentiate from other hypopigmented skin lesions, as shown by the absence of papillary rings and the presence of large dendritic melanocytes around hair follicles. Features of repigmentation were recorded and examined. Melanin loss was indicated by a noticeable dark band in the skin of vitiligo lesions, as shown by the CRFF-OCT study.[22]

Early therapeutic intervention is recommended as treatment is more effective for newly established lesions than for older ones.[26] As no quantitative diagnostic tools are already available, more objective methods are essential in differentiating vitiligo from other hypopigmented disorders so an accurate treatment plan can be carried out.[27]

The included studies have limitations; mainly, all the designs were non-randomised controlled trials. Furthermore, there is a heterogeneity in devices used to take the picture of the lesion and the NNs that were used to process the images. To acquire better evidence-based quality, research with a randomised controlled trial (RCT) design is required to validate the use of AI in diagnosing and evaluating the severity of vitiligo.

Conclusion

The AI for diagnosing and evaluating the severity of vitiligo showed high sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy, with the majority of the included studies showing a low risk of bias. However, none of the studies were RCTs. Therefore, to get a higher evidence-based quality, studies with an RCT design on this topic need to be conducted.

Key messages

Artificial intelligence with its subset, neural network, such as Inception V3, visual geometry group, and ResNet, showed potential in diagnosing and evaluating the severity of vitiligo. However, none of the studies were RCTs. Therefore, to get a higher evidence-based quality, studies with an RCT design on this topic need to be conducted.
