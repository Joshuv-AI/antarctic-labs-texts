Assessing the use of the input of care users in long-term care homes during 2020–2023 in the Netherlands: an exploratory study
Authors: Flora Palimetaki, Roel Schouteten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealthcare inspectorates are increasingly expected to involve care users during assessments of long-term care (LTC). Although healthcare inspectorates promise to improve the involvement of care users’ experiences, they struggle to address this expectation. This study aims to explore the advancements in the participation of care users or informal caregivers during assessments of LTC homes, as reported in the Dutch Health and Youth Care Inspectorate reports in the Netherlands between 2020 and 2023.ObjectiveThis study aims to explore the advancements in the participation of care users or informal caregivers during assessments of LTC homes in the Netherlands between 2020 and 2023.MethodsThis study comprised 273 publicly accessible inspection reports concerning LTC homes from 2020 to 2023. The frequency with which care users or informal caregivers were cited as sources of information was examined and compared across years.ResultsThe results show a significant increase in reporting the use of positive citations from care users for 3 items out of 16 of the assessment instrument between 2020 and 2023. In particular, significant differences in positive citations from care users were found only for items regarding the theme of attention to person-centred care.ConclusionsThe study concludes that although care users are particularly increasingly cited in inspection reports on LTC homes, there is still room for increasing the incorporation of care users’ experiences in the reports on assessing quality of care in LTC homes.Keywords: Nursing homes, Patient-centred care, Long-Term Care, Health policy, Patient Participation

Introduction

The involvement of care users in long-term care (LTC) homes is a crucial aspect of person-centred care that respects human dignity and rights.1 2 LTC home care users must participate in their care, and their wishes should be heard.2 The WHO recommends using the input of care users as a source of information during regulation, alongside information from healthcare professionals and risk management plans.3 The input of care users and informal caregivers can be used by regulatory organisations to indicate the quality and safety of care received.4 5

National healthcare inspectorates worldwide function as public stakeholders responsible for monitoring and promoting the quality and safety of care in LTC homes.3 6 7 In several countries, inspectorates increasingly seek to involve care users and informal caregivers in regulatory processes.6 8–10 However, research indicates that inspectors experience challenges in incorporating care users’ experiences and reported signals into LTC home assessments, particularly in interpreting and weighting this information.6 10–12 Moreover, effectively integrating such experiences or signals requires specific expertise and competencies, which may further complicate the involvement of clients in assessment processes.13 14 Nonetheless, international research indicates that assessments of LTC homes provide valuable insights into care quality, which can inform the development of strategies aimed at improving outcomes for care users.15

The inspection process for LTC homes typically includes a risk assessment based on the analysis of multiple data sources, such as reported signals and incidents, media reports and ratings.6 11 16–18 During (un)announced inspections, two inspectors engage in informal discussions with staff, managers, care users and informal caregivers, review care user records and collect relevant documentation from the LTC home.19–21

This paper focuses on the work of the Dutch Health and Youth Care Inspectorate (hereafter referred to as ‘the Inspectorate’) in the period 2020–2023 focusing on the use of care user input in LTC homes. The Inspectorate is mandated to supervise healthcare and youth care services in the public interest and operates within the framework of national and international legislation.22–24 The law authorises inspectors to enter LTC homes and request information, and it requires employees and care users to cooperate reasonably.25

As part of the Dutch Inspection Council,26 27 the Inspectorate reports to the Ministry of Public Health, Welfare and Sport.22 28 The Dutch Inspection Council supports the Inspectorate according to principles of good supervision, which include independence, transparency, selectiveness, decisiveness and cooperation.26 27

In 2018, responding to societal demands for transparency regarding regulatory tasks and results of the Inspectorate,29 30 the Dutch Inspection Council committed to the Dutch government to incorporate care users’ perspectives into regulatory practices and to be transparent about the results. Accordingly, the Inspectorate publishes assessment reports and information on achieved outcomes on its website.27 Transparency is expected to strengthen public trust and encourage care users to provide input.29 30 Consequently, the Inspectorate aims to strengthen the involvement of care users and informal caregivers in its regulatory processes. In addition, in 2018, the Inspectorate introduced a new framework for assessing LTC homes and updated it in 2024.31 32 Inspectors use this framework to evaluate whether LTC homes meet the standards for quality of nursing home care, including attention to person-centred care as people’s basic rights.2 31 32

The Inspectorate has conducted several individual pilot studies aimed at involving care users in its assessment processes. For example, a pilot study starting in 2011 using ‘mystery shoppers’ identified discrepancies between the information collected by mystery shoppers and that gathered by inspectors.33 In addition, a 2017 study found that inspectors valued their own assessment as more legitimate than those of the experts by experience.34 Experts by experience are care users, informal caregivers or others whose perspectives bridge the experiential knowledge of care users and the professional domain of LTC home inspectors.34

These findings are consistent with research conducted by the Care Quality Commission, the regulator of health and social care in the UK.9 Together, these studies suggest that inspectors experience difficulties or at least challenges in using care users and informal caregivers as reliable sources during LTC home assessments.9 33 34 In addition, they indicate that care users’ experiential knowledge is often attributed less value than inspectors’ professional expertise.

Other studies show that care users, including those with dementia, can effectively share their experience and knowledge if the conditions are favourable. This requires adapting the length, time and place of the interview, as well as making the care user feel at ease and able to concentrate during the interview.35 36 Besides the need for resources and time, inspectors need to change attitudes that marginalise care users’ experiences.28 Inspectors should try to develop a ‘partnership’ with care users and informal caregivers during assessments, instead of marginalising the experience of care users.28 This includes empowering care users in their interactions with inspectors.29 As a result, inspectors require training and experience to effectively interpret and integrate information provided by care users and informal caregivers during LTC home assessments.6 34

Following the Dutch Inspection Council’s 2018 commitment to incorporate care users’ perspectives, the Inspectorate introduced a new multiyear policy plan for 2020–2023.37 As a value-driven organisation, the Inspectorate aims to involve care users and informal caregivers in regulatory processes.38 A key priority of this plan is involving care users during regulation, including assessments of LTC homes.37 To promote transparency, the Inspectorate publishes annual plans and corresponding internal evaluations on its website.39–46 However, the internal evaluations for the period 2020–2023 do not provide specific information on the extent to which care users in LTC homes were involved in these assessments.39–42

The involvement of care users and informal caregivers has remained a recurring theme in the Inspectorate’s successive multiyear policy plans until 2027.37 47 Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether the Inspectorate’s commitment to increasing care user involvement has been reflected in inspection reports of LTC homes during the period 2020–2023. Given that care users’ and informal caregivers’ experiences are increasingly prioritised by regulators in many countries, including the Dutch Inspectorate,10 37 47 this study aims to explore whether and to what extent the Inspectorate increasingly incorporated information provided by these groups during 2020–2023. As a structured analysis of this development is currently lacking, this study provides a first exploration into the extent to which inspectors disclose information derived from care users and informal caregivers in publicly available inspection reports. We hypothesise that, over the period 2020–2023, inspectors have increasingly incorporated care users and informal caregivers as sources of information in these reports.

Methods

To explore the extent to which inspectors involve care users and informal caregivers in their assessments of LTC homes, we conducted a content analysis of all publicly available inspection reports (2020–2023) on the Inspectorate’s website.48 When reports stated a reason for an assessment, the most common reason was the identification of potential risks.

The reports are the result of assessments by the Inspectorate of LTC homes. For such assessments, inspectors use the Inspectorate’s framework for supervising LTC homes.31 This framework comprises 16 items organised into three overarching themes: ‘attention to person-centred care’, ‘working towards sufficient and competent care staff’ and ‘focusing on quality and safety’ (see table 1).

Each item is rated independently by two inspectors using a four-point scale: ‘does not meet the standard’, ‘largely does not meet the standard’, ‘largely meets the standard’ or ‘meets the standard’. These ratings are based on multiple sources of information, applying a triangulation approach.20 49 50 Inspectors draw on a variety of available sources during an assessment: care staff, care users, informal caregivers (eg, family members), (in)formal client councils, physicians, managers, board members, direct observations of care users, client files or institutional documents and protocols (see table 2). Inspectors substantiate their ratings by describing these sources in either positive or negative terms. For example, for items rated as ‘meets the standard’, inspectors report only positive observations, whereas items rated as ‘largely meets the standard’ include predominantly positive observations alongside some negative findings.49

The inspection reports on the Inspectorate’s website comprise two types of assessments; using a regular or a shorter version of the Inspectorate’s inspection framework.51 The regular instrument includes all the 16 items from the assessment framework (see table 1). Item 1.5 became mandatory in 2022, and items 3.4 and 3.5 were added in 2023.31 32 The second, shorter instrument contains 12 items from the regular instrument and is based on meetings with the board of directors and board of supervisors, if available. This short instrument was primarily used from 2017 to 2021 in order to focus on the long-term care sector.52 There is significant overlap between the two instruments: 75% for ‘attention to person-centred care’, 83% for ‘working towards sufficient and competent care staff’ and 80% for ‘focusing on quality and safety’. The overlap of items between the two instruments makes reports based on both types suitable for inclusion in this study (see table 1).

By analysing both the content and frequency of information provided by care users and informal caregivers during assessments, these reports offer insight into the extent to which these groups are involved as sources of information. As an exploratory approach, this method yields relevant initial insights. However, it is important to acknowledge that the reporting of sources may not fully capture actual involvement, as not all consulted sources are necessarily documented in the reports. Consequently, the findings may underestimate the true extent of care user and informal caregiver involvement. This limitation should therefore be taken into account when interpreting the results.

LTC homes in the Netherlands

In the Netherlands, LTC homes vary in form, ranging from traditional large-scale nursing homes to small-scale living arrangements.11 53 In addition to differences in scale, regular and private LTC homes differ in their financing structures. In regular care settings, the Dutch government covers the costs of care services, accommodation and related provisions. In contrast, in most private care settings, care users rent their accommodation and pay for additional services, such as cleaning and meals, while the government covers the costs of care.54 55 Care users with complex care requirements are mostly living in regular LTC homes, due to the availability of specialised expertise among care staff and the broader range of services provided. In general, most private care homes do not offer complex care.56

For all types of LTC homes, whether regular or private, eligibility for admission is determined by the Care Needs Assessment Centre (CIZ), in accordance with the Long-Term Care Act (WLZ). One of the key eligibility criteria is the need for permanent care or supervision.57

Data

This study examines the period between January 2020 and December 2023. The first criterion is assessment reports of LTC homes. The Inspectorate assessed 296 LTC homes. Of these assessments, 23 reports (7.7%) were excluded as a second criterion because scoring was incomplete across all items. For instance, in case of follow-up visits, inspectors assess only those items that did not meet the standard during the initial assessment. As a result, these reports are not comparable to full first-time assessments and were therefore excluded. The final dataset comprises 273 first-time assessment reports, including 47 assessments in 2020, 87 assessments in 2021, 67 assessments in 2022 and 72 assessments in 2023.

The included LTC homes ranged in size from 3 to 244 residents (M=58; SD=48) and were part of care organisations operating between 1 and 255 LTC facilities (M=11; SD=29). The sample consisted of 108 private and 165 regular LTC homes. According to ZorgkaartNederland,58 there are 2345 LTC locations in the Netherlands; thus, the sample represents 11.6% of all LTC homes nationwide.

During the strict COVID-19 containment measures (March 2020 to July 2020), the Inspectorate did not assess any LTC homes. After July 2020, the Inspectorate resumed assessing LTC homes in compliance with government measures such as wearing face masks and keeping a distance of at least 1.5 m. All COVID-19-related restrictions were lifted in April 2022.59

Content analysis

The content of the assessment reports was coded per item in three rounds, using ATLAS.ti 25.1.1 for Windows.60 In the first round, codes were assigned based on the source of information mentioned in the reports. Two independent raters were trained, and they coded a sample of eight assessments (two per year), achieving an inter-rater reliability of 95%.

During the second round, raters identified whether inspectors reported positive, negative or mixed statements (eg, “The care user appreciates …” or “The informal caregiver expresses dissatisfaction …”), reflecting how inspectors substantiated item ratings.49 Based on the assessment of two randomly chosen reports per year, eight coded assessments by two raters showed a high inter-rater reliability of 100%.

In the final round, all identified sources were coded as positive or negative, while unidentifiable sources were coded as ‘source unknown’. In total, 14 distinct sources were identified (see table 2). Inter-rater reliability, based on a random sample of eight assessments (two per year), was 95%. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, after which the remaining assessments were rated under supervision.

After the three rounds of coding all sources, a total of 7541 citations compiled our dataset. These citations entailed the number of times the 14 different sources, positive or negative, were mentioned in all 273 reports from 2020 to 2023.

Statistical analyses

We analysed the distribution of four independent variables—‘care user positive’, ‘care user negative’, ‘informal caregiver positive’ and ‘informal caregiver negative’—between 2020 and 2023. Mean citation frequencies were calculated by dividing the number of citations per source by the number of assessment reports per year. After assessing normality, we applied Welch’s ANOVA and Games–Howell post hoc tests when distributions were non-normal. Spearman rank correlations were subsequently used to examine whether observed trends were independent of the other 13 sources. All analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics.61

Trend analyses of mentions of ‘care user positive or negative’ and ‘informal caregiver positive’, and ‘informal caregiver negative’ show that ‘care users’ and ‘informal caregivers’ are cited mainly in theme 1 (‘Attention to person-centred care’) and rarely in themes 2 and 3. The findings in table 3 indicate that there are only a few significant correlations (18 significant correlations on a potential number of 81) and the significant correlations are rather weak (<0.3). Nevertheless, we can carefully interpret that the positive trend in increased use of the source ‘care user positive’ coincides with an increase in the use of ‘care user negative’ in all items. This fits the Inspectorate’s intention to increasingly use input from care users. On the other hand, the increased use of input from care users appears to have come at the expense of the use of observations, for all three items.

Discussion

In 2019, the Dutch Inspectorate introduced a new multiyear policy plan for the period 2020–2023 aiming to enhance the involvement of care users and informal caregivers in LTC home assessments.37 This study explored the developments regarding the use of positive and negative accounts by care users and informal caregivers in inspection reports about LTC homes.

For the theme ‘attention to person-centred care’ (items 1.1–1.5), we observed a significant increase in the incorporation of the source ‘care users positive’ to assess the quality of LTC homes, especially regarding the items 1.2 (Every care user has a say in the goals of care, treatment, and support), 1.3 (Care users manage their own lives within their means) and 1.4 (Care users experience proximity, security, trust and understanding are treated with respect). This trend coincides with an increased use of the source ‘care user negative’. These findings partly support the expectation that, following the Inspectorate Council’s 2018 commitment to the Dutch government, inspectors would increasingly incorporate the perspectives of care users and informal caregivers in publicly available assessment reports. However, no significant increase was observed in the use of negative input from care users or in the use of input from informal caregivers.

The Inspectorate’s ambition to strengthen the role of care users aligns with developments in other countries.6 8–10 33 However, in the Dutch context, the increased use of care users’ or informal caregivers’ perspectives during assessments of LTC homes was only significant for the source category ‘care user positive’. The incorporation of informal caregivers’ perspectives remained limited overall. Moreover, for the themes ‘working towards sufficient and competent care staff’ and ‘focusing on quality and safety’, the observed use in the inclusion of care users’ and informal caregivers’ perspectives in assessment reports between 2020 and 2023 was limited. These findings indicate substantial room for improvement. In addition, promises that cannot be integrated into actual policy are likely to prompt questions regarding accountability and reliability in both media and public opinion, and may be leveraged against policymaking authorities during legal proceedings.64

Maintaining the public accessibility of all assessment reports on the Inspectorate’s website constitutes an important mechanism for accountability regarding commitments made to the Dutch government and society. In line with its stated objective of incorporating care users’ perspectives, the Inspectorate could strengthen its annual evaluations by more explicitly assessing and reporting what has been achieved in this regard. Transparency may further be enhanced through the systematic collection and reporting of data on the use of care users as a source during LTC home assessments. In addition, the structural involvement of care users in assessment processes may improve the effectiveness of inspections, as signals and incidents reported by care users or informal caregivers frequently serve as triggers for inspection activities. Maintaining an appropriate balance between transparency and effectiveness remains crucial for sustaining public trust in, and the legitimacy of, external inspection processes.

Strengths and limitations of the study

With our study, we explore the use of care users and informal caregivers as sources of information during assessments of LTC homes in the Netherlands between 2020 and 2023. This study provides a complete overview of how the use of care users and informal caregivers has been reported in a sample of 92.2% of all assessment reports of LTC homes in the Netherlands during 2020–2023.

Despite the robustness of this dataset, our study has several limitations. First, the use of sources during the assessment of an LTC home and the sources mentioned in the report cannot be validated by analysing reports alone. Only in the type of assessments with all 16 items, a list of used sources is added to the report. It is not clear whether this list is complete or how all sources are actually used. The other type of assessment, with 12 items, does not even require a list of used sources. Further research into the actual practices during assessments and the opportunities for involving care users and informal caregivers is required to provide more detailed insights beyond this first exploration.

Further, the quantitative nature of our study did not allow for deeper insights into the reasons for using some sources more than others. In addition, numbers of other used sources are also low, limiting the opportunities for robust statistical analyses of all used sources. Moreover, inspectors are trained to use triangulation during assessments.20 49 However, in the reports, there are no mentions of whether and how inspectors apply triangulation. Qualitative field research may offer insights into how and why care users and informal caregivers are involved during assessments of LTC homes and whether their increased involvement is at the expense of other sources of information, such as observations.

As the results only indicate the reported use of care users and informal caregivers as sources, the reasons why these sources have been used are not evident from the reports. This use may depend on the way the new guidelines for assessments were implemented and the extent to which inspectors were encouraged and trained to use the information of care users and informal caregivers as a valid source of information. Follow-up qualitative research can investigate the underlying motivations of inspectors for actually using the care users and informal caregivers as sources for the assessments of LTC homes and reporting about that in the assessment reports.

Finally, the signals and incidents should be a central focus in both policy and practice.14 15 Care users and informal caregivers provide inspectorates with relevant information; however, inspectors often experience difficulties in interpreting and weighing these signals. Future research could examine whether the use of care users as a source of information differs when signals or incidents constitute the primary reason for initiating an assessment.

Conclusions

This study found a partial increase in reporting the involvement of care users or informal caregivers as information sources during assessments of Dutch LTC homes from 2020 to 2023. No increase was noted for informal caregivers alone. Further research is recommended to understand why inspectors do (not) report on the use of certain sources of information and how inspectors can better incorporate care users’ experiences, especially regarding person-centred care. Key recommendations include enhancing the evaluation of plans and improving transparency about the achieved results to contribute to society’s confidence.
