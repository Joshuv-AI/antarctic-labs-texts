Immune checkpoint inhibitor-associated hypothyroidism, macular rash and adrenal insufficiency: case report and review of the literature
Authors: Zhong-qing Mou, Feng-shou Yi, Xing-ping Wu, Peng Wang, Sheng-li Wu, Li-xin Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) represent the most significant therapeutic advancement in oncology over the past decade, with sintilimab demonstrating efficacy as a programmed cell death-1 (PD-1) inhibitor. While these agents have markedly improved tumor response rates and overall survival across multiple malignancies, their use is complicated by a growing incidence of immune-related adverse events (irAEs), particularly endocrine toxicities. To our knowledge, this is the first case report of adrenal crisis during levothyroxine supplementation for ICI-induced hypothyroidism and undiagnosed adrenal insufficiency.Case presentationA 71-year-old male patient was admitted to our hospital with complaints of anorexia, fatigue, and weight loss. He had a confirmed diagnosis of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma 18 months prior and received systemic therapy, including sintilimab and esophagectomy (with normal thyroid and renal function at baseline). Sintilimab was administered for 10 treatment cycles. 4 months after completion of chemotherapy, the patient developed macular rash on the trunk and extremities and was treated with high-dose topical corticosteroids, and 1 month later, he began to suffer from anorexia and weight loss of 4.0 kg until this admission. At the same time, hypothyroidism and hyponatremia were detected, and levothyroxine (100 µg/day) was commenced. However, he worsened, with two episodes of fever, hypotension, and somnolence, as well as hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, anemia, and elevated serum creatinine. Cortisol and adrenocorticotrophic hormone (ACTH) levels decreased in the morning, and concurrent pericardial effusion and diffuse T-wave inversions were detected via electrocardiogram. He was diagnosed with secondary adrenal insufficiency and took prednisone (5 mg twice a day), and the symptoms improved rapidly. 1 week later, the serum electrolytes were normal, his weight increased by 3.5 kg, his physical activity improved, and the T wave on the electrocardiogram normalized. After taking prednisone for 18 days, his condition improved further. The morning cortisol level still decreased, while ACTH levels normalized, and pericardial effusion disappeared.ConclusionThis is a typical case of endocrine irAEs caused by ICIs, and the patient’s macular rash may also be related to ICIs. Background

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are the most important development and have become a cornerstone of cancer therapy over the past decade. ICIs mainly include anti-cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4), anti-programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) and anti-programmed cell death ligand 1 (PDL-1) antibodies. They have improved response rates and survival in many malignant diseases and simultaneously lead to an increasing number of irAEs, including those in the endocrine system, skin, lungs, and liver [1]. Among the most common of these irAEs are ICI-induced endocrine disorders [2]. The thyroid and pituitary are the most commonly affected endocrine glands [3], and the adrenal gland (primary adrenal insufficiency) or pancreatic islets are rarely affected.

Endocrine irAEs can arise at any time during therapy or even months after medication discontinuation, with one or more endocrine glands affected at the same time and diverse, atypical clinical manifestations. These conditions can delay the diagnosis and treatment of endocrine disorders.

We present a case of an elderly patient who developed hypothyroidism, macular rash, and adrenal insufficiency several months after receiving sintilimab due to esophageal carcinoma, during which an adrenal crisis occurred. To our knowledge, this is the first case report of adrenal crisis during levothyroxine supplementation for ICI-induced hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old man was admitted to our hospital due to anorexia, fatigue, and weight loss since4 months ago.

Regarding the onset of the above complaints, the patient stated that 5 months prior, he was admitted to the dermatology department due to rash (treated with antihistamines and topical cream), and 1 month after discharge from dermatology department, digestive symptoms occurred. He went to the gastroenterology department and underwent gastroscopy, and the results revealed postoperative esophageal changes (his medical history is more detailed later). Laboratory tests revealed a serum sodium level of 127.7 (137.0–147.0) mmol/L, blood glucose (BG) 3.27 (3.89–6.11) mmol/L, serum creatinine 234.9 (57.0–111.0) µmol/L, and B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) 126.4 (0–125.0) pg/ml. The serum free thyroxine (FT4) level was 1.44 (12–22) pmol/L, free triiodothyronine (FT3) was 0.78 (3.1–6.8) pmol/L, thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) was > 100.0 (0.27–4.2) mIU/L, and the antithyroid peroxidase (TPOAb) and antithyroglobulin (TGAb) levels were within normal ranges, suggesting primary hypothyroidism. Levothyroxine (100 µg/d) was given. However, his condition did not improve and was further aggravated. 1 month prior to this admission, the patient complained of dizziness, and his blood pressure (BP) decreased to 80/55 mmHg (he had a history of hypertension for more than 10 years, and nifedipine sustained release tablets 30 mg were administered daily, with his BP remaining stable at approximately 120/80 mmHg). The antihypertensive drug was discontinued, and his BP recovered to 100/60 mmHg. However, a progressive exacerbation of fatigue and appetite loss was observed, accompanied by mild nausea in the absence of abdominal pain or emesis. The clinical course was further complicated by the development of fever (39.9 °C) and somnolence. He was admitted to another hospital, the serum sodium level was 124.3 mmol/L, serum chloride was 92.3 (99.0–110.0) mmol/L, BG was 3.10 mmol/L, serum creatinine was 185.2 µmol/L, white blood cell (WBC) count was 2.7 (3.5–9.5)×109/L, hemoglobin (Hb) was 85.0 g/L, neutrophil ratio was 34.9% (40–75%), eosinophil ratio was 10.8% (0.4–8.0%), and hypothyroidism persisted., however, the FT4 level was 7.13 pmol/L, FT3 was 1.65 pmol/L, both higher than prior values, and TSH was still > 100.0 mIU/L. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed cerebral arteriosclerosis. The patient was considered to have a viral infection of the upper respiratory tract, fluid replacement and nutritional support were given, and he was discharged after clinical remission. 3 days prior to this admission, the patient developed recurrent fever (39.0 °C) with somnolence, fatigue, and unimproved anorexia. After taking ibuprofen, his temperature normalized, but he remained somnolent.

Physical examination at admission: temperature, 36.8 °C; pulse, 62 beats per minute; BP, 105/54 mmHg; body mass index (BMI), 16.7 kg/m2 (weight loss, 4.0 kg in the past 4 months). The patient presented with a thin body habitus, somnolence, slowed response time, decreased skin turgor, muffled heart sounds on auscultation, and a scaphoid abdomen without tenderness or rebound tenderness.

Past medical history was as follows. 18 months prior, the patient underwent endoscopy for investigation of progressive dysphagia, which revealed esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. Initial staging cannot definitively exclude hepatic metastases, and therefore the patient received chemotherapy with paclitaxel, nedaplatin, and sintilimab for 6 cycles. Following the fourth cycle, the patient underwent esophagectomy. Postoperatively, he completed an additional 4 cycles of sintilimab monotherapy, resulting in a total of 10 cycles of sintilimab administration. All anticancer therapy was completed 9 months prior to the current evaluation, with subsequent gradual improvement in both appetite and physical activity. A cumulative weight loss of 10 kg (from 61.0 kg to 51.0 kg) was documented during the treatment period. Baseline pretreatment laboratory tests revealed normal thyroid function and serum creatinine levels. 5 months before this admission, the patient developed an extensive macular rash on the trunk and extremities and was diagnosed with pityriasis rosea. Treatment included oral antihistamines (olopatadine) and once-daily topical corticosteroid ointments applied extensively to the trunk and extremities for 6 weeks, consisting of 0.05% halometasone cream (5 g × 5 tubes) and 0.1% triamcinolone acetonide and econazole cream (15 g × 2 tubes).

During this admission, the patient was found to have leukopenia, eosinophilia, normocytic anemia, hyponatremia, elevated serum creatinine and creatine kinase (CK) but normal CK isoenzyme MB (CKMB) and significantly elevated BNP (Table 1). An electrocardiogram (ECG) revealed a right bundle branch block and widespread T-wave inversions in the precordial and limb leads (Fig. 1). Echocardiography revealed an ejection fraction of 58%, with evidence of a small, circumferential pericardial effusion (5 mm in maximal depth).

Thyroid function tests demonstrated partial improvement of hypothyroidism under levothyroxine replacement (100 µg/day), characterized by normalization of the FT4 level, a persistently reduced FT3 concentration, and a paradoxical increase in the TSH level. TPOAb and TGAb levels remained within normal ranges.

Endocrine evaluation revealed an abnormal cortisol circadian rhythm characterized by low morning serum cortisol and subnormal ACTH levels (Table 1). Renin aldosterone axis evaluation was normal, with renin 0.10 (0.10–6.56) ng/ml/h and aldosterone 148.4 (77.7–1043.0) pmol/L. Other findings revealed normal levels of sex hormones, including testosterone, estradiol, and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). However, elevated levels of prolactin (PRL) and luteinizing hormone (LH) were observed, with PRL 30.98 (4.04–15.2) µg/L and LH 14.51 (1.7–8.6) IU/L. Owing to clinical constraints, testing for growth hormone (GH) and ACTH stimulation could not be performed.

Adrenal computed tomography (CT) revealed no abnormalities. Pituitary MRI revealed that the pituitary stalk was midline, and the gland was normal in size. A possible microadenoma (0.1–0.2 cm) was noted. The patient was diagnosed with secondary adrenal insufficiency and initiated oral glucocorticoid replacement therapy (prednisone 5 mg twice daily, hydrocortisone not available).

The patient demonstrated rapid clinical improvement following the initiation of oral prednisone therapy, with notable enhancements in mental status and appetite within 24 h of treatment, a weight gain of 0.5 kg by day 4, and a cumulative gain of 3.5 kg (current weight of 47.0 kg) by day 7. The functional capacity progressed from bedbound status to independent ambulation (200 m) with single-cutch assistance within 1 week.

One week later, the patient presented the following findings: BP 126/65 mmHg, laboratory improvements: normalized electrolytes, reduced creatinine and BNP, persistent anemia despite other hematologic normalization (Table 1), and resolved ECG abnormalities (Fig. 2). He was provided with all necessary information and instructions when discharged from the hospital on 7.5 mg of prednisone daily (5 mg 8am, 2.5 mg 4pm).

At the 18-day follow-up, the patient exhibited continued clinical improvement, including restored nutritional status (weight 48.5 kg), independent ambulation (1.0 km) without a crutch, and BP 120/60 mmHg. Laboratory tests revealed hematologic recovery (Hb 103 g/L), stable metabolism, and improved endocrine function. Compared with previous measurements, the ACTH level normalized despite a decrease in morning cortisol to 10.2 ng/mL (Table 1). An echocardiogram confirmed that the pericardial effusion had resolved (Fig. 3: chronological summary of disease progression).

Discussion

We report an elderly male patient with a history of esophageal carcinoma who developed an extensive macular rash on the trunk and extremities 5 months after the discontinuation of sintilimab. Following 6 weeks of high-dose topical corticosteroid therapy, the patient experienced progressive symptoms, including anorexia, fatigue, and weight loss. Initial evaluation led to a diagnosis of primary hypothyroidism, and levothyroxine replacement was initiated. However, the patient’s condition failed to improve and subsequently deteriorated, with the onset of hypotension, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, somnolence, and fever. On the basis of adrenocortical hormone testing and clinical assessment, the deterioration was attributed to adrenal crisis.

Sintilimab is a PD-1 inhibitor, a type of ICI used to treat various malignancies, including esophageal, gastric, lung, and gynecological cancers, and has improved overall and progression-free survival. Immune checkpoints are critical regulatory molecules that prevent excessive immune activation and protect healthy tissues from autoimmune damage. However, tumors exploit these pathways to evade immune surveillance and facilitate uncontrolled proliferation [4]. ICIs block these inhibitory signals, thereby enhancing T-cell-mediated antitumor immunity. Nevertheless, this mechanism is frequently associated with irAEs, including endocrine disorders [2, 5]. ICI-induced endocrine irAEs commonly involve the thyroid (hypothyroidism or thyrotoxicosis), pituitary (hypophysitis), adrenal (primary adrenal insufficiency), and pancreas (new-onset diabetes) glands. These complications often lead to clinical deterioration in ICI-treated patients and may pose life-threatening risks if unrecognized.

Thyroid dysfunction is the most prevalent endocrine irAE induced by ICIs, particularly PD-1 inhibitors [6]. Approximately 10% of patients receiving PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor monotherapy develop thyroid irAEs, with hypothyroidism constituting the majority of cases. The onset is variable throughout treatment (median: 6 weeks) [7], and most patients exhibit persistent dysfunction, necessitating early detection and therapeutic management.

The patient had no preexisting thyroid disorders, with baseline TPOAb and TGAb levels within normal ranges. Marked hypothyroidism was detected 4 months after cessation of sintilimab-containing therapy for esophageal carcinoma. Hypothyroidism cannot be ruled out because of unmonitored thyroid status during therapy.

Given the patient’s medical history and ICI therapy, his hypothyroidism was attributed to ICI-induced thyroid dysfunction. Hypothyroidism represents the most frequent endocrine irAE associated with ICIs. Notably, TPOAb and TGAb are typically normal in such cases [8], as the underlying mechanism involves T-cell-mediated immunity rather than humoral autoimmunity, a condition entirely consistent with this patient’s presentation. Contributing factors such as advanced age and preexisting thyroid disease may influence the severity of ICI-related thyroid dysfunction. Importantly, patients with elevated thyroid autoantibodies at baseline are at greater risk of developing ICI-induced thyroid disorders than those with normal antibody levels.

Hypophysitis represents another frequent irAE associated with ICIs, with a higher incidence reported with CTLA-4 inhibitors [6] than with PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitor monotherapy. This predilection may be attributed to the constitutive expression of CTLA-4 in pituitary cells [9], making them direct targets of CTLA-4 blockade. ICI-induced hypophysitis typically affects multiple hormonal axes, with 2–3 axes commonly involved [7].

The onset of hypophysitis varies by ICI class [10], with a median latency of 9–12 weeks for patients receiving CTLA-4 inhibitors versus 26 weeks for those receiving PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [11]. Clinical manifestations may include headache, nausea, vomiting, and diplopia, along with symptoms of secondary hormonal deficiencies (adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, or hypogonadism), such as fatigue, cold intolerance, and cognitive impairment. Characteristic MRI findings include pituitary enlargement. Diagnostic evaluation requires assessment of pituitary hormones and MRI. In the present case, while ACTH decreased, other pituitary hormones remained normal or elevated (TSH, PRL, LH; GH unmeasured), with no radiographic evidence of pituitary enlargement.

Primary adrenal insufficiency remains an uncommon complication of ICI therapy [12]. Although its clinical presentation resembles secondary adrenal insufficiency, key distinguishing features include mineralocorticoid deficiency, which was notably absent in this patient. Therefore, the observed secondary adrenal insufficiency likely resulted from prolonged high-dose topical corticosteroids administration, the reasons were as follows: (1) Treatment history: Extended (6-week) application of high-dose topical corticosteroids for the management of extensive macular rash; (2) Pharmacokinetic factors: Impaired skin barrier function likely enhances the systemic absorption of corticosteroids; (3) Temporal progression: Clinical manifestations emerged 1 month following corticosteroid initiation, which was consistent with the HPA axis suppression timeline. This clinical course demonstrates a classic presentation of iatrogenic adrenal suppression secondary to cutaneous corticosteroid absorption.

Another important possible cause of secondary adrenal insufficiency in this patient is ICI-related ACTH deficiency. While ICI-induced hypophysitis could lead to ACTH deficiency with multiple hormonal axes involved, isolated ACTH deficiency (IAD) secondary to ICIs has also been reported [13, 14]. Elderly males are more susceptible to this condition. Unlike anti-CTLA4-associated panhypophysitis, IAD occurs more frequently with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy [15, 16]. In a study by Wang et al. involving 123 cases of ICI-related IAD, the mean age was 64.3 ± 12.6 years, with a predominance of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor use (78.9%) and a male-to-female ratio of 1.8:1, the median number of ICI cycles before adrenal insufficiency diagnosis was 8 (range: 6–12), 11 patients developed IAD 1 to 11 months after ICI discontinuation, most patients (n = 88) had normal pituitary MRI findings, and these clinical features largely align with the present case. Additionally, only 18% of anti-PD-1/PD-L1-associated hypophysitis cases exhibit initial pituitary enlargement on imaging, which often resolves spontaneously within weeks [15, 17], so normal MRI examination could not rule out hypophysitis. Although topical corticosteroids can induce secondary adrenal insufficiency, they are less likely to precipitate adrenal crisis. The exact mechanism of ICI-induced IAD remains unclear, some studies suggest it may involve ICI-triggered anti-pituitary antibodies [18, 19]. ACTH stimulation testing and pituitary antibody assays could aid in diagnosing IAD, but these were not performed in this patient due to limited conditions. IAD is unlikely to resolve spontaneously, necessitating lifelong hormonal replacement and long-term follow-up, which should be communicated to the patient.

The patient subsequently developed progressive clinical deterioration characterized by hemodynamic instability, fever, somnolence, electrolyte imbalances, hypoglycemia and other serious conditions, and combined with the cortisol rhythm results, he was considered an adrenal crisis caused by the aggravation of adrenal insufficiency.

The important cause of the adrenal crisis in this case was hypothyroidism, which was not diagnosed or treated in a timely manner. Hypothyroidism is a metabolic state requiring lower cortisol levels, and levothyroxine increases metabolic rates and anabolic states, thereby increasing the patient’s demand for cortisol and resulting in relative cortisol deficiency. In patients with adrenal insufficiency, such as this patient, replacement therapy with levothyroxine exacerbated the condition, contributing in part to adrenal crisis. In addition, thyroid hormones play important roles in cortisol clearance, and the initiation of levothyroxine enhances cortisol clearance and increases the risk of adrenal crisis [20].

Additionally, dermatologic toxicities are among the most common irAEs induced by ICI therapy. In this case, the patient developed a macular rash involving the trunk and extremities 5 months after discontinuation of sintilimab. Although delayed in onset, this cutaneous manifestation cannot exclude ICI-related toxicity. ICI-associated dermatologic adverse events commonly affect the trunk and extremities, with a highly variable latency period, manifesting during treatment or months after drug cessation [21].

One interesting aspect of this case is that the patient had a marked increase in BNP on admission, suggesting heart failure, along with T wave inversion in multiple leads, both of which improved rapidly after 1 week of oral prednisolone, and pericardial effusion disappeared after 18 days of oral prednisolone. This may be associated with the coexistence of hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency, which can lead to decreased myocardial contractility, decreased blood pressure and hypoperfusion, resulting in increased myocardial ischemia. Similarly, elevated creatinine levels may also be related to these factors.

In summary, we present a complex case of hypothyroidism, macular rash, and adrenal insufficiency following ICI therapy. The initial manifestations of ICI-related endocrine dysfunction often present with ambiguous clinical features that may mimic either tumor progression or treatment complications. They can occur at unpredictable times, leading to delayed diagnosis and even life-threatening outcomes. This case highlights the importance of early screening and timely treatment for ICI-related irAEs. Physicians should be aware of these adverse events, and preventive monitoring of related symptoms and associated endocrine hormones is crucial for early diagnosis and treatment.
