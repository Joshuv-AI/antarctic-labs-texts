Pyrethroids resistance and distribution of kdr mutations (L1014F and N1575Y) in Anopheles gambiae s.l. along the south-north transect of Benin
Authors: Razaki A. Ossé, Steve Zinsou Hougbe, Minassou Juvenal Ahouandjinou, Aboubacar Sidick, Zul-kifl Affolabi, Koffi Djigbodi Koumodji, Filémon Tokponnon, Rock Aikpon, Arthur Sovi, Haziz A. Sina Orou, Lamine Baba-Moussa, Martin Akogbeto

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPyrethroid resistance in Anopheles gambiae s.l. compromises malaria vector control in sub-Saharan Africa. In Benin, resistance has been documented for over two decades, but the geographic distribution of emerging mutations such as N1575Y remains poorly characterized. This study investigated pyrethroid susceptibility, kdr mutations, and metabolic resistance mechanisms across twelve communes spanning a south–north transect of the country. MethodsLarvae were collected from twelve sites between January 2024 and January 2025, reared to adults, and tested for susceptibility to deltamethrin (0.05%) and alphacypermethrin (0.05%) using WHO tube assays. Molecular species were identified by PCR, kdr mutations (L1014F, N1575Y) detected by TaqMan genotyping, and enzymatic activities (oxidases, esterases, GSTs) quantified through biochemical assays.ResultsAll populations showed high resistance to pyrethroids, with mortality consistently below 60%. An. coluzzii predominated (58.2%), followed by An. gambiae s.s. (39.2%) and An. arabiensis (2.8%). The L1014F mutation was highly prevalent across all communes (50–100%), while N1575Y was detected in six communes at frequencies ranging from 1 to 10%. Detoxification enzymes were significantly overexpressed compared to the Kisumu reference strain, confirming the contribution of metabolic resistance.ConclusionsThis large-scale survey confirms widespread pyrethroid resistance in An. gambiae s.l. across Benin, driven by both kdr mutations and metabolic mechanisms. The heterogeneous distribution of N1575Y highlights localized selection pressures and its emerging role in resistance intensification. These findings emphasize the urgent need to deploy PBO-based or dual-active ingredient LLINs, strengthen molecular surveillance, and implement integrated resistance management strategies to sustain malaria control in Benin.

Background

Insecticide resistance in Anopheles gambiae s.l. remains a major obstacle to the effectiveness of malaria vector control strategies in sub-Saharan Africa. In Benin, resistance was first reported in 1999 with pyrethroids, particularly deltamethrin and permethrin [1], both widely used in Insecticide-Treated Nets (ITNs). Over time, resistance has extended to other classes of insecticides employed in public health, such as organochlorines and carbamates [2–4]. A retrospective analysis of insecticide resistance trends over the past two decades in Benin revealed a progressive spatio-temporal spread of resistance across all ecological zones of the country. This dissemination is attributed to several factors, including the agricultural use of insecticides, localized entomological pressure, and environmental changes [5].

Resistance mechanisms in An. gambiae s.l. are generally categorized into two main types: metabolic resistance and target-site resistance. Metabolic resistance involves the mosquito’s ability to detoxify or metabolize insecticides before they reach their neuronal target, thereby reducing their efficacy [6]. This mechanism is mainly mediated by the overexpression of three key enzyme families: cytochrome P450 monooxygenases (CYP450), which oxidize insecticides to facilitate excretion; glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), which conjugate insecticides to glutathione, thereby reducing toxicity; and carboxylesterases (COEs), which hydrolyze ester-based insecticides such as organophosphates and carbamates [6, 7].

Target-site resistance is one of the most well-studied mechanisms, especially in response to neurotoxic insecticides. It primarily involves point mutations in the voltage-gated sodium channel (VGSC) gene, such as the kdr mutations, which reduce insecticide binding affinity to neural targets [8]. Two main forms of the kdr mutation have been identified: L1014F (leucine to phenylalanine substitution), which predominates in West Africa [8], and L1014S (leucine to serine substitution), more commonly found in East Africa [9].

In recent years, an additional kdr mutation, N1575Y, has been identified in An. gambiae populations across West Africa, initially in Burkina Faso [10], and subsequently in Côte d'Ivoire [11], Guinea [12], Cameroon [13], Sierra Leone [14], and Senegal [15]. This mutation involves a substitution of asparagine (N) with tyrosine (Y) at codon 1575 of the VGSC gene. N1575Y is frequently found in combination with L1014F, and although it confers moderate resistance when present alone, its synergistic presence alongside L1014F significantly enhances resistance to pyrethroids by stabilizing the altered conformation of the sodium channel [10]. Several studies have associated this mutation combination with reduced mortality rates in mosquitoes exposed to standard insecticide-treated nets, posing a serious threat to the effectiveness and longevity of current vector control tools [16].

In Benin, limited data are available on the occurrence and geographic distribution of the N1575Y mutation. Improved knowledge of its spatial spread is crucial for adapting national vector control strategies, particularly through the deployment of dual-active or synergist-enhanced insecticidal nets.

This study aims to assess the susceptibility of An. gambiae s.l. populations to pyrethroids and to map the geographic distribution of the N1575Y mutation across twelve communes spanning a south-to-north transect in Benin, with the goal of informing more effective and context-specific vector control interventions.

Method

Study area

The study was conducted from January 2024 to January 2025 in twelve communes distributed along a south-to-north transect in Benin, West Africa (Fig. 1). In the southern zone, the communes of Cotonou, Ifangni, Aguégués, Dogbo, Grand-Popo, and Allada are characterized by traditional subsistence agriculture (cassava, yam, taro, oil palm), market gardening, and fishing due to the proximity of water bodies. In the central part of the country, Abomey and Glazoué are dominated by large-scale cultivation of legumes and cereals, including maize, beans, and groundnuts, often associated with the intensive use of chemical fertilizers. In the northern zone, Malanville, Natitingou, N’Dali, and Djougou are known for the cultivation of rice, soybean, and cotton, involving widespread use of herbicides and insecticides.Fig. 1Map of Benin showing the study commune

Map of Benin showing the study commune

The southern and central regions experience a sub-equatorial climate with two rainy seasons (April to mid-July and September to November) and two dry seasons (December to March and mid-July to August), while the north has a Sudanian climate with a single rainy season from May to October and a dry season from November to April [17].

Mosquito larval collection and rearing

Larval collections were carried out during the rainy season from diverse breeding habitats, including temporary pools, puddles, and semi-permanent water bodies, in order to reduce the risk of oversampling related individuals. Collected larvae were grouped by site, transported to the insectary, and reared under standard conditions (ambient temperature of 27–28 °C and relative humidity of 70–80%). Larvae were fed daily with Tetramin® fish food until pupation. Pupae were transferred into cardboard cups and placed inside mesh cages for adult emergence.

WHO insecticide susceptibility tests

Susceptibility tests were performed following the standard protocol of the World Health Organization (WHO) [18]. For each site, batches of 20–25 non-blood-fed female An. gambiae s.l., aged 3–5 days, were exposed for one hour to WHO-treated filter papers impregnated with 0.05% deltamethrin and 0.05% alpha-cypermethrin. Two control batches of the same size were exposed to untreated papers. Knockdown was recorded at 15, 30, 40, 50, and 60 min. After exposure, mosquitoes were transferred to holding tubes and supplied with 10% glucose solution. Mortality was recorded 24 h post-exposure.

Molecular analysis

DNA extraction

A total of 50 mosquitoes (dead and alive) per site were selected post-bioassay for molecular analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted using the 2% CTAB protocol described by Morlais et al. [19]. Each specimen was individually grinded in 200 µL of 2% CTAB buffer using a sterile pestle. Tubes were incubated at 65 °C for 5 min, then 200 µL of chloroform were added, mixed, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube, and 200 µL of isopropanol were added to precipitate DNA. Tubes were centrifuged again (12,000 rpm, 15 min), and the DNA pellet was washed with 200 µL of 70% ethanol, centrifuged, air-dried, and finally resuspended in 40 µL of sterile distilled water before storage at − 20 °C.

Molecular species identification

Species within the An. gambiae complex were identified using PCR following the protocol of Santolamazza et al. [20], with species-specific primers F6.1a (5’-TCGCCTTAGACCTTGCGTTA-3’) and R6.1b (5’-CGCTTCAAGAATTCGAGATAC-3’). The PCR reaction contained 10X buffer, 1.5 mM MgCl₂, 0.2 mM dNTPs, and 10 pmol of each primer. Amplification conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 54 °C for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

Genotyping of kdr L1014F and N1575Y mutations

Detection of the kdr L1014F and N1575Y mutations was performed using TaqMan® real-time PCR assays, based on the methods described by Bass et al. [21] and Jones et al. [10], respectively. For L1014F, each reaction (10 µL) contained 1 × SensiMix (Bioline), 800 nM of each primer (Forward: 5′-CATTTTTCTTGGCCACTGTAGTGAT-3′; Reverse: 5′-CGATCTTGGTCCATGTTAATTTGCA-3′), and 200 nM of each TaqMan-MGB probe (wild-type probe WT, VIC-5′-CTTACGACTAAATTTC-3′; mutant probe KdrW, 6FAM-5′-ACGACAAAATTTC-3′). Amplifications were carried out on a QuantGene 9600 thermocycler under the following conditions: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 45 s.

For N1575Y, each reaction (10 µL) contained 1 × SensiMix (Bioline), 800 nM of each primer (1575-F: 5′-TGGATCGCTAGAAATGTTCATGACA-3′; 1575-R: 5′-CGAGGAATTGCCTTTAGAGGTTTCT-3′), and 200 nM of each TaqMan-MGB probe (6FAM-5′-TTTTTCATTGCATAATAGTAC-3′-NFQ; VIC-5′-ATTTTTTTCATTGCATTATAGTAC-3′-NFQ). PCR cycling conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 92 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 60 s on a QuantGene 9600 thermocycler.

Biochemical enzyme assays

Biochemical tests were conducted on 30–50 female mosquitoes per site, aged 2–5 days and not previously exposed to insecticides. Samples were stored at − 80 °C until analysis. According to WHO guidelines [22], enzymatic activities of glutathione-S-transferases (GSTs), mixed-function oxidases (MFOs), and non-specific esterases (α- and β-) were measured and compared to those of a known susceptible laboratory strain (An. gambiae s.s. Kisumu).

Mosquitoes were homogenized on ice in 200 µL of distilled water and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 min. Enzymatic activity readings were taken at 340 nm for GSTs, 630 nm for MFOs, and 550 nm for esterase.

Data analysis

Bioassay data were recorded manually and entered into Microsoft Excel for processing. Mortality rates were calculated as the ratio of dead mosquitoes to the total number exposed after 24 h. Resistance status was determined according to WHO criteria [23]:Mortality rate ≥ 98%: susceptible mosquito population;Mortality rate between 90 and 97%: mosquito population suspected of being resistant, further examination required;Mortality rate < 90%: mosquito population with confirmed resistance.

Allelic frequency of kdr mutations was calculated using the formula:

F(R) = [2n.RR + n.RS]/[2(n.RR + n.RS + n.SS)] [20]

(n = number of mosquito samples with a given genotype) [4].

A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, was used to compare mean enzyme activity levels between sites. An independent-samples t-test was performed to compare enzyme activity between field mosquitoes and the Kisumu reference strain. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.1.3) and SPSS® version 18.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

For deltamethrin (0.05%), the lowest mortality was recorded in Allada (1.9%), and the highest in Aguégués (54.44%). Enzymatic activity levels were compared with those of the laboratory-susceptible An. gambiae s.s. Kisumu strain. Analyses were conducted on 30 to 50 females per site (n = 30–50). Discussion

This study provides an updated overview of pyrethroid resistance profiles and the distribution of the kdr mutations L1014F and N1575Y in An. gambiae s.l. populations across a south–north transect in Benin. The results confirm widespread resistance to deltamethrin and alphacypermethrin, with mortality consistently below 60%. Molecular assays revealed the predominance of An. coluzzii and An. gambiae s.s., while An. arabiensis was detected at lower frequencies. The L1014F mutation was highly prevalent across all communes, whereas the N1575Y mutation showed a heterogeneous distribution, being present in only six communes. Additionally, biochemical assays indicated significant overexpression of detoxification enzymes, supporting the co-occurrence of metabolic and target-site resistance mechanisms.

The high level of pyrethroid resistance observed in this study is consistent with reports from Benin [24-31], and other West African countries, including Burkina Faso [10], Côte d’Ivoire [11], Ghana [41], and Nigeria [41]. In most of these contexts, mortality to pyrethroids is often below the WHO threshold for confirmed resistance (< 90%), reflecting a regional crisis of reduced insecticide efficacy. Similar to our findings, studies from southern Benin and coastal Ghana have highlighted the predominance of An. coluzzii, while An. gambiae s.s. remains more common in central and northern regions, likely reflecting ecological adaptation to breeding sites [27, 32, 33]. This ecological differentiation is a key driverof vector resilience and complicates resistance management strategies.

The L1014F kdr mutation was detected at very high frequencies in all sites, corroborating previous studies across Benin and West Africa [34, 35]. This mutation remains under strong selection pressure, maintained by both public health insecticide uses and widespread agricultural pesticide application. The N1575Y mutation, first reported in Burkina Faso and subsequently across West Africa [10–15], was observed at variable frequencies in six communes, with the highest prevalence recorded in Malanville (10%). Its absence in other sites suggests localized selection pressures, likely influenced by intensive cotton and cereal farming in northern Benin [36-37]. The frequency of this mutation observed in Benin remains relatively low, with a maximum of approximately 10%, compared to much higher levels reported in Burkina Faso, where it can reach 30–40% in some areas.

This discrepancy may be explained by several non-mutually exclusive hypotheses. It may reflect differences in selection pressure, driven by local variations in the intensity and patterns of insecticide use. Another plausible explanation is a more recent introduction of this mutation into Anopheles populations in Benin, which may not yet have reached equilibrium. Finally, underlying genetic differences in vector populations between these countries may also contribute to the dynamics and spread of this resistance marker [34–36]. Importantly, N1575Y is known to act synergistically with L1014F, stabilizing sodium channel alterations and amplifying resistance to pyrethroids. The detection of this mutation, even at moderate frequencies, signals a growing risk to the long-term efficacy of standard ITNs.

In addition to kdr mutations, overexpression of detoxification enzymes, particularly mixed-function oxidases (MFOs), non-specific esterase, and glutathione-S-transferases (GSTs), was observed in multiple communes. Similar findings have been reported in northern and southern Benin [3, 4, 38, 39] and in neighboring countries, confirming that metabolic resistance substantially contributes to pyrethroid tolerance. The coexistence of metabolic and target-site resistance mechanisms likely explains the very low mortality rates observed in WHO bioassays, even in sites with near fixation of L1014F. This combination of resistance mechanisms represents a serious challenge for vector control, as metabolic resistance can amplify cross-resistance to multiple insecticide classes [40].

The widespread resistance documented here has direct implications for malaria transmission control in Benin. Reduced susceptibility to pyrethroids threatens the efficacy of standard ITNs, which remain the cornerstone of vector control. Previous studies in Côte d’Ivoire and Burkina Faso have demonstrated that kdr mutations and metabolic resistance are associated with decreased ITN effectiveness in reducing mosquito survival and blood-feeding [16]. If such trends are sustained in Benin, malaria incidence and morbidity could increase despite high ITN coverage. This highlights the urgent need to adapt resistance management strategies and integrate newer vector control tools.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. First, this study was cross-sectional and did not capture seasonal or temporal fluctuations in resistance profiles. Second, while enzymatic overexpression was documented, functional bioassays or transcriptomic analyses were not performed, limiting our ability to quantify the precise metabolic contribution. The absence of synergist assays, particularly pre-exposure to piperonyl butoxide (PBO), which would have allowed a clearer distinction between the relative contributions of metabolic resistance mechanisms and target-site resistance (kdr). Although detoxification enzyme activities (MFOs, GSTs, and esterases) were assessed using non-insecticide-exposed mosquitoes, the lack of synergist assays prevents a precise quantification of their role in the observed resistance. In addition, this study did not include detailed data on agricultural practices and environmental factors (e.g., crop types, treatment schedules, insecticide usage volumes) across the study sites. This limitation constrains the interpretation of the spatial variation observed in resistance mechanisms, particularly for mutations such as N1575Y. Future studies integrating these parameters would be valuable to better understand the ecological and anthropogenic drivers of resistance. Finally, this study focused exclusively on pyrethroids; other insecticide classes currently recommended by WHO, such as chlorfenapyr and clothianidin, were not tested.

Our findings strongly suggest that conventional pyrethroid-only ITNs are insufficient for malaria control in Benin. In line with WHO recommendations [41], the deployment of dual-active ingredient ITNs (e.g., pyrethroid-chlorfenapyr) should be prioritized, particularly in areas with high kdr allele frequencies. Regular molecular surveillance of both L1014F and N1575Y is essential to monitor resistance trends and inform decision-making. Moreover, strengthening integrated vector management (IVM) approaches, including insecticide rotation, targeted indoor residual spraying (IRS) with non-pyrethroids, and reduction of agricultural pesticide misuse, will be critical to slowing the spread of resistance alleles. Collaboration between agricultural and public health sectors will be indispensable to achieve sustainable resistance management.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that An. gambiae s.l. populations across Benin exhibit widespread pyrethroid resistance, driven by both kdr mutations and metabolic mechanisms. The detection of N1575Y in several communes underscores its emerging role in resistance intensification. These findings call for urgent adaptation of vector control strategies, including the deployment of next-generation ITNs, strengthened molecular surveillance, and integrated management of insecticide use across sectors. Without such measures, the long-term effectiveness of malaria vector control in Benin and the wider West African region could be severely compromised.
