How do climate conditions and urbanisation impact nontuberculous mycobacterial infections? An Italian nationwide case–control study
Authors: Paola Faverio, Lorenzo Del Castello, Maura Spotti, Paola Rebora, Enrico Cesare Maggioni, Raffaele Salerno, Alessandro Perotto, Fabrizio Luppi, Francesco Rocco Bertuccio, Stefania Reale, Valentina Fornari, Filippo Gozzi, Bianca Granozzi, Federico Lorenzo Dente, Francesca Mariotti, Jacopo Simonetti, Gina Gualano, Niccolò Riccardi, Maurizio Ferrarese, Luigi Ruffo Codecasa, Giovanni Sotgiu, Andrea Gori, Andrea Gramegna, Margherita Ori, Edoardo Simonetta, Francesco Blasi, Stefano Aliberti, on behalf of METEO-NTM study group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionNontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are opportunistic pathogens primarily found in soil and water. Previous studies have linked higher population density and tropical/subtropical climates with increased NTM pulmonary disease (NTM-PD). However, the effects of meteorological parameters and population density on NTM incidence in European countries remain unclear. This study aimed to assess whether climate conditions (temperature, humidity, wind speed, precipitation) and urbanisation influence the occurrence of NTM-PD.MethodsWe conducted a case–control study evaluating meteorological conditions (in the 12 months before the first NTM isolate) and urbanisation in the municipalities of residence of 1061 adults with NTM-PD enrolled in an Italian multicentre observational study (2017–2023), compared with a random sample of 10 000 adults from the Italian population.ResultsThe mean age of patients was 63.1 years, and 67.2% were female. Less densely populated areas were at lower risk for NTM-PD, even after adjusting for age, sex and meteorological parameters in a multivariable mixed-effects logistic regression model (OR 0.66 (95% CI: 0.56–0.78) and 0.68 (95% CI: 0.52–0.89) for semi-urban and rural areas, respectively, versus urban areas). Hotter climates were found to be at higher risk for NTM infection (OR 1.21 (95% CI: 1.10–1.33) for each °C increase in mean annual temperature). More ventilated climates with higher precipitation were at lower risk for NTM infection (OR 0.90 (95% CI: 0.86–0.95) for each km·h−1 increase in mean annual wind speed; OR 0.99 (95% CI: 0.99–0.99) for each 10 mm increase in mean annual precipitation).ConclusionsClimate change, particularly global warming and urbanisation, may increase NTM infections in regions with Mediterranean and continental climates.

Introduction

Nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are opportunistic agents whose incidence and prevalence have increased in recent years, especially in those countries where the prevalence of tuberculosis is low [1, 2]. NTM may cause pulmonary disease in humans and are not transmitted person-to-person but are acquired from the environment, mostly by inhalation. The main sources of infections are soil, natural waters and drinking-water distribution systems [3, 4].

Prior studies showed how different geographical and environmental conditions may influence the development of NTM infection [5]. NTM appear to be more frequent in tropical and subtropical regions; however they can also be found at other latitudes. In the USA the higher incidence of NTM isolates was observed in the states of Hawaii, California, Florida and Louisiana, in counties with greater population densities and those with higher percentages of soil covered by surface water [5, 6]. Similarly, in a Japanese study the prevalence of NTM was found to be higher in southwestern Japan, industrialised and with a warm climate, compared to northern Japan, rural and colder [7]. Furthermore, geographical area with higher annual precipitations, evapotranspiration and atmospheric moisture have been linked to increased risk for NTM exposure and pulmonary infection [6, 8]. In the European framework, only a recent Portuguese study evaluated the impact of the main environmental variables on NTM disease incidence, finding that only a higher population density, but not climate conditions, were associated with an increase of the disease [9].

Therefore, the effective impact of different meteorological parameters and population density on NTM incidence is not yet completely understood, particularly in the European setting where it needs to be investigated. Italy is a good representative of the main European climates, presenting on its territory both the mediterranean, continental and alpine climate zones.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate whether the occurrence of NTM pulmonary disease (NTM-PD) is associated with climate conditions and degree of urbanisation.

Material and methods

Study design

A multicentre, observational, prospective study was conducted from 2017 to 2023 across 69 infectious diseases and pulmonary centres in Italy. Consecutive adults (≥18 years of age) either with a NTM isolated from a respiratory sample in the previous year or undergoing anti-NTM treatment were enrolled. All patients received a diagnosis of NTM-PD according to the 2007 ATS/IDSA statement [10]. Both those requiring a specific treatment and those in whom “watchful waiting” was adopted were included in the study. Each participating centre obtained local Institutional Review Board approval.

For this study, the first NTM isolate ever was the one considered for the association between infection and environmental and climate exposure. The time frame of the first NTM isolate ranged between January 2013 and August 2023. For every patient, month and year of the first NTM isolation were recorded, together with clinical and sociodemographic characteristics.

In this study, for every patient with NTM respiratory isolates, the climate conditions in the municipality of residence in the 12 months immediately prior to the NTM isolation were considered. The authors of this study reached a consensus regarding this time-frame, although limited literature data are available on the latency period between environmental NTM exposure and clinical manifestation. Different factors including the virulence and load of the NTM species and the host susceptibility (e.g. immunodeficiencies) may impact on this delay. Furthermore, the diagnostic delay may be enhanced by the geographical location of patients and the limited access to high-level healthcare services [11]. Despite all these factors, 1 year of exposure appeared a reasonable period to evaluate the effect of climate and environment on NTM-PD.

In order to compare climate conditions and degree of urbanisation in the municipality of residence of the NTM cases with the reference population (Italian adults), we randomly drew a sample of 10 000 subjects (controls) from the entire adult Italian population, for which distribution by sex, age and city/town of residence was derived by the Italian National Institute of Statistics (www.istat.it). In controls we considered climate conditions and urbanisation in the municipality of residence in the calendar year before to be consistent with the cases. As NTM diagnoses were updated until August 2023, we considered among the cases only patients with pulmonary NTM isolates diagnosed in the period 2013–2022. Urbanisation degree and meteorological parameters definition

The degree of urbanisation of each municipality of residence was derived by the Italian National Institute of Statistics (www.istat.it) and is based on the criteria of geographical contiguity and minimum population thresholds on the regular grid with 1 km2 cells (Geostat 2011 Population Grid), as described in supplementary table S1. Climate conditions of the municipalities involved in the study for the period of interest were reconstructed using an interpolation technique called MLRLI (multi-linear regression with local improvement) [12, 13].

The input data come from various networks of meteorological weather stations, spread all over the Italian territory, which continuously measure temperature at 2-m height, relative humidity, wind speed and direction, barometric pressure and precipitation. Among these networks it is notable to mention the MNW network (Meteonetwork) [14], a citizen-science network with a number of active meteorological stations ranging between 1200 in 2012 and 3500 in 2022. Other networks (regional, private and aerodrome networks) are also included in the present analysis [15].

For the rain precipitation field, satellite data are also included. This type of data comes from the Global Precipitation Measurement (GPM) mission [16], which provides rainfall estimation for every point of the globe, with a spatial resolution of 10 km.

Measurements data are then combined through the use of the MLRLI technique, interpolating the observations into a 2 km×2 km regular lat/lon grid on the Italian territory, with a temporal resolution of 1 h. Among the various advantages of MLRLI technique, it is worth mentioning: 1) the interpolated data are constructed with the use of multiple stations and sources of information, reducing the single-station bias; and 2) every point on the grid always has an appropriate value associated with it, even if no working stations are present nearby.

Data from meteorological stations, before entering the MLRLI technique, experience a quality control check: range tests, to avoid out of range values, and a cross validation check, to avoid anomalous values with respect to nearby stations.

The final step of the procedure is computation of the observation values for all the Italian municipalities: this passage is completed by an interpolation from the 2 km grid. Since the majority of Italian municipalities have a linear dimension far greater than 2 km, the assumption to use the grid data is reasonable.

At the end of the observation pre-processing, every Italian municipality has an hourly value for the associated meteorological fields mentioned above. These data are used to calculate mean climatic conditions for each municipality of residence of the patients and overall Italian population, in particular time intervals.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics summarising sociodemographic characteristics of cases were performed.

Age- and sex-adjusted incidence rates of NTM-PD were computed by direct standardisation using data from the entire Italian adult population as reference. In order to estimate the incidence rate of NTM-PD, we considered only the prospective cases (period 2017–2022). Incidence rates were calculated within each Italian administrative region and by urbanisation level of the municipalities of residence.

With regard to weather conditions, we defined as meteorological variables both the mean annual values of each meteorological parameter and the percentage of the year spent at extreme climate conditions in each municipality. Cut-off values to define the extreme ranges were: >70%, >85%, <45% and <30% for relative humidity; >25°C and <10°C for temperature; and <3 km·h−1 and >15 km·h−1 for wind speed [17].

Summary statistics and violin plots were included to compare the distributions of such meteorological variables in cases’ and controls’ municipalities.

The impact on the odds of developing pulmonary NTM was evaluated through a multivariable mixed effects logistic regression model, with the region of residence as random intercept to account for the dependence of municipalities within the same region. The explanatory variables considered were sex, age, degree of urbanisation of the residence municipality, year of diagnosis and the mean annual values of temperature (°C), relative humidity (%), wind speed (km·h−1) and precipitation (mm). We checked the linearity of all continuous variables and modelled it appropriately when the assumption was not fulfilled. Cases occurring in the period 2013–2022 were compared with the random sample of the Italian population. Two subanalyses were conducted to verify the results’ robustness: 1) the first on the subset of patients and controls resident in the northern area of the country, where there is a higher number of reference centres which may imply a more accurate and exhaustive identification of the disease (n=5347, 715 NTM cases); 2) the second on the subset of patients with a first NTM isolate from January 2017 (i.e. prospective cases, n=6876, 934 NTM cases), because before that date the information on isolates was retrospectively collected.

Results are reported in terms of odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical analyses were done using the software R version 4.2.0.

Over 17 different NTM species were isolated during the study period. The most common ones were Mycobacterium avium, responsible for 371 (35.0%) of all NTM cases, followed by M. intracellulare with 300 (28.3%), M. abscessus complex with 87 (8.2%), M. chimaera with 75 (7.1%), M. xenopi with 52 (4.9%) and M. kansasii with 44 (4.1%) (table 1).

Pulmonary NTM incidence according to region and urbanisation

The majority of patients, 588 (55.4%), lived in densely populated areas (according to ISTAT definition, see table 1), 372 (35.1%) in areas with intermediate population density (towns and suburbs) and 101 (9.5%) in rural areas. In the period 2017–2022 the incidence rates of NTM respiratory isolates per 100 000 inhabitants were 0.476 (95% CI: 0.435–0.518) in cities, 0.235 (95% CI: 0.210–0.260) in towns and suburbs, and 0.175 (95% CI: 0.139–0.211) in rural areas, accounting for the distribution of age and sex of the Italian population.

Patients with NTM lived in areas in which the temperature was higher than 25°C for 14.3% of the year (13.2% in the control group) and lower than 10°C for 29.9% of the year (26.8% in the control group) (figure 4). Mean annual wind speed and precipitation were found to be respectively equal to 6.3 km·h−1 and 923 mm·year−1 in the residence places of NTM cases (8.1 km·h−1 and 1168 mm·year−1 in the Italian territory). Similarly, patients with NTM exhibited a mean annual percentage of 31.3% of cases with wind speeds below 3 km·h−1 (compared to 22.3% in the control group), and, concurrently, an annual percentage of 7.7% with wind speeds exceeding 15 km·h−1 (versus 13.3% in the control group) (figure 4). The median annual humidity of the place of residence of individuals with NTM infection (71%, IQR: 69–72) was very similar to that of the Italian territory (71%, IQR: 69–73) (supplementary table S2).

Multivariable model

According to the multivariable mixed model's results, females and persons aged between 61 and 80 years had a higher odds of developing NTM infection (figure 5a and c). Subjects residing in areas with lower population density experienced a lower odds of developing NTM pulmonary infection: the OR was 0.659 (95% CI: 0.557–0.779) for subjects living in towns and suburbs, and 0.68 (95% CI: 0.52–0.89) for those living in rural areas, compared to citizens living in cities (figure 5b). This result was confirmed in the subanalysis on Northern Italy, whilst over the period 2017–2022 no significant difference was found between subjects living in rural versus urban areas (OR 0.79, 95% CI: 0.59–1.06) (supplementary table S3).

As far as meteorological parameters were concerned, both precipitation and wind speed had a protective role on the development of NTM infection. The risk of infection decreased by 1% for each 10 mm increment in mean annual precipitation (OR 0.99, 95% CI: 0.99–0.99) and by almost 10% for each km·h−1 increment in mean annual wind speed (OR 0.902, 95% CI: 0.859–0.946) (figure 5e and f and supplementary table S3) The two subanalyses of Italian northern regions and for the time period 2017–2022 yielded consistent results.

On the other hand, temperature was found to be a risk factor for the occurrence of NTM infection (figure 5g). For each °C increment in mean annual temperature the risk of NTM pulmonary infection increased by ∼21% (OR 1.209, 95% CI: 1.098–1.332). Similar findings were observed for the time period 2017–2022, but not in the subanalysis of Italian northern regions (OR 1.131, 95% CI: 0.984–1.300).

Interestingly, the (log-)odds of NTM infection did not linearly increase with the increase in humidity, but showed a quadratic (or bell-shaped) trend (figure 5h). In particular, the odds ratio of developing an NTM pulmonary infection (using the sample mean as the reference) was essentially close to 1 for people residing in places with mean annual humidity below 71%, while it decreased for higher values.

Discussion

Using a large Italian nationwide NTM database, we investigated the influence of urbanisation and climate conditions on NTM pulmonary infection. Our analysis showed that subjects residing in semi-urban and rural areas experienced a lower risk of infection compared to those who live in urban regions (OR 0.66, 95% CI: 0.56–0.78 and 0.68 (0.52–0.89) in semi-urban and rural areas, respectively versus urban areas). Furthermore, living in hotter climates was associated with a greater risk of NTM infection (OR 1.21 (95% CI: 1.10–1.33) for each °C increase in mean annual temperature), whereas residing in ventilated areas with a greater amount of precipitation resulted in a reduced risk of infection (OR 0.90 (95% CI: 0.86–0.95) for each km·h−1 increase in mean annual wind speed, and OR 0.99 (95% CI: 0.99–0.99) for each 10 mm increase in mean annual precipitation).

As far as population density was concerned, consistent results were found in prior studies conducted in Portugal, the USA and Australia [8, 9, 18, 19]. The positive association between urbanisation and NTM pulmonary infection may be attributed to the contamination of municipal water systems, reservoirs and plumbing. Furthermore, a diagnostic bias may also have had an impact since patients living in cities may have better access to specialised medical centres.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first evaluating the impact of all four key meteorological parameters – temperature, precipitation, wind speed and relative humidity – on NTM pulmonary infection and accounting for the distribution of the population in terms of sex and age, although our study did not account for ecological variables, such as land coverage by surface water, soil composition and potable water contamination and disinfection system. We observed that higher temperatures were linked to an increased risk of NTM respiratory disease, and this finding is in line with other studies conducted in Japan and the USA [7, 8]. On the other hand, precipitation and wind speed had a protective role, which may be explained by an “air cleaning effect”. In support of this hypothesis, previous research conducted in the USA reported an increase in the risk for environmental NTM exposure in areas with high atmospheric moisture (potentially associated with lower wind speed), such as Hawaii, California, Florida and Louisiana [5]. Nevertheless, the role of precipitation is still controversial, and recent data from the USA on precipitation and evapotranspiration showed that higher levels were linked to an increased risk of NTM infection [5]. The role of humidity in our study is more debatable, probably because variations in relative humidity across Italy are modest, and most of the population resides in areas not subjected to extreme humidity levels.

Based on our findings, hotter and less ventilated climates were associated with an increased risk of NTM infection, whilst the impact of precipitation and relative humidity was less pronounced, which is more debated in the literature with conflicting results. We speculated that humidity and precipitation are closely connected to the presence of natural and artificial water basins, which may themselves be an environmental source of NTM exposure. Therefore, the impact of precipitation and humidity on NTM infection may differ based on the geographical proximity to water basins (e.g. lakes and streams); however we did not have this information available.

Other non-European studies (Australian and Japanese) have shown a different distribution of NTM families based on soil characteristics and agricultural activities [2, 19–20]. A study by Thomson et al. [2] conducted in Queensland, Australia, demonstrated a variation in the incidence of slow-growing and rapid-growing NTM with cyclical incidence following increased rainfall and temperature peaks. Similarly, we performed a subanalysis to assess whether there were any differences in the distribution of meteorological variables among the most frequently isolated NTM families, but no significant differences were observed.

Although it has many strengths, we acknowledge some limitations of our study. First, we did not account for a possible diagnostic gap between southern and northern Italy. In the latter there is a higher presence of reference centres for NTM-PD. Nevertheless, the findings from the subgroup analysis of patients diagnosed in the northern regions are basically consistent with our primary analyses. Second, we did not consider the possible indoor exposure (e.g. indoor pools, heating systems and shower water), environmental factors (e.g. soil exposure, such as gardening, and proximity to ponds, streams or lakes) or water source contamination that may have impacted NTM transmission. On the other hand, we adjusted our meteorological analyses for urbanisation levels, as well as for age and sex distribution. Third, although the main infectious diseases and pulmonary reference centres for NTM-PD in Italy were involved in our study, we have to acknowledge that not all NTM cases in Italy have been identified by this study, leading to a possible underestimation of incidence of NTM cases. Moreover, not all the centres were recruited at the same time, leading to further underestimation in the early years. Furthermore, this study prospectively recruited patients from 2017, while data on the NTM isolates prior to that year were retrospectively collected. Fourth, air pollution may also have impacted NTM transmission, but we did not have data for this, and this could be the objective of future studies.

Our study emphasises the importance of large-scale monitoring of NTM-PD cases (e.g. through a laboratory reporting system) to obtain more precise epidemiological data, enabling the assessment of both environmental (natural and anthropogenic) and climatological impacts.

In conclusion, in Italy, a country with a predominantly mediterranean and continental climate, climate change, in particular global warming, reduced precipitations and increasing urbanisation may exacerbate the increase in incidence of NTM-PD.
