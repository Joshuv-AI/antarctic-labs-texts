Restorative macrophage-derived RNAseT2 stimulates muscle stem cell fusion via an SLK/N-WASP/actin bundling dependent axis
Authors: Michéle Weiss-Gayet, Gaëtan Juban, Emmeran Le Moal, Antonio Moretta, Camilla Farnetani, Christelle Gobet, Jules Guillemaud, Marie-Catherine Le Bihan, Oded Shoseyov, Annie Adrait, Katharina Ternka, Odile Boespflug-Tanguy, Matthias Kettwig, Yohann Couté, Rémi Mounier, Francesco Acquati, Robert D. Knight, Bénédicte Chazaud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Muscle stem cells (MuSCs) fuse to form myofibers to repair skeletal muscle after injury. Within the regenerative MuSC niche, restorative macrophages stimulate MuSC fusion, although the molecular mechanisms involved are largely unknown. Here, we show that restorative macrophages secrete ribonuclease T2 (RNAseT2) to stimulate MuSC fusion. RNAseT2 enters MuSCs via the mannose receptor and induces the formation of actin bundles in MuSCs, enabling cell/cell fusion. Mechanistically, RNAseT2 binds to Ste20-like kinase (SLK), which itself triggers the phosphorylation-mediated activation of N-WASP, through Paxillin phosphorylation, allowing actin bundling necessary for MuSC fusion. In vivo, overexpressing RNAseT2 in regenerating muscle increases fusion in newly formed myofibers in mouse and zebrafish while macrophages deficient for RNAseT2 gene lead to fusion defect and smaller myofibers. This study reveals a new function for the highly conserved RNAseT2 and provides a new molecular mechanism by which restorative macrophages support MuSC fusion during muscle repair.

Introduction

Muscle stem cells (MuSCs) fuse to form multinucleated cells to repair skeletal muscle after a lesion1. Adult MuSCs express a cell autonomous myogenesis program, but experimental manipulations in vivo have revealed that the environment is required for efficient muscle regeneration1,2. Macrophages support myogenesis and muscle regeneration by promoting MuSC proliferation through inducing a pro-inflammatory environment, then transitioning to resolution and promoting an anti-inflammatory/restorative inflammatory profile, which stimulates MuSC differentiation and fusion2,3. A number of molecular effectors from macrophages have been shown to regulate the various steps of myogenesis. Indeed, several molecules secreted by pro-inflammatory macrophages stimulate MuSC proliferation, including TNFα, Interleukin (IL)−6, NAMPT, IL1-β and VEGF4–6. However, the molecular mechanisms by which restorative macrophages stimulate MuSC fusion are still largely unknown.

Using human cells, we have previously shown that culturing MuSCs with conditioned medium from restorative macrophages promotes the differentiation and fusion of MuSCs as efficiently as direct cocultures5. This suggests that the main effects of restorative macrophages on the terminal steps of myogenesis are triggered by secreted factors. It was shown that at the time of the resolution of inflammation, restorative macrophages secrete Insulin-Growth Factor (IGF)−1 and Oncostatin M that stimulate MuSC differentiation and fusion7,8, and GDF3 that stimulates MuSC fusion9. The role of TGFβ is more complex, since both pro- and anti-fusogenic effects have been reported in various in vitro and in vivo set ups5,10, likely due to the complexity of its activation. To identify factors secreted by restorative macrophages and, therefore, potentially involved in MuSC fusion, we performed a screen for molecules that are differentially secreted by pro- and anti-inflammatory human macrophages in vitro.

In this study, we show that ribonuclease T2 (RNAseT2) is expressed in restorative macrophages in vitro and anti-inflammatory macrophages in vivo in regenerating mouse muscle. RNAseT2 secreted by anti-inflammatory macrophages specifically stimulated MuSC fusion without affecting proliferation and differentiation. RNAseT2 is taken up by MuSCs via the mannose receptor and stimulates fusion through stimulation of actin remodeling and promoting increased actin bundling. We identified Ste20-like kinase (SLK) as a direct partner of RNAseT2 that triggered the phosphorylation and activation of N-WASP, a major effector of actin bundling and necessary for MuSC fusion11,12. The ability of RNAseT2 to regulate MuSC fusion was assessed in vivo in mouse and zebrafish models of skeletal muscle regeneration and was shown to induce elevated fusion. In humans, macrophages derived from patients carrying a mutation that results in the absence of RNAseT2 were not able to stimulate MuSC fusion as compared with normal macrophages.

Results

RNAseT2 is expressed by restorative macrophages and boosts MuSC fusion

A differential transcriptomic analysis was performed using human macrophages activated in vitro into pro-inflammatory (LPS-treated), alternatively activated (IL-4 treated) or anti-inflammatory macrophages (IL-10 and dexamethasone-treated) and filtered for the genes encoding secreted proteins (Fig. S1A, Supplementary Data 1). We previously showed that these macrophages exert differential effects on human MuSC proliferation, differentiation and fusion, with alternatively activated and anti-inflammatory macrophages stimulating their fusion3,5. Among the molecules expressed strongly in anti-inflammatory macrophages and predicted to be secreted (Cluster 6 in Supplementary Data 1) was Ribonuclease T2 (RNAseT2). Increased expression of RNAseT2 in anti-inflammatory macrophages relative to pro-inflammatory macrophages was confirmed by RTqPCR (Fig. S1B) and immunoblotting (Fig. S1C). RNAseT2 was previously identified in the human MuSC secretome during differentiation in vitro13. However, its secretion was minimal, representing only 0.1% of the total secretome, and was still secreted at low levels during differentiation (Fig. S1D). This was confirmed by immunoblotting showing that both undifferentiated and differentiated MuSCs expressed low levels of RNAseT2, comparable to that observed in pro-inflammatory macrophages, and 2.7 to six-fold less than anti-inflammatory macrophages (Fig. S1C). In vivo, western blot analysis of whole muscle extracts showed a concomitant increase in RNAseT2 expression and the profusogenic marker Myomaker (Fig. S1E). Analysis of inflammatory (Ly6Cpos) and restorative (Ly6Cneg) macrophages present in regenerating skeletal muscle 4 days after injury indicated that RNAseT2 was mainly expressed by restorative macrophages (Fig. 1A), which were in the vicinity of regenerating myofibers (Fig. S1F). At day 8 post-injury, a time point when mainly restorative macrophages are present14, RNAseT2-expressing cells were all positive for the pan-macrophage marker CD64 while the vast majority of macrophages (CD64pos) were positive for RNAseT2 (Fig. 1B). RNAseT2 belongs to the transferase-type Rh/T2/s Ribonuclease family and is highly conserved among eukaryotic and prokaryotic species17. It has 2 catalytic sites and 3 N-glycosylation sites17. To investigate the role of RNAseT2, we first used a fungal ortholog of RNAseT2, Actibind, extracted from Aspergillus niger18. Actibind protein was applied to human primary MuSCs cultured in vitro. Actibind had no impact on primary MuSC proliferation (ki67 labeling) (Fig. S1G) or differentiation (myogenin expression) (Fig. S1H), but it stimulated primary MuSC fusion from 1 μM (Fig. 1C). Similar results were obtained with human immortalized MuSCs (hereafter named MuSCs)19 when exposed to recombinant human RNAseT2, with fusion increased by +32% (Fig. 1D) but no impact on proliferation (Fig. S1I).

To demonstrate that RNAseT2 derived from macrophages was the source of increased cell fusion, we first treated MuSCs with conditioned medium from the macrophage cell line THP1 and from an established RNAseT2-deficient THP1 cell line20. MuSC fusion was decreased by −31% when macrophages were deficient for RNAseT2 (Fig. 1E). We also inhibited RNAseT2 expression using siRNAs (which reduced RNAseT2 expression by −74%, Fig. S1J) on human primary macrophages, activated to assume a pro- or anti-inflammatory state. As expected, conditioned medium from pro-inflammatory macrophages did not stimulate primary MuSC differentiation (Fig. S1K) nor fusion (Fig. 1F) and silencing RNAseT2 had no effect. In contrast, conditioned medium from anti-inflammatory macrophages stimulated primary MuSC fusion (+ 162%), in line with previous studies3,5. Silencing RNAseT2 in anti-inflammatory macrophages abolished their pro-fusogenic effect by −38% (Fig. 1F). We did not observe any effect of silencing RNAseT2 on the capacities of anti-inflammatory macrophages to stimulate primary MuSC differentiation (Fig. S1K).

To confirm the physiological relevance of macrophage-derived RNAseT2 for MuSC fusion, we compared the expression of RNAseT2 and the fusogenic properties of Ly6Cneg macrophages in various conditions of muscle regeneration in mouse. Ly6Cneg macrophages were isolated from day 4 post-injury regenerating muscle, where these restorative macrophages are known to promote MuSC fusion, from fib-mdx muscle, where chronic inflammation is established and macrophages exhibit a pro-inflammatory profile, and from the muscle of fib-mdx mice treated with metformin, which promotes the shift of macrophages towards a restorative profile, regenerative inflammation and improves tissue repair21. Macrophages from fib-mdx mice showed both reduced RNAseT2 expression (−52%) and fusogenic capacity (−30%) as compared with macrophages isolated from regenerating muscle (Fig. S1L), and the two parameters were rescued by metformin treatment (Fig. S1L). These results reveal that RNAseT2 released by anti-inflammatory macrophages has a specific stimulatory effect on MuSC fusion.

RNAseT2 enters MuSCs via the mannose receptor

Omega-1, an orthologue of RNAseT2 from Schistosoma mansoni, is the major component of the parasite’s eggs and is responsible for Th2 polarization of dendritic cells through the mannose receptor (CD206) expressed at their surface22. In mouse MuSCs, the mannose receptor is increasingly expressed during differentiation and is involved in their fusion23. We found that human primary MuSCs expressed the mannose receptor (hMRC1) during their differentiation (Fig. S2A, B). To block recognition of RNAseT2 by the mannose receptor, we treated RNAseT2 with Peptide:N-Glycosidase F, resulting in a partial deglycosylation of the protein (shown in Fig. S2C). Deglycosylated RNAseT2 was 27% less efficient than the native protein to promote MuSC fusion (Fig. 1G). Moreover, we performed competition experiments in which allyl-α-D-mannopyranoside, a specific mannose receptor ligand, was added at increasing concentrations to the MuSC cultures. While mannopyranoside had no impact on untreated MuSCs, it blunted the stimulating effect of RNAseT2 on their fusion up to −21% for the highest concentration (Fig. 1H).

To investigate the intracellular route of RNAseT2, we treated MuSCs with recombinant RNAseT2 at 4 °C and then switched the cells to 37 °C to allow endocytosis. As exogenous RNAseT2 was His-tagged, it was labeled with anti-His antibody, which showed a non-specific labeling in the nucleus but no staining in the cytoplasm in untreated cells (Fig. S2D). After 15 min, exogenous RNAseT2 was frequently colocalized with the early endosomal marker EEA1 (Fig. S2E, F), as well as with the late endosomal marker Rab7 (Fig. S2E, G). Very low colocalization was observed with the lysosomal marker LAMP1 (Fig. S2E, H). Forty-five minutes later, no localization of RNAseT2 was observed in endosomes or lysosomes (Fig. S2I–K), suggesting endosomal escape to reach the cytosol (see discussion section).

These results show that RNAseT2 secreted from anti-inflammatory macrophages can enter MuSCs, at least partially via the mannose receptor, to reach the cytosol to promote their fusion.

RNAseT2 affects actin organization in MuSCs

To investigate gene function during human MuSC fusion, we defined fusion kinetics in MuSCs. Following culture in differentiation medium, cells were fused by 20% at day 1, increasing to 30% and 50% at days 3 and 5, respectively (Fig. S3A). We showed that expression of the fusogen MYOMAKER appeared at day 1 and increased as the cells were fusing, while that of the fusion regulator MYOMIXER remained stable (Fig. S3B). Accordingly, the expression of the fusion inhibitor, the CD9 tetraspanin, decreased with time (Fig. S3B). Based on these observations, we therefore chose day 3 to investigate how RNAseT2 affects MuSC fusion.

Actin remodeling, and particularly cortical actin bundling, is required for myoblast fusion11,24. We then first sought actin remodeling at the cellular level. MuSCs treated with RNAseT2 showed a higher phalloidin intensity in their cytoplasm, indicative of changes in actin organization (Fig. 2A). We further showed that RNAseT2 treatment increased the F/G actin ratio in MuSCs (Fig. S3C), confirming a link between RNAseT2 and actin polymerization. To investigate the relationship between RNAseT2 and actin, we analyzed the behavior of cells treated with recombinant RNAseT2. We observed, at the cellular level, a colocalization of RNAseT2 with actin (Fig. 2B). We investigated whether colocalization of high-intensity actin labeling (top 50% intensity) with high-intensity RNAseT2 labeling (top 50% intensity) in MuSCs was associated with different cell behavior. Using confocal microscopy, we analyzed the actin and RNAseT2 distribution in the ventral part of the cells (within the first micron from the bottom) and observed a colocalization of RNAseT2 with actin (Fig. 2C). We found that actin intensity was 2.6-fold higher when it colocalized with RNAseT2 in the cells than in areas where RNAseT2 was absent (Fig. 2D), suggesting higher polymerization of actin when it colocalized with RNAseT2. The link between RNAseT2 and actin was confirmed in higher magnification confocal imaging of cells treated with RNAseT2, which showed that RNAseT2 within the cells was preferentially localized and concentrated where actin intensity was higher, particularly at the edges of the cells (Figs. 2E, S3D). Altogether, these results show that RNAseT2 modulates actin remodeling towards polymerization, that RNAseT2 co-localizes with actin at sites of polymerization, and this is associated with increased fusion of human MuSCs.

RNAseT2 promotes actin reorganization via binding to SLK

Previous studies have shown that RNAseT2 may directly bind actin25,26. To identify RNAseT2 binding partners in myoblasts, and because macrophages are difficult to transfect, we used the C2C12 myogenic cell line and transfected them with a plasmid encoding an RNAseT2-GFP fusion protein (or a GFP-coding plasmid)27. Co-immunoprecipitation using a GFP-TRAP revealed that RNAseT2 did not co-immunoprecipitate with actin (Fig. S4A). This indicates that RNAseT2 action on actin is indirect. To identify the binding partners of RNAseT2 in C2C12 cells, we therefore analyzed co-immunoprecipitation eluates using mass spectrometry (MS)-based quantitative proteomics. Five proteins were found significantly enriched with RNAseT2 (Supplementary Data 2, Fig. S4B). Among them, SLK STE20-like serine/threonine-protein kinase was deemed an interesting candidate, since some of its functions relate to actin organization28,29 and myogenic cell fusion30,31.

In a functional assay, silencing of SLK (using siRNA, which decreased the SLK expression by 55% in MuSCs, Fig. S4D) blunted the effect of RNAseT2 in enhancing MuSC fusion (Fig. 3B). In smooth muscle cells, SLK has been shown to indirectly activate N-WASP via its phosphorylation32. N-WASP is a major actor in actin remodeling at the time of MuSC fusion33,34. We therefore tested the effect of silencing SLK in MuSCs and found this resulted in a decrease in both the area (−27%) and number (−40%) of phospho-N-WASP clusters at the tips of the cells after RNAseT2 treatment relative to control RNAseT2 treated cells (Fig. 3C). Eventually, in the same conditions, silencing SLK in RNAseT2 treated MuSCs decreased the area of actin filaments back to the control value (Fig. 3D). SLK being shown to promote paxillin phosphorylation, resulting in actin polymerization via N-WASP phosphorylation and activation32, we silenced paxillin in MuSCs. Similarly, the increase in the number of phospho-N-WASP and actin bundling area observed upon RNAseT2 treatment was blunted in the absence of paxillin (Fig. 3E, F). Finally, we observed an increase of Paxillin phosphorylation (Ser-272) at the tip of actin filaments at the edges of the cells upon RNAseT2 treatment, that was decreased when SLK was silenced in the cells (Fig. 3G). We confirmed by western blot that paxillin phosphorylation was increased upon RNAseT2 treatment in muscle cells (Fig. S4E). These results show that RNAseT2 interacts with SLK to promote N-WASP phosphorylation, via paxillin, triggering actin bundling and MuSC fusion.

To explore the chemical interactions of SLK with RNAseT2, the direct docking of SLK and RNAseT2 was evaluated using the ClusPro 2.0 webserver (Fig. 3H). The docking analysis, suggesting a direct interaction between RNAseT2 and SLK, revealed the occurrence of 7 salt bridges, 25 hydrogen bonds and 336 non-covalent interactions between the partners (Fig. S4F). Most identified interactions involved 3 specific regions of the SLK protein: 333–349 and 699–723 (2 regions of the protein containing polar residues), and 957–1171 (ATH Domain, previously shown to be involved in the regulation of SLK functions29,35). As shown in Fig. S4G, the 3D structure analysis revealed a potential interaction of the ATH domain with the C-terminus of the RNAseT2 protein. This suggests the existence of tight and multiple chemical interactions between the two proteins. Moreover, the 3D structure analysis revealed that the RNAseT2 pocket (the active site) can perfectly accept and interact with a loop of the SLK protein (309–351 disordered region) (Figs. 3I, S4H). The 63–69 β-strand and the 107–124 α-helix contained the residues crucial for the ribonuclease activity of RNAseT2: His65, Glu114, and His118. Strikingly, the docking analysis suggested that mutation of His65 and His118 in the RNAseT2 catalytic site, as we previously showed27, could alter its interaction with SLK. Indeed, the molecular docking analysis performed between SLK and mutated RNAseT2 revealed that the regions of the SLK protein involved in the complex formation are disordered regions: 394–406, 448–471, 598–612 (Fig. 3J). Most importantly, the interactions occurring at the protein-protein interface did not involve the active site of RNAseT2 in the mutant protein (Fig. 3K) when compared with the WT (Fig. 3I). Moreover, we immunoprecipitated mutated RNAseT2-GFP27 (produced in C2C12 cells) and we observed a 75% decrease of co-immunoprecipitated SLK as compared with WT-RNAseT2-GFP (Fig. 3L), indicating that the interactions between the 2 proteins were lost in the mutant. Finally, to address the functionality of this interaction, we showed in vitro using a kinase assay that RNAseT2 (either recombinant or purified from C2C12 transfected cells) stimulated the kinase activity of SLK, while the mutated form of RNAseT2 was much less efficient (Fig. 3M). Adding RNAses to the assay did not alter SLK activation, discarding potential involvement of RNAs in SLK activation (Fig. S4I). Altogether, these results suggest that RNAseT2 exerts its fusogenic activity through binding to and activation of SLK, which induces Paxillin phosphorylation, which in turn, phosphorylates/activates N-WASP. This last step induces actin bundling necessary for fusion.

Macrophage-secreted RNAseT2 stimulates MuSC fusion in vivo

To demonstrate that macrophage-derived RNAseT2 affects MuSC fusion in vivo and ex vivo, we evaluated its function in mouse, zebrafish and human.

To understand how MuSCs respond to secretion of RNAseT2 in macrophages in vivo, transgenic zebrafish over-expressing RNAseT2 under the control of a macrophage-specific promoter were injured, and MuSC behavior was observed using a pax7a:egfp transgene as previously described36. Zebrafish rnaset2 was cloned into an expression construct with a UAS driver (Fig. S5A). A stable transgenic line was established using the mpeg:gal4 driver to over-express dUAS:rnaset2_dsRed in macrophages (Fig. S5B). We then electroporated regenerating muscles of mice with RNAseT2 plasmids. We confirmed in vitro the profusogenic activity of RNAseT2-GFP produced by C2C12, which was not observed in the mutated form (Fig. S5E). Electroporation, which induces a mild muscle injury as compared with cardiotoxin38, is done in a post-injury regenerating muscle. It was shown that various muscle mononucleated cells may incorporate the plasmid39. Being a secreted molecule, RNAseT2 will be released in the regenerating milieu of the muscle, whatever the cell type that has incorporated the plasmid40. Electroporation of RNAseT2 5 days after the injury increased the amount of RNAseT2 in the regenerating muscle (Fig. S5F). This led to an increase of the number of nuclei per myofiber 4 days later with the appearance of myofiber sections harboring 4 or more nuclei and an increase of the size of regenerating myofibers (Fig. 4C). When the RNAseT2 mutant for the active site was electroporated in the same conditions, no effect was observed on the number of myonuclei per myofiber (Fig. 4C). This accords with the in vitro results above indicating that the link with SLK is reduced in the mutant version of RNAseT2.

We then performed loss-of-function experiments by transplanting bone marrow from Rnaset2-/- mice into WT recipients. As 75% of immune cells are macrophages in the regenerating muscle, this strategy is an acceptable surrogate for investigating the role of macrophages in muscle regeneration9,41. Irradiation delaying the process of regeneration9,42, muscles were analyzed 14 days after injury. Deficiency of RNAseT2 in immune cells led to a decrease of the number of nuclei/myofiber, indicative of a fusion defect, associated with a decreased cross section area of the new myofibers (Fig. 4D). Finally, to test whether RNAseT2 has a similar function in humans, we obtained macrophages from a human patient exhibiting a homozygous mutation in the RNAseT2 gene, triggering the deletion of the initiating codon and that of 5 exons (Fig. 4E). Conditioned medium from these macrophages did not sustain the increase of MuSC fusion observed with macrophages coming from an age-matched healthy person or with macrophages bearing one mutated RNAseT2 allele (Fig. 4E).

These examples from three different species confirmed that RNAseT2 delivered by restorative macrophages boosts MuSC fusion during skeletal muscle regeneration.

Discussion

Although MuSCs are very effective at regenerating muscle after injury, their in vivo environment is critical for their efficient function. A variety of cell types sustain the sequential steps of adult myogenesis1. Among them, restorative macrophages promote the last steps of myogenesis, including fusion3,5. The molecular mechanisms underlying the profusogenic effect of restorative macrophages on MuSCs are still elusive, and only a few factors have been investigated. Interleukin 4 has been shown to stimulate MuSC fusion in mouse43, but its secretion by restorative macrophages is still uncertain44. On the other way around, macrophage-secreted insulin-like growth factor-1 is necessary for myofiber formation45,46, but its direct role in fusion is not demonstrated yet. In coculture experiments using human cells, we identified macrophage-secreted low concentrations of TNFα and TGFβ impacting positively MuSC fusion5. We have previously shown that restorative macrophages upregulate the expression of the metabolic regulator PPARγ, which induces the expression of the growth factor GDF3 that itself directly stimulates MuSC fusion9.

In the present study, we show evidence for a new mechanism by which restorative macrophages stimulate MuSC fusion. They secrete the glycoprotein RNAseT2, which enters, at least in part, into MuSCs via the mannose receptor. In the cell, RNAseT2 binds to SLK, induces Paxillin phosphorylation, promoting in turn the phosphorylation of N-WASP, actin polymerization and actin bundling, which are necessary for MuSC fusion. We demonstrate in vivo the beneficial effects of RNAseT2 on MuSC fusion during muscle regeneration in two animal models and highlight how macrophages from human patients with RNASET2 mutations lack this ability, highlighting its conserved function in vertebrates.

The mannose receptor constantly shuttles between the membrane and the early endosomal compartment, whether bound to a ligand or not. Once in the early endosomes, the slightly acidic environment leads to the dissociation of the mannose receptor from its ligand. This later, depending on its nature and on a variety of complex regulatory processes, may pursue until the lysosomal compartment for degradation (collagens, pathogens) or may enter into the cytosol (toxins, antigens). Thus, there is an uncoupling between ligand uptake and its processing47. RNAseT2 has previously been reported to act in the lysosomal compartment of human macrophages as a ribonuclease acting upstream of TLR8, which senses RNAseT2-produced RNA degradation products involved in the innate immune response, suggesting that there is no need to escape the lysosomal compartment48. The translocation pathway has also been investigated in the antigen cross-presentation process, where the mannose receptor prevents the fusion of early endosomes with lysosomes, a process controlled by its own ubiquitination49. Due to a technical issue, we were unable to track RNAseT2-GFP to investigate its fate in MuSCs upon internalization. Given that RNAseT2 is mainly found in a cytosolic localization in several cell types and tissues (https://www.proteinatlas.org), and that it binds to the cytosolic SLK, it is tempting to suggest that a similar cytoplasmic translocation process allows RNAseT2 to access the cytosol where it interacts with its SLK target.

RNAseT2 is highly conserved among prokaryotes and eukaryotic species, including plants and animals. This secreted glycoprotein has been involved in a variety of cellular processes depending on its localization and the cell type involved, and was also shown to exert both structural (non-enzymatic) and enzymatic50 functions. RNAseT2 has been associated with actin remodeling in a variety of cell types, and particularly subcortical actin polymerization51–53. Here, we show that in MuSCs, RNAseT2 does not directly link to actin, but is associated with SLK. It was shown that in muscle cells, SLK expression and phosphorylation status monitor subcortical actin remodeling29. The ATH domain of SLK is involved in actin reorganization29 and regulates SLK function through protein binding35. Here, the model suggests that RNAseT2 interacts with SLK through the ATH domain in MuSCs. However, it also indicates that the active site of RNAseT2 is involved in binding with SLK, and therefore, SLK activity. Indeed, a mutated form of RNAseT2, devoid of its active site, decreased its interactions with SLK by two-fold and showed less activation of SLK than the active form. In smooth muscle cells, siRNA knockdown of SLK reduces the F/G actin ratio32. SLK also promotes paxillin phosphorylation in smooth muscle cells and fibroblasts32,54, resulting in actin polymerization via N-WASP phosphorylation and activation32. N-WASP is an important regulator of actin organization by controlling Arp2/3-dependent actin branching and is necessary for MuSC fusion33,34. In skeletal muscle, SLK expression increases during MuSC differentiation; inactive SLK or SLK deletion prevents the formation of myotubes, associated with alteration of paxillin distribution30,31. This suggests a mechanism by which RNAseT2 binding to SLK results in actin polymerization, necessary for myoblast fusion. We showed that SLK, via paxillin, is indeed required for RNAseT2 stimulation of N-WASP phosphorylation and of actin polymerization and bundling in human MuSCs, resulting in cell fusion. Macrophages bearing a homozygous genetic deficiency of RNASET2, which causes leukoencephalopathy55, were not capable of stimulating MuSC fusion. In vivo, the presence of RNAseT2, but not of the mutated form, in regenerating muscle stimulated MuSC fusion in both mouse and zebrafish. Tissue-specific expression of RNAseT2 by macrophages in zebrafish resulted in a similar increase in the fusion rate, indicating that RNAseT2 released by macrophages can regulate MuSC fusion in vivo. Inversely, RNAseT2-deficient macrophages are defective in providing an adequate profusogenic niche to MuSCs at the time of myofiber formation.

We here report a preferential expression of RNAseT2 in restorative macrophages in situ, and its expression directly correlated with their profusogenic properties. Previous reports have highlighted the complexity of the link between RNAseT2 and inflammation. RNAseT2 modulates the inflammatory status of macrophages in vitro20, is involved in intracellular degradation of ssRNAs upon infection, leading to TLR8 activation in macrophages48, but supports dendritic cell skewing toward a Th2 profile22 and promotes an inflammatory response necessary for tissue remodeling in the medicinal leech Hirudo verbana56. The present study did not explore how RNAseT2 may affect the programming of immune cells, but has explored the impact of secreted RNAseT2 on MuSC fusion.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates a new role for the highly conserved RNAseT2 that acts exogenously to regulate MuSC fusion through a cascade of phosphorylation controlling actin bundling, thus promoting tissue repair.

Methods

The research described here complies with all relevant ethical regulations. For human cell use, agreement from the Ministry of Research Codecoh DC-2022-5055; for animal experiments, protocols were approved by the ethics committee and the French Ministry of Research (#49217, #10463, #48604).

Cell culture

Human primary MuSCs

Human primary MuSCs were isolated from an adult skeletal muscle sample according to the French legislation (DC-2022–5055). Briefly, cells were obtained and cultured as previously described5 in HAMF12 medium (#31765027 Life Technologies) containing 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (proliferating medium) and were sorted with anti-CD56 magnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotec #130-050-401) according to the manufacturer instructions. For proliferation experiments, MuSCs were seeded at 3000 cells/cm2 in proliferating medium with or without Actibind18 for one day, and Ki67 immunolabelling was performed. For differentiation or fusion experiments, MuSCs were cultured in differentiating medium (HAMF12, 5% FBS) at 1000 and 5000 cells/cm2, respectively, with or without Actibind or with macrophage-conditioned medium for 3 days before immunolabeling for myogenin and desmin, respectively. After 4 passages, cells were destroyed if not used.

Human immortalized MuSCs

MuSCs derived from healthy donors19 were cultured in proliferating medium (skeletal muscle basal medium containing supplemental mix (PromoCell GmbH #39365 and #C23260) and 10% FBS. For proliferation experiments, MuSCs were seeded at 3000 cells/cm2 in proliferating medium with or without 5 µg/ml recRNAseT2 (#92–334 VWR) for 24 h and 10 μM EdU was added for 6 h before revelation as described by the manufacturer (#C10350 InVitrogen). For fusion experiments, cells were seeded at 30000 cells/cm2 in differentiating medium (skeletal muscle basal medium containing insulin 10 µg/ml (Sigma-Aldrich #I2643), transferrin 10 µg/ml (Sigma-Aldrich #T8158), with or without 5 µg/ml recRNAseT2 for 3 days before immunostaining was processed. In some experiments, MuSCs were treated with allyl-α-D-mannopyranoside (Sigma-Aldrich #772208). In other experiments, cells were treated overnight with 2 nM siRNA SLK (Qiagen, mix of # SI02224551-Hs SLK-6 and SI02224544 Hs SLK-5) or siRNA Paxillin (Qiagen, mix of # SI04777549 Hs PXN-12 and SI04777542 Hs PXN-11) using RNAiMax lipofectamine (Invitrogen #13778-075) as described by the manufacturer. The day after, the medium containing or not containing recRNAseT2 (5 mg/ml) was added to the cells. Two days after transfection, the cells were harvested to assess the efficacy of siRNA by Western blot.

RNAseT2 uptake

Cells were washed with cold PBS and placed on ice for 10 min in differentiating medium and were incubated with recRNAseT2 protein (5 μg/ml) during 1 h on ice. They were then washed and placed into differentiating medium at 37 °C during 15 or 60 min before immunostaining.

Primary human macrophages

Healthy human blood monocytes (obtained from blood from Etablissement Français du Sang) were differentiated into macrophages as described in5 and plated in 12-well plates at 180000 cells per well in complete advanced RPMI medium (Gibco #12633, 1% glutamax, 10 mM Hepes, 1X MEM vitamins, 50 µM β2-mercaptoethanol and 0.5% FBS). Cells were activated into pro-inflammatory, alternatively activated or anti-inflammatory using LPS and IFNγ, IL-4 and IL-10 and Dexamethasone, respectively, as described in5. In some experiments, siRNA RNAseT2 (Qiagen # SI04163012 Hs RNAseT2-5) was transfected using RNAiMax lipofectamine (Invitrogen #13778-075) as described by the manufacturer. After 2 days, conditioned medium was prepared in Advanced RPMI medium containing 0.5% FBS for 24 h, centrifuged at 700 g for 10 min and used for treatment on MuSCs.

RNAseT2-deficient primary macrophages were obtained from a 14-year-old girl with a cystic leukoencephalopathy without megalencephaly (OMIM# 612951) at the reference center for leukodystrophies and rare leukoencephalopathies (LEUKOFRANCE) of the Robert Debré Hospital (Paris, France). Consents were obtained according to the research protocol LeucoEpiMar (Ethical committee autorization AU 788; CNIL 1406552 and AFSSAPSs B90298-60). Genomic DNA analysis of peripheral leukocytes was performed using a leukodystrophic NGS panel (link to: Orphanet: Diagnostic des leucodystrophies Panel) and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Analysis demonstrated two deletions in the RNASET2 gene on both alleles: a large in-frame deletion encompassing exons 2 to 5 and a c.2 deletion affecting the initiation codon. Both parents were heterozygotes for the two deletions (exon 2 to 5 and c.2del).

THP1 human macrophages

WT THP1 or shRNAseT2 THP127 were seeded in 12-well plates at 200000 cells per well in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS and supplemented with 20 ng/ml phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (Sigma-Aldrich #P8139). One day later, the medium was changed, and the day after, cells were activated with IL-10 (10 ng/ml R&D #217-Il/CF) and dexamethasone (80 ng/ml Sigma-Aldrich #D4902) in complete advanced RPMI medium. After 1 day, conditioned medium was prepared in Advanced RPMI medium containing 0.5% FBS for 24 h.

C2C12 cells

C2C12 cells were used to produce WT RNAse-T2 and mutated RNAseT2-GFP. Cells were grown in DMEM supplemented with 20% FBS. They were transfected by pEGFP-N1-hRNAseT2, mutated pEGFP-N1-hRNAseT2-H65/H11827 or control empty pEGFP-N1 (Clontech) plasmids with jetPRIME or jetOPTIMUS Transfection reagent (Polyplus) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and harvested 24 h later for co-immunoprecipitation experiments. For stable transfection, transfected cells were selected for one week with 0.5 mg/ml G418 and GFP+ cells were FACS-sorted on a BD FACSAria II and maintained in culture with 0.5 mg/ml G418 for production of the proteins. RNAseT2-GFP was immunoprecipitated using GFP-Trap™ beads (ChromoTek, Proteintech), with an acidic elution with 200 mM glycine, pH 2.5 and followed by neutralization with 1 M Tris, pH 10.4. Eluted proteins were precipitated with ethanol 100% during 1 h at −20 °C (1 V of proteins/ 9 V of ethanol), and after centrifugation, the pellet was resuspended in 1X PBS for treating MuSCs.

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.5% TritonX100, washed in 1X PBS and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (anti-Ki67 [1:100 Abcam #ab15580], anti-myogenin [1:50 BD Pharmingen #556358]; anti-desmin [1:200 Abcam #ab32362], anti-CD206 [1:200 Abcam #ab64693], anti-phospho-NWASP [1:200 MerckMillipore #AB1966], anti-phospho-Paxillin [1:100 Sigma-Aldrich #SAB4301321], anti-HisTag [1:1000 Abcam # ab18184], anti-EEA1 antibody [1:1000 BD Biosciences #610456], anti-Rab7 antibody [1:100 CellSignaling #9367], anti-Lamp1 antibody [1:400 DSHB #H4A3-c]. In vitro image analyses

For all in vitro analyses, 5 to 15 pictures were randomly taken in each condition for each independent cell culture using, depending on the experiment, an epifluorescence Zeiss Z1 AxioImager, a Zeiss Axio Observer Apotome. 2 microscope or a confocal Leica SP5 X microscope.

Proliferation and differentiation of MuSCs

Proliferation was measured as the number of ki67 or EdUpos cells reported to the total number of cells. Differentiation was measured as the number of myogeninpos cells reported to the total number of cells. 7 to 15 pictures were analyzed for a total of 500-1000 cells counted.

Fusion of MuSCs

The number of nuclei within multinucleated cells (identified as desminpos cells) was counted and reported to the total number of nuclei, defining the fusion index. 5 to 10 pictures were analyzed for a total of 500–1000 nuclei counted.

Actin intensity in the whole cell culture was analyzed using the threshold tool in ImageJ software. 5 to 10 pictures were analyzed for a total of 100–200 cells counted.

Colocalization of RNAseT2 and actin was analyzed using the colocalization plug-in in ImageJ using 150 as the threshold for green and red filter, with a ratio of 50%. Cells exhibiting or not exhibiting actin/RNAseT2 colocalization were compared for i) the distance between the cell and its nearest neighbor, manually quantified from its nucleus to the nearest nucleus (expressed in pixels), and ii) whether the cell is in contact with other cells (expressed as a percentage of cells in contact among all cells in each category). The plug-in was also used to measure actin intensity in the colocalization areas (where RNAseT2 was present) versus other areas in the cell (Fig. 2D). 7–9 pictures were analyzed for a total of 70–90 cells counted.

For PLA labeling, particle number and intensity were measured using the threshold tool in ImageJ software. 7 pictures were analyzed for a total of 50–70 cells counted.

Actin bundling area was analyzed in high magnification confocal pictures after phalloidin labeling in Fiji57 using a home-made macro, that includes a preprocess (background subtraction, contrast enhancement at 0.35, normalization and histogram equalization) and the measurement of objects detected with the Ridge Detection58,59, StarDist60, MorpholibJ61 and CLIJ2 Plugins62. For the filament automatic detections, the macro used the following parameters: line_width = 10; high_contrast = 600; low_contrast = 100; sigma = 3.39; lower_threshold = 0.51; upper_threshold = 3.40. Some code lines of the macro allowed the exclusion of the detected Junction Points in the RoiManager. 5–7 pictures were analyzed for a total of 50–70 cells counted.

Phospho N-WASP dots were quantified manually in each field and reported as the number of cells. 5–10 pictures were analyzed for a total of 50 cells counted.

Phospho-Paxillin dots were also quantified manually and reported to the number of cells, the counting being restricted to the tips of actin filaments at the edges of the cells. 5–10 pictures were analyzed for a total of 100 cells counted.

Colocalization of endocytosed RNAseT2 with organites was done in Fiji using a home-made macro including a threshold for each channel (C1 and C2), analyze particles to make masks, and C1nC2 to measure the intersection areas of the 2 labelings.

Molecular biology

Microarray

After validation of the RNA quality from activated macrophages with Bioanalyzer 2100 (using Agilent RNA6000 nano chip kit), 240 ng of total RNA was reverse transcribed following the GeneChip® WT2 Reagent Kit (Affymetrix). Briefly, the resulting double-strand cDNA was used for in vitro transcription with T7 RNA polymerase. After purification according to Affymetrix protocol, 5.5 μg of Sens Target DNA were fragmented and biotin labeled. After control of fragmentation using Bioanalyzer 2100, cDNA was then hybridized to GeneChip® HumanGene1.0ST (Affymetrix) at 45 °C for 17 h. After overnight hybridization, chips were washed on the fluidic station FS450 following specific protocols (Affymetrix) and scanned using the GCS3000 7 G. The scanned images were then analyzed with Expression Console software (Affymetrix) to obtain raw data (cel files) and metrics for Quality Controls. Data were normalized using RMA with Partek Genomics Suite.

RT-qPCR

RNA extraction was performed with the Nucleospin RNA Plus XS kit (Macherey-Nagel, #740990.250). RNAs were retro-transcribed into cDNA using Superscript II Reverse Transcriptase, and qPCR was carried out in triplicate on a CFX Connect™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Reaction mixtures had a final volume of 10 μl, consisting of 2 μl of cDNA, 5 μl of LightCycler® 480 SYBR Green I Master and 0.5 μM primers. After initial denaturation, amplification was performed at 95 °C (10 s), 60 °C (5 s) and 72 °C (10 s) for 45 cycles. Calculation of relative expression was determined by the Bio-Rad CFX Manager™ software, and fold change was normalized as Normalized Relative Quantity (or ΔΔCq) for each series, as NRQ=2ΔCqT−CqCal2/ΔCqR−CqCal, where T is the target sample, Cal the calibrator value (i.e., the mean of all sample Cqs of the series) and R is the housekeeping gene S14 for human macrophages or Ap3d1 for MuSCs. Log2-NRQ values were used to perform statistical analyses. Primer details are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Biochemistry

RNAseT2 deglycosylation

RecRNAseT2 (10 mg) was deglycosylated by incubation with PNGaseF (10 Units, Sigma-Aldrich #G5166) in 50 µl of 1X PBS for 72 h at 37 °C. Proteins, deglycosylated or not, were resolved by SDS-PAGE and subjected to immunoblot analysis using an anti RNAseT2 antibody (1:1000 SigmaAldrich # SAB3501006). The ratio short form/long form was quantified using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories).

F/G actin ratio was determined using the G-actin/F-actin in vivo assay kit (Cytoskeleton #BK037). In brief, after 3 days in differentiation medium with or without recRNAseT2 protein, MuSCs were lyzed in lysis buffer (Cytoskeleton #LAS01) and centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min. Supernatants were centrifuged at 100000 g for 1 h at 37 °C, which segregated F-actin in the pellet from G-actin in the supernatant. The F-actin-containing pellet was solubilized in F-actin depolymerization buffer at a volume equal to that of the G-actin-containing supernatant. Equivalent volumes of supernatant and pellet were resolved by SDS-PAGE and subjected to immunoblot analysis using an anti-pan-actin antibody (Cytoskeleton #AAN01). The F/G-actin ratio was quantified using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories).

Molecular docking analysis

RNAseT2 and SLK 3D structure models were obtained from the UniProt Database (UniProt Consortium, 2019), which are modeled by the AlphaFold software63. The models in.pdb format were exploited to perform the molecular docking analysis using the ClusPro 2.0 webserver64. The PDBsum webserver65,66 was used to identify all the interactions occurring at the protein-protein interface. In the RNAseT2 sequence, the His65 and His118 residues were substituted with a Phenylalanine residue (His65Phe, His118Phe). The 3D structure of the mutated proteins was generated with the I-TASSER webserver67. Figures were generated through the UCSF CHIMERA molecular graphics software68.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Transfected C2C12 cells (for the expression of WT RNAseT2-GFP or mutated RNAseT2-GFP) were transferred on ice and washed twice in cold PBS containing 0.2 mM PMSF. Cells were lysed by scraping in cold Lysis buffer (10 mM Tris pH 7.5 / 150 mM NaCl / 0.5 mM EDTA / 0.5% IGEPAL-CA630 / 1 mM PMSF / 1 mM B-glycerophosphate / 1 mM Na pyrophosphate / 1 mM Na Orthovanadate / 1% Protease inhibitor (Sigma #P8340)) and transferred into a cold 1.5 ml tube. After a 30 min incubation on ice, samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 13200 rpm at 4 °C. Supernatant was transferred into a new cold 1.5 ml tube and diluted with 1.5 ml Dilution Buffer (10 mM Tris pH 7.5 / 150 mM NaCl / 0.5 mM EDTA / 1 mM PMSF / 1 mM B-glycerophosphate / 1 mM Na pyrophosphate / 1 mM Na Orthovanadate / 1% Protease inhibitor (Sigma #P8340)). Proteins were quantified using Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermofisher). For immunoprecipitation, protein extracts (0.5 mg for western blot analysis or 4 mg for mass spectrometry analysis) were first pre-cleared with 20 or 80 μL of Chromotek Binding Control Agarose Beads (Proteintech) for 30 min at 4 °C on a rotating wheel. In order to pull down GFP or RNASET2-GFP fusion proteins, pre-cleared extracts were incubated with 20 or 80 μl of Chromotek GFP-Trap Agarose Beads (Proteintech) or Chromotek Binding Control Agarose Beads (Proteintech) for 1 h at 4 °C on a rotating wheel. For SLK pulldown, 1 mg of total proteins was incubated with Protein A Dynabeads (Invitrogen) coupled to 10 µg of anti-SLK antibody (Abcam #ab226986). Beads were washed 3 times in Dilution Buffer for 10 min at 4 °C on a rotating wheel, and proteins were eluted in 2X Laemmli buffer. Immunoprecipitation efficiency was verified by silver stain and Western blot after SDS-PAGE. Immunoprecipitated proteins were processed for immunoblotting or mass spectrometry.

Western-blot

Cells were harvested and incubated in a lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5; 150 mM NaCl; 0.5 mM EDTA; 1 mM PMSF; 1 mM B glycerophosphate; 1 mM Na Pyrophosphate; 1 mM Na orthovanadate; 1% protease inhibitor) during 30 min on ice. Protein extracts were obtained after centrifugation at 17000 g during 5 min. For muscle protein extracts, TA muscles were collected at different time points post-injury, minced and diluted in cold Lysis buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 7.5 / 150 mM NaCl / 0.5 mM EDTA / 0.5% IGEPAL-CA630 / 1 mM PMSF / 1 mM B-glycerophosphate / 1 mM Na pyrophosphate / 1 mM Na Orthovanadate / 1% Protease inhibitor (Sigma #P8340)). They were transferred into Lysing Matrix D tubes (MP Biomedicals) and lysed on a Precellys Evolution Homogenizer (Bertin Technologies; 3 cycles of 15 s at 18000 g with 30 s pause intervals at 5 °C). Muscle debris was removed by a 14000 g centrifugation for 10 min at 4 °C.

Proteins from the cells or from immunoprecipitation preparations were resolved by SDS-PAGE and subjected to immunoblot analysis using an anti-beta-actin antibody (1:2000, Sigma-aldrich #A2228), anti-tubulin antibody (1:5000 Sigma-Aldrich #T4026), anti SLK antibody (1:1000, Abcam #ab226986), anti-RNAseT2 antibody (1:1000 Sigma-Aldrich SAB3501006), anti-Myomixer antibody (1:500 R&D Systems #AF4580), anti-Paxillin antibody (1:10000 BD Biosciences #610052), anti-phospho-Paxillin antibody (1:500 Sigma #SAB4301321) or anti-GFP antibody (1:1000 Roche #11814460001). The antibodies are diluted in TBS-Tween 0.1% containing 5% of fat free milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C, then secondary antibodies (anti mouse HRP [1/10000 SigmaAldrich #A9044] or anti rabbit HRP [1/10000 SigmaAldrich #A9044]) were incubated 1 h at room temperature. Detection of proteins was performed with Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Thermo Scientific #32109) as described by the manufacturer. The intensity of the bands was quantified using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories). For RNAseT2 quantification in macrophages and myogenic cells (Fig. S1C), both bands (previously shown69,70 and likely due to various levels of glycosylation) where taken into account. For RNAseT2 and Myomixer quantification during muscle regeneration (Fig. S1E), 2,2,2-Trichloroethanol (Sigma #T54801) was incorporated into the gel. RNAseT2 and Myomixer protein levels were normalized to the total protein level in each lane. In the RNAseT2 pulldown experiments (Fig. S3C), the ratio of the SLK signal in IP vs input was normalized by the ratio of RNAseT2 signal in IP vs input, after subtraction of the local background for each lane. For quantification of phospho-Paxillin levels (Fig. S4E), the phospho-Paxillin/total Paxillin ratio was determined after normalization by beta-actin level.

Mass spectrometry (MS)-based proteomic analyses

Above described co-immunoprecipitated protein samples (3 samples of each condition) were solubilized in Laemmli buffer were stacked at the top of a 4-12% NuPAGE gel (Invitrogen), stained with Coomassie blue R-250 (Bio-Rad) before in-gel digestion using modified trypsin (Promega, sequencing grade) as previously described71. The resulting peptides were analyzed by online nanoliquid chromatography coupled to MS/MS (Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano and Q-Exactive HF, Thermo Fisher Scientific) using 120 min acetonitrile gradient. For this purpose, the peptides were sampled on a precolumn (300 μm x 5 mm PepMap C18, Thermo Scientific) and separated in a 75 μm x 250 mm C18 column (Reprosil-Pur 120 C18-AQ, 1.9 μm, Dr. Maisch). The MS and MS/MS data were acquired using Xcalibur 2.8 (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Peptides and proteins were identified by Mascot (version 2.7.0.1, Matrix Science) through concomitant searches against the Uniprot database (Mus musculus taxonomy, September 2020 version), a homemade database containing the sequences of classical contaminant proteins found in proteomic analyses (human keratins, trypsin…), and the corresponding reversed databases. Trypsin/P was chosen as the enzyme, and two missed cleavages were allowed. Precursor and fragment mass error tolerances were set at 10 and 20 ppm, respectively. Peptide modifications allowed during the search were: Carbamidomethyl (C, fixed), Acetyl (Protein N-term, variable) and Oxidation (M, variable). The Proline software (72, version 2.1.0) was used for the compilation, grouping, and filtering of the results: conservation of rank 1 peptides, peptide score ≥ 25, peptide length ≥ 7 amino acids, peptide-spectrum-match identification false discovery rate <1% as calculated on scores by employing the reverse database strategy, and minimum of 1 specific peptide per identified protein group. Proline was then used to perform an MS1 label-free quantification of the identified protein groups based on razor and specific peptides. Statistical analysis was performed using the ProStaR software (73, version 1.20) based on the quantitative data obtained with the three biological replicates analyzed per condition. Proteins identified in the contaminant database, proteins identified by MS/MS in less than two replicates of one condition, and proteins quantified in less than three replicates of one condition were discarded. After log2 transformation, abundance values were normalized by median centering before missing value imputation (SLSA algorithm for partially observed values in the condition and DetQuantile algorithm for totally absent values in the condition). Statistical testing was conducted with limma, whereby differentially expressed proteins were selected using a log2(Fold Change) cut-off of 2 and a p-value cut-off of 0.01, allowing for a false discovery rate inferior to 2% according to the Benjamini-Hochberg estimator.

SLK kinase assay

Measurement of the SLK kinase assay was performed using the ADP-Glo SLK kinase system kit (Promega #V4243). Briefly, 12 ng of SLK protein was incubated with increasing amounts of RNAseT2 protein (0 to 5 ng) for 1 h at room temperature in the presence of 10 µM ATP and 100 ng of substrate (Histone H3). In control experiments, SLK and RNAseT2 proteins were incubated with RNAse cocktail (RNAse A (0.5 U) and RNAseT1 (20 U), Invitrogen # AM2286). ADP-Glo solution was then added and incubated for 40 min at room temperature. Finally, the kinase detection reagent was added and incubated for 30 min. Luminescence was recorded using a Varioskan LUX instrument (Fisher Scientific), with an integration time of 0.5 sec. Control included samples without the substrate, which value was substracted from each result.

Mouse experiments

WT C57BL/6 J mice (8–10-week-old males) were bred, and experiments were conducted in compliance with French and European legislations, and the protocols have been validated by an ethical committee. Skeletal injury was caused by intramuscular injection of cardiotoxin (CTX, Latoxan #L8102) in the tibialis anterior (TA) muscle (12 µM CTX, 50 µl). Muscles were harvested at day 4 for macrophage isolation and day 8 for immunohistology. In electroporation experiments, electroporation was performed 5 days after injury by injecting 50 µl of 100 μg/ml (in physiological serum) of PCDNA3 plasmid or RNAseT2 plasmid27 intramuscularly. Immediately after injection, electric stimulation was applied to the muscle by a pulse generator (ECM 830, BTX) of 50 volts, for 2 × 4 pulses, with a 60 ms duration and an interval of 100 ms using 16 G golden needles. Four days later, the muscles were harvested. Fibrotic (fib)-mdx and metformin-treated fib-mdx mice were obtained as described in21, for macrophage isolation.

Bone marrow transplantation was performed as previously described41. Total bone marrow cells were isolated by flushing of the tibiae and femurs of 2–3-month-old Rnaset2+/+ or Rnaset2−/− donor mice74 with RPMI 1640 / 10% FBS and frozen in 45% RPMI 1640 / 45% FBS / 10% DMSO. On the day of the transplantation, donor cells were quickly thawed out in RPMI 1640 / 10% FBS and incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 1 h. They were then transplanted into 2-month-old C57BL/6 recipient males previously lethally irradiated by gamma rays with a dose of 8.5 Gy on a Synergy apparatus (Elekta). Total bone marrow cells were injected (107 cells diluted in 100 μl of RPMI 1640/50% mouse serum) into the tail vein of recipient mice. Muscle injury was induced as described above, 5 weeks after the transplantation and TA muscles were harvested 14 days later.

Immunohistology

Muscles were quick frozen in cold isopentane and stored at −80 °C. 10 µm cryosections were permeabilized in 0.5% Triton-X100 for 10 min, washed in PBS 1X and then incubated with Bovin Serum Albumin 4% for 1 h. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C (anti-CD64 1:200 Biolegend #161002) or for 2 h at 37 °C (anti-RNAseT2 1:50 SigmaAldrich #SAB3501006; anti-Laminin (1/1000, Santa Cruz Biotech sc-59854), revealed with FITC anti-rat or Cy3 anti-rabbit or Cy5 anti mouse secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories Inc, #715-095-150 #711-165-152 and #712-175-150) for 1 h at 37 °C. Slides were washed with 1X PBS, counterstained with Hoescht 33342 and mounted in Fluoromount-G medium.

Isolation of macrophages from regenerating mouse muscle (day 4 post injury), from fib-mdx and metformin-treated fib-bmdx muscles was performed by FACS using Ly6C and CD64 markers as described in21. These macrophages were used for RT-qPCR, as previously described21 with the following sequences: 5’-TGGCCCGATAGAGCAGAAGA-3’ (Rnaset2 forward), 5’-TGGCTGCGATTAGAAGACCG-3’ (Rnaset2 reverse), 5′-GTGACTTTACACGCCATAATG-3′ (cyclophilin A forward), and 5′-ACAAGATGCCAGGACCTGTAT-3′ (cyclophilin A reverse). Macrophages were also tested for their fusogenic properties on murine myogenic precursor cells (MPC). MPCs were obtained from resting muscles as previously described75 and maintained on 0.02% Gelatin-coated plates in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 20% FBS, 2% Ultroser G (Sartorius). MPCs were seeded on Matrigel reduced growth factor (1:10 dilution, Corning) at a density of 30,000 cells/cm2. After 6 h, FACS-sorted macrophages were added onto MPCs at a ratio of 2:1 in DMEM/F12 medium containing 2% heat-inactivated horse serum (Sigma-Aldrich) and incubated for 3 days. MPC fusion was determined as described above for human cells.

In vivo image analyses

Pictures of the muscle section were recorded using a Zeiss Z1 Observer microscope. For quantification of the number of nuclei per myofiber, about 8 to 15 pictures were randomy taken per section, representing about 400 to 800 mofibers The number of myofibers containing 1 to >5 myonuclei was manually counted using ImageJ. The size of regenerative myofibers was calculated using an ImageJ macro on the entire muscle section76. The quantification of RNAseT2 intensity after electroporation was calculated on the entire muscle section using a threshold in ImageJ on 2–4 serial sections per sample as the mean gray intensity for the whole area.

Zebrafish experiments

Zebrafish husbandry and manipulations

Adult zebrafish were maintained using a standard protocol77. Embryos were collected and raised in E3 media at 28.5 °C. Embryos at 24 h post fertilization (hpf) were transferred into E3 Phenylthiourea (PTU) solution to inhibit pigment formation78. Transgenic line TgBAC(pax7a:eGFP)t32239Tg in a pfeffer mutant background (herein referred to as pax7a:eGFP) was used for visualizing MuSCs and differentiating myoblasts37,79. Gene over-expression in macrophages was performed using a Tg(mpeg1:gal4) line80. A transgenic line co-expressing rnaset2 and dsRed under the control of a dual UAS promoter was made by injecting the plasmid pTol2-dUAS:rnaset2_dsRed into single-cell embryos with Tol2 RNA81 to generate transgenic fish Tg(dUAS:rnaset_dsRed). To perform injuries, larvae were anesthetised in 0.004% w/v Tricaine (Sigma- 200 mg/ml) in E3 media and immobilized in 1.5% w/v low-melt agarose (Sigma). Larvae were orientated to expose the left lateral muscle. Using a sharpened tungsten wire mounted in a micromanipulator, larvae were injured in the 13th left ventral myotome. Injured larvae were then carefully removed from the agarose and placed in multi-well dishes containing fresh E3 PTU37.

Immunolabelling and in situ hybridization

Larvae were labeled as previously described (Sultan et al., 2021). Antibodies used were anti-BrdU (Abcam ab6326), anti-dsRed (Takara) and anti-GFP (Invitrogen). In situ hybridization was performed as previously described82 using antisense riboprobes generated against rnaset2 (kind gift from Noemie Hamilton).

Molecular biology

A construct for over-expressing zebrafish rnaset2 (ENDARG00000036282) was generated by cloning the full-length open reading frame of rnaset into a Gateway vector with Tol2 sites and containing a dual UAS promoter driving dsRed in the opposite orientation83 to create plasmid pTol2-dUAS:rnaset2_dsRed.

Image acquisition and processing

Images were acquired either by epifluorescent illumination on a Nikon SMZ1500 with a Leica Firewire camera, on a Leica SP5 confocal microscope with a 20x water dipping objective (NA 0.7) or on a Zeiss 7MP multiphoton microscope with a 25x water dipping objective (NA 1.0). Image processing was performed in Fiji, and panels assembled in Illustrator software (Adobe). Image stacks were used to make projections in Fiji, and the brightness of individual channels was manipulated to show relevant detail.

Statistics

Each experiment was run at least 3 times independently, meaning 3 different cell cultures or 3 animals. All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism. For in vivo analyses, data were tested for normality and comparisons made either using 1-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests. Post-hoc comparisons between conditions were performed by Dunn’s test. For cellular analyses, 1-way, 2-way ANOVA or student-t-tests (paired or unpaired depending on the experiments, two-sided) were performed. Details are given in the figure legends.
