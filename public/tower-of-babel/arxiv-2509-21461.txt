Stochastic analysis of ultra-high energy cosmic ray interactions This work employed the software packages Astropy ( Astropy Collaboration et al. 2013 ; Astropy Collaboration et al. 2018 ; Astropy Collaboration et al. 2022 ) , Matplotlib ( Hunter 2007 ) , Numpy ( Harris et al. 2020 ) , Scipy ( Virtanen et al. 2020 ) .
Leonel Morejon, Karl-Heinz Kampert

Abstract
Photonuclear interactions between ultra-high-energy cosmic ray (UHECR) nuclei and surrounding photon fields are key to understanding the connection between the compositions observed at Earth and those emitted from the sources. These interactions can completely disintegrate a nucleus of iron over trajectory lengths of a few and up to hundreds of megaparsecs, depending on the energy of the UHECR. The stochastic nature of these interactions means that it is not possible to describe them deterministically for a single cosmic ray, and an exact formulation of the probability distributions is not yet available. Current approaches describe these interactions using either Monte Carlo simulations or solving ordinary differential equations that neglect stochasticity. Because of the limitations of these approaches, only partial capture of the process is achieved. This paper presents an analytic probabilistic description of UHECR interactions and the resulting nuclear cascades, establishing their connection to Markov jump processes. The fundamental properties of these cascades are presented, as is the computation of the usual quantities of interest, such as the horizon, spectrum, and composition. The benefits of this description are outlined using astrophysical examples related to extragalactic propagation and UHECR sources.



I
Introduction

Experimental observations of cosmic rays alone are insufficient to answer the fundamental questions about their origins. A precise understanding of the magnetic deflections and interactions that affect their production and propagation is essential for reconstructing their past history. In the case of ultra-high-energy cosmic rays (UHECRs) it is now understood that the sources must be extragalactic
(
Aab et al. 2015
;
Abdul Halim et al. 2024a
)
. The plausibility of hypothetical sources is assessed by using knowledge of interactions and magnetic deflections to produce synthetic quantities that can be compared with the main observables, such as the energy spectrum and fluctuations in the depth of shower maximum
(
Abdul Halim et al. 2023
)
and, more recently, arrival directions
(
Abdul Halim et al. 2024b
)
). The present work focuses on the interactions of UHECRs, with some mention of the effects of turbulent magnetic fields. The effect of Galactic magnetic fields
(
Unger & Farrar 2024
;
Korochkin et al. 2024
)
will not be addressed here.

During acceleration and diffusion within the sources, as well as during propagation, UHECRs interact with surrounding photon fields

, such as the cosmic microwave background (CMB), cosmic infrared background (IRB), or non-thermal spectra in the source. These interactions result in the loss of energy and photodisintegration of the UHECR. Some interactions are well characterized as deterministic (often referred to as
continuous energy losses
(CEL)), if fluctuations of the inelasticity and the interaction length are negligible. Examples include Bethe-Heitler pair production and synchrotron losses, which typically do not change the nuclear species of the UHECR. Conversely, stochastic interactions with variable possible products and/or significant variations in interaction lengths (referred to as
stochastic losses
(SL)) often result in the transformation or loss of the interacting particle, yielding multiple outcomes for the resulting species. Examples of such interactions include the photodisintegration of cosmic ray nuclei, where the number of nucleons lost is not deterministic; and photomeson production, where multiple meson-producing channels are available (depending on the energy), each with a distribution of inelasticity and number of secondaries. This process also leads to nuclear fragments
(
Morejon et al. 2019
)
.

Although the fundamental differences between SL and CEL have been recognized since early works
(
Puget et al. 1976
;
Yoshida & Teshima 1993
)
, a CEL approach has been widely employed (
e.g.

Hill & Schramm 1985
;
Berezinskii & Grigor’eva 1988
) in describing the evolution of the cosmic ray spectrum. However, improvements in experimental precision and indications of a heavier composition have increased the need for more sophisticated approaches that account for the probabilistic nature of SL. Today, approaches to computing the interactions of UHECR nuclei can be grouped into two types: The first type uses the continuous-limit approximation
(
Boncioli et al. 2017
;
Heinze et al. 2019
)
, where SL and CEL are treated as continuous losses, and the energy densities of different nuclear species are computed by solving a coupled system of differential equations. The second type uses Monte Carlo methods
(
Epele & Roulet 1998
;
Hanisch & Biemesderfer 1989
;
Alves Batista et al. 2016
;
Aloisio et al. 2017
)
, which simulate the underlying stochasticity by tracking each particle individually. The former approach has the advantage of faster computation and analytic solutions have even been put forward by limiting the number of disintegration channels
(
Hooper et al. 2008
;
Ahlers & Taylor 2010
;
Ahlers et al. 2013
;
Aloisio et al. 2013a
;
Aloisio et al. 2013b
)
.
The latter is considered more theoretically correct because it best reflects the nature of the interactions and allows for the estimation of stochastic effects. However, there are intrinsic limitations to the method, such as being computationally expensive, depending on assumptions, and the problem of convergence. Furthermore, Monte Carlo simulations provide a limited theoretical insight because the impact of input uncertainties (
e.g.
nuclear cross sections and photon field models) cannot be easily determined without an exhaustive and computationally demanding parameter space scan. In contrast, an analytic framework can facilitate studying correlations between inputs (in some cases explicitly), and the computation is considerably more efficient. It can also achieve arbitrary precision at modest computational effort. Conversely, Monte Carlo methods often waste computational resources on uninteresting events as they are blind to the underlying probability space.
Currently, there is no formal theoretical framework that can describe the stochasticity of the UHECR interactions analytically. Existing analytic descriptions (
e.g.

Hooper et al. 2008
;
Ahlers & Taylor 2010
;
Ahlers et al. 2013
;
Aloisio et al. 2013a
;
Aloisio et al. 2013b
;
Ptuskin et al. 2015
) employ the continuous-limit approximation and make additional assumptions to avoid stochasticity, thereby making the evolution equations deterministic.

This paper presents an analytic theoretical framework that addresses the interactions of UHECRs with photon fields that are dominant in extragalactic propagation and within sources. The resulting closed-form expressions describe the probability distribution as a function of target thickness for an arbitrary initial condition. This approach can easily be extended to include nuclear masses beyond iron, enabling the independent study of the effects of uncertainties in inputs such as nuclear cross sections and photon fields.

II
Stochastic description

The continuous-limit temporal evolution of the energy densities of UHECR nuclei interacting with photon fields is described (
e.g.

(
Khan et al. 2005
;
Ahlers & Taylor 2010
;
Boncioli et al. 2017
;
Heinze et al. 2019
)
) by a coupled system of ordinary differential equations

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}n_{i}(E_{i},t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{i}}(bn_{i})+q_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}(E_{i})+\sum_{j}\lambda_{j\to i}(E_{j})n_{j}(E_{j})

(1)

where
n_{i}(E_{i},t)
is the differential number density of nuclear species
i
. The first term on the right-hand side includes all CEL processes, such as synchrotron and escape losses in the case of source scenarios, and pair production and adiabatic losses in the case of extra-galactic propagation. The term
q_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}
describes the injection of particles with energy
E_{i}
, which could represent the acceleration mechanisms within sources, or emission from different sources in the case of extra-galactic propagation.
The terms
\lambda_{j\to i}(E_{j})
denote the interaction rates for all SL processes incurred by species
j
, leading to the production of species
i
, with photons of energy
\epsilon
and number density
n(\epsilon)
. The total cross section for species
j
as a function of photon energy
\varepsilon
(in the center-of-mass rest frame)
\sigma_{j}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{i}\sigma_{j\to i}(\varepsilon)
includes all possible products
i
and is given by the sum of the interaction rates
\lambda_{j}(\gamma)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{j\to i}(\gamma)
, which are computed as

\lambda_{j\to i}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{n(\epsilon)}{\epsilon^{2}}d\epsilon\int_{0}^{2\epsilon\gamma}\varepsilon\sigma_{j\to i}(\varepsilon)d\varepsilon\,,

(2)

with
\gamma
representing the Lorenz factor. The system described by Eq.
1
may comprise between
\sim
50-200 nuclear species when including elements up to iron, and an energy grid with enough resolution (
\sim
100 bins in logarithmic scale) to capture the details of the spectra.

The approach presented here aims at describing nuclear cascades initiated by individual cosmic rays and, because of the boost preserving property of SLs, this implies solving Eq.
1
for individual values of the Lorentz boost
\gamma\approx E_{k}/m_{k}
, so we can write

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\tilde{n}_{i}(\gamma,t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}(\tilde{b}\tilde{n}_{i})+\tilde{q}_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}(\gamma)+\sum_{j}\lambda_{j\to i}(\gamma)\tilde{n}_{j}(\gamma)

(3)

where the tilde reflects that quantities are now differential in boost instead of energy and the densities
\tilde{n}_{i}
,
\tilde{n}_{j}
are coupled only for the same boost. This linear system of ordinary differential equations can be written as a matrix differential equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{N}-\bm{N}\bm{\Lambda}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}(\tilde{b}\bm{N})+\bm{Q}^{\mathrm{ext}}

(4)

where
\bm{N}
is a row vector containing all densities
\{\tilde{n}_{k}(\gamma,t)\}
,
\bm{Q}^{\mathrm{ext}}
is a row vector with elements
\{\tilde{q}_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}(\gamma)\}
, and
\bm{\Lambda}
is the interaction rate matrix
\{\lambda_{ji}=\lambda_{j\to i}(\gamma)\}
which is a square matrix with zeros for elements
j
with no production of element
i
. The numerical integration of Eq.
1
, or equivalently Eq.
4
, yields the time evolution of the species densities
\bm{N}(t)
requiring initial densities
\bm{N}(t=0)
and the form of the injections
\bm{Q}^{\mathrm{ext}}
. Notably, for certain functions
\bm{Q}^{\mathrm{ext}}
, the solution may have an analytic form when the term
\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}(\tilde{b}\bm{N})
is negligible (no CEL), since this term is the only one coupling the equations corresponding to different values of
\gamma
.

Equation
4
reflects the mean behavior of individual cascades (continuous-limit) but it does not describe the stochastic behavior of the interactions and the resulting fluctuations of the underlying quantities. The accurate underlying process is as follows: an initial UHECR nucleus propagates along a path of random length (determined by the relevant magnetic field) until it decays or interacts with the surrounding photon field. This produces a random number of secondaries according to a given set of probabilities. The secondaries and the remnant species (the secondary with the largest mass) continue to propagate under the influence of magnetic fields and subsequently interact randomly with further random products. This corresponds to a Markov jump process
(
Bladt & Nielsen 2017
)
where the transient states are the nuclear species with transition probabilities determined by the current state. The transitions (jumps) are exponentially distributed as a function of the path length (or time). In this probabilistic framework, the homogeneous form of Eq.
4
(without CEL and no injections) is analogous to Kolmogorov’s differential equation

\frac{d}{dt}\bm{P}^{t}=\bm{G}\bm{P}^{t}=\bm{P}^{t}\bm{G}\>,

(5)

where instead of the density vector
\bm{N}
, the more appropriate
\bm{P}^{t}
appears, which is a matrix where each row
i
contains the probability
p^{t}_{ij}
of transitioning to state
j
at time
t
, and the infinitesimal generator
\bm{G}
is related to the interaction matrix

\bm{G}(\gamma)=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\Lambda}&-\bm{\Lambda}\bm{1}\\
\bm{0}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}

(6)

and fulfills
\bm{G}\bm{1}=\bm{0}
where
\bm{1}
and
\bm{0}
are a column vector of ones and a row vector of zeroes of the same dimension as
\bm{\Lambda}
. The connection of Eq.
5
to the homogeneous form of Eq.
4
is evident since the solution
\bm{P}^{t}=e^{\bm{G}t}
for time-homogeneous conditions (length/time independence of
\bm{\Lambda}
) in the former, is similar to the solution of the latter
\bm{N}(t)=\bm{N}(0)e^{\bm{\Lambda}t}
for a suitable normalization of
\bm{N}
. However, it should be emphasized that the two equations are in fact describing different quantities and are not completely equivalent: Eq.
5
describes the time evolution of stochastic quantities, such as the occupation probability for each state of the nuclear cascade; while Eq.
4
describes the time evolution of the deterministic quantities (the number density distribution for each nuclear species). These two descriptions can be connected in the continuous limit, when stochastic effects are less important and the evolution of the system behaves like a fluid flow between a network of containers (see
(
Bladt & Nielsen 2017
)
Section 4.6).

Finally, the stochastic description presented here is not limited to stationary conditions, because it reflects a dependence on any variable that governs the process globally, such as the path length. Therefore, changes in time or the path of the system (
e.g.
a global change in the normalization of the target photon field density) can be treated as a type of time-inhomogeneity (see Section
III
). This approach is also used to incorporate CELs within this framework and considers other time-inhomogeneous conditions.
However, before addressing more complex inhomogeneous cases, it is helpful to outline the fundamental properties of the homogeneous cases and establish a framework for comparison. To this end, we will focus on the distributions of distance until reaching a defined final state.

II.1
Serial and regular cascades: the canonical form

First we consider the case in which there is only one nuclear species for each mass and only one possible interaction channel at each state (or possibly multiple channels, but some
m
-th channel has the largest branching ratio
\lambda_{i}(\gamma)\approx\lambda_{i\to m}(\gamma)
). A typical case is the one-nucleon-loss assumption
(
Hooper et al. 2008
)
, in which the cascade of nucleus with mass
A
proceeds in a chain of nuclei with descending mass
\{A,A-1,A-2,...,A-k+2,A-k+1\}
denoting the sequence of states visited over
k
consecutive interactions. The interactions of the species are governed by the respective rates, making up the interaction vector
\bm{\lambda}_{A\to A-k}(\gamma):=\{\lambda_{A}(\gamma),\lambda_{A-1}(\gamma),\lambda_{A-2}(\gamma),...,\lambda_{A-k+2}(\gamma),\lambda_{A-k+1}(\gamma)\}
, computed by substituting the relevant cross section into Eq.
2
, and evaluated on the common boost
\gamma
.
The sequential nature of these cascades implies that the probability distribution of the propagation path lengths until
k
disintegrations,
L_{k}
, is the convolution of
k
exponential distributions. This is a hypoexponential distribution with parameter vector
\bm{\lambda}(\gamma)
. The expected value of this distribution has a straightforward physical meaning:
\mathbb{E}[L_{k}]=\sum_{i=A-k+1}^{A}1/\lambda_{i}
, the sum of the mean interaction length of each species in the chain. This assumption was used in
Morejon 2021
to understand the behavior of more complex disintegration networks for nuclei of masses up to lead. Cascades in which each interaction produces only one channel with one nuclear species at each stage are referred to as Serial Cascades (SeCs) herein.

For the canonical cascade, consider the case in which the photonuclear interaction rates are proportional to the mass number of the species, i.e.
\lambda_{A}(\gamma)=A\lambda_{1}(\gamma)
, where
\lambda_{1}(\gamma)
is the interaction rate per nucleon, which implies the relations
\lambda_{A_{l}}=\frac{A_{l}}{A_{k}}\lambda_{A_{k}}
for any
k
and
l
. This is motivated by the proportionality of the photonuclear cross section to the mass number, as reflected by the Thomas-Reiche-Kuhn sum rule (
\int\sigma(\varepsilon)d\varepsilon\propto\frac{ZN}{A}
) for Giant Dipole Resonances (GDR) and the mass scaling of the cross section in photomeson interactions. Cascades where the rates follow this type of proportionality with mass are called
regular
.
In general, photonuclear cross sections deviate from this behavior from one species to another. However, these relations are a good approximation of the mean interaction rates and constitute a suitable benchmark for analyzing realistic distributions (see Fig.
1
).

At this point, we define the canonical form that will be used as a benchmark in the following: the regular sequential cascade (RSeC) as a SeC that obeys the regularity condition. The probability density of distances until reaching the last nucleus is given by (see Appendix
A
):

f^{\mathrm{RS}}_{A\to A-k}(L)=\\
\lambda_{A-k+1}e^{-\lambda_{A-k+1}L}\binom{A}{k-1}\left(1-e^{-\lambda_{1}L}\right)^{k-1}\>.

(7)

The interpretation of this expression is very intuitive: the distribution consists of
k
independent events: the probability that any
k-1
nucleons out of the initial
A
interact within the trajectory length
L
(the term
{A\choose k-1}\left(1-e^{-\lambda_{1}L}\right)^{k-1}

A-k+1
(the term
\lambda_{A-k+1}e^{-\lambda_{A-k+1}L}
) which is the last species that leads to the production of
A-k

\xi=1-e^{-\lambda_{1}L}
yields the following equation:

f^{\mathrm{RS}}_{A\to A-k}(\xi)=\frac{k}{\xi}\mathrm{B}(A,k,\xi)=\frac{A-k+1}{1-\xi}\mathrm{B}(A,k-1,\xi)\>,

(8)

where the relation
\frac{d}{dL}=\lambda_{1}(1-\xi)\frac{d}{d\xi}
has been employed. The binomial distribution, denoted by
\mathrm{B}(A,k,\xi)

k
disintegrations (successes) out of
n
independent trials. This is a consequence of the regularity of the cascade. The constancy of the interaction rate per nucleon,
\lambda_{1}
, implies that nuclear effects are negligible, and thus, the cascade is insensitive to the specific nuclei involved. The factors
\frac{k}{\xi}
,
\frac{A-k+1}{1-\xi}
result from the change of differential variable in the density, and the arbitrary choice of the “success” probability
\xi
,

II.2
Irregular cascades and the nuclear decays

The regularity condition assumes that nuclear cross sections are unaffected by nuclear effects. In reality, changes in the number of protons and neutrons have a significant impact on the properties of the GDR, including the peak energy and the width. Consequently, the mass scaling of the interaction rates exhibits deviations from the regular values
2
2

2

This possibility also implies that the interaction matrix may be defective for a number of boosts, since different nuclei may have the same rate for some boost values. To avoid numerical problems, these values of the boost can be identified and excluded from the computation. The probability distributions for these values can then be determined by interpolating between adjacent values.

Another cause of irregularity is spontaneous nuclear decay because in this framework the decay rate is part of the total interaction rate:

\lambda^{\mathrm{tot}}_{A_{i}\to A_{j}}(\gamma)=\lambda_{A_{i}\to A_{j}}(\gamma)+\gamma/c\tau

(11)

Unlike Eq.
8

An ISeC is distributed according to a hypoexponential distribution, and its density can be expressed as a linear combination of the exponential distributions with interaction vector
\bm{\lambda}_{A\to A-k}(\gamma)
as long as they are all different:

f^{\mathrm{IS}}_{A\to A-k}(L)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}(0)\lambda_{A-i}e^{(-\lambda_{A-i}L)}.

(12)

Here,
p_{j}(x)=\prod_{j=1,\,j\neq i}^{k}\frac{\lambda_{A-j}-x}{\lambda_{A-j}-\lambda_{A-i}}
are the coefficients of the Lagrange interpolation polynomials evaluated at
x=0
. This expression facilitates estimating the impact of irregularity on the density. It reduces to Eq.
7

The more general expression, which is also applicable to cases where not all rates differ, is

f^{\mathrm{IS}}_{A\to A-k}(L)=-\bm{\phi}e^{\bm{\Lambda}L}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{1},

(13)

where
\bm{\phi}

\bm{\Lambda}
contains the negative interaction rates
\{\lambda_{A}(\gamma),\lambda_{A-1}(\gamma),...,\lambda_{A-k+1}(\gamma)\}
on the main diagonal, and positive interaction rates
\{\lambda_{A}(\gamma),\lambda_{A-1}(\gamma),...,\lambda_{A-k+2}(\gamma)\}
on the upper diagonal contiguous to the main diagonal.

II.3
Concurrent cascades

The general cascade requires the inclusion of multiple channels at each step, producing a network of states. Unlike ISeCs, where the path between any pair of states is unique, in these cascade types paths at each node branch into multiple options, forming a network of intersecting ISeCs that develop concurrently. These more general cascades are referred to as Concurrent Cascades (CoCs) herein. One of the simplest examples in the literature is the disintegration scheme proposed by
Puget et al. 1976
, the PSB model. In the PSB model, there is only one species for each mass; however, each nucleus can lead to multiple products due to additional disintegration channels, such as one- and two-nucleon emission in the GDR region and 6-15 nucleons in the quasi-deuteron region.
The density function for the distance until absorption is the same as in Eq.
13
, but the matrix
\bm{\Lambda}
has additional terms in each row representing jumps to other nuclei in the chain. This is unlike the matrix for ISeCs, which contains only jumps to the immediate species with lower mass. An expression in the form of Eq.
14
may not exist in general for CoCs as no set of coefficients
c_{k}
can produce the equivalent function (see Appendix
B
).

In their most general form, CoCs should include all known nuclear species, including multiple nuclei with the same mass number. However, Eq.
13
still applies, even though the interaction matrix
\bm{\Lambda}
and the starting vector
\bm{\phi}

II.4
Light secondary products

In addition to the leading mass, the products of nuclear cascades include multiple light nuclei, such as deuterium and
\alpha
-particles, which can be considered boost-preserving products, as well as light secondaries, such as pions and single nucleons that are produced with a broad spectrum of energies. Larger nuclear fragments may also be present. For example, photo-fission leads to at least two fragments of similar mass. In this stochastic description, all these products are treated as additional particles of each stochastic jump. The largest mass nucleus has been used in the above sections as the nominal species, denoting the current state of the cascade. Here, we describe the treatment of the secondary products mentioned, whose production follows the state jumps in the cascade.

Clearly, the production of light secondaries is also a stochastic quantity, as it is related to the transitions of the cascade as it develops. However, a detailed stochastic treatment can be quite involved as it requires constructing the two-dimensional distribution of distance covered and secondaries produced. A simplified approach is to compute the production of the

III
Continuous energy losses

The stochastic processes discussed so far do not account for the effect of CELs, which are deterministic (non-stochastic) interactions that cause energy losses without altering the nuclear species. This degradation in energy affects the Markov property of the cascade because the rates are no longer constant due to the changes in the Lorentz boost. These processes are described in the theory of stochastic processes by inhomogeneous continuous-time Markov chains, where the time homogeneity (the independence of the rates of jumps between states over time) is violated. This makes the current state of the cascade a function of the complete past history instead of only the previous state.

In our context, it is useful to distinguish between two types of inhomogeneities caused by CELs:
Coherent inhomogeneities
(CI) where the present state depends on the total time (distance) elapsed, but not on the specific history of the process (
i.e.
the sequence of species), and
dispersive inhomogeneities
(DI), where the probability of the present state depends on the detailed sequence of species in the past history. These terms are appropriate because of the their respective impacts on the boost of the cascade. The latter type leads to differences in the boost evolution of the underlying concurrent cascades (dispersion), whereas the former type results in all concurrent cascades experiencing the same boost evolution (coherence). The effects of CI can be accommodated analytically through variable transformations if the time-dependence of the CI is known. DI effects are not generally analytically computable, but approximations and numerical methods are available to deal with such cases
(
Arns et al. 2010
)
. The relevant cases are discussed below for both the propagation of UHECRs and in-source interactions.

III.1
Coherent inhomogeneities

Cases of coherent inhomogeneities involve target photon fields that vary over time. These fields affect all rates according to a predetermined, time-dependent function, regardless of the state of the cascade. For sources, a fireball scenario fits this description, given the adiabatic cooling of the interaction volume as it expands. In the case of propagation, the adiabatic losses produce such inhomogeneities because their effect is only a function of propagation time and the initial epoch, regardless of the nuclear species transited.

III.2
Dispersive inhomogeneities

Energy losses that depend on the nuclear species affect the cascade development in variable degrees depending on the specific sequence of states, thus the total energy loss after multiple disintegrations can vary significantly among the concurrent disintegration chains. This implies that different sequences within CoCs produce diverging Lorentz boost evolutions, thus gradually rendering the cascade incoherent.

Examples of CELs that cause DIs include: synchroton losses, which are relevant within sources with strong magnetic fields; and pair production losses, which are relevant for extragalactic propagation. The rate at which the boost changes (equivalent to the energy loss rate) for synchrotron losses is

-\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{d\gamma}{dL}=\frac{\sigma_{T}m_{e}^{2}}{6\pi m_{p}^{4}}\gamma B^{2}\left(\frac{Z}{A}\right)^{4}

(29)

where
\sigma_{T}
is the Thomson cross section, and
m_{e}
,
m_{p}
are the masses of electrons and protons, respectively, and
B
is the magnetic field intensity in the source. The relation for the boost change in this expression depends on the nuclear species, given the factor
\left(\frac{Z}{A}\right)^{4}
. Thus, the losses are affected by the specific sequence of nuclei and the distances traveled by each nucleus.

The rate of boost change for pair production losses
(
Blumenthal 1970
)

-\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{d\gamma}{dL}=\alpha r_{0}m_{e}^{2}c^{4}\frac{Z^{2}}{\gamma A}\int_{2}^{\infty}d\xi\,n\left(\frac{\xi m_{e}c^{2}}{2\gamma}\right)\frac{\phi(\xi)}{\xi^{2}}

(30)

is also dependent on the nuclear charge and mass numbers. Following the notation
\beta_{0}/c=\frac{1}{\gamma^{p}}\frac{d\gamma^{p}}{dL}
for the loss length of protons
(
Aloisio et al. 2013a
)
, the losses of nuclei in general can be written as

\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{d\gamma}{dL}=\frac{Z^{2}}{A}\frac{\beta_{0}(\gamma)}{c}\>.

(31)

For cosmological propagation the redshift dependence must be included. As discussed above, the CI can be implicitly taken into account in terms of the comoving boost. With the redshift dependence, the rate of boost change can be written as

\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{d\gamma}{dL}=(1+z)^{3}\frac{Z^{2}}{A}\frac{\beta_{0}((1+z)\gamma)}{c}

(32)

assuming that the photon density scales with the redshift of the CMB density

n_{\mathrm{CMB}}(\varepsilon,z)=(1+z)^{3}n_{\mathrm{CMB}}((1+z)\varepsilon,z=0)

(33)

since the CMB is the dominant target for pair production losses. Including the term for adiabatic losses yields the boost evolution, as in the kinetic equations formalism
(
Aloisio et al. 2013a
)

\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{d\gamma}{dL}-\frac{1}{1+z}\frac{dz}{dL}=(1+z)^{3}\frac{Z^{2}}{A}\frac{\beta_{0}((1+z)\gamma)}{c}\>,

(34)

which can be written in terms of the comoving frame and the cosmological thickness

\frac{1}{\gamma_{c}}\frac{d\gamma_{c}}{d\delta_{c}}=\frac{Z^{2}}{A}\frac{\beta_{0}(\gamma_{c})}{c}

(35)

and represents the principal relation that governs boost changes for cosmological propagation. Equation
35
is analogous to Eq.
31

\gamma_{c}
in the absence of CEL (right-hand side null).

The evolution of the boost over cosmological thickness can be obtained by integrating the equation via variable separation,

\int_{\gamma^{1}_{c}}^{\gamma^{2}_{c}}\frac{cd\gamma_{c}}{\gamma_{c}\beta_{0}(\gamma_{c})}=\Phi(\gamma_{c}^{2})-\Phi(\gamma_{c}^{1})=\frac{Z^{2}}{A}\delta_{c}

(36)

where it is evident that the change in comoving boost from the initial
\gamma^{1}_{c}
to a final
\gamma^{2}_{c}
for any nuclear species is proportional to the cosmological thickness for protons, represented by the function
\Phi(\gamma_{c})
, which can be precomputed numerically and is only dependent on the target photon field.

The relevance of DI is limited to a small region of boost and redshift phase space relevant to the propagation of UHECRs (see Appendix
C
). Within this range, they can be accounted for using a quasi-homogeneous approach, in which the thickness is divided into segments small enough to ensure the applicability of the CI description. This is possible because, in any cascade, there is a dominant rate (typically for the species with the largest mass), and, for sufficiently small values of
\delta_{c}
, the constancy of
\gamma_{c}
can be ensured. The cascade can then be described by a set of CI descriptions, each applying within a segment, with an interaction matrix evaluated at the constant boost of the segment. The boost values are updated after each segment by selecting the most likely value. The total boost change is also stochastic, but its distribution can be determined via a reward transformation
(
Bladt & Nielsen 2017
)
.

\delta^{\ell}_{c}
(see Fig.

) and defining our horizon
L^{\ell}_{\rm FD}
called the

. Such a limit makes sense even when considering magnetic deflections: the heaviest species in a composition has the largest horizon, due to the DD effect, and their rigidity tends to be the largest. Indeed,
R=E/Z=\gamma/\kappa
and the charge-to-mass ratio
\kappa=Z/A
(typically within 0.3-0.6 for all nuclei and within 0.4-0.5 for stable nuclei) tends to be lower the for heavier nuclei. Thus, the products of the heaviest nuclei emitted would propagate further and experience the least magnetic deflections (see Sec.
IV.4
). The full disintegration limit constrains the propagation length, which is equivalent to the distance reached under ballistic propagation but, under diffusive propagation, the distance reached by nuclei would be shorter as during diffusive motion the propagation lengths tend to be larger than the rectilinear distances reached
(

. The effect of diffusive motion in sources is illustrated in Sec.

.

IV.2
Reverse propagation

Under certain conditions, the direct Markov jump process that describes nuclear cascades can be reversed. This is particularly relevant to the problem of inferring the composition of cosmic rays at their source, given the a composition measured on Earth.

The simplest case for the reverse-propagation process is the quasi-stationary regime. In Markov jump processes, the stationary distribution
\bm{\phi^{s}}
is determined by the condition
\bm{\phi^{s}}\bm{\Lambda}(\gamma)=\bm{0}
, meaning that the composition remains unchanged as time evolves. However, in the cascades discussed here, all nuclear states are transient, so no such stationary distribution exists. Nevertheless, a quasi-stationary state can be reached with a corresponding distribution
\bm{\tilde{\phi}^{s}}
defined by the relation
\bm{\tilde{\phi}^{s}}\bm{\Lambda}(\gamma)=-\tilde{\lambda}^{s}\bm{\tilde{\phi}^{s}}
, which implies that the reverse-propagation preserves the Markov property and the corresponding reverse interaction matrix is easily constructed

\bm{\tilde{\Lambda}}_{r}(\gamma)=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\tilde{\phi}^{s}})^{-1}\bm{\Lambda}(\gamma)^{T}\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\tilde{\phi}^{s}})

(37)

where
\bm{\tilde{\phi}^{s}}
is the same for both the forward and the reverse process by construction. The reverse process can then be computed with
\bm{\tilde{\Lambda}}_{r}(\gamma)
integrating Kolmogorov’s differential equation or building the probability distributions of distance until absorption as above, but here absorption corresponds to probing the original species or composition assumption.

Figure
6
illustrates the likelihood to observe a cosmic ray nucleus of given energy from different distances for different assumptions about the original species. These likelihoods were computed as the evolution of the probability vector with Kolmogorov’s differential equation, and the likelihood for each species is the point probability for that species as a function of distance, normalized to a common value for comparison with the other species, however, relative probabilities cannot be inferred by this approach. As expected the heavier the assumed original species, the larger the distance of maximum likelihood. This approach can be used to estimate the origin of individual events of extreme energies
(
Morejon 2025
)
, such as the recent Amaterasu detected by Telescope Array.

Assuming a quasi-stationary distribution is a very specific condition that may not be met in reality. Verifying this assumption for the observed UHECR spectrum would require a level of precision in energy and composition that is currently impossible to achieve. A more general approach is to solve Kolmogorov’s differential equation numerically for the inverse process.

IV.3
UHECR sources

The approach of solving Kolmogorov’s differential equation can be applied to modeling UHECR sources in two ways. The simplest method is to compute the distributions until absorption to
e.g.
determine the probability of escape of a given species
(
Morejon 2023
;
Morejon & Rautenberg 2025
)
. For example, the escaping probability vector
\bm{\phi}_{\rm esc}
can be obtained applying Eq.
5
on an assumed injected composition
\bm{\phi}_{\rm inj}

\bm{\phi}_{\rm esc}(\gamma)=\bm{\phi}_{\rm inj}\bm{P}^{t_{s}}(\gamma)

(38)

with
t_{s}\approx L_{s}/c
the characteristic crossing time of the source. Here, the rates contained in
\bm{\Lambda}
(and therefore
\bm{G}(\gamma)
, used to find
\bm{P}(\gamma)
) are computed with the source target photons (
e.g.
a broken power law). The effects of CI and DI can be taken into account as discussed in Sec.
III
which define the boost evolution and the corresponding evolution of
\bm{G}(\gamma(t))
. Furthermore, additional effects can be taken into consideration, like the impact of different assumptions for the escape. For instance, if the distribution of trajectory lengths until escape
F_{\rm esc}(\gamma,L)
is known (a cumulative density as a function of trajectory lengths and the boost) the escape probability vector as a function of the boost would be

\bm{\phi}_{\rm esc}(\gamma)=\bm{\phi}_{\rm inj}\int_{0}^{L}\bm{P}(\gamma)^{L^{\prime}/c}\left(1-F_{\rm esc}(\gamma,L^{\prime})\right)dL^{\prime}\>.

(39)

This expression assumes that changes in rigidity during successive disintegrations can be ignored, and needs to be assessed for the specific scenario. When this is not the case, a more nuanced treatment is also available, as illustrated in subsection
IV.4
for propagation.

Of special interest is simulating the time evolution of the emitted composition in sources with a time-dependent cosmic ray injection. This type of modeling has been achieved with full nuclear cascades (
e.g.
NEUCOSMA
(
Biehl et al. 2018
;
Rodrigues et al. 2018
)
) by numerically integrating Eq.
4
yielding time-dependent spectral densities for each nuclear species. This task can also be achieved with the stochastic approach if we adopt a regularization,
i.e.
if all jumps take place in regular intervals of the elapsed time or distance, which can be arbitrarily small. This assumption is valid given the large luminosities, which justify a continuous limit approach, allowing us to treat the occupation probabilities as volumes in a fluid-like system of equations (such as Eq.
4
) where the changes in occupation probability represent the amounts transferred between species as a function of time. In these cases, the injection rate
\bm{\tilde{Q}}^{\rm ext}(\gamma,t)
represents the injection vector which may be in general a function of time and is typically a power-law of the energy or boost. The time evolution of the probability vector for a later time
t^{\prime}
is thus given as above
\bm{\tilde{Q}}^{\rm ext}(\gamma,t)\bm{P}^{t^{\prime}-t}(\gamma)
and the total yield can be computed by integrating over certain injection time
t_{\rm inj}
or as a convolution product

\bm{N}(\gamma,t_{\rm inj})=\int_{0}^{t_{\rm inj}}\bm{\tilde{Q}^{\rm ext}}(\gamma,t^{\prime})\bm{P}^{t_{\rm inj}-t^{\prime}}(\gamma)dt^{\prime}\>,

(40)

where
\bm{N}(\gamma,t_{\rm inj})
is a vector with the final yields for each species in the casscade as a function of the boost. In simple cases where the DIs are negligible,
\bm{P}^{t}=e^{\bm{G}t}
as discussed previously, however, the general form, including the DIs, requires computing
\bm{P}^{t}
numerically or following a similar approach as described in Sec.
III.2
. This expression allows for arbitrary choices for the temporal evolution of the injection.

In addition, other assumptions for the escape can be included, as discussed above. Figure

One advantage of this approach is that interactions within the source and during propagation can be handled consistently within a single model. This is different from current approaches, which simulate each environment separately. This advantage allows us to perform a fit to the spectrum and composition of cosmic rays, including the source parameters in the minimization, such as the source’s optical thickness or the injected composition. For example, this approach enables us to link UHECR emissions to nuclear cascade models, which describe optical observations of kilonovae resulting from neutron star mergers and associated gamma-ray bursts (GRBs).

IV.4
Magnetic deflections and distribution of arrival direction

The probabilistic disintegration of nuclei during propagation also affects the arrival directions of cosmic rays. Earlier works (
e.g.

(
Lee et al. 1995
;
Waxman & Miralda-Escudé 1996
)
) have discussed the angular deviations that occur during UHECR propagation under the influence of extragalactic magnetic fields (EGMFs) in the regime of small-angle scattering (
i.e.
when the gyroradius exceeds the coherence length
\lambda_{B}
of the EGMFs). These and similar studies neglect energy losses and disintegrations by assuming a constant rigidity
R
, thus leading to the mean squared angular deviation formula
\langle\Delta\theta\rangle^{2}\approx\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}\frac{B^{2}}{R^{2}}\lambda_{B}d

(
Lee et al. 1995
)
where
d
denotes the distance to the source and
B
the strength of the magnetic field.

This expression is sometimes used even in the presence of disintegrations, arguing that changes of nuclear species do not affect the rigidity, since
R=E/Z=\gamma/\kappa
,
\gamma
is conserved and
\kappa=Z/A
can be considered 0.5 for most stable species up to iron. However, the actual variation of
\kappa
in nuclear cascades can be up to 30 % of the mean value, and the changes are stochastic, like the cascade itself. Currently, there is no expression for the angular deviation that includes disintegrations, and the most realistic treatment involved Monte Carlo simulations with a code that incorporates magnetic effects and disintegrations, such as CRPropa
(
Alves Batista et al. 2022
)
.

Using the stochastic description in this work, we can derive an analytic expression for the expected angular spread of individual products. The mean squared angular deviation for each species
i
in the cascade is given by
\theta_{\rm ms,i}=\langle\Delta\theta_{i}\rangle^{2}\approx\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}\frac{B^{2}\kappa_{i}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\lambda_{B}d_{i}
, where
d_{i}
would be the rectilinear distance the species travels from creation until interacting. The distribution for the total mean squared angular deviation would be the sum of all stochastic deviations experienced by each species
\theta_{\rm ms}=\sum_{i}\theta_{\rm ms,i}
and thus, it is a stochastic variable itself. However, it can be shown that
\theta_{\rm ms}
is also a phase-type distribution which can be obtained by applying a ”transformation via rewards” to the distribution for the distance traveled until absorption, where the ”reward” would be the deviation per unit distance for each species
\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}\frac{B^{2}\kappa_{i}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\lambda_{B}
which establish the linear dependence between the propagation distance and
\theta_{\rm ms,i}
. Examples of the obtained distributions for
\theta_{\rm ms}
are shown in Fig.

(top) for the distance until producing
10
B,
12
C and
14
N in the propagation of a 5 EeV iron nucleus for a total distance of 20 Mpc. These products were chosen to compare species with similar masses and charges produced for this distance scale.

V
Conclusions

Until now, the stochasticity of UHECR interactions has been addressed using Monte Carlo approaches, which are limited by available computational resources. This work demonstrates that interactions of UHECRs with photon fields in astrophysical scenarios can be described analytically with arbitrary precision. This description has additional advantages including the ability to obtain closed-form probability distributions, such as the distance until loss of a number of nucleons and the deflections of UHECRs in the EGMF including interactions and secondary nuclei.

The stochastic approach presented here provides physical insights such as the equivalency between UHECR propagation with and without cosmological effects, given that a change of variable substituting the distance by the photon thickness traversed. Describing cosmological propagation in this simple form is a noteworthy result, since it combines losses that previous approaches handled with separate prescriptions, namely the redshift-scaling of photon fields and adiabatic losses. In this framework, the physical meaning of such effects is made apparent: opacity to interactions. Additionally, this method revealed the regularity of photodisintegration processes over a broad range of initial and final masses and Lorentz boosts, which considerably reduces the complexity of UHECR propagation.

The applications of this approach are not limited to the study of probabilities for compound quantities, it also applies to interactions in scenarios where stochasticity is less important due to the large number of expected events. We demonstrated this by computing the cosmic ray production for an example scenario of a GRB source and showing how some source properties, such as the magnetic field and time-dependent injection, affect the variability of the produced spectrum. Furthermore, this approach allows for a combined source-propagation framework that avoids “coupling defects” between the two scenarios, as propagated compositions are often reduced to a few mass groups. At the same, a common treatment for both scenarios allows for consistency in the cross-section tables and decay tables without the simplifications often required for efficiency.

This framework will be made available through an open source python package called CRISP (Cosmic Ray Stochastic Interactions for Propagation) with some utility functions implementing the expressions in this paper and the computation of input quantities, etc. Future works will discuss how to study the observed UHECR spectrum and composition with fewer assumptions (
e.g.
, excluding the spectral index or source evolution) and exploring the sensitivity of fitted parameters.

To estimate the impact of DI caused by Bethe-Heitler pair-production losses (BHL) during propagation over cosmological distances we will compare the survival probability function including cosmological effects with and without BHL. This is sensible because in photodisintegration cascades the shortest scale is determined by the injected species with the largest mass as its interaction rates are typically the largest. For scales where the differences are sufficiently small, neglecting the effects of DI is justified.

\gamma_{c}

Appendix D
Details of source examples

The source scenario considered is based on the GRB example in
(
Morejon et al. 2019
)
and discussed in more detail as the
Optically Thick Case
in
(
Biehl et al. 2018
)
. The GRB model is based on the “fireball” picture, where cosmic rays are injected pre-accelerated into the photon emission zone over a certain time interval and the emitted composition is produced by photointeractions with this zone. The emission mechanism assumed is guided by the “internal shock model” in which relativistic shells collide and a fraction of their kinetic energy powers the emission. The model conceives one such collision as a spherical shell expanding relativistically with a Lorentz factor
\Gamma=300
, radius of collision
R_{c}=2\cdot 10^{8}
km and volume
V_{\rm iso}=4\pi R_{c}^{2}\Delta d^{\prime}
where
\Delta d^{\prime}
is the shell’s thickness. The photon spectral number density is given by a broken power law between energies 100 eV–100 keV, with a power -1 below the break energy 1 keV, a power of -2 above the break, and an energy density
u_{\gamma}^{\prime}=\frac{L_{\gamma}}{4\pi\Gamma^{2}R_{c}^{2}}
with a luminosity
L_{\gamma}=10^{53}
ergs/s. The magnetic field intensity is estimated assuming its energy density is comparable to the luminosity of photons
B^{\prime}=\sqrt{\frac{2L_{\gamma}}{c\Gamma^{2}R^{2}}}
. The injected cosmic ray species is
56
Fe and the spectrum has the form
Q_{{}^{56}{\rm Fe}}^{\prime}(E^{\prime})=C^{\prime}{E^{\prime}}^{2}\exp\left[-\left(E^{\prime}/E^{\prime}_{\rm max}\right)^{2}\right]
with
E^{\prime}_{\rm max}
the energy where the acceleration rate is comparable to the sum of the rates for all energy losses, and
C^{\prime}
is determined from
\int_{0}^{10E^{\prime}_{\rm max}}\tilde{E}Q_{{}^{56}Fe}^{\prime}(\tilde{E})d\tilde{E}=10u_{\gamma}^{\prime}c/\Delta d^{\prime}
which stems from assuming that the baryonic loading factor (ratio of the luminosity in cosmic rays to the photon luminosity) is 10.

The additional considerations in this work for the source models involve the variability in the time dependence of the injection, and the inclusion of rigidity-dependent escape rates. For the effect of changes in the temporal form of the injection, the baseline case is a constant injection of cosmic rays of
C^{\prime}(t)=C^{\prime}_{0}\approx 2.679\cdot 10^{12}\rm{GeV/cm^{3}s}
and the variability is represented by difference between the lower values for
C^{\prime}(t)=4(1-(t-1)^{2})\cdot 10^{12}\rm{GeV/cm^{3}s}
and the higher values for
C^{\prime}(t)=(4.157-2.952t)\cdot 10^{12}\rm{GeV/cm^{3}s}
, all of which were defined requiring the same total injection over an interval of one second. The rigidity-dependent escape was implemented by taking the advective escape as the nominal case (thus all species take one second to escape) and two other diffusion cases: Bohm and Kolmogorov. In both cases, the escape is exponentially distributed
F_{esc}=1-\exp(t/t_{\rm diff}(R))
with dependencies
t_{\rm diff}(R)=3\cdot 10^{6}/R
to model the Bohmian case (diffusion coefficient proportional to rigidity) and
t_{\rm diff}(R)=2\cdot 10^{2}/R^{1/3}
to model the Kolmogorov case (diffusion coefficient proportional to the cubic root of the rigidity). These choices of
F_{\rm esc}(t,R)
illustrate the changes in the crossing time through the source medium with a broadening for lower rigidities as expected for diffusive propagation.

Appendix E
Efficiency over a Monte Carlo approach

The stochastic framework presented here provides a more efficient way to compute quantities related to UHECR interactions in astrophysical scenarios. Monte Carlo methods, commonly employed for UHECR propagation, have known drawbacks regarding computational efficiency in comparison to the evaluation of closed-form expressions. Nevertheless, for the sake of completeness, we estimate the efficiency of this approach compared to a generic Monte Carlo.

Determining the computational cost or efficiency for Monte Carlo Methods is not trivial because the conclusion depends on the aspects taken into consideration,
e.g.
the desired precision, the convergence of the algorithm(s) employed, the concrete simulated scenario, etc. For simplicity, we focus on a few quantities that characterize the efficiency in terms of the time spent and number of computational operations required. We leave out estimating the energy costs in the comparison, but it is clear that Monte Carlo methods are in general a very poor choice when rare events are of interest because the input phase space most relevant is not known, and additionally, there are always costs incurred in training and testing simulations which are often discarded due to errors. Nevertheless, many drawbacks can be addressed with either specific sampling techniques or introducing weights and biases, but these still require some exploratory simulations to gain some understanding of the underlying phase space.

The aim here is to provide an ideal limit with two simple assumptions: a) all Monte Carlo trials have similar computational cost; b) the sampling of the input parameter space leads to a uniform sampling of the desired quantity.

A different estimate of the computational cost per trial can be made based on the number of propagation steps. However, there is a limit to the resolution achievable with a given step size, since in a Monte Carlo approach the choice of step is in general taken without knowledge of the form of the distribution. While a smaller step size would yield a better resolution for the entire distribution, it comes at the price of a larger amount of discarded trials and a corresponding increase cpu-time. Additionally, the asymmetry of these distributions implies that any choice of step size cannot describe the small scale of the rise and the large scale of the tails with the same precision without considerable cpu-time. A proper step size that describes all underlying distributions suitably is often not possible as the rise and decrease of different distributions can be differ in orders of magnitude, not only for different injected species but also for the same species at different values of the Lorentz boost. These limitations of Monte Carlo methods are overcome when the underlying distributions are known in closed form.
