Nerve block or wound infiltration with liposomal bupivacaine for postoperative pain management in open orthopedic surgery: a meta-analysis of 38 randomized controlled trials involving 3,389 patients
Authors: Jinsheng Yu, Zixing Zhao, Yan Lu, Jinda Dong, Wenjun Chen, Xiaodong Wei, Jiahong Nong, Chenglong Wang, Jianxun Wei, Zhuhai Li, Xiaoping Mu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients undergoing open orthopedic surgeries often report substantial postoperative pain. The present work aimed to provide effective evidence for perioperative pain management in open orthopedic surgeries through comprehensive electronic database searches and the inclusion of more high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that report on the safety and effectiveness of liposomal bupivacaine (LB) in these surgeries.MethodsWe performed the present work strictly under the guidelines of the Cochrane handbook and PRISMA recommendations. Under the guidance of the established retrieval strategy, two researchers independently performed the literature retrieval. Each included study was assessed in according with Cochrane risk of bias tool. We utilized STATA software to perform all meta-analyses in the current study.Results38 RCTs encompassing 3,389 patients were included in this study. The results demonstrated that there was statistical significance in pain intensity scores on POD2 [SMD = −0.25, 95% CI (−0.49 to −0.02)] and a trend toward statistical significance on postoperative day 1 (POD1) [SMD = −0.17, 95% CI (−0.35 to 0.00)] between the two groups. However, no statistical differences in pain scores on POD0, POD3, opioid consumption (total, POD1, POD2, POD3), adverse events, patient satisfaction, and the length of hospital stay.ConclusionCompared to conventional medications, LB does not demonstrate a significant advantage in perioperative pain management, nor does it significantly reduce opioid consumption. Further high-quality RCTs are required to confirm these findings and greater attention should be given to the long-term safety of LB to ensure its reliability for practical use.

Introduction

Surgical procedures involving the musculoskeletal system emerge as one of the most prevalent and painful procedures in clinical practice (1). A survey released in 2013 (2) revealed that 22 of the 40 surgical procedures with the highest-rated pain were related to orthopedic or trauma surgeries of the extremities. Despite the advancements in perioperative pain control, patients undergoing open orthopedic surgeries, including but not limited to arthroplasty, lumbar fusion, and fracture surgery, still report substantial postoperative pain (3). A cross-sectional observational study (4) involving over 15,000 surgical patients in the UK reported that 11% of patients experienced severe pain, and 37% reported moderate pain within the first 24 h after surgery. Moreover, approximately 47% of patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty (THA) and 58% undergoing total knee arthroplasty (TKA) experienced moderate to severe pain on the first postoperative day (5).

Acute pain following orthopedic surgery can result in a variety of detrimental effects, such as delayed postoperative recovery, prolonged hospital stays, increased postoperative complications, limited rehabilitation participation, prolonged reliance on opioids, and even the development of chronic pain (6). Postoperative pain after open orthopedic surgery is an unfavorable prognostic factor (7). Consequently, postoperative pain management has emerged as a critical component of the healthcare team's responsibilities. Attention and proactive efforts in perioperative pain prevention and management strategies are imperative (8), allowing patients to traverse the postoperative recovery phase smoothly.

Opioids have traditionally been the most frequently prescribed category of pain-killer drugs during the perioperative period in orthopedic surgery (9). Over the past decade, opioid-related costs in the United States have surged dramatically, with an increase of up to an astonishing 660% (10). Although opioids are effective in pain control, their well-known issues with abuse, addiction, and even overdose deaths are significant concerns (11–13). To reduce unnecessary opioid use, multimodal analgesia approaches have gradually gained acceptance and popularity in clinical practice. Specifically, orthopedic surgeons increasingly emphasize the utilization of local anesthetics for regional blocks or wound infiltration as part of a multimodal analgesia strategy.

Liposome bupivacaine (LB) is a multi-vesicular liposome formulation containing bupivacaine that is approved for wound infiltration and regional nerve block. This formulation achieves analgesia for over 72 h through a sustained and slow release of bupivacaine (14). Currently, research on the use of LB in perioperative pain control for orthopedic surgery has yielded inconsistent results. Previous studies (15, 16) have reported that LB effectively reduces postoperative pain, minimizing the need for rescue analgesics, and mitigates opioid-related adverse effects after orthopedic surgeries. However, several studies (17, 18) have failed to demonstrate any significant advantage of LB compared to traditional analgesic drugs or non-opioid alternatives.

Previous meta-analyses (19, 20) have reported on the use of LB during the perioperative period for certain orthopedic procedures. However, a consistent pattern among the conclusions drawn by various studies has been elusive, with evidence strength varying accordingly. Nowadays, more studies on the local administration of LB in open orthopedic surgeries have been published. These fresh insights may bridge the previously identified discrepancies in research outcomes. Furthermore, given that using higher-cost LB in clinical practice is inappropriate without confirmed clinical benefits (21), its application in total joint arthroplasty has also shown a downward trend (22). The present work aims to analyze more published studies on the local application of LB during the perioperative period following open orthopedic surgeries, addressing previous gaps and producing findings that are backed by a higher level of evidence.

Materials and methods

The present work was a comprehensive secondary literature review focusing on the published randomized controlled trials (RCTs) exploring the local administration of LB in open orthopedic surgeries. It did not involve any ethical issues related to human or animal subjects. Therefore, the research protocol has been exempted from approval by our institution's ethics committee. We performed the present work strictly under the execution standards of systematic review as outlined in the Cochrane manual (23). The reporting of our findings conformed to the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) recommended by the PRISMA work group (24). The protocol of the present work was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (registration number CRD42024618098).

Literature search strategy and data sources

The literature retrieval strategy for this meta-analysis was developed after multiple trial electronic searches conducted by two researchers with over 3 years of experience in literature retrieval. Under the guidance of the established retrieval strategy, we employed Boolean operators “AND,” “OR,” and “NOT” to effectively combine the keywords “liposomal bupivacaine”, “liposome”, “exparel”, with other keywords “orthopedic surgery”, “spine”, “arthroplasty”, “joint replacement”, “shoulder surgery”, “fracture”. The following electronic databases were searched: Medline via PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Clinical Trials, and Google Scholar. The search strategy for each database is detailed in Supplementary File 1. To ensure a comprehensive search, the present work imposed no language restrictions on the published studies, and the timeframe for literature retrieval was set from January 2010 to September 2025. Furthermore, we manually searched for the studies included in the present work and references from reviews or meta-analyses under the same or similar topic to ensure comprehensive literature retrieval.

Eligibility criteria

To directly address the clinical questions this study aims to clarify, we used the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study (PICOS) framework to establish the inclusion criteria for the present work. (1) Population: adult patients who received any open orthopedic surgeries (including but not restricted to hip/knee replacement, spinal surgery, extremity fracture surgery, etc.) with a peripheral field or nerve block. (2) Intervention: local administration of LB. (3) Comparison: placebo (saline or nothing) or any local analgesia (bupivacaine, ropivacaine, etc.), combined or not with perineural or subarachnoid adjuncts. (4) Outcomes: patient-reported pain scores (including all types of pain scales with standardization of pain intensity), opioid consumption, and adverse events (including the incidence of nausea or vomiting, dizziness, constipation, pruritus). (5) Study design: RCT, combined or not with the blind.

Study selection

Two researchers independently screened the literature and checked with each other to guarantee the accuracy of the screening process. For any disputed records, a consensus was reached through internal discussion, with the introduction of a third senior researcher if necessary to resolve the disagreements. All records from electronic databases were first imported into literature management tools to remove duplicate literature. During the initial screening, two researchers assessed the relevance of the studies by reading the titles and abstracts, then labeled each record as “include,” “exclude,” or “uncertain”. For records marked as “include” or “uncertain,” the full texts were downloaded and reviewed to determine whether they met the inclusion criteria for the current work.

Data extraction

Two researchers independently extracted the data and entered it into the corresponding fields of a pre-designed standardized electronic data collection form. For studies with missing, incomplete, or inadequately formatted data unsuitable for statistical analysis, we would email the authors or study sponsors. Given the controversy over estimating data from multiple scoring standards, especially those with small sample sizes (25), we did not perform data estimation in this meta-analysis. In this meta-analysis, we extracted the following data from each included study: (1) Study characteristics: first author name, study location, publication year, study design, surgical approach, and patient baseline characteristics. (2) Intervention: drug, dose, mode of administration, duration. (3) Outcome indicators: postoperative pain intensity scores, opioid consumption, patient satisfaction, length of hospital stay, and treatment-emergent adverse events.

Risk of bias assessment

The present study only included published RCTs that investigated the local application of LB in open orthopedic surgeries. Two researchers who have received training in literature quality evaluation independently evaluated the included studies using the Cochrane risk of bias tool. Each included RCT was required to respond to “high risk,” “low risk,” or “unclear” for the seven domains of random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of study participants, blinded outcomes assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other biases (23). If more than two domains in an RCT were rated as “high risk,” the risk of bias for that study was defined as high (26). Once the evaluations are completed, the results obtained by both researchers are cross-checked and compared. Disagreements between the researchers were first resolved through internal negotiation. A third senior researcher was consulted to resolve the discrepancies if no consensus could be reached.

Statistical analysis

We utilized STATA software (Version 17, StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA) to perform all meta-analyses in the current study. The software estimates the weighted/standardized mean differences (W/SMD) for continuous data, and the risk ratio (RR) for binary data, with an overall estimate of the pooled effect. The I2 coefficient was employed to detect heterogeneity among studies, with pre-defined ranges to classify them as low (25%−49%), moderate (50%−74%), or high (≥75%) (27). We applied a random-effects model for results with moderate or high heterogeneity, whereas a fixed-effects model was used for results with low heterogeneity (28).

Taking into account the distinct features of the studies included in this meta-analysis, we surmised that the following factors might significantly impact heterogeneity: type of surgery (joint replacement, spinal surgery, fracture surgery), the dosage of LB (133 mg or 266 mg), interventions in the control group (plain bupivacaine, ropivacaine, or saline), administration technique (nerve block, local infiltration). We will conduct the subgroup analysis and meta-regression on the primary outcomes with high heterogeneity based on these factors to explore the drivers of heterogeneity.

We performed the sensitivity analysis using a stepwise exclusion method to verify the robustness of the pooled results for outcome measures. We have employed the Egger's test to detect potential publication bias. The results of this meta-analysis would be presented as W/SMD or RR, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-tailed P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Among the initially retrieved 2,827 articles, we excluded 1,225 duplicate studies. Subsequently, 1,471 articles were further excluded by reviewing the titles and abstracts. Among the remaining 131 articles, we carefully selected 38 studies (16, 21, 29–64) for inclusion in our current work after thoroughly reading their full texts. Flow diagram of study selection.

The overall risk of bias was assessed as low in 8 trials, unclear in 25 trials, and high in 5 trials. In the five studies rated as high risk, blinding of study participants and blinded outcome assessment emerged as the primary risk domains according to the seven-domain framework of the Cochrane risk of bias tool. In contrast, within the 25 studies rated as unclear risk, selective reporting (15 studies) and other biases (19 studies) emerged as the predominant risk domains. Trial characteristics

A total of 38 RCTs (16, 21, 29–64) encompassing 3,389 patients (LB: 1,689 cases, non-LB: 1,700 cases) reported on the local administration of LB for pain control in orthopedic open surgeries. The included RCTs were published from 2012 to 2025, with a significant proportion of these studies conducted in the United States. Notably, there was one study each conducted in China, Virginia, and Belgium. The open surgical procedures involved in this meta-analysis mainly included THA, TKA, lumbar fusion, total shoulder replacement (TSA) or major shoulder surgery, as well as open reduction and internal fixation for fractures of the extremities. The application of LB during the surgeries predominantly included peripheral nerve block or local infiltration, with a dosage of 133 mg or 266 mg. Furthermore, the control group was mainly treated with plain bupivacaine and ropivacaine. Trial characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Meta-analysis results of postoperative pain intensity scores

Pain intensity scores on POD0

Nine studies (32, 35, 36, 39, 43, 44, 57, 58, 64) reported pain intensity scores on postoperative day 0 (POD0). Significant heterogeneity were observed among the studies (I2 = 82.7%, P < 0.001), and the data were therefore pooled using a random-effects model. The meta-analysis results demonstrated no statistically significant difference in pain intensity scores on POD0 between the two groups [SMD = 0.09, 95% CI (−0.25 to 0.42); Figure 3].

Forest plot of the meta-analytic estimate for pain intensity scores on postoperative day 0 (POD0).

Pain intensity scores on POD1

Eighteen studies (21, 29–32, 35, 39, 43, 44, 52, 53, 55–59, 63, 64) reported pain intensity scores on postoperative day 1 (POD1). Significant heterogeneity was observed among the studies (I2 = 68.4%, P < 0.001), and a random-effects model was used to pool the data. The results of the meta-analysis indicated a marginal significant difference in pain intensity scores on POD1 [SMD = −0.17, 95% CI (−0.35 to 0.00); Figure 4A].

Forest plot of the meta-analytic estimate for pain intensity scores on postoperative day 1 (POD1) (A), along with subgroup analyses stratified by surgical procedure type (B), liposomal bupivacaine dosage (C), administration technique (D), and interventions in the control group (E).

Subgroup analysis revealed that patients undergoing TKA [SMD = −0.23, 95% CI (−0.39 to −0.08); Figure 4B] and open reduction and internal fixation for limb fractures [SMD = −1.07, 95% CI (−1.53 to −0.60); Figure 4B] in the LB group experienced a statistically significant reduction in pain intensity scores on POD1. However, no significant differences were observed in subgroups based on other types of surgery (Figure 4B), LB dosages (Figure 4C), administration techniques (Figure 4D), and control group interventions (Figure 4E).

Pain intensity scores on POD2

Fourteen studies (29, 30, 32, 35, 39, 43, 48, 52, 55, 57–59, 63, 64) reported pain intensity scores on postoperative day 2 (POD2). Significant heterogeneity was detected among the studies (I2 = 75.6%, P < 0.001), and the data were pooled using a random-effects model. The results showed that pain intensity scores on POD2 were significantly lower in the LB group compared to the control group [SMD = −0.25, 95% CI (−0.49 to −0.02); Figure 5A].

Forest plot of the meta-analytic estimate for pain intensity scores on postoperative day 1 (POD2) (A), along with subgroup analyses stratified by surgical procedure type (B), liposomal bupivacaine dosage (C), administration technique (D), and interventions in the control group (E).

Subgroup analysis revealed that the use of LB was associated with lower pain intensity scores on POD2 in patients undergoing TKA [SMD = −0.28, 95% CI (−0.50 to −0.06); Figure 5B] and distal metaphyseal osteotomy (DMO) procedures [SMD = −1.05, 95% CI (−1.38 to −0.72); Figure 5B]. However, no significant difference was detected in LB dosage (133 mg versus 266 mg) between the groups (Figure 5C). Furthermore, statistically significant reductions in postoperative pain scores were observed when LB was administered via nerve block [SMD = −0.51, 95% CI (−0.97 to −0.05); Figure 5D], or when plain bupivacaine was used as the intervention in the control group [SMD = −0.42, 95% CI (−0.68 to −0.16); Figure 5E].

Pain intensity scores on POD3

Eleven studies (29, 30, 32, 35, 39, 52, 55, 57, 59, 63, 64) reported pain intensity scores on postoperative day 3 (POD3). Significant heterogeneity was observed among the studies (I2 = 75.3%, P < 0.001), and the data was pooled using a random-effects model. The results of the meta-analysis demonstrated no statistically significant difference in pain intensity scores on POD3 between the two groups [SMD = −0.22, 95% CI (−0.49 to 0.05); Figure 6A].

Discussion

Perioperative pain management in orthopedic surgery has always been a significant clinical concern for orthopedic medical teams. Over the years, multimodal analgesia has played an active role in perioperative pain control following open orthopedic surgeries. Guided by the Cochrane Handbook, the present work aims to generate robust evidence for perioperative pain management in open orthopedic surgeries. This will be achieved through comprehensive electronic database searches and the inclusion of a higher number of high-quality RCTs that provide insights into the safety and efficacy of local anesthetics (LB) in these surgical procedures. The findings of this meta-analysis are as follows: (1) Since 2015, the use of LB in orthopedic surgery has been primarily in the United States, with peak popularity between 2016 and 2018, globally. (2) The analgesic efficacy of LB may differ among various types of open orthopedic surgeries. Subgroup analyses in the present study demonstrated that LB significantly reduced pain intensity scores on POD1 and POD2 in patients undergoing TKA, as well as on POD2 and POD3 in patients with bunion deformities undergoing DMO. (3) LB does not increase the incidence of postoperative complications, but it does not effectively reduce opioid consumption.

LB employs liposomal technology to deliver drugs directly and precisely to the surgical site, enabling a gradual release process that significantly enhances the bioavailability of the medication and offers consistent analgesic effects lasting up to 72 h (66, 67). Therefore, the primary advantage of LB lies in its long-acting postoperative analgesia. Our study demonstrated that pain intensity scores on POD2 were significantly lower in the LB group than in the control group, whereas pain intensity scores on POD1 showed a trend toward statistical significance. These findings indicated that LB may offer superior analgesic effects compared with standard treatment during this early postoperative period. However, studies (55, 58, 60) observing short-term (up to 48 or 72 h postoperative) pain scores did not reveal significant differences in pain scores compared to other drugs.

Currently, the effectiveness of LB in providing prolonged pain relief in open orthopedic surgeries remains uncertain. A recent single-center, double-blind, RCT (68) assessed the analgesic effects of LB in patients undergoing unilateral primary TKA. The study demonstrated that incorporating LB into a multimodal analgesic regimen, including a adductor canal block and periarticular infiltration, resulted in sustained pain relief and superior control of activity-related pain. In contrast, our study found that LB reduced pain intensity scores on POD1 and POD2 in TKA patients. However, previous research (19) reported contradictory findings, showing that LB did not significantly reduce postoperative pain compared to conventional analgesic approaches in TKA patients.

Furthermore, a recent study (69) evaluating the analgesic efficacy of LB vs. conventional anesthetic therapy in patients undergoing hand or wrist surgery reported that, compared with standard bupivacaine, LB did not significantly reduce postoperative pain or opioid consumption, nor did it improve patient-reported analgesic satisfaction. In lumbar fusion surgery, the combination of LB with retrolaminar block has been shown to effectively reduce pain within the first 72 h postoperatively (70). However, the use of ultrasound-guided erector spinae plane block combined with LB demonstrated only a modest advantage in alleviating postoperative pain and improving quality of recovery after lumbar fusion, and these benefits may be of limited clinical significance (71).

In the present study, subgroup analysis revealed that the type of surgery was a major contributor to the substantial heterogeneity observed in postoperative pain intensity scores. Nevertheless, the clinical outcomes of LB administration within the same surgical procedure have been inconsistent (19, 68, 70, 71). This discrepancy is largely attributable to differences in LB administration techniques and the analgesic regimens employed in control groups across studies. Furthermore, interpreting findings from studies that combine local anesthetics with adrenergic agents, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, or morphine remains challenging (19). Pharmacokinetic studies (72) revealed that the first peak of LB occurred within 1 h after injection, with a second peak occurring 12–36 h later. This provides a reasonable explanation for the findings of our study. However, due to the short follow-up period in the included studies, the analgesic effect of LB after 72 h could not be observed and requires further validation.

Theoretically, opioid consumption is negatively correlated with pain intensity (67). A meta-analysis (19) on the perioperative use of LB in TKA reported that the morphine consumption in the LB group was similar to that of the control group on POD 0, POD 1, and POD 3; however, the rescue dose of morphine in the LB group was lower before POD 2. A recent RCT [4] also found that retrolaminar block combined with LB did not reduce opioid consumption during the perioperative period of lumbar fusion surgery. However, ultrasound-guided erector spinae block combined with LB demonstrated a more significant reduction in opioid consumption during lumbar fusion surgery compared to local infiltration of LB (71). Our study did not find any differences between the two groups in terms of opioid consumption. The variations in surgical techniques, anesthesia methods, and interventions in the control group may have contributed favorably to these findings. A recent network meta-analysis (73) exploring the optimal analgesic strategy for TSA reported that the LB group had the lowest total postoperative morphine consumption. This is primarily due to the gradual and sustained release process of LB inflation, which mainly prolongs the duration of anesthesia (59).

Meanwhile, a study (19) reported adverse events such as nausea or vomiting associated with LB, but our study did not make the same finding. This may be related to the fact that the application dose of LB included in the current study was always the minimum effective dose ( ≤ 266 mg). Previous study (72) has shown that the incidence of adverse events with a dose of LB ≤ 266 mg was significantly lower than >266 mg.

To our best knowledge, the current work involved the most significant number of RCTs and comprehensively analyzed outcome measures among meta-analyses on similar topics. However, this study has certain limitations. (1) Although our study only included RCTs, several included studies may still have random errors, selection biases, and other issues that could affect the reliability and validity of the findings. Moreover, a relatively high number of studies were rated as having a high or unclear risk of bias, which could directly lead to overestimation and exaggeration, making the conclusions unreliable. Some high-quality studies could not be fully included in the analysis due to a lack of relevant data or incomplete data, potentially introducing biases into the results. (2) Despite our efforts to search relevant literature across multiple databases, some valuable data from unpublished or gray literature may not have been incorporated into this study. Moreover, certain data may not be fully utilized due to limitations or missing information in the original studies, which could also affect the study's results. (3) The included studies in the present work have exhibited significant heterogeneity, mainly due to differences in factors such as the administration methods, drug concentrations, and types of surgeries used in the various studies. These factors could influence the analgesic efficacy and safety of LB. While subgroup analyses and meta-regression were performed to explore sources of heterogeneity, some subgroups had few studies with small sample sizes, which may undermine the clinical relevance and stability of the results. Hence, clinical translation of these findings should be cautious. (4) LB has been shown to provide significant analgesia for up to 72 h post-surgery, which is a key advantage in postoperative pain management. Most studies included in the present work assessed postoperative pain primarily within the first 72 h, reflecting the pharmacologic profile of LB—which is designed to provide extended analgesia mainly within this time frame. However, longer-term data were not available, then we were unable to evaluate analgesic outcomes in the longer follow-up.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study indicates that the use of LB in open orthopedic surgeries does not increase the incidence of related complications. However, compared to other conventional medications (bupivacaine, ropivacaine, etc.), LB does not show a significant advantage in perioperative pain management, nor does it significantly reduce opioid consumption. Due to the heterogeneity among the studies, more high-quality RCTs are needed to confirm this conclusion further. Furthermore, we should pay more attention to the long-term safety of LB to ensure its reliability in practical applications.
