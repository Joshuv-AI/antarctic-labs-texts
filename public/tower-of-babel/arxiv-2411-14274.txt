Quantum Vacuum Self-Propulsion and Torque
Kimball A. Milton, Nima Pourtolami, Gerard Kennedy

Abstract
This article summarizes our recent efforts to understand spontaneous quantum vacuum forces and torques, which require that a stationary object be out of thermal equilibrium with the blackbody background radiation. We proceed by a systematic expansion in powers of the electric susceptibility. In first order, no spontaneous force can arise, although a torque can appear, but only if the body is composed of nonreciprocal material. In second order, both forces and torques can appear, with ordinary materials, but only if the body is inhomogeneous. In higher orders, this last requirement may be removed. We give a number of examples of bodies displaying second-order spontaneous forces and torques, some of which might be amenable to observation.



keywords
Spontaneous vacuum force; spontaneous vacuum torque;
nonequilibrium phenomena.

2
Quantum Vacuum Torque in Nonreciprocal Media

This phenomenon was first discussed in Ref.
[
8
]
, and then
subsequently in our paper, Ref.
[
4
]
. We summarize the latter here.

Classically, the torque on a stationary dielectric
body with polarization vector
\mathbf{P}
is
given by
[
9
]

\bm{\tau}=\int(d\mathbf{r})\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{d\nu}{2\pi}e^{-i(\omega+\nu)t}\left[\mathbf{P(r};\omega)\times\mathbf{E(r};\nu)+P_{i}(\mathbf{r};\omega)(\mathbf{r}\times\bm{\nabla})E_{i}(\mathbf{r};\nu)\right].

(1)

The first term here is called the
internal
torque and the second the

external
torque,
because the latter is reflective of the force on the body.
We expand this out to first order in generalized susceptibilities, using

\mathbf{E}^{(1)}(\mathbf{r};\omega)=\int(d\mathbf{r^{\prime}})\bm{\Gamma}(\mathbf{r-r^{\prime}};\omega)\cdot\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\omega),\quad\mathbf{P}^{(1)}(\mathbf{r};\omega)=\bm{\chi}(\mathbf{r};\omega)\cdot\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r};\omega),

(2)

in terms of the (local) electric susceptibility
\bm{\chi}
and
the (vacuum) electromagnetic Green’s dyadic
\bm{\Gamma}
.
We evaluate the two contributions (PP and EE)
to the quantum vacuum torque by use of the
fluctuation-dissipation theorem (FDT):

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{P}_{i}(\mathbf{r};\omega)\mathbf{P}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\nu)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi\delta(\omega+\nu)\delta(\mathbf{r-r^{\prime}})\bm{\chi}^{A}_{ij}(\mathbf{r};\omega)\coth\frac{\beta^{\prime}\omega}{2},

(3a)

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{E}_{i}(\mathbf{r};\omega)\mathbf{E}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\nu)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi\delta(\omega+\nu)\Im\bm{\Gamma}_{ij}(\mathbf{r-r^{\prime}};\omega)\coth\frac{\beta\omega}{2},

(3b)

in terms of the inverse temperatures
of the vacuum background,
\beta=1/T
, and of the body,
\beta^{\prime}=1/T^{\prime}
.

Here,
\bm{\Gamma}
is taken to be the usual vacuum retarded Green’s dyadic,
which at coincident points is (rotationally averaged)

\bm{\Gamma}(\mathbf{r-r^{\prime}};\omega)\to\bm{1}\left(\frac{\omega^{2}}{6\pi R}+i\frac{\omega^{3}}{6\pi}+O(R)\right),\quad R=|\mathbf{r-r^{\prime}}|\to 0,

(4)

while
\bm{\chi}^{A}
is the anti-Hermitian part of the susceptibility:

\bm{\chi}^{A}=\frac{1}{2i}(\bm{\chi}-\bm{\chi}^{\dagger}),\quad\Im\bm{\chi}^{A}_{ij}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{2}\Re[\chi_{ij}(\omega)-\chi_{ji}(\omega)],

(5)

which displays the antisymmetric part of
\bm{\chi}^{A}
,
which is even in
\omega
. The real part of
\chi^{A}
is symmetric in
indices and odd in
\omega
. Only the former contributes to the torque.

Using this, we readily calculate the torque on a nonreciprocal body in vacuum,
due to PP and EE fluctuations:

\tau_{i}=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{\omega^{3}}{6\pi}\left[\coth\frac{\beta^{\prime}\omega}{2}-\coth\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\right]\epsilon_{ijk}\Re\alpha_{jk}(\omega),

(6)

where the mean polarizability of the body is given by

\alpha_{jk}(\omega)=\int(d\mathbf{r})\chi_{jk}(r;\omega)
.
This result exactly agrees with that of Strekha et al.
[
8
]

However, there is no quantum
vacuum force in this static situation.
To create a nonreciprocal medium typically requires an external magnetic
field, so this is not exactly a pure vacuum phenomenon.
If the nonreciprocity is generated by a magnetic field of 1 T, the
corresponding torque for a gold nanoball of radius 100 nm
is
\sim 10^{-24}
N m for
T^{\prime}=600
K,
T=300
K.

3
Self-Propulsive Force

The Lorentz force on a dielectric body is
[
9
]
, where we have
omitted a term which vanishes when the FDT is used,

\mathbf{F}=-\int(d\mathbf{r})\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{d\nu}{2\pi}e^{-i(\omega+\nu)t}\frac{\omega}{\nu}P_{i}(\mathbf{r};\omega)\cdot(\bm{\nabla})\cdot E_{i}(\mathbf{r};\nu).

(7)

Now we expand the fields out to second order (4th order in generalized
susceptibilities,
\bm{\chi}
and
\bm{\Gamma}
, after use of the FDT):

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{(2)}(\mathbf{r};\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int(d\mathbf{r^{\prime}})\,\bm{\Gamma}(\mathbf{r-r^{\prime}};\nu)\cdot\bm{\chi}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\nu)\cdot\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\nu),

(8a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{(3)}(\mathbf{r};\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int(d\mathbf{r^{\prime}})(d\mathbf{r^{\prime\prime}})\,\bm{\Gamma}(\mathbf{r-r^{\prime}};\nu)\cdot\bm{\chi}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\nu)\cdot\bm{\Gamma}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}-r^{\prime\prime}};\nu)\cdot\mathbf{P(r^{\prime\prime};\nu)},

(8b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{(2)}(\mathbf{r};\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int(d\mathbf{r^{\prime}})\,\bm{\chi}(\mathbf{r};\omega)\cdot\bm{\Gamma}(\mathbf{r-r^{\prime}};\omega)\cdot\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega),

(8c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{(3)}(\mathbf{r};\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int(d\mathbf{r^{\prime}})\,\bm{\chi}(\mathbf{r};\omega)\cdot\bm{\Gamma}(\mathbf{r-r^{\prime}};\omega)\cdot\bm{\chi}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega)\cdot\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega).

(8d)

Using the symmetries of the integrand we find the following general
expression for the force on a body composed of isotropic material:

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int(d\mathbf{r})(d\mathbf{r^{\prime}})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}X(\mathbf{r,r^{\prime}};\omega)\Im\Gamma_{ji}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}-r};\omega)

(9)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\bm{\nabla}\Im\Gamma_{ij}(\mathbf{r-r^{\prime}};\omega)\left[\coth\frac{\beta\omega}{2}-\coth\frac{\beta^{\prime}\omega}{2}\right],

where the second-order susceptibility product is

X(\mathbf{r,r^{\prime}};\omega)=\Im\chi(\mathbf{r};\omega)\Re\chi(\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega)-\Re\chi(\mathbf{r};\omega)\Im\chi(\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega).

(10)

Obviously, if the body is homogeneous, so the susceptibility is independent
of position, then
X
vanishes, and there is no force on the body.

Consider a body composed of two parts, each of
which is separately made of homogeneous material.
In such a case, the force in the
z
direction reduces to

F_{z}=8\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}X_{AB}(\omega)I_{AB}(\omega)\left[\frac{1}{e^{\beta\omega}-1}-\frac{1}{e^{\beta^{\prime}\omega}-1}\right],

(11)

where the geometric integral is

I_{AB}(\omega)=\int_{A}(d\mathbf{r})\int_{B}(d\mathbf{r^{\prime}})\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{z}\left[\Im\Gamma_{ji}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}-r};\omega)\Im\Gamma_{ij}(\mathbf{r-r^{\prime}};\omega)\right],

(12)

and the susceptibility product becomes

X_{AB}(\omega)=\Im\chi_{A}^{\vphantom{q}}(\omega)\Re\chi_{B}^{\vphantom{q}}(\omega)-\Re\chi^{\vphantom{q}}_{A}(\omega)\Im\chi_{B}^{\vphantom{q}}(\omega).

(13)

From the form of the vacuum electromagnetic Green’s dyadic,

\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}(\mathbf{r-r^{\prime}};\omega)=(\bm{\nabla\nabla}-\bm{1}\nabla^{2})\frac{e^{i\omega R}}{4\pi R},\quad\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}=\bm{\Gamma}+\bm{1},

(14)

we find for the gradient of the product of the imaginary parts of
\bm{\Gamma}

\frac{1}{2}\bm{\nabla}\left[\Im\Gamma_{ji}(-\mathbf{R};\omega)\Im\Gamma_{ij}(\mathbf{R};\omega)\right]=\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{2}}\frac{\mathbf{R}}{R^{8}}\phi(v),\quad\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{r-r^{\prime}},\quad v=\omega R,

(15)

where

\phi(v)=-9-2v^{2}-v^{4}+(9-16v^{2}+3v^{4})\cos 2v+v(18-8v^{2}+v^{4})\sin 2v.

(16)

The limiting behaviors are

\phi(v)\sim-\frac{4}{9}v^{8}+\frac{28}{225}v^{10}+\cdots,v\ll 1,\phi(v)\sim-v^{4}+v^{5}\sin 2v+3v^{4}\cos 2v+\cdots,v\gg 1.

(17)

3.1
Friction and thermalization lead to terminal velocity

Once the body starts to move due to the nonequilibrium quantum force,
it will experience quantum friction, nonrelativistically given by
the Einstein-Hopf formula,

F_{f}=-\frac{v\beta}{12\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\omega^{5}\Im\alpha(\omega)\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}\beta\omega/2},

(27)

and, therefore, it will reach a terminal velocity:

v(t)=v^{\vphantom{q}}_{T}\left(1-e^{-t/t_{0}}\right).

(28)

For example, a needle having a 1 cm half-length and 10 nm radius,
would have a terminal
velocity of order 5 m/s. But the time scale,
t_{0}\sim 15
yrs, is
very long.

More important is thermal cooling.
Unless there is some mechanism supplied to maintain the temperature difference, the body will eventually cool to the temperature of the
environment.
From Newton’s law, the terminal velocity is

v^{\vphantom{q}}_{T}=\frac{1}{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,F(T^{\prime}(t),T).

(29)

Here, the cooling rate is given by

\frac{dQ}{dt}=C_{V}(T^{\prime})\frac{dT^{\prime}}{dt}=P(T^{\prime},T),

(30)

where
C_{V}(T^{\prime})
is the specific heat of the body at temperature
T^{\prime}
.
For a slowly moving body the power radiated is
[
2
]

P(T^{\prime},T)=\frac{1}{3\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\omega^{4}\Im\tr\mbox{\boldmath{$\alpha$}}(\omega)\left[\frac{1}{e^{\beta\omega}-1}-\frac{1}{e^{\beta^{\prime}\omega}-1}\right].

(31)

In terms of
u=T^{\prime}/T
, the time taken for the body to cool from temperature

T_{0}^{\prime}
to temperature
T_{1}^{\prime}
,

T_{0}^{\prime}>T_{1}^{\prime}>T
, is (for temperatures well above the Debye temperature)

t_{1}=\int_{T_{0}^{\prime}}^{T^{\prime}_{1}}dT^{\prime}\frac{C_{V}(T^{\prime})}{P(T^{\prime},T)},\quad\mbox{or}\quad\frac{t_{1}}{t_{c}}=\int_{u_{0}}^{u_{1}}du\frac{1}{p(u;T)},

(32)

where the dimensionless power radiated is modeled by
[
4
]

p(u;T)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\frac{x^{3}}{x^{2}+1}\left(\frac{1}{e^{\beta\nu x}-1}-\frac{1}{e^{\beta\nu x/u}-1}\right).

(33)

Therefore, the terminal velocity is

v^{\vphantom{q}}_{T}=\frac{t_{c}}{m}\int_{u_{0}}^{1}du\frac{F(u;T)}{p(u;T)}.

(34)

The time scale for a weak susceptibility model of the metal portion of
an object is

t_{c}=\frac{3\pi^{2}nT}{\nu^{3}\omega_{p}^{2}}\sim 10^{-4}\,\mbox{s},\quad n=\,\mbox{number}\,\mbox{density},

(35)

For a Janus ball, of radius 100 nm,
half made of gold and half dielectric,
with an initial temperature twice that of the background,
the terminal velocity is only about 0.1 nm/s. For a
spherical shell model with radius 1 cm and skin-depth thickness,

v_{T}
would be about the same.
So, observation might prove challenging.

4
Spontaneous Torque on an Inhomogeneous Chiral Body

In Sec.
2
,
we calculated the torque in first order, which required the body
be composed of nonreciprocal material, which usually necessitates an external
field be applied. In second order, a torque can arise on a reciprocal
body, but again only if the
body is
inhomogeneous
. It must further be
chiral
, in that its mirror reflection cannot be turned into the original object by translations
or rotations.

For a body with isotropic but inhomogeneous susceptibility there is only
an external torque, calculated in second order:

\displaystyle\tau_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\left(\coth\frac{\beta\omega}{2}-\coth\frac{\beta^{\prime}\omega}{2}\right)\epsilon_{ijk}\int(d\mathbf{r})(d\mathbf{r^{\prime}})X(\mathbf{r,r^{\prime}};\omega)

(36)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\Im\Gamma_{lm}(\mathbf{r-r^{\prime}};\omega)r_{j}\nabla_{k}\Im\Gamma_{ml}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}-r};\omega).

Again the susceptibility product
X
[Eq. (
10
)]
vanishes if the body is homogeneous.
If the body consists of two homogeneous parts,
A
and
B
,
X
is replaced
by
X_{AB}
defined in Eq. (
13
).
Using Eq. (
15
), we find
the torque in this situation is

\bm{\tau}=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}X_{AB}(\omega)\left(\frac{1}{e^{\beta\omega}-1}-\frac{1}{e^{\beta^{\prime}\omega}-1}\right)\mathbf{J}_{AB}(\omega),

(37a)

in terms of the geometric factor

\mathbf{J}_{AB}(\omega)=-\int_{A}(d\mathbf{r})\int_{B}(d\mathbf{r^{\prime}})\frac{\mathbf{r\times r^{\prime}}}{|\mathbf{r-r^{\prime}}|^{8}}\phi(\omega|(\mathbf{r-r^{\prime}}|).

(37b)

That the integral is convergent is evident from the behavior of
\phi

given in Eq. (
17
).

5
Conclusions

{itemlist}

In first order in electric susceptibility, a vacuum torque, but no
force, can arise for a body made of
nonreciprocal
material.

In second order, a vacuum force can arise only if the body is

inhomogeneous
, but no exotic material properties are required.

A vacuum torque can also arise for ordinary chiral
bodies in second order,
but again only if the body is also
inhomogeneous
. This is in contrast
to the findings of Ref.
[
10
]
.

We consider several examples of bodies which exhibit
possibly observable vacuum forces and torques, although cooling to equilibrium
with the vacuum environment may make it challenging
to observe the resulting
linear and angular terminal velocities, unless
the temperature imbalance is maintained.
The terminal angular velocity for a dual flag
appears measurable.

For dielectric-metal bodies, the force (and torque) is toward the metal
side,
due to the low emissivity and a high reflectivity of the metal.

One has to go to third order to see forces and torques on bodies made
of homogeneous material. This will be discussed in future work.
