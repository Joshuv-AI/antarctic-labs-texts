Abstract
. An ordered r r -uniform matching of size n n is a collection of n n pairwise disjoint r r -subsets of a linearly ordered set of r ​ n rn vertices. For n = 2 n=2 , such a matching is called an r r -pattern , as it represents one of 1 2 ​ ( 2 ​ r r ) \tfrac{1}{2}\binom{2r}{r} ways two disjoint edges may intertwine. Given a set 𝒫 \mathcal{P} of r r -patterns, a 𝒫 \mathcal{P} -clique is a matching with all pairs of edges order-isomorphic to a member of 𝒫 \mathcal{P} . In this paper we are interested in the size of a largest 𝒫 \mathcal{P} -clique in a random ordered r r -uniform matching selected uniformly from all such matchings on a fixed vertex set [ r ​ n ] [rn] . We determine this size (up to multiplicative constants) for several sets 𝒫 \mathcal{P} , including all sets of size | 𝒫 | ≤ 2 |\mathcal{P}|\leq 2 , the set ℛ ( r ) \mathcal{R}^{(r)} of all r r -partite patterns, as well as sets 𝒫 \mathcal{P} enjoying a Boolean-like, symmetric structure.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we are interested in the maximum size of sub-matchings of random ordered uniform matchings with all pairs of edges forming prescribed patterns. Before stating our results we present necessary definitions and background.

A natural and convenient way to represent ordered

Just for convenience, to represent a given matching we typically choose a word in which the first occurrences of letters appear alphabetically.

An important subclass of matchings can be defined via
the notion of interval chromatic number introduced in
[
15

Clearly, if

an ordered

. An ordered

-partite
. In such a matching, every edge contains exactly one vertex from each of the
r
consecutive blocks of size

r
-partite

-matching must consist of

-partite

For instance, the following

\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{X}\>\!\color[rgb]{0,1,1}{\underline{X}}\>\!\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\underline{\underline{X}}}
.
Also, the sub-matching consisting only of the edges represented by letters

We will often use the power notation

1.3.
Multi-patterned cliques

In this paper our goal is to study more complex substructures of random
r
-matchings than
P
-cliques. Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{(r)}
be a fixed subset of
r
-patterns. An ordered
r
-matching
M
is called a
\mathcal{P}
-clique
if every pair of edges in
M
forms a pattern belonging to
\mathcal{P}
. Let
z_{\mathcal{P}}(M)
denote the maximum size of a
\mathcal{P}
-clique in
M
. Note that if
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathcal{P}
, then every
\mathcal{Q}
-clique is a
\mathcal{P}
-clique and thus,
z_{\mathcal{Q}}(M)\leq z_{\mathcal{P}}(M)
. Also, if
N\subset M
, then
z_{\mathcal{P}}(N)\leq z_{\mathcal{P}}(M)
. Throughout, we will be referring to both these trivial properties as
monotonicity
.

Let
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
denote a random ordered
r
-matching of size
n
, that is, an
r
-matching picked uniformly at random from the set
{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{(r)}
of all ordered
r
-matchings on the set
[rn]
. Using the introduced notation, our main problem can be stated as follows.

Problem.

For a fixed
r\geqslant 2
and for a given subset of patterns
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{(r)}
, determine the order of magnitude of the random variable
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})
.

By “order of magnitude” we mean some function
f(n)
such that
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}_{n}^{(r)})=\Theta(f(n))

asymptotically almost surely
, that is, with probability tending to one as
n\to\infty
.
In the course of the paper we will use the common abbreviation
a.a.s.
Occasionally, we will be able to pinpoint the value of
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}_{n}^{(r)})

asymptotically
. We write
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
whenever
a_{n}/b_{n}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
and, for a sequence of random variables
X_{n}
, we write
X_{n}\sim a_{n}
if
X_{n}/a_{n}
converges to 1 in probability.

As was signaled earlier, the order of magnitude of
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})
when
|\mathcal{P}|=1
has been already known.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
5
]
)
.

For
r\geq 2
and every collectable
r
-pattern
P
a.a.s.

z_{P}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})=\Theta(n^{1/r}).

Let us mention that for
r=2
the above result had been proved, even in a sharper form, already by Baik and Rains
[
2
]
for
P=ABBA
and
P=ABAB
, and by Justicz, Scheinerman, and Winkler
[
8
]
for
P=AABB
.

The only other case that has been investigated so far was
r=2
and
|\mathcal{P}|=2
.
It is natural to expect that the value of
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})
depends on the size
|\mathcal{P}|
. However, as we will soon see, the situation is far more intricate. There are three possible pairs of
2
-patterns:
\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{AABB,ABAB\}
,
\mathcal{P}_{2}=\{AABB,ABBA\}
and
\mathcal{R}=\{ABAB,ABBA\}
. It turns out that typically the largest size of an
\mathcal{R}
-clique differs significantly from the largest sizes of
\mathcal{P}_{i}
-cliques,
i=1,2
.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{1}
,
\mathcal{P}_{2}
, and
\mathcal{R}
be as defined above. Then, a.a.s.,

z_{\mathcal{P}_{i}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(2)}_{n})=\Theta(n^{1/2}),\quad i=1,2,

while

z_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(2)}_{n})\sim\frac{n}{2}.

Let us remark that the first formula was only mentioned without proof in the Final Remarks section of
[
3
]
, so we prove it in Section
5.3
. The second formula follows from Theorem 1.2 in
[
3
]
and was also obtained (much) earlier by Scheinerman
[
17
]
, via a different approach.

The reason behind such a diverse behavior of random variables
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(2)}_{n})
with the same size of
\mathcal{P}
is structural. Indeed, the pairs of patterns appearing in
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
are not harmonious, while the patterns in
\mathcal{R}
are.
In fact,
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}^{(2)}
, which
leads to a reformulation of the second statement in Theorem
1.2
:
The maximum size of a 2-partite sub-matching in
\mathbb{RM}^{(2)}_{n}
is asymptotically equal to
n/2
. This reformulation is legitimate, because, more generally, it is easy to see that for every
r\geq 2
a matching is
r
-partite if and only if it is an
\mathcal{R}^{(r)}
-clique.

2.
New results

Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
quoted in Introduction give us a full picture of the asymptotic behavior of
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(2)}_{n})
, the size of the largest
\mathcal{P}
-clique in
(\mathbb{RM}^{(2)}_{n})
, for
r=2
.
For
r\geq 3
our grasp on
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}_{n})
is still far from complete. In this section we present our new results.

2.1.
Generalizations of Theorem
1.2

One case in which we are able to pinpoint the value of
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})
quite precisely is when
\mathcal{P}
consists of
all

r
-partite
r
-patterns, that is, when
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{R}^{(r)}
. Generalizing the second part of Theorem
1.2
, we show that the largest
\mathcal{R}^{(r)}
-clique in
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
has size linear in
n
and find the value of the multiplicative constant asymptotically.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
\mathcal{R}^{(r)}
be the set of all
r
-partite
r
-patterns. Then, a.a.s.,

z_{\mathcal{R}^{(r)}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})\sim\frac{(r-1)!}{r^{r-1}}n.

As noted before, any
r
-partite sub-matching of
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(r)}
is an
\mathcal{R}^{(r)}
-clique, hence the same asymptotic formula holds for the maximum size of such a sub-matching in
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
. The following statement is a convenient reformulation of Theorem
2.1
.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
R_{n}^{(r)}
be the largest (lexicographically first)
r
-partite sub-matching of
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
. Then, a.a.s.,

|R_{n}^{(r)}|\sim\frac{(r-1)!}{r^{r-1}}n.

Another case in which we can asymptotically determine
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})
for all
r\geq 2
is that of
all
sets of
r
-patterns
\mathcal{P}
with
|\mathcal{P}|=2
. Generalizing Theorem
1.2
in a different direction than in Theorem
2.1
we are able to prove the following result.
We call a pair of
r
-patterns
\{P,Q\}
a
mismatch
if one of them is collectable, while the other is not, or both are collectable, but then not harmonious. For instance, for
r=3
, the pairs
P_{0}=AABABB,P_{1}=AAABBB
and
P_{1}=AAABBB,P_{2}=AABBBA
are both mismatches. When
\{P,Q\}
is a mismatch, we will also say that
P
and
Q
are
mismatched
.

Theorem 2.3
.

For
r\geq 2
, let
\mathcal{P}=\{P,Q\}
be a pair of distinct
r
-patterns. Then, a.a.s.,

z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})=\begin{cases}\Theta(n^{1/(r-1)})&\text{if $P$ and $Q$ are harmonious,}\\
\Theta(n^{1/r})&\text{if $P$ and $Q$ form a mismatch,}\\
z&\text{if neither $P$ nor $Q$ is collectable,}\end{cases}

where
z=z_{\mathcal{P}}
is an integer,
2\leq z\leq 5
.

It is worth noticing that the thresholds for
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})
in the above theorem do not depend on
\lambda_{P}
or
\lambda_{Q}
but only on
r
and the relation between
P
and
Q
. As we will demonstrate in the proof, the constant
z
appearing in the last statement equals the largest size of a
\{P,Q\}
-clique whatsoever.

2.2.
Boolean cubes

The most challenging part of Theorem
2.3
concerns harmonious pairs. The approach we are using in that case, however, is applicable in a far more general setting of
arbitrarily large yet highly symmetric sets of patterns.
In order to describe them, let us consider a collectable pattern
P

with
\lambda_{P}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{s})
and its splitting
P=S_{1}\cdots S_{s}
into blocks of sizes
|S_{i}|=2\lambda_{i}
. Next, let a partition
\pi
of the form
[s]=T_{0}\cup T_{1}\cup\cdots\cup T_{t}
be given, with
1\in T_{0}
and no
T_{j}=\varnothing
.
For
j=0,\dots,t
, set
P_{j}
for the concatenation of segments
S_{i}
, with
i\in T_{j}
. Let us call the
P_{j}
’s the
mega-blocks
of
P
.

A
flip
of a mega-block involves exchanging all letters
A
for
B
and vice versa,
B
for
A
, in this mega-block. For a subset
F\subseteq\{1,\dots,t\}
, let
P_{F}
be the pattern obtained from
P
by flipping all mega-blocks
P_{j}
with
j\in F
. Clearly, the patterns
P
and
P_{F}
are harmonious.
For instance, for
P
as in (
1.1
) and
\pi
given by
[8]=\{1,3,5\}\cup\{2,8\}\cup\{4,6,7\}
, the pattern
Q=AB|\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{AABB}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{|BA|AAABBB|BBAA|BA|AB|}\color[rgb]{0,0,1}BA
is obtained by flipping the mega-block
P_{1}=S_{2}S_{8}
, so we have
Q=P_{\{1\}}
.

Definition
.

Any set of
r
-patterns of the form

\mathcal{C}(P,\pi)=\{P_{F}:\;\;F\subseteq[t]\}

is called a
t
-cube
or just
cube
if the dimension is not specified.

To explain this terminology note that
\mathcal{C}(P,\pi)
corresponds one-to-one to the Boolean cube
\{A,B\}^{t}
, where the letter on the
j
-th position means that the
j
-th mega-block begins with that letter. For practical purposes we distinguish one pattern (
P
) as a sort of creator of the cube, though, due to symmetry, every pattern of the cube could play that role.
Clearly,
|\mathcal{C}(P,\pi)|=2^{t}
.

.

The following set of four 12-patterns

.

Notice that for any collectable pattern

\mathcal{P}(\lambda_{P})
of all patterns harmonious with

is an

-cube, where

. Indeed,
\mathcal{P}(\lambda_{P})=\mathcal{C}(P,\pi)
with

being the partition of

into singletons. At the other extreme, observe that a 0-cube is just the singleton set

, while if

{\mathcal{P}}_{P}(\pi)
, where

is the partition of

The main result of this paper determines asymptotically the size of the largest
\mathcal{C}(P,\pi)
-clique in
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
for any cube
\mathcal{C}(P,\pi)
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
r\geq 2
,
P

3.
Operations on matchings

In this short section we define a couple of operations on matchings which will be used throughout the paper.

The first operation “glues” disjoint matchings of equal size. Let
V
be an ordered set and let
M_{1}=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{k}\}
be an
r_{1}
-matching on vertex set
V_{1}
and
M_{2}=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\}
be an
r_{2}
-matching on vertex set
V_{2}
, with
V_{1}\cup V_{2}\subseteq V
and
V_{1}\cap V_{2}=\varnothing
(the edges of the matchings are numbered in the order of their left-ends). For a permutation
\sigma:[k]\to[k]
define the
\sigma
-concatenation

M_{1}\overset{\sigma}{-}M_{2}
of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
as the
(r_{1}+r_{2})
-matching with the edge set
\{e_{1}\cup f_{\sigma(1)},\dots,e_{k}\cup f_{\sigma(k)}\}
. This definition can be extended naturally to
(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{h})
-concatenations of
h+1
matchings

M_{1}\overset{\sigma_{1}}{-}M_{2}\overset{\sigma_{2}}{-}\cdots\overset{\sigma_{h}}{-}M_{h+1}
.
For instance, having in addition an
r_{3}
-matching
M_{3}=\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\}
on vertex set
V_{3}\subset V
disjoint from
V_{1}\cup V_{2}
,

M_{1}\overset{\sigma_{1}}{-}M_{2}\overset{\sigma_{2}}{-}M_{3}

has the edge set
\{e_{1}\cup f_{\sigma_{1}(1)}\cup g_{\sigma_{2}(1)},\dots,e_{k}\cup f_{\sigma_{1}(k)}\cup g_{\sigma_{2}(k)}\}
.

Further, define the
product
of
M_{1},\dots,M_{h+1}
as

M_{1}\times M_{2}\times\cdots\times M_{h+1}=\bigcup_{(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{h})}M_{1}\overset{\sigma_{1}}{-}M_{2}\overset{\sigma_{2}}{-}\cdots\overset{\sigma_{h}}{-}M_{h+1}

where the set union runs over all
t
-tuples of permutations of
[k]
. Equivalently,

M_{1}\times M_{2}\times\cdots\times M_{h+1}=\{e^{(1)}\cup e^{(2)}\cup\ldots\cup e^{(h+1)}:e^{(1)}\in M_{1},e^{(2)}\in M_{2},\ldots,e^{(h+1)}\in M_{h+1}\}.

Finally, let us define an operation on a single matching. For an
r
-matching
M
and an integer
1\leq j\leq r
, let
M^{-j}
denote the
(r-1)
-matching obtained by removing the
j
-th letter from each edge of
M
. For instance, if
M=AABCBDBCCDDA
, then
M^{-1}=ABBCCDDA
,
M^{-2}=ABCDBCDA
, and
M^{-3}=AABCBDCD
.
In particular, this removal operation can be applied to
r
-patterns. For example,
if
P=AABBBABA
, then
P^{-1}=P^{-2}=ABBABA
, while
P^{-3}=AABBBA
. Clearly, if
P
is collectable, then so is
P^{-j}
for every
j
, but the opposite does not necessarily hold.

4.1.
Concentration inequalities for random matchings

Recall that
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
denotes a random ordered
r
-uniform matching of size
n
, that is, a matching picked uniformly at random out of the set
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(r)}
of all

\alpha^{(r)}_{n}:=\frac{(rn)!}{(r!)^{n}\,n!}

ordered
r
-matchings on the set
[rn]
. For future reference note that the probability that a given
r
-element subset
e
of
[rn]
is an edge of
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
is

(4.1)

\Pr(e\in\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})=\frac{\alpha^{(r)}_{n-1}}{\alpha^{(r)}_{n}}=\frac{1}{\binom{rn-1}{r-1}}.

There is another, equivalent way of drawing
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
, the
permutational scheme
.
Indeed, let
\pi
be a permutation of
[rn]
. It can be chopped off into an
r
-matching

M_{\pi}:=\left\{\{\pi(1),\dots,\pi(r)\},\{\pi(r+1),\dots,\pi(2r)\},\dots,\{\pi(rn-r+1),\dots,\pi(rn)\}\right\}

and, clearly, there are exactly
(r!)^{n}n!
permutations
\pi
yielding the same matching.
This amounts to a basic fact that the probability of choosing a matching with given features while picking it uniformly at random is the same in the case when we pick a random permutation instead, and then chop it into the matchings’ edges.

We will benefit from this straightforward observation, as it allows direct applications of
high concentration results for random permutations in the context of ordered matchings. By high concentration we mean that a random variable is very close to its expectation with probability very close to 1.

One of them is the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality for random permutations (see, e.g., Lemma 11 in
[
6
]
or Section 3.2 in
[
12
]
).
By swapping two elements in a permutation
\pi_{1}
we mean fixing two indices
i<j
and creating a new permutation
\pi_{2}
with
\pi_{2}(i)=\pi_{1}(j)
,
\pi_{2}(j)=\pi_{1}(i)
, and
\pi_{2}(\ell)=\pi_{1}(\ell)
for all
\ell\neq i,j
. Let
\Pi_{N}
denote a permutation selected uniformly at random from all
N!
permutations of
[N]
.

Lemma 4.1

(
[
6
,
12
]
)
.

Let
h(\pi)
be a function defined on the set of all permutations of order
N
which, for some positive constants
c
, satisfies the following Lipschitz-type condition:

(L)

if
\pi_{2}
is obtained from
\pi_{1}
by swapping two elements, then
|h(\pi_{1})-h(\pi_{2})|\leq c
.

Then, for every
\eta>0
,

\Pr(|h(\Pi_{N})-{\mathds{E}}[h(\Pi_{N})]|\geq\eta)\leq 2\exp\{-2\eta^{2}/(c^{2}N)\}.

Lemma
4.1
yields sharp concentration only when
{\mathds{E}}[h(\Pi_{N})]\gg\sqrt{N}
.
This is not sufficient for the proof of the lower bound in Theorem
2.4
and therefore we will use there
a Talagrand’s concentration inequality from
[
19
]
, which can be applied whenever the expectation tends to infinity. The drawbacks are that it requires another, so called “certificate” condition and that it is expressed in terms of the median rather than the expectation.

We quote here a slightly simplified version from
[
9
]
(see also
[
13
]
).

Lemma 4.2

(
[
9
,
13
]
)
.

Let
h(\pi)
be a function defined on the set of all permutations of order
N
which, for some positive constants
c
and
d
, satisfies

(L)

if
\pi_{2}
is obtained from
\pi_{1}
by swapping two elements, then
|h(\pi_{1})-h(\pi_{2})|\leq c
;

(C)

for each
\pi
and

, if
h(\pi)=s
, then in order to show that
h(\pi)\geq s
, one needs to specify only at most

\pi(i)
.

Then, for every
\varepsilon>0

\Pr(|h({\Pi}_{N})-m|\geq\varepsilon m)\leq 4\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}m/(32dc^{2})),

where

is the median of
h({\Pi}_{N})
.

We will now employ Lemma
4.1
to prove Theorem
2.1
. The second concentration lemma, Lemma

will be used in the main proof in Section

4.2.

For most upper bound proofs in this paper we use a standard first moment method. Here we state a generic, technical “meta-statement” which will be applied throughout. For a set of

-cliques of size

Lemma 4.3

For

\mathcal{P}
be a set of

Proof.

For each

, let

be the number of
\mathcal{P}
-cliques of size

.
In order to compute the expectation of

-clique will be planted. Formally, for each

Thus,

and by the linearity of expectation, noticing that

{\mathds{E}}X_{k}=\binom{rn}{rk}a_{\mathcal{P}}(k)\frac{\alpha^{(r)}_{n-k}}{\alpha^{(r)}_{n}}=\frac{(r!)^{k}}{(rk)!}\cdot a_{\mathcal{P}}(k)\cdot\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}\leq\left(\frac{C^{\prime}n}{k^{r-x}}\right)^{k},

Then, by Markov’s inequality,

Finally note that

. ∎

5.
Combinatorial tools

In this section we present several combinatorial tools and apply them right away to prove some of our results.

5.1.
Posets on matchings

As remarked in
[
7
]
, one can often define a partially ordered set (poset) on the edges of a random matching
\mathbb{RM}_{n}^{(r)}
and then utilize Dilworth’s Lemma, which we state here in a version most suited for us.

Lemma 5.1

(Dilworth, 1950)
.

If the largest chain in a finite poset
(X,\prec)
has size at most
a
, then the largest anti-chain in
(X,\prec)
has size at least
|X|/a
.

A general template of how to proceed in such scenarios is described in the next lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{(r)}
be a set of
r
-patterns and let
N
be a
\mathcal{P}
-clique in an
r
-matching
M
.
Suppose that for a subset
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathcal{P}
, a poset
(N,\prec)
can be defined in such a way that for all
e,f\in N
we have
e\prec f
if
\min e<\min f
and
e,f
form an
r
-pattern from
\mathcal{Q}
.
Then,
z_{\mathcal{Q}^{c}}(M)\geq|N|/z_{\mathcal{Q}}(M)
, where
\mathcal{Q}^{c}=\mathcal{P}\smallsetminus\mathcal{Q}
.

Proof.

Note that for such a poset, every chain is a
\mathcal{Q}
-clique, while every anti-chain is a
\mathcal{Q}^{c}
-clique. As
N\subseteq M
, by monotonicity,
z_{\mathcal{Q}}(N)\leq z_{\mathcal{Q}}(M)
and
z_{\mathcal{Q}^{c}}(N)\leq z_{\mathcal{Q}^{c}}(M)
. By Lemma
5.1
applied to
(N,\prec)
we thus conclude that

z_{\mathcal{Q}^{c}}(M)\geq z_{\mathcal{Q}^{c}}(N)\geq|N|/z_{\mathcal{Q}}(N)\geq|N|/z_{\mathcal{Q}}(M).

∎

In this paper we benefit from Lemma
5.2
only in the case of
r
-partite patterns, relying, in addition, on Corollary
2.2
. We now deduce the lower bound in Proposition
2.6
from the upper bound in Theorem
2.4
.

Proof of Proposition
2.6
, lower bound.

Let

, where

for

, and let

be a partition

such that

and all other parts

are singletons. Further, let

, and let

. Recall that

denote the largest

5.2.
Pattern avoiding permutations

For the proof of Proposition
2.5
we will need a result of Gunby and Pálvölgyi from
[
7

concerning parallel pattern avoidance by permutations.

For integers

, let
\sigma
be a permutation of
[k]
and let
\tau
be a permutation of
[m]
(called pattern). We say that
\sigma

avoids

such that the sub-permutation

is order-isomorphic to permutation

. This notion can be generalized in a natural way to sequences of permutations. Let
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{d})
be a
d
-tuple of permutations of
[k]
and let
\tau=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{d})
be a
d
-tuple of permutations of
[m]
(patterns). We say that
\sigma

avoids

\tau
if there is no sequence
a_{1}<\cdots<a_{m}
such that sub-permutation
\sigma_{i}(a_{1}),\dots,\sigma_{i}(a_{m})
is order-isomorphic to permutation
\tau_{i}
for

. Given a
d
-tuple

of permutations of
[m]
, let

denote the number of

-tuples of permutations of

avoiding

Generalizing the celebrated result by Markus and Tardos
[

]
, Gunby and Pálvölgyi proved the following estimates on

Theorem 5.3

(
[
7
]
)
.

Let

and

be a fixed

-tuple of permutations of
[m]
. Then there exist positive constants

and

(depending on

c_{1}^{k}\cdot k^{k(d-1/d)}\leqslant f_{d}(k)\leqslant c_{2}^{k}\cdot k^{k(d-1/d)}.

Proof of Proposition
2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}(P,\pi)
be a

and all other notation is as in the proof of the upper bound in Theorem

presented in Section

are all non-empty, and

. Further, set

with

, and

If we were after the

5.3.
Traces of matchings and reconstruction

In some proofs of upper bounds, in order to estimate the number
a_{\mathcal{P}}(k)
of distinct
\mathcal{P}
-cliques of size
k
appearing in Lemma
4.3
, we employ the concept of the trace of a matching introduced in
[
3
]
.

Let
M
be an ordered
r
-matching of size
n
. Assign number
i
to the
i
-th (from the left) vertex of each edge of
M
. The obtained
r
-ary sequence (with alphabet
[r]
) will be called the
trace of
M
and denoted by
\tr(M)
. For instance,
for
M=AABCBDBDACCD
, we have
\tr(M)=121121323233
. In general, there may be many matchings with the same trace, e.g.,
M^{\prime}=AABCCDADDBBC
has
\tr(M^{\prime})=\tr(M)
.
However, for some restricted families of matchings
\mathcal{F}
it may happen that no two members of
\mathcal{F}
have the same trace. Then, knowing the trace of a matching
M\in\mathcal{F}
, one is able to uniquely reconstruct
M
. In that case we call the family
\mathcal{F}

reconstructible
.
Using this concept, we obtain the following upper bound on the size of the largest
\mathcal{P}
-clique in
\mathbb{RM}_{n}^{(r)}
for a class of sets
\mathcal{P}
.

Observation 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of
r
-patterns such that the family of all
\mathcal{P}
-cliques is reconstructible. Then,a.a.s.,
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}_{n}^{(r)})=O(n^{1/r})
.

Proof.

By assumption, there are no more
\mathcal{P}
-cliques on a vertex set of size
rk
than there are words of length
rk
over alphabet
[r]
. So,
a_{\mathcal{P}}(k)\leqslant r^{rk}
and the conclusion follows by Lemma
4.3
with
x=0
and
C=r^{r}
.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

In fact, traces of ordered matchings have a special structure: for every pair
i<j
, in every prefix of the trace
\tr(M)
the number of occurrences of
i
is not smaller than the number of occurrences of
j
. Thus, for
r=2
the traces form the well known family of Dyck words defined already in Section
2.3
and enumerated by
Catalan numbers

C_{k}=\frac{1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k}
. For arbitrary
r\geqslant 2
one gets families of
r
-dimensional Dyck words
enumerated jointly in table A060854 of the OEIS date base (cf.
[
14
]
).
Due to the robustness of our estimates in Lemma
4.3
, their exact enumeration is, however, irrelevant for us. To distinguish from Dyck words over “letter” alphabets, we will be referring to Dyck words over digits
1,2,...
as
Dyck sequences
.

When reconstructing a matching from its trace we will be adopting two conventions: always scanning the trace from left to right and introducing letters in the alphabetical order. Under these assumptions, we will be applying one of two specific rules or their mixture.

The
left-hand rule
assigns to the current digit
i\geq 2
of the trace the leftmost available letter, that is, the alphabetically first letter which have appeared so far exactly
i-1
times.
(Equivalently, one fills up all positions occupied by digit
i=2,\dots,r
in
\tr(M)
by the sequence of all letters representing the edges in alphabetical order.) For example, from trace
121121323233
, the left-hand rule reconstructs matching
AABCBDACBDCD
.

The
right-hand rule
assigns to the current digit
i\geq 2
of the trace the rightmost available letter, that is, the alphabetically last letter which have appeared so far exactly
i-1
times. For example, from the same trace
121121323233
the right-hand rule reconstructs matching

AABCCDCDDBBA
.

As a warm-up, we will now apply Observation
5.4
to prove the first part of Theorem
1.2
stated in Introduction.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
, first part.

In view of Theorem
1.1
and monotonicity, it suffices to show the upper bound. Recall our notation:
\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{AABB,ABAB\}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}=\{AABB,ABBA\}
.

It is well known (see, e.g.,
[
18
]
) that the numbers of
\mathcal{P}_{1}
-cliques and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
-cliques on a given set of
2k
vertices are both equal to the Catalan number. (Note that in the terminology of
[
18
]

\mathcal{P}_{1}
-cliques are the same as
ABBA
-free, or nesting-free matchings, while
\mathcal{P}_{2}
-cliques are the same as
ABAB
-free, or crossing-free matchings.)

The first statement can be verified by applying the
left-hand rule
when decoding the trace of an
ABBA
-free matching. Note that this rule never creates pattern
ABBA
, while, on the other hand, any alteration of this rule leads to a pattern
ABBA
. So, it yields a bijection between all
ABBA
-free matchings on vertex set
[2k]
and all binary Dyck sequences of the same length. Similarly, the
right-hand rule
never creates pattern
ABAB
, so it establishes a bijection between
ABAB
-free matchings and the Dyck sequences.

It follows that both families are reconstructible
and, by Observation
5.4
,
z_{\mathcal{P}_{1}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(2)}_{n})=O(n^{1/2})
and
z_{\mathcal{P}_{2}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(2)}_{n})=O(n^{1/2})
.
∎

We split the proof into two cases according to the mismatch definition.

. For clarity of presentation, assume that

and
\ell=n^{1-\frac{1}{r-t}}
are integers (the general case brings only additional
(1-o(1))
multiplicative factors in the estimates, which have a negligible effect.)

Set

Q=(AB)^{r}
for convenience and let
\pi
be a partition of the form
[r]=T_{0}\cup T_{1}\cup\dots\cup T_{t}
where the
T_{i}
’s are blocks of consecutive integers of length
r_{i}
, that is,
T_{0}=[r_{0}]
,
T_{1}=\{r_{0}+1,\dots,r_{0}+r_{1}\}
, and so on. Further, let

w_{1}^{1},w_{2}^{1},\dots,w_{k}^{1},w_{1}^{2},w_{2}^{2},\dots,w_{k}^{2},\ldots,w_{1}^{r},w_{2}^{r},\dots,w_{k}^{r}

be the (linearly ordered) vertices of
H_{k}(Q,\pi)
(cf. Figure

).

Suppose a copy
H
of the ordered blow-up
H_{k}(Q,\pi)^{(\ell)}
of
H_{k}(Q,\pi)

is fixed on the vertex set
[rk\ell]

and let

W_{1}^{1},W_{2}^{1},\dots,W_{k}^{1},W_{1}^{2},W_{2}^{2},\dots,W_{k}^{2},\ldots,W_{1}^{r},W_{2}^{r},\dots,W_{k}^{r}

be the corresponding blow-up sets (each of size
\ell
).

For an edge of
\mathbb{RM}_{n}^{(r)}
to belong to a scattered matching of
H
, it is necessary that it takes at most one vertex from each set
W^{i}_{h}
and, most importantly, within the blow-up of each
Q_{j}
-clique
K_{j}
, the
r_{j}
sets
W^{i}_{h}
from which it takes its vertices have the same lower index (this is because
Q_{j}
is an
r_{j}
-wave). Consider, for example, a blow-up of the hypergraph in Figure
7.4
. Here a good edge should take one vertex from each set
W^{1}_{h_{0}},W^{2}_{h_{0}},W^{3}_{h_{0}},W^{4}_{h_{0}}
,
one vertex from each set
W^{5}_{h_{1}},W^{6}_{h_{1}},W^{7}_{h_{1}}
, and one vertex from each set
W^{8}_{h_{2}},W^{9}_{h_{2}},W^{10}_{h_{2}},W^{11}_{h_{2}},W^{12}_{h_{2}}

for some
h_{0},h_{1},h_{2}\in[3]
.

Generally, an edge
e\in\mathbb{RM}_{n}^{(r)}
is deemed
good
if, for some vector
\omega=(h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{t})\in[k]^{t+1}
,
e
spans the sets

W_{h_{0}}^{1},W_{h_{0}}^{2},\dots,W_{h_{0}}^{r_{0}},W_{h_{1}}^{r_{0}+1},W_{h_{1}}^{r_{0}+2},\dots,W_{h_{1}}^{r_{0}+r_{1}},\dots,W_{h_{t}}^{r_{0}+\dots+r_{t-1}+1},W_{h_{t}}^{r_{0}+\dots+r_{t-1}+2},\dots,W_{h_{t}}^{r}.

We then say that such an edge is of
type

\omega
.

A pair of vectors
\omega=(h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{t})
and
\omega^{\prime}=(h^{\prime}_{0},h^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,h^{\prime}_{t})
is called
totally distinct
if
h_{j}\neq h^{\prime}_{j}
for all
j=0,\dots,t
.
A pair of good edges
e,e^{\prime}
of types
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
, respectively, is called

if the pair
(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
is totally distinct.
Note that a set of pairwise separated good edges is simply a scattered
H_{k}(Q,\pi)^{(\ell)}
-matching.

Let
X
be the largest size of a scattered
H_{k}(Q,\pi)^{(\ell)}
-matching in
\mathbb{RM}_{n}^{(r)}
.
We will show that
{\mathds{E}}X=\Omega(k)
and afterwards apply Lemma
4.2
to get
X=\Omega(k)
a.a.s.
In order to bound the expectation of
X
we will use the deletion method. To this end, let
Y
be the number of good edges in
\mathbb{RM}_{n}^{(r)}
. Since there are
k^{t+1}
choices of a vector
\omega=(h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{t})
and
|W_{h}^{j}|\equiv\ell
, we have, by (
4.1
),

{\mathds{E}}Y=k^{t+1}\ell^{r}\cdot\frac{1}{\binom{rn-1}{r-1}}\geq k^{t+1}\ell^{r}\frac{(r-1)!}{(rn)^{r-1}}=kn^{\frac{t}{r-t}}n^{r-\frac{r}{r-t}}\frac{(r-1)!}{(rn)^{r-1}}=\frac{(r-1)!}{r^{r-1}}k.

Now, let
Z
count non-separated pairs
\{e,e^{\prime}\}
of good edges. Let
\omega=(h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{t})
and
\omega^{\prime}=(h_{0}^{\prime},h_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,h_{t}^{\prime})
be the types of
e
and
e^{\prime}
, respectively. Since
e
and
e^{\prime}
are not separated,
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
are not totally distinct and, consequently, there must be at least one index
j\in\{0,\dots,t\}
such that
h_{j}=h_{j}^{\prime}
. Hence, for a fixed
e
the number of choices for
e^{\prime}
is at most
(t+1)k^{t}\ell^{r}\leq rk^{t}\ell^{r}
and

{\mathds{E}}Z\leq\frac{1}{2}k^{t+1}\ell^{r}rk^{t}\ell^{r}\cdot\frac{1}{\binom{rn-1}{r-1}\binom{rn-r-1}{r-1}}={\mathds{E}}Y\cdot rk^{t}\ell^{r}\cdot\frac{1}{2\binom{rn-r-1}{r-1}}\leq{\mathds{E}}Y\cdot\frac{r!}{r^{r-1}}.

For
r=2
one may apply a slightly more subtle estimates to get, say,
{\mathds{E}}Z\leq\frac{2}{3}{\mathds{E}}Y
(for
n
large enough).

Consider all
Y
good edges in
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
. Among them are
Z
non-separated pairs of good edges.
After removing one edge from each non-separated pair, we get a set of good, pairwise separated edges, and thus, a scattered matching of size at least
Y-Z
. Therefore,

(7.1)

{\mathds{E}}X\geq{\mathds{E}}(Y-Z)={\mathds{E}}Y-{\mathds{E}}Z\geq{\mathds{E}}Y\left(1-\frac{r!}{r^{r-1}}\right)\geq\frac{(r-1)!}{r^{r-1}}\left(1-\frac{r!}{r^{r-1}}\right)k=\Omega_{r}(k)

for
r\geq 3
, and
{\mathds{E}}X\geq\frac{1}{6}k=\Omega(k)
for
r=2
.

It remains to apply Lemma
4.2
to show sharp concentration of
X
aroundits median
m
, and, ultimately, around
{\mathds{E}}X
. Recall (cf. Section
4.1
) that
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
can be generated by using the permutational scheme. Thus, one can view
X
as a function
h
of an
N
-permutation
\Pi_{N}
with
N=rn
, that is,
X=h({\Pi}_{N})
. Notice that swapping two elements of
\Pi_{N}
affects at most two edges of
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
, so it changes the value of
h
by at most 2. Moreover, to exhibit the event
X\geq s
, it is sufficient to reveal a scattered
H_{k}(Q,\pi)^{(\ell)}
-matching
of size
s
. Since every edge in such a matching can be encoded by specifying
r
values of
\Pi_{N}
, it suffices to reveal only
rs
values of
\Pi_{N}
.
Hence, Theorem
4.2
, applied with
N=rn
,
c=2
,
d=r
and
\varepsilon=1/2
, implies that

(7.2)

\Pr(|X-m|\geq m/2)\leq 4\exp(-m/(512r)).

To finish the proof and show sharp concentration of
X
around
{\mathds{E}}X
we need to switch from median
m
to expectation
\mu:={\mathds{E}}X
. Since,under concentration (
7.2
),
|m-\mu|=O(\sqrt{m})
(see for example
[
19
]
or Lemma 4.6 in
[
11
]
) and, by (
7.1
),
\mu\to\infty
, it follows that
m\to\infty
too and, in particular,
|m-\mu|\leq(1/12)\mu
. This and (
7.2
) imply together that
\Pr(|X-m|\geq m/2)=o(1)
and, consequently,

\displaystyle\Pr(|X-\mu|\geq(3/4)\mu)=\Pr(|X-m+m-\mu|\geq(3/4)\mu)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr(|X-m|+|m-\mu|\geq(3/4)\mu)\leq\Pr(|X-m|+(1/12)\mu\geq(3/4)\mu)

\displaystyle=\Pr(|X-m|\geq(2/3)\mu)\leq\Pr(|X-m|\geq m/2)=o(1).

Hence, a.a.s.
X\geq(1/4)\mu=(1/4){\mathds{E}}X
, which combined with (
7.1
) finishes the proof of Lemma
7.3
.

8.
Open problems

8.1.
Orders of magnitude

There are several sets of
r
-patterns
\mathcal{P}
for which the order of magnitude of the size of the largest
\mathcal{P}
-clique remains unknown. For instance, we wonder if
one can match the upper bound in Proposition
2.5
with a lower bound of the same order of magnitude. We were only able to do it in the special case of
r
-partite patterns (cf. Proposition
2.6
).

An intriguing question pops up in the context of Theorem
2.4
. Recall that a
t
-cube
\mathcal{C}(P,\pi)
has size
2^{t}
for some
t\geq 1
and

z_{\mathcal{C}(P,\pi)}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})=\Theta(n^{1/(r-t)})
. We believe that, for every
r
and
t\leq r-1
, if
\mathcal{P}
is a set of
r
-patterns of size
|\mathcal{P}|=2^{t}
, then a.a.s.

z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})=O(n^{1/(r-t)}),

and moreover,
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})=\Theta(n^{1/(r-t)})
only if
\mathcal{P}
is a
t
-cube.

Ultimately, one would like to learn the spectrum of all functions
f(n)
for which there exist sets of
r
-patterns
\mathcal{P}
such that a.a.s.

z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})=\Theta(f(n))
. So far, all such functions have been constrained to the set
\{n^{1/r},n^{1/(r-1)},\dots,n\}
. Does the spectrum extend beyond that?

8.2.
Triplets of patterns

In view of Theorem
2.3
, a next realistic goal might be to find orders of magnitude of
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})
for all sets
\mathcal{P}
of triplets of
r
-patterns. Again, the most challenging case seems to be when at least two of these patterns are harmonious. So far, we only know that a.a.s.
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(3)}_{n})=\Theta(n^{2/3})
for any triplet of 3-partite 3-patterns (see Proposition
2.6
).

In contrast, the case when all three patterns in
\mathcal{P}
are pairwise non-harmonious and at least one is collectable, should be more tractable. (If all three are non-collectable, we know, by the argument opening Section
6
, that
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(3)}_{n})=\Theta(1)
.)
In fact, we conjecture that then a.a.s.
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})=\Theta(n^{1/r})
, the same as for collectable singletons and mismatch pairs. Again, we have confirmed this only for some triplets of 3-patterns (see Proposition
2.7
).

One obstacle in proving the conjecture is that the analog of Observation
5.6
holds only for
r\geq 5
. Indeed, it fails for a number of triplets of 4-patterns, e.g., for

\{ABABABAB,ABBAAABB,AABBBBAA\}
, and is doomed to fail for
all
triplets of 3-patterns, as there are only two different compositions for 2-patterns (2 and
1+1
), so after removing a letter, there must be a harmonious pair.

Thus, already for
r=3
we need either a new idea or try some ad hoc techniques for particular triplets.
For instance, for
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{2},P_{4},P_{9}\}
we are able to show reconstructability of
\mathcal{P}
-cliques by directly analyzing the structure of such cliques. On the other hand, for
\{P_{3},P_{5},P_{7}\}

even reconstructability fails, as the following counterexample shows:
M=ABBCAACBC
and
N=ABACCABBC
are both
\{P_{3},P_{5},P_{7}\}
-cliques with the same trace
\tr(M)=\tr(N)=112123233
.
Recall, however, that as remarked at the end of Section
5
, reconstructability was not vital for the proof of Theorem
2.3
for mismatched pairs.

8.3.
The role of non-collectable patterns

We already know that, even deterministically, the largest
\mathcal{P}
-cliques for sets
\mathcal{P}
consisting entirely of non-collectable patterns have size
\Theta(1)
. It is plausible that they are likewise meaningless in combination with collectable patterns. In particular, we speculate that for every set
\mathcal{P}
of collectable
r
-patterns and every set
\mathcal{Q}
of non-collectable
r
-patterns, a.a.s.
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})=\Theta(z_{\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{Q}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}))
.

We know it is true when
|\mathcal{P}|=|\mathcal{Q}|=1
(cf. Theorem
2.3
, the mismatch case) as well as when
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{R}^{(r)}
, the set of all
r
-partite
r
-patterns (cf. Theorem
2.1
) as then a.a.s.
z_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})=\Theta(n)
. Two other instances confirming this speculation are provided by Propositions
2.7
and
2.8
.

In the context of Theorem
2.1
, a related question emerges: does
z_{\mathcal{R}^{(r)}}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})
increase if one replaces
\mathcal{R}^{(r)}
with the set of all collectable
r
-patterns.
More specifically, a matching is deemed
clean
if every pair of edges forms a collectable pattern. Estimate the largest clean sub-matching of
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
, that is, find a constant
c_{r}
such that a.a.s. the size of a largest clean sub-matching in
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
is
\sim c_{r}n
. By Theorem
2.1
we know that
c\geq(r-1)!/r^{r-1}
.
(The deterministic version of this question seems also interesting and is probably harder.)

8.4.
Matching processes

There is yet another way of generating
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
, besides the uniform sampling and the permutational scheme described in Section
4.1
.
In the
online scheme
, for an ordered set of
rn
vertices, sequentially, for each next available vertex
v
a set of
r-1
vertices is selected uniformly at random (from all still available vertices) to form with
v
an
r
-element edge.

In this paper we have been interested in the final stage of the process, that is, in
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
.
But ultimately, one could try to get asymptotic properties of the sub-matching formed by the first
t
edges of the process,
t=1,\dots,n
. Note that it is not the same as
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{t}
, except for
t=n
. Indeed, even for
r=t=2
the probability of getting
AABB
is exactly
1/(2n-1)
but each of
ABAB
and
ABBA
appears with probability
(n-1)/(2n-1)
. Hence, in contrary to
\mathbb{RM}^{(2)}_{n}
, for
n\geq 3
not all matchings occur with the same probability.

In relation to this paper, the goal might be, for a given set
\mathcal{P}
of
r
-patterns and any sequence
1\leq t=t(n)\leq n
, to determine the order of magnitude of the size of a largest
\mathcal{P}
-clique in the ordered matching
t
-process, and reversely, given a function
k=k(n)
, to determine the threshold
t=t(n)
(or even the hitting time) for the
t
-process to grow a
\mathcal{P}
-clique of size
k(n)
.
