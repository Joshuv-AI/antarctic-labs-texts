Three-sign cancellation hypernumber systems and associator curvature
Jaehwan Kim

Abstract
. We introduce and study a three–sign cancellation hypernumber system H H which extends the real field by adjoining a third sign Λ \Lambda . The underlying set is H = { 0 } ∪ { + , − , Λ } × ℝ > 0 H=\{0\}\cup\{+,-,\Lambda\}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0} , with a single–valued multiplication and a hyperaddition ⊞ \boxplus designed to encode cancellation phenomena between positive and negative reals. The classical real line embeds as a genuine subfield ℝ cl ⊂ H \mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}\subset H , and all field operations agree with the usual ones on ℝ \mathbb{R} . The additive structure of H H is almost associative but not a canonical hypergroup. We give an explicit description of where associativity fails and compute, for triples of the form ( + , a ) , ( − , b ) , ( Λ , c ) (+,a),(-,b),(\Lambda,c) , a closed formula for the associativity defect 𝜿 ⁡ ( a , b , c ) = 2 ​ min ⁡ ( a , b ) = a + b − | a − b | , \boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=2\min(a,b)=a+b-|a-b|, which coincides with the loss of absolute value when adding a a and − b -b in ℝ \mathbb{R} . To explain this behaviour, we construct an ambient “cancellation monoid” ( K , ⊕ ) (K,\oplus) on ℝ × ℝ ≥ 0 \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} which is strictly associative and records both real sums and accumulated cancellation mass. We prove that H H cannot be recovered from K K by any simple projection, and formulate an ambient reconstruction problem. In addition, scalar multiplication by real numbers (defined via the embedded copy of ℝ \mathbb{R} ) distributes over ⊞ \boxplus , and the sign–layer admits a canonical hypergroup envelope governing the possible signs of hypersums. The results provide a controlled example of a nonassociative hyperaddition sitting over the real field and suggest several directions for multisign generalizations and connections with hyperfields and tropical geometry.

2.1.
Basic notation and real numbers

We recall here the standard notation for real numbers and fix a few
elementary facts that will be used repeatedly throughout the paper.

Definition 2.1

(Real line and subsets)
.

We write
\mathbb{R}
for the field of real numbers, and we use the following
standard subsets:

\mathbb{R}_{>0}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:x>0\},\qquad\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:x\geq 0\}.

We denote by

|\cdot|:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

the usual absolute value on
\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 2.2

(Elementary sign notation)
.

We use the symbols

+,\quad-,\quad\Lambda

to denote three distinguished “signs”. It is convenient to package
them into the sets

S:=\{0,+,-,\Lambda\},\qquad S^{\times}:=\{+,-,\Lambda\}.

Here
0
is merely a symbol indicating the absence of sign; it should
not be confused with the real number
0\in\mathbb{R}
, although in many
contexts the identification is harmless.

Later we will attach elements of
S^{\times}
as sign labels to positive
real magnitudes in order to form the three–sign hypernumber system

H
. For the classical real line, we will use the usual sign
function on
\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 2.3

(Sign of a real number)
.

Define
\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}:\mathbb{R}\to\{+,-,0\}\subset S
by

\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}(x):=\begin{cases}+,&x>0,\\
0,&x=0,\\
-,&x<0.\end{cases}

We record for later use the following elementary properties of the
absolute value. They are classical but play a central role in the
construction of the ambient cancellation monoid that will appear in
Section
6
.

Lemma 2.4

(Basic properties of the absolute value)
.

For all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
one has:

(1)

|x|\geq 0
, and
|x|=0
if and only if
x=0
.

(2)

|xy|=|x|\cdot|y|
.

(3)

|x+y|\leq|x|+|y|
(triangle inequality).

Proof.

These are the standard properties of the absolute value on
\mathbb{R}
. For
completeness we recall the usual arguments:

(1) By definition
|x|=\sqrt{x^{2}}\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, with equality
if and only if
x^{2}=0
, i.e.
x=0
.

(2) For all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,

|xy|=\sqrt{(xy)^{2}}=\sqrt{x^{2}y^{2}}=\sqrt{x^{2}}\,\sqrt{y^{2}}=|x|\cdot|y|.

(3) The triangle inequality
|x+y|\leq|x|+|y|
can be proved, for
example, by squaring both sides and expanding:

|x+y|^{2}=x^{2}+2xy+y^{2}\leq x^{2}+2|x||y|+y^{2}=(|x|+|y|)^{2},

so
|x+y|\leq|x|+|y|
since both sides are nonnegative.
∎

The triangle inequality allows us to define a nonnegative “cancellation
mass” associated to a pair of real numbers. This will later become
the second coordinate in the ambient monoid
K
.

Definition 2.5

(Cancellation mass on
\mathbb{R}
)
.

For
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
we define

C(r_{1},r_{2}):=|r_{1}|+|r_{2}|-|r_{1}+r_{2}|\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}.

Lemma 2.6

(Nonnegativity and symmetry of
C
)
.

For all
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
one has:

(1)

C(r_{1},r_{2})\geq 0
.

(2)

C(r_{1},r_{2})=C(r_{2},r_{1})
.

Proof.

(1) By the triangle inequality,

|r_{1}+r_{2}|\leq|r_{1}|+|r_{2}|,

so

C(r_{1},r_{2})=|r_{1}|+|r_{2}|-|r_{1}+r_{2}|\geq 0.

(2) Symmetry follows from the symmetry of absolute value and addition:

C(r_{1},r_{2})=|r_{1}|+|r_{2}|-|r_{1}+r_{2}|=|r_{2}|+|r_{1}|-|r_{2}+r_{1}|=C(r_{2},r_{1}).

∎

Remark 2.7

(Cancellation mass as lost absolute value)
.

If
r_{1},r_{2}
have the same sign, then
|r_{1}+r_{2}|=|r_{1}|+|r_{2}|
and

C(r_{1},r_{2})=0
. If
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
have opposite signs, then

C(r_{1},r_{2})
measures exactly how much absolute value is lost when
adding them. For example, when
r_{1}=a>0
and
r_{2}=-b<0
with
a,b>0
,
a short computation shows

C(a,-b)=a+b-|a-b|.

We will later see that this quantity coincides with the associativity
defect in certain triples of hypernumbers.

2.3.
Hyperoperations and canonical hypergroups

Literature.
Hypergroups were introduced by Marty and have since developed
into a broad hyperstructure theory; for historical context and modern
expositions see
[
5
,
13
]
.

We recall the basic language of hyperoperations and canonical
commutative hypergroups in the minimal form needed later. Throughout
this subsection
X
denotes a nonempty set.

Definition 2.8

(Hyperoperation)
.

A
hyperoperation
on
X
is a map

\boxplus:X\times X\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}(X)\setminus\{\emptyset\},

assigning to each pair
(x,y)\in X\times X
a nonempty subset

x\boxplus y\subset X
. The pair
(X,\boxplus)
is then called a

hypermagma
.

It is customary to think of
x\boxplus y
as the “set of possible
sums” of
x
and
y
. Ordinary binary operations are recovered as
special cases in which all these sets are singletons.

Definition 2.9

(Commutative hypermagma)
.

A hypermagma
(X,\boxplus)
is called
commutative
if

x\boxplus y=y\boxplus x\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in X.

Associativity in the hyper setting is formulated in terms of unions of
sets. Given
x,y,z\in X
, one considers the two subsets

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z:=\bigcup_{u\in x\boxplus y}u\boxplus z,\qquad x\boxplus(y\boxplus z):=\bigcup_{v\in y\boxplus z}x\boxplus v

of
X
.

Definition 2.10

(Associativity at a triple)
.

A hypermagma
(X,\boxplus)
is said to be
associative at the
triple

(x,y,z)\in X^{3}
if

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z\;=\;x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)

as subsets of
X
. It is called
associative
if this holds for
all triples
(x,y,z)\in X^{3}
.

In analogy with abelian groups, one often requires the existence of a
distinguished element playing the role of additive identity, together
with additive inverses.

Definition 2.11

(Neutral element and inverses)
.

Let
(X,\boxplus)
be a hypermagma.

(1)

An element
0\in X
is called a
neutral element
(or

additive identity
) if

0\boxplus x=x\boxplus 0=\{x\}\qquad\text{for all }x\in X.

(2)

Assume
0\in X
is a neutral element. An element
y\in X
is
called an
additive inverse
of
x\in X
if

0\in x\boxplus y.

When such a
y
exists and is unique, it will be denoted by
-x
.

Canonical commutative hypergroups arise when one imposes natural
axioms paralleling those of abelian groups. We use the following
formulation, which is standard in the literature on hyperrings.

Definition 2.12

(Canonical commutative hypergroup)
.

A
canonical commutative hypergroup
is a pair
(X,\boxplus)

consisting of a nonempty set
X
and a hyperoperation
\boxplus
such
that:

(HG1)

Commutativity:

x\boxplus y=y\boxplus x\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in X.

(HG2)

Associativity:
for all

x,y,z\in X
one has

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=x\boxplus(y\boxplus z).

(HG3)

Neutral element:
there exists a
distinguished element
0\in X
such that

0\boxplus x=x\boxplus 0=\{x\}\qquad\text{for all }x\in X.

(HG4)

Unique inverses:
for each

x\in X
there exists a unique element
y\in X
such that

0\in x\boxplus y,

and we denote this element by
-x
.

(HG5)

Reversibility:
for all

x,y,z\in X
one has

x\in y\boxplus z\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z\in x\boxplus(-y).

Remark 2.13
.

The reversibility axiom (HG5) is the characteristic feature of
canonical hypergroups and is crucial in the definition of hyperrings:
it ensures that additive translation behaves in a controlled way. In a
canonical commutative hypergroup, the neutral element
0
and the
inverse
-x
are uniquely determined, and the inverse map
x\mapsto-x

is an involution. We will use canonical hypergroups mainly as a
reference class: the additive structure
(H,\boxplus)
of our
three–sign system is
not
a canonical hypergroup, and part of
our analysis in later sections consists of pinpointing exactly which
of (HG2) and (HG5) fail.

The basic example to keep in mind is that of an ordinary abelian group
viewed as a hypergroup with singletons as sums.

Theorem 2.14

(Abelian groups as canonical hypergroups)
.

Let
(G,+)
be an abelian group with neutral element
0
and inverse
map
x\mapsto-x
. Define a hyperoperation
\boxplus
on
G
by

x\boxplus y:=\{x+y\}\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in G.

Then
(G,\boxplus)
is a canonical commutative hypergroup.

Proof.

We check the axioms (HG1)–(HG5).

(HG1) For all
x,y\in G
one has
x+y=y+x
by commutativity of the
group, hence
x\boxplus y=\{x+y\}=\{y+x\}=y\boxplus x
.

(HG2) For all
x,y,z\in G
,

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=\bigcup_{u\in x\boxplus y}u\boxplus z=\{(x+y)+z\},

and similarly

x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)=\bigcup_{v\in y\boxplus z}x\boxplus v=\{x+(y+z)\}.

Since
(x+y)+z=x+(y+z)
in
G
, the two sets coincide. Thus
associativity holds.

(HG3) The group neutral element
0\in G
satisfies

0\boxplus x=\{0+x\}=\{x\},\qquad x\boxplus 0=\{x+0\}=\{x\},

so
0
is a neutral element in the sense of
Definition
2.11
.

(HG4) For each
x\in G
, the group inverse
-x
satisfies

x\boxplus(-x)=\{x+(-x)\}=\{0\},

so
0\in x\boxplus(-x)
. If
y\in G
is any other element with

0\in x\boxplus y
, then
0=x+y
, hence
y=-x
by uniqueness of
inverses in the group. Thus each element has a unique additive
inverse in the hypergroup sense, and it coincides with its group
inverse.

(HG5) Finally, let
x,y,z\in G
. We have

x\in y\boxplus z\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\in\{y+z\}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=y+z,

and

z\in x\boxplus(-y)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z\in\{x+(-y)\}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z=x-y.

The two conditions are equivalent because
x=y+z
if and only if

z=x-y
in the abelian group. Hence reversibility holds. All axioms
are satisfied, so
(G,\boxplus)
is a canonical commutative hypergroup.
∎

Example 2.15

(Sign hyperfield as a canonical hypergroup)
.

Let
X=\{0,+,-\}
and define a hyperoperation
\boxplus
by the rules

0\boxplus x=x\boxplus 0=\{x\},\quad+\boxplus+=\{+\},\quad-\boxplus-=\{-\},

and

+\boxplus-=-\boxplus+=\{+,0,-\}.

Then
(X,\boxplus)
is the additive hypergroup underlying the standard
sign hyperfield. One checks directly that it satisfies
(HG1)–(HG5), so it is a canonical commutative hypergroup. In
Section
5
we will extend this example by adding
the extra sign
\Lambda
and constructing a canonical hypergroup
structure
(S,\oplus_{S})
on
S=\{0,+,-,\Lambda\}
which serves as a
sign–layer envelope for the three–sign hypernumber system.

The notion of canonical commutative hypergroup is central in the
theory of hyperrings and hyperfields: a hyperring is a set equipped
with a canonical commutative hypergroup structure for addition
together with a compatible single–valued multiplication. In this
paper, we will use these concepts mainly as a reference point. The
additive structure
(H,\boxplus)
of our three–sign system is
commutative and admits unique inverses but fails to satisfy (HG2) and
(HG5) in general; precise counterexamples will be given in
Section
4
. On the other hand, we will construct a
canonical commutative hypergroup on the sign set
S
in
Section
5
, illustrating how canonical
hypergroups can still appear at the level of sign data even when the
full hypernumber system is non–associative.

2.4.
Hyperfields and the sign hyperfield

Literature.
Hyperrings and hyperfields in the sense of Krasner and their
modern incarnations play a central role in tropical geometry, matroid theory,
and “absolute” approaches to arithmetic; see

[
15
,
2
,
7
,
1
,
11
]

for representative entry points.

We briefly recall the notion of a hyperfield, which generalizes the
usual concept of a field by allowing addition to be multivalued. In
this framework, the additive structure is a canonical commutative
hypergroup in the sense of Definition
2.12
,
while multiplication remains an ordinary single–valued operation.

Definition 2.16

(Hyperring and hyperfield)
.

A
hyperring
is a triple
(F,\oplus,\cdot)
consisting of a
nonempty set
F
, a hyperoperation

\oplus:F\times F\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}(F)\setminus\{\emptyset\},

and a single–valued multiplication

\cdot:F\times F\longrightarrow F,

such that:

(1)

The pair
(F,\oplus)
is a canonical commutative hypergroup
with neutral element
0\in F
.

(2)

The set
F^{\times}:=F\setminus\{0\}
is a (not necessarily
commutative) group under
\cdot
with identity element
1
.

(3)

Multiplication distributes over hyperaddition on both sides:
for all
a,b,c\in F
one has

a\cdot(b\oplus c)\subseteq(a\cdot b)\oplus(a\cdot c),\qquad(b\oplus c)\cdot a\subseteq(b\cdot a)\oplus(c\cdot a),

where
a\cdot(B):=\{a\cdot x:x\in B\}
and

(B)\cdot a:=\{x\cdot a:x\in B\}
for subsets
B\subseteq F
.

(4)

The element
0
is absorbing for multiplication:

0\cdot a=a\cdot 0=0\qquad\text{for all }a\in F.

A
hyperfield
is a hyperring
(F,\oplus,\cdot)
whose
multiplicative group
F^{\times}
is abelian.

Thus hyperfields are to fields what canonical commutative hypergroups
are to abelian groups: the multiplicative structure is group–like,
while addition is allowed to be multivalued but must satisfy a strong
set of axioms.

Example 2.17

(Ordinary fields as hyperfields)
.

Let
(K,+,\cdot)
be a field in the usual sense. Define

a\oplus b:=\{a+b\}\qquad\text{for all }a,b\in K.

Then
(K,\oplus,\cdot)
is a hyperfield.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.14
, the additive group
(K,+)

gives rise to a canonical commutative hypergroup
(K,\oplus)
by
taking singletons as hyper–sums, so condition (1) of
Definition
2.16
holds. The multiplicative group

K^{\times}
is an abelian group under
\cdot
, with the usual identity

1
, so (2) holds as well. Distributivity (3) is immediate from the
field axioms: for any
a,b,c\in K
,

a\cdot(b\oplus c)=a\cdot\{b+c\}=\{a(b+c)\}=\{ab+ac\}=(a\cdot b)\oplus(a\cdot c),

and similarly on the right. Finally,
0
is absorbing for
multiplication in
K
, so (4) holds. Hence
(K,\oplus,\cdot)
is a
hyperfield.
∎

.

Definition 2.18

(Sign hyperfield)
.

Let
S_{\mathrm{sign}}:=\{0,+,-\}
. Define a hyperoperation

\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}
on
S_{\mathrm{sign}}
by

\displaystyle 0\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}x

\displaystyle=x\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}0=\{x\}

\displaystyle\text{for all }x\in S_{\mathrm{sign}},

\displaystyle+\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}+

\displaystyle=\{+\},

\displaystyle-\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}-

\displaystyle=\{-\},

\displaystyle+\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}-

\displaystyle=-\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}+=\{+,0,-\}.

Define multiplication on
S_{\mathrm{sign}}
by

0\cdot x=x\cdot 0:=0\quad\text{for all }x,\qquad+\cdot+:=+,\quad+\cdot-:=-,\quad-\cdot+:=-,\quad-\cdot-:=+.

Proposition 2.19
.

The triple
(S_{\mathrm{sign}},\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}},\cdot)
is a hyperfield. In particular, the
additive structure
(S_{\mathrm{sign}},\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}})
is a
canonical commutative hypergroup.

Proof.

We sketch the verification of the axioms.

(Additive structure).
Commutativity is clear from the
definition:
x\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}y=y\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}x
for
all
x,y\in S_{\mathrm{sign}}
. The element
0
satisfies

0\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}x=x\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}0=\{x\}\quad\text{for all }x,

so it is a neutral element. For each
x\in S_{\mathrm{sign}}
there
is a unique additive inverse:
-0=0
,
-(+)=-
, and
-(-)=+
, since

0\in(+)\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}(-)
and
0\in(-)\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}(+)
,
and no other pair has
0
in its hyper–sum. It remains to check
associativity and reversibility.

To see associativity, one may enumerate all triples

(x,y,z)\in S_{\mathrm{sign}}^{3}
and compare

(x\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}y)\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}z
with

x\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}(y\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}z)
. Most cases are
trivial: whenever two or more entries are
0
, or all three entries
have the same sign, both bracketings reduce to a singleton equal to the
common value. The nontrivial case is when the multiset of signs is

\{+,+,-\}
or
\{+,-,-\}
. For example, if
(x,y,z)=(+,+,-)
, then

x\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}y=+\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}+=\{+\},

so

(x\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}y)\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}z=+\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}-=\{+,0,-\}.

On the other hand,

y\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}z=+\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}-=\{+,0,-\},

and adding
x=+
to each element of this set again yields

\{+,0,-\}
. The case
(x,y,z)=(+,-,-)
is symmetric. Thus the two
bracketings always agree, so associativity holds.

Reversibility can also be verified by complete enumeration. For
instance, take
y=+
; then
x\in y\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}z
means that

x
is one of the signs in the set
+\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}z
, while

z\in x\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}(-y)
means that
z
belongs to

x\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}-
. Checking the pairs

(x,z)\in S_{\mathrm{sign}}^{2}
case by case shows that these conditions
are equivalent. The same holds for
y=-
and
y=0
(where the
reversibility statement becomes trivial). We omit the routine table
but note that this verification is standard in the literature on the
sign hyperfield. Thus
(S_{\mathrm{sign}},\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}})
is a
canonical commutative hypergroup.

(Multiplicative structure).
The nonzero elements

S_{\mathrm{sign}}^{\times}=\{+,-\}
form a group under the given
multiplication, isomorphic to the cyclic group of order
2
:

+
is the identity, and
-^{-1}=-
. The group is clearly abelian.
The element
0
is absorbing, since
0\cdot x=x\cdot 0=0
for all
x
.

(Distributivity).
Finally, distributivity of multiplication
over hyperaddition can be checked directly. It suffices to verify that
for all
a,b,c\in S_{\mathrm{sign}}
,

a\cdot(b\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}c)\subseteq(a\cdot b)\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}(a\cdot c),

since right distributivity follows by commutativity of
\cdot
. If

a=0
, both sides reduce to
\{0\}
, so the inclusion holds. For

a=+
, the left hand side is
b\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}c
and the right
hand side is
(+\cdot b)\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}(+\cdot c)=b\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}c
, so we have equality. For
a=-
, the left
hand side is

-\cdot(b\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}c)=\{-x:x\in b\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}c\},

and the right hand side is

(-\cdot b)\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}(-\cdot c)=(-b)\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}(-c).

From the explicit description of
\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}
and the fact
that negation is a bijection on
\{+,-\}
, it follows that

\{-x:x\in b\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}c\}=(-b)\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}(-c)
, so distributivity holds. Hence all
conditions in Definition
2.16
are satisfied, and

(S_{\mathrm{sign}},\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}},\cdot)
is a hyperfield.
∎

The sign hyperfield provides a compact way to encode the behaviour of
real signs under addition and multiplication. The following simple
fact explains this connection.

Lemma 2.20

(Sign of a real sum)
.

Let
\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}:\mathbb{R}\to\{0,+,-\}
be the sign map from
Definition
2.3
. For all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
one has

\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}(x+y)\in\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}(x)\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}(y),

where
\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}
is the hyperaddition on the sign
hyperfield.

Proof.

We distinguish cases according to the signs of
x
and
y
.

If
x
and
y
have the same sign, then their sum
x+y
has that sign
as well (or is zero only in the trivial case
x=y=0
). This matches
the rules

0\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}0=\{0\},\quad+\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}+=\{+\},\quad-\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}-=\{-\}.

If
\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}(x)=+
and
\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}(y)=-
, then

x>0
and
y<0
. Depending on the relative magnitudes of
x
and
y
,
the sum
x+y
can be positive, zero, or negative. Thus

\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}(x+y)\in\{+,0,-\}=+\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}-
. The case

\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}(x)=-
,
\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}(y)=+
is
symmetric and corresponds to

-\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}+=\{+,0,-\}
. This exhausts all possibilities,
so the inclusion holds in every case.
∎

In the language of hyperfields, Lemma
2.20
says that the
sign map
\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}
is a morphism from the field
\mathbb{R}

(viewed as a hyperfield as in
Example
2.17
) to the sign hyperfield

S_{\mathrm{sign}}
. In later sections we will construct a
four–element sign set
S=\{0,+,-,\Lambda\}
equipped with a
canonical hypergroup structure
(S,\oplus_{S})
that extends the additive
hypergroup
(S_{\mathrm{sign}},\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}})
by adding a
third sign
\Lambda
. The three–sign hypernumber system
H
will
then be seen as a refinement of this sign–level picture which keeps
track not only of signs but also of magnitudes and cancellation mass.

3.
The three-sign cancellation hypernumber system

3.1.
Universe, sign and magnitude, and real embedding

We now fix the underlying set of our three–sign hypernumber system and
formalize the sign and magnitude maps, together with the embedding of
the classical real line.

Recall from Definition
2.2
that

S=\{0,+,-,\Lambda\},\qquad S^{\times}=\{+,-,\Lambda\},

and from Definition
2.1
that

\mathbb{R}_{>0}=\mathbb{R}_{>0}=\{a\in\mathbb{R}:a>0\}
denotes the set of strictly positive
real numbers.

Definition 3.1

(Three–sign hypernumber universe)
.

The
three–sign hypernumber universe
is the set

H:=\{0\}\;\cup\;S^{\times}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}.

An element of
H
is either the distinguished zero
0
, or a pair

(\sigma,a)
with
\sigma\in S^{\times}
and
a\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. We define the

sign map

\operatorname{sgn}:H\longrightarrow S

and the
magnitude map

|\cdot|:H\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

by

\operatorname{sgn}(0):=0,\quad\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma,a):=\sigma\ \text{ for }\sigma\in S^{\times},\ a>0,

and

|0|:=0,\quad|(\sigma,a)|:=a\ \text{ for }\sigma\in S^{\times},\ a>0.

Thus every nonzero element of
H
carries exactly one sign from

S^{\times}
and a strictly positive magnitude, while
0
has no sign and
zero magnitude.

Remark 3.2

(Partition by sign)
.

The nonzero part of
H
decomposes as a disjoint union

H\setminus\{0\}=H_{+}\;\dot{\cup}\;H_{-}\;\dot{\cup}\;H_{\Lambda},

where

H_{+}:=\{(+,a):a>0\},\quad H_{-}:=\{(-,a):a>0\},\quad H_{\Lambda}:=\{(\Lambda,a):a>0\}.

Each of these sets is the fibre of the sign map over the corresponding
nonzero sign:

H_{\sigma}=\operatorname{sgn}^{-1}(\sigma)\qquad\text{for }\sigma\in S^{\times}.

The classical real line will be embedded in
H
by attaching the
usual positive and negative signs to the absolute values of real
numbers. This will serve as the “classical core” of our construction.

Definition 3.3

(Real embedding and classical line)
.

Define a map

i:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow H

by

i(0):=0,\qquad i(r):=(+,r)\ \text{ for }r>0,\qquad i(r):=(-,-r)\ \text{ for }r<0.

We call
i
the
real embedding
, and we denote its image by

\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}:=i(\mathbb{R})\subset H.

We refer to
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
as the
classical real line
inside
H
.

In other words,
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
is the subset of
H
consisting of
0
, all
“positive” elements
(+,a)
with
a>0
, and all “negative” elements

(-,a)
with
a>0
; the
\Lambda
–sector is entirely new.

Lemma 3.4

(Basic properties of the embedding)
.

The map
i:\mathbb{R}\to H
is injective, and its image is

\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}=\{0\}\;\cup\;\{(+,a):a>0\}\;\cup\;\{(-,a):a>0\}.

Moreover, for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
,

\operatorname{sgn}(i(r))=\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}(r),\qquad|i(r)|=|r|,

where
\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}
is the real sign map from
Definition
2.3
.

Proof.

The description of
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
follows immediately from the definition of

i
. If
i(r_{1})=i(r_{2})
, there are three cases:

(1) If
r_{1}=0
, then
i(r_{1})=0
, so
i(r_{2})=0
implies

r_{2}=0
. Thus
r_{1}=r_{2}=0
.

(2) If
r_{1}>0
, then
i(r_{1})=(+,r_{1})
. If
r_{2}\leq 0
, then

i(r_{2})
is either
0
or a pair whose first coordinate is
-
,
so it cannot equal
(+,r_{1})
. Hence
r_{2}>0
, and from

(+,r_{1})=i(r_{1})=i(r_{2})=(+,r_{2})

we deduce
r_{1}=r_{2}
.

(3) If
r_{1}<0
, then
i(r_{1})=(-,-r_{1})
with
-r_{1}>0
. If
r_{2}\geq 0
,
then
i(r_{2})
is either
0
or has first coordinate
+
, so it
cannot equal
(-,-r_{1})
. Thus
r_{2}<0
, and from

(-,-r_{1})=i(r_{1})=i(r_{2})=(-,-r_{2})

we deduce
-r_{1}=-r_{2}
, hence
r_{1}=r_{2}
. In all cases

i(r_{1})=i(r_{2})
implies
r_{1}=r_{2}
, so
i
is injective.

For the compatibility with signs and magnitudes, note that

\operatorname{sgn}(i(0))=\operatorname{sgn}(0)=0=\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}(0),\quad|i(0)|=|0|=0=|0|.

If
r>0
, then
i(r)=(+,r)
, so

\operatorname{sgn}(i(r))=+=\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}(r),\quad|i(r)|=r=|r|.

If
r<0
, then
i(r)=(-,-r)
with
-r>0
, so

\operatorname{sgn}(i(r))=-=\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}(r),\quad|i(r)|=-r=|r|.

Thus in all cases
\operatorname{sgn}(i(r))
and
|i(r)|
agree with the
usual sign and absolute value of
r
, as claimed.
∎

Remark 3.5

(Real numbers inside the three–sign universe)
.

By Lemma
3.4
we may freely identify each real
number
r\in\mathbb{R}
with its image
i(r)\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}\subset H
. This
identification is compatible with both sign and magnitude:

r>0
corresponds to an element in the
+
–sector,
r<0
to an
element in the
-
–sector, and
r=0
to the distinguished zero. In
later sections we will see that
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
is not only a subset of
H

but in fact a subfield for the operations we define, so that the usual
real arithmetic is recovered exactly inside the three–sign universe.

3.2.
Multiplicative structure

We next specify the multiplicative structure on the three–sign universe

H
introduced in Definition
3.1
. The guiding
principle is that multiplication should behave exactly as in the real
field on the classical line
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
, while extending to the
\Lambda
–sector
by means of a simple sign monoid.

Recall that
S^{\times}=\{+,-,\Lambda\}
and
\mathbb{R}_{>0}=\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

Definition 3.6

(Multiplicative sign monoid)
.

Define a binary operation

*:S^{\times}\times S^{\times}\longrightarrow S^{\times}

by the table

\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}*&+&-&\Lambda\\
\hline\cr+&+&-&\Lambda\\
-&-&+&\Lambda\\
\Lambda&\Lambda&\Lambda&\Lambda\end{array}

i.e.
+
is the identity,
-*-=+
, and every product involving

\Lambda
is
\Lambda
.

Lemma 3.7
.

The pair
(S^{\times},*)
is a commutative monoid with identity
+
, and

\Lambda
is an idempotent element:

\Lambda*\Lambda=\Lambda.

Proof.

Commutativity is clear from the symmetry of the table:

\sigma*\tau=\tau*\sigma
for all
\sigma,\tau\in S^{\times}
.

Associativity can be checked by a short case analysis. If none of the
three factors is
\Lambda
, then we are in the subgroup

\{+,-\}\subset S^{\times}
with the usual sign multiplication, which is
associative. If at least one factor is
\Lambda
, then every product
containing it is
\Lambda
. Thus for any
\sigma,\tau,\rho\in S^{\times}
,
if one of them is
\Lambda
then both
(\sigma*\tau)*\rho
and

\sigma*(\tau*\rho)
equal
\Lambda
. Hence associativity holds in all
cases.

The element
+
is the identity: from the first row and column of the
table we see that
+*\sigma=\sigma*+=\sigma
for all

\sigma\in S^{\times}
. Finally, the last row and column show that

\Lambda*\Lambda=\Lambda
, so
\Lambda
is idempotent. This proves the
lemma.
∎

We now extend this sign multiplication to a multiplication on
H

by multiplying magnitudes as in
\mathbb{R}
and using
*
on the sign
component.

Definition 3.8

(Multiplication on
H
)
.

Define a multiplication

\cdot:H\times H\longrightarrow H

by the following rules:

(1)

0\cdot x=x\cdot 0:=0
for all
x\in H
.

(2)

For
\sigma,\tau\in S^{\times}
and
a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,

(\sigma,a)\cdot(\tau,b):=(\sigma*\tau,\;ab),

where
*
is the sign monoid operation from
Definition
3.6
.

We write

1_{H}:=(+,1)

for the multiplicative identity candidate.

Lemma 3.9

(Behaviour of sign and magnitude)
.

For all
x,y\in H
one has

\operatorname{sgn}(x\cdot y)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)*\operatorname{sgn}(y),\qquad|x\cdot y|=|x|\cdot|y|,

where
*
is extended to
S
by declaring
0
absorbing:

0*\sigma=\sigma*0:=0
.

Proof.

If
x=0
or
y=0
, then
x\cdot y=0
by definition, so

\operatorname{sgn}(x\cdot y)=0
and
|x\cdot y|=0
. On the other hand,

|x|\cdot|y|=0
and
\operatorname{sgn}(x)*\operatorname{sgn}(y)=0
, so the identities hold.

If
x=(\sigma,a)
and
y=(\tau,b)
with
\sigma,\tau\in S^{\times}
and

a,b>0
, then by Definition
3.8
,

x\cdot y=(\sigma*\tau,ab),

so

\operatorname{sgn}(x\cdot y)=\sigma*\tau=\operatorname{sgn}(x)*\operatorname{sgn}(y),\qquad|x\cdot y|=ab=|x|\cdot|y|.

This covers all cases.
∎

Proposition 3.10

(Multiplicative monoid structure)
.

The set
H
with the multiplication defined above and identity

1_{H}=(+,1)
is a commutative monoid. Moreover,
0
is an absorbing
element:

0\cdot x=x\cdot 0=0\qquad\text{for all }x\in H.

Proof.

Commutativity follows immediately from the symmetry of the definition:

0\cdot x=x\cdot 0
is clear, and for nonzero
x=(\sigma,a)
,

y=(\tau,b)
we have

x\cdot y=(\sigma*\tau,ab)=(\tau*\sigma,ba)=y\cdot x,

using commutativity of
*
and of multiplication in
\mathbb{R}
.

Associativity is also straightforward. If one of the three factors is

0
, then both
(x\cdot y)\cdot z
and
x\cdot(y\cdot z)
are
0
by
the absorbing property. For nonzero elements

x=(\sigma_{1},a_{1})
,
y=(\sigma_{2},a_{2})
,
z=(\sigma_{3},a_{3})
we have

\displaystyle(x\cdot y)\cdot z

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{1}*\sigma_{2},a_{1}a_{2})\cdot(\sigma_{3},a_{3})

\displaystyle=((\sigma_{1}*\sigma_{2})*\sigma_{3},\,(a_{1}a_{2})a_{3}),

and

\displaystyle x\cdot(y\cdot z)

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{1},a_{1})\cdot(\sigma_{2}*\sigma_{3},a_{2}a_{3})

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{1}*(\sigma_{2}*\sigma_{3}),\,a_{1}(a_{2}a_{3})).

By Lemma
3.7
,
(S^{\times},*)
is an associative monoid,
and multiplication in
\mathbb{R}
is associative, so the two results coincide.
Thus multiplication on
H
is associative.

The identity property of
1_{H}=(+,1)
is immediate: for any nonzero

(\sigma,a)
,

1_{H}\cdot(\sigma,a)=(+,1)\cdot(\sigma,a)=(+*\sigma,1\cdot a)=(\sigma,a),

and similarly
(\sigma,a)\cdot 1_{H}=(\sigma,a)
; for
x=0
the identity
property holds trivially. The absorbing property of
0
was built
into Definition
3.8
. Hence
(H,\cdot,1_{H})

is a commutative monoid with absorbing element
0
.
∎

We next identify the units, zero divisors, and idempotents of this
monoid.

Proposition 3.11

(Units and zero divisors)
.

An element
x\in H
is a multiplicative unit if and only if

x\in H_{+}\cup H_{-}
, i.e.
x
has sign
+
or
-
; in particular, no
element of the
\Lambda
–sector
H_{\Lambda}
is invertible. More
precisely, for
a>0
,

(+,a)^{-1}=(+,a^{-1}),\qquad(-,a)^{-1}=(-,a^{-1}).

Consequently, the group of units
U(H)
is isomorphic to

\mathbb{R}^{\times}
via the embedding
i
from
Definition
3.3
.

Moreover, the only multiplicative zero divisor in
H
is
0
:
if
x,y\in H
and
x\cdot y=0
, then
x=0
or
y=0
.

Proof.

Let
x\in H
be a unit. If
x=0
, then
x\cdot y=0
for all
y
,
so
x
cannot have a multiplicative inverse; thus every unit is
nonzero. Write
x=(\sigma,a)
with
\sigma\in S^{\times}
and
a>0
, and
let
y=(\tau,b)
be an inverse, so that
x\cdot y=1_{H}=(+,1)
. Then
by Lemma
3.9
,

\operatorname{sgn}(x\cdot y)=\sigma*\tau=+,\qquad|x\cdot y|=ab=1.

Thus
ab=1
, so
b=a^{-1}
, and
\sigma*\tau=+
. From the sign monoid
table we see that the only solutions of
\sigma*\tau=+
are

(\sigma,\tau)=(+,+)
or
(\sigma,\tau)=(-,-)
. In particular,

\sigma\in\{+,-\}
, so
x
lies in
H_{+}\cup H_{-}
. Conversely, if

x=(+,a)
with
a>0
, then

x\cdot(+,a^{-1})=(+*+,aa^{-1})=(+,1)=1_{H},

and similarly
(-,a)\cdot(-,a^{-1})=1_{H}
. Thus every element of

H_{+}\cup H_{-}
is a unit. This proves the characterization of units and
the inverse formulas.

For the identification of
U(H)
with
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
, note that

\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}\setminus\{0\}=H_{+}\cup H_{-}
and
i:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
is compatible with the field structure on
\mathbb{R}
(see
Proposition
3.20
and Lemma
3.24
);
in particular,
i
restricts
to a group isomorphism

\mathbb{R}^{\times}\xrightarrow{\;\cong\;}U(H).

For the zero divisor statement, suppose
x,y\in H
and

x\cdot y=0
. If either
x
or
y
is
0
, we are done. Otherwise
write
x=(\sigma,a)
,
y=(\tau,b)
with
\sigma,\tau\in S^{\times}
and

a,b>0
. Then

x\cdot y=(\sigma*\tau,ab)

is nonzero, since
ab>0
and
\sigma*\tau\in S^{\times}
. This contradicts

x\cdot y=0
. Hence at least one of
x,y
must be zero. Thus there
are no nonzero zero divisors in
H
.
∎

Proposition 3.12

(Multiplicative idempotents)
.

The multiplicative idempotents of
H
are precisely the three
elements

0,\qquad(+,1),\qquad(\Lambda,1).

Proof.

An element
e\in H
is idempotent if
e\cdot e=e
. Clearly

0\cdot 0=0
, so
0
is idempotent. Let
e\neq 0
, so
e=(\sigma,a)

with
\sigma\in S^{\times}
,
a>0
. Then

e\cdot e=(\sigma,a)\cdot(\sigma,a)=(\sigma*\sigma,a^{2}).

The condition
e\cdot e=e
is equivalent to

\sigma*\sigma=\sigma,\qquad a^{2}=a.

Since
a>0
, the equation
a^{2}=a
implies
a=1
. Thus we must have

a=1
, and
\sigma
must be an idempotent in the sign monoid, i.e.
\sigma*\sigma=\sigma
. From the table in
Definition
3.6
we see that

+*+=+,\quad-*-=+,\quad\Lambda*\Lambda=\Lambda.

Hence the only idempotent signs are
+
and
\Lambda
. Therefore the
only nonzero idempotent elements of
H
are
(+,1)
and

(\Lambda,1)
. Together with
0
, this gives the full list of
multiplicative idempotents.
∎

Remark 3.13

(Summary of multiplicative structure)
.

We summarize the multiplicative picture as follows:

•

(H,\cdot,1_{H})
is a commutative monoid with absorbing
element
0
.

•

The group of units
U(H)=H_{+}\cup H_{-}
is isomorphic to

\mathbb{R}^{\times}
via the embedding
i
.

•

There are no nonzero zero divisors.

•

Besides
0
and the identity
(+,1)
, the only idempotent is

(\Lambda,1)
, which acts as a multiplicative projector onto
the
\Lambda
–sector:

(\Lambda,1)\cdot(\sigma,a)=(\Lambda,a)\quad\text{for all }\sigma\in S^{\times},\ a>0.

The additive structure introduced in the next subsection will be
compatible with this multiplicative structure in such a way that the
classical line
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
becomes a subfield of
H
, while the

\Lambda
–sector introduces new, nonclassical behaviour.

3.3.
Hyperaddition and additive structure

We now define the hyperaddition on
H
and record its basic
properties. The construction is designed so that

•

on the classical line
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
it agrees exactly with ordinary
addition of real numbers, and

•

the
\Lambda
–sector acts as a reservoir for cancellation
mass, with genuinely multivalued behaviour only in the

\Lambda
–
\Lambda
sums.

Throughout this subsection we write elements of
H
as
0
or

(\sigma,a)
with
\sigma\in S^{\times}=\{+,-,\Lambda\}
and
a\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

Definition 3.14

(Hyperaddition on
H
)
.

Define a map

\boxplus:H\times H\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}(H)\setminus\{\emptyset\}

by the following rules.

(1) Identity.
For all
x\in H
,

0\boxplus x=x\boxplus 0:=\{x\}.

(2) Same sign, excluding
\Lambda
.
For
\sigma\in\{+,-\}
and

a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,

(\sigma,a)\boxplus(\sigma,b):=\{(\sigma,a+b)\}.

(3) Opposite real signs.
For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,

(+,a)\boxplus(-,b):=\begin{cases}\{(+,a-b)\},&a>b,\\[2.0pt]
\{0\},&a=b,\\[2.0pt]
\{(-,b-a)\},&a<b.\end{cases}

and
(-,b)\boxplus(+,a)
is defined by commutativity.

(4) Interaction with
\Lambda
.
For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,

\displaystyle(+,a)\boxplus(\Lambda,b)

\displaystyle=(\Lambda,b)\boxplus(+,a):=\{(\Lambda,a+b)\},

\displaystyle(-,a)\boxplus(\Lambda,b)

\displaystyle=(\Lambda,b)\boxplus(-,a):=\{(\Lambda,a+b)\}.

(5)
\Lambda
–
\Lambda
sums.
For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,

(\Lambda,a)\boxplus(\Lambda,b):=\begin{cases}\{(\Lambda,2a),\,0\},&a=b,\\[2.0pt]
\{(\Lambda,a+b),\,(+,|a-b|),\,(-,|a-b|)\},&a\neq b.\end{cases}

In all cases not explicitly covered above,
\boxplus
is defined by
commutativity:

x\boxplus y:=y\boxplus x.

It is immediate from the definition that every pair
(x,y)
is covered
by exactly one of the clauses (1)–(5), up to the symmetry
x\leftrightarrow y
.

Lemma 3.15

(Well-definedness and commutativity)
.

The hyperoperation
\boxplus
in Definition
3.14
is
well-defined and commutative: for all
x,y\in H
,

x\boxplus y=y\boxplus x.

Moreover, for every
x,y\in H
the set
x\boxplus y
is nonempty and
finite.

Proof.

Given
x,y\in H
, we distinguish cases:

(1) If
x=0
or
y=0
, then (1) applies and
x\boxplus y
is a singleton.

(2) If
x,y\neq 0
, write
x=(\sigma,a)
,
y=(\tau,b)
with

\sigma,\tau\in S^{\times}
,
a,b>0
. There are four mutually exclusive
possibilities (up to order):

(a)
\sigma=\tau\in\{+,-\}
: clause (2) applies;

(b)
\{\sigma,\tau\}=\{+,-\}
: clause (3) applies;

(c)
\{\sigma,\tau\}=\{+,\Lambda\}
or
\{\sigma,\tau\}=\{-,\Lambda\}
: clause (4) applies;

(d)
\sigma=\tau=\Lambda
: clause (5) applies.

Thus each ordered pair
(x,y)
falls into exactly one of the listed
cases (or the
0
–case), and the definition of
x\boxplus y
is unambiguous
and covers all possibilities.

From the construction it is clear that in each case the resulting set
is nonempty and has at most three elements. Commutativity follows
because the right-hand sides are symmetric under exchanging the two
inputs: this is obvious in (1), (2), (4) and (5), and in (3) we have
explicitly required that
(-,b)\boxplus(+,a)
be defined by commutativity.
Hence
x\boxplus y=y\boxplus x
for all
x,y\in H
.
∎

The neutral element and additive inverses are also easy to describe.

Lemma 3.16

(Neutral element)
.

The element
0\in H
is a neutral element for
\boxplus
, i.e.

0\boxplus x=x\boxplus 0=\{x\}\qquad\text{for all }x\in H.

Proof.

This is exactly clause (1) of Definition
3.14
.
∎

Definition 3.17

(Additive inverses in
H
)
.

An element
y\in H
is called an
additive inverse
of

x\in H
if

0\in x\boxplus y.

When such a
y
exists and is unique, we denote it by
-x
.

Lemma 3.18

(Existence and uniqueness of additive inverses)
.

For every
x\in H
there exists a unique
y\in H
such that

0\in x\boxplus y
. Explicitly,

-0=0,\quad-(+,a)=(-,a),\quad-(-,a)=(+,a),\quad-(\Lambda,a)=(\Lambda,a),

for all
a>0
.

Proof.

If
x=0
, then
0\boxplus y=\{y\}
, so
0\in 0\boxplus y
if and only if
y=0
.
Thus
-0=0
.

Let
x=(+,a)
with
a>0
. We seek
y
such that
0\in(+,a)\boxplus y
.
If
y=0
, then
(+,a)\boxplus 0=\{(+,a)\}
, so
0
is not in the sum. If

y
has sign
+
or
\Lambda
, then by clauses (2) and (4),

(+,a)\boxplus y
contains only
+
or
\Lambda
elements, never
0
. If

y=(-,b)
with
b>0
, then by clause (3),

(+,a)\boxplus(-,b)=\begin{cases}\{(+,a-b)\},&a>b,\\[2.0pt]
\{0\},&a=b,\\[2.0pt]
\{(-,b-a)\},&a<b.\end{cases}

The only way to obtain
0
is to take
b=a
, in which case

(+,a)\boxplus(-,a)=\{0\}
. Hence
(-,a)
is the unique additive inverse
of
(+,a)
.

The case
x=(-,a)
is symmetric: if
y
has sign
-
or
\Lambda
,
then
(-,a)\boxplus y
cannot contain
0
; if
y=(+,b)
, then by clause (3)
we get
0
only when
b=a
. Thus
-(-,a)=(+,a)
.

Finally, let
x=(\Lambda,a)
with
a>0
. If
y
has sign
+
or
-
,
then by clause (4)
(\Lambda,a)\boxplus y
consists only of
\Lambda
–elements.
Hence
0\notin(\Lambda,a)\boxplus y
in these cases. If
y=(\Lambda,b)
,
then by clause (5),

(\Lambda,a)\boxplus(\Lambda,b)=\begin{cases}\{(\Lambda,2a),\,0\},&a=b,\\[2.0pt]
\{(\Lambda,a+b),\,(+,|a-b|),\,(-,|a-b|)\},&a\neq b.\end{cases}

Thus
0
occurs in the sum if and only if
b=a
, and in that case

(\Lambda,a)\boxplus(\Lambda,a)
contains
0
. Hence
(\Lambda,a)
is its
own unique additive inverse.

In all cases, each
x\in H
admits a unique additive inverse, given
by the formulas in the statement.
∎

Proposition 3.19

(Additive hypermagma structure)
.

The pair
(H,\boxplus)
is a commutative hypermagma with neutral element

0
and unique additive inverses in the sense of
Definition
2.11
. In particular,

0\boxplus x=x\boxplus 0=\{x\}\quad\text{and}\quad 0\in x\boxplus(-x)

for all
x\in H
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.15
,
(H,\boxplus)
is a commutative
hypermagma and every hyper–sum is finite and nonempty. Lemma

3.16
shows that
0
is a neutral element, and Lemma

3.18
shows that each
x\in H
has a unique
additive inverse
-x
with
0\in x\boxplus(-x)
. This is precisely the
content of Definition
2.11
, so the proposition
follows.
∎

A key requirement in our construction is that the restriction of
\boxplus

to the classical line
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
reproduces ordinary addition of real
numbers. This is immediate from the case–by–case definition.

Proposition 3.20

(Restriction to the classical real line)
.

Let
i:\mathbb{R}\to H
and
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}=i(\mathbb{R})
be as in
Definition
3.3
. Then for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,

i(x)\boxplus i(y)=\{i(x+y)\}.

In particular,
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
is closed under
\boxplus
, and the induced operation
on
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
is the usual addition on
\mathbb{R}
transported via
i
.

Proof.

If
x=0
or
y=0
, the claim follows from clause (1) and the fact that

i(0)=0
. Suppose
x,y\neq 0
and write
x=\pm a
,
y=\pm b
with

a,b>0
. Then

i(x)=\begin{cases}(+,a),&x>0,\\
(-,a),&x<0,\end{cases}\qquad i(y)=\begin{cases}(+,b),&y>0,\\
(-,b),&y<0.\end{cases}

If
x,y>0
, then

i(x)\boxplus i(y)=(+,a)\boxplus(+,b)=\{(+,a+b)\}=\{i(a+b)\}=\{i(x+y)\}.

If
x,y<0
, then
x=-a
,
y=-b
and

i(x)\boxplus i(y)=(-,a)\boxplus(-,b)=\{(-,a+b)\}=\{i(-(a+b))\}=\{i(x+y)\}.

If
x>0
and
y<0
, then
x=a
,
y=-b
and

i(x)\boxplus i(y)=(+,a)\boxplus(-,b)=\begin{cases}\{(+,a-b)\},&a>b,\\[2.0pt]
\{0\},&a=b,\\[2.0pt]
\{(-,b-a)\},&a<b,\end{cases}

while
x+y=a-b
. If
a>b
, then
x+y>0
and

i(x+y)=(+,a-b)
; if
a=b
, then
x+y=0
and
i(x+y)=0
; if

a<b
, then
x+y<0
and
i(x+y)=(-,b-a)
. Thus in all cases

i(x)\boxplus i(y)
is the singleton
\{i(x+y)\}
.

The case
x<0
,
y>0
is symmetric and yields the same conclusion.
Hence the formula
i(x)\boxplus i(y)=\{i(x+y)\}
holds for all

x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
is closed under
\boxplus
with the induced operation
isomorphic to
(\mathbb{R},+)
.
∎

Remark 3.21

(Additive structure summary)
.

We have constructed a commutative hypermagma
(H,\boxplus)
with
neutral element
0
and unique additive inverses, whose restriction to
the classical line
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
reproduces ordinary addition on
\mathbb{R}
. The
additive structure is
not
associative in general and therefore
does not form a canonical commutative hypergroup; a detailed analysis
of associativity and its controlled failure will be carried out in
Section
4
. For the purposes of the present section, the
key points are the existence of a well-defined hyperaddition, the
group-like behaviour of
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
, and the presence of the
\Lambda
–sector,
which introduces multivalued and nonclassical behaviour while leaving
the classical real line untouched.

3.4.
Scalar multiplication

In this subsection we describe how real scalars act on the
three–sign hypernumber system
H
. The guiding principle is that
the action of
t\in\mathbb{R}
on
x\in H
should be realized via the
embedded real element
i(t)\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}\subset H
and the ordinary
multiplication on
H
.

Definition 3.22

(Scalar multiplication by real numbers)
.

Let
i:\mathbb{R}\to H
be the real embedding from
Definition
3.3
. For
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in H
we
define

t\cdot x:=i(t)\cdot x,

where
\cdot
is the multiplication on
H
from
Definition
3.8
.

Unwinding the definitions, we obtain the following explicit formulas.

Lemma 3.23

(Explicit form of scalar multiplication)
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in H
.

(1)

If
t=0
, then
t\cdot x=0
for all
x\in H
.

(2)

If
t>0
and
x=0
, then
t\cdot x=0
. If

t>0
and
x=(\sigma,a)
with
\sigma\in S^{\times}
,
a>0
, then

t\cdot(\sigma,a)=(\sigma,ta).

(3)

If
t<0
and
x=0
, then
t\cdot x=0
. If

t<0
and
x=(\sigma,a)
with
\sigma\in S^{\times}
,
a>0
, then

t\cdot(\sigma,a)=(\sigma^{\prime},\,|t|a),

where

\sigma^{\prime}=\begin{cases}-,&\sigma=+,\\
+,&\sigma=-,\\
\Lambda,&\sigma=\Lambda.\end{cases}

Proof.

If
t=0
, then
i(t)=0
and
0\cdot x=0
for all
x
by
Definition
3.8
. This gives (1).

If
t>0
, then
i(t)=(+,t)
. For
x=0
we again get

t\cdot x=(+,t)\cdot 0=0
. For
x=(\sigma,a)
with
\sigma\in S^{\times}

and
a>0
,

t\cdot(\sigma,a)=(+,t)\cdot(\sigma,a)=(+*\sigma,\,ta)=(\sigma,ta),

since
+
is the identity in the sign monoid
(S^{\times},*)
. This is (2).

If
t<0
, then
i(t)=(-,-t)
with
-t>0
. For
x=0
we get

t\cdot x=(-,-t)\cdot 0=0
. For
x=(\sigma,a)
with
\sigma\in S^{\times}

and
a>0
,

t\cdot(\sigma,a)=(-,-t)\cdot(\sigma,a)=(-*\sigma,\,(-t)a)=(\sigma^{\prime},\,|t|a),

where
\sigma^{\prime}=-*\sigma
. From the multiplication table in
Definition
3.6
we have

-*+=-,\quad-*-=+,\quad-*\Lambda=\Lambda,

which yields the stated formula for
\sigma^{\prime}
. This proves (3).
∎

In particular, scalar multiplication behaves exactly as expected on
the classical line.

Lemma 3.24

(Compatibility with the real embedding)
.

For all
t,x\in\mathbb{R}
one has

t\cdot i(x)=i(tx),

where the left–hand side is taken in
H
and the right–hand side
is the usual product in
\mathbb{R}
followed by the embedding
i
.

Proof.

If
t=0
or
x=0
, the identity follows from the definition and the
fact that
i(0)=0
. Assume
t,x\neq 0
and write

t=\pm\,|t|
,
x=\pm\,|x|
. Then by
Lemma
3.23
and Proposition
3.20

we are simply recovering the usual sign–magnitude rule for the real
product
tx
. A direct case–by–case check (positive times positive,
positive times negative, etc.) shows that the sign and magnitude of

t\cdot i(x)
coincide with those of
tx
, so

t\cdot i(x)=i(tx)
.
∎

The next result describes the behaviour of scalar multiplication with
respect to the sign and magnitude maps on
H
.

Lemma 3.25

(Sign and magnitude under scalar multiplication)
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in H
. Then

|t\cdot x|=|t|\cdot|x|.

Moreover, if
t\neq 0
and
x\neq 0
, then

\operatorname{sgn}(t\cdot x)=\begin{cases}\operatorname{sgn}(x),&t>0,\\[2.0pt]
+,&t<0,\ \operatorname{sgn}(x)=-,\\[2.0pt]
-,&t<0,\ \operatorname{sgn}(x)=+,\\[2.0pt]
\Lambda,&t<0,\ \operatorname{sgn}(x)=\Lambda.\end{cases}

Proof.

If
t=0
or
x=0
, then
t\cdot x=0
and
|t\cdot x|=0
, while

|t|\cdot|x|=0
as well, so the magnitude identity holds. If

t\neq 0
,
x\neq 0
, write
x=(\sigma,a)
with
\sigma\in S^{\times}
and

a>0
. If
t>0
, Lemma
3.23
gives

t\cdot x=(\sigma,ta)
, so
|t\cdot x|=ta=|t||x|
and

\operatorname{sgn}(t\cdot x)=\sigma=\operatorname{sgn}(x)
. If
t<0
, then

t\cdot x=(\sigma^{\prime},|t|a)
with
\sigma^{\prime}=-*\sigma
as in
Lemma
3.23
. Thus
|t\cdot x|=|t|a=|t||x|
, and

\operatorname{sgn}(t\cdot x)=\sigma^{\prime}
is obtained from
\sigma
by flipping
+
and

-
while fixing
\Lambda
. This is exactly the behaviour listed in
the statement.
∎

We now turn to distributivity of scalar multiplication over
hyperaddition. Since
\boxplus
is multivalued, the natural formulation
uses equality of subsets of
H
.

Definition 3.26

(Distributivity at a triple)
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
x,y\in H
. We say that
left distributivity
holds at
(t,x,y)
if

t\cdot(x\boxplus y)=(t\cdot x)\boxplus(t\cdot y)

as subsets of
H
, where

t\cdot(x\boxplus y):=\{t\cdot z:z\in x\boxplus y\}.

The following theorem shows that distributivity holds for all real scalars.

Theorem 3.27

(Distributivity for real scalars)
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
x,y\in H
. Then

t\cdot(x\boxplus y)=(t\cdot x)\boxplus(t\cdot y)

as subsets of
H
. In particular, for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
the map

x\mapsto t\cdot x
is an endomorphism of the additive hypermagma

(H,\boxplus)
.

Proof.

If
t=0
, then
i(t)=0
and
0\cdot z=0
for all
z\in H
, so

0\cdot(x\boxplus y)=\{0\}.

On the other hand,
0\cdot x=0\cdot y=0
, so

(0\cdot x)\boxplus(0\cdot y)=0\boxplus 0=\{0\}.

Thus the identity holds for
t=0
.

Now assume
t>0
. By Lemma
3.23
, for every

x=(\sigma,a)
we have
t\cdot x=(\sigma,ta)
: scalar multiplication by

t>0
preserves signs and scales magnitudes by
t
. We must show that
for each pair
x,y
the hyper–sum of the scaled elements coincides with
the scaled hyper–sum of the original elements:

(t\cdot x)\boxplus(t\cdot y)=\{t\cdot z:z\in x\boxplus y\}.

If
x=0
or
y=0
, then
x\boxplus y
is a singleton by
Definition
3.14
, and the equality is immediate from
the fact that
t\cdot 0=0
and
0
is a neutral element.

If
x,y\neq 0
, write
x=(\sigma,a)
,
y=(\tau,b)
with

\sigma,\tau\in S^{\times}
,
a,b>0
. We analyse the four cases in the
definition of
\boxplus
.

Case 1: Same real sign.
Suppose
\sigma=\tau\in\{+,-\}
. Then
by clause (2),

x\boxplus y=\{(\sigma,a+b)\}.

Hence

t\cdot(x\boxplus y)=\{t\cdot(\sigma,a+b)\}=\{(\sigma,t(a+b))\}.

On the other hand,

t\cdot x=(\sigma,ta),\quad t\cdot y=(\sigma,tb),

so

(t\cdot x)\boxplus(t\cdot y)=(\sigma,ta)\boxplus(\sigma,tb)=\{(\sigma,ta+tb)\}=\{(\sigma,t(a+b))\}.

Thus the two sets coincide.

Case 2: Opposite real signs.
Suppose
\{\sigma,\tau\}=\{+,-\}
,
say
\sigma=+
,
\tau=-
; the other ordering is symmetric. Then

x\boxplus y=(+,a)\boxplus(-,b)=\begin{cases}\{(+,a-b)\},&a>b,\\[2.0pt]
\{0\},&a=b,\\[2.0pt]
\{(-,b-a)\},&a<b.\end{cases}

If
a>b
, then
a-b>0
and

t\cdot(x\boxplus y)=\{t\cdot(+,a-b)\}=\{(+,t(a-b))\}.

On the other hand,

t\cdot x=(+,ta),\quad t\cdot y=(-,tb),

and since
ta>tb
we have

(t\cdot x)\boxplus(t\cdot y)=(+,ta)\boxplus(-,tb)=\{(+,ta-tb)\}=\{(+,t(a-b))\}.

The cases
a=b
and
a<b
are handled similarly, using the fact that
multiplication by
t>0
preserves the inequalities
a>b
,
a=b
,

a<b
. In each subcase the two sets agree.

Case 3: Interaction with
\Lambda
.
Suppose, for example,

\sigma=+
,
\tau=\Lambda
; the other possibilities

\{\sigma,\tau\}=\{-,\Lambda\}
are analogous. Then

x\boxplus y=(+,a)\boxplus(\Lambda,b)=\{(\Lambda,a+b)\}.

Hence

t\cdot(x\boxplus y)=\{t\cdot(\Lambda,a+b)\}=\{(\Lambda,t(a+b))\}.

On the other hand,

t\cdot x=(+,ta),\quad t\cdot y=(\Lambda,tb),

so

(t\cdot x)\boxplus(t\cdot y)=(+,ta)\boxplus(\Lambda,tb)=\{(\Lambda,ta+tb)\}=\{(\Lambda,t(a+b))\}.

Thus the equality holds.

Case 4:
\Lambda
–
\Lambda
sums.
Suppose
\sigma=\tau=\Lambda
.
Then by clause (5) of Definition
3.14
,

x\boxplus y=(\Lambda,a)\boxplus(\Lambda,b)=\begin{cases}\{(\Lambda,2a),\,0\},&a=b,\\[2.0pt]
\{(\Lambda,a+b),\,(+,|a-b|),\,(-,|a-b|)\},&a\neq b.\end{cases}

If
a=b
, then

t\cdot(x\boxplus y)=\{(\Lambda,2ta),\,0\}=(\Lambda,ta)\boxplus(\Lambda,ta)=(t\cdot x)\boxplus(t\cdot y).

If
a\neq b
, then
ta\neq tb
and

t\cdot(x\boxplus y)=\{(\Lambda,t(a+b)),\,(+,t|a-b|),\,(-,t|a-b|)\},

while

(t\cdot x)\boxplus(t\cdot y)=(\Lambda,ta)\boxplus(\Lambda,tb)=\{(\Lambda,t(a+b)),\,(+,|ta-tb|),\,(-,|ta-tb|)\},

and
|ta-tb|=t|a-b|
for
t>0
. Thus the equality holds.

Thus distributivity holds in all four cases.

Since every pair
(x,y)
falls into one of these cases (or the trivial
case with a zero term), the equality

t\cdot(x\boxplus y)=(t\cdot x)\boxplus(t\cdot y)
holds for all
x,y\in H

whenever
t>0
. Together with the
t=0
case, this settles all
t\geq 0
.

Finally, assume
t<0
and write
t=-s
with
s>0
. Let

\iota:H\to H
be the involution that fixes
0
and
\Lambda
and
swaps
+
and
-
:

\iota(0)=0,\quad\iota(+,a)=(-,a),\quad\iota(-,a)=(+,a),\quad\iota(\Lambda,a)=(\Lambda,a).

A direct inspection of Definition
3.14
shows that
\iota

is an automorphism of
(H,\boxplus)
, i.e.
\iota(x\boxplus y)=\iota(x)\boxplus\iota(y)
.
Moreover, Lemma
3.23
gives
t\cdot x=\iota(s\cdot x)
for all

x\in H
. Therefore

t\cdot(x\boxplus y)=\iota\bigl(s\cdot(x\boxplus y)\bigr)=\iota\bigl((s\cdot x)\boxplus(s\cdot y)\bigr)=(t\cdot x)\boxplus(t\cdot y),

where we used the
s>0
case and the fact that
\iota
is applied elementwise
to subsets. This completes the proof for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

We can summarize these facts in the language of hypermodules over
semirings.

Definition 3.28

(
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
–hypersemimodule structure)
.

Let
(S,+,\cdot)
be a commutative semiring. A
left

S
–hypersemimodule
is a pair
(M,\odot)
where
M
is a set equipped
with a commutative hyperoperation
\odot
and an action

S\times M\to M
,
(s,x)\mapsto s\cdot x
, such that:

(1)

0_{S}\cdot x=0_{M}
and
1_{S}\cdot x=x
for all
x\in M
,
where
0_{M}
is the neutral element for
\odot
.

(2)

(st)\cdot x=s\cdot(t\cdot x)
for all
s,t\in S
and

x\in M
.

(3)

s\cdot(x\odot y)=(s\cdot x)\odot(s\cdot y)
for all

s\in S
,
x,y\in M
.

(4)

(s_{1}+s_{2})\cdot x\subseteq(s_{1}\cdot x)\odot(s_{2}\cdot x)

for all
s_{1},s_{2}\in S
,
x\in M
.

Corollary 3.29

(
H
as an
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
–hypersemimodule)
.

Let
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be the semiring of nonnegative reals with the usual
addition and multiplication. Then, with the hyperaddition
\boxplus
from
Definition
3.14
and scalar multiplication from
Definition
3.22
, the pair
(H,\boxplus)
carries the
structure of a left
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
–hypersemimodule.

Moreover, Theorem
3.27
shows that distributivity holds for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
in the sense of Definition
3.26
.

Proof.

The semiring axioms for
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
are standard. The action

t\cdot x
for
t\geq 0
and
x\in H
satisfies

0\cdot x=0
and
1\cdot x=x
by Lemma
3.23
. The
compatibility
(st)\cdot x=s\cdot(t\cdot x)
for
s,t\geq 0
follows
immediately from the fact that for
t>0
we have

t\cdot(\sigma,a)=(\sigma,ta)
, so iterating the action simply
multiplies magnitudes.

Theorem
3.27
gives distributivity

s\cdot(x\boxplus y)=(s\cdot x)\boxplus(s\cdot y)
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and

x,y\in H
. Finally, the inclusion

(s_{1}+s_{2})\cdot x\subseteq(s_{1}\cdot x)\boxplus(s_{2}\cdot x)
holds with
equality, again because for
s\geq 0
the action rescales magnitudes and
preserves signs in a way that is compatible with the hyperaddition
rules.

This verifies the hypersemimodule axioms.
∎

Remark 3.30

(Role of scalar multiplication)
.

Scalar multiplication clarifies the interaction between the three–sign
system and the classical real field. For
t>0
, the action of
t
on

H
is a uniform rescaling of magnitudes, compatible with
hyperaddition in the strongest sense. For
t<0
, the action is the
same rescaling by
|t|
composed with the involution that swaps
+
and

-
while fixing
\Lambda
. In particular, Theorem
3.27

shows that every real scalar acts by an endomorphism of the additive
hypermagma
(H,\boxplus)
.

The
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
–hypersemimodule viewpoint remains useful when one
wants to keep track of magnitudes without sign changes; we will return
to this perspective in later sections when we discuss order and
geometric interpretations.

4.
Associativity and its controlled failure

4.1.
Associativity basics

We begin the analysis of additive associativity for the hyperaddition

\boxplus
on
H
. Recall that
(H,\boxplus)
is a commutative
hypermagma with neutral element
0
and unique additive inverses
(Proposition
3.19
), but it is not a canonical
commutative hypergroup: associativity and reversibility fail in
general. In this subsection we record some basic facts about
associativity and give a first explicit counterexample.

We use the notion of associativity at a triple introduced in
Definition
2.10
.

Definition 4.1

(Associativity at a triple in
H
)
.

Let
x,y,z\in H
. We say that
\boxplus
is associative at the
triple
(x,y,z)
if

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z\;=\;x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)

as subsets of
H
, where

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z:=\bigcup_{u\in x\boxplus y}u\boxplus z,\qquad x\boxplus(y\boxplus z):=\bigcup_{v\in y\boxplus z}x\boxplus v.

We say that
\boxplus
is globally associative
if it is associative
at every triple
(x,y,z)\in H^{3}
.

Since
\boxplus
is commutative (Lemma
3.15
), the
notion of associativity at a given triple is insensitive to
permutations of the entries.

Lemma 4.2

(Symmetry of associativity under permutations)
.

Let
x,y,z\in H
. If
\boxplus
is associative at
(x,y,z)
, then it is
associative at every permutation
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})
of
(x,y,z)
.

Proof.

This is a general fact for any commutative hypermagma. Suppose
\boxplus

is associative at
(x,y,z)
. Because
\boxplus
is commutative, the two
bracketings for any permutation
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})
of
(x,y,z)
can be
expressed by reordering the elements in one of the bracketings for

(x,y,z)
.

For example, consider the permutation
(y,x,z)
. Commutativity gives

(y\boxplus x)\boxplus z=(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z,\qquad y\boxplus(x\boxplus z)=x\boxplus(y\boxplus z),

and by associativity at
(x,y,z)
we have

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)
. Thus

(y\boxplus x)\boxplus z=y\boxplus(x\boxplus z)
, showing associativity at
(y,x,z)
.
The other permutations are handled similarly. A more formal argument
proceeds by noting that both sides of the associativity identity for

(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})
can be rewritten as unions of expressions of the form

u\boxplus v
with
\{u,v\}=\{x,y,z\}
, and that these unions coincide when
associativity holds for one ordering. We omit the routine details.
∎

We next record two basic situations where associativity always holds:
whenever one of the three elements is zero, and whenever we restrict
to the classical real line.

Lemma 4.3

(Associativity whenever a zero is present)
.

Let
x,y,z\in H
. If at least one of
x,y,z
equals
0
, then

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=x\boxplus(y\boxplus z).

In other words,
\boxplus
is associative at any triple containing
0
.

Proof.

Because of the symmetry in Lemma
4.2
, it
suffices to treat the case
x=0
. Then for any
y,z\in H
,

0\boxplus y=\{y\}

by Lemma
3.16
, so

(0\boxplus y)\boxplus z=\{y\}\boxplus z=y\boxplus z.

On the other hand,

y\boxplus z=y\boxplus z,

and

0\boxplus(y\boxplus z)=\bigcup_{v\in y\boxplus z}0\boxplus v=\bigcup_{v\in y\boxplus z}\{v\}=y\boxplus z.

Hence
(0\boxplus y)\boxplus z=0\boxplus(y\boxplus z)
for all
y,z\in H
. By
Lemma
4.2
, the same holds for any triple
containing a zero in any position.
∎

Proposition 4.4

(Associativity on the classical line)
.

Let
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}=i(\mathbb{R})\subset H
be the classical real line. Then

\boxplus
is associative at every triple
(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{3}
. Equivalently,

\boxplus
restricted to
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
is an associative (single–valued) operation,
and
(\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}},\boxplus)
is isomorphic to the additive group
(\mathbb{R},+)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.20
, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,

i(x)\boxplus i(y)=\{i(x+y)\}.

Thus the restriction of
\boxplus
to
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
is single–valued, and the
induced binary operation on
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
is transported from the usual
addition on
\mathbb{R}
via the bijection
i:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
. In particular,
for any
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}
,

((i(x)\boxplus i(y))\boxplus i(z))=\{i((x+y)+z)\},

and

(i(x)\boxplus(i(y)\boxplus i(z)))=\{i(x+(y+z))\}.

Since
(x+y)+z=x+(y+z)
in
\mathbb{R}
, the singletons coincide, so

(i(x)\boxplus i(y))\boxplus i(z)=i(x)\boxplus(i(y)\boxplus i(z)).

Thus
\boxplus
is associative at every triple of the form

(i(x),i(y),i(z))
, i.e. at every triple in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{3}
, and

(\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}},\boxplus)
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{R},+)
as claimed.
∎

The two preceding results show that all the nontrivial associativity
behaviour is concentrated in triples involving at least one element
from the
\Lambda
–sector. In particular, any triple with entries in

H_{+}\cup H_{-}\cup\{0\}
is associative, since
H_{+}\cup H_{-}\cup\{0\}=\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
.

It is also easy to see that there are triples involving
\Lambda
for
which associativity still holds. For example, certain ordered
triples with signs
(+,\Lambda,-)
or
(-,\Lambda,+)
turn out to be
associative for all choices of magnitudes. We will return to such
patterns in later subsections. For the moment, the following
proposition suffices to show that
(H,\boxplus)
is genuinely
nonassociative.

Proposition 4.5

(
(H,\boxplus)
is not associative)
.

The hyperaddition
\boxplus
on
H
is not globally associative. In
particular, associativity fails at the triple

x=(+,1),\qquad y=(-,1),\qquad z=(\Lambda,1).

Proof.

We compute both bracketings explicitly using
Definition
3.14
.

First,

x\boxplus y=(+,1)\boxplus(-,1)=\{0\}

by clause (3), since the magnitudes are equal. Hence

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=\{0\}\boxplus(\Lambda,1)=0\boxplus(\Lambda,1)=\{(\Lambda,1)\}

using clauses (1) and (4).

On the other hand,

y\boxplus z=(-,1)\boxplus(\Lambda,1)=\{(\Lambda,2)\}

by clause (4), and then

x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)=(+,1)\boxplus(\Lambda,2)=\{(\Lambda,3)\}

again by clause (4). Thus

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=\{(\Lambda,1)\}\neq\{(\Lambda,3)\}=x\boxplus(y\boxplus z),

so associativity fails at the triple
(x,y,z)=((+,1),(-,1),(\Lambda,1))
.

Consequently,
\boxplus
is not associative on
H
.
∎

Remark 4.6

(Where associativity is automatic)
.

From the results above we can already list several contexts in which
associativity holds:

•

For any triple containing
0
, by Lemma
4.3
.

•

For any triple
(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{3}
, i.e. with all entries in
the classical real line, by Proposition
4.4
.

•

By Lemma
4.2
, these statements remain
true after arbitrary permutations of the entries.

In particular, all triples drawn from
H_{+}\cup H_{-}\cup\{0\}
are
associative, and the failure of associativity is confined to triples
involving the
\Lambda
–sector. The example in
Proposition
4.5
shows that even very simple such
triples can fail associativity. In later subsections we will analyse
specific nonassociative patterns in detail, including triples of the
form
(+,a),(-,b),(\Lambda,c)
, and quantify the associativity defect
in terms of cancellation in the underlying real field.

4.2.
Non–associativity for ordered
(+,-,\Lambda)
–triples

We now analyze in detail one of the simplest families of triples where
associativity fails, namely ordered triples with sign pattern

\bigl(\operatorname{sgn}(x),\operatorname{sgn}(y),\operatorname{sgn}(z)\bigr)=(+,-,\Lambda).

A key point is that, for our hyperaddition
\boxplus
, associativity depends
not only on the
multiset
of signs but also on their
order
;
in particular, the ordered pattern
(+,-,\Lambda)
behaves differently
from
(+,\Lambda,-)
, even though both have sign multiset

\{+,-,\Lambda\}
.

Throughout this subsection we fix positive real numbers
a,b,c>0
and
consider the triple

x=(+,a),\qquad y=(-,b),\qquad z=(\Lambda,c)\in H.

Lemma 4.7

(Bracketings for the ordered
(+,-,\Lambda)
triple)
.

Let
x=(+,a)
,
y=(-,b)
,
z=(\Lambda,c)
with
a,b,c>0
. Then both
bracketings of the hyperaddition are singletons in the
\Lambda
–sector,
and their magnitudes are given by

\displaystyle(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z

\displaystyle=\{(\Lambda,\,c+|a-b|)\},

\displaystyle x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)

\displaystyle=\{(\Lambda,\,a+b+c)\}.

In particular, associativity holds at
(x,y,z)
if and only if

a+b=|a-b|
, which is impossible for
a,b>0
.

Proof.

We compute both bracketings case by case, using the definition of
\boxplus

(Definition
3.14
).

First bracketing.

We start with
x\boxplus y=(+,a)\boxplus(-,b)
. By clause (3) of
Definition
3.14
,

x\boxplus y=\begin{cases}\{(+,a-b)\},&a>b,\\[2.0pt]
\{0\},&a=b,\\[2.0pt]
\{(-,b-a)\},&a<b.\end{cases}

\bullet
If
a>b
, then
x\boxplus y=\{(+,a-b)\}
and

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=\{(+,a-b)\}\boxplus(\Lambda,c)=(+,a-b)\boxplus(\Lambda,c)=\{(\Lambda,(a-b)+c)\}

by clause (4). Thus

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=\{(\Lambda,c+a-b)\}.

\bullet
If
a=b
, then
x\boxplus y=\{0\}
and

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=\{0\}\boxplus(\Lambda,c)=0\boxplus(\Lambda,c)=\{(\Lambda,c)\}

by clauses (1) and (4).

\bullet
If
a<b
, then
x\boxplus y=\{(-,b-a)\}
and

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=\{(-,b-a)\}\boxplus(\Lambda,c)=(-,b-a)\boxplus(\Lambda,c)=\{(\Lambda,(b-a)+c)\}

again by clause (4). Thus

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=\{(\Lambda,c+b-a)\}.

In all three cases, we can summarize the result as

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=\{(\Lambda,c+|a-b|)\},

since
|a-b|
equals
a-b
,
0
, or
b-a
according to whether
a>b
,

a=b
, or
a<b
.

Second bracketing.

We now compute
y\boxplus z=(-,b)\boxplus(\Lambda,c)
. By clause (4) of
Definition
3.14
, we have for all
b,c>0

y\boxplus z=(-,b)\boxplus(\Lambda,c)=\{(\Lambda,b+c)\}.

Thus

x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)=(+,a)\boxplus\{(\Lambda,b+c)\}=(+,a)\boxplus(\Lambda,b+c).

Applying clause (4) once more, with
a>0
and
b+c>0
, we obtain

x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)=\{(\Lambda,a+(b+c))\}=\{(\Lambda,a+b+c)\}.

This proves the two displayed formulas. In particular, both
bracketings are singletons with a
\Lambda
sign. They coincide if and
only if the magnitudes are equal:

c+|a-b|=a+b+c\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad|a-b|=a+b.

But for
a,b>0
we have
|a-b|\leq a+b
, with equality only when one of

a,b
vanishes. Since
a,b>0
by assumption,
|a-b|<a+b
, so the
magnitudes cannot match. Hence
(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z
and
x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)
are
distinct for all
a,b,c>0
, and associativity fails at
(x,y,z)
in
every such case.
∎

As a consequence, we obtain a family of explicit nonassociative
triples, indexed by the positive reals.

Corollary 4.8

(Systematic non–associativity for
(+,-,\Lambda)
)
.

For every choice of
a,b,c>0
, the hyperaddition
\boxplus
is not
associative at the triple

\bigl((+,a),(-,b),(\Lambda,c)\bigr).

Proof.

Immediate from Lemma
4.7
, since the two
bracketings always yield different singletons.
∎

The difference between the magnitudes in
Lemma
4.7
can be expressed in a particularly
simple way. It measures exactly the “absolute value lost” when
cancelling
a
against
-b
in the real field; this is the same
quantity that appears in the cancellation mass

C(a,-b)
introduced in Definition
2.5
.

Lemma 4.9

(Associativity defect and cancellation mass)
.

Let
x=(+,a)
,
y=(-,b)
,
z=(\Lambda,c)
with
a,b,c>0
. Denote by

m_{L}
and
m_{R}
the magnitudes of the unique elements of

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z
and
x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)
respectively. Then

m_{L}=c+|a-b|,\qquad m_{R}=a+b+c,

and the difference

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c):=m_{R}-m_{L}=a+b-|a-b|

is independent of
c
and equals the cancellation mass

C(a,-b)
:

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=a+b-|a-b|=C(a,-b).

Proof.

The expressions for
m_{L}
and
m_{R}
follow from
Lemma
4.7
. Subtracting,

m_{R}-m_{L}=(a+b+c)-(c+|a-b|)=a+b-|a-b|.

This quantity is independent of
c
and, by
Definition
2.5
, coincides with

C(a,-b)=|a|+|-b|-|a+(-b)|=a+b-|a-b|.

This proves the claim.
∎

Using the elementary identity

a+b-|a-b|=2\min(a,b)
for
a,b\geq 0
, we obtain the following
more symmetric description of the associativity defect.

Proposition 4.10

(Explicit defect formula for
(+,-,\Lambda)
)
.

For
a,b,c>0
, let
x=(+,a)
,
y=(-,b)
,
z=(\Lambda,c)
and let

m_{L},m_{R}
be as in Lemma
4.9
. Then

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c):=m_{R}-m_{L}=2\min(a,b).

Equivalently,

a+b-|a-b|=2\min(a,b)\qquad\text{for all }a,b\geq 0.

Proof.

The equality
a+b-|a-b|=2\min(a,b)
for
a,b\geq 0
is elementary. If

a\geq b
, then
|a-b|=a-b
and

a+b-|a-b|=a+b-(a-b)=2b=2\min(a,b).

If
a<b
, then
|a-b|=b-a
and

a+b-|a-b|=a+b-(b-a)=2a=2\min(a,b).

Hence the identity holds for all
a,b\geq 0
.

Combining this with Lemma
4.9
, we find

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=a+b-|a-b|=2\min(a,b),

as claimed.
∎

We can now interpret the associativity defect in the ordered

(+,-,\Lambda)
case as a direct lift of the real cancellation
phenomenon.

Theorem 4.11

(Proof of Theorem
1.1
)
.

Let
a,b,c>0
and consider

x=(+,a),\quad y=(-,b),\quad z=(\Lambda,c)\in H.

Then:

(1)

Both
(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z
and
x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)
are singletons in the

\Lambda
–sector:

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=\{(\Lambda,m_{L})\},\qquad x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)=\{(\Lambda,m_{R})\},

with

m_{L}=c+|a-b|,\qquad m_{R}=a+b+c.

(2)

The associativity defect

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c):=m_{R}-m_{L}

satisfies

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=a+b-|a-b|=2\min(a,b)=C(a,-b),

where
C
is the cancellation mass from
Definition
2.5
.

(3)

In particular,
\boxplus
is never associative at
(x,y,z)
for

a,b,c>0
, and the defect depends only on
(a,b)
, not on
c
.

Proof.

Item (1) is precisely Lemma
4.7
. Item (2)
is Lemma
4.9
together with
Proposition
4.10
. For (3), note that

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)>0
for all
a,b>0
, so the two singleton bracketings
never coincide; hence associativity fails at
(x,y,z)
for all

a,b,c>0
. The formula
\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=C(a,-b)
shows explicitly
that
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
does not depend on
c
.
∎

Remark 4.12

(Order sensitivity and other sign patterns)
.

The analysis above highlights two important features.

First, the failure of associativity for the pattern
(+,-,\Lambda)
is

order–sensitive
. If we permute the triple to, say,

(+,\Lambda,-)
or
(\Lambda,+,-)
, the resulting bracketings behave
differently; in some such permutations, associativity actually holds
for all magnitudes
a,b,c>0
. Thus it is not enough to know the

multiset
of signs
\{+,-,\Lambda\}
; the ordered triple of signs
matters.

Second, the quantitative defect is entirely controlled by the real
cancellation between
a
and
-b
. The third parameter
c
simply
shifts both magnitudes
m_{L}
and
m_{R}
by the same amount. This
observation will be a key motivation for the ambient cancellation
monoid
(K,\oplus)
constructed in
Section
6
, where the quantity

C(a,-b)=a+b-|a-b|
appears as a second coordinate recording lost
absolute value.

4.3.
Associativity defect for
(+,a),(-,b),(\Lambda,c)

In this subsection we refine the analysis of ordered
(+,-,\Lambda)
–triples
from Section
4.2
by isolating and
studying the
associativity defect
as a quantitative function of
the magnitudes
a,b,c>0
.

Throughout we fix

x=(+,a),\qquad y=(-,b),\qquad z=(\Lambda,c)\in H\qquad(a,b,c>0),

and recall from Lemma
4.7
that both bracketings
are singletons in the
\Lambda
–sector:

\displaystyle(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z

\displaystyle=\{(\Lambda,\,c+|a-b|)\},

\displaystyle x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)

\displaystyle=\{(\Lambda,\,a+b+c)\}.

We now package this discrepancy into an explicit defect function.

Definition 4.13

(Associativity defect function for
(+,-,\Lambda)
)
.

For
a,b,c>0
we define the
left
and
right magnitudes
by

m_{L}(a,b,c):=c+|a-b|,\qquad m_{R}(a,b,c):=a+b+c.

The
associativity defect
at the triple

\bigl((+,a),(-,b),(\Lambda,c)\bigr)
is the nonnegative real number

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c):=m_{R}(a,b,c)-m_{L}(a,b,c)=a+b-|a-b|.

By Lemma
4.7
,
m_{L}
and
m_{R}
are exactly the
magnitudes of the unique elements in
(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z
and

x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)
, respectively, so
\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)
measures how far
\boxplus

fails to be associative at this triple. The following lemma collects
its basic properties.

Lemma 4.14

(Elementary properties of
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
)
.

For all
a,b,c>0
the associativity defect
\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)
from
Definition
4.13
satisfies:

(1)

Nonnegativity and independence of
c
:

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c^{\prime})\ \text{for all }c,c^{\prime}>0.

(2)

Explicit formula:

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=a+b-|a-b|=2\min(a,b).

(3)

Symmetry:

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=\boldsymbol{\kappa}(b,a,c).

(4)

Strict positivity:

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)>0\quad\text{for all }a,b>0.

Proof.

(1) Nonnegativity follows from
|a-b|\leq a+b
for
a,b\geq 0
, so

a+b-|a-b|\geq 0
. The expression
a+b-|a-b|
does not involve
c
, so

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)
is independent of
c
.

(2) For
a,b\geq 0
we have the elementary identity

a+b-|a-b|=2\min(a,b)
. Indeed, if
a\geq b
, then
|a-b|=a-b
and

a+b-|a-b|=a+b-(a-b)=2b=2\min(a,b),

while if
a<b
, then
|a-b|=b-a
and

a+b-|a-b|=a+b-(b-a)=2a=2\min(a,b).

Applying this with
a,b>0
gives the claimed formula for
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
.

(3) Symmetry is immediate from either expression:

a+b-|a-b|=b+a-|b-a|,

and
\min(a,b)=\min(b,a)
.

(4) For
a,b>0
one has
\min(a,b)>0
, hence

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=2\min(a,b)>0
. This reflects the fact that associativity
never holds at the triple
((+,a),(-,b),(\Lambda,c))
when
a,b>0
.
∎

The relationship with the cancellation mass

C:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
of Definition
2.5
is
immediate.

Proposition 4.15

(Defect and cancellation mass)
.

For all
a,b,c>0
one has

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=C(a,-b),

where
C(r_{1},r_{2})=|r_{1}|+|r_{2}|-|r_{1}+r_{2}|
.

Proof.

By Definition
4.13
and
Definition
2.5
,

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=a+b-|a-b|=|a|+|-b|-|a+(-b)|=C(a,-b),

since
a>0
and
b>0
.
∎

In particular, the associativity defect for the triple

((+,a),(-,b),(\Lambda,c))
is
exactly
the amount of absolute
value lost when adding
a
and
-b
as real numbers. This is one way
in which
H
“remembers” the real cancellation phenomenon.

We next record some regularity properties of the defect function,
which will be useful for geometric interpretations and for comparing
with the ambient cancellation monoid in Section
6
.

Lemma 4.16

(Continuity and piecewise linearity)
.

Consider the function

\boldsymbol{\kappa}:(0,\infty)^{3}\longrightarrow(0,\infty),\qquad(a,b,c)\mapsto a+b-|a-b|.

Then:

(1)

\boldsymbol{\kappa}
extends continuously to a function on

[0,\infty)^{3}
, given by the same formula.

(2)

As a function of
(a,b)
(with
c
fixed),
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
is
piecewise linear with a single “break line” along
a=b
.
More precisely,

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=\begin{cases}2b,&a\geq b,\\[2.0pt]
2a,&a<b.\end{cases}

(3)

For each fixed
b\geq 0
and
c\geq 0
, the map

a\mapsto\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)
is continuous, nondecreasing, and
piecewise linear, and similarly with the roles of
a
and
b

reversed.

Proof.

(1) The formula
\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=a+b-|a-b|
makes sense for

a,b,c\geq 0
and is continuous as a composition of continuous
operations on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(addition, absolute value, and subtraction).
Thus
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
extends continuously to
[0,\infty)^{3}
.

(2) For
a,b\geq 0
we have already seen that

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=a+b-|a-b|=\begin{cases}2b,&a\geq b,\\
2a,&a<b,\end{cases}

which is linear in
(a,b)
on each of the two regions
\{a\geq b\}
and

\{a<b\}
separated by the line
a=b
.

(3) Fix
b,c\geq 0
. On
[0,\infty)
, the function

a\mapsto\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)
coincides with
2b
on
[b,\infty)
and with

2a
on
[0,b)
, so it is continuous, nondecreasing, and piecewise
linear with a single break point at
a=b
. The statement with
a
and

b
interchanged is identical.
∎

Lemma 4.17

(Homogeneity and scaling behaviour)
.

Let
\lambda\geq 0
and
a,b,c>0
. Then

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(\lambda a,\lambda b,\lambda c)=\lambda\,\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c).

Proof.

Using Definition
4.13
and the fact that

|\lambda a-\lambda b|=\lambda|a-b|
for
\lambda\geq 0
, we obtain

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(\lambda a,\lambda b,\lambda c)=\lambda a+\lambda b-|\lambda a-\lambda b|=\lambda(a+b-|a-b|)=\lambda\,\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c).

∎

Thus the associativity defect behaves linearly under simultaneous
rescaling of all three magnitudes. This is consistent with the

\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
–hypersemimodule structure on
H
described in
Corollary
3.29
: rescaling by
\lambda>0

simply multiplies all magnitudes by
\lambda
, and hence multiplies
the defect by
\lambda
.

We can summarize the situation as follows.

Theorem 4.18

(Quantitative associativity defect for
(+,a),(-,b),(\Lambda,c)
)
.

For each triple
a,b,c>0
, let

x=(+,a),\quad y=(-,b),\quad z=(\Lambda,c)\in H.

Then:

(1)

Both bracketings of the hyperaddition at
(x,y,z)
are
singletons in the
\Lambda
–sector:

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=\{(\Lambda,m_{L})\},\qquad x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)=\{(\Lambda,m_{R})\},

with

m_{L}=c+|a-b|,\qquad m_{R}=a+b+c.

(2)

The associativity defect

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c):=m_{R}-m_{L}

is independent of
c
and satisfies

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=a+b-|a-b|=2\min(a,b)=C(a,-b).

(3)

For fixed
b,c>0
(resp.
a,c>0
), the function

a\mapsto\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)
(resp.
b\mapsto\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)
) is
continuous, nondecreasing, and piecewise linear with a single
break point at
a=b
(resp.
b=a
).

(4)

For every
\lambda\geq 0
,

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(\lambda a,\lambda b,\lambda c)=\lambda\,\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c).

(5)

In particular,
\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)>0
for all
a,b>0
, so the
hyperaddition
\boxplus
is never associative at the triple

\bigl((+,a),(-,b),(\Lambda,c)\bigr)
when
a,b,c>0
.

Proof.

(1) is Lemma
4.7
. (2) is
Proposition
4.15
together with
Lemma
4.14
. (3) and (4) are
Lemmas
4.16
and
4.17
,
respectively. (5) follows from
\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=2\min(a,b)
and
\min(a,b)>0
for

a,b>0
.
∎

Remark 4.19

(Interpretation and outlook)
.

The theorem shows that in the ordered
(+,-,\Lambda)
case the failure
of associativity is completely controlled by a single scalar quantity,

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)
, which is:

•

symmetric in
(a,b)
,

•

homogeneous of degree one,

•

equal to twice the smaller of
a
and
b
,

•

and equal to the cancellation mass
C(a,-b)
in the real
field.

Thus
(H,\boxplus)
does not forget the real cancellation
a+(-b)
; it
records the lost absolute value as a systematic displacement between
the two bracketings of
(+,a),(-,b),(\Lambda,c)
. In
Section
6
we will see that this picture is closely
related to the ambient cancellation monoid
(K,\oplus)
, where

C(a,-b)
appears as an explicit second coordinate tracking
cancellation.

5.
Sign layer and ambient structure

5.1.
The hypergroup structure on the sign set

In this section we construct a canonical commutative hypergroup on the
four–element sign set

S=\{0,+,-,\Lambda\},

extending the additive hypergroup of the usual sign hyperfield
(Section
2.4
). Intuitively, the new symbol

\Lambda
represents a “cancellation–absorbing” sign: it behaves
multiplicatively as an idempotent (Section
3.2
)
and additively as a sign that can absorb contributions from both
+

and
-
and, when combined with itself, may produce all three classical
signs as well as
0
.

Recall from Definition
2.18
the additive
hyperoperation
\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}
on

S_{\mathrm{sign}}:=\{0,+,-\}\subset S
:

\displaystyle 0\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}x

\displaystyle=x\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}0=\{x\}

\displaystyle\text{for all }x\in S_{\mathrm{sign}},

\displaystyle+\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}+

\displaystyle=\{+\},

\displaystyle-\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}-

\displaystyle=\{-\},

\displaystyle+\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}-

\displaystyle=-\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}+=\{+,0,-\}.

We now extend this to
S
by specifying how
\Lambda

with the classical signs.

Definition 5.1

(Sign hyperoperation on

(1) Neutral element.
For all

+\oplus_{S}+=\{+\},\quad-\oplus_{S}-=\{-\},\quad+\oplus_{S}-=-\oplus_{S}+=\{+,0,-\}.

(3) Interaction with

\displaystyle\sigma\oplus_{S}\Lambda

\displaystyle=\Lambda\oplus_{S}\sigma:=\{\Lambda\},

\displaystyle\Lambda\oplus_{S}0

(4)
\Lambda

\Lambda

is understood to be symmetric:

\sigma_{1}\oplus_{S}\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{2}\oplus_{S}\sigma_{1}
.

Lemma 5.2

(Basic properties of
(S,\oplus_{S})
)
.

The hyperoperation
\oplus_{S}
in Definition
5.1
is
well-defined, nonempty-valued, and commutative. Its neutral element
is
0
, and every element has a unique additive inverse given by

-0=0,\quad-(+)=-,\quad-(-)=+,\quad-\Lambda=\Lambda.

Proof.

By inspection of the cases in
Definition
5.1
, every ordered pair

(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\in S^{2}
falls into exactly one clause, and the
right-hand side is a nonempty subset of
S
; thus
\oplus_{S}
is
well-defined and nonempty-valued. Symmetry is built in by the
explicit requirement
\sigma_{1}\oplus_{S}\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{2}\oplus_{S}\sigma_{1}
, so

\oplus_{S}
is commutative.

From clause (1),
0
satisfies

0\oplus_{S}\sigma=\sigma\oplus_{S}0=\{\sigma\}\quad\text{for all }\sigma\in S,

so
0
is a neutral element.

An additive inverse of
\sigma\in S
is an element

\tau\in S
such that
0\in\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau
. From the table we read
off:

•

0\in 0\oplus_{S}0=\{0\}
, and there is no other
\tau
with

0\in 0\oplus_{S}\tau
, so
-0=0
.

•

0\in+\oplus_{S}-=+\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}-=\{+,0,-\}
, and there is
no other
\tau
with

, so
-(+)=-
.

•

Symmetrically,
-(-)=+
.

•

\Lambda\oplus_{S}\Lambda=\{0,+,-,\Lambda\}
contains
0
, and

\Lambda\oplus_{S}\sigma=\{\Lambda\}
for
\sigma\in\{0,+,-\}
does
not contain
0
, so
-\Lambda=\Lambda
.

Hence each element of
S
has a unique additive inverse, given by
the formulas in the statement.
∎

We now verify that
(S,\oplus_{S})
is a canonical commutative
hypergroup in the sense of Definition
2.12
.

Theorem 5.3

(Canonical hypergroup structure on
S
)
.

The pair
(S,\oplus_{S})
is a canonical commutative hypergroup. More
precisely:

(1)

(S,\oplus_{S})
is a commutative hypermagma.

(2)

0
is a neutral element, and each
\sigma\in S
has a
unique inverse
-\sigma
(Lemma
5.2
).

(3)

Associativity holds:

(\sigma_{1}\oplus_{S}\sigma_{2})\oplus_{S}\sigma_{3}\;=\;\sigma_{1}\oplus_{S}(\sigma_{2}\oplus_{S}\sigma_{3})\qquad\text{for all }\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}\in S.

(4)

Reversibility holds:

\rho\in\sigma_{1}\oplus_{S}\sigma_{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sigma_{2}\in\rho\oplus_{S}(-\sigma_{1})\qquad\text{for all }\rho,\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in S.

Proof.

Items (1) and (2) have already been established in
Lemma
5.2
.

Associativity (HG2).
Since
S
is finite, associativity
can be verified by direct case analysis; we outline the main cases and
omit the exhaustive table.

If all three signs lie in
\{0,+,-\}
, then the operation reduces to

\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}
on the sign hyperfield, which is known to be
associative (Proposition
2.19
). Thus all
triples with no
\Lambda
are associative.

Next, consider triples involving
\Lambda
.

\bullet

Exactly one
\Lambda
.
By symmetry it suffices to
consider
(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})=(\Lambda,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
with

\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\{0,+,-\}
. Using clauses (1) and (3), we find

\Lambda\oplus_{S}\tau=\{\Lambda\}\quad\text{for }\tau\in\{0,+,-\},

and
\tau_{1}\oplus_{S}\tau_{2}\subset\{0,+,-\}
with no
\Lambda
produced.
Therefore

(\Lambda\oplus_{S}\tau_{1})\oplus_{S}\tau_{2}=\{\Lambda\}\oplus_{S}\tau_{2}=\{\Lambda\},

and

\Lambda\oplus_{S}(\tau_{1}\oplus_{S}\tau_{2})=\Lambda\oplus_{S}B=\{\Lambda\}

for any
B\subset\{0,+,-\}
. Thus both bracketings yield

\{\Lambda\}
, and associativity holds.

\bullet

Two
\Lambda
’s.
Up to symmetry, it suffices to
consider triples of the form
(\Lambda,\Lambda,\tau)
with

\tau\in\{0,+,-,\Lambda\}
. If
\tau\in\{0,+,-\}
, then

\Lambda\oplus_{S}\Lambda=\{0,+,-,\Lambda\},

so

(\Lambda\oplus_{S}\Lambda)\oplus_{S}\tau=\{0,+,-,\Lambda\}\oplus_{S}\tau.

For each
\sigma\in\{0,+,-,\Lambda\}
,
\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau
is either a
singleton subset of
\{0,+,-,\Lambda\}
(if
\sigma\neq\Lambda
and

\tau\neq\Lambda
) or
\{\Lambda\}
if at least one input is

\Lambda
. It follows that

(\Lambda\oplus_{S}\Lambda)\oplus_{S}\tau=\{0,+,-,\Lambda\}.

On the other hand,

\Lambda\oplus_{S}(\Lambda\oplus_{S}\tau)=\begin{cases}\Lambda\oplus_{S}\{\Lambda\}=\Lambda\oplus_{S}\Lambda=\{0,+,-,\Lambda\},&\tau\in\{0,+,-\},\\[2.0pt]
\Lambda\oplus_{S}(\Lambda\oplus_{S}\Lambda)=\Lambda\oplus_{S}\{0,+,-,\Lambda\}=\{0,+,-,\Lambda\},&\tau=\Lambda,\end{cases}

since
\Lambda\oplus_{S}\tau=\{\Lambda\}
for
\tau\in\{0,+,-\}
and

\Lambda\oplus_{S}\Lambda=\{0,+,-,\Lambda\}
by (3) and (4). Thus
associativity holds whenever at least two entries are
\Lambda
.

In summary, for every triple in
S^{3}
both bracketings of
\oplus_{S}

coincide. This establishes associativity.

Reversibility (HG5).
We must show that for all

\rho,\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in S
,

\rho\in\sigma_{1}\oplus_{S}\sigma_{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sigma_{2}\in\rho\oplus_{S}(-\sigma_{1}).

Since
S
is finite, this can again be checked by case analysis.
We sketch the argument.

If
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\rho\in\{0,+,-\}
, then the statement reduces to
the reversibility axiom for the additive hypergroup of the sign
hyperfield
(S_{\mathrm{sign}},\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}})
, which holds
by Proposition
2.19
. Thus we may assume that at
least one of the three signs is
\Lambda
.

If
\sigma_{1}=\Lambda
, then
-\sigma_{1}=\Lambda
by
Lemma
5.2
. For any
\sigma_{2}\in S
,

\Lambda\oplus_{S}\sigma_{2}=\begin{cases}\{\Lambda\},&\sigma_{2}\in\{0,+,-\},\\[2.0pt]
\{0,+,-,\Lambda\},&\sigma_{2}=\Lambda.\end{cases}

A quick inspection shows that the membership

\rho\in\Lambda\oplus_{S}\sigma_{2}
is equivalent to

\sigma_{2}\in\rho\oplus_{S}\Lambda
, using the same table and the fact that

-\Lambda=\Lambda
. The cases where
\sigma_{2}=\Lambda
or

\rho=\Lambda
are similar.

Finally, if
\sigma_{1}\in\{+,-\}
and
\sigma_{2}=\Lambda
or

\rho=\Lambda
, one checks that the only way
\Lambda
appears in a
hyper–sum is via the clauses in Definition
5.1
,
and that in each such case reversing the roles of “summand” and
“inverse” yields the same membership relation. In other words, the
pattern of which signs occur in which sums is symmetric with respect
to replacing
\sigma_{1}
by
-\sigma_{1}
and swapping
\rho
with

\sigma_{2}
. We omit the routine enumeration of all triples.

Thus the reversibility condition holds for all

\rho,\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in S
, completing the proof that

(S,\oplus_{S})
is a canonical commutative hypergroup.
∎

The three–element sign hyperfield embeds naturally into

(S,\oplus_{S})
as a sub–hypergroup.

Proposition 5.4

(Classical sign hypergroup as a substructure)
.

Let
S_{\mathrm{sign}}=\{0,+,-\}\subset S
. Then the restriction
of
\oplus_{S}
to
S_{\mathrm{sign}}
coincides with

\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}
, and
(S_{\mathrm{sign}},\oplus_{S})
is a
canonical commutative hypergroup isomorphic to the additive hypergroup
of the sign hyperfield.

Proof.

By Definition
5.1
, for
x,y\in\{+,-\}
we have

x\oplus_{S}y=x\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}y
, and for
x\in S_{\mathrm{sign}}
,

0\oplus_{S}x=x\oplus_{S}0=\{x\}.

Thus the restriction of
\oplus_{S}
to
S_{\mathrm{sign}}\times S_{\mathrm{sign}}
is exactly
\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}}
. Since

(S_{\mathrm{sign}},\oplus_{\mathrm{sign}})
is a canonical
commutative hypergroup by
Proposition
2.19
, the same holds for

(S_{\mathrm{sign}},\oplus_{S})
, and the inclusion

S_{\mathrm{sign}}\hookrightarrow S
is a homomorphism of
canonical commutative hypergroups.
∎

Remark 5.5

(Role of
(S,\oplus_{S})
as sign envelope)
.

The hypergroup
(S,\oplus_{S})
will serve as a
sign envelope
for
the additive structure on
H
. For each pair
x,y\in H
we
will define the
sign image

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y):=\{\operatorname{sgn}(z):z\in x\boxplus y\}\subset S

and show that

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)\subseteq\operatorname{sgn}(x)\oplus_{S}\operatorname{sgn}(y).

Moreover, as one varies the magnitudes of
x
and
y
, every sign in

\operatorname{sgn}(x)\oplus_{S}\operatorname{sgn}(y)
is realized. Thus
(S,\oplus_{S})
captures the
full range of sign–level behaviour of the hyperaddition
\boxplus
on

H
, in a setting where associativity and reversibility hold
exactly. The precise envelope statement will be developed in
Section
5.1
.

5.2.
Sign images of hyper-sums

In this subsection we make precise the relationship between the sign
hypergroup
(S,\oplus_{S})
from
Section
5.1
and the hyperaddition
\boxplus
on

H
from Section
3.3
. The main point is
that the sign hypergroup does
not
arise by projecting individual
hyper-sums
x\boxplus y
to their signs; rather, it describes the

envelope
of all sign patterns that can occur as we vary the
magnitudes of summands with fixed signs.

Throughout we write
\operatorname{sgn}:H\to S
for the sign map from
Definition
3.1
.

Definition 5.6

(Sign image of a hyper-sum)
.

For
x,y\in H
we define the
sign image
of their hyper-sum as

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y):=\{\operatorname{sgn}(z):z\in x\boxplus y\}\subset S.

Since
x\boxplus y
is always a nonempty finite subset of
H
(see
Lemma
3.15
), the sign image

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)
is a nonempty finite subset of
S
.

Lemma 5.7

(Basic properties of sign images)
.

Let
x,y\in H
. Then:

(1)

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)
is nonempty and contained in

\{0,+,-,\Lambda\}
.

(2)

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=E_{\mathrm{sign}}(y,x)
.

(3)

If
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
(the classical real line), then

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\{\operatorname{sgn}(x\boxplus y)\}=\{\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}(r+s)\},

where
x=i(r)
,
y=i(s)
and
\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}
is the

2.3
).

Proof.

(1) follows directly from the definition and the fact that
x\boxplus y
is
nonempty and
\operatorname{sgn}:H\to S
is well-defined.

(2) Since
\boxplus
is commutative (Lemma
3.15
), we
have
x\boxplus y=y\boxplus x
, hence

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\{\operatorname{sgn}(z):z\in x\boxplus y\}=\{\operatorname{sgn}(z):z\in y\boxplus x\}=E_{\mathrm{sign}}(y,x).

(3) If
x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
, then by Proposition
3.20

x\boxplus y
is a singleton
\{i(r+s)\}
, where

x=i(r)
,

. Thus

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\{\operatorname{sgn}(i(r+s))\}=\{\operatorname{sgn}_{\mathbb{R}}(r+s)\}

by Lemma
3.4
.
∎

We now compare
E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)
with the sign hyperoperation

\oplus_{S}
on
S
. As noted in Remark

, it is

not
true in general that
E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)
equals

\operatorname{sgn}(x)\oplus_{S}\operatorname{sgn}(y)
for fixed magnitudes; instead we have the
following inclusion.

Theorem 5.8

(Sign-envelope property, pointwise inclusion)
.

For all
x,y\in H
one has

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)\subseteq\operatorname{sgn}(x)\oplus_{S}\operatorname{sgn}(y)\subset S.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in H
and write

\sigma:=\operatorname{sgn}(x),\qquad\tau:=\operatorname{sgn}(y)\in S.

We must show that every sign appearing in
x\boxplus y
lies in

\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau
as defined in
Definition
5.1
. We proceed case by case.

Case 1: One of
x,y
is
0
.
Suppose
x=0
. Then by
Definition
3.14
,

x\boxplus y=0\boxplus y=\{y\},

so
E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\{\operatorname{sgn}(y)\}=\{\tau\}
. On the other hand,

\sigma=0
and clause (1) of
Definition
5.1
gives

0\oplus_{S}\tau=\{\tau\}.

Thus
E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\{\tau\}\subseteq 0\oplus_{S}\tau
, as
required. The case
y=0
is symmetric.

Case 2: Nonzero
x,y
with signs in
\{+,-\}
.
Write

x=(\sigma,a)
,
y=(\tau,b)
with
a,b>0
and

\sigma,\tau\in\{+,-\}
.

If
\sigma=\tau
, then clause (2) of
Definition
3.14
gives

x\boxplus y=\{(\sigma,a+b)\},

so
E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\{\sigma\}
. On the other hand,

\sigma\oplus_{S}\sigma=\{\sigma\}
by clause (2) of
Definition
5.1
(classical part). Thus

\{\sigma\}\subseteq\sigma\oplus_{S}\sigma
.

If
\{\sigma,\tau\}=\{+,-\}
, say
\sigma=+
,
\tau=-
, then clause
(3) of Definition
3.14
yields

x\boxplus y=(+,a)\boxplus(-,b)=\begin{cases}\{(+,a-b)\},&a>b,\\[2.0pt]
\{0\},&a=b,\\[2.0pt]
\{(-,b-a)\},&a<b.\end{cases}

Hence
E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)
is one of
\{+\}
,
\{0\}
, or

\{-\}
, depending on the relative magnitudes of
a
and
b
. In all
cases we have

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)\subseteq\{+,0,-\}.

By Definition
5.1
,

+\oplus_{S}-=-\oplus_{S}+=\{+,0,-\},

so
E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)\subseteq\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau
.

Case 3: Interaction with
\Lambda
.
Suppose

x=(\sigma,a)
,
y=(\Lambda,b)
with
a,b>0
and

\sigma\in\{+,-\}
(or the same with
x
and
y
swapped). Then
clause (4) of Definition
3.14
gives

x\boxplus y=(\sigma,a)\boxplus(\Lambda,b)=\{(\Lambda,a+b)\},

so
E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\{\Lambda\}
. By
Definition
5.1
,

\sigma\oplus_{S}\Lambda=\Lambda\oplus_{S}\sigma=\{\Lambda\},

so again
E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)\subseteq\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau
.

If
x=(\Lambda,a)
,
y=(0)
or vice versa, then

x\boxplus y=\{(\Lambda,a)\}

and
E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\{\Lambda\}
, while

0\oplus_{S}\Lambda=\Lambda\oplus_{S}0=\{\Lambda\}
, so the inclusion holds.

Case 4:
\Lambda
–
\Lambda
sums.
Suppose

x=(\Lambda,a)
,
y=(\Lambda,b)
with
a,b>0
. Then clause (5) of
Definition
3.14
yields

x\boxplus y=\begin{cases}\{(\Lambda,a+b),\ (+,a-b)\},&a>b,\\[2.0pt]
\{(\Lambda,a+b),\ (-,b-a)\},&a<b,\\[2.0pt]
\{(\Lambda,2a),\ 0\},&a=b.\end{cases}

Hence the sign image is one of

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\{\Lambda,+\},&a>b,\\[2.0pt]
\{\Lambda,-\},&a<b,\\[2.0pt]
\{\Lambda,0\},&a=b.\end{cases}

In each subcase we have

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)\subseteq\{0,+,-,\Lambda\}.

But by clause (4) of Definition
5.1
,

\Lambda\oplus_{S}\Lambda=\{0,+,-,\Lambda\},

so again
E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)\subseteq\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau
with

\sigma=\tau=\Lambda
.

This exhausts all possibilities (up to symmetry), so the inclusion

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)\subseteq\operatorname{sgn}(x)\oplus_{S}\operatorname{sgn}(y)
holds for all

x,y\in H
.
∎

The preceding theorem shows that
(S,\oplus_{S})
is a genuine
“sign-envelope” for the hyperaddition on
H
: for fixed
x,y
,
the signs that actually appear in
x\boxplus y
form a subset of the sign
hyper-sum
\operatorname{sgn}(x)\oplus_{S}\operatorname{sgn}(y)
. We now show that this envelope is
sharp when we allow the magnitudes to vary.

Definition 5.9

(Reachable sign set for a sign pair)
.

For
\sigma,\tau\in S
we define the
reachable sign set

R(\sigma,\tau):=\{\rho\in S:\exists\,x,y,z\in H\ \text{with}\ \operatorname{sgn}(x)=\sigma,\ \operatorname{sgn}(y)=\tau,\ z\in x\boxplus y,\ \operatorname{sgn}(z)=\rho\}.

Thus
R(\sigma,\tau)
consists of all signs that can occur as signs of
hyper-sums of elements whose signs are exactly
\sigma
and
\tau
.

Theorem 5.10

(Realization of all sign-level hyper-sums)
.

For all
\sigma,\tau\in S
one has

R(\sigma,\tau)=\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau.

In particular, the sign hypergroup
(S,\oplus_{S})
is the minimal
canonical commutative hypergroup that encodes all sign patterns arising
from hyper-sums in
(H,\boxplus)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.8
, for any
x,y\in H

we have

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)\subseteq\operatorname{sgn}(x)\oplus_{S}\operatorname{sgn}(y).

Taking the union over all
x,y
with
\operatorname{sgn}(x)=\sigma
and

\operatorname{sgn}(y)=\tau
, we obtain

R(\sigma,\tau)\subseteq\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau.

Thus it remains to show the reverse inclusion:
for each
\rho\in\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau
we must produce
x,y
with signs

\sigma,\tau
such that
\rho\in E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)
.

We proceed by case analysis on
(\sigma,\tau)
using the explicit
hyperaddition rules.

Case 1:
(\sigma,\tau)=(0,\rho_{0})
or
(\rho_{0},0)
.
By
Definition
5.1
,

0\oplus_{S}\rho_{0}=\{\rho_{0}\}\quad\text{for all }\rho_{0}\in S.

To realize
\rho_{0}
, take
x=0
and any
y\in H
with

\operatorname{sgn}(y)=\rho_{0}
. Then
x\boxplus y=0\boxplus y=\{y\}
and

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\{\rho_{0}\}
.

Case 2:
(\sigma,\tau)\in\{+,-\}^{2}
.
If

\sigma=\tau=+
, then
\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau=\{+\}
; we can take

x=(+,1)
,
y=(+,1)
, giving

x\boxplus y=(+,1)\boxplus(+,1)=\{(+,2)\},

so
E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\{+\}
.

If
\sigma=\tau=-
, then
\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau=\{-\}
; taking

x=(-,1)
,
y=(-,1)
yields

x\boxplus y=(-,1)\boxplus(-,1)=\{(-,2)\},

and
E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\{-\}
.

If
\{\sigma,\tau\}=\{+,-\}
, say
\sigma=+
,
\tau=-
, then

\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau=+\oplus_{S}-=\{+,0,-\}.

We realize each of these three signs by appropriate choices of
magnitudes:

\bullet
For
\rho=+
, take
x=(+,2)
,
y=(-,1)
. Then

x\boxplus y=(+,2)\boxplus(-,1)=\{(+,1)\},

so
E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\{+\}
.

\bullet
For
\rho=0
, take
x=(+,1)
,
y=(-,1)
. Then

x\boxplus y=(+,1)\boxplus(-,1)=\{0\},

so
E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\{0\}
.

\bullet
For
\rho=-
, take
x=(+,1)
,
y=(-,2)
. Then

x\boxplus y=(+,1)\boxplus(-,2)=\{(-,1)\},

so
E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\{-\}
.

Thus every
\rho\in+\oplus_{S}-
is realized, and by symmetry the same holds
for
-\oplus_{S}+
.

Case 3:
(\sigma,\tau)=(\pm,\Lambda)
or
(\Lambda,\pm)
.

By Definition
5.1
,

\sigma\oplus_{S}\Lambda=\Lambda\oplus_{S}\sigma=\{\Lambda\}\quad\text{for }\sigma\in\{+,-\}.

To realize
\rho=\Lambda
, take
x=(\sigma,1)
and
y=(\Lambda,1)
.
Then clause (4) of Definition
3.14
gives

x\boxplus y=(\sigma,1)\boxplus(\Lambda,1)=\{(\Lambda,2)\},

so
E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\{\Lambda\}
.

The case
(\sigma,\tau)=(\Lambda,0)
or
(0,\Lambda)
was already
covered in Case 1.

Case 4:
(\sigma,\tau)=(\Lambda,\Lambda)
.
By
Definition
5.1
,

\Lambda\oplus_{S}\Lambda=\{0,+,-,\Lambda\}.

We must realize each of the four signs as the sign of some

(\Lambda,a)\boxplus(\Lambda,b)
.

\bullet
Take
a=2
,
b=1
. Then clause (5) of
Definition
3.14
gives

(\Lambda,2)\boxplus(\Lambda,1)=\{(\Lambda,3),\,(+,1),\,(-,1)\},

so
\Lambda,+,-\in E_{\mathrm{sign}}\bigl((\Lambda,2),(\Lambda,1)\bigr)
.

\bullet
For
\rho=0
, take
a=b=1
. Then

(\Lambda,1)\boxplus(\Lambda,1)=\{(\Lambda,2),0\},

so
0\in E_{\mathrm{sign}}\bigl((\Lambda,1),(\Lambda,1)\bigr)
.

Thus every sign in
\Lambda\oplus_{S}\Lambda
is realized.

Combining the four cases, we have shown that for each

\sigma,\tau\in S
and each
\rho\in\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau
there exist

x,y\in H
with
\operatorname{sgn}(x)=\sigma
,
\operatorname{sgn}(y)=\tau
and

\rho\in E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)
. This proves

\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau\subseteq R(\sigma,\tau)
and completes the proof of

R(\sigma,\tau)=\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau
.
∎

Corollary 5.11

(Sign envelope equality)
.

For each fixed
\sigma,\tau\in S
, the set of signs that can appear
in hyper-sums of elements with signs
\sigma
and
\tau
is exactly

\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau
. Equivalently,

\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in H\\
\operatorname{sgn}(x)=\sigma,\ \operatorname{sgn}(y)=\tau\end{subarray}}E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau.

Proof.

The left-hand side is precisely
R(\sigma,\tau)
by
Definition
5.9
, and the right-hand side is

\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau
. The equality follows from
Theorem
5.10
.
∎

Remark 5.12

(Summary of the sign-level picture)
.

The results of this subsection can be summarized as follows:

•

For each fixed pair
x,y\in H
, the sign image

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)
is a
subset
of the sign
hyper-sum
\operatorname{sgn}(x)\oplus_{S}\operatorname{sgn}(y)

(Theorem
5.8
).

•

As one varies magnitudes while keeping the signs fixed, every
sign in
\operatorname{sgn}(x)\oplus_{S}\operatorname{sgn}(y)
occurs as the sign of some
hyper-sum (Theorem
5.10
and
Corollary
5.11
).

•

Thus the canonical hypergroup
(S,\oplus_{S})
is precisely the
sign-level envelope of
(H,\boxplus)
: it captures all possible
sign patterns of hyper-sums in a setting where associativity
and reversibility hold exactly, even though they fail for the
full hypernumber system.

This justifies viewing
(S,\oplus_{S})
as the “sign shadow” of

(H,\boxplus)
, abstracting away magnitudes while retaining the full
combinatorics of sign interaction.

5.3.
Envelope property

The purpose of this subsection is to package the results of
Sections
5.1
and
5.2

into a conceptual “envelope” statement: the sign hypergroup

(S,\oplus_{S})
is the canonical sign–level shadow of the additive
hypermagma
(H,\boxplus)
.

We first introduce a general notion of a
lax homomorphism
of
hypermagmas, and then specialize to the sign projection

\operatorname{sgn}:H\to S
.

Definition 5.13

(Lax hypermagma morphism)
.

Let
(M,\odot)
and
(N,\boxplus)
be commutative hypermagmas. A map

f:M\to N
is called a
lax homomorphism of hypermagmas
if for
all
x,y\in M
,

f(x\odot y)\;\subseteq\;f(x)\boxplus f(y),

where

f(x\odot y):=\{f(z):z\in x\odot y\}.

In other words,
f
sends each hyper-sum
x\odot y
into the
hyper-sum of their images, but not necessarily onto it. Equality
would give an honest homomorphism; in our setting we only have an
inclusion.

Definition 5.14

(Sign envelope for a hypermagma)
.

Let
(H,\boxplus)
be a commutative hypermagma. A triple

(S,\oplus_{S},\pi)
, where
(S,\oplus_{S})
is a canonical commutative
hypergroup and
\pi:H\to S
is a map, is called a

sign envelope
for
(H,\boxplus)
if:

(1)

Lax morphism condition.

\pi
is a lax hypermagma morphism from
(H,\boxplus)
to

(S,\oplus_{S})
, i.e.

\pi(x\boxplus y)\subseteq\pi(x)\oplus_{S}\pi(y)\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in H.

(2)

Realization of all sign-level sums.

For every
\sigma,\tau\in S
and every
\rho\in\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau
,
there exist
x,y,z\in H
with

\pi(x)=\sigma,\quad\pi(y)=\tau,\quad z\in x\boxplus y,\quad\pi(z)=\rho.

In this situation we also say that
(S,\oplus_{S})
is the

sign envelope
of
(H,\boxplus)
with respect to
\pi
.

Specializing to our setting, we take
(H,\boxplus)=(H,\boxplus)
,

(S,\oplus_{S})=(S,\oplus_{S})
and
\pi=\operatorname{sgn}
.

Recall from Definition
5.6
that for
x,y\in H
we
defined the
sign image
of their hyper-sum by

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y):=\{\operatorname{sgn}(z):z\in x\boxplus y\}\subset S,

and from Definition
5.9
that for

\sigma,\tau\in S
we set

R(\sigma,\tau):=\{\rho\in S:\exists\,x,y,z\in H\text{ with }\operatorname{sgn}(x)=\sigma,\ \operatorname{sgn}(y)=\tau,\ z\in x\boxplus y,\ \operatorname{sgn}(z)=\rho\}.

The key sign-level results from Section
5.2
can
now be rephrased in the envelope language.

Lemma 5.15

(Lax morphism property of
\operatorname{sgn}
)
.

The sign map
\operatorname{sgn}:H\to S
is a lax hypermagma morphism from

(H,\boxplus)
to
(S,\oplus_{S})
. Equivalently, for all

x,y\in H
,

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)\subseteq\operatorname{sgn}(x)\oplus_{S}\operatorname{sgn}(y).

Proof.

This is exactly Theorem
5.8
, rewritten in
the language of Definition
5.13
. By definition,

\operatorname{sgn}(x\boxplus y)=E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y),

and Theorem
5.8
asserts that

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)\subseteq\operatorname{sgn}(x)\oplus_{S}\operatorname{sgn}(y),

which is precisely the lax morphism condition.
∎

Lemma 5.16

(Realization of all sign-level sums)
.

For all
\sigma,\tau\in S
one has

R(\sigma,\tau)=\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau.

In particular, every
\rho\in\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau
occurs as the sign of
some hyper-sum
x\boxplus y
with
\operatorname{sgn}(x)=\sigma
and
\operatorname{sgn}(y)=\tau
.

Proof.

This is precisely Theorem
5.10
together with
Corollary
5.11
. By definition,

R(\sigma,\tau)
is the union of all sign images

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)
with
\operatorname{sgn}(x)=\sigma
,
\operatorname{sgn}(y)=\tau
, and
Theorem
5.10
shows that

R(\sigma,\tau)=\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau
.
∎

We can now state the envelope property for our three–sign hypernumber
system.

Theorem 5.17

(Sign envelope property for
(H,\boxplus)
)
.

Let
(H,\boxplus)
be the three–sign hypernumber system and

(S,\oplus_{S})
the sign hypergroup from
Section
5.1
, with sign map

\operatorname{sgn}:H\to S
. Then
(S,\oplus_{S},\operatorname{sgn})
is a sign envelope for

(H,\boxplus)
in the sense of Definition
5.14
.
More explicitly:

(1)

For all
x,y\in H
, the sign image of their hyper-sum is
contained in the sign hyper-sum of their individual signs:

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)=\operatorname{sgn}(x\boxplus y)\subseteq\operatorname{sgn}(x)\oplus_{S}\operatorname{sgn}(y).

(2)

For all
\sigma,\tau\in S
and all

\rho\in\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau
, there exist
x,y,z\in H
with

\operatorname{sgn}(x)=\sigma,\quad\operatorname{sgn}(y)=\tau,\quad z\in x\boxplus y,\quad\operatorname{sgn}(z)=\rho.

Proof.

Item (1) is Lemma
5.15
, and item (2) is
Lemma
5.16
. These are exactly the two
conditions of Definition
5.14
specialized
to
(H,\boxplus)
,
(S,\oplus_{S})
, and
\operatorname{sgn}
.
∎

It is often convenient to view the envelope property in terms of the
reachable sign sets
R(\sigma,\tau)
.

Proposition 5.18

(Envelope equality for reachable sign sets)
.

For all
\sigma,\tau\in S
,

R(\sigma,\tau)=\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau,

and for all
x,y\in H
,

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)\subseteq R(\operatorname{sgn}(x),\operatorname{sgn}(y)).

Consequently, the map

(\sigma,\tau)\longmapsto R(\sigma,\tau)

is exactly the sign hyperoperation
\oplus_{S}
on
S
.

Proof.

The equality
R(\sigma,\tau)=\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau
is again
Lemma
5.16
. By definition,

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)
is one of the sets appearing in the union
defining
R(\operatorname{sgn}(x),\operatorname{sgn}(y))
, so

E_{\mathrm{sign}}(x,y)\subseteq R(\operatorname{sgn}(x),\operatorname{sgn}(y))
. The last
statement follows by identifying
R(\sigma,\tau)
with

\sigma\oplus_{S}\tau
.
∎

Remark 5.19

(Conceptual summary of the envelope property)
.

Theorem
5.17
and
Proposition
5.18
can be summarized informally as:

The sign hypergroup
(S,\oplus_{S})
is the smallest canonical
commutative hypergroup that records all sign patterns of hyper-sums
in
(H,\boxplus)
, in a way that is compatible with the hyperaddition.

More precisely:

•

The sign map
\operatorname{sgn}:H\to S
is a lax morphism of
commutative hypermagmas, so the hyperaddition on
H

always projects into the hyperaddition on
S
.

•

The hyperoperation
\oplus_{S}
on
S
is
sharp
: every
sign that is allowed at the sign level for a pair

(\sigma,\tau)
actually occurs as the sign of some hyper-sum
of elements with signs
\sigma
and
\tau
.

•

The nonassociativity of
\boxplus
is therefore entirely a matter
of how magnitudes interact; at the sign level, the envelope

(S,\oplus_{S})
is a fully associative canonical hypergroup
(Theorem
5.3
).

This perspective will be used in later sections to organize the
interaction between
(H,\boxplus,\cdot)
and various ambient
constructions (such as the cancellation monoid) via compatible
projections to sign and magnitude layers.

6.
Ambient cancellation structure

6.1.
Cancellation mass and the ambient monoid

In this section we isolate the real–valued
cancellation mass

that already appeared in our analysis of associativity defects, and use
it to build a simple commutative monoid
(K,\oplus)
which serves
as an ambient “cancellation recorder” for the real field. This
structure will later be related to the hyperaddition on
H
by a
suitable interpretation map.

We begin by recalling the basic notion of cancellation mass.

Definition 6.1

(Cancellation mass)
.

Let
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. The
cancellation mass
of the pair

(r_{1},r_{2})
is the nonnegative real number

C(r_{1},r_{2}):=|r_{1}|+|r_{2}|-|r_{1}+r_{2}|.

Intuitively,
C(r_{1},r_{2})
measures the amount of absolute value
lost when adding
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}
; it vanishes exactly when
there is no cancellation between the two numbers.

Lemma 6.2

(Basic properties of
C
)
.

For all
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
the cancellation mass
C
satisfies:

(1)

Nonnegativity:

C(r_{1},r_{2})\geq 0.

(2)

Symmetry:

C(r_{1},r_{2})=C(r_{2},r_{1}).

(3)

Homogeneity:
For all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

C(\lambda r_{1},\lambda r_{2})=|\lambda|\,C(r_{1},r_{2}).

(4)

Vanishing and sign pattern.

One has
C(r_{1},r_{2})=0
if and only if either

r_{1}=0
or
r_{2}=0
, or
r_{1},r_{2}
have the same sign.

(5)

Opposite–sign case.

If
a,b\geq 0
, then

C(a,-b)=a+b-|a-b|=2\min(a,b).

Proof.

(1) By the triangle inequality,

|r_{1}+r_{2}|\leq|r_{1}|+|r_{2}|,

so
|r_{1}|+|r_{2}|-|r_{1}+r_{2}|\geq 0
.

(2) Symmetry is immediate from the formula, since
|r_{1}|+|r_{2}|
and

|r_{1}+r_{2}|
are symmetric in
(r_{1},r_{2})
.

(3) For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

C(\lambda r_{1},\lambda r_{2})=|\lambda r_{1}|+|\lambda r_{2}|-|\lambda r_{1}+\lambda r_{2}|=|\lambda|\bigl(|r_{1}|+|r_{2}|-|r_{1}+r_{2}|\bigr)=|\lambda|\,C(r_{1},r_{2}).

(4) If
r_{1}=0
or
r_{2}=0
, then

C(r_{1},r_{2})=|r_{1}|+|r_{2}|-|r_{1}+r_{2}|=0
. If
r_{1},r_{2}
have the same
sign, then
|r_{1}+r_{2}|=|r_{1}|+|r_{2}|
, so again
C(r_{1},r_{2})=0
.
Conversely, if
C(r_{1},r_{2})=0
and neither
r_{1}
nor
r_{2}
is
0
,
then
|r_{1}+r_{2}|=|r_{1}|+|r_{2}|
. By the equality case in the triangle
inequality, this implies that
r_{1},r_{2}
have the same sign.

(5) Let
a,b\geq 0
. Then

C(a,-b)=|a|+|-b|-|a-b|=a+b-|a-b|.

If
a\geq b
, then
|a-b|=a-b
and

a+b-|a-b|=a+b-(a-b)=2b=2\min(a,b).

If
a<b
, then
|a-b|=b-a
and

a+b-|a-b|=a+b-(b-a)=2a=2\min(a,b).

Thus
C(a,-b)=2\min(a,b)
.
∎

Remark 6.3

(Cancellation mass and associativity defect)
.

Lemma
6.2
shows that
C
exactly recovers the
associativity defect for ordered
(+,-,\Lambda)
–triples analysed in
Theorem
4.18
: in that setting,

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=C(a,-b)=2\min(a,b).

We will systematically encode this quantity as a second coordinate in
an ambient monoid.

We now introduce the ambient object that records both the ordinary
real sum and the total cancellation mass.

Definition 6.4

(Ambient cancellation monoid)
.

Let

K:=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}.

We define a binary operation

\oplus:K\times K\longrightarrow K

by

(x,c)\oplus(y,d):=\bigl(x+y,\;c+d+C(x,y)\bigr),

and set

\mathbf{0}:=(0,0)\in K.

The heuristic is that the first coordinate records the usual real sum,
while the second coordinate accumulates all cancellation mass generated
by additions performed so far.

Lemma 6.5

(Basic properties of
\oplus
)
.

The operation
\oplus
is well-defined and commutative, and

\mathbf{0}=(0,0)
is a neutral element:

\mathbf{0}\oplus(x,c)=(x,c)\oplus\mathbf{0}=(x,c)\quad\text{for all }(x,c)\in K.

Proof.

For
(x,c),(y,d)\in K
, the pair
(x+y,c+d+C(x,y))
clearly
lies in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, since
c,d\geq 0
and
C(x,y)\geq 0
by
Lemma
6.2
(1). Thus
\oplus
is well-defined.

Commutativity follows from commutativity of
+
in
\mathbb{R}
and symmetry
of
C
:

(x,c)\oplus(y,d)=(x+y,c+d+C(x,y))=(y+x,d+c+C(y,x))=(y,d)\oplus(x,c).

For the neutral element, note that

C(0,x)=|0|+|x|-|0+x|=|x|-|x|=0,

so

\mathbf{0}\oplus(x,c)=(0,c)\oplus(x,c)=(x,c+0+C(0,x))=(x,c),

and similarly
(x,c)\oplus\mathbf{0}=(x,c)
.
∎

The key property is associativity, which is encoded in a simple
“cocycle” identity satisfied by
C
.

Lemma 6.6

(Cocycle identity for
C
)
.

For all
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}
one has

C(x,y)+C(x+y,z)=C(y,z)+C(x,y+z).

Proof.

Expanding both sides using Definition
6.1
,

\displaystyle C(x,y)+C(x+y,z)

\displaystyle=\bigl(|x|+|y|-|x+y|\bigr)+\bigl(|x+y|+|z|-|x+y+z|\bigr)

\displaystyle=|x|+|y|+|z|-|x+y+z|.

Similarly,

\displaystyle C(y,z)+C(x,y+z)

\displaystyle=\bigl(|y|+|z|-|y+z|\bigr)+\bigl(|x|+|y+z|-|x+y+z|\bigr)

\displaystyle=|x|+|y|+|z|-|x+y+z|.

The two expressions coincide, proving the identity.
∎

Proposition 6.7

(Associativity of
(K,\oplus)
)
.

The pair
(K,\oplus)
is a commutative monoid with neutral element

\mathbf{0}=(0,0)
, i.e.

(u\oplus v)\oplus w=u\oplus(v\oplus w)\quad\text{for all }u,v,w\in K.

Proof.

Commutativity and the identity property of
\mathbf{0}
follow from
Lemma
6.5
. It remains to show associativity.

Let
u=(x,c)
,
v=(y,d)
,
w=(z,e)\in K
. Then

\displaystyle(u\oplus v)\oplus w

\displaystyle=(x+y,\,c+d+C(x,y))\oplus(z,e)

\displaystyle=\bigl(x+y+z,\,c+d+C(x,y)+e+C(x+y,z)\bigr),

while

\displaystyle u\oplus(v\oplus w)

\displaystyle=(x,c)\oplus(y+z,\,d+e+C(y,z))

\displaystyle=\bigl(x+y+z,\,c+d+e+C(y,z)+C(x,y+z)\bigr).

By Lemma
6.6
,

C(x,y)+C(x+y,z)=C(y,z)+C(x,y+z),

so the second coordinates agree. Thus

(u\oplus v)\oplus w=u\oplus(v\oplus w)
for all
u,v,w
, proving that

(K,\oplus,\mathbf{0})
is an associative commutative monoid.
∎

The projection to the first coordinate recovers the usual additive
structure of
\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 6.8

(Projection to the real line)
.

Define
\pi:K\to\mathbb{R}
by
\pi(x,c):=x
. Then:

(1)

\pi
is a surjective monoid homomorphism from

(K,\oplus)
onto
(\mathbb{R},+)
:

\pi\bigl((x,c)\oplus(y,d)\bigr)=\pi(x,c)+\pi(y,d)\quad\text{and}\quad\pi(\mathbf{0})=0.

(2)

The kernel of
\pi
is

\ker\pi=\{(0,c):c\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\}.

Proof.

(1) For
(x,c),(y,d)\in K
,

\pi\bigl((x,c)\oplus(y,d)\bigr)=\pi\bigl(x+y,\,c+d+C(x,y)\bigr)=x+y=\pi(x,c)+\pi(y,d),

and
\pi(\mathbf{0})=\pi(0,0)=0
. Surjectivity is obvious, since for
any
r\in\mathbb{R}
we have
\pi(r,0)=r
.

(2) If
(x,c)\in\ker\pi
, then
\pi(x,c)=x=0
, so

(x,c)=(0,c)
for some
c\geq 0
. Conversely, every
(0,c)
satisfies

\pi(0,c)=0
, so
(0,c)\in\ker\pi
.
∎

Remark 6.9

(Interpretation of the ambient monoid)
.

Informally, one can think of an element
(x,c)\in K
as describing a

state
obtained by summing some real numbers:

(x,c)=(r_{1},0)\oplus(r_{2},0)\oplus\cdots\oplus(r_{n},0),

in which:

•

x
is the resulting real sum
r_{1}+\cdots+r_{n}
, and

•

c
is the total cancellation mass accumulated along the way,
i.e. the difference between the sum of absolute values and
the absolute value of the final result, aggregated in an
associative manner via the cocycle identity
(Lemma
6.6
).

Because
(K,\oplus)
is a genuine commutative monoid, the final
pair
(x,c)
is independent of the order and bracketing of the
summands. The projection
\pi
forgets
c
and recovers the ordinary
real sum; the second coordinate
c
may be viewed as bookkeeping
information encoding “how much cancellation happened”. In
Section
6.2
we will construct a map from

H
into
K
that sends associativity defects of the
three–sign hyperaddition to differences in the second coordinate of

K
.

6.2.
Embedding of
H
and interpretation of the defect

In this subsection we relate the three–sign hypernumber system

(H,\boxplus)
to the ambient cancellation monoid

(K,\oplus)
constructed in
Section
6.1
. The guiding idea is that each
hypernumber has a
real shadow
and a
cancellation reserve
;
the ambient monoid records both, in a way that is fully associative,
while the hyperaddition
\boxplus
only partially converts cancellation
into
\Lambda
–mass. For ordered
(+,-,\Lambda)
–triples, this
mechanism explains the associativity defect computed in
Section
4.3
.

An ambient embedding of
H

We first define a simple embedding of
H
into
K
.

Definition 6.10

(Real shadow and ambient embedding)
.

Define the
real shadow
map

\rho:H\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

by

\rho(0):=0,\quad\rho(+,a):=a,\quad\rho(-,a):=-a,\quad\rho(\Lambda,a):=0

for all
a>0
. The
ambient embedding

\iota:H\longrightarrow K=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

is then defined by

\iota(x):=\bigl(\rho(x),\,c(x)\bigr),

where

c(0):=0,\quad c(+,a):=0,\quad c(-,a):=0,\quad c(\Lambda,a):=a.

Equivalently,

\iota(0)=(0,0),\quad\iota(+,a)=(a,0),\quad\iota(-,a)=(-a,0),\quad\iota(\Lambda,a)=(0,a).

Thus each hypernumber is encoded as a pair consisting of a net real
value and a nonnegative “stored cancellation mass” carried by the

\Lambda
–sector.

Lemma 6.11

(Basic properties of
\iota
)
.

The map
\iota:H\to K
has the following properties:

(1)

\iota
is injective.

(2)

\rho=\pi\circ\iota
, where
\pi:K\to\mathbb{R}
is the
projection
\pi(x,c)=x
.

(3)

The restriction of
\iota
to the classical line

\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}=i(\mathbb{R})\subset H
is given by

\iota(i(r))=(r,0)\quad\text{for all }r\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

(1) If
\iota(x)=\iota(y)
, then
\rho(x)=\rho(y)
and
c(x)=c(y)
.
Inspecting the four cases in Definition
6.10

shows that these two equalities force
x=y
; for instance, the only
element with second coordinate
>0
is of the form
(\Lambda,a)
, and
within each sign sector the real shadow uniquely determines the
magnitude. Hence
\iota
is injective.

(2) This is immediate from the definition: the first coordinate of

\iota(x)
is exactly
\rho(x)
.

(3) If
r>0
, then
i(r)=(+,r)
and
\iota(i(r))=(r,0)
. If

r<0
, then
i(r)=(-,|r|)
and

\iota(i(r))=(-|r|,0)=(r,0)
. Finally,
i(0)=0
and

\iota(0)=(0,0)
. Thus
\iota(i(r))=(r,0)
for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

Remark 6.12

(Ambient viewpoint on
H
)
.

Under the embedding
\iota
, the classical line
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
appears as the
subset
\{(r,0):r\in\mathbb{R}\}\subset K
, i.e. states with no stored
cancellation. Pure
\Lambda
–elements
(\Lambda,a)
are embedded as

(0,a)
, which can be interpreted as “zero net real value with
a

units of stored cancellation mass”. The ambient monoid operation

\oplus
then allows us to add these states in a fully associative
way, aggregating both real shadows and cancellation masses.

Ambient addition for
(+,a),(-,b),(\Lambda,c)

We now compute the ambient sum of the embedded triple

x=(+,a),\quad y=(-,b),\quad z=(\Lambda,c)\in H,\quad a,b,c>0,

and compare it with the two bracketings of the hyperaddition
\boxplus
.
Recall from Theorem
4.18
that

\displaystyle(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z

\displaystyle=\{(\Lambda,m_{L})\},

\displaystyle m_{L}

\displaystyle=c+|a-b|,

\displaystyle x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)

\displaystyle=\{(\Lambda,m_{R})\},

\displaystyle m_{R}

\displaystyle=a+b+c,

and that the associativity defect

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c):=m_{R}-m_{L}=a+b-|a-b|=2\min(a,b)=C(a,-b)

is independent of
c
.

Lemma 6.13

(Ambient sum of the embedded triple)
.

Let
x=(+,a)
,
y=(-,b)
,
z=(\Lambda,c)
with
a,b,c>0
, and define

X:=\iota(x),\quad Y:=\iota(y),\quad Z:=\iota(z)\in K.

Then

X=(a,0),\quad Y=(-b,0),\quad Z=(0,c),

and for any bracketing of
X\oplus Y\oplus Z
one has

X\oplus Y\oplus Z=(a-b,\;c+C(a,-b)).

Proof.

The expressions for
X,Y,Z
follow from
Definition
6.10
. Since
\oplus
is
associative and commutative (Proposition
6.7
), the
value of
X\oplus Y\oplus Z
is independent of bracketing, but we
compute it explicitly for definiteness:

\displaystyle X\oplus Y

\displaystyle=(a,0)\oplus(-b,0)

\displaystyle=\bigl(a-b,\;0+0+C(a,-b)\bigr)

\displaystyle=(a-b,C(a,-b)).

Then

\displaystyle(X\oplus Y)\oplus Z

\displaystyle=(a-b,C(a,-b))\oplus(0,c)

\displaystyle=\bigl(a-b+0,\;C(a,-b)+c+C(a-b,0)\bigr).

Since
C(r,0)=|r|+0-|r|=0
for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
, we obtain

(X\oplus Y)\oplus Z=(a-b,\;c+C(a,-b)).

By associativity of
\oplus
, this equals
X\oplus(Y\oplus Z)
and any
other bracketing.
∎

Thus the ambient monoid records the triple by a single state: the net
real shadow
a-b
together with a total cancellation mass

c+C(a,-b)
.

Recovering
\Lambda
–mass from the ambient state

To compare with the hyperaddition in
H
, we need a way of
extracting a
\Lambda
–magnitude from an ambient state.

Definition 6.14

(Canonical
\Lambda
–projection)
.

Define a map

P_{\Lambda}:K\longrightarrow H_{\Lambda}

by

P_{\Lambda}(x,c):=\begin{cases}0,&x=0,\ c=0,\\[2.0pt]
(\Lambda,\,|x|+c),&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
H_{\Lambda}:=\{(\Lambda,a):a>0\}\cup\{0\}\subset H
is the

\Lambda
–sector together with
0
.

Intuitively,
P_{\Lambda}
forgets the sign of the real shadow, takes its
absolute value, adds the stored cancellation mass, and realizes the
result as a
\Lambda
–magnitude. The exceptional case
(0,0)\mapsto 0

is just a harmless convention.

Lemma 6.15

(Basic properties of
P_{\Lambda}
)
.

For all
(x,c)\in K
:

(1)

P_{\Lambda}(x,c)\in H_{\Lambda}
.

(2)

If
(x,c)\neq(0,0)
, then
P_{\Lambda}(x,c)
has sign
\Lambda

and magnitude
|x|+c
.

(3)

P_{\Lambda}
is homogeneous for nonnegative scalars, i.e.,
for
\lambda\geq 0
,

P_{\Lambda}\bigl(\lambda x,\lambda c\bigr)=\begin{cases}0,&\lambda=0,\ (x,c)=(0,0),\\[2.0pt]
(\Lambda,\lambda(|x|+c)),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

(1) and (2) are immediate from Definition
6.14
.
For (3), if
\lambda=0
, then
(\lambda x,\lambda c)=(0,0)
and

P_{\Lambda}(0,0)=0
. If
\lambda>0
and
(x,c)\neq(0,0)
, then

P_{\Lambda}\bigl(\lambda x,\lambda c\bigr)=(\Lambda,|\lambda x|+\lambda c)=(\Lambda,\lambda(|x|+c)).

∎

We can now express the two hyper-sums
(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z
and

x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)
for the triple
(+,a),(-,b),(\Lambda,c)
in terms of

P_{\Lambda}
and the ambient sum
X\oplus Y\oplus Z
.

Proposition 6.16

(Ambient interpretation of the two bracketings)
.

Let
x=(+,a)
,
y=(-,b)
,
z=(\Lambda,c)
with
a,b,c>0
, and let

X,Y,Z\in K
be as in Lemma
6.13
. Set

U:=X\oplus Y\oplus Z=(a-b,\;c+C(a,-b)).

Then:

(1)

The right–associated hyper-sum corresponds to the full

\Lambda
–projection of
U
:

x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)=\{P_{\Lambda}(U)\}=\{(\Lambda,\,|a-b|+c+C(a,-b))\}=\{(\Lambda,a+b+c)\}.

(2)

The left–associated hyper-sum corresponds to the partial

\Lambda
–projection that only uses the original
\Lambda
–mass

c
:

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=\{(\Lambda,\,|a-b|+c)\}=\{P_{\Lambda}(a-b,c)\}.

In particular, the associativity defect

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=(|a-b|+c+C(a,-b))-(|a-b|+c)=C(a,-b)

is precisely the additional cancellation mass contributed by the
ambient state
U
beyond the original
\Lambda
–reserve
c
.

Proof.

(1) By Lemma
6.13
,

U=(a-b,\;c+C(a,-b)).

Since
a,b>0
and
(a,b)\neq(0,0)
, we have
U\neq(0,0)
, so

P_{\Lambda}(U)=(\Lambda,\,|a-b|+c+C(a,-b)).

But by Theorem
4.18
,

x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)=\{(\Lambda,m_{R})\}\quad\text{with }m_{R}=a+b+c.

Using Lemma
6.2
(5),

|a-b|+C(a,-b)=|a-b|+(a+b-|a-b|)=a+b,

so

|a-b|+c+C(a,-b)=a+b+c=m_{R}.

Thus
x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)=\{P_{\Lambda}(U)\}
.

(2) The left–associated sum is given by

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=\{(\Lambda,m_{L})\},\qquad m_{L}=c+|a-b|

by Theorem
4.18
. On the other hand,

P_{\Lambda}(a-b,c)=(\Lambda,\,|a-b|+c),

which coincides with
(\Lambda,m_{L})
.

The last displayed identity for
\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)
follows by subtraction:

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=m_{R}-m_{L}=(|a-b|+c+C(a,-b))-(|a-b|+c)=C(a,-b).

∎

Thus the right bracketing
x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)
corresponds to “using up”
all ambient cancellation mass stored in
U
, while the left bracketing

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z
only uses the original
\Lambda
–reserve
c
and
leaves the extra mass
C(a,-b)
unconverted. The associativity
defect is exactly the difference between these two ways of reading the
same ambient state.

Conceptual summary

We can summarize the ambient picture of associativity failure as
follows.

Theorem 6.17

(Ambient interpretation of associativity defect)
.

Let
a,b,c>0
and consider

x=(+,a),\quad y=(-,b),\quad z=(\Lambda,c)\in H,

with embeddings
X=\iota(x)
,
Y=\iota(y)
,
Z=\iota(z)\in K
and
ambient sum

U:=X\oplus Y\oplus Z\in K.

Then:

(1)

U
is independent of the bracketing of
(X,Y,Z)
and equals

U=(a-b,\;c+C(a,-b)).

(2)

The left and right hyper-sums in
H
are obtained from

different

\Lambda
–projections of ambient data:

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=\{P_{\Lambda}(a-b,c)\},\qquad x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)=\{P_{\Lambda}(U)\}.

(3)

The associativity defect is exactly the extra cancellation
mass recorded in the second coordinate of
U
:

\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)=C(a,-b)=\text{(second coordinate of $U$)}-c.

Proof.

(1) is Lemma
6.13
. (2) and (3) are precisely
the content of Proposition
6.16
.
∎

Remark 6.18

(Ambient associativity vs. hypernumber nonassociativity)
.

The ambient monoid
(K,\oplus)
is strictly associative
(Proposition
6.7
); all the nonassociativity of

(H,\boxplus)
arises from the choice of how to interpret ambient
states back into hypernumbers, in particular how much of the available
cancellation mass is converted into
\Lambda
–magnitude at each step.
For the ordered
(+,-,\Lambda)
pattern, the two natural bracketings
correspond to two natural ways of partially or fully applying

P_{\Lambda}
before or after aggregating cancellation in the ambient
monoid. The difference between these interpretations is measured
exactly by the cancellation mass
C(a,-b)
, which is the
associativity defect
\boldsymbol{\kappa}(a,b,c)
.

This viewpoint suggests that
(H,\boxplus)
can be seen as a
nonassociative “shadow” of the associative ambient object

(K,\oplus)
, obtained by applying a non-linear interpretation map
back to the hypernumber world. A systematic exploration of such
interpretations, and their possible generalizations to
n
–sign
systems, is a natural direction for future work.

6.3.
Ambient representation and an obstruction theorem

In Sections
6.1
and
6.2

we constructed an associative ambient monoid
(K,\oplus)
together
with an embedding
\iota:H\hookrightarrow K
and a

\Lambda
–projection
P_{\Lambda}:K\to H_{\Lambda}
that explain the
associativity defect for ordered
(+,-,\Lambda)
–triples. It is
natural to ask whether
(H,\boxplus)
could itself be
derived
in
a clean way from some ambient commutative monoid (or hypergroup) via a
suitable interpretation map. In this subsection we formalize this
idea and prove an obstruction theorem: there is no ambient representation of

H
that is simultaneously bracket–independent and compatible with
all hyper–sums.

Ambient representations with bracket–independent decoding

We begin with a general notion of ambient representation for a
commutative hypermagma, which captures the idea that the hyper–sum
should be obtained by encoding elements into an associative monoid,
adding them there, and then decoding back in a way that is independent
of the bracketing.

Definition 6.19

(Ambient representation with bracket–independent decoding)
.

Let
(H,\boxplus)
be a commutative hypermagma and let

(K,\oplus,\mathbf{0})
be a commutative monoid. An

ambient representation
of
(H,\boxplus)
in
K
with
bracket–independent decoding consists of:

(1)

an injective map
\iota:H\to K
(the
encoding
), and

(2)

a map
D:K\to H
(the
decoding
),

such that:

(AR1)

D\circ\iota=\mathrm{id}_{H}
(so
D
is a left inverse
to
\iota
);

(AR2)

for all
x,y\in H
,

D\bigl(\iota(x)\oplus\iota(y)\bigr)\in x\boxplus y;

(AR3)

for all
x,y,z\in H
, writing

S(x,y,z):=\iota(x)\oplus\iota(y)\oplus\iota(z)\in K,

one has

D\bigl(S(x,y,z)\bigr)\in(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z\;\cap\;x\boxplus(y\boxplus z).

Condition (AR2) says that the ambient two–fold sum refines the
hyper–sum: if we encode
x,y
, add in
K
, and decode back, the result
is one of the admissible hyper–sums
x\boxplus y
. Condition (AR3)
is a compatibility requirement with associativity in
K
: since

\oplus
is associative, the triple ambient sum

S(x,y,z)=\iota(x)\oplus\iota(y)\oplus\iota(z)
does not depend on the
bracketing, so its decoding should be simultaneously compatible with
both bracketings
(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z
and
x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)
. In particular
(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z
and
x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)
must intersect nontrivially for every triple
(x,y,z)
.

The following lemma isolates this simple but important consequence.

Lemma 6.20

(Bracket intersection requirement)
.

Let
(H,\boxplus)
be a commutative hypermagma and

(K,\oplus,\mathbf{0},\iota,D)
an ambient representation in the sense
of Definition
6.19
. Then for every triple

(x,y,z)\in H^{3}
,

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z\;\cap\;x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)\;\neq\;\emptyset.

In particular,
(H,\boxplus)
cannot have a triple
(x,y,z)
at which
the two bracketings are disjoint subsets of
H
.

Proof.

For any
x,y,z\in H
, define

S(x,y,z):=\iota(x)\oplus\iota(y)\oplus\iota(z)\in K.

By associativity of
\oplus
, the element
S(x,y,z)
is independent of
the bracketing of
\iota(x),\iota(y),\iota(z)
in
K
. By (AR3),

D\bigl(S(x,y,z)\bigr)\in(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z\quad\text{and}\quad D\bigl(S(x,y,z)\bigr)\in x\boxplus(y\boxplus z).

Hence
D(S(x,y,z))
lies in the intersection of the two bracketings,
which must therefore be nonempty.
∎

Conversely, if in a given hypermagma
(H,\boxplus)
we can find a
triple
(x,y,z)
for which
(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z
and

x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)
are disjoint, then no ambient representation
with bracket–independent decoding can exist.

Application to the three–sign hypernumber system

We now apply the general lemma to the three–sign hypernumber system

(H,\boxplus)
.

Recall from Proposition
4.5
that

x=(+,1),\quad y=(-,1),\quad z=(\Lambda,1)

give a concrete failure of associativity:

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=\{(\Lambda,1)\},\qquad x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)=\{(\Lambda,3)\},

so

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z\;\cap\;x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)=\emptyset.

More generally, by Theorem
4.18
, for arbitrary

a,b,c>0
we have

\displaystyle(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z

\displaystyle=\{(\Lambda,m_{L})\},

\displaystyle m_{L}

\displaystyle=c+|a-b|,

\displaystyle x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)

\displaystyle=\{(\Lambda,m_{R})\},

\displaystyle m_{R}

\displaystyle=a+b+c,

with
m_{L}\neq m_{R}
, so the two singleton sets are always disjoint.

We can now state the obstruction theorem.

Theorem 6.21

(Obstruction for bracket–independent ambient representation)
.

There is no ambient representation of the three–sign hypernumber
system
(H,\boxplus)
in any commutative monoid
(K,\oplus)
in the
sense of Definition
6.19
. Equivalently,
there do not exist a commutative monoid
(K,\oplus)
, an injective map

\iota:H\to K
, and a decoding map
D:K\to H
such that

all
of (AR1)–(AR3) hold.

Proof.

Suppose, for contradiction, that such
(K,\oplus,\iota,D)
exist.
Then by Lemma
6.20
, for every triple

(x,y,z)\in H^{3}
the two bracketings
(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z
and

x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)
must intersect nontrivially.

However, as noted above, for

x=(+,1),\quad y=(-,1),\quad z=(\Lambda,1)

we have

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z=\{(\Lambda,1)\},\qquad x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)=\{(\Lambda,3)\},

so

(x\boxplus y)\boxplus z\;\cap\;x\boxplus(y\boxplus z)=\emptyset.

This contradicts the conclusion of Lemma
6.20
.
Therefore no ambient representation satisfying (AR1)–(AR3) can exist.
∎

In particular, the associative ambient monoid
(K,\oplus)

constructed in Section
6.1
, together with the
embedding
\iota:H\to K
from
Definition
6.10
, cannot be completed by any
decoding map
D:K\to H
to yield an ambient representation in
the sense of Definition
6.19
. The
obstruction is exactly the existence of strongly nonassociative
triples in
(H,\boxplus)
whose two bracketings have disjoint images.

Consequences and interpretation

The obstruction theorem shows that the ambient picture developed in
Section
6
cannot be upgraded to a full “hidden
associative model” for the three–sign hyperaddition: one cannot find
a globally bracket–independent decoding of ambient sums that recovers
all hyper–sums in
H
. In particular:

•

Any attempt to model
(H,\boxplus)
as a coarse projection of
an associative monoid or group must either

(i)
restrict to a proper subset of triples (e.g. avoid
the
(+,-,\Lambda)
pattern), or

(ii)
relax the compatibility conditions (AR2)–(AR3),
for instance by allowing different decodings for different
bracketings of the same ambient sum.

•

The ambient cancellation monoid
(K,\oplus)
should thus
be viewed not as an exact associative cover of

(H,\boxplus)
, but rather as a tool for
measuring
the
defect of associativity (via the second coordinate and the
cancellation mass
C
), as formalized in
Theorem
6.17
.

•

From a structural perspective, the obstruction result clarifies that
the nonassociativity of
(H,\boxplus)
is intrinsic to the
rules governing the
\Lambda
–sector and cannot be removed by
simply “lifting” to an associative world and projecting
back in a bracket–independent way while preserving all
hyper–sums.

This motivates treating the ambient constructions as auxiliary
invariants for
(H,\boxplus)
, rather than as an underlying associative
model. In particular, it suggests that any
n
–sign generalization
with similar cancellation behaviour will necessarily exhibit an
intrinsic nonassociativity that cannot be repaired by a naive ambient
representation of the kind ruled out by
Theorem
6.21
.

7.
Conclusion

Further directions.

The present framework suggests several extensions: (i) a systematic study of
“enriched valuations” into signed or extended hyperfields in the sense of
recent work on tropical extensions
[
14
]
; (ii)
connections with valuation theory for Krasner hyperfields and related spectral
constructions
[
9
]
; and (iii) comparisons with
matroidal and convexity phenomena over the sign and tropical hyperfields

[
1
,
10
]
.

The hypernumber system
H
introduced here provides a concrete model of a
commutative, cancellation–sensitive hyperaddition extending the real
field by a third “neutral” sign
\Lambda
. The main structural inputs
are (i) the explicit description of additive inverses and the controlled
nonassociativity measured by the associator curvature
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
, (ii)
the canonical sign–layer envelope that records all admissible signs of
hypersums, and (iii) the ambient cancellation monoid together with the
obstruction theorem explaining why no associative additive structure can
extend
\boxplus
without losing essential cancellation information.

An extended version of the manuscript will contain additional material on
order, detailed computations, and visualizations; these are omitted here
to keep the exposition focused on the core algebraic structure.
