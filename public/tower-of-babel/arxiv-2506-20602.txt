On the temperature of an active nematic
Jay Armas, Akash Jain, Ruben Lier

Abstract
We employ a novel hydrodynamic framework for active matter coupled to an environment to study the local temperature of an active nematic, assuming proximity to thermal equilibrium. We show that, due to the mechanosensitivity of fuel consumption, linearized temperature correlations in a homogeneous active nematic steady state remain unaffected by activity. However, we demonstrate that local shearing and twisting cause a confined active nematic undergoing a spontaneous flow transition to develop a distinctive inhomogeneous temperature profile, serving as a thermal signature of activity.



Active matter consists of microscopic, fuel-consuming agents, enabling it to exhibit macroscopic behavior that is distinct from ordinary passive matter. In particular, when active matter admits a continuum hydrodynamic description for collective transport, it possesses the remarkable ability to circumvent various constraints that govern passive systems, arising from the local second law of thermodynamics, Onsager’s reciprocity relations, and fluctuation-dissipation theorems (FDTs).

The transport phenomena in active matter become particularly striking when some form of spontaneous order is present, such as in nematic liquid crystals, which exhibit spontaneous rotational order
Chaikin and Lubensky 1995
;
de Gennes and Prost 1993

;

Active nematics are realized in a variety of physical systems, such as bacterial populations

Hydrodynamics describes the near-equilibrium macroscopic behavior of a varied classes of physical systems. It governs the evolution of collective variables, such as energy, momentum, and mass, as well as Goldstone modes of spontaneously broken symmetries
Martin et al. 1972
.
A central assumption of hydrodynamics is
local thermal equilibrium
, which breaks down in the presence of microscopic active agents that drive the system away from equilibrium. However, when active effects remain reasonably small and comparable to the local gradients away from thermal equilibrium, activity can be introduced perturbatively within the hydrodynamic framework
De Groot and Mazur 2013
;
Julicher et al. 2018
;
Parmeggiani et al. 1999
.

Extending the framework of hydrodynamics to active matter requires accounting for the fuel-consuming processes inherent to active agents, which collectively serve as a local energy source supporting activity
Julicher et al. 2018
. As such, fuel consumption leads to a continuous build-up of energy in the system, preventing it from reaching a stable steady state. Consequently, such a framework inevitably cannot make long-time predictions for temperature. To address this issue, one must recognize that active matter is not only defined by the presence of fuel-consuming agents but also by its ability to dissipate excess energy into the environment.

In earlier work, we incorporated this idea into the hydrodynamics of active matter by allowing the system to exchange of energy and entropy with an environment at a fixed temperature
T_{{\mathsf{E}}}

Armas et al. 2024
. This restores the balance of energy, thereby allowing for non-equilibrium steady states maintained at a temperature above
T_{{\mathsf{E}}}
. The loss of entropy to the environment also modifies the local second law of thermodynamics, making it possible for active matter to acquire unique transport properties that are not present in passive systems
Armas et al. 2024
.

Passive hydrodynamics—
To set the stage, let us begin with a passive two-dimensional incompressible fluid

\displaystyle=0\penalty\ ,\qquad\partial_{t}\pi^{i}-\partial_{j}t^{ij}=0\penalty\ ,

(1)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{i}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon v^{i}+\varepsilon^{i}_{\text{nhs}}\penalty\ ,\qquad t^{ij}=-\pi^{i}v^{j}-p\,\delta^{ij}+t^{ij}_{\text{nhs}}\penalty\ .

Hydrodynamics with fuel consumption—
We now introduce fuel consumption into our hydrodynamic model, which provides the energy source needed for supporting activity

Active nematics—
The effect of fuel consumption has been somewhat trivial in our hydrodynamic model thus far. The temperature evolution in

The temperature profile depends qualitatively on the energy relaxation length

. Firstly, larger

means that the energy gained from inhomogeneous shearing and twisting effects is transported over longer distances before relaxing to the environment, thereby heating the fluid more across the slab; see

Discussion and perspectives—
In this work we used a novel hydrodynamic framework for active matter coupled to an environment to study the local temperature dynamics of a two-dimensional active nematic. This framework is based on the usual assumptions underlying hydrodynamics, namely local thermal equilibrium and microscopic reversibility
De Groot and Mazur 2013
;
Julicher et al. 2018
but includes the coupling to an external environment
Armas et al. 2024
. The interplay between energy inflow caused by microscopic self-driven agents and the outflow of energy due to exchanges with the environment allows for the existence of non-equilibrium steady states.

We found that the mechanosensitive nature of the fuel consumption makes it so that the activity of active nematics does not linearly affect energy balance. However, when studying the well known spontaneous flow transition for a confined active nematic, we find that this does leave an imprint on the temperature profile. The nature of the temperature profile depends on the ratio between the diffusion along the confined geometry and the relaxation to the environment. It would be interesting to explore whether such temperature profiles could be observed in an experimental realization of a confined active nematic
Duclos et al. 2018
;
Chandrakar et al. 2020
.

Appendix A
Dissipation rate and fluctuation-dissipation theorem

In this section, we derive the the active nematic dissipation rate that accounts for entropy exchanges with the environment. We further obtain the distribution of stochastic noise fields for active nematics by using FDT near thermal equilibrium. Since we consider the active steady state to be close to thermal equilibrium, we can assume that the thermodynamic identities hold locally, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\varepsilon

\displaystyle=T\mathrm{d}s+\mu\mathrm{d}\rho+\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{nem}}\penalty\ ,\qquad\varepsilon=Ts+\mu\rho-p+\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{nem}}\penalty\ ,

(A.24)

where
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{nem}}
denotes the nematic free-energy contribution in
eq.

12
. Using this, together with the conservation equations, we can show that

\displaystyle T\partial_{t}s

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\epsilon-v_{i}\partial_{t}\pi^{i}-\left(\mu-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}\right)\partial_{t}\rho+\mathcal{H}^{ij}\partial_{t}Q_{ij}-K\partial_{i}\!\left(\partial^{i}Q^{jk}\partial_{t}Q_{jk}\right)\penalty\ .

(A.25)

Using equations of motion and conservation equations, we find

\displaystyle T\Theta_{\text{tot}}\equiv T\Big(\partial_{t}s+\partial_{i}s^{i}+{\text{k}}_{\resizebox{}{}{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{{\hbox{{\scriptsize B}}}}}}r_{{\mathsf{E}}}\Big)

\displaystyle=r_{{\mathsf{F}}}\Delta\mu+r_{{\mathsf{E}}}{\text{k}}_{\resizebox{}{}{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{{\hbox{{\scriptsize B}}}}}}\Delta T-\varepsilon^{i}_{\text{diss}}\frac{1}{T}\partial_{i}T+t^{ij}_{\text{diss}}u_{ij}+V^{ij}_{\text{diss}}\mathcal{H}_{ij}\penalty\ ,

(A.26)

where we have identified the heat flux

\displaystyle Ts^{i}=\varepsilon^{i}+(p-\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{nem}}-\rho\mu)v^{i}+K\partial^{i}Q^{jk}(\partial_{t}+v^{l}\partial_{l})Q_{jk}\penalty\ ,

(A.27)

and isolated the dissipative part of the constitutive relations via

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{i}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon v^{i}-K\partial^{i}Q^{jk}(\partial_{t}+v^{l}\partial_{l})Q_{jk}\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle t^{ij}

\displaystyle=-\rho v^{i}v^{j}-p\delta^{ij}+\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{nem}}\delta^{ij}-K\partial^{i}Q^{kl}\partial^{j}Q_{kl}+Q^{i}_{\penalty\ k}\mathcal{H}^{jk}-Q^{j}_{\penalty\ k}\mathcal{H}^{ik}+t^{ij}_{\text{diss}}\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+v^{k}\partial_{k})Q^{ij}

\displaystyle=-\Big(\Omega^{ik}Q_{k}^{\,\,j}-Q^{i}_{\,\,k}\Omega^{kj}\Big)+V^{ij}_{\text{diss}}\penalty\ .

(A.28)

r_{{\mathsf{E}}}\\
\varepsilon^{i}_{\text{nhs}}\\
t^{ij}_{\text{nhs}}\\
V^{ij}_{\text{nhs}}\end{pmatrix}\penalty\ ,\qquad f_{\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta\mu\\
{\text{k}}_{\resizebox{}{}{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{{\hbox{{\scriptsize B}}}}}}\Delta T\\
-\frac{1}{T}\partial_{i}T\\
u_{ij}\\
\mathcal{H}_{ij}\end{pmatrix}\penalty\ .

(A.30)

In accordance with the second law of thermodynamics, the constitutive relations take the form

\displaystyle J^{\text{nhs}}_{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta}O_{\alpha\beta}f_{\beta}+\xi_{\alpha}\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(A.31)

where
O_{\alpha\beta}
is the Onsager matrix and
\xi_{\alpha}
represent the noise fluxes. Comparing with the main text, the Onsager matrix takes the form

\displaystyle O_{\alpha\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{{\mathsf{E}}}&0&0&0&0\\
0&\gamma_{{\mathsf{F}}}&0&\alpha_{{{\mathsf{F}}}}Q^{kl}&0\\

(A.32)

where we have defined

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{ijkl}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\delta^{ik}\delta^{jl}+\delta^{il}\delta^{jk}-\delta^{ij}\delta^{kl}\Big)\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(A.33)

We have not introduced any off-diagonal mechanosensitive terms in

where

\displaystyle=2{\text{k}}_{\resizebox{}{}{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{{\hbox{{\scriptsize B}}}}}}T_{0}^{2}\kappa\,\delta^{ij}\delta^{(2)}(x-x^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle=2{\text{k}}_{\resizebox{}{}{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{{\hbox{{\scriptsize B}}}}}}T_{0}\gamma_{{{\mathsf{F}}}}\delta^{(2)}(x-x^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle\Big\langle\xi_{{{\mathsf{E}}}}(x,t)\,\xi_{{{\mathsf{E}}}}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=2{\text{k}}_{\resizebox{}{}{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{{\hbox{{\scriptsize B}}}}}}T_{0}\gamma_{{{\mathsf{E}}}}\delta^{(2)}(x-x^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle\Big\langle\xi^{ij}_{Q}(x,t)\,\xi^{kl}_{Q}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2{\text{k}}_{\resizebox{}{}{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{{\hbox{{\scriptsize B}}}}}}T_{0}}{\sigma_{Q}}\mathcal{L}^{ijkl}\delta^{(2)}(x-x^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})\penalty\ ,

(A.36)

while all noise cross-correlations are zero.

Appendix B
Linear instability of the homogeneous state in active nematics

In this appendix, we review the linearized instability of homogeneous steady states in active nematics. The homogeneous state given by
T=T_{0}
,
v^{i}=0
,
S=S_{0}
, and
\theta=\theta_{0}
. We can reach any state with
v^{i}\neq 0
by performing a Galilean boost transformation. We consider linearized plane-wave perturbations around this state
\propto\exp(-i\omega t+ik_{i}x^{i})
. The linearized hydrodynamic equations yield

\displaystyle i\omega c_{v}\delta T-ik\delta\varepsilon_{\|}+t_{\perp\|}^{0}ik\delta v_{\perp}

\displaystyle=-r_{{\mathsf{F}}}\Delta\mu+r_{{\mathsf{E}}}{\text{k}}_{\resizebox{}{}{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{{\hbox{{\scriptsize B}}}}}}T_{{\mathsf{E}}}\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle i\omega\delta v_{\perp}+\frac{ik}{\rho_{0}}\delta t_{\perp\|}

\displaystyle=0\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle i\omega\delta S+\lambda S_{0}s_{\theta}ik\delta v_{\perp}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\sigma_{Q}}\Big(c_{\theta}\mathcal{H}_{\|\|}+s_{\theta}\mathcal{H}_{\|\perp}\Big)\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle i\omega\delta\theta+\frac{1+\lambda c_{\theta}}{2}ik\delta v_{\perp}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{S_{0}\sigma_{Q}}\Big(s_{\theta}\mathcal{H}_{\|\|}-c_{\theta}\mathcal{H}_{\|\perp}\Big)\penalty\ .

(B.37)

Here
\perp
and
\|
denote the components transverse and parallel to
k_{i}
respectively. For a vector
X^{i}
, these are defined as
X_{\|}=k_{i}X^{i}/|k|
and
X_{\perp}=k_{i}\epsilon^{ij}X_{j}/|k|
. Note that
\delta v_{\|}=0
due to incompressibility.
We have denoted
\cos(2\Delta\theta)=c_{\theta}
and
\sin(2\Delta\theta)=s_{\theta}
for brevity, where
\Delta\theta=\theta-\arctan(k_{y}/k_{x})
is the relative angle between the nematic orientation and the wavevector. In particular,
Q_{\|\|}=S/2\,c_{\theta}
and
Q_{\|\perp}=S/2\,s_{\theta}
.
Various constitutive quantities appearing above are given by

\displaystyle\delta\varepsilon_{\|}

\displaystyle=-ikc_{v}D_{\epsilon}\delta T\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle\delta t_{\perp\|}

\displaystyle=ik\eta\,\delta v_{\perp}+S_{0}\Big(s_{\theta}\mathcal{H}_{\|\|}-c_{\theta}\mathcal{H}_{\|\perp}\Big)-\lambda S_{0}\mathcal{H}_{\|\perp}-\frac{1}{2}\zeta s_{\theta}\delta S-\zeta S_{0}c_{\theta}\delta\theta\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\|\|}

\displaystyle=Kk^{2}S_{0}s_{\theta}\delta\theta-\left(\frac{1}{2}Kk^{2}+bS_{0}^{2}\right)c_{\theta}\delta S\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\|\perp}

\displaystyle=-Kk^{2}S_{0}c_{\theta}\delta\theta-\left(\frac{1}{2}Kk^{2}+bS_{0}^{2}\right)s_{\theta}\delta S\penalty\ ,

(B.38)

together with
t_{\perp\|}^{0}=-\frac{1}{2}S_{0}\zeta s_{\theta}
, and the rate terms

\displaystyle r_{{\mathsf{F}}}\Delta\mu-r_{{\mathsf{E}}}{\text{k}}_{\resizebox{}{}{\scalebox{0.8}[1]{{\hbox{{\scriptsize B}}}}}}T_{{\mathsf{E}}}

\displaystyle=-c_{v}\Gamma_{\epsilon}\delta T+\frac{1}{2}S_{0}\zeta s_{\theta}ik\delta v_{\perp}\penalty\ .

(B.39)

As argued in the main text, the temperature fluctuations decouple and give rise to simple diffusional dynamics

\displaystyle\left(i\omega-k^{2}D_{\epsilon}-\Gamma_{\epsilon}\right)\delta T

\displaystyle=0\penalty\ .

(B.40)

The remaining three degrees of freedom give rise to

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}i\omega-D_{\pi}k^{2}&-\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\left(1+\lambda c_{\theta}\right)S_{0}^{2}Kk^{4}+\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}c_{\theta}\zeta S_{0}k^{2}&-\frac{1}{2\rho_{0}}s_{\theta}\left(\lambda S_{0}\left(Kk^{2}+2bS_{0}^{2}\right)-\zeta\right)k^{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\lambda c_{\theta}\right)&i\omega-D_{Q}k^{2}&0\\
S_{0}\lambda s_{\theta}&0&i\omega-D_{Q}k^{2}-2bS_{0}^{2}/\sigma_{Q}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}ik\delta v_{\perp}\\
\delta\theta\\
\delta S\end{pmatrix}=0\penalty\ ,

(B.41)

where
D_{\pi}=\eta/\rho_{0}
,
D_{Q}=K/\sigma_{Q}
. The linearized mode spectrum is obtained by setting the determinant of the matrix above to zero, i.e.

\displaystyle\left(i\omega-D_{Q}k^{2}-\frac{2bS_{0}^{2}}{\sigma_{Q}}\right)\left(\left(i\omega-D_{\pi}k^{2}-\frac{\lambda^{2}s_{\theta}^{2}}{2\rho_{0}}S_{0}^{2}\sigma_{Q}k^{2}\right)\left(i\omega-D_{Q}k^{2}\right)+\frac{1+\lambda c_{\theta}}{2\rho_{0}}S_{0}^{2}\left(\left(1+\lambda c_{\theta}\right)Kk^{2}-\frac{c_{\theta}\zeta}{S_{0}}\right)k^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\lambda s_{\theta}^{2}}{2\rho_{0}}S_{0}^{2}\left(i\omega-D_{Q}k^{2}\right)\left(i\omega\sigma_{Q}\lambda-\frac{\zeta}{S_{0}}\right)k^{2}=0.

(B.42)

In general, the roots of this equation are quite complicated. However, note that
\delta S
fluctuations are gapped with relaxation rate
2bS_{0}^{2}/\sigma_{Q}
. We can focus on the low-energy regime, characterized by
|\omega|\ll 2bS_{0}^{2}/\sigma_{Q}
and
k^{2}\ll 2bS_{0}^{2}/K
, or, equivalently, send
b\to\infty
in the above equation. This leaves us with quadratic dispersion relations for
\delta v_{\perp}
and
\delta\theta
, taking the form

\displaystyle\left(i\omega-D_{\pi}k^{2}-\frac{\lambda^{2}s_{\theta}^{2}}{2\rho_{0}}S_{0}^{2}\sigma_{Q}k^{2}\right)\left(i\omega-D_{Q}k^{2}\right)+\frac{1+\lambda c_{\theta}}{2\rho_{0}}S_{0}^{2}\left(\left(1+\lambda c_{\theta}\right)Kk^{2}-\frac{c_{\theta}\zeta}{S_{0}}\right)k^{2}=0.

(B.43)

This leads to two low-energy modes
\omega=-i\omega_{\pm}
, such that

\displaystyle\omega_{+}+\omega_{-}

\displaystyle=\left(D_{Q}+D_{\pi}+\frac{\lambda^{2}s_{\theta}^{2}}{2\rho_{0}}S_{0}^{2}\sigma_{Q}\right)k^{2}\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle\omega_{+}\omega_{-}

\displaystyle=\left(D_{Q}D_{\pi}+\frac{1+\lambda^{2}+2\lambda c_{\theta}}{2\rho_{0}}S_{0}^{2}K\right)k^{4}-\frac{(1+\lambda c_{\theta})c_{\theta}}{2\rho_{0}}S_{0}\zeta k^{2}\penalty\ .

(B.44)

Due to the second law constraints, the sum of
\omega_{\pm}
is decisively non-negative. As a consequence, we only need to require that the product of
\omega_{\pm}
is non-negative to ensure that both the modes are stable and reside in the lower-half complex-
\omega
plane. To wit, the stability criterion is

\displaystyle k^{2}\geq\frac{S_{0}\zeta}{D_{Q}}\frac{(1+\lambda c_{\theta})c_{\theta}}{2\eta+S_{0}^{2}\sigma_{Q}\big(1+\lambda^{2}+2\lambda c_{\theta}\big)}\equiv k_{c}(\Delta\theta)^{2}\penalty\ .

(B.45)

Wave vectors smaller than this bound are unstable and precisely lead to the critical wavevector in
eq.

19
. This implies that the homogeneous state is unstable for
\cos(2\Delta\theta)<0
for a contractile active nematic (
\zeta<0
), peaking at
\Delta\theta=\pm\pi/2
when the perturbations are transverse to the nematic orientation
Aditi Simha and Ramaswamy 2002
. Similarly, for an extensile active nematic (
\zeta>0
), the instability arises for
\cos(2\Delta\theta)>0
, peaking at
\pm\Delta\theta=0,\pi
when perturbations are along the nematic orientation. This is why the homogeneous state can be made stable by confining contractile active nematics perpendicular to the nematic order
L_{\perp}<\pi/k_{c}(\pi/2)
, while confining extensile active nematics parallel to the nematic order
L_{\|}<\pi/k_{c}(0)

Giomi et al. 2012
;
Giomi et al. 2014
;
Ramaswamy and Rao 2007
;
Voituriez et al. 2005
;
Lavi et al. 2024
.

can again be set to zero by requiring that the average velocity along the slab is zero.

Using these solutions in
eqs.

C.53
and
C
, one may verify that the approximate shear profile near the spontaneous flow transition is given by
eq.

21
with

\displaystyle A_{u}=\frac{\pi^{2}A_{\theta}D_{Q}}{(1+\lambda\cos(2\theta_{0}))L^{2}}\penalty\ .

(C.55)

Appendix D
Temperature profile of nematic flow transition

To compute the temperature profile of this solution, we invoke the energy balance equation (
18
), which results in

\displaystyle\kappa\partial_{x}^{2}T+\frac{\partial\kappa}{\partial T}(\partial_{x}T)^{2}-c_{v}\Gamma_{\epsilon}(T-T_{0})

\displaystyle=-\eta\,(\partial_{x}v_{y})^{2}-\frac{2}{\sigma_{Q}}\left(\mathcal{H}_{xx}^{2}+\mathcal{H}_{xy}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(\eta+\frac{S^{2}\sigma_{Q}}{2}\left(1+\lambda^{2}+2\lambda\cos(2\theta)\right)\right)(\partial_{x}v_{y})^{2}\penalty\ .

(D.56)

In the second line, we have used
eq.

C.48
to eliminate
\mathcal{H}_{ij}
. To solve this equation perturbatively around the homogeneous state, we introduce the expansion

\displaystyle T(x)

\displaystyle=T_{0}+A_{\theta}T^{(1)}(x)+A_{\theta}^{2}T^{(2)}(x)+\ldots\penalty\ .

(D.57)

At
\mathcal{O}(A_{\theta})
, the temperature fluctuations decouple and satisfy
D_{\epsilon}\partial_{x}^{2}T^{(1)}-\Gamma_{\epsilon}T^{(1)}=0
. Solving these with Dirichlet boundary conditions
T(\pm L/2)=0
or Neumann boundary conditions
\partial_{x}T(\pm L/2)=0
, we find the trivial profile
T^{(1)}(x)=0
. Hence, first non-trivial corrections to the temperature profile arise at
\mathcal{O}(A_{\theta}^{2})
, with the differential equation

\displaystyle D_{\epsilon}\partial_{x}^{2}T^{(2)}-\Gamma_{\epsilon}T^{(2)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\tilde{\eta}}{c_{v}}\left(\partial_{x}v^{(1)}_{y}\right)^{2}\penalty\ ,\qquad\tilde{\eta}=\eta+\frac{S_{0}^{2}\sigma_{Q}}{2}\left(1+\lambda\cos(2\theta_{0})\right)^{2}\penalty\ .

(D.58)

Substituting the solution for
v^{(1)}_{y}
, this can be expressed as an inhomogeneous differential equation

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{x}T^{(2)}(x)-k_{\epsilon}^{2}T^{(2)}(x)=-\Phi(x)\penalty\ ,

(D.59)

where
k_{\epsilon}^{2}=\Gamma_{\epsilon}/D_{\epsilon}
and the source is given as

\displaystyle\Phi(x)\Big|_{\text{free-slip}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4A_{u}^{2}\tilde{\eta}}{\kappa}\cos^{2}(\pi x/L)\penalty\ ,\qquad\Phi(x)\Big|_{\text{no-slip}}=\frac{16A_{u}^{2}\tilde{\eta}}{\kappa}\cos^{2}(2\pi x/L)\penalty\ .

(D.60)

D.1
Dirichlet boundary conditions

Let us first solve for the temperature profile with Dirichlet boundary conditions
T^{(2)}(\pm L/2)=0
.
The differential equation can be solved with

\displaystyle T^{(2)}(x)=\int^{L/2}_{-L/2}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}G(x,x^{\prime})\Phi(x^{\prime})\penalty\ ,

(D.61)

where
G(x,x^{\prime})
is the Green’s function that satisfies

\displaystyle\Big(k^{2}_{\epsilon}-\partial^{2}_{x}\Big)G(x,x^{\prime})=\delta(x,x^{\prime})\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(D.62)

Imposition of Dirichlet boundary conditions at
x,x^{\prime}=\pm L/2
leads to

\displaystyle G^{D}(x,x^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\sinh\!\Big(k_{\epsilon}(L/2+x)\Big)\sinh\!\Big(k_{\epsilon}(L/2-x^{\prime})\Big)}{k_{\epsilon}\sinh(k_{\epsilon}L)},&x<x^{\prime}\penalty\ ,\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\sinh\!\Big(k_{\epsilon}(L/2+x^{\prime})\Big)\sinh\!\Big(k_{\epsilon}(L/2-x)\Big)}{k_{\epsilon}\sinh(k_{\epsilon}L)},&x>x^{\prime}\penalty\ .\end{cases}

(D.63)

We then plug this into (
D.61
) to find the temperature profile

\displaystyle T^{(2)}(x)\Big|_{\text{free-slip}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{L^{2}A_{u}^{2}\tilde{\eta}/\kappa}{\pi^{2}(1+L^{2}/L_{\epsilon}^{2})}\left(\frac{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}{2L^{2}}\left(1-\frac{\cosh(2\pi x/L_{\epsilon})}{\cosh(\pi L/L_{\epsilon})}\right)+\cos^{2}(\pi x/L)\right)\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle T^{(2)}(x)\Big|_{\text{no-slip}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{4L^{2}A_{u}^{2}\tilde{\eta}/\kappa}{\pi^{2}(4+L^{2}/L_{\epsilon}^{2})}\left(\left(1+\frac{2L_{\epsilon}^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)\left(1-\frac{\cosh(2\pi x/L_{\epsilon})}{\cosh(\pi L/L_{\epsilon})}\right)-\sin^{2}(2\pi x/L)\right)\penalty\ ,

(D.64)

where
L_{\epsilon}=2\pi/k_{\epsilon}
. We have recovered the results presented in the main text and arrived at the temperature profile in
On the temperature of an active nematic
.

D.2
Neumann boundary conditions

Let us now look at the temperature profile when subjected to Neumann boundary conditions,
\partial_{x}T^{(2)}(\pm L/2)=0
.
Imposing Neumann boundary conditions to the Green’s function in
eq.

D.62
at
x,x^{\prime}=\pm L/2
leads to

\displaystyle G^{N}(x,x^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\cosh\!\Big(k_{\epsilon}(L/2+x)\Big)\cosh\!\Big(k_{\epsilon}(L/2-x^{\prime})\Big)}{k_{\epsilon}\sinh(k_{\epsilon}L)},&x<x^{\prime}\penalty\ ,\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\cosh\!\Big(k_{\epsilon}(L/2+x^{\prime})\Big)\cosh\!\Big(k_{\epsilon}(L/2-x)\Big)}{k_{\epsilon}\sinh(k_{\epsilon}L)},&x>x^{\prime}\penalty\ .\end{cases}

(D.65)

We then plug this into (
D.61
) to find the temperature profile

\displaystyle T^{(2)}(x)\Big|_{\text{free-slip}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{L^{2}A_{u}^{2}\tilde{\eta}/\kappa}{\pi^{2}(1+L^{2}/L_{\epsilon}^{2})}\left(\frac{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}{2L^{2}}+\cos^{2}(\pi x/L)\right)\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle T^{(2)}(x)\Big|_{\text{no-slip}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{4L^{2}A_{u}^{2}\tilde{\eta}/\kappa}{\pi^{2}(4+L^{2}/L_{\epsilon}^{2})}\left(\frac{2L_{\epsilon}^{2}}{L^{2}}+\cos^{2}(2\pi x/L)\right)\penalty\ ,

(D.66)

where
L_{\epsilon}=2\pi/k_{\epsilon}
. The resulting temperature profile is shown in Fig.
D.1
. Note that unlike for the case with Dirichlet boundary conditions, the shape of the profile is not affected by
L_{\epsilon}
for the case of Neumann boundary conditions. The reason for this is that Neumann boundary conditions force the energy current to be zero at the boundary, thus allowing temperature to be lost exclusively through thermal relaxation and not through diffusion. Therefore, the profile does not display the same competition between diffusive and relaxational energy loss that was found for the profile with Dirichlet boundary conditions studied in the main text.

Appendix E

In this appendix, we attempt to estimate the strength of thermal oscillations for the spontaneous flow transition that arise for a cell monolayer in a confined geometry as observed in
Duclos et al. 2018

\displaystyle\kappa_{\text{water}}\sim 0.6\,\text{W/(m$\cdot$K)}=2.16\times 10^{15}\text{Pa}\cdot\mu\text{m}^{2}/(\text{hr}\cdot\text{K})\penalty\ \penalty\ .
