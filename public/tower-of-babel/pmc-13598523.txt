A rare case report: Malignant transformation of lichen planus
Authors: HaiCheng He, Yongzhu Pu, Xunrui Zhao, Chengtao Feng, Yujie Du, Long Chen, Ran Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lichen planus is a chronic inflammatory disease that mainly affects the skin and mucous membranes. Malignant transformation is uncommon and is most frequently reported in oral or genital lesions. We report a rare case of cutaneous lichen planus on the left lower leg that transformed into squamous cell carcinoma with left inguinal lymph node metastasis. 18F-FDG PET/CT demonstrated increased uptake in the primary skin lesion and metastatic lymph nodes, providing important information for staging and treatment planning. This case highlights the potential malignant transformation of long-standing cutaneous lichen planus and the value of PET/CT in evaluating disease extent

Introduction

Lichen planus (LP) is a chronic inflammatory disease that can affect the skin, oral cavity, genitalia, and other mucosal areas. The pathogenesis of LP has not been fully elucidated yet. Currently, it is believed to be related to immune-mediated inflammatory responses. The overall prognosis of LP is good, but malignant transformation has been occasionally reported, with oral and genital mucosa being the most common sites. Skin LP rarely undergoes canceration. Therefore, reporting such cases is helpful in enhancing clinicians’ understanding of the malignant transformation of LP and strengthening long-term follow-up and monitoring. This article reports a case of LP canceration and analyzes it in conjunction with relevant literature.

Patient information

A 37-year-old male visited the dermatology department due to itching on the outer side of his left leg. The patient had previously experienced the appearance of a plaque on the outer side of his left calf 10 years ago without any obvious cause, accompanied by depigmentation on the plaque surface and itching. Additionally, a mass was felt in the left inguinal area. The patient underwent biopsy of the affected skin at the local hospital’s dermatology department, and the results showed: lichen planus with local epidermal ulceration. Subsequently, the patient received antibiotic and traditional Chinese medicine treatment, but the symptoms did not improve significantly. Four months ago, the patient’s affected skin began to show more severe itching symptoms, along with skin erosion, and the size of the left inguinal mass had increased compared to before. Subsequently, the patient went to another hospital’s dermatology department for examination, and after inquiring about the medical history, the doctor performed a biopsy of the affected area on the outer side of the left calf and the left inguinal mass. The results showed that the skin on the outer side of the left calf suggested high-grade squamous cell carcinoma, and the left inguinal lymph nodes had metastasis of high-grade to medium-grade squamous cell carcinoma. Subsequently, the patient came to our hospital for further treatment and underwent serological tumor marker tests and PET/CT examinations. The tumor marker test results showed that the cytokeratin 19 fragment was 5.4 g/mL, which was higher than the normal value (normal value was 3.3 ng/mL), and the other indicators showed no obvious abnormalities. The 18F-FDG PET/CT examination results indicated: the skin on the anterior lateral side of the left calf showed increased radioactive metabolic uptake, with a lesion size of approximately 3.4×2.4 cm, SUVmax value of 2.8, and SUVmean value of 1.9. The left external iliac lymph nodes were enlarged with significantly increased radioactive uptake, with a size of approximately 1.7×1.2 cm, SUVmax value of 10.4, and SUVmean value of 7.3. No obvious abnormal signs were found in the PET/CT imaging of other parts of the body. Subsequently, the patient received further treatment at our hospital, and the clinical doctor, based on the patient's examination results, assessed the TNM stage as T2N1M0 and prescribed the chemotherapy regimen of “docetaxel + cisplatin” for the patient. Discussion

Lichen planus is a relatively rare and benign lesion of a special nature, often affecting the skin, oral mucosa, external genitalia, and the surface of flexor muscles on the limbs. However, the location of the lesion varies, and the incidence also differs. The cause of its occurrence may be related to the immune system mistakenly attacking its own skin and mucosal cells. Additionally, studies have shown that hepatitis C virus infection, drugs, and contact allergens may also be associated with the occurrence of lichen planus [[1], [2], [3]].

The literature indicates that lichen planus most commonly affects the oral mucosa, with an incidence rate of 46%-54%, while the incidence rate in other areas is only about 8%.[4]. Currently, it is believed that the risk of malignancy mainly concentrates in the oral and external genital regions. A study on the malignancy of lichen planus in the Finnish region showed that the standardized incidence ratios of oral lichen planus malignancy transforming into oral squamous cell carcinoma and external genital lichen planus malignancy transforming into vulvar squamous cell carcinoma were 7.79 and 1.99, respectively [5]. Additionally, a review mentioned that the first case of lichen planus malignancy occurring in the oral cavity was reported in 1910 [6]. In contrast, the reports of lichen planus malignancy occurring in the skin are relatively rare. Therefore, the case of lichen planus malignancy in the left lower limb skin accompanied by lymph node metastasis in the inguinal region has certain clinical reference value.

Lichen planus can present a chronic and protracted course. Long-term chronic inflammation may increase the risk of malignancy. Short-term glucocorticoid treatment can effectively alleviate the condition. Altman and Perry pointed out that when mucosal involvement is accompanied by or without skin lesions, the course of the disease may be prolonged, and studies have shown that 12% of patients will experience recurrence after treatment [7]. Therefore, the World Health Organization considers lichen planus as a disease with potential malignant transformation. Timely treatment and regular follow-up are crucial for the management of this disease.

The uniqueness of this case lies in the fact that previous cases of squamous cell carcinoma developing from lichen planus mostly had the primary lesion in the oral cavity or external genitalia, and the lesion location was relatively limited. However, the primary lesion of this patient was located in the left lower limb, accompanied by metastasis to the inguinal lymph nodes, which is relatively rare in clinical practice. The 18F-FDG PET/CT examination provided important evidence for the clinical staging and treatment decision-making of this case, timely obtaining the location and metabolic status of the lesion, and excluding information such as distant lymph node metastasis. This provided basic diagnostic and therapeutic ideas for the clinicians regarding the staging and treatment of this patient. Clearly, this case further indicates that squamous cell carcinoma of the skin also has the potential for malignancy. Therefore, health education should be strengthened, early and standardized treatment should be emphasized, and long-term follow-up should be conducted to achieve early identification and intervention of malignancy and improve the prognosis of the patient.
