Scaling laws for passive polymer dynamics in active turbulence
Zahra K. Valei, Tyler N. Shendruk

Abstract
Biological systems commonly combine intrinsically out-of-equilibrium active components with passive polymeric inclusions to produce unique material properties. To explore these composite systems, idealized models—such as polymers in active fluids—are essential to develop a predictive theoretical framework. We simulate a single, freely jointed passive chain in two-dimensional active turbulence. Active flows advect the polymer, producing a substantially enhanced diffusivity. Our results reveal that the dimensionless diffusivity obeys scaling laws governed by the Péclet, Weissenberg, and Ericksen numbers, which paves the way for designing active/polymeric hybrid materials with predictable properties that differ significantly from those of nondeformable inclusions.

We find that passive polymers within active nematic turbulence experience a thousand-fold increase in diffusivity, arising from short-time advection of the polymers by active flows with a finite correlation time.

The interplay between these dimensionless numbers fully determines the enhanced diffusivity of passive polymers in active turbulence.

To model this effect, consider a sufficiently long polymer as a chain of

This study investigated the dynamics of single flexible polymers embedded in active nematic turbulence, finding that activity significantly enhances polymer diffusion.
The enhancement arises from the short-time advection of the polymer and decorrelation of active turbulence.
The advection rate for shorter polymers increases with activity and is directly proportional to the characteristic velocity of the active turbulence; whereas, longer polymers reach a plateau.
From the ratio of Péclet number, which reveals the scaling for advection speed, and Weissenberg number, which characterizes the shear, the scaling for effective diffusion of polymers can be predicted.

Appendix A
Numerical Methods

A.1
Active Nematic Multi-Particle Collision Dynamics

To model active nematic fluids, we use active-nematic multi-particle collision dynamics (AN-MPCD)
[
109
]
.
It is chosen for its capability to simulate embedded particles within a fluctuating active nematohydrodynamic background.

This mesoscopic simulation technique represents the fluid as point particles characterized by mass
m_{i}
, position
{\underline{r}}_{i}(t)
, velocity
{\underline{v}}_{i}(t)
, and nematic orientation
{\underline{u}}_{i}(t)

[
110
]
.
The number of lines beneath the notation represents the tensor rank.
While time is discretized into steps of size
\Delta t
, other quantities evolve continuously.
AN-MPCD consists of two steps: the streaming step and the collision step.
In the steaming step, particle positions evolve ballistically as

\displaystyle{\underline{r}}_{i}\left(t+\Delta t\right)

\displaystyle={\underline{r}}_{i}\left(t\right)+{\underline{v}}_{i}\left(t\right)\Delta t.

(16)

The collision step involves two phases:
(1)
momentum exchange and
(2)
orientation fluctuations.

(1) Momentum exchange.

The velocity of particle
i
within cell
c
is updated as
\underline{v}_{i}(t+\Delta t)=\underline{v}^{\text{cm}}_{c}(t)+\underline{\Xi}_{i,c}
, where
\underline{v}^{\text{cm}}_{c}(t)=\left\langle\underline{v}_{i}(t)\right\rangle_{c}
is the velocity of the cell center of mass at time
t
,
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle_{c}
represents the mass-weighted average over the
N_{c}
particles instantaneously within the cell
c
and
\underline{\Xi}_{i,c}
is the collision operator.
The collision operator consists of a passive contribution
{\underline{\Xi}}^{\text{P}}_{i,c}
and an active part
{\underline{\Xi}}^{\text{A}}_{i,c}
.
We assume all the fluid particles have the same mass
m
.
For the passive contribution, a modified angular-momentum conserving Andersen-thermostatted collision operator is employed.
In the absence of angular momentum conservation, the Andersen-thermostatted collision operator is
{\underline{\Xi}}^{\text{P}}_{i,c}={\underline{v}^{\text{ran}}_{i}}-\left\langle{\underline{v}^{\text{ran}}_{i}}\right\rangle_{c}
, where the components of
{\underline{v}^{\text{ran}}_{i}}
are Gaussian random numbers
with variance
k_{\text{B}}T/m
and zero mean
[
111
,
112
]
.
Although the individual velocity of each particle is randomized, subtracting
\left\langle{\underline{v}^{\text{ran}}_{i}}\right\rangle_{c}
assures that the cell center-of-mass velocity—and consequently the total momentum—remains unchanged
[
111
]
.
To conserve angular momentum, an additional term must be added to the collision operator to remove any angular velocity generated by the collision operation
[
113
]
.
In an isotropic system, the change in the angular momentum can rise due to the stochastically generated velocities

\delta\underline{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{vel}}=m\sum_{j=1}^{N_{c}(t)}\underline{r}_{j,c}\times(\underline{v}_{j}-{\underline{v}^{\text{ran}}_{j}})
, where
\underline{r}_{i,c}=\underline{r}_{i}-\left\langle\underline{r}_{i}\right\rangle_{c}
is the relative position of particle
i
with respect to the center of mass of all particles in the cell.
In a nematic system, changes in the orientation will contribute additional angular velocity
\underline{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{ori}}
, which will be elaborated below.
For angular-momentum conserving Andersen-thermostatted AN-MPCD the passive collision operator is

\displaystyle{\underline{\Xi}}^{\text{P}}_{i,c}

\displaystyle={\underline{v}^{\text{ran}}_{i}}-\left\langle{\underline{v}^{\text{ran}}_{i}}\right\rangle_{c}+\left({\underline{\underline{I}}^{-1}_{c}}\cdot\left(\delta\underline{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{vel}}+\delta\underline{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{ori}}\right)\right)\times\underline{r}_{i,c},

(17)

where the moment of inertia tensor
\underline{\underline{I}}_{c}
is for the particles in cell
c
.

Activity is introduced as a force dipole that locally injects energy at the scale of one MPCD cell without affecting momentum, applied by the active component of the collision operator

\displaystyle{\underline{\Xi}}^{\text{A}}_{i,c}

\displaystyle=\zeta_{c}\Delta t\left(\frac{\kappa_{i}}{m_{i}}-\frac{\left\langle\kappa_{i}\right\rangle_{c}}{\left\langle m_{i}\right\rangle_{c}}\right){\underline{n}}_{c},

(18)

where
\zeta_{c}
sets the strength of cellular force dipole
[
109
]
.
To determine the direction of the active force acting on particle
i
, cell
C
is divided by a plane passing through its center of mass, with a normal vector
{\underline{n}}_{c}
.
Depending on whether
{\underline{r}}_{i}
lies above or below this plane, the value of
\kappa_{i}({\underline{r}}_{i},{\underline{n}}_{c})
is assigned as
\pm 1
, indicating the direction of the active force.
The first term in
Eq.
18
represents individual impulses per unit mass on particle
i
, and the second term removes any residual impulse to locally conserve momentum.

The strength of the active dipole in cell
c
can be assigned in various ways, depending on the physics of interest.
For instance, if all
N_{c}
fluid particles within a cell are treated as active units,
\zeta_{c}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{c}}\zeta_{j}
.
In this scheme there is a positive feedback between activity and density that can exacerbate density inhomogeneities
[
114
]
.
Since, density inhomogeneities affects the dynamics of inclusions
[
115
]
, we have chosen a modulated strength to mitigate activity-induced density fluctuations
[
114
]
.
This modulated activity is given by

\zeta_{c}=S_{c}(N_{c};\sigma_{p},\sigma_{w})\sum_{j=1}^{N_{c}}\zeta_{j},

(19)

where
S_{c}
is a sigmoidal function expressed as

S_{c}(N_{c};\sigma_{p},\sigma_{w})=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\tanh\left(\frac{N_{c}-\left\langle N_{c}\right\rangle\left(1+\sigma_{p}\right)}{\left\langle N_{c}\right\rangle\sigma_{w}}\right)\right).

(20)

This function compares a cell instantaneous number density
N_{c}(t)
to the system-wide average
\left\langle N_{c}\right\rangle
and returns a value between 0 and 1.
The parameters
\sigma_{p}
and
\sigma_{w}
control the position and width of the sigmoid drop.
Specifically,
\sigma_{p}
determines the position of the sigmoid midpoint, with
\sigma_{p}=0
placing the midpoint at
\left\langle N_{c}\right\rangle
, while
\sigma_{p}>0

(\sigma_{p}<0)
shifts the midpoint to higher (lower) densities.

(2) Orientation fluctuations.

Orientation of particle
i
updates as
{\underline{u}}_{i}\left(t+\Delta t\right)=\Psi_{c}
, where
\Psi_{c}
is the nematic collision operator acting on particles in cell
c
.
The collision operator acts as a rotation, modifying the orientation of each AN-MPCD particle during the time step
\Delta t
.
The reorientation process involves (i) a
stochastic
contribution
\left(\delta{\underline{u}}_{i}^{ST}/\delta t\right)
and (ii) a
flow-induced
contribution
\left(\delta{\underline{u}}_{i}^{J}/\delta t\right)
.

The
stochastic
orientations are drawn from the canonical distribution of the Maier-Saupe mean-field approximation
f_{\text{ori}}\left(\underline{u}_{i}\right)=f_{0}\exp\left(Us_{c}\left({\underline{u}}_{i}\cdot{\underline{n}}_{c}\right)^{2}/k_{\text{B}}T\right)
, centered around
{\underline{n}}_{c}
, with normalisation constant
f_{0}
and mean field interaction constant
U

[
110
]
.
The local director
{\underline{n}}_{c}
is the eigenvector of the cell’s tensor order parameter
\underline{\underline{Q}}_{c}\left(t\right)=\frac{1}{d-1}\left\langle d{\underline{u}}_{i}\left(t\right){\underline{u}}_{i}\left(t\right)-\underline{\underline{1}}\right\rangle_{c}
, where
\underline{\underline{1}}
is the identity tensor in
d
dimensions.
This eigenvector is associated with the largest eigenvalue
s_{c}
, which is the scalar order parameter.
The global nematic interaction constant
U
governs the alignment strength between the MPCD nematogens.

The
flow-induced
reorientations arise from the response of MPCD nematogens to velocity gradients.
These orientation changes are captured through Jefferey’s equation

\displaystyle\frac{\delta{{\underline{u}}_{i}}^{J}}{\delta t}

\displaystyle=\alpha\left[{\underline{u}}_{i}\cdot\underline{\underline{\Omega}}_{c}+\lambda\left({\underline{u}}_{i}\cdot\underline{\underline{E}}_{c}-{\underline{u}}_{i}{\underline{u}}_{i}{\underline{u}}_{i}\colon\underline{\underline{E}}_{c}\right)\right],

(21)

for a bare tumbling parameter
\lambda
and hydrodynamic susceptibility
\alpha
in a flow with strain rate tensor
\underline{\underline{E}}_{c}\left(t\right)=\left(\nabla{\underline{v}}_{c}+\left(\nabla{\underline{v}}_{c}\right)^{T}\right)/2
and rotation rate tensor
\underline{\underline{\Omega}}_{c}\left(t\right)=\left(\nabla{\underline{v}}_{c}-\left(\nabla{\underline{v}}_{c}\right)^{T}\right)/2
.
The rotations of nematogens generate hydrodynamic motion, known as backflow, which is accounted for by the change in angular momentum

\delta\underline{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{ori}}=-\delta t\left(\gamma_{R}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{c}(t)}{\underline{u}}_{i}\left(t\right)\times{\underline{\dot{u}}}_{i}\right)
, where
\gamma_{R}
is the rotational friction coefficient and
{\underline{\dot{u}}}_{i}=\left(\delta{\underline{u}}_{i}^{ST}/\delta t\right)+\left(\delta{\underline{u}}_{i}^{J}/\delta t\right)
.

A.2
Molecular Dynamics

The flexible polymer is simulated using a molecular dynamics (MD) technique
[
116
]
, consisting of
N
beads with mass
M
, interacting through bond and excluded volume potentials.
Each monomer
j
obey the equation of motion

\displaystyle M{\ddot{\underline{r}}}_{j}=-\nabla V_{j}+{\underline{\Xi}}_{j,c},

(22)

in which
V_{j}
is the total potential of the particle
j
and
{\underline{\Xi}}_{j,c}

represents the momentum exchange between MD beads and fluid particles.
This term accounts for thermal, active, and hydrodynamic drag forces by including MD particle
j
in the MPCD collision of cell
c
.
The beads are connected by a finitely extensible nonlinear elastic (FENE) bond potential
[
117
,
118
,
119
]
:

\displaystyle V_{\text{FENE}}(r_{jl})=-\frac{k_{\text{FENE}}}{2}{r_{0}}^{2}\ln\left(1-\frac{r_{jl}^{2}}{{r_{0}}^{2}}\right),

(23)

where
k_{\text{FENE}}
is the bond strength,
r_{0}
the maximum bond length and
r_{jl}=\left|\underline{r}_{j}-\underline{r}_{l}\right|
the distance between monomers
j
and
l
, with
l=j-1
for bonded pairs.
Excluded volume effects are modeled using a purely repulsive variant of the Lennard-Jones potential, known as the Weeks-Chandler-Andersen (WCA) potential
[
120
,
121
]

V_{\text{WCA}}(r_{jl})=4\epsilon\begin{cases}\left(\frac{\sigma}{r_{jl}}\right)^{12}-\left(\frac{\sigma}{r_{jl}}\right)^{6}+\frac{1}{4},&r_{jl}<\sigma_{\text{co}}\\
0,&r_{jl}\geq\sigma_{\text{co}},\end{cases}

(24)

where
\epsilon
controls the strength of the repulsive potential and
\sigma
represents the effective size of a bead.
The potential smoothly approaches zero when the distance between beads
r_{jl}
exceeds the cutoff distance
\sigma_{\text{co}}=2^{1/6}\sigma
.
We employ the velocity-Verlet algorithm to numerically integrate
Eq.
22
.

A.4
Mean-Squared Displacement

To understand the MSD of the polymers in an active nematic, we can treat the center of mass
\underline{r}=N^{-1}\sum_{i}^{N}\underline{r}_{i}
as a persistent random walker with a passive translational diffusion coefficient
D_{0}=R_{g}^{2}/\tau_{\text{r}}
, constant instantaneous speed
v
and a persistence time
\tau_{\text{p}}
for the direction of motion
\hat{e}
.
The equations of motion are

\displaystyle\frac{d\underline{r}}{dt}

\displaystyle=v\underline{\hat{e}}+\sqrt{2D_{0}}\underline{\xi}_{\text{r}}

(25a)

\displaystyle\frac{d\underline{\hat{e}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\tau_{\text{p}}^{-1}}\underline{\xi}_{\underline{\hat{e}}}\times\underline{\hat{e}},

(25b)

where noises are white with zero mean
\left\langle\underline{\xi}_{i}(t)\right\rangle=0
and unit variance
\left\langle\underline{\xi}_{i}(t)\ \underline{\xi}_{i}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\underline{\underline{\delta}}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
for both the translational and orientational dynamics,
i\in\left\{\text{r},\underline{\hat{e}}\right\}
.

In the passive limit, the persistence time and the speed go to zero, such that the dynamics are entirely determined by the translational diffusion coefficient.
When activity is nonzero, the motion is modeled by Langevin equations for the center of mass position and orientation, and the MSD is
[
122
]

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta\underline{r}^{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2dD_{0}t+2v^{2}\tau_{\text{p}}t-2v^{2}\tau_{\text{p}}^{2}\left[1-e^{-t/\tau_{\text{p}}}\right].

(26)

The MSD of center-of-mass of polymers agrees well with this model (
Fig.
2
c; dashed line).
By fitting our simulation data for MSD to this model, we extract the short time speed of the center of mass of the polymers
v
and the persistence time
\tau_{\text{p}}
(
Fig.
2
d,e).

A.5

The scaling arguments presented in the main text rely on characteristic scaling in active turbulence.
In particular, active turbulence has a characteristic length scale that arises due to the interplay of elasticity and activity, as
l_{a}\sim\sqrt{K/\zeta}\sim\zeta^{-1/2}

The expected scaling is reproduced in 2D AN-MPCD simulations, which reproduce a power law dependence of

\tau_{a}\sim\eta/\zeta\sim\zeta^{-1}
.
The AN-MPCD simulations agree with this expectation, reproducing a scaling of
\tau_{a}\sim\zeta^{-1.02\pm 0.04}
(
Fig.
4

Appendix B
Passive polymer properties

In the dilute limit, the gyration radius
R_{g}
and the diffusion coefficient of the polymer’s center of mass
D_{0}
, as functions of the number of monomers
N
, are expected to follow the scaling relations

\displaystyle R_{g}

\displaystyle\sim N^{\nu},

(27)

with scaling exponent
\nu
.
Without hydrodynamic interactions and excluded volume effect, the simple Rouse model gives
\nu=1/2
.
For dilute systems in 3D, excluded volume effects result in
\nu\approx 3/5
.
In the presence of excluded volume effect in 2D,
\nu=3/4
.
The scaling of the gyration radius for passive polymers in our simulations is
\nu=0.77\pm 0.01
, in close agreement with the expectation value of
3/4
(
Fig.
5
).

While intuition from 3D systems may lead us to expect the thermal diffusion to scale as a power law with the polymer length, extensive computational finite-size scaling studies have demonstrated that, in 2D, the diffusion coefficient follows the form
D_{0}\sim\ln{\left(l_{\text{sys}}/R_{g}\right)}\sim\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{N}
, where
l_{\text{sys}}
is the linear size of the system
[
74
,
75
]
.
Our simulations results exhibit good concordance with the logarithmic form of
the passive diffusion coefficient, with the expected logarithmic behavior falling within the standard deviation of these measurements except for
N=200
(
Fig.
6
).
