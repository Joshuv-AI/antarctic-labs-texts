Asymptotic structure. I. Coarse tree-width
Tung Nguyen, Alex Scott, Paul Seymour

Abstract
In this paper, we develop a coarse analogue of tree-width. We prove that a graph G G admits a tree-decomposition in which each bag is contained in the union of a bounded number of balls of bounded radius, if and only if G G admits a quasi-isometry to a graph with bounded tree-width. (The “if” half is easy, but the “only if” half is challenging.) This generalizes a recent result of Berger and Seymour, concerning tree-decompositions when each bag has bounded radius. We also prove a similar result for line-width, which is an extension of path-width to infinite graphs.

Introduction

This is the first in a sequence of papers on asymptotic structure in graphs, looking at properties of graphs that are preserved under
quasi-isometry. The notion of quasi-isometry is well established in geometric group theory: following work of Gromov
(see
[
15
,
16
]
), the large-scale geometric structure of a
group can be investigated by looking at properties of its
Cayley graphs that are invariant under quasi-isometry. More recently, the emerging area of “coarse graph theory” has begun to
look at large-scale geometry of graphs in general, using the lens of quasi-isometry. It has begun to emerge that many classical
notions from graph theory have counterparts in the coarse context: for example, a coarse analogue for graph minors is provided by
“fat minors” (see
[
3
,
5
]
); and there are many questions about which graph-theoretic results have natural counterparts
in the coarse context
[
14
]
. In this paper, we investigate a coarse analogue of tree-width that was introduced in
[
12
]
, and connect it via quasi-isometry to the
standard notion of tree-width. Our results also hold for path-width; however, more can be said about path-width, and we will pursue
this further in
[
18
,
19
]
.

We need to begin with some definitions.
Graphs in this paper may be infinite.
If

A path-decomposition is essentially a sequence of sets of vertices satisfying the “betweenness” condition. There is a more
general notion (see
[

,
9
,

,
18
,
19

We will show in the second half of the paper that

works with line-width in place of path-width. The proof uses the same basic approach
as that for

, but is significantly different in the details.

How sharp is our bound on tree-width in
1.1
?
In

, we start with a tree-decomposition in which each bag is the

k

Obviously not for

On the other hand, for all

, if

is large enough then there is no

-quasi-isometry from

to a graph
with tree-width at most

(take a

Again,

has a sort of converse, because if

-quasi-isometry to a graph with tree-width at most

is the union of at most

that admits a quasi-isometry to a graph with tree-width at most

,
and apply this converse, we obtain a tree-decomposition in which each bag is a union of

A way to get rid of it is to make a small tweak in the definition of tree-decomposition;

For all

, there exist

such that if

admits a tree-decomposition

The proof of
1.5

Let us state the definition of pseudo-tree-width more formally.
A
pseudo-tree-decomposition
of a graph

is a pair
(T,(B_{t}:t\in V(T)))
, where
T
is a tree, and
B_{t}

is a subset of

for each

(called a

•

is the union of the sets

•

with

, or there is an edge

•

, if

lies on the path of

between

, then

.

The

of a pseudo-tree-decomposition
(T,(B_{t}:t\in V(T)))
is the maximum of the numbers

for

,

If

, and the

of

is defined analogously.
When

is a finite path, we sometimes use the notation

(with the usual meaning) in place of

Before we prove the main part of

2.1

-quasi-isometry to a graph with pseudo-tree-width at most

, and let

. Let

-quasi-isometry from a graph

.
For each

We claim that

•

•

•

For the second statement, let

, and choose

,

For the third statement, let

, such that

lies on the path of

between

, and

. Hence for

Since

Finally, for the fourth statement, let

. For each

, let

. Thus

, and there are

,

.

Let

, and suppose that there are

We proceed by induction on

. If some vertex

, then

We will recursively define a set of pairs, called “cores”.

its

Each core

with root

Here is the inductive definition.
If there exists

such that we have not yet defined the set of cores with birthday
t
,
choose some such

with minimum height.
We say

is

if there is a core
(s,C)
such that
s<_{T}t
, and
t\in S(s,C)
,
and either

or

. Let

For each

.
For each core

is the set of all

•

;

•

•

This completes the inductive definition of the set of all cores.
It is easy to check that we could replace the third bullet above with the equivalent condition:

•

for every core

such that

We remark that if

, but if

, or even

. Here are a few observations about spreads that may aid the reader’s intuition:

•

•

If

•

For each core
(t,C)
, its spread is a subset of

\{t^{\prime}\in V(T):t\leq_{T}t^{\prime}\text{ and }C\cap B_{t^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset\}

(which is itelf the vertex

is the intersection of this set and all previously-defined spreads
that contain

Two subsets

v_{1}v_{2}\in B_{s}
, since

v_{1}v_{2}
is an edge; and by choosing
s
of minimum height we may assume that
s
is the source of one of
v_{1},v_{2}
, say
v_{2}
,
and so
s=t_{2}^{\prime}
, and
t_{1}^{\prime}\leq_{T}t_{2}^{\prime}
, and
t_{1}\leq_{T}t_{1}^{\prime}
. Since
t_{2}^{\prime}\in S(t_{2},C_{2})
and

t_{1}\notin S(t_{2},C_{2})
, it follows that
t_{1}<_{T}t_{2}
; and so
t_{1}^{\prime}<_{T}t_{2}
, since
t_{1}^{\prime}\in S(t_{1},C_{1})
and
t_{2}\notin S(t_{1},C_{1})
.
We therefore have
t_{1}\leq_{T}t_{1}^{\prime}<_{T}t_{2}\leq_{T}t_{2}^{\prime}
.

Since
t_{2}
is in the path of
T
between
t_{1}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime}
, and
v_{1}\in B_{t_{1}^{\prime}}\cap B_{t_{2}^{\prime}}
, it follows that
v_{1}\in B_{t_{2}}
.
By (4), there is a core
(d,D)
such that
t_{2}\in S(d,D)
and either
v_{1}\in D
, or
(d,D)\in\phi(v_{1})
. If
v_{1}\in D
,
then
\psi(v_{1})
is joined to
(d,d,D)
by a path of
J
with at most
k+1
red edges, by (7); and if
(d,D)\in\phi(v_{1})
,
then
\psi(v_{1})
is equal or adjacent (by a red edge) to
(d,d,D)
. In either case,
(d,d,D)
is joined to
(t_{2},d,D)

by a green path; and
(t_{2},d,D)
is adjacent to
(t_{2},t_{2},C_{2})
via a red edge, since
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(C_{2},D)\leq 2

(because
v_{2}
belongs to or has a neighbour in both). This proves (8).

(9)
For each core
(t,C)
,

has diameter at most
2k-1
.

By hypothesis,
\alpha(B_{t})\leq k
, and since
C\subseteq B_{t}
it follows that

G[C]
has no induced path with
2k+1
vertices. Since
G[C]
is connected,
it has diameter at most
2k-1
. This proves (9).

(10)
If
(s_{1},t_{1},C_{1})
and
(s_{2},t_{2},C_{2})
are joined by a green path of
J
, and
v_{1}\in C_{1}
and
v_{2}\in C_{2}
,
then
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(v_{1},v_{2})\leq 2k-1
.

Any two vertices of
J
joined by a green edge have the same second and third coordinates, and so
t_{1}=t_{2}
and
C_{1}=C_{2}
.
Consequently
v_{1},v_{2}\in C_{1}
, and the result follows from (9). This proves (10).

(11)
Let
v_{1},v_{2}\in V(G)
, and suppose
P
is a path of
J
between
\psi(v_{1}),\psi(v_{2})
containing at most
n
red
edges. Then
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(v_{1},v_{2})\leq(2k+2)n+2k-1
.

If
n=0
the result follows from (10), so we assume that
n\geq 1
.
Let
P
have ends
b_{0}
and
a_{n+1}
, and let the red edges of
P
be
a_{1}b_{1},a_{2}b_{2},\ldots,a_{n}b_{n}
in order, numbered such that
there there is a green subpath of
P
between
b_{i},a_{i+1}
for
0\leq i\leq n
. For
1\leq i\leq n
, define
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in V(G)

as follows: let
a_{i}=(s,t,C)
and
b_{i}=(s^{\prime},t^{\prime},C^{\prime})
say; choose
\alpha_{i}\in C
and
\beta_{i}\in C^{\prime}
with distance at most three
in
G
. (This is possible from the definition
of red edges.) Let
\beta_{0}=v_{1}
and
\alpha_{n+1}=v_{2}
. Thus
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})\leq 3
for
1\leq i\leq n
;
and
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(\beta_{i},\alpha_{i+1})\leq 2k-1
by (10). Consequently
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(v_{1},v_{2})\leq(2k+2)n+2k-1
.

(12)
For each
j\in J
, there exists
v\in V(G)
such that there is a path of
J
between
j
and
\psi(v)
using at most
k+1
red edges.

Let
j=(s,t,C)
, and choose
v\in C\cap B_{s}
. There is a green path between
j
and
(t,t,C)
; and by (7), since
v\in C\subseteq B_{t}
,
there is a path between
(t,t,C)
and
\psi(v)
containing at most
k+1
red edges. This proves (12).

Let
H
be obtained from

by contracting all green edges. Thus each vertex of

is formed by identifying
all the vertices
(s,t,C)
for a fixed core
(t,C)
, and so we can identify

with the set of all cores in the natural way.
From (6), and since contraction does not increase pseudo-tree-width,

has pseudo-tree-width at most
k
, and from (8), (11), (12),
the function
\psi
is a
(2k+2,2k-1)
-quasi-isometry
from

to

. This proves
2.3
and hence (with

) proves
1.5
.

3
Coarse line-width

In this section and the next we will prove a version of
1.2
for line-width, but first let us give the definitions more carefully.
A
line
is a set
L
that is linearly ordered by a relation
\leq_{L}
. A
line-decomposition
of a graph
G

is a pair

(L,(B_{t}:t\in L))
where
L
is a line, and each
B_{t}
is a subset of
V(G)
, satisfying:

•

G=\bigcup_{t\in L}G[B_{t}]
, and

•

B_{t}\cap B_{t^{\prime\prime}}\subseteq B_{t^{\prime}}
for all
t,t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}\in L
with
t\leq_{L}t^{\prime}\leq_{L}t^{\prime\prime}
.

We define the
width
of such a decomposition to be the maximum of
|B_{t}|-1
over all
t\in L
, if this maximum exists, and
\infty
otherwise; and the
line-width
of
G
is the minimum width of a line-decomposition.
(This definition was used in
[
18
,
19
,
6
]
, to increase the generality of some results about graphs with bounded path-width.
It is also used in
[
9
,
11
]
, where what we call “line-decompositions” are called “linear decompositions”.)
We will prove the following analogue of
1.2
:

3.1

For all
k,r
, there exist
L,C\geq 1
such that if
G
admits a line-decomposition
(L,(B_{t}:t\in L))
such that for each
t\in L
,

B_{t}
is the union of at most
k
sets each with diameter at most
r
in
G
, then

G
admits an
(L,C)
-quasi-isometry to a graph with line-width at most
k
.

This was first proved by Hickingbotham
[
17
]
, with weaker constants. (Actually, he proved it at our request, because at that time
we needed the result for an application, and did not know how to use the approach of
1.2
to handle line-width.)
We will deduce
3.1
as a consequence of the next result, but first we need some definitions.
If
L
is a line, an
interval
of
L
is a nonempty subset
I\subseteq L
such that if
r,s,t\in L
with
r\leq_{L}s\leq_{L}t
, and
r,t\in I
,
then
s\in I
.
An interval
I
is
initial
if
I\neq L
and
for all
s,t\in L
with
s\leq_{L}t
, if
t\in I
then
s\in I
. We say
I
is a
final
interval if
L\setminus I
is an initial interval.
(If
I
is an initial interval then
I,L\setminus I
are
both nonempty, and so
L\setminus I
is an initial interval of the line obtained from
L
by reversing its order.)
If
I,J
are intervals of
L
, we say
J

abuts

I
and
\{I,J\}
is an
abutment
if
I\cap J=\emptyset
and
I\cup J

is an interval of
L
.

If
(L,(B_{t}:t\in L))
is a line-decomposition of
G
, and
I
is an interval, we define

B(I)=\bigcup_{t\in I}B_{t}.

If
I
is an initial interval, then
B(I)\cap B(L\setminus I)

is called the
I
-split
; every path between
B(I),B(L\setminus I)
has a vertex in this set.
We say
X
is a
split
if
X
is the
I
-split for some initial interval
I
.

Let
(L,(B_{t}:t\in L))
be a line-decomposition of a graph
G
. A vertex
v\in V(G)
is a
left-limit vertex
of

(L,(B_{t}:t\in L))
if for all
s,t\in L
with
s\leq_{L}t
, either
v\in B_{s}
or
v\notin B_{t}
; and a
right-limit vertex
if for
all
s,t\in L
with
s\leq_{L}t
, either
v\notin B_{s}
or
v\in B_{t}
. We say
v
is a
limit vertex
if it is either a left- or right-limit vertex.

Given a line-decomposition
(L,(B_{t}:t\in L))
of a graph
G
such that each bag is the union of
k
sets with bounded diameter, our
goal is to construct a graph
H
with line-width at most
k
and a quasi-isometry
\phi
from
G
to
H
. To do this, we will make use of the line-decomposition of
G
as a
“scaffold” to construct
H
, building
H
with a line-decomposition
(L,(C_{t}:t\in L))
, and a mapping
\phi
that is suitably compatible with the line-decomposition of
G
.

How do we ensure this?
For each
vertex
v\in V(G)
, let
I_{v}=\{t:v\in B_{t}\}
, and for each
w\in V(H)
let
J_{w}=\{t:w\in C_{t}\}
. If we could arrange that
I_{v}\subseteq J_{\phi(v)}
for every
vertex of
G
, then for every edge
uv
of
G
we would know that some bag in the line-decomposition of
H
contains both
\phi(u),\phi(v)
, and so we could make

\phi(u),\phi(v)
adjacent in
H
if we wanted. For
\phi
to be a quasi-isometry, we also need that the set of vertices mapping to any given
w\in V(H)
has
small diameter in
G
; and (to satisfy the last condition in the definition of a quasi-isometry) it will be convenient to demand that
\phi
is surjective.
However, it is not straightforward to construct such an
H
; and we will also want to carry out an induction that divides up
L
into a sequence of intervals
and then glues together decompositions of these intervals. So we adjust the conditions slightly. First, we will map each vertex
v
of
G
to either one or two vertices of
H
; and we demand that
I_{v}
is contained in the union of the ione or two corresponding intervals
J_{w}
. Second, we will be more restrictive when
handling limit vertices: these are only allowed to map to a single vertex of
H
(which means that they will not end up mapping to many intervals at the gluing stage). We capture these requirements with the notion of a striation.

Let
(L,(B_{t}:t\in L))
be a line-decomposition of a graph
G
.
For each
v\in V(G)
let
I_{v}=\{t\in L:v\in B_{t}\}
.
If
N
is a set,
N^{(2)}
denotes the set of one- and two-element subsets of
N
.
A
striation
for
(L,(B_{t}:t\in L))
is a
pair
(\sigma,(J_{n}:n\in N))
, where

(J_{n}:n\in N)
is a family of intervals of
L
, and
\sigma
is a map from
V(G)
to
N^{(2)}
, satisfying:

•

for each
v\in V(G)
,
I_{v}\subseteq\bigcup_{n\in\sigma(v)}J_{n}
; and moreover, if
m,n\in\sigma(v)
are distinct, then
v
is not a limit vertex of
(L,(B_{t}:t\in L))
, and

\{J_{m},J_{n}\}
is an abutment, and
I_{v}\cap J_{m},I_{v}\cap J_{n}\neq\emptyset
;

•

for each
n\in N
and each
t\in J_{n}
, there exists
v\in V(G)
such that
v\in B_{t}
and
n\in\sigma(v)
(in other words,

J_{n}\subseteq\bigcup_{\sigma(v)\ni n}I_{v}
); and

•

for each
n\in N
, the set
\{v\in V(G):n\in\sigma(v)\}
has diameter at most three.

If
B\subseteq V(G)
is a union of finitely many cliques of
G
, then we write
\kappa(B)
for the minimum number of cliques in such a union (that is, the chromatic number of the complement of
G[B]
). We say that
a collection of

\kappa(B)
cliques with union
B
is a
clique min-cover
of
B
.
We will prove:

3.2

Let
(L,(B_{t}:t\in L))
be a line-decomposition of a graph
G
, and let
k\geq 1
such that
\kappa(B_{t})\leq k
for each
t\in L
.
Then there is a striation
(\sigma,(J_{n}:n\in N))
for

(L,(B_{t}:t\in L))
, such that
|\{n\in N:t\in J_{n}\}|\leq\kappa(B_{t})
for each
t\in L
.

Proof.
We proceed by induction on
k
. Thus, either
k=1
or we may assume the result holds for
k-1
.

(1)
We may assume that
G
is connected.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the set of components of
G
.
For each
C\in\mathcal{C}
,
(L,(B_{t}\cap V(C):t\in L))
is a line-decomposition of
C
of finite width. Suppose that for
each
C
, there is a striation
(\sigma^{C},(J_{n}:n\in N^{C}))
for

(L,(B_{t}\cap V(C):t\in L))
, such that for each
t\in T
, the number of
n\in N^{C}
such that

|\{n\in N^{C}:t\in J_{n}^{C}\}|\leq\kappa(B_{t}\cap V(C))
for each

t\in J_{n}^{C}
. We may assume that the sets
N^{C}\;(C\in\mathcal{C})
are pairwise
disjoint. Let
N
be their union.
For each
v\in V(G)
, define
\sigma(v)=\sigma^{C}(v)
where
v\in V(C)
. Then
(\sigma,(J_{n}:n\in N))
is a
striation for
(L,(B_{t}:t\in L))
. Moreover, let
t\in L
. Then for each
t\in L
,

|\{n\in N:t\in J_{n}\}|=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}|\{n\in N^{C}:t\in J_{n}^{C}\}|\leq\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\kappa(B_{t}\cap V(C))=\kappa(B_{t}),

since each clique of a clique min-cover of
B
is a subset of
B_{t}\cap V(C)
for a unique
C\in\mathcal{C}
.
Consequently, if the theorem holds for each component of
G
then it holds for
G
.
This proves (1).

Since
G
is connected, it follows that if
t\in L
and
B_{t}=\emptyset
, then either
B_{s}=\emptyset
for all
s\in L
with
s\leq_{L}t
,
or
B_{s}=\emptyset
for all
s\in L
with
t\leq_{L}s
. Consequently the set
L^{\prime}
of
t\in L
such that
B_{t}\neq\emptyset
is an interval
of
L
, and
(L^{\prime},(B_{t}:t\in L^{\prime}))
is a line-decomposition of
G
; and if the result holds for
(L^{\prime},(B_{t}:t\in L^{\prime}))
then it holds for

(L,(B_{t}:t\in L))
. We may therefore assume that
B_{t}\neq\emptyset
for each
t\in L
.

(2)
Let
L^{\prime}
be an interval of
L
, and let
U=\bigcup_{t\in L^{\prime}}B_{t}
. Suppose that for each
t\in L^{\prime}
, some vertex in
B_{t}

is a left-limit vertex of
(L^{\prime},(B_{t}:t\in L^{\prime}))
. Then there is a striation
(\sigma,(J_{n}:n\in N))
for

(L^{\prime},(B_{t}:t\in L^{\prime}))
, such that
|\{n\in N:t\in J_{n}\}|\leq\kappa(B_{t})

for each
t\in L^{\prime}
.

Let
S
be the set of left-limit vertices of
(L^{\prime},(B_{t}:t\in L^{\prime}))
. For every finite subset
P
of
S
, there exists
t\in L^{\prime}

with
P\subseteq B_{t}
, and consequently
\kappa(P)\leq k
for each finite subset
P
of
S
. By compactness (or more exactly, by a theorem of De Bruijn and Erdős
[
8
]
applied to the complement of
G[S]
),
\kappa(S)\leq k
.
Fix some clique min-cover
\mathcal{S}
of
S
, and choose
X\in\mathcal{S}
with

\{t\in L^{\prime}:X\cap B_{t}\neq\emptyset\}

maximal. Thus
X
is a clique.

We claim that
X\cap B_{t}\neq\emptyset
for each
t\in L^{\prime}
.
For each
v\in U
, let
I^{\prime}_{v}
be the set of all
t\in L^{\prime}
with
v\in B_{t}
. For each
s\in S
, since
s

is a left-limit vertex of
(L^{\prime},(B_{t}:t\in L^{\prime}))
, the set
I_{s}^{\prime}

is either null, or equal to
L^{\prime}
, or an initial interval of
L^{\prime}
.
Suppose that
X\cap B_{t}=\emptyset
for some
t\in L
. Then for each
s\in X
,
I^{\prime}_{s}
is either an initial interval of
L^{\prime}
not containing
t
, or null.
Let
I^{\prime}_{X}=\bigcup_{s\in X}I^{\prime}_{s}
. It follows that
I^{\prime}_{X}
is also either an initial interval of
L^{\prime}
not containing
t
, or null. Choose
s\in S
with
s\in B_{t}
. Thus
I^{\prime}_{s}
is either an initial interval of
L^{\prime}
containing
t
, or equals
L^{\prime}
,
and in either case properly contains
I^{\prime}_{X}
. But
s
belongs to a member of
\mathcal{S}
, contrary to the choice of
X
.
This proves that
X\cap B_{t}\neq\emptyset
for each
t\in L^{\prime}
.

Let
Y
be the set of vertices in
U\setminus X
with no neighbour in
X
.
Then
(L^{\prime},(B_{t}\cap Y:t\in L^{\prime}))

is a line-decomposition of
G[Y]
. Moreover, for each
t\in L^{\prime}
, since
X\cap B_{t}\neq\emptyset
,
it follows that
\kappa(Y\cap B_{t})\leq k-1
. If
k=1

then
Y=\emptyset
, and we may define
N=\{1\}
say, and

J_{1}=L^{\prime}
,
and define

\sigma(v)=1
for each
v\in U
, to satisfy (2). Thus we may assume that
k\geq 2
. From the inductive hypothesis,
there is a striation
(\sigma^{\prime},(J_{n}^{\prime}:n\in N^{\prime}))
for

(L^{\prime},(B_{t}\cap Y:t\in L^{\prime}))
, such that
|\{n\in N^{\prime}:t\in J_{n}^{\prime}\}|\leq\kappa(B_{t}\cap Y)
for each
t\in L^{\prime}
.
We may assume that
1\notin N^{\prime}
; let
N=N^{\prime}\cup\{1\}
. For each
n\in N
, define
J_{n}=J^{\prime}_{n}

if
n\in N^{\prime}
, and
J_{1}=L^{\prime}
. For each
v\in U
, define
\sigma(v)=\sigma^{\prime}(v)

if
v\in Y
, and
\sigma(v)=\{1\}
if
v\notin Y
. We claim that

(\sigma,(J_{n}:n\in N))
satisfies (2). To check this, we must show that:

•

for each
v\in U
,
I_{v}^{\prime}\subseteq\bigcup_{n\in\sigma(v)}J_{n}
; and moreover, if
m,n\in\sigma(v)
are distinct, then

v
is not a limit vertex of
(L^{\prime},(B_{t}:t\in L^{\prime}))
, and

\{J_{m},J_{n}\}
is an abutment, and
I_{v}^{\prime}\cap J_{m},I_{v}^{\prime}\cap J_{n}\neq\emptyset
;

•

for each
n\in N
and each
t\in J_{n}
, there exists
v\in U
such that
v\in B_{t}
and
n\in\sigma(v)
(in other words,

J_{n}\subseteq\bigcup_{\sigma(v)\ni n}I_{v}^{\prime}
);

•

for each
n\in N
, the set
\{v\in U:n\in\sigma(v)\}
has diameter at most three; and

•

for each
t\in L^{\prime}
,
|\{n\in N:t\in J_{n}\}|\leq\kappa(B_{t}\cap U)
.

For the first, let
v\in U
. If
v\notin Y
, then
\bigcup_{n\in\sigma(v)}J_{n}=J_{1}=L^{\prime}
,
and therefore includes
I_{v}^{\prime}
.
If
v\in Y
,
then
\bigcup_{n\in\sigma(v)}J_{n}=\bigcup_{n\in\sigma^{\prime}(v)}J_{n}^{\prime}
,
and the latter contains
I_{v}^{\prime}
since

(\sigma^{\prime},(J_{n}^{\prime}:n\in N^{\prime}))
is a striation. Moreover, if
m,n\in\sigma(v)
are distinct, then
m,n\in\sigma^{\prime}(v)
, and so

v
is not a limit vertex of
(L^{\prime},(B_{t}\cap Y:t\in L^{\prime}))
, and
\{J_{m}^{\prime},J_{n}^{\prime}\}
is an abutment, and
I_{v}^{\prime}\cap J_{m}^{\prime},I_{v}^{\prime}\cap J_{n}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
; but then

v
is not a limit vertex of
(L^{\prime},(B_{t}:t\in L^{\prime}))
, and
\{J_{m},J_{n}\}
is an abutment.

To see the second, if
n=1
then
J_{n}=L^{\prime}
, and
n\in\sigma^{\prime}(v)
if and only if
v\in U\setminus Y
;
so we must show that
L^{\prime}
is
the union of the sets
I_{v}^{\prime}
over all
v\in U\setminus Y
. But for each
t\in L^{\prime}
,
X\cap B_{t}\neq\emptyset
,
and so there exists
v\in U\setminus Y
(indeed,
v\in X
) such that

, as required. If
n\neq 1
,
then
n\in N^{\prime}
, and
J_{n}=J^{\prime}_{n}
, and
v\in U
satisfies
n\in\sigma(v)
if and only if
v\in Y
and
n\in\sigma^{\prime}(v)
.
So we need to show that
J^{\prime}_{n}
is the union of the sets
I_{v}^{\prime}
where
v\in Y
satisfies

, which is true
since
(\sigma^{\prime},(J_{n}^{\prime}:n\in N^{\prime}))
is a striation.

To see the third, if

, and so
n_{1},n_{2}\in C_{t}
, as claimed. On the other hand, if there exists
v\in V(G)
with
n_{1},n_{2}\in\sigma(v)
, then
\sigma(v)=\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
, and
\{J_{n_{1}},J_{n_{2}}\}
is an abutment, as claimed.

Next, we claim that

•

|C_{t}|\leq k
for each
t\in L
; and

•

for every initial interval
I
of
L
, there are at most
k
edges
mn
of
H
such that

\{J_{m},J_{n}\}

is an abutment with border
I
.

To see the first statement, let
t\in L
. From the choice of the striation,
the number of
n\in N
such that
t\in J_{n}
is
at most
k
, and so
|C_{t}|\leq k
. For the second statement, let
I
be an initial interval of
L
and let
S
be the
I
-split.
Let
F
be the
set of edges
mn\in E(H)
such that
\{J_{m},J_{n}\}

is an abutment with border
I
. For each
mn\in F
, since
J_{m}\cap J_{n}=\emptyset
,
mn
is contained in
E(H)
because of the
second bullet in the definition of
H
; and so there is an initial interval
I^{\prime}
of
L
and a component
C
of the
I^{\prime}
-split
such that
\sigma(v)=\{m,n\}
where
v
is the
(I^{\prime},C)
-representative. Thus
I^{\prime}
is the border of the abutment
\{J_{m},J_{n}\}
.
On the other hand, since
mn\in F
, it follows that
I
is the border of
\{J_{m},J_{n}\}
, and so
I^{\prime}=I
. Since
G[S]

has at most
k
components, and there is at most one
(I,C)
-representative for each component
C
, it follows that
|F|\leq k
.
From
4.1
, this proves (1).

For each
v\in V(G)
, let
\phi(v)

be some member of
\sigma(v)
. To complete the proof, we will show that
\phi
is an
(7,3)
-quasi-isometry from
G
to
H
.

(2)
For each
v\in V(G)
, if
m,n\in\sigma(v)
then
\operatorname{dist}_{H}(m,n)\leq 3
. Consequently,
for each
n\in V(H)
, there exists
v\in V(G)
such that
\operatorname{dist}_{H}(\phi(v),n)\leq 3
.

Since
m,n\in\sigma(v)
and we may assume that
m\neq n
, it follows that
\{J_{m},J_{n}\}
is an abutment; let
I
be its border,
and let
S
be the
I
-split. Since
I_{v}\cap J_{m},I_{v}\cap J_{n}\neq\emptyset
, it follows that
v\in S
; let
C
be the component
of
S
containing
v
. Since
v\in V(C)
and
\{J_{m},J_{n}\}
is an abutment with border
I
, it follows that there is an

(I,C)
-representative
v^{\prime}
say. Thus
v,v^{\prime}
are equal or adjacent in
G
. Let
\sigma(v^{\prime})=\{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}\}
where
J_{m^{\prime}}\subseteq I

and
J_{n^{\prime}}\cap I=\emptyset
; so
m^{\prime},n^{\prime}
are adjacent in
H
. If
v^{\prime}=v
then
m^{\prime}=m
and
n^{\prime}=n
and
\operatorname{dist}_{H}(m,n)\leq 1

as required, so we assume that
v^{\prime}\neq v
. Since
v,v^{\prime}\in S
, there exists
t\in I
such that
v,v^{\prime}\in B_{t}
. But
I_{v}\subseteq J_{m}\cup J_{n}
,
and so
I_{v}\cap I\subseteq J_{m}
, and therefore
t\in I_{v}\cap J_{m}
. Similarly
t\in I_{v^{\prime}}\cap J_{m^{\prime}}
, and so

I_{v}\cap J_{m}\cap I_{v^{\prime}}\cap J_{m^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset,

and therefore
m,m^{\prime}
are equal or adjacent in
H
from the definition of
H
. Similarly
n,n^{\prime}
are equal or adjacent, and
so
\operatorname{dist}_{H}(m,n)\leq 3
. This proves the first statement of (2).

For the second statement, let
n\in V(H)
, and choose
v\in V(G)
with
n\in\sigma(v)
(this is possible from the definition of a striation). We may assume that

\phi(v)\neq n
, and so
\phi(v)=m
where
\sigma(v)=\{m,n\}
. Consequently
\operatorname{dist}_{H}(\phi(v),n)\leq 3
by the first statement.
This proves (2).

(3)
For all
u,v\in V(G)
,
\operatorname{dist}_{H}(\phi(u),\phi(v))\leq 7\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v)
.

First, let us assume that
u,v\in V(G)
are adjacent.
Since
(L,(B_{t}:t\in L))
is a line-decomposition, there exists
t\in L
with
u,v\in B_{t}
. Since

t\in I_{u}\subseteq\bigcup_{m\in\sigma(u)}J_{m}
, there exists
m\in\sigma(u)
with
t\in J_{m}
, and similarly there exists

n\in\sigma(v)
with
t\in J_{n}
.
Consequently
\operatorname{dist}_{H}(m,n)\leq 1
, since

I_{u}\cap J_{m}\cap I_{v}\cap J_{n}\neq\emptyset.

But by (2),
\operatorname{dist}_{H}(\phi(u),m)\leq 3
, and
\operatorname{dist}_{H}(\phi(v),n)\leq 3
, and so
\operatorname{dist}_{H}(\phi(u),\phi(v))\leq 7
.
Hence, for every choice of
u,v\in V(G)
, it follows that
\operatorname{dist}_{H}(\phi(u),\phi(v))\leq 7\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v)
, by summing what we just proved over the edges of a shortest path of
G
joining
u,v
. This proves (3).

(4)
For all
u,v\in V(G)
, and all
m\in\sigma(u)
and
n\in\sigma(v)
, if
m=n
then
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leq 3

and if
m,n
are adjacent in
H
, then
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leq 7
.

If
m=n
, then
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leq 3
, since
\{w\in V(G):n\in\sigma(w)\}
has diameter at most three from the definition of
a striation. Thus we assume that
m\neq n
.
There are two reasons why
m,n
might be adjacent in
H
: either

•

there are adjacent
v_{1},v_{2}\in V(G)
such that
m\in\sigma(v_{1})
,
n\in\sigma(v_{2})
, and there exists

t\in I_{v_{1}}\cap J_{m}\cap I_{v_{2}}\cap J_{n},

or

•

there is an initial interval
I
of
L
and a component
C
of the
I
-split, and
w\in C
such that
w
is the
(I,C)
-representative and

\sigma(w)=\{m,n\}
where
J_{m}\subseteq I
and
J_{n}\cap I=\emptyset
.

In the first case, since
m\in\sigma(u)\cap\sigma(v_{1})
, it follows that
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v_{1})\leq 3
, and similarly
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(v,v_{2})\leq 3
,
and so
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leq 7
. In the second case, since
m\in\sigma(u)\cap\sigma(w)
it follows that
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,w)\leq 3
, and
similarly
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(v,w)\leq 3
, and so
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leq 6
. This proves (4).

(5)
For all
u,v\in V(G)
,
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leq 7\operatorname{dist}_{H}(\phi(u),\phi(v))+3
.

Let
d=\operatorname{dist}_{H}(\phi(u),\phi(v))
. As before, we may assume that
d\geq 1
.
Let
P
be a shortest path of
H
between
\phi(u),\phi(v)
, and let its vertices be
n_{0},\ldots,n_{d}
in order, where
n_{0}=\phi(u)

and
n_{d}=\phi(v)
.
For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
, choose
v_{i}\in V(G)
such that
n_{i}\in\sigma(v_{i})
; and define

v_{0}=u
and
v_{d}=v
. Hence, by (4), for
1\leq i\leq d
,
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(v_{i-1},v_{i})\leq 7
, and so
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leq 7d
.
This proves (5).

From (2), (3) and (5), it follows that
\phi
is a
(7,3)
-quasi-isometry from
G
to
H
. This proves
4.3
.
