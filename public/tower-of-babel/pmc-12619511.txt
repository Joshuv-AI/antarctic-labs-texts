Detect early TB infection, evaluate contacts for TB (DETEC-TB) – a protocol for a prospective cohort study on active case finding for tuberculosis disease and tuberculosis infection source tracing
Authors: Maryam Amour, Petra Joseph, Jackline Ngowi, Anab Issa, Candida Moshiro, George Ruhago, Patricia Munseri, Emmanuel Balandya, Lulu Fundikira, C. Robert Horsburgh, Christopher C. Whalen, Mecky Matee, C. Fordham von Reyn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberculosis (TB) is a leading cause of death from infectious disease, with 1.25 million deaths in 2023. Among the estimated 10.8 million people who developed TB in 2023, only 75% were diagnosed and reported to national programs and the World Health Organization (WHO). Tanzania has one of the highest burdens of TB in the world, with an estimated 29% of cases unreported. Innovative control strategies, including improved active case finding, are needed to close this gap. This study aims to determine the proportion of persons with TB disease among contacts of adolescents with newly-acquired TB infection and the cost-effectiveness of this strategy.MethodsThis 5-year prospective cohort study will perform serial Interferon Gamma Release Assay (IGRA) T-SPOT.TB testing for TB infection among 1200 adolescent school children aged 13 to 15 years in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. Participants with negative T-SPOT.TB test at baseline will repeat T-SPOT.TB testing every 4 months for 16 months to detect new TB infections. Participants will then be categorized into three groups based on T-SPOT.TB test results: prevalent TB infection ( IGRA positive at baseline), incident TB infection (conversion from IGRA-negative to IGRA-positive at follow-up), or no TB infection (persistent IGRA-negative). Household and other close contacts of 100 adolescents (4 contacts each) selected from the three categories will be tested to detect TB disease source cases. Tests will include expectorated sputum for gene X-pert and chest radiography. Data will be analyzed using Stata version 16, with the chi-square test used to compare differences in proportions of TB disease cases among contacts in each category. Logistic regression analysis will be used to estimate the odds ratios for the probability of source cases of tuberculosis disease among close contacts. A multivariate logistic regression model will adjust for potential confounders. A micro-costing approach will be used to determine the cost-effectiveness of this strategy for identifying community transmission of TB.DiscussionThe DETEC-TB study proposes a novel approach to active tuberculosis case finding and infection source tracing. Serial T-SPOT.TB testing of adolescents will detect prevalent and incident TB infections. Close contacts of these adolescents will be tested to identify cases of infectious TB disease that may have transmitted the infection.

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) disease has a global burdening effect, with an estimated 1.25 million deaths and 10.8 million new cases in 2023 [1]. Of the estimated 10.8 million people who developed TB in 2023 worldwide, only 8.2 million were diagnosed and reported to national programs and the World Health Organization (WHO) [1–3]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to improve case detection to achieve the End TB Strategy to reduce TB deaths by 95% and incidence by 90% by 2035.

A quarter of TB cases occur in sub-Saharan Africa, and Tanzania is ranked 14th among the 30 high TB burden countries, with an estimated incidence rate of 253 cases per 100,000 population in 2018 [2, 3]. It is also listed among the ten countries with the largest gaps between notifications of new and relapse TB cases compared to the estimates of TB incidence [2]. The first Tanzania national survey results confirm the low TB case detection rate, with over 100,000 missed TB cases every year [4].

The tuberculin skin test (TST) has been used over decades to detect TB infection [5]. Newer interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs) measure IFN-γ release from T cells in response to MTB specific antigens [6]. IGRAs are performed on a single blood sample and are not associated with false positives from prior BCG as with the TST. However, these tests cannot differentiate between TB infection and TB disease [7].There are two IFN-γ release assays (IGRAs) licensed: the Quantiferon-TB Gold Plus (QFN-Plus) and the enzyme-linked immunospot (IFN-γ ELISpot) T-SPOT.TB test. Both tests measure IFN-γ release from T cells in response to MTB specific antigens [8]. In this study, we propose to use the T-SPOT.TB test.

Innovative TB control strategies are needed to meet the End TB Strategy’s goal [9]. A key component of current “active case finding” is testing close contacts (CCs) of new cases of TB disease (index case). This strategy only detects TB disease in 2–7% of HCs [10]. TB disease typically develops 2–5 years after acquiring TB infection (the transmission event) [11]. Therefore, if the source for the new case of TB disease was a household or other close contact, that person might have already been treated, recovered, or died from TB disease. In this study, we hypothesize that testing close contacts of persons with incident TB infection will get closer to the transmission event and result in the detection of more cases of TB disease, possibly including the source TB case for the new infection.

Epidemiologic studies have concluded that TB disease in adolescents is typically acquired in the community because a source case of TB disease is not typically found in the household [12]. This conclusion should be questioned, as a source TB disease case in the household would have had the disease 2–5 years previously and may have since been treated, died, or left the household. By screening HCs of adolescents with newly diagnosed TB infection (new IGRA conversion), the approximate time of initial transmission and a higher proportion of HCs with TB disease can be obtained. The proportions of CC TB cases of an index case of incident TB infection will be substantially higher than with an index case of TB disease. As noted in Table 1, screening CCs of individuals with TB disease has been shown to detect a modest number of new TB disease cases, of which a sufficient number is shown to be cost-effective for managing the disease [13–15, 17–19]. Since it typically takes 2–5 years from exposure and infection to the development of active TB disease, it is unsurprising that the number of HCs of a new index case of infectious TB disease is not higher [11]. Thus, Innovative strategies for earlier, more effective case detection are needed to realize the 2035 End TB Strategy goals [9].

The cost-effectiveness (CE) of Active Case Finding (ACF) has been studied for several strategies, with the majority of studies involving household contacts [20, 21]. Studies from countries bordering Tanzania have estimated costs per case detected through testing HCs of patients with newly diagnosed TB. ACF in Uganda among household contacts revealed that it was a cost-effective intervention for detecting TB cases compared to Passive Case Finding (PCF) (TB cases detected at medical facilities in patients who seek care on their own, independently). The cost per additional TB case detected by ACF of household contacts was estimated to be $444 [22]. Similarly, ACF based on testing CCs of patients with TB disease in Zambia showed $435 per case detected and was considered cost-effective [23]. The current study hypothesizes that testing close contacts (CCs) of adolescents with recently acquired and newly diagnosed TB infection will be more cost-effective than the current ACF method of testing CCs of patients with remotely acquired and newly diagnosed TB disease.

The study therefore aims to determine the proportion of persons with confirmed TB disease among CCs of adolescents with either prevalent or newly-acquired incident TB infections. It also seeks to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of regularly testing adolescent school children for TB using the T-SPOT.TB test in community clinics.

Methods

Study design and setting

This is a 5-year prospective cohort study designed to enroll adolescent school children, perform serial T-SPOT.TB test to detect TB infection, and then test their CCs for source cases of TB disease. The study is being conducted in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. Dar es Salaam has the highest notification rate of TB in Tanzania, contributing to about 15% of all notified cases [24]. The study began on 1 st April 2023 and is expected to last until 30th April 2028. Participants will have T-SPOT.TB testing every 4 months for 16 months, with a minimum of four visits per participant.

The enrollment of participants is being performed at the selected secondary schools in districts with high TB notification rates, specifically the Ilala and Temeke districts [24]. The data on the respective districts is based on the District Health Information Software (DHIS2) database of the Ministry of Health under the National TB program. Follow-up visits for adolescent participants will take place at the schools, and close contact visits will be conducted at their households. All close contacts identified to have TB disease will be referred to the National Tuberculosis and Leprosy Program (NTLP) for treatment as per national guidelines [25].

Study population

The study is designed to enroll adolescents aged 13 to 15 years from secondary schools in Dar es Salaam, together with their close contacts. Participants will be identified from secondary schools in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania in districts with high TB notification rates. The study team will visit households and close contacts selected for further study and obtain additional consent/assent from household members.

Participants will be included based on the following criteria: (1) age 13 to 15 years (2), enrolled in a secondary school (3), able to provide informed assent (4), have had a parent/guardian to complete an informed consent and assured willingness to have household contacts evaluated for TB, and (5) be able to provide phone contact and map ques to their household. Participants will be excluded from the study if they have had a previous diagnosis of TB disease or have ever used TB preventive therapy.

Sample size and power calculation

In the first two years of this study, 1200 adolescent students aged 13–15 years at schools in the target area will be screened. Based on results from the recent DAR-PIAT trial, we anticipate that 15% of these will have positive T-SPOT.TB test at baseline (n = 180) and 85% will have negative T-SPOT.TB test at baseline (n = 1020) [26]. Similarly, IGRA testing at 4-month intervals x4 is expected to yield a 4% IGRA conversion rate per year [26]. Adolescents who are T-SPOT.TB negative at baseline will repeat T-SPOT.TB test every 4 months over 16 months of the study [4, 8, 12, 13]. CCs of three IGRA categories (prevalent, incident, and persistent negative) will be tested for TB disease using sputum for Gene X-pert test and chest x-radiography, regardless of TB symptom status. After excluding the adolescent we assume 4 HCs for each adolescent with T-SPOT.TB test conversion [28]. We will therefore, study households of 50 subjects from the incident positive group, i.e. 200 CC. Additionally, households of 25 subjects from each of the remaining two categories (prevalent, persistent negative) will be studied, with 100 CCs per each, totaling 400 CCs.

Sampling procedure

Multistage sampling will be used to select adolescents from government secondary schools, and the population divided into separate clusters. A three-stage cluster sampling technique will be used, starting at the district level, specific wards, and finally at schools. A list of all-day government secondary schools in Dar-es-Salaam will be obtained. Secondary schools will be selected randomly by cluster sampling from districts with high TB notification rates, and schools will be selected randomly within district and weighed equally according to numbers, age and sex. Potential participants from secondary schools will then be identified.

Data collection methods and tools

The study will be conducted per the current version of “Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects” as adopted by the World Medical Association (WMA) [29]. Ethical clearance was obtained from the National Health Research Ethics Committee (NaTHREC), the local Ethical review board at MUHAS, and the Dartmouth Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects. Permission to collect Data was obtained from the President’s Office, Regional Administration and Local Government (PO-RALG). Clinical and demographic data and blood samples will be stripped of all personal identifiers and assigned special identification number. Data will be stored in a highly secure server in a secured access area, with access restricted to key study personnel. A confidential participant list with participant identifiers will be used to assist participant tracking during follow-up of adolescents in schools.

Protocol for adolescent participants

The screening of the study participants will be performed at the selected secondary schools. The study staff will visit these schools and explain the study to students, and thereafter call parents to the school to seek written consent. After assent and consent, adolescents who meet eligibility criteria will be interviewed using a questionnaire to obtain demographic information, medical history, and household information. In addition, participants will be administered a TB risk factor questionnaire developed in our previous DAR-PIAT study [26]. Phlebotomy will be performed to collect 4–6 mL of a venous blood sample for the T-SPOT.TB test (Oxford Immunotec, UK) and a return visit scheduled in 2 weeks to provide results.

Adolescents with negative T-SPOT.TB test at baseline will be followed up with repeat T-SPOT.TB test every 4 months for 16 months. Participants with borderline or invalid T-SPOT.TB test results will be considered ineligible and will have no further follow-up. Participants with baseline positive results will be followed up once at 4 months after the screening visit to determine whether they will remain positive or convert to negative. In all follow-up visits, the Ministry of Health’s TB disease screening questionnaire will be administered to all participants for TB disease screening. Participants with positive responses to any of the TB symptoms and those who convert from T-SPOT.TB test negative to positive will be screened for TB disease by sputum for Gene X-pert test and chest radiography.

Blood samples will be transported to the DarDar study laboratory within 4 h in 20–22 degree° C temperature-controlled coolers to perform the T-SPOT.TB test within 32 h. Assays will be performed and interpreted according to the manufacturer’s instructions using manual microscopic counting of Spot Forming Cells [7].

IGRAs (T-SPOT.TB test) interpretation

IGRAs are based on the T-cell mediating IFN-γ release induced by specific M. tuberculosis (MTB) antigens. MTB-specific antigens mainly include early secretory antigenic target 6 (ESAT-6) and culture filtrate protein-10 (CFP-10) [6]. IGRAs are performed on a single blood sample and are not associated with false positives from prior BCG as with the TST. There are two IFN-γ release assays (IGRAs) licensed: the Quantiferon-TB Gold Plus (QFN-Plus) and the enzyme-linked immunospot (IFN-γ ELISpot) T-SPOT.TB test. In this study, we propose using the T-SPOT.TB test because the inclusion of a borderline category supports serial testing by improving interpretation [30]. Additionally, our study team has an experience using T-SPOT.TB test in a previous high-burden adolescent cohort [31].

To conduct the tests, phlebotomy will be performed to collect 4–6 mL of blood for the T-SPOT.TB test (Oxford Immunotec, UK). Blood samples will be transported to the DarDar study laboratory within 4 h in 20-22 °C temperature-controlled coolers for the performance of the T-SPOT.TB test within 32 h [32].

Each T-SPOT.TB test includes four wells: a negative control (Nil) consisting of sterile media, a positive control (PHA) consisting of phytohemagglutinin, and two wells consisting of TB early secreted antigen-6 (ESAT-6) and TB culture filtrate protein (CFP-10). Spot forming counts (SFCs) will then be determined manually using a microscope, and the technician will count SFCs only up to 20 per well and categorize all other wells as “>20” [32]. Protocol for close contacts

Close contacts of adolescents in three T-SPOT.TB test subgroups (negative, prevalent, and incident) will be identified, and their households will be visited for recruitment into the study. Close contacts will be categorized into two groups, household contact and other close contacts. A household contact will be defined as an individual aged 5 years and above who has resided in the household with the adolescent for at least 7 consecutive days during the 3 months before the diagnosis of tuberculosis infection [18]. Other close contacts are defined as individuals older than 5 years who share airspace with the adolescent for at least 15 h a week or for at least 180 h or more during the prior 4 months. Visits for prevalent IGRA positives, incident IGRA positives, and persistent IGRA negatives will be conducted within 4 weeks of testing. Random sampling will be used to select T-SPOT.TB test negative adolescents who were tested during the same month as the incident converters. Any persistent negative participant who subsequently converts to T-SPOT.TB test positive will be excluded from the persistent negative group.

After assent and consent, CCs will be interviewed for demographic information and medical history. Then, they will be administered a TB exposure and risk factor questionnaire [33]. One 4–6 mL sample of blood will be collected for T-SPOT.TB testing in CCs ≥ age 5.

Based on extensive epidemiologic studies, CCs younger than age 5 are considered unlikely to have been the source of TB infection in adolescents and will not be screened. CCs will be examined (general and respiratory exam) and a TB symptom screening questionnaire (TSQ) based on five symptoms: cough, hemoptysis, fever, excess sweating for more than 2 weeks, and noticeable weight loss [34]. All close contacts will be instructed to provide one on-spot or early morning expectorated sputum for Gene Xpert testing based on the Tanzania National Tuberculosis and Leprosy Program (NTLP) and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guide for the collection of good quality sputum [35]. Close contacts will be transported to the selected hospitals equipped with a digital diagnostic X-ray system for chest radiography. chest radiography will be interpreted by the study radiologist, and the findings will be categorized as probable active TB, possible active TB, probable remote TB, and not consistent for active or remote TB. CCs with a positive Gene Xpert will be classified as microbiologically-confirmed TB, while clinical TB will be defined as Gene Xpert negative (or not performed) participants who meet Tanzanian NTLP clinical categories, including the presence of one or more TB symptoms, chest x-ray findings suggestive of active TB, or expert’s decision to treat [34, 36]. CCs with clinically- or microbiologically confirmed TB will be referred to NTLP clinic for treatment and HIV testing per national policy. The data collected will be entered on paper case report forms and saved in the REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) database by Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN, and hosted at MUHAS [37, 38].

Variables

Independent variables will be (1) socio-demographic characteristics, including the name of the district where the adolescent resides, attended School, date of birth, age, and gender. (2) clinical factors including height and weight, past medical history, history of previous exposure to TB, characteristics/conditions of the household and close contacts, history of disease, transport modality to school, and visits to crowded areas, including markets. The dependent variable to be analyzed is incident TB infection among CCs of adolescents.

Data analysis plan

Data will be entered into the REDCap database and analyzed by the study biostatistician at MUHAS using Stata version 16.

The primary outcome is the proportion of CCs of adolescents with incident TB infection who have microbiologically confirmed TB disease. The proportion of confirmed TB cases among CCs of adolescents with incident TB infection will be compared to those of CCs of adolescents who are persistently T-SPOT.TB test negative. A second primary outcome will be the comparison of the proportion of confirmed TB cases among CCs of adolescents with prevalent TB infection with those of CCs of adolescents who are persistently T-SPOT.TB test negative. Chi-square tests will be used for all comparisons. Logistic regression analyses will be used to estimate the odds ratio for the probability of disease in the CCs. Clinically plausible predictor variables will be included in a multivariate logistic regression model to adjust for potential confounders.

The secondary outcome is the cost-effectiveness of this method, which will evaluate whether testing CCs of adolescents with new TB infection is more cost-effective than the current method in Tanzania of testing CCs of patients with TB disease. A cost-effectiveness analysis will provide a detailed comparison of -based community active case finding compared to the standard care household contact investigation. A detailed process map will be created to outline the entire process of identifying TB cases using both approaches. The map will identify key stakeholders, activities, resources, locations, and timeframes involved in each approach.

Data will be collected to identify the capital costs (costs for one-time expenses such as training, equipment, and furniture) and recurrent costs for ongoing expenses such as personnel costs, utilities, and medical and non-medical supplies. Socio-economic characteristics, including age, gender, place of residence, number of household members, household size, and household income or wealth, will be collected from study participants. These characteristics will be analyzed to assess their association with T-SPOT.TB test positive results among adolescents and the effectiveness of household-based contact investigations. The number of TB cases detected using each approach will be recorded.

The Incremental Cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) will be calculated by comparing the incremental cost of detecting one TB case using T-SPOT.TB test diagnosis to the incremental cost of detecting one TB case using the usual care household contact investigation. An alternative scenario will be analyzed where T-SPOT.TB tests are used without any other intervention to assess their standalone cost-effectiveness.

Discussion

This study proposes a novel approach to detecting source cases of TB disease among the close contacts of adolescents with new TB infections identified through serial T-SPOT.TB testing. We have shown previously that serial T-SPOT.TB testing among school children in Tanzania to identify new TB infections is feasible using current T-spot technology [26, 39]. We found that annual T-SPOT.TB testing of 13–15-year-old adolescent schoolchildren identified 2.9% new (incident) TB infections each year as indicated by conversion of an IGRA from negative to positive [26]. We hypothesize that the source cases of TB disease for these incident infections may be detectable through testing their close contacts for active TB disease. Although IGRA assays have been used in our research studies in Tanzania, the cost and cost-effectiveness of implementing this ACF approach are unknown. Numerous new Point of Care (POC) IGRA assays are being developed, which could facilitate programmatic IGRA testing of school children by reducing costs and technological requirements [40].

The strategy of TB infection source tracing (TBIST) as a method of active case finding has never been studied and, if successful, will represent a new strategy for TB control. Traditionally, contact tracing is based on an index case of new active TB disease. Our innovation is to base CC screening for TB disease on an index case of newly acquired TB infection in an adolescent. The strength of this study lies in its innovative approach to testing the concept that serial T-SPOT.TB testing of adolescent school children could be an improved strategy to identify and treat source cases of TB disease among their close contacts. It will also have the potential to determine whether adolescent TB infections are acquired from identifiable close contacts with TB disease, or conversely, from unidentifiable community exposures. Additionally, this approach may serve as a complementary tool alongside the traditional household contact tracing, extending detection earlier in the transmission cycle. The information learned from this study will increase our understanding of characteristics that predispose people to TB, and further the development of a new game-changing strategy for more effective active TB case finding which ultimately may have a major global impact on public health policies for eliminating TB.

This study may face some challenges regarding active participation. First, TB disease is associated with stigma, and some CCs may refuse to participate in the study. To mitigate this, we will emphasize education on TB transmission and the potential for a cure of TB disease if identified early. If consent is obtained from fewer than 100 CCs for any of the 3 IGRA categories of adolescents (negative, prevalent, and incident), the number of households will be expanded to > 25. There is an expected loss of follow-up among adolescents as well as CCs. To reduce this loss, adolescents and CCs will be called via cell phone and receive text messages regularly for follow-up reminders. Map ques will also be documented to identify the location of the households for ease of follow-up at homes.

In Tanzania, treatment of TB infection is currently recommended to under-five children and adults living with HIV only. In addition to serving as a better strategy for the detection of TB disease in households, the current study would also identify school children with new TB infection as a potential group for treatment of TB infection as recommended by WHO. Participants who qualify for TB infection treatment and those with confirmed or clinical TB disease will be reported and sent to the National Tuberculosis and Leprosy Control Program for further evaluation and treatment.
