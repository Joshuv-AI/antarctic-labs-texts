Neo-gintonin: production of high concentrations of lysophosphatidic acids from Korean Red ginseng marc
Authors: Ji-Hun Kim, Rami Lee, Sung-Hee Hwang, Yebeen Kang, Hyun-Mee Park, Sun-Hye Choi, BaiCheng Chen, Jong-Hoon Kim, Seung-Yeol Nah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGintonin is a novel non-saponin glycolipoprotein complex that is derived from ginseng. Its functional components are lysophosphatidic acids (LPAs), which act as ligands for six G protein-coupled LPA receptor subtypes. Thus, gintonin exerts it activity through the LPA receptor signaling pathway. Previous studies have shown that crude gintonin derived from Korean Red ginseng marc (KRGM) contains approximately 0.2–0.3% LPAs. The aim of this study was to develop a method for concentrating the LPA content in KRGM-derived gintonin.MethodsFirst, we prepared a gintonin-enriched fraction from KRGM and removed its ethanol-insoluble portion. Then, the ethanol-soluble fraction was subjected to anion-exchange chromatography followed by gel filtration. Subsequently, the gintonin fractions were collected and freeze-dried. The presence of the glycolipoprotein complex was confirmed using SDS-PAGE and gel staining. The newly acquired product was designated neo-gintonin.ResultsCompared with KRGM total extract, neo-gintonin contained 15.4-fold higher concentrations of LPA C18:2, LPA C16:0, and LPA C18:1. The approximately 66 kDa neo-gintonin was H2O highly water soluble. In primary cortical astrocytes treated with fluorescent neo-gintonin (prepared using Alexa Fluor 488 NHS ester), the fluorescent complex was found to bind to the cell membrane. This binding could be blocked using an LPA1/3 receptor antagonist. Neo-gintonin also stimulated nerve growth factor release from mouse primary cortical astrocytes.ConclusionWe developed a novel method for extracting neo-gintonin from KRGM and demonstrated that it exerted its physiological effects via LPA receptors. Thus, KRGM is a novel gintonin source and can be upcycled to prepare high LPA-yielding neo-gintonin.Keywords: Korean red ginseng marc, LPAs, Neo-gintonin, Astrocytes, NGF, Upcycling

Introduction

Ginseng is a well-known herbal medicine with a long history of use as a tonic. Recently, we reported that white/red ginseng and Korean Red ginseng marc (KRGM) contain gintonin, a non-saponin bioactive glycolipoprotein complex [1,2]. The physiological and pharmacological effects of gintonin are derived from its lysophosphatidic acids (LPAs), which act as exogenous ligands for six G protein-coupled LPA receptor subtypes [2]. Gintonin contains three LPAs in the following decreasing order: LPA C18:2 > LPA C16:0 > LPA C18:1. Its main mode of action via the LPA receptors is to elicit [Ca2+]i transients by means of phospholipase C and inositol 1,4,5-triphosphate (IP3) receptor activation in neuronal and non-neuronal cells [2]. Ginseng-derived gintonin has been reported to exhibit diverse physiological and pharmacological activities [[3], [4], [5]], including wound-healing [6], gastric protective [7], and immune-enhancing effects [8,9]. In mouse models and human, it has also been shown to prevent Alzheimer's disease and improve cognitive functions in animals with early Alzheimer's disease or subjective memory impairment [10,11].

Pyo et al. [12,13] were the first to isolate gintonin from white and red ginseng using anion-exchange chromatography and gel filtration after methanol extraction and butanol fractionation processes. Subsequently, our research team prepared a gintonin-enriched fraction (GEF) by subjecting an ethanol extract of white ginseng to water precipitation [14]. Existing methods for gintonin preparation from ginseng involve the extensive use of organic solvents such as methanol and butanol, which are not suitable for producing edible gintonin-based products [12]. Although the technique for preparing GEFs from ginseng is relatively easy compared with the methanol and butanol methods, the product still contains other ginseng components such as water-insoluble compounds (e.g., high-molecular-weight water-insoluble carbohydrates), rendering it only partially water soluble. Recently, we found that a simple 70% ethanol extract of Korean Red ginseng marc (KRGM) contained approximately 0.2–0.3% LPAs without further processing [1], indicating that KRGM can be a potentially rich source of bioactive gintonin LPAs [10,15]. We referred to it as crude KRGM-derived gintonin.

In the present study, we developed a novel method for synthesizing high LPA-yielding gintonin from the KRGM-derived GEF. The product prepared with this method was named neo-gintonin. The new method yielded high concentrations of the three types of LPAs compared with that obtained from previously reported gintonins. Neo-gintonin is highly water soluble and stimulates nerve growth factor (NGF) release from mouse primary cortical astrocytes. Herein, we discuss the physiological and pharmacological effects of neo-gintonin and the upcycling of KRGM as a novel source of this glycolipoprotein complex.

Materials and Methods

Materials

KRGM, a by-product generated from the hot aqueous extraction of Korean Red ginseng, was obtained from a local Korean Red ginseng product company. Fermented ethanol was purchased from Daehan Ethanol Life (Gyeonggi-do, Korea), filter paper (150 mm, type 2) from ADVANTEC (Tokyo, Japan), and Sudan Black B (190,160,250) from ACROS Organics (Fair Lawn, NJ, USA). Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 (B0149) and G-250 (27,815) dyes, periodic acid (375,810), Schiff's reagent (S5133), PD-10 resin (Sephadex G-25, G-25-50), and 2-(N-morpholino) ethanesulfonic acid hydrate (M2933) from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The Tricorn (28,406,418), XK (28,988,947), Superdex 200 (10/300) (28,990,944), Superdex 75 (10/300) Increase (29-1487-21), and Hitrap Capto DEAE Resin 5 mL (28,916,540) columns from Cytiva (Marlborough, MA, USA). The Biologic FPLC System (Maximizer, Duoflow, Quadtec, Biofrac) was from Bio-Rad Laboratories (Hercules, CA, USA). The 1200 Series HPLC System from Agilent (Santa Clara, CA, USA), the XBridge C18 column (3.5 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) from Waters (Milford, MA, USA), and the ABSciex API 3200 LC-MS/MS System from SCIEX (Vaughan, ON, Canada).

Preparation of Korean Red ginseng marc extract

KRGM was stored in a dried state. Prior to GEF preparation, the dried KRGM was pulverized into particles of approximately 3 mm diameter. Subsequently, a 70% ethanol extract of KRGM was prepared as described in a previous report [1]. Finally, four fractions (including the GEF) were obtained from the KRGM extract using the “ginpolin protocol” developed in a previous study [16].

Removal of ethanol-insoluble components from the KRGM-derived GEF

Prior to gintonin isolation via anion-exchange chromatography, the GEF was subjected to a pretreatment process to remove unwanted components (e.g., ginseng pectin and other unidentified components) that might interfere with the binding of gintonin to the anion resin or the enrichment of its LPAs. For this purpose, the GEF (60 mg/mL) was dissolved in 20 mM Tris-Cl buffer in 70% ethanol to precipitate the ethanol-insoluble components. The mixture was thoroughly vortexed and shaken on an orbital shaker for 4 h at ambient temperature to ensure complete dissolution. The sample was then centrifuged at 4 °C and 2000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected and further incubated at 4 °C for 2 days. This procedure was repeated two more times. Finally, the sample was centrifuged once more, and the supernatant was used for the isolation and purification of salt-free neo-gintonin via anion-exchange chromatography and gel filtration.

Anion-exchange chromatography of the ethanol-soluble GEF

Anion-exchange chromatography of the GEF was performed using an FPLC system (Bio-Rad, Biologic FPLC system: Maximizer, Duoflow, Quadtec, Biofrac) to select only the gintonin components, as described in previous reports [12,13]. The FPLC parameters used are listed in Table S1. A Hitrap Capto DEAE Resin column was used as the stationary phase. Sample loading and elution were performed as previously described [12]. In total, 20 fractions were collected in 2.5 mL volumes using a fraction collector and freeze-dried into powdered form for product stabilization.

Isolation of neo-gintonin using gel filtration

The freeze-dried fractions obtained from anion-exchange chromatography were subjected to gel filtration for salt removal. The specific FPLC conditions for gel filtration are listed in Table S2. Gel filtration was performed using the Bio-Rad Biologic FPLC system (Maximizer, Duoflow, Quadtec, Biofrac) equipped with a pre-packed Superdex 200 (10/300) column. Aromatic amino acids in proteins were detected at 280 nm using the Quadtec UV-vis detector in line with the conditions used for anion-exchange chromatography. The sample loading and elution procedures were performed according to previously described methods [12].

SDS-PAGE and qualitative analysis of neo-gintonin

A 12% SDS-polyacrylamide gel was prepared using a hand-cast system (Bio-Rad). The neo-gintonin sample was dissolved in methanol at a concentration of 100 mg/mL and then diluted 2-fold with deionized water to a final methanol concentration of 50%, resulting in a final fraction concentration of 50 mg/mL. Subsequently, 4× Laemmli sample buffer (1,610,747; Bio-Rad) was added to the dissolved sample at a 1:2 ratio (sample:buffer). After thorough mixing, the samples were heated at 70 °C for 10 min and then incubated at ambient temperature for 5 min. After cooling, the samples were centrifuged, and a 12 μL volume containing 200 μg of sample was loaded into each well of the SDS-polyacrylamide gel. Electrophoresis at 50 V for 30 min, followed by 100V for 90 min, was performed using a Tetra Vertical Electrophoresis Cell (Mini-PROTEAN, 1,658,004; Bio-Rad) connected to a basic power supply (PowerPac, 1,645,050; Bio-Rad). Qualitative analyses of the carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins in the fractions were performed using gels as described in another report [12].

Determination of the molecular weight of neo-gintonin

To determine the molecular weight of neo-gintonin, size-exclusion chromatography was performed using an FPLC system (Biologic FPLC set: Maximizer, Duoflow, Quadtec, BioFrac; Bio-Rad) equipped with a Superdex 75 10/300 Increase column and Quadtec UV-vis detector. The wavelengths used were 203 and 210 nm for detecting phospholipids such as LPAs, 280 nm for detecting aromatic amino acids in proteins, and 230 nm for detecting peptide bonds. The mobile phase consisted solely of deionized water in Line B. For standard curve construction, one vial of commercial marker (M-3913; Sigma Aldrich) was injected into the column via the autosample-loop injector at a flow rate of 0.7 mL/min. After the injection, zero-baseline correction was performed for all four detection wavelengths [12].

Quantitative analysis of neo-gintonin using LC-MS/MS

The LPA C16:0, LPA C18:1, and LPA C18:2 contents in neo-gintonin were determined using LC-MS/MS and each LPA standard, as described in other reports [13,14]. MS analysis was performed using an AB Sciex API 3200 system with negative-mode electrospray ionization and multiple reaction monitoring. The curtain gas was set to 20 psi, the ion spray voltage to 4000 V, and the source temperature to 500 °C. Nitrogen was used as ion source gas 1 at a flow rate of 40 units and as ion source gas 2 at a flow rate of 35 units for drying and heating. A stock solution of the test sample was prepared by dissolving it in deionized water to a concentration of 100 mg/10 mL. The stock solution was vortexed to form a suspension, and 1 mL was immediately removed from the center of the solution and diluted with 19 mL of methanol. The diluted solution was vortexed to maintain the suspension, after which 1 mL was immediately removed from the center and further diluted with 1 mL of methanol at a 1:1 ratio to prepare the final sample solution for analysis. The prepared solution was filtered using a polytetrafluoroethylene syringe filter (13JP020AN; ADVANTEC MFS, Dublin, CA, USA), and 1 mL of the filtrate was dispensed into an HPLC vial (5190-9591, 5182-0728; Agilent) to complete the preparation of the sample for analysis. The LC-MS/MS analytical conditions are listed in Table S3. For HPLC, an Agilent 1200 Series instrument equipped with an XBridge C18 column (3.5 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) was used. The column oven temperature was maintained at 25 °C. The mobile phase consisted of solution A (10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in deionized water) and solution B (acetonitrile) applied in a gradient mode. A 3 μL sample injection volume was used.

Conjugation of neo-gintonin with fluorescent dyes and isolation of fluorescent gintonin

The methods used to conjugate neo-gintonin with the fluorescent dye Alexa Fluor 488 NHS ester (hereinafter Alexa Fluor 488) (A20000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were as described in previous studies [17,18]. After completion of the conjugation step, the reaction sample was subjected to gel filtration on the Biologic FPLC System (Maximizer, Duoflow, Quadtec, BioFrac) to filter out free unconjugated dye molecules. A Tricorn column packed with PD-10 (Sephadex G-25) resin was used. The Quadtec UV-vis detector was set to simultaneously monitor the absorbance at 280 nm (for the detection of aromatic amino acids in proteins) and 230 nm (for the detection of peptide bonds). The mobile phase was deionized water, which was delivered solely through Line A. The fractions corresponding to fluorescent gintonin were collected and lyophilized.

Verification of conjugation between Alexa Fluor 488 and neo-gintonin

Conjugation between the neo-gintonin and Alexa Fluor 488 molecules was assessed using 12% SDS-PAGE according to a previously reported method [1]. In brief, each gel was prepared in quadruplicate and cut into four pieces, three of which were subjected to periodic acid–Schiff staining for carbohydrates, Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining for proteins, and Sudan Black B staining for lipids, respectively. The remaining piece was first analyzed with an in vivo imaging system and then stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue. To quantify the amount of Alexa Fluor 488 molecules that were conjugated to gintonin in the lyophilized samples, Alexa Fluor 488 was dissolved in 1% Triton X-100 solution and serially diluted to 1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.0625, 0.03125, and 0.015625 μg/mL concentrations to generate a standard series. The lyophilized Alexa Fluor 488–gintonin conjugates were dissolved in 1% Triton X-100 solution at a concentration of 2.5 mg/mL and then further diluted 200-fold prior to analysis. The standards and samples were dispensed at 100 μL aliquots into the wells of a 96-well plate (3585; Corning, Corning, NJ, USA) in triplicate. The fluorescent intensity in each well was measured at excitation and emission wavelengths of 640 and 695 nm (Auto cut off: 705 nm), respectively, using a SpectraMax Gemini XPS/EM microplate reader. The binding ratio of Alexa Fluor 488 to gintonin was calculated in both weight and molar units. The conjugation ratio of Neo-gintonin to Alexa Fluor 488 was determined to be approximately 1:7.1.

In vitro cell binding assay using fluorescent gintonin

Mouse primary cortical astrocytes were cultured as described in previous reports [19,20] and used to demonstrate the binding of Alexa Fluor 488–neo-gintonin conjugates to cells in vitro. Cover glasses precoated with poly-l-lysine were placed in the wells of 24-well cell culture plates (3524; Corning), and astrocytes were seeded at a density of 2.5 × 105 per well. Once the cultured cells have reached 80% confluency, they were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline and starved in serum-free medium for 4 h. After starvation, Alexa Fluor 488–gintonin conjugates were added at a concentration of 67.2 μg/well in the absence or presence of Ki16425. After incubation for 24 h, the cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline, mounted on glass slides using DAPI mounting medium (H-1800; Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA), and observed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (AxioVert200; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Quantitation of nerve growth factor release

For the NGF assay, astrocytes were seeded in the wells of 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. Dulbecco's modified Eagle's media with or without different concentrations of neo-gintonin were then added to the designated wells, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C. The level of NGF in the culture medium was measured using a mouse NGF/NGFβ ELISA kit (Bosterbio, Pleasanton, CA, USA) according to the procedures provided by the manufacturer [21].

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired Student's t-test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's multiple comparison test versus the control group.

Results

Preparation and ethanol precipitation of the GEF and preparation of neo-gintonin after anion-exchange chromatography and gel filtration

First, we prepared a 70% ethanol extract of KRGM and used it to generate the GEF [14], obtaining a product yield of approximately 7.9 ± 0.3%. Next, we subjected the GEF to 70% ethanol precipitation to remove its ethanol-insoluble components while conserving the ethanol-soluble molecules. This novel and advanced but also simple approach serves to remove other components (e.g., water-insoluble polysaccharides) from the GEF through the sole additional ethanol precipitation of the fraction, as described in the Materials and Methods section.

We then subjected the ethanol-soluble GEF to anion-exchange chromatography, collected the fractions that were eluted with high salts, and freeze-dried the samples (Fig. 1A). The freeze-dried fractions were desalted using gel filtration and the eluate—named neo-gintonin—was again freeze-dried (Fig. 1B). The molecular weight of neo-gintonin was 66.2 kDa, which was almost the same as that of previously isolated gintonins (Fig. 1C).

Anion-exchange chromatography (A) and gel filtration (FPLC) (B) of the KRGM-derived GEF, and (C) molecular weight of neo-gintonin. R2 = 0.9303 for the standard curve. The 66.2 kDa value is the mean of triplicate measurements with an error range of ±0.3 kDa.

Neo-gintonin exhibited properties different from those of other white or red ginseng-derived GEFs [14]. For example, neo-gintonin was highly water soluble whereas the GEF was only partially soluble in water. Moreover, neo-gintonin powder appeared dark brown, resembling wet sand in texture (Fig. 2A). The yield of neo-gintonin from the GEF was approximately 10.5 ± 3.2%. We then subjected the neo-gintonin to SDS-PAGE, after which the gels were stained for analyses of the polysaccharides (periodic acid–Schiff staining), lipids (Sudan Black B staining), and proteins (Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining). Neo-gintonin stained positively for polysaccharides, lipids, and proteins, confirming that it is a glycolipoprotein complex, as previously reported [12] (Fig. 2B). Since the molecular weight of neo-gintonin determined by gel filtration was approximately 66.2 kDa but was less than 17 kDa according to the SDS-PAGE results, the molecule is likely a tetramer.

(A) Morphological characteristics of neo-gintonin and (B) gel staining for its carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins. The water-insoluble KRGM-derived GEF powder was light brown with a ginseng aroma. The highly water-soluble neo-gintonin powder was dark brown, heavy, moist, and odorless.

Composition of lysophosphatidic acids in neo-gintonin

The LPAs (the functional components of gintonin) in neo-gintonin were quantified using LC-MS/MS [13] and determined to be LPA C18:2, LPA C16:0, and LPA C18:1. The amounts of these three types of LPAs in crude KRGM, the GEF, and neo-gintonin are summarized in Table 1. Consistent with previous reports [13,14], LPA C18:2 was the most abundant of the three LPAs in the samples. The amounts of the three LPAs in neo-gintonin were about 13-fold higher than those in the GEF. Thus, we demonstrated that this simple but novel procedure for preparing neo-gintonin from a KRGM-derived GEF can concentrate the three LPAs by more than 10-fold compared with the amount in the GEF.

Binding of Alexa Fluor 488–neo-gintonin conjugates to mouse primary cortical astrocytes

Fluorescent neo-gintonin molecules were prepared using Alexa Fluor 488 and tested for their binding to mouse primary cortical astrocytes. Microscopic images confirmed the binding of fluorescent neo-gintonin to the cell membrane (Fig. 3A, white arrows). Furthermore, to determine whether the neo-gintonin-binding sites correspond to LPA receptors, we pretreated cells with Ki16425, an LPA1/3 receptor antagonist. The Ki16425 treatment resulted in the almost complete loss of astrocyte-binding activity, indicating that neo-gintonin binds to LPA1/3 receptor subtypes on primary astrocytes (Fig. 3B). These results are consistent with previous studies that showed fluorescent gintonin binding to cells expressing LPA receptors [17,18].

Binding of fluorescent neo-gintonin to mouse primary cortical astrocytes in the absence (A) or presence (B) of 10 μM Ki16425. Microscopic images were captured at the same magnification: bright field for morphological observation, DAPI for nucleus and fluorescent neo-gintonin. Scale bar: 50 μm.

Effects of neo-gintonin, LPAs, and ginsenoside Rb1 on NGF release in mouse primary cortical astrocytes and the signaling pathway for NGF release

Given that cultured cortical astrocytes express LPA receptor subtypes [22] and astrocytes produce NGF [23], we examined the effects of neo-gintonin (GT), LPAs, and ginsenoside Rb1 on NGF release in cultured mouse primary cortical astrocytes. Treatments with neo-gintonin or LPA C18:1 (3 μM) as a positive controls for 12 h induced an increase in NGF release in primary astrocytes (Fig. 4). By contrast, ginsenoside Rb1, a representative ginseng saponin, did not affect NGF release. Neo-gintonin also induced NGF release in a concentration- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 4A and B) with an EC50 of 0.24 ± 0.01 μg/mL. These results indicate that neo-gintonin, rather than ginsenoside Rb1 (100 μM), is the active ingredient in ginseng that induces the release of NGF from primary cortical astrocytes. We also examined the effects of neo-gintonin on NGF release in the absence or presence of Ki16425 (10 μM). The LPA1/3 receptor antagonist significantly attenuated neo-gintonin-mediated NGF release (Fig. 4C). Additionally, the phospholipase C inhibitor U73122 (10 μM), the IP3 receptor antagonist 2-APB (10 μM), and the intracellular Ca2+ chelator BAPTA-AM (10 μM) all blocked neo-gintonin-mediated NGF release in astrocytes (Fig. 4C). These results indicate that neo-gintonin elicits the release of Ca2+ from intracellular stores to induce NGF release in astrocytes via activation of the LPA receptor–phospholipase C–intracellular IP3 receptor signal transduction pathway.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a novel method for enriching gintonin LPAs by removing ethanol-insoluble components from the KRGM GEF. As ethanol-insoluble components (e.g., ethanol-insoluble polysaccharides) can interfere with the enrichment of LPAs, the pretreatment serves to conserve lipid-soluble components, such as LPAs. We named the ethanol-soluble GEF component neo-gintonin (from the Greek word “neo-,” meaning “new”). Thus, neo-gintonin contains much higher amounts of LPAs than those in gintonins prepared using previously reported methods [13,14]. Additionally, the novel neo-gintonin preparation technique is simpler and faster than previous methods, and the product is free of harmful organic solvents such as methanol and butanol, thereby significantly broadening its potential applications. Aside from containing a higher concentration of LPAs, neo-gintonin is also water soluble. This might be due to the complexation of carbohydrates and proteins in addition to lipid components and distinguishable from free LPAs, which are known to form insoluble micelle aggregates at high concentrations [24]. Moreover, neo-gintonin can be prepared from KRGM, thus upcycling the high additive value of KRGM as a source of exogenous LPA receptor ligands. Otherwise, KRGM is usually only partially recycled for feed/compost or discarded as a leftover material from the production of red ginseng products.

On the other hand, since KRGM is produced in large quantities every year, large-scale production of neo-gintonin is possible if the scale of KRGM ethanol extraction, anion exchange resin chromatography, and gel filtration is scaled up. It is expected that neo-gintonin produced in this way can be utilized in functional food and pharmaceutical industries [25].

Qualitative and quantitative analyses of neo-gintonin revealed that its composition was similar to that of previously isolated gintonins; that is, it exists as a glycolipoprotein complex but with a higher content of LPAs. The molecular weight of neo-gintonin (66.2 kDa) is almost the same as that of other gintonins isolated from white ginseng (67 kDa) (Fig. 1C) [12], indicating that both are same molecules albeit from different ginseng sources. LC-MS/MS analysis revealed that neo-gintonin contained an approximately 15.4- and 13-fold higher concentration of all three LPAs in total than the amounts in crude KRGM and the GEF, respectively (Table 1). These findings indicate that the LPA content in the KRGM-derived GEF can be enriched using ethanol precipitation and the two chromatography procedures.

Given the similarity of neo-gintonin to previously isolated gintonins from white ginseng and Korean red ginseng, we further examined whether it can bind to the cell membrane in the same way that other gintonins do [17,18]. The in vitro experimental results showed that fluorescent neo-gintonin did indeed bind to the astrocytic cell membrane (Fig. 3A), and this binding was blocked by treatment with the LPA1/3 receptor antagonist Ki16425 (Fig. 3B), confirming that neo-gintonin interacts with LPA receptor subtypes through the same pathway as that used by previously isolated gintonins [17,18]. Thus, we also showed the further potential of using fluorescent gintonin instead of radioisotope-labeled LPAs for in vitro and in vivo LPA-binding assays in nervous and non-nervous systems [26].

In the physiological and pharmacological experiments using primary cortical astrocytes, neo-gintonin treatment increased NGF release in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 4). Neo-gintonin-mediated NGF release was pharmacologically blocked by Ki16425 and inhibitors of the phospholipase C–IP3 receptor–Ca2+ chelator signal transduction pathway. These results indicate that neo-gintonin LPAs and previously isolated gintonins utilize the same signaling pathways. Interestingly, in previous studies, we also showed that gintonin stimulates the release of growth factors such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and NGF in neurons and astrocytes [27,28]. These growth (tropic) factors play diverse and important roles in the development and growth of the nervous system and maintain homeostasis of the central and peripheral nervous systems. They also protect the nervous system from exogenous injuries and stress [29]. We had also shown that gintonin attenuated in vitro astrocyte damage under hypoxia in vitro and that long-term gintonin treatment in vivo ameliorated or improved neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, and Huntington's diseases [10,30,31]. Thus, the gintonin-mediated release of these growth factors via LPA receptors may contribute to the attenuation of the symptoms of these neurological diseases.

In previous studies, we demonstrated that gintonin can be prepared from KRGM, with a 70% ethanol extract containing approximately 0.2–0.3% LPAs, which is almost the same amount as that in the GEF prepared from white ginseng [1,14]. KRGM-derived gintonin provides skin anti-aging and anti-obesity benefits and improves cognitive dysfunction in neurological conditions such as Alzheimer's disease by acting on LPA receptors and their signaling pathways [1,10,15]. In this study, we showed that the LPA amounts in the KRGM-derived GEF can be increased 13-folds through anion-exchange chromatography after simple ethanol precipitation [[32], [33], [34]]. Although the reasons why the prior ethanol precipitation of the GEF results in high amounts of LPAs remain unclear for now, two possibilities can be inferred: 1) ethanol precipitation removes ethanol-insoluble components such as high-molecular-weight pectin and polysaccharides, dietary fiber, and ginseng proteins and nucleic acids, which are not closely related but could interfere with neo-gintonin LPA isolation; 2) ethanol precipitation helps to enhance the binding capacity of gintonin to anion-exchange resins by removing unnecessary components in the GEF. Thus, this simple ethanol precipitation step before anion-exchange chromatography can replace the methanol extraction and butanol fractionation steps of previous protocols. Hence, compared with previous techniques, our new method is more efficient, less expensive, safer (by eliminating butanol and methanol requirements), and better at achieving high amounts of LPAs [12].

In summary, we have developed a novel method for obtaining high yields of gintonin LPAs from KRGM-derived GEFs. As an exogenous ligand for LPA receptors, neo-gintonin is able to exert its biological effects because of its high-water solubility. Importantly, KRGM can be upcycled as a novel source of this exogenous G protein-coupled LPA receptor ligand.
