Desa SIAGA, a community participation model for maternal and neonatal health in Indonesia, barriers and facilitators to implementation: findings from a comparative case study design
Authors: Vinami Yulian, Linda McGowan, Tomasina Stacey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground‘Desa SIAGA’ (alert village) is a community participation model (CPM) aiming to improve maternal and neonatal health (MNH) outcomes by empowering communities to prepare women for safe birth in Indonesia. However, there is limited evidence exploring the implementation of CPM within the Desa SIAGA programme. This study aimed to explore the implementation of Desa SIAGA in preparing women for birth and the potential to improve MNH in practice.MethodsA qualitative comparative case study with 23 in-depth interviews was conducted involving pregnant and post-natal women, village midwives, cadres and key stakeholders in two cases in Central Java, Indonesia in 2019. Interview data were analysed using thematic analysis approach. The interpretation was strengthened by non-participant observation of essential community meetings, fieldnotes, documentary analysis and cross-case synthesis. All relevant ethical approvals were obtained.ResultsFive key facilitators and barriers were found to influence the effective implementation and potential of the Desa SIAGA programme to improve MNH outcomes, these included: lack of empowerment in decision making, knowledge, and economic constraints; lack of clearly defined roles; workload; and social, cultural, and religious values.ConclusionsThe Desa SIAGA programme could bring benefits to improve MNH outcomes through the active participation of the community, but this will remail limited unless its delivery and implementation is underpinned by strong policy and supported by key actors. The findings of this study provide important evidence for policymakers in similar context in constructing policy towards the delivery and implementation of community participation programme.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08320-6.Keywords: Community participation, Maternal mortality, Maternal morbidity, Neonatal mortality

Background

Improving maternal and neonatal health (MNH) status is a priority in Indonesia. Indonesia has the highest neonatal mortality rate (NMR) in South East Asia [1] as well as one of the highest maternal mortality ratios (MMR), with 173 deaths per 100,000 live births in 2020, far from the WHO target of below 70 [2] Indonesia faces a number of challenges to MNH, including the quality of maternal health care, and geographical and political conditions which lead to inequality of health care services and health facilities across the country [3]. Although the Indonesian government has provided a health insurance programme to provide universal health coverage since 2014, the health system’s readiness to provide services that are covered by this programme has faced challenges and been shown to require improvement [4].

Reducing the number of maternal deaths and improving maternal health is a global concern and is clearly outlined as a priority within the United Nations (UN) Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) programme [5, 6]. Diverse approaches have been implemented by governments across the world to try to improve MNH outcomes [7]. Community participation is emerging as a promising approach to improve MNH, particularly in lower resource settings [8–10], and has been found to be a cost-effective approach to improving maternal and neonatal outcomes [8]. The community participation model is an intervention that focuses on the empowerment of individuals and families to assume responsibility for their health and wellbeing, as well as that of their wider community [11]. However, there are a number of factors that influence the successful implementation of community participation and therefore its effectiveness in improving MNH outcomes [10]. While studies have tried to assess the potential link between community participation interventions and improved MNH outcomes, evidence is lacking regarding the implementation of community participation programmes in practice [9, 10].

A community participation approach to improve maternal wellbeing has also been implemented in Indonesia. The ‘Alert Village Programme’ or ‘Desa SIAGA’ in Indonesia, is a scheme aimed to empower the community to take on more responsibility for their own health conditions rather than rely solely on health care providers [12]. The programme consists of eight criteria: (1) A village forum. (2) Community health workers (cadres) to empower the community. (3) A basic health service that can be accessed everyday by the community. (4) Community based-resources health efforts, including surveillance, disaster and emergency management, and environmental health improvement. (5) Financial resources from the village government, community, and business sectors. (6) Community participation and community-based organisation participation. (7) A policy in the village government about ‘Desa SIAGA Aktif’. (8) Healthy lifestyle development at the household level [12]. The programme was rolled out by the Indonesia government in early 2000 [13]. It was initially developed as part of the Safe Motherhood Initiative in Indonesia, yet gradually shifted into broader coverage of health issues such as sanitation, healthy lifestyles, disease surveillance, emergency conditions, and disasters with a specific focus on the safe-motherhood programme [13]. Initial research suggests that Desa SIAGA has a positive impact on antenatal visit attendance, the percentage of deliveries assisted by health professionals, the percentage of deliveries in health facilities and knowledge about family planning methods [14]. It also found that involvement of family and community in Desa SIAGA increased knowledge and awareness of danger signs and birth preparedness that could help tackle the ‘three delays’ in safe motherhood, improved social support and community capacity building [15]. The community was able to create a community-based transportation system and community-based funds that were found to make healthcare services more accessible and affordable. However, there is limited research on how well the programme is implemented, the factors that influence the programme delivery and how community participation works within it. Prior studies on Desa Siaga mainly focused on intervention outcomes, rather than the processes influencing them, with most conducted shortly after program initiation. In this study we aimed to explore the implementation and delivery of Desa SIAGA in two villages in Central Java, Indonesia.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in two villages – Meranti and Sendang (pseudonyms) in a region within Central Java. These two sites were purposefully sampled as they had both adopted the Desa Siaga programme but we were interested to see if there were any subtle differences in implementation. Meranti was a rural, village with a population of over 6300 people. Sendang, on the other hand was more urban as it was adjacent to a larger city and had a population of over 10,000 residents. Desa SIAGA has four main programmes in preparing pregnant women for birth namely: notification system, blood donor system, transport and communication, and financial support [15]. In 2012, the region launched a local programme called DASIAT (Pemuda Siaga Sehat or ‘Alert Youth in Health’) which aimed to empower the youth within each ‘alert’ village to help the community in developing their health status including MNH in the village: in one village (Sendang) DASIAT had been established, while it had not been introduced in the other (Meranti).

Study design

This study took a comparative case study design approach based on the principles set out by Yin [16]. According to Yin, case study research is “an empirical inquiry that investigates a contemporary phenomenon (the ‘case’) in depth and within its real-world context, especially when the boundaries between phenomenon and context may not be clearly evident.” [16] p282). This approach aims to explore a case, such as a programme, event, activity, persons, decisions, policies, or other systems in order to understand “how” and “why” that case may occur [16, 17]. It intends to illustrate and understand a specific issue, problem or concern [18]. Therefore, case study allows in-depth understanding and focus on how and why a complex phenomenon happens in a real-world, contemporary context or setting through multiple data collection such as in-depth interviews, observations, audio-visual material, and documentary analysis [16, 19]. Multiple methods of data collection helped the study to develop an in-depth description of the case and increase the validity of the research [19].

According to conceptual and theoretical debates, participation is a dynamic process that requires a range of implementation mechanisms that might differ from one location to another and that are dependent on the situations and the needs of communities [20]. In this circumstance, community participation is a complex social structure and needs to be looked at in specific context and cannot be generalised [20] and a case study was suitable for the research design. By collecting data through multiple sources of information, this study gained a deeper understanding from many perspectives about community participation in improving MNH and answered the “how” question of this study [16]. The in-depth interviews provide explanations and personal views from participants, observation gives an insight of a real-life phenomenon of community participation, and documentary analysis can confirm and add evidence to the interviews and observations [16].

Participants and recruitment

The participants of this study were pregnant women, post-natal women, neighbours of pregnant women, village midwives, kader (community health workers/ cadres), local governments (chief of neighbourhoods, chief of hamlets, and village heads), religious leaders and DASIAT members (in Sendang village). The strategies to recruit participants in this study were through distributing general information leaflets about the study to all targeted participants. The leaflets were distributed by the researcher in several strategic places, often visited by village citizens, including community health centres (Puskesmas), auxiliary health centres (Pustu), monthly integrated health service posts (Posyandu), village midwives’ houses, village health posts, village government offices, places of worship, and village security posts. Informed written consent was obtained from the participants once they agreed verbally to take part in this study.

Data collection

Data were collected from a range of sources by the first author. The qualitative data consisted of 23 in-depth interviews with women and key stakeholders (collected between March and September 2018). Interview schedules were drawn from the existent literature, informants working with the Desa Siaga model and discussions with the research team. In each case study site, the researcher interviewed two pregnant women, two post-natal women, one neighbour of a pregnant woman, one cadre, the village midwife, one chief of neighbourhood, one chief of hamlet, the village head, and one religious leader. In Sendang where DASIAT programme was implemented, the researcher also interviewed a DASIAT member who happened to be the chairman of DASIAT. For details about all informants please see supplementary file (S1). The interviews explored the different perspectives of the implementation of Desa SIAGA and the respective roles and involvement of the participants. The interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim with participants’ consent to for data analysis. All interviews were conducted in Bahasa Indonesian, translated into English and a sample were then back translated to ensure accuracy of translation and that meaning was not lost.

In addition, documentary evidence relating to the process of Desa SIAGA and maternity care in the study sites were collected and non-participant observations during five essential community meetings were conducted. In order to capture and structure the data from the observed meetings, the researcher used an inventory that was adopted from Spradley “Descriptive question matrix for observation” [21]. The matrix had nine elements that generally guided the observations in terms of location, the objects that were being observed, what was happening and when, who were the actors, the purpose of the activities, and the feeling expressed by the actors [21].

Data analysis

The interview data were analysed using the general thematic analysis approach developed by Braun and Clarke [22]. NVivo 12 and Microsoft Excel were used to help organise the data a facilitate mind mapping, leading to a final identification of several themes and sub-themes. Initial codes were identified and sorted into potential themes, which were then reviewing and refined further, data analysis was led by the first author and supported and verified by the other two authors at every stage. Data from the non-participant observations, documentary data and field notes were integrated to provide further insight into the interpretation of the findings. Once the data analysis for each individual case was performed (Fig. 2), the findings from both cases were then synthesised using a narrative approach, for further detail on the methods please see the original thesis [23].

Data analysis process in each case

This research obtained ethical approval from the School of Healthcare Research Ethics Committee (SHREC), University of Leeds (HREC17-017) and the Government of Sukoharjo Regency (No: 503/PEN/071/III/2018).

Findings

The overall synthesis showed that, although it was felt, by the participants, that Desa SIAGA had the potential to significantly improve MNH, several factors influenced the successful delivery and implementation of the initiative, including: lack of empowerment in decision making, knowledge, and economic constraints; lack of clearly defined roles; workload; and social, cultural, and religious values.

Lack of empowerment in decision making, knowledge, and economic constraints

Data from both the interviews with key actors and documents demonstrated that community empowerment is essential in the success of the programme. However, this study found that community engagement and empowerment within the programme was limited. The data showed that in the delivery and implementation phases of the Desa SIAGA programme, the women and community were not adequately engaged in participatory meetings. Empowerment to gain more knowledge and confidence to make decisions was lacking. The meetings only included village government officials, cadres and village midwives but not the community members themselves. This suggests that the voices of women and the community were not directly heard. Accordingly, the strategic plans developed to overcome MNH problems were limited to MNH promotion delivered by the village midwives and cadres.

“Desa SIAGA… Do you mean there is a midwife who help pregnant women? I have heard [about Desa SIAGA], but I don’t know what that [Desa SIAGA] means.” (Mirna a 30-year-old 9-month gestation, Meranti).

“There has not been any other information session yet [about Desa SIAGA]. During my service [about 6 years], there is no information session on Desa SIAGA.” (Sudrajat, 62-year-old, male, village head Meranti).

The interviews and documentary evidence showed that evaluation of the Desa SIAGA initiative was also unclear. According to the documentary reviews and personal communication with the Health Promotion Officer of Nampan Community Health Centre, it was found that there were no evaluation meetings held in the two villages. The Health Promotion Officer stated there were only two meetings, one was called as Survey Mawas Diri (SMD)/ Community Self Survey and the other was Musyawarah Mufakat Desa (MMD) or Village Community Meeting. The SMD meeting was to identify the problems, while the MMD meeting was to plan the intervention to address the problems. Those meetings were attended by cadres, members of PKK (Pemberdayaan dan Kesejahteraan Keluarga/ Family Welfare Movement), village midwives, health promotion officer, village officials, and DASIAT members. However, none of the MMD meetings, either in Meranti or Sendang, were attended by community leaders, women, and community representatives.

“… But actually, there is no Desa SIAGA that is truly active.” (Arum, 31-year-old, female, village midwife, Meranti).

The findings of this current study revealed that there was limited financial support for women that came from the community (which was one of the key criteria of Desa Siaga), the main financial resource remained the immediate family. If the immediate family had insufficient funds, then women would borrow money from others (friends and wider family), and this would lead to debt. Whilst the government provided a national health insurance scheme (Kartu Indonesia Sehat/ Indonesia Health Card/KIS) for childbirth, it was found that the insurance provision was problematic. It was reported that the insurance only covered the childbirth expenses, and it did not automatically cover the newborn expenses. For example, if the newborn needed to be cared by a paediatrician and the newborn had no insurance, then the mother needed to pay for the service. One of the women participants had difficulty in paying her hospital costs during her childbirth, which meant she was prevented from taking her baby home from the hospital until she paid the fees.

“When I was discharged from the hospital, I didn’t go home together with my baby. Because [the treatment of] my baby hadn’t been paid. I went home on Wednesday. I could bring my baby home on Saturday evening. Well, I was so sad since I couldn’t go home together with my baby. I borrowed some money from my boss to pay my baby’s hospital bill.” (Prapti, 39-year-old, post-natal woman, Sendang).

Lack of clearly defined roles

The main actors in the Desa SIAGA programmes were village midwives, cadres, health promotion officers of the community health centre (Puskesmas) and village government officers. However, the respective roles of each of these, in relation to the delivery of Desa SIAGA was not clear. It appeared that the key actors had different perspectives towards who was responsible for promoting Desa SIAGA.

The village midwives felt that it was the responsibility of the Health Promotion Officers and village government officers to promote the Desa SIAGA programme. They saw their key role as providing direct care to women and babies in the community and as just supporters of Desa SIAGA.

“… The Health Office may only provide a programme, but the one who [should] encourages the community is the Health Promotion Officers of the Community Health Centre and the village government. In these circumstances, the village midwives only give an approach to the community for Desa SIAGA so that the village can be truly alert.” (Retno, 44-year-old, female, Sendang village midwife).

The cadres saw themselves as providing support for the midwives, without any specific role or responsibility in relation to Desa SIAGA.

“I don’t have experience of preparing a woman for a birth. It is usually the village midwife who do that. The society trusts the village midwife more rather than [towards] the cadres. So, sometimes we [cadres] only accompany the village midwife. The one who conducts information session [about health] is usually the village midwife. We work together with the village midwife in the village. She [village midwife] has more knowledge [on health issues] and experience [than cadres]. We, as cadres, only assist the village midwife.” (Suryani, 40-year-old, female, cadre, Meranti).

The health promotion officers were identified as the key promoter of the Desa SIAGA. However, this was not necessarily aligned to how they perceived their role themselves. The village midwives’ participants identified that the promotion of the programme was the duty of the Health Promotion Officers from the community health centre. They suggested that one of the contributing factors to the limited information was that the Health Promotion Officers did not actively promote the programme to the community and stakeholders, due in a large part to high workload and officer shortage.

Meanwhile, the findings also showed that village government officers appeared to be the figure head of the Desa SIAGA but with limited actual involvement in the programme. The village head participants felt that it was the obligation of village midwives and DASIAT members to implement the programme including its promotion.

“There is no direct information [to residents] from the village government [about preparing pregnant women for safe childbirth], because such activities [mobilising residents to help pregnant women] have been managed by DASIAT and village midwives.” (Santi, 34-year-old, female, village head, Sendang).

Workload

Workload emerged as a key issue for the participants, in particular the midwives. The village midwives and cadre participants from both study sites reported that their duties were not only taking care of pregnant women and the babies but also the health of the community in general, for example in the prevention of dengue fever. The Desa SIAGA initiative has many programmes to overcome various health problems (in addition to maternity care), however it was identified that there was no clear role allocation as to who should carry out the specific elements of work.

“My role here as a village midwife is that I must be ready for 24 hours, whenever it is needed by the community. And my role here especially must be ready to help either the pregnant woman or anyone who is sick.” (Retno, 44-year-old, female, Sendang village midwife).

The midwife was challenged both by the geography and infrastructure of the area that needed to be covered and the range of responsibility, which included providing general health services (not just maternal and newborn health), working in the community health centre and disease surveillance. The workload hindered the village midwives performing their duties.

“When it is my shift to work in Puskesmas, then I couldn’t provide service in the PKD [Pos kesehatan desa/ village health post] so that women couldn’t meet me when they come to PKD.” (Arum, 31-year-old, female, Meranti Village Midwife).

The socio-cultural and religious context of maternity care in Indonesia

The findings from both case study sites suggested the community was not actively involved in the community participation under the Desa SIAGA initiative, most of the other participants reported that pregnancy and childbirth were the responsibility of their own families. It was suggested that the community felt that pregnancy was a normal part of life and therefore did not necessarily need additional support or attention. It became apparent throughout the interviews with the pregnant women that they did not want to think about the possibility that a negative outcome could happen during the pregnancy and childbirth.

“I suggested her [the expectant mother] to go to the hospital but she refused, neither to the community health centre. So, it happened [the expectant mother was treated in intensive care unit]. Well, there is still society that think pregnancy is a something usual and the baby will be delivered in a healthy condition, does the mother, she will be fine. It is very hard to give understanding about severe pre-eclampsia to the society, whereas pre-eclampsia is one of the severe cases in pregnancy.” (Arum, 31-year-old, female, Meranti Village Midwife).

These beliefs appeared to create a significant challenge the success of Desa SIAGA programme in achieving its goals.

“I believe that the main job of a village head is to constantly raise the awareness of the people. Ignorant people will become so stubborn if you let them be. They will think that nothing wrong will ever happen during pregnancy. Always like that. They think that a woman’s duty is [naturally] to carry babies. The older generation told them so… People think it’s the nature of women to be pregnant and to deliver babies…” (Santi, 34-year-old, female, Sendang village head).

The findings of the current study also demonstrated that there was a perception that pregnancy and childbirth were the responsibility of the women alone. These perceived values appeared to hinder men from being actively involved in the safe motherhood programme under the Desa SIAGA initiative.

“Yeah, I mean, pregnancy related matter is a women’s task, since it relates to the women themselves. If the men discuss pregnancy issues and if it is heard by somebody, it seems to be inappropriate. It is normal if the women [discuss] it. If the men, who do it, it is fine. But, in my opinion, it is not appropriate [for men to discussing about pregnant women]. It should be [discussed and managed] by cadres, Family Welfare Movement (PKK) and integrated health service posts.” (Darmadi, 60-year-old, male, Chief of Hamlet, Meranti).

In addition, women experienced a complex tension of being perceived as responsible for pregnancy and birth, but not always being in a position to make decisions. For instance, they were required to obtain their husbands’ permissions to decide the place of birth, even in the case of emergency.

“Usually it [where to give birth] is the patient [decision] along with the husband’s permission.” (Giyem, 43-year-old, female, cadre, Sendang).

The religious beliefs and practices were identified to traverse social and cultural values. In this study, religious beliefs were found to be a challenge for women’s freedom to make decisions and seek the most appropriate support. Religious practices limited women’s interaction with men, therefore men other than women’s husbands (such as neighbours) faced challenges to support women in the event of emergency.

“There was once a case of a pregnant woman experiencing severe pre-eclampsia to give birth. The pregnant woman did not want to make a decision [to be transported to the hospital] without her husband, even though her husband left for two days. She must be truly getting the permission from her husband because she is truly very Islamic. Well, I cannot blame, it is true that all actions must get permission by her husband, but if urgent means that we unavoidably have to make our own decisions.” (Arum, 31-year-old, female, Meranti Village Midwife).

These factors led to a limited empowerment of women and overall engagement in the Desa SIAGA programme and restricted the community in participating in the provision of support in maternity care. Nevertheless, this study also revealed that social, cultural and religious values acted as programme facilitators. It was identified that there were three main elements that influenced the community to support women in pregnancy and childbirth including social capital, altruism, and religious beliefs. The social capital was found to influence villagers to support pregnant women through a tradition called as ‘gotong royong’. The term ‘gotong royong’ in the Indonesian language means a sense of togetherness or working together/mutual assistance [24]. This tradition has encouraged the villagers to help each other.

“So, such thing [helping each other] will come as a result of gotong royong.” (Sudrajat, 62-year-old, male, Meranti village head) .

“I am happy of that (receiving help). Glad to get support from them [neighbours]. Because they [neighbours] are not indifferent to me. It’s not like in the city where they [people] live individually. People in the village tend to help each other.” (Mirna, 30-year-old, 9-month gestation, Meranti) .

In addition, altruism appeared to influence people to provide support to pregnant women. Sayekti, a neighbour participant, expressed that he felt a satisfaction after accompanying his neighbour’s wife in childbirth.

“At that time, he [my neighbour] had no friends for waiting her wife, then I just helped accompany him because I felt pity of him, he had no friend to talk. Just that… Well, I was happy, I can help my neighbours.” (Sayekti, 33-year-old, male, neighbour of a pregnant woman, Meranti).

Furthermore, it was identified that religious beliefs have influenced the community to spontaneously support women. Providing help for other people was believed to be one of religious tenets. Consequently, ignoring someone that needed help was considered to infringe God’s rules and was considered to be a disgrace.

“If one is religious, she/he is not afraid of people, but Lord the Almighty. So, without any instruction [to help others], they understand that if they violate the religious or social norms, they will be a shame.” (Tarmidi, 51-year-old, male, chief of neighbourhood unit, Meranti).

The findings revealed that the social, cultural, and religious values encouraged the community to help each other irrespective of whether they are ‘ordered’ to or not since providing help was regarded as a good deed. The fear of God’s power appeared to control people’s action. However, almost all participants identified support for women as a ‘spontaneous act’. Accordingly, it appeared that the assistance given was unorganised. Whereas, in birth preparation, there are a number of elements that need to be prepared in a comprehensive way.

Discussion

The Desa SIAGA initiative has the key combination of components of CPMs that previous evidence [10] has shown leads to successful outcomes, suggesting that it has the potential to improve MNH in Indonesia. This study found that the mechanism to implement and deliver Desa SIAGA is a multifaceted process and that several factors potentially contributed to the lack of the delivery and the uptake of the programme. The community support (as laid out in Desa SIAGA) appeared to be spontaneous and was not well organised, nor specifically enhanced by the implementation of Desa SIAGA. The findings strengthen the ongoing debate that community participation is a complex phenomenon and context dependent [10]. The delivery and implementation of a community participation model needs to consider the micro, meso, and macro elements that underpin the complexity of MNH.

Previous studies provide evidence that the commitment and active involvement of local leaders in community participation programmes could bring positive impacts to the programme [25–27]. In Malawi, the role of traditional leaders to encourage women to utilise maternal and neonatal healthcare services reduced the use of traditional birth attendants (TBAs), and that improved the MNH status [25]. All local governments (village heads, chief of hamlets, chief of neighbours) in this current study were aware that improving maternal health status was important. However, it was found that there was a lack of active involvement from the local government, and the village leaders handed over responsibility to protect community health to the village midwives and Community Health Workers (CHW).

This study suggests that lack of role clarity hindered the key actors in the Desa SIAGA programme to effectively deliver and implement the programme. These aligns with other studies that found many health professionals and CHWs were not aware of their roles in implementing or supporting community participation programmes [28, 29]. Similarly, previous studies demonstrated that unstipulated roles and responsibilities of key actors would lead poor participation in CPMs [28]. Inadequate supervision is reported to hinder CHWs to facilitate the community to actively participate in participatory programmes, since CHWs feel that they lacked the confidence and motivation to perform their duties [30, 31].

Furthermore, gender stereotypes were one of the barriers to the Desa SIAGA programme to reduce the three delays in childbirth (delay in decision making, delay to reach the health facility, and delay to receive care). These findings support the existing literature which argue that gender inequalities have been associated with maternal health problems and may inhibit improvement programmes [31]. In two studies, from Ghana and Pakistan, it was evident that women with limited power in decision making were less likely to have access to and use maternal health services [32, 33]. The studies found that the decisions to utilise maternal health services were taken by husbands and mothers-in-law. A study from Mozambique found that men tended to avoid accompanying their wives to attend antenatal services since it was taboo for men to take responsibility in pregnancy [34]. In contrast, the high level of male attendance at antenatal care in Uganda was evident to the improved uptake of skilled ANC [35]. These findings support the literature that suggests male involvement in maternal and child healthcare is crucial to improve MNH outcomes [36].

It was found that in some communities, mothers or mothers-in-law had the power in decision-making. Gendered roles appeared to change with age and status due to the influence of ethnicity. This finding is supported by the existing literature which argues the ethnicity influences the position of women’s autonomy in decision making [37]. All of the participants in this current study were of Javanese ethnicity, Javanese is renowned as one of many ethnicities in Indonesia that upholds patrilineal tradition in which men have the power to control over the family. Yet, the tradition also teaches the Javanese people to respect the older people [38]. Within the Javanese context, mothers earn a highly respected status since the figures of mothers nurture the children and the whole family [38]. Hence, it might be the reason why mothers or mothers-in-law can have similar power as men in a family.

Religious beliefs appear to bring a complex influence on MNH outcomes. Literature from LMICs demonstrates that religious beliefs are associated with the limited uptake of maternal health services [39, 40]. In India, religious judgments were reported to hinder CHWs to deliver their tasks, such as maternal health education and immunisations. It was reported that the Hindu CHWs (the majority) were not allowed to enter Muslim women’s houses, thus restricting the delivery of optimal care [41]. Nevertheless, a study in Nigeria [42] showed that it was not always the case that religious beliefs impeded women accessing maternal health services, and therefore some communities have found a way to overcome these barriers.

However, the findings of this study also illustrated that religious beliefs might have a positive aspect in delivering and implementing the Desa SIAGA programme. Religious beliefs appeared to be a significant motivation for the community to provide social support, in line with the principles of Desa SIAGA. These findings strengthened the body of literature that studied the association between religious beliefs and health improvement which found the religious beliefs could have both positive and negative implications in health development [43]. Previous studies found that the correlation between religiosity and social support came from the role of the religious congregations [43, 44]. A frequent and regular congregation were suggested to build a friendship and sense of belonging that allowed people to seek social support [45]. Different mechanisms of how religious beliefs influenced social support were found in this current study. It was revealed that social support was related to the religious tenet of ‘rewards’ from and ‘fear’ of God. In this case, providing support for other people would bring benefit in return, in contrast ignorance would cause punishment from God. The complexity of religious beliefs’ influences in Indonesian society showed that there is a need to use approaches which are sensitive to Islamic beliefs within the delivery and implementation of the Desa SIAGA programme to improve MNH outcomes.

Another facilitator in delivering and implementing the Desa SIAGA programme was the value of social capital. Social capital refers to social networks among individuals or groups in a community that result in social support [46]. Social capital drives individuals to invest in social relations and expect that these investments will give a benefit in return [47]. Evidence has shown that social capital is one of the determining factors to influence people in rural areas to support women in solving maternal health problems [48] and can result in health benefits [49, 50]. This study found that the strong social capital in the villages encouraged local participation to support women in pregnancy and childbirth. Social capital enables the community to work together and improve their capacities to resolve the problems by utilising their own resources [51]. In the Indonesian context, strong social capital is embedded in everyday life practices, for example working together to hold a wedding party and visit a community member who is ill [24]. This value is utilised by the national government to be the root of the development of the Desa SIAGA initiative. This finding obviously showed that although community values were already an asset in the success of the Desa SIAGA programme, yet the limited empowerment of the community in the initiative might weaken the positive value of social capital.

A core strength of this study was the use of the case study approach. The case study method has allowed the researcher to gain an insider perspective and (crucially) understand the underlying processes to enable a detailed identification of the facilitators and barriers to the Desa SIAGA initiative; providing evidence on ways the programme could be tailored and improved going forward. The study had several limitations. First, this study did not interview husbands and the wider family since this study intended to investigate the community support outside the core family. Desa SIAGA was aimed to empower the whole village to be alert and ready to support pregnant women. In the Indonesian context, it is common that husbands are working out of the village or town [52]. Hence, examining the support outside the core family could provide further insights. Secondly, this study was conducted in only two study sites only. It was relatively a small number compared to the total number of Desa SIAGA in Sukoharjo regency (167 Desa SIAGAs).

In addition, the first author and primary researcher in this study (VY) had experience as a community nurse working in maternal care. Since she is also a Javanese woman, and therefore knows the language as well as social and cultural values upheld by the participants. Thus, the participants may have been more open to the researcher and allowed the researcher to gain a deeper and wider understanding to the data gathered. The researcher was also aware that being an insider also might have disadvantages. For example, she shared the same religious context as the majority of participants which may have influenced her interpretation of the cultural and religious factors. However, other members of the research team (TS and LMc) had different cultural and religious background and through discussion contributed to the interpretation of the findings.

This research has shown that the mechanism to deliver and implement the CPM through Desa SIAGA is a multifaceted process which requires the involvement of micro, meso, and macro elements. In order to initiate the enhancement of the potential of the Desa SIAGA initiative to improve MNH in Indonesia, there is a need to develop further training and education of the programme for key players, including the health providers and community leaders. Further research is to investigate how women can be more involved within the Desa SIAGA programme and to understand the perspectives and experiences of husbands, wider family (mothers and mothers-in-law), and key stakeholders. Participatory evaluation research to build in an evaluation cycle in the Desa SIAGA initiative would also add value.

Conclusion

The potential benefits of Desa SIAGA are unlikely to be realised unless the implementation is underpinned by strong policy and supported by key actors who are committed to work in partnership across disciplines and sectors to deliver and implement the initiative. The findings of this study can inform policymakers in similar contexts in developing policy regarding the implementation of community participation programmes.
