Maternal separation increases anxiety-like phenotypes, fear responses, and alters Bdnf methylation in a predator odor exposure model of early life adversity
Authors: Nicholas J Collins, Sydney E Ross, Miguel Prysakar, Olivia Bigham, Luke W Ayers, Jeffrey B Rosen, Tania L Roth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly life adversity (ELA) can increase incidences of psychopathology later in life, partly through aberrant expression of the brain-derived neurotrophic factor (Bdnf) gene. One mechanism whereby ELA can alter Bdnf expression is through DNA methylation, typically reducing expression. Previously, our lab has utilized a predator odor model of ELA, whereby dams with their pups are exposed to either 2,5-dihydro-2,4,5-trimethylthiazoline (TMT), a synthetic derivative of fox feces, or butyric acid (BTA) as a noxious control for the first three postnatal weeks. Developmental exposure to TMT reduced adolescent freezing to TMT, suggesting the presence of the caregiver may be buffering later fear responses. However, the contribution of the caregiver versus mere developmental exposure remains unclear. In the present study, pups with or without their dams present were exposed to either TMT or BTA for the first three weeks of life. We subsequently assessed TMT-induced freezing, general anxiety-like behaviors in the open-field test, and collected brain tissue from the medial prefrontal cortex , amygdala, and periaqueductal gray at key developmental timepoints to assess Bdnf expression and DNA methylation. Results indicated that maternal separation increased freezing to TMT in males, lowered 40 kHz vocalizations, increased anxiety-like behavior, and produced epigenetic changes of Bdnf in a region and age-specific manner. Together, our data illustrate the powerful effects of brief disruptions in the caregiving environment on brain methylation and behavior. Such data help deepen our understanding of neurobiological mechanisms impacted by ELA and a potential underlying root cause of psychopathology.Keywords: Predator odor, Maternal separation, Epigenetic, DNA methylation, mPFC, Amygdala, PAG

Introduction

Early life adversity (ELA) has numerous consequences for offspring later in life, including increasing the propensity for depression (Dittrich et al., 2018; Humphreys et al., 2020; Klumparendt et al., 2019) anxiety (Lähdepuro et al., 2019; Fonzo et al., 2016; Quenneville et al., 2020), and bipolar disorder (Aas et al., 2016; Garno et al., 2005). However, not all of those who experience ELA display increased incidences of psychopathology (Collins et al., 2023), suggesting the presence of moderating factors that warrant further exploration. One well established moderating factor is adequate caregiving behavior from a parental figure. Indeed, in both humans and rodents, sound caregiving can mitigate the physiological, biological, and behavior consequences of exposure to ELA (Bernard et al., 2012, 2015, 2017; Dozier and Bernard, 2017; Miller, 2017; Olds et al., 2003). This phenomenon, termed parental buffering, helps explain how the availability of a parent or caregiver can improve stress regulation in offspring, which is observed across numerous species (Gunnar and Sullivan, 2016; Gunnar et al., 2015; Kikusui et al., 2019). Work by Sullivan and colleagues has demonstrated that maternal buffering of stress responses in rodent pups is mediated in part by suppression of corticosterone and modulation of amygdala–prefrontal circuitry, and is constrained to an early developmental sensitive period between postnatal days (PN) 10–15 (Santiago et al., 2017; Sullivan and Perry, 2015).While maternal buffering has been extensively characterized as a protective regulator of behavioral and neuroendocrine stress responses, including well-defined mechanisms involving corticosterone modulation and developmental timing (Gunnar et al., 2016; Santiago et al., 2017; Sullivan and Perry, 2015), how these processes translate into persistent changes in gene regulation within the brain remains less well understood.

Rodent models have been important research tools in advancing this field; exposure to caregiving can be easily manipulated, brain regions or genetics can be perturbed, and tissues can be easily collected for analysis (Phillips and Roth, 2019). One way to manipulate the early caregiving environment is through exposure to predator odor during early postnatal development. Indeed, work in our lab and others has found that exposure to predator odors in early development results in altered maternal behavior during the exposure period, with concomitant changes in anxiety-like, depressive-like, and innate fear behaviors in the progeny in later life (Ayers et al., 2015; Hacquemand et al., 2010; Mashoodh et al., 2009; Wu et al., 2020). For example, a preliminary investigation in our lab showed the ability of developmental exposure to the molecular derivative of fox feces, 2,5-dihydro-2,4,5-trimethylthiazoline (TMT) to increase maternal arched back nursing posture (ABN), and lower freezing behavior of adolescent rat pups (Ayers et al., 2015). However, given the design of that study, it is unclear whether mere developmental odor exposure and/or if changes in caregiving quality were driving the observed change in offspring behavior. Moreover, it is unclear what mechanisms in the brain may have contributed to the behavioral changes.

One neural mechanism that may help explain these changes in offspring behavior is epigenetics, literally translating to “above the genome”. As a field, epigenetics seeks to understand how genetic material is activated, or deactivated, in different environmental contexts (Moore, 2015). Seminal work by Levine and colleagues first demonstrated that early-life experiences, particularly variations in maternal care and stress exposure, produce enduring alterations in stress responsivity and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis function across development (Levine, 1967; Levine, 1957). These foundational studies established the principle that early environmental conditions can program long-term biological and behavioral outcomes, which laid the groundwork for later mechanistic investigations. Indeed, future work extended this framework to identify epigenetic processes, most notably DNA methylation, as a molecular substrate through which these early developmental experiences may become biologically embedded (McGowan et al., 2009; Roth et al., 2009; Meaney and Szyf, 2005; Weaver et al., 2004). Changes in epigenetic activity, in particular DNA methylation in which methyl groups are added to cytosines, continue to be explored to help us understand how ELA can lead to pathological behavior, and how the caregiving environment can be protective. Indeed, in both human and animal models, ELA has been associated with both aberrant epigenetic activity and psychopathological behaviors (Fuchikami et al., 2011; McGowan et al., 2009; Nestler, 2014; Wiegand et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021), both of which may be mitigated or rescued by the presence, availability, or quality of a caregiver or through other strategies that can alter the epigenome (Collins et al., 2022; Doherty et al., 2019; McGowan et al., 2009; Roth et al., 2009; Weaver et al., 2004).

One critically important gene throughout development is the Brain-derived neurotrophic factor gene (Bdnf), which is broadly involved in brain development and cellular survival (Ortiz-López et al., 2017), synaptic plasticity (Duman et al., 2016), and learning and memory (Miranda et al., 2019). Long-lasting aberrant Bdnf expression or DNA methylation in numerous brain regions and peripheral tissues has been associated with both ELA (Choy et al., 2008; Collins et al., 2022; Dandi et al., 2018; Doherty et al., 2016; Roth et al., 2009) and increased incidences of psychopathology (Grande et al., 2010; Perroud et al., 2013; Suliman et al., 2013; Yang et al., 2020). In particular, deficits in caregiving behavior have been shown to cause aberrant Bdnf epigenetic activity (Collins et al., 2022; Doherty et al., 2019; Keller et al., 2019a; Roth et al., 2009), a known biomarker of depression (Lee and Kim, 2010), with the aberrant methylation responsive to pharmacological or behavioral treatments (Campbell et al., 2024; Doherty et al., 2019; Keller et al., 2019a; Polyakova et al., 2015; Roth et al., 2009).

The goal of this study was to first examine the role of maternal buffering during TMT exposure on offspring behavior. We followed exposure methods (through PN21) established in our previous study (Ayers et al., 2015), though we acknowledge that work has shown that the ability of maternal presence to buffer against noxious stimuli declines by PN15 (Santiago et al., 2017; Sullivan and Perry, 2015). Second, we wanted to examine whether maternal buffering also produced epigenetic changes in the offspring. While methylation is often a critical up-stream regulator of gene expression, we first examined gene expression followed by methylation to see if any observed gene expression findings were concomitant with methylation findings. Rodent pups were exposed to either TMT with or without their dam present, during their first three weeks of life. In adolescence, rodent pups were then tested for freezing to TMT and were administered the open-field test to assess anxiety-like behaviors. Finally, whole brains were collected to assess Bdnf expression and DNA methylation levels in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), amygdala, and periaqueductal gray (PAG), all brain regions known to be affected by early experiences and caregiving and involved in the circuitry underlying the examined behaviors.

Methods

All animal procedures were conducted with the approval of the University of Delaware Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC).

Subjects

Subjects were derived from Long-Evans timed-pregnant dams acquired from Charles River Laboratories. On the day of birth (PN0) dams and their pups were left undisturbed. On PN1, litters were culled down to an equal number of males and females (when possible) not exceeding 12 per litter (4-8 males or females per litter). Animals underwent weaning between PN21-23 and were housed in same-sex pairs from the same litter. From each litter, two animals from each sex (2 males and 2 females) were assigned to undergo behavioral testing, two animals from each sex (2 males and 2 females) were assigned for PN22 tissue collection, and two animals from each sex (2 males and 2 females) were assigned for PN30 tissue collection. All animals were housed in clear polypropylene cages in a temperature-controlled colony room on a 12H light/dark cycle (lights on at 7:00am) and given ad libitum access to food and water. 9-10 litters were used per group (odor exposure x maternal presence) for behavioral testing analyses.

Dam and pup odor exposures

Starting on PN1-3, pups with or without (maternal separation; MS) their dams were exposed to either butyric acid (BTA) or TMT for the first three weeks of life (PN1-21) for 20 min per day, 5 days per week (animals were not run on weekends). Exact PNs varied slightly between litters depending on birth date; for example, pups born immediately before a weekend began exposures on the following Monday. Dams and their pups were initially assigned to one of four conditions: BTA, BTA-MS, TMT, or TMT-MS. Dams with their pups or only the pups in the MS conditions were transported in their home cage into a ventilated fume-hood. The conditions by which the dam was present were left alone to acclimate each day to testing conditions for 10 min, prior to the 20-min odor exposure.

Liquid samples of TMT (300μmole total, 150μmole/19.4 μl per strip), or BTA (900μmole total, 450μmole/39.6 μl per strip) were then applied to two strips of filter paper and affixed to an isolator lid placed on top of the home cage. The dose of TMT was chosen due to previous work in our laboratory demonstrating robust behavioral effects given this dose developmentally (Ayers et al., 2015) and later in life (Asok et al., 2013; Ayers et al., 2015; Wallace and Rosen, 2000). Since work has established that TMT is three times more volatile than BTA (Hotsenpiller and William, 1997), a comparable dose of BTA was administered to serve as a noxious control. A white noise machine (HoMedics Sound Spa Sound Machine) was set to medium volume on the waterfall option throughout experimental testing.

Up to two dams and their litters were run concurrently in separate chambers, with only like-conditions run together. During the exposure, dams had access to food and water ad libitum. After each 20-min exposure, the filter paper strips were removed, and the cages were left to air out for 10 min prior to being returned to the colony. To avoid cross-exposure of odorants, all BTA conditions were run prior to TMT animals, and the fume hood was wiped down with 70% ethanol solution in-between each 20-min exposure. To minimize an order-effect confound, when feasible within each condition the cage order was alternated daily.

Maternal behavior recordings and pup USV's

During the 20-min exposure period, maternal behaviors (licking/grooming and ABN) were recorded (Sony Handycam) and later scored continuously for 6 out of the 15 total exposure sessions (two sessions during each postnatal week), for only groups with mom present. This majority subset was scored by two trained observers, with the average score being tallied for each video file. All interrater reliability measures were analyzed using Pearson's R, with each coefficient for the correlation of behavior scores between observers being >.85.

Pup 40 kHz ultrasonic vocalizations were collected with Batbox III D, UK, which were collected concurrently with video recordings during each session (in both MS and maternally present groups). Vocalizations were scored in 1-min time bins, and data came from the same 6 exposure sessions that examined maternal behavior. 40 kHz USV's were scored by two trained observers, with the average score being taken for each file and calculated as a percent. Interrater reliability was analyzed using Pearson's R, with the correlation coefficient being r = .81 between raters.

Freezing to TMT and body weight

From PN28-29, pups were transported to the testing room and handled for 5- minutes each under testing conditions (room light off; fume-hood light on). On PN30, all subjects were tested for their behavioral response to TMT (100μmole total, 50μmole/6.50μl/strip) for 10 min. All behavioral testing during this period occurred in four cylindrical Plexiglas chambers (8.6 cm diameter; 20.0 cm length). Liquid samples of TMT were affixed to filter paper and placed on the inserts inside of the chamber which kept subjects inside the chamber and from visualizing other subjects being tested at the same time.

Freezing behavior was captured via a video recorder transmitted to a Dell computer, and recorded using FreezeFrame software (Actimetrics, Wilmette, IL) set to four-chamber mode at 3.75 fps and was quantified by FreezeView software (Actimetrics). Individual movement thresholds were manually set for each animal as described elsewhere (see Asok et al., 2013; Rosen et al., 2008), with freezing being defined as a cessation of all movement besides breathing for periods of 1s or longer (Blanchard and Blanchard, 1969). Following TMT exposure, animals were left to air out for 10 min before being returned to the colony, and chambers were wiped with a 70% ethanol solution between animals. The mean percent freezing for each animal was calculated for the duration of the 10-min testing period. To determine if body weight correlated with freezing, all animals were weighed prior to testing.

Open field testing

From PN33-PN34, pups were transported to the open-field testing room and left for 10 min undisturbed to acclimate to testing conditions. On PN35, animals were placed in a circular open-field arena (H:20.3 cm x D:40.6 cm x C:127 cm) to freely explore for 10 min. All animals were tested under red light illumination, with a white noise machine (HoMedics Sound Spa Sound Machine) set to medium volume playing throughout the testing period. All open-field testing occurred in the morning (between 7am and 12pm). The number of center entries, rearing bouts, peripheral line crosses, and the duration in the center was video recorded using a Sony Handycam and later scored by two trained observers. For all measurements, the interrater reliability was analyzed using Pearson R, with the correlation coefficient for each behavior being r > .75. Line crosses were counted when an animal moved its forelimbs over a demarcated subsection of the arena, rearing behaviors were counted when an animal stood on its hindlegs, and center entries/duration were counted when an animal placed their forelimbs over an isolated circular subsection of the arena in the center.

Brain and tissue collection

To assess immediate epigenetic consequences following developmental exposure of odorants as well as the stability of epigenomic changes, on PN22 and PN30, a separate subset of littermate animals not undergoing behavioral testing in adolescence (2 males and 2 females per litter, when available) were sacrificed. 1 male and 1 female were then chosen at random from each litter, at each time point to undergo biochemical analyses. Whole brains were flash frozen and stored at −80C until later processing. Brains were sectioned at 100 μm in a cryostat (Leica Biosystems), at −13C, with the mPFC, amygdala, and PAG being dissected using coordinates from a rat atlas (Paxinos and Watson, 2007). These tissues were stored at −80C for later processing.

Bdnf gene expression and DNA methylation

As described by our lab elsewhere (e.g. Collins et al., 2022, 2024), DNA and RNA were extracted from the mPFC and amygdala using the Qiagen AllPrep DNA/RNA Mini kit, with quantity and quality of nucleic acid being analyzed using spectrophotometry (NanoDrop 2000). DNA and RNA from the PAG were extracted using the Qiagen AllPrep DNA/RNA Micro kit, due to less tissue volume. DNA and RNA were stored at −80C, with freeze/thaw cycles being kept to a minimum. RNA was then processed through reverse transcription utilizing a cDNA synthesis kit (Qiagen Quantitect Reverse Transcription Kit) according to manufacturer instructions. Real-time PCR (Bio-rad CFX96) was conducted using Taqman probes on cDNA (Thermo Fisher Life Technologies). For each target gene, Tubulin was used as a reference gene, and all PCR reactions were run in triplicates. The average CQ value was taken for each triplicate. Gene expression was calculated for target genes using the 2−ΔΔCT method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).

To quantify methylation at each gene target, the DNA was bisulfite converted (Li and Tollefsbol, 2011), using the Qiagen Epitect Bisulfite Kit, and stored at −20 °C. Methylation levels of each gene target were determined using methylation specific real-time PCR (MSP; Bio-Rad CFX96) as described elsewhere (Hattori and Ushijima, 2011; e.g., Roth et al., 2009). For each target gene, Tubulin was used as a reference gene, and all PCR reactions were run in triplicates. The average CQ value was taken for each triplicate. Methylation was calculated for target genes using the 2−ΔΔCT method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). Bdnf probes and primers were used based on sequences previously used in our laboratory (see Roth et al., 2009).

Statistical analyses

All Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9. For behavioral measures, whenever possible, the data from 2 males and 2 females were each averaged, with each score representing the male and female value of each litter respectively. The analyses of maternal behaviors (percent time licking/grooming and ABN) were conducted using two-way mixed ANOVA (odor exposure x observation period). 40 kHz vocalizations were analyzed using a three-way mixed ANOVA (maternal presence x odor exposure x observation period). For each mixed ANOVA, sphericity was not assumed and either a Geisser-Greenhouse (ε < .75) or Hyun-Feldt (ε > .75) correction was applied.

Freezing to TMT, body weight, and all open-field measures were analyzed using a three-way ANOVA (sex x odor exposure x maternal presence) and later followed up with a two-way ANOVA (where appropriate). Post-hoc tests utilized the Bonferroni multiple comparisons test as the alpha correction method. All statistical analyses were considered significant at p < .05. Any outliers were analyzed using Grubb's test (a = .05) or ROUT analyses (Q = 1%) where appropriate and were conducted both prior to the three-way ANOVA and after collapsing on a factor (if applicable).

All gene expression and methylation data were analyzed using a three-way ANOVA (sex x maternal presence x odor exposure) and later followed up with a two-way ANOVA (when appropriate). Post-hoc tests utilized the Bonferroni or Tukey multiple comparisons test as the alpha correction method. All statistical analyses were considered significant at p < .05. Any outliers were analyzed using Grubb's test (a = .05) or ROUT analyses (Q = 1%) where appropriate and were conducted both prior to the three-way ANOVA and after collapsing on a factor (if applicable).

Results

Maternal and pup behavior

For each mixed ANOVA below, sphericity was not assumed and either a Geisser-Greenhouse (ε < .75) or Hyun-Feldt (ε > .75) correction was applied. A two-way mixed ANOVA examining the effect of odor exposure across the six observation periods on licking/grooming revealed no effect of observation period, F (3.36,60.49) = 1.67, p = .18, no effect of odor exposure, F (1,18) = .30, p = .59, and no significant interaction, F (5,90) = 1.76, p = .13 (Fig. 1a). Similarly, a two-way ANOVA examining the effect of odor exposure across the six observation periods on ABN revealed no significant effect of odor exposure, F (1,18) = 1.0, p = .33, and no significant time by odor exposure interaction, F (5,90) = 2.01, p = .07 (Fig. 1b). However, there was a significant main effect of observation period, F (3.88,69.91) = 3.61, p = .01, such that all dams decreased their ABN across the observation periods.

Odor exposures had no effect on maternal licking/grooming (A) or arched back nursing (B) during the developmental exposure period. Data are shown for six observation periods: periods 1-2 (PN1-7), periods 3-4 (PN8-14), periods 5-6 (PN15-21). n = 10 litters/group, error bars represent standard error.

To elucidate if developmental odor exposure or maternal presence influenced infant 40 kHz vocalizations, a three-way ANOVA examining the effect of maternal presence, odor exposure, and observation period on infant 40 kHz ultrasonic vocalizations was conducted. Results from the three-way ANOVA revealed a significant observation period by maternal presence interaction F (5,175) = 6.26, p < .0001, and a significant time by maternal presence by odor exposure interaction, F (5,175) = 3.28, p = .0074 (Fig. 2). Post-hoc analyses revealed that, regardless of odor exposure, pups without their mothers present displayed a significant reduction of 40 kHz vocalizations at several observation periods. Specifically, at observation period 1 (p < .0001) and 6 (p = .02), animals that experienced maternal separation (MS) had significantly lowered 40 kHz vocalizations (p = .02) compared to animals with maternal presence.

Maternal separations, but not predator odor exposure, significantly lowered 40 kHz vocalizations during observation period 1 (PN1-4) and 6 (PN17-21) throughout the three-week paradigm. Data are shown for six observation periods: periods 1-2 (PN1-7), periods 3-4 (PN8-14), periods 5-6 (PN15-21). n = 9-10 litters per group, error bars represent standard error. ∗∗∗p < .001, ∗p < .05.

Freezing to TMT and body weight

A three-way ANOVA examining the effect of sex, maternal presence, and developmental odor exposure on PN30 freezing to TMT revealed a significant sex by maternal presence interaction, F (1,72) = 6.15, p = .02 (Fig. 3a). Post-hoc analyses collapsed across infant odor exposure revealed that males, but not females, with MS throughout development displayed a significant (p = .03) increase in PN30 freezing to TMT compared to males with maternal presence. These data together suggest that males and females display behavioral differences to TMT as a function of MS in our paradigm.

Maternal separations (MS), but not developmental odor exposure, increased freezing to TMT in males, but not females (A). Freezing was defined as the percentage of time spent immobile during TMT exposure. Animals exposed to TMT had significantly increased body weight compared to animals exposed to BTA (B). However, body weight was not a significant predictor of freezing levels to TMT (C), suggesting the body weight findings did not meaningfully influence freezing results. In a separate experiment, we found no observed sex differences between males or females at increasing doses of TMT (D). n = 8-20 animals per group, error bars represent standard error. ∗∗∗p < .001, ∗p < .05.

To determine if there were any developmental consequences of MS or predator odor exposure on body weight, a three-way ANOVA examining the effect of sex, maternal presence, and developmental odor exposure on PN30 body weight was conducted (Fig. 3b). Results indicated a significant main effect of sex, F (1,147) = 62.22, p < .0001, such that males weighed significantly more than females. There was also a main effect of odor exposure, F (1,147) = 16.06, p < .0001, such that animals developmentally exposed to TMT weighed more than animals developmentally exposed to BTA.

Further, to assess whether body weight was significantly predictive of freezing to TMT on PN30 and acting as a confounder, a correlation utilizing Pearson's R examining body weight and freezing to TMT was conducted (Fig. 3c). Results indicated that body weight did not significantly correlate with freezing levels, r (141) = .06, p = .50. These data suggest that differences observed in freezing were not due to fluctuations in body weight.

Finally, to elucidate if the differences observed in freezing were partly due to sex differences in sensitivity to the odorants, a subset of animals (Fig. 3d) from the BTA-MS group were tested for freezing to BTA (900μmole total, 450μmole/39.6 μl per strip), and TMT at the developmental exposure dose (300μmole total, 150μmole/19.4 μl per strip) or experimental freezing dose (100μmole total, 50μmole/6.50μl/strip). A two-way mixed ANOVA (sex x consecutive odorant delivery) indicated a main effect of odorant type/dose exposed, F (1.92, 26.94) = 38.95, p < .0001, but no significant main effect of sex, F (1,14) = .26, p = .61, and no sex by odorant interaction, F (2,28) = .08, p = .92. Post-hoc analyses revealed that animals exposed to TMT at doses of 100μmole and 300μmole froze significantly more compared to BTA at a dose of 900μmole (p = .04 and p < .0001, respectively). Furthermore, TMT at a dose of 300μmole also instigated significantly more freezing bouts compared to TMT at a dose of 100μmole (p < .0001).

These results replicate previous findings of a dose-response function to TMT in our laboratory (Rosen et al., 2008) further suggesting the differences in freezing previously observed are not due to sex related odor sensitivities per se. Taken together, data suggest that MS influence later fear responses to TMT in a sex-specific manner, which is not influenced by differences in body weight or sex differences in TMT associated fear responses.

Open-field testing

To examine if there were any differences in general anxiety-like behavior, separate three-way ANOVAs examining the effect of sex, maternal presence, and developmental odor exposure was conducted on peripheral line crosses, rearing behavior, center duration, and the number of center entries in the open-field test. In regard to general locomotive behaviors, there was a main effect of sex, F (1,71) = 12.94, p = .0006, such that females made significantly more peripheral line crosses than males (Fig. 4a). Similarly, there was a main effect of rearing behavior such that females engaged in significantly more rearing bouts in the paradigm compared to males (p = .03) (Fig. 4b). There were no differences in peripheral line crosses or rearing bouts as a function of maternal presence or developmental odor exposure.

Females displayed significantly increased locomotive behaviors in the open-field test, including increased peripheral line crosses (A) and rearing bouts (B). However, both males and females with maternal separation (MS) displayed increased anxiety-like behaviors, with observed decreased center entries (C) and decreased time spent in the center of the apparatus (D). There were no effects of developmental odor exposure on anxiety-like behaviors. n = 9-10 animals per group, error bars represent standard error. ∗∗∗p < .001, ∗∗p < .01, ∗p < .05.

In regard to measures of anxiety-like measures, there were no effects of sex, F (1,71) = .79, p = .38, and no effect of developmental odor exposure, F (1,71) = 1.32, p = .25 on center entries (Fig. 4c). However, there was a main effect of maternal presence, F (1,71) = 6.61, p = .012, such that animals that underwent MS had significantly reduced center entries compared to animals with maternal presence. Similarly, there was also a main effect of maternal presence on the duration animals spent in the center, F (1,71) = 9.46, p = .003, such that animals that were maternally separated spent significantly less time in the center compared to animals who had mom present during the paradigm (Fig. 4d). There were no effects of developmental odor exposure, F (1,71) = .41, p = .52, or sex, F (1,71) = 1.07, p = .30 on time spent in the center of the open field. Taken together, these data suggest that males and females undergoing brief MS over the three-week paradigm later display anxiety-like behavior in the open-field test compared to pups without MS.

PN22: Bdnf Exon IX mRNA and DNA methylation

To elucidate if any of the observed anxiety- and fear-like phenotypes were associated with aberrant Bdnf activity, DNA and RNA from the mPFC, amygdala, and PAG were collected on PN22 after the conclusion of the paradigm. All tissues were collected in a separate subset of animals not undergoing behavioral testing. In all tissues, there were no sex differences in either Bdnf activity, DNA nor RNA. Accordingly, data were collapsed for sex and a two-way ANOVA (maternal presence x developmental odor exposure) was conducted. In the mPFC, there were no observed effects associated with developmental TMT exposure (Fig. 5a). However, there was an observed main effect of maternal presence, F (1,63) = 20.31, p < .0001, such that animals with MS had significantly lowered Bdnf Exon IX expression compared to animals with maternal presence. In the amygdala, there was a significant main effect of odor exposure, F (1,67) = 14.53, p = .0003, such that TMT exposed animals had significantly lower Bdnf Exon IX expression compared to BTA exposed animals (Fig. 5b). Moreover, there was a main effect of maternal presence, F (1,67) = 9.22, p = .0034, such that animals with MS had significantly lower Bdnf Exon IX expression compared to animals with mom present during the paradigm. In the PAG, we observed a significant interaction between maternal availability and odor exposure, F (1,61) = 7.27, p = .003 (Fig. 5c). Post-hoc tests utilizing Tukey's correction method revealed that animals in the BTA-MS group had increased expression compared to animals in the BTA group (p = .04). Taken together, gene expression data suggest that MS drives reductions in Bdnf gene expression in the mPFC and the amygdala, whereas TMT exposure is sufficient to lower expression in the amygdala. In the PAG, effects are more nuanced, whereby animals exposed to BTA and MS show increased gene expression.

Given that DNA methylation is a common mechanism that drives changes in gene expression, we next wanted to see if any of our gene expression findings paralleled differences of DNA methylation at Bdnf Exon IX. Similarly, there were no observed effects of sex, so all data were collapsed on sex, and a two-way ANOVA (maternal presence x developmental odor exposure) was conducted. In the mPFC, we observed a significant effect MS had significantly reduced Bdnf Exon IX methylation compared to animals with mom present (Fig. 5d). In the amygdala, we similarly observed a main effect of maternal presence, F (1,74) = 4.51, p = .037, such that animals with MS have significantly decreased methylation compared to animals with mom present (Fig. 5e). In the PAG, a two-way ANOVA examining the effect of odorant and maternal availability on methylation revealed a significant two-way interaction F (1,62) = 11.32, p = .0013 (Fig. 5f). Post-hoc tests utilizing Tukey's method revealed that animals in the BTA-MS group had significantly decreased methylation compared to animals in the BTA group (p = .0049). These data largely align with the idea that increased DNA methylation is typically concomitant with reductions in gene expression in the PAG.

PN30: Bdnf Exon IX mRNA and DNA methylation

We next wanted to see if any of the observed epigenetic changes were stable across time to a timepoint that matched when we ran anxiety-like behavioral measures in the open-field test. Whole brains were removed and flash frozen on PN30, and RNA and DNA was extracted from the mPFC, amygdala, and PAG for expression analyses. All tissues were collected in a separate subset of animals not undergoing behavioral testing. Similar to PN22 data, there were no observed sex differences, so all data were collapsed across sex, and a two-way ANOVA (maternal presence x developmental odor exposure) was conducted.

In the mPFC there was a main effect of maternal presence, F (1,70) = 4.35, p = .04, such that animals in the MS group had significantly higher Bdnf Exon IX expression compared to animals with mom present during the paradigm (Fig. 6a). These findings are in direct contrast to the PN22 data, suggesting that Bdnf Exon IX expression levels in the mPFC initially decrease immediately after MS, but are increased by PN30 comparable to control animals. In the amygdala, there was an observed main effect of infant odor exposure, F (1,73) = 4.72, p = .033, such that animals exposed to TMT (M = 1.32, SD = .72) had significantly higher Bdnf Exon IX expression compared to BTA animals (M = 1.02, SD = .48) (Fig. 6b). Similar to the findings in the mPFC, these data in the amygdala are in direct contrast to PN22 animals, which suggests that there is an immediate decrease of Bdnf Exon IX expression after the paradigm, followed by an increase on PN30 compared to control animals. In the PAG, a two-way ANOVA examining the effect of odorant and maternal availability on gene expression yielded a main effect of odorant, F (1,67) = 9.79, p = .0026, such that exposure to TMT group significantly lowered expression compared to groups exposed to BTA (Fig. 6c). Moreover, we also observed a main effect of maternal presence, F (1,67) = 7.726, p = .0041, such that the maternally separated groups had significantly lower expression compared to groups with mom present. Together, data suggest that the earlier observed changes in gene expression in PN22 are largely transient in the brain regions analyzed.

Finally, we examined whether any of the PN30 changes in gene expression were concomitant with any changes in DNA methylation of Bdnf Exon IX. In the mPFC, we observed a significant infant odor exposure by maternal presence interaction, F (1,66) = 14.97, p = .0003 (Fig. 6d). Post-hoc tests utilizing Tukey's alpha correction method revealed that animals in the TMT group had significantly increased methylation compared to the BTA group (p < .0001), BTA-MS group (p = .0162), and TMT-MS group p = .0046). Moreover, animals in the TMT exposed group had significantly higher methylation compared to BTA exposed animals, F (1,71) = 11.05, p = .0014. Given increased DNA methylation is typically (but not exclusively) concomitant with decreased expression, these data partially align with the previously observed expression finding, with the TMT group having higher methylation and lower expression of Bdnf Exon IX in the mPFC. Moreover, the effect of developmental TMT exposure increasing methylation of Bdnf Exon IX in the mPFC does not appear until this later timepoint, suggesting that the early life experiences can yield delayed epigenetic consequences at this locus.

In the amygdala, there was a significant infant odor exposure by maternal presence interaction, F (1,67) = 5.76, p = .0192 (Fig. 6e). The two-way interaction was followed up with Tukey's post-hoc alpha correction criterion applied for all pairwise comparisons. Post-hoc results indicated that animals in the TMT-MS group had significantly higher methylation compared to animals in the BTA group (p < .0001), the BTA-MS group (p = .0005), and the TMT group (p < .0001). These data indicate that methylation levels of this locus are changing as a function of environmental conditions experienced. In the PAG, A two-way ANOVA examining the effect of odorant and maternal presence on Bdnf exon IX methylation revealed a significant two-way interaction, F (1,73) = 5.53, p = .02 (Fig. 6f). Post-hoc tests utilizing Tukey's correction method revealed that animals in the TMT-MS group had increased methylation compared to animals in the TMT group (p = .0451) and the BTA-MS group (p = .012). There was no statistically significant increase in methylation of the TMT-MS group when compared to the BTA group (p = .0603). Given that the TMT-MS group had the greatest reduction in expression and highest observed methylation, as previously concluded with the PN22 data, it is plausible that methylation in the PAG may be associated with the observed changes in gene expression.

Taken together, similar to the PN22 data, it is likely not methylation driving the observed changes in gene expression in the mPFC and amygdala. However, data in the PAG suggest that DNA methylation is a possible mechanism worthy of further review. Future work is of course necessary to determine any causal relationships between the methylation and gene expression data.

Discussion

We have several main findings worth noting in the present study. First, we show that while developmental exposure to TMT did not alter maternal caregiving behaviors toward offspring, MS lowered 40 kHz pup vocalizations at specific time points. Second, we report that males, but not females, who were maternally separated had increased freezing to TMT at PN30 irrespective of odor exposure during development. This was not due to sex differences in sensitivity to the odorant nor changes in body weight. Third, we observed increased anxiety-like behavior in the open-field test as a function of MS. Finally, we observed time-dependent changes in gene expression and methylation of Bdnf in the mPFC, amygdala, and PAG.

Behavior

We did not observe the ability of TMT to change ABN behavior. In our prior work we observed a transient effect, such that ABN was significantly higher during the first half of the TMT exposures and then was significantly reduced in the second half (Ayers et al., 2015). Here, we do see a reduction in ABN behavior on last observation period 6, though this was not a significant result based on our ANOVA analysis. One possibility for the incongruence in results is that specific exposure days may be particularly relevant. Consistent with this notion is work from others measuring maternal behavior in the presence of predator odor that has documented the variability in maternal behavior across specific exposure days (Mashoodh et al., 2009; St. Cyr and McGowan, 2015). Consequently, one limitation that may preclude a fully comprehensive comparison between our studies is that exposures occurred on slightly variable days between PN 1–21 to avoid weekend testing. While all pups received the same total number of exposures, not every pup was exposed on the exact same postnatal days. Our divergent findings suggest that the specific postnatal days of exposure may represent an important variable to consider when interpreting our results across studies. Future research should seek to find the critical window whereby ELA, in the form of predator odor, has long-lasting behavioral and molecular consequences on the progeny.

Though TMT did not affect maternal behavior, we found that MS is sufficient to effectively disturb pup behavior. Specifically, pups separated from dams emitted less 40 kHz USV's during both early and late observation periods compared to pups with their dam present. Other studies have reported changes in USVs with manipulation of the caregiving environment, with increased 40 kHz vocalizations produced by MS (Portfors, 2007; Simola, 2015) or maltreatment (Boulanger-Bertolus et al., 2017; Collins et al., 2022; Roth et al., 2009). Additional work has demonstrated a shift in USV frequency during MS bouts and both the co-occurrence of ∼40 kHz and ∼60 kHz vocalizations during stressful experiences (Boulanger-Bertolu and Mouly, 2021; Boulanger-Bertolus et al., 2017 Kaidbey et al., 2019).

In the present study, we replicated previous findings that TMT exposure increased freezing behavior (Rosen et al., 2008), indicating this odorant indeed produces a robust and innate freezing response. We also found that males undergoing MS had enhanced freezing responses to TMT compared to males with maternal presence, with no similar difference observed in females. Moreover, we report that this sex difference was not due to sensitivities in detecting the odorant or body weight, suggesting the difference may be in part due to differences in maternal availability or sex hormones. This aligns with prior work showing that dams may provide differential care to male versus female pups under stress, which can influence offspring stress reactivity and behavioral outcomes (Keller et al., 2019b;Hao et al., 2011; Barha et al., 2007; Moore and Morelli, 1979). Consequently, it is plausible that MS may impact males and females differently, as each sex may rely differently on maternal behavior for physiological stress regulation. Finally, given it has recently been established that MS alone may induce behavioral changes in the dam (Alves et al., 2020), it is unclear how maternal behavior changes as a function of MS and the sex of the progeny. Future studies should seek to establish and further refine sex-specific interactions from the dam in the context of ELA.

We also found that developmental exposure to TMT, but not MS, increased body weight, as measured on PN30. Given no observed differences in maternal care during the assay, there are several possible explanations worth considering. First, it is possible that dams exposed to TMT engaged in increased maternal care after the formative assay was concluded and they were returned to the colony room, as our lab has previously reported increased maternal care with TMT exposure (Ayers et al., 2015). Second, it has been reported that specific developmental stressors can shape metabolic and neuroendocrine signaling (Spencer and Malik, 2019;Malcon et al., 2020), leading to increased weight gain. In the present study, we are not able to discern if the weight was gained prior to or post-weaning, and future work should explore how specific types of stressors influence diet, metabolism, and HPA-axis regulation.

In the open-field test, we observed that both males and females displayed increased generalized anxiety-like behavior as a function of MS, with no behavioral consequences of developmental TMT exposure. It is worth noting that MS protocols in the literature are highly heterogeneous (Wang et al., 2020), and males are more commonly studied, making direct comparisons between studies difficult. For example, while some studies have reported an MS-induced increase in anxiety-like phenotypes in both male and females (Jin et al., 2018), others have shown this phenotype only in males (Cevik et al., 2023 Malcon et al., 2020), others have reported decreased anxiety-like behavior in females but not males Eklund and Arborelius (2006); or no change in behavior (Hulshof et al., 2011; Millstein and Holmes, 2007). Brief bouts of MS appear sufficient to alter some behavioral domains in males, whereas longer or more repeated bouts may be necessary to produce comparable effects in females (Eklund and Arborelius, 2006; Lehmann et al., 1999; Malcon et al., 2020). Nonetheless, our data suggest that even brief bouts of MS are sufficient to induce a generalized anxiety-like phenotype in both sexes.

Gene expression and epigenetic changes

Along with behavioral consequences, we found epigenetic consequences of MS or TMT exposure that depended on the age and brain area examined. Gene expression and methylation effects varied depending on developmental timing, maternal availability, and brain region. Transient and later emerging, as well as brain-region dependent epigenetic consequences, are observations consistent with those in another line of our research examining the effects of exposure to ELA in the context of caregiving (e.g. Roth et al., 2009, 2014).

On PN22, in the mPFC we report lowered Bdnf exon IX expression immediately after the paradigm as a consequence of MS. Similar studies demonstrate that a MS paradigm of 3 h during the first three weeks of life transiently lowered Bdnf exon IX expression in the mPFC on PN7 and PN10, with no differences observed on PN14, and a marginal decrease observed on PN21 (Tenkumo et al., 2020). Transient reductions in Bdnf expression as a consequence of repeated MS had a profound impact on GABAergic neuron development in the mPFC (Tenkumo et al., 2020), which may play a role in the etiology of anxiety-like behavior (Suwaluk and Chutabhakdikul, 2022), as the mPFC is known to inhibit fear responses through amygdala inter-connectivity partially based on GABA levels (Delli Pizzi et al., 2017). Additionally, other studies report that MS of three or 4 h per day from PN1-21 lowered Bdnf expression or protein levels in the mPFC in later adolescent development (Wang et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2020), with MS further being associated with inducing anxiety-like phenotypes (Abraham et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2018; Malcon et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020) and the behavioral change linked to the reduction in Bdnf expression (Zhang et al., 2020).

Likewise for the amygdala on PN22, we report lowered Bdnf exon IX expression immediately after the paradigm as a function of MS or TMT developmental exposure. While studies on MS associated changes in amygdala Bdnf expression is sparse, one study found that 3 h of daily MS between PN8-21 was sufficient to transiently increase basal BDNF protein levels in the amygdala in adulthood, which returned to normal levels after exposure to an acute stressor (Récamier-Carballo et al., 2017), suggesting the window of measurement may be critical. Nonetheless, in the amygdala, reductions in BDNF have been associated with anxiety disorders and impaired prefrontal-amygdala connectivity (Gorka et al., 2020), with aberrant Bdnf expression associated with psychopathology such as anxiety and depression (Hamilton et al., 2008; Lorenzetti et al., 2020).

We also report that MS was sufficient to reduce Bdnf DNA methylation in both the mPFC and amygdala on PN22. This was somewhat surprising, given our observed decrease in gene expression, considering methylation typically (albeit not exclusively) reduces gene expression (Moore et al., 2013). An association however between lower levels of exonic DNA methylation and lower levels of gene expression have been documented in other studies (St-Cyr and McGowan, 2015; McGowan et al., 2011). The incongruency may also be due to other Bdnf loci at play or other epigenetic mechanisms not measured. Indeed, while DNA methylation is the most common mechanism altering gene expression (Moore et al., 2013; Weinhold, 2006), other mechanisms may be driving the observed aberrant Bdnf expression. Mechanisms not measured in the present study worthy of further exploring include histone modifications, acetylation, sumoylation, and phosphorylation (Weinhold, 2006; Zhang and Pradhan, 2014) and epigenetic regulators such as methyl CpG binding protein 2 or histone-deacetylases (Chahrour et al., 2008; Nan et al., 1993; Tillotson and Bird, 2020), that can further recruit co-activator or co-repressor proteins. Additional regulatory layers that may also contribute to the observed expression patterns as a function of stress include exon-specific transcription, post-transcriptional regulation, and differential processing of proBDNF versus mature BDNF (Miao et al., 2020; Fuchikami et al., 2011; Tognoli et al., 2010; Nair et al., 2007). Incorporating these mechanisms into future studies may help clarify the relationship between methylation and expression changes observed in our data. Nonetheless of expected directional changes between gene expression and methylation data, the epigenetic changes are consistent with literature demonstrating the remarkable sensitivity of methylation to early life experiences (Collins et al., 2022, 2023; Cheng et al., 2022; Mitchell et al., 2016).

On PN30, MS animals were observed to have increased Bdnf expression in both the mPFC and amygdala, which paralleled an anxiety-like phenotype. Increased Bdnf expression in the amygdala has been observed as a function of various adult stress paradigms, including social defeat (Taylor et al., 2011) and chronic restraint stress associated with anxiety-like behavior (Lakshminarasimhan and Chattarji, 2012). However, there is profound heterogeneity in either increased or decreased Bdnf expression in the amygdala related to anxiety phenotypes across developmental periods and the particular stress paradigm implemented (Bath et al., 2013). As aforementioned, one study found that animals exposed to 3 h of MS from PN8-21 demonstrated increased basal BDNF protein levels that returned to normal upon experiencing an environmental stressor (Récamier-Carballo et al., 2017), highlighting the time-dependent sensitivity of measuring gene expression. Likewise, there is heterogeneity with the relationship between stress and Bdnf expression in the PFC, with time-dependent fluctuations in levels of expression following a range of stressors (Miao et al., 2020). Indeed, another recent study posited that even the type of stressor may lead to differential methylation levels in the brain (Catale et al., 2020). Taken together, our transient effects are not particularly surprising and highlight the plasticity of the epigenome to respond to specific time-dependent environmental conditions received.

While we saw lower levels of methylation with MS in the PN22 mPFC and amygdala, methylation levels were higher at PN30. The increased levels followed an increase in gene expression. Interestingly, similar to the PN22 data, methylation changed in the same direction as gene expression. We also report in the mPFC on PN30 that the TMT group had higher Bdnf Exon IX methylation compared to all other groups. These Bdnf methylation data are in part expected, as the maternally present groups on PN30 displayed reduced Bdnf expression compared to animals with MS.

Finally, we report novel consequences of ELA on epigenetic activity in the PAG. Specifically, on PN22 we found that the BTA-MS group had increased Bdnf expression, which was concomitant with a decrease in methylation. On PN30, we found a reduction in Bdnf expression as a function of MS and TMT exposure, which paralleled an increase in methylation in the TMT-MS group. Given our observed behavioral differences in freezing and given the known role of the PAG in regulating freezing vs flight defensive responses (e.g., Tovote et al., 2016), these data suggest that the PAG may be a region susceptible to epigenetic influences that gate defensive responses. Future work should consider Bdnf activity in the PAG as an important target for understanding early environmental impacts on defensive behaviors.

Limitations and conclusion

The present study has several limitations. First, we only explored a single potential epigenetic mechanism, and further studies seeking mechanistic explanations for gene expression changes should consider a broader screening of mechanisms to understand MS-induced changes in Bdnf expression and behavior. Second, a more thorough analysis of maternal behavior across the exposure period would provide further insight into the ability and reliability of TMT to alter maternal behavior. Third, we only included two time-points to collect brain tissue. As our data and data from others indicate time-dependent fluctuations in gene expression and methylation, studies that incorporate multiple time points stand to provide the best understanding of the dynamic interaction between the environment and brain development and its role in the emergence of behavior outcomes. Indeed, we were unable to determine how ELA) interacts with these molecular signatures across development due to the use of independent, rather than repeated-measures, molecular sampling. Fourth, we included only a relative control condition (BTA) and not a true naïve control (water), which limits our ability to distinguish baseline differences that may arise from the mild noxious properties of BTA exposure.

Despite limitations, our present study provides further insight into the capacity of the early postnatal environment to shape later behavior and epigenetic activity. We provide evidence that brief MS is sufficient to influence later anxiety-like and fear behaviors in a sex-specific manner, and that these early separations have measurable effects on epigenetic activity in multiple brain regions underlying anxiety- and fear-related responses. The observed relationship between gene expression and DNA methylation highlights the complexity of Bdnf regulation, which may involve numerous mechanisms, including exon-specific transcription, post-transcriptional regulation, and differential processing of proBDNF versus mature BDNF, suggesting multiple layers of control that respond dynamically to early environmental experiences. Although our study did not directly assess transgenerational effects, the observed epigenetic modifications raise the possibility that some changes may serve adaptive or protective functions, rather than solely representing risk markers. Future work examining longitudinal and cross-generational outcomes could further elucidate how early-life experiences influence both epigenetic programming and behavioral phenotypes across development.
