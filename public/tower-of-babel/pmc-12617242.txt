Hypopharyngeal Lymphangioma Causing Persistent Dysphagia in an Adult: A Case Report
Authors: Maryann Zhao, Nabil F Darwich, Erica R Thaler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLymphangiomas are rare in adults but have the potential to impede patients' swallowing function and obstruct their airway. Hypopharyngeal lymphangiomas can be difficult to identify because they do not always display the expected imaging characteristics and can fluctuate in size. Treatment using a transoral endoscopic approach can dramatically improve symptoms.Keywords: dysphagia, endoscopic surgery, hypopharyngeal mass, lymphangioma, lymphatic malformation, Zenker's diverticulum

Introduction

Lymphangiomas, or lymphatic malformations, are rare, benign, congenital abnormal growths of lymphatic channels. Nearly 90% of lymphangiomas are detected in children during their first 2 years of life, and only approximately 150 cases have been reported in adults [1]. The majority develop in the head and neck region due to rich lymphatics, most commonly in the oral tongue, tonsils, and supraglottic larynx [2]. Risk factors, such as prior neck surgery or infection of the cervical region, have been proposed as contributors to lymphangioma growth, but little is known about the natural history of the disease in adults.

While lymphangiomas are already an uncommon entity in adults, these malformations in the hypopharyngeal region are even more unusual, with only four reported cases in the literature (2 postcricoid, 1 posterior hypopharynx, and 1 lateral hypopharynx) [2]. Adult patients with lymphangiomas are often asymptomatic due to the soft characteristics of the structure, and those with upper aerodigestive tract lesions may display symptoms of respiratory distress, dysphagia, and dysphonia [2]. The diagnosis of lymphangiomas is typically made clinically based on history and exam and can be confirmed with biopsy [3]. Surgery is the treatment of choice although other options, such as sclerotherapy, can be used [3, 4]. We present a rare case of hypopharyngeal lymphangioma in an adult that is hypothesized to cause persistent symptoms for over a decade. Our objective is to highlight the challenges associated with diagnosing hypopharyngeal lymphangiomas in adults as they masquerade as more common diagnoses and treatment options to help resolve the associated symptoms.

Case History

A 73‐year‐old male with prior anterior cervical discectomy and fusion surgery (ACDF) presented for evaluation of a Zenker's diverticulum. He reported a 10‐year history of dysphagia. A barium swallow demonstrated a small Zenker's diverticulum. Office examination, including nasopharyngolaryngoscopy, was unremarkable.

Fifteen years before the patient was admitted for difficulty breathing, stridor, and significant neck swelling in the setting of a productive cough. He was started on ceftriaxone, azithromycin, and metronidazole and then required intubation and subsequent tracheostomy. A CT revealed a right hypopharyngeal 1.6 × 3.1 × 1.4 cm mass. Direct laryngoscopy and biopsy could not confirm a diagnosis but ruled out malignancy. He was discharged and eventually decannulated.

Three years before the patient presented with difficulty breathing and subjective neck swelling. A CT showed ACDF hardware and demonstrated a separate right 2.6 × 2.3 × 2.9 cm hypopharyngeal mass (Figure 1). Nasopharyngolaryngoscopy at that time demonstrated right hypopharyngeal fullness. The patient was diagnosed with a submucosal cyst and scheduled for outpatient follow‐up. He was discharged and lost to follow‐up until current presentation.

CT with contrast and immunohistochemistry of hypopharyngeal mass. CT Neck with contrast revealed a 1.6 × 3.1 × 1.4 cm right hypopharyngeal mass (red arrow).

Methods

Our differential diagnosis included benign masses (hemangiomas, lipomas, and bronchogenic cysts) and malignant tumors (squamous cell carcinoma) [2, 5]. Given the patient's presentation, he was scheduled for direct laryngoscopy with biopsy and endoscopic repair of Zenker's diverticulum. In the operating room, he had a cystic‐appearing mass in the right hypopharynx and a 2.5 cm Zenker's diverticulum. The mass was excised, and the Zenker's diverticulum was repaired (Figure 2A,B).

Endoscopy and histology of hypopharyngeal mass. Endoscopic evaluation of Zenker's diverticulum (A) before and (B) after repair. (C) H&E stain of excised soft tissue mass reveals benign squamous mucosa with dilated lymphatic channels (red arrow). (D) Immunohistochemistry reveals the endothelial cells lining the channels are D2‐40 positive (red arrow), which is consistent with a lymphangioma.

The pink‐tan mass was 1.7 × 1.3 × 0.8 cm. The pathology featured squamous mucosa with underlying dilated lymphatic channels, and on immunohistochemistry, stained positive for D2‐40, a marker for lymphatic endothelial cells (Figure 2C,D). On follow‐up, the patient reported significant improvement in his dysphagia.

Conclusion

Lymphangiomas are rare in adults and difficult to diagnose without histopathologic evaluation. Managing patients with transoral endoscopic resection has been demonstrated to be an effective approach for resolving symptoms.

Discussion

Here, we described a case of hypopharyngeal lymphangioma that represents only the fifth reported case of this diagnosis in adults [2]. In a literature review of hypopharyngeal lymphangiomas, globus sensation and dysphagia were reported to be the most common presenting symptoms [2]. While less is understood about the etiology and characteristics of adult lymphangiomas due to their rarity, it has been suggested that there is delayed proliferation of cells as a result of acquired processes such as trauma, inflammation, infection, or lymphatic obstructions. In our case, the patient presented twice with a vague infectious clinical picture, suggesting that an underlying infection could have exacerbated his piriform sinus lymphangioma and precipitated airway obstruction, consistent with prior reports in the literature [5]. In addition, his prior ACDF possibly could have contributed to the formation of dilated lymphatic channels and the development of the lymphangioma. Lymphatic malformations can also be associated with abnormal chromosome disorder, including Turner syndrome, Noonan syndrome, or Down syndrome, none of which our patient had [5]. CT typically demonstrates a low‐intensity mass with peripheral enhancement, while on MRI, lymphangiomas are typically hypointense on T1 and hyperintense on T2 [5]. In our case, the patient's imaging did not show these findings.

Treatment options include observation, steroids, sclerotherapy, and surgery [3, 4]. The definitive diagnosis is made through histopathologic analysis after surgical excision. On pathology, lymphangiomas are characterized by dilated lymphatic channels with thin walls and flat endothelial cells; lymphoid tissue and proteinaceous fluid may also be present. Lymphatic channels can be confirmed with immunostains such as D2‐40 (glycoprotein found in lymphatic endothelial cells), ERG (nuclear transcription factor in blood and lymphatic endothelial cells), and CD31 (PECAM‐1, membrane protein on blood and lymphatic endothelial cells). Approaches to surgical excision in the hypopharynx can be endoscopic or transcervical. Sclerotherapy can be used when complete surgical resection is unachievable but is typically limited to macrocystic areas [4].

Here, we repaired the patient's Zenker's diverticulum endoscopically and simultaneously performed an excisional biopsy of the hypopharyngeal mass. The mass was only appreciated with direct laryngoscopy given its small size, hypopharyngeal location, and subtle lymph node‐like appearance. At the 1 month post‐operative follow‐up visit, the patient reported improvement in his symptoms with no issues of dysphagia or cough while eating. While rare, hypopharyngeal lymphatic malformations have the potential to impede patients' airway and cause swallowing dysfunction. They may be challenging to diagnose on imaging alone. Thorough assessment and evaluation with direct laryngoscopy allow for identification and excision of these lesions. Future studies are needed to improve our understanding of the natural progression of lymphangiomas in adults and how to detect them earlier before they impact patients' function.
