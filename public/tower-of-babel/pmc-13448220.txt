A low-cost vision based hand gesture interface for real time industrial motor control in resource constrained environments
Authors: Asad Malook, Muhammad Ismail Mohmand, Adam Khan, Afrasiab Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vision based hand gesture interfaces offer an intuitive means of human machine interaction, but their adoption in industrial control systems remains limited, particularly in small and medium scale industries where cost and hardware con- straints are critical factors. This study presents a low cost framework for real time industrial motor control using a simplified vision-based hand gesture approach. A lightweight convolutional neural network based on MobileNetV2 is adapted through transfer learning to recognize a binary set of hand gestures selected to ensure reliable operation under constrained computational conditions. Ges- ture recognition is executed on an external edge device, while control commands are transmitted wirelessly to an Arduino-based controller interfaced with a vari- able frequency drive for three phase motor actuation. Experimental evaluation shows stable gesture classification performance with low end-to-end latency and memory usage compatible with low cost hardware. System-level testing with an operational industrial motor confirms consistent real time response and safe con- trol behavior. The findings indicate that practical gesture based industrial motor control can be achieved without reliance on expensive automation platforms, offering a feasible pathway for incremental adoption of intelligent interfaces in resource limited manufacturing environments.

Introduction

The ongoing transition to Industry 4.0 has reshaped modern manufacturing by inte- grating automation, intelligent sensing, and data-driven control systems1,2. These developments aim to enhance productivity, improve operational safety, and enable flexible interaction between human operators and industrial equipment. Despite these advances, the adoption of Industry 4.0 technologies remains uneven. While large- scale industries increasingly deploy AI-assisted monitoring and control solutions, many small and medium-scale manufacturing units, particularly in developing regions such as Pakistan, continue to rely on conventional programmable logic controller (PLC)–based systems3,4.

PLCs remain robust and reliable for deterministic control tasks; however, extending them to support vision-based interaction or learning-driven intelligence often requires proprietary hardware modules, specialized software, and skilled personnel5. These requirements substantially increase system cost and complexity, creating a barrier to adoption for cost-sensitive industrial environments. As a result, there is growing inter- est in alternative control architectures that can deliver intelligent functionality while remaining affordable, maintainable, and compatible with locally available expertise.

Artificial intelligence (AI) provides the overarching framework for intelligent indus- trial systems, encompassing machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) techniques that enable perception, decision-making, and adaptive control6. Within this hierar- chy, deep learning methods, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have demonstrated strong performance in visual perception tasks, including hand gesture recognition. As illustrated conceptually in Fig. 1, DL forms a specialized subset of ML within the broader AI paradigm, enabling systems to interpret complex visual inputs and translate them into actionable control signals.Fig. 1Simplified block diagram of artificial intelligence, machine learning and deep learning6.

Gesture-based human–machine interfaces (HMIs) have emerged as a practical alter- native to conventional push-button or touch-panel controls, especially in environments where physical contact is undesirable due to safety, hygiene, or operational constraints7,8. Vision-based gesture recognition allows operators to interact with machinery using natural hand movements, potentially reducing mechanical wear and improving usability9. Recent advances in deep learning have significantly improved the robust- ness of gesture recognition systems, making real-time deployment increasingly feasible10. Nevertheless, many existing approaches assume access to high-performance com- puting resources, large training datasets, or controlled environments, assumptions that limit their applicability in resource-constrained industrial settings11,12.

Hardware selection plays a critical role in determining system cost, scalability, and maintainability9. Embedded platforms such as the Raspberry Pi or NVIDIA Jetson provide sufficient computational capacity for deep learning inference but may still be financially or energy-intensive for small-scale industrial deployment13. In contrast, low-cost microcontrollers such as the Arduino Uno14 are widely available, inexpen- sive, and familiar to local technicians. However, their limited processing power and memory impose strict constraints on model size, inference strategy, and communica- tion overhead15,16. These constraints necessitate lightweight deep learning models and carefully designed system architectures.

Lightweight CNN architectures, particularly MobileNetV2, have been developed to address such limitations by reducing computational complexity through depth- wise separable convolutions and inverted residual blocks11,17. MobileNetV2 has demonstrated strong performance in embedded vision tasks, including gesture recog- nition and object classification11,18. However, much of the existing literature emphasizes classification accuracy or simulation-based evaluation, with comparatively limited focus on real-world deployment issues such as system latency, memory utiliza- tion, wireless communication delays, and interaction with actual industrial actuators19–22.

From a practical deployment perspective, increasing gesture complexity often intro- duces instability when models are deployed on constrained hardware or integrated into real-time control loops. Preliminary experimentation in this study indicated that multi-class gesture schemes led to inconsistent actuation behavior under embedded and wireless communication constraints23,24. Consequently, this work adopts a simplified binary gesture set, open hand and closed hand20, to prioritize reliability, responsiveness, and operational safety over gesture expressiveness.

In this study, a MobileNetV2-based gesture recognition model is trained using transfer learning on a small, locally collected dataset and integrated into a complete industrial control pipeline. Gesture inference is performed on an edge computing unit, while control commands are transmitted wirelessly to an Arduino-based controller interfaced with a variable-frequency drive (VFD) for real-time actuation of a three- phase induction motor25. This hybrid architecture balances computational feasibility with real-time performance and cost efficiency.

Limitations of existing approaches

A critical review of the existing literature reveals three recurring limitations. First, many gesture recognition systems are evaluated primarily using offline accuracy metrics, with limited discussion of deployment constraints such as latency, mem- ory usage, and system stability. Second, the reliance on high-performance embedded platforms or cloud services reduces accessibility for cost-sensitive industrial environ- ments. Third, there is a lack of end-to-end studies that integrate vision-based gesture recognition with real industrial actuators, such as three-phase motors controlled by variable-frequency drives (VFDs). These limitations highlight the need for practical, deployment-oriented research that emphasizes system-level integration rather than isolated algorithmic performance. Addressing this need requires a holistic approach that considers model efficiency, hardware constraints, communication mechanisms, and real-world operational reliability.

Motivation of the study

Motivated by the identified gaps, the present study focuses on developing a low-cost, end-to-end gesture-controlled industrial motor system that can operate reliably in resource-constrained environments. By leveraging a lightweight MobileNetV2-based model and deploying it within a complete control pipeline involving wireless com- munication and real industrial hardware, this work seeks to complement existing studies by emphasizing practical feasibility and deployment realism. The proposed approach is positioned not as a replacement for PLC-based systems, but as an afford- able and adaptable alternative for small- and medium-scale industrial settings seeking incremental adoption of Industry 4.0 technologies.

Literature review

Research on hand gesture recognition has evolved steadily over the past two decades, driven by the need for more intuitive and contactless human–machine interaction. In industrial settings, this interest has been shaped by practical concerns such as system reliability, computational cost, and ease of integration with existing control infrastructure. This section reviews key developments in gesture recognition techniques and highlights their relevance to real-time industrial motor control applications.

Gesture recognition techniques

Early research on hand gesture recognition primarily relied on traditional image processing and handcrafted feature extraction techniques, such as contour analy- sis, skin color segmentation, and shape descriptors26,27. While these approaches were computationally lightweight, their performance was susceptible to variations in illumination, background clutter, and hand orientation28,29. As a result, their applicability in dynamic industrial environments remained limited.

The introduction of machine learning methods, including support vector machines (SVMs)30 and k-nearest neighbors (k-NN)31, improved classification robustness by enabling data-driven feature learning. However, these methods still depended heavily on manually engineered features and struggled to generalize across diverse operating conditions. With the advent of deep learning, convolutional neural networks (CNNs).

32 became the dominant paradigm for vision-based gesture recognition due to their ability to automatically learn hierarchical spatial features directly from raw images. CNN-based models have demonstrated superior accuracy across a wide range of gesture recognition tasks, including static and dynamic hand gestures33.

Despite these advances, many CNN-based gesture recognition systems are devel- oped with a primary focus on accuracy, often assuming controlled environments or access to powerful computing resources. In industrial settings, where lighting con- ditions, operator positioning, and background variability are difficult to control, such assumptions can limit real-world applicability. Consequently, there is a grow- ing interest in designing gesture recognition systems that balance robustness with computational efficiency23,33.

Deep learning for embedded and edge devices

Deploying deep learning models on embedded and edge devices has emerged as an active research domain, driven by the need for low-latency inference, reduced commu- nication overhead, and enhanced data privacy21,34. Several studies have explored model compression techniques, including pruning, quantization, and knowledge dis- tillation, to reduce the computational footprint of deep neural networks without significant loss of accuracy35,36. These techniques are particularly relevant for embedded platforms with limited memory and processing capabilities.

In parallel, the development of lightweight CNN architectures has provided an alternative pathway for efficient edge deployment. Architectures such as MobileNet, ShuffleNet, and EfficientNet are specifically designed to minimize computational complexity while maintaining competitive performance11,37. Among these, MobileNetV2 has attracted significant attention for its use of depthwise separable convolutions and inverted residual structures, which substantially reduce the parame- ter count and inference cost. Prior studies have successfully applied MobileNet-based models in mobile vision, surveillance, and human–computer interaction tasks17,38. However, most embedded deep learning studies target platforms such as Raspberry Pi, NVIDIA Jetson, or ARM-based single-board computers. Fewer works investigate deployment on ultra-low-cost microcontrollers, where memory limitations and the lack of hardware acceleration pose additional challenges. This gap is particularly rel- evant for industrial applications in developing regions, where affordability and ease of maintenance are critical considerations.

Gesture-based industrial control systems

Gesture-based control has been explored in various industrial and semi-industrial contexts, including robotic manipulation, smart manufacturing, and assistive control systems. Existing studies demonstrate that gesture interfaces can improve operational flexibility and reduce physical interaction with machinery, especially in hazardous environments39–41. In many implementations, gesture recognition outputs are mapped to control commands for robots, conveyors, or actuators using middleware or supervisory control layers42–44.

Nevertheless, a significant proportion of these systems relies on high-end cameras, external processing units, or cloud-based inference frameworks. While such configurations can achieve high recognition accuracy, they introduce latency, a reliance on network connectivity, and increased system costs. Moreover, several studies focus on proof-of-concept demonstrations rather than sustained real-time operation with actual industrial hardware. As a result, the feasibility of these approaches for small-scale industrial deployment remains uncertain21,23.

Dataset collection and pre-processing

The dataset was created in real time using Google Teachable Machine, which enabled rapid collection and labeling of hand gesture images. This approach facilitated efficient dataset generation and model training within a unified platform46. Hand gesture images were captured directly through a standard RGB camera interface within the platform and labeled during acquisition. The dataset was created specifically for this research and does not rely on any publicly available or third-party datasets. The dataset incorporates variations in hand orientation, camera distance, and ambient illumination. Two gesture classes were considered: open hand and closed hand, resulting in a binary classification problem. This design choice was motivated by the need to ensure stable and reliable control behavior when operating on low- cost hardware. The dataset was divided into three subsets: 70% for training, 15% for validation, and 15% for testing. This partitioning ensures sufficient data for model training while maintaining independent subsets for unbiased validation and performance evaluation. The dataset split was performed using a stratified sampling approach, ensuring that each gesture class was proportionally represented across all subsets. This strategy minimizes class imbalance and supports consistent model learning and evaluation. A single hold-out validation scheme was employed, in which the dataset was partitioned once into training, validation, and testing sets. This approach was selected due to the real-time system constraints and deployment-oriented nature of the proposed framework.

The test set consisted of 120 samples, divided equally between the two gesture classes, with 60 samples per class to ensure balanced evaluation. The test set was completely independent from the training and validation datasets, with no overlapping samples used during model training. This separation ensures unbiased performance evaluation and prevents data leakage.

Data acquisition was performed using a consumer-grade camera, with images captured at different times of the day to introduce natural lighting variability. Before training, all images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels to match the input requirements of MobileNetV2. Preprocessing steps included normalization and background simplification to reduce noise while preserving salient hand features. To improve generalization, limited data augmentation techniques such as rotation and scaling were applied, avoid- ing excessive artificial variability that could misrepresent real operational conditions47,48.

In addition, Model training and preliminary data analysis were also performed within the Google Teachable Machine environment prior to deployment46. Moreover, all experiments were repeated five times, and the reported results represent the average performance across runs to ensure reliability and reduce experimental bias.

The dataset used in this study was intentionally designed as a two-class gesture set to evaluate the feasibility of real-time motor control in a controlled environment. Although the dataset size is relatively small, variations were introduced during data collection, including differences in hand orientation, distance from the camera, and minor lighting variations. These variations help improve dataset representativeness to some extent. However, the controlled experimental conditions and binary classification setup may contribute to optimistic performance estimates. Therefore, the current results should be interpreted as a proof-of-concept demonstration. Future work will focus on expanding the dataset with additional gestures and collecting data under more realistic and challenging industrial conditions to evaluate system robustness and generalization further.

Model selection and training strategy

MobileNetV2 was selected as the core feature extraction architecture due to its estab- lished balance between recognition accuracy and computational efficiency. Its use of depthwise separable convolutions and inverted residual blocks significantly reduces the parameter count and inference cost, making it well-suited for embedded and edge- deployment scenarios. Instead of training the network from scratch, transfer learning49 was employed by initializing the model with pre-trained weights and fine-tuning the final layers for the specific hand gesture recognition task. The training strategy prioritized stable convergence and generalization over maximum achievable accuracy. Hyperparameters, including learning rate, batch size, and number of epochs, were selected empirically based on observed training and validation behavior. Overfitting was monitored through validation loss trends, and early stopping was applied where necessary. This pragmatic approach reflects real-world deployment constraints, where extensive hyperparameter optimization may be impractical.

The proposed recognition system is based on a transfer learning approach using the MobileNetV2 architecture. The pre-trained MobileNetV2 model, originally trained on ImageNet, is used as a feature extractor. The final classification layer is replaced with a custom classification head consisting of fully connected dense layers followed by a softmax activation function for binary gesture classification. Only the upper layers of the network are fine-tuned, while the lower layers remain frozen to preserve learned feature representations. This reduces training time and computational complexity while maintaining high recognition accuracy.

In terms of computational complexity, MobileNetV2 is designed using depth-wise separable convolutions, which significantly reduce the number of parameters and floating-point operations compared to traditional CNNs. The deployed model has a lightweight architecture suitable for real-time applications, with reduced memory footprint and efficient inference performance. The model size, memory usage, and inference latency were carefully considered to ensure compatibility with resource-constrained embedded systems.

Embedded deployment and communication framework

The hardware implementation of the proposed system includes an Arduino Uno microcontroller, a USB camera for gesture acquisition, a relay-based motor driver module, and a three-phase motor. A host computer is used to execute the trained deep learning model for gesture recognition. The Arduino Uno is responsible for receiving control signals and actuating the motor accordingly. This architecture ensures separation of computation and control, enabling efficient system operation.

Following training, the gesture recognition model was integrated into the real-time inference pipeline. Due to the limited computational and memory resources of the Arduino Uno, direct on-device inference was not feasible. Instead, gesture classification was performed on an edge device, and the resulting control commands were trans- mitted wirelessly to the microcontroller. This hybrid deployment strategy balances computational feasibility with real-time responsiveness. Wireless communication between the inference unit and the Arduino Uno was implemented using a lightweight serial communication protocol. Upon receiv- ing a gesture command, the microcontroller maps it to predefined control actions, such as motor start or stop. The control logic was intentionally kept simple to min- imize latency and to reduce the risk of unintended actuation caused by transient or ambiguous gesture classifications.

Due to hardware constraints of the Arduino Uno, including limited SRAM and processing capability, the neural network inference is not executed directly on the microcontroller. Instead, the trained MobileNetV2 model is deployed on a host system where gesture classification is performed, and only control signals are transmitted to the Arduino Uno. This design ensures efficient system operation while overcoming embedded hardware limitations.

Industrial motor control setup

The Arduino Uno interfaces with a variable frequency drive (VFD) to control a three- phase induction motor50. The VFD enables safe, controlled motor operation by regulating voltage and frequency in response to digital input signals from the micro- controller. In the proposed configuration, gesture-based commands are translated into VFD-compatible control signals, allowing the motor to respond in real time to opera- tor input. Safety considerations were explicitly incorporated into the control logic to prevent unintended or unsafe operation. Gesture confirmation mechanisms and default safe states were implemented to ensure that motor activation occurs only under stable recognition conditions. These measures are essential for ensuring reliable operation in practical industrial environments51.

Performance evaluation criteria

System performance was evaluated using a combination of algorithmic and deployment-oriented metrics. Gesture recognition accuracy was measured to assess classification reliability, while end-to-end latency was evaluated to quantify the response time from gesture capture to motor actuation. In addition, memory utiliza- tion and computational load were monitored to verify compatibility with resource- constrained hardware platforms. Beyond quantitative metrics, qualitative observations related to system stability, responsiveness, and ease of deployment were recorded during experimental testing. This comprehensive evaluation framework was designed to provide a realistic assessment of the proposed system’s feasibility for small- and medium-scale industrial applications. Individual latency components were measured independently and averaged over multiple trials; therefore, their sum may exceed the observed end-to-end latency due to pipeline overlap.

The latency measurement procedure was conducted by segmenting the system pipeline into five distinct components: (i) image acquisition, (ii) preprocessing, (iii) inference, (iv) communication, and (v) actuation. For each component, timestamps were recorded at the start and end of execution using high-resolution system timers. The latency of each stage was computed as the difference between the corresponding timestamps, ensuring precise temporal measurement. A total of six independent measurements were recorded for each latency component under consistent operating conditions, and the reported values correspond to the average of these measurements.

Comparison with a baseline convolutional neural network model

The baseline model employed in this study is a conventional convolutional neural network (CNN) designed for image classification tasks. The architecture consists of an input layer where images are resized and normalized, followed by two convolutional layers with small kernel sizes and Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activations. The baseline model was trained using the same dataset, preprocessing steps, and training parameters as the proposed MobileNetV2 model to ensure fair comparison. Both models were trained using identical epochs, batch size, and optimizer settings.

In addition, lightweight deep learning architectures such as MobileNet variants and other compact models are commonly used for real-time applications due to their reduced computational complexity. The proposed MobileNetV2 model leverages depthwise separable convolutions and inverted residual blocks, which significantly reduce parameters while maintaining high accuracy. This makes the proposed model more suitable for real-time embedded motor control compared to the baseline CNN architecture.

Each convolutional layer is followed by a max-pooling layer to reduce spatial dimensions. The extracted feature maps are flattened and passed through fully connected dense layers, with an optional dropout layer to mitigate overfitting. The final output layer uses a softmax activation function for classification. The model is trained using categorical cross-entropy loss and the Adam optimization algorithm.

The experimental validation presented in this study is based on a limited number of trials aimed at demonstrating system feasibility. While the results are encouraging, additional experiments under more challenging conditions, such as lighting variations, visual disturbances, and communication noise, would provide a more comprehensive performance assessment. Furthermore, the comparison between MobileNetV2 and the baseline CNN model has been clarified by ensuring consistent training conditions, dataset usage, and evaluation metrics. These improvements enhance the transparency and reliability of the experimental validation.

Furthermore, both models were evaluated using the same dataset partitions and performance metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and inference time, ensuring the validity and consistency of the comparative analysis.

Therefore a comparative evaluation between the proposed MobileNetV2-based model and a baseline CNN is presented in Fig. 11. The baseline CNN achieved an accuracy of 88%, whereas the MobileNetV2 model correctly classified all test samples. These results are consistent with the architectural design of MobileNetV2, which employs depthwise separable convolutions to reduce computational complexity. The combination of higher classification reliability and lower inference latency supports the suitability of MobileNetV2 for real-time industrial control applications operating under computational constraints.

In addition, it is important to note that the total system latency is not equal to the sum of the individual component latencies. This is due to partial pipeline overlap and parallel execution of certain stages. In particular, image acquisition and preprocessing can occur concurrently, while inference, communication, and actuation are primarily sequential. This overlap reduces the effective end-to-end latency compared to a strictly sequential pipeline, resulting in a total latency that is lower than the sum of the individual measured components.

Limitations of the study

Despite the promising results, several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the dataset used for training and testing is relatively limited in size, which may affect the generalization capability of the model. Second, experiments were conducted under controlled lighting and environmental conditions, and performance may vary in more complex or dynamic industrial settings. Third, the system currently supports only two gesture classes, which restricts the range of possible control commands. Fourth, the communication between the vision system and motor controller relies on Bluetooth, which may introduce latency, range limitations, and potential interference in industrial environments.

The limited processing and memory capacity of the Arduino Uno restrict the inte- gration of more complex control logic or on-device intelligence. The proposed system demonstrates a practical, low-cost approach to gesture-based industrial control. Iden- tified limitations define clear directions for future system expansion and robustness improvement. Conclusion and future work

The proposed system demonstrates reliable real-time gesture-based motor control using a lightweight deep learning approach. The experimental results indicate high classification accuracy and low latency under controlled testing conditions. These findings highlight the feasibility of the proposed framework for real-time automation tasks. However, the current evaluation was conducted in a controlled environment using a limited gesture dataset, and further validation in complex industrial settings is required to assess system robustness and scalability.

Overall, this research demonstrates that low-cost, AI-assisted industrial control is achievable using lightweight deep learning models and simple embedded hardware. The proposed framework provides a scalable foundation for gesture-based industrial interfaces and contributes toward broader accessibility of intelligent automation in developing and resource-limited industrial settings.

This study investigated the feasibility of implementing a low-cost, vision-based gesture control system for industrial motor operation in resource-constrained environ- ments. A binary hand gesture recognition approach was adopted using a customized MobileNetV2 architecture trained through transfer learning on a locally collected dataset. The experimental results indicate that, under the controlled conditions con- sidered in this work, the proposed model reliably distinguishes between open- and closed-hand gestures while maintaining low inference latency and modest memory requirements.

To support practical deployment, the system was implemented with a hybrid architecture in which gesture inference was performed on an external edge comput- ing unit and real-time control logic executed on an Arduino Uno microcontroller. Wireless communication enabled the transmission of gesture-based commands to a variable-frequency drive controlling a three-phase induction motor. System-level test- ing confirmed stable motor actuation behavior, acceptable end-to-end latency, and consistent operation without reliance on expensive PLC-based vision modules or cloud-dependent inference.

The primary contribution of this work lies not in introducing a novel gesture recognition algorithm, but in demonstrating that existing lightweight deep learning models can be effectively integrated into an end-to-end industrial control pipeline using low-cost and widely available hardware. By prioritizing simplicity, reliability, and deployment feasibility, the proposed approach offers a practical alternative for small- and medium-scale industries seeking to adopt intelligent automation technologies incrementally.

Although the proposed system shows promising performance, several limitations remain. These include the use of a small dataset, limited gesture classes, controlled lighting conditions, and reliance on Bluetooth communication. In real industrial environments, factors such as environmental noise, varying illumination, and communication interference may affect system performance. Future work will focus on expanding the dataset, testing under realistic industrial conditions, and exploring more robust communication technologies to enhance system reliability. Furthermore, the limited memory and processing capabilities of the Arduino Uno constrain the scalability of the system toward more complex on-device intelligence.

Future work will focus on extending the system to support multiple hand gestures by developing a larger and more diverse dataset, while maintaining real-time performance and computational efficiency on embedded platforms. Incorporating lighting- invariant pre-processing techniques and collecting more diverse datasets will help improve robustness under practical industrial conditions. Alternative wireless commu- nication protocols, such as Wi-Fi or industrial-grade low-power wide-area networks, may further enhance system reliability. Additional research will also explore model compression, on-device adaptation, and integration with auxiliary sensors to improve safety and operational awareness. Comparative benchmarking against commercial PLC-based solutions and long-term deployment studies will provide further insight into the system’s industrial viability.
