Abstract
. There is no known polynomial-time algorithm for graph isomorphism testing, but elementary combinatorial “refinement” algorithms seem to be very efficient in practice. Some philosophical justification for this phenomenon is provided by a classical theorem of Babai, Erdős and Selkow: an extremely simple polynomial-time combinatorial algorithm (variously known as “naïve refinement”, “naïve vertex classification”, “colour refinement” or the “1-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm”) yields a so-called canonical labelling scheme for “almost all graphs”. More precisely, for a typical outcome of a random graph 𝔾 ⁡ ( n , 1 / 2 ) \mathbb{G}(n,1/2) , this simple combinatorial algorithm assigns labels to vertices in a way that easily permits isomorphism-testing against any other graph. We improve the Babai–Erdős–Selkow theorem in two directions. First, we consider randomly perturbed graphs, in accordance with the smoothed analysis philosophy of Spielman and Teng: for any graph G G , naïve refinement becomes effective after a tiny random perturbation to G G (specifically, the addition and removal of O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log n) random edges). Actually, with a twist on naïve refinement, we show that O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) random additions and removals suffice. These results significantly improve on previous work of Gaudio, Rácz and Sridhar (resolving one of their conjectures), and are in certain senses best-possible. Second, we complete a long line of research on canonical labelling and automorphisms for random graphs: for any p p (possibly depending on n n ), we prove that a random graph 𝔾 ⁡ ( n , p ) \mathbb{G}(n,p) can typically be canonically labelled in polynomial time. This is most interesting in the extremely sparse regime where p p has order of magnitude c / n c/n ; denser regimes were previously handled by Bollobás, Czajka–Pandurangan, and Linial–Mosheiff. Our proof also provides a description of the automorphism group of a typical outcome of 𝔾 ⁡ ( n , p ) \mathbb{G}(n,p) (slightly correcting a prediction of Linial–Mosheiff).

Given a pair of graphs

Perhaps the most influential approach along these lines is called

1.1.
Smoothed analysis

As our first main direction in this paper, we take the philosophy of
Theorem

1.1
much further, combining it with the celebrated
smoothed analysis
framework of Spielman and Teng
[
74
]
. To give some context: the

for linear optimisation is another fundamental example of an algorithm which seems to perform well in practice but takes exponential time in the worst case. As a very strong explanation for this, Spielman and Teng proved that if one takes
any
linear optimisation problem and applies a slight (Gaussian) random perturbation to its coefficients, then for a typical outcome of the resulting perturbed linear optimisation problem, the simplex algorithm succeeds in polynomial time. This shows that poorly-performing instances are “fragile” or “unstable”, and perhaps we should not expect them to appear in practice.

Linear optimisation is a continuous problem, and for discrete problems one needs a different notion of random perturbation. In a later paper, Spielman and Teng
[
73
]
suggested that the most natural way
to define a discrete random perturbation is in terms of
symmetric difference

G,G^{\prime}
on the same vertex set, write
G\triangle G^{\prime}
for the graph containing all edges which are in exactly one of
G
and
G^{\prime}

(we can think of

as the “perturbation graph”, specifying where we should “flip” edges of
G
to non-edges, or vice versa). Significantly strengthening
Theorem

1.1

any
graph, randomly perturbing each edge with probability about
\log n/n
(i.e., adding and removing about
n\log n
random edges

6

Note that
\mathbb{G}(n,p)

p\binom{n}{2}\approx pn^{2}

Fix a constant

Note that the

We remark that we are not the first to consider smoothed analysis for graph isomorphism: this setting was also recently considered by Gaudio, Rácz and Sridhar

It is not hard to see that the assumption on

Of course, while tiny regular components may foil colour refinement, they are not really a fundamental problem for canonical labelling (as we can afford to use very inefficient canonical labelling algorithms on these tiny components). We are able to go beyond

1.2
via a modification of the colour refinement algorithm in this spirit. Indeed, with a variation on the colour refinement algorithm called the

, and a notion of a

The proof of

1.2.
Sparse random graphs

After the Babai–Erdős–Selkow theorem (
Theorem

1.1
) on canonical labelling for
\mathbb{G}(n,1/2)
, one of the most obvious directions for further study was to consider sparser random graphs
\mathbb{G}(n,p_{n})
(note that it suffices to consider

The first work in this direction was by Bollobás (see
[
18

1.1

does not decay too rapidly with

[
17

(which considered a very different type of canonical labelling scheme, under different assumptions on

[
28

(which considered colour refinement for an intermediate range of

), one obtains polynomial-time canonical labelling schemes as long as

is significant because it is the same range where random graphs are typically

[

]
(see also
[
79
]
), such random graphs typically have only trivial automorphisms, whereas if
np_{n}-\log n\to-\infty
then there typically exist many automorphisms.

Even sparser graphs were recently considered by Linial and Mosheiff
[
60
]
; they handled the regime where
np_{n}\to\infty
using another different type of canonical labelling scheme. The regime
np_{n}\to 0
is easy, as in this regime (see
[
36
, Section 2.1]
) the components of
\mathbb{G}(n,p_{n})
are all trees with size at most
o(\log n)
(so various different types of trivial canonical labelling schemes suffice; see for example
[
4
]
). That is to say, the only regime left unaddressed is the regime where
p_{n}
is about
c/n
for some constant

As our next main result, we close this gap, finding a canonical labelling scheme for all

Theorem 1.4
.

There is a set of graphs
\mathcal{H}
, and an explicit polynomial-time canonical labelling algorithm for graphs in
\mathcal{H}
(which can also detect

a graph lies in
\mathcal{H}
), such that the following holds.

For any sequence
(p_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
, and
G_{n}\sim\mathbb{G}(n,p_{n})
, whp

. In particular, whp

can be tested for isomorphism with any other graph in polynomial time.

In fact, we prove that colour refinement on its own yields a canonical labelling scheme whp, unless
p_{n}
has order of magnitude

p_{n}
has order of magnitude
c/n
, then colour refinement still

Remark 1.5
.

There are important connections between the colour refinement algorithm and mathematical logic (see for example the monograph
[
40
]
). Our proof shows that random graphs of any density typically have

A related problem is to characterise the automorphism group of a random graph. Linial and Mosheiff achieved this when

Definition 1.7
.

For a graph

be its

Note that any graph

.

Consider any sequence
(p_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

•

\operatorname{core}_{2}(G)

•

\operatorname{core}_{2}(G)

1.3.1.
Exploring universal covers

The starting point for all the proofs in this paper is an observation essentially due to Angluin
[
2
]
, that the stable colouring obtained by the colour refinement algorithm assigns two vertices the same colour if and only if the
universal covers
rooted at those vertices are isomorphic. We will use a minor modification of the notion of a universal cover called a
view
, which encodes the same information but which is slightly more convenient for our purposes. Roughly speaking, the view
\mathcal{T}_{G}(v)
rooted at a vertex
v
in a graph
G
is a (potentially infinite) tree encoding all possible walks in
G
starting from
v
(see
Section

3
for a precise definition).

Without random perturbation, we are helpless to deal with the fact that many vertices may have isomorphic views (e.g., if
G
is
d
-regular, then
\mathcal{T}_{G}(v)
is always isomorphic to the infinite rooted tree where each vertex has
d
children). The power of random perturbation is that, if two vertices
u
and
v
“see different vertices in their walks” (for example, if the set of neighbours of
u
is very different from the set of neighbours of
v
), then the random perturbation is likely to affect
\mathcal{T}_{G}(u)
and
\mathcal{T}_{G}(v)
differently, distinguishing them from each other. One of the key technical results in this paper is a general lemma (
Proposition

4.1
) which makes this precise.

1.3.2.
Distinguishing vertices via random perturbation: expansion and anticoncentration

It is difficult to give a quick summary of
Proposition

4.1
without the necessary definitions, but to give a flavour: we define sets
\mathcal{S}^{i}(\{u,v\})
which describe the vertices which “feature differently” in length-
i
walks starting from
u
and length-
i
walks starting from
v
.
Proposition

4.1
says that even extremely mildly randomly perturbed graphs typically have the property that if for some vertices
u,v
, and some
i
, the set
\mathcal{S}^{i}(\{u,v\})
contains more than about
\log n
vertices, then
u
and
v
are assigned different colours by colour refinement.

To prove this, we first use the expansion properties of random graphs (via a coupling of the type often appearing in analysis of branching processes) to “boost” the condition on
\mathcal{S}^{i}(\{u,v\})
, showing that if
\mathcal{S}^{i}(\{u,v\})
has more than about
\log n
vertices then there is typically some
j\geq i
such that
\mathcal{S}^{j}(\{u,v\})
has
n^{1-o(1)}

There is then a huge amount of “space” in

1.3.5.
Sprinkling via the 3-core

For

For the unfamiliar reader: sprinkling, in its most basic form, is the observation that a random graph

1.3.6.
Small components in disparity graphs

Recall that

For the proof of

Definition 1.10

For a graph

•

\omega

\omega

\omega^{\prime}

Informally speaking, the majority graph

If

1.3.7.
Percolation for a weaker result

Let

As discussed in

With one round of sprinkling (i.e., with the random assignment of unique colours provided by

With a union bound over pairs of vertices, it is easy to show that no such pairs persist after a round of sprinkling (the random assignment of unique colours typically allows the colour refinement algorithm to distinguish all such pairs).

For our second round of sprinkling (provided by

1.3.8.
Splitting colour classes and components

In order to go beyond the ideas in the previous subsection, to work with random perturbation probabilities as small as
O(1/n)
, we need to get a much stronger conclusion from our sprinkled random perturbation. Every time a vertex is added to the 3-core (and gets a unique colour), this vertex is not simply removed from the disparity graph: the new colour information has a cascading effect (via the colour refinement algorithm) that affects the colours of many other vertices, indirectly affecting the connected components of the disparity graph.

First, it is instructive to think about the effect of sprinkling on the connected components (of the disparity graph) which intersect a given colour class
C
. If there is a vertex
v
which has a neighbour in
C
(with respect to the disparity graph), then if we were to assign
v
a unique colour this would cause
C
to break into multiple colour classes: namely, the colour refinement algorithm would be able to distinguish the vertices in
C
adjacent to
v
from the vertices in
C
which are not adjacent to
v
(it is a simple consequence of the definition of the disparity graph that
v
is adjacent to at most half the vertices in
C
).

So, we can consider an exploration process that starts from the vertices of
C
, and explores
14
14

14

For the purposes of this outline, the reader can imagine that at each step we choose the unexplored vertex which is closest to
C

all the vertices which share a connected component (in the disparity graph) with a vertex in
C
. Each time we consider a new vertex, we reveal whether it is assigned a unique colour, and propagate this information via the colour refinement algorithm
15
15

15

The resulting changes to the colouring also change the disparity graph. In particular, vertices which used to be in the same connected component may later be spread over multiple connected components, but this is not really a problem.
. As we continue this exploration/refinement process, the colour classes will start to break up into smaller pieces. There is a limit to how long this process can continue, since colour classes of size 1 cannot be broken up further. Indeed, by considering an auxiliary submartingale (which measures how the number of colours we have discovered so far compares to the number of vertices we have explored so far, at each point in the process), we can prove that our exploration process is likely to terminate after
O(|C|)
steps, having explored all the vertices that share a component with a vertex in
C

always refers to our colour class

Unfortunately, we cannot hope to show that the colour classes are small, in general (indeed, if

has many isolated vertices, then all these vertices will be assigned the same colour by any canonical vertex-colouring scheme). However, the above idea can be “localised” to a connected component:
We prove a crucial lemma (
Lemma

6.10

O(\log n)
vertices (after sprinkling), it suffices to show that, before sprinkling, for all colour classes

and connected components

of the disparity graph, we have
|C\cap X|=O(\log n)
.

In order to show that these intersection sizes
|C\cap X|
are small, we need another round of sprinkling which “shatters large components into small colour classes”. In order for this sprinkling to have a strong enough effect, we need to consider a more powerful variant of the colour refinement algorithm, as follows.

1.3.9.
Distinguishing vertices via the 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm

The
2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm
refines colourings of
pairs
of vertices: starting with a certain “trivial” colouring
\phi_{G}:V(G)^{2}\to\Omega
, we repeatedly refine
\phi_{G}
based on statistics of 3-vertex configurations, until a stable colouring
f:V(G)^{2}\to\{0,1\}
is reached. This colouring of pairs of vertices then gives rise to a colouring of individual vertices
v\mapsto f(v,v)
, which contains a lot more information than the result of ordinary colour refinement.

In particular, this more sophisticated refinement operation allows us to distinguish vertices based on
distances
: in the final vertex-colouring, every two vertices of the same colour see the same number of vertices of every given colour at any given distance (in the disparity graph). As discussed in
Section

1.3.7
, after a single round of sprinkling whp the disparity graph has maximum degree
O(\log n)
, so if a connected component
X
has more than logarithmically many vertices then it is quite sparse, meaning that there is a very rich variety of different pairs of vertices at different distances. So, when sprinkling causes new vertices to receive unique colours, hopefully the 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm will be able to significantly break up the colour classes in
X
(recall that our goal is now to prove that the intersections between colour classes and connected components have size
O(\log n)
).

Naïvely, one might hope to prove this via a simple union bound over subsets
Z\subseteq X
of about
\log n
vertices: for any such set
Z
, one might try to prove that after a round of sprinkling, and the 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm, it is overwhelmingly unlikely that all vertices of
Z
have the same colour. To prove this, it would suffice to show that for any such
Z
there are many vertices which see some vertices of
Z
at different distances (so if any of these many vertices receive a new unique colour, this could be used by the 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm to give different colours to some of the vertices of
Z
).

Unfortunately, this direct approach does not seem to yield any nontrivial bounds, without making structural assumptions about
G
. Instead, we use a “fingerprint” technique reminiscent of the method of
hypergraph containers
(see
[
71
,
10
]
) in extremal combinatorics, which reduces the scope of our union bound. Specifically, if there were a large colour class
C^{\prime}\subseteq X
after sprinkling, we show that this would imply the existence of a much smaller “fingerprint” set
S\subseteq X
which sees many vertices (specifically, many vertices of
C^{\prime}
) at a variety of different distances. We can then take a cheaper union bound over the smaller fingerprint sets
S
.

We remark that the above sketch was very simplified, and serves only to illustrate the rough ideas. The full proof of
Theorem

1.3
(which appears in
Section

6

1.4.
Further directions

There are a very large number of natural directions for further research. First is the question of optimising the probability implicit in the “whp” in each of
Theorems

1.2
,
1.3
, and
1.4
, and improving the runtimes of the relevant algorithms. In the setting of
Theorem

1.1
, both these issues were comprehensively settled by Babai and Kucera
[
9
]
: they showed that, except with exponentially small probability, two steps of colour refinement suffice (yielding a linear-time algorithm).

The algorithm in
Theorem

1.2
(colour refinement) runs in time
O((n+m)\log n)
, where
n
and
m
are the numbers of vertices and edges of our graph of interest, while the algorithm in
Theorem

1.4
naïvely runs in time
O(n(n+m)\log n)
, due to repeated iteration of the colour refinement algorithm. With some careful analysis, it may be possible to bound the necessary number of colour refinement steps, to obtain an optimal linear-time algorithm in the setting of
Theorem

1.2
. In fact, the work of Gaudio, Rácz and Sridhar
[
39
]
, mentioned earlier in the introduction, already makes some initial steps in this direction: they essentially show that three steps of colour refinement suffice, as long as
p
has order of magnitude between
(\log n)^{2}/n
and
(\log n)^{-3}
. It seems plausible that in fact three steps of colour refinement suffice as long as
p\geq(1+\varepsilon)\log n/n
, and recent work on “shotgun reassembly”
[
38
,
48
]
indicates that there should be a phase transition between two steps of colour refinement being necessary, and three steps being necessary, at around
p=(\log n)^{2}(\log\log n)^{-3}/n
.

With similar considerations on the necessary number of colour refinement steps, and the necessary number of iterations of the colour refinement algorithm, it may also be possible to improve the runtime in the setting of
Theorem

1.4
to near-linear-time. Regarding
Theorem

1.3
: this algorithm also naïvely runs in about quadratic-time, due to the use of the 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm. However, we do not need the full power of the general algorithms that we cite, and it seems plausible that special-purpose variants could be designed that might also yield a near-linear-time algorithm.

Also, there is the possibility of improving on the amount of random perturbation in
Theorem

1.3
. In particular, taking advantage of group-theoretic techniques (e.g., using the quasipolynomial-time algorithm of Babai
[
7
]
), it might be possible to design a canonical labelling scheme that becomes effective with tiny amounts of random perturbation (e.g., perturbation probability
o(1/n)
). In the case where
G_{0}
is a regular graph, it may also be worth considering alternative models of random perturbation that do not destroy the regularity of
G_{0}
, as colour refinement is completely ineffective for regular graphs. Perhaps surprisingly, this may actually make the problem
easier
, as sparse random regular graphs are much better expanders than sparse Erdős–Rényi random graphs (cf. the work of Bollobás
[
17
]
showing that distance profiles provide an efficient canonical labelling scheme even for very sparse random regular graphs).

Next, there is the possibility of stronger connections between our results on smoothed analysis for graph isomorphism, and the work of Spielman and Teng on smoothed analysis for linear optimisation. Indeed, (a slight variant of) the colour refinement algorithm is used for
dimension reduction
in linear optimisation (see
[
42
]
) and it would be interesting to consider smoothed analysis in this setting.

There is also the possibility of considering smoothed analysis for many different types of graph algorithms other than isomorphism-testing. Some early work in this direction was undertaken by Spielman and Teng
[
73
]
(see also
[
62
,
63
,
12
,
13
]
), but somewhat surprisingly, despite the algorithmic origin of the smoothed analysis framework, there is now a much larger body of work on randomly perturbed graphs in extremal and probabilistic graph theory (see for example
[
57
,
58
,
16
,
20
,
11
,
44
,
21
,
56
,
30
,
49
,
43
,
22
,
15
,
29
,
3
,
1
]
) than on algorithmic questions.

In particular, it is worth remarking that canonical labelling is closely related to the so-called “network alignment problem” (also known as the “graph matching problem”), in which the objective is to find a mapping between the vertex sets of two graphs such that the number of adjacency disagreements between the two graphs is minimised. See for example
[
33
]
(and the references therein) for recent work on alignment of random graphs; it would be interesting to explore to what extent this can be extended to the smoothed analysis stetting.

2.1.
Canonical labelling

We start by formally defining the notion of canonical labelling.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{n}
be the set of all graphs on the vertex set
\{1,\dots,n\}
, and let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
be the set of all permutations of
\{1,\dots,n\}
. A
labelling scheme
is a map
\Phi:\mathcal{G}_{n}\to\mathcal{S}_{n}
. For a graph
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
, we write
\Phi((G))\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
for the graph obtained by applying the permutation
\Phi(G)
to the vertices and edges of
G
.

We say that a family of graphs
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{n}
is
isomorphism-closed
if it is a disjoint union of isomorphism classes in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
. For an isomorphism-closed family of graphs
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{n}
, we say that
\Phi:\mathcal{G}_{n}\to\mathcal{S}_{n}
is a
canonical
labelling scheme for
\mathcal{F}
if
\Phi((G))=\Phi((G^{\prime}))
whenever
G,G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
are isomorphic.

Intuitively speaking, one should think of a canonical labelling scheme as a way to assign labels to an
unlabelled
graph
G
, where we are not allowed to make “arbitrary choices” (e.g., arbitrarily breaking ties), unless different outcomes of those choices correspond to automorphisms of
G
.

Note that a canonical labelling scheme
\Phi
for an isomorphism-closed family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{n}
gives rise to a simple way to test for isomorphism between a graph
G\in\mathcal{F}
and any other graph
G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
: simply compute
\Phi((G))
and
\Phi((G^{\prime}))
, and compare the two labelled graphs (e.g., by their adjacency matrices).

The primary engine underlying all canonical labelling schemes in this paper is the so-called
colour refinement algorithm
. It was informally introduced in the introduction; here we define it more formally.

Definition 2.2
.

For a graph
G
and a colouring
c:V(G)\to\Omega
, the
refinement

\mathcal{R}_{G}c:V(G)\to\Omega\times\mathbb{N}^{\Omega}
of
c
is defined by
\mathcal{R}_{G}c(v)=(c(v),(d_{\omega}(v))_{\omega\in\Omega})
, where
d_{\omega}(v)
denotes the number of neighbours of
v
which have colour
\omega
(with respect to
c
). Note that every colouring
c:V(G)\to\Omega
defines a partition of
V(G)
into colour classes. If
c,c^{\prime}
define the same vertex partition, then
\mathcal{R}_{G}c,\mathcal{R}_{G}c^{\prime}
also define the same vertex partition. So, we can actually view
\mathcal{R}_{G}
as an operator on vertex partitions.

Also, denote the
t
-fold iteration of
\mathcal{R}_{G}
by

\mathcal{R}_{G}^{t}c=\underbrace{\mathcal{R}_{G}\dots\mathcal{R}_{G}}_{t\text{ times}}c.

Note that for any colouring
c
, the partition defined by
\mathcal{R}_{G}c
is always a refinement of the partition defined by
c
. For a vertex set of size
n
, the longest possible chain of proper refinements has length
n
, so if we start with any colouring
c
in an
n
-vertex graph, and repeatedly apply the refinement operation
\mathcal{R}_{G}
(at most
n
times), we always end up with a “stable colouring”. That is to say, there is always some
t\leq n
such that
\mathcal{R}_{G}^{t}c
and
\mathcal{R}_{G}^{t+1}c
define the same vertex partition.
We denote the corresponding stable colouring by
\mathcal{R}_{G}^{*}c=\mathcal{R}_{G}^{t}c
.

We will write
\sigma_{G}:V(G)\to\{0\}
for the trivial colouring in which every vertex is given the same “0” colour.

So,
\mathcal{R}_{G}\sigma_{G}
distinguishes vertices by their degree, and
\mathcal{R}_{G}^{*}\sigma_{G}
is the stable colouring obtained by the colour refinement algorithm as described in the introduction. See
Figure

1
for an example. For all the notation introduced in
Definition

2.2
, we will often omit the subscript “
G
” when it is clear from context.

If
\mathcal{R}^{*}\sigma
assigns every vertex in

from

: we simply fix some total order of all the potential colours of vertices (e.g. lexicographical order), and assign the labels

in increasing order. However, it is not completely obvious that this gives rise to an efficient canonical labelling scheme: naïvely, after many steps of refinement the sizes of the labels become extremely large. In order to address this, after each step of colour refinement we can re-encode all colours with some subset of the integers
\{1,\dots,n\}
(i.e., we do not actually care about the identities of the colours, only the induced partition of the vertices), but we must be careful to do this “in a canonical way” (i.e., all choices can only depend on the isomorphism class of our graph). This issue is comprehensively handled by Berkholz, Bonsma and Grohe
[
14
]
(building on ideas in
[

45

]
), who present an algorithm that computes a canonical stable colouring of

O((n+m)\log n)
(where

).

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{n}
be the set of all graphs on the vertex set
\{1,\dots,n\}
for which
\mathcal{R}^{*}\sigma
assigns each vertex a distinct colour. Then, the colour refinement algorithm defined in
[
14

is a canonical labelling scheme for
\mathcal{F}
. It computes a canonical labelling for a graph

in time
O((n+m)\log n)
(where
m
is the number of edges in

We will use

Definition 2.4
.

A
canonical colouring scheme

(c_{G})_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}
is an assignment of a colouring

Note that the colour refinement algorithm, starting from the trivial colouring, defines a canonical colouring scheme (i.e., let

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}
be a canonical labelling scheme for a graph family
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{n}
, and let
(c_{G})_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}
be a canonical colouring scheme, such that
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}(G)
and

can both be computed in time
T
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the family of all graphs
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
such that
D(G,c_{G})\in\mathcal{H}
. Then there is a canonical labelling scheme
\Phi
for
\mathcal{F}
, such that for every
G\in\mathcal{F}
, we can compute
\Phi(G)
in time
O(n^{2}+T)
.

Proof.

For a graph
G\in\mathcal{F}
, our canonical labelling
\Phi(G)\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
is computed as follows. First compute the colouring
c_{G}
(in time
T
).
Then, compute the disparity graph

(this can be done in time
O(n^{2})
). To each vertex

\Big(c_{G}(v),\,\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}(D(G,c_{G}))(v)\Big).

Then, obtain

by ordering the vertices lexicographically by their associated pairs.
To see that this indeed describes a canonical labelling scheme, note that every automorphism of

which fixes the colouring

is an automorphism of

.
∎

We will need to combine

2.5
with an efficient canonical labelling scheme for graphs with small connected components (it is easy to deal with each connected component separately, so this really comes down to worst-case guarantees for graphs on a fixed number of vertices). There are algorithms based on group-theoretic ideas with very strong theoretical guarantees (due to recent work of Babai

Theorem 2.6

There is a canonical labelling scheme

Corollary 2.7

Fix a polynomial-time computable canonical colouring scheme

To use

For a graph

Let

Remark 2.12
.

With the same considerations, it is straightforward to characterise the automorphisms of a graph

Basic facts about colour refinement

We collect some simple observations about colour refinement. First, we observe that stable colourings of
G

16

Definition 2.15
.

For a graph

of

Consider any graph
G

\mathcal{R}^{*}c

In fact,

Consider any graph

Finally, we observe that the colour refinement algorithm can “see” which pairs of colours in a graph

Definition 2.18

For a graph

Then, define the

.

Fix a graph

The key point is that

2.3.
The 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm

In this section we introduce a variant of colour refinement called the
2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm
, which is a refinement algorithm for colourings of pairs of vertices, instead of colourings of single vertices
17
17

17

As the reader may have guessed, the colour refinement algorithm (also known as the 1-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm) and the 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm are both special cases of a more general
k
-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm
, first defined in an influential paper of Cai, Fürer and Immerman
[
23
]
. This will not be relevant for the present paper.
.

Definition 2.20
.

For a graph
G
and a colouring
f:V(G)^{2}\to\Omega
of the ordered pairs of vertices of
G
, the
2-dimensional refinement

(2\mathcal{R})f:V(G)^{2}\to\Omega\times\mathbb{N}^{\Omega}
of

is defined by
(2\mathcal{R})f(u,v)=(f(u,v),(d_{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}}(u,v))_{(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in\Omega^{2}})
,
where
d_{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}}(u,v)
denotes the number of vertices
w\in V(G)
such that
(f(u,w),f(w,v))=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
. As in
Definition

2.2
, let
(2\mathcal{R})^{*}f
be the “stable colouring”, obtained by iterating the refinement operation
2\mathcal{R}
until we see the same vertex-pair partition twice in a row.

For a vertex colouring
c:V(G)\to\Omega
, we write
\phi_{G,c}:V(G)^{2}\to\Omega\sqcup\{0,1\}
for the colouring defined by
\phi_{G,c}(u,v)=1
if
uv
is an edge,
\phi_{G,c}(u,v)=c(v)
if
u=v
, and
\phi_{G}(u,v)=0
otherwise (here we write
\sqcup
for disjoint union). We write
\phi_{G}
as shorthand for
\phi_{G,\sigma_{G}}
, recalling that
\sigma_{G}
is the trivial vertex-colouring of
V(G)
assigning every vertex the same colour.

Also, for a colouring
f:V(G)^{2}\to\Omega
, define the “vertex-projection”
\Pi f:V(G)\to\Omega
by
\Pi f(v)=f(v,v)
. Let
\mathcal{V}^{*}=\Pi(2\mathcal{R})^{*}
be the vertex-projection of the 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm.

Remark 2.21
.

There are (at least) two slightly different definitions of the 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm, with different properties. The definition above was the original one introduced by Cai, Fürer and Immerman
[
23
]
, and is sometimes called the “2-folklore-Weisfeiler–Leman” algorithm (see
[
66
]
).

The first crucial fact we need about the 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm is that it is efficiently computable. This was first observed by Cai, Fürer and Immerman
[
23
]
.

Fact 2.22
.

For any graph
G
, the stable vertex-pair-colouring
(2\mathcal{R})^{*}\phi_{G}
can be computed in polynomial time. Moreover, this can be done “canonically” in the sense that different relabellings of the same graph always yield the same colouring
(2\mathcal{R})^{*}\phi_{G}
.

Specifically, Cai, Fürer and Immerman
[
23
]
proved that the stable vertex-pair-colouring
(2\mathcal{R})^{*}\phi_{G}
can be computed in time
O(n^{3}\log n)
, but we will need an additional
O(\log n)
“overhead” in order to make sure that all choices are made canonically (as discussed before
Theorem

2.3
, to prevent the sizes of the labels becoming too large we need to re-label the colours at each step; if we do not wish to make “arbitrary choices” then for this re-labelling we need a lexicographic sorting step). In the setting of the colour refinement algorithm, Bonsma, Berkholz and Grohe showed how to remove this “
O(\log n)
overhead”; this may also be possible for the 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm, but we were not able to find this in the literature.

The next crucial fact we need about the 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm (really, the entire reason we need to consider this algorithm, instead of the simpler colour refinement algorithm) is that it can “detect distances between vertices”, generalising
Fact

2.19
. Specifically,
\mathcal{V}^{*}\phi_{G}(u)=(2\mathcal{R})^{*}\phi_{G}(u,u)
tells us the number of vertices of each colour at every given distance from
u
. This can be easily proved by induction (see also
[
25
, Theorem 2.6.7]
for a proof in a somewhat more general framework). We will need a version of this fact for the generalised disparity graphs defined in
Definition

2.18
, which can be proved in the same way.

Fact 2.23
.

Fix a vertex-colouring
c
of a graph
G
, and let
\Omega
be the set of colours used by
\mathcal{V}^{*}c
. Consider any
L\in\{0,1\}^{\Omega\times\Omega}
, any two vertices
u,v
for which
\mathcal{V}^{*}c(u)=\mathcal{V}^{*}c(v)
, any colour
\omega\in\Omega
and any
i\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
. Then, with respect to the graph
D_{L}(G,\mathcal{V}^{*}c)
, the number of
\omega
-coloured vertices at distance
i
from
u
is exactly the same as the number of
\omega
-coloured vertices at distance
i
from
v
.

A special case of
Fact

2.23
(where
L
is the all-0 matrix, and
i=1
) is that
\mathcal{V}^{*}\phi_{G,c}(u)
is always an equitable partition. Since
\mathcal{V}^{*}\phi_{G,c}(u)
is a refinement of
c
, it is a refinement of the stable colouring
\mathcal{R}^{*}c
produced by the colour refinement algorithm, by
Fact

2.16
. (Informally, the 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm is “at least as powerful” as the colour refinement algorithm).

Fact 2.24
.

For any vertex-colouring
c
of a graph
G
, the vertex-colouring
\mathcal{V}^{*}\phi_{G,c}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{R}^{*}c
.

Finally, we need an analogue of
Fact

2.17
, on the “consistency” of the 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm: if we start the 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm from any coarsening of
(2\mathcal{R})^{*}f
, we will end up with a coarsening of
(2\mathcal{R})^{*}f
.
This is a direct consequence of, e.g., the discussion in
[
25
, Section 2.6.1]
(see also
[
37
, Proposition 2.1]
), which characterises the vertex-pair-partition defined by

as the unique coarsest coherent configuration refining

(here “coherent configurations” are the 2-dimensional analogues of the equitable partitions defined in
Definition

2.15
).

Fact 2.25
.

Fix any graph
G
and any vertex-pair colouring
f:V(G)^{2}\to\Omega
. If
g:V(G)^{2}\to\Omega
is a coarsening of
(2\mathcal{R})^{*}f
, then
(2\mathcal{R})^{*}g
is a coarsening of
(2\mathcal{R})^{*}f
.

2.4.
Probabilistic estimates

At several points in the paper we will need some general-purpose probabilistic estimates. First, we need a Chernoff bound for sums of independent random variables; the following can be deduced from
[
47
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Lemma 2.26

(Chernoff bound)
.

Let
X
be a sum of independent random variables, each of which take values in
\{0,1\}
.
Then for any
\delta>0
we have

\mathbb{P}[X\leq(1-\delta)\mathbb{E}X]\leq\exp(-\delta^{2}\mathbb{E}X/2),\qquad\mathbb{P}[X\geq(1+\delta)\mathbb{E}X]\leq\exp(-\delta^{2}\mathbb{E}X/(2+\delta)).

We also need a Littlewood–Offord type
anticoncentration estimate
, showing that certain sums of independent random variables are unlikely to take any particular value. Specifically, we need a generalisation of the classical Erdős–Littlewood–Offord theorem to the “
p
-biased” setting, essentially due to Juškevičius and Kurauskas
[
50
]
and Singhal
[
72
]
.

Definition 2.27
.

Let
M(n,p)
be the modal probability of a
\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p)
random variable, i.e.,

M(n,p)=\binom{n}{x}p^{x}(1-p)^{n-x},

for
x=\lfloor(n+1)p\rfloor
. (It is well-known—see for example
[
51
]
—that
\lfloor(n+1)p\rfloor
is always a mode for
\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p)
).

Theorem 2.28
.

Fix a positive integer
n
and a probability
p\in(0,1/2)
. Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
be non-zero real numbers, and let
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
be independent
\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)
random variables (meaning
\mathbb{P}[\xi_{i}=1]=p
and
\mathbb{P}[\xi_{i}=0]=1-p
). Then

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}[a_{1}\xi_{1}+\dots+a_{n}\xi_{n}=x]\leq M(\lfloor n/2\rfloor,p)=O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{np}}}}\right).

The estimate
O(1/\sqrt{np})
was first proved by Costello and Vu
[
27
, Lemma 8.2]
. The sharper bound
M(\lfloor n/2\rfloor,p)
can be deduced from a recent result due to Juškevičius and Kurauskas
[
50
]
and independently Singhal
[
72
]
, as follows.

Proof of
Theorem

2.28
.

First note that we may assume
n
is even (otherwise, condition on any outcome of
\xi_{n-1}
to reduce to the same question about
a_{1}\xi_{1}+\dots+a_{n-1}\xi_{n-1}
, noting in this case that
\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
).

The main result of
[
72
,
50
]
is that (given our assumption that
n
is even), the quantity
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}[a_{1}\xi_{1}+\dots+a_{n}\xi_{n}=x]
is maximised when all of the
a_{i}
are equal to
1
or
-1
. By symmetry, we can assume
a_{1},\dots,a_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}=1
. Conditioning on arbitrary outcomes of
a_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1},\dots,a_{n}
, we see that the probability in question is bounded by

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\big[a_{1}\xi_{1}+\dots+a_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\xi_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor n/2}}\right\rfloor}=x\big]=M(\lfloor n/2\rfloor,p).

(here we used that
a_{1},\dots,a_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}=1
to see that
a_{1}\xi_{1}+\dots+a_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\xi_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor n/2}}\right\rfloor}\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(\lfloor n/2\rfloor,p)
).

Lemma 3.5
.

Let

\mathcal{L}^{i}(u,v)

\mathcal{R}^{*}\sigma(u)\neq\mathcal{R}^{*}\sigma(v)

\mathcal{L}^{i}(u,v)

Proof of
Lemma

.

be the multiset of vertices of degree

\mathcal{L}_{d}^{i}(u,v)

for all

. To this end, we will prove (by induction on

(of degree-

\mathcal{M}_{d}^{i}(u)=\mathcal{L}_{d}^{i}(u,v)\cup W_{d}^{i}(\{u,v\})
and
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{i}(v)=\mathcal{L}_{d}^{i}(v,u)\cup W_{d}^{i}(\{u,v\})
(these are multiset unions, respecting multiplicity). By

3.2
, we have
|\mathcal{M}_{d}^{i}(u)|=|\mathcal{M}_{d}^{i}(v)|
, so this suffices.

As the (

\mathcal{R}^{*}\sigma(u)=\mathcal{R}^{*}\sigma(v)

have the same degree, thus the statement holds with

, and assume that for all

there exists a set

\mathcal{M}_{d}^{i-1}(u)=W_{d}^{i-1}(\{u,v\})\cup\mathcal{L}_{d}^{i-1}(u,v)\text{ and }\mathcal{M}_{d}^{i-1}(v)=W_{d}^{i-1}(\{u,v\})\cup\mathcal{L}_{d}^{i-1}(v,u)\text{ for }d\geq 1.

4.
Expansion for views

Recall the notation
\mathcal{S}^{i}(\{u,v\})
defined in
Definition

3.4
, describing the set of vertices which appear differently in the views
\mathcal{T}(u)
and
\mathcal{T}(v)
for the first time at depth
i
.

In this section we prove that, for two vertices
u,v
in an appropriately randomly perturbed graph
G
, it is very likely that whenever
\mathcal{S}^{\leq i}(\{u,v\})
grows reasonably large,
u
and
v
end up with different colours in the stable colouring
\mathcal{R}^{*}\sigma
. This is formalised in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
G_{0}
be a graph on the vertex set
\{1,\dots,n\}
and let
G_{\mathrm{rand}}\sim\mathbb{G}(n,p)
, for some
p\in[0,1/2]
satisfying

\frac{1+(\log n)^{-40}}{n}\leq p\leq\frac{100\log n}{n}.

Then whp
G=G_{0}\triangle G_{\mathrm{rand}}
satisfies the following property. For every pair of vertices
u,v
for which there is some
i
satisfying

(4.1)

|\mathcal{S}^{\leq i}_{G}(\{u,v\})|\geq\frac{100\log n}{\min\{(np-1)^{2},1\}},

we have
\mathcal{R}^{*}_{G}\sigma(u)\neq\mathcal{R}^{*}_{G}\sigma(v)
.

We remark that, while we did not necessarily make an effort to optimise the assumption on
p
, there is some significance to the “
1/n
”
essentially appearing in
Proposition

4.1
. Indeed, this corresponds to the celebrated
phase transition
for Erdős–Rényi random graphs (as briefly discussed in
Section

1.3.4
).

Morally speaking, the main reason why
Proposition

4.1
is true is that random graphs are extremely good
expanders
: starting from any reasonably large set of vertices
S
, the set of vertices within distance
i
of
S
grows geometrically in
i
until it spans nearly the whole graph. The following lemma (a key ingredient in our proof of
Proposition

4.1
) is a counterpart to this fact for the sets
\mathcal{S}^{i}(\{u,v\})
in a randomly perturbed graph: whenever
\mathcal{S}^{\leq i}(\{u,v\})
is reasonably large, there is some
j
for which
\mathcal{S}^{j}(\{u,v\})
is huge, spanning a significant proportion of the whole graph.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
G_{0},p,G_{\mathrm{rand}}
be as in
Proposition

4.1
. Then whp
G=G_{0}\triangle G_{\mathrm{rand}}
satisfies the following property. For every pair of vertices
u,v
for which there is some
i
satisfying
4.1
, there is some
j
satisfying

(4.2)

\big|\mathcal{S}^{j}_{G}(\{u,v\})\big|\geq\frac{n}{4(\log n)^{140}}.

We defer the proof of
Lemma

4.2
to the end of the section. Given
Lemma

4.2
, the strategy for the proof of
Proposition

4.1
is as follows. For each
u,v
satisfying
4.1
, and
j
as guaranteed by
Lemma

4.2
, we first observe that it is possible to reveal the set
\mathcal{S}^{j}_{G}(\{u,v\})
without revealing too much information about the random perturbation
G_{\mathrm{rand}}
inside this set. We wish to use the remaining randomness, together with a Littlewood–Offord-type anticoncentration inequality (
Theorem

2.28
), to show that it is very unlikely that the multisets
\mathcal{L}^{j}_{G}(u,v)
and
\mathcal{L}^{j}_{G}(v,u)
have the same degree statistics.

This final step is actually rather delicate. Indeed, if we let
Q_{d}^{i}(u,v)
be the number of vertices of degree
d
in
\mathcal{L}^{i}(u,v)
, minus the corresponding number in
\mathcal{L}^{i}(v,u)
, it is not too hard to see that
\sum_{d}dQ_{d}^{i}(u,v)
(which is the number of edges incident to
\mathcal{L}^{i}(u,v)
plus the number of edges spanned by
\mathcal{L}^{i}(u,v)
) can be expressed as a weighted sum of Bernoulli random variables (as required for
Theorem

2.28
). So, we can use
Theorem

2.28
to show that usually we have
\sum_{d}dQ_{d}^{i}(u,v)\neq 0
(in which case some
Q_{d}^{i}(u,v)\neq 0
, so the degree statistics of
\mathcal{L}^{i}(u,v)
and
\mathcal{L}^{i}(v,u)
must be different). However, this estimate is not good enough for a union bound over choices of
u,v
, and it is necessary to understand in much more detail the
joint
anticoncentration of
dQ_{d}^{i}(u,v)
, between different
d
. This is accomplished by first revealing almost all information about
G_{\mathrm{rand}}
except the edges inside a tiny subset of
\mathcal{S}^{j}_{G}(\{u,v\})
(which we call a “hole”). In the resulting conditional probability space, we obtain fairly weak anticoncentration bounds on the
dQ_{d}^{i}(u,v)
, but we can show that
dQ_{d}^{i}(u,v)
is essentially independent from
d^{\prime}Q_{d^{\prime}}^{i}(u,v)
whenever
d
and
d^{\prime}
are reasonably far apart (so we can multiply weak anticoncentration bounds together for a much stronger final bound).

The details of the proof of
Proposition

4.1
are as follows.

Proof of
Proposition

4.1
.

Fix vertices
u,v
. Our objective is to prove that with probability
1-o(n^{-2})
, there is no
i
satisfying
4.1
, or the property in
Lemma

4.2

4.1.
Exploring views: proof of

As mentioned earlier, roughly speaking

and

Proof of

.

Recall that we are trying to prove that for all pairs of distinct vertices

holds for some

holds for some

Step 1: Reducing to single-step expansion.

Our main objective will be to prove that whp

(4.4)

\frac{100\log n}{\min\{(np-1)^{2},1\}}\leq|\mathcal{S}^{\leq q}(\{u,v\})|\leq\frac{n}{(\log n)^{100}},

throughout the revelation procedure.

Second, we will see that type-

. For a vertex

Step 3: Coupling with a sequence of coin flips.

We first make some observations about the success probabilities in critical moments.

Claim 4.8
.

Let
p^{\prime}=1-400/(\log n)^{99}
.

•

Every type-
Z
critical moment succeeds with probability at least
p^{\prime}
(conditional on all information revealed until that moment).

•

Every type-
W
critical moment advances with probability
p
. Given that we advance, we then succeed with probability at least
p^{\prime}
.

Proof of claim.

For the first bullet point: at a type-
Z
critical moment, we are revealing the status of
|\mathcal{S}^{t}(\{u,v\})|<n/(\log n)^{100}
edges of
G_{\mathrm{rand}}
(recalling that we continue the revelation procedure only as long as

, the critical moment succeeds if none of these edges are present. So, the probability that the critical moment succeeds is at least

(1-p)^{n/(\log n)^{100}}\geq e^{-2np/(\log n)^{100}}\geq e^{-{200}/{(\log n)^{99}}}\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{400}{(\log n)^{99}}}}\right)=p^{\prime},

recalling that
p\leq 100\log n/n
.

The second bullet point is very similar: at a type-
W
critical moment, we advance with probability

(as that is the probability any particular edge appears in

), and then we succeed if no edges are present among a certain set of at most

∎

Now, let
(\alpha_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}

•

whenever we encounter a

-critical moment, we check the next unrevealed random variable

, and if
\alpha_{i}=1
then the critical moment succeeds;

•

whenever we encounter a
W
-critical moment, we check the next unrevealed random variable
\beta_{j}
, and if
\beta_{j}=1
then the critical moment advances. We then check the next unrevealed random variable
\alpha_{i}
, and if
\alpha_{i}=1
then the critical moment succeeds.

Let
R_{\alpha}^{\leq q}
be the total number of
\alpha_{i}
that we reveal in the first

\mathcal{S}^{\leq q+1}(\{u,v\})
). Let

be the number of times we see
\alpha_{i}=1
, among these
R_{\alpha}^{\leq q}

different
\alpha_{i}

Step 4: Interpreting the coin flips.

Every time we see

(4.6)

Every time we see

Also, note that at step

\alpha_{i}
is revealed for each

|Z^{t}|\leq R_{\alpha}^{\leq q}
for each

.
We can also relate

to

Claim 4.9
.

If

then

R_{\beta}^{\leq q}\geq|\mathcal{S}^{\leq q}(\{u,v\})|\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-2R_{\alpha}^{\leq q}-\frac{n}{(\log n)^{100}}}}\right).

Proof of claim.

For every
t\leq q
and every pair of vertices

and

, the only reason there might not be a type-

\displaystyle R_{\beta}^{\leq q}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{t\leq q}|\mathcal{S}^{t}(\{u,v\})|\cdot|W^{t}\setminus Z^{t}|-\max_{t\leq q}|\mathcal{S}^{t}(\{u,v\})|\cdot R_{\alpha}^{\leq q}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{t\leq q}|\mathcal{S}^{t}(\{u,v\})|\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-|\mathcal{S}^{\leq t}(\{u,v\})|-|Z^{t}|}}\right)-|\mathcal{S}^{\leq q}(\{u,v\})|\cdot R_{\alpha}^{\leq q}

\displaystyle\geq|\mathcal{S}^{\leq q}(\{u,v\})|\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-|\mathcal{S}^{\leq q}(\{u,v\})|-R_{\alpha}^{\leq q}}}\right)-|\mathcal{S}^{\leq q}(\{u,v\})|\cdot R_{\alpha}^{\leq q}.

The desired result follows, recalling our assumption

∎

Step 5: Concluding with Chernoff bounds.

Similarly, let

i.e.,

holds with probability

.

Now, recall that we are trying to prove that, with probability

, whenever

. It suffices to show that this property follows from

So, for the purpose of contradiction, suppose that

. First, note that

This and

R^{\leq q}_{\beta}\geq|\mathcal{S}^{\leq q}(\{u,v\})|\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-\frac{200n}{(\log n)^{99}}-\frac{n}{(\log n)^{100}}}}\right)\geq|\mathcal{S}^{\leq q}(\{u,v\})|\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-\frac{201n}{(\log n)^{99}}}}\right),

, and

Recalling that

∎

5.
Distinguishing vertices in the 2-core

In the previous section we showed that given two vertices
u,v
in an appropriately randomly perturbed graph
G
, it is very likely that whenever the sets
\mathcal{S}^{i}(\{u,v\})
grow reasonably large,
u
and
v
end up with different colours in the stable colouring
\mathcal{R}^{*}\sigma
. In this section we prove that this condition is typically satisfied for pairs of vertices
u,v
satisfying certain combinatorial conditions (defined in terms of the
2-core
of the random perturbation graph
G_{\mathrm{rand}}
). In particular, the main result of this section will directly imply
Theorem

1.2
(as we will see at the end of this section).

Definition 5.1
.

Recall from
Definition

1.7
that the
k
-core of a graph
G
is its maximum subgraph with minimum degree at least
k
.

•

Write
V_{k}(G)
for the vertex set of this
k
-core (so the
k
-core itself is
\mathrm{core}_{k}(G)=G[V_{k}(G)]
).

•

As in
Definition

2.10
, let
V_{2,3}(G)\subseteq V_{3}(G)
be the set of vertices which have degree at least
3
in the 2-core
G[V_{2}(G)]
.

•

Let
V_{2,3}^{\mathrm{safe}}(G)\subseteq V_{2,3}(G)
be the set of vertices
v\in V_{2,3}(G)
satisfying the following property. If we delete any two vertices (other than
v
), the connected component of
v
still contains at least three vertices of
V_{2,3}(G)
(including
v
).

In words, note that the 2-core can be viewed as a network of “hubs” (vertices with degree at least 3), joined by bare paths in which every interior vertex has degree 2. Each hub has at least three bare paths emanating from it, but it could happen that several of these bare paths lead to the same hub. We say a hub
v
is “safe” if it is not possible to delete two vertices to create a tiny connected component containing
v
and at most one other hub.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
G_{0}
be a graph on the vertex set
\{1,\dots,n\}
and let
G_{\mathrm{rand}}\sim G(n,p)
for some
p\in[0,1/2]
satisfying

p\geq\frac{1+(\log n)^{-40}}{n}.

Let
G=G_{0}\triangle G_{\mathrm{rand}}
. Then whp
G
has the property that
\mathcal{R}^{*}_{G}\sigma(u)\neq\mathcal{R}^{*}_{G}\sigma(v)
for every pair of distinct vertices
u,v
satisfying one of the following assumptions.

(1)

u\in V_{2,3}^{\mathrm{safe}}(G_{\mathrm{rand}})
, or

(2)

u,v\in V_{2,3}(G_{\mathrm{rand}})
, and
G_{0}
is the empty graph.

The idea of the proof of
Proposition

5.2
is as follows. We fix two distinct vertices
u,v
, and consider the same exploration procedure that featured in the proofs of
Propositions

4.1
and
4.2
: at step
t
we reveal the edges of
G_{\mathrm{rand}}
incident to
\mathcal{S}^{t}(\{u,v\})
to determine the next set
\mathcal{S}^{t+1}(\{u,v\})
.
Vertices of
\mathcal{S}^{t}(\{u,v\})
which lie in
V_{2,3}(G_{\mathrm{rand}})
are typically connected, via bare paths in
V_{2}(G)
, to at least three vertices of
V_{2,3}(G_{\mathrm{rand}})
. Our exploration process will follow these paths, and typically each of these “neighbouring” vertices in
V_{2,3}(G_{\mathrm{rand}})
will end up in some future
\mathcal{S}^{t^{\prime}}(\{u,v\})
. That is to say, each time our exploration process reaches a hub, it branches out into at least two more hubs; the resulting exponential growth means it will not take long for
\mathcal{S}^{\leq t}(\{u,v\})
to grow large enough to apply
Proposition

4.1
.
Indeed, if the exploration process “gets stuck” (with
\mathcal{S}^{i+1}(\{u,v\})=\emptyset
) before
4.1
is satisfied, some very unlikely events need to have happened: we need to have been in a subset of the 2-core with very poor expansion properties, or some edges must have shown up in atypical places during the iterative revelation process.

Proof of
Proposition

5.2
.

Before we do anything else, the first step is to reduce to the case where
p
is in a suitable range to apply
Proposition

4.1
.

Step 1: Making assumptions on
p
.

We can assume
p\leq 2\log n/n
, because for larger
p
we can simply view the random perturbation as a composition of two random perturbations. Specifically, we can choose
p_{1}\in[0,1]
in such a way that, if we take
G_{\mathrm{rand}}^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{G}(n,2\log n/n)
and
G_{1}\sim\mathbb{G}(n,p_{1})
, then
G_{\mathrm{rand}}
has the same distribution as
G_{1}\triangle G_{\mathrm{rand}}^{\prime}
. (Namely, we need
p_{1}
to satisfy the equation
p_{1}(1-2\log n/n)+(2\log n/n)(1-p_{1})=p
). Then, letting
G_{0}^{\prime}=G_{0}\triangle G_{1}
we have
G=G_{0}^{\prime}\triangle G_{\mathrm{rand}}^{\prime}
, so after conditioning on an outcome of
G_{0}^{\prime}
we are considering a randomly perturbed graph with edge perturbation probability exactly
2\log n/n
. Moreover, whp the sets
V_{2,3}^{\mathrm{safe}}(G_{\mathrm{rand}}^{\prime})
and
V_{2,3}^{\mathrm{safe}}(G_{\mathrm{rand}})
are identical: in this regime whp both,
G_{\mathrm{rand}}^{\prime}
and
G_{\mathrm{rand}}
are
3
-connected (see for example
[
36
, Theorem 4.3]
) hence
V_{2,3}^{\mathrm{safe}}(G_{\mathrm{rand}}^{\prime})=V_{3}(G_{\mathrm{rand}}^{\prime})=V(G)=V_{3}(G_{\mathrm{rand}})=V_{2,3}^{\mathrm{safe}}(G_{\mathrm{rand}})
.

Step 2: Expansion.
We observe a (very weak) expansion property of
G_{\mathrm{rand}}
: whp there are no small vertex cuts that disconnect a small dense subgraph.

Claim 5.3
.

Whp
G_{\mathrm{rand}}
has the following property. Consider disjoint vertex sets
T,U
, where
|T|\leq 2
and
|U|\leq(\log n)^{100}
. Suppose that
G_{\mathrm{rand}}
has no edges between
U
and
V(G)\setminus T
(i.e.,
T
disconnects
U
from the rest of the graph), and suppose that
G_{\mathrm{rand}}[T\cup U]
is connected. Then the number of edges in
G_{\mathrm{rand}}[U\cup T]
is at most
|U|+|T|
.

Proof of claim.

If the desired conclusion were to fail, there would be a pair of vertex sets
U,T
, with
|T|\leq 2
and
|U|\leq(\log n)^{100}
, such that
G_{\mathrm{rand}}
has no edges between
U
and
V(G)\setminus T
, and such that
G_{\mathrm{rand}}[U\cup T]
has at least
|U|+|T|+1
edges.

Summing over possibilities for
t=|U|+|T|
, the probability that such a pair of vertex sets exists is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{t=4}^{(\log n)^{100}+2}\binom{n}{t}t^{2}t^{t-2}(t^{2})^{2}p^{t+1}(1-p)^{(t-2)(n-t)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{t=4}^{n^{o(1)}}\bigg(\frac{en}{t}\bigg)^{t}t^{t+4}p^{t+1}e^{-pnt}e^{2np}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{t=4}^{n^{o(1)}}(enpe^{-pn})^{t}e^{2np}pt^{4}=o(1).

In the last equality we used that if
np\leq 1000
then
e^{2np}
, whereas if
np\geq 1000
then
(enpe^{-pn})^{t}e^{2np}\geq e^{-tnp/10}
, for
t\geq 4
.

To provide a bit of explanation for the above calculation: first note that
U\cup T
always spans at most
|U|+|T|
edges if
|U|+|T|\leq 3
, so we only need to consider
t\geq 4
. The number of possible ways to choose
U
and
T
is at most
\binom{n}{t}t^{2}
(first choose
U\cup T
, then choose
T
). Then, note that
G_{\mathrm{rand}}[U\cup T]
, being connected, must contain a spanning tree (which has
t-1

t^{t-2}
different ways, by Cayley’s formula), plus two additional edges (which can be chosen in at most
(t^{2})^{2}
different ways). These

edges are present in

with probability

. Also, since

disconnects
U
from the rest of the graph, there are

specific pairs of vertices which are
not
edges of
G_{\mathrm{rand}}
, namely the edges from

to
G-(U\cup T)
) (which happens with probability
(1-p)^{(t-2)(n-t)}
).

In the last line, we used the inequality
xe^{-x}\leq 1/e
(which holds for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
), and the assumption
p=n^{o(1)-1}
.
∎

Step 3: Exploration.
Define

f(n)=\frac{100\log n}{\min\{(np-1)^{2},1\}}\leq 100\log^{81}n\leq(\log n)^{100}.

Fix distinct vertices
u,v
. We will prove that with probability at least

, either the property in

, and

.

As in the proofs of

4.1
and

(this is condition

in

incident to

to determine the next set

or

Our goal is to rule out (with probability

) the possibility that

or

\mathcal{S}^{i+1}(\{u,v\})=\emptyset
and

Step 4: Atypical events during exploration.
Recall the definitions of the sets

from

(in

). Namely,

is the set of “candidate vertices” for

Recalling

, if there is exactly one edge between

and

(with respect to

between

and

always contributes to the growth of

, or unless there is more than one edge between

Claim 5.4
.

For

, we have

Proof of claim.

First, note that with probability

, we have

for all

. Indeed, consider the

(if such a step exists), and suppose that
|\mathcal{S}^{\leq t}(\{u,v\})|<f(n)

, each vertex in

independently ends up in

Now, let

, where the sum is over all

and

Note that, if we condition on the information revealed up to step

), then each vertex

, and each vertex

with probability at most

. So,

is stochastically dominated by a sum of at most
f(n)\cdot n=n^{1+o(1)}
Bernoulli trials with probability
n^{-2+o(1)}
, plus a sum of at most
f(n)\cdot 2f(n)=n^{o(1)}
Bernoulli trials with probability
n^{-1+o(1)}
(all independent). We conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[X_{W}+X_{Z}\geq 3]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{q=0}^{3}\mathbb{P}[X_{W}\geq q,\,X_{Z}\geq 3-q]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{q=0}^{3}\binom{n^{1+o(1)}}{q}(n^{-2+o(1)})^{q}\cdot\binom{n^{o(1)}}{3-q}(n^{-1+o(1)})^{3-q}=n^{-3+o(1)}=o(n^{-2}),

as desired.
∎

6.
Small components with very mild perturbation

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.3
. The proof is fairly involved, so we start with a rough description of the overall structure of the proof, supplementing the discussion in
Section

1.3
. In fact, the reader may benefit from re-reading
Section

1.3.8
before starting with this section. We also remark that a much simpler implementation of similar ideas can be found in
Appendix

B
, where we prove a weaker version of
Theorem

1.3
. Recall the definitions of the colour refinement and 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithms, from
Definitions

2.2
and
2.20
.

Recalling the notation in
Definitions

1.10
and
2.20
(on disparity graphs and the 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm), our goal is to prove that if
G=G_{0}\triangle G_{\mathrm{rand}}
is a randomly perturbed graph with perturbation probability as small as
O(1/n)
, then the connected components of the disparity graph
D(G,\mathcal{V}^{*}\phi_{G})
have size
O(\log n)
. Indeed, we saw in
Corollaries

2.7
and
2.22
that there is an efficient canonical scheme for graphs satisfying this property.

Towards this goal, we view the random perturbation
G_{\mathrm{rand}}
as the union of eight slightly sparser independent random perturbations
G_{\mathrm{rand}}^{1},\dots,G_{\mathrm{rand}}^{8}\sim\mathbb{G}(n,p^{\prime})
(this is called
sprinkling
). Assuming
p\geq 100/n
(thus
p^{\prime}>10/n
), standard results show that the 3-core of
G_{\mathrm{rand}}^{1}
(which is a subgraph of the 3-core of
G_{\mathrm{rand}}
) has at least
n/2
vertices, and by
Proposition

5.2
, whp all these vertices are assigned unique colours by
\mathcal{R}_{G}^{*}\sigma
(therefore by
\mathcal{V}^{*}\phi_{G}
, recalling
Fact

2.24
).

So, we first reveal the 3-core of
G_{\mathrm{rand}}^{1}
, and fix a set
V_{\mathrm{core}}
of
n/2
vertices in this 3-core. We then define the complementary set
U=V(G)\setminus V_{\mathrm{core}}
, and reveal all edges of
G_{\mathrm{rand}}
except those between
U
and
V_{\mathrm{core}}
. For the rest of the proof we work with the remaining seven rounds of random edges (in
G_{\mathrm{rand}}^{2},\dots,G_{\mathrm{rand}}^{8}
) between
U
and
V_{\mathrm{core}}
.

Specifically, whenever a vertex in
U
has at least three neighbours in
V_{\mathrm{core}}
, that vertex is guaranteed to be in the 3-core of
G_{\mathrm{rand}}
, and therefore we can assume it will be assigned a unique colour. So, we can consider a vertex-colouring of

that is refined with each round of sprinkling: at each round, new random vertices in
U
are assigned unique colours, and then the 2-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm propagates this information to obtain a new stable vertex-colouring. In each round, we show that the colour classes, and the components of the disparity graph, are broken into smaller and smaller pieces.

6.2.
Bounding the degrees

Now we begin to get to the heart of the proof of

Our first key lemma is the simple observation that this random assignment of unique colours typically causes the disparity graph to have maximum degree

Lemma 6.8
.

Consider an
n
-vertex graph

be a random refinement of

We remark that in our proof of

Proof of
Lemma

.

For a pair of vertices

be the set of vertices in

Claim 6.9
.

Whp the following holds. For every pair of vertices

\mathcal{R}^{*}c^{\prime}(u)\neq\mathcal{R}^{*}c^{\prime}(v)

Proof of claim.

For any particular pair of vertices

\mathcal{R}^{*}c^{\prime}(u)\neq\mathcal{R}^{*}c^{\prime}(v)
. So, if

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{R}^{*}c^{\prime}(u)=\mathcal{R}^{*}c^{\prime}(v)]\leq 0.3^{2\log n}=o(n^{-2})

∎

Now, suppose that the conclusion of

6.3.
Splitting connected components via colour classes

Our next key lemma is that in order to control the sizes of the connected components of the disparity graph (after sprinkling), it suffices to control the number of vertices of a single colour in a single component. Along the way we also prove that sprinkling typically ensures that large components can be partitioned into full colour classes.

Near-critical random graphs

In this section we prove that for random graphs of any density, colour refinement whp assigns distinct colours to each vertex of degree at least 3 in the 2-core. By

Theorem 7.1
.

For any sequence
(p_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}

Theorem

7.1

5.2

In this near-critical regime we cannot apply the machinery developed in
