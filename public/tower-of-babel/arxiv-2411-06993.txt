WALLABY Pilot Survey: Gas-Rich Galaxy Scaling Relations from Marginally-Resolved Kinematic Models
N. Deg, N. Arora, K. Spekkens, R. Halloran, B. Catinella, M. G. Jones, H. Courtois, K. Glazebrook, A. Bosma, L. Cortese, H. Dénes, A. Elagali, B.-Q. For, P. Kamphuis, B.S. Koribalski, K. Lee-Waddell, P. E. Mancera Piña, J. Mould, J. Rhee, L. Shao, L. Staveley-Smith, J. Wang, T. Westmeier, O.I. Wong

Abstract
We present the first set of galaxy scaling relations derived from kinematic models of the Widefield ASKAP L-band Legacy All-sky Blind surveY (WALLABY) pilot phase observations. Combining the results of the first and second pilot data releases, there are 236 available kinematic models. We develop a framework for robustly measuring H i disk structural properties from these kinematic models; applicable to the full WALLABY survey. Utilizing this framework, we obtained the H i size, a measure of the rotational velocity, and angular momentum for 148 galaxies. These comprise the largest sample of galaxy properties from an untargetted, uniformly observed and modelled H i survey to date. We study the neutral atomic Hydrogen ( H i ) size-mass, size-velocity, mass-velocity, and angular momentum-mass scaling relations. We calculate the slope, intercept, and scatter for these scaling relations and find that they are similar to those obtained from other H i surveys. We also obtain stellar masses for 92 of the 148 robustly measured galaxies using multiband photometry through the Dark Energy Sky Instrument Legacy Imaging Survey Data Release-10 images. We use a subset of 61 of these galaxies that have consistent optical and kinematic inclinations to examine the stellar and baryonic Tully Fisher relations, the gas fraction-disk stability and gas fraction-baryonic mass relations. These measurements and relations demonstrate the unprecedented resource that WALLABY will represent for resolved galaxy scaling relations in H i .

The currently released

It is worth noting that the PDR fields are overdense with many galaxies lying in group and cluster environments, which means many of the galaxies may be disturbed by interactions, ram pressure stripping, and more

As described in

,

fits galaxies using the Fully Automated TiRiFiC code (

;

, which itself is built around the Tilted Ring Fitting Code,

[
27

3DBAROLO

. For optimization,
WKAPP
first smooths the WALLABY observations to a
12~\textrm{km s}^{-1}
resolution before fitting. The two codes are set to only fit ‘flat disk’ models where the geometric parameters (such as position angle and
inclination
) are constant as a function of galactocentric radius.

In order for a
WKAPP
fit to be successful, first both
3DBAROLO
and
FAT
must individually fit the galaxy. Then the fits are visually inspected to determine whether the both fits are successful and consistent with each other. If both are indeed determined to be successful, the two fits are averaged together to produce a final
WKAPP
model.

The
WKAPP
geometric parameters are the average of the fit values from
FAT
and
3DBAROLO
with the uncertainty set to half difference between the two fit parameters.
The reason for using the difference between the codes is that it is typically larger than the errors reported by the individual codes
[
24
]
.
The two rotation curves (RCs) are then corrected to the model inclination, and these inclination corrected RC’s are averaged together to produce the output RC and uncertainties.

The procedure for
H
i
surface density (SD) profile is slightly different as
FAT
and
3DBAROLO
fit the SD profiles in fundamentally different manners. In
WKAPP
, the output SD profile is constructed by ellipse fitting the WALLABY moment 0 map using the model geometry. As such, the
WKAPP
SD profile for a system can extend beyond the model RC. These
projected
SD profiles can be corrected to face-on profiles using a simple
\cos(i)
approximation,
which we apply using the inclination returned by
WKAPP
.

While this inclination correction is
reliable
for WALLABY detections
in the outer SD profile when coupled with a beam correction, it fails in the beam-smeared inner regions
[
24
,
38
]
.

II.1
H
i
Masses

We calculated the
H
i
mass of the WALLABY galaxies from the WALLABY PDR1 and PDR2 fluxes, which are themselves calculated during the source finding analysis
[
96
]
. These fluxes are generally lower than single dish observations of the same galaxies due to issues in the data cleaning and the dirty beam
[
96
]
. We apply the statistical flux corrections recommended by
Westmeier et al. [96]
for PDR1 galaxies and
Murugeshan et al. [66]
for PDR2 galaxies.

The
H
i
masses of the kinematically modelled galaxies are

M_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}/\textrm{M}_{\odot}=49.7F_{\rm{corr}}d^{2}/[\rm{Jy~Hz~Mpc}^{2}]~,

(1)

where
F_{\rm{corr}}
is the corrected flux in units of
\rm{Jy~Hz}
and
d
is the distance in Mpc.
We then propagate the uncertainty in the uncorrected fluxes to uncertainties in the corrected
H
i
masses by keeping the relative uncertainty constant between the uncorrected flux and calculated
H
i
mass
(i.e. a 10% uncertainty in the uncorrected flux measurement will lead to a 10% uncertainty in the calculated
H
i
mass). It is also worth noting that we do not propagate uncertainties in the distances into mass uncertainties due to the non-linear relationship between velocity and distance caused by the use of CF3 distances and CMB corrected distances.

II.2
D_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
Extraction

The
H
i
diameter of a galaxy is twice the
H
i
radius,
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
, which itself is defined as the point where the de-projected
H
i
SD profile reaches a threshold of
1~\rm{M}_{\odot}~\rm{pc}^{-2}
.
This can be determined from the
WKAPP
SD profiles, but there are some complications to consider.
The
WKAPP
profiles are subject to beam smearing, so the point where they cross the
1~\textrm{M}_{\odot}~\rm{pc^{-2}}
limit is the uncorrected
H
i
radius,
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}},uc}
. We determine
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}},uc}
via linear interpolation between the outermost pair of points in the deprojected
WKAPP
SD profile bounding
1~\rm{M}_{\odot}~\rm{pc}^{-2}
.
Once the uncorrected radius is extracted, the corrected diameter can be found via the beam smearing correction of
Wang et al. [94]
:

D_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}^{2}=D_{\text{H\,\sc{i}},uc}^{2}-B^{2}~,

(2)

B=30\arcsec
is the full-width-at-half-maximum of the WALLABY clean beam.

Estimating the uncertainty on
D_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
is nontrivial due to uncertainties in both the inclination and
projected
SD profile. The procedure adopted for this work is:

1.

Calculate the upper and lower
projected
SD profiles via the addition/subtraction of the
WKAPP
profile uncertainties.

2.

Deproject these profiles using the minimum and maximum inclinations via

\Sigma_{\rm{min/max}}=\Sigma_{\rm{min/max,projected}}\cos\left(i\pm\epsilon_{i}\right)~,

(3)

where
i
is the inclination and
\epsilon_{i}
is
its
uncertainty. Note that the combination of the minimum profile with the maximum inclination, and vice versa, ensures that the deprojected curves are truly the minimum and maximum possible.

3.

Extract the uncorrected upper and lower limits on
D_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
using the maximum and minimum de-projected SD profiles.

4.

Correct these upper and lower limits for beam smearing using Eq.
2
.

II.3
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
Extraction

There are a variety of different rotational velocity definitions that can be used to characterize a galaxy. The most common is the velocity at the flat portion of the RC,
V_{\rm{flat}}
. Others include
V_{2.2}
,
V_{2R_{e}}
,
V_{\rm{max}}
, and
V_{\rm{out}}
, which are the velocities at 2.2 times an exponential disk scale length, at 2 times the radius which encloses half the light, at the maximum of the RC, and at the last point in the outer RC respectively (see
Catinella et al. 15
,
Lelli et al. 55
for a more in depth discussion of these velocities). Each of these have different motivations and strengths. More importantly, the use of different methods of characterizing the galaxy velocity can change various scaling relations like the bTFR
[
55
]
.

In this work we use
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
, the velocity of the galaxy at
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
, to characterize a galaxy’s rotation. There are a number of advantages to this definition. It does not presuppose a shape to the RC (e.g.
Catinella et al. 16
) or SD profiles, it is tied explicitly to the disk size (allowing for a straightforward propagation of uncertainties), it is located at a specific isodensity contour meaning that it should probe the same portion of every galaxy, and it can be used for models with very few RC points (where the calculation of
V_{\rm{flat}}
is difficult). Once
D_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
and its associated limits are calculated,
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
and its uncertainty,
\epsilon_{V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}}
can be calculated by linearly interpolating the RC to obtain the velocity at
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}},uc}
(the use of
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}},uc}
removes the need of beam correcting the entire RC before interpolating to get
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
).

Determining the uncertainties in
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
is slightly more complex as there are three distinct sources: the model RC uncertainty, the uncertainty in the RC due to the model inclination, and the uncertainty of
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
. The first two sources of uncertainty
can be combined analogously to the approach for
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}},uc}
in Sec.
II.2
:

V_{\rm{max/min}}(R)=\left[V(R)\pm\epsilon_{V}(R)\right]\frac{\sin(i\pm\epsilon_{i})}{\sin(i)}~.

(4)

Then the maximum and minimum model velocities at
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
can be calculated by linearly interpolating the maximum and minimum RCs at
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
. The combined model uncertainty,
\epsilon_{V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}},\rm{model}}
, is the average of the absolute differences of these maximum and minimum model velocities from
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
.

The third source of uncertainty to consider is that of
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}},uc}
. To account for this, we calculate the velocities of the model at the lower and upper limits of
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}},uc}
. Then the uncertainty on
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
due to the uncertainty in
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
,
\epsilon_{V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}},R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}}
, is set as the average of the absolute differences of these velocities from
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
. With both terms calculated, the total uncertainty for
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
is

\epsilon_{V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}}^{2}=\epsilon_{V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}},R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}}^{2}+\epsilon_{V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}},\rm{model}}^{2}~.

(5)

While the method of calculating
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
uncertainties is robust,
they may be artificially low because the
WKAPP
RC uncertainties arise from the difference between the
FAT
and
3DBAROLO
fits (see Sec.
II
): when the fits are similar, their relative difference will be low, leading to very low uncertainties in
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
. Those
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
measures with very low uncertainties then pull strongly on the fits presented in Sec.
III.2
.
In order to account for low
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
uncertainties, we have added
6~\textrm{km s}^{-1}
in quadrature to
\epsilon_{V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}}
for all galaxies. The
6~\textrm{km s}^{-1}
term is half the size of the smoothed
12~\textrm{km s}^{-1}
channels used in the
WKAPP
modelling.

The upper panels of Fig.
1
show this extraction for WALLABY J130618-173039 and WALLABY J133541-240428. In the case of WALLABY J130618-173039 (upper left panel), the extraction goes well and
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
is well constrained. But, because the limits on
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
are unknown for WALLABY J133541-240428, the uncertainty in
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
cannot be calculated.

In
WKAPP
it is possible for the SD profile to extend further than the RC for a given galaxy

Such is the case for WALLABY J133541-240428 (the right-hand panels of Figure
1
). Calculating
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
for these cases requires extrapolating the RC, which is not necessarily accurate. Since the accuracy of the extrapolation decreases
with its extent
, we restrict our sample to those galaxies where
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}-R_{\rm{outer,RC}}<0.5
beams, where
R_{\rm{outer,RC}}
is the outermost radial point of the RC. Because we wish to preserve gradient information, linear extrapolation of the last two points in the RC to determine
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
(as well as the velocity at the upper limit of
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
) is used.

II.4
Angular Momentum

Studies of well-resolved
H
i
disks
[
51
,
57
,
83
, e.g.]
typically measure specific angular momentum
j
as

j=\frac{\int_{0}^{R}2\pi R^{{}^{\prime}2}\Sigma(R^{{}^{\prime}})V(R^{{}^{\prime}})dR}{\int_{0}^{R}2\pi R^{{}^{\prime}}\Sigma(R^{{}^{\prime}})dR^{{}^{\prime}}}~,

(6)

where
\Sigma(R^{{}^{\prime}})
is the SD profile and
V(R^{{}^{\prime}})
is the RC. However, in the marginally resolved regime, the calculation of
\Sigma(R^{{}^{\prime}})
in the innermost regions is uncertain due to beam smearing (even 3D modelling is susceptible to beam smearing in the innermost region). And, when the surface density profile is derived from ellipse fitting, the
\cos(i)
correction is also unreliable in the inner region
[
24
,
38
]
. Instead, we use a simpler
j_{\rm{X},\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
defined as

j_{\rm{X},\text{H\,\sc{i}}}=\frac{1}{2}D_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}~.

(7)

This is effectively an upper limit on the specific angular momentum, which treats the system as though all the mass is located at
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
. As it is defined by the scale radius and velocity, the uncertainties can be calculated by propagating the relative error in the relevant quantities to
j_{\rm{X},\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
.

II.5
Stellar Masses

An important addition to the gas properties of galaxies is information about the distribution of stars retrieved through multi-band photometry.

In this work, we use
the deep optical imaging of the Dark Energy Sky Instrument Legacy Imaging Survey Date Release-10
[
25
,
26
, hereafter DESI;]
within the
grz
bands to extract stellar masses for the WALLABY
galaxies in the DESI footprint
.

For each galaxy, the surface brightness (SB) profile in each of the DESI
grz
photometric bands is calculated using the
AutoProf
package
[
85
]
.
AutoProf
examines an image and performs a non-parametric azimuthally-averaged decomposition to obtain radial profiles of position angles (PA), ellipticity and SB.

Owing to its high signal-to-noise ratio and minimal dust extinction, the optical centres, PA, and ellipticity profiles are calculated using the DESI-
r
band image. To obtain SB profiles in
gz
-bands, we apply forced photometry using the DESI-
r
geometric profiles to ensure a uniform extraction in all bands.

Our procedure for optical stellar mass estimates
is similar to that of

[
3
]
.
Firstly,
grz
SB profiles are truncated once an SB
uncertainty
of 0.22 mag arcsec
-2
is
reached
. Then the light inside that region is used to calculate the global luminosities and optical colors are calculated.
The stellar mass-to-light ratios (
\rm\Upsilon_{*}
) for converting the luminosity to the stellar mass estimates are calculated using various mass-to-light color relations
[
21
, MLCRs;]
.
Rather than use a single MLCR, we use five different MLCRs from
[
79
]
,
[
97
]
, and
[
34
]
which differ in assumptions about stellar population synthesis model, star formation history, dust extinction, and calibration input data (global vs. resolved
spectral energy distributions
). Each of these MLCRs can be used to obtain a
\rm\Upsilon_{*}
using any of the three colors (
g-r
,
g-z
, and
r-z
).
The use of the multiple colors provides better constraints on
\rm\Upsilon_{*}
estimates, reducing the random error on the final stellar mass measurement due to various assumptions in the different MLCRs. Each of the
\rm\Upsilon_{*}
are multiplied by the luminosities from the
g
,
r
and
z
bands yielding 45 stellar mass estimates (5 MLCRs
\times
3 colours
\times
3 luminosities).

The adopted stellar mass is the median of these 45 measurements and its uncertainty is their standard deviation
.

In principle, mid-infrared (MIR) imaging can also be used to estimate stellar mass estimates; but in practice the results are independent of the flux tracer implemented
[see
3
, for a comparison for optical-MIR
M_{*}
comparison]
.
Therefore, our choice of using the optical photometry emerges from the high resolution and the surface brightness depth of the DESI imaging.

While our procedure to extract optical stellar masses reduces the random error, it should be noted that a systematic error of
\sim 0.3\,{\rm dex}
(related to assumptions about star formation history, dust extinction, calibration input data, etc.) still remains
[
21
]
. It is
also
important to note here that we are unable to obtain stellar masses for every kinematically modelled galaxy in this study.
Some galaxies lie in the zone of avoidance and have no known optical counterpart, while for some other galaxies the DESI imaging is unavailable in all three bands.
Ultimately, there are 92 galaxies with robust
H
i

structural properties
and stellar masses.

The stellar masses can be combined with the
H
i
masses to obtain baryonic masses for galaxies. We set the baryonic mass,
M_{b}
to

M_{b}=M_{*}+1.35M_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}~,

(8)

where
M_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
is the
H
i
mass,
M_{*}
is the stellar mass. The factor of
1.35
is used to convert the
H
i
mass to a total gas mass including helium and metals
[
1
,
71
]
.

II.6
Gas Fraction and Stability

The combination of stellar masses and angular momentum allows for probes of the gas fraction and disk stability that were posited in
Obreschkow et al. [70]
. The atomic gas fraction is

f_{\rm{atm}}=\frac{1.35M_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}}{M_{b}}~,

(9)

which means that it goes to 1 as the baryonic mass becomes gas dominated.

The ‘global disk stability’ parameter
q
introduced in
Obreschkow & Glazebrook [69]
, which is related to the Toomre Q parameter
[
88
]
. Explicitly this parameter is defined as

q=\frac{j_{b}V_{\rm{disp}}}{GM_{b}}~,

(10)

where
j_{b}
is the baryonic specific angular momentum and
V_{\rm{disp}}
is the gas velocity dispersion. The baryonic specific angular momentum is obtained by integrating Eq.
6
with the baryonic surface density profile rather than the gas surface density. As discussed in Sec.
II.4
, such integrations are unreliable for the majority of the WALLABY models, so we adopt an alternate stability parameter definition,

q_{\rm{X}}=\frac{j_{\rm{X},\text{H\,\sc{i}}}V_{\rm{disp}}}{GM_{b}}~.

(11)

For the WALLABY PDR models, all velocity dispersions are fixed to
8~\textrm{km s}^{-1}

[
24
]
. This value was chosen based on the choice to Hanning smooth the cubes to
12~\textrm{km s}^{-1}
prior kinematic modelling as well as the results of
Tamburro et al. [87]
who found that galaxies typically have a tight range of gaseous velocity dispersions of
8~\textrm{km s}^{-1}\leq V_{\rm{disp}}\leq 12~\textrm{km s}^{-1}
(see also
Iorio et al. 43
,
Bacchini et al. 5
,
Mancera Piña et al. 57
). Since
j_{\rm{X},\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
is an upper limit on the gaseous angular momentum,
q_{\rm{X}}
is the maximal or outer stability of a pure gas disk.

III.2
Quantitative Scaling Relations

With qualitative trends in the
H
i
scaling relations for the WALLABY sample and existing scaling relations explored in Sec.
III.1
, we now proceed to a quantitative analysis. An important factor to consider are the outliers and the distribution of uncertainties. As seen in Figures
4
and
5
, many of these galaxies have large uncertainties. However, these uncertainties have little constraining power and do not strongly affect the fits. Of more concern are outlier galaxies with low uncertainties, as these will pull on the fits. Unfortunately, there are no clear cuts in galaxy properties like inclination or observed size that will provide a cleaner sample. Given this, we simply fit these gas-only scaling relations using the full sample of 148 galaxies.

A relatively straightforward approach to fitting
a scaling relation with differing uncertainties in both parameters
is to use orthogonal distance regression (ODR) where the distance
\delta_{i,p}
of a line to a data point normalized by the uncertainty is minimized.
Explicitly, the orthogonal distance of a data point to another point is

\delta_{i,p}^{2}=\left(\frac{X_{i}-X_{p}}{\epsilon_{X,i}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{Y_{i}-Y_{p}}{\epsilon_{Y,i}}\right)^{2}~,

(15)

where
i
denotes the data point,
p
is a point on a line, and
\epsilon
is the uncertainty in the
X
or
Y
coordinates respectively. Note that when
X
and
Y
are logarithmic, the uncertainties must be in that same logarithmic space.
For a given line, the total distance is the sum of
\delta_{i,p}
where
p
is explicitly the point of closest approach. In ODR, the line that minimizes the total distance represents the best fit.
In this work, we have adopted the ODR package implemented in
SciPy

[
92
]
.

A limitation of the
SciPy
ODR package is that it only allows for symmetric uncertainties
on a measured value (i.e. the upper and lower errorbars are equal)
.
Such a restriction is common in many tools, but it is important as, even when uncertainties are equal in linear space (which is not necessarily true for the WALLABY
H
i
disk structural properties), in the logarithmic space of scaling relations, such uncertainties are not equal.
For simplicity, we present here the results of the ODR analysis using only the upper errors as they are slightly smaller than the lower errors on average. However, the results are consistent regardless of which set of
uncertainties
are used for fitting.

In order to obtain the uncertainties on the best fit parameters a customized bootstrapping algorithm is adopted. In a bootstrapped approach to uncertainties, the aim is to subtract a model, scramble the residuals, add the model back, and refit the data. Given that we are using ODR for the fitting, the residuals to be scrambled are the ODR distances. Therefore, once the best fitting line is found we calculate the full set of residuals at their closest approach to the line. Then, for each data point,
(X_{i},Y_{i})
, a random residual pair is drawn from the full set. The bootstrapped point is then shifted to a new position
(X_{i}^{{}^{\prime}},Y_{i}^{{}^{\prime}})
such that its
orthogonal
distance to the best fit line matches the drawn residual. The uncertainties
in the scaling relations
are calculated by generating
500
different bootstrapped samples and re-fitting each sample. Then the uncertainty in either the slope or intercept of the best fitting line is set to

\delta\gamma^{2}=\textrm{std}(\gamma_{bs})^{2}+\left(\overline{\gamma_{bs}}-\gamma_{bf}\right)^{2}~,

(16)

where
\gamma
is either the slope or intercept of the fit,
\gamma_{bf}
is the best fitting value,
\textrm{std}(\gamma_{bs})
is the standard deviation of the bootstrapped distribution and
\overline{\gamma_{bs}}
is the mean of the bootstrapped sample.
In other words, the uncertainty in the fit is the sum of the standard deviation of the bootstrap fit distribution and the difference between the best fit value and the bootstrap mean in quadrature.

Figure
6
shows the bootstrapped ODR fits graphically, while Table
3
lists the best fit scaling parameters. In addition to the
slope and intercept
,
\alpha
and
\beta
,
we also calculated the standard deviation
\sigma
and the median absolute derivation (MAD) of the observed vertical scatter from the best fit line.
If the distribution is a Gaussian with no outliers, then the relation between the scatter and MAD is given by
\sigma=1.4826\rm{MAD}

[
81
]
. For ease in comparison with standard scatter measures, in both Figure
6
and Table
3
we record
\rm{MAD*}=1.4826\rm{MAD}
.
In Figure

V
Gas Fraction and Angular Momentum

Obreschkow et al. [70]
posit from theoretical arguments that angular momentum regulates the gas fractions of galaxies. In their models of exponential disks, the gas fraction is related to the global disk stability via

f_{\rm{atm}}=\rm{min}\left[1,2.5q^{1.12}\right]~.

(17)

This gas fraction is also related to the baryonic mass of the galaxy via

f_{\rm{atm}}\approx 0.5\left(\frac{M_{b}}{10^{9}M_{\odot}}\right)^{-0.37}~.

(18)

Figure
8
shows a comparison of the inclination matched stellar sample to these two relations using
q_{\rm{X}}
as a proxy for
q
. However,
q_{\rm{X}}
is essentially a maximal/outer stability parameter rather than the global stability parameter of
Obreschkow et al. [70]
.

The left-hand panel of Figure
8
shows that the WALLABY galaxies
have a slight offset towards lower values of
q_{\rm{X}}
for a given gas fraction than predicted by Eq.
17
.
Hardwick et al. [39]
also probed this relation using the eXtended GALEX Arecibo SDSS Survey
[
17
,
18
]
. They find a similar offset over the range of
0.1\leq f_{\rm{atm}}\leq 1.0
, but, at lower gas fractions, they find a different slope and a larger scatter than predicted by
Obreschkow et al. [70]
. In the range of WALLABY gas fractions, the majority of galaxies in both the WALLABY and
Hardwick et al. [39]
samples lie within the
\pm 40\%
region suggested by
Obreschkow et al. [70]
and have a similar slope as the predicted line
. This result is slightly different than the analysis of WALLABY pre-pilot observations in Eridanus by
Murugeshan et al. [65]
, who found an offset towards higher
q_{\rm{X}}
values. A key difference is that
Murugeshan et al. [65]
were able to extract a measure of
q
, as opposed to our use of
q_{\rm{X}}
. For future studies of the disk stability we will need to either reduce the sample further to those where
q
can be reliably calculated or return to the
Obreschkow et al. [70]
formalism and find a theoretical model of
q_{\rm{X}}
.

Mancera Piña et al. [58]
also examined gas fraction-baryonic mass relation in the context of a

Obreschkow et al. [70]
model needs to be expanded to fully capture the physics of angular momentum and gas fractions. With the full WALLABY survey, coupled with multiwavelength observations, it will be possible to confirm these predictions.

VI
Discussion and Conclusions

The kinematically modelled WALLABY PDR galaxies are currently the largest sample of untargetted, uniformly observed and analyzed
H
i
kinematic models available.
This makes them the ideal sample for studies of scaling relations that involve
H
i
. In this work we have designed a framework for calculating
H
i
disk structural properties that is applicable to the marginally and moderately resolved WALLABY galaxies, that is robust and scalable for the full WALLABY sample. Armed with robust structural properties, we have obtained the first measures of the size-mass, size-velocity, mass-velocity, angular momentum-mass, sTFR, bTFR, and gas fraction scaling relations for the WALLABY survey.

The key
H
i
disk structural properties we attempt to extract are the disk size, outer velocity, and angular momentum. We measure the disk size,
R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
, as the radius where
\Sigma(R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}})=1~\textrm{M}_{\odot}~\rm{pc}^{-2}
. We characterize the disk velocity as
V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}=V(R_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}})
rather than
v_{\rm{flat}}
in order to include galaxies with potentially rising or falling rotation curves. And, due to beam-smearing effects in the integration of the specific angular momentum, we characterize the angular momentum as
j_{\rm{X},\text{H\,\sc{i}}}=1/2D_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}V_{\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
. For those with available optical DESI 3-band images, we also extract the stellar mass, the baryonic mass, the atomic gas fraction, and the disk stability,
q_{\rm{X}}=j_{\rm{X},\text{H\,\sc{i}}}\sigma/(GM_{\rm{bary}})
.

While there are 236 available WALLABY kinematic models, many of these are marginally resolved and do not have robustly measured structural properties. However, the advantage of such a large sample is the ability to curate it and probe scaling relations using only the well measured galaxies. There are 148 galaxies with robustly measured sizes, velocities, and angular momenta.

The WALLABY galaxies match the
H
i
size-mass relation of
Wang et al. [94]
, with a very tight scatter about the relation. This scatter, which can be fully explained by observational uncertainties, may actually be smaller than the
Wang et al. [94]
sample, although it spans a much smaller range of masses. The WALLABY galaxies also match the
Meurer et al. [61]
size-velocity relation, albeit with much more scatter than the size-mass relation. However, the WALLABY sample size is much larger than the
Meurer et al. [61]
, allowing for a much more robust measure of this scaling relation. Moving to the other relations, we have obtained one of the first measures of the
H
i
mass-velocity relation. A clear relation is seen, albeit with a great deal of scatter. And, while there are other empirical relations probing the specific angular momentum-mass relation, our adoption of
j_{\rm{X},\text{H\,\sc{i}}}
prevents a quantitative comparison of those relations to our results.

Beyond just empirical relations, we also compared the WALLABY sample to SPARC galaxies
[
54
]
and LVHIS galaxies
[
47
]
. In general the three samples agree for all four of these
H
i
scaling relations.

For studying the sTFR and bTFR, as well as the disk stability and gas fraction, we use a slightly different sample that leverages the power of our optical measurements. There are 92 galaxies with robust
H
i
structural properties and stellar mass measurements. The further constraint that the optical and kinematic angles are consistent reduces the sample to 61 galaxies. And it is this inclination matched sample that we use to the constrain the sTFR and bTFR.

The WALLABY sTFR is consistent with the sTFR measured by
Arora et al. [2]
using 2368 MaNGA galaxies. It is also consistent with SPARC and MIGHTEE-HI measures of the sTFR.

The WALLABY bTFR shows less scatter than the sTFR, which is consistent with
Lelli et al. [55]
. This suggests that the bTFR is a more fundamental relation than the sTFR
(as previously stated in
McGaugh et al. [59]
) and
Lelli et al. [55]
.
There are some outliers remaining in our inclination matched sample that will be investigated in future work. Additionally, as WALLABY continues we expect to reach to lower baryonic masses and enabling deeper investigations of the TFRs.

Comparing the WALLABY galaxies gas fraction scaling relations to the theoretical models of
Obreschkow et al. [70]
show some interesting behaviour. As expected, the gas fraction increases with the disk stability parameter and decreases with the baryonic mass. The WALLABY galaxies are offset from the
Obreschkow et al. [70]
disk stability relation, but this is likely due to our use of
q_{\rm{X}}
rather than
q
. There is also a
significant
offset from the gas fraction-baryonic mass relation,
likely due to the untargetted nature of WALLABY preferentially detecting galaxies with high gas fractions.

Moving forwards, there are two key limitations of this study that will need to be addressed. Firstly, it will be necessary to extend the concept of orthogonal distances to include fully asymmetric uncertainties in order to calculate a more robust likelihood. Secondly, it will be necessary to switch to Bayesian techniques that can fit the intrinsic scatter as separate parameters from the observed scatter.

WALLABY is the premier survey for studying gaseous scaling relations in the local Universe. The untargetted nature of the survey means that the selection effects and biases are easily understood. And the wide area of the survey means that the number of detected and modelled galaxies will be far beyond any other contemporary survey. The pilot data releases comprise approximately 1% of the total survey area, and has already provided the largest sample of uniformly observed and analyzed kinematic models. This study is the first to study scaling relations using WALLABY kinematic models and provides the framework for future studies.
With the full set of
\sim 10^{4}
kinematic models expected for the WALLABY
[
47
,
96
]
, we will improve the constraints beyond even this study, probe the intrinsic scatter of the various relations, and be able to investigate differences in scaling relations due to differing environments.
