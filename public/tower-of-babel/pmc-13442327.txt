DNA Sensing Pathways in Innate Immunity: Implications for Alzheimer’s Disease Progression and Therapy
Authors: Chenmo Shi, Ye Liu, Yanli Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDNA sensors are emerging regulators of innate immune activation in Alzheimer’s disease (AD). In addition to detecting microbial DNA, these pattern-recognition receptors can respond to cytosolic or endosomal self-DNA generated by DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired DNA repair, cellular stress, or neuronal injury. Aberrant activation of DNA-sensing pathways may amplify neuroinflammation through type I interferon (IFN-I) signaling, inflammasome activation, pyroptosis, and microglial dysfunction, thereby contributing to amyloid pathology, tau-related inflammation, synaptic loss, and cognitive decline. In this review, we summarize current evidence linking major DNA sensors, including cyclic GMP-AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING), absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2), toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) to AD pathogenesis. We emphasize the strongest available evidence for cGAS-STING and AIM2, discuss the context-dependent roles of TLR9, and highlight less-established sensors requiring further validation. Finally, we evaluate therapeutic strategies targeting DNA-sensing pathways, including pathway inhibition and controlled immune activation, and discuss translational challenges such as blood–brain barrier penetration, off-target effects, dose dependency, and disease-stage-specific modulation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12035-026-06081-7.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, DNA sensors, Innate immunity, CGAS-STING, AIM2 inflammasome, Neuroinflammation, Therapeutic targeting

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a debilitating neurodegenerative disorder affecting approximately 44 million people worldwide [1–3]. Disease progression varies among individuals, and clinical symptoms may develop over decades. AD is the leading cause of dementia, which is characterized by progressive impairment of memory and cognitive function [4]. As dementia advances, patients gradually lose independence in daily activities and require continuous care. Consequently, AD imposes a heavy burden on individuals and society [5–7].

While several mechanisms, including impaired mitophagy and cellular senescence, have been implicated in age-related neurodegeneration [8–10], genomic instability caused by excessive DNA damage or defective DNA repair is increasingly recognized as an important contributor to neurodegenerative disorders [11, 12]. In AD, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuronal injury may promote the accumulation of nuclear or mitochondrial DNA fragments outside their normal compartments [13]. These DNA species can be recognized by DNA sensors, a subset of pattern-recognition receptors that detect microbial or self-DNA and activate innate immune signaling [14]. In the context of neurodegeneration, inappropriate sensing of self-DNA may amplify inflammatory responses rather than provide host protection.

This review focuses on the role of DNA sensors in AD progression and therapeutic modulation. We first briefly summarize the innate immune context of AD, particularly the bidirectional role of microglia in amyloid-β (Aβ) clearance and inflammatory injury. We then discuss major DNA-sensing pathways, including cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING), absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2), toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9), Z-DNA-binding protein 1 (ZBP1), interferon gamma-inducible protein 16 (IFI16), and DEAD-box helicase 41 (DDX41), and evaluate their links with type I interferon (IFN-I)-related pathology, tau-related inflammation, microglial activation, and synaptic dysfunction. Because microglial activation and neuroinflammation provide the cellular context in which many DNA-sensing pathways operate, the following section briefly summarizes innate immune responses in AD before focusing on individual DNA sensors.

Innate immunity and microglial responses in AD

Extracellular Aβ accumulation is a major pathological feature of AD and a potent trigger of innate immune activation [15, 16]. Microglia, the principal innate immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS), respond to fibrillar and oligomeric Aβ through multiple receptors, leading to phagocytosis, cytokine production, and phenotypic remodeling [10, 15, 17]. This response is bidirectional. On one hand, microglial activation can promote Aβ uptake and clearance, partly through receptors such as triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2), which recognizes Aβ oligomers and supports microglial migration, plaque-associated clustering, and phagocytic activity [18]. On the other hand, sustained microglial activation can increase the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, amplify Aβ-associated inflammation, impair synaptic integrity, and contribute to neuronal dysfunction [16, 17, 19].

Tau pathology is also closely linked to innate immune activation and should be considered alongside Aβ pathology in AD. Misfolded or extracellular tau can be taken up by microglia, and microglia isolated from human AD/tauopathy brains and tauopathy mouse models have been shown to contain bioactive tau seeds, suggesting that microglia may contribute not only to tau clearance but also to inefficient tau processing and pathological tau propagation [20]. In addition, aggregated tau can activate the NOD-, LRR- and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3)–ASC inflammasome after microglial uptake and lysosomal sorting, thereby promoting interleukin (IL)−1β-mediated inflammatory responses and exacerbating both seeded and non-seeded tau pathology in vivo [21]. Consistently, NLRP3 inflammasome activation has been shown to drive tau hyperphosphorylation and aggregation, whereas loss of NLRP3 function reduces tau pathology by modulating tau kinases and phosphatases [22]. More recent work has further connected tau pathology with DNA-sensing-related innate immune signaling, showing that tau can activate microglia through the polyglutamine-binding protein 1 (PQBP1)–cGAS–STING pathway and that tau-associated microglial cGAS–IFN-I signaling may reduce myocyte enhancer factor 2 C (MEF2C)-mediated cognitive resilience [23, 24]. Together, these findings indicate that neuroinflammation in AD is not only a response to Aβ deposition but also participates in tau-associated microglial activation, inflammasome signaling, tau propagation, synaptic dysfunction, and cognitive decline.

Innate immune signaling may also influence Aβ production. Hur et al. showed that interferon-induced transmembrane protein 3 (IFITM3), an innate immune protein induced by inflammatory cytokines, interacts with γ-secretase and enhances γ-secretase activity, thereby increasing Aβ production in neurons and astrocytes [25]. IFITM3 expression increases with aging and is elevated in late-onset AD and AD mouse models, suggesting a mechanistic link between age-related inflammation, innate immune activation, and Aβ generation [25] (Fig. 1). Therefore, innate immunity in AD should not be viewed solely as a response to existing amyloid plaques; it may also participate in feed-forward loops that promote Aβ production, tau-associated inflammatory signaling, glial activation, synaptic injury, and neurodegeneration [15, 17, 21–23, 25]. This dual role of innate immunity provides the conceptual basis for investigating DNA sensors in AD. DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuronal injury can generate self-DNA ligands that activate cytosolic or endosomal DNA sensors in microglia, astrocytes, neurons, and possibly infiltrating immune cells. Depending on disease stage and cellular context, these pathways may either support protective clearance responses or drive chronic IFN-I signaling, inflammasome activation, pyroptosis, and neurotoxic inflammation.

DNA sensors in AD

DNA sensors serve as a link among DNA injury, leakage, and neuronal degeneration in neurodegenerative disorders. Capable of recognizing and attaching to DNA within the cytoplasm, these entities have the potential to initiate direct cellular apoptosis and/or neuroinflammatory responses [13, 26, 27]. DNA damage occurs early in AD pathogenesis. The staining of γ-phosphorylated H2A histone family member X (γH2AX), a particular marker for double-strand breaks (DSBs), is significantly elevated in neurons and astrocytes inside postmortem brain tissues of individuals diagnosed with AD [28, 29]. Increased γH2AX staining has also been substantiated in the classical AD murine model five familial Alzheimer’s disease (5xFAD), as it can be observed as early as in mice at the age of 5 months, prior to the development of typical Aβ plaques and neurofibrillary tangles [30]. Damaged nuclear DNA may detach from chromosomes and exist as free DNA fragments. In the course of mitosis, the nuclear membrane breaks down, leading to a situation where DNA fragments are not regulated by the spindle apparatus, resulting in their spontaneous aggregation with remnants of the previous nuclear membrane to create a micronucleus (MN) [31]. MN is marked by instability and vulnerability, and its breakdown promotes the liberation of nuclear DNA into the cytoplasm, which is quickly recognized by cytosolic DNA receptors, including cGAS [32]. MN is recognized as a prospective early-stage biomarker for AD, as it is present in the CNS as well as in peripheral cells, including lymphocytes, fibroblasts, and oral mucosal epithelial cells in individuals with AD, suggesting that cytosolic DNA leakage is an early and common occurrence in the disease [33]. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) damage is increased in patients with AD and in AD mouse model. Mitochondria are crucial to aerobic respiration and possess incomplete DNA repair mechanisms, thus facing elevated risks of mtDNA damage and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, leading to a detrimental loop of oxidative stress and DNA damage [34]. Alongside heightened DNA damage, the mechanisms responsible for DNA repair are markedly compromised in AD. The expression levels of 8-oxoguanine DNA glycosylase-1 (OGG1), Breast cancer gene 1 (BRCA1), Poly [ADP-ribose] polymerase 1 (PARP1), and DNA-dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit (DNA-PKcs), which serve as indicators for base excision repair (BER), homologous repair (HR), and non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), respectively, have been noted to decline in the context of AD as a result of increasing oxidative stress [34, 35]. Another possible origin of cytosolic DNA is extracellular DNA that is discharged by deceased cells, as it has the capacity to serve as a scaffold for Aβ protein, leading to the formation of plaques that contain nucleic acids, which can subsequently be internalized by microglia [36].

cGAS

cGAS is the best-characterized cytosolic DNA receptor. It directly binds double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) and catalyzes the synthesis of the second messenger 2′3′-cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) [37]. cGAMP then binds STING, which activates tank-binding kinase 1 (TBK1), interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB). Activation of these signaling pathways induces IFN-β and other pro-inflammatory or antiviral cytokines [38, 39]. Since its identification in 2013, cGAS has been recognized as a key sensor of microbial DNA, retroviral DNA intermediates, and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including released mitochondrial DNA [40, 41]. Evidence from several CNS disorders suggests that cGAS and its downstream signaling pathway are functionally relevant in the brain.Among the DNA sensors, cGAS-STING currently has the strongest mechanistic and experimental connection with AD. This pathway provides a biologically plausible link between age-related DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, amyloid/tau pathology, and chronic IFN-I-driven neuroinflammation. In 5xFAD mice, cGAS expression increases after the accumulation of DNA damage, and genetic deletion of cGAS or pharmacological inhibition of STING attenuates cognitive impairment, Aβ pathology, microgliosis, astrogliosis, and inflammatory responses [42]. Consistently, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) supplementation in amyloid precursor protein/presenilin-1 (APP/PS1) mice reduces DNA damage, cellular senescence, and neuroinflammation through suppression of cGAS-STING activation [43]. These findings support the interpretation that cGAS-STING is not merely a downstream inflammatory marker, but may function as an active amplifier of AD pathology.

The relevance of cGAS-STING is further strengthened by its connection with tau pathology and IFN-I signaling. Tau can activate microglial innate immune responses through the PQBP1-cGAS-STING axis, providing a DNA-sensing-related mechanism by which tau pathology may promote neuroinflammation [23]. In addition, tau-associated activation of microglial cGAS-IFN signaling has been linked to reduced MEF2C-mediated cognitive resilience, suggesting that this pathway may influence the transition from neuropathology to cognitive decline [24]. IFN-I responses are also prominent in AD models, where IFN-stimulated gene signatures in plaque-associated microglia contribute to synapse loss and neuroinflammation [44]. Therefore, the cGAS-STING-IFN-I axis represents one of the most AD-relevant DNA-sensing pathways and should be prioritized over less-studied sensors when discussing disease mechanisms and therapeutic implications.

Nevertheless, the therapeutic interpretation of cGAS-STING modulation remains complex. STING inhibition may reduce chronic neuroinflammation in AD models, but STING activation has also been reported to enhance TREM2-associated microglial responses and promote Aβ clearance in some experimental settings. These apparently divergent findings suggest that the outcome of cGAS-STING modulation may depend on disease stage, cell type, ligand source, and the balance between protective amyloid clearance and detrimental chronic interferon signaling. Future studies should therefore distinguish acute, protective innate immune activation from sustained cGAS-STING-driven neuroinflammation. AIM2

Another IFN-inducible protein belonging to the Pyrin and HIN domain (PHYIN) family that has been discovered to function as a DNA sensor is AIM2 [45]. AIM2 detects dsDNA and activates the inflammasome, leading to pyroptosis, an inflammatory form of cell death [45]. Upon interaction with dsDNA, AIM2 forms a complex with the downstream signaling component apoptosis-associated speck-like protein that possesses a caspase recruitment domain (CARD) domain (ASC), which subsequently recruits and activates caspase-1 [46]. Caspase-1 subsequently functions as the effector protein that cleaves the precursor forms of IL-1β and IL-18, resulting in the maturation and release of these powerful inflammatory cytokines [47]. Caspase-1 can also cleave gasdermin D, triggering pyroptotic cell death, which is marked by pore development in the plasma membrane and the spilling of intracellular contents into the extracellular space [48]. The AIM2 inflammasome has been demonstrated to assemble in response to infections caused by either DNA or RNA viruses within peripheral myeloid and lymphoid immune cell types, including monocytes, bone marrow derived dendritic cells (BMDCs), fibroblasts, and bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDM) [49, 50]. While the AIM2 inflammasome is acknowledged for its antiviral role in peripheral cell types, its function in CNS infections remains poorly understood, even though it has been demonstrated to be expressed in both neurons and glial cells [51]. AIM2 represents the second major DNA sensor with relatively strong relevance to AD, but its biology differs from cGAS-STING. Whereas cGAS primarily promotes STING-TBK1-IRF3-dependent IFN-I signaling, AIM2 detects cytosolic dsDNA and assembles an inflammasome complex with ASC and caspase-1, leading to IL-1β/IL-18 maturation, gasdermin-D cleavage, and pyroptotic inflammatory cell death [52, 53]. This difference is important because AIM2 links DNA sensing more directly to inflammasome activation and pyroptosis, both of which are increasingly recognized as contributors to AD-related neuroinflammation.

Several experimental studies support a role for AIM2 in AD models, although the findings are not entirely uniform. Aim2 deletion in 5xFAD mice reduces Aβ deposition and microglial activation, but does not consistently improve memory or reduce IL-1β/IL-18 release, suggesting that AIM2 inhibition may be insufficient once substantial plaque burden and inflammatory remodeling are established [54]. In contrast, AIM2 deletion in APP/PS1-related experimental settings has been associated with improved spatial memory, enhanced hippocampal long-term potentiation, and changes in synaptic plasticity-related proteins [55]. More recent work further supports a microglia-specific role for AIM2, showing that conditional knockout of AIM2 in microglia ameliorates synaptic plasticity and spatial memory deficits in an AD mouse model, at least partly by reducing complement-dependent microglial synapse phagocytosis [56]. These findings suggest that AIM2 may contribute to AD not only through classical inflammasome-mediated cytokine release, but also through microglial synaptic pruning and circuit-level dysfunction.

AIM2-related pathways may also interact with mitochondrial quality control and cell-death mechanisms. optineurin (OPTN) downregulation in APP/PS1 mice has been linked to impaired mitophagy and activation of AIM2 inflammasome- and RIPK1-related inflammatory mechanisms [57]. In addition, systemic inflammasome activation and pyroptosis have been associated with progression from amnestic mild cognitive impairment to AD, supporting the clinical relevance of inflammasome-related inflammatory programs, although these peripheral findings do not prove AIM2-specific causality in the brain [58]. Overall, AIM2 should be presented as a high-priority AD-associated DNA sensor, but with a balanced interpretation: the available data support its involvement in amyloid pathology, microglial activation, synaptic dysfunction, and pyroptosis, while model-dependent discrepancies indicate that disease stage, mouse model, and cellular context strongly influence its functional consequences.

TLR9

TLR9 is the sole identified endosomal DNA sensor and was the first documented pattern recognition receptor to identify DNA. TLR9 is abundantly expressed in plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) and B cells, detecting unmethylated cytosine–phosphate–guanosine (CpG) motif-containing DNA from viral and bacterial genomes. AD is the predominant neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the deposition of amyloid plaques and the creation of tau tangles as its principal pathological features [59], leading to the production of IFN-α, IFN-λ, and several chemokines and cytokines [60]. The CpG motifs in mammals exhibit methylation at the cytosine residue [61], whereas bacterial and viral CpG sites remain unmethylated; hence, TLR9 can differentiate between self and non-self to avert undesired immune responses [62]. In unstimulated pDCs, TLR9 is localized with the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) in an inactive state. In the presence of CpG DNA, TLR9 is transported to lysosomes through the activity of the twelve-membrane-spanning ER protein UNC93 homolog B1 (UNC93B), which engages directly with TLR9 [63]. TLR9 is transformed into an active processed form in endolysosomal compartments by proteolytic cleavage in response to the presence of CpG DNA [64]. Clathrin-mediated endocytic routes facilitate the internalization of CpG DNA, which is subsequently relocated to lysosomes, where it engages with active TLR9. The mechanism by which TLR9 is activated to move from the ER to lysosomes containing CpG remains unclear. Upon the detection of CpG DNA, TLR9 associates with its adaptor protein myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88), encompassing both a death domain and a Toll/interleukin-1 receptor (TIR) domain [65]. MyD88 additionally engages with interleukin-1 receptor (IL-1R) associated kinase 1 (IRAK-1), IRAK-4, and interferon regulatory factor 7 (IRF-7), which subsequently facilitates the recruitment of TNF receptor-associated factor 3 (TRAF3) and TNF receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6), thereby activating mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), transforming growth factor β-activated kinase 1 (TAK1), and NF-κB, ultimately leading to the induction of inflammatory cytokines [65]. Regarding the role of TLR9 in AD pathogenesis, Cacace et al. demonstrated that the cytokines produced following TLR9 activation diminished inflammation and facilitated the phagocytosis of Aβ42 oligomers in human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC)-derived microglia. TLR9 signaling plays a protective role in the pathogenesis of AD, and the impairment of TLR9 function may disrupt the communication between peripheral and central immune systems that is essential for reducing inflammation and clearing toxic protein aggregates, consequently resulting in the accumulation of neuroinflammation and pathological protein aggregates in the progression of AD [66]. A Chinese cohort study linked the TLR9 rs187084 promoter polymorphism to the risk of late-onset Alzheimer’s disease (LOAD) and identified the G allele as a protective factor in Han Chinese participants [67]; however, whether this association extends to other ethnic populations remains uncertain [68]. AD is marked by the existence of cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), parenchymal Aβ plaques, and neurofibrillary tangles. Amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI) transitioning to AD is observed in a considerable proportion of aMCI patients; however, the factors that predict this transition remain inadequately understood. It is hypothesized that neuroinflammation and the reactivation of chronic viral infections may contribute to this process. Research indicated that at baseline, among those aMCI individuals who did not progress to AD: (1) The production of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL6 and IL1β by cluster of differentiation 14-positive (CD14+) cells in response to Aβ1–42 was significantly diminished. Additionally, there was an increase in CD14+/IL-33+ cells, a notable rise in the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of TLR8 and TLR9, as well as better preservation of hippocampal volumes that correlated with IL33+/CD14+ cells [69]. In contrast, AIM2 primarily links cytosolic DNA sensing to inflammasome activation, pyroptosis, microglial dysfunction, and synaptic plasticity impairment. Other DNA Sensors

DDX41, ZBP1, and IFI16 are interferon-inducible nucleic acid sensors central to innate antiviral immunity. DDX41 is a DEAD-box helicase that binds cytosolic double-stranded DNA and signals through STING to induce type I interferon and NF-κB responses in myeloid dendritic cells [70]. ZBP1 (also known as DAI) detects left-handed Z-form DNA/RNA via its Zα domains and, through RHIM-domain interactions with RIPK3, triggers both interferon induction and necroptotic cell death during viral infection [71]. IFI16, a member of the AIM2-like receptor/PYHIN family, is unique in sensing foreign dsDNA mainly in the nucleus, using tandem HIN domains to bind DNA and a PYD domain to nucleate inflammasome assembly with ASC, and has also been implicated in autoimmune diseases such as lupus [72]. Compared with cGAS-STING and AIM2, the AD-specific roles of DDX41, ZBP1, and IFI16 remain less clearly defined. These sensors are important for understanding the broader biology of cytosolic nucleic-acid sensing, STING-related signaling, and inflammatory cell death, but current AD evidence is either indirect, emerging, or derived from non-AD neuroinflammatory contexts. Therefore, in the present review, these sensors are retained as mechanistic background but are not discussed with the same level of detail as cGAS and AIM2. This evidence-weighted structure better reflects the current state of the field and avoids implying equivalent relevance across all DNA sensors.

Overall, current evidence indicates that cGAS–STING and AIM2 have the strongest mechanistic and experimental links to AD, although they contribute through distinct pathways. Also, TLR9 appears to exert context-dependent effects, with controlled activation potentially enhancing amyloid clearance.

Current Therapeutics to Target DNA Sensors in AD

Given the accumulating evidence that aberrant activation of cytosolic DNA sensors contributes to sustained neuroinflammation and neuronal damage in AD, these pathways have emerged as promising targets for therapeutic intervention. No cGAS-STING inhibitor is currently approved for AD. However, converging preclinical evidence indicates that pharmacological or genetic modulation of DNA-sensing pathways either by suppressing chronic inflammatory signaling or by enhancing protective innate immune clearance can influence Aβ pathology, tau pathology, glial activation, and cognitive outcomes in AD models. These strategies are described below as (4.1) inhibition-based approaches and (4.2) activation-based approaches, followed by a synthesis of their effects on tau pathology (4.3) and the translational challenges that remain (4.4).

Inhibition-Based Approaches

Inhibition-based strategies aim to reduce sustained IFN-I signaling, inflammasome activation, and microglia-driven neuroinflammation by blocking cGAS-STING or AIM2 activity.

Hou et al. reported an upregulation of cGAS and STING proteins in the brains of APP/PS1 mice relative to wild-type controls, and showed that the STING inhibitor H-151 effectively suppressed Aβ42-triggered IL-6 production in HMC3 human microglial cells. Because IFN-I levels are elevated in postmortem AD brains, STING activation may contribute to AD progression, making its inhibition a rational therapeutic target [73].

Fiala et al. discovered that in the brains of individuals with AD (analyzed postmortem), monocyte/macrophage cells uptake Aβ present in plaques and transport it into brain microvessels without any degradation. Subsequently, these cells undergo apoptosis and release Aβ, which contributes to vasculitis. The epoxyeicosatetraenoic acid (EEQ) metabolites, in conjunction with the inhibitors TPPU and H-151, reduce inflammatory cytokines and modulate the macrophage’s unfolded protein response in relation to ER stress. Administration of TPPU together with EEQ or the STING inhibitor H-151 to AD macrophages resulted in enhanced uptake of Aβ after a period of 2 h and promoted degradation of Aβ after 24 h [74].

In another study, Gao et al. [75] illustrated that a tetrahydroxy stilbene glycoside (TSG), which is a naturally occurring compound, inhibits the cGAS-STING pathway by diminishing the levels of cGAS, phosphorylated STING (p-STING), and phosphorylated TBK1 (p-TBK1). This treatment alleviated the inflammatory response in both in vitro and in vivo conditions, ultimately enhancing cognitive function in APP/PS1 mice.

AIM2 inhibition has also been investigated, though with more complex outcomes. AIM2 ablation reduces Aβ accumulation and microglial activation in 5XFAD mice but does not improve memory or anxiety-related phenotypes, and paradoxically increases inflammatory cytokine expression [76]. In contrast, in APP/PS1 mice, AIM2 ablation improves spatial memory, promotes hippocampal long-term potentiation (LTP), and alters dendritic morphology, with transcriptional changes in proteins such as Pten, Ppp2r3a, and Homer1 [77]. Additionally, ectopic OPTN expression blocks Aβ oligomer-induced AIM2 inflammasome activation in APP/PS1 mice [78].

Where available, directly measurable outcomes were incorporated to improve precision of comparison across inhibition-based studies. NAD⁺ supplementation reduced neuroinflammation, cellular senescence, and microglial/astrocytic activation in APP/PS1 mice, while H-151 reduced Aβ42-induced IL-6 production in HMC3 cells [43]. In 5xFAD mice, cGAS deficiency or H-151 treatment reduced Aβ pathology, microgliosis, astrogliosis, inflammatory signaling, and cognitive impairment [42]. TSG suppressed cGAS-STING activation markers (cGAS, p-STING, p-TBK1) and reduced IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IFN-α, and IFN-β in APP/PS1 mice and microglial cells [75]. In AD macrophages, EEQ + TPPU or H-151 increased Aβ uptake at 2 h and reduced residual Aβ at 24 h, indicating improved clearance capacity [79].

Activation-Based Approaches

In contrast to inhibition-based strategies, activation-based approaches aim to harness or enhance protective innate immune clearance mechanisms.

Recombinant cGAMP treatment improved neuroinflammation, reduced Aβ plaques and neuronal death, and recovered memory skills in a 5-month APP/PS1 mice model of AD. Inducing the expression of TREM2 through stimulation of the cGAS–STING pathway reduced Aβ and neuronal death. Additionally, TREM2 caused microglia to polarize from the M1 to the M2 phenotype, which reduced neuroinflammatory reactions [80].

TLR9 stimulation using CpG ODNs represents the most extensively studied activation-based strategy. Scholtzova et al. discovered that the activation of the innate immune system and the stimulation of microglia/macrophages through TLR9, utilizing CpG ODNs, results in cognitive enhancements and a decrease in CAA, without any linked toxicity [81]. Patel et al. extended these findings to a non-human primate model, showing that long-term class B CpG ODN 2006 administration in aged squirrel monkeys with extensive CAA induced beneficial innate immune activation without excessive or prolonged inflammation, mitigating both CAA and tau-related pathology alongside behavioral improvement and without microhemorrhages [82].

Notably, TLR9 activation appears context-dependent: Lotz et al. found that Aβ1–40 potentiates TLR2/4 agonist responses while antagonizing TLR9-mediated anti-inflammatory signaling in primary microglia, with co-administration of Aβ1–40 and LPS or Pam3Cys producing additive nitric oxide and TNF-α production a mechanism potentially contributing to AD clinical decline during infections [83].

CpG ODN has already gained regulatory approval as a vaccine adjuvant, and the AD vaccine UB-311 (Aβ peptide immunogens combined with CpG ODN) is currently in Phase II trials (NCT02551809). While CpG ODN is generally thought to induce a pro-inflammatory adjuvant response, Scholtzova et al. suggest that in AD models it may instead produce a transient, balanced, and ultimately beneficial response enhancing phagocytosis and anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 [81]. Dosage appears critical to this balance, positioning CpG ODN as a promising immunomodulatory adjunct that shifts microglia/macrophages toward a neuroprotective phenotype and facilitates parenchymal and vascular amyloid clearance.

Effects on Tau-Related Pathology

Although most studies above focus on amyloid and glial endpoints, a subset provides direct evidence relevant to tau pathology, spanning both strategy classes.

In 3xTg-AD mice, peripheral CpG ODN-mediated TLR9 stimulation reduced both Aβ and tau pathology alongside cognitive rescue; pathological tau markers (MC1, PHF1, AT8) were reduced without increasing total tau [84]. In aged squirrel monkeys, long-term CpG ODN 2006 similarly reduced CAA and tau-related pathology with behavioral improvement [82].

The clearest tau-related therapeutic evidence comes from TLR9 stimulation and cGAS-STING inhibition. In 3xTg-AD mice, peripheral CpG ODN-mediated TLR9 stimulation reduced both Aβ and tau-related pathologies and was associated with cognitive rescue; immunohistochemical analyses showed reduced pathological tau markers, including MC1, PHF1, and AT8, whereas total tau levels were not increased [84].

In App^NL-G-F/hTau knock-in mice, STING inhibition with H-151 reduced Aβ burden, tau phosphorylation (pT181, AT8, pS356, pS396, pS422), microglial synapse engulfment, gliosis, and memory impairment, without altering total tau [85]. In tauopathy models, cGAS inhibition improved synaptic integrity and memory via restoration of the MEF2C transcriptional network, though not necessarily via reduced tau load [24]. In P301S tauopathy mice, STING deficiency or H-151 alleviated neuroinflammation and tau pathology driven by microbial/inflammatory triggers [86].

Direct evidence for AIM2-targeted effects on tau remains limited. Overall, TLR9 stimulation and STING/cGAS inhibition currently have the strongest tau-related evidence, while AIM2, ZBP1, IFI16, and DDX41 require further validation (Table 3).

Synthesis and Translational Challenges

Overall, therapeutic targeting of DNA sensors in AD reflects two conceptually distinct but not mutually exclusive strategies: suppressing chronic inflammatory signaling and enhancing protective immune clearance. Their relative benefit likely depends on disease stage, cell type, inflammatory context, ligand source, and treatment duration rather than any uniform “protective” or “detrimental” classification of a given pathway.

Clinical translation nonetheless faces substantial hurdles: blood–brain barrier permeability, CNS drug delivery, pathway specificity, and off-target effects remain major limitations, particularly for small-molecule STING inhibitors and oligonucleotide-based immunomodulators. Excessive inhibition of cGAS-STING or AIM2 may compromise antiviral defense and tissue surveillance, while excessive activation of STING or TLR9 may aggravate neuroinflammation. Dose dependency is especially critical for CpG ODNs, which can shift from beneficial, balanced immune activation to pro-inflammatory effects if stimulation is excessive or sustained. Future work should prioritize brain-penetrant, cell-type-selective, disease-stage-specific modulators, paired with biomarkers capable of distinguishing protective immune activation from chronic neurotoxic inflammation.

Conclusion

DNA-sensing pathways are emerging as important links between genomic instability, mitochondrial dysfunction, innate immune activation, and AD progression. Among the DNA sensors discussed in this review, cGAS-STING and AIM2 currently have the strongest AD-related evidence. cGAS-STING mainly connects cytosolic DNA accumulation and tau-associated innate immune activation to IFN-I signaling, microglial activation, synapse loss, and cognitive decline. AIM2 primarily links cytosolic DNA sensing to inflammasome activation, pyroptosis, microglial dysfunction, and synaptic impairment. TLR9 appears to have a more context-dependent role, with evidence suggesting that controlled activation may enhance amyloid clearance, whereas ZBP1, IFI16, and DDX41 remain less established in AD-specific settings.

Importantly, DNA sensors may exert both protective and harmful effects in AD. Transient or controlled activation may support immune surveillance, microglial phagocytosis, and Aβ clearance, while chronic or excessive activation may amplify IFN-I responses, inflammasome signaling, neuroinflammation, and neuronal injury. This duality is central to the therapeutic interpretation of DNA-sensing pathways.

Therapeutically, the most relevant strategies include inhibition of chronic cGAS-STING and AIM2 inflammasome signaling, controlled modulation of TLR9-mediated innate immune activation, and context-specific regulation of STING-TREM2-related microglial responses. Future studies should define disease-stage-specific windows for inhibiting chronic cGAS-STING or AIM2 signaling, evaluate controlled TLR9 or STING-TREM2 modulation, identify the pathogenic DNA ligands that drive these pathways, and develop brain-penetrant, cell-type-selective agents guided by pathway-specific biomarkers and validated in human-relevant models.
