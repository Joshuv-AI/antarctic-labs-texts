The Neurocognitive Impact of CAR T Cell Therapy in Hematological Cancers: A Systematic Review of Cognitive Function, Quality of Life, and Psychological Outcomes
Authors: Ashvath Arumugam Pillai, Megha Tiwari, Sanjana B Patil, Dhruv Gandhi, Aparna Nanda, Sukriti Chugh, Sumit Chauhan, Shivangi Jha, Gaurish Angawalkar, Siddharth Prabhakar Shinde, Sanjit Sah, Amogh Verma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‐cell therapy has revolutionized the treatment of hematological malignancies, offering durable remissions. However, concerns regarding its neurocognitive and psychological impact have emerged, particularly in patients experiencing immune effector cell‐associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS). Despite growing clinical use, the long‐term effects on cognitive function, psychological well‐being, and quality of life (QoL) remain unclear.ObjectiveThis systematic review evaluates the neurocognitive, psychological, and QoL outcomes associated with CAR T‐cell therapy.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and Scopus was conducted following PRISMA guidelines. Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, and observational studies assessing cognitive function, psychological outcomes (anxiety, depression, PTSD), and QoL post‐CAR T‐cell therapy. Study quality was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for RCTs and the Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale for observational studies.ResultsEighteen studies met the inclusion criteria. Up to 44% of CAR T‐cell recipients experienced cognitive impairments, particularly in memory, attention, and executive function. ICANS was reported in 25%–70% of patients, with severe ICANS in 10%–20%. Persistent cognitive deficits were observed beyond 12 months in a subset of patients, particularly those with severe ICANS. Psychological outcomes included anxiety and depression in 35% of patients, with PTSD reported in those experiencing severe neurotoxicity. While QoL improved over time, patients with prolonged neurotoxicity had delayed recovery. EEG‐based biomarkers, including the EICANS Score, showed promise in predicting ICANS severity.ConclusionsCAR T‐cell therapy is associated with neurocognitive and psychological sequelae, particularly in patients with severe ICANS. Long‐term cognitive monitoring, standardized assessments, and targeted rehabilitation strategies are needed to optimize patient outcomes.Keywords: CAR T‐cell therapy, cognitive impairment, hematological malignancies, ICANS, neurotoxicity, psychological outcomes, quality of life

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‐cell therapy has fundamentally changed how we approach blood cancer treatment. CAR T‐cell therapy represents a revolutionary advance in oncology, achieving durable remissions that were previously unattainable for many patients [1, 2]. The technology works by taking a patient's immune cells and engineering them with a CAR. This receptor combines several components: intracellular signaling domains, costimulatory molecules like CD28 and CD137, and an extracellular targeting system. Together, these allow the modified cells to hunt down cancer without requiring the usual immune recognition pathways [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Yet this breakthrough comes with serious complications. Two major side effects concern clinicians most: cytokine release syndrome and immune effector cell‐associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) [8, 9, 10, 11]. CRS triggers body‐wide inflammation. ICANS, however, creates a more complex clinical picture. It affects brain function in ways that can be devastating ‐ from mild attention problems to severe neurological damage involving speech, memory, decision‐making, and spatial awareness [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18].

Brain Function Under Attack

The cognitive problems we see after CAR T‐cell therapy involve complicated immune‐brain interactions. When these engineered cells activate, they release massive amounts of inflammatory molecules. IL‐6, IL‐1β, TNF‐α, and IFN‐γ flood the system, triggering damaging brain inflammation. This process breaks down the blood‐brain barrier ‐ normally our brain's protective shield and activates microglia, the brain's own immune cells. Different brain regions suffer distinct damage. The frontal cortex, which controls attention and executive function, becomes impaired. Memory and language processing in the temporal lobe deteriorate. Spatial processing abilities in the parietal regions also decline. Blood vessel dysfunction makes everything worse by reducing brain blood flow and allowing immune cells to invade neural tissue. Current data suggests roughly one in four patients (25%–35%) develops noticeable cognitive problems. Some recover within weeks. Others struggle with persistent deficits lasting well over a year. Memory, attention, language, and executive function take the biggest hits. These aren't just “test results”—they disrupt real life. Patients lose their ability to work effectively, manage daily tasks, or maintain relationships. Families often need extensive support systems to cope with these changes.

Assessment Problems We Face

Despite growing recognition of these cognitive complications, we're still struggling with major gaps in patient care. Different hospitals use completely different testing approaches. Some rely on quick screening tools like the MoCA. Others conduct extensive testing batteries including FACT‐Cog and Hopkins Verbal Learning assessments. This inconsistency makes comparing research findings nearly impossible and hampers guideline development. The relationship between early severe brain toxicity and long‐term cognitive outcomes remains puzzling. Some studies suggest severe early symptoms predict lasting problems. Other research contradicts this entirely. We also lack clear protocols for monitoring timing and frequency. When should patients see specialists? What constitutes appropriate intervention? These questions remain unanswered. Patient variability adds another layer of complexity. Children might recover differently than elderly patients. Someone's pretreatment cognitive status seems to matter. Different CAR T‐cell products may cause varying problems. A further challenge lies in the mismatch between patient‐reported difficulties and formal neurocognitive test results.

Why This Study Matters

CAR T‐cell therapy continues expanding to new patient groups across different ages and conditions. Healthcare providers desperately need reliable information about cognitive risks. How likely are these problems? Which patients face highest risk? How should we monitor effectively?

Doctors need this information for honest patient conversations and appropriate care planning. The situation becomes more complex because cognitive problems rarely occur alone. Many patients also develop anxiety, depression, and stress reactions from their cancer journey. These psychological challenges interact with cognitive difficulties in ways that affect recovery and quality of life. Unfortunately, we understand very little about these mental health complications ‐ their frequency, predictors, or specific relationships to cognitive problems.

Our Research Goals

This systematic review synthesizes all available evidence on cognitive function, psychological outcomes, and quality of life following CAR T‐cell therapy for blood cancers. We're addressing several critical questions: How common are cognitive problems across different CAR T‐cell treatments? What severity patterns emerge? Can we identify recovery trajectories over time? Which factors predict better outcomes? How do psychological complications like anxiety and depression affect patients? Does severe early brain toxicity reliably predict long‐term cognitive difficulties?

Our analysis aims to provide healthcare teams with evidence‐based information for accurate patient counseling, effective monitoring strategies, and comprehensive support systems. As CAR T‐cell use expands rapidly, a robust synthesis of neurocognitive and psychological outcomes is essential to optimize patient care, guide survivorship planning, and inform future research.

Results

Overview of Study Selection

A total of 18 studies were included in this systematic review, all focusing on the neurocognitive, psychological, and quality‐of‐life (QoL) outcomes following CAR T‐cell therapy in patients with hematological malignancies. Studies were selected based on predefined inclusion criteria, and a PRISMA flow diagram (not included in this section) was used to document the screening and selection process.

Characteristics of Included Studies

The 18 included studies comprised diverse designs across hematological malignancies, with sample sizes ranging from 40 to 419 patients (total N = 2847). Studies included 3 Phase 3 RCTs, 6 prospective studies, and 9 observational studies. Patient populations spanned multiple myeloma (5 studies), DLBCL/LBCL (8 studies), and mixed hematological malignancies (5 studies). CAR‐T products were predominantly axi‐cel (8 studies) and ide‐cel (4 studies), with age distributions favoring older adults (median ages 60–68 years in most studies). Further detailed in Table 3.

Synthesis of Results

Neurocognitive Outcomes

Across the 18 included studies, neurocognitive changes after CAR T‐cell therapy were variably reported but clinically relevant in a subset of patients. In longitudinal cohorts, early cognition was often stable to 90 days, followed by small but persistent declines in domains such as memory, language, organization, and divided attention up to day 360, particularly among those who developed ICANS ≥ grade 2 [19]. In trial and cohort settings, unresolved cognitive/neurological events (e.g., confusion, tremor, mental status changes) were still observed at 30–90 days in older LBCL populations [20]. Patient‐reported cognitive difficulties among longer‐term survivors were also noted (≈38% in one cohort) and correlated with worse global mental health and symptom burden [21]. ICANS incidence varied widely across products and settings (e.g., ~24% grade 2–4 in one axi‐cel cohort; 56% in a single‐center axi‐cel series; 65% neurological events with 27% grade ≥ 3 in an older LBCL RCT subgroup), while severe ICANS ranged from ~10% to ≥ 27% where reported [20, 22, 23]. In contrast, myeloma cohorts frequently showed low investigator‐identified neurotoxicity with ide‐cel (≈3%–10%) [24, 25], and cilta‐cel trials reported mostly grade 1–2 ICANS at low frequency [26].

Psychological Outcomes (Anxiety, Depression, PTSD)

Psychological morbidity was common. In prospective/observational cohorts, anxiety and depression were present in roughly 30%–35% of patients, and PTSD symptoms were reported in about 21% in one study [27]. Baseline psychological distress and coping profiles were consistently associated with subsequent outcomes: higher baseline fatigue, anxiety, or depression predicted worse perceived cognition at 90 days [19], and better emotional coping was linked to higher QoL and lower distress (depression, anxiety, PTSD) [27]. In acute PRO tracking after axi‐cel, concentration/memory problems peaked around day 14, with variable trajectories for mood symptoms by day 90 [23].

Quality of Life (QoL) and Patient‐Reported Outcomes

A biphasic QoL pattern emerged across multiple studies: deterioration in the first 2–3 weeks (fatigue, function, mood), followed by clinically meaningful improvement by ~day 90–100 or by month 2–3, with maintenance thereafter in many cohorts [23, 24, 25, 28]. In randomized settings, axi‐cel recipients recovered QoL more rapidly than standard‐of‐care comparators and maintained gains over follow‐up [20, 28]. In relapsed/refractory myeloma, ide‐cel cohorts showed significant improvements in global health status/HRQoL and physical well‐being by 2–3 months, sustained to 9–18 months in longitudinal analyzes [24, 25]. Notably, patients experiencing moderate‐to‐severe ICANS exhibited worse cognitive trajectories up to 1 year, consistent with delayed functional recovery relative to those without ICANS [19, 20]. Importantly, while most patients achieved QoL improvement by Day 90, those who experienced grade ≥ 2 ICANS showed persistent impairments in physical and emotional functioning, with some deficits extending to 12 months post‐treatment [19, 20, 29]. These findings indicate that ICANS severity is a major determinant of long‐term QoL outcomes, in contrast to patients without ICANS who typically returned to baseline by 3 months.

ICANS and EEG/Biomarker Findings

EEG abnormalities (e.g., background slowing, focal changes) tracked with ICANS severity in clinical series [30]. A quantitative EEG‐based score (EICANS) demonstrated strong correlations with ICANS severity and with neurotoxicity duration (Spearman's R² ≈ 0.69 and R² ≈ 0.31, respectively) in small cohorts [31]. Given the limited sample sizes and number of studies, these EEG markers should be interpreted as preliminary and require external validation before routine clinical adoption [30, 31].

Publication Bias

We considered assessing publication bias using funnel plots and Egger's regression test. However, because fewer than eight studies reported ICANS outcomes using consistent grading (grade ≥ 3) or cognitive impairment with standardized assessment tools, the available data set was insufficient for robust quantitative testing. Formal application of Egger's regression to such small, heterogeneous datasets is underpowered and risks spurious results. Instead, we conducted a qualitative assessment of potential bias. Several included studies were industry‐funded pivotal trials, which may increase the likelihood of selective outcome reporting, while smaller single‐center cohorts tended to report higher ICANS rates. Taken together, these factors suggest that ICANS incidence in the published literature may represent an upper bound of the true population risk. In contrast, cognitive impairment findings were more evenly distributed across study sizes, reducing the likelihood of strong publication bias.

Discussion

Key Findings and Their Significance

This systematic review highlights the neurocognitive, psychological, and quality‐of‐life (QoL) outcomes associated with CAR T‐cell therapy in hematological malignancies. Three consistent patterns emerged across the 18 included studies. First, ICANS remains the most significant neurotoxic complication, occurring in 25%–70% of patients, with severe forms affecting 10%–20% [22, 29, 32]. The most impacted domains were working memory, attention, and executive function, with up to 44% of patients experiencing deterioration posttreatment [29]. While some patients recovered within months, others showed persistent dysfunction beyond 1 year [32]. Importantly, the EICANS Score showed promising correlations with ICANS severity (R² = 0.69, p < 0.001) in preliminary studies, though larger validation cohorts are needed to establish its clinical utility [22]. Second, QoL trajectories were biphasic. Early declines in fatigue, anxiety, and depression (Day 14) generally improved by Day 90, and patients treated with axi‐cel showed significant improvements by Day 100 (+18.1 points, p < 0.0001) [28, 30]. However, patients with severe ICANS showed delayed or incomplete recovery, with prolonged deficits in emotional and physical functioning. Up to 35% of patients developed persistent anxiety or depression, and 21% of those with severe ICANS reported PTSD symptoms [27, 32]. Third, age was an important modifier. Older patients (> 65 years) exhibited prolonged neurocognitive impairments, while pediatric patients—despite having a 25%–44% ICANS incidence—demonstrated more robust cognitive recovery [20, 29]. These findings suggest that baseline cognitive reserve, neuroplasticity, and inflammatory burden influence recovery, emphasizing the need for age‐tailored rehabilitation strategies.

Comparison With Existing Literature

Our findings are consistent with prior studies (e.g., Santomasso et al., Neelapu et al.) that identified ICANS as a major adverse effect of CAR T‐cell therapy, with heterogeneous neurocognitive impacts. However, differences in recovery patterns remain. While studies such as Delforge et al. reported full cognitive recovery at 12 months, Kuznetsova et al. documented ongoing executive dysfunction, suggesting that patient characteristics, treatment variables, and inflammatory pathways may account for outcome heterogeneity. The relationship between CRS and ICANS also remains debated. While early reports suggested a direct correlation, our synthesis aligns with emerging evidence that ICANS arises from distinct neuroimmune mechanisms [31], likely involving endothelial activation and blood–brain barrier disruption. Similarly, while prior pivotal trials such as ZUMA‐7 emphasized early physical recovery, our review demonstrates that psychological recovery lags behind physical function in patients with severe ICANS. This highlights a gap in survivorship care, where neurocognitive and psychosocial sequelae remain under‐recognized.

Clinical Practice Implications

Our synthesis indicates that survivorship after CAR T therapy must extend beyond disease control to include cognitive and psychosocial monitoring.

Early Identification and Risk Stratification

EEG‐based screening using the EICANS Score should be considered during the first week post‐infusion, when neurotoxicity risk is highest. Patients with pre‐existing cognitive impairment, psychiatric comorbidities, or severe CRS may benefit from intensified surveillance.

Targeted Support

Patients who experience Grade ≥ 3 ICANS should undergo routine neuropsychological evaluation at 1, 3, and 12 months. Evidence‐based interventions such as CBT, mindfulness‐based therapy, and pharmacologic management of anxiety or depression should be integrated into care pathways.

Personalized Rehabilitation

Older patients with persistent deficits may require a multidisciplinary rehabilitation approach, incorporating cognitive training, aerobic and resistance exercise, and dietary strategies targeting neuroinflammation.

Research Priorities

Several urgent gaps demand immediate attention.

Assessment Standardization

The current testing heterogeneity makes meaningful comparisons nearly impossible. We desperately need consensus on core assessment batteries. Memory evaluation should include the Hopkins Verbal Learning Test and Wechsler Memory Scale. Executive function assessment requires the Trail Making Test Part B, Stroop Task, and Dimensional Change Card Sort. Processing speed and attention demand evaluation through the Digit Symbol Substitution Test, Continuous Performance Task (CPT‐3), and Flanker Inhibitory Control and Attention Test. Language abilities need assessment via the Boston Naming Test, Controlled Oral Word Association Test, and semantic fluency measures.

Objective Biomarkers

We can't continue relying solely on subjective assessments. EEG‐based monitoring using the EICANS Score shows real promise but needs validation in much larger populations. Neuroimaging approaches—MRI, fMRI, PET—should complement blood‐based markers like Neurofilament light chain and Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein as neuroinflammation indicators.

Implementation Timeline

Every single patient deserves pretreatment baseline testing. No exceptions. Short‐term follow‐ups at 1, 3, and 6 months post‐CAR‐T are absolutely essential for capturing early trajectory changes. Long‐term assessments extending 12 months and beyond become critical for understanding actual recovery patterns.

Mechanistic Understanding

ICANS pathophysiology remains frustratingly incomplete. Future investigations must integrate cytokine profiling (IL‐6, IL‐1β, TNF‐α, IFN‐γ), microglial activation studies, endothelial dysfunction markers, and blood‐brain barrier assessments. Advanced neuroimaging using DTI and PET can reveal structural and functional changes that correlate with neurotoxicity severity. Vascular permeability markers—VEGF, sICAM‐1, Ang‐2—might serve as early warning signals for impending brain injury. We also need rigorous testing of whether targeted TNF‐α or IL‐1β inhibition can reduce ICANS without compromising antitumor efficacy. Diffusion Tensor Imaging studies should focus specifically on white matter changes and microstructural alterations that correspond with ICANS severity. The EICANS Score requires validation in substantially larger cohorts, though early results appear genuinely encouraging.

Neuroprotective Trials

Given how disturbingly common lasting dysfunction proves to be, randomized trials testing anti‐inflammatory agents (anakinra, tocilizumab), neuroprotective compounds (N‐acetylcysteine, omega‐3 fatty acids), and structured rehabilitation programs have become essential. These interventions should thoughtfully combine exercise, cognitive‐behavioral therapy, mindfulness training, and targeted dietary modifications.

Economic Impact

We must rigorously assess the cost‐effectiveness of routine monitoring and long‐term rehabilitation. Studies should quantify the true economic burden of cognitive impairment on healthcare systems, employment capacity, and caregiver support networks. This information becomes absolutely crucial for informing insurance coverage policies for survivorship care.

Expanding Research Into Understudied Populations

Critical knowledge gaps persist in several populations that deserve focused research attention. Pediatric patients need specialized assessment of long‐term neurodevelopmental outcomes and educational interventions. Their developing brains may respond quite differently to immune‐mediated injury—potentially offering valuable insights into protective mechanisms we could harness for older patients. Geriatric population (over 65 years) often enter therapy with already‐reduced cognitive reserve and may require entirely customized rehabilitation strategies. Our age‐related findings suggest this population faces unique challenges that standard approaches don't address adequately. Research must also include diverse ethnic and socioeconomic groups. Current evidence comes primarily from relatively homogeneous populations, potentially missing important disparities in post‐treatment cognitive care access. Equitable implementation of neurocognitive monitoring protocols absolutely depends on understanding these differences. This comprehensive approach to understanding CAR T‐cell therapy's cognitive consequences will ultimately serve patients far better by providing clinicians with the evidence‐based tools they actually need for optimal care planning and honest patient counseling. Conclusion

CAR T‐cell therapy represents a major therapeutic advance, but its benefits must be weighed against significant neurocognitive and psychosocial risks. Up to 44% of patients experience cognitive impairments in memory, attention, and executive function, with 35% developing anxiety or depression and 21% of severe ICANS patients reporting PTSD symptoms. While most patients experience QoL improvements by Day 100, a subset face persistent deficits beyond 12 months. EEG‐based tools such as the EICANS Score show promise for early detection but require validation. These findings have immediate therapeutic applications for clinical practice. Routine neurocognitive monitoring using standardized assessment tools should be integrated into CAR‐T survivorship protocols, with age‐tailored rehabilitation strategies particularly for older patients. Early identification systems can guide timely interventions including cognitive‐behavioral therapy, targeted pharmacologic management, and multidisciplinary support to optimize long‐term functional recovery. Future research should prioritize standardized assessments, mechanistic studies, and neuroprotective strategies to enhance the safety profile of this transformative therapy.
