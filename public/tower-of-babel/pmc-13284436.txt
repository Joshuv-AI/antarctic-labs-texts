Wide dural radiation therapy for NF2-SWN associated meningiomatosis
Authors: Jaiwo Lee, Chan Woo Wee, Seung Yeon Do, Seo Hee Choi, Jiyoung Oh, Jong Hee Chang, Hong In Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Wide dural radiation therapy (WDRT) provides a novel management strategy for neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2)-related meningiomatosis.•WDRT achieved 100% 2-year local control for 91 irradiated meningiomas larger than 5 mm.•The treatment was well-tolerated, with only manageable acute grade 2 toxicities such as nausea and headache.•Outfield progression occurred in 30% of patients, primarily in areas intentionally omitted from the target volume.•Findings support WDRT as an effective local treatment, warranting prospective validation of target volume definitions.Keywords: Neurofibromatosis type 2-related schwannomatosis, Meningiomatosis, Wide dural radiation therapy, Local control, RT-related toxicity

Introduction

Neurofibromatosis type 2-related schwannomatosis (NF2-SWN) is a rare autosomal dominant disorder (estimated incidence of 1 in 25,000-33,000 births) caused by mutations in the NF2 gene encoding the tumor suppressor protein merlin [1], [2]. Hallmark clinical features of NF2-SWN include neurologic lesions such as bilateral vestibular schwannomas (VS), multiple cranial and spinal schwannomas, meningiomas, and ependymomas [3], [4]. Intracranial meningiomas are one of the most common manifestations in NF2-SWN patients, with lifetime risk rising as high as 80% by 70 years of age [5]. Multiple meningiomas or meningiomatosis are often present [6], and even though most are benign, the cumulative mass effect of these lesions is a leading cause of morbidity in NF2-SWN patients. Progression of meningiomas may cause symptoms such as neurologic deficits and impact patients’ quality of life, consequently necessitating treatment [7].

The lack of sufficient understanding of the disease itself and the chronic nature of the disease hinders the development of effective treatment for NF2-SWN associated tumors. Currently treatment of meningiomas relies on a multidisciplinary approach including surgery, radiation therapy, and targeted therapy [8]. Targeted agents, such as dual mTORC1/2 inhibitors, proteasomal pathway inhibitors, and ALK inhibitors, have shown heterogeneous efficacy and significant toxicity, and most of the evidence is from early phase or preclinical studies [9], [10]. As for radiation therapy, most of the studies are focused exclusively on radiosurgery, which has been proven to be effective against visible lesions present in limited numbers [11], [12]. However, against NF2-SWN associated meningiomatosis with extensive dural involvement, GKRS and SRS have their limitations. Wide dural radiation therapy (WDRT) represents a treatment strategy that involves treating larger volumes of at-risk dura mater with the rationale of targeting not only visible meningiomas but also areas harboring microscopic lesions or regions with high propensity for tumor development. We hereby present our institute’s clinical experience with WDRT as a treatment for NF2-SWN patients presenting with meningiomatosis.

Materials and Methods

Patient characteristics

Patients diagnosed with NF2-SWN associated meningiomatosis and treated with WDRT at Yonsei Cancer Center between March 2018 and December 2022 were reviewed. Data from clinical records and imaging studies from a total of 10 patients were retrospectively collected and reviewed. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Yonsei University Health System, Severance Hospital (approval no. 4-2025-1508) and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

The patients were diagnosed with NF2-SWN according to the Manchester criteria: presence of bilateral VS and/or a combination of family history and other NF2 associated tumors [5]. The diagnosis remained unchanged with the application of 2022 international consensus diagnostic criteria [13]. Histological diagnosis of NF2-SWN associated tumors was made in all 10 patients and genetic confirmation was made in 3 of the patients. All patients were diagnosed with multiple meningiomas by contrast-enhanced T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and underwent serial MRIs annually for further evaluation. The tumor sizes were assessed by measuring the greatest diameter in the axial images.

The baseline patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median patient age was 41 years (range, 25-53 years). Eight patients were female and 2 were male. The Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) before treatment were 0 for 5 patients and 1 for the other 5 patients. Reasons for treatment were either aggravation of symptoms (4 patients, 40%) or tumor growth (4 patients, 40%) or both (2 patients, 20%). Symptoms included both objective neurological deficits (seizure, gait disturbance, and hearing loss) and subjective complaints (headache and dizziness). Given the subjective and multifactorial nature of headache in this patient population, treatment decisions based on symptoms were made only after excluding other potential etiologies and following multidisciplinary consensus. For patients treated due to tumor growth, progression was defined on serial MRI as an increase in the greatest tumor diameter exceeding 20% on at least one follow-up scan prior to treatment initiation.

Treatment characteristics

All 10 patients received either wide dural or whole dural radiation therapy including the supratentorial and/or infratentorial dura mater, depending on the location and distribution of meningiomatosis on brain MRI (Fig.1). Of the 10 patients, 7 received surgical resection prior to WDRT, primarily for debulking of symptomatic lesions or relief of mass effect, after which WDRT was administered as adjuvant therapy to address residual disease and the broader dural field at risk. The remaining 3 patients received WDRT as primary treatment without prior surgical intervention due to the diffuse multifocal nature of their meningiomatosis rendering surgical resection unfeasible. The median interval between surgery and initiation of radiotherapy was 2.9 months (range, 1.6-7.1 months). The clinical target volume 1 (CTV1) was defined as the tumor bed and gross meningiomas with sizes greater than or equal to 5 mm and measurable with an addition of a 0-5 mm margin. CTV2 was defined as wide dural field with its extent depending on the original distribution and pathology of the tumor. The range of this dural field was determined in a multi-disciplinary approach after discussion between radiation oncologists, neurosurgeons, and radiologists. If the meningiomas were concentrated on a single hemisphere, the contralateral dura was excluded from the treatment field. If the meningiomas only existed in the supratentorial dura, the infratentorial dura was not treated. When the whole dura was treated, meningiomas that existed in close proximity to the VS were not treated as they were intended to be treated with GKS at a later time. The planning target volume 1 (PTV1) and PTV2 were defined as CTV1 and CTV2 expanded by 3mm margin, respectively.

Follow-up

After receiving radiotherapy, patients were clinically followed up every 3 to 6 months and MRI was performed every year. Tumor response was determined by evaluation of serial MRIs. Progression was defined as an increase in the greatest diameter of an existing tumor by over 20% or the development of a new lesion with a size greater than 5 mm that was undetectable in previous images. Partial response indicated that the tumor size had reduced to 80% or less of the original diameter. Any other response was defined as stable. Local control was defined as no disease progression within the 80% isodose line of the radiotherapy field.

The dose-volume parametric data of the organs-at-risk (OARs) were evaluated for all the patients. MRI images were exported from our institution’s PACS server to a standard volumetric software package (MIM 7.3.7, MIM Software Inc.) for contouring of the OARs such as the hippocampus, optic pathway, and the brainstem. Dose to these OARs were calculated using the RayStation (RaySearch Laboratories, Stockholm, Sweden) treatment planning system.

Statistical analysis

The endpoints of this study were local control and toxicity. Local control rate and overall survival (OS) from the date of WDRT completion were calculated according to the Kaplan-Meier method. Toxicity was evaluated according to the CTCAE v5.0 criteria.

Results

Survival outcomes and toxicity.

The median follow-up duration was 45.8 months (range, 31.1-89.2 months). The 2-year local control rate and overall survival were 100%. Outfield progression occurred in 3 patients with median time to progression of 31.3 months (range, 19.1-60.3 months). For two of these patients, the lesions were originally observed in the pre-treatment MRIs but were excluded from the radiation treatment field as it was decided in a multidisciplinary setting that the lesions would receive subsequent treatment with GKRS or surgery. For one patient who was treated with supratentorial WDRT, marginal recurrence occurred in the left sphenoid ridge at 19.1 months after WDRT completion.

A total of 91 tumors greater than 5 mm were irradiated with a median of 7.5 tumors per patient. After a median follow-up period of 44.1 months, 11 of these tumors decreased in size, 80 remained stable, and 0 tumors showed progression. Two patients presented acute grade 2 toxicities presenting either headache or nausea. No patients showed acute or chronic grade 3 toxicities. Discussion

Our study indicates that WDRT is a safe and effective treatment for patients with NF2-SWN associated meningiomatosis, showing excellent local control rates of 100% at both 1 and 2 years with minimal treatment-related toxicity. While progression was observed in 3 out of the 10 patients, all of them were outfield progression, with 2 lesions intentionally left out from the treatment fields and 1 lesion being marginal recurrence. Furthermore, the dose-volume parametric data in OARs showed that radiation to critical brain structures was kept at an acceptable level despite the wider radiation field compared to that of GKRS or SRS.

Our findings are consistent with existing literature supporting radiation therapy for NF2-SWN associated meningiomas. Wentworth et al. [11] provided early evidence for the clinical utility of radiation therapy in treating NF2-SWN associated CNS tumors. In a retrospective analysis of 12 patients with NF2-SWN, a total of 49 meningiomas were treated. Most patients were treated with GKRS with a 5-year PFS of 86% while some patients with multiple lesions received fractionated RT with a similar PFS. Mohammed et al. [12] conducted a retrospective study with 39 patients across seven international institutions to evaluate tumor control and complications after GKRS. A total of 204 intracranial meningiomas were treated with a 10-year PFS rate of 94.3%. Of all the tumors treated, 4.9% showed progression with a median progression time of 3 years. Radiation-induced adverse events were observed in 4 of the patients (perilesional edema in 3 and radiation necrosis in 1). In another retrospective study, Wei et al. [14] recently reported outcomes of 45 patients who underwent either primary and salvage SRS for NF2-SWN associated meningiomas. The 5-, 10-, and 15-year local control rates per tumor were 90.21%, 84.46%, and 84.46% respectively. However, 20 (44%) patients developed new meningiomas, all of whom underwent additional SRS. Patients with greater number of meningiomas at the time of SRS showed a greater chance of developing new meningiomas (p=0.01).

Some studies have questioned the benefit of treating dural margins in sporadic meningiomas. Piper et al.’s [15] retrospective study on 177 patients with grade I meningioma treated with SRS (15 Gy/1 fraction) or fractionated stereotactic radiation therapy (FSRT) (54 Gy/30 fractions) suggested that radiation of meningioma dural tail may not improve tumor control rates. Their study included 187 meningiomas with radiographic evidence of a dural tail, 24.6% of which were treated with non-covered dural tail plans while 75.4% were treated with covered dural tail plans. The results showed no significant difference in recurrence rates or time to recurrence between the two groups. However, a distinction must be made regarding the fundamental differences between sporadic and NF2-SWN associated meningiomas, where the latter arise in a multiple fashion from a field of genetically altered dural tissue [16]. Additionally, their study showed that radiation of the dural tail did not increase risk for adverse events. These findings are consistent with those of our study as WDRT also showed safe dose-volume parameters in OARs with no reported cases of severe toxicity.

Treatment indication in our cohort was either tumor growth or symptom aggravation. However, the use of subjective symptoms, particularly headache, as a treatment indication warrants careful consideration. Headache in NF2-SWN patients is multifactorial and may not always be directly attributable to meningioma growth or mass effect, potentially arising from intracranial hypertension, medication overuse, or other non-tumor-related causes [17]. In our cohort, no patient was treated on the basis of headache as the sole indication as treatment decisions were made through multidisciplinary consensus incorporating both clinical and radiological findings. Nevertheless, the use of subjective symptoms as a contributing indication introduces possible bias into patient selection and this limitation must be acknowledged. For tumor growth, WDRT was employed as either consolidative adjuvant therapy following surgical debulking or as primary treatment in patients with diffuse meningiomatosis not amenable to surgery. This dual indication reflects the clinical reality of managing NF2-SWN associated meningiomatosis, where treatment decisions must be individualized based on tumor burden, symptom severity, and surgical feasibility [8].

Our study is novel in being the first to explore the concept of WDRT as a treatment for NF2-SWN associated meningiomatosis. While extensive evidence exists for radiation therapy in sporadic meningiomas [18], [19], data specific to NF2-SWN patients have been limited to small case series and retrospective reports, and the techniques used in these studies focused primarily on GKRS or SRS. The results showed that despite the lower target dose compared to that of GKRS or SRS, the short-term local control rate was still very high while the dose to the brain OARs remained at an acceptable level [20]. The risk of adverse events such as perilesional edema or radiation necrosis may also decrease due to such lower dose. Wei et al. [14] reported that despite the high control rate in tumors treated with SRS, nearly half of the patients developed new meningiomas in non-irradiated areas. As WDRT covers a wider treatment area, it can preemptively target any genetically altered dural tissue and possibly reduce tumor recurrence. Further investigation is necessary to test this. It must be acknowledged, however, that focused radiosurgery retains several advantages over WDRT for non-growing or slow-progressive discrete meningiomas. WDRT, by contrast, is most rationally indicated in patients with diffuse meningiomatosis where the multifocal nature and extent of dural involvement render lesion-by-lesion radiosurgical treatment impractical.

An important consideration in the use of WDRT for NF2-SWN patients is the risk of radiation-induced secondary malignancies. Radiation-induced meningiomas and malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNSTs) are well-documented late complications of cranial irradiation, with latency periods typically exceeding 10 to 15 years [21]. This risk is of particular concern in NF2-SWN patients, as germline loss-of-function mutations in the NF2 gene may be connected to increased radiosensitivity and a higher susceptibility to radiation-induced tumorigenesis compared to the general population [22]. The wider treatment volumes inherent to WDRT, compared to GKRS or SRS, should be carefully considered in this context. In our cohort, no secondary malignancies were observed during follow-up. However, given the long latency periods involved, extended follow-up and prospective data collection are essential before definitive conclusions can be drawn about the efficacy and safety of WDRT in this population.

Late-onset brain and neurocognitive complications represent another critical long-term concern with WDRT. Radiation-induced leukoencephalopathy, cerebral atrophy, and neurovascular injury are well-recognized late effects of large-field cranial irradiation and may manifest years after treatment [23]. Radiation-induced cognitive decline, especially, has been associated with hippocampal dose in patients receiving whole or large-field brain irradiation [24], [25]. While NF2-SWN patients receiving WDRT have a different clinical context from those receiving whole brain radiation therapy for brain metastases, the implications of these hippocampal doses on long-term neurocognitive function remain unknown and warrant prospective evaluation.

Our study had several limitations. Firstly, the relatively small sample size of 10 patients limits our finding’s generalizability, although this reflects the rarity of the disease NF2-SWN. The median patient age of 41 years (range, 25–53 years) in our cohort likely reflects a population with moderate-severity NF2-SWN phenotypes. It is well established that age at onset is inversely correlated with disease severity in NF2-SWN; patients with severe forms typically present at a younger age with a higher tumor burden and more aggressive disease course [26]. Therefore, the findings of this study may not be directly applicable to patients with severe NF2-SWN phenotypes. Combined with the study’s retrospective design, there was a risk of selection bias. Next, the inclusion of patients treated both after surgery and without surgery introduced potential confounding variables. Lastly, while the median follow-up period of 44.1 months was sufficient to capture early tumor responses and acute toxicity, it was insufficient to evaluate long-term treatment durability, late toxicity, and risk of secondary malignancies. Long-term surveillance is therefore mandatory for all patients treated with WDRT, particularly in younger patients and those with milder disease phenotypes.

Future directions in the study of WDRT should focus on larger prospective multicenter studies to validate these findings. Inclusion of broader spectrum of disease severity and longer follow-up periods are essential to assess durability of tumor control and identify potential toxicity. Furthermore, prospective comparative studies directly examining WDRT versus GKRS or SRS are necessary to define the relative efficacy, toxicity, and quality of life outcomes of these approaches. Such studies should stratify patients by disease severity and tumor burden to identify the subpopulations most likely to benefit from WDRT over radiosurgery. Additional endpoints should include PFS, OS, subsequent major brain surgery incidence, neurocognitive outcomes, and patient-reported quality of life measures. However, the extended disease course and heterogeneity of the patient population render the feasibility of a randomized clinical trial challenging.

In conclusion, our study suggests that WDRT, in addition to existing methods of treatment, is a promising treatment strategy for NF2-SWN associated meningiomatosis. It offers excellent local control with minimal toxicity in this rare, challenging patient population. However, long term follow-up is necessary to further evaluate the treatment’s efficacy. The results suggest that this approach can effectively address the multifocal nature of NF2-SWN associated meningiomas while avoiding the cumulative morbidity of repeated surgical interventions. WDRT should therefore be considered in patients with diffuse meningiomatosis where radiosurgical treatment of individual lesions is no longer feasible or practical.
