Abstract
. Motivated by a construction of Gorelik and Shaviv, we show that the real roots of a root generated subalgebra associated with a π \pi -system contained in the positive roots are obtained by successive applications of even and odd reflections to the π \pi -system, and that they form a real closed subroot system. Using this result, we establish an analogue of Dynkin’s bijection in the setting of symmetrizable quasisimple Kac–Moody superalgebras. In addition, we obtain several results on root strings in the super setting, analogous to those of Billig and Pianzola, and show that graded embeddings arise as root generated subalgebras associated with linearly independent π \pi -systems.



1.
Introduction

Understanding graded embeddings of Kac–Moody (super)algebras is important both from a structural perspective and due to their applications in mathematical physics. Of particular interest are graded embeddings in which the Chevalley generators are mapped to real root vectors. Such embeddings admit both algebraic and combinatorial interpretations.

For a finite-dimensional semisimple Lie algebra
\mathring{\mathfrak{g}}
, studying graded embeddings is equivalent to understanding its semisimple subalgebras. In his seminal work
[
9
]
, Dynkin classified all semisimple subalgebras of
\mathring{\mathfrak{g}}
and showed that they are in bijection both with linearly independent
\pi
-systems of
\mathring{\Delta}
(the root system of
\mathring{\mathfrak{g}}
) contained in the set of positive roots and with the closed subroot systems of
\mathring{\Delta}
. It is well known that in the finite-dimensional setting, any
\pi
-system consisting of positive roots is automatically linearly independent. Closed subroot systems have been classified via the classification of maximal closed subroot systems, which was carried out in
[
4
]
. Together, these results provide a complete combinatorial solution to the graded embedding problem for finite-dimensional semisimple Lie algebras.

The finite-dimensional picture does not extend to arbitrary Kac–Moody algebras
\mathfrak{g}
, and counterexamples already appear for low-rank untwisted affine Lie algebras. In this broader setting, the appropriate replacements for closed subroot systems and semisimple subalgebras are, respectively, real closed subroot systems and root-generated subalgebras; see Section
2.5
for precise definitions. For general symmetrizable Kac–Moody algebras, foundational results were obtained by Morita
[
24
]
and Naito
[
25
]
, who showed that linearly independent
\pi
-systems give rise to graded embeddings. This idea has since been used repeatedly to construct graded embeddings in subsequent works; see, for example,
[
11
,
16
,
31
]
. In the affine case, a more classification-oriented approach was developed in
[
27
]
, where the authors classified (maximal) real closed subroot systems of affine Lie algebras and, using this classification, proved that every root-generated subalgebra is generated by a
\pi
-system (see
[
17
,
22
]
for generalizations to affine reflection systems).
Recently, the authors of
[
15
]
unified the results described above and proved that an analogue of Dynkin’s bijection holds for arbitrary symmetrizable Kac–Moody algebras. In this setting, every real closed subroot system admits a unique
\pi
-system (see Section
4.1
for the definition), and every root-generated subalgebra is generated by its associated
\pi
-system. Moreover, an explicit presentation of these root-generated subalgebras is provided, and they are shown to be of Kac–Moody type.

The motivation of this article is to study graded embeddings in the super setting from a combinatorial perspective, with the broader goal of investigating the structure of Kac–Moody superalgebras. We focus on the root-generated subalgebras of quasi-simple, regular, symmetrizable Kac–Moody superalgebras that are generated by
\pi
-systems. Motivated by a construction of Gorelik and Shaviv
[
14
]
, we determine their set of real roots (see Theorem
1
) and show that they form a real closed subroot system. Just as in the Lie algebra case, the properties of root strings play a central role in understanding root-generated subalgebras. We determine the real roots inside a root string for Kac–Moody superalgebras in the spirit of
[
2
,
23
]
(see Corollary
Corollary
) and prove that in the symmetrizable case a root string contains at most four real roots (see Proposition
Proposition
). Finally, we establish Dynkin’s bijection in the super setting, providing a correspondence between root-generated subalgebras arising from
\pi
-systems, closed subroot systems that admit
\pi
-systems, and
\pi
-systems contained in positive root systems (see Theorem
2
). In the concluding subsection, we discuss how
\pi
-systems and closed subroot systems arise naturally in the graded embedding problem and outline several applications of these embeddings in mathematical physics.

However, the definitions and conventions for Kac–Moody superalgebras - such as the definition of the algebras themselves or of real roots - are not uniform in the literature (see, e.g.,
[
13
,
28
,
32
]
). In this article, we adopt the conventions of
[
28
]
. Several fundamental properties that hold for Kac–Moody algebras fail in the super setting. For instance, a real closed subroot system may fail to admit a
\pi
-system, and a root-generated subalgebra arising from a
\pi
-system need not be generated by one contained in the positive roots; we provide explicit examples along the way. This makes the study of root-generated subalgebras, and consequently the graded embedding problem, particularly interesting.
Even in the finite-dimensional case, the graded embedding problem remains far from fully understood, and only a few works address regular subalgebras in the super setting. In
[
30
]
, embeddings of graded subalgebras were constructed using specific linearly independent
\pi
-systems, depending on the type. In
[
26
]
, the analogue of
[
31
]
was established for the hyperbolic Kac–Moody superalgebra
HD(4,1)
of rank
6
, although their definition uses only the Serre and contragredient relations, rather than the maximal ideal intersecting the Cartan subalgebra trivially.

The paper is organized as follows:
In Section
2
, we recall basic facts about Kac–Moody superalgebras and introduce the notions used throughout the paper. In Section
3
, we study root strings, their symmetries and determine the positions of real roots. In Section
4
, we analyze root-generated subalgebras arising from
\pi
-systems containing positive roots, determine their sets of real roots, and prove that Dynkin’s bijections hold in this context. Finally, we discuss the graded embedding problem in the super setting and highlight some applications of these embeddings in mathematical physics.

2.
Preliminaries and regular subalgebras

2.1.

We denote by
\mathbb{N},\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and
\mathbb{Z}
the set of positive integers, non-negative integers, and integers, respectively. Unless otherwise stated, all vector spaces are assumed to be complex vector spaces and a super vector space is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded vector space. We denote by
p(x)
the parity of a homogeneous element
x
in a super vector space.

2.2.

Let
I=\{1,\dots,n\}
be a finite index set,
p:I\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{2}
be a parity function, and
A=(a_{ij}),\ i,j\in I
be a complex matrix. Fix an even vector space
\mathfrak{h}
of dimension
2n-\mathrm{rank}(A)
and linearly independent vectors
\alpha_{i}\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
and
h_{i}\in\mathfrak{h}
such that
\alpha_{j}(h_{i})=a_{ij}
for all
i,j\in I
; see
[
21
]
for the existence and uniqueness up to a linear transformation of
\mathfrak{h}
. Define a Lie superalgebra
\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(A)
by generators
x_{i}^{\pm},\ i\in I
and
\mathfrak{h}
with relations

[h,h^{\prime}]=0,\ \ [h,x_{i}^{\pm}]=\pm\alpha_{i}(h)x_{i}^{\pm},\ \ [x_{i}^{+},x_{j}^{-}]=\delta_{ij}h_{i},\ \ p(x_{i}^{\pm})=p(i)

for all
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{h}
and
i,j\in I
.
It is known that there exists a unique maximal ideal
\boldsymbol{\tau}
of
\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(A)
which intersects
\mathfrak{h}
trivially (see
[
29
, Proposition 2.2]
). We define
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}(A)
to be the quotient of
\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(A)
by the ideal
\boldsymbol{\tau}
and omit the dependence on
A
when the context is clear. Moreover, we have
\mathfrak{g}(A)\cong\mathfrak{g}(DA)
for any invertible diagonal matrix
D
and thus we will assume without loss of generality that
a_{ii}\in\{0,2\}
for all
i\in I
. Such matrices are called
normalized
.

The action of
\mathfrak{h}
on
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{1}
is diagonalizable and induces a root space decomposition

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\ \ \ \mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}:=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}:[h,x]=\alpha(h)x,\ \forall h\in\mathfrak{h}\}

where
\Delta=\{\alpha\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}:\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\neq 0\}
is called the set of
roots
. We define as usual the set of
even roots
and
odd roots
by

\Delta_{0}=\{\alpha\in\Delta:\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{0}\},\ \ \ \Delta_{1}=\{\alpha\in\Delta:\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{1}\}.

Moreover, we denote by
\Delta^{\pm}
the sets of
positive
and
negative roots
, respectively - that is, those roots which are linear combinations of the
\alpha_{i}
’s with non-negative and non-positive coefficients, respectively. Then we have

\Delta=\Delta_{0}\cup\Delta_{1}=\Delta^{+}\cup\Delta^{-}

and we can extend the parity function
p
to
\Delta
in the obvious way.
The set of simple roots is denoted by
\Pi=\{\alpha_{i}:i\in I\}
. The Chevalley involution
\tilde{w}:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}
is defined as follows

\tilde{w}(x_{i}^{+})=-(-1)^{p(i)}x_{i}^{-},\ \ \tilde{w}(x_{i}^{-})=-x_{i}^{+},\ \ \tilde{w}(h)=-h,\ \ h\in\mathfrak{h},\ i\in I.

Setting
I_{1}=\{i\in I:p(i)=1\}
we have that
\tilde{w}
has order 4 if
I_{1}\neq\emptyset
and order 2 otherwise. The center
\mathfrak{c}
of
\mathfrak{g}
is given by
\mathfrak{c}=\{h\in\mathfrak{h}:\alpha_{i}(h)=0\ \forall i\in I\}
(see
[
29
, Section 2.5]
) and denote by
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}=[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
the derived superalgebra. We have
\mathfrak{c}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\oplus\mathfrak{h}^{\prime\prime}
where
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}\oplus\mathfrak{h}^{\prime\prime}
and
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}
is the span of the
h_{i}
’s and
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime\prime}
is a linear complement.

Remark
.

In contrast to the situation for Lie algebras, one obtains several distinct types of rank-one subalgebras.
For each

, the elements
x_{i}^{\pm}
and

generate a subalgebra

\Diamond

\Diamond

\mathfrak{sl}(1,1)

\Diamond

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}

a_{ii}=2
and
p(i)=0
,

\Diamond

\mathfrak{osp}(1,2)

and

We call

and

otherwise and

is called

The (normalized) matrix

(2)

∎

We say that a matrix
A

symmetrizable
if there exists an invertible diagonal matrix

2.3.

A linearly independent subset
\Sigma\subseteq\Delta
is called a

of
\mathfrak{g}
if for each
\alpha\in\Sigma

x_{\alpha}^{\pm}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\pm\alpha}

Setting

For example,
\Pi

. We denote by

Given

Define for such a reflection and

x_{s_{\alpha}(\beta)}^{\pm}=x_{\alpha}^{\mp},\ \text{if $s_{\alpha}(\beta)=-\alpha$},\ \ x_{s_{\alpha}(\beta)}^{\pm}=[x_{\alpha}^{\pm},x_{\beta}^{\pm}],\ \ \text{if $s_{\alpha}(\beta)=\alpha+\beta$}.

Note that

a_{\beta\alpha}\neq 0

h_{s_{\alpha}(\beta)}=[x_{s_{\alpha}(\beta)}^{+},x_{s_{\alpha}(\beta)}^{-}]=\begin{cases}h_{\beta}&\text{ if }a_{\alpha\beta}=a_{\beta\alpha}=0,\\
\vskip 5.0pt(-1)^{p(\beta)}(a_{\alpha\beta}h_{\beta}+a_{\beta\alpha}h_{\alpha})&\text{ if }a_{\alpha\beta}\neq 0\text{ or }a_{\beta\alpha}\neq 0\end{cases}

A root
\alpha

\Sigma

a_{\alpha\beta}=0\implies a_{\beta\alpha}=0

Note that this definition extends the previous one for the standard base and depends on the choice of base. Moreover, a root

(resp.

Given a base
\Sigma

An even root

is regular. Hence, the ideal generated by
[x^{+}_{\alpha},x^{+}_{\beta}]
is a non-zero proper ideal intersecting the Cartan subalgebra trivially. This is a contradiction.

We recall the definition of regular subalgebras and some combinatorial subsets associated to them.

Definition
.

(1)

A
\mathfrak{h}
-invariant Lie subsuperalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
is called a
regular subalgebra
. Given a regular subalgebra
\mathfrak{s}
, we define

\Delta(\mathfrak{s}):=\{\alpha\in\Delta:(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\cap\mathfrak{s})\neq 0\},\ \ \mathfrak{s}_{\alpha}:=\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\cap\mathfrak{s},\ \alpha\in\Delta(\mathfrak{s})

to be the set of roots of
\mathfrak{s}
with respect to
\mathfrak{h}
. Furthermore, we set

\Delta(\mathfrak{s})^{\mathrm{re}}=\Delta^{\mathrm{re}}\cap\Delta(\mathfrak{s}),\ \ \Delta(\mathfrak{s})^{\mathrm{im}}=\Delta^{\mathrm{im}}\cap\Delta(\mathfrak{s}),

\Delta(\mathfrak{s})^{\mathrm{re},+}=\Delta(\mathfrak{s})^{\mathrm{re}}\cap\Delta^{+},\ \ \Delta(\mathfrak{s})^{\mathrm{im},+}=\Delta(\mathfrak{s})^{\mathrm{im}}\cap\Delta^{+}.

(2)

A regular subalgebra
\mathfrak{s}
is called a
root generated subalgebra
if there exists a subset
S\subseteq\Delta^{\mathrm{re}}
such that
\mathfrak{s}=\mathfrak{g}(S)
, where
\mathfrak{g}(S)
is defined to be the Lie subsuperalgebra generated by all root spaces
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\pm S
.

Note that a regular subalgebra admits the following decomposition

\mathfrak{s}=(\mathfrak{h}\cap\mathfrak{s})\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta(\mathfrak{s})}\mathfrak{s}_{\alpha}.

A non-empty subset
\Psi\subseteq\Delta^{\mathrm{re}}
is called

•

symmetric
if
\Psi=-\Psi
.

•

a subroot system
if
s_{\alpha}(\beta)\in\Psi
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Psi
.

•

real closed
if
\alpha,\beta\in\Psi
and
\alpha+\beta\in\Delta^{\mathrm{re}}
implies
\alpha+\beta\in\Psi.

For simplicity, we will write closed instead of real closed from now on. Note that every subroot system is symmetric, and by Remark
Remark
, the set
\Delta^{\mathrm{re}}
is a closed subroot system.

Lemma
.

The finite-dimensional irreducible
\mathfrak{osp}(1,2)
-modules are up to isomorphism in bijective correspondence with
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. To be more precises, for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},
the associated irreducible module has a basis
\{v_{j}:0\leq j\leq 2k\}
with action

v_{j}=f^{j}v_{0},\ \ \ hv_{j}=(2k-2j)v_{j}

ev_{j}=\begin{cases}-jv_{j-1}&\text{ if $j$ is even},\\
(2k+1-j)v_{j-1}&\text{ if $j$ is odd}\end{cases}

where
\{e,f\}
is a basis of
\mathfrak{osp}(1,2)_{1}
and
h=[e,f].

The next proposition proves important properties of
\Delta(\mathfrak{s})^{\mathrm{re}}
.

Proposition
.

Let
\mathfrak{s}
be a regular subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta(\mathfrak{s})^{\mathrm{re}}.

(1)

If
\alpha
or
\beta
is non-isotropic, then
[\mathfrak{s}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{s}_{\beta}]\neq 0
if and only if
\alpha+\beta\in\Delta.

(2)

If both
\alpha
and
\beta
are isotropic such that
(\alpha+\beta)\in\Delta^{\mathrm{re}}
and

(2.3)

h_{\alpha+\beta}\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{h_{\alpha},h_{\beta}\},

then
[\mathfrak{s}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{s}_{\beta}]\neq 0.

(3)

If
\alpha
is non-isotropic such that
-\alpha\in\Delta(\mathfrak{s})
, then we have
s_{\alpha}(\beta)\in\Delta(\mathfrak{s})^{\mathrm{re}}.

Proof.

We first prove part
(1).
Without loss of generality we assume that
\alpha
is a non-isotropic real root. Hence
\mathrm{ad}_{x_{\alpha}^{\pm}}
acts locally nilpotently and
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
or
\mathfrak{osp}(1|2)
by Remark
Remark
. If
\alpha+\beta
is a root and
[\mathfrak{s}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{s}_{\beta}]=0
, we consider the
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)
-module generated by
x_{\beta}^{+}
which is finite-dimensional and irreducible. Thus
\beta(h_{\alpha})\geq 0
by the representation theory of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
or
\mathfrak{osp}(1,2)
(see Lemma
Lemma
). By assumption, there exists a non-zero element
v\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha+\beta}
and the
h_{\alpha}
-eigenvalue is strictly positive. Thus
0\neq[x_{\alpha}^{+},[x_{\alpha}^{-},v]]\in[\mathfrak{s}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{s}_{\beta}]
, which is a contradiction.

To prove
(2)
assume by contradiction
[x^{+}_{\alpha},x^{+}_{\beta}]=0.
Note that
\alpha,\beta
are real isotropic odd roots and
\alpha+\beta
is an even non-isotropic real root by Lemma
Lemma
. Hence
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha+\beta)
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
by Remark
Remark
. Moreover,
(\alpha+\beta)-\alpha=\beta
implies that
[x^{+}_{\alpha+\beta},x^{+}_{-\alpha}]=-ax^{+}_{\beta}
for some non-zero scalar
a
by part (1) applied to
\mathfrak{s}=\mathfrak{g}
. Now, using the super Jacobi identity we obtain (note that
[x^{+}_{-\alpha},x^{+}_{-\alpha}]=0
, since
\alpha
is odd isotropic)

[x^{+}_{-\alpha},[x^{+}_{-\alpha},x^{+}_{\alpha+\beta}]]=-[x^{+}_{-\alpha},[x^{+}_{-\alpha},x^{+}_{\alpha+\beta}]]\implies[x^{+}_{-\alpha},[x^{+}_{-\alpha},x^{+}_{\alpha+\beta}]]=0.

Thus
a[x^{+}_{-\alpha},x^{+}_{\beta}]=0
and so
[x^{+}_{-\alpha},x^{+}_{\beta}]=0.
Consequently, the vector
x^{+}_{\beta}
is killed by both
x^{+}_{\alpha}
and
x^{+}_{-\alpha}
and so
[h_{\alpha},x^{+}_{\beta}]=0.
This implies
\beta(h_{\alpha})=0
and similarly one can show
\alpha(h_{\beta})=0.
This is absurd by (
2.3
), since
\alpha+\beta
is non-isotropic.

The proof of
(3)
is similar to
[
15
, Proposition 2.4]
and we give some details. If
\beta(h_{\alpha})=0,
then
s_{\alpha}(\beta)=\beta

and there is nothing to show. So assume that
\beta(h_{\alpha})\neq 0.
Since the
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)
module generated by
x_{\beta}^{+}
is finite dimensional, it follows from the representation theory of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
or
\mathfrak{osp}(1,2)
that

\displaystyle(\mathrm{ad}\,x_{\alpha}^{-})^{\beta(h_{\alpha})}(x_{\beta}^{+})=[x_{\alpha}^{-},[x_{\alpha}^{-},\dots[x_{\alpha}^{-},[x_{\alpha}^{-},x_{\beta}^{+}]]\cdots]\neq 0

\displaystyle\ \text{ if }\beta(h_{\alpha})>0,

\displaystyle(\mathrm{ad}\,x_{\alpha}^{+})^{-\beta(h_{\alpha})}(x_{\beta}^{+})=[x_{\alpha}^{+},[x_{\alpha}^{+},\dots[x_{\alpha}^{+},[x_{\alpha}^{+},x_{\beta}^{+}]]\cdots]\neq 0

\displaystyle\ \text{ if }\beta(h_{\alpha})<0,

Therefore, we have
s_{\alpha}(\beta)=\beta-\beta(h_{\alpha})\alpha\in\Delta(\mathfrak{s})^{\mathrm{re}}
.
∎

Remark

If

Corollary
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be symmetrizable and
\mathfrak{s}
be a regular subalgebra.

(1)

If
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta(\mathfrak{s})^{\mathrm{re}},
then
[\mathfrak{s}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{s}_{\beta}]\neq 0
if and only if
\alpha+\beta\in\Delta.

(2)

If
\Delta(\mathfrak{s})^{\mathrm{re}}
is symmetric, then
\Delta(\mathfrak{s})^{\mathrm{re}}
is a closed subroot system.

(3)

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta^{\mathrm{re}}
be such that
\alpha
is isotropic. Then
\alpha\pm\beta\in\Delta^{\mathrm{re}}
implies
\alpha\mp\beta\notin\Delta
.

Proof.

Since
\mathfrak{g}
is symmetrizable, property (
2.3
) always holds and part (2) follows from Proposition
Proposition
. Part (1) again follows from Proposition
Proposition
unless
\alpha,\beta
are isotropic and
\alpha+\beta
is imaginary. In this case the claim follows from Remark
Remark
by the explicit description of roots. For part
(3),
assume that
\alpha\pm\beta
is real. Then we have by part (1) (up to non-zero scalars)

x_{\pm\beta}^{+}=[x_{\alpha}^{-},x_{\alpha\pm\beta}^{+}]\implies[x_{\alpha}^{-},x_{\pm\beta}^{+}]=[x_{\alpha}^{-},[x_{\alpha}^{-},x_{\alpha\pm\beta}^{+}]]=0\implies\alpha\mp\beta\notin\Delta.

∎

In this section, we study the root strings of Kac-Moody superalgebras. The Lie algebra counterpart of this section can be found in

2
,
23
]
. For
\alpha\in\Delta^{\mathrm{re}}
and
\beta\in\Delta
, the
\alpha
-string through
\beta,
denoted as
\mathbb{S}(\beta,\alpha),
is defined by

\mathbb{S}(\beta,\alpha):=\{\beta+k\alpha:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}\cap\Delta.

Note that, if
\alpha
and
\beta
are scalar multiples of each other we have

(3.1)

\mathbb{S}(\beta,\alpha)\subseteq\{\pm\alpha,\pm 2\alpha\},\ \ \text{if}\ \ \beta\in\{\pm\alpha,\pm 2\alpha\},\ \ \mathbb{S}(\beta,\alpha)\subseteq\left\{\pm\frac{\alpha}{2}\right\},\ \ \text{if}\ \ \beta\in\left\{\pm\frac{\alpha}{2}\right\}

3.1.

The proof of the first part of the following proposition is analogous to
[
21
, Proposition 5.1]
. We give the details for completeness.

Proposition
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta
be two roots of a Kac-Moody superalgebra such that
\alpha
is real.

(1)

If
\alpha
is non-isotropic, then there exist non-negative integers
p
and
q
related by the equation
p-q=\beta(h_{\alpha})
such that
\beta+k\alpha\in\Delta\cup\{0\}
if and only if
-p\leq k\leq q.
In particular,
s_{\alpha}
reverses the root string
\mathbb{S}(\beta,\alpha)
.

(2)

If
\alpha
is isotropic, then
|\mathbb{S}(\beta,\alpha)|
is finite.

Proof.

We start proving part (1). The result is easily checked if
\beta
and
\alpha
are scalar multiple of each other. Otherwise, consider the subspace
U=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathfrak{g}_{\beta+k\alpha}
of
\mathfrak{g}.
Since
\alpha
is non-isotropic, the subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)

is isomorphic to either
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
or
\mathfrak{osp}(1,2).
Let
v
be a root vector in
U.
Since
\mathrm{ad}_{x_{\alpha}^{\pm}}
acts locally nilpotently, the
\mathfrak{g}{(\alpha)}
submodule of
U
generated by
v
is finite-dimensional. Thus, by the representation theory of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
or
\mathfrak{osp}(1,2)
, we have that
U
is a sum of finite-dimensional irreducible
\mathfrak{g}{(\alpha)}
-modules.
Since the eigenvalues of
h_{\alpha}
in
U
are of the form
\beta(h_{\alpha})+2k
, it follows that every irreducible module that occur in
U
contains a non-zero vector in
\mathfrak{g}_{\beta+t\alpha}
where

t=\begin{cases}\frac{-\beta(h_{\alpha})}{2}&\text{ if }\beta(h_{\alpha})\text{ is even},\\
\frac{1-\beta(h_{\alpha})}{2}&\text{ if }\beta(h_{\alpha})\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

Therefore,
U
is equal to the
\mathfrak{g}{(\alpha)}
submodule generated by the root space
\mathfrak{g}_{\beta+t\alpha}
. In particular,
U
is finite-dimensional and the claim follows from the representation theory of
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha).

Now we prove part (2). If
\alpha
and
\beta
are scalar multiples of each other, the statement is clear. Since
\alpha
is real and odd we must have
\alpha\in\Sigma
for a base
\Sigma
. Moreover,

(3.2)

[x^{+}_{\alpha},[x_{\alpha}^{+},y]]=0,\ \ y\in\mathfrak{g}.

We first assume that
\beta\in C^{+}_{\Sigma}
, i.e.,
\beta=\sum_{\gamma\in\Sigma}a_{\gamma}\gamma
where
a_{\gamma}\geq 0
. If
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
is such that
\beta+k\alpha\in\Delta
, we can express any element in
\mathfrak{g}_{\beta+k\alpha}
as a linear combination of Lie words in the root vectors corresponding to the base
\Sigma
. However, by (
3.2
) we must have

\mathfrak{g}_{\beta+k\alpha}\neq 0\implies k\leq\sum_{\gamma\in\Sigma\setminus\{\alpha\}}a_{\gamma}-a_{\alpha}.

Moreover, if
t>a_{\alpha}
, we claim that
\beta-t\alpha
is never a root. Indeed, we have

\beta-t\alpha=\sum_{\gamma\in\Sigma\setminus\{\alpha\}}a_{\gamma}\gamma+(a_{\alpha}-t)\alpha.

Since
\beta
is not a scalar multiple of
\alpha
, there exists some
\gamma\in\Sigma\setminus\{\alpha\}
with
a_{\gamma}>0
. Since
t>a_{\alpha}
, we must have
\beta-t\alpha\notin\Delta
. The proof for the case
\beta\in-C^{+}_{\Sigma}
is similar.
∎

We will identify the real roots in a root string. We begin with the following lemma.

Lemma
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta
be such that
\alpha
is real. Then any root lying in between two imaginary roots in
\mathbb{S}(\beta,\alpha)
is imaginary.

Proof.

Assume that there exist a real root
\beta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}(\beta,\alpha)
and
r,k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\beta^{\prime}-r\alpha,\beta^{\prime}+k\alpha\in\Delta^{\mathrm{im}}.
Note that
\beta
and
\alpha
are not scalar multiple of each other by (
3.1
).
Since
\alpha
and
\beta^{\prime}
are both real roots, there exist bases
\Sigma^{\prime}
and
\Sigma^{\prime\prime}
and
s,t\in\{1,1/2\}
such that
s\beta^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\prime}
and
t\alpha\in\Sigma^{\prime\prime}.
Using Lemma
Lemma
, we first assume that
\beta^{\prime}-r\alpha\in D(\Pi)
. Note that
t\alpha\in\Sigma^{\prime\prime}
gives
s\beta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\Sigma^{\prime\prime}
and since
s\beta^{\prime}
is a root we must actually have
s\beta^{\prime}\in C_{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}}^{+}
. Moreover, we write
\beta^{\prime}-r\alpha=\sum_{\gamma\in\Sigma^{\prime}}c_{\gamma}\gamma
for some non-negative integers
c_{\gamma}.
Now since

r\alpha=(-c_{s\beta^{\prime}}+1/s)s\beta^{\prime}+\sum_{\gamma\in\Sigma^{\prime}\backslash\{s\beta^{\prime}\}}(-c_{\gamma})\gamma

and
\alpha
and
\beta^{\prime}
are not scalar multiples of each other, it follows that there exists
\gamma\in\Sigma^{\prime}\backslash\{s\beta^{\prime}\}
such that
c_{\gamma}>0.
This gives
\beta^{\prime}+k\alpha\in-D(\Pi)
since
\beta^{\prime}+k\alpha
is an imaginary root and

\beta^{\prime}+k\alpha=\left[\frac{1}{s}+\frac{k}{r}\left(\frac{1}{s}-c_{s\beta^{\prime}}\right)\right]s\beta^{\prime}+\frac{k}{r}\left(\sum_{\gamma\in\Sigma^{\prime}\backslash\{s\beta^{\prime}\}}(-c_{\gamma})\gamma\right)\in-C_{\Sigma^{\prime}}^{+}.

We write
\beta^{\prime}+k\alpha=\sum_{\mu\in\Sigma^{\prime\prime}}d_{\mu}\mu
with
d_{\mu}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
and at least one of the coefficients is strictly negative. Then we have

\beta^{\prime}=(d_{t\alpha}-k/t)t\alpha+\sum_{\mu\in\Sigma^{\prime\prime}\backslash\{t\alpha\}}d_{\mu}\mu\in-C^{+}_{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}},

which contradicts the fact that
s\beta^{\prime}\in C_{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}}^{+}.
The proof for the case
\beta^{\prime}-r\alpha\in-D(\Pi)
is similar.
∎

4.
Root generated subalgebras and
\pi
-systems

In this section, we study root generated subalgebras arising from
\pi
-systems and determine their sets of real roots. This will allow us to generalize
[
15
, Theorem 1]
(and hence Dynkin’s bijection mentioned in the introduction) to the super setting. The study of such subalgebras is important, as every graded embedding (more precisely, its derived algebra) is isomorphic to a root generated subalgebra defined by a
\pi
-system.

Throughout this section, we assume that
\mathfrak{g}
is a symmetrizable, quasisimple, regular Kac-Moody superalgebra.

4.1.

We study one of our main combinatorial object, namely the
\pi
-systems and see how they arise in the study of closed subroot systems. We begin with the following definition. For similar study for symmetrizable Kac-Moody algebras, we refer to
[

]
.

Definition
.

A non-empty subset

is called a

-system
if

(1)

\alpha-\beta\notin\Delta
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Sigma

(2)

\alpha\notin\sum_{\beta\in\Sigma\backslash\{\alpha\}}\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\beta
for all
\alpha\in\Sigma.

Furthermore, for

, we define inductively

S_{0}=(S\cup 2S)\cap\Delta^{\mathrm{re}},\ \ S_{k}=\pm\{s_{\alpha}(\beta):\alpha,\beta\in S_{k-1}\},\ \ k\in\mathbb{N},\ \ S_{\infty}=\displaystyle{\cup_{k=0}^{\infty}}S_{k}.

We say that a closed subroot system
\Psi
admits a
\pi
-system if there exists a
\pi
-system
\Sigma\subseteq\Delta^{+}
such that
\Psi=\Sigma_{\infty}.

A construction similar to
S_{\infty}
appeared in
[
14
]
in the context of studying subroot systems of generalized reflection root systems.

Remark
.

Note that the condition in Definition

(2) is redundant for all non-isotropic roots. Indeed, if
\alpha\in\Sigma
is non-isotropic, then for every
\beta\in\Sigma\backslash\{\alpha\}
we have
\beta(h_{\alpha})\leq 0
. If
\Sigma
contains only non-isotropic roots, then we have
\Sigma_{\infty}=W_{\Sigma}(\Sigma_{0})
, where
W_{\Sigma}=\langle s_{\beta}:\beta\in\Sigma\rangle
.

By
[

, Theorem 1]
, every root generated subalgebra of a symmetrizable Kac-Moody algebra is generated by a
\pi
-system in
\Delta^{+}
. Moreover, every closed subroot system admits a
\pi
-system
[
15
, Proposition 3.3]
. The following example shows that such statements fail in the super setting.

Example

Let
\mathring{\mathfrak{g}}
be a finite dimensional basic classical simple Lie superalgebra and
\mathfrak{g}
be the corresponding (untwisted) affine Lie superalgebra. Let
\alpha

Let

Then

Thus,

is generated by the

However,

(every such subset has to satisfy

).

In this subsection we address the fundamental problem of determining the real roots of root generated subalgebras defined by

,
16
,

,

,

,

,

Lemma
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\Delta^{\mathrm{+}}
be a
\pi
-system and let
\mathfrak{g}(\Sigma)
be the associated root generated subalgebra.

(1)

Every root vector of

is a linear combination of right-normed Lie words in either

or
\{x_{\alpha}^{-}:\alpha\in\Sigma\}
. In particular, every root of

is an integral linear combination of elements of
\Sigma

(2)

Let
\Psi=\Delta(\mathfrak{g}(\Sigma))^{\mathrm{re}}
. Then we have

(3)

Let
\alpha\in\Sigma

\beta\in\Delta(\mathfrak{g}(\Sigma))^{\mathrm{re},+}
.

(a)

Assume that either
\bigl|\{\beta-k\alpha:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\}\cap\Delta^{\mathrm{im}}\bigr|\geq 1

or
\bigl|\{\beta-k\alpha:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\}\cap\Delta^{\mathrm{re}}\bigr|\geq 3.

Then
\beta(h_{\alpha})\in\mathbb{N}
.

(b)

Let
\beta\notin\mathbb{N}\alpha
and
\beta(h_{\alpha})\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
s_{\alpha}(\beta)\in\Delta(\mathfrak{g}(\Sigma))^{\mathrm{re},+}
.

Proof.

The proof of part (1) follows directly from the fact that
\Sigma
is a
\pi
-system and the super Jacobi identity.
For part (2), let
\beta\in\Psi^{+}\setminus\Sigma
and write

\beta=c_{1}\beta_{1}+c_{2}\beta_{2}+\cdots+c_{k}\beta_{k},\ \ \ c_{i}\in\mathbb{N},\ \beta_{i}\in\Sigma,\ \beta_{i}\neq\beta_{j}\ \forall i\neq j

which is possible by part (1). If
k=1
, then
c_{1}=2
, and by definition we have
\beta\notin\Pi(\Psi)
.
If
k\geq 2
, then
\beta_{1}\notin\mathbb{N}\beta
and
\beta_{1}\leq\beta
, which again implies
\beta\notin\Pi(\Psi)
.
Thus we obtain
\Pi(\Psi)\subseteq\Sigma
.
Now let
\beta\in\Sigma
and suppose that there exists
\gamma\in\Psi^{+}
with
\gamma\preceq\beta
and
\gamma\notin\mathbb{N}\beta
. Then we can write

\beta=a\gamma+\sum_{\tau\in\Psi^{+}\backslash\{\beta,\gamma\}}a_{\tau}\tau,\ \ \ a,a_{\tau}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0},\ a\neq 0.

Since
\Psi^{+}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{+}\Sigma
we can replace each

and
\tau
in (

) to obtain an expression of the form

\beta=\sum_{\mu\in\Sigma}b_{\mu}\mu
for some

with
b_{\nu}\neq 0
for some

(recall

). If

then we have

\sum_{\mu\in\Sigma\backslash\{\beta\}}b_{\mu}\mu+(b_{\beta}-1)\beta=0,

which is a contradiction. If
b_{\beta}<1,
then we can write

\beta=\sum_{\mu\in\Sigma\backslash\{\beta\}}\frac{b_{\mu}}{1-b_{\beta}}\mu,

which is again a contradiction to Definition

(2)

and the proof of part

is completed.
Now we shall prove part

Part (a) follows from Proposition

(1), Corollary

, and Proposition

(2). For part (b) assume if possible that

Then we have

Using part (1) we can write

Therefore,

\left(1-\frac{b_{\alpha}}{\beta(h_{\alpha})}\right)\alpha=\frac{1}{\beta(h_{\alpha})}\left(\beta+\sum_{\gamma\in\Sigma\backslash\{\alpha\}}b_{\gamma}\gamma\right).

Note that

Theorem 1

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\Delta^{+}

\Sigma_{\infty}=\Delta(\mathfrak{g}(\Sigma))^{\mathrm{re}}.

Proof.

By Remark

, it is enough to prove the result when

has at least one isotropic real root. Therefore, the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
is either of finite or (twisted) affine type. In the reminder of the proof we shall show that

x_{\beta}^{+}=[x_{\beta_{1}}^{+},[x_{\beta_{2}}^{+},\cdots,[x_{\beta_{k-1}}^{+},x_{\beta_{k}}^{+}]\cdots]],

and thus the induction begins when

. So suppose that

Since

is a real root, the positive integer

.

Case 1:

Then we can write

If

, then

Thus we can assume that

By induction hypothesis, we have

Moreover, since

If either

If

is not a root, then by the representation theory of

Thus

If

Therefore

Let

We can write

where

is an imaginary root. In this case we must have

, since the explicit description of root vectors shows that

If

is non-isotropic, then using Lemma

4.4.

In this subsection, we describe the graded embedding problem for Kac–Moody superalgebras and explain how
\pi
-systems arise naturally in this setting. Since their introduction by Dynkin
[
9
]
,
\pi
-systems have played a central role in this area, and numerous graded embeddings have been constructed using them. For further details, we refer the reader to
[
11
,
31
]
.

In contrast, the literature on graded embeddings for Kac–Moody superalgebras remains relatively limited. In
[
30
]
, the authors showed that, when
\mathfrak{g}
is finite-dimensional, the root generated subalgebra associated with a linearly independent
\pi
-system is semisimple. Furthermore, in
[
26
]
, an analogue of the result in
[
31
]
was established in the superalgebra setting. However, rather than working with the maximal ideal intersecting
\mathfrak{h}
trivially, they considered only the Serre relations together with the contragredient relations.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be symmetrizable, and fix a matrix
B
with index set
J
such that there exists a graded embedding, i.e., a map sending the Chevalley generators to real root vectors:

f:\mathfrak{g}(B)\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{g},\quad y_{j}^{\pm}\mapsto x_{\beta_{j}}^{\pm},\quad j\in J,

where
\Sigma:=\{\beta_{j}:j\in J\}\subseteq\Delta^{\mathrm{re}}
, and
y_{j}^{\pm}
denote the Chevalley generators of
\mathfrak{g}(B)
.

Lemma
.

We have that
\Sigma=\{\beta_{j}:j\in J\}
is a linearly independent
\pi
-system and
f
induces an isomorphism
\tilde{f}:\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}(B)\to\mathfrak{g}(\Sigma)
.

Proof.

From the injectivity and Corollary
Corollary
we obtain that
\Sigma
is a linearly independent
\pi
-system. The restriction of
f
to
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}(B)
remains injective and the image is given by the root generated subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}(\Sigma)
.
∎

Thus the derived algebra of any such embedding is generated by some
\pi
-system of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Remark
.

Although not every root generated subalgebra in the super setting is generated by a
\pi
-system contained in
\Delta^{+}
, this is the case in many examples of graded embeddings. It would be interesting to establish a general proof of this phenomenon. If
\mathfrak{g}
is a symmetrizable Kac-Moody algebra, then this fact has been proven in
[
15
, Proposition 3.4]
, i.e., there is a
\pi
-system
\Sigma^{\prime}\subseteq\Delta^{\mathrm{re},+}
with
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}(B)\cong\mathfrak{g}(\Sigma)=\mathfrak{g}(\Sigma^{\prime}).

The graded embedding problem has also various applications in physics, see for example
[
5
,
10
,
18
]
. For instance, the real roots and representations of the hyperbolic Kac-Moody algebra
E_{10}
are known to correspond to the fields of 11-dimensional supergravity at low levels
[
5
]
and the graded embedding of
AE_{3}
into
E_{10}
(proven in
[
31
]
) reflects the embedding of Einstein gravity into 11-dimensional supergravity. An alternative embedding of
AE_{3}
into
E_{10}
was obtained through the gravity truncation method described in
[
6
]
.

In the Gaiotto–Witten Janus configuration (
[
12
]
), the authors show how a four-dimensional
\mathcal{N}=4
super Yang–Mills theory with a spatially varying coupling (including the theta-angle) gives rise, at the interface, to a three-dimensional
\mathcal{N}=4
Chern–Simons theory. This can be viewed algebraically as embedding the smaller 3d supersymmetry (and its associated superalgebra) into the larger 4d superalgebra across the interface, realizing a graded embedding of lower-dimensional supersymmetry in a higher-dimensional theory. In
[
1
]
the authors showed that a graded subalgebra of
\mathfrak{osp}(n,2)
acts as a spectrum-generating symmetry on the space of physical fields in certain superconformal field theories. The constraints of this hidden symmetry are then used to compute correlation functions.
