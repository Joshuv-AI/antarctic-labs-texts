Oncoplastic chest masculinization using lateral thoracic adipofascial flaps after risk-reducing mastectomy
Authors: Abbas M Hassan, Pedro E Ruiz-Medina, Olivia R Simcox, Carla S Fisher, Ivan Hadad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe high-risk transgender man with a pathogenic variant in a cancer predisposition gene presents a clinical dilemma wherein the requisite oncologic mastectomy creates an inherent dichotomy with the aesthetic goals of chest masculinization. A standard gender-affirming mastectomy leaves residual glandular tissue, necessitating burdensome surveillance. Conversely, a complete oncologic mastectomy, particularly in obese patients, frequently yields a contour deformity characterized by anterior chest hollowing and exacerbated lateral chest rolls. We present novel use of a single-stage technique that repurposes the tissue that would otherwise form a “dog ear” deformity as a robust, pedicled adipofascial flap for immediate autologous reconstruction. This approach synergistically resolves the dichotomy between oncologic safety and aesthetic outcomes by simultaneously achieving complete resection, obliterating the mastectomy defect, and eliminating lateral chest rolls.Keywords: Oncoplastic surgery, Chest masculinization, Transgender health, Gender-affirming surgery, High-risk breast cancer, Lateral thoracic flap

Gender-affirming mastectomy is predicated on sculpting a masculine chest contour to alleviate gender dysphoria, resulting in profound improvements in psychosocial well-being.1 However, a central clinical challenge arises when this aesthetic objective must be reconciled with the imperatives of oncologic extirpation. For the emerging population of high-risk transgender men—individuals with pathogenic variants in genes such as BRCA1/2, PALB2, ATM, or CHEK2—management presents a difficult choice between lifelong high-risk surveillance and risk-reducing mastectomy.2

Standard gender-affirming mastectomy, which prioritizes contour by preserving some glandular tissue, is insufficient for definitive risk reduction. This necessitates a lifetime of enhanced screening, often including annual MRIs—a process psychologically incongruous with the patient’s goal of alleviating chest-related gender dysphoria. Thus, a complete risk-reducing mastectomy is often the only path to both safety and freedom from surveillance. This mandate creates a significant aesthetic conflict, particularly in obese patients, where total glandular extirpation frequently results in a concave, hollowed anterior chest and the exacerbation of redundant lateral chest rolls.3 Herein, we describe novel use of a technique that repurposes this lateral excess as an autologous flap for immediate reconstruction.

Technique and illustrative case

We applied this technique to a 27-year-old transgender man (BMI 40) identified with a pathogenic CHEK2 mutation who elected for definitive risk-reducing mastectomy. Preoperative evaluation focused on the anticipated central concavity and the volume of lateral chest rolls. Markings followed standard gender-affirming incisions, but the lateral extension was designed to encompass the full extent of the excess thoraco-adipose tissue (Figure 1).

Preoperative frontal and lateral views demonstrating breast volume, lateral chest rolls, and surgical markings for the mastectomy and planned flap.

The operation commenced with a complete oncologic mastectomy, ensuring extirpation of all at-risk glandular tissue and pectoralis fascia (Figure 2). Crucially, the lateral extension was not excised. Instead, this pre-marked excess was elevated as a thick, fasciocutaneous flap based on a robust pedicle of intercostal artery perforators. Dissection proceeded from lateral to medial off the serratus fascia until perforators were identified just lateral to the pectoralis border.

depicts the intraoperative view after completion of the left total mastectomy, showing the defect with the pectoralis major muscle exposed.

The flap was de-epithelialized (Figure 3) and rotated medially into the mastectomy defect, securing it to the pectoralis muscle to obliterate the concavity. The flap size was estimated at 150–200 g This simultaneously reconstructed the anterior defect and eliminated the lateral chest rolls. The donor site was closed primarily. Postoperatively, the patient had an uncomplicated recovery with drain removal on day 6. At three months, he reported high satisfaction. Physical examination demonstrated a stable, masculine contour with correction of the anticipated concavity and elimination of lateral rolls (Figure 4).

Three-month postoperative result. The frontal and lateral views show a stable, masculine chest contour and well-healed nipple grafts. The technique successfully mitigated a significant central concavity and eliminated the prominent lateral chest rolls, yielding an aesthetic outcome with which the patient reported high satisfaction.

Discussion

While lateral intercostal and lateral thoracic perforator flaps are well established in reconstructive surgery, this study describes their novel application as local adipofascial flaps for contour optimization in oncologic chest masculinization. The significance of this technique lies in its capacity to resolve the inherent dichotomy between oncologic safety and aesthetic masculinization. By synergistically integrating an immediate lateral thoracic adipofascial flap with complete mastectomy, it functions as a single-stage, definitive procedure. The patient is no longer forced to compromise between an oncologically sound operation that risks deformity and an aesthetic approach that is oncologically incomplete.

A notable component is the conceptual reframing of the lateral chest roll, applying principles from post-bariatric body contouring.4 Whereas standard techniques view this excess as a “dog ear” requiring excision or secondary revision, our approach proactively reconceptualizes it as a primary reconstructive asset. This was borne out in our case, where the repurposing of local tissue provided a robust, vascularized volume replacement.

This approach offers distinct advantages over autologous fat grafting. While fat grafting is a viable alternative, it is limited by unpredictable resorption rates often necessitating multiple stages, and the potential for fat necrosis to create diagnostic artifacts on future imaging—a salient concern in this high-risk population. In contrast, the adipofascial flap provides stable, immediate volume. Furthermore, should secondary fat grafting be desired, this flap provides a vascularized recipient bed. It is worth noting that this technique also holds potential for cisgender mastectomy care, aligning with the increasing interest in “aesthetic flat closure.”

By transforming a purely ablative procedure into a single-stage reconstruction, this approach ensures that gender affirmation and oncologic safety are not mutually exclusive but are achieved synergistically. While this report demonstrates feasibility, future investigation incorporating patient-reported outcome measures, such as the BODY-Q, will be critical for validating this therapeutic paradigm.
