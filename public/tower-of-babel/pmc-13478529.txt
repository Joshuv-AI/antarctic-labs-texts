Post-wildfire housing recovery simulation via an agent-based model
Authors: Rodrigo Costa, Ali Nejat, Sara Hamideh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs climate change increases the frequency and severity of disasters, proactive planning for post-disaster housing recovery is essential to mitigate long-term social and economic disruption. Computational models can support this planning by simulating potential recovery trajectories, yet many existing approaches are limited by overwhelming data requirements or narrow applicability to past events. Here, we introduce RAAbIT (Recovery Assessment using Agent-based Tools), a novel agent-based model designed to simulate housing recovery using data available within weeks of a disaster. RAAbIT models individual households, insurers, and contractors as agents governed by empirical behavior rules, and incorporates modifiable system-level constraints, such as contractor availability, to reflect context-specific recovery dynamics. We demonstrate the model’s utility by hindcasting two California wildfires–the 2017 Tubbs Fire in Santa Rosa and the 2018 Camp Fire in Paradise–and capturing their divergent recovery trajectories. Despite similar hazards, the two communities experienced significantly different reconstruction outcomes, with Santa Rosa rebuilding 57% of destroyed homes and Paradise only 9% within five years. RAAbIT can reproduce temporal and spatial patterns of recovery observed in building permit and construction data. By balancing generalizability with data realism, RAAbIT provides a flexible and transferable tool for post-disaster recovery planning, supporting more effective decision-making under uncertainty and enhancing community resilience in the face of escalating climate risks.Subject terms: Climate sciences, Developing world, Environmental studies, Natural hazards

Introduction

Proactive planning for post-disaster housing recovery is critical to mitigating the long-term social and economic consequences of disasters, particularly as climate change exacerbates the frequency, scale, and unpredictability of extreme natural events. Ad hoc recovery efforts often result in delayed reconstruction, increased displacement, and a deepening of existing inequalities1. For example, recovery from the 2018 Camp Fire in California was hindered by a lack of affordable and temporary housing, resulting in prolonged displacement. Many low-income and elderly residents were unable to return because of high reconstruction costs and inadequate insurance coverage. Moreover, the failure to integrate long-term housing planning with disaster preparedness limited the community’s capacity to manage residual risk, leaving it vulnerable to future events. The shortcomings of ad hoc recovery management can be mitigated with pre-disaster planning, enabling governments and communities to establish clear recovery frameworks, streamline resource allocation, and prioritize the needs of marginalized populations, thereby reducing recovery time and mitigating future risks.

However, planning for disaster recovery is challenging because it requires predicting the uncertain impacts and the effectiveness of recovery and mitigation actions. While lessons learned from previous disasters can be helpful in this planning, their effectiveness is limited for possible but previously unseen events. In this context, computational simulations of post-disaster recovery have been proposed as a tool to highlight unforeseen long-term consequences of disasters and support decision-making2,3. These models facilitate ‘what-if’ analysis under simulated futures, allowing decision-makers to understand extreme impact values and tipping points for recovery. Simulations can provide insights into post-disaster needs and help allocate resources effectively, facilitating the development of a roadmap to guide short-term relief and long-term action.

Disaster recovery is a complex, non-linear, spatially distributed process that comprises multiple heterogeneous physical and societal systems and agents with bounded rationality (i.e., they do not have perfect information nor make optimal decisions). Consequently, it is difficult to simulate this problem using approaches based on general equilibrium, graph theory, or optimization. Recent developments in this area have highlighted the use of agent-based models (ABMs) due to their versatility4. Agent-based models represent individual agents – such as people or organizations – with defined behavioral rules that interact within a specified environment. These models are used to explore how macro-level patterns and dynamics emerge from micro-level interactions, often providing insight into complex, adaptive systems5. Although ABMs tend to be complex to build and explain, be computationally expensive, and require more granular data, they excel at simulating complex, heterogeneous, non-linear, spatial problems and do not rely on strict boundary conditions6,7.

In the last decade, agent-based models have been developed to study disaster recovery under different hazards [e.g.,8–14]. These efforts demonstrated how the bottom-up nature of ABMs can offer insights into recovery inequalities by disaggregating community-level recovery trends into results by building type, household characteristics, neighborhood and others. They also showed how ABMs enable investigation of whether recovery outcomes would have improved if decision-makers had acted differently during the recovery process (e.g., by allocating federal funds differently). However, existing models have limitations. Most existing models focus solely on replicating housing reconstruction rates [e.g.,8,10,11], with minimal attention to other important planning variables, such as the breakdown of financing and the spatial distribution of recovery. These metrics are essential to anticipating inequalities in recovery. Another limitation is regarding contractor availability - a critical factor in housing recovery simulation15. Existing models simulate the availability of contractors as binary, e.g., available or not11,12, or as a function of time, e.g., time for contractor mobilization is an independent and identically distributed random variable [e.g.,8,11]. Lastly, and arguably more importantly, previous efforts have developed ABMs for data-rich environments, such as past events or virtual testbeds16. In these contexts, developers can rely on agency reports and labor statistics as inputs to their models. While a valid exercise, this approach underestimates the complexities of obtaining reliable data shortly after a disaster, thereby limiting the applicability of these models for post-disaster recovery planning, where these data have not yet been compiled. These limitations stem primarily from the guiding principles behind the development of these models: they were designed as proofs of concept rather than as planning tools.

This study presents a novel agent-based model for simulating disaster housing recovery called RAAbIT (Recovery Assessment using Agent-based Tools). RAAbIT possesses the capabilities of predecessor models; however, it is developed specifically as a tool for early-recovery planning. That is, it is designed to be deployed after a disaster, demand a modest set of inputs, and generate actionable outputs that can inform decisions in the early stage of the recovery process. We consulted recovery planning documents developed by US state housing agencies to determine that the number of homes impacted and the extent of damage can be realistically estimated within weeks of a disaster. RAAbIT also requires American Community Survey statistics and insurance penetration rates from the previous year, which we expect to be available based on precedents we examined. To model agents’ behaviours using historical data, we rely on publicly available datasets to develop conditional probability models. For variables that we cannot confidently model due to a lack of publicly available data for a specific disaster event, we include ’levers’ in the model based on empirical observations of past disasters. These levers (e.g., contractor availability) allow users to control aspects of the simulation to best reflect their expectations for the community’s recovery process (e.g., whether recovery is expected to be limited by contractor availability). While housing recovery is intertwined with the recovery of other community functions (e.g., infrastructure, services), limiting RAAbIT’s scope to housing allows us to work with fewer variables and better understand emerging behaviors, a challenge in ABM.

The consequence of these design decisions is that all sub-models in RAAbIT are event-agnostic, and most are hazard-agnostic. For example, sub-models representing funding agencies were developed using data from multiple disasters spanning two decades, rather than from a single disaster. Thus, RAAbIT estimates the recovery financing breakdown rather than requiring it as an input. Consequently, RAAbIT offers unparalleled granularity for testing the effects of specific, realistic policy changes (e.g., reducing the interest rate on Federal loans or increasing the cap on Federal grants). Another advantage of RAAbIT is that it simulates the competition for skilled workers in the community as a zero-sum game that accounts for the behaviors of individual contractors. Demand is determined by households’ access to financing, and supply is determined by supply-and-demand imbalances and does not require users to input the availability of contractors over time within the community. Thus, RAAbIT avoids the extremes of overly simplistic abstractions (common in early ABMs) or overly complex, case-bound models. By grounding agents in empirical rules while keeping data requirements modest, it enables more analysis of what could have been done to improve recovery in a past event and supports recovery decisions that have not yet been made.

To demonstrate RAAbIT’s application, we present two case studies in which the model was used to hindcast recovery in Santa Rosa (CA) after the 2017 Tubbs Fire and in Paradise (CA) after the 2018 Camp Fire. Although the triggering hazard type is the same, the immediate impact and recovery from these disasters differ substantially. Table 1 presents aggregated statistics for each region, highlighting differences in demographics and extent of damage. The October 2017 Tubbs Fire destroyed 5,636 structures17, including 3040 homes in Santa Rosa–approximately 5% of the city’s housing inventory18. FEMA issued a Presidential Major Disaster Declaration following the wildfire event (FM-5215-CA)19, and the direct losses across Sonoma County, where Santa Rosa is located, were estimated to be $7.9 billion20. The November 2018 Camp Fire burned roughly 18,000 structures near Paradise and Concow – approximately 95% of the cities’ housing stock21. FEMA issued Major Disaster Declaration FM-5278-CA22 in response to the fire. In addition to structural losses, the Camp Fire severely affected infrastructure and disrupted basic services in Paradise, including the water and power supply and access to healthcare. The claims filed for direct losses were estimated at $8.5 billion by the California State Insurance Commissioner23. These case studies are selected because empirical recovery data are available and because the extent and outcomes of the recovery process differ across these communities. While Santa Rosa rebuilt 57% of the destroyed housing stock by October 2022 (i.e., within five years of the Tubbs Fire), when data were collected, Paradise had rebuilt only 9% of its destroyed homes during this period (i.e., within four years of the Camp Fire).

We quantify the reconstruction of single-family housing 60 months after the Tubbs Fire and 47 months after the Camp Fire. We assume one building per parcel and use these terms interchangeably. The data used to validate the analysis include the recovery plans developed by the California Department of Housing and Community Development24,25, the locations of buildings destroyed by the fires, and the dates of construction permit applications for buildings that have begun reconstruction. Permit data were collected via personal communication with city officials and data portals for disaster recovery for Santa Rosa26 and Paradise27. Although each region tracks different milestones in the recovery process, they provide dates for both the initial construction permit application and the completion of construction, which we use for subsequent analysis. These data did not include buildings that had not applied for a permit. Our subsequent analysis must be interpreted with this limitation in mind. When a building had a permit application date but no construction completion date, we assumed that the building had not yet finished reconstruction. Results

The following analyses investigate RAAbIT’s ability to hindcast the empirical results in Fig. 1. In these simulations, only data that would have been available shortly after the disaster are input into RAAbIT (e.g., the number of destroyed buildings and Census data from the previous year). Multiple scenarios are simulated, in which a few model parameters are modified to elucidate each parameter’s influence on RAAbIT’s estimates. For each scenario, 1,000 realizations (i.e., model runs) are generated to simulate housing recovery and capture uncertainties. In each realization, random variables dictate the agents’ behaviors and decisions.

RAAbIT average housing recovery curves match benchmark data

The first analysis examines how assumptions about contractor availability over time and delays in the reconstruction decision affect RAAbIT’s simulation results. Starting with the effect of contractor availability, the results for Santa Rosa are summarized in Fig. 2. Figure 2a shows the results obtained if uncertainty about contractor availability is significantly reduced. To obtain these results, we calculated the number of building completions per 90-day period and used this time series to estimate the number of contractors in the community. RAAbIT’s results align with empirical values, highlighting the model’s performance as uncertainty about contractor availability decreases. However, the contractor availability data would be unavailable in a real-world application. To provide flexibility, RAAbIT includes two other alternative models for contractor availability.

(a and d) use empirically-estimated time series of available contractors. (b and e) use a randomly selected constant number of available contractors. (c and f) use supply-demand relationships to dynamically estimate contractor availability.

The results in Fig. 2b assume contractor availability is constant during the simulated period. Under this assumption, a fixed number of Local Contractor Agents are responsible for reconstruction, and no Out-of-town Contractor Agents are present in the simulation. The number of individuals working in the construction sector in the year before the disaster is obtained from the Bureau of Labor Statistics https://www.bls.gov/oes/current/oes_ca.htm (last accessed February 11, 2025) and used as a reference value. In each realization, this reference value is multiplied by a uniformly random number between 0.5 and 1.5 to add variability. The mean results for this scenario, shown as a solid red line, indicate that RAAbIT estimates align relatively well with empirical results under this modeling assumption. However, the arbitrarily selected uncertainty bounds (i.e., 0.5 and 1.5) add substantial uncertainty that is not minimized over time.

The last modeling alternative in RAAbIT is to use the supply-demand relationship. In this case, the recovery will be entirely driven by Out-of-town Contractor Agents attracted to the community due to a surge in demand. Contractors’ willingness to come is a value between 0 (i.e., none comes) and 1 (i.e., one contractor comes per unit of unmet demand). At the beginning of each realization, one value from this interval is chosen and held constant. The results in Fig. 2c indicate that this modeling approach approximates the pace of recovery over time well, and uncertainty is reduced over time, yielding more accurate results at the five-year mark. The subsequent analyses rely on this assumption because it better aligns with the empirical results and eliminates the need to estimate the number of contractors in the community prior to the disaster.

The simulations described above are repeated for Paradise in Fig. 2d,e,f. The key difference in Paradise is that RAAbIT underestimates the onset of the recovery process when employing constant and supply-demand dynamic assumptions for contractor availability. The Camp Fire’s impact on Paradise was devastating, resulting in the loss of most homes and infrastructure. Consequently, the decision-making process for Paradise homeowners to rebuild was expected to be more complex than that for a disaster with a more limited geographic scope. As such, the contribution of is likely underestimated by RAAbIT’s default assumption for cases similar to Paradise Camp Fire (e.g., ). A more sophisticated assumption for would shift the start of the recovery to the right and better approximate the empirical curve. This highlights the importance of assessing and incorporating the community and disaster context when employing RAAbIT.

Employing the supply-demand relationship to model contractor availability (i.e., Fig. 2c,f), we test how three assumptions regarding decision delays affect recovery. In the first assumption, households will start their rebuilding process once they secure financing, or days after the disaster. The other two assumptions address cases in which financing is not the only constraint; for example, households may wait for clear indications that rebuilding is the best alternative for them. Capturing spatial recovery patterns requires integrating other models

The final verification is whether simulation results can capture spatial patterns in recovery. Spatial patterns are expected in housing recovery due to uneven distribution of damages, resources, and demographics [e.g.,28,29]. However, these spatial dependencies are likely strongly tied to the context of community and disaster, making it challenging to develop a model that can generally capture these patterns. In RAAbiT, the only variable that affects this pattern is the behavior of the contractor agents. As discussed in Methodology Section, three modeling alternatives are available to simulate how contractors prioritize the jobs they accept: urban density, first-come-first-served, or random. Figure 5a–c shows the results obtained with each modeling approach for Santa Rosa. The plots compare the spatial lag of the empirical recovery time (ordinate) with that of the simulated results (abscissa). Each dot represents a building. For a building at location l, the spatial lag is calculated as the average recovery time for the five closest buildings to l. The agreement between empirical and simulated results is indicated by the dots aligning along the red dashed line, and it can be measured using a linear correlation metric, such as Pearson’s correlation coefficient, ρ. While none of the modeling approaches yields close to perfect correlation (i.e., 1), assuming that contractor agents prefer to work in denser areas substantially increases the model’s capacity to capture spatial patterns in housing recovery in Santa Rosa, reflecting the faster recovery in the denser southwest portion of the impacted area as shown in Fig. 1b.

Estimated spatial correlation of recovery times for Santa Rosa (a–c) and Paradise (d–f). (a and d), Assumes contractors prioritize jobs in denser areas. (b and e), Assumes contractors prioritize jobs based on a first-come-first-served basis. (c and f), Assumes contractors randomly select among the potential jobs.

Figure 5d–f presents the same estimates for Paradise. Unlike in Santa Rosa, RAAbIT cannot capture the spatial correlation in Paradise’s recovery under any assumptions. Recovery in Paradise was slow and spatially uneven due to a set of interlinked conditions. The cleanup process included a hazard tree program that removed nearly 90,000 hazardous trees across nearly 8700 parcels, leaving some parcels inaccessible longer than others30. There was widespread contamination of the public drinking water distribution system with volatile organic compounds, including benzene and other combustion byproducts, which delayed safe re-occupancy in particular areas31. Plans to improve infrastructure systems added uncertainty in the recovery process. The city developed a 5-year plan to bury the power distribution lines and planned to create a sewer service area along segments of Skyway Road and Clark Road32. The recovery patterns in Fig. 1 partially align with these infrastructure plans. Improving these results would require including these context-specific spatial constraints into the model. For specific applications (i.e., intentionally overfitting the model to one application), this could be achieved by incorporating a spatially aware decision delay into the model.

Assumptions regarding repair time and insurance coverage have the highest impact on RAAbIT’s results

To investigate the stability of RAAbiT’s results due to changes in its parameters, we conduct an output-based and a variance-based sensitivity analysis for two of the model’s output metrics. First, we examine how changes in parameters affect the area under the recovery curve (AUC) (e.g., Fig. 2). The AUC captures the shape of the curve. Compared with simpler metrics such as the number of rebuilt homes after y years, AUC provides insight into whether recovery speed is affected by changes in model parameters. The second metric used in the sensitivity analysis is the demand for CDBG-DR dollars. CDBG-DR funding is contingent on other funding sources; therefore, it is a useful metric for evaluating the sensitivity of the results in Fig. 4 to changes in the model’s parameters. We conducted the output-based sensitivity analyses using a one-factor-at-a-time (OAT) approach. OAT is an intuitive method for local sensitivity analysis, but it has limitations in capturing global effects33. Hence, we complement the analyses with variance-based sensitivity analysis using Sobol indices, capturing first-order and total-order (global) effects34,35. The variance-based analysis uses n=1024 repetitions, resulting in 13,312 model runs per case study, per metric (i.e., 53,248 runs in total).

We include 11 parameters in the sensitivity analyses: private loan gross-debt-to-income ratio requirement (Bank GDSR), private loan interest rate (Bank interest), private loan disbursement time median (Bank time), CDBG-DR disbursement time median (CDBG-DR time), percent of households that apply for FEMA assistance (FEMA applicants), FEMA grants disbursement time (FEMA time), insurance disbursement time median (Insurance time), percent of uninsured individuals (Percent uninsured), repair time median (Repair time), percent of households that apply for SBA loans (SBA applicants), SBA loans disbursement time median (SBA time).

Figure 6 summarizes the sensitivity analysis results. The AUC-related results, shown in Fig. 6a,b,e,f, highlight the model’s sensitivity to assumptions about building repair time. The OAT output analysis indicated that a 20% change in average repair time resulted in a corresponding change in median AUC of approximately 10%. The variance-based results yield first-order Sobol indices of 0.95 and 0.94 for AUC in Santa Rosa and Paradise, respectively (i.e., 95% and 94% of the variance in the results is attributable to changes in average repair time). Similarly, we obtained total-order Sobol indices of 0.96 and 0.94 (first-order and total-order results overlap in Fig. 6b,f), indicating minor interaction effects between repair time and other variables. The results for other parameters are consistent across the two case studies and indicate minor sensitivities in the AUC.

Model’s sensitivity analysis for for Santa Rosa (a–d) and Paradise (e–h). Plots (a, c, e, and g) show results for an output-based assessment. Plots (b, d, f, and h) show results for a variance-based assessment. Plots (a, b, e, and f) assess sensitivities in the area under the recovery curve, while (c, d, g, and h) evaluate sensitivities in the demand for CDBG-DR funding.

Sensitivities in the estimated CDBG-DR funding demand were driven by three parameters: the percent of uninsured individuals, the percent of households that apply for SBA loans, and the percent of households that apply for FEMA assistance. These results are consistent in Santa Rosa and Paradise, as well as across the output- and variance-based analyses. Consistent with expectations, the demand for CDBG-DR funding is not sensitive to any time-related parameter. Total-order Sobol indices are noticeably larger than first-order indices for the three parameters, indicating that simultaneous changes in these parameters magnify their effect on the model’s variance. We highlight that insurance information can be based on the previous year’s data, and that FEMA and SBA applicants’ data can be obtained shortly after a disaster, since the number of applicants, not the outcome of the application, is required. Thus, we anticipate that uncertainty regarding these parameters can be eliminated through effective inter-organizational communication.

Simulations provide insights on how housing recovery can be improved

Finally, we discuss how RAAbIT can deepen our understanding of recovery by evaluating the effects of ’what-if’ scenarios. Figure 7 shows the mean results from 1000 realizations under the different assumptions. The black lines represent the baseline simulated results, i.e., the red lines in Fig. 2c–f. The blue line represents a scenario in which rebuild times do not exceed 1 year. This is achieved by assuming a zero probability that homes will take longer than 13 months to repair and re-normalizing the probabilities. This can be implemented in practice by improving supply chains and expediting the occupancy permitting process. The yellow line shows a scenario where the city can attract more out-of-town contractors, i.e., R in Eq. (22) is R ∼ U(0.5,1), instead of R ∼ U(0.25,0.75) in the baseline scenario. Finally, the red curve combines the improvements in take-up rates and contractor availability. Increasing contractor availability expedites recovery but does not affect the number of rebuilt buildings in the long term. While reducing rebuild times and improving contractor availability confer benefits, implementing both simultaneously yields synergistic effects, resulting in a more expedited and robust recovery process. For example, under the scenario with both improvements, there are nearly 75% more rebuilt single-family houses two years after each disaster. While the results Fig. 7 reflect how recovery could have been different in a past event, a similar exercise during recovery planning for a new event could evaluate the potential benefits of recovery-improving decisions, reducing uncertainty for decision-makers.

Discussion

We demonstrate that our proposed model can accurately approximate empirical housing recovery trends following two wildfires. Beyond estimating recovery trends under the status quo of funding programs and recovery service provider timelines, access to a housing recovery model enables decision-makers to assess the potential benefits and drawbacks of various recovery-enhancing strategies. For example, after the 2017 Tubbs Fire, the HCD decided to use CDBG-DR funds to provide grants of up to $150,000. However, after the 2018 Camp Fire, this grant’s cap was $200,000. A practical application of RAAbIT could be to forecast recovery trends in the 2025 Los Angeles Fires and investigate whether recovery can be enhanced if an eventual CDBG-DR allocation is used to provide $150,000 to more individuals or $200,000 to potentially fewer households. In this context, RAAbIT can inform decisions and help minimize the long-term impacts of delayed and inefficient resource allocations. Accurate estimates of recovery progress can also help communities prepare for increased demand for skilled workers or materials, allowing them to train professionals locally if a shortage is expected and establish supply chain contracts for the provision of reconstruction materials.

Another practical use of RAAbIT is to estimate unmet housing needs to inform CDBG-DR funding allocation under limited information. Currently, estimates of unmet needs rely on processing FEMA IA and SBA HPPL applications, which can take several months, as well as on data from insurance claims that state housing authorities may not readily access. Moreover, individuals who do not apply for FEMA assistance are not accounted for in HUD’s estimates of unmet housing needs. Incorrect estimates of unmet housing needs have led to delays in implementing CDBG-DR programs in past events [e.g.,36]. As shown in Fig. 4, RAAbIT’s estimates of the required CDBG-DR, which can be obtained as soon as the number of damaged buildings is determined, align well with the revised calculations conducted by HCD about several years after the Tubbs and Camp fires, when better data were available. This indicates RAABiT’s potential to save valuable time by providing more accurate unmet needs assessments earlier.

The models and applications discussed in this study, as shown in Fig. 8, primarily focus on a US application. However, RAAbIT is versatile and can be adapted to other contexts. An application in another context (e.g., Canada) could be achieved by replacing the US-specific agents (e.g., FEMA, SBA, and HUD) with agents representing local financing organizations (e.g., Public Safety Canada). Moreover, RAAbIT is hazard-agnostic as long as structural damage is the main driver of repair time, e.g., earthquakes and wind hazards. This may not hold in applications where repairs are a consequence of water damage, e.g., mold remediation instead of structural repairs. In addition, the Finance Provider Agents may vary depending on the hazard. For example, for applications in the US, flood insurance is provided primarily by FEMA through the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP), which will require the inclusion of a new NFIP Agent in RAAbIT. This agent would interact with Household Agents similarly to the Insurance Company Agent, but may have different attributes, e.g., the NFIP has a coverage cap. These contextual complexities in housing recovery make it impossible to develop a single generic model. However, we believe that RAAbIT provides a solid foundation for simulating these multi-agent interactions, which can be adapted to other contexts with minor modifications, e.g., by including/excluding a few agents. Lastly, RAAbIT can be easily integrated into workflows with other software dedicated to estimating regional disaster losses [e.g.,37]. We anticipate that this versatility will be a key factor in the adoption of RAAbIT.

Overview of agents in RAAbiT.

While we strive to develop an accurate model in this study, any computational model incurs simplifications. Post-disaster housing recovery is a complex, nonlinear problem, and predicting exact outcomes may be unrealistic, especially at the household level. Despite the good performance shown in this study, we argue that models such as RAAbIT are best suited for relative comparisons of trends across ’what-if’ scenarios. Consider the example above, in which RAAbIT is used to forecast recovery following the 2025 Los Angeles Fires, and investigate the optimal cap for CDBG-DR grants. RAAbIT’s limitations would be a common factor in the $150,000 and $200,000 cap scenarios. Thus, the impact of these limitations on the relative simulation results (e.g., ratios) is minimized, making the relative results a more robust metric.

Despite our efforts to collect empirical data to validate our model, we acknowledge limitations in the validation process. The first issue is that the permit data used for validation includes only buildings that had applied for a reconstruction permit at the time the data were collected. Thus, our data likely overrepresents buildings with a less challenging housing recovery process. Understanding the decision-making process of homeowners who did not rebuild requires a different type of model that focuses on individuals rather than on buildings. Another issue concerns mismatches in official data reports. For example, the figures for FEMA assistance reported by HCD and FEMA diverge. Thus, it is challenging to confidently determine benchmark values that indicate the accuracy of our model. In cases of divergence, we adopted the values closest to the source, for example, FEMA in the case of FEMA assistance, but acknowledge that this is a limitation.

Another limitation of our study is that RAAbIT, in its current implementation, lacks variables that capture spatial correlations in the housing recovery process. As a process, housing recovery is closely intertwined with the recovery of infrastructure and social networks, community services (e.g., schools, healthcare, employment opportunities), and macroeconomic and political factors. By simulating housing recovery as a resource-constrained, homeowner-driven process, RAAbIT does not fully capture some of these nuances, particularly in building-level results, as shown in Fig. 5. However, as shown in this study, this approach can capture aggregate recovery metrics useful for planning recovery at the community level.

Methods

Figure 8 presents an overview of the agents in RAAbIT and their interactions. Household Agents are the drivers of the simulation. These agents represent households whose homes were affected by the disaster and who wish to rebuild. For certain housing types, the reconstruction of the housing unit and the household’s post-disaster state may be decoupled (e.g., rental housing). RAAbIT does not simulate this process. Hence, the Household Agents in RAAbIT are better interpreted as the household responsible for the costs of the building reconstruction - i.e., homeowners of owner-occupied homes or landlords. The first step in the reconstruction process for Household Agents is to secure the required financing. To do so, they interact with finance provider agents. The primary attributes of finance provider agents include their approval criteria, maximum amounts, and disbursement time. The approval criteria depend on the requesting households’ socioeconomic status and losses, which may lead to inequalities in access to financing. Household Agents may interact with one or multiple finance provider agents. In Fig. 8, finance provider agents represent organizations that exist in the US. The types of existing finance provider agents may change for applications outside the US. If a Household Agent obtains sufficient funds, they procure a contractor to conduct the physical repairs or reconstruction of the housing unit. In RAAbIT, the Job Market Agent tracks the supply and demand for contractors. This agent can create Contractor Agents when demand exceeds supply (e.g., a contractor arrives in the community) or remove Contractor Agents when supply exceeds demand (e.g., a contractor leaves the community). Service provider agents possess behaviors enabling them to prioritize specific households and benefit themselves, creating inequalities in the recovery process. While the behaviors of Finance Provider Agents are primarily adopted from previous work, Household and Service Provider Agents and their interactions are novel in this study. The following sections provide a detailed description of these agents.

Finance provider agents

RAAbIT includes multiple agents representing public and private organizations that can provide Homeowner Agents funding. The behaviors of the Finance Provide Agents mimic the criteria employed by their real-world counterparts to provide funds to a given homeowner. The approval probability and the amount received from each Finance Provider Agent are functions of the losses and attributes of the Homeowner Agents. Homeowner Agents interact with Finance Provider Agents sequentially; e.g., they wait for a decision from a grant agency before seeking a loan. It is assumed that homeowners prefer to seek funding from grants and low-interest loans before committing to market-rate loans. Some Finance Provider Agents are not activated for several months after the disaster. In this case, Homeowner Agents who can obtain funding from other sources, even at a higher cost, will not wait. Insurance company agent

The Insurance Company Agent represents the insurance market in the community. Materials provider agents

RAAbIT does not include a model to account for delays due to construction materials shortages. That is, by default, in Eq. (1), what we expect underestimates reality. Properly estimating would require simulating the supply chain for various materials and household reconstruction decisions (e.g., rebuild to the pre-disaster standard or not). Disasters can disrupt supply-demand patterns, affect community access, and alter supply chains, leading to changes in the types of materials used. This makes pre-disaster data a limited predictor of post-disaster demand. In the absence of a detailed model of material availability, RAAbIT offers users the ability to input a probability distribution that represents the expected delays due to material shortages. We believe that data from previous disasters can help build such a model.

Job market and contractor agents

The Job Market Agent is a supporting agent whose main purpose is to simulate the supply and demand for Contractor Agents. Here, a Contractor Agent represents a crew of workers with the necessary skill set to rebuild a home. The Job Market Agent’s main attribute is its responsiveness, R. If the market is fully responsive, R=1, for each unit of contractor demand, one new Contractor Agent is added to the simulation. Similarly, for each unit of contractor surplus, one Contractor Agent is removed from the simulation. If R=0.5, two units of demand are required for a new contractor to be added to the simulation. If R=0, no contractors are added or removed. The Job Market Agent may also have a baseline number of contractors which permanently exist in the simulation.
