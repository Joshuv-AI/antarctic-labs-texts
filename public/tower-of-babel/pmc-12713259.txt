Decoding the dynamics of calves’ respiratory and gut microbiota: exploring stability, resistance, and individual patterns
Authors: Núria Mach, Maverick Monié--Ibanes, Fatima Z Sikht, Marie-Claude Hygonenq, Gwendoline Pot, Hortensia Robert, David Bars, Yves Farizon, Eric Richard, Xavier Nouvel, Christine Citti, Eric Baranowski, Mariette Ducatez, Gilles Meyer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe investigated the early-life interplay between microbiota, host, and environment by longitudinally monitoring nasal and gut microbiota in 30 veal calves over their first five months of life. Microbial composition and stability were highly compartmentalized. Fecal communities showed increasing biomass, diversity, and homogeneity with age. Conversely, nasal microbiota remained less diverse and highly variable between individuals, likely influenced by mucociliary clearance and transient colonization. Environmental factors, diet, and clinical indicators were associated with both microbial communities, alongside distinct cytokine profiles. As calves matured, microbiota dynamics and composition covaried across body sites, reflecting systemic immune development and environmental adaptations. This study provides a comprehensive, multi-layered characterization of microbial dynamics, identifying the first month of life as a critical window for interventions targeting the fecal microbiota. The nasal microbiota, by contrast, remains flexible and amenable to modulation throughout the production cycle.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42523-025-00494-w.

Introduction

In mammals, the microbiota comprises a dynamic community of microorganisms found in a defined environment and engaging in intricate interactions with their host [1, 2]. Advancements in multi-omics technologies have shed light on the complex molecular mechanisms that govern these microbial ecosystems, and their crucial roles in livestock health and diseases are beginning to be unraveled [3, 4]. The microbiota plays a central role in various physiological processes, including immune system regulation and maturation, vitamin production, metabolic homeostasis, mucosa development, and disease prevention [5, 6, 7, 8]. Microorganisms colonize nearly all anatomical surfaces of mammalian hosts, including the respiratory tract. In livestock species, the upper and lower respiratory tracts exhibit distinct microbial communities, characterized by differences in taxonomic composition, microbial load, and function [9].

In calves, current research on respiratory microbiota is predominantly framed within the context of the bovine respiratory disease complex (BRDC) [10]. The BRDC poses a significant challenge in beef production, particularly in intensive systems, affecting animal health and welfare, as well as public health due to the use of antibiotics [11, 12, 13]. This complex involves several viral pathogen agents, such as bovine respiratory syncytial virus (BRSV), parainfluenza-3 virus (PI-3), bovine viral diarrhea virus (BVDV), bovine herpesvirus 1 (BHV-1, also known as infectious bovine rhinotracheitis virus), bovine adenovirus (BadV), bovine coronavirus (BCoV) and influenza D virus (IDV), along with bacteria like Mycoplasma bovis, Histophilus somni, Pasteurella multocida, and Mannheimia haemolytica [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. While many studies have advanced our understanding of the role of the respiratory microbiota within the context of BRDC [21, 22, 23, 24, 16, 25, 6, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30], they have primarily focused on elucidating pathogen dynamics, dysbiosis, and host-microorganism interactions associated with disease susceptibility and progression.

The compositional development, ecological stability, and immunological imprinting of the respiratory microbiota during early-life maturation in ruminants remain poorly characterized [31, 32]. Moreover, although the concept of the gut–lung axis—a bidirectional signaling network integrating microbial, immune, and metabolic molecules between gastrointestinal and respiratory compartments—is increasingly recognized [33, 34], its functional role in shaping microbial trajectories during calf maturation has yet to be investigated. Evidence from animal and human studies suggests that these two large mucosal surfaces play a critical role in shaping host-microbe and immune interactions, with key implications for respiratory diseases such as asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and infections [33, 34, 35]. For instance, the airway and gut microbiomes can modulate immune responses at distal mucosal sites through cell-associated components and secreted bioactive molecules [36]. Microorganism-derived metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), also influence the immune system [37].

Previous studies in calves have demonstrated that oral probiotics in milk affect the gut and lung microbiota, underscoring the interconnected nature of these microbial communities [38]. Conversely, respiratory infections caused by BRSV and BCoV in calves alter the composition of the fecal microbiota and the production of SCFAs [39]. Recently, a study evaluating the respiratory and fecal microbiota of beef calves from birth to eight weeks of life reported that the abundance of respiratory Lactobacillus and fecal Oscillospiraceae UCG-005 negatively affected the abundance of two respiratory pathobionts in calves, Mannheimia and Pasteurella, suggesting that gut phylotypes may modulate respiratory bacteria in cattle [32]. Analysis of the respiratory microbiome in lung lavage samples after probiotic treatment also revealed that oral probiotics in calves influence not only the gut microbiota but also the respiratory microbiome [38].

Despite these recent findings on the gut-lung axis in calves, the effects of environmental, nutritional, and immunological pressures on the stability of respiratory and gut microbiota remain poorly understood. Stability refers to the ability of a microbial community to resist change or recover its structure and function following disturbances [40, 41]. In ecological terms, stability intrinsically encompasses both resistance and resilience [42, 43]. Precisely, resistance refers to the capacity of a microbial community to remain compositionally and functionally stable in the face of perturbations [44, 43], while resilience reflects the amount of stress or perturbation that an ecosystem can tolerate before its trajectory changes towards a different equilibrium state [43]. Whether a particular disturbance will disrupt microbial stability depends on the community’s resistance or resilience to a specific type of perturbation [42, 43].

To gain a deeper understanding of the complex dynamism of the respiratory and gut microbiota, we conducted longitudinal profiling of fecal and nasal microbial communities, integrating host physiological parameters and environmental metadata across a cohort of 30 veal calves monitored from 7 days to 5 months of age. Particular attention was given to the critical developmental windows between three and six weeks of age, a period hypothesized to represent a phase of microbial plasticity for targeted modulation of the gut microbiota [45]. The cohort consisted of female calves of similar age and predominantly of the Holstein breed, providing strong genetic and physiological uniformity among the individuals. The calves were reared under the same environmental and management conditions throughout the entire fattening period. This controlled setup ensured minimal variability, allowing us to focus on the interplay between microbiota, the host, and the environment.

Results

Experimental design and subjects

A total of 30 female veal calves (initial age: 7 ± 3 days; and body weight: 57 ± 0.61 kg) were monitored longitudinally, from 7 days (day 1 of experimentation) to 5 months of age (147 days of experimentation; Fig. 1a). We studied three sampling points within the first month (on days 1, 9, and 16) to capture a fattening period characterized by a high prevalence of respiratory and digestive illnesses and antibiotic use [21, 46].

Temporal dynamics of nasal and fecal microbiota in calves: niche-specific ecological trajectories

The 16S rRNA gene sequencing was used to analyze the microbiota profiling in the nasal cavity and feces. On average, 209,000 high-quality reads were processed per sample, with nasal samples yielding a median of 66,000 ± 93,000 reads and fecal samples producing 495,000 ± 270,000 reads. The rarefaction curves reached saturation for both nasal and fecal samples, indicating comprehensive taxa coverage in these samples (Fig. S1).

After removing singletons, we identified a total of 12,932 bacterial Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) across all 429 samples, spanning 44 different phyla, 459 families, and 1,186 genera (Supplementary Table S2). In the feces, Bacillota (50.83%) and Bacteroidota (37.16%) were the most abundant phyla (Fig. 1b). This pattern was consistent throughout the study period, except for the initial 10 days, during which Actinomycetota (5.91%) was also abundant (Supplementary Table S3). The first colonizers within the ruminant gastrointestinal tract were the Prevotellaceae (22.1% ± 11.21), Oscillospiraceae (11.4% + 5.74), Lachnospiraceae (12.4% ± 6.72), Bacteroidaceae (5.6% ± 8.64), and Bifidobacteriaceae taxa (3.5% ± 6.12), representing up to 62% of the absolute abundance. At the onset of the study, three genera, namely Bifidobacterium (15.4% ± 14.8%), Segatella (11.8% ± 12.6%), and Streptococcus (9.23% ± 11.7%), emerged as dominant in the gut microbial ecosystem. Strict anaerobes were nearly absent in the early time points but progressively increased in abundance as the gut ecosystem matured. Notably, Succinivibrio showed a dramatic rise from a mean relative abundance of 0.024% at early time points (days 0, 9, and 16) to 1.63% in later stages, representing a 66-fold increase. Similarly, Roseburia increased 47-fold (from 0.025% to 1.18%), while Treponema, UCG-005, and Prevotella rose by 8.8-, 8.7-, and 5.4-fold, respectively.

The nasal microbiota was primarily dominated by three phyla, the Pseudomonadota (32.7% ± 20.82), the Bacillota (23.4% ± 16.87), and the Mycoplasmatota (14.2% ± 21.71) (Fig. 1c; Supplementary Table S3). At the genus level, Mycoplasma (32.4% ± 28.1), Burkholderia-Caballeronia-Paraburkh (18.3% ± 9.61), and Leifsonia (4.89% ± 4.06) emerged as the most abundant taxa in the first weeks of live (Fig. 1d). Over time, a marked decline in Mycoplasma spp. was observed, with relative abundance falling below 3% at the end of the monitoring period. Conversely, Pasteurella, initially present at negligible levels, underwent a substantial expansion, reaching 7.7% in late-stage samples, representing a 66-fold increase in abundance. Bifidobacterium also expanded substantially, with almost a 3-fold increase (from 1.70 to 4.62%).

Consistent with the different developmental paths of the two niches, fecal bacterial loads, measured as Avogadro-scaled estimates from 16S rRNA gene copy numbers, grew steadily from the first month of life, in sharp contrast to the nasal microbiota (Kruskal-Wallis test, P = 3.87 × 10⁻¹⁶; Fig. 2a). By later stages, the fecal microbiota showed a biomass approximately 100-fold higher than that of the nasal microbiota. Considering the potential bias that can arise from comparing the taxonomic composition of microbial communities that differ so much in their microbial load, we complemented compositional analysis with absolute quantification [47, 48, 49, 50]. Briefly, relative abundances obtained from 16S rRNA gene sequencing were normalized to the total number of 16S rRNA gene copies per sample, as quantified via quantitative PCR (qPCR) using universal primers targeting the V3–V4 region. By multiplying the relative abundance of each taxon by the corresponding bacterial load, we derived taxon-specific absolute abundances.

Based on these absolute abundance estimates, ordination analysis revealed clear separation between nasal and fecal communities (Bray-Curtis, Permutational Multivariate analysis of Variance (PerMANOVA): R² = 0.17161, Fₙicₕₑ = 94.95, P < 0.001; Fig. 2b). Moreover, we observed significantly divergent temporal patterns between the nasal and fecal microbiota (PerMANOVA, R² = 0.06928, Ftime = 5.4761, P < 0.001; Fig. 2c). As calves matured, the fecal microbiota showed progressively higher richness (Fig. 2d). Consistent with its ecological context, the nasal microbiota exhibited low diversity and high unevenness throughout the study period (Fig. 2de), with early-life communities dominated by few genera, most notably Mycoplasma. However, overall sparsity, reflecting the proportion of zero counts across taxa, did not differ significantly between compartments (Wilcoxon test, W = 25090, P = 0.09; Fig. 2g).

We then identified the core microbiota in our cohort, which consisted of taxa present in at least 95% of individuals. In total, 69 highly conserved core genera were detected in feces, accounting for ~ 86.52% of the overall absolute abundance (Supplementary Table S4). Bifidobacterium, Faecalibacterium, Lactobacillus, and Butyricicoccus occupied central positions within this core, all of which are known producers or modulators of SCFAs [51, 8]. Among the 10 most abundant core genera, half belonged to the Bacteroidales order, namely Parabacteroides, Bacteroides, Prevotella, Rikenellaceae spp, and Alloprevotella. In contrast, the nasal microbiota maintained a limited core of 19 genera across time, with dominance of Burkholderia-Caballeronia-Paraburkholderia, Mycoplasma, Leifsonia, Bifidobacterium, Caulobacter, Faecaelibacterium, and Ralstonia (Fig. 3ab; Supplementary Table S5). Prevalence-rank curves revealed distinct patterns of microbial persistence across compartments (Fig. 3c). Fecal samples showed higher prevalence among top-ranked genera, reflecting a more conserved and dominant core microbiota. In contrast, airway samples declined more steeply, indicating greater compositional variability and a less stable core (Fig. 3c).

The evolution of the fecal core microbiota over time was accompanied by significant shifts in fecal SCFA output. Acetate levels remained dominant (~ 60–70%), followed by propionate and butyrate levels (~ 10–20%), while branched-chain SCFAs (iso-butyrate, iso-valerate) remained low (< 5%; Fig. 3d). Notably, from day 30 onward, acetate levels increased (Kruskal-Wallis test, P = 9.116 × 10⁻⁴) while propionate (P = 2.399 × 10⁻⁵), iso-butyrate (P = 2.533 × 10⁻⁶), and iso-valerate (P = 4.219 × 10⁻⁶) proportions declined (Fig. 3e). These changes, along with a reduction in redox potential after the first months of age (P = 2.697 × 10⁻³; Fig. 3f), suggest a transition toward a more anaerobic and fermentative gut environment (Supplementary Table S1).

Ecological divergence of nasal vs. fecal microbiota: high stability in the gut ecosystem

We then explored two key bacterial community properties: individuality, which refers to the host-specific composition of microbial taxa shaped by niche interactions, host physiology, and environmental exposure [52]; and stability, which encompasses both resistance and resilience intrinsically [42].

Individuality was assessed through within-host temporal consistency using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), Spearman’s ρ values between consecutive time points, and ASV turnover. Stability was evaluated through between-host structural variation (ANOSIM, beta dispersion), ecological ordering via nestedness analysis, and resistance values.

The ICC, revealed that fecal samples exhibited stronger individualization at the ASV level compared to broader taxonomic ranks (Fig. 4a), highlighting fine-scale variability within the microbial community. In contrast, nasal samples exhibited uniformly low ICC values across all taxonomic ranks, suggesting higher individual variability. Then, to assess the intra-animal temporal stability of microbial communities, we compared pairwise correlations across time points within each compartment. From week 2 onwards, fecal samples consistently exhibited higher Spearman’s ρ values than nasal samples, indicating stronger temporal stability. For instance, fecal correlations increased steadily up to 0.89 (days 117–147). In contrast, nasal correlations remained low, fluctuating between 0.30 and 0.45, with no clear upward trend. The rate of complete ASV turnover over time was higher in the nasal microbiota compared to fecal samples (two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P = 0.045; Fig. 4c). Interestingly, while turnover remained high and stable in nasal samples (~ 0.87–0.90), fecal turnover declined progressively from 0.82 to 0.68 by day 147. Nestedness remained low in both compartments (< 0.07), suggesting that compositional shifts were driven by taxonomic replacement rather than ordered loss.

We then analyzed how microbial variation between individuals evolved. The nasal microbiota exhibited high inter-individual variability (Mean Bray-Curtis distance = ~ 0.85) over time. In contrast, the fecal microbiota exhibited a steady decline in dissimilarity between animals, indicating a progressive convergence toward a more stable community (Fig. 4d). This convergent pattern occurred in the absence of dietary changes, as calves were reared in closed buildings and received milk replacements throughout the fattening period. The within-group boxplots from ANOSIM analysis in feces also narrowed significantly compared to between-group variability from day 30 onwards, indicating increasing homogeneity and stability over time between individuals (ANOSIM, R = 0.606, P = 0.001, Fig. 4e). This temporal trend was less evident in the nasal microbiota (ANOSIM, R = 0.383, P = 0.001), which remained highly dynamic and variable. These findings were further supported by centroid-based dispersion metrics, where elevated distances to the centroid in nasal samples indicated persistent instability over time, whereas fecal communities showed clear signs of stabilization after the first month of life (Wilcoxon test, P < 0.001 for all time points except the first; Fig. 4f).

Lastly, complementary analysis of resistance, defined as 1 minus the mean Jaccard dissimilarity between consecutive time points, also confirmed distinct temporal patterns across compartments. The fecal microbiota showed increasing resistance over time, echoing the reduced compositional turnover and enhanced temporal consistency. Nasal communities, however, maintained lower resistance values, reflecting persistent instability and susceptibility to perturbation (Fig. 4g).

Temporal co-variation of nasal and gut microbiota during calf maturation: insights into the gut-lung axis

We next explored whether fecal and nasal microbiotas demonstrate significant co-variation in microbial composition. We first assessed the Spearman’s (ρ) values between fecal and airway microbial profiles across time points. During the first two months, correlation values remained low, indicating minimal synchrony between the two niches. However, from day 50 onward, ρ increased markedly (peaking at ~ 0.45) and then stabilized near 0.40 by day 150 (Fig. 4h), suggesting parallel ecological maturation processes or shared host- and environment-driven constraints that shape both compartments over time. Complementary to this tendency, similarity values (assessed by Bray-Curtis similarity [1-dissimilarity]) remained low (~ 0.20) during the first months (Fig. 4i). From day 30 onward, however, similarity increased progressively, reaching ~ 0.40 by day 120 (Fig. 4i).

Influence of early-life antibiotics administration and environmental variables on the nasal and fecal microbial structure

Building on the observed greater instability of the nasal microbiota compared to the gut microbiota, we examined whether environmental factors, such as weather (Fig. 5a), diet (Fig. 5b), and antibiotic administration (Fig. 5c), differentially impacted microbial dynamics.

The average air temperature during the study was 16.7 °C (range: − 3.6 °C to 36.6 °C), with humidity levels ranging from 24.5% to 96.8%. Light rainfall (< 1.41 mm/day) occurred for 66 days during the study period. The calves were fed milk from a consistent distributor, with intake increasing to nearly 15 L/day by the end of the fattening period. Their diet also included increasing amounts of texturized calf starter (up to 1 kg/day), while straw intake remained low and stable (100 g/day). During the first 30 days of life, calves received ~ 47.6 g of antibiotics through their milk. Doxycycline was administered over 8 days (11.6 g), colistin for 5 days (7.25 g total), and tilmicosin for 5 days (10 g total). Then, at 22 days post-arrival, animals received an additional day course of doxycycline (18.75 g).

Within this set of variables, the PerMANOVA model showed that collective antibiotic treatment had the strongest effects on the nasal microbiota: colistin (R² = 2.1%, F = 5.01, P = 0.001), doxycycline (R² = 1.3%, F = 3.11, P = 0.001), and tilmicosin (R² = 1.6%, F = 3.76, P = 0.001). Nutritional factors also played a role, with milk intake (R² = 2.0%, F = 4.96, P = 0.001), straw intake (R² = 1.6%, F = 3.80, P = 0.001), and relative humidity (R² = 1.11%, F = 2.70, and P = 0.001) showing significant associations. These findings were consistent with the envfit analysis, which further underscored that nasal microbial communities were sensitive to external factors such as milk (R2 = 0.59) and straw intake (R2 = 0.74), along with mean temperature (R2 = 0.53), colistin (R2 = 0.21), doxycycline (R2 = 0.13), and tilmicosin collective treatment (R2 = 0.01, Fig. 5d). In the fecal microbiota, collective antibiotic treatments had the strongest and most significant effects in the PerMANOVA model: colistin (R² = 2.11%, F = 5.11, P < 0.001), tilmicosin (R² = 1.6%, F = 3.85, P < 0.001), and doxycycline (R² = 1.33%, F = 3.22, P < 0.001). Additionally, milk intake (R² = 2.05%, F =, 4.96, P < 0.001), straw intake (R² = 1.6%, F = 3.80, P < 0.001), mean temperature (R² = 0.6%, F = 1.48, P = 0.013) and relative humidity (R² = 1.11%, F = 2.70, P < 0.001) also affected the fecal microbiota variance.

We further investigated how collective and individual antibiotic treatments shaped microbial community trajectories over time. The PCoA plots reveal clear temporal shifts in microbial composition across both gut and nasal sites (Fig. 5e). The most pronounced shifts occurred between day 0 and 30, coinciding with the period of collective antibiotic administration, suggesting that early-life antibiotic treatments exerted a strong structuring effect on microbial communities, driving initial divergence from baseline. Beyond collective treatment, individual-level antibiotic administration caused additional stratification. Animals treated individually multiple times displayed more dispersed ordination patterns, particularly in the nasal cavity and at intermediate time points (e.g., days 58, 91, and 117; Fig. 5e).

Host health and immune regulation shape fecal and nasal microbial stability

Throughout the study, calves exhibited variations in clinical parameters. Early signs of digestive and ocular disturbances were prominent but transient. Diarrhea was frequent at the beginning of the study and declined steadily over time (Fig. 6a), whereas ocular mucosa redness was initially widespread and resolved rapidly (Fig. 6b). Rectal temperature fluctuated and peaked around day 58, which correlated to the highest value of the composite clinical score (Fig. 6c–d). After two months of age, the overall incidence of illness, defined by digestive or respiratory symptoms, stabilized and gradually declined (Fig. 6e).

Gut microbial stability is closely linked to host physiology, particularly age, immune maturation, and clinical health [53, 40]. To determine the influence of host factors on both fecal and nasal microbial structuring, we integrated clinical phenotypes, physiological variables, and cytokine profiles into PerMANOVA and envfit models. According to PerMANOVA models, IFN-γ emerged as the dominant explanatory variable for feces (R² = 0.66, F = 1.42, P < 0.001) and nasal microbiota (R² = 0.68, F = 1.49, P = 0.005). IL-1α showed consistent associations in both sites (R² = 0.05 in feces and R² = 0.06 in URT, respectively, P < 0.05), while IL-6 was significant only in the gut (R² = 0.09, F = 1.37, P = 0.039. Rectal temperature correlated with microbial variation in both niches, more strongly in the nasal cavity (R² = 7.3%) than in feces (R² = 6.4%). Compartment-specific immune influences were further revealed by envfit analysis. The chemotactic and inflammatory markers—IP-10, IFN-γ, and MIP-1b—also explained substantial variance in both niches, whereas MCP-1, IL-17 α, and IL-1α were specifically associated with the fecal microbiota variation (Fig. 6f). In contrast, the nasal and fecal microbiotas were modestly influenced by clinical indicators, such as nasal discharge and overall clinical score (P < 0.05), while diarrhea, coughing, and lung sounds showed no significant associations.

To explore these host–microbiota interactions in greater depth, we examined longitudinal correlations between 11 circulating cytokines and chemokines (Fig. S1c) and microbial genera within both the fecal and nasal microbiota. This revealed 172 significant associations in feces and 238 in nasal samples (Spearman, adjusted P < 0.05; Tables S6 and S7). Redundancy Analysis (RDA) further highlighted temporal structuring. In feces, cytokines such as IFN-γ, IP-10, and MCP-1 were strongly associated with early time points, accounting for 8.19% of the total variance. Later stages were characterized by the expression of IL-1β and MIP-1β (Fig. S1d). In the nasal compartment, the cytokine-constrained RDA model explained 4.22% of microbiota variance, revealing a subtler temporal pattern.

At the genus level, the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were linked to pathogen-associated taxa in the nasal cavity, including Streptococcus, Staphylococcus, Mannheimia, and Haemophilus. Conversely, anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-10) correlated with commensals such as Roseburia, Oribacterium, Eubacterium, and Coprococcus, suggesting roles in immune modulation and epithelial homeostasis. Notably, many cytokine–genus associations were niche-specific, underscoring the localized nature of host–microbiota immune crosstalk (Fig. S1e).

Discussion

In this study, we provide a comprehensive longitudinal analysis of the nasal and fecal microbiota in young calves. By integrating date-matched microbial, environmental, and host physiological data, our findings extend previous work, which demonstrates that microbial communities in mammals are influenced by anatomical compartmentalization, host development, and environmental exposure [1, 66, 84, 52].

In ruminants, respiratory and gastrointestinal niches harbor distinct assemblages. Yet, most studies on the respiratory tract, focus on taxonomic diversity without addressing longitudinal persistence or host-specific trajectories [54, 22, 55, 24, 25, 3, 56, 26, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61, 32]. Our repeated sampling approach advances this field by capturing individualized microbial patterns and temporal dynamics.

We observed that the nasal microbiota was less diverse, more unstable, and highly individualized compared to the fecal microbiota. This challenges the Anna Karenina principle, which posits that healthy microbial communities converge toward a common structure, suggesting instead that eubiosis in the upper respiratory tract may involve a spectrum of highly individualized microbial compositions. During early life, the URT microbiota undergoes pronounced fluctuations in diversity and temporal stability [6, 4, 62, 28, 32]. These patterns are consistent with stochastic assembly processes, in which the microbial composition is shaped by random colonization events, priority effects (i.e., early-arriving taxa influencing the subsequent community structure), mucociliary clearance, and variable environmental inputs [55, 6, 4]. This is particularly relevant given evidence from human studies showing that microbial colonization of both the upper and lower airways maintains a sustained association with respiratory disease susceptibility and outcomes across the lifespan [63]. Aligning with these stochastic assembly processes, environmental filtering explained only ~ 11% of the variation in nasal microbiota. Contrary to presumptions [60, 32], the nasal community remained largely stochastic throughout the fattening period, with frequent turnover and limited persistence. These findings suggest that in low-biomass ecosystems, particularly externally exposed niches such as the URT, microbial composition is predominantly shaped by ecological drift and transient colonization, rather than deterministic selection. Nevertheless, we observed that several environmental factors, including ambient temperature, milk and straw intake, and antibiotic treatments, were significantly associated with the nasal microbial structure. For instance, environmental temperature exerted a strong influence, consistent with studies linking climatic fluctuations to shifts in respiratory microbiota [64, 60]. Milk and straw intakes also affected the variation in the ordination space, likely due to reseeding of oral microbiota during feeding. In dairy cows, up to 32% of oral ASVs are shared with the nasal microbiota, supporting microbial transfer between these sites [65]. Antibiotic treatments had modest but detectable effects, underscoring the niche’s sensitivity of nasal microbiota to pharmacological perturbation during clinical instability [66].

In contrast, the fecal microbiota showed consistent increases in taxonomic richness, evenness, and biomass, along with higher SCFA production and greater compositional stability. This agrees with previous studies (Dill-McFarland et al., [67]; Fan et al., [68]; Lima et al., [56, 69]; Meale et al., [70]; Uddin et al., [32]. This progressive microbial enrichment coincides with physiological development of the enteric digestive tract and mucosal immune response (Malmuthuge et al., [71]) and the dietary transitions, notably the increased intake of solid, fiber-rich feed (up to 1 kg/day of texturized calf starter), which promotes SCFA production and compositional stability [72, 73]. Seasonal changes also significantly influenced fecal dynamics, consistent with temperature-driven effects on gut microbiota in livestock [64, 74]. The initial antibiotic regimen (doxycycline, colistin, tilmicosin) collectively explained 5.04% of the total variance in microbial community composition, with no long-term impact on gut structure, likely due to the resilience of the gut microbiome. This aligns with studies showing limited disruption of gut communities following antibiotic treatments targeting respiratory diseases [75, 69]. However, early-life antibiotics for diarrhea have been shown to impair gut diversity and stability [76], emphasizing the importance of timing and indication.

Lastly, to examine which host variables could modulate microbial composition and structure, we integrated systemic cytokine profiles, clinical phenotypes, and physiological variables. The cytokines IFN-γ and IL-1α showed the strongest correlation with fecal and nasal microbial compositions, suggesting a shared inflammatory signature. [32] emphasized that IFN-γ and IL-1α are central to maintaining epithelial integrity and regulating microbial composition in both the gut and the respiratory tract. We also observed that rectal temperature, a proxy for systemic inflammation [77], was significantly associated with microbial variation in both sites, particularly in the respiratory tract.

Despite their anatomical and ecological differences, we demonstrate for the first time that the gut and nasal microbiotas in calves are significantly correlated, with up to 50% shared composition as animals mature. This co-variation supports the existence of a functional gut–lung axis, increasingly recognized in both human and veterinary microbiome research [33, 34]; h [78]). It suggests that microbial communities across mucosal compartments are shaped not only by local niche conditions but also by systemic host factors, including immune signaling and physiological status. This concept echoes the ancient wisdom of Hippocrates’ assertion that “all diseases begin in the gut”, and its modern adaptation in veterinary medicine, “Happy gut, healthy calf” [79].

This developmental synchrony and divergence between gut and nasal microbiotas reveal a dynamic interplay across mucosal compartments. During the first month of life, we observed that the fecal microbiota matures in a predictable and progressive manner, marked by early successional shifts and growing compositional stability. This predictable ecological progression makes the gut particularly receptive to targeted strategies, such as microbial supplementation, dietary adjustments, or environmental conditioning. In contrast, the nasal microbiota remains ecologically unstable, characterized by persistent inter-individual variability and stochastic colonization patterns throughout the production cycle. Consequently, interventions targeting the respiratory tract may yield greater impact when aligned with ecological disruptions, such as weaning, changes in housing, or clinical events, rather than during the neonatal phase itself.

By distinguishing the temporal and ecological dynamics of gut versus nasal communities, this study provides a framework for niche-specific, time-sensitive microbial interventions. These insights not only refine our understanding of microbiota development in calves but also inform precision strategies to enhance health, resilience, and welfare during critical developmental phases.

Limitations of the study

While our findings offer valuable insights into the temporal dynamics of the fecal and airway microbiota, several limitations warrant cautious interpretation of these results. First, using 16S rRNA gene sequencing means we cannot detect viruses or fungi, nor can we assess the functional potential of genes, a gap that can be filled by metagenomic approaches. Additionally, because our samples represent only a single sample per designated site, they may not capture the full complexity of the respiratory or intestinal ecosystems, each of which comprises distinct microbial niches. Our analyses primarily identify statistical associations and mediation effects based on P-value thresholds. Although the longitudinal design enhances our study’s statistical power, it is still possible that phenomena with small sample sizes or subtle effect sizes may have been missed. Future studies with larger cohorts might reveal additional nuances. Importantly, the absence of control animals limits our ability to determine the long-term effects of antibiotic exposure from natural microbial succession. Longitudinal studies incorporating untreated controls are essential to assess microbiota resilience and to evaluate the impact of biotic and abiotic perturbations over time. Finally, while our results highlight significant associations and suggest potential causal relationships, they do not establish causality. Confirming any causal links will require more targeted follow-up research.

Experimental model and subject details

Ethics approval

The local animal care and use committee reviewed and approved the study protocol for the calves’ study (ComEth EnvA-Upec-ANSES, reference: 11–0041, dated July 12th, 2011). All protocols were conducted in accordance with EEC Regulation (No. 2010/63/UE) governing the care and use of laboratory animals, which has been effective in France since January 1, 2013. In all cases, the owners and caretakers provided their informed consent before starting the study procedures with the animals.

Animals and housing

The study was conducted on a commercial veal farm in the Tarn department of France. The veal-calf industry is an intensive farming system that produces meat from milk-fed calves [69]. The farm has a capacity of 350 calves and is housed in a single barn. The farm operates an all-in/all-out management system, meaning all animals enter and leave the facility simultaneously. A total of 326 healthy French cross-breed female calves were introduced to the farm and reared together until they were slaughtered. Animals were purchased from a local auction market and transported to the farm, which was located within a 10 km radius.

Upon arrival, each calf was examined to ensure they were free of disease, injury, and dehydration. The calves were then grouped into seven pens, each containing 40–50 calves, on a fully slatted floor until the end of the fattening cycle (approximately 150 days). Each stall measured around 70 m², providing a space allowance of 1.4 ± 0.5 m² per calf. The grilled separation between adjacent pens allowed visual and tactile contact between neighboring calves, but the pens did not share equipment, food, water, space, or fomites.

The housing facilities and farm management practices complied with the European Council Directive on the protection of calves (2008/119/EC). Of the 326 calves, 30 healthy individuals (mean ± SD age: 7 ± 3 days; initial body weight: 57 ± 0.61 kg) were randomly selected. They were housed in three out of a total of seven indoor pens with other conspecifics.

Feeding program

Upon arrival at the farm, the calves were fed twice with 2 L of an electrolyte solution (50 g/L water, Hydravo, Agrial, Caen, France). Starting two days after arrival, a milk replacer (Sobeval Blue, Boulazac Isle Manoire, France) was provided using an automated calf feeder (Forster Technique, Co., Ltd, Germany). This computerized feeder utilized information from radio frequency identification (RFID) transponders in each calf’s ear tag to mix and distribute the milk replacer according to individual feeding thresholds.

Standard practice in veal calf fattening was followed, with the calves’ milk allocation increasing by 0.5 L per day, from an initial 3.44 L to 14.65 L daily (Supplementary Table S1). Initially, each calf received two feeds of 1.7 L, evenly spaced throughout the day. Access to the feeder was regulated; once the threshold was met, calves had to wait two to four hours before accessing their next allowance. Milk replacers were reconstituted in hot water (65 °C) and fed at approximately 41 °C. Each pen’s automatic feeder teat was positioned 68 cm above ground level, allowing only one calf to drink at a time, with a ratio of 50 calves per feeding station.

The initial milk replacer was formulated with 63% skim milk powder, protein powder, lard, soy proteins, starch, palm, wheat flour, and linseed, palm, and coconut oils. It contained 210 g/kg of crude protein (CP), 185 g/kg of crude fat (CF), and 69 g/kg of ash. Between weeks 6 and 7, this was replaced with Sobeval White (Boulazac Isle Manoire, France), containing 18.0% CP, 18.5% CF, and 6.6% ash. Throughout the study, all calves were fed the same calf starter, free from antibiotics.

A small amount of solid feed was introduced during the first weeks for the animals’ welfare. The texturized calf starter (Muesli finition Vrac, Sobeval, Boulazac Isle Manoire, France) included corn, expelled cornmeal, barley, lupine, corn gluten, molasses, oats, colza oil, and minerals. The texturized starter amounts were adjusted according to the calves’ nutritional needs, beginning at 100 g/day upon arrival and rising to 1.14 kg/day by the end of the study. Individual consumption of the starter was not recorded, as it was provided to the entire group within each pen using a Behlen Country galvanized 3 m poly trough feed bunk (Behlen Mfg., Columbus, NE). Similarly, straw allowance increased incrementally from day ten, starting at 10 g every 0.5 weeks until day 45, and was maintained at 100 g/day for the duration of the study. Ad libitum access to water was available in each pen using a Ritchie WaterMatic 100 heated waterer (Ritchie Industries Inc., Conrad, Iowa, USA).

Collective and individual medical treatments

All calves were reared according to the Integrated Chain Management System (IKB) regulations and standard clinical veterinary practices.

Both collective and individual antibiotic treatments were meticulously recorded. Upon arrival, all calves were vaccinated against Trichophyton verrucosum (Bovilis Ringvac, Intervet, Beaucouze, France; 2 mL/animal intramuscular) and treated for endoparasites with Ivermectin (Puromec; Chanelle Pharmaceuticals Manufacturing, Galway, Ireland; 1 cc/10 kg of body weight).

Under the guidance of the responsible veterinarian, collective antibiotic treatments were administered orally. Calves received oral doxycycline (Doxy 20 HVP; Huvepharma, Segre en Anjou Bleu, France) at a dose of 1.45 g/animal on days 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, and 11. Additionally, doxycycline was administered again at a dose of 3.75 g/animal for five days, starting on day 22. Lastly, colistin (Colivet; Ceva Sante Animale, Libourne, France) was administered at a dose of 1.45 g/animal on days 2, 4, 9, 10, and 11, and tilmicosin (Tilmovet; Huvepharma, Segre en Anjou Bleu, France) at a dose of 2 g/animal on days 9, 10, 11, 12, and 13.

For the 30 calves included in the study, individual antimicrobial treatment records were maintained, detailing the dates, duration, and types of antibiotics used (Supplementary information Table S1). Following antibiotic treatment, a two-day course of aspirin was administered at a dose of 2 g per animal on days 1, 2, 7, 8, 9, 10, and 11. All treatments were delivered orally in the milk replacer. None of the calves were vaccinated against primary respiratory cattle diseases (e.g., PI3, BRSV, BVD, or Mannheimia haemolytica). Farm records collected mortality rates and early culling numbers for the same batch. Reasons for culling were classified into three categories: respiratory syndromes, musculoskeletal system pathologies, and other causes (e.g., parasites, heart failure, and urolithiasis).

Meteorologic observations

We use daily weather station observations from Météo France, the French national meteorological service (https://meteofrance.fr). All the observations were made at a station 5 km from the commercial farm. The stations calculate daily Tmin as the lowest Ta observed from 0 coordinated universal time (UTC) the previous day until 0 UTC; daily Tmax is the highest Ta observed from 0 UTC on the day until 0 UTC the following day. The Tmean was calculated as the mean of all (at least 24) Ta observations from 0 UTC on the day until 0 UTC the next day. The weather and altitude conditions remained constant for all animals throughout the study (Supplementary Table S1).

Methods details

Animal handling and clinical examination

Fecal, nasal, and blood samples were collected simultaneously from each of the 30 individuals at eight different time points: 24 h after arrival (day 1) and at 9, 16, 30, 58, 91, 117, and 147 days post-arrival. Samples were taken while the animals were restrained in a squeeze chute.

The same investigator examined the calves at each sampling point for body temperature, nasal discharge, coughing, general state, and abnormal lung sounds, as previously described [80]. A health scoring method was adapted from our group [81]. Scores for rectal temperatures (T) were 0 (T < 39 °C), 1 (39.1 °C < T < 40 °C), 2 (40.1 °C < T < 41 °C), or 3 (T >41 °C); a score of 0 (normal), 1 (mild), or 2 (severe) was assigned for nasal discharge, coughing, or the general state, respectively. The accumulated scores for each calf were calculated by summing the scores of all parameters.

Nasal sampling and measurements

To characterize the upper respiratory tract microbiota, we focused on the nasal compartment, which serves as a reliable ecological proxy due to its accessibility and its established association with bovine respiratory health [22, 13]. Deep nasal samples for microbiota profiling were collected from each calf’s left and right nostrils. Sterile cotton swabs (Eurotubo®; Deltalab, Barcelona, Spain) were inserted inside the nostrils (approximately 15 cm) and rubbed against the mucous membrane for 15 s. All swabs were placed immediately in portable coolers with ice packs and shipped to the laboratory. The time between sampling and preprocessing in the lab was, at most, four hours.

At the laboratory, right swab tips were cut and placed into a sterile 2 mL cryovial containing 1.5 mL brain heart infusion (BHI) with 20% glycerol. The swab tips were then vortexed and stored at -80 °C until DNA extraction for microbiota profiling.

Blood sampling and measurements

Blood samples for biochemical assays were collected at each time point using 10 mL BD Vacutainer dry tubes (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). After clotting, the tubes were centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 10 min at 20 °C, and the harvested serum was stored at -80 °C until analysis. Sera were assayed for cytokines using the MILLIPLEX® Bovine Cytokine/Chemokine Panel 1, based on the Luminex® xMAP technology2. The MILLIPLEX® Bovine Cytokine/Chemokine Magnetic Bead Panel 1 (Merck, KGaA, Darmstadt, Deutchland) is an 11-plex kit that simultaneously quantifies the following cytokines: IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-17α, and MIP-1β, and cytokines involved in viral immune responses, including IFN-γ and IP-10. The MCP-1, a chemokine that primarily recruits monocytes to infection and inflammation sites, was also analyzed. The assay followed the manufacturer’s protocol, which included reagent preparation, sample addition, bead addition, incubation, washing, detection antibody addition, and reading with a Luminex xMAP instrument. Data were analyzed using Luminex xPONENT software, ensuring accuracy through quality controls and validation steps.

Fecal sampling and measurements

Single-use gloves and sterilized sampling tubes were used to collect rectal samples upon rectal stimulation from each animal and each time point. The fecal samples were cooled upon collection and shipped to the laboratory. Since some of the calves experienced diarrhea, the gastrointestinal tract was empty, and consequently, we could not recover the feces. The time between sampling and preprocessing in the lab was, at most, four hours.

At the lab, fecal pH and redox potentials were immediately determined after 10% fecal suspension (wt/vol) in saline solution (0.15 M NaCl solution) with a multi-parameter portable meter (Multi 3620 IDS, WTW, GmbH, Weilheim, Germany) equipped with field redox and pH electrodes (SenTix, WTW, Weilheim, Germany). Briefly, 1 g of fecal samples was diluted in 10 mL of distilled water and centrifuged at 8,000 rpm for 10 min.

Additionally, 1 g of fecal sample was mixed with 0.2 ml of sulfuric acid (25% v/v) for SCFA analysis and stored at -20 °C until analysis. Fecal SCFA concentrations were measured as previously described [82]. Fecal samples and sulfuric acid mixes were thawed on ice overnight for gas chromatography analysis. These samples were centrifuged at 2,880 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to separate the liquid phase. For protein removal, 1 mL of supernatant was mixed with 200 µL of 25% metaphosphoric acid (v/v) and centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Then, 100 µL of the supernatant was added to 75 µL (0.2% v/v) of 4-methyl valeric acid as an internal standard and 900 µL of ultrapure water. From this mixture, 1 µL was then injected into a gas chromatograph (Hewlett Packard, Model 7890 A) equipped with a DB-FFAP column (30 m × 0.53 mm i.d., 1-µm film thickness, Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, United States) and an FID detector (Avondale, PA, United States). Chromatograms were integrated using Chromeleon software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, version 6.8, Waltham, MA, United States). Six SCFAs, acetic acid (C2:0), propionic acid (C3:0), butyric acid (C4:0), valeric acid (C5:0), isobutyric acid (iso-C4:0), and isovaleric acid (iso-C5:0), were quantified using automated gas separation, according to the method of Playne [83] and modified as follows. The sum of the six SCFA concentrations was defined as the total concentration and was used to obtain the molar proportions of each SCFA. The proportion or relative abundance of each fatty acid was calculated by dividing the concentration of each fatty acid by the total fatty acid content.

Another 1 g fecal sample was snap-frozen and stored at -80 °C for DNA extraction.

SCFA proportions, pH, and redox potential values were analyzed across time points using non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis tests, implemented via the kruskal.test()function from the base R stats package. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (p.adjust() function, method = “BH”).

Microbiota study and statistical analysis

Fecal and nasal microbial 16S rRNA gene sequencing data production and analysis

Fecal samples were thawed on ice for the isolation of fecal DNA. Then, the total DNA was extracted using the EZNA Stool DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, Georgia, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Nasal samples were thawed on ice for the extraction of microbial DNA. Total DNA was extracted from samples using the magnetic bead-based ID Gene Mag Fast Extraction Kit and IDEAL 96 extraction robot (Innovative Diagnostics, Grabel, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Fecal and nasal cavity DNA purity and concentration were estimated using a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA) and Qubit 2.0 fluorimeter (dsDNA HS assay kit; Thermo Fisher). In all cases, negative control samples were included alongside biological samples during DNA extraction to control for DNA contamination before sequencing. Additionally, contamination was minimized through laboratory techniques such as UV irradiation of the material, the use of ultrapure water, the DNA-free Taq DNA polymerase, and the separation of pre- and post-PCR areas. We also included the ZymoBIOMICS™ Microbial Community Standard bacterial cells (Catalog No. D6300, Zymo Research Corp., Irvine, CA, United States) (“Zymobiomics-Cells”) as a positive control during DNA extraction. According to the manufacturer’s specifications, Zymobiomics-Cells consist of three gram-negative bacteria within the phylum Proteobacteria [Pseudomonas aeruginosa (theoretical composition in terms of 16S rRNA gene abundance: 4.2%), Escherichia coli (10.1%), and Salmonella enterica (10.4%)] and five gram-positive bacteria within the phylum Firmicutes [Lactobacillus fermentum (18.4%), Enterococcus faecalis (9.9%), Staphylococcus aureus (15.5%), Listeria monocytogenes (14.1%), and Bacillus subtilis (17.4%)]. All samples were diluted to 10 ng/µL and sent to the sequencing facility.

The V3-V4 hyper-variable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified, as previously reported by our team [84, 85, 86, 87]. The 16S rRNA gene libraries were prepared according to Illumina’s protocol (# 15044223 RevB, Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA,

United States). To minimize the risk of cross-contamination and pipetting errors, the workflow was automated using a high-throughput liquid handler, the Freedom EVO NGS workstation (TECAN). Briefly, V3-V4 hypervariable regions were first amplified from 10 ng of genomic DNA using the following primers: (i) Forward Primer: TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG and (ii) Reverse Primer: GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC and 2X KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (Kapa Biosystems). PCR cycle conditions were 95 °C for 3 min, 25 cycles of (95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s), and then a final extension of 72 °C for 5 min. The libraries were purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter). Dual indexes and sequencing adapters from the Illumina Nextera XT index kits (Illumina) were added in a second PCR using 2× KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (Kapa Biosystems). Cycle conditions were 95 °C for 3 min, 8 cycles of (95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s), then a final extension of 72 °C for 5 min. Ready-to-sequence libraries were purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman) and quantified by fluorescence using the QuantiFluor One dsDNA kit (# E4870, Promega) with the GloMax system (Promega). Quality control was performed using a 2200 TapeStation system with the DNA 1000 ScreenTape (# 5067–5582, Agilent). The library pool was quantified by qPCR with the KAPA Library Quantification Kit for Illumina platforms (Kapa Biosystems). Sequencing was performed on a MiSeq system (Illumina) with the MiSeq Reagent v3 kit (600 cycles) in a 2 × 250 bp mode. The mock community DNA sample, “20 Strain Staggered Mix Genomic Material” (ATCC® MSA-1003TM), was used to control sequencing errors at concentrations ranging from 6.08 ng/µL to 10.14 ng/µL. The mock community declared components are: 0.18% Acinetobacter baumannii (ATCC 17978), 0.02% Actinomyces odontolyticus (ATCC 17982), 1.80% Bacillus cereus (ATCC 10987), 0.02% Bacteroides vulgatus (ATCC 8482), 0.02% Bifidobacterium adolescentis (ATCC 15703), 1.80% Clostridium beijerinckii (ATCC 35702), 0.18% Cutibacterium acnes (ATCC 11828), 0.02% Deinococcus radiodurans (ATCC BAA-816), 0.02% Enterococcus faecalis (ATCC 47077), 18.0% Escherichia coli (ATCC 700926), 0.18% Helicobacter pylori (ATCC 700392), 0.18% Lactobacillus gasseri (ATCC 33323), 0.18% Neisseria meningitidis (ATCC BAA-335), 18.0% Porphyromonas gingivalis (ATCC 33277), 1.80% Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC 9027), 18.0% Rhodobacter sphaeroides (ATCC 17029), 1.80% Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC BAA-1556), 18.0% Staphylococcus epidermidis (ATCC 12228), 1.80% Streptococcus agalactiae (ATCC BAA-611), 18.0% Streptococcus mutans (ATCC 700610).

A subsequent amplification was necessary to index sequences on the sequencing cell (NexteraXT Index Kit, FC-131–1001/FC-131–1002).

Processing of 16S rRNA gene sequencing

The quality of the reads was verified using FASTQC (v. 0.11.9) and MultiQC (v. 1.17).

Primers were trimmed from the forward and reverse reads using cutadapt (v.4.4) using the following parameters: --front CCTACGGGNBGCNSCAG --adapter GGATTAGANACCCNNGTAGTC -G GACTACNNGGGTNTCTAATCC -A CTGSNGCBNCCCGTAGG --minimum-length 225 --length 225 --overlap 10 --times 2 --error-rate 0.15 --max-n 0 --trim-n --cores 4.

Then, the Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm (DADA) was implemented using the DADA2 plug-in for QIIME 2 (v. amplicon-2023.9) to perform quality filtering and chimera removal and to construct a feature table consisting of read abundance per amplicon sequence variant (ASV) by sample [88]. DADA2 models the amplicon sequencing error to identify unique ASVs. It infers sample composition more accurately than traditional Operational Taxonomic Unit (OTU) picking methods that identify representative sequences from cluster sequences based on a % similarity cut-off [88]. The output of DADA2 was an abundance table in which each unique sequence was characterized by its abundance in each sample.

To achieve an increase in the proportion of ASVs classified at the species level, we combined the taxonomic assignments of ASVs using two different protocols: (i) importing the SILVA 16S rRNA Database (v 138.1; release 2023_09) to QIIME 2 and classifying representative ASVs using the naive Bayes classifier plug-in [89]; and (ii) mapping the representative ASV against the 16S database of Bacteria and Archaea of NCBI RefSeq Targeted Loci Project database through Kraken (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/RefSeq/targetedloci/) using Kraken2 [90] as a metagenomic classifier.

Then, as described by Bars-Cortina et al. [91], each ASV classification method was evaluated for similarities and discrepancies using an R code automatic R code check to establish the best ASV taxonomic level of resolution. This gave additional confidence about the taxonomic lineages obtained. Moreover, as a final step, the best ASV taxonomic lineage assignment was updated and homogenized according to the NCBI taxonomy (ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pub/taxonomy/) using the myTAI (v. 0.9.3) R package.

Prevalence filtering was applied to remove singletons and doubletons, thereby reducing the likelihood of data artifacts affecting the analysis. Sequences flagged as chloroplasts, mitochondria, or eukaryotes were discarded from the dataset.

The phyloseq (v. 1.46.0) [92], vegan (v. 2.6.4) [93], and microbiome packages (v. 1.24.0; http://microbiome.github.io) were used in RStudio (v. 2023.09.03) for the downstream analysis steps. Data were aggregated at genus, family, order, class, and phylum levels throughout the taxonomic-agglomeration method in the phyloseq R package, which merges taxa of the same taxonomic category for a user-specific taxonomic level.

To assess and mitigate potential contamination in sequencing data, we employed the decontam (v. 1.22.0) R package to identify and visualize possible contaminant ASV features in negative control samples. The isContaminant () function was applied using the frequency-based method, which models the prevalence of each ASV as a function of input DNA concentration. ASVs statistically classified as contaminants (p < 0.05) were excluded from downstream analyses. To further reduce the likelihood of false-positive taxonomic assignments arising from misalignment or low-level background noise, we applied an abundance threshold of 0.01% using the filterfun_sample() function from the phyloseq R package, thereby retaining only biologically relevant taxa. To validate sequencing accuracy and compositional fidelity, we analyzed mock community samples using the ChkMocks R package (v0.1.4). The checkZymobiomics() function was used to compute Spearman’s rank correlation between observed and theoretical community profiles. We confirmed that negative controls consistently failed to produce visible bands on agarose gel electrophoresis, and purified amplicon concentrations were below the detection threshold (< 1 ng/µL), supporting the absence of amplifiable DNA contamination.

Microbiota diversity and richness analysis: α- and β-diversity, core and accessory microbiota

The microbiome R package generated measures of evenness, dominance, divergence, and abundance indexes.

The gut α-diversity indices between time points were compared using a generalized linear model (glmer() function of the lme4 R package [94] (v. 1.1.35.2), where the day was included as a fixed effect and individuals as a random effect. Comparisons of α-diversity parameters between body sites were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, a non-parametric statistical test used to compare the medians of three or more independent groups, using the function kruskal.test() from the stats R package.

Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was calculated using the phyloseq R package to estimate β-diversity. The β-diversity was visualized using non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) in the vegan R package, specifically the metaMDS() function. The stress value was calculated to determine the dimensions for each NMDS. The partitioning of β-diversity into turnover (βTURN) and nestedness (βNEST) components was performed with the betapart R package (v. 1.6) [95].

To assess the influence of compartment and time on microbial community structure, we performed a permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PerMANOVA) using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity. ASV tables were extracted from the phyloseq objects and converted to distance matrices using the distance() function from the phyloseq package (method = “bray”). PerMANOVA was conducted using the adonis2() function from the vegan package, with Samples_type and Time_point_day as explanatory variables and Animal_ID as a strata to account for repeated measures. The model was run with 10,000 permutations to assess statistical significance.

The core genera, which are cross-sectional and found across time points and individuals, were estimated using a prevalence threshold of at least 95% in the microbiome R package. The accessory microbiota was calculated by subtracting the core microbiota from the overall microbiota. To compare genus-level prevalence patterns between fecal and airway microbiota, we calculated the proportion of samples in which each genus was detected (i.e., prevalence) and ranked genera accordingly. Absolute abundance data were extracted from genus-level phyloseq objects. Prevalence was defined as the fraction of samples in which a given genus had non-zero abundance. For each compartment, genera were grouped by name and sorted in descending order of prevalence using the dplyr R package. Genus ranks were scaled from 0 to 100 and normalized to allow direct comparison across compartments. Prevalence values were also scaled to percentages.

Assessing the individuality of the microbial communities

Individuality refers to the host-specific composition of microbial taxa driven by niche interactions, host physiology, and environmental exposure [52]. Individuality was assessed through within-host temporal consistency using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), Spearman’s ρ values between consecutive time points, and ASV turnover.

The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) estimation uses the variance components from a one-way ANOVA (among-group variance and within-group variance; ICC = variance between/[variancebetween + variancewithin]). We calculated the ICC for both nasal and fecal samples using absolute abundance data. For each body site, the phyloseq object was agglomerated at successive taxonomic levels—Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus—using the tax_glom() function. At each level, the ASV table was extracted and converted into a data frame, which was then analyzed using the ICC() function from the sjmisc (https://strengejacke.github.io/sjmisc/; v. 2.8.9) R package. We focused on the ICC1 metric (single raters, absolute agreement), which quantifies the proportion of variance attributable to within-individual differences over time. Higher ICC1 values indicate greater temporal consistency of taxa within individuals. This procedure was repeated for each taxonomic rank, including ASV level.

Additionally, to assess temporal correlation in microbial community structure across time points for a particular animal, we first calculated beta diversity using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity on absolute abundance data from nasal and fecal samples independently (phyloseq::distance() function). The resulting distance matrix was converted into a data frame and enriched with metadata, including animal ID and sampling day. We reshaped the data into long format using the melt() function to facilitate mixed-effects modeling. Then, we computed Spearman’s ρ values between consecutive time points for each animal, ensuring comparisons were restricted to individuals sampled at both time points. This yielded a series of pairwise correlation values reflecting the degree of microbial similarity over time, which were summarized in a time-resolved correlation profile. Higher correlation values indicated more stable microbial communities, while lower values suggested greater variability.

Dynamic microbial dispersion and divergence between individuals

We first used the vegdist () function from the vegan package to calculate the inter-individual variation over time for URT and fecal microbiota, employing the pairwise Bray-Curtis dissimilarities among the samples. Specifically, we focused on the inter-individual variation by calculating the mean Bray-Curtis distance between all pairs of individuals at each time point. This allowed us to quantify the differences in microbial community composition between different individuals over time. Next, we used the mandist() function from the vegan R package to compute the mean distances.

We also used the study of similarities (ANOSIM) to test for intragroup dispersion. ANOSIM is a permutation-based test where the null hypothesis states that within-group distances are not significantly smaller than between-group distances. The test statistic (R) can range from 1 to − 1, with a value of 1 indicating that all samples within groups are more similar to one another than to any representative from a different group. R is ≈ approximately 0 when the null hypothesis is true, meaning that the distances within and between groups are the same on average. Because multiple comparison corrections for ANOSIM were unavailable, the number of permutations used in those calculations increased to 9,999.

We then calculated β-dispersion for fecal and airway samples separately using the mean distance to the centroid. Bray–Curtis distance matrices were computed for each compartment, and dispersion was assessed using the betadisper() function from vegan, grouping samples by Time_point_day. Distances to group centroids were extracted. For each time point, the mean and standard deviation of dispersion values were calculated per compartment. Statistical comparisons between fecal and airway heterogeneity were performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (wilcox.test()), and p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method (p.adjust(method = “BH”)).

Resistance of the microbial communities

The ability to remain essentially unchanged despite disturbances is referred to as resistance [44]. In this study, microbial ecosystem resistance was quantified to assess the temporal stability of the fecal and nasal microbiota in calves from 7 to 150 days of age. Resistance was calculated using presence/absence-transformed ASV data derived from absolute abundance phyloseq objects. For each niche, ASV tables were extracted and converted to binary format, where taxa were considered present if their abundance exceeded zero. Sample metadata, specifically the Time variable, was used to group samples by time. Resistance at a given time point t was defined as one minus the mean Jaccard dissimilarity between samples collected at time t and those collected at the subsequent time point t + 1. This approach captures the proportion of shared taxa between adjacent time points, with higher resistance values indicating greater community stability. To compute this, samples from each pair of consecutive time points were subset, and their ASV matrices were merged. A Jaccard dissimilarity matrix was then calculated using the vegdist() function from the vegan package. From this matrix, pairwise distances between samples from t and t + 1 were extracted, and the mean dissimilarity was computed. Resistance was then calculated as one minus this mean value and assigned to the time point t. This approach is based on the established ecological theory of community similarity [96].

Covariance between nasal and fecal bacterial communities across time

To assess the interconnectedness between fecal and nasal microbiota over time, we calculated Spearman’s (ρ) values of taxon-level abundance profiles at each time point. ASV tables were extracted from absolute abundance phyloseq objects using otu_table() and converted to matrices with samples as rows. Shared taxa were retained using intersect(), and sample metadata was retrieved via sample_data(). For each time point, samples from both compartments were subset, and mean abundances per taxon were computed using colMeans(). Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was calculated with cor(method = “spearman”), and results were stored in a time-resolved data frame. Visualization was performed using ggplot2, with LOESS smoothing (geom_smooth(method = “loess”)) to highlight temporal trends.

To evaluate temporal shifts in compositional similarity between fecal and nasal microbiota, Bray-Curtis similarity was computed at each sampling time point using abundance data. ASV tables were extracted from the respective fecal and airway phyloseq objects and filtered to retain only shared taxa, ensuring cross-niche comparability. For each time point, samples from each niche were grouped, and ASV counts were normalized to relative abundance by dividing each sample’s counts by its total sum. Mean relative abundance profiles were then calculated by averaging across all samples within each niche. These two profiles—one for feces and one for the nasal cavity—were assembled into a two-row matrix and used to compute Bray-Curtis dissimilarity via the vegdist() function from the vegan package. Similarity was defined as 1 minus the dissimilarity value, yielding a bounded metric from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (identical composition). This procedure was repeated for all time points with paired fecal and airway data. Resulting similarity scores were visualized over time using LOESS smoothing (geom_smooth(method = “loess”)) to highlight trends in cross-niche microbial convergence. All analyses were conducted using the phyloseq, vegan, dplyr (v1.1.3), and ggplot2 (v3.4.4) packages.

Effect of environmental and host factors on the microbiota structure

The PerMANOVA test, implemented in the adonis2() function from the vegan R package, allowed for testing the global association between environmental and host factors and the ecological community structure across time. The strata option (strata = calf) was used for the repeated sampling of individuals. Specifically, we tested the effects of time, corrected for environmental and host variables, on the variation in total dissimilarity between microbiotas. The significance of the effect of variables was assessed in an F-test based on the sequential sum of squares estimated from a 10,000-permutation procedure. The significance threshold was chosen at an adjusted P < 0.05.

The effect size of the host and environmental variables on the ASV or genus-level community ordination was tested using the envfit() function in the vegan R package (10,000 permutations, Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P < 0.05) for each body site. The envfit() function performs multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) and linear correlations for categorical and continuous variables. The effect size and significance of each covariate were determined by comparing the difference in the centroids of each group relative to the total variation. The obtained R² value indicates the proportion of variability (i.e., the main dimensions of the ordination) that can be attributed to the explanatory variables.

Centroid shifts of nasal and fecal microbiotas in animals treated only with collective antibiotics compared to those also treated with individual antibiotics once or more were first calculated. Centroids, representing the average positions of microbial communities in Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) space, were calculated for each body site at each time point using the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrices in the vegan R package. Then, the variation of the centroids across time points was visualized through PCoA. Temporal shifts in centroids were assessed by tracking positional changes over time using ggplot2 for visualization. This approach provided insights into the dynamic evolution of microbiota in nasal and fecal environments, highlighting distinct trajectories and the interplay between sites.

To analyze the relationship between cytokines and microbiota, we employed RDA using the vegan package in R. We prepared the microbial community data, including the absolute abundances of microbial genera, and the cytokine data, including the concentrations of various cytokines for each sample. The RDA was performed using the rda() function to assess the variance in cytokine profiles explained by the microbial genera. To explore correlations between cytokine levels and microbial genera, we calculated Spearman’s ρ values using the cor() function. Significant correlations were identified by applying multiple testing corrections using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) method with the p.adjust() function, retaining only those with an adjusted P < 0.05.

qPCR quantification of bacterial biomass in feces and nasal samples

As detailed by (Mach, Moroldo, et al., [9]; Plancade et al., [97], concentrations of bacteria in fecal and UTR samples were quantified by qPCR using a QuantStudio 12 K Flex platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, United States). Primers for real-time amplification of bacteria (FOR: 5′-CAGCMGCCGCGGTAANWC-3′; REV: 5′-CCGTCAATTCMTTTRAGTTT-3′) were purchased from Eurofins Genomics (Ebersberg, Germany).

Amplified fragments of the target amplicons were used to create a seven-point 10-fold standard dilution series. The dilution points ranged from 2.25 × 107 to 2.25 × 1013 copies per µg of DNA for bacteria. qPCR reactions were performed in a final volume of 20 µL, containing 10 µL of Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher), 2 µL of standard or DNA template at 0.5 ng/µL, and 0.6 µM of each primer to a final concentration of 200 mM for bacteria.

In all the cases, the thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min; 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing, and extension at 60 °C for 60 s. To check for the absence of nonspecific signals, a dissociation step was added after each amplification. It was carried out by ramping the temperature from 60 to 95 °C. All qPCR runs were performed in triplicate, and the standard curve obtained using the target amplicons was used to calculate the number of microorganism copies in feces and nasal samples.

Taking into account the molecular mass of nucleotides and the amplicon length, the number of copies was obtained using the following equation: copies per nanogram = (NL × A × 10− 9)/(n × mw), where “NL” is the Avogadro constant (6.02 × 1023 molecules per mole), “A” is the molecular weight of DNA molecules (ng), “n” is the length of the amplicon in base pairs, and “mw” is the molecular weight per base pair.

The number of bacterial copies (biomass) was log-transformed to align the sampled data with the assumptions of parametric statistics, i.e., that the residuals from a model fit are normally distributed with homogeneous variance. Accordingly, the results are expressed as log10 of gene copies (per gram of wet weight; Supplementary information Table S1). Comparisons of biomass parameters between body sites were done using the Kruskal-Wallis test using the function kruskal.test() from the stats R package.
