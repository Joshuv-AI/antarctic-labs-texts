Heat priming enhances cross-tolerance to subsequent drought stress in Haloxylon ammodendron seedlings via transcriptional reprogramming and metabolic adjustment
Authors: Xu Wang, Yameng Wang, Meng Yuan, Hua Zhang, Bo Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHaloxylon ammodendron, a keystone species for restoring desert ecosystems, has extremely low seedling survival rates under natural conditions due to surface high-temperature and secondary drought stress. This study investigated the effects of heat priming on the tolerance of H. ammodendron seedlings to subsequent drought stress and the underlying molecular mechanisms.ResultsThe results demonstrated that heat priming (37 °C for 12 h) significantly improved seedling survival under drought stress (simulated with 480 mmol·L⁻¹ sorbitol) and reduced cell mortality. Physiological analyses revealed that heat priming increased the activities of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD, and CAT) and the accumulation of proline and chlorophyll, albeit with aggravated membrane lipid peroxidation (elevated MDA content). Transcriptomic profiling revealed 909 heat-priming-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs), which were predominantly enriched in plant‒pathogen interactions, the MAPK signaling pathway, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. Key genes such as WRKY24 and MAPK3 potentially mediate cross-regulation to reinforce stress memory. Furthermore, heat priming suppressed drought-induced excessive lignin accumulation, suggesting that cell wall remodeling contributes to drought tolerance.Conclusions Collectively, heat priming activates oxidative defense, signal transduction, and metabolic reprogramming, thereby increasing cross-tolerance to secondary drought stress in H. ammodendron seedlings. These findings provide theoretical insights into its ecological adaptation and stress resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07906-8.

Background

Throughout their life cycle, plants often face multiple stresses of varying duration and intensity. Cross-stress tolerance refers to the phenomenon where prior exposure to a primary stress enhances an individual plant’s (or even its progeny’s) metabolic efficiency and phenotypic performance under subsequent secondary stresses of different types [1–3]. This stress preconditioning is commonly termed cross-priming or trans-priming [4–7]. Thus, cross-stress tolerance represents a stress memory phenomenon based on cross-priming.

Studies have shown that epigenetic mechanisms—such as chromatin remodeling, histone modifications, DNA methylation, and regulation by noncoding RNAs—are essential for the formation of stress memory in plants [8]. For instance, whole-genome bisulfite sequencing in rice (Oryza sativa) revealed that dynamic changes in DNA methylation regulate the activity of transposable elements, thereby contributing to short-term memory of drought stress [9]. Additionally, reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis has been identified as a key factor in establishing cross-stress tolerance. In chickpea (Cicer arietinum), for example, elevated superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activities following mild drought stress facilitated faster ROS scavenging during subsequent heat stress [5].

Haloxylon ammodendron (Amaranthaceae) is a perennial shrub or small tree [10, 11]. Characterized by rapid growth, it primarily inhabits mobile/semifixed dunes and saline‒alkali soils. With well-developed taproots and lateral roots and leaves reduced to assimilating shoots for photosynthesis, H. ammodendron exhibits exceptional drought, heat, and salt tolerance [12]. These traits are ecologically vital for sand fixation, desertification control, regional ecological balance, and sustainable development [13].

Unlike mature plants, which are highly adaptable to harsh environments, the natural survival rates of H. ammodendron seedlings under nonirrigated conditions are critically low (≤ 10%). Field observations in Xinjiang deserts revealed that although spring temperatures (April; 24–28 °C maximum) and snowmelt provide favorable conditions for seed germination, surface soil temperatures (1–5 cm depth) frequently exceed 50 °C, causing seedling shoot yellowing, growth arrest, or even lethal scalding [14, 15]. Moreover, surface heat stress accelerates topsoil water evaporation, inducing secondary drought stress that reduces seedling water content and growth, ultimately killing shallow-rooted plants. Consequently, combined surface heat and secondary drought stresses constitute major ecological constraints limiting natural regeneration and transplanted seedling survival [16]. Investigating these cross-stresses is therefore key to deciphering the stress resilience mechanisms of H. ammodendron.

Preliminary studies confirmed heat stress memory in H. ammodendron, with memory-related genes being coinduced by heat and drought, suggesting potential cross-talk between these stress signaling pathways. This study elucidates the molecular basis of heat priming-enhanced drought tolerance in seedlings. Our findings will advance the understanding of the ecological acclimation of H. ammodendron and facilitate gene mining for stress-resistant breeding and germplasm conservation.

Results

Heat priming enhanced the tolerance of H. ammodendronseedlings to subsequent drought stress

H. ammodendron seedlings at 60 days were subjected to the following treatments (Fig. 1A): control (CK, maintained at 26 °C); heat priming plus drought (HD, a 12-h 37 °C heat priming followed by 3-day recovery and then drought stress); drought alone (D); and heat priming alone (H, a 12-h 37 °C heat priming followed by recovery). The results revealed that after D treatment, the assimilating branches of H. ammodendron turned significantly yellow, and the plant survival rate was the lowest. In contrast, compared with that in the D treatment, the yellowing of assimilating branches was markedly reduced in the HD-treated seedlings, and the survival rate was significantly greater than that in the D treatment (Fig. 1A, B; p < 0.05). Analysis of physiological indicators revealed that the activities of SOD, peroxidase (POD), and CAT, as well as the contents of proline and chlorophyll under HD treatment, were significantly greater than those under D treatment (Fig. 1C, D, E; p < 0.05). However, the malondialdehyde (MDA) content under the HD treatment was greater than that under the D treatment (Fig. 1F; p < 0.05). Heat priming suppressed cell death in H. ammodendron seedlings to subsequent drought stress

To investigate the effect of heat priming on cell death in H. ammodendron seedlings under subsequent drought stress, trypan blue staining was performed on the assimilating branches of seedlings subjected to the CK, HD, D, and H treatments. The staining and mortality rates of vascular bundle cells and epidermal tissues were observed and quantified (Fig. 2). Compared with those in the HD treatment group, the vascular and epidermal cells in the D treatment group exhibited deeper staining (Fig. 2A). Statistical analysis revealed that the mortality rate of epidermal cells in the HD treatment group was lower than that in the D treatment group (Fig. 2C; p < 0.05).

Heat priming altered transcriptional gene expression in H. ammodendron seedlings to subsequent drought stress

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the improved survival rate and reduced cell mortality in H. ammodendron seedlings under subsequent drought stress, transcriptome analysis was conducted on assimilating branches subjected to CK, HD, D, and H treatments. Sequencing of 12 libraries generated 64.49 Gb of clean data, with each library exceeding 5.66 Gb. The Q30 base percentage was above 97.22%, and the transcriptome sample correlation assessment yielded an r value greater than 0.85 (Supplementary Fig. 1 A). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed contributions of 38.43% and 15.44% for the first two principal components, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1B). The heatmap (Fig. 3) revealed distinct differences in gene transcription levels among the CK, HD, D, and H treatments. These results demonstrate the reliability of the transcriptome data, supporting their use in subsequent experimental analyses.

Heat priming reduces lignin biosynthesis to subsequent drought stress

The KEGG-enriched pathways of specifically upregulated DEGs in HD vs. CK included the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, an upstream pathway associated with lignin synthesis. We subsequently examined the distribution and content of lignin in assimilating branches subjected to CK, HD, D, and H treatments.

Quantitative analysis revealed that, compared with the CK and H treatments, both the HD and D treatments resulted in greater lignin contents. However, the D treatment resulted in significantly greater lignin accumulation than did the HD treatment (Fig. 9A; p < 0.05).

Histochemical analysis via safranin-fast green staining of cross-sections revealed fast green staining in vascular bundles and epidermal cells across all treatments (CK, HD, D, and H), whereas safranin staining was exclusively observed in D-treated samples (Fig. 9B). Phloroglucinol staining of cross-sections further revealed deeper coloration in vascular and epidermal cells under D treatment than under the CK, HD, and H treatments (Fig. 9C).

Safranin-fast green staining (Fig. 9B) and phloroglucinol staining (Fig. 9C) in assimilating branches confirmed that the lignin content was significantly greater in D-treated plants than in the other treatments. The moderated lignification observed in HD-treated seedlings, compared to the excessive accumulation under D treatment, likely represents a strategic physiological acclimation that enhances drought tolerance. While lignin reinforcement is a common stress response, its overproduction under severe drought (D treatment) may incur substantial metabolic costs and potentially lead to rigid, embrittled tissues that are more susceptible to damage.

By fine-tuning the phenylpropanoid pathway, heat priming appears to steer the metabolic flux towards a more optimal level of cell wall fortification. This balanced approach may conserve carbon and energy resources, which could be reallocated to crucial protective processes such as osmotic adjustment (reflected by higher proline content)(Fig. 1) and enhanced antioxidant capacity (reflected by higher SOD, POD, and CAT activities)(Fig. 1). Consequently, the reduced but significant lignin deposition in HD plants likely provides essential structural integrity without compromising metabolic flexibility, thereby contributing to the higher survival rate and reduced cell mortality under subsequent drought stress.

Discussion

Plants have evolved diverse mechanisms to cope with various environmental stresses, and phenotypic plasticity serves as an effective strategy for stress acclimation [8, 9]. Plants can achieve rapid, dynamic acclimation to short-term environmental fluctuations through mechanisms that may include induced epigenetic modifications. A previous study demonstrated that, compared with nonprimed plants, heat-primed Taxus wallichiana var. mairei presented superior drought tolerance, revealing a cross-acclimation phenomenon between temperature and drought stress in this species [17]. Our findings demonstrate that heat priming improved the tolerance of H. ammodendron seedlings to subsequent drought stress, as evidenced by a significantly higher survival rate, reduced yellowing of assimilating branches, and enhanced activities of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD, CAT) and proline content under HD treatment compared to D treatment (Fig. 1). However, this improved tolerance coincided with exacerbated cell membrane damage under secondary drought stress, indicated by the higher MDA content in HD-treated seedlings (Fig. 1F). This suggests that the protective mechanisms activated by heat priming come at a metabolic cost, yet this membrane damage appears not to be lethal under the conditions tested.

A key finding of this study is that heat priming reduces H. ammodendron mortality following subsequent drought stress. This conclusion is supported by the observed reduction in trypan blue staining of vascular and epidermal cells, lower epidermal cell mortality rate, and downregulated expression of the apoptosis-related gene BI-1 and autophagy-related gene ATG12 in the HD group compared to the D group (Fig. 2). These findings indicate that the enhanced ROS-scavenging capacity and proline accumulation help maintain cellular homeostasis by suppressing programmed cell death pathways. Given that elevated temperatures are known to trigger adaptive metabolic and physiological changes in plants [3], we propose that the altered transcriptional state observed in heat-primed plants, which could be underpinned by epigenetic mechanisms, contributes to this increased tolerance to secondary drought stress.

Moreover, studies have revealed that ROS-related redox signaling plays a crucial role in plant growth, development and environmental acclimation. For example, Huang et al. [18] reported that tomato plants slow shoot apical meristem maturation in response to heat stress to avoid reproductive failure. Heat-induced ROS prolong the transcriptional condensation of TERMINATING FLOWER (TMF), triggering transcriptional reprogramming that extends vegetative growth, which may represent a bet-hedging strategy for heat resistance in plants. Therefore, the ROS generated during heat priming may serve as signaling molecules that prolong the transcriptional condensation of TMF, inducing transcriptional reprogramming that promotes the synthesis of SOD, POD, CAT and proline under drought stress.

When subjected to abiotic stress, plants undergo chlorophyll degradation, which triggers a series of physiological responses, including leaf discoloration, wilting, yellowing and premature termination of the life cycle, consequently affecting stress resistance [19]. Drought stress impairs photosynthetic activity and causes the overaccumulation of ROS, leading to subsequent oxidative damage. In the present study, heat priming increased the chlorophyll content under subsequent drought stress. The observed simultaneous increase in SOD, POD, and CAT activities during secondary drought stress suggests a robust antioxidant response. As evidenced in other systems, such coordinated enhancement can be powerful enough to not just neutralize but drastically lower ROS levels below baseline, as demonstrated during short-term heat events [20]. Furthermore, the key role of specific enzymes like catalase in orchestrating this systemic tolerance is highlighted by studies on PtCAT2; its overexpression strengthens the entire antioxidant system (SOD, POD, CAT) and is directly linked to the preservation of chlorophyll and membrane integrity by modulating ROS balance [21]. Therefore, we propose that the enhanced ROS-scavenging capacity in our study creates a conducive cellular environment that supports the stability of proteins crucial for chlorophyll synthesis.

Transcriptome analysis provides a molecular basis for these physiological observations. Our transcriptome analysis reveals that heat priming systematically modulates metabolic pathways and transcriptional regulatory networks, reshaping the gene expression patterns of H. ammodendron seedlings under subsequent drought stress. This transcriptional reprogramming, potentially facilitated by epigenetic mechanisms, represents a key component of the observed stress memory. Specifically, the upregulation of DEGs enriched in pathways such as plant-pathogen interaction, MAPK signaling, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, along with the activation of AP2/ERF, GRAS, and WRKY transcription factor families (Figs. 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7), points to a coordinated response that enhances cell wall stability, stress signal transduction, and antioxidant capacity, ultimately improving survival rates and reducing cell mortality under secondary drought stress.

Notably, we identified potential crosstalk between the plant‑pathogen interaction and MAPK signaling pathways induced by heat priming. The common upregulation of MAPKKK1, MAPKK1, MAPK3, WRKY24, and WRKY241 in both pathways (Fig. 8) suggests that heat priming co-opts defense-related signaling networks to mount a more robust response to subsequent abiotic stress. This pre-activated signaling backbone could form the basis of the observed stress memory, enabling faster and more effective responses to drought.

Previous studies have shown that when plants interact with pathogens, their immune systems become activated, with the MAPK signaling pathway playing a key role. When pathogens invade, pattern recognition receptors on plant cell surfaces recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), thereby activating the MAPK signaling pathway. Through a series of phosphorylation events, this pathway transmits signals from the cell surface to the nucleus, regulating the expression of related genes and initiating plant defense responses such as the production of antimicrobial compounds and the strengthening of cell walls [22, 23].

High temperatures affect plant‒pathogen interactions in multiple ways. On the one hand, heat may alter pathogen physiology, increasing pathogen infectivity; on the other hand, heat interferes with plant immune responses, disrupting effector-triggered immunity (ETI) and rendering R gene-based resistance mechanisms ineffective. Therefore, the heat priming applied in this study may lead to inactivation of immune response proteins. Under such conditions, the plant’s MAPK signaling pathway likely requires fine-tuning in response to the combined stress of high temperature and pathogen infection [24].

Similarly, drought stress impacts plant‒pathogen interactions. Under drought conditions, plant growth and metabolism are suppressed, and the immune system of plants may also be affected. However, plants can increase their stress resistance by activating the MAPK signaling pathway to cope with the dual stresses of drought and pathogens. For example, under drought stress, the MAPK signaling pathway can regulate the synthesis and signaling of plant hormones, thereby increasing both drought resistance and disease resistance [25, 26]. The MAPK signaling pathway may also play an important role in the formation of stress memory. After stress, activation of the MAPK signaling pathway may cause changes in the expression of certain genes, which may be “remembered” by cells, enabling faster activation of the MAPK signaling pathway during subsequent stress events [27]. Therefore, the plant‒pathogen interaction pathway may participate in the formation of stress memory. In this study, the specific enrichment of plant‒pathogen interactions and MAPK signaling pathways in HD vs. CK indicates that, through heat priming, the MAPK pathway becomes activated, resulting in stress memory that enables faster and more effective responses to subsequent drought stress. This represents an acclimation mechanism, facilitated by phenotypic plasticity, that allows H. ammodendron to survive in prolonged arid environments.

Additionally, previous research revealed that CaWRKY40 in pepper (Capsicum annuum) participates in plant responses to heat stress [28], with the overexpression of CaWRKY40 reducing tobacco sensitivity to heat treatment. Another study [29] revealed that ZmWRKY40 overexpression increased drought tolerance in transgenic Arabidopsis. On the basis of these findings, we speculate that the significantly enriched and specifically upregulated differentially expressed gene WRKY24 (SS11020) in this study, potentially regulated by upstream HaMAPK3 (SS33086), may increase stress resistance during drought and represents an important candidate gene for functional analysis.

Our data demonstrate that heat priming suppresses lignin production under subsequent drought stress. While both HD and D treatments increased lignin content compared to controls, the accumulation was significantly lower in HD-treated seedlings (Fig. 9). Given that excessive lignification under stress can be detrimental to growth, this moderated lignin deposition may represent a more balanced resource allocation strategy, diverting energy from costly structural reinforcement towards protective metabolic processes and contributing to the improved tolerance observed.

Furthermore, among the specifically upregulated KEGG pathways in the transcriptome, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis was enriched. Plant phenylpropanoid metabolites serve as important precursors for lignin and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways, playing crucial regulatory roles in plant growth. Our results suggest that the heat priming-induced moderation of lignin biosynthesis is not merely a reduction in a stress response but a recalibration towards a more cost-effective defense strategy, optimizing resource allocation between structural reinforcement and metabolic protection to enhance overall drought tolerance. Lignin also influences plant reproductive development and provides resistance against both biotic and abiotic stresses. According to previous research [30, 31], as the first barrier against external threats, the plant cell wall relies heavily on lignin in this process. The accumulation of ROS caused by plant stress often accompanies lignin accumulation. Stress conditions can alter the expression levels of genes involved in lignin synthesis, leading to changes in plant lignin content, which may also cause alterations in xylem composition and ectopic lignin deposition, modifying the degree of lignification and affecting plant growth status [31]. In this study, the results of lignin content measurement, safranin-fast green staining, and phloroglucinol staining analysis revealed that after heat priming, the assimilating branches of H. ammodendron seedlings presented relatively low lignin contents under subsequent drought stress, indicating that heat priming improved seedling tolerance to secondary drought stress. Moreover, as suggested by the intriguing findings on lignin, future research should delve deeper into the specific enzymes and genes regulating the lignin biosynthesis pathway under combined stress priming. Investigating the spatial distribution of lignin polymers and their compositional changes will provide a more comprehensive understanding of their physiological significance in drought tolerance.

Our physiological and transcriptomic data suggest the establishment of a stress memory induced by heat priming. While the current transcriptome study reveals the reprogramming of gene expression networks, the priming phenomenon itself strongly implies the involvement of stable epigenetic marks that could underlie this transcriptional memory. Although not globally assessed in the present study, support for this hypothesis comes from our prior research on the specific epigenetic regulation of a key thermotolerance gene, HaFT−1, in H. ammodendron [14]. We previously demonstrated that heat stress priming (a first heat stress of 37 °C) led to the upregulation of the HaFT−1 gene following a subsequent, more severe heat stress (45 °C). Crucially, this transcriptional activation was accompanied by a significant reduction in the DNA methylation level at CGN sites within the HaFT−1 promoter, as determined by bisulfite sequencing PCR (BSP) [32]. This finding is further strengthened by our another analysis of the HaHsfA1e promoter, which showed that heat priming significantly reduced its DNA methylation level—predominantly in a CHH context—during a second heat stress. Interestingly, the expression patterns of five DNA methyltransferase genes under this memory treatment did not fully align with the observed demethylation trend, suggesting that the methylation status of these gene promoters is likely influenced by multiple regulatory factors [33]. Concurrently, the expression of several protein methyltransferase (PMT) genes was upregulated in the HaFT−1 case, suggesting a potential interplay between DNA and histone methylation in fine-tuning this stress-responsive expression. These cases provide direct evidence that heat priming can indeed induce locus-specific epigenetic modifications—specifically, DNA demethylation—in H. ammodendron, which is associated with the memorized gene expression response. Therefore, it is plausible that similar epigenetic mechanisms, such as the demethylation of key transcription factor promoters (e.g., the identified WRKYs) or stress-responsive genes within the MAPK and phenylpropanoid pathways, may be activated by the heat priming treatment in this study. These modifications could serve as a molecular basis for the more robust transcriptional activation observed under subsequent drought stress, ultimately contributing to the cross-stress resilience reported here.

Conclusions

In summary, H. ammodendron seedlings subjected to heat priming presented improved survival rates and reduced mortality under subsequent drought stress, along with significantly increased activities of the enzymes CAT, SOD, and POD, as well as elevated chlorophyll and proline contents. Among the enriched KEGG pathways in the transcriptome, the plant‒pathogen interaction, MAPK signaling, and phenylpropanoid metabolic pathways were the most prominent, with the plant‒pathogen interaction and MAPK signaling pathways showing the most significant upregulation under secondary drought stress. These pathways represent key mechanisms involved in plant responses to combined heat and drought stress, thereby providing valuable insights for cultivating stress-resistant H. ammodendron seedlings.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Seeds of Haloxylon ammodendron (C. A. Mey.) Bunge ex Fenzl were used in this study. The seeds were collected in October 2023 from a natural population located in the Gurbantünggüt Desert, near Fukang City (44°28′N, 88°11′E), Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, China. The collection site is characterized by typical desert vegetation and sandy soil [34]. The seeds were surface sterilized via sequential treatment with 75% ethanol for 3 min, three rinses with sterile water, 10 min in 10% sodium hypochlorite solution, and nine washes with sterile water. The sterilized seeds were germinated on Murashige-Skoog (MS) media (Ximeijie, China) and grown in a climate-controlled chamber at 22 °C under 16 h light/8 h dark cycles (light intensity: 120 µmol·m − 2·s − 1; relative humidity: 60%) for 60 days prior to the stress treatments.

Cross-stress treatment

Following established protocols [16, 35, 36], 60-day-old seedlings were subjected to four treatments. Each treatment consisted of three biological replicates. Each replicate comprised a pooled sample of 20 seedlings grown together in one culture flask, resulting in a total of 60 seedlings per treatment. The specific treatments were as follows: (1) control (CK): 26 °C for 12 h → 26 °C for 3 d → 26 °C for 19 d (sampled at the endpoint as CK); (2) heat priming + drought (HD): 37 °C for 12 h → 26 °C for 3 d → 480 mmol·L − 1 sorbitol (drought simulation) at 26 °C for 19 d (sampled at the endpoint as HD); (3) drought alone (D): 26 °C for 12 h → 26 °C for 3 d → 480 mmol·L − 1 sorbitol at 26 °C for 19 d (sampled at the endpoint as D); and (4) heat priming alone (H): 37 °C for 12 h → 26 °C for 3 d → 26 °C for 19 d (sampled at the endpoint as H).

Cell death assay

The assimilating shoots from each treatment group were collected for cell death assessment. For each treatment, three independent biological replicate samples were analyzed. Each replicate sample consisted of assimilating shoots pooled from one entire culture flask (initially containing 20 seedlings). These samples were stained using a Trypan Blue Staining Kit (Solarbio) according to the manufacturer’s protocol [34].

The stained samples were imaged immediately using an upright microscope system (ECLIPSE Ni, Nikon, Japan). For each of the three replicate samples per treatment, at least 30 cells were randomly selected and counted across multiple fields of view. The cell death rate for each replicate was calculated as (number of dead cells/total number of cells counted)×100%. The final result for each treatment is presented as the mean ± SD of the rates obtained from the three independent replicate samples.

The microscope was controlled using the NIS-Elements software series, and the acquired images were processed for contrast and brightness adjustment with Adobe Photoshop.

Physiological measurements

The proline content; peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) activities; and malondialdehyde (MDA) content were measured using commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng, China) [14]. The chlorophyll content was determined spectrophotometrically at OD645 and OD663 after shoot decolorization [14].

For all assays, approximately 100 mg of fresh assimilating shoot tissue was used per measurement. Each measurement was considered one biological replicate. The tissue for each replicate was collected by pooling assimilating shoots from one entire culture flask (initially containing 20 seedlings). Therefore, each treatment had 3 independent biological replicates, with each replicate representing a pooled sample from a distinct culture flask.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted from 100 mg of shoots via Biospin kits, followed by gDNA removal and cDNA synthesis with TransScript® One-Step SuperMix (TransGen, China). Three technical replicates were performed per sample.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Samples taken from the CK, HD, D and H groups were used for transcriptome analysis, with three independent biological replicates for each genotype at each time point. A total of 2 µg of RNA per sample was used for the RNA preparations. The sequencing libraries were generated via the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. A Qubit 2.0 fluorometer was used for preliminary quantification of the constructed library. The library was diluted to 1.5 ng·µL− 1, and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer was used to detect the insert size of the library. After passing the library quality control, clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System via a TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After cluster generation, the library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated.

Raw data (raw reads) in fastq format were first processed with in-house Perl scripts to remove reads with adaptor contamination, reads containing N bases and low-quality reads. Additionally, the Q20, Q30 and GC contents were calculated for the resulting clean reads. All the downstream analyses were based on high-quality clean reads.

The H. ammodendron reference genome and gene model annotation files were downloaded from the Figshare website (https://fgshare.com). HISAT2 v2.0.5 was employed to construct a genome index from the genome and align paired-end clean reads to it. The mapped reads of each sample were then assembled via StringTie (v1.3.3b) [37, 38]. FeatureCounts v1.5.0-p3 was used to count the read numbers mapped to each gene, and the FPKM of each gene was calculated on the basis of the length of the gene and the number of reads mapped to it. Pairwise differential expression analysis was carried out for each group and condition via the DESeq2 R package (1.20.0). Genes with an adjusted p value < 0.05 were considered to be differentially expressed. We used the clusterProfiler R package to determine the enrichment of differentially expressed genes in KEGG pathways with criteria of Padj < 0.05 and a p value < 0.05.

Lignin quantification and histochemistry

The lignin content was measured from 100 mg of shoots per treatment [39]. For histochemical staining, (1) phloroglucinol-HCl (lignin: red‒brown) [40] and (2) safranin-fast green (lignin: red; cellulose: green) [40] were used. The samples were then observed with an ECLIPSE Ni objective lens system for a Lambda D biological microscope (Nikon, Japan), and the NIS-Elements series was used as control software. All the images were processed with Adobe Photoshop.

qRT-PCR analysis

The expression of BI−1 (apoptosis-related), ATG12 (autophagy-related), and 10 randomly selected DEGs was analyzed via TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara) on a QuantStudio system. The 2−ΔΔCt method with normalization to 18 S rRNA was used, with three replicates per sample [14]. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Statistical analysis

All data from physiological measurements, cell death assays, lignin quantification, and qRT-PCR analysis are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of at least three independent biological replicates. Each biological replicate consisted of a pooled sample of assimilating shoots from one entire culture flask (initially containing 20 seedlings).

Statistical significance of differences among the four treatment groups (CK, HD, D, H) was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM, USA).
