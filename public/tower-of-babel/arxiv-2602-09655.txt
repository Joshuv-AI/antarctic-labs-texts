Strategy optimization for Bayesian quantum parameter estimation with finite copies: Adaptive greedy, parallel, sequential, and general strategies Preprint: APS/123-QED
Erik L. André, Jessica Bavaresco, Mohammad Mehboudi

Abstract
In this work, we study Bayesian quantum parameter estimation given a finite number of uses of the process encoding one or more unknown physical quantities. For multiple uses, it is conventional to classify quantum metrological protocols as parallel, sequential, or indefinite causal order. Within each class, the central question is to determine the optimal strategy—namely, the choice of optimal input state, control operations, measurement, and estimator(s)—to perform the estimation task. Using the formalism of higher-order operations, we develop an algorithm that looks for the optimal solution, and we provide an efficient numerical implementation based on semidefinite programming. Our benchmark examples, specifically those against existing analytical solutions, demonstrate how powerful and precise our method is. We further explore the potential of adaptive greedy strategies, which are based on classical feedforward to design the optimal protocol for the next round. Using this framework, we compare the optimal achievable Bayesian score across classes. We demonstrate the strength of our algorithm in several examples, from single to multiparameter estimation and with various prior distributions. Particularly, we find examples in which there is a strict hierarchy between different classes.



I
Introduction

The Bayesian approach to metrology is a very successful method in estimating unknown parameters of physical systems
von Toussaint 2011
;
Trotta 2008
;
Thrane and Talbot 2019
. In quantum systems specifically, it has the potential to advise on how to design the probing protocol as well, namely, how to prepare the optimal quantum state for the probe and the optimal measurement on the probe after acquiring information about the unknown parameter(s)
Helstrom 1969
;
Personick 1971
;
Li et al. 2018
;
Morelli et al. 2021
;
Rubio and Dunningham 2020
. Particularly when dealing with finite data
Bavaresco et al. 2024
;
Meyer et al. 2025
;
Rubio 2021
, it can advise us how to generate and optimally use such data. Nonetheless, except in specific cases
Personick 1971
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański 2011
;
Macieszczak et al. 2014
;
Rubio 2022
—where the Bayesian cost function is simple enough, such as quadratic distance—analytical solutions to the problem do not exist.
Recently, there has been an extensive focus in coming up with algorithms that efficiently find the optimal protocol numerically
Bavaresco et al. 2024
;
Meyer et al. 2025
;
Kaubruegger et al. 2023
. Specifically, the approach proposed in
Bavaresco et al. 2024
allows one to find the optimal single-shot strategy approximately, with arbitrary precision, being also suitable for multiparameter estimation.

On a different front, talking about parameter estimation with quantum systems always brings up the subject of quantum advantages
Braunstein and Caves 1994
;
Giovannetti et al. 2006
;
Giovannetti et al. 2011
;
Paris 2009
;
Tóth and Apellaniz 2014
. That is, when having access to a fixed number of calls to the process that encodes the parameter (a quantum channel), how can quantum resources assist to better estimate the set of parameters. One often categorizes causally ordered quantum-assisted protocols into two main classes: parallel (PAR) and sequential (SEQ) strategies
Giovannetti et al. 2006
;
van Dam et al. 2007
, which we will define rigorously later, with quantum or classical memory
Ohst et al. 2026
. Beyond this, indefinite causal order (ICO) protocols
Hardy
;
Chiribella et al. 2013
are also explored for potential quantum advantages; however, quantum realizations of these processes are generally unknown.
The comparison between the different protocols, and whether there is a strict hierarchy between them, is a topic that is well studied beyond quantum metrology, for instance, in quantum channel discrimination
Chiribella 2012
;
Bavaresco et al. 2021
;
Bavaresco et al. 2022
, quantum communication
Ebler et al. 2018
;
Feix et al. 2015
and computation
Renner and Brukner 2022
;
Araújo et al. 2014
. The study of parallel, sequential and ICO strategies for quantum metrology has mainly been carried out in the asymptotic limit of large data with the frequentist approach, namely, when the quantum Fisher information (QFI) is a suitable figure of merit
Zhao et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Yin et al. 2023
;
Kurdziałek et al. 2025
.

In a Bayesian formalism with a general cost function, performing such a comparison has not been possible yet, due to the difficulty in finding the optimal solution in each class. This is the gap that we intend to bridge here. Particularly, we develop the necessary tools and provide their numerical implementation, to find the optimal strategies for parallel, sequential, and indefinite causal order protocols. We also consider adaptive
greedy
protocols as a viable solution for scenarios with limited or no access to quantum memory. A priori, one knows that the class of sequential strategies contains, as particular cases, both parallel strategies and adaptive greedy strategies with classical memory, while adaptive greedy and parallel protocols are not subsets of one another. At the same time, the class of general strategies, which may exhibit an indefinite causal order, contains sequential strategies as a particular case.

We use our methods to demonstrate via several examples that these hierarchies can be strict in certain Bayesian metrology protocols. Furthermore, we also find examples in which all protocols perform equally well.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In Section
II
we define a generic Bayesian estimation problem which also sets up our notation. In Section
III
we discuss different classes of protocols for parameter estimation, namely adaptive greedy, parallel, sequential, and indefinite causal order. In Section
IV
we present the algorithms that enable an efficient numerical solution to the different protocols. In Section
V
we exploit our formalism to find the optimal protocols in several examples, from single to multiparameter estimation, from unitary to dissipative encoding, and with various prior distributions. In simple cases with specific symmetries where analytical answers exist, our numerical results predict exactly the same solutions, demonstrating their validity and strength. Finally, in Section
VI
we conclude by discussing potential future directions.

II
Framework: Single-shot scenario

We start with the basic setting, i.e. a single-shot scenario. Multiple-shot scenarios, which are the main subject of this work, will be discussed in the next section. The Bayes’ rule is used to update one’s belief about a set of
q
unknown parameters
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, captured by the
prior
probability density
p(\bm{\theta})
conditioned on new evidence (observation)
i
to obtain the
posterior
distribution
p({\bm{\theta}}|i)

Berger 1985
;
Bernardo and Smith 1994
;
von Toussaint 2011

\displaystyle p({\bm{\theta}}|i)=\frac{p(i|{\bm{\theta}})p({\bm{\theta}})}{p(i)},

(1)

where
p(i|{\bm{\theta}})
is the probability of observing outcome
i
conditioned on the parameters being
\bm{\theta}
, and with
p(i)=\int\differential{\bm\theta}p(i|{\bm{\theta}})p({\bm{\theta}})
being the marginal likelihood.
The Bayesian formalism is a strong approach to metrology, as it brings together both the prior knowledge and the new evidence together to assign an estimate
{{\bm{\hat{\theta}}}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
to the unknown parameters
{\bm{\theta}}
.
The quality of the estimation is often quantified by using a cost function
c({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\hat{\theta}}}_{i})
that needs to be chosen thoughtfully depending on the problem. The choice of the cost function is also determining in designing the optimal estimation procedure.

In quantum physics, the parameters are encoded via completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) channels
\Lambda_{{\bm{\theta}}}:{\cal L}({\cal H}_{\rm I})\mapsto{\cal L}({\cal H}_{\rm O})
—which we also refer to as quantum channels

;
Nielsen and Chuang 2012
;
Wilde 2016
. Here,
{\cal L}({\cal H}_{\rm I})
and
{\cal L}({\cal H}_{\rm O})
represent the set of operators acting on the input Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{\rm I}
with dimension
d_{\rm I}
and the output Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{\rm O}
with dimension
d_{\rm O}
, respectively. Often, it is advantageous to consider probes (inputs to the channel) that are correlated to auxiliary systems that do not undergo the action of the CPTP map. Denoting their joint density operator with
\rho\in{\cal L}({\cal H}_{\rm I}\otimes{\cal H}_{\rm aux})
, the output reads
\rho_{{\bm{\theta}}}\coloneqq(\Lambda_{{\bm{\theta}}}\otimes\mathds{1})\rho\in{\cal L}({\cal H}_{\rm O}\otimes{\cal H}_{\rm aux})
, where the auxiliary Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{\rm aux}
has dimension
d_{\rm I}
. Furthermore, the label
i
refers to the different outcomes of a measurement represented by a positive operator-valued measure (POVM)
M\coloneqq\{M_{i}\}_{i}
, with
M_{i}\in{\cal L}({\cal H}_{\rm O}\otimes{\cal H}_{\rm aux})
. The Bayesian figure of merit, the

\displaystyle{\cal S}\coloneqq\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}})\sum_{i}p(i|{\bm{\theta}})c({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\hat{\theta}}}_{i}),

(2)

where we took a discrete POVM for simplicity.
Note that the summation corresponds to the average cost over outcomes. The integral is then averaging over the parameter space using the prior.
By using the Born’s rule, one can rewrite the score as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}})\sum_{i}\Tr\left[M_{i}\left(\Lambda_{\bm{\theta}}\otimes\mathds{1}\right)\rho\right]c({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\hat{\theta}}}_{i}).

(3)

For this single-shot setup, solving the optimization problem amounts to finding the triplet
\{\rho^{\ast},M^{\ast},{\bm{\hat{\theta}}}^{\ast}\}
that maximizes or minimizes the score, depending on whether
c(\bm{\theta},\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i})
is a reward or penalty function, respectively. Generally, a known analytical solution does not exist. However, there exists a viable algorithm, based on the formalism of higher-order operations
Taranto et al. 2025
, to find the optimal solution numerically
Bavaresco et al. 2024
. Inspired by this, in the present work, we extend this framework to protocols that can access several uses of the channel. This allows full exploitation of quantum correlations and joint quantum operations. Before that, let us briefly review the single-shot algorithm.
The score (
3
) can be rewritten as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}})\sum_{i}\Tr\left[T_{i}J_{\bm{\theta}}\right]c({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\hat{\theta}}}_{i}),

(4)

with operator
J_{\bm{\theta}}\coloneqq\sum_{ij}\outerproduct{i}{j}\otimes\Lambda_{\bm{\theta}}[\outerproduct{i}{j}]\in\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{I}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{O}}\right)
being the Choi-Jamiołkowski (CJ) representation of the channel
\Lambda_{\bm{\theta}}
. The operators
T\coloneqq\{T_{i}\}_{i}
with
T_{i}\in{\cal L}({\cal H}_{\rm I}\otimes{\cal H}_{\rm O})
, are called
testers

Chiribella et al. 2008a
;
Chiribella et al. 2008b
;
Ziman 2008
;
Chiribella et al. 2009
, and can be constructed from the state
\rho
and measurement
M
according to

\displaystyle T_{i}\coloneqq\Tr_{\rm aux}\left[(\rho^{T_{\rm aux}}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\rm O})(\mathds{1}^{\rm I}\otimes M_{i}^{T})\right],

(5)

with
\rho^{T_{\rm aux}}
being the partial transpose of
\rho
with respect to the auxiliary Hilbert space. Noting that
p(i|{\bm{\theta}})=\Tr(T_i J_{\bm\theta})
, one can prove (
4
). This expression is useful because it replaces the nonlinear dependence on
\rho
and
M
with a linear dependence on
T
. As a result, the optimization problem over
T
can be solved using semidefinite programming (SDP). However, one must restrict to the set of physical testers, which can be fully characterized via the following linear constraints
Chiribella et al. 2008a
;
Chiribella et al. 2008b
;
Ziman 2008
;
Chiribella et al. 2009

\displaystyle T_{i}

\displaystyle\geq 0,\quad\forall i

(6a)

\displaystyle\sum_{i}T_{i}

\displaystyle=\sigma\otimes\mathds{1}^{\rm O},

(6b)

where
\sigma\in{\cal L}(\cal H^{\rm I})
is a valid quantum state satisfying
\sigma\geq 0
and
\Tr(\sigma)=1
. Furthermore, one can construct
\rho
and
M_{i}
from the optimal

as follows

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\mathds{1}^{\rm I}\otimes\sqrt{\sigma^{T}})\sum_{ij}\outerproduct{ii}{jj}(\mathds{1}^{\rm I}\otimes\sqrt{\sigma^{T}}),

(7a)

\displaystyle M_{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\sqrt{\sigma}^{-1}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\rm O})\ T_{i}^{T}\ (\sqrt{\sigma}^{-1}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\rm O}),\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall i.

(7b)

Different sets of state and measurement yielding the same tester will also give the same score, as (
4
) just depends on the probability distribution
p(i|\bm{\theta})
.

III
Multi-copy quantum parameter estimation

Having reviewed the single-shot case, we are now ready to address the multi-copy scenario, the main focus of this work.

1

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\coloneqq{\cal S}(T,\hat{\bm{\theta}})=\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}})\sum_{i}\Tr\left(T_{i}J_{\bm{\theta}}^{\otimes k}\right)c({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\hat{\theta}}}_{i}),

III.2
Sequential strategies

Sequential strategies allow for adaptive uses of the channel: one can interleave the

III.3
Indefinite causal order strategies

Finally, we define general/ICO strategies as the most general transformations that map
k
independent quantum channels
into a valid probability distribution, even when acting only on part of the input quantum channels. In terms of testers, we define
T^{\mathrm{gen}}\coloneqq\{T_{i}^{\mathrm{gen}}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{O}}}
, with
T_{i}^{\mathrm{gen}}\in{\cal L}(({\cal H}_{\rm I}\otimes{\cal H}_{\rm O})^{\otimes k})
. For
W^{\mathrm{gen}}\coloneqq\sum_{i}T_{i}^{\mathrm{gen}}
, the set of general testers is defined by the following constraints

\displaystyle T_{i}^{\mathrm{gen}}

\displaystyle\geq 0,\quad\forall i,

(13a)

\displaystyle\Tr\left[W^{\mathrm{gen}}(J_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes J_{k})\right]

\displaystyle=1,

(13b)

for all
J_{l}
, with
l\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, that are Choi operators of quantum channels.
Note that the last condition, when
J_{l}=J_{\bm{\theta}}
for all

, is equivalent to the normalization of the outcome probability distribution
p(i|{\bm{\theta}})
. This constraint sets no assumption on the causal order of the
k

k=2
and

The realization of a general/ICO tester can be understood in terms of a process matrix

and additionally on a “future” Hilbert space

on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{F}}

W^{\mathrm{gen}}

and the measurement elements can be taken to be

, where

III.4
Adaptive greedy strategy (Hybrid quantum-classical)

Finally, we introduce the adaptive greedy algorithm with two motivations: first, it can be seen as one of the most sophisticated protocols that does not use quantum memory, and hence can provide a good benchmark for memory-assisted protocols. Second, it can complement and boost the previous protocols in practical scenarios that one has access to limited capacity. Namely, that given a total number of calls to the channel
k^{\prime}=m\times k
, one has the limited capacity to perform parallel, sequential, or ICO strategies on at most
m
copies at a time (e.g. due to memory restrictions). Yet, one can use an adaptive greedy algorithm between consecutive
k
calls to further improve the precision via a Hybrid quantum-classical approach.

In what follows, we briefly explain this scenario for a general
m
. Numerical implementation is explained in Sec.
IV
.
Given
k^{\prime}=m\times k
calls to the channel, the algorithm uses them in adaptive rounds
c\in\{1,\dots,k\}
as follows:

1.

Local Optimization:

Using the current prior distribution
p^{(c-1)}
, calculate the optimal protocol (testers
T^{*(c)}
and estimators
\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{*(c)}
) for the current batch of
m
channel uses. This step is
greedy
because it optimizes the immediate score for the current batch without foreseeing future calls.

2.

Measurement:

Apply the optimal protocol to the
m
channel copies. In a practical scenario, one performs the physical measurement to obtain outcome
s
. In the numerical implementation (see Sec.
IV
), this outcome is simulated probabilistically based on the sample’s “true” parameter(s).

3.

Bayesian Update:

Use the obtained outcome
s
to compute the posterior distribution via Bayes’ rule (Eq. (
1
)). This posterior becomes the prior
p^{(c)}
for the next adaptive round.

4.

Repeat:

Iterate steps 1–3 until the total budget of
k^{\prime}=m\times k
calls is exhausted.

IV
Numerical implementation

As already mentioned, expressing the score in terms of testers allows for an efficient optimization via an SDP. In principle, the optimal tester has at most
d_{\max}=(d_{\rm I}^{k}\times d_{\rm O}^{k})^{2}
many outcomes, since the optimal POVM should be extremal, and since extremal POVMs in dimension
d
have at most
d^{2}
many outcomes. Nonetheless, it is sometimes beneficial to inflate the number of outcomes such that
N_{\mathrm{O}}\geq d_{\rm max}
, which is a useful approach in finding the optimal estimators as well
Bavaresco et al. 2024
.

Furthermore, the integral over the hypothesis parameters
\bm{\theta}
in (
8
) can be approximated by a finite sum. To this aim, we discretize the parameter space by replacing the continuous domain
\Theta
with a finite set of sample points
\{\bm{\theta}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{\rm H}}
, where
\bm{\theta}_{j}\in\Theta
for all
j
. The choice of this set depends on the nature of the prior distribution
p(\bm{\theta})
and the cost function. For distributions close to uniform, a regular mesh (grid) is often suitable. However, for sharper priors—which naturally arise as posteriors after several measurement updates—more adaptive strategies are beneficial. Namely, importance sampling (Monte Carlo) can be used to allocate samples according to the probability density
Press et al. 2007
.

Regardless of the sampling method, this discretization allows us to enumerate the
q
-dimensional vector of parameters using a single linear index
j\in\{1,\dots,N_{H}\}
. This unifies the notation, allowing us to treat single and multiparameter estimation problems identically by using a single summation. Furthermore, the estimators naturally form a finite list, as they are in a one to one correspondence with the POVM outcomes, so we define
\{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\rm O}}\eqqcolon{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}
. Consequently, the continuous score (
8
) is approximated by a discrete sum (with an approximation error typically scaling as
\mathcal{O}(N_{\rm H}^{-2/q})
for a regular mesh and
\mathcal{O}(N_{\rm H}^{-1/2})
for importance sampling):

\widetilde{\cal S}(T,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}})\simeq\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm H}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm O}}p_{j}c({\bm{\theta}}_{j},\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{i})\text{Tr}\left(T_{i}J_{{\bm{\theta}}_{j}}^{\otimes k}\right),

(14)

where we defined
p_{j}\coloneqq p({\bm{\theta}}={\bm{\theta}_{j}})/\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm H}}p({\bm{\theta}}={\bm{\theta}_{j}})
.

Eq. (
14
) is a good approximation to the exact score, as it can be made very close to the true score by simply increasing
N_{\mathrm{H}}
, which is computationally not demanding.
Our optimization problem is thus reduced to

\displaystyle\widetilde{\cal S}^{*}\coloneqq\operatorname*{opt}_{\{T,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}\}}\ \widetilde{\cal S}(T,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}})\text{ s.t. }\{T,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}\}\text{ satisfy }\eqref{eq:const_par},\ \eqref{eq:const_seq},\ \text{or }\eqref{eq:const_gen},

where
{\rm opt}
denotes either maximization or minimization depending on the cost function.

For a fixed set of estimators, we can formulate the problem of finding the optimal testers as an SDP, which is highly efficient. By taking
N_{\mathrm{O}}\gg d_{\max}
and choosing uniformly picked fix estimators, one can also assure that the problem is fully solved without requiring to optimize over estimators
Bavaresco et al. 2024
. However, we take the seesaw approach as explained in Box
IV
, which is numerically more efficient.

Box 1:
Finding the optimal Bayesian protocol

1.

Initialization:

Select an initial set of estimators denoted by
{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}^{(0)}
. Set the iteration counter
c=1
.

2.

Step 1 (Optimize Testers):

Fix the estimators to their current value
{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}^{(c-1)}
. Solve the SDP problem to find the optimal testers
T^{(c)}
that optimize the score:

T^{(c)}=\underset{T}{\text{arg opt}}\ {\widetilde{\cal S}}(T,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}^{(c-1)})

subject to constraints given by (
9
), (
10
), or (
13
).

3.

Step 2 (Optimize Estimators):

Fix the testers to the optimal values
T^{(c)}
found in the previous step. Optimize the estimators to find
{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}^{(c)}
:

{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}^{(c)}=\underset{\hat{\theta}}{\text{arg opt}}\ {\widetilde{\cal S}}(T^{(c)},{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}).

4.

Convergence Check:

Evaluate the change in the score:

\Delta=|{\widetilde{\cal S}}(T^{(c)},{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}^{(c)})-{\widetilde{\cal S}}(T^{(c-1)},{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}^{(c-1)})|

If
\Delta<\epsilon
(where
\epsilon
is a pre-defined tolerance), terminate and output
T^{*}=T^{(c)}
,
{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}^{*}={\hat{\bm{\theta}}}^{(c)}
, and

. Otherwise, increment

and return to Step 1.

All codes implementing this algorithm for the three protocols are available in
André 2026
.

Lastly, let us remark on the numerical implementation of the adaptive greedy protocol. In this case, we take a Monte Carlo approach to simulate the optimal score, which is explained in Box
IV
.

Box 2:
The adaptive greedy Algorithm

1.

Initialization:
Define cost
c(\bm{\theta},\hat{\bm{\theta}})
and the initial prior
\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{H}}}
.

2.

Initial Optimization (independent of the Monte Carlo round)

Find optimal testers

and estimators
{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}^{*(1)}

according to Box

.

3.

Monte Carlo simulation Loop:
Set the total number of Monte Carlo trajectories,
N_{m}\gg 1
. For
l\in\{1,\dots,N_{m}\}
:

(a)

Initialize the prior:
(Re)set the current prior to
\{p^{(0)}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{H}}}=\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{H}}}
.

(b)

Pick
j_{\text{true}}
from
\{p_{j}^{(0)}\}_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{H}}}
. Set

\bm{\theta}^{(l)}_{\text{true}}={\bm{\theta}}_{j_{\text{true}}}
.

(c)

Adaptive Rounds:
For copy
c\in\{1,\dots,k\}
:

i.

Optimization:
If c>1, find optimal testers
T^{*(c)}
and estimators
{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}^{*(c)}
using prior
\{p^{(c-1)}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{H}}}

.

Simulation:

P(\nu|\bm{\theta}^{(l)}_{\text{true}})=\text{Tr}\big(T_{\nu}^{(c)}J_{\bm{\theta}^{(l)}_{\text{true}}}^{\otimes m}\big).

Update Estimator:
Set

Update prior. For

p^{(c)}_{j}=\frac{p^{(c-1)}_{j}\text{Tr}\big(T_{s}^{(c)}J_{{\bm{\theta}}_{j}}^{\otimes m}\big)}{\mathcal{N}}

(15)

V
Examples

We will now use our methods to solve several problems. Some of these have analytical solutions, namely the SU(2) multiparameter estimation problem, and are hence ideal for benchmarking our methods. In thermometry, we find no hierarchy between strategies, with the greedy approach at
m=1
matching the optimal sequential protocol. In noisy SU(2), in contrast, we find a strict hierarchy across the considered protocols. We leave a fourth example on quantum phase estimation to Appendix
D
, where we also observe a strict hierarchy between the different strategies. Furthermore, our methods are not bound to specific prior distributions. We remind that even if one starts with an elegant prior that enables partial analytical solutions (e.g., in the SU(2) problem), in a greedy strategy posteriors do not necessarily respect any symmetries. Our methods treat the problem equally, regardless of the prior.

V.1
SU(2) multiparameter estimation

Let us start with a multiparameter estimation problem. To this aim, take a (single-qubit) unitary transformation
U_{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathrm{SU(2)}
to be parametrized by a vector of unknown angles,
\bm{\theta}\coloneqq(\theta_{x},\theta_{y},\theta_{z})
such that
U_{\bm{\theta}}\coloneqq e^{-i\bm{\theta}\cdot\bm{\sigma}}
, with
\bm{\sigma}\coloneqq(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})
being the vector of Pauli matrices. This estimation task is crucial, for instance, in spatial orientation and reference-frame alignment
Bartlett et al. 2007
;
Bagan et al. 2004a
. In the context of quantum metrology, several applications include two-mode interferometry
Pezzè et al. 2015
and Ramsey-type spectroscopy
Kaubruegger et al. 2023
;
Schulte et al. 2020
.

The channel corresponding to the unitary that encodes the parameters is then given by
\Lambda_{\bm{\theta}}[\cdot]\coloneqq U_{\bm{\theta}}\cdot U_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger}
, with
d_{\rm I}=d_{\rm O}=2
.
Note that the SU(2) group is isomorphic to the group of quaternions with norm 1. That is, for any
U\in{\rm SU(2)}
there exists a vector
q\coloneqq(q_{0},q_{1},q_{2},q_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
with
\norm{q}^{2}=q^{T}q=1
such that
U\equiv q_{0}I-i(q_{1}\sigma_{x}+q_{2}\sigma_{y}+q_{3}\sigma_{3})
. Conversely, any
q\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
with
q^{T}q=1
defines an element of
\mathrm{SU(2)}
through the same formula.

Regarding the cost function, it is conventional to take the fidelity between the Choi operators of the true parameter and its estimator
Raginsky 2001
;
Gilchrist et al. 2005
. Namely,

c({\bm{\theta}},\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{i})=\dfrac{1}{4}\Tr(J_{\bm\theta} J_{\hat{{\bm\theta}}_i})=\dfrac{1}{4}\absolutevalue{\Tr\left( U^{\dagger}_{\bm\theta} U_{\hat{\bm\theta}_i} \right)}^{2}=\left(q_{\bm{\theta}}^{T}q_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}}\right)^{2}

(16)

Note that
0\leq c({\bm{\theta}},\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{i})\leq 1
, and we are dealing with a
maximization
problem. In a memory-assisted scenario with
k
simultaneous calls to the channel, the optimal tester has
d_{\rm max}=4^{2k}
many outcomes.
As we discuss in Appendix
A.1
, one can conveniently identify the optimal estimator for a given observation by its quaternion representation,

q_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}}^{*}\coloneqq\underset{q}{\rm argmax}\penalty\ q^{T}Kq,

(17)

with
K\coloneqq\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}}|i)q_{\bm{\theta}}q_{\bm{\theta}}^{T}
, being
q_{\bm{\theta}}
the quaternion associated to
U_{\bm{\theta}}
. Note that this problem reduces to finding the eigenstate of
K
with the maximum eigenvalue. As
K
is a
4\times 4
matrix, the optimal estimators can be found efficiently with (
17
).

Furthermore, in the specific case that the prior over SU(2) is Haar random
Spengler et al. 2012
, the optimal score can be found analytically and is achieved by parallel strategies. In particular
Bagan et al. 2004b
;
Quintino and Ebler 2022
,

\displaystyle{\cal S}^{*}=\cos^{2}\left(\dfrac{\pi}{k+3}\right).

(18)

The optimal parallel strategy achieving this score was established earlier in
Acín et al. 2001
. Furthermore, neither sequential
Chiribella et al. 2008b
nor ICO
Bavaresco et al. 2022
;
Hayashi 2025
strategies can outperform the parallel case.

We apply our methods to determine the optimal score for this problem using the Haar random prior distribution, details of which are provided in Appendix
A.2
. This serves as a robust benchmark due to the existence of an analytical solution for this prior. Taking
k=2
, we find that
{\cal{\widetilde{S}}}^{*}\approx 0.6545=\cos^{2}(\pi/5)
—matching the exact solution (
18
)—for parallel, sequential, and indefinite causal order strategies.
We then benchmark the performance of the adaptive greedy algorithm against the optimal strategy in Figure
2
. The performance gap between the two strategies indicates that global operations are indeed advantageous, however, at the limit of few copies the greedy algorithm remains a viable alternative; e.g., to get the same score as the one that the best quantum strategy achieves with
k=4
simultaneous calls to the channel, one can use only
k=5
single calls with the adaptive greedy strategy. To complete the analysis, we simulate a non-adaptive strategy without memory in between the uses of the channels. In this approach, we optimize the testers for the first channel use and fix them for all remaining consecutive calls. This strategy generally under-performs compared to the greedy strategy due to the lack of adaptivity, as it is also seen in the figure.

Comparison between different strategies in the SU(2) multiparameter estimation. The optimal protocol (blue dashed) outperforms the greedy strategy (solid green) which in turn outperforms the non-adaptive and non-memory-assisted strategy (gray solid).

calls is as good as the best quantum memory-assisted strategy with

.

V.2
Thermometry

In this example we focus on quantum thermometry
Mehboudi et al. 2019
;
De Pasquale and Stace 2018
. Contrary to our previous example which involved a unitary encoding, the channel that encodes the temperature is inherently dissipative.
In this setting, the unknown parameter is the temperature
\theta
of a sample (or thermal reservoir) at equilibrium. We take the channel’s input and output dimensions to be
d_{\rm I}=d_{\rm O}=2
, i.e., a two-level system (qubit). While initially at time
t=0
the input to the channel can be correlated to auxiliary systems, and other probes entering copies of the channel, the probes and the sample are assumed to be uncorrelated. After a fixed interaction time
t
, a joint measurement on the probes and auxiliaries is performed in order to estimate the bath temperature. In order to identify the encoding channel, recall that in a single call case the input to the channel—i.e., the marginal input of the probe-auxiliary
\rho^{p}_{\theta}\coloneqq\Tr_{A}(\rho_{\theta})
—evolves according to a standard Markovian quantum master equation
Breuer and Petruccione 2007

\dot{\rho}_{\theta}^{p}(t)=-i\commutator{H}{\rho_\theta^p (t)}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}\mathcal{D}\left[\sigma_{+}\right]\rho_{\theta}^{p}(t)+\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}}\mathcal{D}\left[\sigma_{-}\right]\rho_{\theta}^{p}(t),

(19)

where
H=\epsilon\outerproduct{1}{1}
is the Hamiltonian of the probe,
\sigma_{-}
(
\sigma_{+}
) is the lowering (raising) Pauli operator, and
\mathcal{D}[A]\rho=A\rho A^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\anticommutator{A^\dagger A}{\rho}
denotes the usual Lindblad dissipator, describing the coupling to the bath. The only temperature-dependent quantities are the excitation and relaxation rates
\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}
and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}}
, which for a bosonic reservoir are given by
\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}=J(\epsilon)N_{\rm B}(\epsilon)
and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}}=J(\epsilon)\left(1+N_{\rm B}(\epsilon)\right)
, where
J(\epsilon)
is the bath spectral density and
N_{\rm B}(\epsilon)=(e^{\epsilon/\theta}-1)^{-1}
is the thermal occupation number of the bosonic mode at energy
\epsilon
. We note that our methods can be seamlessly applied to fermionic baths as well. We also note that the setup covers equilibrium thermometry (
t\to\infty
) where the initial state and control operations are expected not to matter, only the measurement choice to have an impact, as well as non-equilibrium scenarios where in principle all parts of the protocol may be determinant.

For this problem, the channel
\Lambda_{\theta}(t)
is generated by the dynamics given in (
19
), and the analytical expressions for both the action of the channel on an initial state and the Choi operator have already been derived in the literature
Bavaresco et al. 2024
(see also Appendix
B
for the explicit expressions). Regarding the cost function in thermometry, it is conventional to take either the mean square logarithmic error or the relative mean square error
Rubio et al. 2021
;
Mehboudi et al. 2022
;
Jørgensen et al. 2022
;
Boeyens et al. 2021
. Here, we take the latter, but we expect a similar behavior using the former:

c(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{i})=\left(\dfrac{\theta-\hat{\theta}_{i}}{\theta}\right)^{2},

(20)

which needs to be
minimized
to find the optimal strategy. An advantage of this figure of merit is that the optimal measurements are PVMs and thus
d_{\rm max}=4^{k}
. Furthermore, conveniently, the optimal estimator is known to be
Boeyens et al. 2021

\hat{\theta}_{i}^{*}=\dfrac{\expectationvalue{1/\theta_k}^{(i)}}{\expectationvalue{1/\theta_k^2}^{(i)}},

(21)

where
\expectationvalue{\cdot}^{(i)}
indicates taking the mean over the posterior probability distribution
p(\theta|i)
.

In Figure
3
, we benchmark the optimal score for parallel, sequential, and ICO strategies against the adaptive greedy strategy. We observe that the latter equals the general sequential case for all times, which means that the strategy is optimal without the need for entanglement between the different uses of the channel (note that this does not rule out entanglement with auxiliary systems, which can give an advantage over separable states in the transient regime
Bavaresco et al. 2024
). Moreover, even non-adaptive classical strategies perform equally good, hence adaptive manipulation of the setting seems unnecessary.
Let us further point out that the fact that the parallel approach is expected to scale with
k
in the asymptotic limit. To this aim, one can study the QFI—a relevant figure of merit in the asymptotic limit of many channel calls
k\gg 1
. As we show in Appendix
B
, the QFI for the parallel strategies scales at most linearly with the number of calls to the channel, therefore not beating the shot-noise limit and being realizable with a classical strategy.

\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}

and

V.3
Dissipative encoding of SU(2)

As our last example we take a more complex channel, in which we showcase a strict hierarchy between the memory-assisted strategies. Namely, that the sequential strategy outperforms the parallel strategy, while being outperformed by an ICO strategy. In this case, the greedy algorithm underperforms compared to all the memory-assisted strategies, as it happened in the noiseless unitary estimation problem considered before. The parameters to be estimated and the figure of merit are the same ones as the SU(2) example.

Following
Liu et al. 2023
, we consider a family of channels obtained by first encoding a vector of desired parameters
\bm{\theta}
via the SU(2) unitary
U_{\bm{\theta}}
as described previously, followed by an undesired amplitude-damping (AD) channel that adds noise to the system. We note that, unlike the thermometry problem, this noise here is not inherent to the encoding; it rather arises from imperfect transmission. The Kraus operators of the amplitude damping channel are given by
Nielsen and Chuang 2012

{\bar{K}}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sqrt{1-p}\end{pmatrix},\quad{\bar{K}}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt{p}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

(22)

where
p\in[0,1]
quantifies the decay (
p=0
corresponds to the identity map, while
p=1
maps every input state to the ground state, hence wiping out the encoded information). The overall channel is therefore
\Lambda_{\bm{\theta}}=\mathrm{AD}\circ\mathcal{U}_{\bm{\theta}}

which admits Kraus operators
\{K_{1}={\bar{K}}_{1}U_{\bm{\theta}},\,K_{2}={\bar{K}}_{2}U_{\bm{\theta}}\}
.

As already advanced, our goal is to estimate the set of parameters
\bm{\theta}
encoded in the unitary
U_{\bm{\theta}}
(and not to estimate the full noisy channel).
The cost function that we consider remains the same as that given by Eq. (
16
), i.e.,
c({\bm{\theta}},{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}_{i})=\frac{1}{4}{\rm Tr}(J^{U}_{\bm{\theta}}J^{U}_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}})
, where to avoid confusion, we denoted
J_{\bm{\theta}}^{U}
to be the Choi-Jamiolkowski representation of the unitary channel
U_{\bm{\theta}}
. In contrast,
J_{\bm{\theta}}^{p}
is the Choi-Jamiolkowski representation of the total channel
\Lambda_{\bm{\theta}}
, which is what we need to substitute for calculation of the outcome probabilities—that is
{\rm Tr}[T_{i}(J^{p}_{\bm{\theta}})^{\otimes k}]
and
{\rm Tr}[T_{i}(J^{p}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{j}})^{\otimes k}]
in (
8
) and (
14
), respectively.
In Figure
4
we plot the maximum approximate score versus
p
. For the extreme cases of
p\in\{0,1\}
, one observes no hierarchy between parallel, sequential and ICO strategies, while greedy underperforms all three. Namely, for
p=0
the encoding is ideal, and the problem reduces to that of Sec.
V.1
. Furthermore, at
p=1
, all collected data is erased, and therefore the choice of the strategy does not matter after all. In fact, the only contribution to the score comes from the prior information, which as we show in Appendix
C
is analytically equal to
1/4
. Notably, for intermediate values of
p
, a strict hierarchy PAR<SEQ<ICO is observed, despite the score values being very close.

VI
Discussion

In this work, we have developed a comprehensive framework for Bayesian quantum parameter estimation in the regime of finite data. By exploiting the formalism of quantum testers and higher-order operations, we provided a versatile algorithm and the accompanying numerical tools capable of approximating the optimal metrological strategy—encompassing the probe state, the control operations, the POVM elements, and the estimators—for arbitrary channel uses. This approach allowed us to compare the performance of parallel, sequential, and indefinite causal order strategies, as well as an adaptive greedy scheme based on classical feedforward.

Our results reveal a rich landscape of performance hierarchies. We demonstrated that the superiority of a particular class of strategies is highly problem-dependent. For the ideal SU(2) encoding, the hierarchy collapses, rendering parallel strategies as effective as the most general ICO protocols. Here we show that adaptive greedy strategies, which rely on classical instead of quantum memory, provide a great alternative for such strategies in this problem: while a
k
-copy adaptive greedy strategy performs worse than a
k
-copy parallel or sequential strategy, we observe that greedy strategies using
k+1
copies are already able to nearly match or in some cases even outperform the best parallel strategy with
k
uses. This has been explicitly observed in the regime of
k\in\{2,\ldots,6\}
.

Conversely, in the presence of noise, such as the SU(2) channel followed by amplitude damping, a strict hierarchy emerges in the
2
-copy case (
\text{PAR}<\text{SEQ}<\text{ICO}
). Interestingly, for dissipative thermometry, we found that the adaptive greedy strategy, and even a non-adaptive strategy performs on par with the optimal sequential strategy. This result is of significant practical value, as it suggests that for certain dissipative tasks, classical feedforward suffices to attain the ultimate precision limits without requiring long-lived quantum memories to maintain entanglement across channel uses. Identifying the crucial feature of the encoding process (the quantum channel) that leads to a hierarchy between the different strategies deserves further exploration.

A distinct advantage of our Bayesian framework lies in its operational feasibility compared to frequentist approaches. Frequentist bounds, such as the Quantum Cramér-Rao Bound, often rely on the QFI
Tóth and Apellaniz 2014
;
Paris 2009
. While powerful, the QFI formalism can suffer from the incompatibility of optimal measurements. Specifically, in the multiparameter setting, the optimal input, measurements, and estimators for different parameters may suffer incompatibility issues
Belliardo and Giovannetti 2021
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Hu et al. 2024
. Consequently, the bound may not be attainable by any physical strategy. In contrast, our approach optimizes the Bayesian score directly, rather than a bound on it. By construction, the resulting strategy yields a specific initial state, a specific set of intermediate control operations, a single valid POVM, and an explicit set of estimators.

Finally, we address the scalability of numerical methods in quantum metrology. The semidefinite programming approach employed here provides arbitrary precision but suffers from the curse of dimensionality. The size of the testers scales exponentially with the number of channel uses
k
, limiting the direct application of SDPs to small
k
. While in this work we limited to
k=2
, we believe with current state of the art computational resources, one could solve the problem for
k\leq 4
as well, but not far beyond. To tackle the regime of higher channel uses, integrating tensor network techniques into the Bayesian framework remains as an interesting future direction. These techniques have been successfully applied to calculate the QFI for large systems by efficiently representing the relevant quantum states and channels
Kurdziałek et al. 2025
; adapting them to the Bayesian formalism is a natural future direction.

A.1
The optimal estimators

We devote this section to derive the analytical expression for the optimal estimators in the SU(2) problem (namely, (
17
)). Recalling (
2
) and using the reward function for the SU(2) estimation problem (already in the quaternion representation) we have

\mathcal{S}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{O}}}\int\differential{\boldsymbol{\theta}}p(\bm{\theta})p(i|\bm{\theta})\left(q_{\bm{\theta}}^{T}q_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}}\right)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{O}}}\int\differential{\boldsymbol{\theta}}p(\bm{\theta})p(i|\bm{\theta})q_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}}^{T}q_{\bm{\theta}}q_{\bm{\theta}}^{T}q_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{O}}}q_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}}^{T}\left[\int\differential{\boldsymbol{\theta}}p(\bm{\theta})p(i|\bm{\theta})q_{\bm{\theta}}q_{\bm{\theta}}^{T}\right]q_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}}.

(23)

Therefore, for a fixed set of testers—hence a fixed set of
\{p(i|\bm{\theta})\}_{i}
—the optimal estimators can be found by maximizing the above
\mathcal{S}
over the set of
q_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}}
:

q_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{*}}\coloneqq\underset{q_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}}}{\rm arg\penalty\ max}\penalty\ \penalty\ q_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}}^{T}\left[\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}}|i)q_{\bm{\theta}}q_{\bm{\theta}}^{T}\right]q_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}}.

(24)

If we define

K\coloneqq\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}}|i)q_{\bm{\theta}}q_{\bm{\theta}}^{T},

(25)

we recover exactly (
17
).

A.2
Further details on the Haar measure

In the SU(2) example, we took the Haar random prior distribution. In our parametrization, we defined
U_{\bm{\theta}}=e^{-i\bm{\theta}\cdot\bm{\sigma}}
, which is a conventional notation. To benchmark our numerical results with analytical expressions, we need to express the Haar measure in this notation,
\differential{U_{\bm\theta}}=\differential{\bm\theta}p(\bm{\theta})
.
To this aim, we note that a Haar measure is already available in the literature in a different parametrization. Namely, Ref.
Spengler et al. 2012
, represents any unitary in SU(2) as

U_{\bm{\lambda}}=e^{i\sigma_{z}\lambda_{3}}e^{i\sigma_{y}\lambda_{2}}e^{i\sigma_{z}\lambda_{1}},

(26)

with the Haar measure given by

\differential{U}_{\bm{\lambda}}=\dfrac{1}{\pi^{2}}\sin\lambda_{2}\cos\lambda_{2}\differential{\lambda_1}\differential{\lambda_2}\differential{\lambda_3}.

(27)

To proceed further, we need to find the change of variables that relates both representation of the SU(2) unitaries. Let us start by explicitly expanding (
26
) using the identity
e^{i\lambda\sigma_{i}}=\cos\lambda\mathds{1}+i\sin\lambda\sigma_{i}
:

\displaystyle U_{\bm{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\left(\cos\lambda_{3}\mathds{1}+i\sin\lambda_{3}\sigma_{z}\right)\left(\cos\lambda_{2}\mathds{1}+i\sin\lambda_{2}\sigma_{y}\right)\left(\cos\lambda_{1}\mathds{1}+i\sin\lambda_{1}\sigma_{z}\right)=c_{0}\mathds{1}+i(c_{x}\sigma_{x}+c_{y}\sigma_{y}+c_{z}\sigma_{z}),

(28)

with

\displaystyle c_{0}

\displaystyle=\cos(\lambda_2)\cos(\lambda_1 + \lambda_3),

(29)

\displaystyle c_{x}

\displaystyle=\sin(\lambda_2)\sin(\lambda_3 - \lambda_1),

(30)

\displaystyle c_{y}

\displaystyle=\sin(\lambda_2)\cos(\lambda_3 - \lambda_1),

(31)

\displaystyle c_{z}

\displaystyle=\cos(\lambda_2)\sin(\lambda_1 + \lambda_3).

(32)

Furthermore, we note that we can similarly write
U_{\bm{\theta}}=\cos\left(\norm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)\mathds{1}-i\sin\left(\norm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)\bm{n}_{\theta}\cdot\bm{\sigma}
. Comparing the coefficients of the different Pauli operators and the identity operator, we get

\displaystyle\cos\left(\norm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)

\displaystyle=\cos(\lambda_2)\cos(\lambda_1 + \lambda_3)

(33)

\displaystyle\theta_{x}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\norm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\sin\left(\norm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)}\sin(\lambda_2)\sin(\lambda_3 - \lambda_1)

(34)

\displaystyle\theta_{y}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\norm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\sin\left(\norm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)}\sin(\lambda_2)\cos(\lambda_3 - \lambda_1)

(35)

\displaystyle\theta_{z}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\norm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\sin\left(\norm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)}\cos(\lambda_2)\sin(\lambda_1 + \lambda_3).

(36)

To express the measure (
27
) in terms of the new parameters, we just need to compute the Jacobian of the transformation
\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\}\mapsto\{\theta_{x},\theta_{y},\theta_{z}\}
. To do that, we first need to calculate the following partial derivatives:

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\theta_x}{\lambda_1}

\displaystyle=\sin(\lambda_2)\left[\dfrac{r}{\sin(r)}\cos(\lambda_3 - \lambda_1)-\dfrac{\cos(\lambda_2)\sin(\lambda_1 + \lambda_3)(\sin(r)-r\cos(r))}{\sin^{3}(r)}\sin(\lambda_3 - \lambda_1)\right]

(37)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\theta_x}{\lambda_2}

\displaystyle=-\sin(\lambda_3 - \lambda_1)\left[\dfrac{r}{\sin(r)}\cos(\lambda_2)+\dfrac{\sin(\lambda_2)\cos(\lambda_1 + \lambda_3)(\sin(r)-r\cos(r))}{\sin^{3}(r)}\sin(\lambda_2)\right]

(38)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\theta_x}{\lambda_3}

\displaystyle=-\sin(\lambda_2)\left[\dfrac{r}{\sin(r)}\cos(\lambda_3 - \lambda_1)+\dfrac{\cos(\lambda_2)\sin(\lambda_1 + \lambda_3)(\sin(r)-r\cos(r))}{\sin^{3}(r)}\sin(\lambda_3 - \lambda_1)\right]

(39)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\theta_y}{\lambda_1}

\displaystyle=-\sin(\lambda_2)\left[\dfrac{r}{\sin(r)}\sin(\lambda_3 - \lambda_1)+\dfrac{\cos(\lambda_2)\sin(\lambda_1 + \lambda_3)(\sin(r)-r\cos(r))}{\sin^{3}(r)}\cos(\lambda_3 - \lambda_1)\right]

(40)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\theta_y}{\lambda_2}

\displaystyle=-\cos(\lambda_3 - \lambda_1)\left[\dfrac{r}{\sin(r)}\cos(\lambda_2)+\dfrac{\sin(\lambda_2)\cos(\lambda_1 + \lambda_3)(\sin(r)-r\cos(r))}{\sin^{3}(r)}\sin(\lambda_2)\right]

(41)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\theta_y}{\lambda_3}

\displaystyle=\sin(\lambda_2)\left[\dfrac{r}{\sin(r)}\sin(\lambda_3 - \lambda_1)-\dfrac{\cos(\lambda_2)\sin(\lambda_1 + \lambda_3)(\sin(r)-r\cos(r))}{\sin^{3}(r)}\cos(\lambda_3 - \lambda_1)\right]

(42)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\theta_z}{\lambda_1}

\displaystyle=-\cos(\lambda_2)\left[\dfrac{r}{\sin(r)}\cos(\lambda_3 + \lambda_1)+\dfrac{\cos(\lambda_2)\sin(\lambda_1 + \lambda_3)(\sin(r)-r\cos(r))}{\sin^{3}(r)}\sin(\lambda_3 + \lambda_1)\right]=\partialderivative{\theta_z}{\lambda_3}

(43)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\theta_z}{\lambda_2}

\displaystyle=-\sin(\lambda_3 + \lambda_1)\left[\dfrac{\sin(\lambda_2)\cos(\lambda_1 + \lambda_3)(\sin(r)-r\cos(r))}{\sin^{3}(r)}\cos(\lambda_2)-\dfrac{r}{\sin(r)}\sin(\lambda_2)\right],

(44)

with
r\coloneqq\norm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Now, the determinant of the Jacobian matrix can be shown to be

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\det\pdv{(\theta_x, \theta_y, \theta_z)}{(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3)}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{32}r^{2}\csc^{3}r(64r\cos\lambda_{1}\cos^{2}\lambda_{2}\cos\lambda_{3}\sin\lambda_{2}+32\cos(2\lambda_{1})\cos^{3}\lambda_{2}\cos(2\lambda_{3})(-1+r\cot r)\csc r\sin\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle-4(-1+r\cot r)\csc r(6\sin(2\lambda_{2})-\sin(4\lambda_{2}))-64r\cos^{2}\lambda_{2}\sin\lambda_{1}\sin\lambda_{2}\sin\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle-32\cos^{3}\lambda_{2}(-1+r\cot r)\csc r\sin(2\lambda_{1})\sin\lambda_{2}\sin(2\lambda_{3})),

(45)

which can be simplified using the constraint in (
33
) to

\absolutevalue{\det\pdv{(\theta_x, \theta_y, \theta_z)}{(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3)}}=2\left(\dfrac{r}{\sin(r)}\right)^{2}\sin(\lambda_2)\cos(\lambda_2).

(46)

Therefore, (
27
) can be transformed into

\displaystyle\differential{U}_{\bm{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\pi^{2}}\sin\lambda_{2}\cos\lambda_{2}\differential{\lambda_1}\differential{\lambda_2}\differential{\lambda_3}=\dfrac{1}{\pi^{2}}\sin\lambda_{2}(\theta_{x},\theta_{y},\theta_{z})\cos\lambda_{2}(\theta_{x},\theta_{y},\theta_{z})\absolutevalue{\det\pdv{(\theta_x, \theta_y, \theta_z)}{(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3)}}\differential{\theta_x}\differential{\theta_y}\differential{\theta_z}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\left(\dfrac{\sin r}{r}\right)^{2}\differential{\theta_x}\differential{\theta_y}\differential{\theta_z}\equiv\differential U_{\bm{\theta}}.

(47)

Therefore, if we identify
\differential{\bm\theta}\equiv\differential\theta_{x}\differential\theta_{y}\differential\theta_{z}
, then
p({\bm{\theta}})=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}[(\sin r)/r]^{2}
.
One can confirm that Eq. (
47
) is properly normalized

\int\differential{U}_{\bm{\theta}}=\dfrac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential{r}r^{2}\left(\dfrac{\sin r}{r}\right)^{2}\int\differential{\Omega}=\dfrac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\cdot\dfrac{\pi}{2}\cdot 4\pi=1.

(48)

Therefore, if we do not want to overcount points (i.e. that the Haar measure is normalized), the vector of parameters must be inside the ball generated by
(\theta^{x})^{2}+(\theta^{y})^{2}+(\theta^{z})^{2}<\pi
.

Appendix B
The Thermometry Problem

Let us first write explicitly the CJ operator of the thermometry channel
Bavaresco et al. 2024

J_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1+N_{\rm B}(\epsilon)(e^{-(\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}})t}+1)}{2N_{\rm B}(\epsilon)+1}&0&0&e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}}}{2}t}\\
0&\frac{(N_{\rm B}(\epsilon)+1)(1-e^{-(\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}})t})}{2N_{\rm B}(\epsilon)+1}&0&0\\
0&0&\frac{N_{\rm B}(\epsilon)(1-e^{-(\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}})t})}{2N_{\rm B}(\epsilon)+1}&0\\
e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}}}{2}t}&0&0&\frac{N_{\rm B}(\epsilon)+e^{-(\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}})t}(1+N_{\rm B}(\epsilon))}{2N_{\rm B}(\epsilon)+1}\end{pmatrix}

(49)

which can be written in terms of Kraus operators
\{K_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha}
as follows:

\displaystyle J_{\theta}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{4}\Vect(K_{\alpha})\Vect(K_{\alpha})^{\dagger},

(50)

\displaystyle K_{1}=\sqrt{p}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sqrt{\gamma}\end{pmatrix},\penalty\ \penalty\ K_{2}=\sqrt{p}\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt{1-\gamma}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

(51)

\displaystyle K_{3}=\sqrt{1-p}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\gamma}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\penalty\ \penalty\ K_{4}=\sqrt{1-p}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\sqrt{1-\gamma}&0\end{pmatrix},

(52)

with the vectorization operator defined as
\Vect(M)\coloneqq\sum_{ij}M_{ij}\ket{i,j}
for a matrix
M=\sum_{ij}M_{ij}\outerproduct{i}{j}
, and with
p\coloneqq(N_{\rm B}(\epsilon)+1)/(2N_{\rm B}(\epsilon)+1)
and
\gamma\coloneqq e^{-(\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}})t}
.
With this at hand, we can study whether there is an advantage or not in using
parallel
quantum strategies for the thermometry problem presented in Sec.
V.2
.
Following
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2012
, we note that the QFI when using
k
parallel calls to the channel is bounded as

\mathcal{F}^{(k)}\leq 4\min_{\tilde{K}}\left\{k\norm{\alpha_{\tilde{K}}}+k(k-1)\norm{\beta_{\tilde{K}}}^{2}\right\},

(53)

with
\norm{ \bullet}
being the operator norm of
\bullet
, the minimization running over all possible Kraus representations of the channel
{\widetilde{K}}=\{{\widetilde{K}_{i}}\}_{i}
. Furthermore,
\alpha_{\tilde{K}}\coloneqq\sum_{i}\dot{\tilde{K}}_{i}^{\dagger}\dot{\tilde{K}}_{i}
and
\beta_{\tilde{K}}\coloneqq i\sum_{i}\dot{\tilde{K}}_{i}^{\dagger}\tilde{K}_{i}
, where the dot represents the derivative with respect to the parameter being estimated.
We note that only the second term can be proportional to
k^{2}
, which enables super-linear (Heisenberg) scaling.
In our case, since the channel parameters depend directly on
N_{\rm B}
, the problem of estimating
\theta
is equivalent to estimating
N_{\rm B}
up to a change of variable; we can relate the QFI for the two parameters via the chain rule:
\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{T}=\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{N_{\rm B}}\left(\frac{dN_{\rm B}}{dT}\right)^{2}
. For the purpose of analyzing the QFI scaling with the number of probes
k
, it is sufficient to analyze the properties of the channel with respect to
N_{\rm B}
.

To summarize, a sufficient condition to rule out super-linear scaling is to find any valid Kraus representation
\tilde{K}
for which the operator norm vanishes, i.e.,
\norm{\beta_{\tilde{K}}}=0
. If we can show this for our canonical choice of Kraus operators (
50
), then the Heisenberg term vanishes, and the QFI is fundamentally limited to linear scaling,
\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{N_{B}}\propto k
.

Now, let us show that the operator
\beta=i\sum_{k=1}^{4}\dot{K}_{k}^{\dagger}K_{k}
is the zero matrix for our thermometry problem. First, we calculate the derivative of each Kraus operator with respect to
N
:

\displaystyle\dot{K}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{p}}{2\sqrt{p}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sqrt{\gamma}\end{pmatrix}+\sqrt{p}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\frac{\dot{\gamma}}{2\sqrt{\gamma}}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\dot{K}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{p}}{2\sqrt{p}}\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt{1-\gamma}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}+\sqrt{p}\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{-\dot{\gamma}}{2\sqrt{1-\gamma}}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\dot{K}_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\dot{p}}{2\sqrt{1-p}}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\gamma}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}+\sqrt{1-p}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\dot{\gamma}}{2\sqrt{\gamma}}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\dot{K}_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\dot{p}}{2\sqrt{1-p}}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\sqrt{1-\gamma}&0\end{pmatrix}+\sqrt{1-p}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\frac{-\dot{\gamma}}{2\sqrt{1-\gamma}}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Plugging these results, it is easy to see that
\beta=i\sum_{k}\dot{K}_{k}^{\dagger}K_{k}=\mathbf{0}
, with
\mathbf{0}
being the null operator, whose norm is trivially zero. This proves that the term responsible for super-linear scaling of the QFI vanishes, and that the precision of thermometry using this channel is therefore fundamentally limited to the standard quantum limit, scaling at best linearly with the number of probes. The same scaling is achievable with strategies that require no memory. While this behaviour should not necessarily hold in a Bayesian formalism, at the limit of large
k
, we expect the QFI to become a relevant figure of merit and the linear scaling to hold. Our results in the main text show that the classical adaptive greedy strategy is equally good even for a small number of calls, i.e.,
k=2
.

Appendix C
Details of the dissipative SU(2) encoding

In the dissipative SU(2) example, the score for the two extremal points
p=0
and
p=1
can be solved analytically. They can help us monitor whether our algorithm is being implemented properly in that example. While
p=0
should trivially give the optimal score in Eq. (
18
), as the amplitude damping contribution is just the identity channel, the
p=1
case should be studied in more detail. For a general state
\rho
, the action of the channel at
p=1
reads

\Lambda_{\mathrm{\bm{\theta}}}^{(p=1)}\left[\rho\right]=\sum_{i}{\bar{K}}_{i}^{(p=1)}U_{\bm{\theta}}\rho U_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger}\left({\bar{K}}_{i}^{(p=1)}\right)^{\dagger}.

(54)

Applying the Kraus operators given in Eq. (
22
) we get:

\Lambda_{\mathrm{\bm{\theta}}}^{(p=1)}\left[\rho\right]=\outerproduct{0}{0}\rho\outerproduct{0}{0}+\outerproduct{0}{1}\rho\outerproduct{1}{0}=\outerproduct{0}{0},

(55)

as
\Tr(\rho)=1
. Therefore, the channel is independent of
{\bm{\theta}}
and all information on
\bm{\theta}
is erased as expected. We recall that the score is given by

\mathcal{S}^{(p=1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{O}}}\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}})c({\bm{\theta}},\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{i})\Tr\left(J^{(p=1)}_{\bm{\theta}}T_{i}\right).

(56)

We note that
\Tr\left(J^{(p=1)}_{\bm{\theta}}T_{i}\right)=\Tr\left(J^{(p=1)}_{*}T_{i}\right)\eqqcolon q_{i}
, with
J_{*}
the Choi-Jamiołkowski representation of the channel (
55
) being independent of
{\bm{\theta}}
as we showed above. We also note that
\sum_{i}q_{i}=1
. Then, we can write the score as

\mathcal{S}^{(p=1)}=\dfrac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{O}}}q_{i}\Tr\left[\left(\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}})J^{U}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)J^{U}_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}}\right].

(57)

Now, by using that (see below for a proof)

\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}})J^{U}_{\bm{\theta}}=\dfrac{\mathds{1}}{d},

(58)

the score will be

\mathcal{S}^{(p=1)}=\dfrac{1}{d^{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{O}}}q_{i}\Tr( J^U_{\hat{\bm\theta}_i} )=\dfrac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{O}}}q_{i}=\dfrac{1}{d^{2}},

(59)

as we wanted to show. It is left to prove that (
58
) holds. By using
J^{U}_{\bm{\theta}}=\sum_{ij}\outerproduct{i}{j}\otimes U_{\bm{\theta}}\outerproduct{i}{j}U_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger}
we have,

\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}})J^{U}_{\bm{\theta}}=\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}})\left(\sum_{ij}\outerproduct{i}{j}\otimes U_{\bm{\theta}}\outerproduct{i}{j}U_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger}\right)=\sum_{ij}\left(\outerproduct{i}{j}\otimes\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}})U_{\bm{\theta}}\outerproduct{i}{j}U_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger}\right).

(60)

Now, writing the unitary in the computational basis as
U_{\bm{\theta}}=\sum_{ab}[U_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ab}\outerproduct{a}{b}
we get:

\displaystyle\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}})J^{U}_{\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\left[\outerproduct{i}{j}\otimes\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}})\left(\sum_{ab}[U_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ab}\outerproduct{a}{b}\right)\outerproduct{i}{j}\left(\sum_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}[U^{*}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}\outerproduct{b'}{a'}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\left[\outerproduct{i}{j}\otimes\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}})\left(\sum_{ab}[U_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ab}\delta_{bi}\ket{a}\right)\left(\sum_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}[U^{*}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}\delta_{jb^{\prime}}\bra{a'}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\left(\outerproduct{i}{j}\otimes\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}})\sum_{aa^{\prime}}[U_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ai}[U^{*}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{a^{\prime}j}\outerproduct{a}{a'}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\left(\outerproduct{i}{j}\otimes\sum_{aa^{\prime}}\outerproduct{a}{a'}\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}})[U_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ai}[U^{*}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{a^{\prime}j}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{d}\sum_{ij}\left(\outerproduct{i}{j}\otimes\sum_{aa^{\prime}}\outerproduct{a}{a'}\delta_{aa^{\prime}}\delta_{ij}\right)=\dfrac{\mathds{1}}{d},

(61)

where we used that

\int\differential{\bm\theta}p({\bm{\theta}})[U_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ai}[U^{*}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{a^{\prime}j}=\dfrac{1}{d}\delta_{aa^{\prime}}\delta_{ij},

(62)

which is the Haar orthogonality relation for the matrix elements of a unitary matrix (see e.g., theorem 5.8 of
Folland 1995
or corollary 2.4 of
Collins and Śniady 2006
).

frequentist

U_{\theta}=e^{-i\theta t\sigma_{z}/2},

followed by the amplitude damping channel with Kraus operators that we defined earlier in Eq. (

).

c(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{i})=\cos^{2}\left(\dfrac{\theta-\hat{\theta}_{i}}{2}\right).

In
Liu et al. 2023

0<p<1

with

D.1
Connection to the frequentist approach

To establish such a connection, we have to focus on priors that are sharp, hence pushing the problem into the local estimation regime. In such a scenario, the estimators are expected not to be far from the hypothesis values—at least within the region that the prior is significant—such that
\absolutevalue{\hat{\theta}_i - \theta}\ll 1
. Thus, for sharp priors one can write

c(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{i})\approx 1-\dfrac{(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta)^{2}}{4}.

(66)

Thus, maximizing the average cosine score is, up to an affine rescaling, equivalent to minimizing the mean-squared error (MSE). For a sharp prior, we can write the score as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\rm cos}(T,{\hat{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\int\differential{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p(\theta)\cos^{2}\left(\dfrac{\theta-\hat{\theta}_{i}}{2}\right)\Tr\left(T_{i}J_{\theta}^{\otimes k}\right)\approx\int\differential{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p(\theta)\left[1-\dfrac{(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta)^{2}}{4}\right]\Tr\left(T_{i}J_{\theta}^{\otimes k}\right)

(67)

\displaystyle=1-\dfrac{1}{4}\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{MSE}}(T,{\hat{\theta}}),

(68)

where in the last line we introduced
{\cal S}_{\rm MSE}
, the score based on the MSE:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{MSE}}(T,{\hat{\theta}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int\differential{\theta}p(\theta)\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{O}}}p(i|\theta)(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta)^{2}=\int\differential{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{O}}}p(\theta)(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta)^{2}\Tr\left(T_{i}J_{\theta}^{\otimes k}\right).

(69)

For the MSE score, we can use the Van Trees inequality to connect both frequentist and Bayesian approaches
Van Trees 2004
;
Van Trees and Bell 2007
:

\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{MSE}}(T,{\hat{\theta}})\geq\dfrac{1}{F_{0}+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{F}\left[p(i|\theta)\right]\right]}\geq\dfrac{1}{F_{0}+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{F}^{*}(\theta)\right]},

(70)

with
F_{0}
being the prior (Fisher) information

\displaystyle F_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int\differential{\theta}p(\theta)\left[\partial_{\theta}\log p(\theta)\right]^{2},

(71)

and
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{F}\left[p(i|\theta)\right]\right]=\int\differential{\theta}p(\theta)\mathcal{F}\left[p(i|\theta)\right]
, with

\mathcal{F}\left[p(i|\theta)\right]\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm O}}\dfrac{\left[\partial_{\theta}p(i|\theta)\right]^{2}}{p(i|\theta)},

(72)

being the average classical Fisher information acquired from the protocol leading to the outcome distribution
p(i|\theta)
. Lastly, the quantity
{\cal F}^{*}(\theta)=\max{\cal F}[p(i|\theta)]
is the quantum Fisher information, i.e., the maximum of the Fisher information over all allowed states, control operations, and measurements—that is, over allowed testers.
Again, for a sharp prior, we expect that
F_{0}\gg\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{F}^{*}(\theta)\right]
, so we can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{MSE}}(T,{\hat{\theta}})\geq\dfrac{1}{F_{0}+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{F}^{*}(\theta)\right]}\approx\dfrac{1}{F_{0}}-\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{F}^{*}(\theta)\right]}{F_{0}^{2}}.

(73)

As
F_{0}
depends exclusively on the prior distribution
p(\theta)
, and is specifically independent of the protocol, one would expect that for sharp priors the only protocol-relevant contribution to the score
{\cal S}_{\rm cos}
comes from the QFI. As such, one would expect any hierarchy that was observed in the frequentist approach to hold also in the Bayesian approach with sharp priors (note that this does not necessarily extend to cost functions that are not reducible to the MSE). We note that, as the prior distribution is very peaked around
\theta_{0}=\pi
, we can approximate
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{F}^{*}(\theta)\right]\approx\left[\mathcal{F}^{*}(\theta_{0})\right]
, which gives

\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{cos}}^{*}(T,{\hat{\theta}})\approx C+\dfrac{\mathcal{F}^{*}(\theta_{0})}{4F_{0}^{2}},\quad C\coloneqq 1-\dfrac{1}{4F_{0}}\approx 0.995,

(74)

which matches with our numerical simulations in Figure
5b
.
