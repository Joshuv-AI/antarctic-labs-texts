Splenic hilum nodal involvement in resected left-sided pancreatic cancer: meta-analysis
Authors: Caro L Bruna, Charlotte E Baggerman van Houweninge, Faridi S Jamaludin, Guiseppe Malleo, Mohammad Abu Hilal, Marc G Besselink, Jony van Hilst
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSplenectomy is standard of care during left pancreatectomy for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) to obtain adequate lymphadenectomy. However, evidence supporting this approach is lacking. Splenic preservation would reduce short-term morbidity and is essential for emerging oncological adjunctive therapies, including immunotherapy and personalized cancer vaccines. This study reviewed the incidence of splenic hilum nodal involvement (SHNI) in left-sided PDAC.MethodsA systematic review of the PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane databases was performed, identifying studies published from inception to July 2026. Outcomes of interest were the rate of SHNI (station 10), overall survival, and the rate of splenic artery nodal involvement (SANI; station 11). Meta-analyses were conducted using random-effects models. Subgroup analyses for SHNI were performed per tumour localization (pancreatic neck, body, tail).ResultsAmong 2776 screened studies, 22 with 2260 patients undergoing left pancreatectomy for PDAC were included. The pooled prevalence of SHNI was 3.7% (95% confidence interval (c.i.) 2.2% to 6.2%); 1.1% for pancreatic body PDAC (95% c.i. 0.3% to 4.3%) and 9.7% for pancreatic tail PDAC (95% c.i. 3.5% to 24.0%). SHNI was not significantly associated with survival (pooled hazard ratio 2.05; 95% c.i. 0.89% to 4.72; P = 0.072). The pooled prevalence of SANI was 39.1% (95% c.i. 25.1% to 55.1%).ConclusionIn patients undergoing left pancreatectomy for PDAC, the presence of SHNI is rare, particularly in pancreatic body cancer (1.1%). These findings suggest that the relevance of routine splenectomy remains unclear, especially for pancreatic body PDAC. However, because the quality of current evidence is low, further investigation in prospective studies is required.

Introduction

Left pancreatectomy with splenectomy is standard of care in patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) located in the pancreatic body and tail1,2. This procedure routinely includes resection of lymph node (LN) station 10 (splenic hilum), station 11p (proximal splenic artery), and station 11d (distal splenic artery)1.

Reported rates of nodal involvement in these stations vary considerably. Splenic hilum nodal involvement (SHNI) is reported in 0–11% of patients3–10, whereas splenic artery nodal involvement (SANI) is reported more frequently, ranging from 2% to 88%4,6–11. The splenic hilum LNs can only be fully resected with concomitant splenectomy. The potentially low rate of SHNI raises the question as to whether a spleen-preserving approach could be a safe alternative in patients with left-sided PDAC. However, most data come from small single-centre retrospective studies and do not clearly specify tumour location within the pancreatic body or tail, thus limiting generalizability.

Performing a splenectomy is not without consequences. Asplenia carries an increased risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy sepsis, which, although rare, is associated with high mortality rates of 38–69%12–16. In the context of left pancreatectomy, spleen-preserving surgery is associated with fewer postoperative complications17–19 and, as reported in two studies20,21 in patients with PDAC, improved long-term survival. Furthermore, a recent phase I trial by Rojas et al.22 evaluated an individualized neoantigen vaccine for PDAC. Among non-responders, a higher proportion had undergone left pancreatectomy with splenectomy than pancreatoduodenectomy. Rojas et al.22 hypothesized that splenectomy may impair immunotherapy response, potentially reducing the efficacy of future treatments.

The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to summarize the current evidence on the rate of SHNI and its association with survival in patients undergoing left pancreatectomy for PDAC.

Methods

This systematic review was performed according to the PRISMA23 guidelines and Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions24 (see Table S1). The protocol of this systematic review was registered in the PROSPERO international prospective register of systematic reviews (CRD42024521081).

Search strategy

The PubMed, Cochrane, and Embase electronic databases were searched, focusing on studies reporting on SHNI in patients undergoing left pancreatectomy with splenectomy for PDAC. The search included studies from inception until 8 July 2026, using search terms developed in collaboration with an experienced medical librarian (F.S.J.) to maximize retrieval of relevant articles.

Consistent combinations of the following search terms were used: pancreatic intraductal neoplasms (MeSH), pancreatic ductal carcinoma (MeSH), pancreatic adenocarcinoma, pancreatic tumours, pancreatic cancer, pancreatic malignancy, ductal carcinoma, duct-cell carcinoma, pancreatectomy (MeSH), distal pancreatectomy, left pancreatectomy, left-sided pancreatectomy, splenectomy (MeSH), spleen resection, splenic resection, splenopancreatectomy, lymph nodes (MeSH), lymphatic metastasis, nodal metastasis, lymph node metastasis, splenic hilum lymph node metastasis, and splenic artery lymph node metastasis. In addition, the reference lists of included articles were reviewed to identify any eligible studies that were not captured in the original search. Further details of the search strategy are provided in Table S2.

Eligibility criteria

Original studies were included that reported on patients undergoing left pancreatectomy with splenectomy for PDAC and on SHNI and/or splenic artery nodal involvement (SANI; station 11), as well as overall survival in relation to SHNI. Only studies published in English were included. Reviews, case series with fewer than ten patients, conference abstracts, letters, non-human studies, and studies for which the full text could not be retrieved were excluded from the systematic review. To avoid duplication of patient data, publications suspected of reporting on the same study population were carefully reviewed. In cases of overlap, the publication containing the most complete data or the largest sample size was included, and any others were excluded.

Study selection

All identified studies were screened for inclusion by two reviewers (C.L.B. and C.B.H.) independently, based on title and abstract, using the web-based screening platform Rayyan. Full-text selection based on eligibility was also independently performed by these two reviewers. Any conflicts between the reviewers were resolved by discussion and consensus, or by consultation with a third (senior) investigator (J.H.).

Quality assessment

All studies were critically appraised by two authors (C.L.B., C.B.H.) individually. The risk of bias assessment for this study was based on the Newcastle–Ottawa scale25, with scores ranging from 0 to 8 for studies on LN outcomes and from 0 to 9 for studies reporting on survival outcomes. High-quality scores were defined as scores of 7–9, medium-quality scores were defined as scores of 4–6 or as scores ≥ 6 with one critical flaw, and low-quality scores were defined as scores of ≤ 3 for low-risk studies or ≥ 2 for studies with critical flaws.

Outcomes

The outcomes were the rate of SHNI, the association of SHNI with overall survival, and the rate of SANI.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed according to a predefined evidence table and cross-checked by two authors (C.L.B., C.B.H.) independently. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Collected data included study design, study period, the number of patients, tumour location, the total number of retrieved and positive LNs, the number of retrieved and positive nodes for LN station 10 (splenic hilum) and station 11 (including the definition used for the LN stations), and, in the event survival outcomes were reported, follow-up duration and overall-survival.

Additional data collection

For studies with missing or unclear data on SHNI or survival, corresponding authors were contacted to obtain clarification or additional information. Specifically, in three studies7,11,26, authors were asked to clarify discrepancies between the total number of included patients and those with available SHNI data. Furthermore, six studies4,10,26,40,42,44 reported on survival outcomes of patients with SHNI. However, only two of those studies40,42 provided the hazard ratio (HR) from the univariable Cox regression analysis among all included patients. The corresponding authors of two studies4,10 were asked to provide HRs for survival associated with SHNI.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed using R studio for Mac OS, version 5.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the packages meta and metafor. Dichotomous data are expressed as pooled proportions with their corresponding 95% confidence interval (c.i.) and P value. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P < 0.05.

Meta-analyses were conducted to assess the rates of SHNI and SANI separately, as well as the overall survival of patients with SHNI. The rates of SHNI and SANI were reported according to the definitions provided in the original papers. The denominator differed between studies, including either all enrolled patients or only patients in whom LN yield was reported for this station. Studies that did not provide separate data for each station were excluded from the respective meta-analysis. Given the expected heterogeneity among studies, all analyses were performed using a random-effects model27. Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using I2 and τ2, because I2 alone can be influenced by the number and precision of included studies28. Where applicable, 95% prediction intervals were calculated to indicate the expected range of true effects in a future study29. The pooled rate of LN metastasis was estimated with random-effects meta-analyses using generalized linear mixed models. In contrast to conventional meta-analyses, generalized linear mixed models avoid arbitrary continuity corrections in the case of rare events and lead to less biased pooled estimates30. Time-to-event outcomes, such as overall survival, were analysed using extracted HRs from univariable analyses and pooled with a random-effects model. Confidence intervals were calculated using the Hartung–Knapp method to combine log(HRs) and their standard errors, accounting for between-study heterogeneity and providing more accurate confidence intervals, particularly in the context of few studies and varying study precisions31.

Subgroup analyses were performed to determine the rate of SHNI for PDAC located in the pancreatic neck, body, and tail, including all studies reporting tumour locations separately. Subgroups were based on the definitions provided in the individual studies. Because no uniform anatomical classification was used across studies, tumours classified as body, body–tail junction, tail and neck were accepted as reported in the original articles.

To assess publication bias, Peters’ linear regression test was used because the outcomes were dichotomous32. This method is more reliable than Egger’s test for binary data, particularly when event rates are low or study sizes vary, because it reduces the risk of false small-study effects24.

Results

Overall, 2776 studies were retrieved. After removing duplicates, 2091 studies remained. The PRISMA flow chart is shown in Fig. 1. After excluding 1474 studies using the aforementioned selection criteria, 617 full-text studies remained. After adding one study from the references of included studies33 and excluding one study due to data overlap34, 21 articles were included in the systematic review3–6,8–11,26,33–44. Because one report included two studies3, 22 studies are addressed in this systematic review. The reasons for exclusion during full-text screening are provided Table S3. The quality assessment of each study, using the adapted Newcastle–Ottawa scale, is summarized in Table S4. The mean score was 7, indicating an intermediate quality of the included studies. Overall, 11 articles were categorized as high quality4–6,8,11,38,40–44, nine were categorized as of intermediate quality3,7,9,10,26,33,35–37, and one was categorized as low quality39.

Study and participant characteristics

The 22 studies, including 2260 patients, were mostly retrospective analyses. Kim et al.3 (2023) reported a retrospective analysis and a prospective pilot study including 12 patients. Bruna et al.10 (2024) reported a subanalysis with data from a randomized clinical trial. Sample sizes ranged from 10 to 235 patients, with a mean sample size of 103 patients. Patients with pancreatic neck (147 patients), body (893), body–tail junction (416), body/body–tail junction (35), or tail cancers (729) were included. In 19 of 22 studies, it was specified that the surgical specimens and LN stations were reviewed by pathologists. Of the 20 retrospective studies, one8 reported that the specimens were re-evaluated by a pathologist specifically for the study. Thirteen studies included patients who had received neoadjuvant treatment, with proportions ranging from 5.0% to 50.3% of the study population. Four studies explicitly excluded patients treated with neoadjuvant therapy, whereas in five studies this was not specified. Two studies33,39 including a total of 40 patients did not describe tumour localization. With the exception of one small (12 patients) study3, no research has determined the number of LNs that would have remained in situ when performing a ‘circumferentially radical spleen-preserving left pancreatectomy’ in a large cohort, because all but one study assessed the entire specimen en bloc.

All 22 studies described SHNI. Of these studies, 17 identified station 10 as the splenic hilum and included it in the standard lymphadenectomy during pancreatectomy; however, none provided a precise anatomical definition of what was considered to comprise the splenic hilum. Overall, 18 studies reported on the incidence of SANI. An overview of reported rates of SHNI and SANI is provided in Table 1. The rate of SHNI varied between 0% and 29.4%, whereas the rate of SANI varied between 1.7% and 88.1%.

Studies reporting on overall survival

Six of 22 included studies reported on overall survival in relation to SHNI, with four of these studies4,10,40,42 reporting a HR (and included in the survival meta-analysis) and two reporting26,44 a 5-year overall survival rate only4,10,26,40,42,44. An overview of survival outcomes is presented in Table 1. The four studies4,10,40,42 included in the meta-analysis comprised 683 patients, of whom 71 (10.4%) had SHNI. Tumours were located in the pancreatic neck (52), body (304), body–tail junction (51), and tail (226). Three10,40,42 of the four studies included patients who had received neoadjuvant therapy, comprising 5.0–50.3% of the study population.

Meta-analyses

Among 2111 patients in 22 studies, the pooled prevalence of SHNI was 3.7% (95% c.i. 2.2% to 6.2%; 95% prediction interval 0.5% to 21.9%), with substantial between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 81.4% (95% c.i. 72.8% to 87.3%); τ2 = 0.84; χ2 = 113.0; degrees of freedom (d.f.) = 21; P ≤ 0.001; Fig. 2).

Rate of SHNI per tumour location

The studies reporting on SHNI per tumour localization are provided in Table S5. Because no uniform anatomical classification was used across studies, tumour localizations were accepted as reported in the original articles. Six studies3,8,36,38,42,43 with 118 patients reported on the prevalence of SHNI in pancreatic neck PDAC. The pooled prevalence of SHNI in neck cancers was 1.9% (95% c.i. 0.1% to 38.0%; 95% prediction interval 0.1% to 45.4%). Statistical assessment showed no evidence of significant between-study heterogeneity, although the τ2 and the wide c.i. of I2 reflect uncertainty due to the limited number of studies (I2 = 0.0% (95% c.i. 0.0% to 74.6%); τ2 = 0.33, χ2 = 0.00, d.f. = 5, P = 1.000, Fig. S1). Overall, 11 studies3–5,8–10,26,40,42,44, with 742 patients, reported on the prevalence SHNI in pancreatic body cancers. The pooled prevalence of SHNI in body cancers was 1.1% (95% c.i. 0.3% to 4.3%; 95% prediction interval 0.05% to 17.8%). Statistical assessment showed no evidence of significant between-study heterogeneity, although the τ2 and the wide c.i. of I2 reflect moderate uncertainty due to the limited number of studies (I2 = 10.9% (95% c.i. 0.0% to 51.0%); τ2 = 1.41; χ2 = 11.23; d.f. = 10; P = 0.340; Fig. S2). Overall, 13 studies3–6,8–10,26,35,40,42,44, with 645 patients, reported on the prevalence of SHNI in pancreatic tail cancers. The pooled prevalence of SHNI in tail cancers was 9.7% (95% c.i. 3.5% to 24.0%; 95% prediction interval 0.3% to 79.9%) with substantial between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 88.5% (95% c.i. 82.2% to 92.6%); τ2 = 2.51; χ2 = 104.73; d.f. = 12; P < 0.001; Fig. S3).

GRADE assessment

Three GRADE assessments were performed. The GRADE assessment of included studies for SHNI resulted in a very low quality of evidence, because −1 downgrading was applied due to inconsistency (I2 = 81.4%; τ2 = 0.84). The GRADE assessment of included studies for SANI resulted in a very low quality of evidence, because −1 downgrading was applied due to inconsistency (I2 = 93.6%; τ2 = 0.19). The GRADE assessment of included studies that reported on survival outcomes in patients with SHNI resulted in a very low quality of evidence, because of −1 downgrading due to imprecision because of the wide 95% c.i. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis in patients undergoing left pancreatectomy for PDAC including 22 studies and 2260 patients found a 3.7% rate of SHNI. The rate of SHNI was lower in pancreatic neck and body versus pancreatic tail PDAC (1.9% and 1.1% versus 9.7%, respectively). The prevalence of SANI was 39.1%. The survival meta-analysis demonstrated a trend towards worse survival among patients with SHNI, although the association did not reach statistical significance, and the limited number of studies and wide confidence and prediction intervals limit the certainty of this finding. In light of the low rate of SHNI, the role of spleen-preserving left pancreatectomy for PDAC could be reconsidered. The high rate of SANI highlights the importance of performing a circumferential radical resection (including the splenic vessels) during left pancreatectomy for PDAC. However, the available evidence entails small studies of insufficient quality to lead to changes in surgical practice, emphasizing the need for prospective studies to confirm these findings.

The reported prevalence for SHNI (0–11.3%) and SANI (16.7–88.1%) varied considerably across studies. This heterogeneity possibly reflects differences in pathological examination and reporting, because LN detection is susceptible to specimen processing and pathologist review45,46. No standardized pathology reporting was used and definitions of the splenic hilum and LN stations were inconsistent, limiting comparability. The definition of the splenic hilum could vary between studies investigating only the LNs located on the inner surface of the spleen versus those that include all LNs between the pancreatic tail and spleen, including those in the perisplenic fat. This perisplenic fat would also stay in place in a circumferentially radical spleen-preserving left pancreatectomy, and therefore remains a relevant focus for future studies.

This meta-analysis shows that SHNI is substantially less common in pancreatic neck and body tumours than in tail tumours (1.9% and 1.1% versus 9.7%, respectively), possibly reflecting the complex lymphatic drainage pattern of the pancreas. Multiple lymphatic routes have been described, and drainage patterns of neck–body cancers may differ from those of the tail47. Consequently, in tumours located further from the splenic hilum, the likelihood of lymphatic spread to this region appears lower. Unfortunately, most studies included did not use a clear definition of neck, body, or tail tumours47.

Splenectomy is routinely performed out of concern for potential LN metastases. However, splenectomy is associated with both surgical risks and oncological concerns. First, asplenia increases the risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy sepsis, thromboembolic events, and haematological malignancy, necessitating preventative measures such as lifelong vaccinations and antibiotic prophylaxis12,15,48–51. Second, meta-analyses18,19,52 in patients with benign and premalignant disease showed lower postoperative morbidity for spleen-preserving left pancreatectomy than for left pancreatectomy with splenectomy. However, only four studies20,21,53,54 (21–103 patients) have compared survival outcomes of spleen-preserving left pancreatectomy with those of left pancreatectomy with splenectomy in patients with PDAC, with none demonstrating a survival benefit for splenectomy. Although these studies are all small and retrospective, the findings warrant further investigation in prospective studies to identify in which patients with PDAC spleen-preserving left pancreatectomy is feasible and safe and to determine whether this approach can be performed without compromising oncological outcomes.

Oncologically, splenic preservation is also relevant because ongoing studies are investigating immunotherapy regimens in patients with resectable PDAC to enhance survival. The evidence indicates that the spleen is essential for optimal activation of antigen-specific T cells, and that splenectomy may consequently reduce the efficacy of immunotherapy55. For example, in a phase I trial by Rojas et al.22, non-responders were more likely to have undergone left pancreatectomy with splenectomy. Supporting this, the ongoing GO44479 trial (NCT05968326), evaluating adjuvant autogene cevumeran, a personalized mRNA neoantigen vaccine targeting patient-specific tumour mutations, combined with the checkpoint inhibitor atezolizumab, currently excludes patients undergoing splenectomy. Therefore, preserving the spleen may have significant implications for the effectiveness of emerging therapies in pancreatic cancer.

An alternative approach to splenectomy in left pancreatectomy for PDAC could be a circumferential radical spleen-preserving left pancreatectomy, achieved by combining the Warshaw technique with Gerota’s fascia resection, a procedure that could be called a radical Warshaw56. The Warshaw technique has historically been criticized due to complications such as splenic infarction, gastric varices related to left-sided portal hypertension, and the potential need for secondary splenectomy. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis57 confirmed lower rates of radiological splenic infarction and gastric varices (odds ratios 0.14 and 0.1, respectively) when performing splenic vessel preservation, although, clinically, these complications rarely require intervention58. In a long-term follow-up study59, reoperation for spleen-related complications was required in only 1.9% of patients, and gastric varices did not result in any adverse outcomes.

After a radical Warshaw procedure, the spleen’s blood supply is maintained by the short gastric and gastroepiploic vessels, and nodal stations in this wider perisplenic region may remain in place after resection. Because these may harbour metastatic disease, further research to clarify the oncological impact of residual perisplenic LNs after a Warshaw procedure is needed. The international prospective multicentre SPLENDID trial (NCT07157605) is specifically designed to address this issue. After standard left pancreatectomy with splenectomy for PDAC, the specimen is divided by the surgeon on the back table at the level where a Warshaw procedure would have been performed. The rate of nodal involvement in the spleen part, comprising the spleen, splenic hilum, and perisplenic fat, will be determined by the pathologist in order to evaluate the rate of SHNI that would be left in place when performing spleen-preserving left pancreatectomy60.

The results of the present study should be interpreted considering some limitations. First, the survival analysis was based on only four studies with a limited number of patients and events, resulting in a wide confidence interval and statistical uncertainty. Consequently, no firm conclusions regarding the impact of SHNI on overall survival can be drawn. Second, nearly all included studies (20 of 22) were of retrospective design. This also accounts for the discrepancy observed in some studies, where the total number of included patients was considerably higher than the number for whom splenic hilum nodal data were available. Third, most studies did not provide a definition for ‘station 10/splenic hilum’. Fourth, several included studies were limited by relatively small patient cohorts. Combined with the rarity of SHNI, this resulted in only a few affected patients per study, which hindered the ability to perform regression analysis to, for example, assess the impact of SHNI on survival. Fifth, these studies included patients with resectable PDAC but many did not specify the classification of the tumour (that is, upfront resectable, borderline resectable, or locally advanced), which could have influenced the results. Furthermore, in most studies it remains unclear whether intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm-associated PDACs were included. Sixth, none of the studies described whether patients with SHNI had suspicion of SHNI on preoperative imaging. This is an important consideration because spleen-preserving left pancreatectomy would not be performed in these patients. Seventh, the studies did not use a formal classification for tumour localization, which may have led to inconsistencies in defining body versus tail PDAC and could partly explain the heterogeneity observed. The strengths of the present study include it being the first meta-analysis to evaluate SHNI in patients undergoing left pancreatectomy for PDAC in a large data set including 22 studies and 2260 patients.

In conclusion, this meta-analysis found a low rate (3.7%) of SHNI in patients undergoing left pancreatectomy for PDAC, especially in patients with pancreatic body PDAC (1.1%). The survival analysis showed a trend towards worse survival among patients with SHNI, although this did not reach statistical significance. However, due to the low quality of the available evidence, future prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings, and to determine whether spleen preservation could be safely implemented in select patients.
