Efficacy and Safety of Combined Autologous Blood Patch and Doxycycline Pleurodesis for the Management of Persistent Air Leaks in Secondary Spontaneous Pneumothorax: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Mohamed Ahmed, Mohammed Y Alamassi, Ayman F Yousef, Mohammed S Ishaq, Ahmed Gamal Elkhouly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPersistent air leak (PAL) is a frequent and challenging complication of secondary spontaneous pneumothorax (SSP) and often prolongs hospitalization. We compared the efficacy and safety of autologous blood patch pleurodesis (ABPP) plus doxycycline with those of ABPP alone for managing PAL in patients with SSP who were considered unfit for surgery.MethodsThis prospective, randomized controlled trial enrolled 121 patients with SSP and PAL lasting ≥7 days. Participants were randomized to receive ABPP alone (n=62) or ABPP plus intrapleural doxycycline (n=59). The primary outcome was time to air leak cessation. Secondary outcomes included the 7-day success rate, time to drain removal, length of hospital stay, pain score, complications, and recurrence at 1 and 3 months.ResultsKaplan-Meier analysis showed significantly faster air leak cessation in the ABPP plus doxycycline group than in the ABPP-alone group (median, 4 days vs. 6 days; log-rank p<0.001). This was accompanied by earlier intercostal drain removal (median, 5 days vs. 7 days; p<0.001) and a shorter hospital stay (median, 12 days vs. 14 days; p<0.001). The 7-day success rate was 81% in the combination group and 74% in the ABPP group (p=0.34). Post-procedural pain scores were higher in the combination group (p<0.001). However, overall complication rates (36% vs. 40%, p=0.6) and 3-month recurrence rates (3.8% vs. 7%, p=0.68) were statistically comparable between groups.ConclusionCombining doxycycline with ABPP appears to be a safe and effective bedside intervention. Compared with ABPP alone, the combination significantly accelerated air leak cessation and shortened hospitalization, while achieving pleural symphysis without increasing major complications (trial registration number Pan African Clinical Trials Registry, PACTR202505753650802).Keywords: Air leak, Pleurodesis, Autologous blood patch, Doxycycline, Spontaneous pneumothorax

Introduction

Secondary spontaneous pneumothorax (SSP) occurs in the setting of underlying lung disease, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), interstitial lung disease, malignancy, cystic fibrosis, and pulmonary infection [1,2]. Compared with primary spontaneous pneumothorax, SSP is associated with more complications, higher recurrence rates, and longer hospital stays [3]. Persistent air leak (PAL) is a common complication of SSP and is generally considered prolonged when it persists for more than 5–7 days [4].

Several options are available for managing PAL, including prolonged tube drainage, one-way valve placement, chemical pleurodesis, autologous blood patch pleurodesis (ABPP), fibrin glue application, bronchoscopic intervention, and surgical repair [5]. ABPP has been used since 1992 and is particularly relevant for PAL in patients with spontaneous pneumothorax [6]. Compared with conservative treatment, ABPP has been associated with superior outcomes in air leak cessation, success rate, and complications. It can also be performed at the bedside, is inexpensive, and is generally well tolerated [7]. Chemical pleurodesis is another option for controlling prolonged or recurrent pneumothorax, particularly when patients are unwilling or unfit to undergo surgery [7,8]. Reported chemical agents include doxycycline, bleomycin, talc, and tetracycline; their effectiveness requires apposition of the visceral and parietal pleural surfaces [9,10]. Tetracycline derivatives have been reported to reduce prolonged air leaks and recurrence in patients with spontaneous pneumothorax [10,11].

In this randomized controlled study, we aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of ABPP plus doxycycline with those of ABPP alone for managing PAL after SSP in patients who were not candidates for surgical intervention.

Methods

This prospective, randomized, single-blind controlled study was conducted from January through December 2025 in the thoracic surgery departments of Tanta University Hospital, Egypt, and King Saud Medical City, Saudi Arabia. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committees of Tanta University Hospital, Egypt, and King Saud Medical City, Saudi Arabia (approval codes: 36264PR1098, #IORG0010374) and was registered with the Pan African Clinical Trials Registry (PACTR202505753650802). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients with clinical and radiological features of pneumothorax were screened and underwent intercostal tube drainage with 24F or 28F chest tubes placed in the standard safety triangle. Adult patients aged ≥18 years were eligible if they had SSP with PAL lasting ≥7 days and were not candidates for surgical intervention, as determined by a multidisciplinary team. Air leak severity was graded according to the Cerfolio classification [12]. SSP was defined as pneumothorax occurring in the presence of clinically or radiologically evident underlying lung disease, whereas primary spontaneous pneumothorax was defined as pneumothorax occurring in structurally normal lungs. Patients were excluded if they had a history of ipsilateral pleurodesis, traumatic or primary spontaneous pneumothorax, active pulmonary infection, underlying hematological disorders, coagulopathy, or known allergy to doxycycline. Patients with high-grade air leaks (Cerfolio grade 3 or 4) were also excluded because these massive fistulas are usually refractory to pleurodesis and often require surgical management.

A total of 142 patients diagnosed with spontaneous pneumothorax were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 21 were excluded (17 with primary spontaneous pneumothorax and 4 who declined participation), and 121 patients were enrolled (Fig. 1). Participants were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the ABPP group or the ABPP plus doxycycline group. Randomization was performed using a computer-generated sequence and the sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelope method.

Group 1: Autologous blood patch pleurodesis alone

Under aseptic precautions, 50 mL of autologous venous blood was withdrawn from the patient’s cubital vein using a 50-mL syringe with an 18G needle (Fig. 2). The blood was immediately injected into the pleural cavity through the intercostal tube without anticoagulation.

Setup for autologous blood patch pleurodesis. The closed system comprised a cannula, syringe, 3-way stopcock, and extension tubing, allowing immediate aseptic instillation of blood into the pleural cavity through the intercostal chest tube.

Group 2: Combined autologous blood patch pleurodesis and doxycycline

To minimize pain, 200 mg of 2% lignocaine was first instilled into the pleural cavity. Subsequently, 50 mL of autologous venous blood was withdrawn and injected into the pleural cavity. After 15 minutes, 200 mg of doxycycline diluted in 50 mL of normal saline was slowly instilled through the chest tube [13].

In both groups, after instillation, the chest tube was elevated approximately 80 cm above the patient’s body level. This maneuver prevented immediate drainage of the intrapleural agents while allowing air to escape. Patients were instructed to rotate their position in bed every 15 minutes for 2 hours to ensure even distribution of the agents throughout the pleural cavity. After the procedure, all patients were monitored using a digital air leak measurement device (Thopaz+; Medela). Air leak status was recorded immediately after the intervention and every 12 hours thereafter. Baseline analgesia consisted of scheduled non-opioid medication (paracetamol 1 g every 8 hours). Escalation to rescue analgesia (tramadol 50 mg intravenously) was offered to patients reporting a Numeric Pain Rating Scale (NPRS) score ≥4.

The primary outcome was time to air leak cessation. Because the Thopaz+ device continuously records air leak flow, the 24-hour cessation threshold did not depend on specific rounding times. Complete cessation was defined as air flow <20 mL/min for 24 consecutive hours, with radiological confirmation of lung re-expansion [14]. Secondary outcomes included the success rate, defined as cessation by day 7; time to intercostal drain removal; total length of hospital stay (LOS); pneumothorax recurrence at 1 and 3 months; and post-procedural complications, including fever, tachycardia, subcutaneous emphysema, dyspnea, and pain. Pain was assessed using the NPRS [15].

The tube was removed when air flow remained <20 mL/min for 24 continuous hours after complete lung re-expansion had been confirmed. Patients underwent clinical and radiological follow-up at 1 and 3 months after discharge to detect recurrence.

Statistical analysis

The initial sample size calculation estimated that 128 patients (64 per arm) would be required to provide 80% power at a 5% alpha level to detect a clinically meaningful between-group difference of 1.5 days in the primary outcome, time to air leak cessation, assuming a conservative standard deviation of 4 days. However, after a preplanned, blinded interim assessment of pooled variance, the observed standard deviation was substantially narrower than anticipated (2.3 days). Based on this updated variance, formal sample size re-estimation indicated that the enrolled cohort of 121 patients provided robust statistical power (>90%) to evaluate the primary endpoint. Therefore, recruitment was finalized at 121 patients once sufficient statistical power had been achieved.

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 26.0 (IBM Corp.). Normally distributed quantitative data were expressed as mean±standard deviation and compared using the independent t-test. Qualitative data were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. The primary outcome, time to air leak cessation, and secondary time-to-event outcomes, including time to drain removal and length of hospital stay, were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier survival curves. Between-group differences in probability distributions were compared using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Because the NPRS is an ordinal variable, it was expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the nonparametric Mann- Whitney U test. Analyses of baseline characteristics and all inpatient outcomes were performed on an intention-to- treat basis for all 121 randomized patients. Long-term recurrence was analyzed on a per-protocol basis among patients who completed follow-up. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 121 patients with PALs were enrolled and randomized to the ABPP group (n=62) or the ABPP plus doxycycline group (n=59). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics did not differ significantly between groups (Table 1). The mean age was 60±10.5 years in the ABPP group and 58±9.5 years in the combination group (p=0.2). Overall, men predominated in both study groups (65% vs. 35%, p=0.6). No statistically significant between-group differences were observed in body mass index (p=0.27), smoking status (p=0.1), or underlying causes of SSP (p=0.6), with COPD and infections being the most common causes. Baseline air leak severity was also similar between groups, with grade 1 leaks predominating in both the ABPP group (68%) and the combination group (61%) (p=0.47).

The probability of earlier air leak cessation was significantly higher in the combination group than in the ABPP-alone group, as shown by Kaplan-Meier analysis (log-rank p<0.001) (Fig. 3). Median time to air leak cessation was shorter in the combination group (4 days; IQR, 3–5 days) than in the ABPP-alone group (6 days; IQR, 5–8 days). By day 4 after pleurodesis, 64.4% of patients in the combination group had achieved cessation, compared with 25.8% in the ABPP-alone group. When outcomes were categorized by duration, a higher proportion of patients in the combination group achieved cessation within 7 days (81% vs. 74%), although this categorical difference was not statistically significant (p=0.34) (Table 2).

Kaplan-Meier curves for secondary outcomes also showed faster recovery in the combination group. Median time to intercostal tube removal was shorter in the ABPP plus doxycycline group than in the ABPP-alone group (5 days; IQR, 4–6 days vs. 7 days; IQR, 5.3–8.8 days; log-rank p<0.001). Hospital stay was also shorter in the combination group, with a median stay of 12 days (IQR, 11–13 days) compared with 14 days (IQR, 13–15.8 days) in the control group (log-rank p<0.001) (Fig. 4). On average, patients in the combination group were discharged 1.5 days earlier than those in the ABPP-alone group (12.59±2.34 days vs. 14.10±2.18 days).

Post-procedural pain, measured using the NPRS, was significantly higher in the combination group. The median pain score was 4 (IQR, 4–5) in the ABPP plus doxycycline group and 3 (IQR, 2–4) in the ABPP group (Mann-Whitney U test, p<0.001). The need for rescue analgesia was also greater in the combination group. Rescue opioid therapy beyond standard baseline analgesia was required in 49% of patients (n=29) in the ABPP plus doxycycline group and 23% (n=14) in the ABPP-alone group (p=0.002). All episodes of pain requiring opioid escalation with intravenous tramadol 50 mg were transient, resolved within 48 hours, and did not result in opioid-associated respiratory depression or prolonged hospitalization.

Overall safety was comparable between the 2 techniques (Table 3). The total complication rate was 40% (25/62) in the ABPP group and 36% (21/59) in the combination group, a difference that was not statistically significant (p=0.6). Tachycardia and fever occurred with similar frequency in both groups. Severe complications were rare; acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) occurred in 2 patients in the ABPP group and 1 patient in the combination group (p=1.000). Empyema occurred in 2 patients in the ABPP group and in no patients in the combination group.

Recurrence rates were low in both groups. At 1 month, recurrence was observed in 1 patient in the ABPP group and in no patients in the combination group (p=1.000). At 3 months, recurrence occurred in 4 patients (7%) in the ABPP group and 2 patients (3.8%) in the combination group. Discussion

PAL remains a frequent and challenging complication in patients with SSP, particularly in those with limited pulmonary reserve or those who are unfit for surgical intervention [16]. PAL contributes to higher morbidity, delayed chest tube removal, and prolonged hospitalization. No single procedure has been widely adopted for managing PAL, especially in nonsurgical candidates [4].

This randomized controlled study showed that concurrent ABPP and doxycycline was more effective than blood patch pleurodesis alone for managing PAL in patients with SSP. Although combination therapy was associated with a shorter mean air leak duration of approximately 1.5 days (p<0.001), the difference in the overall day 7 success rate was not statistically significant (81% vs. 74%, p=0.34). Faster air leak cessation was accompanied by a shorter hospital stay, which may reduce the burden on healthcare resources.

The present study found a significant reduction in mean time to air leak cessation in the combination therapy group compared with the ABPP-alone group (4.5±2.3 days vs. 6.0±2.2 days, p<0.001). This finding supports the proposed complementary mechanisms of the 2 agents. Autologous blood patch acts as a biological sealant by promoting fibrin formation and physically sealing the leak [6,17,18]. However, this seal may be degraded because blood clots are susceptible to fibrinolysis. Doxycycline is a chemical sclerosant that induces mesothelial inflammation and durable pleural symphysis [19]. Previous evidence also suggests that simple aspiration and drainage combined with minocycline, a tetracycline derivative, is superior to simple aspiration alone for treating spontaneous pneumothorax, supporting the sclerosing properties of this drug class [11]. In 1987, Robinson [20] reported the use of autologous blood for SSP with PAL, with a success rate of 85%. Several prospective studies have shown that, compared with conservative treatment, ABPP is associated with shorter time to air leak cessation, higher success rates, and fewer complications in patients with PAL [7,21,22]. Narenchandra et al. [14] previously compared ABPP and doxycycline as standalone parallel therapies and found comparable efficacy. In contrast, our findings suggest that concurrent administration accelerates physiological healing beyond what is achieved with ABPP alone, possibly by reinforcing the biological seal with chemical fibrosis. Chemical pleurodesis, including tetracycline and doxycycline pleurodesis, typically induces pleural inflammation and adhesion formation over 3–5 days [6]. By contrast, autologous blood can stop air leaks within 48 hours through its immediate patch effect [6]. The combination of ABPP and doxycycline may therefore provide an immediate seal while adding the longer-lasting effect of chemical sclerosis.

Regarding the success rate, defined as complete cessation within 7 days, the combination group had a higher rate than the control group (81% vs. 74%), but the difference was not statistically significant (p=0.34). The 74% success rate in the control group is similar to the pooled success rate of approximately 75% reported in a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of ABPP for PALs [18]. The absence of statistical superiority for the combination arm may reflect the already high baseline efficacy of ABPP. Reported success rates vary widely for autologous blood alone (59%–100%) and chemical agents alone (50%–95%), likely because of heterogeneous study populations [6,13]. A higher success rate has been reported with ABPP (94%) than with doxycycline alone (83%), possibly because of the high prevalence of COPD and interstitial lung disease in that cohort [14]. The safety and efficacy of ABPP observed in our control arm are further supported by studies that evaluated the safety profile and high success rate of ABPP in patients with PAL [23,24].

Cobanoglu et al. [6] reported success rates of 75% for ABPP and 64% for tetracycline, a difference that may have been influenced by preferential allocation of patients younger than 40 years to the ABPP group. Our data suggest that, although ABPP alone is effective, it may achieve complete air leak cessation more slowly than combination therapy. Similarly, 3-month recurrence rates were numerically lower in the combination group (3.8% vs. 7%, p=0.68). Although this difference was not statistically significant, it is consistent with the findings of Bhagat et al. [13], who observed that chemical sclerosants such as doxycycline may provide long-term protection against recurrence by obliterating the pleural space, an effect that a biodegradable fibrin patch alone may not sustain over time. Faster air leak cessation in the combination group directly translated into a shorter hospital stay (12.6 days vs. 14.1 days, p<0.001). Prolonged hospitalization is an important contributor to morbidity, hospital-acquired infection, and healthcare expenditure in frail patients with SSP [19]. The economic implication of our findings may therefore be clinically relevant. Although previous reports advocated ABPP as a cost-effective option because it does not require expensive sclerosing agents [6], the cost of adding a single dose of doxycycline is likely small compared with the potential savings from discharge 1.5 days earlier. Safety and tolerability are essential when treating patients with SSP, who often have limited pulmonary reserve. The addition of doxycycline resulted in significantly higher post-procedural pain scores (NPRS, 4.4 vs. 3.1; p<0.001). This finding is consistent with Narenchandra et al. [14], who reported that doxycycline pleurodesis alone is markedly more painful than the relatively painless ABPP. Other studies using doxycycline have similarly reported high incidences of severe chest pain and fever [25]. Adequate analgesia and intrapleural lignocaine are therefore required when this regimen is used. In the combination group, 200 mg of 2% lignocaine was injected into the pleural cavity before ABPP and doxycycline instillation to reduce anticipated pain. However, the increased pain did not lead to severe systemic adverse events. Overall complication rates were comparable between groups (36% vs. 40%, p=0.6), with no significant increase in severe morbidities such as empyema or ARDS. Prior studies have reported that chemical pleurodesis carries risks of respiratory failure and empyema [13,19]. In our study, sequential administration of autologous blood may have acted as a biological buffer, confining the sclerosing effect of doxycycline to the pleural space and limiting systemic absorption, which is a safety concern with standalone chemical sclerosants. Furthermore, whereas historical cohorts used 50–250 mL of blood in repeated daily injections [6,26,27], we used a single 50-mL blood patch injection under strict aseptic conditions, which may reduce the risk of bacterial contamination. Empyema occurred only in the ABPP group and resolved with intravenous antibiotics. Pleural drain obstruction is another important concern during blood pleurodesis and chemical pleurodesis. Flushing the tube after the procedure with normal saline and using large-bore drains have therefore been advocated [7,28,29]. However, normal saline may dilute the injected agent and reduce the effectiveness of pleurodesis. Cao et al. [7] injected 20 mL of air into the pleural cavity after pleurodesis instead of using normal saline. Air flushing may keep the tube unobstructed without causing clinically significant pneumothorax [7].

This study has several limitations. First, the distinct appearance of the interventions (red blood vs. yellow doxycycline) precluded full blinding and may have introduced observer bias; however, the primary outcome was derived from stored Thopaz+ device data by an independent data analyst who was blinded to group allocation. Second, the sample size was powered for the continuous outcome of time to cessation rather than for success rate or rare complications; therefore, the study may have been underpowered to detect small differences in these secondary endpoints. Third, 12 patients were lost to follow-up, and because recurrence was uncommon (6 total events), these missing data may have obscured long-term differences in recurrence between groups. Longer-term studies are therefore needed to confirm the durability of combined pleurodesis. Finally, the lack of a doxycycline-alone control arm limits our ability to directly compare the combination regimen with chemical pleurodesis alone in this cohort.

In conclusion, the combination of doxycycline with ABPP appears to be an effective, safe, and potentially cost-effective technique for managing PALs in SSP. Although adding doxycycline may increase post-procedural pain and discomfort, it significantly reduced air leak duration and hospital stay while achieving pleural symphysis without increasing major complications. This combined bedside approach may be considered for frail, nonsurgical candidates to expedite recovery and discharge.
