Comparing Add ⁡ ( M ) \mathrm{Add}(M) with Prod ⁡ ( M ) \mathrm{Prod}(M)
Simion Breaz, Cristian Rafiliu

Abstract
. We present characterizations for the inclusions Add ⁡ ( M ) ⊆ Prod ⁡ ( M ) \mathrm{Add}(M)\subseteq\mathrm{Prod}(M) and Prod ⁡ ( M ) ⊆ Add ⁡ ( M ) \mathrm{Prod}(M)\subseteq\mathrm{Add}(M) in locally finitely presented categories and in compactly generated triangulated categories. As applications, we describe the situations when the classes of the form Prod ⁡ ( M ) \mathrm{Prod}(M) and Add ⁡ ( M ) \mathrm{Add}(M) are (pre)covering, respectively (pre)enveloping.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary: 18G80, 18A25; Secondary: 18G05; 20K25.

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an additive category with direct sums and products. If
M\in\mathcal{A}
then
\mathrm{Add}(M)
and
\mathrm{Prod}(M)
denote the class of all direct summands of direct sums, respectively of direct products of copies of
M
. If
\mathcal{A}
is a module category the situations where one of these classes is contained in the other, investigated in
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
and
[
22
]
, lead to useful properties of
M
. For instance, a module
M
has the property
\mathrm{Prod}(M)\subseteq\mathrm{Add}(M)
, such modules were introduced in
[
22
]
under the name
product-complete
, if and only if
\mathrm{Add}(M)
is an (pre)enveloping class (
[
1
, Theorem 5.1]
,
[
22
, Theorem 2.3]
). They play an important role in tilting theory
[
4
]
. Recently, this property was also used in locally finitely presented categories,
[
19
]
, and in compactly generated triangulated categories,
[
16
]
.
On the other hand, the study and the applications of the inclusion
\mathrm{Add}(M)\subseteq\mathrm{Prod}(M)
depend on some set-theoretic hypotheses. In the absence of
\omega
-measurable cardinals, it was proved that
\mathrm{Add}(M)\subseteq\mathrm{Prod}(M)
if and only if
M
is
\Sigma
-pure-injective module (see
[
7
]
and
[
27
]
). However, Šaroch proved in
[
27
]
that if we assume that there are some large cardinals then in every module category there exists a free module
F
such that
\mathrm{Add}(F)\subseteq\mathrm{Prod}(F)
. Such modules are used in
[
10
]
and
[
28
]
.

Our first aim is to study these inclusions for locally finitely presented categories. To do this, we will start with an analysis of Chase’s Lemma
[
9
]
, since this is an important tool in proving characterizations for
\Sigma
-pure-injective modules, see
[
31
]
. The usual statement of this lemma is about maps defined on countable direct products. We also need a version when the domains are direct products indexed by non
\omega
-measurable cardinals, a generalization of
[
11
, Theorem 2]
.
We will use this to prove in Theorem
3.6
that an object
M
, with
\mathrm{Hom}(X,M)
non
\omega
-measurable for all finitely presented objects
X
, is
\Sigma
-pure-injective if and only if
\mathrm{Add}(M)\subseteq\mathrm{Prod}(M)
. As an application, in Corollary
3.10
we prove that if we assume that there are no
\omega
-measurable cardinals, then the class
\mathrm{Prod}(M)
is (pre)covering if and only if
M
is
\Sigma
-pure-injective. This leads to the conclusion that, if we assume that there are no
\omega
-measurable cardinals, Enochs’ Conjecture is true for classes of the form
\mathrm{Prod}(M)
in locally finitely presented categories, Corollary
3.11
.
A characterization for
\mathrm{Prod}(M)\subseteq\mathrm{Add}(M)
is presented in Theorem
3.13
. This theorem extends
[
2
, Proposition 5.2]
(see also
[
3
, Proposition 4.8]
) from module categories to locally finitely presented categories. Moreover,
\mathrm{Add}(M)
is (pre)enveloping if and only if
M
is product-complete (Corollary
3.14
).

In the last part of the paper we will use the results obtained for locally finitely presented categories to approach the same problems in compactly generated triangulated categories. As expected, we have similar characterizations. We include in Corollary
4.9
a positive answer for classes of the form
\mathrm{Prod}(M)
to a triangulated version of Enochs’s conjecture. In Theorem
4.10
we also give a triangulated version of
[
19
, Proposition 12.3.7]
.

2.
A Chase-type Lemma

A tuple

((A_{i})_{i\in I},(B_{j})_{j\in J},\varphi,((A_{i,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}})_{i\in I},((B_{j,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}})_{j\in J})

is called a
Chase system
if

•

(A_{i})_{i\in I}
and
(B_{j})_{j\in J}
are two familes of abelian groups,

•

\varphi:\prod_{i\in I}A_{i}\to\bigoplus_{j\in J}B_{j}
is a morphism of abelian groups,

•

for all
i\in I
,
(A_{i,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a descending family of subgroups of
A_{i}
,

•

for all
j\in J
,
(B_{j,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a descending family of subgroups of
B_{j}
,

•

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varphi(\prod_{i\in I}A_{i,k})\subseteq\bigoplus_{j\in J}B_{j,k}
.

The following result is a version for Chase’s Lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let

((A_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}},(B_{j})_{j\in J},\varphi,((A_{i,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}},((B_{j,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}})_{j\in J})

be a Chase system. Then there exists a finite subset
J^{\prime}\subseteq J
and two positive integers
m_{0}
and
n_{0}
such that

\textstyle\varphi\left(\prod_{i\geq m_{0}}A_{i,n_{0}}\right)\subseteq\bigoplus_{j\in J^{\prime}}B_{j}+\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\bigoplus_{j\in J}B_{j,n}\right).

Proof.

We observe that it is enough to prove the conclusion for
m_{0}=n_{0}
.
Following the steps of Chase
[
9
]
, we assume by contradiction that the conclusion is false.

In particular, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and for every finite subset
J^{\prime}\subseteq J
we have
\varphi\left(\prod_{i\geq n}A_{i,n}\right)\nsubseteq\bigoplus_{j\in J^{\prime}}B_{j}+\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\bigoplus_{j\in J}B_{j,n}\right)
.
We will construct a strictly ascending sequence
(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of positive integers, a sequence
(j_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of elements of
J
and a sequence
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with
x_{k}\in\prod_{i\geq n_{k}}A_{i,n_{k}}
, such that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have:

(a)

q_{j_{k}}\varphi(x_{k})\notin B_{j_{k},n_{k+1}}
;

(b)

q_{j_{k}}\varphi(x_{l})=0
, for all
l<k
,

where
q_{j_{k}}:\bigoplus_{j\in J}B_{j}\to B_{j_{k}}
are the natural projections.

For
k=0
take
n_{0}=0
. It follows that there exists
x_{0}\in\prod_{i\geq 0}A_{i,0}
such that
\varphi(x_{0})\notin\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\bigoplus_{j\in J}B_{j,n}\right)
. This implies that there exists
n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\varphi(x_{0})\notin\bigoplus_{j\in J}B_{j,n_{1}}
, hence there exists
j_{0}\in J
such that
q_{j_{0}}\varphi(x_{0})\notin B_{j_{0},n_{1}}
.

Let
J^{\prime}=\{j\in J\mid q_{j}\varphi(x_{0})\neq 0\}
. Since
\varphi\left(\prod_{i\geq n_{1}}A_{i,n_{1}}\right)
is not contained in
\bigoplus_{j\in J^{\prime}}B_{j}+\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\bigoplus_{j\notin J^{\prime}}B_{j,n}\right)\subseteq\bigoplus_{j\in J^{\prime}}B_{j}+\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\bigoplus_{j\in J}B_{j,n}\right),
there exists
x_{1}\in\prod_{i\geq n_{1}}A_{i,n_{1}}
,
j_{1}\notin J^{\prime}
and
n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\varphi(x_{1})\notin B_{j_{1},n_{2}}
. Since the chains of subgroups are descending, we observe that we can take
n_{2}\geq n_{1}
.

Consequently, the elements
x_{0},x_{1},n_{1},n_{2},j_{0},
and
j_{1}
satisfy the conditions (a) and (b). We can continue in the same manner, and we obtain the required sequences by induction.

We define
x=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}x_{k}\in\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}A_{i}
(because
x_{k}\in\prod_{i\geq n_{k}}A_{i}
, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
the
i
-th components of the elements
x_{k}
are almost all
0
).

Observe that for
l>k
we have
x_{l}\in\prod_{i\geq n_{l}}A_{i,n_{l}}\subseteq\prod_{i\geq n_{l}}A_{i,n_{k+1}}\subseteq\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}A_{i,n_{k+1}}
, so
\varphi(x_{l})\in\varphi(\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}A_{i,n_{k+1}})\subseteq\bigoplus_{j\in J}B_{j,n_{k+1}}.

It follows that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
we obtain

q_{j_{k}}\varphi(x)=q_{j_{k}}\varphi(\sum_{l<k}x_{l})+q_{j_{k}}\varphi(x_{k})+q_{j_{k}}\varphi(\sum_{l>k}x_{l})\neq 0,

because
q_{j_{k}}\varphi(\sum_{l<k}x_{l})=0
,
q_{j_{k}}\varphi(x_{k})\notin B_{j_{k},n_{k+1}}
, and
q_{j_{k}}\varphi(\sum_{l>k}x_{l})\in B_{j_{k},n_{k+1}}
.
This contradicts the fact that
\varphi(x)\in\bigoplus_{j\in J}B_{j}
, and the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Similar results, proved with similar techniques, are also provided in
[
5
, Lemma 4.8]
, in the language of model theory, and in
[
18
, Lemma 4]
for subgroups of finite definition.

In the following, we will give a more general version of the previous version of Chase’s Lemma. It extends similar results proved in
[
7
]
and
[
11
]
, where the chains of subgroups are values of some descending chains of subfunctors of the identity functor.

A cardinal
\kappa=|I|
(or the set
I
) is called
\omega
-measurable
if it is uncountable and there exists a countably-additive, non-trivial,
\{0,1\}
-valued measure
\mu
, whose
\sigma
-algebra is the power set of
I
such that
\mu(I)=1
and
\mu(\{x\})=0
for all
x\in I
. We refer to
[
12
]
for details about (non)
\omega
-measurable cardinals.
The next lemma gives us an easy way to prove that a cardinal is
\omega
-measurable. The reader can find proofs for this in the proofs of
[
11
, Theorem 2]
and
[
8
, Proposition 26]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
I
be an uncountable set. If there exists a subset
\mathcal{I}
in the power-set of
I
such that

(i)

I\notin\mathcal{I}
,

(ii)

if
F\subseteq I
is finite then
F\in\mathcal{I}
,

(iii)

\mathcal{I}
is closed with respect to finite unions,

(iv)

If
X\in\mathcal{I}
and
Y\subseteq X
then
Y\in\mathcal{I}
,

(v)

if
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a countable family of pairwise disjoint subsets of
I
then there exists
n_{0}
such that
\bigcup_{n\geq n_{0}}X_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
,

then
|I|
is
\omega
-measurable.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that the set
I
is not
\omega
-measurable. If

((A_{i})_{i\in I},(B_{j})_{j\in J},\varphi,((A_{i,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}})_{i\in I},((B_{j,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}})_{j\in J})

is a Chase system, then there are two finite subsets
I^{\prime}\subseteq I,J^{\prime}\subseteq J
and a positive integer
n_{0}
such that

\textstyle\varphi\left(\prod_{i\in I\backslash I^{\prime}}A_{i,n_{0}}\right)\subseteq\bigoplus_{j\in J^{\prime}}B_{j}+\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\bigoplus_{j\in J}B_{j,n}\right).

Proof.

If
I
is finite, the conclusion is obvious. For the case
I
countable, we apply Lemma
2.1
.

Suppose that
|I|>\aleph_{0}
. We consider the set
\mathcal{I}
of all subsets
T\subseteq I
with the property that the conclusion of our lemma is valid for the Chase system

\textstyle((A_{i})_{i\in T},(B_{j})_{j\in J},\varphi_{|\prod_{i\in T}A_{i}},((A_{i,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}})_{i\in T},((B_{j,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}})_{j\in J}),

where
\varphi_{|\prod_{i\in T}A_{i}}
denotes the restriction of
\varphi
to
\prod_{i\in T}A_{i}
. We will prove that
\mathcal{I}
satisfies the conditions (ii)–(v) from Lemma
2.3
.

It is not hard to verify the conditions (ii), (iii) and (iv) listed in Lemma
2.3
. For (v), let
(I_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of pairwise disjoint subsets of
I
. We observe first that we can use the identification

\textstyle\prod_{i\in\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}I_{n}}A_{i}=\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\prod_{i\in I_{n}}A_{i}\right)

to obtain the Chase system

\textstyle\left(\left(\prod_{i\in I_{n}}A_{i}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(B_{j})_{j\in J},\varphi^{\prime},\left(\left(\prod_{i\in I_{n}}A_{i,k}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}},((B_{j,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}})_{j\in J}\right),

where
\phi^{\prime}
is the restriction of
\varphi
to
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\prod_{i\in I_{n}}A_{i}\right)
.
We apply Lemma
2.1
, and it follows that
\cup_{n\geq m_{0}}I_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
.

Since the set
I
is not
\omega
-measurable, it follows that
I\in\mathcal{I}
, and the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

Lemma
2.4
is not valid if
I
is
\omega
-measurable. A counterexample is constructed in
[
11
, Example 3]
.

Chase’s Lemma is often used to provide descending chain conditions. In this paper, we will use the following proposition.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let

\textstyle((A_{i})_{i\in I},(B_{j})_{j\in J},\varphi:\prod_{i\in I}A_{i}\to\bigoplus_{j\in J}B_{j},((A_{i,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}})_{i\in I},((B_{j,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}})_{j\in J})

be a Chase system.

If

(i)

I
is a set of non-
\omega
-measurable cardinality,

(ii)

J
an infinite set with
|J|>|A_{i}|
for all
i\in I
,

(iii)

\varphi
is an epimorphism, and

(iv)

the restrictions
\varphi|_{\prod_{i\in I}A_{i,k}}:\prod_{i\in I}A_{i,k}\to\bigoplus_{j\in J}B_{j,k}
are epimorphisms for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

then there exists a positive integer
n_{0}
and an infinite subset
L\subseteq J
such that
B_{j,n}=B_{j,n_{0}}
for all
j\in L
and all
n\geq n_{0}
.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
2.4
for the Chase system

((A_{i})_{i\in I},(B_{j})_{j\in J},\varphi,((A_{i,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}})_{i\in I},((B_{j,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}})_{j\in J}),

we obtain that there are two finite subsets
I^{\prime}\subseteq I,J^{\prime}\subseteq J
and a positive integer
n_{0}
such that

\textstyle\varphi\left(\prod_{i\in I\backslash I^{\prime}}A_{i,n_{0}}\right)\subseteq\bigoplus_{j\in J^{\prime}}B_{j}+\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\bigoplus_{j\in J}B_{j,n}\right).

Following almost the same algorithm like in
[
7
, Proposition 2.3]
, we obtain that

\textstyle\varphi\left(\prod_{i\in I\backslash I^{\prime}}A_{i,n_{0}}\right)\subseteq\bigoplus_{j\in J^{\prime}}B_{j,n_{0}}+\bigoplus_{j\in J\backslash J^{\prime}}\left(\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}B_{j,n}\right)

and, because
\varphi|_{\prod_{i\in I}A_{i,n_{0}}}:\prod_{i\in I}A_{i,n_{0}}\to\bigoplus_{j\in J}B_{j,n_{0}}
is an epimorphism, it induces an epimorphism of abelian groups:

\bar{\varphi}:\frac{\prod_{i\in I}A_{i,n_{0}}}{\prod_{i\in I\backslash I^{\prime}}A_{i,n_{0}}}\to\frac{\bigoplus_{j\in J}B_{j,n_{0}}}{\bigoplus_{j\in J^{\prime}}B_{j,n_{0}}+\bigoplus_{j\in J\backslash J^{\prime}}\left(\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}B_{j,n}\right)}\cong\bigoplus_{j\in J\backslash J^{\prime}}\frac{B_{j,n_{0}}}{\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}B_{j,n}}.

Using (ii), it follows that

\textstyle\left|\bigoplus_{j\in J\backslash J^{\prime}}\frac{B_{j,n_{0}}}{\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}B_{j,n}}\right|\leq\left|\prod_{i\in I^{\prime}}A_{i,n_{0}}\right|<|J|,

hence the set
L=\{j\in J\backslash J^{\prime}|\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}B_{j,n}=B_{j,n_{0}}\}
is infinite. ∎

3.
Comparing
\mathrm{Add}(M)
with
\mathrm{Prod}(M)
in locally finitely presented additive categories

In this section
\mathcal{A}
will be a locally finitely presented category. The subcategory of all finitely presented objects from
\mathcal{A}
is denoted by
\mathrm{fp}\mathcal{A}
. We refer to
[
19
]
for the main properties of these categories. In particular,
\mathcal{A}
is idempotent complete, with cokernels, filtered colimits (hence, with direct sums) and direct products.

3.1.
Purity in locally finitely presented categories

We recall for the reader’s convenience some basic information. According to
[
19
, Lemma 12.1.4]
there is a fully faithful functor

ev:\mathcal{A}\to\textbf{P}(\mathcal{A}),

where
\mathbf{P}(A)
is a locally finitely presented Grothendieck category, called
the purity category of
\mathcal{A}
, and
ev
commutes with all filtered colimits, direct products and cokernels. A sequence of morphisms

0\to K\overset{\alpha}{\to}L\overset{\beta}{\to}M\to 0

from
\mathcal{A}
is a
pure-exact
sequence if for every
X\in\mathrm{fp}\mathcal{A}
the sequence of abelian groups

0\to\mathrm{Hom}(X,A)\overset{\mathrm{Hom}(X,\alpha)}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{Hom}(X,L)\overset{\mathrm{Hom}(X,\beta)}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{Hom}(X,M)\to 0

is a short exact sequence. In this case, we say that
\alpha
is a
pure mononomorphism
and
\beta
is a
pure epimorphism
. If
L\in\mathcal{A}
, an object
K
is a
pure subobject
of
L
if there exists a pure monomorphism
K\to L
, and an object
M
is a
pure quotient
of
L
if there exists a pure epimorphism
L\to M.
For further use, let us remark that from
[
19
, Lemma 12.1.6 (2)]
it follows that these notions coincide with those used in
[
24
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The following are true in a locally finitely presented category
\mathcal{A}
.

a)

A sequence of morphisms
0\to K\overset{\alpha}{\to}L\overset{\beta}{\to}M\to 0
is pure-exact if and only if
0\to ev(K)\overset{ev(\alpha)}{\longrightarrow}ev(L)\overset{ev(\beta)}{\longrightarrow}ev(M)\to 0
is a short exact sequence in
\mathbf{P}(A)
.

b)

A morphism
\alpha:L\to K
in
\mathcal{A}
is a pure monomorphism if and only if
ev(\alpha)
is a monomorphism.

c)

A sequence of morphisms
0\to K\overset{\alpha}{\to}L\overset{\beta}{\to}M\to 0
is pure-exact if and only if
\alpha
is a pure monomorphism and
\beta
is the cokernel of
\alpha
.

d)

A composition of two pure monomorphisms is a pure monomorphism.

e)

A direct product (direct sum) of pure-exact sequences is pure-exact.

Proof.

a) This is proved in
[
19
, Lemma 12.1.6]
.

b) See
[
19
, Lemma 12.1.7]
.

c) Suppose that
0\to K\overset{\alpha}{\to}L\overset{\beta}{\to}M\to 0
is pure-exact. Let
L\overset{\nu}{\to}N
be the cokernel of
\alpha
. Since
ev
preserves the cokernels, it follows that
ev(\nu)
is the cokernel of
ev(\alpha)
. Since the sequence is pure-exact, it follows that
ev(\beta)
is the cokernel of
ev(\alpha)
. Then there exists an isomorphism
\overline{\gamma}:ev(M)\to ev(N)
such that
ev(\nu)=\overline{\gamma}ev(\beta).
But
ev
is fully faithful, hence there exists an isomorphism
\gamma:M\to N
such that
\nu=\gamma\beta
, hence
\beta
is a cokernel for
\alpha
.

Conversely, if
\alpha
is a pure monomorphism and
\beta
is the cokernel of
\alpha
, since
ev
preserves the cokernels, it follows that
0\to ev(K)\overset{ev(\alpha)}{\longrightarrow}ev(L)\overset{ev(\beta)}{\longrightarrow}ev(M)\to 0
is a short exact sequence.

d) This follows from b).

e) This is true since the covariant functors
\mathrm{Hom}(X,-)
(with
X
finitely presented) commute with respect to direct products (direct sums) and in the category of all abelian groups a direct product (direct sum) of short exact sequences is a short exact sequence.
∎

An object
N
is
pure-injective
if for every pure monomorphism
\alpha
the morphism
\mathrm{Hom}(\alpha,N)
is surjective. It follows from
[
19
, Lemma 12.1.8]
that
ev
gives an equivalence between the subcategory of pure-injective objects of
\mathcal{A}
and the subcategory of injective objects from
\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{A})
.

3.2.
Subgroups of finite definition

Every morphism
b:X\to Y
in
\mathrm{fp}\mathcal{A}
induces an additive functor

\mathrm{Hom}(Y,-)b:\mathcal{A}\to\textbf{Ab},

defined in the following way:

•

\mathrm{Hom}(Y,M)b=\{f\in\mathrm{Hom}(X,M)|f=\varphi b,\text{ for some }\varphi\in\mathrm{Hom}(Y,M)\}
for all
M\in\mathcal{A}
, and

•

\mathrm{Hom}(Y,f)b:\mathrm{Hom}(Y,M)b\to\mathrm{Hom}(Y,N)b,gb\mapsto fgb,
for every morphism
f:M\to N
.

The sets
\mathrm{Hom}(Y,M)b
are called the
subgroups of finite definition of
\mathrm{Hom}(X,M)
.

Remark 3.2
.

Let
b:X\to Y\in\mathrm{fp}\mathcal{A}
. Then:

(1)

\mathrm{Hom}(Y,-)b:\mathcal{A}\to\textbf{Ab}
is an additive subfunctor of the covariant functor
\mathrm{Hom}(X,-):\mathcal{A}\to\textbf{Ab}
;

(2)

\mathrm{Hom}(Y,-)b
commutes with respect to direct products and direct sums i.e. for any family
(M_{i})_{i\in I}
of objects in
\mathcal{A}
we have natural isomorphisms

\textstyle\mathrm{Hom}(Y,\prod_{i\in I}M_{i})b\simeq\prod_{i\in I}\mathrm{Hom}(Y,M_{i})b

and

\textstyle\mathrm{Hom}(Y,\bigoplus_{i\in I}M_{i})b\simeq\bigoplus_{i\in I}\mathrm{Hom}(Y,M_{i})b.

(3)

For any morphism
f:\prod_{i\in I}M_{i}\to\bigoplus_{j\in J}N_{j}
in
\mathcal{A}
we can view the morphism
\mathrm{Hom}(Y,f)b
as a morphism

\textstyle\mathrm{Hom}(Y,f)b:\prod_{i\in I}\mathrm{Hom}(Y,M_{i})b\to\bigoplus_{j\in J}\mathrm{Hom}(Y,N_{j})b.

Remark 3.3
.

The subgroups of finite definition are also used in compactly generated triangulated categories in
[
5
]
and
[
13
]

4.

In this section,
\mathcal{T}
will be a compactly generated triangulated category. This means that
\mathcal{T}
is triangulated, has all direct sums (coproducts), the triangulated subcategory
\mathcal{T}^{c}
of all compact objects from
\mathcal{T}
(

commutes with respect to all direct sums) is small and
\mathrm{Hom}(\mathcal{T}^{c},X)\neq 0

4.1.
Purity in compactly generated triangulated categories

We recall here, for further use, some basic information about purity in compactly generated triangulated categories.
We denote by
\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}\mathcal{T}^{c}
the category of functors
(\mathcal{T}^{c})^{op}\to\textbf{Ab}

There is a restricted Yoneda functor, introduced in
[
21
]

H:\mathcal{T}\to\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}\mathcal{T}^{c},\ M\mapsto H_{M}=\mathrm{Hom}(-,M)|_{\mathcal{T}^{c}}.

A triangle
A\overset{\alpha}{\to}B\overset{\beta}{\to}C\overset{\phi}{\to}A[1]
is

if
\mathrm{Hom}(\mathcal{T}^{c},\phi)=0
. This is equivalent to the fact that the induced sequence

is a short exact sequence in
\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}\mathcal{T}^{c}
. In this case, we will say that
\alpha
is
pure monomorphism
and
\beta
is
pure epimorphism
,
A
is a
pure subobject
of
B
and
C
is a
pure quotient
of
B
. An object
M
is

if
\mathrm{Hom}(\alpha,M)
is surjective for all pure monomorphisms
\alpha
(i.e.,
\mathrm{Hom}(-,M)
transforms pure triangles in exact sequences of abelian groups).

The equivalences between (i), (ii) and (iv) in the following theorem were proved in
[

, Theorem 1.8]

-measurable. The following are equivalent:

(i)

M
is
\Sigma
-pure-injective;

(ii)

\mathrm{Prod}(M)
is definable;

(iii)

\mathrm{Prod}(M)
is a precovering class;

(iv)

\mathrm{Prod}(M)
is a covering class.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) This follows from
[
24
, Theorem 4.7]
and Corollary
4.3
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii) It is enough to apply
[
24
, Corollary 4.8]
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i) It is a consequence of Lemma
3.8
and Theorem
4.4
.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(iii) This is obvious.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv) We will follow the main ideas presented in the proof of
[
1
, Proposition 4.1]
.

Let
A
be an object from
\mathcal{T}
. It is not hard to see that it has a
\mathrm{Prod}(M)
-precover of the form
\alpha:M^{I}\to A
. We complete
\alpha
to a triangle
B\overset{\gamma}{\to}M^{I}\overset{\alpha}{\to}A\to B[1],
and consider a
\mathrm{Prod}(M)
-precover
\beta:M^{J}\to B
. If
|J|>|I|
, we can assume
I\subseteq J
and that
J
is infinite. We write
M^{J}=M^{I}\oplus M^{J\setminus I}
and we have the triangle

B\oplus M^{J\setminus I}\overset{\gamma\oplus 1}{\longrightarrow}M^{I}\oplus M^{J\setminus I}\overset{\alpha\oplus 0}{\longrightarrow}A\to(B\oplus M^{J\setminus I})[1].

Observe that
\alpha\oplus 0
is a
\mathrm{Prod}(M)
-precover for
A
and that
\beta\oplus\pi:M^{J}\oplus M^{J}\to B\oplus M^{J\setminus I}
, where
\pi
is the canonical projection, is a
\mathrm{Prod}(M)
-precover for
B\oplus M^{J\setminus I}
.

It follows that we always have a triangle

C\overset{\gamma}{\to}M^{J}\overset{\alpha}{\to}A\to C[1]\text{ and a morphism }\beta:M^{K}\to C

such that
\alpha
and
\beta
are
\mathrm{Prod}(M)
-precovers for
A
and
C
, respectively, and
|K|\leq|J|
. Applying the functor
\mathrm{Hom}(M^{J},-):\mathcal{T}\to\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}\mathrm{End}(M^{J})
, we obtain the exact sequences of
\mathrm{End}(M^{J})
-modules:

\mathrm{Hom}(M^{J},C)\to\mathrm{Hom}(M^{J},M^{J})\to\mathrm{Hom}(M^{J},A)\to 0

and

\mathrm{Hom}(M^{J},M^{K})\to\mathrm{Hom}(M^{J},C)\to 0.

It follows that
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{Hom}(M^{J},\alpha))=\{\delta\in\mathrm{End}(M^{J})\mid\alpha\delta=0\}
is a finitely generated right ideal of
\mathrm{End}(M^{J})
. Using Corollary
4.3
and Lemma
4.6
we observe that
\mathrm{End}(M^{J})
is semiregular, hence every finitely generated right ideal is a direct summand modulo the Jacobson radical. We apply Lemma
4.7
to conclude that
A
has a
\mathrm{Prod}(M)
-cover.
∎

We can formulate a triangulated version of Enochs’ conjecture:
every covering class in an algebraic compactly generated triangulated category is closed under directed homotopy colimits.

Corollary 4.9
.

Assume that there are no
\omega
-measurable cardinals. Then the triangulated Enochs’s conjecture is valid for classes of the form
\mathrm{Prod}(M)
in algebraic compactly generated triangulated categories.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathcal{T}
is an algebraic compactly generated triangulated category. From the proof of
[
6
, Proposition 6.8]
it follows that if
X_{i}
is a directed family in
\mathcal{T}
then the canonical morphism
\oplus_{i\in I}X_{i}\to\mathrm{hocolim}_{i\in I}X_{i}
is a pure epimorphism. Using Corollary
4.8
, we obtain the conclusion.
∎

4.3.
Product-complete objects

As before, we have a triangulated version for the characterization of product-complete objects in locally finitely presented categories.

For information on underlying categories of strong and stable derivators, we refer to
[
23
]
.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
M
be an object of
\mathcal{T}
. The following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathrm{Prod}(M)=\mathrm{Add}(M)
;

(ii)

\mathrm{Prod}(M)\subseteq\mathrm{Add}(M)
;

(iii)

\mathrm{Prod}(H_{M})\subseteq\mathrm{Add}(H_{M})
;

(iv)

(a)

M
is
\Sigma
-pure-injective;

(b)

M
is product-rigid.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{T}
is the underlying category of a strong and stable derivator the above conditions are equivalent with

(v)

\mathrm{Add}(M)
is definable;

(vi)

(a)

M
is
\Sigma
-pure-injective,

(b)

the set of all indecomposable direct summands of
M
form a Ziegler closed set.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) is obvious.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii) This follows by using steps similar to those presented in Theorem
4.4
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv) We have
\mathrm{Prod}(H_{M})\subseteq\mathrm{Add}(H_{M})
, and we use Theorem
3.13
to observe that
H_{M}
is
\Sigma
-pure-injective and product-rigid. Since
H_{M}
is
\Sigma
-pure-injective, we also obtain that
M
is
\Sigma
-pure-injective.

If
X\in\mathrm{Prod}(M)
is an object with local endomorphism ring then
X
is pure-injective, hence
\mathrm{Hom}(X,X)\simeq\mathrm{Hom}(H_{X},H_{X})
. Then the endomorphism ring of
H_{X}
is local, and we obtain that
H_{X}
is isomorphic to a direct summand of
H_{M}
. Using Theorem
4.1
one more time, it follows that
X
is isomorphic to a direct summand of
M
.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(i) Since
\mathrm{Add}(M)\subseteq\mathrm{Prod}(M)
for any
\Sigma
-pure-injective object
M
, we have to prove the inclusion
\mathrm{Prod}(M)\subseteq\mathrm{Add}(M)
. In fact it is enough to prove that
\prod_{i\in I}M\in\mathrm{Add}(M)
for any set
I
.

Because
M
is
\Sigma
-pure-injective,
\prod_{i\in I}M
is isomorphic to a direct sum of indecomposable pure-injective objects with local endomorphism rings (see Theorem
4.2
), so
\prod_{i\in I}M\simeq\bigoplus_{j\in J}X_{j}
such that
X_{j}
are objects with local endomorphism rings from
\mathrm{Prod}(M)
.
Since
M
is product-rigid, every
X_{j}
is isomorphic to a direct summand of
M
,
so
\prod_{i\in I}M\in\mathrm{Add}(M)
.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(v) From Corollary
4.3
, it follows that the class
\mathrm{Add}(M)
is closed under pure subobjects, products and pure quotients. Now we use
[
6
, Proposition 6.8]
to conclude that
\mathrm{Add}(M)
is a definable class.

The proofs for (v)
\Rightarrow
(vi) and (vi)
\Rightarrow
(i) are the same as those presented for finitely presented categories in
[
19
, Proposition 12.3.7]
, this time using the fundamental correspondence presented in
[
20
]
.
∎

Remark 4.11
.

The equivalence (ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii) says that an object
M
is product-complete in
\mathcal{T}
if and only if
H_{M}
is product-complete in
\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}\mathcal{T}^{c}
and the equivalence (iii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iv) also says that, under the hypothesis of
\Sigma
-pure-injectivity on the object
M
,
M
is product-rigid in
\mathcal{T}
if and only if
H_{M}
is product-rigid in
\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}\mathcal{T}^{c}
. We do not know if this correspondence between product-rigid objects holds in the absence of the pure-injectivity hypothesis.

We also have a triangulated version, with a similar proof, of Corollary
3.14
.

Corollary 4.12
.

The following are equivalent for an object
M\in\mathcal{T}
:

(i)

M
is product-complete;

(ii)

\mathrm{Add}(M)
is a preenveloping class in
\mathcal{A}
;

(iii)

\mathrm{Add}(M)
is an enveloping class.
