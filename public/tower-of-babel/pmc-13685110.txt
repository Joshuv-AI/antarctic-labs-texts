EEG-defined differences in functional networks following beta-band transcranial alternating current stimulation in patients with disorders of consciousness: a pilot study
Authors: Sheng Qu, Laigang Huang, Jingjing Shang, Yanqi Qiao, Qiangsan Sun, Fanshuo Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSevere disruption of cortico-thalamocortical circuits underlies disorders of consciousness (DOC). Beta-band (20 Hz) transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS) can be used to probe frontal oscillatory synchrony, yet its potential to acutely modulate large-scale network dynamics in DOC is unknown.MethodsWe applied 20 Hz tACS (1 mA) for 30 min over the frontal cortex (F3 and F4, International 10–20 system) in 28 patients with DOC. Offline functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) was used to quantify hemoglobin (HbO)-based functional connectivity (FC) and graph-theoretical network properties, including small-world properties (clustering coefficient, Cp; path length, Lp) and global efficiency (global efficiency, Eg; local efficiency, Eloc) at pre-stimulation resting-state measurement for 5 min (pre-stim), the first post-stimulation resting-state measurement for 5 min (post-stim1), and the second post-stimulation resting-state measurement for 5 min (post-stim2). Subgroups were defined by baseline video-electroencephalography (vEEG) pathology as moderately abnormal (n = 11) and diffuse slowing (n = 14); 3 patients excluded (incomplete vEEG).ResultsFrontal 20 Hz tACS was associated with transient enhancement of FC across the measured cortical network at post-stim1 (pFDR < 0.05), selectively strengthening intra-and interhemispheric and intra-nodal connectivity before resolving by post-stim2. Crucially, the effects were pathology-dependent, and visual-motor integration pathways (right occipital lobe to left motor cortex; right primary somatosensory cortex to left occipital lobe) emerged exclusively in DOC patients with moderately abnormal background but not in those with diffuse slowing. Concurrent acute network reorganization at post-stim1 (all p < 0.05) featured increased Cp, Eg, and Eloc, along with decreased Lp, all normalizing by post-stim2. Clinically, Coma Recovery Scale-Revised scores correlated positively with average FC of HbO (pre-stim: r = 0.47; post-stim2: r = 0.41) and Area Under the Curve (AUC) of Eg (pre-stim: r = 0.44), and negatively with AUC of Lp (pre-stim: r = − 0.41; all p < 0.05).ConclusionsFrontal beta-tACS was associated with state-dependent changes in functional network organization in DOC, with dynamic fNIRS measures that may be useful for tracking these changes. These findings provide preliminary descriptive evidence of short-term network changes associated with beta-tACS and may help guide future controlled studies of targeted neuromodulation in patients with DOC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12984-026-02102-7.

Introduction

Disorders of consciousness (DOC) represent a spectrum of clinical conditions, most commonly resulting from severe acquired brain injury, primarily comprising unresponsive wakefulness syndrome/vegetative state (UWS/VS) and minimally conscious state (MCS) [1, 2]. The pathophysiological hallmark of impaired consciousness in DOC involves profound disruption of the cortico-thalamo-cortical circuits [3], manifesting as compromised neocortical-thalamic neurotransmission, which leads to depressed cortical excitatory synaptic activity and global neuronal silencing with reduced firing rates [4, 5]. Therefore, functional restoration of these critical circuits is hypothesized to be essential for supporting behavioral responsiveness and conscious awareness [6].

Recent advances in neuromodulation strategies suggest that widespread neuronal dysfunction caused by multifocal brain injuries requires the restoration of functional integrity in cortico-thalamo-cortical circuits to facilitate consciousness recovery [7, 8]. Notably, transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS) noninvasively modulates cortical oscillations using low-intensity (< 2 mA) frequency-specific sinusoidal currents that synchronize with endogenous brain rhythms (e.g., alpha, beta, and gamma bands) [9]. Through subthreshold oscillatory polarization, which is distinct from sustained depolarization, tACS induces phase-locked activity, neuronal synchronization, and neuroplasticity [10]. It delivers a bidirectional current waveform (net zero charge), which minimizes sensory side effects, prevents direct motor neuron activation, while leveraging stochastic resonance to enhance neuronal responsiveness [11]. This frequency-tuned mechanism engages large-scale cortical networks and modulates specific neural circuits through regulatory (non-forcing) effects on neurovascular coupling [12] while permitting targeted modulation of higher-order cognition [13].

The Arnold Tongue framework delineates tACS's frequency-matching principle, stating that optimal entrainment occurs when stimulation aligns with endogenous oscillatory peaks to maximize synchronization (Fig. 1D) [11]. Nevertheless, standardized protocols for patients with DOC are unavailable despite the importance of precise parameter selection (location, intensity, frequency, and dose of stimulation). Based on this principle, our study specifically targeted the frontal cortex with 20 Hz beta-tACS for two primary reasons. First, from a localization perspective, the frontal cortex plays a pivotal role in orchestrating consciousness via frontoparietal networks, and prefrontal stimulation has been shown to improve responsiveness [14]. Second, regarding frequency selection, beta-band oscillations (13–35 Hz) underlie higher cognition by coordinating cortico-cortical dynamics [15]. Consequently, applying 20 Hz stimulation to the frontal site may synergistically optimize entrainment by simultaneously targeting a key node of the consciousness network and a frequency band fundamental to cognitive processing [16]. This approach is further supported by evidence of cognitive enhancement in healthy individuals following frontal beta-tACS, which is thought to modulate the theta/beta ratio [17]. Therefore, our stimulation protocol—employing transcranial arrays to administer alternating currents at 1–2 mA—was designed to leverage this previously demonstrated method of targeted, frequency-tuned cortical modulation via phase-locked neuronal entrainment [18, 19]. To evaluate the effects of tACS in DOC, we employed functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) in an offline design to assess sustained post-stimulation cortical network changes through resting-state hemoglobin (HbO) fluctuations mediated by neurovascular coupling [20]. This optical imaging technique quantifies differences in near-infrared light absorption between oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin to infer neural activity [21]. Compared with functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), fNIRS offers superior temporal resolution (~ 0.1 s) for hemodynamic tracking dynamics and better spatial localization than electroencephalography (EEG) [22], while its portability and motion tolerance make it ideal for bedside DOC assessments [23, 24]. The offline approach circumvents artifacts associated with tACS during stimulation, focusing instead on persistent network-level connectivity changes that are critical for therapeutic aftereffect characterization.

Given the role of beta oscillations (20 Hz) in top-down cognitive control and network plasticity associated with tACS, we hypothesized that prolonged beta-tACS (1 mA, 30 min) would selectively reorganize dysfunctional cortical connectivity in DOC, with offline fNIRS detecting sustained aftereffects. This approach aligns with the meta-analytic findings of stronger offline versus online effects, which are attributed to accumulated neuromodulation via spike timing-dependent plasticity and optimized target engagement [13]. Changes in HbO connectivity directly reflect plasticity associated with tACS through neurovascular coupling. Our triple-phase protocol comprised: (1) pre-stimulation resting-state measurement for 5 min (pre-stim), (2) stimulation delivery to frontal networks, and (3) serial post-stimulation fNIRS acquisitions at 5 min intervals, including the first post-stimulation resting-state measurement for 5 min (post-stim1) and the second post-stimulation resting-state measurement for 5 min (post-stim2), to delineate network reorganization dynamics, enabling noninvasive assessment of short-term changes in network organization in patients with DOC.

Materials and methods

Participants

This prospective cohort study was conducted at a neurorehabilitation center (The Second Qilu Hospital of Shandong University) between May 2024 and February 2025. Participants met the following inclusion criteria: 1) Diagnosis of DOC confirmed by the Coma Recovery Scale-Revised (CRS-R); 2) Age between 18 and 80 years; and 3) post-injury interval ranging from 28 days to 12 months. The following exclusion criteria were systematically applied to ensure cohort homogeneity: (1) Non- right-handed dominance; (2) Radiographic evidence of severe cerebral atrophy or obstructive hydrocephalus; (3) Clinical or electrophysiological signs of locked-in syndrome; (4) Documented premorbid neuropsychiatric conditions; (5) Unrepaired cranial defects (> 3 cm) affecting fNIRS signal quality; (6) Active major systemic comorbidities (e.g., refractory status epilepticus); (7) Ferromagnetic intracranial implants (e.g., aneurysm clips); (8) Invasive neuromonitoring devices; (9) Active implantable pulse generators; and (10) Frontal lobe structural anomalies precluding safe tACS electrode placement. The study protocol was conducted in compliance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Second Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (Approval No. KYLL-2024-456). Before participant enrollment, the trial was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration no. ChiCTR2500105892) to ensure the transparency of the protocol and methodological rigor. Given the impaired decision-making capacity of patients with DOC, written informed consent was obtained from legally authorized representatives (family members or appointed guardians) of all participants.

Demographic characteristics (age, sex, and time since injury) and baseline clinical measurements were also systematically recorded. Before the tACS intervention, baseline behavioral assessments were conducted with the CRS-R scale by experienced rehabilitation physicians. To ensure a stable benchmark, the assessment was performed daily over five consecutive days, with the average score being used as the baseline value for subsequent analysis [22].

Experimental protocol

The total experimental duration was 45 min, consisting of a 5 min pre-stim, a 30 min offline tACS, and two consecutive 5 min post-stimulation measurements (post-stim1 and post-stim2) (Fig. 2). Throughout the session, the participants wore a high-density fNIRS montage to monitor their cortical hemodynamic activity continuously. To ensure sustained vigilance, standardized CRS-R arousal protocols (including tactile and auditory stimuli) were administered before stimulation, with the participants maintained at 30° head elevation.Fig. 2Schematic of the fNIRS experimental paradigm and analysis pipeline. Beta-tACS stimulation

Beta-tACS (20 Hz) was delivered through medical-grade EVA foam electrodes (62 mm diameter, 30 cm2 conductive area; saline-soaked to maintain impedance < 5 kΩ) connected to a certified stimulator (N-ET-100, Neuraplus) (Fig. 1B and C). Electrode placement followed the standardized 10–20 EEG system: the anode was positioned at F3 (left frontal lobe) and the cathode at F4 (right frontal lobe). The stimulation protocol employed a 1 mA (peak-to-peak) sinusoidal waveform at the fundamental frequency, incorporating 30-s linear ramp-up and ramp-down phases of currents at the beginning and end of stimulation. Electrode impedance was maintained below 50 kΩ throughout the stimulation. Both the left and right frontal cortices received synchronized 30 min stimulation (1 mA sinusoidal current) through simultaneously activated electrodes. Electrode-tissue impedance was continuously monitored to prevent interruptions. No skin erythema or adverse effects were observed during beta-tACS administration.

fNIRS

Data preprocessing

Cortical hemodynamics were assessed using the NirSmart-6000A system (Jiangsu Danyang Hui Chuang Medical Equipment Co., Ltd.) operating at 730 and 850 nm wavelengths (sampling rate: 11 Hz). Standard cranial landmarks, including the nasion, inion, and bilateral preauricular points, serve as reference markers for the precise placement of optode arrays. The optode montage comprised 24 sources and 16 detectors, yielding 48 measurement channels with a mean source-detector distance of 3.0 cm (range: 2.7–3.3 cm).

Raw fNIRS signals were preprocessed using the MATLAB-based NirSpark toolbox (v1.7.5; HIRF, Danyang, China) to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) through the following validated procedures: Channels were excluded if they met any of the following three criteria: (1) optical intensity (OI) beyond 0.5–1000 a.u., (2) source-detector distance > 45 mm; or (3) mean OI/std OI < 2 [26]. Participants with ≥ 50% rejected channels were excluded [27]. The surviving channel data were converted to optical density via logarithmic transformation for subsequent analysis.

Four critical preprocessing operations were systematically applied to improve HbO signal fidelity: (1) principal component analysis was employed to detect and correct motion artifacts, with subsequent exclusion of data displaying pronounced abrupt motion-related fluctuations; (2) baseline drift and high-amplitude transient artifacts were addressed using time-derivative distribution repair. Surface interference was minimized using common average reference spatial filtering. (3) Physiological noise (cardiac, respiratory, and Mayer waves of ~ 0.1 Hz) was suppressed using a Butterworth bandpass filter (0.01–0.2 Hz) combined with a 0.1 Hz notch filter. (4) Finally, hemoglobin concentrations were derived using the modified Beer-Lambert law (differential path-length factor, DPF = 6), with subsequent hemodynamic modality separation to isolate neural activity components.

Functional connectivity analysis

Each recording channel was defined as an associated network node. For each participant, we computed pairwise Pearson’s correlation coefficients between all nodal time series to construct functional connectivity (FC) matrices, which were ultimately summarized as individual symmetric 12 × 12 correlation matrices. This final matrix was derived through the following procedure: first, a channel-wise functional connectivity matrix (46 × 46), based on the 46 ROI-assigned channels, was computed via Pearson's correlation of preprocessed HbO signals for each subject and condition. This matrix was then summarized to the regional level by calculating the mean correlation between all inter-ROI channel pairs (for inter-regional connectivity) and all intra-ROI channel pairs (for intra-regional connectivity), ultimately yielding the single symmetric 12 × 12 ROI-based connectivity matrix. To account for potential indirect connections, partial cross-correlations were further calculated using an inverse covariance matrix estimation. Subsequently, Fisher’s R-to-Z transformation was applied to enable parametric statistical analysis of the FC strength (Z-scores) between the regions of interest. Channel positions were co-registered to the Brodmann areas and grouped into 12 bilateral ROIs, excluding channels with indeterminate hemispheric localization (Fig. 3). These included the left and right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (LDLPFC/RDLPFC), prefrontal cortex (LPFC/RPFC), inferior frontal gyrus (LIFG/RIFG), motor cortex (LMC/RMC; encompassing primary motor cortex and pre-motor and supplementary motor cortex due to limited channel coverage), primary somatosensory cortex (LPSC/RPSC), and occipital lobe cortex (LOL/ROL). For ROI-level analyses, two midline channels—the central prefrontal channel (CH20) and the central occipital channel (CH48)—were excluded because they could not be reliably assigned to a specific hemisphere, resulting in 46 channels being grouped into ROIs. For channel-level analyses, all 48 channels were retained, and FC was computed directly between all pairs of individual channels. The average correlation coefficient within each ROI was used to represent the regional mean FC. Theoretical analysis of resting-state functional brain networks

Graph theory provides a powerful mathematical framework for characterizing the topological organization of brain networks, which are modeled as graphs comprising nodes (representing brain regions or channels) and edges (representing FCs). In this study, we conducted a comprehensive graph-theoretical analysis of resting-state functional brain networks using NirSparkBatchToolGT software (version 1.8.9) (Fig. 2C). Below, we detail the methodologies and metrics employed to evaluate network properties. Group-level adjacency matrices were derived by thresholding the mean correlation matrix across subjects. To ensure robust network characterization across different connection densities, we employed a multi-threshold strategy by constructing networks across a wide sparsity range from 0.05 to 0.50 (in steps of 0.05). In addition, 100 randomized networks were generated as null models for comparative benchmarking. To address the threshold-dependent nature of graph metrics and provide a comprehensive summary of network properties, we calculated the area under the curve (AUC) for each network metric across the entire sparsity range, ncluding the AUC of Clustering Coefficient (aCp), AUC of Path Length (aLp), AUC of Global Efficiency (aEg), and AUC of Local Efficiency (aEloc). vEEG

Acquisition and analysis

Video-EEG (vEEG) recordings were obtained from patients with DOC during the initial hospitalization, and fNIRS assessments were conducted within a 7-day window to ensure temporal proximity while preventing interference between examinations. Continuous 24-h vEEG monitoring was conducted in a controlled environment: patients with DOC remained undisturbed in a sound-attenuated room with all therapeutic interventions suspended during the recording. Using a NicoletOne™ system (Natus Medical) with 19 scalp electrodes (international 10–20 system), recordings were acquired at a 500 Hz sampling rate with analog bandpass filtering (0.3–70 Hz) and 50 Hz notch filtering. The signal parameters were maintained at a sensitivity of 7 μV/mm and a paper speed of 30 mm/s. Given the established clinical divergence between moderately abnormal (Grade III) and diffuse slowing (Grade IV), where Grade III shows better recovery potential in DOC populations, we specifically analyzed these distinct subgroups [28, 29]. The EEG backgrounds of all enrolled patients with DOC were characterized by either moderately abnormal or diffusely slowing EEG backgrounds. Statistical analyses

All data processing and statistical analyses were performed using the MATLAB-based NIRSPARK toolbox and SPSS (version 27.0; IBM Corp.). Baseline demographic characteristics were analyzed with normality assumptions verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (95% confidence interval), and categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages).

To assess short-term network changes associated with tACS, we performed paired t-tests on HbO concentrations, comparing the pre-stim condition separately with post-stim1 and post-stim2 (post-stim1 vs. pre-stim; post-stim2 vs. pre-stim). For FC analyses, the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure was applied to control for type I error inflation due to multiple comparisons [30, 31] at both the channel level (1,128 channel pairs) and the ROI level (78 total pairs: 66 inter-regional pairs and 12 intra-regional self-connectivity pairs), an established approach for analyzing fNIRS data in patients with DOC [32, 33]. In contrast, FDR correction was not applied to graph-theoretic metrics, in accordance with standard practices in prior DOC neuroimaging studies [7, 34, 35]. Therefore, these graph-theoretical results should be interpreted as exploratory. For significant FC results, Cohen’s d was calculated as a measure of effect size (0.2: small; 0.5: medium; 0.8: large). Furthermore, the temporal relationships between the baseline CRS-R scores and HbO concentrations were examined at each of the three time points (pre-stim, post-stim1, post-stim2) using Pearson's correlation coefficients (r), with strength interpreted as |r|≥ 0.5 (strong), 0.3 ≤|r|< 0.5 (moderate), and |r|< 0.3 (weak).

Results

Demographic characteristics of DOC patients

This study enrolled 28 patients with DOC (19 with VS/UWS and 9 with MCS), predominantly male (75%, 21/28), with a mean age of 58.46 ± 11.15 years. Stroke (79%, 22/28) was the primary etiology, followed by TBI (14%, 4/28) and hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (7%, 2/28). At a mean post-injury interval of 83.57 ± 66.11 days, patients showed severe functional impairment (mean CRS-R total scores: 6.39 ± 2.59). vEEG analysis was completed for 25 of the 28 patients, revealing moderately abnormal (44%, 11/25) and diffuse slowing (56%, 14/25) backgrounds. Three critically ill patients were excluded from vEEG due to clinical instability. Effects of tACS stimulation on topological network properties

Using graph-theoretical analysis, we quantitatively assessed global network topology across three time points (pre-stim, post-stim1, post-stim2) in all DOC cohort and subgroups stratified by EEG abnormalities (moderately abnormal, diffuse slowing) (Fig. 7). Specifically, we evaluated the global small-world properties (aCp, aLp) and global efficiency (aEg, aEloc). To ensure robustness, we calculated the AUC for each metric across multiple network sparsity thresholds, followed by within-group paired comparisons of network alterations following tACS. In exploratory analyses, increases in aCp were observed at post-stim1 compared with pre-stim (all cohort: t = 2.75, p = 0.01, Cohen’s d = 0.52; moderately abnormal: t = 2.45, p = 0.03, Cohen’s d = 0.74; diffuse slowing: t = 2.67, p = 0.02, Cohen’s d = 0.71); however, these increases were not sustained at post-stim2. Conversely, the aLp significantly decreased at post-stim1 compared with pre-stim in DOC patients (all DOC cohort: t = − 2.55, p = 0.02, Cohen’s d = 0.48; moderately abnormal: t = − 2.73, p = 0.02, Cohen’s d = 0.82; diffuse slowing: t = -2.29, p = 0.04, Cohen’s d = 0.61). Only all DOC cohort demonstrated a statistically significant decrease in aLp (indicating increased efficiency) at post-stim2 compared with pre-stim (t = − 2.23, p = 0.03, Cohen’s d = 0.42). The aEg analysis demonstrated significantly increased responses post-stim1 across all groups (all DOC cohort: t = 2.58, p = 0.02, Cohen’s d = 0.49; moderately abnormal: t = 3.91, p < 0.01, Cohen’s d = 0.96; diffuse slowing: t = 2.27, p = 0.04, Cohen’s d = 0.61), with no significant differences persisting at post-stim2. No significant differences were observed at post-stim2 in any of the three subgroups (all p > 0.05). Correlation analysis between baseline CRS-R scores and fNIRS-derived network parameters

To determine whether brain network features reflect the severity of consciousness impairment, we analyzed correlations between the baseline CRS-R scores and the following fNIRS-derived network parameters: average FC of HbO, global small-world properties (aCp/aLp), and global efficiency (aEg/aEloc) at pre-stim, post-stim1, and post-stim2 (Fig. 8). Significant correlations were observed between the CRS-R scores and fNIRS-derived brain network metrics. Specifically, CRS-R scores were positively correlated with average FC of HbO, both in pre-stim (r = 0.47, p = 0.01) and post-stim2 (r = 0.41, p = 0.03). Graph analysis revealed significant correlations between CRS-R scores and network metrics: a positive correlation with local efficiency in pre-stim (aEg: r = 0.44, p = 0.02) and a negative correlation with path length (aLp: r = − 0.41, p = 0.03). This pattern of findings may reflect that heightened conscious function is supported by more efficient global network integration, manifested as a decrease in characteristic path length. Discussion

Building on the emerging potential of neuromodulation for DOC, our study yields three preliminary observations regarding beta-tACS (20 Hz, 1 mA, 30 min) applied to frontal nodes. First, tACS was associated with a significant, acute potentiation of HbO-based FC, characterized by enhanced intra- and inter-hemispheric communication and nodal integration, with a notable focus on visual-motor circuits in DOC patients with moderately abnormal background. Second, we observed transient global network reorganization post-stim1, evidenced by concurrent increases in Cp, Eg, and Eloc, alongside reduced Lp; these effects returned to pre-stim by post-stim2. Furthermore, higher CRS-R scores were associated with this enhanced network integration, evidenced by stronger average FC of HbO, greater aEg and aEloc, and shorter aLp. Collectively, our findings suggest that frontal tACS was associated with acute network changes in DOC and that fNIRS-derived network metrics may be feasible measures for tracking these changes in future clinical investigations.

Multiple human studies have demonstrated that targeted tACS can effectively modulate cortical oscillations and the associated cognitive and behavioral functions [13, 36–38]. Theta-tACS (5 Hz) enhances working memory and neuroplasticity, elevating frontal-parietal HbO concentrations and delta-theta EEG synchrony [39], while prefrontal alpha-tACS (10 Hz) induces robust hemodynamic shifts in healthy subjects [40]. Emerging EEG evidence shows that gamma-tACS (40 Hz) over right DLPFC selectively modulates large-scale connectivity and cortical excitability, with robust effects in MCS and meaningful responses in some VS/UWS patients [41]. Critically, the entire group of patients with DOC in our study, we observed that beta-tACS (20 Hz) was associated with significant network-level effects in post-stim1: left intrahemispheric integration (including LDLPFC-LPFC/LOL connections and broader fronto-occipital networks), increased LPFC intra-nodal connectivity, and strengthened interhemispheric coupling between the prefrontal, motor, and occipital regions. These findings align mechanistically with established the tACS connectivity effects observed in fMRI studies, which modulate interhemispheric [42], intrahemispheric [43], and global functional connectivity [44]. The observed outcomes reflect the core actions of tACS: neural entrainment (phase-locked spiking), synaptic plasticity (NMDA-dependent), and centrally, frequency-specific network reconfiguration via connectivity modulation [12]. The efficacy of our 20 Hz protocol in DOC likely leverages intrinsic beta-band properties. Analogous to 20 Hz repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, which enhances working memory in LDLPFC-targeted protocols [45], and outperforms lower frequencies (e.g., 10 Hz) in Alzheimer's disease cognition [46], tACS (20 Hz) augments neural efficiency and hemodynamic responses, evidenced by HbO surges in the frontoparietal networks. Collectively, 20 Hz tACS may acutely modulate brain network activity, potentially mediated by its driving of beta-band oscillatory synchrony, which in turn enhances large-scale integration within frontoparietal and motor-occipital pathways and optimizes global network efficiency.

We observed changes in functional network organization following tACS in patients with DOC, characterized by a selective enhancement of left-hemispheric connectivity—a pattern previously reported in association with higher levels of consciousness or recovery-related states in previous studies [47, 48]. This pattern is consistent with fMRI evidence identifying the left DLPFC [47] and the left inferior parietal lobule [48] as core network hubs for conscious awareness. We found that tACS was associated with connectivity changes in multiple frontoparietal and visuomotor networks in DOC patients, characterized by three primary features: (1) increased left intrahemispheric integration across prefrontal-executive (LDLPFC-PFC), sensory-cognitive (LDLPFC-LOL), and motor-occipital pathways; (2) reinforcing left prefrontal nodal connectivity of multimodal cortical/subcortical inputs; and (3) broader changes of global connectivity through interhemispheric synchronization (RDLPFC-LPFC) and cross-modal integration (RM-LOL). These observations may be compatible with transient reconfiguration of large-scale network organization after stimulation. However, given the single-arm design and lack of a sham condition, mechanistic interpretations regarding thalamocortical recruitment or restoration of conscious processing remain speculative. The observed patterns may be discussed in relation to theoretical models such as the mesocircuit framework[49], but the current data do not permit direct mechanistic inference.

EEG stratification revealed distinct tACS response patterns: patients with moderately abnormal exhibited preserved thalamocortical oscillations, enabling transient visuomotor integration enhancement (ROL-LM/RPSC-LOL) via beta-tACS facilitation of residual cross-modal coupling. The lack of responsiveness in DOC patients with diffuse slowing may be attributed to a frequency-specific mismatch between beta-tACS and pathologically dominant δ/θ oscillations, which could prevent effective entrainment owing to insufficient overlap within the Arnold tongue regime [11]. Alternatively, if cross-frequency entrainment is theoretically possible, it might require significantly higher stimulation intensities might be required to overcome the frequency disparity, although this remains speculative in the absence of direct electrophysiological evidence. The absence of aftereffects in this subgroup underscores severe connectivity disruption. Consistent with Naro et al [41]., tACS was associated with frequency-dependent aftereffects: healthy controls showed sustained (60 min) frontotemporal theta/gamma-power increases (~ 140%) with delta suppression (~ 50%); MCS patients had weaker (15–20%) and shorter (≤ 30 min) gamma↑/theta↓ but unchanged delta; VS/UWS patients exhibited minimal (~ 10–15%) and brief (≤ 10 min). Collectively, our findings suggest that the tACS modulation of thalamocortical oscillations likely requires intact circuit integrity. Diffuse slowing activity impairs entrainment efficacy, highlighting how baseline EEG patterns and network integrity determine tACS stimulation efficacy, supporting personalized neuromodulation strategies in DOC.

Converging evidence from multimodal neuroimaging studies has validated fNIRS-based resting-state FC and graph-theoretical metrics as reliable proxies for fMRI in assessing whole-brain integration dynamics [50]. Critically, our findings revealed that beta-tACS was associated with biologically plausible changes in global network topology: increased Cp and Eloc indicate strengthened local information processing through enhanced short-range connectivity [34, 51, 52], while increased Eg and decreased Lp suggest improved long-range integration via accelerated parallel information transfer [34, 51, 52]. This dual modulation—heightening segregation-integration balance—may be related to enhanced neural synchronization and a more compacted network architecture post-stimulation. Notably, the observed Lp reduction and Eg enhancement recapitulate network reorganization patterns seen in patients with DOC following clinically effective neuromodulation interventions (e.g., acupuncture [32] or deep brain stimulation [7]), both of which are known to restore disrupted functional connectivity. Collectively, these results suggest that fNIRS-based graph metrics can quantify acute, tACS-associated reconfigurations of brain network dynamics toward states of heightened integration, presenting a foundational step for future research into therapeutic applications.

DOC manifests as characteristically disrupted directed connectivity across whole-brain topological scales, which serves as well-established biomarkers for functional impairment [7, 35, 53]. Previous studies have established that several neural metrics—including task-evoked hemodynamic responses during motor imagery [33], global communication efficiency [7], nodal local efficiency [14], and nodal clustering coefficient [14]—correlate significantly with CRS-R scores. Our findings additionally identified four potential markers of interest: (i) FC at pre-stim, (ii) FC at post-stim2, (iii) global efficiency at pre-stim (aEg), and (iv) characteristic path length at pre-stim (aLp). These baseline and post-stimulation network properties may be associated with inter-individual differences in residual brain network function. Post-tACS normalization patterns in patients with DOC may indicate a pathology-dependent modulation spectrum, with structural damage (axonal/synaptic loss) [54] potentially constraining neuromodulatory effects to acute temporal windows (< 10 min) and rendering FC recovery baseline-dependent. Consistent with these findings, tACS-associated modulation of V1-MT cross-regional coupling was markedly attenuated and transient in stroke patients compared to healthy controls [55]. This differential response pattern illustrates how structural damage can limit tACS-mediated plasticity in pathological networks. Specifically, this finding suggests that the injured brain initially mounts a stereotyped, all-or-nothing response to tACS, which may saturate its limited residual capacity and thereby temporarily obscure consciousness-level differences. Consequently, we hypothesize that the correlation with CRS-R re-emerges at post-stim2 only after this acute, non-specific excitation subsides, thereby revealing the underlying functional architecture tied to behavioral function. Methodologically, the non-specific hemodynamic plateau at post-stim1 precludes consciousness-state discrimination, while effect decay by post-stim2 underscores the need for repetitive stimulation protocols [39]. Critically, fNIRS-based dynamic connectivity markers (FC, aEg, and aLp) circumvent these constraints and can serve as complementary objective markers when CRS-R is compromised by motor/verbal deficits.

This study has several limitations that require acknowledgment. First, the modest single-center cohort size (n = 28) limits statistical power and generalizability, warranting validation in larger multicenter populations. Additionally, the small subgroup sizes (n = 11 and n = 14) precluded formal interaction testing; subgroup findings should therefore be viewed as hypothesis-generating. Second, the offline fNIRS design and lack of sham control preclude causal attribution, and findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence from a pilot study, laying the groundwork for future randomized controlled trials. Third, offline measurements constrain real-time whole-brain monitoring during stimulation, necessitating online methodologies to capture dynamic tACS-brain interactions. Fourth, restricting post-stimulation observation to 10 min may overlook delayed neuroplastic effects; extended monitoring (> 30 min) is essential to dissociate transient neuromodulation from sustained adaptations. Fifth, unimodal fNIRS assessment limits neurophysiological interpretation; concurrent EEG-fNIRS can resolve critical neurovascular coupling mechanisms by correlating gamma/beta -oscillations with hemodynamics. In addition, graph-theoretic metrics and correlation analyses were reported without correction for multiple comparisons; therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory and interpreted cautiously. Finally, this study focuses on a single stimulation frequency and target (20 Hz, frontal cortex), which hinders a deeper mechanistic understanding of tACS effects in terms of frequency and regional specificity. Future studies should thus systematically screen parameters, testing diverse frequencies (e.g., 10 Hz alpha, 40 Hz gamma) across key network nodes (e.g., posterior cingulate cortex), to develop optimal, patient-tailored neuromodulation strategies for DOC.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that beta-tACS is associated with transient alterations in pathological beta-band desynchronization in DOC patients by entraining prefrontal-thalamocortical circuits, thereby enhancing functional integration between the executive control hubs (LDLPFC) and sensory-motor regions. Crucially, treatment efficacy is contingent on thalamic structural integrity: Patients with moderately abnormal exhibited selective potentiation of visual-motor pathways, whereas those with diffuse slowing subgroup showed no significant response, possibly reflecting more severe underlying network impairment. The transient nature of these neuromodulatory effects (< 10 min decay) highlights a key consideration for the design of future sustained intervention protocols. The observed correlations between fNIRS network metrics and CRS-R scores provide initial support for the potential clinical relevance of fNIRS-derived measures in DOC. Future trials should evaluate personalized multi-session tACS protocols integrated with real-time fNIRS/EEG monitoring to explore their potential for enhancing neuroplasticity and behavioral outcomes.
