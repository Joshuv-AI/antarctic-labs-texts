Classification and structure of generalized Legendrian racks
Lực Ta

Abstract
. This paper develops the structure theory of generalized Legendrian racks, which are algebraic structures used to study Legendrian knots. First, we give a group-theoretic characterization of GL-racks. As applications, we classify GL-racks up to order 8 up to isomorphism via an exhaustive search algorithm, and we classify certain infinite families of GL-racks. Then we compute the center of the category of GL-racks and construct an equivalence of categories between racks and GL-quandles. Finally, we study tensor products of racks and GL-racks coming from universal algebra.

1.
Introduction

Generalized Legendrian racks
, also called
GL-racks
or
bi-Legendrian racks
, are algebraic structures used to distinguish Legendrian links in contact three-space. GL-racks can be traced back to algebraic structures called
kei
, which Takasaki
[
41
]
introduced in 1942 to study symmetric spaces;
quandles
, which Joyce
[
24
]
and Matveev
[
32
]
independently introduced in 1982 to study links in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
S^{3}
; and
racks
, which Fenn and Rourke
[
21
]
introduced in 1992 to study framed links in
3
-manifolds.
Kei, quandles, and racks have enjoyed significant study as link invariants in geometric topology and in their own rights in quantum algebra and group theory.

More recently, various authors have introduced variants of racks suitable for studying Legendrian links. In 2017, Kulkarni and Prathamesh
[
31
]
introduced rack invariants of Legendrian knots. In 2021, Ceniceros, Elhamdadi, and Nelson
[
8
]
refined these invariants by introducing
Legendrian racks
.
In 2023, Karmakar, Saraf, and Singh
[
27
]
and Kimura
[
28
]
independently strengthened these constructions by introducing GL-racks, which are racks equipped with additional structure. In 2026, Cheng and He
[
9
]
[
10
]
studied the efficacy of GL-racks as Legendrian knot invariants, and Karmakar, Saraf, and Singh
[
26
]
studied Beck modules over GL-racks. The author and Wood
[
38
]
studied
4-Legendrian racks
, of which GL-racks form a special class. The author
[
40
]
also found relationships between involutory GL-racks and similar algebraic structures used to study surface-knots.

The purpose of this paper is to develop the structure theory of GL-racks in hopes of strengthening their applications in Legendrian knot theory. In particular, we study group-theoretic, categorical, and universal-algebraic aspects of GL-racks, and we classify GL-racks up to order
8
.

1.1.
Organization of the paper

In Section
2
, we define racks and quandles, consider several examples, and discuss a canonical rack automorphism
\pi
that plays a fundamental role in the theory.

In Section
3
, we give a simplified definition of GL-racks, show its equivalence to the definition in the literature, and discuss examples and universal-algebraic aspects of GL-racks. In particular, we show how to construct free GL-racks on arbitrary generating sets.

In Section
4
, we study group-theoretic aspects of GL-racks. We determine all GL-structures on a given rack, answering a question from a previous version of
[
27
]
. As an application, we classify GL-racks up to order
8
up to isomorphism via a computer algorithm; we provide the program and data in a GitHub repository
[
39
]
. Then we classify all GL-structures on permutation racks, certain conjugation quandles, and certain Takasaki kei, including all dihedral quandles. We also compute automorphism groups of dihedral GL-quandles.

In Section
5
, we study categorical aspects of GL-racks. First, we compute the centers of the categories of GL-racks, GL-quandles, Legendrian racks, and Legendrian quandles. Then we construct an equivalence of categories between the categories of racks and GL-quandles. This equivalence restricts to an equivalence between the full subcategories of medial racks and medial GL-quandles. Our construction corresponds to an isomorphism of algebraic theories.

In Section
6
, we study tensor products of racks and GL-racks coming from universal algebra. We show that the categories of racks and GL-racks have tensor units. This is unusual for noncommutative algebraic theories; for example, the tensor product in the category of groups does not have a tensor unit. The induced symmetric monoidal structure on medial racks is closed, and similarly for medial GL-racks.

In Section
7
, we propose questions for further research.

2.
Racks

2.1.
Racks

In this subsection, we define racks and discuss several major examples.

2.1.1.
Notation

Given a set
X
, we denote the permutation group of
X
by
S_{X}
.
In the case that
X=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, we denote the symmetric group on
n
letters by
S_{n}
.
We also denote the composition of functions
\varphi:X\to Y
and
\psi:Y\to Z
by
\psi\varphi
.

While racks and quandles are often defined as sets
X
with a right-distributive nonassociative binary operation
\triangleright:X\times X\to X
satisfying certain axioms, they may also be characterized in terms of permutations
s_{x}\in S_{X}
assigned to each element
x\in X
; cf.
[
18
]
*Def. 2.1.
One may translate between the two conventions via the formula

x\triangleright y=s_{y}(x).

In this paper, we adopt the convention using permutations due to its convenience for abstract proofs and exhaustive search algorithms.

2.1.2.
Racks and quandles

Having established our notation, we define racks and quandles.
Although we provide all relevant definitions and preliminaries, we also refer the reader to
[
33
]
[
16
]
for accessible introductions to quandles,
[
16
]
[
34
]
for references on racks as they concern low-dimensional topology, and
[
18
]
for a survey of modern algebraic literature on racks.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a set, let
s:X\to S_{X}
be a map, and write
s_{x}:=s(x)
for all elements
x\in X
. We call the pair
(X,s)
a
rack
if

s_{x}s_{y}=s_{s_{x}(y)}s_{x}

for all
x,y\in X
, in which case we call
s
a
rack structure
on
X
.
If in addition
s_{x}(x)=x
for all
x\in X
, then we say that
(X,s)
is a
quandle
.
We also say that
|X|
is the
order
of
(X,s)
.
Finally, if
Y\subseteq X
and
s_{y}^{\pm 1}(z)\in Y
for all
y,z\in Y
, then we say that
(Y,s|_{Y})
is a
subrack
of
(X,s)
.

Definition 2.2
.

-

•

Given two racks
R:=(X,s)
and
(Y,t)
, we say that a map
\varphi:X\to Y
is a
rack homomorphism
if

\varphi s_{x}=t_{\varphi(x)}\varphi

for all
x\in X
. Let
\mathsf{Rack}
denote the category of racks and rack homomorphisms, and let
\mathsf{Qnd}
be the full subcategory of
\mathsf{Rack}
whose objects are quandles.

•

A
rack isomorphism
is a bijective rack homomorphism. The
automorphism group
of
R
is denoted by
\Aut R
.

•

The
inner automorphism group
of
R
is the subgroup
\operatorname{Inn}R:=\langle s_{x}\mid x\in X\rangle
of
\Aut R
.

•

Equip
X\times X
with the product rack structure. If the natural map
X\to X\times X
is a rack homomorphism, then we say that
R
is
medial
. Let
\mathsf{Rack}_{\mathrm{med}}
be the full subcategory of
\mathsf{Rack}
whose objects are medial.

Example 2.3

(
[
16
]
*Ex. 99)
.

Let
X
be a set, and fix
\sigma\in S_{X}
. Define
s:X\rightarrow S_{X}
by
x\mapsto\sigma
, so that
s_{x}(y)=\sigma(y)
for all
x,y\in X
. Then
(X,\sigma)_{\mathrm{perm}}:=(X,s)
is a medial rack called a
permutation rack
or
constant action rack
. (Our notation
(X,\sigma)_{\mathrm{perm}}
is nonstandard, unlike the notation in the next three examples.) Note that
(X,\sigma)_{\mathrm{perm}}
is a quandle if and only if
\sigma=\id_{X}
, in which case we call
(X,\id_{X})_{\mathrm{perm}}
a
trivial quandle
.

Example 2.4

(
[
16
]
*Ex. 54)
.

Let
A
be an abelian additive group. Define
s:A\to S_{A}
by
b\mapsto s_{b}
with
s_{b}(a):=2b-a
for all elements
a,b\in A
. Then
T(A):=(A,s)
is a medial quandle called a
Takasaki kei
. Takasaki kei are the earliest examples of racks in the literature; Takasaki
[
41
]
introduced them in 1943 to study symmetric spaces.

In particular, if
A=\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, then we call
R_{n}:=T(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
the
dihedral quandle
of order
n
.

Example 2.5

(
[
34
]
*Ex. 2.13)
.

Let
X
be a union of conjugacy classes in a group
G
, and
define
c^{G}:X\rightarrow S_{X}
by sending
x
to the conjugation map
c^{G}_{x}:=[y\mapsto xyx^{-1}]
.
Then
\operatorname{Conj}X:=(X,c^{G})
is a quandle called a
conjugation quandle
or
conjugacy quandle
.

Definition 2.6

(
[
5
]
)
.

Given a rack
R=(X,s)
, define
s^{\prime}:X\to S_{X}
by
x\mapsto s_{x}^{-1}
. Then
R^{\operatorname{op}}:=(X,s^{\prime})
is a rack called the
dual rack
of
R
.

The following is straightforward.

Proposition 2.7
.

For all racks
R=(X,s)
, we have
\Aut R=\Aut R^{\operatorname{op}}
and
\operatorname{Inn}R=\operatorname{Inn}R^{\operatorname{op}}
. Moreover,
R
is medial if and only if
R^{\operatorname{op}}
is medial.

2.2.
The canonical automorphism of a rack

In this subsection, we discuss a collection
\Pi
of canonical rack automorphisms
\pi_{R}\in\Aut R
that generates the center of the category
\mathsf{Rack}
.

2.2.1.
Definition of
\pi_{R}

Given a rack
R=(X,s)
, define
\pi_{R}:X\to X
by
{x\mapsto s_{x}(x)}
; see
[
37
]
*Prop. 2.5. This function is sometimes called the
kink map
because of its relationship to kinks in framed knot diagrams; see
[
16
]
*p. 149.

Evidently,
R
is a quandle if and only if
\pi_{R}=\id_{X}
. Thus, we can loosely think of
\pi_{R}
as measuring the failure of
R
to be a quandle. Whenever there is no ambiguity, we will suppress the subscript and only write
\pi:=\pi_{R}
.

2.2.2.
Categorical centers

Recall that the
center
of a category
\mathcal{C}
is the commutative monoid
Z(\mathcal{C})
of natural endomorphisms of the identity functor
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}}
.
Concretely,
\eta\in Z(\mathcal{C})
if and only if, for all objects
R,S
and morphisms
\varphi:R\to S
in
\mathcal{C}
, the component
\eta_{R}
is an endomorphism of
R
, and
\eta_{S}\varphi=\varphi\eta_{R}
.

For example, if
A\text{-}\mathsf{mod}
denotes the category of modules over a ring
A
, then the categorical center
Z(A\text{-}\mathsf{mod})
is isomorphic to the ring-theoretic center
Z(A)
of
A
.

2.2.3.
Properties of
\pi

Let
\Pi
denote the collection of canonical automorphisms
\pi_{R}
for all racks
R
.
Szymik
[
37
]
proved the claims in the following proposition using the binary operation
\triangleright
. We offer a new proof of claim
3
and rewrite Szymik’s proofs of the other claims in terms of permutations.

Proposition 2.8
.

For all racks
R=(X,s)
and all integers
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have the following:

(1)

\pi:X\to X
is a bijection with inverse
\pi^{-1}
defined by
x\mapsto s_{x}^{-1}(x)
.

(2)

\pi^{\pm 1}_{S}\varphi=\varphi\pi^{\pm 1}_{R}
for all racks
S=(Y,t)
and rack homomorphisms
\varphi\in\Hom_{\mathsf{Rack}}(R,S)
.

(3)

s_{\pi^{k}(x)}=s_{x}
for all
x\in X
.

(4)

\Pi^{k}\in Z(\mathsf{Rack})
.

Proof.

Define
\pi^{-1}
as in claim
1
, and fix
x\in X
. We deduce from Proposition
2.7
that

\pi^{-1}\pi(x)=\pi^{-1}s_{x}(x)=s^{-1}_{s_{x}(x)}s_{x}(x)=s_{x}s^{-1}_{x}(x)=x,

as desired. Dually,
\pi\pi^{-1}(x)=x
, which proves claim
1
.
To prove claim
2
, observe that

\pi_{S}\varphi(x)=t_{\varphi(x)}\varphi(x)=\varphi s_{x}(x)=\varphi\pi_{R}(x).

Since
\pi_{S}
and
\pi_{R}
are bijections, we obtain claim
2
.

To prove claim
3
, note that the rack structure
s:X\to S_{X}
is a rack homomorphism from
R
to
\operatorname{Conj}S_{X}
. By claim
2
and the fact that
\operatorname{Conj}S_{X}
is a quandle,

s_{\pi^{k}_{R}(x)}=s\pi^{k}_{R}(x)=\pi^{k}_{\operatorname{Conj}S_{X}}s(x)=s(x)=s_{x}

for all integers
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, which proves claim
3
.
Now, claims
2
and
3
yield

s_{\pi(x)}\pi=\pi s_{\pi(x)}=\pi s_{x},

so
\pi
is a rack endomorphism. Combined with claims
1
and
2
, this proves claim
4
.
∎

Hence,
\Pi
generates a cyclic subgroup of
Z(\mathsf{Rack})
. In fact, this subgroup
is

Z(\mathsf{Rack})
; see
[
37
]
*Thm. 5.4.

The following identities will be useful later on.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
R=(X,s)
be a rack. Then the following hold for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x\in X
:

(1)

\pi^{k}s_{x}=s_{x}\pi^{k}
.

(2)

\pi^{k}(x)=s^{k}_{x}(x)
.

Proof.

Claim
1
is immediate from the inclusions
s_{x}\in\Aut R
and
\Pi^{\pm 1}\in Z(\mathsf{Rack})
.

To prove claim
2
, we induct on
k
. The base case
k=0
is trivial. For
k>0
, we have

\pi^{k}(x)=\pi^{k-1}\pi(x)=\pi^{k-1}s_{x}(x)=s_{x}\pi^{k-1}(x)=s_{x}s^{k-1}_{x}(x)=s^{k}_{x}(x)

as desired; the second equality uses the definition of
\pi
, while the third equality follows from claim
1
. Thanks to part
1
of Proposition
2.8
, a similar argument proves claim
2
for
k<0
.
∎

2.3.
Racks as an algebraic theory

Recall that racks form an algebraic theory with two binary operations
s_{-}(-)
and
s^{-1}_{-}(-)
. In this subsection, we discuss universal-algebraic free objects in
\mathsf{Rack}
and rack structures on certain hom-sets of racks. While we provide the relevant results here, we also direct the reader to
[
4
]
for a reference on the underlying universal algebra.

2.3.1.
Examples of quotient racks

Since
\mathsf{Rack}
is the category of models of the algebraic theory of racks in

,
[

]
*Thm. 3.4.5 states that

is complete and cocomplete. Thus, we can consider quotients of racks by congruences; see
[

*Lem. 3.5.1 and cf.
[

]
. This allows us to state the following definitions, which are actually left adjoints of the inclusion functors from
\mathsf{Qnd}
and
\mathsf{Rack}_{\mathrm{med}}
into
\mathsf{Rack}
.

Definition 2.10

(
[
21
]
*Sec. 2,
[

]
*Sec. 10)
.

Given a rack
R=(X,s)
, the
associated quandle
of

is the quandle
R_{\mathrm{qnd}}:=R/{\sim}
, where
\sim
is the smallest congruence on

s_{x}(x)=x
for all

. Similarly, the
medialization
or

of

is the medial rack

, where

is the smallest congruence on

(1)

{s_{s_{x}(z)}s_{y}(a)=s_{s_{x}(y)}s_{z}(a)}

2.3.2.
Free racks

In analogy to free groups or free modules, the
free rack

\Free_{\mathsf{Rack}}(X)
on a set
X
exists and is uniquely (up to isomorphism) characterized by a universal property; see, for example,
[

*Cor. 3.7.8. Namely, there exists a set map

such that for all racks
R=(Y,t)
and all set maps
\varphi_{0}:X\to Y
, there exists a unique rack homomorphism
{\varphi\in\Hom_{\mathsf{Rack}}(\Free_{\mathsf{Rack}}(X),R)}
such that
\varphi i=\varphi_{0}
. That is, the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\operatorname{Free}_{\mathsf{Rack}}(X)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}

For an explicit set-theoretic construction of free racks, see
[

]
*Prop. 1.3. We can also define the

on

to be

[

Example 2.11

(
[
20
]
*Ex. 1.6)
.

The free rack
F
on one generator is canonically isomorphic to the permutation rack

, where
\sigma(k)=k+1

2.3.3.
Commutative algebraic theories

Medial racks are the largest commutative subtheory of racks. Recall that an algebraic theory
\mathcal{T}
is called

if, for all
\mathcal{T}
-models

, every
n
-ary operation
\alpha:\mathcal{T}^{n}\to\mathcal{T}
defines a homomorphism
A^{n}\to A
; see
[
4
]
*Thm. 3.10.3.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\operatorname{ab}}
be the largest commutative subtheory of an algebraic theory
\mathcal{T}
. For example, if
\mathcal{T}
is the algebraic theory of groups, then
\mathcal{T}_{\operatorname{ab}}
is the algebraic theory of abelian groups.
If
\mathcal{C}
is the category of
\mathcal{T}
-models in
\mathsf{Set}
, let
\mathcal{C}_{\operatorname{ab}}
be the full subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
whose objects are
\mathcal{T}_{\operatorname{ab}}
-models. The following strengthens part of
[
4
]
*Thm. 3.10.3; we could not find a reference for this precise statement.

Proposition 2.12
.

For all
\mathcal{T}
-models
X
in
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\operatorname{ab}}
-models
Y
in
\mathcal{C}_{\operatorname{ab}}
, the set
H:=\Hom_{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)
has a canonical
\mathcal{T}_{\operatorname{ab}}
-model structure defined by

\alpha(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})(x):=\alpha(f_{1}(x),\dots,f_{n}(x))

for all
n
-ary operations
\alpha:\mathcal{T}^{n}\to\mathcal{T}
,
\mathcal{T}
-model homomorphisms

, and elements

.

Proof.

We refer the reader to the proof of the statement in

]
*Thm. 3.10.3 that (1) implies (3). This proof uses the commutativity of

2.3.4.
Mediality and Hom racks

If we take
\mathcal{T}
to be the algebraic theory of racks (resp. quandles), then
\mathcal{T}_{\operatorname{ab}}
is the algebraic theory of medial racks (resp. medial quandles). That is, if
\mathcal{C}=\mathsf{Rack}
, then
\mathcal{C}_{\operatorname{ab}}=\mathsf{Rack}_{\mathrm{med}}
; this follows directly from Proposition
2.7
.

Hence, Proposition
2.12
recovers the following result of Grøsfjeld
[
23
]
*Prop. 3.3 in 2021, which in turn generalized a result of Crans and Nelson
[
12
]
*Thm. 3 in 2014.

Corollary 2.13
.

Let
R=(X,s)
be a rack, and let
M=(Y,t)
be a medial rack. Then the hom-set
H:=\Hom_{\mathsf{Rack}}(R,M)
has a canonical medial rack structure
\tilde{t}:H\to S_{H}
defined by

\tilde{t}_{g}(f)(x):=t_{g(x)}f(x)

for all

and
x\in X
. Moreover, if
R
is a quandle or
M
is a quandle, then

is also a quandle.

Proof.

The only statement that does not directly follow from Proposition

is the final sentence, which is straightforwardly verified.
∎

3.
Generalized Legendrian racks

3.1.
GL-racks

In this subsection, we define
generalized Legendrian racks
(also called
GL-racks
), which Karmakar, Saraf, and Singh
[
27
]
and Kimura
[
28
]
introduced independently in 2023.
Once again, we express our definition in terms of permutations.

Definition 3.1
.

Given a rack
R=(X,s)
, a
generalized Legendrian structure
or
GL-structure
on
R
is a rack automorphism
\mathtt{u}\in\Aut R
such that
\mathtt{u}s_{x}=s_{x}\mathtt{u}
for all
x\in X
.
We call the pair
(R,\mathtt{u})
a
generalized Legendrian rack
or
GL-rack
. If in addition
R
is a quandle or a medial rack, then we also call
(R,\mathtt{u})
a
GL-quandle
or a
medial GL-rack
, respectively.

Definition 3.2
.

A
GL-rack homomorphism
between two GL-racks
(R_{1},\mathtt{u}_{1})
and
(R_{2},\mathtt{u}_{2})
is a rack homomorphism
\varphi\in\Hom_{\mathsf{Rack}}(R_{1},R_{2})
satisfying
\varphi\mathtt{u}_{1}=\mathtt{u}_{2}\varphi
. We denote the category of GL-racks and their homomorphisms by
\mathsf{GLR}
. Finally, let
\mathsf{GLQ}
,
\mathsf{GLR}_{\mathrm{med}}
, and
\mathsf{GLQ}_{\mathrm{med}}
be the full subcategories of
\mathsf{GLR}
whose objects are GL-quandles, medial GL-racks, and medial GL-quandles, respectively.

Remark 3.3
.

Virtual racks
are algebraic structures that can distinguish framed links in certain lens spaces and framed
virtual links
in thickened surfaces; see, for example,
[
7
]
*Sec. 3.2.

By Definition
3.1
, GL-racks are precisely virtual racks in which all inner automorphisms
s_{x}
are endomorphisms of virtual racks. Equivalently, a GL-rack
(X,s,\mathtt{u})
is a virtual rack in which the
operator group
of
(X,s)
identifies
x
with
\mathtt{u}(x)
for all
x\in X
; see
[
21
]
*Sec. 1.1.

Example 3.7

Let

Lemma 3.8
.

If
\varphi
is a GL-rack homomorphism from

to

By hypothesis,

3.1.1.
Bi-Legendrian racks

Next, we reproduce the definition of GL-racks given in
[
27
]
[
28
]
. Following Kimura
[
28
]
, we temporarily use the term
bi-Legendrian racks
to distinguish them from GL-racks in the sense of Definition
3.1
. We also define
Legendrian racks
, which Ceniceros, Elhamdadi, and Nelson
[
8
]
introduced in 2021.

Definition 3.9
.

Given a rack
R=(X,s)
, a
bi-Legendrian structure
on
R
is a pair
(\mathtt{u},\mathtt{d})
of maps
\mathtt{u},\mathtt{d}:X\to X
satisfying the following axioms for all elements
x\in X
:

(1)

\mathtt{u}\mathtt{d}s_{x}(x)=x=\mathtt{d}\mathtt{u}s_{x}(x)
.

(2)

\mathtt{u}s_{x}=s_{x}\mathtt{u}
, and
\mathtt{d}s_{x}=s_{x}\mathtt{d}
.

(3)

s_{\mathtt{u}(x)}=s_{x}=s_{\mathtt{d}(x)}
.

We call the triple
(R,\mathtt{u},\mathtt{d})
a
bi-Legendrian rack
; if
\mathtt{u}=\mathtt{d}
, then we also say that
(R,\mathtt{u},\mathtt{d})
is a
Legendrian rack
. A
bi-Legendrian rack homomorphism
from
(R_{1},\mathtt{u}_{1},\mathtt{d}_{1})
to
(R_{2},\mathtt{u}_{2},\mathtt{d}_{2})
is a rack homomorphism
\varphi\in\Hom_{\mathsf{Rack}}(R_{1},R_{2})
such that
\varphi\mathtt{u}_{1}=\mathtt{u}_{2}\varphi
and
\varphi\mathtt{d}_{1}=\mathtt{d}_{2}\varphi
.

Remark 3.10
.

Axiom
1
shows that, for all bi-Legendrian racks
(R,\mathtt{u},\mathtt{d})
, the underlying rack
R
is a quandle if and only if
\mathtt{d}=\mathtt{u}^{-1}
.

3.2.
Equivalence of definitions

In this subsection, we show the equivalence of Definitions
3.1
and
3.9
. Specifically, we show that the category
\mathsf{BLR}
of bi-Legendrian racks is isomorphic to
\mathsf{GLR}
.

Proposition 3.11
.

If
(\mathtt{u},\mathtt{d})
is a bi-Legendrian structure on a rack
R=(X,s)
, then
\mathtt{d}=\pi^{-1}\mathtt{u}^{-1}
, and
(R,\mathtt{u})
is a GL-rack. In particular, there is a forgetful functor
\Forg:\mathsf{BLR}\to\mathsf{GLR}
sending
(R,\mathtt{u},\mathtt{d})
to
(R,\mathtt{u})
. Moreover,
\Forg
is fully faithful.

Proof.

Axiom
1
states that

\mathtt{u}\mathtt{d}=\mathtt{d}\mathtt{u}=\pi^{-1},

which is bijective, so
\mathtt{u}
is bijective. It follows that
\mathtt{d}=\pi^{-1}\mathtt{u}^{-1}
, as desired. Moreover, axioms
2
and
3
imply that
\mathtt{u}
is a rack endomorphism that commutes with
s_{x}
for all
x\in X
, so
(R,\mathtt{u})
is a GL-rack. Thus, we have a forgetful functor
\Forg:\mathsf{BLR}\to\mathsf{GLR}
, which is clearly faithful.

Now, let
(R_{1},\mathtt{u}_{1},\mathtt{d}_{1})
and
(R_{2},\mathtt{u}_{2},\mathtt{d}_{2})
be bi-Legendrian racks. To show that
\Forg
is full, we need to show that all GL-rack homomorphisms from
(R_{1},\mathtt{u}_{1})
to
(R_{2},\mathtt{u}_{2})
commute with the
\mathtt{d}_{i}
’s. But this follows from the formula
\mathtt{d}_{i}=\pi_{R_{i}}^{-1}\mathtt{u}_{i}^{-1}
, the inclusion
\Pi^{-1}\in Z(\mathsf{Rack})
, and Lemma
3.8
.
∎

Proposition 3.12
.

The forgetful functor
\Forg:\mathsf{BLR}\to\mathsf{GLR}
is an isomorphism of categories.
Hence, the data and axioms of GL-racks are equivalent to those of bi-Legendrian racks.

Proof.

Since the functor
\Forg
is fully faithful, it will suffice to show that
\Forg
is bijective on objects. Injectivity follows from the first claim of Proposition
3.11
.

To show surjectivity, let
(R,\mathtt{u})
be a GL-rack with
R=(X,s)
, and define
\mathtt{d}:=\pi^{-1}\mathtt{u}^{-1}
as in Proposition
3.11
.
It will suffice to show that
(R,\mathtt{u},\mathtt{d})
is a bi-Legendrian rack. To that end, fix
x\in X
. Since
\Pi^{-1}\in Z(\mathsf{Rack})
,

\mathtt{ud}s_{x}(x)=\mathtt{u}\pi^{-1}\mathtt{u}^{-1}\pi(x)=\mathtt{u}\mathtt{u}^{-1}\pi^{-1}\pi(x)=x

and, similarly,
\mathtt{du}s_{x}(x)=x
. This verifies axiom
1
. Lemma
3.8
and the inclusion
\Pi^{-1}\in Z(\mathsf{Rack})
imply that
\mathtt{d}s_{x}=s_{x}\mathtt{d}
, which verifies axiom
2
. Since
\mathtt{u}
is a GL-structure,

s_{\mathtt{u}(x)}\mathtt{u}=\mathtt{u}s_{x}=s_{x}\mathtt{u}.

From the bijectivity of
\mathtt{u}
, we obtain
s_{\mathtt{u}(x)}=s_{x}
.
By construction,
\mathtt{d}\in\Aut R
, so we similarly obtain
s_{\mathtt{d}(x)}=s_{x}
.
This verifies axiom
3
. Hence,
(R,\mathtt{u},\mathtt{d})
is a bi-Legendrian rack, as desired.
∎

In light of these results, we will henceforth call bi-Legendrian racks GL-racks except when specifically citing the axioms in Definition
3.9
or denoting GL-racks as quadruples with
\mathtt{d}
. The previous two propositions make computing and classifying GL-racks significantly easier; see Section
4
. As a bonus, they also yield the following converse of
[
8
]
*Rem. 2.

Corollary 3.13
.

A GL-rack
(R,\mathtt{u})
is a Legendrian rack if and only if
\pi_{R}=\mathtt{u}^{-2}
. In this case,
R
is a quandle if and only if
\mathtt{u}
is an involution.

4.
Group-theoretic aspects of GL-racks

In a previous version of
[
27
]
*Sec. 3, Karmakar, Saraf, and Singh posed the following question: what are all the possible GL-structures on a given rack? In this section, we answer this question and classify various infinite families of GL-racks. As applications, we discuss automorphism groups of GL-racks and compute these groups for all GL-racks whose underlying racks are dihedral quandles.

4.1.
Characterizing GL-structures

Given a rack
R=(X,s)
, let
U_{R}
be the set of GL-structures
\mathtt{u}:X\to X
on
R
, and define an equivalence relation
\sim
on
U_{R}
by identifying
\mathtt{u}_{1}\sim\mathtt{u}_{2}
if and only if
(R,\mathtt{u}_{1})\cong(R,\mathtt{u}_{2})
.
The following theorem completely characterizes
U_{R}
and
U_{R}/{\sim}
.
Given a group
G
and a subset or element
H
of
G
, we will denote the centralizer of
H
in
G
by
C_{G}(H)
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Given a rack
R=(X,s)
, define
C:=C_{\Aut R}(\operatorname{Inn}R)
. Then
U_{R}=C
, and
U_{R}
is a normal subgroup of
\Aut R
. Furthermore,
\mathtt{u}_{1}\sim\mathtt{u}_{2}
if and only if
\mathtt{u}_{1}
and
\mathtt{u}_{2}
are conjugate in
\Aut R
.
In particular, if
\Aut R
is abelian, then
U_{R}=U_{R}/{\sim}=\Aut R
.

Proof.

The claim that
U_{R}=C
is a restatement of Definition
3.1
. It is straightforward to verify that
\operatorname{Inn}R
is a normal subgroup of
\Aut R
, so
C
is normal in
\Aut R
.

On the other hand, given two GL-structures
\mathtt{u}_{1},\mathtt{u}_{2}\in U_{R}=C
, a map
\varphi:X\to X
is a GL-rack isomorphism from
(R,\mathtt{u}_{1})
to
(R,\mathtt{u}_{2})
if and only if
\varphi\in\Aut R
and
\varphi\mathtt{u}_{1}=\mathtt{u}_{2}\varphi
. In other words,
(R,\mathtt{u}_{1})\cong(R,\mathtt{u}_{2})
if and only if
\mathtt{u}_{1}
and
\mathtt{u}_{2}
are conjugate in
\Aut R
, as claimed.

Finally, suppose that
\Aut R
is abelian. Then
\Aut R=C=U_{R}
, and each element of
\Aut R
constitutes its own conjugacy class. Hence,
U_{R}=U_{R}/{\sim}
.
∎

One may ask whether
U_{R}
is conjugacy-closed in
\Aut R
, that is, whether
\mathtt{u}_{1}\sim\mathtt{u}_{2}
in
U_{R}/{\sim}
if and only if
\mathtt{u}_{1}
and
\mathtt{u}_{2}
are conjugate in
U_{R}
. We will give a negative answer later; see Remark
4.12
.

Corollary 4.2
.

For all racks
R
, we have
U_{R}=U_{R^{\operatorname{op}}}
and
U_{R}/{\sim}=U_{R^{\operatorname{op}}}/{\sim}
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Proposition
2.7
and Theorem
4.1
.
∎

4.2.2.
Permutation GL-racks

First, we classify GL-structures on permutation racks.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
X
be a set, let
\sigma\in S_{X}
, and let
P
be the permutation rack
(X,\sigma)_{\mathrm{perm}}
. Then
U_{P}=C_{S_{X}}(\sigma)=\Aut P
, and
U_{P}/{\sim}
is the set of conjugacy classes of
C_{S_{X}}(\sigma)
.

Proof.

An automorphism of
P
is precisely a permutation
\varphi\in S_{X}
such that
\varphi\sigma=\sigma\varphi
. Therefore,
\Aut P=C_{S_{X}}(\sigma)
. On the other hand,
\operatorname{Inn}P=\langle\sigma\rangle
, so Theorem
4.1
states that

U_{P}=C_{C_{S_{X}}(\sigma)}(\langle\sigma\rangle)=C_{C_{S_{X}}(\sigma)}(\sigma)=C_{S_{X}}(\sigma)=\Aut P,

as desired. Combined with Theorem
4.1
, these equalities imply the last part of the claim.
∎

Example 4.4
.

For all trivial quandles
P=(X,\id_{X})_{\mathrm{perm}}
, we have
C_{S_{X}}(\id_{X})=S_{X}
. So, Proposition
4.3
states that
U_{P}=S_{X}
, and
U_{P}/{\sim}
is the set of conjugacy classes of
S_{X}
.

Example 4.5
.

Let
X:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, let
\sigma\in S_{n}
be an
n
-cycle, and let
P:=(X,\sigma)_{\mathrm{perm}}
. Then
C_{S_{n}}(\sigma)
is the cyclic subgroup
\langle\sigma\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
of
S_{n}
; see, for example,
[
14
]
*p. 127. Since
\langle\sigma\rangle
is abelian, Proposition
4.3
implies that
U_{P}/{\sim}=U_{P}=\langle\sigma\rangle
.

Example 4.6
.

Let
F
be the free rack on one element. For all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
\tau_{n}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
be the translation defined by
k\mapsto k+n
. Recall from Example
2.11
that
F
is isomorphic to the permutation rack
(\mathbb{Z},\tau_{1})_{\mathrm{perm}}
.
By Proposition
4.3
,

U_{F}=\Aut F=C_{S_{\mathbb{Z}}}(\tau_{1})=\{\tau_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}\cong\mathbb{Z}.

In particular,
\Aut F
is abelian, so
U_{F}=U_{F}/{\sim}
.
In other words, there are infinitely many GL-structures on
F
, all of which are translations of
\mathbb{Z}
and none of which yield isomorphic GL-racks.

4.2.3.
Conjugation GL-quandles

Next, we use Theorem
4.1
to classify GL-structures on conjugation quandles of centerless or abelian groups. Given a group
G
, let
\Aut_{\mathsf{Grp}}G
and
\operatorname{Inn}_{\mathsf{Grp}}G
denote the automorphism group and inner automorphism group of
G
, respectively.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
G
be a group, and let
Q:=\operatorname{Conj}G
. If
G
is abelian, then
U_{Q}=S_{G}
, and
U_{Q}/{\sim}
is the set of conjugacy classes of
S_{G}
. On the other hand, if
G
is centerless, then
U_{Q}=\{\id_{G}\}
.

Proof.

If
G
is abelian, then
Q
is a trivial quandle, so the first claim follows from Example
4.4
.
Next, recall a result of Elhamdadi, Macquarrie, and Restrepo
[
15
]
*Thm. 2.3 that
\operatorname{Inn}Q=\operatorname{Inn}_{\mathsf{Grp}}G
for all groups
G
.
Also, recall a result of Bardakov, Nasybullov, and Singh
[
2
]
*Cor. 2 that
\Aut Q=\Aut_{\mathsf{Grp}}G
if and only if
G
is centerless. In this case, Theorem
4.1
states that

U_{Q}=C_{\Aut_{\mathsf{Grp}}G}(\operatorname{Inn}_{\mathsf{Grp}}G),

which is the group of central automorphisms of
G
. However, this group is trivial when
G
is centerless; see, for example,
[
36
]
*p. 410.
∎

Example 4.8
.

Let
n\geq 3
be an integer or
n=\infty
. Then the symmetric group
S_{n}
is centerless, so Proposition
4.7
states that the only GL-structure on
\operatorname{Conj}S_{n}
is
\id_{S_{n}}
. Similarly, if
n\geq 4
, then the alternating group
A_{n}
is centerless, so the only GL-structure on
\operatorname{Conj}A_{n}
is
\id_{A_{n}}
.

4.2.4.
Takasaki GL-kei

Next, we classify GL-structures on a certain family of Takasaki kei and, as a consequence, all dihedral quandles of odd order.

We first recall a classification result of Bardakov, Dey, and Singh
[
1
]
*Thm. 4.2. For all abelian additive groups
A
without
2
-torsion,
\Aut T(A)
is isomorphic to the holomorph

G:=A\rtimes\Aut_{\mathsf{Grp}}A

of
A
.
Under this identification,

\operatorname{Inn}T(A)
is the semidirect product

H:=2A\rtimes\{\pm\id_{A}\}\leq G,

where
-\id_{A}
denotes inversion. We prove the following result using these identifications.

Proposition 4.9
.

If
A
is an abelian additive group without
2
-torsion, then the only GL-structure on the Takasaki kei
T(A)
is
\id_{A}
.

Proof.

First, note that for all automorphisms
\psi\in\Aut_{\mathsf{Grp}}A
such that
\psi(2a)=2a
for all
a\in A
, we have
2(\psi(a)-a)=0
. Since
A
is
2
-torsion-free, it follows that
\psi(a)=a
, so
\psi=\id_{A}
. Therefore, by Theorem
4.1
,
it will suffice to show that

C_{G}(H)\subseteq\{(0,\psi)\in G:\psi|_{2A}=\id_{2A}\}

since, as we just observed, the right-hand side is the trivial subgroup of
G
.
To that end, a direct computation shows that conjugation in
G
is given by

(a,\psi)(b,\varphi)(a,\psi)^{-1}=(a+\psi(b)-\psi\varphi\psi^{-1}(a),\psi\varphi\psi^{-1}).

For all
(b,\varphi)\in H
, we have
\varphi=\pm\id_{A}
. It follows that, for all
(a,\psi)\in C_{G}(H)
and
(b,\varphi)\in H
,

(b,\varphi)=(a,\psi)(b,\pm\id_{A})(a,\psi)^{-1}=(a+\psi(b)\mp a,\pm\id_{A}).

Taking
\varphi:=+\id_{A}
yields
\psi(b)=b
; since this equality holds for all
(b,\varphi)\in H
(and, hence, for all
b\in 2A
), we obtain
{\psi|_{2A}=\id_{2A}}
, as desired. Therefore, taking
\varphi:=-\id_{A}
yields
b=2a+b
, so
2a=0
. Since
A
has no
2
-torsion, it follows that
a=0
, as desired.
∎

Example 4.10
.

For all odd integers
n\geq 3
, the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
is
2
-torsion-free. By Proposition
4.9
, the only GL-structure on the dihedral quandle
R_{n}
of order
n
is
\id_{\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}}
.

4.2.5.
Dihedral GL-quandles

Without the assumption that
A
is
2
-torsion-free, there are infinitely many counterexamples to Proposition
4.9
.
To show this, we will complete the classification of GL-structures on dihedral quandles
R_{n}
using the following results of Elhamdadi, Macquarrie, and Restrepo
[
15
]
*Thms. 2.1 and 2.2. The automorphism group
\Aut R_{n}
is the group of affine transfomations of
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
. Thus,
\Aut R_{n}
is isomorphic to the holomorph

G:=\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}

of
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
.
Under this identification,

\operatorname{Inn}R_{n}
is the semidirect product

H:=2\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\rtimes\{\pm 1\}\leq G,

which is isomorphic to the dihedral group
D_{n/2}
of order
n
.

The following result strengthens Example
3.7
, and we state it in terms of the above identifications. Our proof uses the fact that conjugation in
G
is given by

(3)

(a,u)(b,v)(a,u)^{-1}=(a+ub,uv)(-u^{-1}a,u^{-1})=(ub+(1-v)a,v).

Proposition 4.11
.

For all even integers
n\geq 2
, the GL-structures on the dihedral quandle
R_{n}
are

(4)

U_{R_{n}}=\begin{cases}\{0,n/2\}\rtimes\{1\}&\text{if }4\nmid n,\\
\{0,n/2\}\rtimes\{1,1+n/2\}&\text{if }4\mid n.\end{cases}

If
4\nmid n
, then
U_{R_{n}}=U_{R_{n}}/{\sim}
, so
|U_{R_{n}}/{\sim}|=2
.
Otherwise, the only elements of
U_{R_{n}}
that are identified in
U_{R_{n}}/{\sim}
are
(0,1+n/2)
and
(n/2,1+n/2)
, so
|U_{R_{n}}/{\sim}|=3
.

Proof.

Theorem
4.1
states that, to prove equation (
4
), it will suffice to show that the right-hand side equals
C_{G}(H)
. For all elements
(a,u)\in C_{G}(H)
and
(b,v)\in H
, the right-hand side of equation (
3
) equals
(b,v)
. In particular,

b=ub+(1-v)a

for all
b\in 2\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, so taking
v:=1
yields
u\in\{1,1+n/2\}
. However,
1+n/2\in(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
if and only if
4\mid n
, as desired. Since
ub=b
, taking
v:=-1
yields
2a=0
. Hence,
a\in\{0,n/2\}
, as desired.
This shows that
C_{G}(H)
is a subset of the right-hand side of equation (
4
), and verifying the opposite containment is straightforward.

We now prove the second claim. Since
(0,1)
is the identity element of
G
, it is not conjugate to any other element of
U_{R_{n}}
. If
4\nmid n
, then we are done.

Otherwise, let
(b,v),(c,w)\in U_{R_{n}}
. Then
(b,v)
and
(c,w)
are conjugate in
G
if and only if there exists an element
(a,u)\in G
such that
(c,w)
equals the right-hand side of equation (
3
). In particular,
w=v
. It follows that neither
(0,1+n/2)
nor
(n/2,1+n/2)
is conjugate to
(n/2,1)
in
G
.
On the other hand, taking
(b,v):=(0,1+n/2)
and
(a,u):=(1,1)
in equation (
3
) shows that
(0,1+n/2)
and
(n/2,1+n/2)
are conjugate in
G
, so the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 4.12
.

If
n\geq 4
is a multiple of
4
, then equation (
4
) shows that
U_{R_{n}}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, so
U_{R_{n}}
is abelian.
It follows that
(0,1+n/2)
and
(n/2,1+n/2)
are conjugate in
G
but not in
U_{R_{n}}
.
Hence, the condition in Theorem
4.1
that
\mathtt{u}_{1}\sim\mathtt{u}_{2}
in
U_{R}/{\sim}
does not imply conjugacy in
U_{R}
.

4.3.
Automorphism groups of GL-racks

Given a GL-rack
(R,\mathtt{u})
, let
\Aut_{\mathsf{GLR}}(R,\mathtt{u})
denote its group of GL-rack automorphisms. The following characterization is simply a restatement of the definition of GL-rack automorphisms.

Proposition 4.13
.

For all GL-racks
(R,\mathtt{u})
, we have
\Aut_{\mathsf{GLR}}(R,\mathtt{u})=C_{\Aut R}(\mathtt{u})
.

Example 4.14
.

Let
L
be the free GL-rack on one element, and identify
L=(\mathbb{Z}^{2},\sigma,\mathtt{u}_{0})_{\mathrm{perm}}
as in Proposition
3.19
.
It is straightforward to show that GL-rack endomorphisms of
L
are precisely translations of the form
(m,n)\mapsto(m+k,n+\ell)
for some
(k,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Since all maps of this form are permutations of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, the mapping
(k,\ell)\mapsto\mathtt{u}_{0}^{\ell}\sigma^{k}
is a group isomorphism from
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
to
\Aut_{\mathsf{GLR}}(L)
.

4.3.1.
Automorphisms of dihedral GL-quandles

We classify automorphism groups of GL-racks whose underlying racks are dihedral quandles
R_{n}
of order
n
. Once again, we use a result of Elhamdadi, Macquarrie, and Restrepo
[
15
]
*Thm. 2.1 to identify
\Aut R_{n}\cong\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer, let
R_{n}
be the dihedral quandle of order
n
, and let
f_{b,v}:\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
defined by
k\mapsto b+vk
be a GL-structure on
R_{n}
; see Proposition
4.11
. Let
G:=\Aut_{\mathsf{GLR}}(R_{n},f_{b,v})
. Then

G\cong\begin{cases}2\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}&\text{if }4\mid n\text{ and }v=1+\frac{n}{2},\\
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In the latter case,
G=\Aut R_{n}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.13
,
G=C_{\Aut R_{n}}((b,v))
, so for all
(a,u)\in\Aut R_{n}
, we have
(a,u)\in G
if and only if
(b,v)
equals the right-hand side of equation (
3
).
Certainly, if
(b,v)=(0,1)
, then

G=\Aut R_{n}
, as claimed.
Otherwise,
n
is even by Example
4.10
.
By Proposition
4.11
, it suffices to only consider the cases that
(b,v)=(n/2,1)
and
(b,v)=(0,1+n/2)
.

If
(b,v)=(n/2,1)
, then
(a,u)\in G
if and only if
0=(u-1)(n/2)
in
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
.
But
u-1
is even for all elements
u\in(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, so this equation always holds. In other words,

every
element
(a,u)\in\Aut R_{n}
centralizes
(b,v)
, so
G=\Aut R_{n}
.

If
{(b,v)=(0,1+n/2)}
, then
(a,u)\in G
if and only if
0=(-n/2)a
in
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
. This is true if and only if
a
is even, and there are no restrictions placed on
u
. It follows that
G=2\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, which completes the proof.
∎

5.
Categorical aspects of GL-racks

In this section, we compute the centers of
\mathsf{GLR}
and several of its full subcategories. We also prove a surprising equivalence of categories between
\mathsf{Rack}
and
\mathsf{GLQ}
.

5.1.
Categorical centers

Recall that the
center
of a category
\mathcal{C}
is the commutative monoid
Z(\mathcal{C})
of natural endomorphisms of the identity functor
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}}
. In 2018, Szymik
[
37
]
*Thms. 5.4 and 5.5 computed that
Z(\mathsf{Rack})=\langle\Pi\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}
and
Z(\mathsf{Qnd})\cong\{1\}
.
In this section, we similarly compute the centers of
\mathsf{GLR}
,
\mathsf{GLQ}
, and the categories of Legendrian racks and Legendrian quandles.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\Pi
be the collection of canonical automorphisms
\pi_{R}
of racks
R
, and let
\mathtt{u}
be the collection of all GL-structures on racks. Then we have the following:

(1)

The center
Z(\mathsf{GLR})
is the free abelian group
\langle\Pi,\mathtt{u}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
generated by
\Pi
and
\mathtt{u}
.

(2)

The centers of
\mathsf{GLQ}
and the category of Legendrian racks are each the free group
\langle\mathtt{u}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}
.

(3)

The center of the category of Legendrian quandles is the group
\langle\mathtt{u}\mid\mathtt{u}^{2}=1\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

To prove claim
1
, let
\eta
be a natural endomorphism of
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{GLR}}
. By definition,
\eta
is contained in the center
Z(\mathsf{GLR})
if and only if, for all GL-racks
R_{1}=(X,s,\mathtt{u}_{1})
and
R_{2}=(Y,t,\mathtt{u}_{2})
and GL-rack homomorphisms
\varphi\in\Hom_{\mathsf{GLR}}(R_{1},R_{2})
, the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta_{R_{1}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta_{R_{2}}}

To see that
\langle\Pi,\mathtt{u}\rangle\subseteq Z(\mathsf{GLR})
, note by the definition of a GL-rack homomorphism that taking
\eta:=\mathtt{u}^{n}
with
n\in\mathbb{Z}
makes the diagram commute. If we take
\eta:=\Pi^{m}
with
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, then the diagram commutes because
\Pi
generates
Z(\mathsf{Rack})
. In particular, taking
R_{2}:=R_{1}
and
\varphi:=\mathtt{u}_{1}^{n}
shows that
\Pi^{m}
and
\mathtt{u}^{n}
commute in
Z(\mathsf{GLR})
.

To see that
Z(\mathsf{GLR})\subseteq\langle\Pi,\mathtt{u}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, take
R_{1}
to be the free GL-rack on one element
(0,0)
with its identification
R_{1}=(\mathbb{Z}^{2},\sigma,\mathtt{u}_{0})_{\mathrm{perm}}
from Proposition
3.19
.
Let
\varphi_{0}:\{(0,0)\}\to Y
be a set map, let
\varphi:\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to Y
be the induced GL-rack homomorphism from the universal property of free GL-racks, and fix
\eta\in Z(\mathsf{GLR})
. By Example
4.14
, all GL-rack endomorphisms of
R_{1}
have the form
(m,n)\mapsto(m+k,n+\ell)
for some
(k,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, and all of these endomorphisms are in fact automorphisms of
R_{1}
. In particular,
\eta_{R_{1}}
is an automorphism of
R_{1}
, say

\eta_{R_{1}}(m,n)=(m+k,n+\ell).

By commutativity and the proof of Proposition
3.19
,
\eta_{R_{2}}
sends the image of the generator
(0,0)
of
R_{1}
under
\varphi_{0}
to

\mathtt{u}^{\ell}_{2}\pi_{(Y,t)}^{k}\varphi_{0}(0,0).

Therefore,
\eta_{R_{2}}
is completely determined by these powers of
\pi_{(Y,t)}
and
\mathtt{u}_{2}
. Since
R_{2}
was an arbitrary GL-rack, it follows that
Z(\mathsf{GLR})\subseteq\langle\Pi,\mathtt{u}\rangle
, as desired. It also follows from Example
4.14
and Proposition
3.18
that
Z(\mathsf{GLR})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, which proves claim
1
.

To prove claim
2
, observe that
\Pi
fixes
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{GLQ}}
. It follows from Proposition
3.18
that
Z(\mathsf{GLQ})=\langle\mathtt{u}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}
, as desired.
Now, consider the full subcategory of
\mathsf{GLR}
whose objects are Legendrian racks. Using a similar argument as before, one can show using Corollary
3.13
and Proposition
3.18
that the center of this category is

\langle\Pi,\mathtt{u}\mid\Pi\mathtt{u}=\mathtt{u}\Pi,\,\Pi=\mathtt{u}^{-2}\rangle=\langle\mathtt{u}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}.

This proves claim
2
.
Similarly, claim
3
follows from Corollary
3.13
and Proposition
3.18
.
∎

5.2.
Categorical equivalence of racks and GL-quandles

Next, we show that the categories of racks and GL-quandles are isomorphic in a way that preserves mediality.

In a subsequent paper
[
40
]
, the author similarly showed that the category of racks is isomorphic to the category of Legendrian racks.

5.2.1.
Construction of
F

We begin by defining a functor
F:\mathsf{Rack}\to\mathsf{GLQ}
. First, we define how
F
acts on objects.

Proposition 5.2
.

Given a rack
R=(X,s)
, define
{\widetilde{s}}:X\to S_{X}
by

x\mapsto{\widetilde{s}}_{x}:=\pi_{R}^{-1}s_{x}.

Then
F(R):=(X,{\widetilde{s}},\pi_{R})
is a GL-quandle.

Proof.

First, we show that
(X,{\widetilde{s}})
is a quandle. Part
1
of Proposition
2.9
and part
3
of Proposition
2.8
imply that, for all elements
x,y\in X
,

{\widetilde{s}}_{x}{\widetilde{s}}_{y}=\pi^{-1}_{R}s_{x}\pi^{-1}_{R}s_{y}=\pi^{-2}_{R}s_{x}s_{y}=\pi^{-2}_{R}s_{s_{x}(y)}s_{x}=\pi^{-1}_{R}s_{s_{x}(y)}\pi^{-1}_{R}s_{x}=\pi^{-1}_{R}s_{\pi^{-1}_{R}s_{x}(y)}{\widetilde{s}}_{x}={\widetilde{s}}_{{\widetilde{s}}_{x}(y)}{\widetilde{s}}_{x},

so
(X,{\widetilde{s}})
is a rack.
Moreover, part
1
of Proposition
2.8
and part
1
of Proposition
2.9
imply that

{\widetilde{s}}_{x}(x)=\pi^{-1}_{R}s_{x}(x)=s_{x}\pi^{-1}_{R}(x)=s_{x}s_{x}^{-1}(x)=x,

so
(X,{\widetilde{s}})
is a quandle.

Next, we show that
\pi_{R}
is a GL-structure on
(X,{\widetilde{s}})
. Indeed, part
1
of Proposition
2.9
and part
3
of Proposition
2.8
imply that, for all
x\in X
,

(5)

\pi_{R}{\widetilde{s}}_{x}=\pi_{R}\pi_{R}^{-1}s_{x}=\pi_{R}^{-1}s_{x}\pi_{R}=\pi^{-1}_{R}s_{\pi_{R}(x)}\pi_{R}={\widetilde{s}}_{\pi_{R}(x)}\pi_{R},

so
\pi_{R}
is a rack endomorphism of
(X,{\widetilde{s}})
. Since
\pi_{R}
is a bijection, we have
\pi_{R}\in\Aut(X,{\widetilde{s}})
, as desired. Moreover, the third expression of equation (
5
) equals
{\widetilde{s}}_{x}\pi_{R}
, so
\pi_{R}
is a GL-structure.
∎

We now define how
F
acts on morphisms.

Proposition 5.3
.

For all racks
R=(X,s)
and
S=(Y,t)
, and for all rack homomorphisms
f\in\Hom_{\mathsf{Rack}}(R,S)
, we have
{f\in\Hom_{\mathsf{GLQ}}(F(R),F(S))}
. So, if we define
F
to fix
f
as a set map, then
F
is a covariant functor from
\mathsf{Rack}
to
\mathsf{GLQ}
.

Proof.

Certainly,
F
preserves the identity morphism and composition of morphisms, so we only need to verify that
f
is a GL-rack homomorphism from
F(R)=(X,{\widetilde{s}},\pi_{R})
to
F(S)=(Y,{\widetilde{t}},\pi_{S})
. Indeed, since
f\in\Hom_{\mathsf{Rack}}(R,S)
and
\Pi^{-1}\in Z(\mathsf{Rack})
,

f{\widetilde{s}}_{x}=f\pi_{R}^{-1}s_{x}=\pi_{S}^{-1}fs_{x}=\pi_{S}^{-1}t_{f(x)}f={\widetilde{t}}_{f(x)}f

for all
x\in X
, so
f\in\Hom_{\mathsf{Rack}}((X,{\widetilde{s}}),(Y,{\widetilde{t}}))
. Moreover,
f\pi_{R}=\pi_{S}f
since
\Pi\in Z(\mathsf{Rack})
, so
f
is a GL-rack homomorphism.
∎

5.2.2.
Construction of
G

We now define a functor
G:\mathsf{GLQ}\to\mathsf{Rack}
as the restriction of a functor
\widetilde{G}:\mathsf{GLR}\to\mathsf{Rack}
to
\mathsf{GLQ}
. First, we define how
\widetilde{G}
acts on objects.

Proposition 5.4
.

Given a GL-rack
R=(X,s,\mathtt{u})
, define
{\hat{s}}:X\to S_{X}
by

x\mapsto{\hat{s}}_{x}:=\mathtt{u}s_{x}.

Then
\widetilde{G}(R):=(X,{\hat{s}})
is a rack.

Proof.

Fix
x,y\in X
. Since
\mathtt{u}
is a GL-structure and
(X,s)
is a rack,

{\hat{s}}_{{\hat{s}}_{x}(y)}{\hat{s}}_{x}=\mathtt{u}s_{\mathtt{u}s_{x}(y)}\mathtt{u}s_{x}=\mathtt{u}^{2}s_{s_{x}(y)}s_{x}=\mathtt{u}^{2}s_{x}s_{y}=\mathtt{u}s_{x}\mathtt{u}s_{y}={\hat{s}}_{x}{\hat{s}}_{y},

so
(X,{\hat{s}})
is a rack.
∎

Next, we define how
\widetilde{G}
acts on morphisms.

Proposition 5.5
.

For all GL-racks
R_{1}=(X,s,\mathtt{u}_{1})
and
R_{2}=(Y,t,\mathtt{u}_{2})
and GL-rack homomorphisms
g\in\Hom_{\mathsf{GLR}}(R_{1},R_{2})
, we have
g\in\Hom_{\mathsf{Rack}}(\widetilde{G}(R_{1}),\widetilde{G}(R_{2}))
. So, if we define
\widetilde{G}
to fix
g
as a set map, then
\widetilde{G}
is a covariant functor from
\mathsf{GLR}
to
\mathsf{Rack}
, and
G
is a functor from
\mathsf{GLQ}
to
\mathsf{Rack}
.

Proof.

Certainly,
\widetilde{G}
preserves the identity morphism and composition of morphisms, so we only need to verify that
g\in\Hom_{\mathsf{Rack}}((X,{\hat{s}}),(Y,{\hat{t}}))
. Indeed, since
g
is a GL-rack homomorphism,

g{\hat{s}}_{x}=g\mathtt{u}_{1}s_{x}=\mathtt{u}_{2}gs_{x}=\mathtt{u}_{2}t_{g(x)}g={\hat{t}}_{g(x)}g

for all
x\in X
,
as desired.
∎

5.2.3.
Isomorphism of categories

Having defined
F
and
G
, we are now ready to prove the main results of this section.

Theorem 5.6
.

The functors
F
and
G
are isomorphisms of categories
\mathsf{Rack}\cong\mathsf{GLQ}
, and they restrict to isomorphisms
\mathsf{Rack}_{\mathrm{med}}\cong\mathsf{GLQ}_{\mathrm{med}}
.

Proof.

To show that
F
and
G
are isomorphisms of categories, we only need to show that
GF
and
FG
fix the objects in the appropriate categories;
GF
and
FG
clearly fix the morphisms.
To that end, let
R=(X,s)
be a rack. To see that
{GF(R)=R}
, note that
GF(R)=(X,\hat{{\widetilde{s}}})
, where

\hat{{\widetilde{s}}}_{x}=\pi_{R}{\widetilde{s}}_{x}=\pi_{R}\pi^{-1}_{R}s_{x}=s_{x}

for all
x\in X
. That is,
\hat{{\widetilde{s}}}=s
, so
GF=\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{Rack}}
, as desired.

Next, let
Q=(X,s,\mathtt{u})
be a GL-quandle. We must show that
FG(Q)=Q
. Note that, for all
x\in X
, we have

\hat{s}_{x}^{-1}=s_{x}^{-1}\mathtt{u}^{-1}=\mathtt{u}^{-1}s_{x}^{-1}.

Since
(X,s)
is a quandle, we also have
x=s_{x}(x)
, so
s_{x}^{-1}(x)=x
. Now, to see that
FG(Q)=Q
, write
FG(Q)=(X,\widetilde{{\hat{s}}},\pi_{G(Q)})
. Part
1
of Proposition
2.8
implies that, for all elements
x,y\in X
,

\widetilde{{\hat{s}}}_{y}(x)=\pi^{-1}_{G(Q)}{\hat{s}}_{y}(x)={\hat{s}}_{y}\pi^{-1}_{G(Q)}(x)=\mathtt{u}s_{y}\hat{s}^{-1}_{x}(x)=s_{y}\mathtt{u}\mathtt{u}^{-1}s_{x}^{-1}(x)=s_{y}(x).

Thus,
\widetilde{{\hat{s}}}_{y}=s_{y}
. Since

was arbitrary, this shows that
\widetilde{{\hat{s}}}=s
, as desired. Similarly,

\pi_{G(Q)}(x)=\hat{s}_{x}(x)=\mathtt{u}s_{x}(x)=\mathtt{u}(x)

for all
x\in X
, so
\pi_{G(Q)}=\mathtt{u}
.
Hence,
FG=\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{GLQ}}
, so
F
and
G
are isomorphisms of categories, as desired.
Since
\pi^{-1}
and
\mathtt{u}
are always rack automorphisms, the final claim follows straightforwardly from the definition of mediality that uses homomorphisms.
∎

Corollary 5.7
.

In the category of algebraic theories, the theory of racks and the theory of GL-quandles are isomorphic.

Proof.

Since
F
and
G
are left and right adjoints to each other, they both preserve limits and colimits. In particular, they preserve finite products and filtered colimits, so by
[

]
*Lem. 3.8.3,

and

are algebraic functors. Since

and

are equivalences of categories, the claim follows directly from
[
4
]
*Prop. 3.12.1.
∎

6.
Tensor products of racks and GL-racks

In 2014, Crans and Nelson
[
12
]
*Sec. 8.1 categorified the results of Corollary
2.13
by considering universal-algebraic tensor products of medial quandles. However, these tensors remain unexplored in the literature.

With this motivation, we consider universal-algebraic tensor products of racks and GL-racks. We show that, unlike with groups,
\mathsf{Rack}
and
\mathsf{GLR}
have tensor units. This suggests that
\mathsf{GLR}
and
\mathsf{GLR}_{\mathrm{med}}
make natural settings for functorial invariants of Legendrian links.

6.1.
Construction and universal property

We begin by considering tensor products of racks and GL-racks constructed via universal algebra. We discuss these tensors’ universal properties and show that the induced symmetric monoidal structures on
\mathsf{Rack}_{\mathrm{med}}
and
{\mathsf{GLR}_{\mathrm{med}}}
are closed.

Definition 6.1
.

If
R_{1}=(X,r,\mathtt{u}_{1})
and
R_{2}=(Y,t,\mathtt{u}_{2})
are GL-racks, then we define their
tensor product
, denoted by
R_{1}\otimes R_{2}
, to be the free GL-rack
\Free_{\mathsf{GLR}}(X\times Y)
modulo the smallest congruence such that the following hold for all
x,x_{1},x_{2}\in X
and
y,y_{1},y_{2}\in Y
, writing
x\otimes y:=(x,y)
:

(1)

s_{x\otimes y_{2}}(x\otimes y_{1})\sim x\otimes t_{y_{2}}(y_{1})
, and
s^{-1}_{x\otimes y_{2}}(x\otimes y_{1})\sim x\otimes t^{-1}_{y_{2}}(y_{1})
.

(2)

s_{x_{2}\otimes y}(x_{1}\otimes y)\sim r_{x_{2}}(x_{1})\otimes y
, and
s^{-1}_{x_{2}\otimes y}(x_{1}\otimes y)\sim r^{-1}_{x_{2}}(x_{1})\otimes y
.

(3)

\mathtt{u}(x\otimes y)\sim\mathtt{u}_{1}(x)\otimes y\sim x\otimes\mathtt{u}_{2}(y).

We also define the
medial tensor product
of
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
to be

R_{1}\otimes_{\mathrm{med}}R_{2}:=(R_{1}\otimes R_{2})_{\mathrm{med}}.

Similarly, we define the tensor product of two racks
R_{1}=(X,r)
and
R_{2}=(Y,t)
as
\Free_{\mathsf{Rack}}(X\times Y)
modulo the smallest congruence generated by relations
1
and
2
above, and we define the medial tensor product of racks similarly.

Remark 6.2
.

Relation
3
implies a similar relation involving
\mathtt{u}^{-1}
. Namely, in
(X,r,\mathtt{u}_{1})\otimes(Y,t,\mathtt{u}_{2})
,

\mathtt{u}^{-1}(x\otimes y)=\mathtt{u}_{1}^{-1}(x)\otimes y=x\otimes\mathtt{u}^{-1}_{2}(y)

for all
x\in X
and
y\in Y
. To see this, compute

\mathtt{u}_{1}^{-1}(x)\otimes y=\mathtt{u}^{-1}\mathtt{u}(\mathtt{u}_{1}^{-1}(x)\otimes y)=\mathtt{u}^{-1}(\mathtt{u}_{1}\mathtt{u}_{1}^{-1}(x)\otimes y)=\mathtt{u}^{-1}(x\otimes y),

and similarly for
x\otimes\mathtt{u}^{-1}_{2}(y)
.

We note that the medialized associated quandles of the tensor products of racks in Definition
6.1
recover the tensor products of medial quandles that Crans and Nelson
[
12
]
*Sec. 8.1 introduced in 2014. On the other hand, these tensors are distinct from the tensor products that Kamada
[
25
]
*Def. 3.1 introduced in 2021, which are sets without canonical rack structures.

6.1.1.
Bihomomorphisms

The discussion in
[
4
]
*p. 171 shows that the tensor products in Definition
6.1
are each characterized by a universal factorizing property. Before we can state this property, we will need the following definition from universal algebra. This is a special case of a more general definition for arbitrary algebraic theories; see, for example,
[
4
]
*Def. 3.10.2.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
(X,r)
,
(Y,s),
and
(Z,t)
be racks. We say that a map
\beta_{0}:X\times Y\to Z
is a
rack bihomomorphism
if, for all
x\in X
and
y\in Y
, the restricted maps
\beta_{0}(-,y):X\to Z
and
\beta_{0}(x,-):Y\to Z
are rack homomorphisms.
GL-rack bihomomorphisms
are defined similarly.

Example 6.4
.

Let
R=(X,r)
be a rack, and let
S=(Y,s)
and
T=(Z,t)
be medial racks. Recall from Corollary
2.13
that
H_{1}:=\Hom_{\mathsf{Rack}}(R,S)
,
H_{2}:=\Hom_{\mathsf{Rack}}(S,T)
, and
H_{3}:=\Hom_{\mathsf{Rack}}(R,T)
each have a canonical rack structure.

In analogy with the composition of
A
-linear maps in the category of modules over a ring
A
, the composition map
\beta_{0}:H_{1}\times H_{2}\to H_{3}
is a rack bihomomorphism. To see this, fix a homomorphism
g\in H_{2}
. For all homomorphisms
\varphi,\psi\in H_{1}
and elements
x\in X
, we have

\beta_{0}({\widetilde{s}}_{\psi}(\varphi),g)(x)=g{\widetilde{s}}_{\psi}(\varphi)(x)=gs_{\psi(x)}\varphi(x)=t_{g\psi(x)}g\varphi(x)=\widetilde{t}_{g\psi}g\varphi(x)=\widetilde{t}_{\beta_{0}(\psi,g)}\beta_{0}(\varphi,g)(x),

so the restriction
\beta_{0}(-,g):H_{1}\to H_{3}
is a rack homomorphism. Similarly, for all homomorphisms
f\in H_{1}
, the restriction
\beta_{0}(f,-):H_{2}\to H_{3}
is a homomorphism, so
\beta_{0}
is a bihomomorphism.

It is straightforward to verify using Theorem
3.21
that if
R
,
S
, and
T
also have GL-structures, then the restriction of
\beta_{0}
to the respective hom-sets in
\mathsf{GLR}
is a GL-rack bihomomorphism.

6.1.2.
Universal property of tensor products

As suggested in Example
6.4
, the definition of a rack bihomomorphism analogizes the definition of a bilinear map in the category of modules over a ring. The universal property of tensor products, which we state below, extends this analogy.

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
be racks. Then
R_{1}\otimes R_{2}
is characterized up to isomorphism by a universal property. Namely, there exists a rack bihomomorphism
\psi
from
R_{1}\times R_{2}
to
R_{1}\otimes R_{2}
such that, for all racks
R_{3}
and rack bihomomorphisms
\beta_{0}:R_{1}\times R_{2}\to R_{3}
, there exists a unique rack homomorphism
\beta:R_{1}\otimes R_{2}\to R_{3}
such that
\beta_{0}=\beta\psi
. In particular, the following diagram commutes:

(6)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{R_{1}\times R_{2}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{R_{1}\otimes R_{2}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R_{3}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta}

A similar result holds for GL-racks.

Proof.

This is a consequence of universal algebra. Regard racks and GL-racks as algebraic theories with binary operations
s_{-}(-)
and
s^{-1}_{-}(-)
, along with unary operations
\mathtt{u}
and
\mathtt{u}^{-1}
for GL-racks. Due to Remark
6.2
, the tensor product in Definition
6.1
is precisely the tensor product constructed in the proof of
[
4
]
*Thm. 3.10.3, and
\psi(x,y)=x\otimes y
for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
. Thus, the claim follows from the discussion in
[
4
]
*p. 171.
∎

Example 6.6
.

A rack
(X,s)
is called
left-distributive
if

(7)

s_{s_{a}(b)}(x)=s_{s_{a}(x)}(s_{b}(x))

for all
a,b,x\in X
. The name comes from the fact that, in terms of the right-distributive binary operation
\triangleright
often used in the literature, equation (
7
) states that

x\triangleright(b\triangleright a)=(x\triangleright b)\triangleright(x\triangleright a).

For example, it is straightforward to verify that medial quandles are left-distributive.
Moreover, self-distributive quasigroups are precisely left-distributive quandles satisfying an axiom called the
Latin
condition; see, for example,
[
16
]
*p. 143.

Evidently, a rack
R=(X,s)
is left-distributive if and only if, for all
x\in X
, the map
X\to X
defined by
y\mapsto s_{y}(x)
is an endomorphism of
R
. Equivalently, the map
\beta_{0}:X\times X\to X
defined by
(x,y)\mapsto s_{y}(x)
is a bihomomorphism from
R\times R
to
R
.
In this case, the universal property of
R\otimes R
implies the existence of a unique homomorphism
\beta\in\Hom_{\mathsf{Rack}}(R\otimes R,R)
such that
\beta(x\otimes y)=s_{y}(x)
for all
x,y\in X
.

6.1.3.
Internal hom-tensor adjunctions

Mirroring the work of Crans and Nelson
[
12
]
*Thm. 12 on the category of medial quandles, the next result continues the analogy with the category of modules over a ring by describing internal hom-tensor adjunctions in
\mathsf{Rack}_{\mathrm{med}}
and
\mathsf{GLR}_{\mathrm{med}}
.

Theorem 6.7
.

The categories
\mathsf{Rack}_{\mathrm{med}}
and
{\mathsf{GLR}_{\mathrm{med}}}
are closed symmetric monoidal with respect to the medial tensor product
\otimes_{\mathrm{med}}
in each category and the closed structures
\Hom_{\mathsf{Rack}_{\mathrm{med}}}(-,-)
and
\Hom_{{\mathsf{GLR}_{\mathrm{med}}}}(-,-)
from Corollary
2.13
and Theorem
3.21
, respectively.

Proof.

Recall that the algebraic theories of medial racks and medial GL-racks are commutative. Therefore, both claims are special cases of a general result for commutative algebraic theories; see, for example,
[
4
]
*Thm. 3.10.3.

In particular,
\otimes_{\mathrm{med}}
is precisely the tensor product constructed in the proof of
[
4
]
*Thm. 3.10.3, and the tensor unit is the free rack (resp. GL-rack)
L
on one element. Since permutation racks are medial, Example
2.11
(resp. Proposition
3.19
) shows that
L
is medial.
∎

6.2.
Nonmedial tensor products

One may ask how much of the structure in Theorem
6.7
remains if we drop the mediality assumption. By
[
4
]
*Thm. 3.10.3, we lose the closed structure because racks and GL-racks are noncommutative algebraic theories. Nevertheless, we show that tensor products in
\mathsf{Rack}
and
\mathsf{GLR}
surprisingly retain tensor units.

Although universal-algebraic tensor products with the appropriate universal properties exist for all algebraic theories, they are often not well-behaved in noncommutative algebraic theories. For example, the universal-algebraic tensor product of groups
G\otimes H
is isomorphic to the usual
\mathbb{Z}
-module tensor product
G^{\operatorname{ab}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}H^{\operatorname{ab}}
of the abelianizations of
G
and
H
; see, for example,
[
4
]
*p. 171. In particular, no tensor unit exists.

In this subsection, we show that the pathologies in the previous paragraph do
not
apply to tensor products of racks or GL-racks, even though racks and GL-racks are noncommutative algebraic theories. In particular, while the universal-algebraic tensor product of groups is always abelian, tensor products of racks and GL-racks are not necessarily medial.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
R_{1}=(X,r)
,
R_{2}=(Y,s)
, and
R_{3}=(Z,t)
be racks, and let
\beta_{0}:X\times Y\to Z
be a rack bihomomorphism from
R_{1}\times R_{2}
to
R_{3}
. Then
for all integers
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, elements
x\in X
, and elements
y\in Y
,

\beta_{0}(\pi_{R_{1}}^{k}(x),y)=\beta_{0}(x,\pi_{R_{2}}^{k}(y)).

Proof.

For all
y\in Y
, the restriction
\beta_{0}(-,y):X\to Z
is a homomorphism from
R_{1}
to
R_{3}
. For all integers
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, the inclusion
\Pi^{k}\in Z(\mathsf{Rack})
implies that

\beta_{0}(\pi_{R_{1}}^{k}(x),y)=\pi^{k}_{R_{3}}\beta_{0}(x,y)

for all
x\in X
. A similar argument using the restriction
\beta_{0}:(x,-):Y\to Z
yields the claim. ∎

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
F
be the free rack on one element. Then for all racks
R
, we have natural isomorphisms
R\otimes F\cong R\cong F\otimes R
.

Similarly, let
L
be the free GL-rack on one element. Then for all GL-racks
R
, we have natural isomorphisms
R\otimes L\cong R\cong L\otimes R
.

Proof.

First, let
R=(X,s)
be a rack. We will show that
R\otimes F\cong R
; the proof that
F\otimes R\cong R
is similar, and naturality is straightfoward to verify from there. Identify
F=(\mathbb{Z},\sigma)_{\mathrm{perm}}

as in Example
2.11
.

By Proposition
6.5
, it will suffice to show that
R
satisfies the universal property of
R\otimes F
with
\psi:X\times\mathbb{Z}\to X
defined by

(x,k)\mapsto\pi^{k}_{R}(x).

First, we show that
\psi
is a rack bihomomorphism. Fix
x\in X
, and let
s^{\mathbb{Z}}:\mathbb{Z}\to\{\sigma\}
denote the rack structure of
F
. For all
k,n\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\psi(x,s^{\mathbb{Z}}_{k}(n))=\psi(x,\sigma(n))=\psi(x,n+1)=\pi^{n+1}_{R}(x)=\pi^{n}_{R}s_{x}(x)=s_{\pi_{R}^{k}(x)}\pi_{R}^{n}(x)=s_{\psi(x,k)}\psi(x,n).

In the fifth equality, we have used part
1
of Proposition
2.9
and part
3
of Proposition
2.8
. So,
{\psi(x,-):\mathbb{Z}\to Y}
is a rack homomorphism from
F
to
R^{\prime}
.
Next, fix
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. For all
x,y\in X
,

\psi(s_{x}(y),k)=\pi^{k}_{R}s_{x}(y)=s_{x}\pi^{k}_{R}(y)=s_{\pi_{R}^{k}(x)}\pi^{k}_{R}(y)=s_{\psi(x,k)}\psi(y,k)

by part
1
of Proposition
2.9
and part
3
of Proposition
2.8
, so
\psi(-,k):X\to Y
is a rack homomorphism from
R
to
R^{\prime}
. Therefore,
\psi
is a rack bihomomorphism, as desired.

Now, let
R^{\prime}=(Y,t)
be a rack, and let
\beta_{0}:X\times\mathbb{Z}\to Y
be a rack bihomomorphism from
R\times F
to
R^{\prime}
. Define
\beta:X\to Y
by
x\mapsto\beta_{0}(x,0)
. Then
\beta
is precisely the restriction
\beta_{0}(-,0):X\to Y
, so
\beta
is a rack homomorphism, as desired. Since
\pi_{F}=\sigma
, Lemma
6.8
implies that

\beta\psi(x,k)=\beta\pi^{k}_{R}(x)=\beta_{0}(\pi^{k}_{R}(x),0)=\beta_{0}(x,\sigma^{k}(0))=\beta_{0}(x,k)

for all
(x,k)\in X\times\mathbb{Z}
, so diagram (
6
) commutes. Finally, uniqueness follows from the surjectivity of
\psi
. Hence,
R
satisfies the universal property of
R\otimes F
, so
R\cong R\otimes F
, as claimed.

For the second part of the claim, let
R=(X,s,\mathtt{u})
be a GL-rack. Identify
L=(\mathbb{Z}^{2},\sigma,\mathtt{u}_{0})_{\mathrm{perm}}
as in Proposition
3.19
. Again, it suffices to show that
R
satisfies the universal property of
R\otimes L
with
\psi:X\times\mathbb{Z}^{2}
defined by

(x,m,n)\mapsto\mathtt{u}^{n}\pi^{m}_{R}(x).

The proof that
\psi
is a GL-rack bihomomorphism is similar to the argument given above; we leave the details to the reader.

Given a GL-rack
R^{\prime}=(Y,t,\mathtt{u}_{2})
and a GL-rack bihomomorphism
\beta_{0}:X\times\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to Y
from
R\times L
to
R^{\prime}
, define
{\beta:X\to Y}
by
x\mapsto\beta_{0}(x,0,0)
. Once again,
\beta
is a GL-rack homomorphism since it is the restriction
\beta_{0}(-,0,0):X\to Y
. The commutativity of diagram (
6
) and uniqueness of
\beta
are shown in a similar way as before, so
R\cong R\otimes L
.
∎

Recall that the free rack on one element is medial, and similarly for the free GL-rack on one element. Therefore, Theorem
6.9
implies the following result—a surprising statement considering that the universal-algebraic tensor product of groups is necessarily abelian.

Corollary 6.10
.

Even if one of the tensor factors is medial, tensor products of racks are not necessarily medial, and similarly for tensor products of GL-racks.

It would be interesting to study more properties and applications of the tensor products in Definition
6.1
.
In Section
7
, we propose future work in this direction.
