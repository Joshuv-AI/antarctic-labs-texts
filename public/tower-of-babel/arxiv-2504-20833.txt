1
Introduction and results

1.1
Introduction

The (standard dyadic) Branching Brownian motion (BBM) is a classical stochastic process that describes the evolution of a population of particles undergoing both independent diffusion and branching. It is a staple of probability theory and statistical physics, with applications in e.g. population dynamics, reaction-diffusion equations, and random spin-glass models. Formally, BBM starts with a single particle at the origin, which diffuses as a standard Brownian motion. This particle lives for an exponentially distributed lifetime with mean one, and upon its death this particle splits into two offspring at the same position. Each daughter particle then starts an independent copy of the same process.

The behaviour of the extremal particles of the BBM is by now a classical and well developed question which has seen some striking recent progress. Because the BBM is a toy model for log-correlated random fields, those developments have been instrumental in the study of the extremes of several models such as the Gaussian free field and the Random Energy Model universality class.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{t}
be the collection of particles at time
t
. For
v\in\mathcal{N}_{t}
and
s\in\left[0,t\right]
, we say that
u\in\mathcal{N}_{s}
is its ancestor at time
s
if
v
is a descendant of
u
and denote it
u\preceq v
. For
u\in\mathcal{N}_{t}
and
s\in\left[0,t\right]
, we write
X_{u}(s)\in\mathbb{R}
for the position of particle
u
(or its ancestor) at time
s
. We define the branching Brownian motion process to be
X(t)=(X_{u}(t),u\in\mathcal{N}_{t})
and its natural filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\{X(s),s\leq t\}
.

In a series of seminal works, Bramson
[
8
]
, showed that the position of the rightmost particle in a BBM is centred around
m_{t}\coloneqq\sqrt{2}t-\frac{3}{2\sqrt{2}}\log t
, and more precisely that

\max_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{t}}X_{u}(t)-m_{t}\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow\infty]{d}W,

where
W
is a random variable whose distribution function is given by the so-called critical traveling wave solution to the Fisher-KPP equation. Lalley and Sellke
[
15
]
then proved that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(\max_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{t}}X_{u}(t)-m_{t}\leq x)\to\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-CZ_{\infty}e^{-\sqrt{2}x}}\right],

(1)

where
Z_{\infty}>0
is the almost sure limit of the derivative martingale:

Z_{t}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{t}}(\sqrt{2}t-X_{u}(t))e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{u}(t)-\sqrt{2}t)}.

Observe that this suggests that

\max_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{t}}X_{u}(t)-m_{t}-\log(CZ_{\infty})/\sqrt{2}\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow\infty]{d}G,

(2)

where
G
follows a Gumbel distribution, as later confirmed; see, e.g.,
[
1
]
.

To study the genealogy of the process, we will frequently appeal to the branching property. For any time
r\geq 0
and any particle
u\in\mathcal{N}_{r}
, the descendants of
u
form a branching Brownian motion shifted in time and space. We denote by
Z_{\infty}^{(u)}
the almost sure limit of the derivative martingale associated with this shifted subtree. Conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{r}
, the random variables
(Z_{\infty}^{(u)})_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{r}}
are independent and have the same distribution as the global limit
Z_{\infty}
.

The aim of our work is to extend the understanding of the front up to sublinear distance of
m_{t}
. In particular, we investigate the number of particles within a sublinear distance of
m_{t}
. Understanding this quantity provides deeper insights into the fine structure of the front and other existing results.

1.2
Main results

For
t\geq 0
, define the set of particles to the right of
m_{t}-x
,

\mathcal{N}(t,x)\coloneqq\{u\in\mathcal{N}_{t}:X_{u}(t)\geq m_{t}-x\},

(3)

and
N(t,x)\coloneqq\#\mathcal{N}(t,x)
its cardinality.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
x_{t}
be such that, as
t\to\infty
,
x_{t}=o(t)
and
x_{t}\rightarrow\infty
, then,

\frac{N(t,x_{t})}{\pi^{-\frac{1}{2}}x_{t}e^{\frac{m_{t}}{t}x_{t}-\frac{x^{2}}{2t}}}\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow\infty]{\mathbb{P}}Z_{\infty}.

(4)

For convenience, we define

f(t,x)\coloneqq\pi^{-1/2}xe^{\frac{m_{t}}{t}x-\frac{x^{2}}{2t}}.

Remark 1.2
.

The statement of Theorem
1.1
is equivalent to, for any
\delta>0
,

\lim_{x\to\infty}\lim_{c\to 0}\sup_{t:x\leq ct}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{N(t,x)}{f(t,x)}-Z_{\infty}\right|>\delta\right)=0.

(5)

The following theorem establishes that almost sure convergence fails when
x_{t}
grows slowly with
t
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
x_{t}
be such that
0\leq x_{t}\leq t^{1/3}
. Then,

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{N(t,x_{t})}{x_{t}e^{\sqrt{2}x_{t}}}=\infty\qquad\text{a.s.}

(6)

The proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on bounding the number of pairs of particles that do not split early. Together with Theorem
1.1
, this bound yields an explicit limit overlap distribution for two particles sampled uniformly from
\mathcal{N}(t,x)
.

Define the splitting time between two particles
(u,v)\in\mathcal{N}_{t}^{2}
as

Q_{t}(u,v)\coloneqq\sup\left\{s\leq t:\forall\gamma\leq s,\ X_{u}(\gamma)=X_{v}(\gamma)\right\}.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
x_{t}
be such that
x_{t}\to\infty
, and
x_{t}=o(t)
as
t\to\infty
. For
t\geq 0
, let

\mu_{t}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N(t,x_{t})^{2}}\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{N}(t,x_{t})^{2}}\delta_{Q_{t}(u,v)}\quad(\text{setting }\mu_{t}\coloneqq\delta_{0}\text{ if }N(t,x_{t})=0)

(7)

the empirical overlap distribution for two particles sampled uniformly from
\mathcal{N}(t,x_{t})
. Then
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
converges weakly in probability to a limit
\mu_{\infty}
, that is,

\mu_{t}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}\mu_{\infty}\quad\text{in the weak topology}.

(8)

Where
\mu_{\infty}
is characterized by

\mu_{\infty}([r,\infty))=\frac{1}{Z_{\infty}^{2}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{r}}\left(e^{\sqrt{2}X_{u}(r)-2r}\,Z_{\infty}^{(u)}\right)^{2},\qquad\forall r\geq 0,\quad\text{almost surely.}

(9)

Theorem
1.4
directly implies the following.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
U
and
V
be chosen uniformly at random from
\mathcal{N}(t,x_{t})
, and define
Q_{t}=Q_{t}(U,V)
if
\mathcal{N}(t,x_{t})\neq\emptyset
, and
Q_{t}=0
otherwise. Then
(Q_{t},Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
converges in distribution to
(Q_{\infty},Z_{\infty})
, where, conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}=\sigma\left(\cup_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)
,
Q_{\infty}
has distribution
\mu_{\infty}
.

1.3
Discussion and related work

First, observe that a first-moment calculation for
N(t,x)
does not yield the correct order of magnitude. Indeed, a straightforward application of the many-to-One Lemma gives that

\mathbb{E}[N(t,x)]\asymp te^{\frac{m_{t}}{t}x-\frac{x^{2}}{2t}},

(10)

whereas the convergence in Theorem
1.1
suggests an order that is lower by a factor of
x/t
. This discrepancy arises from the correlation structure of branching Brownian motion. Specifically, Arguin, Bovier, and Kistler
[
3
]
proved that extremal particles are unlikely to cross a certain barrier to the right. It is natural to expect that likewise, the particles contributing to
N(t,x)
are similarly localised. The inflation of
\mathbb{E}[N(t,x)]
can thus be attributed to the contribution of unlikely events where particles do manage to cross this barrier.

Theorem
1.4
, Proposition
4.1
and Proposition
2.1
provide the following description of the front of the branching Brownian motion. With high probability, for
x=o(t)
, most of the particles to the right of
m_{t}-x
are in fact located within
m_{t}-x+O(1)
at time
t
and remain below the curve
s\frac{m_{t}}{t}-(s\wedge(t-s))^{\alpha}
for any
\alpha<1/2
, throughout the time interval
[O(1),t-O(1)]
.

More precisely, the trajectory of a particle in
\mathcal{N}(t,x)
, shifted by
m_{t}s/t
, resembles a Brownian bridge
B_{s}
from distance
O(1)
at time
r=O(1)
and reaching
x
at time
t
conditioned to stay positive over the interval
[r,t]
.

•

For
x\ll\sqrt{t}
,
B_{s}
behaves like a Brownian excursion (of length
t
) up to time
\gamma_{\star}=t-x^{2}
and hence
B_{t-x^{2}}/x=O(1)
. After
\gamma_{\star},
the constraint to remain positive only contributes a constant factor, as this is a non-zero probability event. Furthermore, by Lemma
3.1
, the probability for a Brownian bridge starting at
O(1)
at time
r
and being at position
x
at time
t-x^{2}
without crossing 0 is of order
x/t
.

•

For
x\gg\sqrt{t}
,
B_{s}
behaves like a Brownian excursion (of length
t
) up to time
\gamma_{\star}=(t/x)^{2}
and hence
B_{(t/x)^{2}}x/t=O(1)
. After
\gamma_{\star},
the constraint to remain positive only contributes a constant factor, as this is a non-zero probability event. Furthermore, by Lemma
3.1
, the probability for a Brownian bridge starting at
O(1)
at time
r
and being at position
t/x
at time
(t/x)^{2}
without crossing 0 is of order
x/t
.

Thus, in both regimes, the probability of not hitting the barrier provides the correct multiplicative factor
x/t
, as suggested at the beginning of this discussion.

Theorem
1.4
implies that a pair of particles in the set
\mathcal{N}(t,x_{t})
typically splits early in the life of the process. It is interesting to compare this with the genealogy of the extremal particles, studied by Arguin, Bovier, and Kistler. In
[
3
, Theorem 2.1]
, it is established that the branching time of a pair of extremal particles at time
t
(i.e., within a compact distance of
m_{t}
) is typically either close to zero or close to
t
. When
x
is fixed of order 1, there are only finitely many particles in
\mathcal{N}(t,x)
, and as they branch at a constant rate, there is a non-zero probability that two randomly sampled particles share a very recent common ancestor. In the language of the extremal point process, such closely related particles form a “decoration”. If we condition two such extremal particles on having a small branching time (e.g.,
\leq t/2
), they necessarily lie in different decorations. We expect that under this conditioning, their branching time also converges to our limit
Q_{\infty}
.

The key difference in our setting is as
x_{t}\to\infty
, the total number of decorations in
\mathcal{N}(t,x_{t})
tends to infinity, and no single recent branching event produces a positive proportion of the total population. Consequently, the probability of choosing two particles from the same decoration vanishes. Two randomly chosen particles will come from different decorations—and thus branched very far in the past— with probability tending to one, corresponding to the early-branching limit
Q_{\infty}
.

The limit measure
\mu_{\infty}
in Theorem
1.4
can be further characterized through its relationship with the
critical measure

\nu_{\infty}
of the BBM. As established by Mallein
[
18
]
, the derivative martingale naturally induces a sequence of random probability measures
\nu_{t}
on the particles at time
t
:

\nu_{t}\coloneqq\frac{1}{Z_{t}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{t}}(\sqrt{2}t-X_{u}(t))e^{\sqrt{2}X_{u}(t)-2t}\delta_{u},

(11)

which converges weakly almost surely to the critical measure
\nu_{\infty}
on the boundary of the genealogical tree
\partial\mathbb{T}
. In particular if
V\sim\nu_{\infty}
, then the probability that
V
is a descendant of some particle
u\in\mathcal{N}_{r}
is given by the relative mass of the sub-tree rooted at
u
:

\nu_{\infty}(u\preceq V)=\frac{e^{\sqrt{2}X_{u}(r)-2r}Z_{\infty}^{(u)}}{Z_{\infty}}.

(12)

Therefore, sampling
U,V
independently from
\nu_{\infty}
,
the probability that their most recent common ancestor
Q(U,V)
lived after time
r
is given by the sum of the squared masses of the sub-trees at time
r
:

\nu_{\infty}^{\otimes 2}(Q(U,V)\geq r)=\frac{1}{Z_{\infty}^{2}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{r}}\left(e^{\sqrt{2}X_{u}(r)-2r}Z_{\infty}^{(u)}\right)^{2}.

(13)

This is exactly the formula that characterizes
\mu_{\infty}([r,+\infty))
in Theorem
1.4
. This identity reveals that the genealogical structure of the front is invariant across the sublinear regime, and that the ancestral splitting pattern governed by the derivative martingale is a universal feature for all particles at sublinear distance from the tip.

Note that, due to the phenomenon highlighted in the proof of Theorem
1.3
, it is unlikely that the convergence in probability obtained in Theorem
1.4
could be reinforced to an almost sure convergence for the regime
x_{t}\leq t^{1/3}
.

The extremal point process of the BBM has been studied extensively. In particular, Aïdékon, Berestycki, Brunet, and Shi
[
1
]
on the one hand, and Arguin, Bovier, and Kistler
[
5
]
on the other, independently established that, viewed as a point process and shifted by
m_{t}
, the BBM converges in distribution to a limit point process called the
extremal point process
. More precisely, let

\mathcal{E}_{t}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{t}}\delta_{X_{u}(t)-m_{t}}.

(14)

As
t\to\infty
, they showed that the family of point processes
\mathcal{E}_{t}
converges in distribution to a limit, sometimes referred to as a randomly shifted decorated Poisson point process (SDPPP).

Using the convergence of
\mathcal{E}_{t}
, Cortines, Hartung, and Louidor study the asymptotic number of particles in the extremal point process. They state,
[
10
, Theorem 1.1]
, that there exists
C_{\star}>0
such that

\frac{\mathcal{E}\left([-x,\infty)\right)}{C_{\star}Z_{\infty}xe^{\sqrt{2}x}}\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}1.

(15)

Since
\mathcal{E}
is the limit of
\mathcal{E}_{t}
and
\mathcal{E}_{t}\left([-x,\infty)\right)=N(t,x)
, the convergence in (
15
) is a direct consequence of our results. Specifically, letting
t\to\infty
first in the Remark
1.2
, we obtain that for any
\epsilon>0
,

\lim_{x\to\infty}\lim_{t\to\infty}P\left(\left|\frac{\mathcal{E}_{t}\left([-x,\infty)\right)}{C_{\star}Z_{\infty}xe^{\sqrt{2}x}}-1\right|>\epsilon\right)=0.

(16)

This recovers (
15
), yielding the explicit value of the constant
C_{\star}
. Interestingly, if
x_{t}=o(\sqrt{t})
, the denominator in Theorem
1.1
depends only on
x
and satisfies
f(t,x)\sim\pi^{1/2}xe^{\sqrt{2}x}
, which matches the growth of the extremal point process.

In
[
17
]
, Ren and Ma, leveraging previous methods developed in
[
2
]
and
[
19
]
, proved a law of large numbers for a particle chosen according to the Gibbs measure in
[
17
, Proposition 2.6]
. Plugging in the following value we can link their result to our Theorem
1.1
. Let
K>0
and
x_{t}
be such that
\log(t)^{3}\ll x_{t}\leq\sqrt{t}
and plugging in
G(z)=\text{1}_{[-K,0]}(z)e^{\sqrt{2}z}
,
r_{t}=(x_{t}+\frac{3}{2\sqrt{2}}\log(t))t^{-1/2}
,
h_{t}=t^{-1/2}
, one obtain that

\frac{N(t,x_{t})-N(t,x_{t}-K)}{f(t,x_{t})}\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow\infty]{\mathbb{P}}Z_{\infty}\int_{0}^{K}\sqrt{2}e^{-\sqrt{2}z}dz=\left(1-e^{-\sqrt{2}K}\right)Z_{\infty}.

(17)

The convergence in (
17
) is also a direct consequence of Theorem
1.1
. Thus the results are consistent for the regime
x_{t}\in[\log(t)^{3+\epsilon},\sqrt{t}]
. Note that Theorem
1.1
extends their result by considering all the particles to the right of
m_{t}-x
(i.e.
K
in (
17
) could depend on
t
) and extending the interval of
x_{t}
both near the tip for which their proof fails, and above
\sqrt{t}
for which the Gaussian term
e^{-x^{2}/t}
in
f(t,x)
is non-negligible. One can also obtain a functional result from our work since we prove uniform convergence of the density of particles.

Our study is also related to several other results in the literature. In
[
19
]
, Pain studies the convergence of the Gibbs measure. One could wonder whether the convergence in probability in
[
19
, Theorem 1.1]
can be strengthened to an almost sure one. Despite the specific weighting of particles in the Gibbs measure, Corollary 1.3 in
[
19
]
establishes that the particles supporting the Gibbs measure with parameter
1+x/t
are located around
m_{t}-x
. Therefore, for the regime (i) in
[
19
, Theorem 1.1]
, reinforcing the convergence is not possible due to the mechanism exhibited in Theorem
1.3
. Moreover,
[
19
, Theorem 1.1, (iv)]
could help relax the condition on
R
in Proposition
2.3
, potentially paving the way to proving, for
x\gg\sqrt{t}
, the almost sure convergence of
N(t,x)
, and establishing a connection between
N(t,x)
and
Z_{(t/x)^{2}}
.

The regime
x_{t}\asymp t
(the number of particles at a linear distance from the tip) has been studied by Biggins in
[
6
]
for the branching random walk and by Glenz, Kistler, and Schmidt in
[
11
]
for the branching Brownian motion. It is worth noting that our exact normalization
f(t,x)\propto xe^{\frac{m_{t}}{t}x-\frac{x^{2}}{2t}}
is algebraically consistent with these results. Evaluating particles at a distance
ct
from the leading order tip
\sqrt{2}t
corresponds to a distance
x=ct-\frac{3}{2\sqrt{2}}\log t
relative to
m_{t}
. Substituting this into
f(t,x)
, the cross-terms in
c\log t
cancel, recovering the classical scaling factor
t^{-1/2}\exp\left((\sqrt{2}c-c^{2}/2)t\right)
of the linear regime. However, while this deterministic normalization extrapolates to the linear regime, the limiting random variable fundamentally differs: in the bulk, the limit is driven by the additive martingale, whereas for any sublinear distance
x=o(t)
, the fluctuations are governed by the derivative martingale
Z_{\infty}
.

Finally, we note that in the physics literature, the density of particles at a given distance of
m_{t}
has been studied by Brunet and Derrida in the context of BBM with selection
[
9
]
, and by Munier for standard BBM
[
16
]
.

1.4
A conjecture

Theorem
1.3
establishes that, for
x_{t}\leq t^{1/3}
, the renormalised number of particles
N(t,x_{t})/f(t,x_{t})
does
not
converge almost surely. This stems from potential fluctuations at the front around times
t-O(1)x_{t}^{2}
.

By leveraging the techniques developed in this paper and using the barrier
\beta(s)=\sqrt{2}s
for
s\geq r
, one can strengthen the convergence in Proposition
2.6
to an almost sure convergence via a Borel–Cantelli argument. Specifically, in the regime
x_{t}\gg\sqrt{t}
and
R_{t}\gg\log(t)(t/x)^{2}
, we obtain:

\frac{\#\left\{(u,v)\in\mathcal{N}(t,x_{t})^{2}:Q_{t}(u,v)\geq R_{t}\right\}}{f(t,x_{t})^{2}}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}0\quad\text{a.s.}

(18)

Crucially, in Proposition
2.3
, we require
R_{t}=o(\sqrt{t})
and
R_{t}=o(t/x_{t})
, while Proposition
2.6
only requires
R_{t}\to\infty
, allowing these two results to be applied jointly. However, the almost sure convergence in (
18
) is currently proved only for
R_{t}\gg\log(t)(t/x_{t})^{2}
. Therefore, to extend Theorem
1.1
to an almost sure statement in the regime
x_{t}\gg\sqrt{t}
, one must either broaden the admissible range for
R_{t}
in (
18
) or relax the conditions on
R_{t}
in Proposition
2.3
. This could be achieved by showing that a stronger barrier only removes particles that contribute negligibly to
N(t,x_{t})
almost surely.

We conjecture that the convergence in Theorem
1.1
indeed holds almost surely for
x_{t}\gg\sqrt{t}
.

Conjecture 1.6
.

Let
x_{t}
be such that, as
t\to\infty
,
x_{t}\geq\sqrt{t}\log(t)^{1+\epsilon}
and
x_{t}=o(t)
. Then,

\frac{N(t,x_{t})}{f(t,x_{t})}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}Z_{\infty}\quad\text{a.s.}

(19)

2
Proof of the main results

In this section, we prove the theorems stated in Subsection
1.2
. We begin by establishing Theorem
1.1
, proceeding via several intermediate results concerning the path localisation and genealogy of the particles. The proofs of these intermediate results are deferred to Section
4
. We then turn to the proof of Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
1.3
.

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we essentially follow the approach pioneered in
[
4
]
: the key steps are first to show that most of the particles that contribute to
N(t,x)
have a well-localised path. An extra technicality in this first step is to prove that, since
x\to\infty
, the particles contributing to
N(t,x)
are actually near
m_{t}-x+O(1)
. We then use a concentration argument to show that the number of such particles is close to its conditional expectation given the initial behaviour of the branching Brownian motion. Note that this is precisely the strategy employed in
[
11
]
. However, as usual, adapting it to the critical case involves some subtleties.

Let us first introduce some notation related to barrier events. For
a<b
, denote

\mathcal{N}(t,[a,b])=\left\{u\in\mathcal{N}_{t}:m_{t}-X_{u}(t)\in[a,b]\right\}.

For an interval
I\subset[0,t]
and a function
\beta\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
, denote by
\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(t,I)
the set of particles which remain under the curve
s\mapsto\beta(s)
during the time interval
I
,

\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(t,I)\coloneqq\left\{u\in\mathcal{N}_{t}:X_{u}(s)\leq\beta(s)\;\forall s\in I\right\}.

We further define

\mathcal{N}^{\beta}(t,I,x)\coloneqq\mathcal{N}(t,x)\cap\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(t,I),\quad\mathcal{N}^{\beta}(t,I,[a,b])\coloneqq\mathcal{N}(t,[a,b])\cap\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(t,I),

the set of particles above
m_{t}-x
(in
m_{t}-[a,b]
) at time
t
which remain under
\beta
during
I
. Similarly,

\overline{\mathcal{N}}^{\beta}(t,I,x)\coloneqq\mathcal{N}(t,x)\setminus\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(t,I),\quad\overline{\mathcal{N}}^{\beta}(t,I,[a,b])\coloneqq\mathcal{N}(t,[a,b])\setminus\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(t,I),

denote the sets of particles above
m_{t}-x
(in
m_{t}-[a,b]
) at time
t
which cross
\beta
during
I
. For each of the sets we have defined, we denote its cardinality by the corresponding capital letter; for example,
N^{\beta}(t,I,x)\coloneqq\#\mathcal{N}^{\beta}(t,I,x)
. Finally, define the family of functions
\beta_{t}(s)\coloneqq s\frac{m_{t}}{t}
and
\Gamma_{t,\alpha}(s)\coloneqq(s\wedge(t-s))^{\alpha}
for

.

First, we state that for large

, the normalised quantity

becomes negligible in the limit as

Proposition 2.1
.

For any
\delta>0
,

\lim_{K\to\infty}\lim_{x\to\infty}\sup_{t\geq x}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{N(t,x-K)}{f(t,x)}>\delta\right)=0.

(20)

The following proposition allows us to only consider particles whose trajectories remain below a prescribed barrier curve.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\alpha\in[0,1/2)
,
\lambda>\frac{1}{2}
,
\rho_{t,r}(s)=\beta_{t}(s)-\text{1}_{s\in[r,t-r]}\Gamma_{t,\alpha}(s)
. For any
\delta>0
,

\lim_{r\to\infty}\sup_{t,x:t>3r,r^{\lambda}\leq x\leq t}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\overline{N}^{\rho_{t,r}}(t,[r,t],x)}{f(t,x)}>\delta\right)=0.

(21)

Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
are proven in Subsection
4.1

The following proposition establishes the almost sure convergence of the normalised conditional expectation for localised particles to

, under asymptotic conditions on suitably scaled parameters.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
x_{t},K,r,R
be such that, as
t\to\infty
,
x_{t}\to\infty
with
x_{t}=o(t)
,
K\to\infty
,
r\to\infty
,
R\to\infty
with
R=o(\sqrt{t})
,
R=o(t/x_{t})
, and
R\geq r
. Then the following holds:

\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\beta_{t}}(t,[r,t],[x_{t}-K,x_{t}])\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]=f(t,x_{t})Z_{R}(1+o_{t}(1))\quad\text{a.s,}

(22)

where

almost surely as
t\to\infty
.

The proof of Proposition
2.3
is given in Subsection
4.2
.

We now state results to quantify the difference between the normalised conditional expectation and the actual number of localised particles. The following lemma bounds the mean squared difference between the number of localised particles and its conditional expectation at time
R
, in terms of the number of pairs of particles that branch after time

Lemma 2.4
.

For parameters
t\geq 0
,
R\in[0,t]
,

,

and a function

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}&\left[\left(N^{\beta}(t,I,X)-\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\beta}(t,I,X)\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]\right)^{2}\right]\\
&\qquad\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\#\left\{(u,v)\in\mathcal{N}^{\beta}(t,I,X)^{2}:Q_{t}(u,v)\geq R\right\}\right].\end{split}

(23)

Finally, the expected number of
\beta_{t}
-localised particle pairs branching after time

is asymptotically negligible relative to
f(t,x)^{2}
:

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
x_{t},K,r,R_{t}
be such that, as

,
x_{t}\to\infty
with
x_{t}=o(t)
,
R_{t}\to\infty
,
2r\leq R_{t}
,
3r\leq t
and

. Then the following holds:

\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\#\left\{(u,v)\in\mathcal{N}^{\beta_{t}}(t,[r,t],[x_{t}-K,x_{t}])^{2}:Q_{t}(u,v)\geq R_{t}\right\}\right]}{f(t,x_{t})^{2}}\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow\infty]{}0.

(24)

The proofs of Lemma
2.4
and Proposition
2.5
are given in Subsection

.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
I=[r,t]
and
\beta_{t}(s)=s\frac{m_{t}}{t}
. For any

, we have the decomposition:

\begin{split}N(t,x)&=N(t,x-K)+\overline{N}^{\beta_{t}}(t,I,[x-K,x])\\
&\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\beta_{t}}\left(t,I,[x-K,x]\right)\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]\\
&\quad+\left(N^{\beta_{t}}(t,I,[x-K,x])-\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\beta_{t}}(t,I,[x-K,x])\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]\right).\end{split}

Therefore,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}&\left(\left|\frac{N(t,x)}{f(t,x)}-Z_{\infty}\right|>\delta\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{N(t,x-K)}{f(t,x)}\right|>\frac{\delta}{4}\right)\\
&\qquad+\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\overline{N}^{\beta_{t}}(t,I,[x-K,x])}{f(t,x)}\right|>\frac{\delta}{4}\right)\\
&\qquad+\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\beta_{t}}(t,I,[x-K,x])\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]}{f(t,x)}-Z_{\infty}\right|>\frac{\delta}{4}\right)\\
&\qquad+\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\frac{N^{\beta_{t}}(t,I,[x-K,x])-\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\beta_{t}}(t,I,[x-K,x])\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]}{f(t,x)}\right)^{2}>\left(\frac{\delta}{4}\right)^{2}\right).\end{split}

(26)

We aim to demonstrate that the LHS of (
26
) converges to zero as
t\to\infty
for
x=x_{t}
satisfying
x_{t}\rightarrow\infty
and
x_{t}=o(t)
. Since the LHS is independent of

, we choose those quantities as functions of
t
diverging to infinity with

in the most convenient way:

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=R_{t}\text{ such that }R=o(\sqrt{t})\text{ and }R=o(t/x_{t}),

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=r_{t}\text{ such that }3r_{t}\leq t,\,r^{\lambda}\leq x_{t}\text{ for a }\lambda>\frac{1}{2}\text{ and }r_{t}=o(R_{t}),

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=K_{t}\text{ such that }K_{t}=o(r_{t}).

We now prove that each term on the RHS of (

) converges to zero as

.
The first term on the RHS converges to 0 by Proposition
2.1
. Next, observe that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\overline{N}^{\beta_{t}}(t,I,[x-K,x])}{f(t,x)}\right|>\frac{\delta}{4}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\overline{N}^{\beta_{t}}(t,I,x)}{f(t,x)}\right|>\frac{\delta}{4}\right),

(27)

thus the second term on the RHS converges to 0 by Proposition
2.2
.
The third term on the RHS converges to 0 by Proposition
2.3
.
Finally, by Markov’s inequality and Lemma

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}&\left(\left(\frac{N^{\beta_{t}}(t,I,[x-K,x])-\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\beta_{t}}(t,I,[x-K,x])\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]}{f(t,x)}\right)^{2}>\left(\frac{\delta}{4}\right)^{2}\right)\\
&\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(N^{\beta_{t}}(t,I,[x-K,x])-\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\beta_{t}}(t,I,[x-K,x])\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]\right)^{2}\right]}{(4^{-1}\delta f(t,x))^{2}}\\

(28)

Thus, by Proposition

, the last term on the RHS converges to 0.

Having established a bound on the number of pairs of localized particles that do not split early, we derive the following intermediate result: two particles chosen uniformly at random from

must have branched early. This translates to the tightness in probability of the random measures

Proposition 2.6

Let

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\mu_{t}([R_{t},\infty))\left(\frac{N(t,x_{t})}{f(t,x_{t})}\right)^{2}>\delta\right)=0.

\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{P}\left(\mu_{t}([R,\infty))>\epsilon\right)=0.

To convert this tightness into the weak convergence stated in Theorem
1.4
, we rely on the following general criterion for random probability measures.

Lemma 2.7

(Characterization of Weak Convergence)
.

Let
(\nu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a family of random probability measures on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Suppose the following two conditions are satisfied:

(i)

For every
r\geq 0
, there exists a random variable
F_{r}
such that

\nu_{t}([r,\infty))\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}F_{r}.

(37)

(ii)

The family is tight in probability; that is, for every
\epsilon>0
,

\lim_{M\to\infty}\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\nu_{t}([M,\infty))>\epsilon\right)=0.

(38)

Then, there exists a random probability measure
\nu
such that
\nu_{t}
converges weakly in probability to
\nu
as
t\to\infty
. The limit
\nu
is uniquely characterized by the property that, almost surely,
\nu([r,\infty))=F_{r}
for every continuity point
r
of the mapping
x\mapsto\nu([x,\infty))
.

The proof of Lemma
2.7
relies on a standard diagonal extraction argument adapted from the proof of Helly’s Selection Theorem (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Theorem 25.9]
).

With this lemma and Proposition
2.6
in hand, the proof of Theorem
1.4
follows naturally.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

By Proposition
2.6
, the family of random measures
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is tight in probability, which immediately satisfies condition (ii) of Lemma
2.7
. To establish weak convergence, it remains to verify condition (i).

Fix
r\geq 0
. The event
\{Q_{t}(u,v)\geq r\}
implies that
u
and

descend from a common ancestor
w\in\mathcal{N}_{r}
alive at time
r
. Partitioning the pairs in
\mathcal{N}(t,x_{t})
by their ancestor at time
r
yields

\mu_{t}([r,\infty))=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{N}_{r}}\left(\frac{N_{w}(t,x_{t})}{N(t,x_{t})}\right)^{2},

(39)

where
N_{w}(t,x_{t})\coloneqq\#\{v\in\mathcal{N}(t,x_{t}):w\preceq v\}
.

By Theorem
1.1

\frac{N(t,x_{t})}{f(t,x_{t})}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}Z_{\infty}.

(40)

To evaluate
N_{w}(t,x_{t})
, we apply Theorem
1.1
conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{r}
to the shifted subtree rooted at
w
. The target spatial boundary
m_{t}-x_{t}
for these descendants, when viewed from the particle’s position
X_{w}(r)
and over the remaining time
t-r
, corresponds to a relative target of
m_{t-r}-(x_{t}+X_{w}(r)-\sqrt{2}r)+o(1)

\frac{N_{w}(t,x_{t})}{f\big(t-r,x_{t}+X_{w}(r)-\sqrt{2}r\big)}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}Z_{\infty}^{(w)}.

(41)

Taking the ratio, the polynomial and Gaussian terms in

\frac{N_{w}(t,x_{t})}{N(t,x_{t})}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}\frac{Z_{\infty}^{(w)}}{Z_{\infty}}e^{\sqrt{2}X_{w}(r)-2r}.

(42)

Since the population
\mathcal{N}_{r}
is almost surely finite, the finite sum converges in probability:

\mu_{t}([r,\infty))\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}F_{r}\coloneqq\frac{1}{Z_{\infty}^{2}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{N}_{r}}\left(e^{\sqrt{2}X_{w}(r)-2r}Z_{\infty}^{(w)}\right)^{2}.

(43)

This establishes condition (i) of Lemma
2.7
.

To conclude the proof, observe that the process
r\mapsto F_{r}
is piecewise constant, with jumps occurring exclusively at the branching times of the BBM. Since the branching times are continuous random variables, the probability of a jump occurring exactly at a fixed deterministic

is zero. Therefore,
\mathbb{P}(\mu_{\infty}(\{r\})>0)=0
for all
r\geq 0
, implying that every
r
is almost surely a continuity point of the limit measure
\mu_{\infty}
. By Lemma
2.7
,
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
converges weakly in probability to
\mu_{\infty}
, uniquely characterized by
\mu_{\infty}([r,\infty))=F_{r}
almost surely.
∎

This section collects several useful results, primarily from Bramson
[
8

Define the probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{0,x}^{t,y}
under which
(B_{s})_{s\in[0,t]}
is a Brownian bridge from
x
at time
0
to
y
at time
t
. For a function
l:[s_{1},s_{2}]\to\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
B_{l}
(or
B_{l}[s_{1},s_{2}]
in cases of ambiguity) the event that the path lies strictly above
l
on the interval
[s_{1},s_{2}]
. Similarly,
B^{l}
denotes the event that the path lies strictly below
l
.

The following is a well-known expression for the probability that a Brownian bridge stays non-negative.

Lemma 3.1
.

\mathbb{P}_{0,x}^{t,y}(B_{s}\geq 0,s\in[0,t])=1-e^{-\frac{2xy}{t}}.

(53)

The following result provides an upper bound on the probability that a Brownian bridge stays below a linear function.

Lemma 3.2

(Lemma 3.4,
[
3
]
)
.

Let
Z_{1},Z_{2}\geq 0
and
r_{1},r_{2}\geq 0
. Then, for
t>r_{1}+r_{2}
,

\mathbb{P}_{0,0}^{t,0}\left(B_{s}\leq\left(1-\frac{s}{t}\right)Z_{1}+\frac{s}{t}Z_{2},\quad\forall s\in[r_{1},t-r_{2}]\right)\leq\frac{2}{t-r_{1}-r_{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{2}\left(Z(r_{i})+\sqrt{r_{i}}\right),

where

Z(r_{1})\coloneqq\left(1-\frac{r_{1}}{t}\right)Z_{1}+\frac{r_{1}}{t}Z_{2},\quad Z(r_{2})\coloneqq\frac{r_{2}}{t}Z_{1}+\left(1-\frac{r_{2}}{t}\right)Z_{2}.

The following identity, derived from the reflection principle, gives the probability that a Brownian bridge remains non-negative over an interval.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
y>0
and
0<r<\gamma
,

\mathbb{P}(\forall s\in[r,\gamma]\,,\,B_{s}\geq 0\mid B_{\gamma}=y)=1-2\mathbb{P}(B_{r}\leq 0\mid B_{\gamma}=y).

(54)

Proof.

We prove that

\mathbb{P}(\exists s\in[r,\gamma]\,,\,B_{s}<0\mid B_{\gamma}=y)=2\mathbb{P}_{0,0}^{\gamma,y}(B_{r}<0).

(55)

Indeed,

\mathbb{P}_{0,0}^{\gamma,y}(\exists s\in[r,\gamma]\,,\,B_{s}<0)=\mathbb{P}_{0,0}^{\gamma,y}(B_{r}<0)+\mathbb{P}_{0,0}^{\gamma,y}(B_{r}>0\,\cap\,\exists s\in[r,\gamma]\,,\,B_{s}<0).

(56)

By the reflection principle,

\mathbb{P}_{0,0}^{\gamma,y}(B_{r}>0\,\cap\,\exists s\in[r,\gamma]\,,\,B_{s}<0)=\mathbb{P}_{0,0}^{\gamma,-y}(B_{r}>0)=\mathbb{P}_{0,0}^{\gamma,y}(B_{r}<0).

(57)

Hence the result.
∎

We now state a useful monotonicity result.

Lemma 3.4

(Lemma 2.6,
[
8
]
)
.

Assume that
l_{1}(s)
,
l_{2}(s)
, and
\wedge(s)
satisfy
l_{1}(s)\leq l_{2}(s)\leq\wedge(s)
for
s\in[0,t]
, and that
\mathbb{P}(B_{l_{2}}[0,t])>0
. Then,

\mathbb{P}_{0,0}^{t,0}(B^{\wedge}\mid B_{l_{1}})\geq\mathbb{P}_{0,0}^{t,0}(B^{\wedge}\mid B_{l_{2}}),

(58)

and

\mathbb{P}_{0,0}^{t,0}(B_{\wedge}\mid B_{l_{1}})\leq\mathbb{P}_{0,0}^{t,0}(B_{\wedge}\mid B_{l_{2}}).

(59)

The following result establishes that if a Brownian bridge remains above
-\Gamma_{t,\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
on the interval
[r,t-r]
, then it is remains above
\Gamma_{t,\alpha}
on the same interval.

Lemma 3.5

(Proposition 6.1,
[
8
]
)
.

Let
C>0
and for all
t
,
L_{t}:[0,t]\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a family of functions.

Let any
\alpha\in]0,1/2[
, if there exists
r_{0}
such that
L_{t}(s)-C\Gamma_{t,\alpha}(s))\leq 0
for
s\in[r_{0},t-r_{0}]
for all

, then,

\lim_{r\to\infty}\sup_{t:t\geq 3r}\frac{\mathbb{P}_{0,0}^{t,0}(\forall s\in[r,t-r]:B_{s}>L_{t}(s)+C\Gamma_{t,\alpha}(s))}{\mathbb{P}_{0,0}^{t,0}(\forall s\in[r,t-r]:B_{s}>L_{t}(s)-C\Gamma_{t,\alpha}(s))}=0.

(60)

The following result, which is proved via a coupling argument, shows that the ratio considered in Lemma
3.5
is increasing with respect to the endpoint.

Lemma 3.6

(Proposition 6.3,
[
8
]
)
.

Assume that the functions
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
are upper semi-continuous except at at most finitely many points, and that
l_{1}(s)\leq l_{2}(s)
for
s\in[0,t]
. Then, provided that the denominator is nonzero,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}_{0,0}^{t,y}(\forall s\in[r,t-r]:B_{s}>l_{2}(s))}{\mathbb{P}_{0,0}^{t,y}(\forall s\in[r,t-r]:B_{s}>l_{1}(s))}

\displaystyle\text{ is increasing in $y$.}

(61)

The following are standard results (see, e.g.,
[
12
]
) in the theory of branching processes, which enable one to compute the first and second moments by reducing the problem to a single-particle or a two-particle system, respectively.

Lemma 3.7

(Many-to-one Lemma)
.

For any
t\geq 0
and any measurable function
f:C([0,t])\rightarrow\mathbb{R^{+}}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{t}}f(X_{u}(s),s\in\left[0,t\right])\right]=e^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[f(B_{s},s\in\left[0,t\right])\right],

where
(B_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{R}}
is a standard Brownian motion.

Lemma 3.8

(Many-to-two Lemma)
.

For any
t\geq 0
and any measurable function
f:C([0,t])\rightarrow\mathbb{R^{+}}
,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{t}}f(X_{u}(r),r\in\left[0,t\right])\right)^{2}\right]=e^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[f(B_{r},r\in\left[0,t\right])^{2}\right]\\
&\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}2e^{2t-s}\mathbb{E}\left[f(B^{(1,s)}_{r},r\in\left[0,t\right])f(B^{(2,s)}_{r},r\in\left[0,t\right])\right]\mathrm{d}s,\end{split}

(62)

where for
s\in\left[0,t\right]
,
(B^{(1,s)}_{r})_{r\in\mathbb{R}}
and
(B^{(2,s)}_{r})_{r\in\mathbb{R}}
follow a common Brownian motion trajectory until time
s
, then split and evolve as independent Brownian motions afterwards.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Proposition

\frac{\#\{u\in\mathcal{N}(t,x):X_{u}(s)>s\frac{m_{t}}{t}-\Gamma_{t,\alpha}(s),\text{ for some }s\in[r,t-r]\}}{f(t,x)}\xrightarrow[r\rightarrow\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0

r_{0}

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}&\left[\text{1}_{E_{K,t}}\#\mathcal{N}^{\beta_{t}+\Gamma_{t,\alpha}}(t,I_{r},x)\setminus{\mathcal{H}^{\beta_{t}-\Gamma_{t,\alpha}}(t,I_{R})}\right]\\

3.2

3.6

3.5

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0,0}^{t,z}

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\beta_{t}}(t,[r,t],[x-K,x])\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta_{t}}(t,[r,t])}\text{1}_{X_{u}(t)\geq m_{t}-x}\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{p\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta_{t}}(R,[r,R])}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{N}^{p}_{t}}\text{1}_{A_{q}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta_{t}}(R,[r,R])}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{q\in\mathcal{N}^{p}_{t}}\text{1}_{A_{q}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]\\
&=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta_{t}}(R,[r,R])}e^{t-R}\mathbb{P}_{R,X_{p}(R)}\left(m_{t}-B_{t}\in[x-K,x]\cap\forall s\in[R,t]:B_{s}\leq s\frac{m_{t}}{t}\right).\\
\end{split}

(97)

For each
p\in\mathcal{N}_{R}
, denote

. We have that,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}_{R,X_{p}(R)}&\left(m_{t}-x\leq B_{t}\leq m_{t}-x+K\cap\forall s\in[R,t]:B_{s}\leq s\frac{m_{t}}{t}\right)\\

(98)

On one hand, as

uniformly in

\mathbb{P}_{0,y_{p}}^{t-R,z}(\in[0,t-R]B_{s}\geq 0)=1-e^{-2\frac{zy_{p}}{t-R}}=2\frac{zy_{p}}{t-R}(1+o_{t}(1)),

(99)

where, as

o_{t}(1)

p
and

, almost surely.

On the other hand,

\begin{split}&\sqrt{2\pi(t-R)}\mathbb{P}_{R,X_{p}(R)}(m_{t}-B_{t}\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z)\\

where, as
t\to\infty

p
and

R=o(\frac{t}{x})

98

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}_{R,X_{p}(R)}&\left(m_{t}-x\leq B_{t}\leq m_{t}-x+K\cap\forall s\in[R,t]:B_{s}\leq s\frac{m_{t}}{t}\right)\\

(100)

for some

converging to
0
uniformly in
p
almost surely.
The second equality comes from the fact that
R=o(t)
and that

.
Plugging this in (

o_{t}(1)
converging to
0
almost surely, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[N^{\beta_{t}}(t,[r,t],[x-K,x])\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]

(101)

\displaystyle=f(t,x)\,(1+o_{t}(1))\sum_{p\in\mathcal{N}_{R}}\text{1}_{\forall s\in[r,R]:X_{p}(s)\leq\frac{m_{t}}{t}s}\,y_{p}\,e^{-\sqrt{2}y_{p}}

\displaystyle=f(t,x)\,(1+o_{t}(1))\sum_{p\in\mathcal{N}_{R}}\text{1}_{\forall s\in[r,R]:X_{p}(s)\leq\frac{m_{t}}{t}s}\,y_{p}\,e^{-\sqrt{2}(\sqrt{2}R-X_{p}(R))}

\displaystyle\text{\scriptsize($R\log t/t\to 0$)}

\displaystyle=f(t,x)\,(1+o_{t}(1))\sum_{p\in\mathcal{N}_{R}}y_{p}\,e^{-\sqrt{2}(\sqrt{2}R-X_{p}(R))}

\displaystyle=f(t,x)\,Z_{R}\,(1+o_{t}(1))

\displaystyle\text{\scriptsize(eventually a.s.)}

To establish the penultimate equality, recall that

almost surely. Since

\frac{3R\log t}{2\sqrt{2}t}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{N}_{R}}e^{-\sqrt{2}\left(\sqrt{2}R-X_{p}(R)\right)}=\frac{3R\log t}{2\sqrt{2}t}W_{R}\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow\infty]{}0\quad\text{ a.s}.

(102)

∎

4.3

We first prove Lemma
2.4

Proof of Lemma
2.4
.

Take
0\leq R\leq t,X\subset\mathbb{R},I\subset[0,t]
and a function
\beta\colon I\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

For
u\in\mathcal{N}_{R}
, we denote
{\mathcal{N}}^{u}_{t}=\{v\in\mathcal{N}_{t}:u\preceq v\}
the set of descendant of
u
at time
t
. Further define
A_{v}\coloneqq\{m_{t}-X_{v}(t)\in X,\forall s\in[R,t]\cap I\,:X_{v}(s)\leq\beta(s)\}
for
v\in\mathcal{N}_{t}
.

We have that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}&\left[\left(N^{\beta}(t,I,X)-\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\beta}(t,I,X)\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]\right)^{2}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\beta}(t,I,X)^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\beta}(t,I,X)\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]^{2}\right]\\
&\qquad-2\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\beta}(t,I,X)\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\beta}(t,I,X)\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\beta}(t,I,X)^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\beta}(t,I,X)\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]^{2}\right].\end{split}

(103)

Moreover,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}&\left[N^{\beta}(t,I,X)^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\beta}(t,I,X)\mid\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]^{2}\\
&=\sum_{u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(R,I\cap[0,R])}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{v\in\mathcal{N}^{u}_{t}}\text{1}_{A_{v}}\sum_{v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}^{u^{\prime}}_{t}}\text{1}_{A_{v^{\prime}}}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{v\in\mathcal{N}^{u}_{t}}\text{1}_{A_{v}}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}^{u^{\prime}}_{t}}\text{1}_{A_{v^{\prime}}}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{R}\right].\end{split}

(104)

In this latter expression, for
u\neq u^{\prime}
, since the particles branched before time
R
, their path are conditionally independent on
\mathcal{F}_{R}
, the terms in the sum cancel each other. Thus (
104
) is equal to

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}&\left[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(R,I\cap[0,R])}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{v\in\mathcal{N}^{u}_{t}}\text{1}_{A_{v}}\right)^{2}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{v\in\mathcal{N}^{u}_{t}}\text{1}_{A_{v}}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{R}\right]^{2}\right]\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(R,I\cap[0,R])}\sum_{v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}^{u}_{t}}\text{1}_{A_{v}}\text{1}_{A_{v^{\prime}}}\right].\end{split}

(105)

∎

\begin{split}&CK^{2}x^{2}e^{2\frac{m_{t}}{t}x}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{t}}\sqrt{r}\int_{R}^{t-\frac{x}{10}}\frac{e^{3\log(t)-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\gamma}{t}\log(t)}}{\gamma\sqrt{\gamma-r}(t-\gamma)^{3}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}y^{3}e^{-\left(\frac{m_{t}}{t}-2\frac{x}{t}\right)y}\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\gamma.\end{split}

(119)

Since
\left(\tfrac{m_{t}}{t}-2\tfrac{x}{t}\right)\geq 1
for
x\leq t/10
and
t\geq 1
, there exists
B>0
such that:

\int_{0}^{+\infty}y^{3}e^{-\left(\tfrac{m_{t}}{t}-2\tfrac{x}{t}\right)y}\mathrm{d}y\leq B.

Moreover, under the condition
R\geq 2r
(implying
\gamma\geq 2r
), we have
\gamma\leq 2(\gamma-r)
. This yields:

\int\limits_{R}^{t-\frac{x}{10}}\frac{e^{3\log t-\tfrac{3\gamma}{2t}\log t}}{\gamma\sqrt{\gamma-r}(t-\gamma)^{3}}\mathrm{d}\gamma\leq C\int\limits_{R}^{t-\frac{x}{10}}\frac{e^{3\log t-\tfrac{3\gamma}{2t}\log t}}{\gamma^{3/2}(t-\gamma)^{3}}\mathrm{d}\gamma.

There exists
x_{0}>0
such that for all
t\geq x_{0}
and
\gamma\in[R,t-x_{0}/10]
,

\log t\leq\tfrac{\gamma}{t}\log t+\log(t-\gamma).

Therefore, for
x
large enough,

\begin{split}\int_{R}^{t-\frac{x}{10}}\frac{e^{3\log(t)-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\gamma}{t}\log(t)}}{\gamma^{\frac{3}{2}}(t-\gamma)^{3}}\mathrm{d}\gamma\leq\int_{R}^{t-\frac{x}{10}}\frac{e^{\frac{3}{2}\log(t)}}{\gamma^{\frac{3}{2}}(t-\gamma)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\mathrm{d}\gamma,\end{split}

(120)

which tends to 0 with
R\to\infty
and
x\to\infty
.
Thus (

), renormalised by

0
.

Secondly, for
(y,\gamma)\in\mathcal{A}

x

\begin{split}&CK^{2}x^{2}e^{2\frac{m_{t}}{t}x}\sqrt{r}\int_{t-\frac{x}{10}}^{t-1}e^{\frac{3}{2}(1-\frac{\gamma}{t})\log(t)}\int_{\frac{4x}{5}}^{+\infty}y^{3}e^{-\frac{m_{t}}{t}y}\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\gamma\\
&\leq CK^{2}x^{2}e^{\frac{7}{5}\frac{m_{t}}{t}x}\sqrt{r}\int_{t-\frac{x}{10}}^{t-1}e^{\frac{3}{2}(1-\frac{\gamma}{t})\log(t)}\mathrm{d}\gamma\\
&\leq Cx^{3}e^{\frac{7}{5}\frac{m_{t}}{t}x}\sqrt{r}e^{\frac{3}{20}\frac{x\log(t)}{t}}.\end{split}

(121)

When this bound is renormalised by

Since

, the terms
\frac{x}{t}

\frac{3\log t}{20t}
both converge to
0
. Thus, the exponent is dominated by the strictly negative linear term
-\frac{3}{5}\frac{m_{t}}{t}x\sim-\frac{3\sqrt{2}}{5}x
. Therefore, the renormalised bound tends to
0
, and when
\frac{m_{t}}{t}(t-\gamma)+y-x>0
, (
107
) yields a negligible contribution.
