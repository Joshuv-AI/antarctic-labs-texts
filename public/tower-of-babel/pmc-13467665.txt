Experiences of Healthcare Support and Access Among Women Who Use Performance and Image‐Enhancing Drugs
Authors: Hannah C Schuurs, Zoe Walter, Leanne Hides, Mair Underwood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionWomen who use performance and image‐enhancing drugs (PIED) face distinct health risks, yet little is known about how they engage with healthcare or seek support. This study explored how women who use PIEDs navigate healthcare, with a focus on engagement, disclosure and stigma.MethodAn interpretative phenomenological analysis was undertaken, involving semi‐structured interviews with nine women with current/historical experience of PIED use, and engagement in regular weight training. Interviews explored health monitoring, support‐seeking, and experiences with healthcare services and providers in the context of their PIED use.ResultsParticipants described healthcare experiences ranging from respectful, supportive care to dismissal, judgement and clinical uncertainty. Three interrelated themes were identified: (i) participants' proactive engagement in health monitoring and healthcare to manage risks associated with PIED use; (ii) systemic barriers and knowledge gaps within healthcare, which made appropriate care difficult to access and navigate and, at times, shifted the burden of expertise onto participants within clinical encounters; and (iii) the importance of non‐judgemental care, with stigma and fear of judgement shaping disclosure, trust and help‐seeking.Discussion and ConclusionsThis study provides an in‐depth qualitative account of how women who use PIEDs engage with and navigate healthcare. Despite strong health motivation and proactive risk management, participants often encountered structural and interpersonal barriers to care. Respectful, non‐judgemental interactions were central to positive healthcare engagement and sometimes more influential than clinical expertise alone. Findings highlight the need for improved professional education and gender‐sensitive, harm‐reduction‐oriented resources to support women who use PIEDs.Keywords: harm reduction, healthcare, performance and image‐enhancing drugs, stigma, women

Introduction

The expanding use of performance and image‐enhancing drugs (PIED), alongside increasing diversity in both substances themselves and individuals using them, poses increasing challenges for healthcare systems worldwide. PIEDs encompass a heterogeneous group of substances, used primarily off‐label, to enhance athletic performance (strength, endurance, muscle recovery) or alter body composition (increase muscle mass, decrease body fat). Their use is well‐documented among competitive bodybuilders and strength athletes, with lifetime prevalence rates as high as 81.1% in some samples [1]. Among the general population, prevalence is lower (0.9% lifetime use in Australia) but appears to be increasing [2, 3]. Women's PIED use has also been identified as an emerging concern, particularly in strength and physique‐sport contexts, although population‐level trend data specific to women remain limited and inconsistent [2, 4]. Prevalence estimates vary widely due to differences in substances examined (often limited to anabolic‐androgenic steroids [AAS]), sampling characteristics, measurement inconsistencies and reliance on self‐report data [5, 6]. These sampling discrepancies disproportionately affect women, who are frequently underrepresented in PIED research, or subsumed within male‐dominated samples, obscuring gender‐specific patterns of use, motivations and health risks [5, 6].

PIEDs Use, Health Risks and Healthcare Engagement

The health impacts of PIEDs vary widely and are largely dependent on the types of substances used, the protocols and dosages adopted, methods of administration and duration of use [1]. Certain practices, such as high doses of AAS and polysubstance use, have been associated with adverse effects including cardiovascular strain, organ damage, reproductive concerns and psychological disturbances [7, 8, 9]. However, the meaning of “health” in the context of PIED use can introduce complexity. PIED use may be positioned within competing health logics, where substances are understood both as sources of potential harm and as tools for bodily control, self‐improvement, injury management, performance, ageing or wellbeing [10, 11, 12, 13]. This complicates public health accounts that position PIED use as fundamentally antithetical to health and directs attention to the forms of embodied knowledge, self‐monitoring and harm reduction already present among consumers.

Although people who use PIEDs are often stereotyped as deviant or indifferent to health [8, 14], research (conducted largely with men) indicates that many actively seek health monitoring and medical support around their PIED use [15, 16, 17]. Yet healthcare engagement remains constrained by barriers including clinical knowledge gaps, stigma and mistrust [9, 10]. Recent Australian sociological literature provides an important foundation for understanding these barriers as socially and relationally produced, rather than solely individual. This work has challenged reductive accounts that frame PIED use primarily through disordered masculinity, irrational risk‐taking or appearance‐based pathology, demonstrating how PIED use may be embedded within broader practices of embodiment, self‐management and health optimisation [12, 13, 15, 18]. Specifically, qualitative research has shown that men who use PIEDs may seek more open, consistent and thorough engagement with health professionals, particularly general practitioners (GP), yet these encounters can become constrained when consumers' experiential knowledge is discounted or treated as suspect [13, 15].

Mistrust of healthcare providers' knowledge and advice around PIED use has been frequently reported, with some research attributing it to a disconnect between institutional knowledge and experiential expertise within PIED‐using communities [17, 19, 20]. This is reflected in healthcare providers' own reports of limited training, resources and confidence in addressing PIED‐related health concerns [21, 22]. This mistrust may be further shaped by experiences of stigma, which have consistently been cited as a barrier to healthcare engagement among people who use PIEDs [13, 23, 24]. Health professional discourse around PIED use has been shown to frequently categorise PIEDs as inherently dangerous and “unnatural” and those who use them as difficult, deceptive or illegitimate patients, limiting opportunities for knowledgeable, non‐stigmatising harm reduction [13]. This has also been demonstrated empirically, with one study suggesting that patients who use AAS are viewed more negatively by healthcare providers than both healthy patients and those using other illicit substances, such as cocaine [25]. Furthermore, anticipated stigma from health professionals and harm reduction services, such as needle‐syringe programs, has been described by individuals who use PIEDs as a key reason for lack of engagement, or reluctance to engage, with these services [17, 23, 24, 26]. Taken together, healthcare barriers in this context appear embedded in clinical encounters where PIED consumers' knowledge may be discounted, their credibility questioned and their participation in care constrained. Such dynamics can shape disclosure, support‐seeking and the possibility of collaborative care [9, 17].

Women's PIED Use, Healthcare Needs and Experiences

Women's PIED use carries a distinct set of risks and considerations that differ markedly from those experienced by men, making tailored healthcare especially important. Many PIEDs have not been clinically studied in women, leaving limited evidence to guide understanding of sex‐specific effects, dose responses, long‐term outcomes and appropriate clinical monitoring [6, 27, 28]. Due to hormonal profiles and sex‐specific physiological factors, women may be more sensitive to the androgenic and anabolic effects of certain PIEDs, resulting in lower thresholds for adverse outcomes [28]. In response, women commonly seek to minimise virilisation risk by using lower doses and selecting compounds perceived as less androgenic, based largely on information sourced from peer knowledge [2, 29]. However, these practices are not always supported by empirical evidence, increasing vulnerability to unintended harm [27, 30].

Documented risks and side‐effects, specific to women's PIED use, include virilisation (voice deepening, facial hair growth, clitoral growth), disruption of hormonal function and damage to vital organs [2, 30, 31]. Research has suggested that women may also have unique psychological experiences related to PIED use. These include mood disturbances [2, 32], body image concerns [29, 33], and heightened anxiety around both their PIED use and its associated side‐effects [30, 34, 35]. These concerns create distinctive healthcare considerations, as women may be required to disclose gendered, stigmatised or intimate bodily changes in clinical encounters where provider knowledge of women's PIED use is often limited.

Recent feminist, sociological work nevertheless cautions against treating women's PIED use solely through narratives of harm, vulnerability or virilisation. Fomite and colleagues argue that narrow framings of women's PIED use can obscure women's agency, knowledge and active attempts to manage risk [10, 27]. Fomite & Stanton's 2025 feminist analysis is especially relevant here, where it was suggested that women's PIED use may be tied to autonomy, self‐realisation, social participation and embodied transformation, while also being shaped by gender‐related stigma, discrimination and insufficient gender‐sensitive healthcare. The concept of epistemic injustice is central to their analysis, with women's knowledge about their bodies, substance use and side‐effect management shown to be marginalised within research and healthcare systems [27]. Women who use PIEDs also report experiencing gendered forms of stigma, often managing this through secrecy and concealment of their PIED use, which may directly and indirectly impede healthcare access [30, 33, 35]. These challenges are compounded by the limited evidence base surrounding women's PIED use, associated health risks and support needs. Although research with men provides an important foundation for understanding how stigma, mistrust and contested expertise shape healthcare engagement, women's experiences cannot be inferred from men's accounts alone. For women, disclosure may be further complicated by gendered norms surrounding femininity, health and acceptable substance use, alongside concerns about visible or feared virilising effects [30, 33, 34]. Given the established relationship between stigma and healthcare engagement, it is essential to understand how women who use PIEDs navigate healthcare systems and seek support.

The Current Study

Women's PIED use is shaped by intersecting health, social, and healthcare‐related challenges, including gender‐specific risks, stigma, barriers to care, and gaps in provider knowledge. These complexities require analytical approaches that move beyond individual‐level explanations. Drawing on socio‐ecological theory, which conceptualises health behaviours and experiences as shaped by interacting individual, interpersonal, and structural influences, this study situates women's healthcare experiences within broader social and healthcare contexts [36]. This framework is particularly suited to examining how PIED use, stigma, healthcare systems, and policy environments intersect to shape access to care and support.

Building on Australian sociological and feminist scholarship, this study examines healthcare as a context in which women's PIED use, bodily experiences, risk‐management practices and experiential knowledge may be recognised, misunderstood or delegitimised. Recent qualitative work has highlighted the importance of gender‐sensitive healthcare and identified stigma and epistemic injustice as factors that can shape women's healthcare experiences and access to support; however, there remains a need for focused, in‐depth accounts of how women make sense of healthcare engagement, disclosure, support‐seeking, and legitimacy in relation to their PIED use.

Accordingly, this study explores the lived healthcare experiences of women who use PIEDs, with particular attention to access to services, support, and experiences of stigma. By applying a socio‐ecological lens, the study examines how women experience, interpret, and navigate healthcare in the context of PIED use, thereby informing opportunities for intervention at individual, interpersonal, and systemic levels. A clearer understanding of these experiences is needed to improve healthcare responses and support meaningful change within systems that have historically overlooked women who use PIEDs.

Method

Interpretive phenomenological analysis (IPA) is a qualitative research method primarily concerned with lived accounts of meaning and interpretation within these experiences [37, 38]. Central to IPA is a double hermeneutic wherein participants interpret and make sense of their experiences while researchers, in turn, interpret participants' accounts of this process [38, 39]. The approach is idiographic, involving detailed, in‐depth analysis of individual cases before moving to analysing patterns across the dataset [40]. IPA is considered especially well‐suited to topics where personal meaning, subjectivity and context are central to understanding complex and multi‐dimensional phenomena [39, 41, 42, 43].

Given the relatively unexplored nature of the topic, as well as the complex interplay of individual, social and systemic factors involved, IPA's focus on personal meaning, subjectivity and contextualised interpretation of phenomena makes it particularly suitable for studying women's accounts of PIED use [39, 41]. This analytic orientation enabled an in‐depth exploration of how participants experienced and interpreted PIED use, healthcare encounters, disclosure decisions and support‐seeking within their broader sociocultural contexts.

Recruitment and Sampling

Purposeful sampling was used to recruit a sample of women with lived experience of PIED use, primarily through snowball sampling. Authors 1 and 3 invited existing contacts from previous work and research in relevant industries to participate in the study and/or circulate study information within their networks. Recruitment was also supported through targeted social media posts.

Participants who expressed interest in the study were contacted via email and sent a copy of the Study Information Sheet and Participant Consent Form. All participants who expressed interest, remained contactable and participated in at least one interview were included in the dataset. Eligibility was confirmed and verbal informed consent obtained at the beginning of each participant's first interview. Detailed eligibility criteria can be found in Appendix A (Table A1).

No geographic‐based inclusion/exclusion criteria were applied and recruitment was conducted through networks with international reach. Included participants were ultimately located in Australia, Canada and the United States at the time of their interviews. This international inclusion reflected both the geographic dispersion of women who use PIEDs and the practical challenges of recruiting a small, information‐rich sample from a relatively hidden population.

Consistent with IPA's emphasis on idiographic depth, recruitment aimed to generate a small, information‐rich sample rather than broad representativeness [39]. Sample adequacy was considered in relation to information power, including the specificity of the research aim, the relevance of participants' experiences, the quality of interview dialogue, and the depth of the analytic strategy [44]. Recruitment ceased when the research team judged, through consensus‐based discussion, that the dataset provided sufficient within‐case detail and cross‐case convergence to address the study aim. In line with IPA guidelines, a brief description of each participant (n = 9) is recorded (see Table 1, ‘Participants’). Ethics approval was obtained from the University of Queensland Human Research Ethics Committee (HE000662).

Data Collection: Semi‐Structured Interviews

Each participant completed a semi‐structured interview with the first author over 1–2 sessions of approximately 60 min duration via Zoom. This format was selected to support accessibility and accommodate the geographic diversity of participants. A second interview was offered when additional time was required or where participants wished to provide further reflection. Interviews were participant‐led, with the interviewer flexibly adapting questions to foreground participants' lived experience and emergent insights. Discussion focused on participants' personal histories and experiences with PIED use, alongside their broader reflections on sociocultural influences, public discourse and the meanings attached to women's PIED use. Interviews were audio‐recorded and conducted between 6 December 2022 and 12 August 2024.

Data Analysis

Participants' audio‐recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim using Microsoft 365 software and manually checked for accuracy. The first author assigned pseudonyms and removed any potentially identifying information prior to analysis. De‐identified transcripts were uploaded to NVivo 14 for coding and analysis. This process began with repeated close reading of each transcript, as per IPA guidelines [40]. Detailed notes were taken to capture descriptive observations, emerging interpretations, and emotional responses (for both the researcher and participant). Reflexive journaling was undertaken throughout analysis to acknowledge and examine the influence of the researcher's assumptions and positioning. Codes were generated inductively from the data, consistent with IPA's exploratory ethos [38, 42]. These codes were clustered to form preliminary themes within each case. Analysis was conducted idiographically, with each participant's account examined in depth before exploring patterns across the dataset. Themes were then compared and examined across participants to identify points of convergence and divergence. This iterative process culminated in the development of superordinate themes that captured participants' experiences of healthcare in relation to PIED use while preserving idiographic detail.

During analysis, each participant's geographic context and corresponding regional healthcare contexts were retained as part of the idiographic case context. Cross‐case interpretations were developed cautiously, with attention to how different healthcare systems and legal contexts may shape access, trust and perceived legitimacy.

Throughout this process, analytic rigour was enhanced through ongoing discussions with the wider research team. These discussions supported the interrogation of interpretations, consideration of alternate readings, refinement of thematic structures and analytic coherence.

Results

Across interviews, participants described a wide range of interactions with multiple healthcare professionals in relation to their PIED use, including GPs, endocrinologists, gynaecologists and gastrointestinal specialists, with experiences varying considerably in quality and perceived usefulness. Participants sought healthcare support for a variety of reasons including managing and preventing PIED‐related side‐effects, seeking advice about protocols, and monitoring overall health. Six participants disclosed their PIED use to at least one healthcare professional, while three chose not to disclose due to concerns around stigma, or because they felt disclosure was unnecessary. Rather than indicating willingness to engage with healthcare, disclosure appeared to be shaped by participants' assessments of safety, relevance, anticipated judgement, and the likelihood of receiving informed support. Three themes were identified to capture these experiences: (i) ‘Using as Safely as I Can’: health monitoring and healthcare needs in the context of PIED use; (ii) ‘Hard to Access, Hard to Navigate’: systemic barriers and knowledge gaps in healthcare; and (iii) ‘A Doctor I Can Trust’: the importance of non‐judgemental care.

Theme 1: ‘Using as Safely as I Can’: Health Monitoring and Healthcare Needs in the Context of PIED Use

Across accounts, participants conveyed a strong sense of personal responsibility for managing potential risks associated with PIED use. Healthcare engagement was framed as purposeful and directly tied to safeguarding wellbeing in the context of PIED use, rather than routine or incidental care. Central to this was regular and detailed monitoring of blood markers and hormone profiles, described by several participants as essential for detecting adverse effects and maintaining a sense of bodily control.

Rather than positioning themselves as passive recipients of healthcare advice, participants presented themselves as active agents in risk management, using clinical testing, self‐monitoring, and accumulated experiential knowledge to make PIED use and uncertain bodily processes feel more observable, interpretable and manageable.

When you run protocol, you need to be regimented with all that [blood tests and regular medical checks]. (Elle)

Elle's account illustrates healthcare monitoring as an embedded component of PIED use that required proactive engagement, rather than retroactive intervention.

I get blood work done every 12 weeks and if there's something's a bit funky … even if it's only 10 weeks, I'll go and get some bloods done. (Imogen)

Imogen further illustrates the proactive and precautionary nature of this monitoring, framing bloodwork as a routine expectation of PIED use. Together, these accounts position bloodwork and regular medical checks as practices through which participants attempted to make PIED‐related risk visible and manageable. Monitoring functioned as a way of producing reassurance, accountability and a sense of control in the context of uncertain physiological effects. Several participants reflected on their motivation to maintain safety, while recognising that PIED use could never be entirely risk‐free. Amy articulated this ambivalence:

I definitely tried my best to … be as safe as you can … Not that things ever were safe or can be safe. But I tried. (Amy)

Amy's account captures a central tension that emerged across the findings: participants did not necessarily understand safety as the elimination of risk, but as an ongoing attempt to reduce, monitor and remain accountable to risk within a practice they recognised as uncertain. This tension was further highlighted in Imogen's account, where regular monitoring appeared to create routines that provided reassurance despite acknowledged risks, allowing her to feel prepared and informed.

There's always gonna be that caution tape on everything … I'll just stay in my lane and, unfortunately, just wear the consequences later … I know the risks and I have systems in place. I get … regular blood tests, talk through it with the doctor. (Imogen)

Health monitoring therefore extended beyond formal healthcare settings into everyday self‐care practices, particularly during periods of active PIED use. For Imogen, such practices were explicitly framed as a means of preventing or minimising harm.

You will do damage if you don't monitor … I do my blood pressure every couple of days, resting heart rate all the time, my blood glucose. (Imogen)

For Mel, health and monitoring practices were also tied to optimisation and maximising the perceived value of PIED use.

Whilst taking anything, I'm fully focused on health in general … I'm doing it for a purpose, so I'm eating well, sleeping as much as I can, training as much as possible … minimising stress because you don't want to take supplements and just waste it … you really want to get the maximum benefits. (Mel)

Together, these accounts show that health management and self‐monitoring was embedded into everyday practice throughout PIED use, operating as harm reduction and performance optimisation.

The perceived stakes of monitoring were heightened by participants' awareness of sex‐specific risks associated with PIEDs including hormonal disruption, virilisation, irreversibility of certain side‐effects and uncertainty surrounding long‐term outcomes.

Body hair growth, balding, facial hair, your clitoris … I knew that all of that was possible … I tried to monitor and be aware. (Rachael)

Rachael's account shows how this monitoring was shaped by the intimacy and visibility of potential side effects, where participants were required to remain alert to changes that could affect not only health, but bodily appearance, sexual functioning and sense of self. For many, this translated into vigilant and systematic self‐monitoring.

[You need to] keep an eye on everything, as a woman, and the risk of virilisation … noting things down … any changes … recording your voice … Is my skin getting more oily? Am I getting acne? Am I losing hair? … What's my sex drive like? … What's my basal temperature like? There's a lot of different markers that you can and should be monitoring. (Georgia)

Georgia's account conveys the intensity of this vigilance, with monitoring extending beyond formal health indicators into a detailed, ongoing appraisal of the body for signs that risk may be emerging. Health monitoring therefore involved reading the body for changes that were intimate, visible and potentially irreversible, alongside more formal clinical indicators. In this context, self‐monitoring became a demanding and interpretive practice, particularly given the limited gender‐specific guidance available about what changes meant, when to be concerned, and how to respond.

Participants reflected that fertility concerns could carry particular weight in women's decision‐making around PIED use. Imogen contrasted women's experiences with those of men's, emphasising the perceived irreversibility of reproductive consequences.

It's almost irreversible for women to jump on and then jump off [PIEDs] … guys can jump on, come off, conceive with their partner. (Imogen)

Elle described how such concerns shaped the advice she offered to younger women considering PIED use.

When they're a lot younger and they're looking to have families … I always send them to certain people … so they fully understand what they're getting themselves into … I know a few women that really struggled since using [PIEDs] to conceive. (Elle)

Together, Imogen and Elle's accounts position reproductive health as a uniquely high‐stakes domain of risk management. Fertility thus emerged as a site of anticipatory uncertainty, where credible health information was viewed as necessary before PIED use because decisions made early in life were understood as potentially shaping future reproductive possibilities. These accounts also highlight how concern extended beyond individual risk, with peer advice becoming a way of protecting other women from what they perceived to be poorly informed or premature decisions.

Some participants also described uncertainty surrounding substance purity as a distinct source of risk, particularly where compounds perceived as ‘safer’ for women were believed to be frequently mislabelled or counterfeited. When substitution occurred, participants described that side‐effects were often rapid, visible and irreversible. Rachael linked this risk to observed bodily changes in others.

With a lot of the women's drugs, they do tend to fake them … some of these girls … reckon they're on Anavar and then you … watch them change … And it's like, ‘I don't think you're on what you think you're on’. (Rachael)

Rachael's account shifts attention from the expected risks of intended PIED use to the uncertainty produced by illicit supply. Her interpretation of other women's visible changes shows how risk could be read retrospectively through the body, particularly when women appeared to experience effects inconsistent with the substances they believed they were taking. To navigate this uncertainty, some participants described taking extra precautions, such as personally testing substances before use.

I definitely tested them just to make sure that they were [what] they were labelled. (Rachael)

Mel contrasted her own vigilance in this domain with what she perceived as a more careless culture among others.

I was conscious of the health implications … so I tested [PIEDs] myself … before I took anything. I think … people are very sloppy in that regard … they're just trusting whatever they're given is what they say it is. When in fact, if they test it, it's probably something totally different. (Mel)

Taken together, these accounts position health as an active and ongoing concern within participants' PIED use. Bloodwork and medical testing, self‐monitoring and substance testing were not isolated precautions, but part of a broader effort to make risk visible, interpretable, and manageable. At the same time, participants' capacity to enact this responsibility was constrained by limited gender‐specific clinical guidance, uncertain substance quality, and healthcare systems that often sat outside the practical realities of PIED use. Thus, a central tension was highlighted: participants invested considerable effort in protecting their health, while having to do so in conditions where reliable information, clinical support and structural safeguards were often limited.

Theme 2: ‘Hard to Access, Hard to Navigate’: Systemic Barriers and Knowledge Gaps in Healthcare

While participants described extensive health monitoring as central to managing PIED‐related risk, translating these needs into formal healthcare settings often proved challenging. Comprehensive hormone and metabolic testing was not routinely accommodated within standard healthcare pathways and was frequently described as difficult to justify in the absence of acute pathology. This created a disjuncture between participants' preventive orientation to healthcare and systems more readily organised around diagnosis, acute symptoms, or standard testing pathways. Accessing healthcare was therefore often experienced as frustrating, effortful, and constrained by geography, cost and system‐level limitations.

Layla, who lived in Canada, described substantial financial and logistical barriers, including inconsistent public health coverage for diagnostic tests, the need for multiple specialist referrals, and long travel distances to access knowledgeable providers. She characterised the process as fragmented and obstructive, conveying an experience of having to “jump through hoops” to obtain care. Furthermore, financial burden emerged as a recurring concern across accounts. Layla and Amy both described the high cost of comprehensive bloodwork, particularly where public systems failed to accommodate their needs.

It's really expensive, and it's hard to get labs … You can go to private clinics and places, but it's probably running you over $1000 to run a good panel. (Layla)

Blood work needs to be like, very detailed, a lot of doctors here can't get that kind of blood work. And it's expensive. (Amy)

These accounts conveyed a sense of exclusion from routine healthcare, with appropriately detailed and relevant health monitoring often dependent on resources, provider willingness and participants' abilities to justify needs that were not always recognised as legitimate.

Even when participants were able to access healthcare professionals and services, many described encounters where they were met with unfamiliarity about PIEDs, particularly their effects on women's bodies. Amy reflected on interactions characterised by professional neutrality, which she interpreted as stemming from uncertainty rather than respect.

With the doctors, it was just kind of like very neutral, but they're not educated. So I just don't think that they really would say much. (Amy)

Kate offered a more pointed example which left her feeling unsupported, where her openness was met with visible uncertainty rather than guidance.

They have no knowledge of P[I]EDs. I was like, ‘This is this’ and he was on his computer going, ‘OK, let me…. How do you spell that?’. I was open, but they didn't even know. Yeah, they had no opinion from the lack of knowledge, which sadly seems to be the case with GPs. (Kate)

Kate's account illustrates how disclosure did not necessarily lead to informed support in healthcare contexts. Instead, it exposed the clinician's unfamiliarity and shifted explanatory labour, positioning her as a reluctant educator rather than a recipient of care. Imogen described a similar reversal of expected roles, recounting how her partner assisted in educating her GP about how to interpret relevant bloodwork.

We go to the same doctor and my partner is teaching my doctor about … how to read proper blood work. (Imogen)

Across accounts, these experiences contributed to a sense that formal healthcare could be necessary for monitoring but insufficient as a source of guidance. Despite these limitations, many participants continued to engage with healthcare out of necessity, particularly to access testing and monitoring. In such contexts, clinicians were framed as functional gatekeepers rather than trusted sources of guidance. Layla described this relationship as transactional, referring to her doctor as a “puppet” whose role was limited to ordering tests and completing paperwork. Her frustration reflected both the lack of expertise from her doctor, as well as the compulsory nature of the interaction: access to care and monitoring remained contingent on clinicians who were perceived to add little value beyond their professional authority. This distinction between access and guidance was central to participants' accounts. Healthcare professionals could remain necessary because they controlled access to tests, referrals, and prescriptions, while still being experienced as peripheral to participants' actual decision‐making about their PIED use. As a result, practical knowledge about substances, side effects, protocols, and risk management was often sought through peer networks, where experiential knowledge was perceived as more relevant to the realities of women's PIED use. Notably, five participants relied on coaches employed through peer, bodybuilding or PIED‐specific networks for guidance on PIED protocols, risk management and monitoring.

In spite of this, across narratives, participants frequently expressed understanding rather than hostility toward healthcare professionals' limited knowledge.

You can't expect them [doctors] to know everything. (Imogen)

Imogen's comment highlights an important nuance in these findings: participants did not always interpret limited clinical knowledge as individual failure or malice. Rather, some appeared to hold lowered expectations of what general healthcare providers could reasonably know about PIED use, particularly women's use. This softened blame toward individual clinicians, but it did not resolve the practical problem that participants still needed monitoring, interpretation and guidance in an area where accessible expertise was limited.

Overall, systemic and informational barriers shaped how participants approached healthcare, influenced what they felt comfortable disclosing, and contributed to a broader sense that existing healthcare systems were poorly equipped to support people who use PIEDs, particularly women. Healthcare engagement was therefore marked by pragmatism and low expectations, reinforcing reliance on self‐monitoring and peer‐based knowledge. For participants, the difficulty was not only accessing healthcare but encountering care that could make sense of women's PIED use without minimising, pathologising or redirecting their concerns.

Theme 3: ‘A Doctor I Can Trust’: The Importance of Non‐Judgemental Care

For some participants, encounters with supportive healthcare professionals had a powerful and affirming effect on willingness to seek ongoing care. When interactions were experienced as respectful and judgement‐free, healthcare could become a source of validation and emotional safety. In these accounts, trust was grounded in clinical knowledge, relational safety, and the confidence that disclosure would not expose participants to shame, dismissal or moral judgement.

Amy described disclosing her PIED use to her GP and gynaecologist in an effort to ‘be as safe as [she] could’ and remain transparent. Although she recognised her GP's limited knowledge, she emphasised the significance of their non‐judgemental approach.

She was never shameful, that's for sure … I actually had other people that were like, ‘Who do you go to? I can't find a doctor that doesn't shame me’. So I did recommend her to a couple other bodybuilders that just wanted to get a health check without getting … criticised for it. (Amy)

Amy's account positions non‐judgemental care as a scarce and valued resource within bodybuilding networks, significant enough to be shared through peer referral when it was found. Similarly, Elle described her positive relationship with her GP, who had experience working with athletes. What stood out, according to her account, was not just the technical competence of her GP, but the deep trust and security she felt in their relationship.

I found a really good GP who's worked with a lot of sports people and he did a lot of blood testing for me … he was really open about it … I feel really grateful for him. He's like, honestly, one of the best people I know. And he never judges. It doesn't matter what I come to him with. (Elle)

This relationship had broader implications for Elle's engagement with healthcare, extending beyond PIED‐related monitoring alone. Her account suggested that relevant and supportive healthcare could be experienced as transformative, rather than merely functional.

I didn't like doctors. Didn't want anything to do with health … when I was a young child, I had a really bad, traumatic experience with the hospital and was terrified of doctors and hospitals for a long time. But going through this, it's helped me, like having a doctor that I can trust and that I know is gonna give me sound advice no matter what, and made it easier to become more regimented with everything and actually care about my health. (Elle)

In Elle's account, trust changed the practical possibilities of care. A relationship experienced as safe and reliable supported disclosure about PIED use and more broadly, helped her reframe healthcare as something safe, useful and worth engaging with. This demonstrates how a single trusted provider could interrupt prior patterns of fear and avoidance and support more sustained health monitoring.

In contrast, some participants described experiences in which judgement was perceived even in the absence of explicit disclosure. Tara reflected on an encounter in which she felt assumptions about bodybuilding and PIED use shaped her care:

There's a lot of stigma about it. And I experienced this very much when I wanted to get my bloods done after competing and the doctor didn't put any hormone profiles on the test just because I was competing … I think the doctor just assumed that maybe I was on the PIEDs or something and it skewed it for whatever reason. (Tara)

Tara's account shows that stigma did not necessarily require explicit confrontation to shape the encounter. She interpreted the absence of hormone testing through the lens of assumed PIED use, suggesting that perceived judgement could undermine trust even when use was not openly discussed. For Rachael, fear was an ever‐present undercurrent grounded in prior experiences of judgement for participating in bodybuilding itself. In this context, disclosing PIED use felt particularly risky:

I had a lot of fear at the time about … the whole process … about the fact that I was doing bodybuilding in the first place because a lot of people didn't think it was a good idea … and so then I think I was even more protective about the fact that I was using substances. (Rachael)

Rachael's account shows how judgement surrounding bodybuilding shaped expectations of judgement around PIED use. Non‐disclosure was therefore not a sign of disengagement from health, but a protective strategy in a context where revealing substance use felt likely to intensify scrutiny.

Together, these accounts illustrate how deeply healthcare experiences were tied to emotional safety and wellbeing. Participants did not separate clinical care from the relational conditions in which it occurred: knowledge, testing and advice mattered, but became usable only when participants felt respected, believed and able to speak openly. Supportive encounters had the potential to facilitate trust, empowerment and engagement with health, while negative or stigmatising interactions could reinforce secrecy, avoidance and fear.

Discussion

This study provides one of the first in‐depth, qualitative accounts of healthcare experiences of women who use PIEDs, revealing them as active and highly invested participants in their own health. Participants described substantial engagement with formal healthcare, alongside ongoing self‐monitoring and help‐seeking behaviours; however, access to appropriate care was frequently complex, effortful and uneven. Together, these findings highlight how women interpret and navigate healthcare in the context of PIED use and expose the limited capacity of many services to adequately support them. In doing so, they offer new insight into how gender, expertise, and stigma intersect in this under‐examined area.

Participants described sustained investment in safeguarding their health, including proactive engagement in monitoring and help‐seeking throughout their PIED use. This challenges dominant stereotypes that portray people who use PIEDs as reckless or unconcerned with health [8, 14, 18], including stereotypes sometimes reproduced by participants themselves when describing other women who used PIEDs. The high level of health engagement across accounts aligns with previous research among men who use PIEDs, which has similarly documented proactive health practices [13, 16, 45]. However, the meaning and quality of this engagement varied considerably. While some participants described valued therapeutic relationships characterised by trust and continuity of care, others expressed ambivalence or strategic disengagement, using healthcare instrumentally and viewing clinicians as gatekeepers to blood tests, scans and routine check‐ups.

Across narratives, non‐judgemental and stigma‐free care emerged as central to positive healthcare engagement. Stigma was consistently identified as a barrier to access, with previous experiences of judgement, dismissal or invalidation discouraging disclosure of PIED use and, for some participants, future willingness to seek support through formal avenues. These findings align with research from the USA, UK, and Australia, demonstrating that fear of judgement can shape non‐disclosure and avoidance of healthcare among people who use PIEDs [8, 15, 17, 19, 25]. Importantly, participants' accounts indicated that the tone and perceived intent of clinical interactions were often more influential than technical expertise. Respectful and non‐moralising care allowed participants to disclose PIED use, ask questions and pursue health monitoring without feeling shamed or positioned as irresponsible. In this sense, trust was grounded in the interaction between clinical knowledge and relational safety, with care becoming useful when participants felt that their concerns, bodies and self‐monitoring practices were taken seriously.

The present findings extend recent sociological and feminist work on women's PIED use by showing how gendered health concerns become clinically, socially, and relationally significant within healthcare encounters [10, 27]. Participants described concerns about virilising effects, hormonal disruption, menstrual changes and reproductive health, all of which created specific needs for credible guidance and monitoring. These concerns were not only biomedical, but also social and relational, shaping disclosure, secrecy, self‐monitoring, peer advice, and perceptions of whether healthcare providers could understand women's experiences without judgement. In this sense, the findings show how gendered health concerns become meaningful within healthcare encounters, influencing not only what support women need, but whether that support feels accessible, credible and safe to seek.

Specifically, women required care that could engage with the embodied, emotional and social meanings of these effects, including how changes such as voice deepening, clitoral growth, menstrual disruption or fertility uncertainty were interpreted within participants' lives and relationships. This is particularly important because participants were often managing risk in conditions of limited evidence, inconsistent advice and uneven clinical recognition. In some cases, this resulted in participants being required to actively compensate for healthcare providers' knowledge gaps by assuming the role of educator in clinical encounters. These dynamics sometimes led to diminished trust in healthcare systems more broadly, aligning with previous research which has highlighted common scepticism toward formal information sources among the PIED‐using community and a prioritisation of peer networks for information/education [16, 17, 20, 22].

Consistent with this, studies demonstrate that both people who use PIEDs and healthcare professionals recognise substantial gaps in clinical training, with providers reporting low confidence and a need for clearer guidance [9, 22]. While PIED‐specific harm‐reduction guides for use by GPs have been developed in some jurisdictions [46], content addressing women's experiences remains limited, reinforcing participants' perceptions of being overlooked within healthcare. Taken together, these accounts suggest that gender‐responsive healthcare must involve more than information provision and rather requires clinicians to recognise women's experiential knowledge, respond to intimate bodily concerns without moral judgement, and support informed decision‐making in areas where formal evidence remains limited.

Beyond individual clinical encounters, participants' healthcare experiences were also shaped by structural constraints, including financial costs, legislative environments, and limited access to specialised services. These constraints further highlight that women's access to appropriate PIED‐related care cannot be reduced to factors such as individual willingness to seek help or disclose use. Healthcare systems that vary in affordability, accessibility, legal context, and capacity to recognise PIED use as a legitimate health concern must be considered. This is particularly relevant given the international composition of the present participant sample, as participants were navigating diverse healthcare and policy environments while describing shared difficulties in accessing knowledgeable, non‐stigmatising and gender‐responsive care.

Together, these findings demonstrate that women's healthcare experiences, in the context of PIED use, are shaped through the interaction of interpersonal relationships, institutional practices, community‐based knowledge networks, and policy contexts. Addressing these challenges requires responses that extend beyond individual behaviour change, including stigma‐informed clinical training, improved provider education regarding women's PIED use, and the development of accessible, gender‐responsive models of care.

Limitations and Future Directions

This study provides an in‐depth, qualitative exploration of women's healthcare experiences in the context of PIED use. Consistent with IPA, the study was designed to prioritise idiographic depth rather than statistical generalisability, generating a detailed interpretive account of how participants made sense of healthcare access, support, stigma, self‐monitoring and risk management in relation to their PIED use. The findings should therefore be understood as a situated and theoretically informed interpretation of participants' lived experience, shaped by the analytic lens of IPA. Future research could build on this work by employing complementary methodological approaches, including quantitative surveys, mixed‐methods designs and comparative studies across demographic groups, to examine how these dynamics operate across broader populations and contexts.

Six participants were recruited through a harm‐reduction and advocacy network which may have resulted in a sample particularly engaged in health management and connected to information‐sharing communities. As participants themselves noted, not all women approach PIED use with the same level of caution. The findings may therefore underrepresent the experiences of women who use PIEDs in more isolated, less supported or less health‐engaged contexts. Future research should recruit women through a wider range of settings to capture greater diversity in patterns of PIED use, risk management, and healthcare engagement.

Finally, while participants were drawn from multiple geographic locations (Australia, Canada and USA), this diversity may also be understood as a strength, as key experiential themes appeared to resonate across different healthcare systems and contexts. Nonetheless, it also requires consideration given that healthcare systems, insurance arrangements, prescribing contexts and legal frameworks differ across these regions, and these differences may shape participants' access to care, willingness to disclose PIED use, and expectations of healthcare professionals. The present findings do not make comparative claims about national healthcare systems or policy contexts. Instead, region‐specific context was considered as part of participants' idiographic accounts, with the analysis remaining focused on how women made sense of healthcare encounters, stigma, disclosure and support‐seeking across varied contexts. Future research, involving larger samples, would be valuable for examining how women's PIED‐related healthcare experiences may be shaped by, or vary across, different national or regional contexts.

Implications

Collectively, these findings reposition women who use PIEDs as active health managers navigating stigma, uncertainty, and institutional gaps within healthcare systems that have largely failed to account for their needs. They reinforce the need for healthcare practice to move beyond risk‐centric and pathologising frameworks toward approaches grounded in partnership, harm reduction and recognition of women's situated expertise. This is particularly important given that participants often entered clinical encounters with detailed knowledge of substances, bloodwork, bodily changes and risk‐management practices yet still encountered systems in which their concerns were not recognised as legitimate.

Recognising women's proactive health engagement and situated expertise is critical, particularly given that non‐judgemental care was consistently described as more influential than technical knowledge alone in shaping trust, disclosure, and ongoing engagement. The findings also highlight an urgent need for expanded, gender‐specific education and training for healthcare professionals, alongside the development of evidence‐based clinical guidelines for working with PIED‐using populations. Such guidance should explicitly address the sex‐specific physiological risks of women's PIED use, including monitoring for side effects such as virilisation and reproductive concerns, while avoiding framings that reduce women's PIED use solely to harm, vulnerability or bodily risk. Resources should be co‐designed with women who use PIEDs to ensure that lived experience meaningfully informs policy and practice, and that clinical guidance reflects the practical realities of use, disclosure, self‐monitoring and help‐seeking.

Addressing structural barriers is also critical. Financial costs, limited access to appropriate healthcare services, variable provider knowledge and differing legal or policy contexts shaped whether participants could access care that was credible, affordable and safe. Improving healthcare responses therefore requires more than individual clinician education. It also requires systems‐level attention to service accessibility, referral pathways, funding structures, and the institutional recognition of PIED‐related concerns as legitimate healthcare needs.

Finally, these findings should be considered within a broader harm‐reduction landscape. Services increasingly accessed by people who use PIEDs, including needle‐syringe programs and peer‐led harm reduction initiatives, must also be equitable, gender‐sensitive, and responsive to women's specific needs. Strengthening links between clinical care, community‐based knowledge networks and harm‐reduction services may help support women's health without requiring disclosure to occur only in settings where stigma, judgement or limited expertise remain likely.

Conclusion

This study examines healthcare experiences of women who use PIEDs, a population largely overlooked in existing research. The findings demonstrate that participants were highly motivated to safeguard their health, actively seeking monitoring and support while navigating gender‐specific health concerns, stigma and limited clinical guidance. However, these efforts were frequently constrained by stigma, limited provider knowledge and structural barriers to care. Notably, participants reported several instances where they themselves were required to assume the role of educator in clinical encounters due to healthcare providers' limited knowledge about PIEDs. Despite this, respectful and non‐judgemental interactions with healthcare providers were described as more influential and meaningful than clinical expertise alone, and were central to whether care was experienced as safe, valuable and worthy of future engagement. Together, these findings underscore the urgent need for gender‐sensitive, stigma‐informed, and harm reduction–oriented healthcare responses that acknowledge women's expertise and address the longstanding neglect of women within PIED‐related research and practice.
