EIT-based flow index as a peri-extubation monitoring tool: a reply to “new indexes, old habits”
Authors: Rui Zhang, Jing Xu, Hongping Qu, Jialin Liu, Zhanqi Zhao
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We thank Dr. Jonkman and Scaramuzzo for their insightful and rigorous critique [1] of our study on the EIT-derived Flow Index (EFI) [2]. Their comments touch upon critical methodological and physiological aspects that are essential for the robust development of novel weaning indices. We appreciate the opportunity to clarify our rationale and address the specific concerns raised.

First of all, we would like to apologize for the mistake in the supplementary material. Jonkman and Scaramuzzo correctly pointed out that the curves in Supplementary Figure S2B and C are indeed not from real measurement but from sketch illustration. The supplementary material was not thoroughly proofread throughout the review process, and the mistake was not identified. We have corrected the Figure S2 using real patient data. The low-pass filter used in the study was the one embedded in the DraegerEITDataAnalysisTool63 (Draeger Medical, Luebeck, Germany). Cutoff frequency 50 / min, which corresponds to 0.83 Hz.

Jonkman and Scaramuzzo also commented on the rationale of the EIT-based global index. They wish to see a comparison between EIT-based EFI, and the Flow Index based on ventilator waveforms. The clinical context of our study is the peri-extubation period. The ventilator flow signal is a superior signal only available during invasive mechanical ventilation. Although flow signal was available during the spontaneous breathing trial used in our study, it might not always be the case (e.g. SBT with T-piece). The clinical advantage of EIT-based global index goes beyond the SBT where it can help to identify patients who pass the SBT but remain physiologically vulnerable post-extubation. Comparison between EIT-based flow index and ventilator-based flow index is irrelevant in this context, as we demonstrated EIT provides continuous monitoring spanning the transition from invasive ventilation, through the SBT and into the post-extubation period, regardless of whether EIT-based flow index matches the ventilator-based one or not.

Regarding regional analysis, we fully agree that this is a key strength of EIT that deserves dedicated investigation. A preliminary exploration of regional EFI (ventral vs. dorsal) was reported in a prior study by Wang et al. [3, 4]. In the present study, we chose to focus on the global EFI as a first step to establish clinical proof of concept.

We share the view that the physiological interpretation of EFI as a marker of “effort” is indirect, as it is based on the shape of the flow curve rather than a direct measurement of pressure. But the strong association with SBT outcome, the early warning signal, and the post-extubation risk stratification provide strong clinical construct validity. The overlapping information with RSBI and P0.1 supports the idea that EFI captures a similar physiological state (load/capacity imbalance). In addition, the key advantage of EFI lies in its signal source independence. Unlike P0.1 and MIP, which require airway occlusion or voluntary maneuvers that interrupt normal breathing, EFI is derived passively from the EIT signal. And unlike all conventional indices including RSBI, which rely on flow sensors in the ventilator circuit, EFI is derived from the chest wall — enabling a unified metric that seamlessly spans the entire peri-extubation continuum from invasive ventilation, through SBT (including T-piece), to post-extubation spontaneous breathing. This ability to detect evolving physiological deterioration is a paradigm shift from the conventional weaning indices. We believe the continuous nature of the metric, combined with its independent use peri-extubation, constitutes a significant clinical advantage.
