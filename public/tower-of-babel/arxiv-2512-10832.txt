A Variable Step Sizes Frequency Offsets-Compensated Least Mean Squares Algorithm
Karel Pärlin, Aaron Byman, Tommi Meriläinen, Taneli Riihonen

Abstract
Frequency offsets-compensated least mean squares (FO-LMS) algorithm is a generic method for estimating a wireless channel under carrier and sampling frequency offsets when the transmitted signal is beforehand known to the receiver. The algorithm iteratively and explicitly adjusts its estimates of the channel and frequency offsets using stochastic gradient descent-based rules and the step sizes of these rules determine the learning rate and stability of the algorithm. Within the stability conditions, the choice of step sizes reflects a trade-off between the algorithm’s ability to react to changes in the channel and the ability to minimize misadjustments caused by noise. This paper provides theoretical expressions to predict and optimize the tracking and misadjusment errors of FO-LMS when estimating channels and frequency offsets with known time-varying characteristics. This work also proposes a method to adjust the FO-LMS ’s step sizes based on the algorithm’s performance when the time-varying characteristics are not known, which is more often the case in practice. Accuracy of the expressions and performance of the proposed variable step sizes algorithm are studied through simulations.



I
Introduction

The
FO-LMS
adaptive filter
[
1
]
is a robust and low-complexity algorithm for estimating the propagation channel as well as the carrier and sampling frequency offsets between a radio transmitter and receiver. It has been demonstrated to be especially useful in
known-interference cancellation (KIC)

[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
but, owing to its simplicity and generic structure, the algorithm is also of potential use in other signal processing applications, such as wireless communications, distributed beamforming, and bistatic radars
[
1
]
. The algorithm works by iteratively and explicitly updating its estimates of the channel impulse response, carrier frequency offset, and sampling frequency offset between a transmitter and receiver based on
stochastic gradient descent (SGD)
rules according to user-defined step sizes and a cost function, which in the
FO-LMS
’s case is the instantaneous difference between the estimated received signal and the actual received signal. The step sizes control the algorithm’s adaptation rate and in nonstationary conditions their selection becomes a trade-off between tracking ability and misadjustment. There will thus exist optimal step sizes that minimize the total estimation error, which is the sum of the tracking and adjustment errors.

Performance of classical adaptive filters in nonstationary conditions has been thoroughly studied. Some approaches have considered the impact of a cyclic nonstationarity only
[
7
,
8

[

,

,

,
13

. The results in these studies highlighted the vulnerability of classical adaptive filters to cyclic nonstationarities and motivated the development of the
FO-LMS
adaptive filter in the first place. The performance analysis of
FO-LMS
has since demonstrated the benefit of explicitly estimating the cyclic nonstationarities as separate parameters
[

]
.

However, the existing performance analyzes
[

,
8
,

,

,

,

,

have assumed the cause of the cyclic nonstationarities to be static or time-invariant. In practice, the oscillators in radios exhibit both short-term and long-term instabilities that result in time-varying cyclic nonstationarities

]
. For example, the one second Allan deviation
[

and
10^{-10}

[

17

Furthermore, in practice the time-varying characteristic of the cyclic and random nonstationarities are rarely precisely known, and it would be convenient to be able to use the
FO-LMS
algorithm without having to manually control its step sizes anyway. Ideally the algorithm would have the means to self-adapt the step sizes close to their optimal values based on some assessment. In that vein, numerous self-adapting step size techniques have been proposed to enhance classical adaptive filters

The purpose of this paper is twofold. Firstly, to present theoretical expressions for estimating the
FO-LMS
performance under time-varying cyclic and random nonstationarities, therefore allowing, e.g., instead of exhaustive numerical search, to calculate the optimal step sizes for the algorithm when all the system parameters are known. Secondly, to provide a method for the
FO-LMS

FO-LMS
performance closely match with simulation results and that the theoretical optimal step sizes effectively minimize the sum of the tracking and adjustment errors. We also show that the proposed variable step size algorithm performs well in comparison to
FO-LMS
with optimal step sizes.

II-A

System Model

Fig. 1:
System model for estimating a wireless channel under carrier and sampling frequency offsets.

The system model considered in this work is as follows and as illustrated in Fig.
1
. Let
x(n)
denote a discrete-time complex-valued signal that is transmitted over a channel with a finite impulse response

\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}_{n}=[w^{\mathrm{o}}_{1}(n)\quad w^{\mathrm{o}}_{2}(n)\quad\cdots\quad w^{\mathrm{o}}_{M}(n)]

(1)

of order
M
that varies in time according to a first order autoregressive process so that

\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}_{n}=\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}+\bm{\theta}_{n},

(2)

where

\bm{\theta}_{n+1}=\alpha\bm{\theta}_{n}+\mathbf{q}_{n}

(3)

with

\mathbf{q}_{n}

\mathbf{Q}=E(\mathbf{q}_{n}\mathbf{q}^{*}_{n})

\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{Q})=M\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{q}}

Denoting the regressor of the channel by

\mathbf{x}_{n}=[x(n)\quad x(n-1)\quad\cdots\quad x(n-M+1)],

(4)

the received signal becomes

d(n)=(\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}_{n})^{H}\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{o}}_{n}e^{j\phi^{\mathrm{o}}(n)}+v(n),

(5)

where

is the measurement noise that consists of zero-mean Gaussian noise
g(n)
with variance
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{g}}
and potentially of a background signal
s(n)
, altogether having variance
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}
. The term

accounts for the noise and drift in the carrier frequency offset based on the three-state clock model
[
29
,
30
,
31
]
such that

\displaystyle\phi^{\mathrm{o}}(n+1)

\displaystyle=\phi^{\mathrm{o}}(n)+\epsilon^{\mathrm{o}}(n)T_{\mathrm{s}}+\omega_{\upphi}(n),

(6)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\mathrm{o}}(n+1)

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{\mathrm{o}}(n)+\omega_{\upepsilon}(n)+\kappa,

(7)

where
\phi^{\mathrm{o}}(n)
and
\epsilon^{\mathrm{o}}(n)
correspond to the carrier phase and frequency offset, respectively, and
T_{\mathrm{s}}
is the nominal sampling period. The two zero-mean Gaussian random processes,
\omega_{\upphi}(n)
and
\omega_{\upepsilon}(n)
, represent the random-walk phase noise and random-walk carrier frequency offset drift
[
32
]
with variances
\sigma^{2}_{\upphi}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\upepsilon}
, respectively, and the constant term
\kappa
represents the linear carrier frequency offset drift. The received digitized baseband signal is a resampled version of the known transmitted signal so that

\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{o}}_{n}=[y^{\mathrm{o}}(n)\quad y^{\mathrm{o}}(n-1)\quad\cdots\quad y^{\mathrm{o}}(n-M+1)]

(8)

with

y^{\mathrm{o}}(n)=x\left(nT_{\mathrm{s}}+\beta^{\mathrm{o}}(n)\right),

(9)

where the term
\beta^{\mathrm{o}}(n)
accounts for the jitter and drift in the sampling frequency offset based on the three-state clock model so that

\displaystyle\beta^{\mathrm{o}}(n+1)

\displaystyle=\beta^{\mathrm{o}}(n)+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\eta^{\mathrm{o}}(n)}}+\omega_{\upbeta}(n),

(10)

\displaystyle\eta^{\mathrm{o}}(n+1)

\displaystyle=\eta^{\mathrm{o}}(n)+\omega_{\upeta}(n)+\rho,

(11)

where
\beta^{\mathrm{o}}(n)
and
\eta^{\mathrm{o}}(n)
correspond to the sampling jitter and frequency offset, respectively. The two zero-mean Gaussian random processes,
\omega_{\upbeta}(n)
and
\omega_{\upeta}(n)
, represent the random-walk sampling jitter and random-walk frequency offset drift with variances
\nicefrac{{T_{\mathrm{s}}\sigma^{2}_{\upbeta}}}{{2\pi}}
and
\nicefrac{{\sigma^{2}_{\upeta}}}{{2\pi}}
, respectively, and the constant term
\rho
represents the linear sampling frequency offset drift.

The
FO-LMS
algorithm provides estimates
\mathbf{w}_{n}
,
\epsilon(n)
,
\phi(n)
,
\eta(n)
, and
\beta(n)
of the channel and clock offsets relying on the gradient of the instantaneous error

e(n)=d(n)-\mathbf{w}^{H}_{n}\mathbf{y}_{n}e^{j\phi(n)}

(12)

by iteratively updating its parameter estimates using the gradient descent equations

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{n+1}=\

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{n}+\mu_{\mathrm{w}}\mathbf{y}_{n}e^{j\phi(n)}e^{*}(n),

(13a)

\displaystyle\epsilon(n+1)=\

\displaystyle\epsilon(n)+\mu_{{\upepsilon}}\Im{\left\{\mathbf{w}^{H}_{n}\mathbf{y}_{n}e^{j\phi(n)}e^{*}(n)\right\}},

(13b)

\displaystyle\eta(n+1)=\

\displaystyle\eta(n)+\mu_{{\upeta}}\Re{\left\{\mathbf{w}^{H}_{n}\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{n}e^{j\phi(n)}e^{*}(n)\right\}},

(13c)

where
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{n}
is the derivative of
\mathbf{y}_{n}
, and
\mu_{\mathrm{w}}
,
\mu_{{\upepsilon}}
, and
\mu_{{\upeta}}
are the fixed channel, carrier, and sampling frequency offset estimation step sizes, respectively.

II-B

Steady-State Performance

Due to the measurement noise and time-varying system model, the estimated parameters will inevitably differ from the true parameters. These differences can be denoted as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n}=\

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{o}}_{n}-\mathbf{w}_{n},

(14a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}(n)=\epsilon^{\mathrm{o}}(n)-\epsilon(n)

\displaystyle\text{ and }\tilde{\phi}(n)=\phi^{\mathrm{o}}(n)-\phi(n),

(14b)

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}(n)=\eta^{\mathrm{o}}(n)-\eta(n)

\displaystyle\text{ and }\tilde{\beta}(n)=\beta^{\mathrm{o}}(n)-\beta(n),

(14c)

where

\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{o}}_{n}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 3.8889pt\hskip-3.8889pt\hbox{$=$}\hskip-3.8889pt\hskip-3.28473pt\raisebox{4.66875pt}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Delta$}}\hskip-3.28473pt\hskip 3.8889pt}}\mathbf{T}_{n}\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}_{n}e^{j\tilde{\phi}(n)}

(15)

and
\mathbf{T}_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}
is an arbitrary time-shift matrix that, when multiplied with
\mathbf{x}_{n}
, delays the signal
\mathbf{x}_{n}
by
\tilde{\beta}(n)
.

It is reasonable to assume that in steady state the parameter errors vary around zero so that
\tilde{\epsilon}(n)\ll 1
and
\tilde{\eta}(n)\ll\nicefrac{{1}}{{f_{\mathrm{max}}}}
with
f_{\mathrm{max}}
being the maximum frequency component of
x(n)
. The
a priori
estimation errors relating to each of the update equations in (
13
) can be then written as

\displaystyle e^{\mathrm{a}}_{\mathrm{w}}(n)=\

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{H}_{n}\mathbf{y}_{n}e^{j\phi(n)},

(16a)

\displaystyle e^{\mathrm{a}}_{\mathrm{\upepsilon}}(n)=\

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{n}^{H}\mathbf{y}_{n}\tilde{\epsilon}(n)e^{j\phi(n)},

(16b)

\displaystyle e^{\mathrm{a}}_{\mathrm{\upeta}}(n)=\

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{n}^{H}\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{n}\tilde{\eta}(n)e^{j\phi(n)},

(16c)

so that the total instantaneous error in (
12
) is

e(n)=e^{\mathrm{a}}_{\mathrm{w}}(n)+e^{\mathrm{a}}_{\mathrm{\upepsilon}}(n)+e^{\mathrm{a}}_{\mathrm{\upeta}}(n)+v(n).

(17)

Assuming furthermore that in steady state the noise sequence
v(n)
and
a priori
errors in (
16
) are stationary and statistically independent, the
mean-squared error (MSE)
is given by

\mathrm{MSE}=\zeta+\sigma^{2}_{v}=\zeta_{\mathrm{w}}+\zeta_{\upepsilon}+\zeta_{\upeta}+\sigma^{2}_{v},

(18)

where
\zeta
is the total
excess mean-squared error (EMSE)
and

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathrm{w}}=\

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}E\left[|e^{\mathrm{a}}_{\mathrm{w}}(n)|^{2}\right],

(19a)

\displaystyle\zeta_{\upepsilon}=\

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}E\left[|e^{\mathrm{a}}_{\mathrm{\upepsilon}}(n)|^{2}\right],

(19b)

\displaystyle\zeta_{\upeta}=\

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}E\left[|e^{\mathrm{a}}_{\mathrm{\upeta}}(n)|^{2}\right],

(19c)

are the individual
EMSEs
related to the update equations (
13a
), (
13b
), and (
13c
), respectively.

Employing the energy conservation relation
[
11
]
, it is shown in Appendix

that for sufficiently small step sizes and for Gaussian white input signals (
\mathbf{R}=\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}\mathbf{I}
) the individual steady-state
EMSEs
can be approximated as

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathrm{w}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{w}}M\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}}{2}+\frac{M\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{q}}}{2\mu_{\mathrm{w}}}

(20a)

\displaystyle\qquad\ +\frac{\mu_{\upepsilon}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}}{4\mu_{\mathrm{w}}}+\frac{\mu_{\upeta}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}}{2\mu_{\mathrm{w}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\ +\frac{\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\upphi}}{2\mu_{\mathrm{w}}}+\frac{\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\upbeta}}{2\mu_{\mathrm{w}}T_{\mathrm{s}}},

\displaystyle\zeta_{\upepsilon}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\mu_{\upepsilon}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}}{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\upepsilon}T_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}{2\mu_{\mathrm{w}}\mu_{\upepsilon}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}}

(20b)

\displaystyle\qquad\ +\frac{\kappa^{2}T_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}{\mu^{2}_{\upepsilon}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}},

\displaystyle\zeta_{\upeta}

\displaystyle\approx\mu_{\upeta}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\upeta}T_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}{2\mu_{\mathrm{w}}\mu_{\upeta}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}}

\displaystyle\ +\frac{\rho^{2}T_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}{2\left(2+\nicefrac{{2}}{{M}}\right)\mu^{2}_{\upeta}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}}+\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{w}}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\upbeta}}{2\mu_{\upeta}T_{\mathrm{s}}}.

(20c)

At large step sizes, and as long as the algorithm remains in its steady state, accuracy of the
EMSE
estimation is improved by using the complete expressions in (
46
). For example, the estimated
EMSEs
in this paper (i.e., in Section
IV
) are calculated using (
46
). However, equations in (
20
) are used in the following optimal step size derivations leading to more compact expressions.

II-C

Optimal Step Sizes

From (
20
) it is evident that the individual
EMSEs
of the update equations are not monotonically increasing functions of the corresponding step sizes. Instead, the
EMSEs
are composed of multiple terms with differing impacts from each of the step sizes. The first terms on the right-hand sides in (
20
) denote the misadjustments that decrease as the corresponding step sizes are decreased (i.e., as the algorithm becomes less susceptible to noise). The rest of the terms denote the tracking errors that decrease as the corresponding step sizes are increased (i.e., as the algorithm can more rapidly react to changes in the true parameters).

Specifically, the second, fifth and sixth term in (
20a
) denote the tracking errors with regards to the random channel perturbations, random-walk phase noise, and random-walk sampling jitter, respectively. The third and fourth term in (
20a
) denote the tracking errors of the self-induced nonstationarities that the
FO-LMS
inherently produces and these decrease as the step size
\mu_{\mathrm{w}}
is increased and as the step sizes
\mu_{\upepsilon}
and
\mu_{\upeta}
are decreased (i.e., as the variance of the self-induced nonstationarities is reduced). Finally, the second and third terms in (
20b
) and (
20
) denote the tracking errors with regards to the random-walk and linear drift of the frequency offsets. The fourth term in (
20
) denotes the misadjustment due to the increase in the noise floor caused by the accumulated sampling jitter and its inadequate tracking at small
\mu_{\upeta}
.

It is evident that there exists a set of values
\mu^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\mathrm{w}}
,
\mu^{\mathrm{opt}}_{{\upepsilon}}
, and
\mu^{\mathrm{opt}}_{{\upeta}}
that minimizes the
EMSE
. These optimal values can be obtained by taking the partial derivatives of the total
EMSE
with regards to the step sizes and then solving the partial derivatives for the step sizes resulting in

\displaystyle\mu^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\mathrm{w}}

\displaystyle=\biggl\{\frac{1}{M\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}+\frac{\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\upphi}}{\mu_{\upeta}T_{\mathrm{s}}}}

(21a)

\displaystyle\quad\ \times\biggl(M\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{q}}+\frac{\mu^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\upepsilon}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}}{2}+\mu^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\upeta}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}

\displaystyle\ +\frac{\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\upbeta}}{T_{\mathrm{s}}}+\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\upphi}+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\upepsilon}T^{2}_{\mathrm{s}}}{\mu_{\upepsilon}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\upeta}T^{2}_{\mathrm{s}}}{\mu_{\upeta}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}}\biggr)\biggr\}^{\!1/2},

\displaystyle\mu^{\mathrm{opt}}_{{\upepsilon}}

\displaystyle\approx\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^{2}_{\upepsilon}T^{2}_{\mathrm{s}}}{\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}\left(2\mu_{\mathrm{w}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}+1\right)}}

(21b)

\displaystyle\qquad\ +\sqrt[3]{\frac{8\mu^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\mathrm{w}}\kappa^{2}T^{2}_{\mathrm{s}}}{2\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{4}\mu^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\mathrm{w}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}\sigma^{4}_{\mathrm{x}}+\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{4}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}}},

\displaystyle\mu^{\mathrm{opt}}_{{\upeta}}

\displaystyle\approx\sqrt{\frac{\nicefrac{{\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\upbeta}\mu^{2}_{\mathrm{w}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}}}{{T_{\mathrm{s}}}}+\sigma^{2}_{\upeta}T^{2}_{\mathrm{s}}}{\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}\left(2\mu_{\mathrm{w}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}+1\right)}}

(21c)

\displaystyle\qquad\ +\sqrt[3]{\frac{\mu^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\mathrm{w}}\rho^{2}T^{2}_{\mathrm{s}}}{2\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{4}\mu^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\mathrm{w}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}\sigma^{4}_{\mathrm{x}}+\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{4}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}}}.

Given the time-varying characteristics of the system parameters, the system of equations in (
21
) can be solved using numerical optimizers, such as
[
33
]
, to provide step sizes that minimize the total
EMSE
of the
FO-LMS
algorithm when it is in steady state. Despite the numerical optimization, the above equations provide several orders of magnitude reduction in computational effort in comparison to exhaustively testing for the optimality of parameters like required in the prior art.

To assist numerical algorithms in optimizing the system of equations in (
21
), it can be beneficial to provide initial guesses for the optimal step sizes without considering the coupling of the individual
EMSEs
. That is, by deriving an approximal optimal step size
\hat{\mu}^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\mathrm{w}}
based on (
20a
) without the cross-terms so that

\hat{\mu}^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\mathrm{w}}=\sqrt{\frac{M\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{q}}+\nicefrac{{\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\upbeta}}}{{T_{\mathrm{s}}}}+\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\upphi}}{M\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}}}.

(22)

Then, by first calculating
\hat{\mu}^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\mathrm{w}}
based on (
22
),
\mu^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\upepsilon}
and
\mu^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\upeta}
can be directly evaluated with (
21b
) and (
21c
), respectively, using
\hat{\mu}^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\mathrm{w}}
in place of
\mu^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\mathrm{w}}
. Relying on the resulting optimal step size approximations as starting points in numerical optimizers for solving (
21
) will generally lead to a reduced number of required optimization iterations.

III
Variable Step Sizes

In this section, we propose a method to determine reasonable step sizes of the
FO-LMS
algorithm without explicit knowledge of the time varying characteristics of the system parameters.

III-A

Derivation of Variable Step Sizes

As highlighted by the previous section, the individual
EMSEs
of the
FO-LMS
update equations are coupled and the selection of any one of the three step sizes not only affects the
EMSE
of the update equation controlled by that step size but also the
EMSEs
of the other update equations. Since the three update equations of
FO-LMS
operate over the same cost function, we propose to use that cost function as feedback to directly adjust the step size
\mu_{\mathrm{w}}
that is directly linked to the update equation (
13a
), which in practical situations likely contributes the majority of the
EMSE
. We then propose to adjust the remaining step sizes
\mu_{\upepsilon}
and
\mu_{\upeta}
depending on the already selected step size
\mu_{\mathrm{w}}
and the time-averaged gradients of the remaining two update equations.

The equations for selecting the step sizes can be derived by first noting that the step sizes of the
FO-LMS
algorithm will be optimal if

E\left[\lvert e^{\mathrm{p}}(n)\rvert^{2}\right]=\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}\qquad\forall n,

(23)

where

e^{\mathrm{p}}(n)=d(n)-\mathbf{w}^{H}_{n+1}\mathbf{y}_{n}e^{j\phi(n)}

(24)

is the
a posteriori
error signal calculated at sample index
n
using the updated parameter estimates
\mathbf{w}_{n+1}
,
\epsilon(n+1)
, and
\eta(n+1)
. Relying on the assumptions that the signal
x(n)
is white Gaussian and that the channel update equation (
13a
) contributes the majority of the total
EMSE
, the expected value of the power of the
a posteriori
error signal can be approximated as

E\left[\lvert e^{\mathrm{p}}(n)\rvert^{2}\right]\approx\left[1-\mu_{\mathrm{w}}(n)M\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{y}}\right]^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{e}}(n),

(25)

where
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{e}}(n)=E\left[\lvert e(n)\rvert^{2}\right]
is the power of the error signal. Then, taking the right-hand sides in (
24
) and (
25
) to be equal and solving for
\mu_{\mathrm{w}}(n)
results in the nonparametric
variable step size normalized least mean squares (VSS-NLMS)
algorithm
[
22
]
with

\mu_{\mathrm{w}}(n)=\frac{1}{\mathbf{y}^{H}_{n}\mathbf{y}_{n}}\left[1-\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{e}}(n)}\right].

(26)

When the carrier and sampling frequency offsets do not change over time and the
FO-LMS
algorithm has reached a steady state with accurate estimates of the carrier and sampling frequency offsets, the gradients

\displaystyle\Delta_{\upepsilon}(n)

\displaystyle=\Im{\left\{\mathbf{w}^{H}_{n}\mathbf{y}_{n}e^{j\phi(n)}e^{*}(n)\right\}},

(27a)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\upeta}(n)

\displaystyle=\Re{\left\{\mathbf{w}^{H}_{n}\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{n}e^{j\phi(n)}e^{*}(n)\right\}},

(27b)

in (
13b
) and (
13c
) will be zero-mean wide sense stationary processes. However, when the frequency offsets drift over time, these gradients will have non-zero means
\bar{\Delta}_{\upepsilon}(n)
and
\bar{\Delta}_{\upeta}(n)
that are proportional to the carrier and sampling frequency offset drifts as well as scaled by the corresponding step sizes
\mu_{\upepsilon}
and
\mu_{\upeta}
.

Optimal values for
\mu_{\upepsilon}(n)
and
\mu_{\upeta}(n)
depend on both the random-walk and linear drift of the carrier and sampling frequency offsets. However, the random-walk and linear drift components are in practice difficult to separate. We propose to calculate the remaining two variable step sizes by assuming that the gradient follows the linear drift and demonstrate in the next section that this leads to a satisfactory result. Given that the channel update step size
\mu_{\mathrm{w}}(n)
has been calculated using (
26
), optimal values for the remaining step sizes
\mu_{\upepsilon}(n)
and
\mu_{\upeta}(n)
can then be calculated based on (
21b
) and (
21c
) as

\displaystyle\mu_{\upepsilon}(n)

\displaystyle=\sqrt[3]{\frac{8\mu_{\mathrm{w}}(n)\left(\bar{\Delta}_{\upepsilon}(n)\bar{\mu}_{\upepsilon}(n)\right)^{2}}{2\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{4}\mu_{\mathrm{w}}(n)\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}\sigma^{4}_{\mathrm{x}}+\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{4}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}}},

(28a)

\displaystyle\mu_{\upeta}(n)

\displaystyle=\sqrt[3]{\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{w}}(n)\left(\bar{\Delta}_{\upeta}(n)\bar{\mu}_{\upeta}(n)\right)^{2}}{2\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{4}\mu_{\mathrm{w}}(n)\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}\sigma^{4}_{\mathrm{x}}+\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{o}}\rVert^{4}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}}},

(28b)

where
\bar{\mu}_{\upepsilon}(n)
and
\bar{\mu}_{\upeta}(n)
are running averages of the step sizes
\mu_{\upepsilon}(n)
and
\mu_{\upeta}(n)
.

III-B

Practical Considerations

In practice, the quantities
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{e}}(n)
,
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{y}}(n)
,
\bar{\Delta}_{\upepsilon}(n)
, and
\bar{\Delta}_{\upeta}(n)
need to be measured. Furthermore, they need to be averaged over time, since the instantaneous error and gradient values are misleading due to the measurement noise and algorithm’s misadjustments. The prevailing method for keeping track of the changes in measurement errors and system parameters in adaptive filters and neural network training methods is to use the exponentially weighted moving average
[
34
]
, a simple and robust approach. The aforementioned quantities can be straightforwardly tracked as exponentially weighted running averages such that

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{e}}(n)=\

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{e}}(n-1)+(1-\lambda_{\mathrm{e}})\lvert e(n)\rvert^{2},

(29a)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{y}}(n)=\

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{y}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{y}}(n-1)+(1-\lambda_{\mathrm{y}})\lvert y(n)\rvert^{2},

(29b)

\displaystyle\bar{\Delta}_{\upepsilon}(n)=\

\displaystyle\lambda_{\upepsilon}\bar{\Delta}_{\upepsilon}(n-1)+(1-\lambda_{\upepsilon})\Delta_{\upepsilon}(n),

(29c)

\displaystyle\bar{\Delta}_{\upeta}(n)=\

\displaystyle\lambda_{\upeta}\bar{\Delta}_{\upeta}(n-1)+(1-\lambda_{\upeta})\Delta_{\upeta}(n),

(29d)

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{e}}
,
\lambda_{\mathrm{y}}
,
\lambda_{\upepsilon}
, and
\lambda_{\upeta}
are the weighting coefficients that control the exponential decay. The step size averages
\bar{\mu}_{\upepsilon}(n)
and
\bar{\mu}_{\upeta}(n)
can be tracked as exponentially weighted moving averages too. However, a practical solution that avoids introducing additional forgetting factors is to use the average of the last
M
step sizes so that
\bar{\mu}_{\upepsilon}(n)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{M}}\sum^{n-1}_{i=n-M}\mu_{\upepsilon}(i)
and
\bar{\mu}_{\upeta}(n)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{M}}\sum^{n-1}_{i=n-M}\mu_{\upeta}(i)
.

Another aspect to consider in practice is the permanence of the measurement noise. In scenarios where the measurement noise is stable, it is sufficient to measure its variance
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}
during a calibration phase when
x(n)
is not being transmitted. However, if the measurement noise variance changes noticeably after calibration, the calibrated measure of
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}
will lead to a poor choice of step sizes. This will almost certainly be the case in practice when the measurement noise includes a background signal(-of-interest)
s(n)
. To tackle this, the measurement noise variance can in practice be estimated with some accuracy and the choice of the optimal measurement noise estimator depends on the statistical properties of the signals involved
[
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
. For the general case of white Gaussian signals, estimating the measurement noise based on the cross-correlation of the known and error signals can be a straightforward and reliable approach
[
38
]
.

The reasoning behind that method relies on the cross-correlation vector between the known signal and the estimation error, herein being given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{y}\mathrm{e}}(n)=\

\displaystyle E\left[\mathbf{y}_{n}e^{j\phi(n)}e^{*}(n)\right]

(30)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 3.8889pt\hskip-3.8889pt\hbox{$=$}\hskip-3.8889pt\hskip-3.28473pt\raisebox{4.66875pt}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Delta$}}\hskip-3.28473pt\hskip 3.8889pt}}\

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{yy}}\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n},

and the variance of the estimation error being

\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{e}}(n)=\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{H}_{n}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{yy}}\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n}+\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}(n).

(31)

Then, the estimate of the measurement noise is defined as

\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}(n)=\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{e}}(n)-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{y}}(n)}\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{y}\mathrm{e}}^{H}(n)\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{y}\mathrm{e}}(n),

(32)

where an estimate of the cross-correlation between the resampled known signal
y(n)
and the error signal
e(n)
is

\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{y}\mathrm{e}}(n)=\lambda_{\mathrm{R}}\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{y}\mathrm{e}}(n-1)+(1-\lambda_{\mathrm{R}})\mathbf{y}_{n}e^{j\phi(n)}e^{*}(n).

(33)

The exponentially weighted running average of estimation error
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{e}}(n)
may result in a lower magnitude than
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}
which would result in negative step sizes. When this happens, the step sizes should be simply set to zero. Furthermore, it can be beneficial to include regularization to avoid divisions by very small values by adding a small positive constant in the denominator of (
26
). Also, if the time-varying characteristic of any of the system parameters are more or less known in advance, the step sizes can be limited to be within the expected sensible step size ranges, therefore avoiding any extreme step size values and improving the stability of the
VSS-FO-LMS
algorithm. Similarly, if the measurement noise estimation in (
32
) is used because the environment is assumed to contain an unknown background signal
s(n)
but the receiver noise floor
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{g}}
is stable and known, then the noise variance estimate can be lower-bounded by the known
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{g}}
in order to, again, improve the stability of the
VSS-FO-LMS
algorithm.

The sampling frequency offset gradient in (
27b
) includes a time derivative of the resampled signal vector. If the third derivative of
\mathbf{y}_{n}
exists, then it is beneficial to use the centered first-order divided difference, which has an approximation error of order two
[
40
, p. 172]
, so that

\mathbf{w}^{H}_{n}\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{n}\approx\frac{\mathbf{w}^{H}_{n}\mathbf{y}_{n+1}-\mathbf{w}^{H}_{n}\mathbf{y}_{n-1}}{2(1+{\eta}(n))}.

(34)

Alternatively, the first-order backward divided difference

\mathbf{w}^{H}_{n}\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{n}\approx\frac{\mathbf{w}^{H}_{n}{\mathbf{y}}_{n}-\mathbf{w}^{H}_{n}{\mathbf{y}}_{n-1}}{1+{\eta}(n)}

(35)

can be used, which does not require computation of
\mathbf{y}_{n+1}
nor the existence of the third derivative, but has an approximation error of order one. In this work, the computationally more complex centered first-order divided difference (
34
) is used due to its higher accuracy providing a better foundation for comparing the estimated and simulated
EMSEs
.

It should also be acknowledged that, like most
SGD
algorithms, the
FO-LMS
may converge to a local minimum when dealing with multimodal error surfaces. In order to guarantee convergence to a global minimum, combining the
FO-LMS
algorithm with global search algorithms
[
41
,
42
]
or advanced
SGD
techniques can be considered
[
43
]
. However, considering these in detail is out of the scope of this paper. Steps of the resulting

In order to verify the theoretical expressions derived for
FO-LMS
in Section

\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{x}}=0

\text{\,}\mathrm{dBW}

\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{v}}=-60

\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^{2}=0

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied the steady-state performance of the
frequency offsets-compensated least mean squares (FO-LMS)
algorithm in the presence of time-varying random and cyclic nonstationarities. In particular, we provided expressions for estimating the algorithm’s
excess mean-squared error (EMSE)
under time varying channels as well as time varying carrier and sampling frequency offsets. We also provided expressions for calculating the algorithm’s optimal step sizes that minimize the
EMSE
but the evaluation of which requires the system parameters to be known. To simplify practical applications where such knowledge cannot be relied on, we proposed a
variable step sizes frequency offsets-compensated least mean squares (VSS-FO-LMS)
algorithm that self-adjusts its step sizes based on its performance.

We verified the
EMSE
and optimal step size expressions of the
FO-LMS
algorithm and evaluated the performance of the proposed
VSS-FO-LMS
algorithm through simulations. The
EMSE
expressions allow us to accurately estimate the algorithm’s performance when the algorithm is in steady state and the optimal step size expressions offer a scalable and low-overhead way of tuning real-time applications when the system parameters are known. The proposed
VSS-FO-LMS
method offers a straightforward way of letting the algorithm itself find a sensible configuration of step sizes when the system parameters are not know, albeit at a moderate performance penalty compared to operation with optimal step sizes. In a follow-up paper
[
46
]
, we analyze how
VSS-FO-LMS
performs when processing measurement data captured with
software-defined radios (SDRs)
.
