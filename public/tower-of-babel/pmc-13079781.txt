The optimization of neuroprosthetic interfaces relying on biophysical and surrogate digital twins
Authors: Claudio Verardo, Veronica Fossati, Laura Toni, Luca Pierantoni, Elena Losanno, Filippo Agnesi, Simone Romeni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The electrical stimulation of the nervous system has shown great clinical potential in injury and pathology, yet experimentally driven practice makes it challenging to identify effective design choices and personalized stimulation protocols. This review outlines emerging model-based optimization frameworks that address these challenges by leveraging biophysical digital twins of neural interfaces. Enabling acceleration strategies and complementary data-driven approaches are also highlighted, along with key factors that currently limit clinical translation.

Introduction

The nervous system plays a role in all bodily functions, serving as a pervasive network of electrical communication between different districts of the body. Thus, the electrical stimulation of the nervous system can produce an enormous set of different responses, depending on the structure targeted by the stimulation and the chosen stimulation protocol. Technological advances introduced in the last years have led to positive clinical impact in the management of an ever increasing number of pathologies and injuries, with applications ranging from spinal cord stimulation (SCS, see Table 1 for all abbreviations used in the text) to reinstate or enhance lower limb functional movements in spinal cord injury patients1,2 or treat chronic neuropathic pain3, stimulation of peripheral nerves (PNS) to produce tactile feedback from a robotic hand in amputees4,5, retinal stimulation for vision restoration in blind patients6, deep brain stimulation (DBS) to improve motor symptoms in Parkinson’s disease7, vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) for treating epilepsy8, and transcranial electrical stimulation (tES) in post-stroke rehabilitation9 (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1Methods to interface with the nervous system through electrical stimulation.Electrical stimulation-based neuroprostheses organized by targeted neural district, including the brain, retina, spinal cord, and peripheral nerves. Effective modulation of bodily functions requires the design of neuroprostheses to attain maximal efficacy accompanied by minimal adverse effects. General design criteria can be determined heuristically based on the target clinical scenario. For example, tactile feedback can be provided at the level of the peripheral nerves in amputees4,10, but needs to be elicited at a more proximal site in spinal cord injury, like the somatosensory cortex11,12, as otherwise the evoked neural flow would not be able to pass through the lesion and reach the brain. However, owing to the complexity of the nervous system, the optimization of a large portion of design specifications is not straightforward. For example, selective VNS is crucial to avoid side effects, including hoarseness, coughing, and migraine13,14, but it can be achieved through intraneural electrodes placed in proximity to target vagal fibers15, as well as by less invasive cuff electrodes delivering innovative stimulation protocols16. Refinement of such design choices is not feasible through experimental approaches alone, as resource and ethical constraints prevent extensive exploration of the large parametric spaces involved, and the insights that can be gathered are inherently limited by measurement techniques. In contrast, computational modeling has the potential to overcome these limitations17, further enabling testing design robustness to inter-subject variability by instantiating multiple models of potential patients18–21. For instance, modeling has informed the design of novel SCS paddle electrodes to improve locomotor outcomes in paralyzed subjects22.

Although principled design can improve the expected efficacy of a neurotechnology, its actual functional outcomes vary across patients due to anatomical differences and uncertainty inherent to device placement procedures. It is thus not possible to determine a priori the best way to operate a neural interface in a target patient, for example, the DBS programming reducing Parkinson’s disease symptoms while minimizing cognitive side effects23. As a result, an optimization step is required to tune the stimulation parameters, and it is typically performed manually by clinicians and engineers during patient hospitalization and follow-up sessions24, Given the significant time and financial stress on healthcare providers, this approach allows exploration of only a limited set of stimulation protocols and is thus inherently suboptimal. Patient-specific computational models, or “digital twins”, are emerging as potential tools to automatize these operations in silico25,26. To this end, personalization of model parameters is crucial to reproduce stimulation outcomes in the target patient, for example, by using data from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and calibration algorithms.

The gold-standard approach for computational models of neuromodulation is the so-called hybrid modeling framework27, which sequentially determines the electric potential distribution generated by an electric charge injection at a set of electrodes and the resulting neural response. Embedding these models within optimization algorithms enables the automatic adjustment of selected input parameters to improve specific metrics of interest, thereby paving the way for in-silico guidance in the design and operation of neurotechnologies. However, due to the high-level of biophysical detail incorporated into such models, optimization can be computationally prohibitive28. For this reason, surrogated models29–31 have been introduced to accelerate the prediction of the neural response to electrical stimulation. Alternative modeling frameworks have instead relied on statistical descriptions of the relationship between some of the parameters controllable by the experimenter and the desired physiological response, enabling data-driven optimization procedures32.

In this narrative review, we will show how hybrid modeling is a comprehensive framework that can be used to simulate the stimulation and recording across districts of the nervous system with minimal variations to the general approach, and how it can be leveraged to perform neuroprosthetic device optimization. We will define in general terms the optimization problems that are relevant to neuromodulation practice and show how hybrid models have been and can be used to perform model-based optimization. We will describe in detail the methods that have been historically proposed to accelerate the evaluation of neural response prediction under electrical stimulation, also exploring completely data-driven approaches. Finally, we will present and address the main challenges that still hinder the widespread adoption of computational models in clinical practice to optimize electrical stimulation applications.

Hybrid models of neural interfaces

Hybrid models allow computing the response of single neurons or their constitutive sections (e.g., axon, soma, dendritic tree) to electrical stimulation27,33–35. In this section, we outline the practical steps involved to instantiate such models (see “Hybrid modeling of neural stimulation”) and how they can be adapted without substantial modifications to simulate neural recording36–38 (see “Hybrid modeling of neural recording”). Additionally, we discuss the theoretical foundation and domain of validity of this modeling approach (see “General assumptions of hybrid modeling”). In the following, we will denote “neural units” the cellular or sub-cellular components whose response is elicited by the stimulation, and “neural structure” the part of the nervous system that is targeted by the interfacing application. In the section “Adapting hybrid models to different districts of the nervous system”, we will describe the involved neural structures and units for the main methods of interfacing with the nervous system.

Hybrid modeling of neural stimulation

Modular structure of hybrid models

Hybrid models of neuromodulation solve in cascade the problem of determining the electrical potential distribution established across a neural structure through current injection via electrodes (volume conduction problem—VC, see “Volume conduction problem”), and the problem of estimating the response of neural units populating the target neural structure in terms of the temporal variation of their membrane potential (neural response problem—NR, see “Neural response problem”). Additionally, the activity of the targeted neural units will produce a physiological effect (for example, a sensation, a movement, or the modulation of organ functions). The problem of converting neural activity to its physiological correlate will be referred to as the neural transduction problem (NT, see “Neural transduction problem”). Volume conduction problem

VC consists of solving Maxwell equations in the target neural structure under injection of electric currents from the stimulating sites (see Fig. 2 (panel 1). This typically simplifies to solving the Laplace’s equation upon adoption of the “quasi-static approximation” (QSA)39, which claims that the modeled media can be regarded as ohmic conductors by neglecting dispersive, reactive, propagation, and non-linear effects. This is reasonably justified within the parameter ranges relevant to neuromodulation applications, which span frequency bands below approximately 100 kHz39.

In order to produce a well-defined electric potential distribution across the target neural structure, Laplace’s equation needs to be coupled with boundary conditions specifying current injection at the active sites and zero electric potential at the ground electrode. In models of implanted interfaces, it is customary to effectively represent the ground by fixing the electric potential at the external surface of the geometry. A more in-depth treatment of the choice of boundary conditions in VC can be found in40, while a more specific discussion of current source modeling is presented in41. The conductivities assigned to tissues typically correspond to average values from experimental measurements, though studies examining the effects of varying these parameters within experimental ranges are available for several neuromodulation applications42–45.

VC can be solved in analytic closed form in the case of point-sources46 or cylindrical electrodes47 located in an infinite homogeneous medium. However, since the solution for even slightly complex geometries can be very complicated48, finite element modeling (FEM) is normally employed in the case of nontrivial geometries. Because the Laplace’s equation is linear, the solution of the VC problem to an arbitrary set of input currents can be derived by superimposing elemental electric potential fields obtained upon monopolar current injection at each active site. These elemental solutions only need to be computed under stationary conditions and then scaled over time according to the desired stimulation waveforms, owing to the time invariance of the Laplace’s equation. The results of this operation are sampled along the target neural units of interest to form what is referred to as lead-field matrix (LFM). This matrix has been called LFM in previous modeling works31,49–52 by borrowing a terminology from electroencephalography (EEG)53,54 research, as its entries can be interpreted as trans-resistance values linking the current injected at an active site with the corresponding extracellular potential evoked at a specific point. We believe this nomenclature better harmonizes the description of the methodological building blocks of hybrid models for neuromodulation with those used in recording applications (see also “Hybrid modeling of neural recording”).

The QSA assumptions are seldom relaxed in hybrid modeling studies, mainly to test their validity based on theoretical grounds55 or through computational analyses56. When tissues are not described as purely resistive media with frequency-independent impedance values, the Laplace’s equation must be solved in the frequency domain. Namely, the stimulation waveform is represented through its Fourier transform and then combined with all frequency-wise contributions of the LFM through the Fourier inverse transform to obtain the time solution for the extracellular potential. This approach allows study the impact of capacitive and dispersive properties of tissues and has been applied to DBS57, SCS44,58, and tES59, and to investigate the effects of electrode-tissue interface impedance60,61. A similar method can be employed to consider propagation and self-induction, but solving the Helmholtz equation in place of the Laplace’s one56. This approach has been used to test the validity of the QSA in less typical frequency ranges, such as those of kHz stimulation62. When the linearity of the involved media is relaxed, for example, to account for non-linear dependence of the electrode-tissue impedance from the applied electric field60,61, non-linear Maxwell equations must be solved39.

Neural response problem

NR consists of determining the time-course of the membrane potential across the target neural units from the extracellular potentials determined through VC (see Fig. 2 (panel 2). Typically, each section (soma, axon, dendrites) of the target neural units is subdivided into small, cylindrical membrane patches called segments or compartments, each communicating with adjacent ones and with extracellular space63. The dynamics of each compartment are characterized by conductance-based lumped-parameter models derived from the Hodgkin–Huxley model64. Models of nerve fibers are particularly well-established65–67, and large libraries of neuron cell models have been created by large-scale initiatives like the Blue Brain Project68 and the Allen Institute69. However, we remark that caution must be paid when employing neuron models for neuromodulation studies, as they typically neglect the description of axons, which are the most excitable components under external electrical stimulation70.

Network-level effects of neuromodulation can be investigated by interconnecting neurons with synapses71. When justified by the volume conduction problem of the target application, only a subset of neural units can be exposed to the applied electric field, while simulating the rest as a spiking neural network (SNN) composed by point neuron models that reduce computational burden by sacrificing morphological detail72. For example, this scheme has been employed in the study of SCS to decouple simulations of the recruitment of dorsal root fibers from the effects on spinal circuits73.

The description of neural units with multicompartmental models simplifies the spatial aspect of NR from a three-dimensional to a one-dimensional problem. The extracellular potential is thus assumed to be uniform across each compartment membrane, and its value is sampled at the compartment center. This implies that neural polarization is driven solely by the axial component of the applied electric field—computed along the symmetry axis of the neural compartments—while possible transverse modes of polarization are ignored74. When the symmetry assumption for the extracellular potential at each compartment is not met, for instance, in the case of a soma polarized by a very close stimulating electrode75, the three-dimensional geometrical extent of neural units cannot be neglected to accurately predict their electrogenic activity (see also “General assumptions of hybrid modeling”).

Neural transduction problem

The NT consists in relating the NR predictions to meaningful quantities describing the physiological effect of stimulation (see Fig. 2 (panel 3). Although previous studies have mostly presented hybrid modeling as a two-step process—using simple quantifications of neural response as endpoints—we explicitly introduce the NT step to emphasize the importance of accurately predicting physiological outcomes. We envision this addition as fundamental to shift the evaluation of neuroprosthesis effectiveness toward more functional and interpretable endpoints.

In applications targeting sensory-motor or visceral functions, the concepts of neural recruitment and selectivity are commonly used to characterize the effect of stimulation protocols. Recruitment is defined as the proportion of neural units activated within a specific functional group and is interpreted as a proxy for the “intensity” of the associate physiological outcome, such as a perceived sensation, a muscle contraction, or the modulation of an organ function. Selectivity indexes are then introduced to quantify the trade-off between the recruitment of target versus off-target groups76,77. These metrics apply well in motor neuroprostheses and bioelectronic medicine applications, where off-target activations may cause unwanted movements78 or severe adverse effects79. They have also been used in sensory neuroprostheses, although their substantial spatial averaging reduces their utility in this context, as the activation of even single units can elicit a percept80. A major limitation of these metrics is that they are often difficult, if not impossible, to compare directly with in vivo measurements, owing to the lack of clear identification of unitary recruitment conditions. For example, maximal muscle contraction is typically associated to the unitary recruitment of a motor pool, but it cannot be evaluated in paralyzed patients. Moreover, recruitment and selectivity inherently do not capture non-linear and temporal aspects of neural encoding, despite growing evidence of their importance across motor80, sensory81–84, and organic functions85,86. Incorporating more detailed models of neural-physiological transduction—for example, musculoskeletal dynamics77, tactile perception87, and cardiac function88—holds promise to overcome these limitations.

Effective modeling of neural transduction remains essentially an open problem in several other neural interfacing applications. Indeed, limitations of current models are often not merely linked to the need to incorporate additional biophysical detail, but rather reflect gaps in our fundamental understanding of specific physiological functions. A prominent example is neuromodulation for analgesia: despite decades of SCS research, a precise characterization of the neural correlates of pain remains elusive17. These challenges are even more pronounced in brain-stimulation approaches designed to treat neurological and neuropsychiatric disorders, such as DBS89 and tES9. In these cases, the mechanisms of action are not only poorly understood but are likely mediated by the engagement of specific brain circuits, requiring models whose complexity can scale to the level of large-scale network dynamics. Advancing neural transduction modeling is therefore tightly coupled to progress in fundamental neuroscience, with data-driven methods—like explainable machine learning90,91—offering promising avenues for deriving accurate transduction rules. Such developments would substantially enhance the value of computational tools for optimizing neurotechnologies across an expanding range of clinical applications.

Hybrid modeling of neural recording

The hybrid modeling framework for neuromodulation can be readily adapted to account for recording applications. In this context, the two conceptual steps of hybrid modeling are reversed in order. First, the electric currents flowing through the membrane of neural units are simulated (NR). Second, they are mapped into recording signals by weighing them with transfer-resistance values describing the sensitivity of the recording site to possible sources in space (VC). Since neural recordings already represent experimentally measurable quantities, the NT and NR problems coincide in this case.

To compute the sensitivity of recording sites, computational models of neural recording make large use of the so-called reciprocity theorem53,54 (a modern introduction to the topic can be found in ref. 38). Reciprocity can be summarized as the fact that if the potential generated by a current injection can be obtained through the multiplication by a resistance value (referred to as transfer-resistance or trans-resistance), switching the potential measurement and current injection points does not change this resistance value. Thus, the reciprocal formalism allows to recast the problem of finding the trans-resistance linking recording electrodes and neural units into the same volume conduction problem used to model the stimulation directionality of such electrodes. Specifically, a VC problem needs to be solved injecting an arbitrary current from each recording site to find the trans-resistance values associated to the neural unit nodes, thereby assembling the LFM. This dramatically reduces the computational burden, as otherwise we would need to solve an intractable number of VC problems equal to the number of neural current sources. For example, an estimate provided in92 predicted about 300,000 VC solutions would be needed to simulate recording without reciprocity in a small rat nerve (200 µm radius and 2 cm length). When the target application allows to assume a homogeneous medium, analytical expressions for the trans-resistances are available in the form of point-source or line-source approximations93–95. As analytical solutions are limited to simplified geometries such as concentric cylinders96 or spheres97, numerical approaches based on FEM (or boundary element methods – BEM) are employed in practice.

Similarly to the neuromodulation setting, the gold standard method to simulate neural sources is through multicompartmental models. Computational speed-up may be obtained by reducing the amount of data to be stored from neural units, depending on the application. For example, by exploiting the redundancy in the transmembrane currents during the propagation of action potentials along nerve fibers in peripheral nerve recordings36,37. Further, when the recording site is sufficiently far from active neurons, such as in EEG and electrocorticography (ECoG) applications, it is possible to use individual electric dipoles to approximate the distributed transmembrane currents of neurons98 or neuron populations99.

General assumptions of hybrid modeling

The fundamental hypothesis at the basis of hybrid modeling of electric stimulation is that neural activity does not influence extracellular potential distributions100, allowing for the decoupling of VC and NR. This may be justified by observing that the strength of electric fields induced by neuromodulation devices is much larger than that coming from individual neural units. Similarly, in recording applications, the hybrid approach dictates that physiologically evoked extracellular potentials do not perturb neural activity101,102. The effect of the microscopic spatial extent of neural units on the electric field is customarily considered through adequate corrections to the bulk properties of the hosting tissue, which typically exhibits lower conductivity values and possible anisotropies compared to the microscopic extracellular medium42. Without the separation of VC and NR, it would be computationally prohibitive to solve neuroengineering problems at the scales of interest in applications (e.g., the simulation of a full nerve section rather than a few nerve fibers).

When the hybrid modeling assumption is relaxed, “self-consistent” solutions of extracellular electric fields and neural dynamics are found75,103. These approaches explicitly represent the geometry of neural units, and are sometimes adopted to test the validity of the hybrid modeling framework in specific application domains104. Further, self-consistent formulations are necessary to describe phenomena that are otherwise ruled out by hybrid modeling. Examples include the study of ephaptic coupling105, namely the interaction between close neural units via extracellular potentials106, and the spike propagation slowing observed in nerve fibers when entering microchannel recording electrodes107. Finally, we note that VC and NR, either solved separately or self-consistently, should be generalized to incorporate the governing equations of other physical phenomena when relevant in the target application, for example, the electrodiffusion of ionic species108,109 or tissue heating110,111.

Adapting hybrid models to different districts of the nervous system

Versatility of hybrid models

Hybrid modeling is a highly flexible framework that can be used to study neural interfacing across the various districts of the nervous system. This feature is fundamental for the development of neuroprostheses, as they are essentially modular technologies that benefit from re-use and adaptation of key components to address clinical needs112. For example, Utah electrode arrays can be employed to record and decode movement intentions in paralyzed patients113, or they can be used to stimulate the somatosensory cortex to provide tactile sensory feedback12, or even implanted into peripheral nerves to perform movement restoration through electrical stimulation114. Despite the adoption of such modular strategies in experimental research, modeling efforts are still predominantly conducted on an application-specific basis by independent groups27,33–35. This fragmentation is inherently suboptimal for advancing neuroprosthetic technologies, as it limits the exchange of domain knowledge that could otherwise benefit progress across multiple applications.

In this section, we aim to bridge this gap by framing existing in-silico investigations of neurotechnological applications through the lens of hybrid modeling. Specifically, we review state-of-the-art modeling pipelines established to study interfaces with peripheral nerves, the spinal cord, retinal structures, and the brain. For each of them, we detail the practical aspects involved in the formulation of the VC and NR problems27, as both require to solve a large set of sub-tasks that vary depending on the target neural structures and units. We note that, as we focus on clinical-grade applications of electrical interfacing, there are additional application domains beyond those discussed here where hybrid modeling is also widely adopted—for example, non-electric modalities such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)115 and magnetoencephalography (MEG)94, and in-vitro devices116.

Peripheral nerves

Applications

Peripheral nerve stimulation is typically performed for motor neuroprosthetics, targeting the median nerve for grasping movements31,78 or the femoral and sciatic nerves for standing and ambulation77,117, as well as for sensory neuroprosthetics, which provide touch and proprioceptive feedback via the median nerve in upper-limb amputees118,119 or the sciatic nerve in lower-limb amputees120–122. Bioelectronic medicine applications have also emerged to treat internal organ diseases by targeting autonomic nerves, including the vagus nerve for cardiovascular and respiratory functions16,123–126, the splenic nerve for inflammatory regulation127, and the pudendal nerve for bladder activity128,129. Neural recordings, either in the form of electroneurography (ENG) or microneurography (µNG), are studied as feedback signals for closed-loop neuromodulation36,37,130. Non-invasive approaches include functional electrical stimulation (FES) of muscles131,132 and transcutaneous (auricular) vagus nerve stimulation (tVNS/taVNS)133,134.

Volume conduction problem

Peripheral nerve models have been the first application of hybrid models, dating back to the seminal work of McNeal in 1976100. While early studies assumed a homogeneous medium for the VC29,100, modern approaches typically represent peripheral nerves with explicit definitions of the endoneurium, perineurium, and epineurium tissues27,77,135,136. Perineurium can be modeled with finite-size sheaths121,135 or through contact impedances31,42 and is normally assumed to have a width of a fixed fraction of fascicle effective diameter in somatic nerves137 or to be a linear function of it in autonomic ones138. Pelot and colleagues have studied in depth how to set FEM parameters for peripheral nerve simulations in41,42. Nerve geometries are typically obtained by extrusion of segmented histological sections18,138, although we recently proposed to adopt circular or elliptical approximations of fascicles27, as this assumption does not substantially alter the estimated recruitment of fibers49. Digital twins fully capturing the curving, splitting, and merging patterns of fascicles—based, for example, on micro-computed tomography (µCT) imaging data139—are not yet available. However, 3D nerve reconstructions have been introduced by lofting histological sections taken centimeters apart121,123. For transcutaneous applications, more complex anatomical models incorporating additional tissue types are used, often derived from MRI data133.

Neural response problem

The neural units in peripheral nerve stimulation are the axons of primary afferent/efferent neurons, also called nerve fibers. Electrical stimulation is applied far away from the two ends of the axons (initial segment and innervation point), and nerve fibers can thus be assimilated to unbranched cables, with no communication between different fibers. This makes the neural response problem “embarrassingly parallel”140,141 and allows important acceleration. The go-to choices are the MRG model for Aα motor fibers65, its modification to model Aβ tactile sensory fibers142, the model of cutaneous C fibers by Sundt and colleagues143, and the model of autonomic C fibers by Tigerholm and colleagues144 (practical considerations regarding the implementation of these C fiber models are reviewed in66). Recently, models of cutaneous and autonomic C fibers better in agreements with experiments have been proposed by Thio and colleagues145. Fiber diameters and locations section is typically assigned according to statistical distributions obtained from previous studies118,146, though retrieving such information directly from immunohistochemical (IHC) sections is also possible123,147. Fiber paths are generated by following the extrusion direction of fascicles, with some degree of tortuosity occasionally incorporated92.

Publicly available resources

Several open-source frameworks are available to instantiate 2D-extruded models of peripheral nerve interfaces, such as ASCENT136, pyPNS92, NRV95, ViNERS148 (a comparison of their functionalities can be found in95). In addition, the VaStim123 pipeline further provides realistic 3D reconstructions of pig vagus nerves.

Spinal cord

Applications

The main applications are related to epidural electrical stimulation (EES)-based motor neuroprosthetics, for restoring lower-limb2,22,73,149–151 and upper-limb152,153 functions, and stimulation to reduce chronic neuropathic pain154–157. Analgesia induced by dorsal root ganglion stimulation (DRGS) can also be studied with similar methods158. Transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation (tSCS) is investigated for motor applications159,160.

Volume conduction problem

Spinal cord models have been introduced to study epidural EES in a sequence of works by Coburn and colleagues in the 1980s161–164. Early works employed simple extruded volume conductors representing the white and gray matter151,161,165. Subsequent studies introduced increasingly realistic geometries, including models based on rat histological sections150, and, later, volume conductors for non-human primate cervical EES that explicitly model nerve roots and anisotropic electrical conductivities aligned to their paths40,152. Such anatomically detailed models have been translated to humans using manual segmentation of MRI and computed tomography (CT) imaging data22, while more recent work has introduced automatic segmentation pipelines to accelerate this process166. While absent in early works150, a realistic representation of the geometry of vertebral structures may play a fundamental role in the field distribution associated with tSCS167.

Neural response problem

Most works on spinal cord stimulation focus on the action of stimulation on spinal roots22,150,151 and/or dorsal column154 fibers, neglecting the activation directly induced by electrical stimulation in motor neurons and interneurons since they populate the gray matter, which is not substantially engaged through EES and tSCS. Thus, NR is essentially analogous to the case of peripheral nerve stimulation. Models including detailed neurons can be useful in cases of intraspinal stimulation, where deeper structures are engaged168. An interesting line of research is the addition of multi-synaptic pathways to study the interaction between flexor-extensor components73. Such circuit model also includes data from musculoskeletal model simulations169, and it was employed to show the interaction between activity induced by electrical stimulation and natural background activity related to muscle stretch149. Recently, the addition to these models of corticospinal residual firing has been studied153. Preliminary accounts have shown that functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) could be used to inform models with subject-specific muscle innervation maps21,22. Circuit-based approaches have also been introduced to study the mechanisms of pain suppression155,170.

Publicly available resources

While no open-source framework is available at the moment to perform the simulation of spinal cord electrical stimulation, RADO-SCS171 contains very detailed CAD files of the different anatomical structures to instantiate human SCS models. A large repository has been shared with152 for the setup of non-human primate SCS models.

Retina and optic nerve

Applications

Retinal prostheses, either epiretinal47 or subretinal172,173, and optic nerve implants174,175 to restore visual sensations in the form of patterns of bright spots called phosphenes.

Volume conduction problem

Models of retinal stimulation range in complexity of the representation of tissues from47,176, where homogeneous isotropic media with disk electrodes sources were employed, to relatively simple slab models including a few layers typically representing electrode, vitreous, retinal and scleral tissues177, to more stratified models including also variations in conductivity associated with nuclear and plexiform layers178,179. The optic nerve, even though being part of the central nervous system, can be simulated with approaches similar to peripheral nerves174. The main peculiarities are that is not fasciculated and it is surrounded by the meninges180.

Neural response problem

While some studies treat retinal ganglion cells as point neurons with a specified receptive field181, they are generally modeled through multicompartment models. Models range from models not including a dendritic tree177,182,183 to models including detailed arborizations47,184. As an example of the latter case, in47, a multicompartment model including a dendritic tree from185 and soma and axon from186. This may stem from the large number of in-vitro studies on the excitability of retinal ganglion cells, possibly stemming for the relative simplicity of setting up such preparations. In187, a model including a simplified retinal network including photoreceptors, horizontal, bipolar, and retinal ganglion cells was tested, comparing different retinal stimulation methods (epiretinal, subretinal, and intraretinal) and levels of retinal degeneration. Another such model, with more complex dynamics, can be found in181. Simulations of optic nerve fibers typically rely on the axon model presented in67,188.

Publicly available resources

While several of the reference works are accompanied by publicly available code repositories, for example47,174, there is currently no fully functional, comprehensive framework in the literature to simulate retinal or optic nerve stimulation through biophysically accurate hybrid models.

Applications

Transcranial stimulation for subthreshold modulation of brain activity, either in the form of transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS)189,190 or transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS)191, and temporal interference stimulation (TIS) to target deep brain regions192,193. DBS for treating a range of brain disorders, including Parkinson’s disease194,195, dystonia196, epilepsy197, depression198, and obsessive-compulsive disorder199, as well as local field potential (LFP) recording for adaptive DBS200. Intracortical microstimulation (ICMS)46,71 and recording201 for brain-computer interface (BCI) applications. EEG and stereotactic EEG (SEEG) recordings for personalized diagnosis and treatment of neurological conditions, such as epilepsy202,203 and Alzheimer’s disease204.

Volume conduction problem

Three fundamental levels of approximation have been introduced in the formulation of brain hybrid models: the assumption of a single homogeneous medium205,206, the use of simplified geometries (like an idealized cortical gyrus207, or the three- and four-sphere head94,208,209), and the derivation of anatomically accurate models from imaging data (e.g., MRI and CT scans)25,189. Modern studies of non-invasive applications rely on head models based on population-averaged templates, such as MNI210 and MIDA211, or personalized on subjects via automatic segmentation of head tissues from imaging data212. Clinical DBS studies instantiate models of targeted brain structures from pre- and post-operative scans, identifying gray and white matter regions by normalization into a reference atlas, like the MNI space, followed by localization of the stimulation leads213. More detailed DBS models are often employed for fundamental investigations, by deriving heterogeneous anisotropic conductivities from diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)214 through the method presented in215. When the stimulation sites are much smaller than the spatial extension of neural units, like in the case of intracortical implants, it is customary to assume a homogeneous medium46.

Neural response problem

Whenever cellular effects are of interest, compartmental models of complex neurons are needed. For applications involving cortical structures, libraries of reconstructed neurons are available for murine models68,69,216, which have recently adapted for human studies70. In the study of DBS, the modeled neural units are typically local axons, as early studies identified in their excitation the main mediator of therapeutic outcomes compared to local cells217,218. To this end, the MRG fiber model65 represents the gold standard, and realistic fiber paths can be reconstructed through DTI26.

Publicly available resources

Software like SimNIBS219,220 and ROAST221 is commonly used to solve the volume conduction problem in non-invasive brain stimulation approaches. Software like LeadDBS222 is available for DBS applications. Simulations of brain recordings from morphological neurons or equivalent dipoles can be performed with LFPy93,94 in homogeneous media or simplified geometries (e.g., four-sphere head model), while BlueRecording38 supports volume conductor problems to be solved numerically.

Model-based optimization

Optimization problems are pervasive in the field of neural interfacing, and hybrid modeling represents a promising framework for addressing them in silico. In this section, we first introduce the formalism used to instantiate these problems, along with some considerations regarding their specific characteristics in neural engineering applications (see “Taxonomy and formulation of optimization problems”). We then illustrate how these methodologies can be integrated in general workflows to support the development and utilization of neurotechnologies (see “Framework for optimizing the design and operation of neural interfaces”).

Taxonomy and formulation of optimization problems

An optimization problem is a process allowing the determination of the values of a certain set of optimization variables that maximize or minimize a given objective function (see Table 2 for detailed definitions of optimization-related terms). In the following, we will discuss how the choice of optimization variables allows categorizing optimization problems according to whether the parameters to be tuned can or cannot be controlled in experiments (see “Optimization to improve performance or calibrate models”), and what differentiates such problems from traditional comparisons of predefined sets of alternative scenarios (see “Optimization vs. assessment”). Further, we highlight how, to date, the feasibility of solving optimization problems is tightly intertwined with the need of varying the geometry of the underlying models (see “Optimization problems requiring geometrical variations”). Then we will discuss the different choices available for the objective function (see “Which objective function to optimize”), to end with considerations on the algorithms/heuristics employed to solve the optimization problem (see “Optimization routines”). Optimization to improve performance or calibrate models

In the context of neural interfacing, optimization routines can be employed to tune the values of controllable and uncontrollable parameters, which are defined according to whether their values can or cannot be modified by experimenters (see Fig. 3A).

Controllable parameters typically consist of those involved in the improvement of the neuroprosthesis performance, encompassing the stimulation protocols that it can administer to produce a certain effect31,223, the geometry of the interface31,129, and its placement31,78 (for simplicity, we will refer to them as “neuromodulation parameters”).

Instead, uncontrolled parameters include the structural and functional organization of the target neural district, as well as the conductivity of its tissues (for simplicity, we will name them “neural target parameters”). Since several of these parameters tend to be difficult to measure directly in vivo, optimization processes can help in calibrating their values, matching experimental observations of indirect physiological phenomena. For example, tuning could be used to establish the fiber clusters associated to different functions within a peripheral nerve from muscle recruitment curves224, evoked compound action potentials225, or spontaneous activity recordings226.

Optimization vs. assessment

Works on computational models have mostly focused on the characterization of a predefined small set of alternative scenarios, choosing the parameter values leading to highest values of objective functions22,77,117,120,227,228. We refer to these approaches as “assessments”, as compared to the previously introduced optimization problems (see Fig. 3B).

The need to explore a limited portion of the parameter space was likely linked to the large computational time required to evaluate more pervasively the parameter space, as it is commented in28 with respect to the computational burden for parameter exploration performed in77. Normally, when a parameter space is explored manually, it is often explored following regular subdivisions, which may prevent exploring correctly optimal parameter combinations. In fact, we expect optimal parameter combinations to be clustered in the parameter space, which would require to sample more samples from interesting parts of the parameter space. Optimization algorithms are needed for this very reason. They allow balanced exploration and exploitation in an automatic way, focusing on sampling interesting parts of the search space.

Optimization requiring geometrical variations

The formulation of hybrid models as a cascade of VC and NR imposes a natural distinction between optimization problems that require or do not require geometrical variations (see Fig. 3C). Indeed, changes to the 3D domains occupied by the different materials, to the applied boundary conditions, and to the set of points where the LFM must be evaluated require solving again both VC and NR. For the sake of clarity, Fig. 3D illustrates common optimization problems aimed at improving device performance or calibrating models, distinguishing between those that involve geometrical variations and those that do not.

If no geometry changes are required, the only computationally intensive task remains NR, which can be accelerated in several ways (see “Acceleration through surrogate models”). VC is conceptually more difficult to accelerate, and, therefore, FEM simulations are seldom performed within optimization algorithms. For example, placements of tDCS electrodes have been optimized229 using the efficient implementation of FEM solver provided by SimNIBS230. As a result, optimal geometries are commonly determined by performing a limited set of simulations corresponding to feasible designs and determining optimal stimulation protocols for each geometry77,117,227,231,232. To enable “true” geometry optimization without the need to run multiple FEMs, biophysically grounded objective functions based solely on geometrical features have been proposed for peripheral nerve interfaces31,129 and visual neuroprostheses233,234.

Which objective function to optimize

When improving neuromodulation performance, it is common to consider static features related to neural activation as objective functions. For example, stimulation protocols were optimized in terms of selectivity to target vs. off-target fibers in peripheral motor neuroprostheses31,223, VNS51,125, and DBS235. The electric field is also a common optimization target in applications where the spatial scales involved are much larger than individual neural units. For instance, electric field intensity and focality at a target region were maximized for tDCS purposes52,236. Temporal features of neural activity are instead more rarely taken into account. Evoked firing rates were used to optimize stimulation protocols for optic nerve implants237, while fitness functions based on activation patterns of targeted neural populations were employed for DBS applications238–240 and intracortical sensory neuroprostheses241. Non-neural features can also be considered as objectives, for instance, to reduce power consumption242.

Calibration problems make instead use of correlates of neural activity that can be meaningfully put in comparison with experimental measurements. For example, the spatial distributions of fiber groups in peripheral nerves were inferred from the mismatch between measured and predicted recruitment curves224 or evoked compound action potentials225. In the context of large-scale brain modeling, SEEG and EEG recording features were used to personalize neurodegenerative204 and epileptogenic202,203 parameters. We note that these latter works make extensive use of Bayesian inference methods to formulate the calibration problem. They rely on posterior distributions that characterize the probability of parameters of interest given a set of experimental measurements. However, as this statistical formulation lies outside the optimization schemes discussed here, we refer the reader to more specialized accounts for a comprehensive treatment243.

Optimization routines

The high complexity of the exact mappings between parameters and neural response features normally leads to treating objective functions as black box functions. Coherently, gradient-free optimization approaches like genetic algorithms (GAs), particle swarm optimization (PSO), differential evolution (DE), and Bayesian optimization (BO) are most frequently employed244–246. For example, GAs have been employed to identify energy-efficient stimulation waveforms242 and optimal temporal patterns of pulses in DBS238–240. PSO has been used to find stimulation protocols maximizing muscle-wise selectivity in PNS31 and spatial selectivity in DBS235. DE has enabled the identification of optimal montages in tES applications229.

Even though some rules of thumb exist to direct the choice of optimization routine or to tune their hyperparameters247–249, there are no strong theoretical results on their convergence (determination of local or global optima, convergence speed, etc.). For example, GAs and PSO tend to work better than BO on very high dimensional optimization problems250,251 but may require a larger number of evaluation points since they do not exploit any explicit representation of the optimization landscape. At the same time, BO requires to fit a Gaussian process (GP) to the observed optimization variables—objective function pairs, which takes more time the more data has been collected, whereas GAs and PSO are very fast to produce candidate optimization variable values. For these reasons, BO is particularly suited to perform optimization in the case of high (ethical and economical) data sampling costs246, and is at the basis of most current approaches to data-driven optimization (see “Data-driven optimization”).

In contrast, when no geometrical variation is involved in the optimization process, and the objective function is linearly related to the generated electrical field, the problem can be formulated as a convex optimization task252. In fact, the field can be computed as a linear combination of precomputed LFM columns corresponding to the different stimulating electrodes. Examples of such approaches have been proposed by assuming linear features of the electric field as an estimator of neural response232,236,253,254 (see “Acceleration through surrogate models”).

Framework for optimizing the design and operation of neural interfaces

At the design stage, model-based optimization can assist in refining prototypes of neural interfaces to achieve desired outcomes (see Fig. 4A). To this end, parameters that should be possible to treat as optimization variables include the number of active sites and their position in space, along with the applied stimulation protocols. Tailored objective functions can then be used to robustly evaluate performance against inter-subject variability and uncertainties inherent to implantation procedures. This can be achieved by considering a population of plausible models of the neural district of interest, obtained by combining atlases of imaging data with statistical distributions of structural and functional properties. Recent studies integrating all these aspects represent the blueprints for such a design framework, spanning peripheral interfaces for motor31 and autonomic129 functions, as well as retinal233 and intracortical234 prostheses for vision restoration. During the operation of a neuroprosthetic interface, we can distinguish two separate steps consisting of the construction of a personalized biophysical model and then the use of such model to perform the optimization of stimulation protocols based on predicted responses (see Fig. 4B). Promising examples of this workflow have been demonstrated in DBS applications, where stimulation protocols derived from digital twins of patients have prospectively outperformed manual tuning in reducing Parkinson’s disease symptoms while minimizing side effects194,257. We emphasize, however, that model personalization remains the most critical aspect, as imaging data typically provides direct information on only a subset of the relevant structural and functional properties49, while calibration procedures are still in early development224,225. The non-stationarity of neural interfacing systems introduces further difficulty, as anatomical and physiological parameters may require re-adjustment over relatively short time scales. Integrating biophysical modeling with data-driven methods may offer viable strategies to address these challenges (see “Data-driven optimization”).

We highlight that two major limitations remain to be addressed in future developments of these frameworks. First, objective functions are still evaluated at the level of volume conduction or neural response within the hybrid modeling framework, rather than in the neural transduction domain. For example, the optimization of upper-limb motor neuroprostheses relies on fiber recruitment metrics instead of features of the evoked hand movements31. The development and integration of refined transduction models will enable the use of more functional and interpretable endpoints to guide the optimization process (see “Neural transduction problem”). Second, the need to ensure computational feasibility has driven the adoption of substantial simplifications in the level of detail used to describe biophysical phenomena. This constrains the range of dynamical interactions and input–output relationships that can be captured. For instance, optimization frameworks for cortical visual prostheses cannot simulate percepts arising from the concurrent stimulation of multiple brain areas, nor account for the effects of neuroplasticity234. Incorporating more biophysical detail while preserving computational tractability will require extending the available set of model acceleration strategies (see “Acceleration through surrogate models”).

Finally, we remark that the aforementioned optimization schemes should not be regarded as rigid methodological approaches to the development and translation of neural interfaces, but rather as complementary tools. Model-based optimization is particularly well suited to problems in which controllable parameters are left free to vary inside large intervals to find the best choices between all the available ones. In contrast, other clinical questions call for different approaches. For example, assessment procedures are required when comparing a set of predefined alternatives, such as evaluating a novel electrode against existing devices129,189 or establishing the best implantation site among surgically feasible candidates78,120. In this light, all these in-silico methodologies represent modular “building blocks” that can be instantiated and integrated to address specific needs. In combination with preclinical and clinical experimentation, they hold promise for strengthening the evidence for efficacy and safety of neurotechnological approaches, thereby facilitating their regulatory approval17.

Acceleration through surrogate models

Optimization generally requires testing a very large number of candidates to determine the best values for the optimization variables223. Even when performing comparisons and assessments, the unavoidable uncertainty on neural target parameters would require robustness analyses to draw reliable conclusions77,117. While biophysically accurate computational models hold the potential to cover larger parameter spaces compared to in vivo experiments, such explorations can result in prohibitive computation times28. Surrogate models (also called meta-models in some works)258,259 allow the approximation of specific input-output relationships, abstracting from the biophysical detail, and are thus an interesting possibility to accelerate simulations. In the past, the acceleration of NR has seen substantial research effort, which is reviewed in the present section.

Solving this problem is fundamental for neurotechnology applications across all regions of the nervous system, as axons are typically the most excitable parts of neural units under electrical stimulation. Theory-driven surrogate models

From the very first years after the introduction of compartmental models of fiber excitation by McNeal100 it was clear that simulating the response to electrical stimulation of long chains of detailed nonlinear systems defining membrane dynamics would have been computationally prohibitive, leading to the introduction by Rattay of the so-called “activating function” (AF) formalism29. The AF corresponds to the second difference of the extracellular potential, and its value is positively correlated with the likelihood that stimulation will generate an action potential. This reduced NR to find a suitable threshold for the AF for a given fiber model and stimulation configuration. An alternative approach was to exploit that membrane conductances are almost constant in the subthreshold regime and employ a linear model of subthreshold excitation to find threshold stimulation intensities262. Subsequently, Warman et al.263 introduced the “driving function” (DF) method, which combined linear models and the AF formalism by adding to the latter a term accounting for current internal redistribution and stimulus pulse-width dependency. Even with such modifications, the dependency upon fiber-to-electrode distance in activation thresholds264 led to the need to establish active/inactive separation hypersurfaces, namely decision boundaries, in the space of the several parameters influencing activation28. Despite their inherent limitations, theory-driven surrogate models are still often in use, for instance, in peripheral sensory neuroprostheses87, VNS51, and DBS235,265 applications. Recently, a new method named “generalized activating function” (GAF) has been introduced to predict fiber activation based on the Green’s function formalism266.

Machine learning-based surrogate models

Since the main focus of computational models of neuromodulation has mainly been on the prediction of neural activation, we can see the neural response problem as a classification problem, where once we have established a given fiber model, we want to predict whether the set of extracellular potentials leads to an inactive (class 0) or an active (class 1) fiber. The set of features to predict activation can then be expanded, for example adding to the extracellular potential the stimulation pulse-width and the fiber diameter. Traditional machine learning can then be employed to perform such a classification task. When deciding which machine learning classifiers to employ, one natural choice is given by multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), which perform linear combinations followed by nonlinear activation functions of the features. Indeed, both the original AF introduced by Rattay29 and its generalization DF introduced by Warman et al.263 estimate activation from a linear combination of extracellular potentials. The idea that the estimation of fiber activation could be performed using MLPs has been introduced independently by our group in the study of PNS31 and by Golabek et al.30 for DBS. In following works, we then showed that more recently introduced methods derived from random forests, like LightGBM267 and XGBoost268 can lead to improved performance50 in activation decoding, and to some extent of generalizability of prediction ability when changing the set of stimulation sites and nerve sections.

In several applications, though, it is more likely that mere activation may not provide a sufficient description of stimulation outcomes, and that the prediction of firing rate—or even single spike timing—may be necessary (see “Neural transduction problem”). Firing rate prediction can be seen as a regression problem, namely the prediction of a scalar value from a set of features describing the time-varying stimulation of fibers. Most importantly, firing rate depends on above-threshold fiber response and cannot thus be determined with the theory-driven models presented in the last section. Using a random forest regressor, we showed that it is possible to predict the firing rate of optic nerve fibers under electrical stimulation237. To the best of our knowledge, surrogate models capable of predicting the full spatiotemporal response of fibers have not yet been presented. However, a significant simulation speed-up of multicompartmental fiber models was achieved through efficient implementation in software libraries designed for deep learning applications125, thereby exploiting the hardware acceleration provided by graphical processing units. Advancements in this direction could facilitate the optimization of biomimetic stimulation strategies aimed at a more natural restoration of lost or impaired functions87,269.

Multiscale models of neural response

The surrogate models discussed previously focus on accelerating the solution of the neural response problem—or certain aspects of it—while preserving a morphologically accurate description of each neural unit. However, larger-scale estimate of neural activity may be sufficient in specific applications72. This motivates the use of “coarse-grained” models270, which replace the description of individual neural units with spatiotemporal averages of their activity. Although less detailed, these approaches enable modeling frameworks that are substantially more computationally tractable (see Fig. 5B).

In DBS, for instance, there is a long-standing tradition of relying on the concept of volume of tissue activated (VTA), which estimates the spatial region occupied by neural units driven above threshold during stimulation. This volume is determined by considering representative axons placed perpendicularly to the electrode and fitting an ellipsoid that circumscribes those activated271. To this end, it is possible to use predictors of fiber activation, such as isosurfaces of the activating function214 or the electric field magnitude272, and machine learning algorithms directly predicting the ellipsoid parameters from stimulation settings273. The VTA can then be combined with connectivity information—established through structural (DTI-based) or functional (fMRI-based) imaging data—to identify the large-scale brain circuits engaged by the stimulation195. We note that the concept of VTA has also been introduced in other applications like intracortical stimulation234, where quadratic current-distance relationships are often used to represent the thresholds required to excite local neural units46.

Recent developments have also led to the emergence of whole-brain models (WBMs), enabling simulations of neural dynamics at the scale of the entire brain243. Although clinically introduced to guide surgical planning in refractory epilespy202, these models are gaining growing interest in neuromodulation applications203, like DBS274 and tES275. WBMs are obtained by interconnecting neural mass models72, each describing the average activity of a specific brain region, and can be implemented through well-established software platforms like TVB276. However, integrating WBMs into hybrid modeling pipelines requires adapting the standard workflow to interface with the VC step. In tES applications, a possible strategy is the “lambda-E” coupling275, whereby the mean membrane potential predicted by neural mass models is modulated through a term that varies linearly with the applied electric field strength. This builds upon the microscopic effect of tES, where the polarization of the most sensitive neural subunits—such as axon terminals—is proportional to the local electric field strength70,277. We note that a precursor of this approach has been extensively used in previous studies156,236 under the name of quasi-uniform assumption (QUA)278, which prescribes using the applied electric field as direct proxy of local neural polarization.

Data-driven optimization

Hybrid models rely on accurate representations of the anatomical and functional features of the target neural system. Building personalized models that may be employed to optimize the operation of neural interfaces is challenging and requires extensive experimental data, including electrophysiology and imaging, and a large set of strong assumptions on the inner workings of the nervous system. Even so, biophysical models struggle to adapt to time-dependent changes279, since recalibration may require an important amount of new data.

Data-driven methods are an alternative approach that can solve some of these challenges (see Fig. 5C). The input-output relationship between stimulation and evoked physiological or behavioral response is retrieved directly from data recorded during the optimization process, and modeled through universal function approximators, such as neural networks or GPs. An acquisition function then determines which candidate parameter values to explore next, considering the fit statistical model and the optimization objective function. The flexibility of data-driven methods has enabled their use across various neuroprosthetic applications: motor280,281 and sensory restoration282,283, treatment of neurological disorders284,285, and autonomic function regulation286 targeting different districts of the nervous system, including the brain32,287, spinal cord281,288, and peripheral nerves280.

A key requirement for data-driven optimization is the ability to measure in real-time some correlates of the applied stimulation. These correlates range from measurements of neural responses evoked by stimulation, such as cortical recordings289 and fMRI290 in DBS or compound action potentials in peripheral nerve stimulation286, to more functionally relevant correlates like evoked electromyography (EMG)291 or kinematic data32 for motor neuroprostheses, physiological markers such as heart rate for vagus nerve stimulation286, or subjective reports of sensation location and intensity for sensory restoration282,283.

The choice of the employed input-output data-driven model/function approximator can fall on different methods. In281, a “forward” neural network was used to predict the EMG outputs in response to EES in a sheep model, while a “feedback” network leveraged the “forward” model to guide EES optimization. The disadvantages of neural networks are that they require substantial training data and are static once trained, which limits their adaptability in the case of non-stationarities of the effect of neuromodulation due to factors such as neuroplasticity or changes at the neural interface. Other methods have been shown to support re-calibration to compensate for temporal changes in the neuromodulation effect283,288. For example, BO models the unknown input-output function using probabilistic models, usually GPs, and employs acquisition functions to guide the search over the parameter space, balancing exploration of the whole space and exploitation of the most promising regions. Because BO does not require extensive pre-training and can incorporate prior knowledge32, it is especially well-suited for rapid recalibration of neuroprostheses283. Its flexibility also enables multi-objective optimization, which is useful, for example, when aiming to balance treatment efficacy against side effects284. A key advantage of GPs lies in their ability to both predict the output across parameter space and to estimate the uncertainty of these predictions.

Comparing Fig. 5C with Fig. 4B, it becomes clear how BO-based data-driven optimization differs drastically from model-based optimization. In BO, the objective function quantifying the effects of electrical stimulation is statistical rather than biophysical, and it is fitted during the optimization process, which has several important consequences. First, the choice of the candidate stimulation protocols is performed on a model that is evolving and may not be very representative of the true objective function landscape in early iterations. Second, the optimization algorithm needs to balance exploration of possibly suboptimal stimulation protocols to produce a reliable model of the objective function, and the exploitation of promising candidate protocols based on the current model. Additionally, variability and noise in neurophysiological data can undermine stability if not properly accounted for, and hyperparameter choices can significantly affect convergence. On the contrary, model-based optimization leads to having the best available estimates of the response of the system at any timepoint during optimization, but inaccuracies in the model calibration may completely undermine the optimization process. A promising direction is to integrate biophysical models with data-driven methods: initial priors can be defined with biophysical models, and then refined through data-driven optimization for personalized treatment284,292. The convergence of these approaches may allow substantial improvements by exploiting the best of both worlds.

Outlook: the translation to the clinical practice

Barriers to the use of computational models in clinical practice

While the understanding that can be gained by models often goes beyond what is possible by simple intuition and can thus contribute to improving clinical practice293, several challenges need to be addressed.

Existing commercial devices are not developed with the idea to support automatic optimization of stimulation protocols. The strict safety regulations for class III devices and related cybersecurity concerns294 encourage manufacturers to minimize possible usage of the device not envisioned during development and regulatory approval processes. The scarcity of fully programmable neural stimulators means that the many different test stimulations to be performed during an optimization procedure need to be manually input through the graphical interface of the clinician programmers provided by the manufacturers, narrowing down the speed advantages provided by automated optimization. Moreover, synchronization triggers are often absent from commercial stimulation devices, making it difficult to gain a solid quantitative measurement of the physiological correlates of the stimulation employed both in data-driven and model-based optimization, as well as in the process of biophysical model validation.

Collaboration between device makers and the research community may help the development of fully programmable devices compliant with all safety norms imposed by regulatory agencies. The latter may be, in principle, favorable to the use of models as supporting data for clinical applications, as ways to reduce the need to preclinical and clinical testing. A track record showing safety and effectiveness for each target application will substantially simplify regulatory processes required for the approval of the use of software supporting clinical decisions. To motivate device makers to adapt their products, the complexity and costs associated will need to compare favorably with the payout delivered295,296 since anything that changes a clinical practice requires significant payout for several stakeholder297. An important future challenge will be to establish a framework for legal and ethical responsibilities to govern clinical use of models.

Once optimized neuroprosthetic devices are developed and their use approved, the biggest challenges will probably be related to actual clinical adoption. The integration of computational models into clinical practice may require some changes to well established routines298, which are likely to be put in place only if they will lead to improved clinical outcomes on the short term (e.g., increased functionality with electrical stimulation assistance) and on the long term (e.g., improved longitudinal results, reduced disability costs throughout patients’ lives) without complicating the existing clinical workflow.

Another challenge in the development of automatic optimization methods is the large numbers of potential side effects that candidate stimulation protocols may cause while not being directly measurable through the physiological signals used for data-driven optimization, or that have not been included into the neural transduction in model-based optimization. For example, it is known that SCS can produce uncomfortable paresthesias155,299, and DBS can lead to a large variety of side effects including numbness, trouble with speech or balance, lightheadedness, mood changes such as anger, mania and depression300,301. While some of these effects may be captured by computational models as off-target activation, capturing patients’ subjective tradeoffs may be particularly complex (e.g., the level at which a side effect is tolerable compared with the benefit of a given stimulation protocol).

The road towards computational modeling adoption

In the following, we will trace a set of steps that we believe will accelerate clinical adoption, ease the interaction with possible industrial partners, and convince regulatory agencies of the potential of computational models and automatic optimization of neuroprosthetic applications.

First, the large number of simplifying assumptions at the basis of any computational model can easily demotivate researchers from other fields and clinicians about its capability to capture the complex essence of a clinical setup and thus provide useful indications for example in the choice of stimulation parameter values. Effort should be put in transparent reporting of matches and mismatches with experimental models, and adaptation of computational models to solve such mismatches. See for example the indication that past models of C fibers do not match experimental data of electrical stimulation thresholds124 and the following modifications to such models trying to match existing evidences about C fibers145. This kind of modeling efforts transmit a sense of honesty that leads non-experts to trust modeling results rather than sounding naïve over-optimistic estimates. In parallel to this, works investigating the limits of neuroprosthetic devices or applications allow us to make good use of simplifying assumptions: if something is difficult or impossible considering several simplifying assumptions, it is very credible that removing such assumptions will further degrade the result. For example, in175 we claim that optic nerve electrical stimulation is very unlikely to provide a visual acuity comparable to retinal stimulation, but that this downside may be outplayed by the possibility to cover a large part of the visual field. Thus, rather than focusing on high visual acuity, optic nerve neuroprostheses should focus on exploiting this theoretical advantage in order to establish as an alternative technology.

Second, tools to formulate and solve simplified models by non-experts should be developed. Such tools would need to be distributed as standalone applications, without the need to set up complex workspaces with the need for communication between several open-source and proprietary software. Initiatives providing cloud computing capacity and graphical user interfaces like OSParc are paramount. Simplified models, like circular-fascicle nerves27 should not be seen as replacements for personalized models. Rather, they should be used to train intuition about how currents spread in different biological tissues and what the consequences are in terms of simple quantities like activation, VTA, and firing rate of the neural units involved. In this respect, the choice of software like Cicerone302 to visualize VTA has been particularly successful. Similar tools may be developed for applications like SCS, where different multipolar patterns may be used to increase selectivity of stimulation protocols, providing an intuitive understanding of how the positioning of anodes and cathodes influences the recruited structures.

Third, models should be pushed towards the prediction of physiologically measurable quantities rather than abstract quantities related to neural activation. For example, simulated prosthetic vision consists of representing the visual perceptions evoked by specific stimulation protocols175,303,304 and thus provides a very intuitive means for non-expert users, clinicians, and patient family members to visualize the functional gain that goes with the implant and use of a visual prosthesis. For movement restoration, musculoskeletal models169,305 may be cascaded to hybrid models to produce simulated movement that may be measured in the target patient through inertial sensors306 or camera systems307. The introduction of neural transduction models will solve problems linked to the optimization of correct variables and would also increase the ease to interpret simulation results by non-experts. Of course, the addition of further processing steps will worsen the computational burden of the whole modeling framework, and a surrogate of neural transduction will also need to be developed.

Fourth, rather than providing a result (optimal stimulation protocol, optimal electrode placement), optimization should produce a narrowing down of the search space, allowing manual refinement by clinicians. This intrinsically includes the idea that robustness/sensitivity analyses should accompany any modeling attempt aimed at clinics. The definition of a region of feasibility will allow more tolerance to suboptimal assumptions, be it biophysical general assumptions (quasi-staticity, reciprocity, etc) or assumptions on the specific patient anatomy/functional organization. This narrowing down can be produced by biophysical computational models, but may also consist of data collection, organization, and fitting tools similar to those employed during data-driven optimization. Daily stimulation protocol exploration or manual optimization procedures would thus be fed into organized databases, equipped with statistical fitting/regularization methods/uncertainty estimation (e.g., Gaussian process regression), producing intuitive visualization of explored parameter space regions, response variability, parameter sampling recommendations.

Finally, recalibration of model parameters characterizing the target neural district should be incorporated into these data acquisition routines. A set of maximally uncertain parameters should be a priori established, and models should be recalibrated iteratively as data on the target patient are accumulated. As already mentioned above, the immediate need to integrate acquired data into a software framework will also enforce good data maintenance and reproducibility policies that will produce data organized to ease any kind of a posteriori quantitative analysis.

Conclusions

In the present narrative review, we have covered how frameworks for the formulation and simulation of hybrid models of neuromodulation can be easily adapted to switch across neural districts, target clinical applications, and even between neural stimulation and recording. We have explored recent works on the use of such models for model-based optimization, providing some conceptual remarks on how such optimization should be formulated and carried out. Acceleration methods to speed up the evaluation of neural response to electrical stimulation have been covered as a fundamental tool enabling optimization. Finally, we highlighted significant challenges and possible roadmaps lying ahead of the modeling community to maximize adoption of computational tools in clinical settings. Computational models have the potential to provide mechanistic understanding, reduce the ethical cost of animal experimentation and patient testing, and increase neuroprosthesis effectiveness and adoption. Additionally, their systematic adoption inside of experimental/clinical practice will also very likely lead to efforts to systematize data acquisition and handling so that they can be streamlined into computational methods, producing indirect benefits to the whole scientific community.
