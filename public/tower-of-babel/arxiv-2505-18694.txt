AI-Driven Climate Policy Scenario Generation for Sub-Saharan Africa
Rafiu Adekoya Badekale, Adewale Akinfaderin

Abstract
Climate policy scenario generation and evaluation have traditionally relied on integrated assessment models (IAMs) and expert-driven qualitative analysis. These methods enable stakeholders, such as policymakers and researchers, to anticipate impacts, plan governance strategies, and develop mitigation measures. However, traditional methods are often time-intensive, reliant on simple extrapolations of past trends, and limited in capturing the complex and interconnected nature of energy and climate issues. With the advent of artificial intelligence (AI), particularly generative AI models trained on vast datasets, these limitations can be addressed, ensuring robustness even under limited data conditions. In this work, we explore the novel method that employs generative AI, specifically large language models (LLMs), to simulate climate policy scenarios for Sub-Saharan Africa. These scenarios focus on energy transition themes derived from the historical United Nations Climate Change Conference (COP) documents. By leveraging generative models, the project aims to create plausible and diverse policy scenarios that align with regional climate goals and energy challenges. Given limited access to human evaluators, automated techniques were employed for scenario evaluation. We generated policy scenarios using the llama3.2-3B model. Of the 34 generated responses, 30 (88%) passed expert validation, accurately reflecting the intended impacts provided in the corresponding prompts. We compared these validated responses against assessments from a human climate expert and two additional LLMs (gemma2-2B and mistral-7B). Evaluation results show the model’s strong performance in generating factually accurate (faithfulness: 0.760 for human, 0.966 for gemma2-2B, and 0.848 for mistral-7B), highly relevant (answer relevancy: 0.993 for human, 0.888 for gemma2-2B, and 0.893 for mistral-7B), and well-contextualized (context utilization: 0.713 for human, 0.939 for gemma2-2B, and 0.980 for mistral-7B) scenarios. Spearman correlation analysis between human and model evaluations confirms a high degree of alignment, highlighting the reliability of automated evaluation. Our structured, embedding-based evaluation framework shows that generative AI effectively generate scenarios that are coherent, relevant, plausible, and diverse. This approach offers a transformative tool for climate policy planning in data-constrained regions.

Methodology

2.1
Data collection and preprocessing

We collected 94 COP documents from the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) website, covering all conference meetings (convention, kyoto protocol, and paris agreement). We organized documents by meeting and processed them using LangChain’s PyPDFLoader. Preprocessing involved chunking with RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter (chunk size: 1000, overlap: 100) and embedding stored in a chroma vector database using ollama embeddings (model="nomic-embed-text:latest"). Quality control included manual verification and automated filtering to remove duplicates.

2.2
Scenario generation

We used llama3.2-3B to generate scenarios with 34 zero-shot prompts, via a RAG pipeline grounded in the UN COP documents. The prompts were crafted from themes such as renewable energy adoption and technology transfer, identified via the same RAG setup. We set temperature to zero for consistency. To ground the generated scenarios in actionable steps (e.g., KPIs, milestones), we employed llama3-8B to prompt additional questions.

Example prompt:

"You are a climate policy expert tasked with generating innovative and actionable future climate policy scenarios. Your responses should be relevant, detailed, and well-structured.

Generate potential future climate policy scenarios for Sub-Saharan Africa that focus on accelerating renewable energy adoption. Consider policy incentives, grid integration challenges, and mechanisms for ensuring equitable access to clean energy.”

Example follow-up prompt:

“Outline a clear implementation roadmap for each of the generated scenarios with well defined milestones. Define short-term (0-2 years), medium-term (3-7 years), and long-term (8+ years) milestones. Highlight critical decision points, dependencies, and risks that could impact progress. Provide specific action steps that should be taken at each milestone to ensure scenario success.”

Having generated the model responses, we removed any extraneous text that did not align with the structure of the scenarios. This includes section headers and bullet points, which were sometimes added at the beginning or end of the generated responses. The main body of the generated scenarios remained intact and was not subject to manual or automated corrections of any type.

2.3
Scenario validation and evaluation

Two authors independently validated the generated scenarios. Out of 34 generated responses, 30 (88%) were deemed valid (highly depicting designated impacts provided in the corresponding prompt). We evaluated the quality of the validated responses using a three-metric Retrieval-Augmented Generation Assessment (RAGAs)
[
4
]
framework. These metrics were defined without relying on ground-truth human annotations, which was suitable for our case, as future scenarios are speculative rather than definitive.
Faithfulness
which refers to the idea that the answer should be grounded in the given context to avoid hallucinations.
Answer relevancy
which refers to the idea that the generated answer should address the actual prompt that was provided. Finally,
Context utilization
which refers to the idea that the retrieved context should be focused, containing as little irrelevant information as possible. Evaluators included a human climate expert and two LLMs (gemma2-2B, mistral-7B). Performance was analyzed using the mean scores for each evaluator across the 30 validated responses for the three metrics. To validate that the mean scores are representative of each evaluator’s performance, we computed the standard deviation (SD) as a measure of score variability. A low SD relative to the mean indicates that the scores are closely clustered and that the mean is a reliable summary statistic. Agreement between evaluators (inter-model and model-human) was assessed using spearman’s correlation coefficients.
3
3

3

Spearman correlation is a statistic used to measure how well the relationship between two sets of rankings matches. A score close to 1 means the rankings are very similar, while a score near 0 means they’re not.
The correlation was chosen for its ability in capturing monotonic relationships in ordinal, Likert-style scores without assuming linearity.

Result

3.1
Evaluation metrics

As shown in Table
1
, mean scores range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating better performance. Answer relevancy exhibited the most consistent scoring among evaluators, as indicated by the SDs. The SDs for human, gemma2-2B, and mistral-7B were all below 0.060. This is less than 6% of their respective means, indicating that the mean scores are highly representative for this metric. For faithfulness, gemma2-2B and human showed SDs below 11% of their means, suggesting a reasonable level of scoring consistency. However, mistral-7B exhibited greater variability (approximately 27% of the mean), indicating that its mean faithfulness score is less representative due to inconsistency across responses. Regarding context utilization, mistral-7B and human had SDs below 11% of their respective means, supporting the use of the mean as a reliable summary. In contrast, gemma2-2B had a higher variability (approximately 19% of the mean), indicating moderate inconsistency in scoring. Notably, among all evaluators, the human expert delivered the most consistent scoring across the three metrics. Overall, the mean scores are generally representative, particularly for answer relevancy. However, caution is advised when interpreting metrics like mistral-7B’s faithfulness and gemma2-2B’s context utilization, which exhibit higher variability.

Having established the reliability of the means, we noted that the evaluators produced consistently high answer relevancy scores. However, variation was observed in faithfulness and context utilization. For faithfulness, gemma2-2B achieved the highest mean score (0.966), followed by mistral-7B (0.848) and human (0.760). For context utilization, mistral-7B had the highest mean (0.980), followed by gemma2-2B (0.939) and human (0.713). Interestingly, gemma2-2B achieved the highest faithfulness score (0.966) but the lowest answer relevancy (0.888) among the evaluators. This counter-intuitive result reflects the evaluation behavior of gemma2-2B. The model appears to favor responses that adhere strictly to the retrieved context over directly addressing the prompt’s intent—i.e., evaluations that reward caution over directness. This trade-off highlights a potential tension between grounding and relevance in zero-shot settings for automated evaluation of scenario generation.

4
Discussion

Our generative AI approach shows potential for scenario generation in resource-constrained regions
[
5
]
. The RAG pipeline effectively leverages UN COP documents as a knowledge base, with llama3.2-3B demonstrating robust in-context learning capabilities. As shown in Table
1
, all evaluators assigned scores above 70% to the responses generated by llama3.2-3B across all metrics. This indicates broad agreement on the overall quality of the generated responses. However, the correlation results in Table
2
show that while LLMs align closely with humans in assessing factual consistency, gaps remain in interpreting answer relevancy. This suggests that LLMs struggle with subjective and contextual assessments, necessitating human oversight. This highlights the importance of our hybrid evaluation methods combining human judgment with automated tools. Limitations include reliance on zero-shot prompting, which may limit response depth, and potential biases from the base weights of LLMs. LLMs are commonly trained on general-purpose corpora dominated by western sources, which risks introducing a western skew
[
6
]
. This can result in cultural and contextual biases that misalign generated scenarios with the unique climate and energy priorities of SSA. Future work should explore advanced prompt engineering, ensemble models, and expanded datasets (e.g., national climate reports) to enhance accuracy and address biases. Future work could also fine-tune LLMs on region-specific corpora to better reflect local perspectives and values. Addressing representational biases and improving model alignment with human judgment are critical for ethical deployment in policy-making contexts
[
7
]
.

For policymakers, this generative AI approach presents many practical applications. For instance, it could support a Ministry of Energy scenario workshop to explore energy transition pathways tailored to SSA’s unique challenges. It could also facilitate a regional ECOWAS planning exercise to coordinate climate strategies across West African states, informed by data-driven narratives. By improving the accessibility and relevance of policy scenarios, this approach can foster more inclusive and informed climate governance in low-resource settings.

5
Conclusion

Our method provides a scalable, AI-driven framework for generating diverse and coherent climate policy scenarios tailored to SSA. The RAG-based approach, grounded in UN COP documents and the integration of human and automated evaluations confirms the robustness of our approach. While limitations in model alignment and biases require further research, this work provides a foundation for AI-driven policy planning, with broader applications in global climate research.

Appendix C
Evaluation criteria for human climate expert ratings

This appendix outlines the key criteria used by the human climate evaluator to evaluate the generated responses. The criteria assess three dimensions: Faithfulness (A), Answer Relevancy (B), and Context Utilization (C), rated on a scale from 1 (Very Poor) to 5 (Excellent).

•

1 — Very Poor

–

A: Faithfulness
The answer significantly contradicts or ignores the referenced context, introduces major factual errors or “hallucinations,” or cannot be justified by the source material (e.g., COP reports or SSA policy data).

–

B: Answer Relevancy
The answer fails to address the question’s main points, strays off-topic, or provides irrelevant information.

–

C: Context Utilization
The answer barely uses the retrieved context or uses none at all and/or includes large amounts of irrelevant information.

•

2 — Poor

–

A: Faithfulness
The answer partially aligns with the references but contains noticeable inaccuracies or unsupported claims.

–

B: Answer Relevancy
The answer touches on the question but omits key aspects or provides minimal relevant detail.

–

C: Context Utilization
The answer shows only a limited or superficial link to the retrieved context, with more irrelevant than relevant content.

•

3 — Moderate / Fair

–

A: Faithfulness
The answer does not outright contradict the context, though it may include some unverified extrapolations or minor inconsistencies.

–

B: Answer Relevancy
The answer addresses the main question to a reasonable extent but may lack depth or clarity in some areas.

–

C: Context Utilization
The answer uses the retrieved context in a broad or generic way, with a roughly equal mix of relevant and possibly extraneous details.

•

4 — Good

–

A: Faithfulness
The answer is solidly grounded in the references, generally accurate, and avoids significant factual errors or unjustified claims.

–

B: Answer Relevancy
The answer thoroughly addresses the question, providing clear and direct responses to the key points asked for.

–

C: Context Utilization
The answer makes effective use of the retrieved context, selectively incorporating relevant details without much irrelevant information.

•

5 — Excellent

–

A: Faithfulness
The answer is fully consistent with the source material, highly accurate, and all major claims are justified by the references.

–

B: Answer Relevancy
The answer precisely targets the question, comprehensively covering all important aspects in a well-structured manner.

–

C: Context Utilization
The answer draws deeply and accurately on the retrieved context, weaving in the most pertinent points while avoiding extraneous detail.
