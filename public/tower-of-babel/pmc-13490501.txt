Tokenized market learning-based transaction scheduling for hydrogen–carbon chemistry consortium-based green energy communities with stakeholder welfare and sustainable transportation
Authors: SeyedJalal SeyedShenava, Peyman Zare, Amir Mohammadian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The transition toward sustainable cities requires integrated energy planning frameworks that coordinate multiple technologies, policy instruments, and social considerations. This study proposes a robust optimization framework for rich-renewables eco-sustainable urban communities, where multi-energy hubs including electricity, thermal, cooling, and hydrogen systems are jointly managed under uncertainty. A scenario-independent static robust model is developed to ensure reliable operation under renewable intermittency, supported by sensitivity analyses. The framework introduces hydrogen chemistry consortium processes, integrating electrolyzers, methanation, fuel cells, and carbon capture, utilization, and storage to enhance renewable utilization and reduce emissions. Both stationary storage systems and electric public transportation fleets are incorporated to provide distributed and mobile energy flexibility. Demand-side management and policy mechanisms, including carbon taxation and cap-and-trade, are embedded to align operations with environmental targets. A digital-social welfare layer evaluates affordability and equitable access. Simulation results across multiple scenarios demonstrate that the proposed framework reduces operational costs by over 45%, improves grid independence by more than 35%, and achieves emission reductions exceeding 90%. Welfare indicators also show significant improvement, confirming the effectiveness of the integrated approach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54133-8.

Introduction

Background and challenge

Establishing environmentally friendly and socially responsible cities has become the primary objective in contemporary urban development. As urban populations expand and the demand for sustainable infrastructure intensifies, cities must transition rapidly from fossil fuel dependency to large-scale, integrated, efficient, and clean multi-energy systems. The conventional city energy infrastructure, historically associated with negative environmental impacts and greenhouse gas emissions, persists in certain areas. Consequently, recent developments have concentrated on creating Green City Energy Communities (GCECs)1. These communities leverage advanced x-to-x energy conversion technologies, notably hydrogen chemistry-based electrolyzers (ELs), methanation processes, and various Fuel Cell (FC) applications. Furthermore, state-of-the-art Carbon Capture Utilisation and Storage (CCUS) technologies are integrated to enhance sustainability. Complementing mobile energy storage, these communities also employ non-mobile energy storage solutions to improve operational flexibility and facilitate renewable energy integration. The widespread adoption of electric Public Transportation Fleets (e-PTFs) and electric shared mobility services is notably revolutionizing urban transportation, dramatically decreasing carbon footprints and promoting cleaner transit options2.

A critical component of this transition is the application of Market Learning-Based scheduling, which aims to optimize energy distribution across the grid in real-time. In this context, “learning” refers to the adaptive mechanism that allows the system to continuously improve its scheduling decisions based on historical performance data and real-time market conditions. This learning mechanism can involve parameter updates and adjustments to the optimization model, enabling the system to better predict and respond to fluctuating demand and supply patterns. The adaptive algorithm employed in this approach adapts to changing environments, improving its decision-making over time through feedback loops derived from previous experiences.

Related works and research gaps

Building upon the previously discussed background, multi-energy systems and distributed generation technologies are integrated through various configurations to address a broad spectrum of technical and economic requirements. Energy system modeling has been extensively examined from diverse perspectives over the past decade. For instance, Ref1. investigates a tri-level optimization hierarchy but omits the significance of flexible energy systems in city-scale operations. In a related vein, Ref3. optimizes energy hubs, incorporating heat and compressed air storage apparatus under AC power flow constraints. However, this approach falls short in fully capturing the operational value of x-to-x energy conversion or hydrogen chemistry. Concurrently, Ref4. proposes scenario-based uncertainty modeling for energy hub planning, yet it does not account for the interconnection of e-PTFs and consumer-side flexibility, elements crucial for enabling digital-social welfare in contemporary urban societies.

In contrast to these earlier works, more recent studies, such as Ref5., employ chance-constrained stochastic scheduling for vectored energy systems. Ref6. emphasizes the importance of flexibility in energy hub optimization under uncertainty. Additionally, Refs7,8. examine flexible demand-side management and the joint variabilities of renewables with demand response. Nevertheless, the majority of this existing research insufficiently considers the environmental or economic benefits derived from e-PTFs and distributed flexibility, aspects particularly critical for a carbon-neutral urban transformation.

Moreover, with the increasing penetration of Electric Vehicles (EVs) into the energy market, several studies have begun to examine their impact on both emission reduction and system cost efficiency. Ref9. presents a chance-based planning model that incorporates thermal, electric, and hydrogen storage, alongside mobility technologies such as EVs and demand response. In contrast, Ref10. considers multi-energy systems but disregards renewable energy curtailment and emissions issues, which are precisely the challenges that x-to-x conversion and sector-coupling aim to address. Furthermore, the integration of electric thermal energy storage with EVs has been studied in11,12, and deep learning techniques for the operation of energy hubs, accounting for EV-related uncertainty, were introduced in13. Although EVs have been acknowledged within the context of multi-carrier energy coordination10–13, none of these works advance robust optimization, Power-to-Gas (P2G) integration, or Carbon Capture Utilisation and Storage (CCUS) towards achieving deep decarbonization.

Significant issues persist regarding renewable energy curtailment, stemming from mismatches between generation and consumption, which remain central challenges14. Several studies, including15,16, propose P2G, hydrogen storage systems, and x-to-x conversion as mitigation strategies. They advocate for the synergistic operation of P2G plants with natural gas or Combined Heat and Power (CHP) plants. For example, Ref17. investigates the co-optimized charging operation of electric and gas vehicles to maximize wind energy utilization, while Ref18. conducts a techno-economic study for combined seasonal thermal energy storage P2G and fuel cell systems. Additionally, Ref19. discusses a scenario-based demand response model within P2G scenarios. However, studies such as Ref19., which address curtailment and environmental impacts in multi-vector energy hubs, typically do not incorporate carbon market mechanisms or policy-based frameworks.

The modeling of carbon capture and emission markets is increasingly recognized as crucial for low-emission operations. In this regard, Ref20. presents a system-of-systems framework integrating advanced CCUS, while Ref21. introduces low-carbon scenarios encompassing CCS and P2G. Wind curtailment within carbon trading is also addressed in22,23, which propose an optimization of emission efficiency and discuss a combined P2G-CCS system. Furthermore, lifecycle energy and carbon reduction are investigated in24,25. The economic and environmental implications of a benchmarking trading program are explored in26, while Ref27. studies optimized P2G-CCS under wind uncertainty. Additionally, Ref28. applies Information Gap Decision Theory to capture emissions risk in P2G-CCS markets. Nevertheless, many of these works remain primarily at the technical level and have yet to fully address the connection between digital social welfare and a more robust economy.

Meanwhile, handling uncertainty in renewable-rich systems constitutes a highly active research area. Scenario-based stochastic optimization is commonly employed29,30, while robust formulations based on scenarios are studied in31. However, these approaches typically do not consider flexible sectoral resources such as e-PTFs, non-mobile energy storage, or Advanced Compressed Air Energy Storage (A-CAES) within a digitized urban environment. In contrast, Ref32. derives a min-max robust optimization model to minimize the operating costs of natural gas in CCS systems, and other methods have been proposed to address uncertain electricity prices. Improved performance can be partly achieved through robust optimization, as detailed in33,34, and a robust two-phase methodology is proposed in35. Some methods for increasing flexibility and decreasing dispatch costs in a P2G-CCS system are discussed36, with Ref37. illustrating the optimality of robust optimization in the worst-case scenario. Specifically, two-stage robust models for integrated electricity-gas networks, with respect to non-demand response (non-DR) flexibility, are considered, and the differences in capturing electrical versus thermal uncertainty are highlighted. Finally, stochastic methods concerning flexible demand and uncertainty in electro-hydrogen systems are discussed in38,39.

Existing research on energy management for energy hubs is extensive6–8; however, regarding flexibility, sector-coupling, and x-to-x conversion, there is a clear deficiency in the literature concerning digitally coordinated, eco-sustainable urban communities. Although several studies9,10 consider mobile storage and demand-side flexibility, they often lack an integrated analysis of the effects of e-PTFs and CCUS on low-carbon urban design. Furthermore, studies focused on P2X technologies, such as reference15, rarely achieve effective integration of CCUS and transport electrification. Even in references20,28 that examine CCUS, the associated environmental co-benefits are noted, while economic and policy aspects remain neglected, despite the tendency for younger demographics to emphasize the environmental advantages of CCUS. Carbon market strategies presented in31 also fail to address operational issues like self-independence, curtailment, and fair usage. Scenario-based models in38 impose additional computational challenges and lack a holistic digital-social welfare perspective. Consequently, comprehensive, scenario-agnostic models that fully integrate the cap-and-trade market and hydrogen chemistry (including water electrolysis, methanation, and fuel cells), flexibility assets, and digital-social welfare remain rare in the state-of-the-art literature, thereby justifying this work’s contribution.

Main contributions

To address these critical gaps in intelligent, uncertainty-resilient energy optimization, this work develops an integrated market learning-based model. This model incorporates hydrogen chemistry pathways, CCUS mechanisms, and multi-vector energy hubs, along with e-transportation resources, to enhance flexibility and sustainability in eco-sustainable urban energy communities. Building on the foundational concept of coupling robust market operations with sector-coupled flexibility and digital-social welfare, the methodology accounts for systemic constraints inherent in popular city-scale energy scheduling models. These conventional models typically address conversion technologies, carbon constraints, mobility electrification, and welfare assessment in isolation.

The resulting model integrates a comprehensive process encompassing multi-hub modeling, hybrid x-to-x conversion technology, static max-min robust optimization for electricity (and carbon) markets, priority-based flexible demand management, carbon market learning, and digital-social welfare assessment. The system’s architecture couples electricity, heat, cold, and hydrogen vectors with stationary and mobile storage technologies to ensure consistent cross-sectoral interaction. This study conceptualizes the Hydrogen Chemistry Consortium Processes (HCCP) as an integrated process framework that coordinates electrolyzers, methanation reactors, and fuel cells within the proposed energy hub architecture. Concurrently, CCUS modules facilitate deep decarbonization and synthetic methane production. The optimization layer employs robust scheduling, independent of specific scenarios, to manage the volatility of renewables without relying on sampled scenarios. Subsequently, welfare outcomes are quantified through a multi-dimensional digital-social welfare module, aiming for fair and inclusive community-driven results. Generally, the proposed framework offers a coherent methodology for addressing the policy, technological, and social dimensions of sustainable energy communities.

Integrated GCEC modeling

In this study, the concept of GCEC is described as a multi-hub energy system designed to simultaneously deliver multiple energy vectors and efficiently meet a wide range of electrical, cooling, and heating demands. The architecture is centered on a primary energy hub composed of three dedicated subsystems: an Electrical Hub (EH), a Thermal Hub (TH), and a Cooling Hub (CH), which address the sectoral demands within the GCEC, as illustrated in Figs. 1, 2. The primary energy inputs include the main grid (both electricity and natural gas purchases), renewable energy systems (RESs) such as Photovoltaic (PV) and Wind Turbines (WTs) generation plants, as well as conventional Fossil-Fueled Diesel Generators (FFDG).

Energy conversion systems include cogeneration technologies, particularly Combined Heat and Power (CHP) plants, gas boilers (GB), electric chillers (EC), electric heat pumps (EHP), and absorption chillers (AC). The system is further supplemented with advanced HCCP implemented through a range of x-to-x energy conversion technologies and integrated CCUS schemes.

Energy storage systems consist of both mobile and stationary types. The storage components include thermal storage (heat and ice), electrical storage, A-CAES, and hydrogen tank storage (HTS). In parallel, e-PTFs are represented as shared and distributed mobile energy storage systems capable of operating bidirectionally, either as energy consumers or as energy suppliers within the GCEC.

e-PTFs modeling

In this study, e-PTFs are modeled as shared mobile energy storage devices within the GCEC. This representation facilitates both grid-to-vehicle (G2V) charging and vehicle-to-grid (V2G) discharging, contributing to decarbonization and near-zero emissions. Two participation modes are evaluated: bidirectional operation (G2V/V2G) with flexible charging and discharging, and unidirectional charging. For the bidirectional mode, it is assumed that 20% of the fleet is unavailable for grid services. Operational conditions are based on fleet data indicating an average daily travel distance of 68 km and an energy consumption rate of 5.7 km/kWh. This data informs the charging and discharging patterns, where energy inputs are determined by the historical energy demands of the vehicles standing by, calculated via dynamic energy balances over the planning period.

Digital-social welfare

Drawing on welfare-oriented approaches presented in40,41, an indicator-based welfare evaluation framework is proposed. To incorporate the steering effect of GCEC operations within the domain of social welfare, a digital-social welfare performance framework is introduced. This framework evaluates the extent to which decarbonization, cost efficiency, and flexibility strategies influence public well-being, equity, and digital inclusion.

Nine indicators are considered: affordable energy service cost, net emission penalty ratio, equitable access to local green energy, transport electrification service cost, demand response social inclusion index, renewable energy support utility, green access distribution, distributed energy equity index, and digital participation rate. Accordingly, these indicators are specified and explained, and the mathematical modelling structure and explanations are provided in Appendix C of the Supplementary Materials.

Uncertainty handling strategy

To robustly handle the inherent uncertainty of PV and WT power production, a Static Robust Optimization (SRO) scheme is employed. This SRO scheme ensures that the system remains robust to variations in actual renewable generation levels relative to the predicted values while maintaining optimization feasibility. The SRO scheme is developed based on an uncertainty budget constrained by the parameter Γ in Eq. (64). Near-zero greenhouse gas emissions market learning

To accurately quantify and mitigate carbon emissions, environmental impacts associated with grid electricity (e.g., coal), FFDG, and natural gas units are incorporated into the model. The total carbon footprint is consistently quantified using Eqs. (81) and (82), which capture all significant sources of direct and indirect emissions. A dynamic, market-based carbon quota mechanism is introduced in the GCEC operation, requiring a 40% reduction in greenhouse gas emissions relative to the base year. The model is further extended with a cap-and-trade mechanism combined with adaptive carbon tax learning. This mechanism aligns economic incentives with decarbonization targets and dynamically responds to evolving market behaviour and policy development. The economic performance of the proposed GCEC framework is evaluated through a comparative analysis of the four case studies (I–IV), as summarized in Table 3, while the impact of the carbon cap-and-trade market mechanism is further examined in Table 4. The introduction of CCUS and carbon market participation in Case IV slightly increases operational expenditures relative to Case III; however, it provides significant environmental benefits. Consequently, the final operational energy cost declines to 1231.322 $ in Case IV, corresponding to an overall 55.7% cost reduction relative to the baseline scenario after emission market adjustments.

Urban transportation and e-PTFs operational analysis

As illustrated in Fig. 11, the operational strategy of e-PTFs is designed to support both urban mobility services and energy system flexibility within the GCEC framework. The charging and discharging behaviour of e-PTFs is coordinated with the electricity demand profile to improve load balancing and reduce peak demand pressure on the grid.

Specifically, the results indicate that e-PTFs are fully charged around 08:00 h and 17:00 h, corresponding to periods of relatively lower system demand and higher renewable generation availability. During these intervals, surplus electricity is stored in the vehicle batteries, increasing the SOC of the fleet and preparing the system for later energy support.

Conversely, controlled discharging occurs primarily between 12:00 h and 14:00 h, when electricity demand approaches its daily peak. During this interval, the stored energy from e-PTFs is injected back into the energy hub through vehicle-to-grid (V2G) operations. This coordinated discharge reduces peak electricity consumption and mitigates short-term demand spikes, thereby improving overall system stability.

The synchronized interaction between charging and discharging operations enables a more flexible and adaptive energy management structure.

Environmental performance analysis

The environmental performance of the GCEC framework is evaluated using the emissions cap-and-trade mechanism illustrated in Fig. 12. According to the regulatory policy, the allowable emissions of the GCEC must not exceed 40% of the baseline carbon emissions (2458.41 kg CO₂ equivalent). To enforce this constraint, the optimization model incorporates an emission tax that incentivizes greenhouse gas reduction.

As shown in Fig. 12, Case Study I exhibits the highest emission levels. Total emissions reach approximately 6.15 tons, significantly exceeding the carbon cap. Consequently, this configuration incurs the highest emission penalty, amounting to $300.865, due to the absence of emission mitigation technologies.

The introduction of flexibility mechanisms improves environmental performance. In Case Study II, the integration of flexible demand management and electrified mobility reduces emissions and lowers the emission tax to $174.662, representing a 41.6% reduction compared with Case I. Further improvements are observed in Case Study III, where the integration of Power-to-X (P2X) technologies enhances renewable utilization and reduces fossil fuel consumption. As a result, the emission penalty declines to $67.912, corresponding to a 77.4% reduction relative to the baseline scenario.

However, emissions in Case Studies I and III remain above the regulatory carbon cap, requiring the purchase of carbon allowances. In contrast, Case Study IV incorporates CCUS technology, leading to a substantial reduction in emissions. As illustrated in Fig. 12, total emissions decline to approximately 0.45 tons, while net CO₂ emissions decrease to 455.627 kg, representing a 92.7% reduction compared with Case I and an 86.4% reduction relative to Case III. Because emissions fall below the carbon cap, the system can benefit from carbon trading rather than paying emission penalties.

As shown in Table 3, the baseline configuration (Case Study I) records the lowest SIF at 66.1%, indicating high reliance on grid electricity. Although Case Study IV requires additional electricity for CCUS operation, its SIF remains 9.23% higher than the baseline scenario. These results highlight the role of sector-coupled technologies and ESS in improving both environmental performance and system independence within the GCEC framework.

Sensitivity analysis

In addition to the main findings, a thorough sensitivity analysis was conducted to examine the robustness of the GCEC framework in response to variations in natural gas and carbon emission prices. The system’s behavior under these price fluctuations is particularly significant, as natural gas prices can be influenced by political and geographical factors. This study concentrates primarily on how P2X configurations react to varying gas prices.

As depicted in Fig. 14(a), a substantial increase in the natural gas price for both Case Study I and Case Study III leads to an elevation in operating energy costs and a reduction in natural gas utilization levels. However, the GCEC framework effectively offsets gas consumption with any surplus renewable electricity through P2G integration. Figure 14(b) illustrates that when the gas price index reaches 20, natural gas consumption in Case Study III drops to zero due to the complete exploitation of renewable resources and the absence of internal resource limitations within the system.

Consequently, Case Study III operates at a lower energy consumption cost compared to Case Study I across all gas price levels. At a 20-fold increase in the maximum gas price, the operational expenditure for Case Study I is 58.9% higher than that of Case Study III. Without any P2X infrastructure, Case Study I experiences a drastic thermal load curtailment of 3687.37 kWh at the maximum gas price. In contrast, excess clean energy for thermo-space heating in Case Study III is utilized to partially satisfy thermal load demands, reducing shed loads to just 13.95 kWh under similar conditions.

Moreover, Case Study I exhibits a steep increase in its diesel generator fraction, reaching 69.2% at a gas index of 20, which elevates emissions. Case Study III does not employ load shedding; however, its higher electricity consumption increases its emissions, resulting in 58% higher emissions than Case Study I. This implies higher emission taxes for Case Study III unless CCUS units are installed.

Robustness analysis

To confirm the robustness and reliability of the aforementioned model under uncertainty, Fig. 15 illustrates how variations in the error budget influence energy cost and emission tax outcomes. A larger error budget corresponds to reduced generation bounds for stochastic variables such as solar and wind, consequently diminishing the availability of free, clean energy.

The decreased production of green electricity compels the system to rely on more expensive energy sources, thereby increasing both cost and emissions. Furthermore, with diminished renewable availability and persistent limitations on importing grid power, load shedding escalates, imposing an additional financial burden on the system.

Consequently, while a larger uncertainty budget demonstrably elevates energy costs, it also facilitates more conservative planning when renewable generation is inherently unpredictable. This trade-off suggests that energy system operators can adjust the uncertainty budget to optimize resource reliability and cost-effectiveness, ensuring the reliable operation of such a system.

Discussion and practical significance

The results of the GCEC framework demonstrate that integrated multi-vector energy coordination can simultaneously enhance economic performance, environmental sustainability, and community-level welfare outcomes in urban energy systems. From an economic perspective, the coordinated deployment of sector-coupling technologies such as Power-to-X conversion and CCUS reduces operational expenditures while improving renewable utilization and system autonomy. In the advanced configurations of the model, the integration of P2G pathways and e-PTFs significantly decreases dependence on grid electricity purchases and reduces fossil fuel consumption, which contributes to both short-term cost reduction and long-term financial sustainability of urban energy infrastructures.

From an environmental standpoint, the results confirm that the combined application of hydrogen-based conversion technologies and CCUS enables substantial greenhouse gas mitigation. In the fully integrated configuration, the system achieves deep emission reductions while maintaining operational feasibility under renewable uncertainty. This outcome highlights the role of sector-coupled energy infrastructures as practical pathways for cities seeking to comply with stringent decarbonization policies and carbon market mechanisms.

Beyond technical and environmental improvements, an important methodological aspect of this study lies in the integration of a digital-social welfare evaluation layer. Rather than treating system performance solely through conventional economic or emission indicators, the proposed framework introduces a structured indicator-based welfare assessment that captures affordability, equitable access to local renewable energy, digital participation, and community-level inclusiveness. In this sense, the digital-social welfare module functions as a complementary success metric for evaluating energy system transitions. By incorporating welfare indicators into the analytical structure, the framework enables energy planning strategies to be assessed not only by cost efficiency or emission reduction, but also by their societal impact within digitally connected urban communities.

This perspective is particularly relevant for sustainable energy communities, where the success of energy transition initiatives depends not only on technological deployment but also on public accessibility, participation, and fair distribution of benefits. Within this context, mobile storage technologies such as e-PTFs further support welfare-oriented energy management by providing flexible energy redistribution during peak demand periods, improving both operational stability and service accessibility in dense urban environments.

Overall, the proposed framework demonstrates that integrating technological innovation with welfare-oriented evaluation can provide a more comprehensive understanding of sustainable energy system performance. By linking economic optimization, environmental mitigation, and social welfare assessment within a unified modeling structure, the GCEC architecture offers a practical pathway for cities seeking to develop resilient, inclusive, and low-carbon energy ecosystems aligned with global sustainable development objectives.

Conclusions

The transition toward low-carbon urban energy systems is increasingly constrained by the difficulty of coordinating multiple energy carriers, mobility infrastructures, and carbon mitigation technologies under uncertain renewable generation. Conventional city energy infrastructures often treat transportation electrification, hydrogen conversion technologies, and energy storage as loosely connected subsystems. Such fragmented coordination limits the ability of urban energy communities to exploit sector coupling and distributed flexibility, particularly when renewable variability, carbon constraints, and dynamic market conditions must be considered simultaneously.

This work addressed that structural limitation through the development of an integrated scheduling framework for a GCEC that combines hydrogen-based conversion technologies, carbon capture infrastructure, and mobility-linked flexibility within a robust optimisation architecture. The proposed system coordinates electrical, thermal, and cooling hubs with HCCP and carbon capture utilisation and storage, while embedding e-Public Transportation Fleets as mobile energy storage resources within the operational scheduling layer. The mathematical formulation incorporates robust optimisation constraints, flexible demand management, and policy-oriented market mechanisms to coordinate energy conversion, storage utilisation, and mobility participation under renewable uncertainty.

The numerical experiments demonstrated substantial system-level improvements when sector-coupling technologies were progressively integrated. Compared with the baseline configuration lacking mobility flexibility and hydrogen conversion pathways, the advanced configuration achieved more than 45% reduction in operational energy cost while simultaneously eliminating renewable curtailment and increasing system self-independency. Environmental performance improved even further after integrating CCUS technologies, with total CO₂ emissions decreasing by approximately 92.7% relative to the baseline configuration. These results indicate that coordinated operation of hydrogen conversion pathways, carbon capture infrastructure, and distributed flexibility mechanisms can significantly enhance both economic and environmental performance within urban energy communities.

The operational behaviour of the e-PTFs revealed an additional structural advantage of the proposed framework. Unlike conventional private electric vehicle integration, the public transportation fleets operate under predictable and cyclic daily schedules. Charging events occurred during periods of lower system demand and higher renewable availability, while controlled vehicle-to-grid discharging supported peak demand intervals. This predictable mobility pattern simplifies uncertainty management in the scheduling process and allows the fleet to function as a reliable shared storage resource within the energy hub.

Despite these promising results, several limitations remain. The analysis was conducted using representative operational data and a single community-scale test system, and the uncertainty treatment relied on a robust optimisation formulation with bounded renewable variability. Large-scale deployment across interconnected urban districts may introduce additional operational interactions that were not represented in the current model. In addition, the demand profiles employed in the simulations were considered static and predetermined. While this assumption simplifies the evaluation of system-level coordination and welfare indicators, it may slightly overestimate the digital participation rate because real consumer engagement with digital energy services can vary over time due to behavioral diversity and dynamic demand responses.

Future research should therefore investigate multi-community coordination frameworks in which several energy hubs interact through regional electricity and hydrogen networks. In particular, the spatial allocation of hydrogen production assets within large-scale systems should be examined to evaluate how the location of electrolyzers influences transmission loading and overall system costs, thereby extending the planning capability of the HCCP architecture44. In addition, extending the model toward stochastic market environments with high price volatility and real-time mobility data would allow deeper assessment of mobility–energy coupling under realistic urban operating conditions. This extension could further incorporate large-scale e-mobility coordination and charging infrastructure planning, considering the interactions between transmission and distribution networks under increasing electrified transport penetration45. Finally, future work may integrate supply-chain modelling for carbon and fuel logistics within the CCUS and methanation layers, where machine learning–assisted optimization methods could improve the robustness of carbon transport and resource allocation under uncertain operational conditions46.
