Challenges in laparoscopic gonadectomy for complete androgen insensitivity syndrome with nonpalpable inguinal glands

Introduction

Complete Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome (CAIS) is a condition characterized by a 46, XY karyotype, where individuals develop female secondary sexual characteristics despite having male chromosomes due to the body's inability to respond to androgens [1]. Preventive gonadectomy is recommended after puberty because of the risk of malignant transformation in the gonads. While laparoscopic gonadectomy for intra-abdominal gonads in CAIS patients is well documented, there are few reports addressing the challenges of removing nonpalpable gonads from the inguinal canal. We present a case where laparoscopic gonadectomy was performed on nonpalpable gonads located in the bilateral inguinal canal in a patient with CAIS. Despite the initial procedure, a secondary inguinal incision was necessary to remove the remaining left gonad. This case highlights the surgical challenges encountered and the strategies employed to address them.

This case report adheres to SCARE criteria [2].

Case presentation

A 15-year-old phenotypic female presented to a pediatrician with a chief complaint of primary amenorrhea. Physical examination revealed female external genitalia with clitoral enlargement, and chromosomal analysis confirmed a 46, XY karyotype consistent with CAIS. Blood tests showed elevated testosterone levels. Following diagnosis, estrogen replacement therapy was initiated, and the patient was referred to our hospital for prophylactic gonadectomy at 16 years of age.

On physical examination, the patient was 176.7 cm tall and weighed 63.9 kg. She had a female vulva with clitoral enlargement, no urethral abnormalities, and a 7 cm long blind-ending vagina. No masses were palpable bilaterally in the inguinal region. Pelvic MRI revealed the absence of a uterus, with the right gonad located in the abdominal cavity and the left gonad in the inguinal canal, approximately 2 cm from the internal inguinal ring (Fig. 1). Hormonal analysis showed estradiol at 20.6 pg/ml (reference range: 19–487), LH at 20.6 mIU/ml (reference range: 1.13–14.22, ovulatory phase: ∼88.33), FSH at 50.7 mIU/ml (reference range: 1.47–8.49, ovulatory phase: ∼16.60), and testosterone at 1.29 ng/dl (reference range: 0.15–0.44). Both LH and FSH levels were elevated, and testosterone levels were markedly high.

Pelvic MRI.

(a) Right gonad in the abdominal cavity.

(b) Left gonad in the inguinal canal, 2 cm from the internal inguinal ring.

The patient underwent laparoscopic gonadectomy under general anesthesia, with a urologist assisting in the procedure. The patient was positioned supine with a 15-degree Trendelenburg tilt. A 3-port laparoscopy was performed, with two 5-mm lateral ports inserted into the right and left lower abdomens. No uterus was observed, and the right gonad was located intraperitoneally. However, both internal inguinal rings were closed, and the gonads had descended into the inguinal canal. Upon retraction of the spermatic cord from the right internal inguinal ring, the right gonad was easily identified within the abdominal cavity (Fig. 2a). The surrounding retroperitoneum was incised, the external iliac vessels were identified, and the right testicular vessels were coagulated and transected using bipolar cautery. On the left side, the gonad was deeply embedded in the inguinal canal. Following further dissection and retraction of the spermatic cord into the abdominal cavity, a white gonad-like tissue was identified (Fig. 2b) and excised after confirmation by the urologist. The surgery lasted 1 h and 30 min, with minimal blood loss.

Location of the gonads during surgery.

(a) The right gonad was easily revealed in the abdominal cavity.

(b) The left gonad was deeply embedded into the inguinal canal. The surrounding connective tissue was dissected, and white, gonad-like tissue was observed.

Histopathological examination of the excised specimen revealed testicular tissue with spermatic ducts on the right side, whereas the left specimen contained only blood vessels with no testicular tissue (Fig. 3). No malignancy was detected in either specimen. Four months postoperatively, testosterone levels remained unchanged, and a palpable mass was discovered in the left inguinal region. A secondary gonadectomy was planned for the remaining left gonad.

Pathological findings.

(a) Gross examination: No difference was observed between the two bilaterally excised specimens.

(b) The left gonad showed only collections of blood vessels and no testicular tissue.

(c) Testicular tissue was observed in the right gonad.

During the second surgery, the left inguinal mass, measuring 20 mm, was identified via computed tomography (Fig. 4). A direct incision was made over the inguinal mass, and the surrounding tissue was dissected to expose and remove the testes through ligation and dissection of the spermatic cord. Transabdominal ultrasonography confirmed complete removal of the mass. Pathological examination confirmed the presence of degenerated testicular tissue with spermatic ducts, verifying that the mass was indeed testicular in origin. Postoperatively, testosterone levels decreased to 0.15 ng/ml, and estrogen replacement therapy was resumed.

CT scan 4 months after laparoscopic surgery. A 20 mm left inguinal mass was identified.

We have obtained informed consent from the patient and her family.

Discussion

Laparoscopic gonadectomy for nonpalpable glands in patients with CAIS is a technically demanding procedure that has been infrequently reported in the literature [3,4]. Prophylactic gonadectomy is recommended after the development of secondary sexual characteristics due to the increased risk of germ cell tumors [5,6]. Studies indicate that immature gonads are located in the abdominal cavity in 50–70 % of cases, in the inguinal region in 20 % of cases, and in both the abdominal cavity and inguinal region in 10–30 % of cases [4]. MRI is valuable for diagnosing gonadal location [7], and the surgical approach depends on whether the gonads are palpable.

For palpable gonads in the inguinal region, gonadectomy is often performed through an inguinal incision [8]. However, laparoscopic surgery for nonpalpable inguinal gonads has recently been explored [9,10], particularly for patients with CAIS who identify as female [3,4,11]. Laparoscopic procedures are advantageous due to their minimally invasive nature, especially for younger patients. Given the need to remove gonads, collaboration between gynecologists, urologists, and surgeons is essential to successful outcomes [3,10].

Complete laparoscopic removal of gonads located in the inguinal canal requires meticulous dissection of the perigonadal connective tissue and careful traction into the abdominal cavity [3,4,11]. In the present case, the right gonad, located near the internal inguinal ring, was easily removed through intraperitoneal traction. However, the left gonad was deeply embedded in the inguinal canal, necessitating excessive dissection and traction. Despite the assistance of urologists, it was challenging to ensure complete removal of the left gonad, which remained partially within the inguinal canal. Histopathological analysis later confirmed the presence of testicular tissue, and the persistence of elevated testosterone levels postoperatively suggested that residual gonadal tissue might be present. This was later confirmed when a palpable mass was identified in the left inguinal region, requiring secondary surgery. The urologist has confirmed the removal of the gonads by traction of the spermatic cord intraoperatively and identifying the same indentation on both sides in the inguinal region. In this case, the gynecologist only palpated from the abdominal wall; therefore, the method of palpation needs to be improved. I complete removal of the gonads could not be confirmed by palpation, intraoperative UST could have confirmed the disappearance of the testis, thereby avoiding a two-stage surgery.

The locations of the gonads vary. In the fetal period, undifferentiated gonads arise from the reproductive ridges in the abdominal cavity. After the seminal ductus originating from Wolff's duct joins the testis, it descends into the abdominal cavity along a peritoneal sheath-like process to reach the inguinal canal and scrotum. The testes descend intraperitoneally through the inguinal canal under the action of testosterone [12]. In this case, the inguinal gonad left behind after laparoscopic surgery became palpable. Since there was no change in testosterone levels before and after surgery, it is possible that testosterone was produced from the residual gonad and acted on the testes to promote the descent of the inguinal canal, making it palpable from outside the body.

In addition to surgical interventions such as prophylactic gonadectomy and external genital surgery, the management of sexually differentiated disorders like CAIS requires careful consideration of the psychological and social implications for the patient. Since many patients eligible for CAIS surgery are young and still in school, the timing of surgery is critical. It is essential to thoroughly explain to both the patient and their family that surgery for nonpalpable inguinal glands, such as in this case, may necessitate a secondary surgery.

Conclusion

Laparoscopic prophylactic gonadectomy is an effective approach for managing patients with CAIS. However, removing nonpalpable glands in the inguinal canal can be technically challenging, even with the collaboration of urologists and surgeons. It is crucial to recognize the potential for residual testicular tissue and the need for careful postoperative monitoring, as secondary resection may be required if remnants are detected.
