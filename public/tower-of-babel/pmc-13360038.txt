Psychological heterogeneity and transitions of non-suicidal self-injury in adolescents: a two-wave longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Xing Li, Qingqing Xiao, Yan Xiao, Lijuan Huang, Dandan Hou, Xuehua Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is common among adolescents and is associated with substantial psychological distress and elevated suicide risk. From an ecological-developmental perspective, adolescent NSSI may arise from interacting influences across individual vulnerabilities, developmental adversity, family relationships, and social resources. However, most previous studies have examined these factors using variable-centered approaches, which may overlook psychosocial heterogeneity within clinical populations. This study aimed to examine three-year transitions in NSSI among psychiatric adolescents and to investigate whether multidomain psychosocial profiles identified at baseline were associated with these transition outcomes.MethodsA two-wave longitudinal cohort study was conducted among 432 adolescent psychiatric inpatients (mean age = 14.6 years; 81.3% female) with a three-year follow-up interval. NSSI was assessed at baseline and follow-up, and transitions were categorized as stable no NSSI, new-onset NSSI, remission, or persistence. Latent profile analysis was used to identify psychosocial profiles based on multidomain indicators including emotional symptoms, trauma exposure, stressful life events, psychosocial resources, coping styles, and perceived parenting. Multinomial logistic regression models examined predictors of transition outcomes.ResultsNSSI prevalence declined from 22.9% at baseline to 14.1% at follow-up. Transition patterns included stable no NSSI (64.1%), remission (21.8%), new onset (13.0%), and persistence (1.2%). Latent profile analysis identified three psychosocial subgroups: a lower-risk/high-resource profile, an intermediate-risk profile, and a high-stress/low-support profile. Transition distributions differed significantly across profiles, with the lower-risk profile showing the highest remission proportion and the high-stress/low-support profile showing the lowest remission proportion. In multivariable models, low household economic status (RRR = 2.64), personal suicide history (RRR = 2.96), and lower paternal emotional warmth (RRR = 0.52) independently predicted current NSSI, whereas greater childhood trauma (RRR = 0.35) and stressful life events (RRR = 0.68) were associated with lower odds of remission.ConclusionsNSSI among adolescents receiving psychiatric care shows heterogeneous transition patterns and distinct psychosocial profiles. Multidomain psychosocial characteristics, including socioeconomic adversity, prior suicidality, trauma exposure, life stress, and paternal emotional warmth, appear to be important correlates of NSSI transitions. These findings highlight the value of incorporating multidomain psychosocial assessment into clinical risk evaluation and intervention planning.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08057-2.

Introduction

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI), defined as the deliberate destruction of one’s own body tissue without suicidal intent, is a prevalent and clinically significant behavior among adolescents [1]. Epidemiological studies suggest that approximately 15–20% of adolescents in community samples report at least one lifetime episode of NSSI [2], while prevalence in psychiatric populations is substantially higher, often exceeding 40–60% [3, 4]. NSSI has been associated with a wide range of adverse outcomes, including depressive and anxiety symptoms, interpersonal difficulties, functional impairment, and co-occurring suicidality [5–7]. Importantly, these relationships are likely to be reciprocal rather than strictly unidirectional. Psychological distress and psychosocial adversity may increase vulnerability to NSSI, while engagement in NSSI may in turn co-occur with or exacerbate broader emotional and interpersonal difficulties [8]. Consequently, adolescent NSSI is increasingly conceptualized not as an isolated symptom but as a complex behavioral phenomenon embedded within broader developmental and psychosocial contexts.

An ecological-developmental perspective may help explain this complexity [9]. Drawing on Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory, adolescent behavior can be understood as the product of interacting influences across multiple levels of the environment. These influences include individual emotional vulnerability, developmental adversity, family relational context, and broader social environments. Consistent with this perspective, adolescent NSSI may emerge from the combined effects of individual psychological characteristics, exposure to stress or trauma, family relationships, and available social resources [10]. Consistent with this view, previous studies have identified numerous psychosocial correlates of adolescent NSSI, such as depression, anxiety, childhood trauma, stressful life events, adverse parenting experiences, and low self-esteem or social support [11, 12]. Additional contextual factors, including problematic technology use, have also been implicated in recent work [13]. However, much of the existing literature has relied on variable-centered approaches that examine the independent association of individual predictors. While informative, such approaches may provide only a partial picture because psychosocial risks and resources often cluster within individuals rather than operating independently.

This issue may be particularly relevant in psychiatric adolescent populations. Compared with community samples, adolescents receiving psychiatric inpatient care often present with greater symptom severity, higher levels of psychosocial adversity, greater comorbidity, and elevated suicide risk [3]. In such contexts, the course of NSSI may be heterogeneous. Some adolescents may discontinue NSSI over time, others may newly report NSSI during follow-up, and a smaller subgroup may continue engaging in the behavior [14]. Examining transitions in NSSI status across repeated assessments may therefore provide clinically meaningful information about remission, persistence, and incident onset. In the present study, “new onset” refers to incident NSSI identified at follow-up among adolescents who did not meet NSSI criteria at baseline, rather than a distinct long-term developmental trajectory.

Despite extensive research on adolescent NSSI, two important gaps remain. First, many studies have focused on static associations between NSSI and isolated psychosocial variables, whereas relatively fewer have examined transitions in NSSI status across longitudinal assessments [15]. Second, limited work has explored whether multidomain psychosocial characteristics form distinct configurations that are differentially associated with NSSI transitions, particularly in clinically high-risk inpatient samples [16]. Person-centered approaches such as latent profile analysis (LPA) offer a useful framework for addressing this question by identifying subgroups of adolescents who share similar psychosocial characteristics [17]. In the present study, baseline indicators were selected to reflect theoretically relevant ecological domains derived from ecological-developmental models of adolescent behavior: emotional and behavioral symptoms as markers of individual vulnerability; trauma exposure and stressful life events as indicators of developmental adversity; psychosocial resources (including self-esteem, perceived social support, and psychological resilience); coping styles as indicators of regulatory tendencies; and perceived parenting as a representation of family relational context.

The present study therefore sought to extend existing research in two ways. First, rather than focusing solely on whether known psychosocial variables are associated with NSSI, we examined transitions in NSSI status across a two-wave follow-up in a psychiatric inpatient cohort. Second, instead of treating psychosocial variables as isolated predictors, we investigated whether multidomain psychosocial configurations identified at baseline were differentially associated with these transition patterns. In this sense, the primary contribution of the study lies not in replicating well-established correlates of NSSI, but in examining how psychosocial heterogeneity may relate to different courses of NSSI during follow-up.

Accordingly, the aims of this study were threefold: (1) to describe transitions in NSSI status across two assessment waves, including stable non-NSSI, incident NSSI, remission, and persistence; (2) to identify latent psychosocial profiles at baseline using multidomain psychological, family, and contextual indicators; and (3) to examine whether these profiles, together with demographic, clinical, and psychosocial variables, were associated with NSSI transition outcomes. We hypothesized that adolescents characterized by greater psychosocial adversity and fewer protective resources would show lower likelihood of remission and higher likelihood of incident or current NSSI than relatively better-adjusted peers. We further expected that socioeconomic disadvantage, greater trauma exposure, prior suicidality, and lower paternal emotional warmth would be associated with less favorable NSSI transition patterns. By examining NSSI within a heterogeneity-informed and ecological framework, this study aims to provide clinically relevant insights for individualized risk assessment and intervention planning in psychiatric adolescents.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a two-wave longitudinal cohort study of adolescent psychiatric inpatients, with assessments at baseline (T0) and follow-up (T1). Participants were recruited from specialized adolescent inpatient units at a large tertiary psychiatric center in China. Consecutive inpatients who met eligibility criteria and provided informed consent/assent were enrolled. The analytic sample comprised 432 adolescents with complete data at both T0 and T1. The follow-up interval was three years for all participants, with follow-up assessments conducted approximately in the same calendar month as the baseline assessment. Baseline assessments were conducted between September 2020 and November 2021, and follow-up assessments were completed between September 2023 and November 2024. Participants were hospitalized at baseline; however, follow-up assessments were conducted by telephone interview regardless of inpatient status at T1. The study protocol was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (approval No. 20201049). Written informed consent was obtained from legal guardians, and assent was obtained from adolescents prior to participation.

Inclusion criteria were: (i) adolescent inpatients able to complete questionnaires and interviews; and (ii) a primary DSM-5 clinical diagnosis established by treating psychiatrists. Exclusion criteria were: (i) severe neurological or medical conditions precluding participation; or (ii) inability to provide valid self-report (e.g., severe cognitive impairment, acute psychotic symptoms interfering with questionnaire completion, or clinician judgment that the participant could not reliably understand questionnaire items). This cohort forms part of an ongoing inpatient adolescent research program previously described in related cross-sectional reports, which used comparable recruitment procedures and measurement frameworks [18].

Measures

Primary outcome: NSSI status and two-wave transitions

NSSI was assessed at both baseline (T0) and follow-up (T1) using a standardized self-report question followed by a behavior checklist. Participants were first asked a screening question:

“During the past year, when facing stress or problems, have you intentionally hurt yourself on purpose without the intention to die?”. Participants endorsing this item were further asked about specific self-injurious behaviors during the previous 12 months, including cutting or scratching the skin, hitting oneself or objects, hair pulling, biting, pinching, intentional drug overdose, or deliberate ingestion of foreign bodies. The number of occurrences of each behavior was recorded. Consistent with the proposed DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for NSSI disorder, adolescents reporting five or more self-injurious episodes during the past year were classified as having NSSI at that time point [19]. The same assessment framework and criteria were applied at both baseline and follow-up. Two-wave transition patterns were defined as: 0→0 (stable no NSSI), 0→1 (new-onset NSSI), 1→0 (remitted NSSI), 1→1 (persistent NSSI). Because the number of adolescents with persistent NSSI was small (n = 5), the persistent (1→1) and new-onset (0→1) groups were combined as “Current NSSI” in the regression analyses.

Sociodemographic, family, and clinical characteristics

Sociodemographic variables included sex (female/male), age (years), only-child status (Yes/No), family type (Nuclear, Extended, Single-parent, Joint, Skipped-generation), household economic status (Poor, Average, Good), and number of close companions (0, 1–2, ≥3). Sex was recorded according to sex assigned at birth in medical records. Household economic status (Poor, Average, Good) was assessed using a self-reported item indicating the participant’s perceived family economic status. Clinical variables included past-year suicide attempt history (Yes/No), family mental illness history (Yes/No), family suicide history (Yes/No). Past-year suicide attempt history was assessed using the same questionnaire and defined as any self-reported suicide attempt during the previous year (e.g., jumping from heights, hanging, or ingesting large quantities of medication). Suicidal ideation alone was not classified as suicide attempt. The index psychiatric diagnosis at admission was categorized as depression, anxiety, schizophrenia, childhood emotional disorder, bipolar disorder, or other less frequent psychiatric diagnoses (e.g., attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, obsessive–compulsive disorder, and other less common psychiatric conditions).

Psychological instruments

All psychological measures were administered at baseline (T0) using validated Chinese versions of standardized self-report instruments. Cronbach’s α coefficients for all scales in the present sample are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Composite scores were calculated by summing item responses according to the standard scoring procedures of each instrument. Reverse-scored items were recoded prior to score calculation where applicable.

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) [20]. The CES-D contains 20 items assessing depressive affect and related symptoms experienced during the past week. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0–3), with four items reverse-scored. Total scores range from 0 to 60, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms.

Trait anxiety

Trait anxiety was measured using the trait subscale of the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-T) [21]. The subscale consists of 20 items assessing stable individual differences in anxiety proneness. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1–4), with several positively worded items reverse-scored. Total scores range from 20 to 80, with higher scores indicating greater levels of trait anxiety.

Emotional and behavioral difficulties

Emotional and behavioral difficulties were assessed using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) [22]. The SDQ contains 25 items rated on a 3-point scale (0–2) across five domains: emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity/inattention, peer relationship problems, and prosocial behavior. The total difficulties score was calculated by summing the first four domains, excluding the prosocial behavior subscale, yielding scores ranging from 0 to 40.

Childhood trauma

Childhood trauma was assessed using the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire–Short Form (CTQ-SF) [23]. The scale contains 28 items measuring five domains of childhood maltreatment: emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1–5), with higher scores indicating greater exposure to childhood trauma.

Psychological abuse and neglect

Child psychological abuse and neglect were assessed using the Child Psychological Abuse and Neglect Scale (CPANS) [24]. The scale contains 31 items measuring two domains: psychological abuse and psychological neglect. Items are rated on a 5-point scale (0–4), with higher scores indicating greater exposure to psychological maltreatment during childhood. CPANS was included to capture psychological maltreatment dimensions that may not be fully represented in the CTQ-SF.

Stressful life events

Recent life stress was assessed using the Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC) [25]. The scale includes 27 items assessing stressful experiences across several domains, including interpersonal relationships, academic pressure, punishment, loss, and health adaptation. Higher scores indicate greater exposure to stressful life events.

Self-esteem

Self-esteem was assessed using the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (SES) [26]. The scale consists of 10 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with negatively worded items reverse-scored. Total scores range from 10 to 40, with higher scores indicating higher levels of global self-esteem.

Social support

Perceived social support was measured using the Child and Adolescent Social Support Scale (CASSS) [27]. The scale contains 17 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater perceived social support.

Psychological resilience

Psychological resilience was assessed using the Healthy Kids Resilience Assessment (HKRA) [28]. The scale consists of 27 items rated on a 5-point scale and measures resilience across several domains, including goal focus, emotional control, positive cognition, family support, and interpersonal assistance.

Coping styles

Coping strategies were assessed using the Coping Style Scale for Middle-School Students (CSSMSS) [18]. The scale includes 36 items rated on a 4-point scale and comprises two higher-order dimensions: problem-focused coping and emotion-focused coping. Subscale scores were calculated by summing the corresponding item responses.

Internet addiction

Internet addiction was assessed using the Chinese Internet Addiction Scale–Revised (CIAS-R) [29]. The scale contains 19 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale assessing symptoms such as tolerance, withdrawal, compulsive internet use, and time management or interpersonal problems. Higher scores indicate greater internet addiction severity.

Mobile phone addiction

Problematic mobile phone use was assessed using the Mobile Phone Addiction Index (MPAI) [30]. The scale contains 17 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale measuring withdrawal symptoms, loss of control, inefficiency, and escapism associated with excessive mobile phone use.

Parenting style

The short form of the Egna Minnen Beträffande Uppfostran (s-EMBU) was completed by adolescents to assess perceived parental rearing behaviors [31]. The scale includes three parenting dimensions—emotional warmth, rejection, and overprotection—assessed separately for fathers and mothers. Items are rated on a 4-point scale ranging from “never” to “always,” and subscale scores were calculated by summing the corresponding items.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in R v4.4.3. Baseline characteristics are reported as counts and percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations for continuous variables. NSSI prevalence at T0 and T1 and the T0→T1 transition matrix were estimated with 95% confidence intervals using the Wilson and Clopper–Pearson methods. Changes in NSSI prevalence between waves were tested using McNemar’s test. Baseline psychological heterogeneity was examined using Gaussian latent profile analysis (LPA), a person-centered mixture modeling approach used to identify latent subgroups based on patterns of continuous indicators [32]. Models with two to six latent classes were estimated. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), entropy, and bootstrap likelihood ratio tests (BLRT) comparing k and k − 1 class models [33].

Class differences in NSSI transitions were evaluated using Pearson χ2 tests, with Monte Carlo simulation used when expected cell counts were sparse. Effect sizes were estimated using Cramer’s V. Prior to regression modeling, correlations among baseline psychosocial variables were examined to assess potential construct overlap. Multicollinearity among predictors included in the multinomial regression models was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIFs). Predictors of transition categories were estimated using baseline-category multinomial logistic regression, with stable no NSSI (0→0) as the reference category [34]. Continuous predictors were standardized (z-scores), and results are reported as relative risk ratios (RRRs) with 95% Wald confidence intervals. Effect modification by sex and household economic status was tested using interaction terms to explore whether associations between baseline psychosocial predictors and NSSI transitions differed across key demographic contexts, given prior evidence suggesting potential sex- and socioeconomic-related differences in adolescent self-injury [1, 35, 36]. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted by re-leveling outcomes, alternative coding and scaling of predictors, and excluding extreme values. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the main findings under alternative model specifications. These analyses included: (1) re-leveling outcome categories in the multinomial logistic regression models, using Current NSSI as the reference category instead of Stable no NSSI; (2) alternative coding of household economic status, including ordinal and binary representations; (3) alternative scaling of continuous predictors, including mean-centering and interquartile-range (IQR) standardization; and (4) exclusion of extreme values defined as standardized observations exceeding |z| >3. The direction and magnitude of the key associations were compared across models.

Results

Baseline characteristics and two-wave NSSI prevalence and transitions

The analytic sample comprised 432 adolescents (mean age = 14.6 ± 1.7 years; 81.3% female). Slightly more than half were only children (55.8%). Nearly half lived in nuclear families, and household economic status was predominantly average (Table 1). At baseline (T0), 22.9% (99/432) of adolescents reported NSSI, which decreased to 14.1% (61/432) at follow-up (T1). This change was statistically significant (McNemar’s test, p = 0.002). Transition patterns were as follows: stable no NSSI (64.1%), new-onset NSSI (13.0%), remission (21.8%), and persistence (1.2%) (Table 2; Fig. 1). Among adolescents without NSSI at baseline, 16.8% reported NSSI at follow-up. Among those with baseline NSSI, most remitted (94.9%), whereas persistent NSSI was rare (5.1%). Correlations among baseline psychosocial measures were examined to assess potential construct overlap (Supplementary Table S2). Latent profile model selection

Latent profile models with two to six classes were estimated using 19 psychosocial indicators (Table 3). Information criteria decreased as the number of classes increased, indicating improved model fit with additional classes. However, models with four or more classes produced very small groups (<5% of the sample), suggesting potential over-extraction and limited interpretability. The three-class solution was therefore retained as the optimal model because it demonstrated good classification accuracy (entropy = 0.903), acceptable class sizes (smallest class = 21.5%), and interpretable psychosocial patterns. Characteristics of the latent psychological profiles

Mean scores of the psychosocial indicators across the three classes are presented in Table 4. Significant differences were observed across all indicators (all p < 0.001). As illustrated in Fig. 3, Class 2 showed relatively low levels of depressive symptoms, anxiety, behavioral difficulties, trauma exposure, psychological maltreatment, and stressful life events, together with higher levels of self-esteem, perceived social support, resilience, and problem-solving coping. This pattern indicates a lower-risk/high-resource profile. In contrast, Class 3 displayed the highest levels of emotional distress, trauma exposure, psychological abuse and neglect, and stressful life events, along with lower psychosocial resources and more maladaptive coping patterns. Adolescents in this class also reported lower levels of perceived parental emotional warmth and higher levels of parental rejection and overprotection. This configuration was therefore interpreted as a high-stress/low-support profile. Interaction and sensitivity analyses

Potential effect modification by sex and household economic status was examined using interaction terms in the multinomial logistic regression models (Supplementary Table S5; Supplementary Fig. S1). No interaction terms remained statistically significant after FDR correction (all q ≥ 0.56), indicating no robust evidence that the associations between baseline predictors and NSSI transition outcomes differed by sex or household economic status.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the primary findings across alternative model specifications (Supplementary Table S6; Supplementary Fig. S2). Overall, the results were broadly consistent with the main analyses. Effect estimates remained similar in magnitude, and the direction of associations was preserved across models, supporting the stability of the primary findings.

Discussion

Summary of key findings

This study examined NSSI transitions over a three-year follow-up in 432 clinically referred adolescents. NSSI prevalence decreased from 22.9% at baseline to 14.1% at follow-up, with most adolescents remaining non-engagers or remitting over time, whereas persistence was rare and new-onset NSSI occurred in a meaningful minority. LPA identified three psychosocial subgroups characterized by distinct patterns of psychological distress, trauma exposure, coping styles, and family-related factors, including perceived parental rearing behaviors. These profiles differed in their distributions of NSSI transition patterns. Multinomial regression indicated that low household economic status, personal suicide history, and lower paternal emotional warmth were associated with current NSSI, whereas greater life stress and childhood trauma were linked to lower odds of remission. These findings highlight both heterogeneity in psychosocial profiles and specific baseline risk factors relevant to understanding patterns of NSSI transitions in adolescents receiving psychiatric care.

Patterns of NSSI transitions over time

Consistent with prior clinical studies [8, 37], NSSI in this inpatient sample appeared to follow a largely episodic course rather than a chronic trajectory. Remission was relatively common, whereas persistent NSSI was rare. Several factors may be related to this pattern. First, many adolescents may have received psychiatric treatment or psychosocial support during follow-up, which may be associated with changes in self-harm behavior over time [38]. Second, adolescence is characterized by rapidly changing emotional and social contexts; NSSI may therefore vary alongside changes in stressors, peer dynamics, and coping resources [39].

Methodological factors may also be related to the observed transition patterns. Self-report measurement and the interval between assessments may influence estimates of remission or new onset, and regression to the mean may partially inflate apparent improvement across waves. At the same time, the non-trivial proportion of adolescents who newly reported NSSI highlights that risk remains dynamic even among those without prior self-injury. This finding underscores the importance of ongoing monitoring and early identification of emerging cases, particularly within clinical populations already experiencing psychological distress, consistent with recent research emphasizing careful monitoring and risk assessment of self-injurious and suicidal behaviors in vulnerable populations [40, 41].

Psychological heterogeneity in adolescents with NSSI

The latent profile analysis suggested that adolescents in this clinical sample were not psychologically homogeneous. Instead, three profiles emerged that differed in patterns of psychological distress, adversity exposure, coping resources, and perceived parental rearing behaviors. One profile was characterized by relatively lower levels of depressive symptoms, anxiety, behavioral difficulties, trauma exposure, and stressful life events, together with higher levels of psychosocial resources such as self-esteem, perceived social support, resilience, and problem-solving coping. Adolescents in this profile also reported relatively higher levels of parental emotional warmth and lower levels of rejection and overprotection, suggesting a comparatively lower-risk and resource-rich psychosocial profile. In contrast, another profile showed elevated emotional distress, higher levels of childhood trauma, psychological maltreatment, and stressful life events, accompanied by lower levels of psychosocial resources. Adolescents in this group also reported lower parental emotional warmth and higher levels of parental rejection and overprotection, reflecting a psychosocial environment characterized by high stress and limited support. A third profile showed intermediate levels across most psychosocial indicators and was therefore interpreted as an intermediate psychosocial risk profile.

These findings are consistent with previous research indicating that NSSI is often associated with multiple interacting vulnerabilities rather than a single pathway [42]. The present findings further suggest that these vulnerabilities may cluster into distinct psychosocial configurations at baseline, which in turn may be associated with different patterns of NSSI transitions over time. Adolescents experiencing cumulative stress and limited coping resources may be more likely to report self-injury, possibly reflecting difficulties in managing intense emotional distress. At the same time, the relationship between trauma exposure and perceived parental support may be complex. Stressful experiences may arise from multiple contexts, including peer, academic, or broader environmental factors, and therefore may coexist with relatively supportive family relationships. In addition, adolescents’ perceptions of parental warmth may vary depending on emotional states or recall processes. It is also possible that stress experienced by adolescents may partly originate within family environments even when parental warmth is perceived as relatively high, reflecting complex family dynamics and differing perceptions of family relationships.

Such complexity highlights that psychosocial risk factors do not necessarily operate independently and that the pathways associated with NSSI may differ across individuals. Importantly, these profiles were derived from baseline psychosocial characteristics rather than longitudinal trajectories, and therefore reflect underlying psychological heterogeneity within this clinical population. From a clinical perspective, these profiles suggest that psychosocial assessment should consider both individual vulnerabilities and contextual resources. Interventions focusing on stress reduction, coping skills, and strengthening social support may be particularly relevant for adolescents characterized by high stress and limited resources. At the same time, family-based assessment may remain important across profiles, as family relationships can influence both risk and recovery processes.

Psychosocial predictors of NSSI transitions

Several baseline factors were associated with NSSI transition outcomes. Adolescents from low-economic households were more likely to report current NSSI, suggesting that socioeconomic adversity may be associated with increased vulnerability. Prior studies have linked economic hardship to chronic stress exposure, reduced access to supportive resources, and greater family strain, which may be related to increased susceptibility to self-injurious behaviors [43]. Personal suicide history was also associated with both current NSSI and reduced likelihood of remission, consistent with evidence that suicidal behavior and NSSI often co-occur and may reflect overlapping emotional and behavioral vulnerabilities [44]. The magnitude of these associations suggests clinically meaningful differences in risk. For example, adolescents with a personal suicide history showed nearly a threefold higher likelihood of current NSSI (RRR = 2.96), while low household economic status was also associated with increased risk (RRR = 2.64). These effect sizes indicate that these factors may be particularly important for risk stratification in clinical settings.

Lower paternal emotional warmth was similarly associated with current NSSI. Previous research suggests that paternal involvement may represent a potentially protective factor in adolescent emotional development, possibly reflecting associations with emotional security and adaptive coping strategies [45]. In contrast, greater childhood trauma and stressful life events were associated with lower odds of remission. Trauma exposure may be associated with persistent emotional dysregulation, maladaptive coping patterns, and heightened stress sensitivity, which may be associated with lower odds of remission observed in this study [46]. Consistent with this, the observed effect size for childhood trauma (RRR = 0.35) indicates a substantial reduction in the likelihood of remission, highlighting the potential importance of trauma-related factors in shaping recovery trajectories. Finally, adolescents with primary diagnoses other than depression showed higher odds of remission, possibly reflecting differences in symptom course, treatment responsiveness, or contextual factors across diagnostic groups [47].

Although these associations provide insight into potential correlates of NSSI transitions, causal interpretations should be made cautiously. These associations may reflect complex interactions between individual vulnerability and environmental context rather than direct causal mechanisms. The cross-sectional measurement of several baseline predictors also raises the possibility of reverse causality or shared reporting biases, and future longitudinal studies with repeated assessments will be necessary to clarify these relationships.

Clinical and preventive implications

The findings have several potential implications for clinical assessment and intervention. First, the episodic nature of NSSI suggests that early identification and timely intervention may be relevant for reducing the risk of escalation or recurrence. Screening for psychosocial risk factors such as trauma exposure, stressful life events, and socioeconomic adversity may help clinicians identify adolescents at heightened risk of unfavorable NSSI trajectories.

Second, the presence of distinct psychosocial profiles suggests that clinically referred adolescents with or at risk of NSSI may benefit from individualized assessment strategies that consider both vulnerabilities and protective resources. Interventions focusing on coping skills, emotion regulation, and strengthening social support networks may be beneficial for adolescents experiencing elevated stress. However, parental and family-related factors may also contribute to adolescents’ stress experiences, and therefore family dynamics should be considered when interpreting these profiles and planning interventions. Family-informed approaches may also be relevant, particularly in contexts where family relationships influence emotional well-being and recovery processes. However, given the complexity of psychosocial pathways identified in this study, interventions should be tailored flexibly rather than assuming a single underlying mechanism for NSSI.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the two-wave design limited the ability to capture more complex and potentially non-linear trajectories of NSSI over time. Additional assessment waves would allow more precise modeling of developmental patterns and temporal dynamics. Second, reliance on self-report measures may introduce reporting bias, recall bias, and shared method variance. Although behaviors with suicidal intent were assessed separately, misclassification of intent cannot be entirely ruled out when relying on adolescents’ self-reports. Third, several psychosocial predictors were measured at baseline only, which limits the ability to establish temporal ordering and raises the possibility of reverse causality. The observed associations may therefore reflect shared underlying vulnerabilities rather than directional effects. Fourth, the sample was drawn from a clinically referred population of Chinese adolescents and was predominantly female. This imbalance may partly reflect the higher prevalence of non-suicidal self-injury and related emotional disorders among female adolescents in clinical settings, which may increase the likelihood of help-seeking and psychiatric hospitalization. However, because sex differences have been reported in the prevalence, motivations, and clinical correlates of non-suicidal self-injury, the predominance of female participants may limit the generalizability of the findings to male adolescents or to more gender-balanced populations. Fifth, persistent NSSI cases were relatively rare, requiring aggregation with new-onset cases in regression analyses. This approach may obscure potential differences between these transition patterns.

Future studies using multi-wave longitudinal designs, multi-informant assessments, and more diverse samples are needed to better clarify the mechanisms underlying NSSI transitions.

Conclusion

This study identified distinct psychosocial profiles and heterogeneous transition patterns of NSSI among adolescents receiving psychiatric inpatient care. The findings suggest that NSSI does not follow a uniform course in this population and may vary according to underlying psychosocial contexts. Baseline factors such as socioeconomic disadvantage, childhood trauma, personal suicide history, and paternal emotional warmth were associated with different NSSI transition outcomes during follow-up. These results highlight the potential value of incorporating multidomain psychosocial assessment into clinical evaluation. Integrating psychosocial screening with attention to family and contextual factors may help clinicians identify adolescents at risk for unfavorable NSSI trajectories and provide more individualized care.
