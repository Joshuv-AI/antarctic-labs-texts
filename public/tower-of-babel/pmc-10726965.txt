Filtering Robust Graphite without Incommensurate Interfaces by Electrical Technique
Authors: Weipeng Chen, Tielin Wu, Yelingyi Wang, Yiran Wang, Ming Ma, Quanshui Zheng, Zhanghui Wu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Two-dimensional (2D) van der Waals (vdW) layered materials have attracted considerable attention due to their potential applications in various fields. Among these materials, graphite is widely employed to achieve structural superlubricity (SSL), where the interfacial friction between two solids is almost negligible and the wear is zero. However, the development of integrated SSL systems using graphite flakes still faces a major obstacle stemming from the inherent delamination-induced instability in vdW layered materials. To address this issue, we propose a nondestructive filtering technique that utilizes electrical measurement to identify robust graphite flakes without delamination. Our experimental results confirm that all the filtered graphite flakes exhibit delamination-free behavior after more than 7000 cycles of sliding on a series of 2D and 3D substrates. Besides, we employ three types of characterizing methods to confirm that the filtering process does not impair the graphite flakes. Moreover, with focused ion beam (FIB) assisted slicing characterization and statistical analysis, we have discovered that all of the filtered flakes possess a graphite layer thickness below 100 nm. This is consistent with the thickness of the single crystalline graphite layer of our samples reported in the literature, suggesting the absence of incommensurate interfaces in the filtered graphite flakes. Our work contributes to a deeper understanding of the relationship between graphite conductance and incommensurate interfaces. In addition, we present a possible solution to address the delamination problem in layered materials, and this technique shows the potential to characterize the internal microstructure of grains and the distribution of grain boundaries in vdW materials on a large scale.

Introduction

Two-dimensional (2D) van der Waals (vdW) layered materials have garnered significant attention owing to their outstanding mechanical,1−4 electrical,5,6 thermal,7 and optical properties.8 These materials share a common feature: the weak vdW interactions between layers, contrasting with the strong covalent bonds within each layer.9 Especially at incommensurate interfaces, such weak vdW interactions even allow for the interlayer sliding degree of freedom.10,11 One intriguing mechanical behavior that arises from incommensurate vdW interfaces is structural superlubricity (SSL),12−15 characterized by nearly zero friction and zero wear between two contacting solid surfaces, which has offered revolutionary solutions to friction and wear challenges in nano- and micromechanical systems.3,4 Additionally, the interlayer sliding ability of 2D vdW layered materials presents exciting possibilities for device design,16−19 exemplified by the realization of highly stretchable and malleable vdW thin films composed of 2D nanosheets with staggered arrangements.20

However, excessive sliding behaviors also compromise the structural integrity and introduce the delamination issue in 2D vdW layered materials, which raises concerns about the reliability and stability of these materials,21 such as load-induced and shear-induced delamination phenomena.22,23 What’s even worse is that the fragmentation caused by delamination can sometimes lead to a cascading system failure. For example, the delamination-induced instability of SSL graphite flakes represents one of the main hurdles in developing robust integrated SSL systems,24 as detailed in Section 1 of the Supporting Information. In addition to the possibility of introducing a new sheared surface with defects, this issue also triggers chain failure in the entire SSL system due to the wear fragments generated by delamination. For the application of SSL, the superlubric flakes need to stay intact after many cycles of sliding. Hence, addressing the challenge of delamination in 2D vdW layered materials is of the utmost importance.

In the case of SSL graphite flakes, the delamination-induced instability arises from the competition between the friction force at the sliding interface and the internal resistance at the delamination interface, as detailed in Section 1 of the Supporting Information. The internal resistance can be categorized regionally into interlayer interaction forces (such as friction and self-retraction forces) and edge bonding forces.25 Recent work suggests that delamination can be eliminated after proper annealing treatment,24 as it reduces the friction force at the sliding interface to a greater extent than the internal resistance against delamination. According to their findings, this can ensure that the friction force remains lower than the internal resistance while sliding, thereby suppressing delamination. However, this method may be insufficient in practical applications, as it fails to address the underlying cause of delamination. For example, delamination does not always occur at the onset of sliding, indicating a more complex situation probably in reality.26 Actually, studies have shown that the shear strength of graphite’s commensurate interface is approximately 0.1 GPa,13,14 3 orders of magnitude higher than that of incommensurate interface (i.e., interface with lattice mismatch due to a twist angle). This implies that graphite’s commensurate stacking promises relatively robust performance in various application scenarios, while delamination may only occur at incommensurate interfaces. Although the specific mechanisms of graphite delamination behavior have yet to be fully understood, we believe that the existence of an incommensurate interface is a necessary factor for delamination.

In this paper, we have realized filtering robust SSL graphite flakes without delamination by their electrical resistance. This is achieved by establishing a direct correlation between the information on incommensurate interface and the electrical resistance of graphite flakes,27−29 where the robust graphite flakes are defined as those without incommensurate interfaces. Further experimental results indicate that all the filtered flakes exhibit delamination-free behavior when sliding on different substrates containing 3D materials (Au, Si, SiO2) and 2D materials (graphite, MoS2, WS2). It is worth mentioning that the filtering process is proven to cause no damage to graphite flakes, as confirmed by characterizations. Through focused ion beam (FIB) assisted slicing characterization and statistical analysis, we have found that all the filtered flakes possess a graphite layer thickness below 100 nm, which aligns with the c-axis single crystalline size of highly oriented pyrolytic graphite (HOPG).30 This confirms that the robustness of the filtered flakes is attributed to the intrinsic properties of their single crystalline graphite layers.

Methods and Materials

Preparation of Graphite Flake

The square graphite flake arrays with a platinum (Pt) cap used in this paper were fabricated on HOPG (ZYB grade (Bruker)), with a detailed process in Section 2 of the Supporting Information. In step 1, mechanical exfoliation was performed on HOPG to obtain a freshly cleaved smooth surface as the substrate. In step 2, photoresist was spun onto the freshly cleaved surface of HOPG. In step 3, the photoresist in the region was selectively removed according to the flake pattern array through electron beam lithography. In step 4, the Pt film was deposited on the surface via electron beam evaporation, with titanium/chromium metal chosen as the bonding layer between the graphite and Pt layer. In step 5, Pt pattern array was obtained by performing the lift-off process. In step 6, the graphite flake arrays were obtained by reactive ion etching process, with Pt cap as the mask.

Friction Measurement of AFM System

The friction experiment was conducted in a commercial NTEGRA upright AFM instrument (NT-MDT) under an ambient atmosphere. The detailed AFM setup also included a 100 μm XYZ piezoelectric displacement platform, a high numerical aperture objective lens (×100 (Mitutoyu)) and visualized AFM tip (VIT_P/IR (TipsNano)). The selected AFM probe model has a force constant of 25–95 N/m and a resonance frequency of 200–400 kHz. We achieve relative sliding at the SSL interface between the graphite and the substrates by pressing the graphite flake with an AFM probe, thereby recording the friction at the SSL interface. As for the calibration of the sensitivity of AFM tip, the normal sensitivity was calibrated in situ by the Sader method,31,32 and the lateral sensitivity was calibrated by the diamagnetic levitation spring system.33 In Sections 3 and 4 of the Supporting Information, the experimental setup schematic, the measurement of the coefficient of friction (COF) and the calibration process can be found.

Sample Characterizations

Raman spectra were acquired using a Raman microscope (LabRAM HR Evolution, Horiba) employing a 532 nm laser (2.33 eV) under standard ambient conditions. Height profiles were obtained by using an atomic force microscope (Cypher ES, Asylum Research) in its tapping mode for scanning morphology. The used silicon AFM probe model (Tap300DLC (BudgetSensors)) has a force constant of 40 N/m and a resonance frequency of 300 kHz. Slicing characterization was performed by a focused ion beam scanning electron microscope (Zeiss FIB, Auriga).

Results

The experimental samples were typical square graphite flakes, fabricated from HOPG (ZYB, Bruker) 1 cm × 1 cm. These flakes contained two distinct sizes: 4 μm × 4 μm as the targeted SSL graphite to be filtered and 13 μm × 13 μm as the substrate for filtering. Each flake was covered with a Pt cap, serving two purposes: enhancing the normal stiffness to resist deformation and ensuring a stable electrical contact between graphite and the probe for subsequent measurements. All manipulations related to graphite flakes were performed using tungsten probes, controlled by micromanipulator MM3A (Kleindiek). Our electrical measurement setup was based on 4-wire resistance measurements, employing a source meter (Keithley 2450). Two terminals were connected to the HOPG substrate, while the other two terminals were linked to two tungsten probes for current conduction and voltage measurement respectively, as depicted in Figure 1a. To effectively filter robust graphite, it is crucial to accurately measure the electrical resistance of the graphite flake itself. Although 4-wire resistance measurement can eliminate interference from contact resistance, the resistance of the graphite flake itself is still obscured by the presence of SSL interface resistance, rendering direct measurement difficult. Actually, the assembled graphite homojunction in a lock-in state is supposed to be an excellent platform for such electrical measurement, where the additional introduced interface resistance can be disregarded compared to the resistance of the graphite flake. This can be well understood from the crystalline structure of the graphite flake itself, which consists of numerous commensurate stacked graphene layers, with only a few possible incommensurate interfaces inside. Further considering that the resistance of graphite’s commensurate interface is significantly smaller than that of the incommensurate interface, the introduction of an additional commensurate interface resistance will have almost no impact on the electrical measurement results of the graphite flake itself.

The specific procedures for electrical measurement, as shown in Figure 1a, are as follows: (i) The large graphite flake was sheared away to obtain a flat graphite crystalline surface. (ii) The targeted small graphite flake was sheared to determine self-retracting motion (SRM) feature.11,34 (iii) The SRM graphite flake was then transferred to the exposed crystalline surface of large graphite flake. (iv) Graphite homojunction was assembled. (v) The SRM graphite flake was carefully rotated to be locked under the torque applied by dual probes, as depicted in Figure 1c. Two criteria for judging the lock-in state can be found in Section 6 of the Supporting Information. (vi) 4-wire resistance measurement was conducted using the dual probes. The measured resistance was independent of the applied normal force, as detailed in Section 7 of the Supporting Information. One probe served as the current source probe, pressed on the flake cap to allow current flow I, while the other probe functioned as the voltmeter probe, first placed on the flake cap to measure the voltage V1 and then on the graphite substrate to measure the corresponding voltage V2, as described in Figure 1b. To further investigate the impact of the contact interface on electrical measurement, we conducted an additional experiment as shown in Figure 1d, where angle dependence on resistance (Rflake + Rinterface) of the assembled graphite homojunction was measured at I = 5 mA. This was achieved by rotating the flake using dual probes, with detailed manipulations available in Section 8 of the Supporting Information. As anticipated, the measured resistance exhibited a 6-fold rotational symmetry, reflecting the inherent hexagonal lattice structure of graphite. Besides, the measured resistance results are in the same magnitude as the previous work,27 with differences possibly attributed to interface contamination.36,37 Interestingly, it was observed that the resistance peak at each 60° periodicity gradually decreased, indicating the effect of the graphite nanoeraser.38 In contrast, the minimum resistance at the commensurate state remained small and stable. This suggests that the interfacial inclusion has a lesser impact due to the high conductance of the commensurate interface itself. This explains why we utilized the system resistance measured at the commensurate state (lock-in state) as a reliable benchmark for our assessment.

To construct the filtering criteria, we conducted a long-distance reciprocating sliding test to gather information about the incommensurate interfaces inside graphite. This test was carried out on a nonsuperlubricated silicon (Si) substrate, with a relatively high COF (approximately 0.02). This Si substrate was chosen for high friction to ensure delamination happened while the graphite flake contained incommensurate interfaces. During the test, wear debris was commonly observed, indicating harsh working conditions featuring high friction and wear. If still no delamination occurred after the sliding test, the graphite flake was believed robust without an incommensurate interface. The reciprocating sliding test was performed using a piezoelectric displacement platform XP-611 (CoreMorrow). The reciprocating motion was set to 7000 cycles, with a sliding distance of 8 μm per cycle and a frequency of 6 Hz.

In this manner, we established the relationship between delamination and resistance Rs, as presented in Figure 2a. 39 graphite flakes with SRM characteristics were selected for electrical measurement (methods in Figure 1a) and long-distance sliding tests. The voltage–current characteristic curves exhibit a high level of dispersion, which is primarily attributed to the presence of internal incommensurate graphite interfaces. Among these flakes, the resistance of graphite without delamination is generally very low (mostly below 3 Ω), whereas the resistance of graphite with delamination is typically high (mostly exceeding 5 Ω). How to determine the upper threshold for the robust region remains a problem, namely, the minimum resistance of graphite flakes with incommensurate interfaces. To provide a more convincing definition, a combination of theoretical estimation and experimental results is employed. Previous studies on graphite indicate that the lowest misoriented angle of incommensurate interface is approximately 5°, below which the interface tends to rotate to a commensurate state.39 Hence, the up-limit of the resistance (Rup) for the robust region can be approximated by the resistance of the incommensurate interface (Rincom) at a misoriented angle of 5°:3Given is negligible, where Rincom (5°) is theoretically estimated to be 3.14 ± 0.12 Ω in our graphite samples. Detailed elaboration can be found in Section 9 of the Supporting Information. Additionally, when considering the experimental results from Figure 2a, we observe that the lowest measured Rs (3.15 Ω) for graphite with delamination falls precisely within the estimated range. Based on the above analysis, we concluded that the up-limit resistance can be determined to be Rup = 3.1 Ω, serving as the upper threshold for the robust region.

To further validate the aforementioned criteria for robust graphite, we conducted long-distance reciprocating tests on six SSL substrates: Au, Si, SiO2, graphite, MoS2, and WS2. The first three were 3D bulk substrates, while the last three were 2D vdW substrates. Two sets of graphite flakes with SRM characteristics were well prepared: one set was carefully filtered according to the Rup criterion, and the other set was transferred stochastically (i.e., transferred directly without filtering). Each set consisted eight samples per substrate. The parameters of long-distance sliding tests were configured the same as the previous test on the nonsuperlubricated Si substrate. As depicted in Figure 2b, the COF on these substrates was on the order of 0.001, lying in the scale of SSL. The specific process of the COF measurement is detailed in Section 3 of the Supporting Information. Remarkably, all of the filtered graphite flakes remained robust against delamination on these substrates, whereas those stochastically transferred graphite flakes all exhibited a certain probability of delamination. This experimental result effectively demonstrated the robustness of the filtered graphite flakes and the correctness of the filtering criteria Rup. Furthermore, we also counted on the delamination status of all experimentally transferred graphite flakes with SRM characteristics in our study and obtained an average delamination ratio of 53.85% based on data from 104 selected samples.

In order to examine whether defects were induced by the filtering process, we performed both indirect characterizations through electrical measurement and frictional behavior as well as direct Raman characterization of the bottom surface of the graphite flake. Since the filtering process involved locking and unlocking operations on the graphite homojunction, its potential impact on graphite SSL surface was uncertain, given the strong adhesion force of graphite commensurate interface.13,14 For the electrical characterization, we conducted in situ electrical measurements on the graphite homojunction, utilizing dual probes to switch between the locked and unlocked states, as illustrated in Figure 1a. Three flakes were selected. As shown in Figure 3a, system resistance Rs remained unchanged during the repeated locking and unlocking operations, indicating no damage to the electrical contact. Furthermore, the sliding friction on the graphite substrate was measured before and after the filtering process, as illustrated in Figure 3b. No significant change in the load independence of friction characteristics was observed, with nearly overlapping curves of each flake. Lastly, Raman characterization was performed on the bottom surface of the graphite flake after the filtering process. As displayed in Figure 3c, five representative points were carefully chosen to assess the defect situation of the bottom surface, with their intensity spectra stacked with a bias of 500 au. The ratio of ID/IG can quantitatively reflect the structure defects such as sp3 dangling bonds, where ID and IG represent the intensities of D peak and G peak, respectively.40 Upon careful examination within the highlighted red dashed box (1300–1400 cm–1), no observable D peak was detected, implying that no detectable defects were caused during the filtering process.26 Besides, the 2D band consists of two Lorentzian peaks, which is the typical band shape of defect-free HOPG.41 In brief, our characterization results confirm that the filtering process does not cause damage to the SSL surface of graphite flakes.

Our electrical filtering technique is essentially based on the following mechanism: the presence of an incommensurate interface within graphite leads to an increase in the c-axis resistance. To further substantiate this claim, we conducted an in situ comparative experiment. In some cases, delamination occurred directly when we applied torque to unlock the graphite flakes with a potential incommensurate interface inside. Hence, we can directly measure the variation of system resistance Rs caused by the delamination interface in situ. As shown in Figure 4a, we first measured Rs = 8.35 Ω at “Lock_1” state, where the graphite flake locked on the graphite substrate. Then the delamination interface is exposed, under the impact of the unlocking torque. We continued rotating the flake, until the exposed delamination interface locked again, namely “Lock_2” state.34 This time, we measured much smaller Rs = 4.06 Ω, despite smaller contact area of the delamination interface. The anomalous phenomenon originates from the internal incommensurate interface, whose resistance is strongly influenced by the misoriented angle.27,42 Specially, the interface resistance reaches its minimum value only at its commensurate state, namely the locking state from the perspective of mechanics.34

To better understand the common delamination behavior in graphite flakes from the perspective of material properties, we performed scanning electron microscope (SEM) slicing characterization on the filtered graphite flake, assisted by FIB. As shown in Figure 4b, the graphite layer was only 35 nm thick, while the Pt cap had a thickness of approximately 300 nm. Considering the thickness of single crystalline graphite layer in HOPG measuring only about 10–100 nm,30 maybe the key point to the robustness of flakes was the single crystalline graphite layer. Hence, statistical analysis of the graphite layer thickness was conducted to confirm this point based on the height profiles obtained by AFM (tapping mode). Twenty-six filtered graphite flakes and 30 stochastically transferred graphite flakes were used to obtain the representative distribution comparison of the graphite layer thickness in flakes. As shown in Figure 4c and Figure 4d, the frequency count method combined with Gauss fit was performed to derive the thickness distribution probability for both sets of flakes. As described in Figure 4e, the discrete distributions of the graphite layer thickness are denoted by blue squares (stochastically transferred flakes) and red circles (filtered flakes), with Gauss fit results (lines) representing the distribution probability for each set. As expected, filtered flakes had a graphite layer thickness generally less than 100 nm and mainly concentrated around 42 nm, whereas the thickness of stochastically transferred flakes was highly scattered (ranging from 10 to 400 nm) with a peak around 134 nm. Detailed information for the Gauss fit parameters can be found in Section 10 of the Supporting Information. Furthermore, we performed long-distance sliding tests on nonsuperlubricated Si substrates to assess the delamination status of these flakes, utilizing the same parameters as in the previous test. Notably, almost all of the flakes with a thick graphite layer over 100 nm exhibited delamination behavior. Due to the thickness restriction of the single crystal in HOPG, flakes with thicker graphite layers (over 100 nm) tend to contain incommensurate interfaces, which ultimately lead to delamination.

The aforementioned statistical results undoubtedly demonstrate that the robustness of the filtered graphite flake originates from its intrinsic single crystalline graphite layer. Based on this insight, we can make the following prediction: According to the information about the c-axis thickness of the single crystalline layer in the corresponding HOPG sample, an appropriate etching depth for the graphite flakes can be established, thereby significantly reducing the delamination proportion in the stochastically transferred flakes. Remarkably, if uniformly thick single crystalline graphite layer can be successfully obtained,43 it may be possible in the future to directly fabricate robust SSL graphite flakes with the same thickness, which will have high application value and commercial potential for the development of SSL technology.35,44

Discussion

Although the electrical filtering method proposed in this article still faces challenges such as manual inefficiency and difficulty in scaling up, all of these operations can be integrated into recently developed automated transfer and measurement techniques.35 It is expected that these advancements will enable practical applications of our electrical filtering method in the near future. Notably, the proposed electrical measurement method holds the potential to develop into a characterization technique for the internal properties of vdW materials on a horizontally large scale: (i) micropillar arrays are etched on the surface of the investigated material, utilizing microfabrication techniques; (ii) electrical measurements are conducted on each pillar to obtain electrical transport information (resistance, capacitance, etc.), by combining automation technology; (iii) the internal crystal structure information on each micropillar can be inferred from the measured electrical transport information; (iv) by consolidating the collected data, it becomes possible to obtain the macroscopic crystal structure information within the material, such as grain microstructure and grain boundary orientation.

Conclusion

We have successfully addressed the long-standing problem of delamination-induced instability in SSL graphite, in both technique and mechanism, by employing the filtering method based on electrical measurement. Our experimental results demonstrate that all the filtered graphite flakes exhibit delamination-free behavior sliding on six types of commonly used SSL substrates and that the filtering process does not impair the intrinsic SSL of the graphite flakes. Remarkably, the proposed filtering method is expected to be integrated into our mature automated transfer and measurement system, thereby enabling practical implementation. Moreover, further characterization and statistical results reveal that the robustness of the filtered graphite flake indeed originates from its intrinsic single crystalline graphite layer. Such understanding of the robustness in SSL graphite has pioneered a new pathway for enhancing the stability, by decreasing the thickness of the graphite layer to that of the intrinsic single crystalline graphite layer. Last but not least, our electrical measurement method holds the potential to develop into a characterization method specifically targeting the internal crystal information on vdW materials on a horizontally large scale.
