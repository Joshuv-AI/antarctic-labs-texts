Healthy live birth after microsurgical enucleation of tripronuclear human zygote derived from ICSI: a case report
Authors: Xiaoming Xu, Xiuhe Zuo, Shan Du, Chunlai Zhang, Haowen Xu, Yabing Luo, Ran Shi, Shumin Hu, Huan Shen, Yu Wang, Haiwei Lin, Chun So, Fuxi Zhu, Xi Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the process of in vitro fertilization (IVF), zygote is considered as normally fertilized when there are two pronuclei (PN), one from sperm and the other from oocyte, and with two polar bodies (PBs). However, it is not uncommon to encounter tripronuclear (3PN) zygotes when conducting PN check for IVF or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). Embryos derived from 3PN zygotes often lead to implantation failure, miscarriages and molar pregnancies, and these embryos are routinely discarded or not allowed to be transplanted. Here we report a case where most of the patient’s oocytes (14/20) were immature (MI, GV), or with large or giant PB. After fertilization with ICSI, almost all resulting zygotes (7/8) were 3PN. By removing one female PN in 6 3PN zygotes, two microsurgically enucleated zygotes developed into high-quality blastocysts. One euploid blastocyst was selected for transfer after non-invasive chromosome screening (NICS), vitrification and thawing. The transfer resulted in the live birth of a healthy boy who is now 9-month-old and has no any health or developmental impairments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01816-x.Keywords: tripronuclear(3PN), Microsurgical enucleation, Zygote, Blastocyst, ICSI

Introduction

The incidence of 3PN zygotes in conventional in vitro fertilization (c-IVF) is between 5.0 and 8.1% [1], whereas in ICSI it ranges from 1 to 4% [2]. In cases of spontaneous abortion, 3PN embryos accounts for 15–18% of cytogenetic abnormalities [1]. Embryos derived from 3PN zygotes often lead to miscarriage, intrauterine growth restriction, abdominal wall and central nervous system defects, and neonatal mortality [3]. Currently, the general handling principle adopted for embryos derived from 3PN zygotes, both domestically and internationally, is to discard them and not proceed with transplantation [4].

However, are all 3PN embryos genetically abnormal? Studies have shown that 54.5% of ICSI-derived 3PN blastocysts are biparental diploid [5], indicating that some of the ICSI-derived 3PN embryos may be due to pronuclear fragmentation or chromosomal dispersion, rather than having an extra set of paternal or maternal chromosomes. In 2016, a case of a healthy live birth following the transfer of a fresh ICSI-derived 3PN blastocyst was reported [2]. This study further confirms that some ICSI-derived 3PN embryos may be normal biparental diploid. Recently, even the transfer of a blastocyst from ICSI-derived tetrapronuclear (4PN) zygote has been reported to result in healthy live birth and normal postnatal development for up to 4 years [6].

Is there a method to rescue chromosomal abnormalities of certain 3PN embryos, restoring their normal diploid characteristics, thereby increasing the chances of embryo transfer and pregnancy for subsets of patients? In 2003, a group in Singapore successfully achieve live birth of a health boy by microsurgical enucleation of one surplus male PN in c-IVF-derived 3PN zygote [7]. Since then, many researchers explored methods for removing one surplus male PN from c-IVF-derived 3PN zygotes [8–11]. However, the rate of blastocyst formation after microsurgical enucleation was very low (< 20%), and the efficiency still needs to be improved.

This article describes the case of a patient with spontaneous abortions for 5 times and obtained seven 3PN zygotes after ICSI. After microsurgical enucleation of one female PN, two zygotes developed into high-quality blastocysts. One of these blastocysts was transferred after vitrification and thawing, resulting in a successful pregnancy. The patient underwent a cesarean section at 35 weeks and 2 days due to placenta previa and delivered a healthy boy weighing 2540 g. To our knowledge, this is the first report of a healthy offspring following the transfer of a blastocyst derived from microsurgical correction ICSI-derived 3PN zygote.

Case report

A woman aged 33 years was admitted to our fertility clinic with bilateral tubal obstruction. She was married in 2011 and experienced natural miscarriages between 6 and 8 weeks of pregnancy for 5 times from 2013 to 2019. Since then, she used contraceptive tools for 3 years and had been trying to conceive without contraception for one year. However, she failed to become pregnant. The female patient’s basic endocrine levels are normal (FSH 7.27 mIU/ml, E2 41.71 pg/ml, P 0.55 ng/ml, PRL 12.58 ng/ml, LH 4.7 mIU/ml, T 0.52 ng/ml, AMH 2.6 ng/ml). Both partners had normal karyotypes. Her partner is 41 years old, in healthy conditions and with no family history or significant medical history. Semen analysis revealed normal parameters except mild teratozoospermia (normal sperm morphology rate is 2.5%).

She underwent a long ovarian stimulation protocol with 3.75 mg of GnRH agonist (Leuprorelin Acetate Microspheres for Injection, Shanghai Lirui Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. China) for down-regulation, followed by initiation with 300 IU recombinant follicle stimulating hormone (rFSH) (Puregen, N.V.Organon, The Netherlands) and one month later an additional 5 IU of growth hormone (GenSci, China) was added daily for 12 days. Recombinant human choriogonadotrophin (hCG) alfa Solution for Injection (Ovidrel, Merck Serono, Italy) was administered when more than three ≥ 18 mm follicles were seen. Oocytes were collected 36 h after hCG administration. On the trigger day, the E2 level was 2858 pg/ml, LH was 0.42 mIU/ml, and P was 1.74 ng/ml.

Sperm was prepared using the swim-up method. Briefly, semen was collected by masturbation. After semen liquefaction at room temperature for 30 min, the sperm sample was checked under the microscope. The sperm volume was 1.5 ml; the sperm density was 10 million/ml and the proportion of forward progression sperm was 30%. 1.5 ml of semen was slowly added onto 2 ml of G-IVF PLUS (Vitrolife, Sweden) medium, and the tube was placed at an obliquity of 45° for sperm swim-up for 45 min. 1 ml of the upper layer liquid was gently aspirated into another 5 ml tube. Total sperm count of forward progression was 1 million. To ensure fertilization, we chose ICSI for fertilization. Five hours after oocyte retrieval, cumulus cells were removed with 80 IU of hyaluronidase and mechanically with a Pasteur pipette with an inner diameter of 150 μm. Oocytes were at metaphase II (MII) with one PB, and subsequently used for ICSI (about 3:00 P.M. D0). After ICSI operation, zygotes were cultured in CSCM (Irvine Scientific, USA) medium individually in a 37 ℃, 5% O2 and 6% CO2 COOK incubator(K-MINC-1000, USA). At 17 h after ICSI operation (about 8:00 A.M. D1), the fertilization status was evaluated and the number of PN and polar bodies were recorded.

The couple was informed of the formation of 3PN zygotes and of the possibility of performing microsurgical enucleation to remove one female PN. The advantages and disadvantages of removing one PN operation were also discussed with the patients. The patients approved the procedure to be carried out and gave their written consent (9:00 A.M. D1). Before proceeding with embryo transfer, this work was overseen and approved by the ethical committee (reference number of the approval: 2024-01).

Microsurgical enucleation (about 10:00 A.M. D1, approximately 19 h after ICSI injection) was conducted with the same equipment used for routine ICSI operations, including a micromanipulator (Narishige, Japan), an inverted microscope (NIKON, Japan), a thermostatic stage (TOKAI, Japan), a clean bench (K-SYSTEMS, Denmark), and a stereo microscope (NIKON, Japan). Zygotes for enucleation were transferred to an ICSI operation dish containing G-MOPS (Vitrolife, Sweden) as the operating solution, and the embryo number was marked on the bottom of the dish. The field of view was centered around the microdrop containing the zygotes, and zygotes were carefully rotated for observing the PB conditions. For ICSI-derived 3PN zygotes, if there are two PBs, microsurgical enucleation is not required (Figure S2); but if there is only one PB, one female PN needs to be removed [8]. The criteria for distinguishing male and female PN are as follows: Firstly, the female PN is closer to the PB [12, 13]. Secondly, the female PN is smaller in size than male PN [14, 15]. Thirdly, the number of nucleolar precursor bodies (NPBs) in the female PN is less than that in the male PN (5.6 ± 1.78 vs. 7.67 ± 1.91, p < 0.001, Figure S1). According to these criteria for distinguishing the male and female PN, the female PN could be precisely selected for removal. The holding needle (Vitrolife, Sweden) was first lowered and the zygote was rotated. After determining the PN to be removed and orient it is towards the 3 o’clock position, a mild negative pressure was applied to fix the zygote from the 9 o’clock position The enucleation needle (I.D. 4.7 μm, K-MPIP-3130,Cook, USA) was adjusted to the same level as the PN to be removed and the equator of the zygote. The enucleation needle then entered the zygotes from the 3 o’clock position, first penetrating the zona pellucida (ZP), then entering the oolemma in vicinity of the PN to be removed. The focus and the position of the enucleation needle were slightly adjusted such that the oolemma and the PN membrane basically overlap. Negative pressure was further slowly applied to aspirate the PN into the enucleation needle without interfering the other two PNs. Once the PN is completely aspirated into the enucleation needle, the enucleation needle was slowly retracted and a mild positive pressure was applied to release the zygote (Movie 1). The aspirated PN, the remaining two PNs and the oolemma were checked again to ensure that the PN was successfully removed. Before proceeding to the next zygote, the enucleation needle was cleaned in a PVP (Irvine Scientific, USA) drop several times. After all extra PNs in the zygotes have been removed, they were transferred to CSCM and washed three times before placing them in a 37.0℃,5%O2, 6% CO2 COOK incubator for further culture. On D3, the embryo development was observed and recorded. On D4, the residue cumulus cells were thoroughly removed with an appropriate stripper (I.D. 140 μm) gently and washed three times before transferring into a new culture dish containing about 20 µl of CSCM medium per drop. These operations were used to effectively remove exogenous cell free DNA (cfDNA) contamination. All embryos were morphologicially evaluated on D5/6 before vitrification. The remaining blastocyst culture media were sent to a gene testing company (Yikang, China) for non-invasive chromosome screening (NICS) of the embryos according to methods published by Huang et al. [16].

Immunofluorescence staining

Oocytes were fixed in 100 mM HEPES (pH 7.0, titrated with KOH), 50 mM EGTA (pH 7.0, titrated with KOH), 10 mM MgSO4, 2% methanol-free formaldehyde, and 0.5% triton X-100 for 15 to 60 min at 37℃. Fixed oocytes were further extracted and blocked in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with 0.5% triton X-100 and 5% BSA (PBT-BSA). Oocytes were incubated with rat anti-α-tubulin antibody (MCA78G; Bio-Rad) at 10 µg/mL overnight at 4℃, washed in PBT-BSA five times for 3 min each and incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated AffiniPure Fab Fragment donkey anti-rat (Jackson ImmunoResearch Europe) at 20 µg/mL and Hoechst 33,342 (Thermo Fisher) for 1 h at room temperature. After five washes in PBT-BSA for 3 min each, oocytes were imaged on LSM980 confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss).

Whole exome sequencing and variants analyses

Genomic DNA samples were extracted from peripheral blood using a QIAamp DNA blood mini kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instruction. Whole exome sequencing (WES) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform. Exomes were captured by Agilent SureSelect and sequenced by the strategy of PE150. The human genome (GRCh37/hg19) were used as reference to align reads and ANNOVAR were used to functionally annotated refer to public databases, consisting of population frequencies, HGVS variant descriptions, phenotypes or diseases and variant function predictions. Candidate pathogenic variants were filtered according to the following criteria: (1) coding variants (including nonsynonymous exonic, indel or splice site variants); (2) variants with minor allele frequency < 0.1% in the human population genome datasets; (3) variants predicted to be deleterious by SIFT, PolyPhen2, MutationTaster, etc.; (4) homozygous, compound heterozygous or X-linked hemizygous variants; (5) relevancy for phenotype of patients or not reported [17].

Results

A total of 20 oocytes were collected 36 h after hCG administration. 10 mature oocytes (MII) including 4 with large PB were selected for ICSI fertilization, and the remaining 5 MI, 3 giant PB, and 2 GV oocytes were fixed for immunofluorescence staining. A fertilization check was performed 17 h after microinjection. Only one oocyte was normally fertilized (i.e. 2PN) but was derived from a large PB oocyte, one was unfertilized (0PN), one was at 2-cell stage and the others were 3PN, with only one of these zygotes having two PBs and the rest having only one PB. Microsurgical enucleation of a female PN (Fig. 1. A, B,C, D; Movie 1) was performed on the 6 3PN zygotes with one PB. After the operation, all 6 zygotes survived and became 2PN. After culture, 4 out of 6 embryos underwent cleavage. On Day 3, 3 embryos were available for use, and after blastocyst culture, on Day 6, 1 developed into a 4AB blastocyst (Fig. 1. E, F) and 1 into a 4BB blastocyst according to Gardner’s blastocyst grading criteria. The two blastocysts were cryopreserved for use in future. One 2PN embryo was arrested at the 4-cell stage, and one embryo derived 0PN was arrested at the morula stage. The test results of NICS showed that the 4BB blastocyst from sample 3 had mosaicism on chromosome 15 with a deletion ratio of 51%, while the 4AB from sample 9 was euploid (Fig. 2.). The 4AB euploid blastocyst was thawed and transferred after approval by the ethics committee and consent by the patient. Patient’s serum hCG (227.96 mIU/mL) was detected 10 days after embryo transfer. Clinical pregnancy was confirmed by ultrasound 24 days after embryo transfer. A single sac with pulsation was observed. The triple test for Down’s syndrome was performed at 16 weeks gestation and was negative. Obstetric ultrasound scanning revealed no major structural abnormality at 22 weeks gestation. A healthy boy weighing 2540 g was delivered via cesarean section due to placenta previa at 35 weeks. So far, the child’s health status is normal.

Non-invasive chromosome screening (NICS) results. The 4BB blastocyst from sample 3 had mosaicism on chromosome 15 with a deletion ratio of 51%, whereas the 4AB blastocyst from sample 9 was euploid

On the day of oocyte retrieval, we discovered more than half of mature oocytes with 4 large and 3 giant PB, and we suspect that the oocytes may exist meiotic division error. Therefore, we examined the spindle in these immature oocytes on the second day of oocyte retrieval. As expected, these oocytes all exhibited spindle abnormalities and meiotic errors. We observed that although the MI oocytes were at metaphase, the spindle displayed obvious multipolarity (Fig. 3. B, C,D). One of the five MI that extruded the PB before fixation and staining. Surprisingly, there were no chromosomes in the PB, while sister chromatids could be seen in the cortical area near the PB (Fig. 3. E, F,G, H). This indicates that meiosis I has completed with a failure in segregating half of the chromosomes into PB. Our staining results also suggested that the oocytes with giant PB were often mistaken for 2-cell stage embryos morphologically, but they were indeed MII oocytes with giant PB (Fig. 3. I, J,K, L). Taken together, there were different meiotic errors in the patient’s oocytes.

Immunofluorescence images of MI, MII and MII oocytes with giant first PB (PB1). Although the oocyte successfully underwent germinal vesicle breakdown (C), the first meiotic spindle displayed obvious spindle multipolarity (B, D). No chromosomes were detected in the PB1 (E, G), while clear signs of sister chromatids could be observed in the second meiotic spindle near the PB1 (G, H), implying failure in segregating half of the chromosomes into PB1. The oocyte was indeed MII egg containing a giant PB rather than a 2-cell stage embryo as could be judged by the two second meiotic spindles (J, L) near the cortex of the oocyte (K). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first report of live birth of a healthy baby achieved by removing one female PN from ICSI-derived 3PN through micromanipulation. At the time of this report, the 9-month-old boy has had a normal development.

The main causes of 3PN formation include: (1) oocyte aging causing cortical granule migration, insufficient cortical granule reaction, or immature oocyte cytoplasm, leading to inadequate cortical granule migration and exocytosis, which results in multiple sperm penetrating the oocyte, primarily occurring in the case of IVF fertilization; (2) obstruction of the second PB extrusion; (3) fertilization by diploid sperm; (4) tetraploid oocytes, usually giant oocytes; (5) pronuclear fragmentation; (6) nuclear dispersion caused by abnormal spindle formation, etc. Multipronuclear is a common phenomenon in both IVF and ICSI treatment, with triploidy being the most common. It has been reported that 66.4% of triploid infants are due to double sperm fertilization, 23.6% due to diploid sperm fertilization, and 10% due to diploid oocytes or failure to eliminate the second PB. Typically, embryos with a karyotype of 69, XXY or XXX have a better chance of reaching the second trimester of pregnancy or even being born. The probability of a live birth for a triploid fetus is one in ten thousand, and their lifespan after birth is unlikely to exceed six months [18]. Thus, 3PN embryos are routinely discarded in the clinic owing to their increased risk of abnormalities. However, not all embryos from 3PN are chromosomally abnormal [5]. The first mitosis of the zygote plays an important role, as it is possible to reunit the separated nuclei to obtain a normally developing embryo [2, 6]. However, a majority of embryos derived from 3PN underwent arrest on Day 3 likely owing to chromosome segregation errors caused by multipole spindles [19], and only 10% embryos could develop to blastocysts [20].

Most 3PN embryos derived from IVF are caused by two penetrating sperm. After sperm entry, each sperm carries two centrioles, one with the typical barrel shape (the proximal centriole, PC), and the other one having atypical shape (distal centriole, DC) [21]. The time for routine PN check is always around 18 h after ICSI operation, when the resulting centrosomes begin to migrate to the opposite sides of PN and locate in extremely close proximity to the PN [22]. Performing PN removal at 18 h after fertilization makes it difficult to remove the extra set of centrioles. And if there is an extra set of centrioles remaining in the zygote, it can cause abnormalities in the first mitotic division, leading to subsequent chromosomal abnormalities in the embryo [23].

On the other hand, the removal of one female PN from 3PN embryos derived from ICSI is much safer than IVF, as only one sperm is injected during ICSI, eliminating the possibility of an extra set of centrioles being introduced as in the case of IVF with two penetrating sperm. Therefore, there is a much higher chance for the embryo to undergo normal chromosome segregation during the first mitosis after removing one female PN. In ICSI-derived zygotes, if there are 3PN and 2PBs, it is likely that the male or female PN is fragmented, or there is an abnormality in the second meiotic spindle of the oocyte with a dispersed distribution of the female PN [6]. In these cases, there is no extra set of chromosomes present, so microsurgical enucleation is unnecessary, and blastocyst culture can be performed. After preimplantation genetic testing-aneuploidy (PGT-A), if the embryo’s chromosomes are normal, transfer can be considered. But if there are 3PN and 1 PB, it is likely that the second PB has not been emitted, and the extra PN originates from the oocyte’s sister chromatids. In this case, one female PN should be removed. After determining the basic conditions, such as the fertilization method and the number of PB, the first technical key point is how to distinguish between male and female PN as described in advance, which is crucial for the success of the microsurgical enucleation.

Immunofluorescence staining results (Fig. 3. G, H homologous chromosomes nondisjunction in meiosis I) and 3PN derived from the second PB (sister chromatids nondisjunction in meiosis II) indicate that the patient’s oocytes are prone to chromosome segregation errors during meiosis I and meiosis II. Some studies [24–26] have elaborated that multiple factors contribute to nondisjunction of homologous chromosomes and sister chromatids. One example is if sister kinetochores are separated in meiosis I which separated sister kinetochores interact with microtubules independently, it will result in the nondisjunction of homologous chromosomes [26].

Based on the analysis of the WES results (Tables 1 and 2), we have not yet identified any known genetic basis linked to involved in meiosis, spindle formation and chromosome segregation. Further validation in the screened variants of uncertain significance (VUS) is currently underway. Moreover, the appearance of this phenotype may not rule out the possibility caused by specific epigenetic mechanisms disrupting meiotic spindle integrity and chromosomal segregation, such as histone modification abnormalities (e.g., H3K4me3, H3S10ph), epigenetic pathway regulating cohesin incorrectly, aberrant DNA methylation affecting gene expression during meiosis [27, 28]. The answers may need seek from the future epigenomic profiling (e.g., ChIP-seq, WGBS) from oocytes or peripheral tissues.

We must clarify that the removal of one PN from a 3PN zygote is not a routine procedure in assisted reproductive technology. It should only be considered as an experimental rescue measure, despite our successful microsurgical removal one female PN and healthy live birth. However, there is still space for improvement and enhancement in the technology of microsurgical enucleation. For example, how to quickly and accurately detect the removed female PN in order to determine that it is indeed female PN. Even without a direct phenotype, the theoretical risk of epigenetic mutations or imprinting diseases should be of concern. Long term developmental follow-up of these offspring is essential. In any case, for cases where oocytes are extremely precious, such as patients with poor ovarian response or unwilling to undergo the next ovulation induction cycle due to the high expenses, the removal of one PN from a 3PN zygote could be an option, given the fact that this procedure is much safer and the technical challenge is much lower when it comes from ICSI compared to IVF fertilization. Just like the patient that we encountered, she had experienced multiple natural miscarriages, which caused her immense mental and physical pressure. After ICSI fertilization, most of the zygotes were 3PN. If we follow the conventional treatment without performing microsurgical enucleation, the patient will not have any embryo for transfer and cannot have their own child. Therefore, more researches need to be encouraged and ethically supported in order to benefit more patients.
