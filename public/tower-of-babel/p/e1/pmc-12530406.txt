Effects of Tai Chi combined with Dynamic Neuromuscular Stabilization on postural control and balance in post-stroke patients with sarcopenia: protocol for a randomised single-blind controlled trial
Authors: Jie Zhuang, Dorota Duhova, Lei Song, Tingting Meng, Mengxue Sun, Zekai Hu, Jue Wang, Junwen Long, Yimin Shen, Yueren Liu, Wenshu Guo, Jin Hu, Fen Rao, Tingting Shi, Xu-chen Tao, Lei Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPost-stroke patients with sarcopenia commonly exhibit impaired postural control and balance, which elevates fall risk and diminishes quality of life. This study investigates if combining Tai Chi with Dynamic Neuromuscular Stabilization (DNS) has synergistic benefits for neuromuscular control, core stability and balance in post-stroke patients with sarcopenia. Tai Chi enhances dynamic balance and lower limb strength through controlled weight-shifting movements. In contrast, DNS aims to restore neuromuscular synergy and regulate intra-abdominal pressure. It applies principles from developmental kinesiology. This study seeks to determine whether the combined intervention demonstrates significantly greater improvements in postural stability relative to isolated interventions or conventional rehabilitation approaches.Methods and analysisA single-blind randomised controlled trial will recruit 60 post-stroke patients with sarcopenia, randomly allocated into four groups: Tai Chi, DNS, Tai Chi+DNS and a control group (receiving conventional rehabilitation). Interventions will be conducted over 5 weeks, with primary outcomes evaluated using the Berg Balance Scale and Trunk Impairment Scale. Secondary outcomes comprise metrics from the Balance Manager System, 30-second Chair Stand Test, Timed Up and Go Test, bioelectrical impedance-derived phase angle, Modified Falls Efficacy Scale and Geriatric Depression Scale-Short Form.Ethics and disseminationThe Ethics Committee of the Second Rehabilitation Hospital of Shanghai has approved this study. The findings will be distributed by publication in indexed journals and presentation at global academic forums.Trial registration numberChiCTR2500102577.

Introduction

Stroke is a neurological disorder characterised by significant global prevalence and high rates of long-term disability.1 Its sequelae frequently result in motor dysfunction, manifesting as impaired postural control and compromised balance. Evidence suggests that a majority of stroke survivors develop chronic balance impairments, substantially elevating fall risk and diminishing quality of life.2 3 Furthermore, prolonged immobility and muscle disuse render post-stroke patients susceptible to sarcopenia, a chronic and advancing disorder characterised by the progressive deterioration of skeletal muscle tissue, accompanied by a rapid decline in muscular strength and physical performance capabilities.4 The incidence of post-stroke sarcopenia is notably higher than in the general elderly population. Motor unit degeneration begins within 4–30 hours after a stroke. Contralateral limb muscle atrophy and strength deficits usually appear later, between 3 weeks and 6 months post-stroke. This sarcopenic progression exacerbates postural instability, thereby perpetuating a cycle of heightened fall risk.5 6 Additionally, research in stroke and sarcopenia consistently shows impaired function in the diaphragm and abdominal muscles. Asymmetric breathing patterns are also common. Together, these problems disrupt the regulation of intra-abdominal pressure (IAP). This disruption contributes to reduced core control capacity.79 Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore intervention strategies to improve muscle strength, dynamic balance and core control in post-stroke sarcopenia.

In recent years, non-pharmacological interventions such as exercise therapy have emerged as promising strategies for enhancing muscle function and balance in post-stroke sarcopenia.10 Tai Chi, a traditional Chinese mind-body exercise, features slow movements, weight shifting and single-leg support. It has been demonstrated to significantly enhance dynamic balance in older adults and improve motor function and lower limb strength in patients who had a stroke.11 12 Since the reduction in core strength among post-stroke patients with sarcopenia is also a contributing factor to impaired postural control and balance, we have incorporated Dynamic Neuromuscular Stabilization (DNS) as an intervention. DNS, developed by Czech scholar Pavel Kolar based on developmental kinesiology theory, emphasises restoring neuromuscular synergy patterns from infant developmental stages to optimise central nervous system (CNS) control of posture and movement.13 The core principles focus on activating the Integrated Spinal Stabilizing System (ISSS) and regulating IAP, establishing biomechanical prerequisites for spinal stability. Studies confirm that DNS significantly enhances spinal stability and kinetic chain efficiency by improving co-activation patterns of deep core muscles, thereby optimising neuromuscular coordination.14 The training emphasises diaphragmatic breathing, which facilitates restoring diaphragmatic and abdominal muscle function, stabilising IAP and establishing biomechanical prerequisites for core stability.15 16 Existing research suggests DNS’s potential to improve motor patterns in patients with CNS injuries.17 Furthermore, studies support combining interventions in patients who had a stroke. For example, adding core muscle training to balance training significantly reduced falls and improved quality of life in hemiplegic patients. This was compared with using balance training alone.18 Similarly, integrating Tai Chi posture training with Bobath therapy improved gait symmetry, walking speed and plantar pressure distribution in patients who had a hemiplegic ischaemic stroke.19 These findings collectively underscore the utility of integrating dynamic balance training with neuromotor activation strategies. However, the synergistic effects of combining Tai Chi with DNS in post-stroke sarcopenia rehabilitation have not been systematically investigated.

This research investigates the therapeutic effects of integrating Tai Chi with DNS interventions on postural stability and equilibrium maintenance among post-stroke patients with sarcopenia while conducting a systematic analysis of the biomechanical and neuromuscular mechanisms involved. Compared with single interventions, we hypothesise that the combined approach will synergistically enhance neuromuscular control and muscle function, leading to greater postural stability and balance improvements. If validated, this integrated protocol could be translated into standardised clinical rehabilitation programmes, offering a practical, multimodal strategy to reduce fall risk and improve functional independence. This would advance the implementation of ‘neuro-muscular’ integrative therapies and provide evidence-based support for healthy ageing initiatives.

Methods

Study design and setting

This single-blind randomised controlled trial will be implemented at the Second Rehabilitation Hospital of Shanghai through a 5-week intervention period. Participants will undergo random assignment in equal ratios to four experimental groups: the Tai Chi group, the DNS group, the Tai Chi combined with the DNS group and the control group. They will undergo baseline rehabilitation programming involving five weekly 30 min sessions of conventional therapy. The primary outcome measures will include scores from the Berg Balance Scale (BBS) and the Trunk Impairment Scale (TIS). Secondary outcome measures encompass metrics from the Balance Manager System (NeuroCom, USA), the 30-second Chair Stand Test (30s CST), the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test and bioelectrical impedance-derived phase angle (PhA) and scores from the Modified Falls Efficacy Scale (MFES) and Geriatric Depression Scale-Short Form (GDS-SF). Data collection will occur at two time points: pre-intervention baseline and post-intervention follow-up (5 weeks post-rehabilitation). The study design flow chart is presented in figure 1.

Upon enrolment, individuals providing written consent will complete pre-trial functional and clinical evaluations to establish baseline metrics.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this study is determined using Cohen’s standardised effect size to compare the means of four independent groups.22 23 With a significance level (α) of 0.05, a statistical power (1−β) of 0.9 and an effect size (Cohen’s f) of 0.5772, the G*Power V.3.1 software estimates a requirement of 12 participants per group. Assuming a 20% attrition rate, we plan to recruit 60 participants (15 in each group).

Randomisation and allocation concealment

Participants who meet the inclusion/exclusion criteria and provide informed consent will be assigned to one of four study arms through a computerised randomisation process following baseline assessments. Using IBM SPSS Statistics V.20 with default seed parameter (20 000 000), a randomisation sequence spanning 1–60 will be computationally generated and subsequently sorted in ascending order. The allocation scheme is structured as follows: positions 1–15 corresponded to the Tai Chi group, 16–30 to the DNS group, 31–45 to the combined intervention group and 46–60 to the control group. An independent staff member, unaffiliated with participant recruitment, outcome assessment or treatment delivery, communicates group assignments via telephone before initiating interventions.

Blinding

Given the inherent characteristics of the intervention, participant blinding to group allocation was not feasible. To mitigate potential bias, all participants will receive standardised information stating that their assigned exercise protocol is expected to enhance physical functioning and quality of life outcomes. Treatment groups are coded as 1, 2, 3 and 4 throughout the trial. Blinding procedures regarding group allocation are strictly maintained for outcome assessors, data management personnel and statisticians. The principal investigator will preserve the master code list, with group designation disclosure occurring only after the finalisation of all statistical analyses.

Interventions

Tai Chi group (group 1)

Based on conventional rehabilitation, the Tai Chi group will receive Tai Chi training lasting 5 weeks, with three sessions per week. Each 30 min session is consisted of standardised components: a 5 min warm-up, a 20 min core training and a 5 min cool-down. The intervention protocol incorporates six postures from the 24-form simplified Tai Chi promulgated by the General Administration of Sport of China: Commencing, Parting the Wild Horse’s Mane, Brush Knee and Twist Step, Reverse Reeling Forearm, Wave Hands Like Clouds and Closing.12 To ensure standardised implementation, an expert-led intervention team comprising certified Tai Chi instructors and experienced therapists will be established. The experts will provide standardised training and competency assessments for the therapists responsible for the Tai Chi intervention. During the initial acclimatisation phase (week 1), participants will undergo systematic instruction in movement patterns and procedural sequence familiarisation. Subsequent sessions (weeks 2–5) will prioritise functional capacity-based standardisation of motor sequences. The detailed steps of the actions are illustrated in onlinesupplemental table 2 figure 1.

DNS group (group 2)

Based on conventional rehabilitation, the DNS group will receive a 5-week training programme consisting of three weekly sessions, each lasting 30 min. To ensure standardised DNS protocol implementation, the intervention will be conducted by two therapists certified in DNS training. The standardised DNS procedures will be established as follows: (1) the therapist will first position the participants in a supine position to achieve neutral alignment of the sternum, thoracolumbar spine and ribs. With hands placed on the participant’s abdominal wall, the therapist will guide diaphragmatic breathing to activate the transversus abdominis and pelvic floor muscles, thereby enhancing IAP and core stability, and (2) following respiratory control, participants start with sagittal plane stabilisation in key motor patterns such as the following: supine 3-month position, prone 3-month position, side-lying, quadruped, sitting, kneeling and standing, and (3) once participants can correctly perform the aforementioned exercises, they should progress to advanced core stability exercises including dead bug exercise, prone position exercises, quadruped position training, seated reaching and sit-to-stand exercises. Each session will be performed in five positions (supine, prone, quadruped, sitting and standing). From weeks 1 to 5, the number of sets and repetitions will gradually increase based on individual performance. If the patient experiences pain, spasms or similar symptoms during training, simplify the corresponding movements or regress to easier exercises. The team responsible for DNS intervention is led by two therapists who have completed DNS training and hold certifications. They provide standardised training and competency assessments for other therapists to ensure consistency among the treatment providers. The detailed steps of the actions are also illustrated in onlinesupplemental table 2 figure 2.

Tai Chi+DNS group (group 3)

Based on conventional rehabilitation, the combination group’s intervention protocol integrates DNS training and Tai Chi practice in each session. This regimen will be administered three times weekly over 5 weeks, with each session lasting 60 min. The sequential design, initiating with DNS to activate IAP and engage the spinal stabilisation system before Tai Chi training, enhances neuromuscular coordination and maintains optimal postural control throughout the exercise progression.

Control group (group 4)

The control group will receive conventional rehabilitation training lasting 5 weeks, with five sessions per week (30 min per session). This training comprises muscle strength training for the upper and lower limbs, stretching exercises, sit-to-stand balance training, gait training and other related therapeutic exercises.

Primary outcome measures

Static and dynamic balance performance will be assessed using the BBS, a clinically validated tool extensively applied in stroke and sarcopenia populations.24 25 This 14-item instrument quantifies balance control across seated, standing and functional mobility tasks (eg, sit-to-stand transfers, eyes-closed stance) with cumulative scores ranging from 0 to 56. Concurrently, trunk functional integrity will be measured through the TIS, a psychometrically robust assessment evaluating static stability, dynamic coordination and postural adaptability via 23-item metrics. Both instruments require standardised administration protocols: participants perform tasks seated without backrest support or upper limb engagement. Scoring methodologies follow summation principles, where elevated total scores directly correlate with superior motor performance. Previous studies have confirmed the TIS’s reproducibility and criterion validity in stroke cohorts.

Secondary outcome measures

The Limits of Stability (LOS) test and sit-to-stand transition will be assessed using the Balance Manager System (NeuroCom, USA). The postural stability limits test recorded three key parameters: (1) maximal excursions (MXE), representing the percentage of theoretical limit achieved in anterior-posterior and medial-lateral directions without losing balance, where higher values indicate superior balance control; (2) directional control (DCL), quantifying movement path accuracy during LOS testing as a percentage, with elevated percentages reflecting better directional precision; and (3) movement velocity (MVL), measuring the rate of centre of gravity displacement in degrees/second, where lower values indicate better postural stabilisation. These metrics collectively evaluate patients’ dynamic postural control capabilities, with MXE reflecting maximal stability boundaries, DCL assessing movement trajectory accuracy and MVL characterising movement efficiency during weight-shifting tasks.26

Patients are instructed to remove footwear and sit on a standardised seat cushion during the sit-to-stand test. With feet positioned shoulder width apart, they stand up without using their hands for support. Three biomechanical parameters are recorded: centre of gravity transfer time (the time required to shift body weight from the seated position to full bipedal stance), post-standing sway velocity (rate of centre of gravity oscillations measured in degrees/second following standing completion) and bilateral weight symmetry percentage (quantifying lower extremity load distribution symmetry post-rising, expressed as a percentage deviation from ideal 0% asymmetry). Shorter transfer times indicate better balance control. Reduced sway velocities and symmetry percentages close to 0% also indicate better control. These improvements reflect optimised neuromuscular coordination during sit-to-stand transitions.26

Lower limb strength and functional capacity will be assessed using two standardised measures: the 30s CST and the TUG test. The 30s CST is a validated measure for assessing lower extremity muscle strength and endurance in sarcopenia, quantifying the number of unsupported transitions from a seated to a fully upright position completed within 30 s.27 The TUG test, a validated clinical tool, quantifies functional mobility by measuring the time (in seconds) required for participants to rise from a chair, walk 3 m at a safe pace, turn around and return to a seated position.25 These metrics are critical for identifying sarcopenia-related impairments, where suboptimal performance (fewer stands in the 30s CST or prolonged TUG times) correlates with diminished neuromuscular capacity and elevated fall risk. Additionally, the MFES assesses fear of falling and activity-specific confidence levels. It comprises 14 daily activities (eg, dressing, stair climbing), each rated on a 0–10 scale. The total score ranges from 0 to 140, where higher scores indicate greater confidence in performing these activities without falling.

The therapeutic efficacy of sarcopenia interventions will also be assessed via PhA, derived from bioelectrical impedance analysis using the InBody S10 device, to evaluate exercise training effects on muscle quality. Higher PhA values are associated with enhanced nutritional status and muscular health, with prior studies validating PhA as a reliable biomarker of muscle quality improvement.28 In parallel, psychological status will be evaluated using the GDS-SF, a 15-item instrument in which scores ≥5 indicate clinically relevant depressive symptoms.

Assessment of adverse events

Principal investigators shall systematically document and report all adverse events (AEs) in case report forms (CRFs). For each AE, precise documentation will include temporal characteristics (date of onset and duration), intensity grading according to standardised criteria, therapeutic interventions administered and subsequent clinical outcomes. In serious AEs, immediate notification must be submitted to the principal investigator and institutional review board (IRB) within 24 hours of detection. The IRB shall convene promptly to determine the continuation of therapeutic protocols through a comprehensive risk-benefit analysis. Protocol-mandated discontinuation of investigational interventions will occur upon clinically significant disease progression documentation. Participants experiencing trial-related injuries will be entitled to appropriate compensation as stipulated in the study’s insurance provisions and informed consent documentation.

Statistical analysis

Interventional effects on heterogeneous endpoints will be evaluated through tailored analytic approaches, including longitudinal modelling (repeated measures/mixed models), parametric/non-parametric group comparisons (t-tests/Kruskal-Wallis) or categorical analyses (χ2) based on variable characteristics and distributional assumptions. The primary analysis will employ mixed models for between-group comparisons, with the Bonferroni correction method applied to adjust the overall significance level for multiple comparisons. All comparative analyses (primary and secondary) will incorporate covariate adjustments for baseline measurements and demographic variables (age, sex). Incomplete data will be addressed via multiple imputations to maximise analytic completeness. Computational procedures will be executed in SPSS Statistics (V.27.0, IBM), with inferential significance defined a priori as p<0.05.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public will not be involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. However, if participants want to know the results of the research, they will receive the publicly distributed materials, including publications or academic presentations on this study.

Ethics and dissemination

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Second Rehabilitation Hospital of Shanghai (no. 2025-12-01). The trial is registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500102577) and adheres to the Declaration of Helsinki and Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines. The ethics committee oversees all study procedures, including recruitment, randomisation, implementation and data management. Any protocol modifications require written approval from the committee. Participation in this study is contingent upon the provision of written informed consent. Study subjects maintain full autonomy to discontinue involvement at their discretion without penalty. Findings from the investigation will be shared with the scientific community through publication in indexed journals and presentation at global academic forums. Digital records will be stored using advanced encryption protocols with tiered access privileges, whereas hardcopy materials will be preserved in access-controlled repositories compliant with security benchmarks. Lead researchers will exclusively manage post-trial data stewardship, aligning with established frameworks for preserving academic rigour and safeguarding participant anonymity. At the same time, to ensure the accurate implementation of interventions, we will establish a chat group and produce a standardised intervention manual for intervention personnel. Participant attendance at every session will be meticulously recorded using a standardised attendance log. We will regularly monitor implementation through methods such as taking photos and videos, while also holding periodic meetings (at least once a month) to discuss any issues that arise during the trial process and make timely adjustments.

Discussion

This study aims to investigate the synergistic effects of combining Tai Chi with DNS on postural control and balance in post-stroke patients with sarcopenia. The rationale behind this approach is grounded in the complementary benefits of both interventions. Tai Chi, known for enhancing dynamic balance and lower limb strength, improves motor function in patients who had a stroke through a holistic approach.29 Meanwhile, DNS focuses on restoring neuromuscular synergy patterns. It emphasises core stability through diaphragmatic breathing and activation of deep core muscles.30

The integration of Tai Chi and DNS offers a comprehensive rehabilitation strategy by addressing dynamic balance and core stability. Tai Chi’s emphasis on weight shifting and single-leg support can enhance lower extremity strength and postural control. DNS also focuses on IAP regulation and neuromuscular coordination to improve core stability and overall motor patterns.3133 Critically, in the combined intervention group, DNS exercises are administered before Tai Chi practice within each session. This sequential design is grounded in developmental kinesiology principles. Initiating with DNS facilitates the activation of the ISSS and optimal regulation of IAP. Establishing this foundational core stability and neuromuscular synergy first is hypothesised to prime the neuromuscular system. Consequently, when participants transition to Tai Chi’s dynamic weight-shifting and balance challenges, they can achieve better neuromuscular coordination, maintain more efficient postural alignment and execute movements with greater control and stability throughout the exercise progression. Our hope is that this combined approach will offer superior outcomes to single interventions by synergistically enhancing neuromuscular control and muscle function.

This study, designed as a randomised controlled trial with multiple intervention groups, enables a comprehensive comparison of the effects of Tai Chi, DNS and their combination. Including conventional rehabilitation as a baseline ensures all participants receive standard care, while the control group provides a reference for evaluating the interventions’ efficacy. However, several limitations warrant consideration. The 5-week intervention period restricts assessment of long-term efficacy, necessitating future studies with extended follow-up. Generalisability is limited by excluding participants with severe spasticity or significant cognitive impairment. Future research should explore intervention feasibility in these subgroups, optimise dosing protocols, evaluate long-term adherence and investigate underlying mechanisms using electromyography or advanced imaging.

The primary outcome measures, including the BBS and TIS, will comprehensively assess postural control and balance improvements. Secondary measures like the Balance Manager System and TUG test will provide additional insights into functional capacity and mobility. Meanwhile, the PhA will monitor improvements in sarcopenia and identify specific body regions with enhanced muscle recovery outcomes from the intervention program.34 35 The assessment of psychological factors via the GDS-SF will also evaluate the interventions’ broader impact on quality of life.

If the combined Tai Chi and DNS intervention demonstrates superior efficacy, it could offer novel insights for clinical rehabilitation, promote the practical application of integrative therapies and support healthy ageing initiatives.
