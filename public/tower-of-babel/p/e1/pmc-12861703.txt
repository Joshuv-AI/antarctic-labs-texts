Circulating miRNAs combined with AFP improve diagnostic accuracy for HBV-associated hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Ngo Dieu Hoa, Dao Phuong Giang, Nguyen Thi Thu Hien, Dang Thi Ngoc Dung, Mai Thanh Binh, Ngo Tat Trung, Tran Thi Thanh Huyen, Hoang Van Tong, Thirumalaisamy P Velavan, Le Huu Song, Nghiem Xuan Hoan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly detection of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains challenging due to the suboptimal diagnostic accuracy of alpha-fetoprotein (AFP). Circulating microRNAs (miRNAs), given their blood stability and role in cancer biology, have emerged as promising non-invasive biomarkers. This study aimed to assess the diagnostic value of serum miR-21-5p, miR-34a-5p, and miR-30d-3p, individually and in combination with AFP, for distinguishing HBV-related HCC from chronic hepatitis B (CHB) and liver cirrhosis (LC) in a Vietnamese patient cohort.MethodsA total of 450 participants were recruited, including 150 patients with HBV-related HCC, 150 with HBV-related LC, and 150 with CHB. Serum miRNA expression levels were quantified using RT-qPCR and were assessed for the diagnostic performance using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve.ResultsExpression of circulating miR-21-5p, miR-34a-5p, and miR-30d-3p was significantly upregulated in HCC patients compared to those with CHB and LC (p < 0.001). In which, miR-21-5p presented the highest relative increase, with a 5.43-fold elevation versus CHB. Generalized linear models incorporating single miRNAs (GLM1-3) adjusted for age and gender achieved AUCs = 0.74–0.75 (HCC vs LC); 0.82–0.83 (HCC vs. non-HCC), and 0.9–0.92 (HCC vs. CHB). Notably, the 3-miRNA model adjusted for age and gender (GLM7) demonstrated strong discriminatory power: AUC = 0.92 (HCC vs. CHB), 0.75 (HCC vs. LC), and 0.83 (HCC vs. non-HCC). When AFP was integrated, diagnostic performance improved markedly across all comparisons: AUC = 0.97 (HCC vs. CHB), 0.84 (HCC vs. LC), and 0.91 (HCC vs. non-HCC).ConclusionCombining circulating miR-21-5p, miR-34a-5p, and miR-30d-3p with AFP significantly enhances diagnostic accuracy for HBV-related HCC, supporting their utility as complementary non-invasive biomarkers for early screening.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, MicroRNA biomarkers, Early detection, Liver cancer screening, Diagnostic biomarkers, Circulating miRNAs

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the most common type of primary liver cancer, accounts for about 80 % of liver cancer cases globally [1,2]. It is the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths, with a five-year survival rate of just 18 % [3]. By 2030, liver cancer is projected to claim over one million lives annually, underscoring its growing impact on global health [4]. The high mortality rate is largely due to late-stage diagnosis, which limits treatment options [5]. Given HCC's aggressive progression and poor prognosis, early detection is essential for improving survival outcomes.

Current diagnostic methods for HCC include serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) testing and imaging modalities such as abdominal ultrasound (US), computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [6,7]. Although widely used, each method has significant limitations. AFP lacks adequate sensitivity and specificity, as elevated levels can also be seen in chronic liver conditions like hepatitis and cirrhosis [8]. Ultrasound, while non-invasive and cost-effective, is highly operator-dependent, resulting in variable accuracy [7]. CT offers detailed imaging but involves ionizing radiation, whereas MRI, though more precise, is costly and often inaccessible in low-resource settings [9]. Liver biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard but is invasive, carries risks such as bleeding, and may miss small tumors due to sampling error [7,10].

In recent years, microRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as promising biomarkers for the diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic monitoring of HCC [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. These small, non-coding RNAs regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally and are frequently dysregulated in various cancers, including HCC [7]. Circulating miRNAs such as miR-21-5p, miR-34a-5p, and miR-30d-3p have shown particular promise as non-invasive diagnostic tools due to their stability in serum and their ability to reflect tumor-specific molecular changes [7,17,18]. miR-21-5p, one of the most well-characterized oncomiRs in HCC, promotes tumorigenesis by suppressing tumor suppressor genes like PTEN and modulating EGFR activity, thereby activating PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways that support cell proliferation, survival, and metastasis [19]. Conversely, miR-34a-5p acts as a tumor suppressor and is often downregulated in HCC. It is directly regulated by p53 and inhibits key oncogenic targets, including c-Met, Bcl-2, Cyclin D1, and SIRT1, disrupting the PI3K/AKT and RAS/MAPK pathways and limiting tumor growth and invasion [20]. Similarly, miR-30d-3p, a member of the miR-30 family, exerts tumor-suppressive effects by targeting elements of the PI3K/AKT pathway and modulating apoptosis through the SIRT1/FOXO3a/PUMA axis and inhibition of USP22, ultimately promoting cell death and suppressing tumor progression [21].

Vietnam faces a significant burden of HCC, with an age-adjusted incidence rate exceeding 20 per 100,000 individuals. A major driver of this high incidence is the widespread prevalence of chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, a well-established risk factor for HCC development [3,22]. Although microRNAs (miRNAs) have gained attention as promising biomarkers for HCC [23], their diagnostic value remains underexplored in the Vietnamese population. Given the pressing need for more effective early detection strategies, further research is warranted to assess the potential of miRNAs in identifying HBV-related HCC in this high-risk group.

This study aimed to assess the diagnostic potential of circulating miR-21-5p, miR-34a-5p, and miR-30d-3p in HBV-related HCC in Vietnam. By analyzing the expression profiles of these microRNAs, we sought to contribute to the development of more accurate and non-invasive diagnostic approaches for HCC. The findings highlighted the clinical utility of miRNAs as biomarkers for early detection and improvement of clinical outcomes in HCC patients.

Materials and methods

Methods and subjects

This study employed a cross-sectional design, incorporating both retrospective and prospective data collection methods. Prospective data were collected between May 10, 2023, and November 30, 2024. Retrospective data were retrieved for the period from January 1, 2020, to November 30, 2024. Each patient was assigned a unique identification code to facilitate access to relevant clinical data from the hospital's electronic medical record system, and the authors were granted permission to access these codes for research purposes. The study did not include any minors.

The sample size for this diagnostic study was calculated based on specificity to ensure sufficient precision in evaluating the diagnostic performance of circulating miRNAs for HCC. The calculation followed the standard formula for estimating proportions in diagnostic accuracy studies, incorporating the expected specificity (Sp = 89 %) for cirrhosis and specificity (Sp = 75 %) for HBV-related HCC reported in recent miRNA panel analyses [24,25], a 90 % confidence level (Za = 1.65), and an allowable margin of error (w = 0.06). The prevalence of chronic liver disease in the general Asian population was estimated at 1.6 % (pdis = 0.016) according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [26]. Applying these parameters yielded a minimum sample size of approximately 144 participants per group, which was rounded to 150 patients for the HCC group to ensure adequate statistical power and account for potential data loss. The same number of participants (n = 150) was used for the liver cirrhosis and chronic hepatitis B groups to maintain balance across diagnostic categories.

A total of 450 patients with chronic HBV infection were enrolled and divided equally into three groups: HBV-related HCC (HBV-HCC, n = 150), HBV-related liver cirrhosis (LC, n = 150), and chronic hepatitis B (CHB, n = 150). All participants were assigned unique ID codes to ensure data linkage and confidentiality. The diagnosis of HCC was established according to the Vietnamese Ministry of Health guideline (Decision No. 3310/QĐ-BYT, July 7, 2019). A hepatic lesion ≥1 cm with typical dynamic imaging features, arterial phase hyperenhancement with portal or delayed washout -on contrast-enhanced CT or MRI, in the context of chronic HBV or cirrhosis, was sufficient for diagnosis. In cases with atypical imaging or normal AFP levels, histological confirmation by ultrasound-guided liver biopsy was performed. All biopsy specimens were reviewed independently by two experienced pathologists.

Inclusion criteria for CHB were HBsAg positivity and/or detectable HBV DNA for ≥6 months, or HBsAg-positive with anti-HBc IgM-negative. Cirrhosis was diagnosed based on clinical signs (e.g., portal hypertension, hepatic dysfunction), imaging (ultrasound), biochemistry, hematology, and Fibroscan. HCC diagnosis relied on characteristic imaging findings (contrast-enhanced CT or MRI), with or without elevated AFP, and was histologically confirmed when necessary. Staging followed the BCLC system. Exclusion criteria included alcohol-related liver disease, drug-induced liver injury, HCV or HIV co-infection, metastatic liver cancer, and use of immunosuppressive or myelosuppressive therapy.

Data were collected using a standardized clinical form capturing demographics, clinical history, physical examination, laboratory results (e.g., AST, ALT, bilirubin, AFP, AFP-L3, PIVKA-II, HBV DNA), and imaging findings (tumor number/size, portal vein thrombosis, fibrosis stage). Liver function and fibrosis severity were assessed using Child-Pugh, APRI, and FIB-4 scores.

Plasma samples for the HCC group were retrospectively collected at the 108 Military Central Hospital. In contrast, plasma samples for the chronic hepatitis B and liver cirrhosis groups were prospectively collected from patients enrolled at the National Hospital for Tropical Diseases between 2020 and 2024. All samples were processed using the same standardized protocol: blood was drawn into EDTA tubes, centrifuged within 1 h to isolate plasma, and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction. This uniform handling procedure across all cohorts was implemented to minimize pre-analytical bias.

Ethical statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the 108 Institute of Clinical Medical and Pharmaceutical Sciences, Hanoi, Vietnam (Approval No. 3092/HĐĐĐ). Written informed consent was obtained from all prospectively enrolled participants after clear explanation of the study's objectives and procedures.

miRNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA, including the miRNA fraction, was extracted from 300 μL of serum using 600 μL of TRIzol reagent. The resulting RNA was resuspended in 60 μL of diethylpyrocarbonate (DEPC)-treated water. RNA quality and purity were evaluated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, USA) by measuring absorbance at 260/280 nm. For reverse transcription, 500 ng of total RNA was converted into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Singapore), following the manufacturer's protocol. Stem-loop primers for cDNA synthesis were designed based on sequences from the miRBase database (http://www.mirbase.org/). Primer design was carried out using an online miRNA primer design tool (http://genomics.dote.hu:8080/mirnadesigntool/index.jsp), and the melting temperatures of the selected primers were validated using Oligo Analyzer software.

Quantification of miRNA by real-time PCR

After reverse transcription, cDNA was diluted in 80 μL of DNase- and RNase-free DEPC-treated water. Quantification of miRNAs was carried out using real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) on the Roche LightCycler 480 II system. Each reaction contained 10 μL of SensiFAST™ SYBR No-ROX Kit master mix (Bioline, London, UK), 4.6 μL of cDNA, 5 pmol of a universal reverse primer (GTGCAGGGTCCGAGGT), and 5 pmol of a specific forward primer. The RT-qPCR protocol included an initial pre-incubation at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 45 amplification cycles: denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s, annealing at miRNA-specific temperatures (detailed in S1 Table) for 10 s, and extension at 72 °C for 10 s. A melting curve analysis was performed at the end of the run to confirm the specificity of the amplified products (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Data were carefully quality-checked, and only samples with clear single peaks were included in the final analysis. The relative expression levels of target miRNAs were determined using the comparative Ct (ΔCt) method, with miRNA-16 serving as the normalization control. The expression changes were calculated as described previously [27]. miR-16-5p was selected as the endogenous reference gene for normalization based on its reported stability and high abundance in plasma samples from patients with HBV-related liver diseases. Previous studies have demonstrated that miR-16 shows consistent expression across different stages of liver disease, including chronic hepatitis B, cirrhosis, and HCC [[28], [29], [30]]. Melting curve analysis confirmed sharp, single peaks with minimal background for miR-16 and all target miRNAs. Distinct peak separation further confirmed primer specificity, and no amplification was detected in negative controls.

In silico analysis and target prediction of selected circulating miRNAs

To investigate the biological roles of the selected circulating miRNAs (miR-21-5p, miR-34a-5p, and miR-30d-3p) in HCC, we conducted an in-silico analysis using publicly available bioinformatics tools and databases.

Selection criteria: Candidate miRNAs were identified through a comprehensive literature review using databases such as PubMed, Google Scholar, and Science Research. Inclusion criteria included: (i) H-index >100; (ii) ≥2-fold differential expression in HCC tissues or serum compared to controls; and (iii) validated involvement in oncogenic signaling pathways including PI3K/Akt, MAPK, TGF-β, and Wnt/β-catenin. Based on these criteria, miR-21-5p, miR-34a-5p, and miR-30d-3p were selected for further analysis due to their consistent dysregulation in HCC and mechanistic relevance supported by multiple studies and computational resources (TargetScan, miRTarBase, UALCAN).

Target prediction: For miR-21-5p and miR-34a-5p, predicted and validated gene targets were extracted from three independent databases (TargetScan v8.0, miRDB, and miRTarBase). Genes appearing in at least two databases were retained for downstream analysis. These gene sets were subjected to enrichment analysis using DAVID and KEGG pathway tools. miR-21-5p targets critical oncogenes such as PTEN, PDCD4, and BCL2, which are components of the PI3K/Akt and MAPK pathways that regulate proliferation and apoptosis evasion. miR-34a-5p, a p53-regulated tumor suppressor, targets CCND1, CDK6, and SMAD4, thereby contributing to cell cycle arrest and modulation of TGF-β signaling in HCC. For miR-30d-3p, which lacks extensive experimentally validated targets in liver cancer, its expression profile was analyzed using the UALCAN web portal (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.2 (http://www.r-project.org). Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR: 25th–75th percentile), while categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for two-group comparisons and the Kruskal-Wallis test for comparisons across three or more groups, depending on data distribution.

To evaluate the diagnostic utility of individual and combined miRNAs in distinguishing HCC from other HBV-related conditions (CHB, LC, and non-HCC), generalized linear models (GLMs) with binomial family and logit link were applied. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated based on the predicted probabilities of HCC diagnosis, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was used as a measure of diagnostic performance. Comparative AUC values were reported to assess the relative accuracy of different GLM models. In addition, Spearman's rank correlation coefficients were calculated to explore associations between miRNA expression levels and clinical parameters. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value <0.05.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study subjects

The clinical and laboratory characteristics of patients with CHB, HCC, and LC are summarized in Table 1. Significant differences were observed across groups for gender, BCLC stage, and Child-Pugh classification (all P < 0.001), with a higher proportion of males in the HCC group. HCC patients predominantly presented with advanced BCLC stages, while cirrhosis patients were more often classified as Child-Pugh B or C. Age, fibrosis scores (FIB-4, APRI), and AFP levels were significantly higher in the HCC and LC groups compared to CHB. Elevated white blood cell counts were noted in HCC patients, whereas platelet counts, and prothrombin levels were lowest in cirrhotic patients. Liver function parameters (ALT, GGT, bilirubin, albumin, and total protein) varied significantly across groups, indicating differing degrees of hepatic impairment. HBV DNA levels were highest in CHB and declined markedly in HCC and LC groups (P < 0.001). Glucose and urea levels also differed significantly, while creatinine, AST, and sodium showed no significant variation. HBeAg levels were significantly lower in HCC patients, though differences in Anti-HBe levels were not statistically significant.

Expression of miR-21-5p, miR-34a-5p, and miR-30d-3p in HBV related patients

We analyzed the fold changes in serum expression levels of miR-21-5p, miR-34a-5p, and miR-30d-3p across diagnostic groups. The results showed that miR-21-5p showed the greatest upregulation in HCC patients, with a 5.43-fold increase compared to CHB (P < 0.0001) and a 2.36-fold increase compared to LC (P < 0.0001); LC patients also had a 2.30-fold elevation over CHB (P < 0.002). miR-34a-5p was similarly elevated, with a 4.73-fold increase in HCC versus CHB (P < 0.0001), 2.62-fold versus LC (P < 0.0001), and 1.81-fold in LC relative to CHB. For miR-30d-3p, expression was 2.16-fold higher in HCC than CHB (P < 0.0001), 1.26-fold higher than LC (P = 0.025), and 1.71-fold higher in LC compared to CHB (P = 0.025). These results indicate distinct expression patterns of the three miRNAs across disease stages, with the highest levels consistently observed in HCC, supporting their potential role as biomarkers for disease progression (Fig. 1 and Table 2). In addition, no significant differences in circulating miRNA levels were observed between patients with normal (ALT ≤40 IU/ml) and elevated ALT levels (ALT >40 IU/ml), indicating that these miRNAs may provide diagnostic information independent of liver enzyme activity (S2 Tables).

Correlation of miR-21, miR-34a, and miR-30d expression levels with clinical parameters

We analyzed the correlation between circulating levels of miR-21, miR-34a, and miR-30d and various clinical parameters in patients with CHB, LC and HCC. Overall, most correlations were weak and statistically non-significant, though several noteworthy associations emerged. In CHB patients, miR-21 levels were inversely correlated with age (ρ = −0.17, p < 0.05), while in LC patients, miR-21 showed a positive correlation with AST (ρ = 0.17, p < 0.05) and HBV DNA (ρ = 0.25, p < 0.05). In HCC patients, miR-34a was positively associated with AFP levels (ρ = 0.19, p < 0.05), and in LC, it correlated with albumin (ρ = 0.22, p < 0.05). These results suggest that select miRNAs may reflect disease-specific biological processes in HBV-related liver diseases (Table 3).

Diagnostic performance and differentiation capability of miR-21, miR-34a, and miR-30d

To assess the diagnostic capacity of circulating miR-21-5p, miR-34a-5p, and miR-30d-3p in distinguishing HCC from other HBV-related conditions, we developed generalized linear models (GLMs) incorporating miRNA(s) with simple covariates (age, gender and with or without AFP). ROC curve analyses shown in Fig. 2 were performed across three major comparisons: HCC vs. CHB (Panel A), HCC vs. LC (Panel B), and HCC vs. non-HCC (CHB + LC, Panel C).

Diagnostic performance of GLM models combining circulating miR-21, miR-34a, and miR-30d with clinical variables in HBV-related liver diseases.

This figure illustrated the diagnostic performance of generalized linear models (GLMs) incorporating circulating miRNAs, age, gender, and AFP to differentiate HCC from other HBV-related liver diseases. Models A1 - C1 represent single-miRNA models adjusted for age and gender, specifically: miR-21 (A1, HCC vs. CHB), miR-34a (B1, HCC vs. LC), and miR-30d (C1, HCC vs. non-HCC). Models A2-C2 represent two-miRNA combinations (any two out of miR-21, miR-34a, and miR-30d) plus age and gender, allowing assessment of additive effects of miRNA pairs in different comparisons. Models A3 - C3 show the full 3-miRNA panel (miR-21 + miR-34a + miR-30d) integrated with AFP, alongside age and gender, which consistently produced the highest diagnostic performance across all comparisons. Abbreviations: HCC: hepatocellular carcinoma; CHB: chronic hepatitis B; LC: liver cirrhosis; GLM: generalized linear model; AFP: alpha-fetoprotein; ROC: receiver operating characteristic; AUC: area under the curve.

Panel A (HCC vs. CHB): Single-miRNA models adjusted for age and gender (GLM1–GLM3) demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance, with AUCs ranging from 0.90 to 0.92 (Fig. A1). The combined 3-miRNA model (GLM7), integrating miR-21-5p, miR-34a-5p, and miR-30d-3p with age and gender, slightly improved accuracy with an AUC of 0.92 (Fig. A3). Importantly, the addition of AFP in this model (GLM8, Fig. A3) led to a marked increase in diagnostic performance, achieving an AUC of 0.97, substantially exceeding that of the AFP-only model (GLM9: AUC = 0.86).

Panel B (HCC vs. LC): Diagnostic performance in differentiating HCC from liver cirrhosis was more modest. The 3-miRNA GLM7 model showed an AUC of 0.75 (Fig. B3), while AFP alone (GLM9) yielded a lower AUC of 0.73. Again, the addition of AFP to the 3-miRNA model (GLM8) improved discrimination (AUC = 0.84), confirming its complementary value in more clinically challenging scenarios such as distinguishing HCC from cirrhosis.

Panel C (HCC vs. non-HCC [CHB + LC]): In this comparison, both single-miRNA models and two-miRNA combination models generated similar diagnostic performance (0.82–0.83, Fig. C1). The three-miRNA GLM7 model, incorporating age and gender, achieved an AUC of 0.83 (Fig. C3), indicating no substantial advantage. However, when AFP was added (GLM8, Fig. C3), diagnostic accuracy improved markedly, reaching an AUC of 0.91, significantly higher than the AFP-only model (GLM9: AUC = 0.80).

Across all comparisons, the GLM model combining three miRNAs (miR-21, miR-34a, and miR-30d) with age and gender demonstrated strong and consistent diagnostic performance. These results underscore the synergistic value of combining multiple circulating miRNAs with demographic and clinical biomarkers for the early detection of HCC in CHB patients.

Discussion

This study highlights the clinical utility of circulating miRNAs (miR-21, miR-34a, and miR-30d) in the diagnosis of HCC, particularly in differentiating it from CHB and LC. Individually, these miRNAs showed moderate to high diagnostic performance, especially when distinguishing HCC from CHB, while their performance was more modest for HCC versus LC. Importantly, combining these miRNAs with AFP significantly improved diagnostic accuracy, supporting a multimodal biomarker strategy.

The role of miR-21 as a biomarker for HCC has been widely supported in the literature. Its upregulation in both tumor tissues and circulation has been consistently observed across diverse cohorts. Prior studies report AUCs ranging from 0.84 to 0.90 in HCC diagnosis, with sensitivity and specificity frequently above 80 % [[31], [32], [33]]. Our findings align closely, as miR-21 demonstrated the strongest fold change and highest diagnostic potential among the three miRNAs tested. Moreover, the combination of miR-21 with AFP yielded a significant increase in AUC, consistent with a previous study that noted improved sensitivity and specificity in AFP-negative cases [34]. This synergy between miR-21 and AFP supports its utility in real-world screening settings, where reliance on AFP alone may lead to underdiagnosis.

miR-34a, often characterized as a tumor suppressor, has demonstrated variable behavior depending on the biological matrix. While its expression is generally downregulated in HCC tissues, recent serum-based studies suggest elevated circulating levels may reflect hepatic stress responses or tumor microenvironment interactions [35,36]. Our study confirmed increased miR-34a levels in HCC versus CHB and LC, and its positive correlation with AFP further supports its relevance in tracking tumor burden. Although miR-34a's standalone diagnostic power was moderate, its inclusion in two-miRNA and three-miRNA panels enhanced performance, supporting the notion that miR-34a provides complementary diagnostic information. miR-30d, though less extensively studied, also demonstrated diagnostic relevance. Our findings showed a modest but statistically significant elevation in HCC versus CHB and LC, with an AUC consistent with previous reports (∼0.82) [37]. While its individual diagnostic power was not as strong as miR-21, its contribution to combined models was evident. Earlier studies have emphasized the value of including miR-30d in multi-miRNA panels, where it enhances classification performance due to its unique role in regulating apoptosis and epithelial-mesenchymal transition, processes central to HCC progression [38,39].

Collectively, multi-miRNA panels showed clear diagnostic advantages over individual miRNAs. In our models, combining miR-21, miR-34a, and miR-30d with AFP improved AUCs across all comparisons (HCC vs. CHB, HCC vs. LC, and HCC vs. non-HCC). These findings are consistent with studies reporting that miRNA panels yield higher diagnostic accuracy than AFP alone, particularly in early-stage or AFP-negative HCC [38]. For example, a five-miRNA panel achieved a sensitivity of 90 % and specificity of 92 %, highlighting the value of integrative diagnostics [39]. Our results reinforce the utility of such panels, especially in high-risk populations like HBV-infected individuals, where traditional markers may be insufficient.

The integration of age and gender into diagnostic models evaluating miRNA biomarkers in HCC is not merely a statistical adjustment; it reflects a biologically grounded necessity given the growing evidence that both factors significantly modulate miRNA expression. As recent studies have shown, miR-21 levels tend to increase with age, potentially due to age-related inflammation and cellular senescence processes [40,41]. This age-associated upregulation could confound the interpretation of elevated miR-21 in HCC if not properly accounted for. By adjusting for age in the GLMs, the study mitigates this potential bias and ensures that observed miR-21 elevations more accurately reflect tumor-specific biology rather than age-related variation. Gender also plays a crucial role in shaping miRNA expression patterns [42]. Both miR-21 and miR-34a have been reported to exhibit sex-specific expression, likely due to the influence of estrogen and androgen signaling pathways on miRNA regulation [40,43]. These hormonal effects may contribute to baseline differences in miRNA levels between males and females, complicating the diagnostic utility of circulating miRNAs in mixed-gender populations. Including gender in the model thus improves the precision of the diagnostic estimates and enhances generalizability across sexes.

The relatively stable AUCs observed across age- and gender-adjusted models in the current study, particularly for HCC vs. CHB, where AUCs exceeded 0.90, suggest that the biological signal captured by miR-21, miR-34a, and miR-30d is strong enough to persist even after controlling for demographic variability. This strengthens the case for these miRNAs as reliable biomarkers. Moreover, the improved performance of AFP-miRNA combined models, even after adjustment, supports the idea that such models not only offer greater accuracy but also maintain robustness against demographic confounders.

Clinically, integrating miRNA biomarkers with AFP presents a promising approach to enhance HCC detection, particularly in at-risk HBV populations. The use of blood-based miRNA assays is minimally invasive and benefits from the stability of circulating miRNAs. Notably, our study showed that miRNA expression levels were not significantly influenced by liver enzyme fluctuations, supporting their potential to distinguish HCC from non-neoplastic hepatic conditions such as CHB or LC. This independence from transient inflammation is advantageous, especially in surveillance settings where ALT levels may vary. However, it is important to critically interpret the diagnostic performance. While our 3-miRNA panel demonstrated a high AUC of 0.92 when distinguishing HCC from CHB, its AUC in the HCC vs. cirrhosis comparison was lower (0.75 for miRNA panel alone, 0.84 when combined with AFP). These values reflect moderate discriminative ability and may be explained by biological and clinical overlap between advanced cirrhosis and early-stage HCC. Both conditions can share molecular signatures and imaging characteristics, which may limit biomarker specificity. Furthermore, the heterogeneous nature of HBV-related liver disease, across different fibrosis stages, viral loads, and host responses, adds another layer of complexity to biomarker performance.

Moving forward, several steps are needed to bridge the gap between research and clinical practice. Large-scale, multi-center prospective studies are critical to validate our findings and assess generalizability across diverse populations. Our findings are in strong concordance with two recent meta-analyses. The first study demonstrated that miR-21 alone had an AUC of 0.89 for HCC diagnosis across 10 studies [44]. The more recent study reinforced the high diagnostic accuracy of circulating miRNAs, particularly when distinguishing HCC from cirrhosis with an overall AUC of 0.90 [45]. Our 3-miRNA panel, comprising miR-21-5p, miR-34a-5p, and miR-30d-3p, yielded comparable or superior performance (AUC = 0.92 for HCC vs CHB; AUC = 0.84 for HCC vs LC when combined with AFP). These results underscore the robustness and clinical value of multi-miRNA panels, particularly in enhancing HCC detection in AFP-negative or diagnostically equivocal cases.

To enhance the translational impact of these findings, future research should prioritize the standardization of miRNA measurement protocols, including pre-analytical processing, normalization strategies, and cross-platform validation. Implementing these biomarkers into clinical practice would benefit from the development of cost-effective multiplex assays or point-of-care diagnostics capable of simultaneously measuring miR-21, miR-34a, miR-30d, and AFP. Furthermore, longitudinal cohort studies are needed to evaluate the predictive value of these miRNA signatures for early HCC detection and treatment response monitoring, particularly in AFP-negative or cirrhotic populations. Integration of miRNA profiles with imaging modalities and other omics data (e.g., methylation, proteomics) could enhance risk stratification tools in HBV-related liver disease. Policymakers and healthcare systems in high-burden settings should also consider investing in miRNA-based surveillance strategies to improve early diagnosis and outcomes for HCC.

Despite promising results, several limitations remain. First, the diagnostic performance of miRNAs is influenced by pre-analytical variables, including sample source (plasma vs. serum), normalization methods, and detection platforms (qRT-PCR vs. digital PCR). Heterogeneity across studies remains a barrier to standardization and cross-study comparison [37,46]. Second, while miRNA testing offers high accuracy, its cost and technological demands may limit widespread implementation, particularly in low-resource settings where HBV prevalence and HCC burden are high. Third, our study's cross-sectional design restricts inference about the temporal relationship between miRNA expression and disease progression. Longitudinal studies are necessary to assess the predictive value of miRNA profiles for HCC development in at-risk populations.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that circulating miR-21, miR-34a, and miR-30d are significantly elevated in HCC and possess diagnostic value both individually and in combination. Integrating these miRNAs with AFP enhances diagnostic accuracy across comparisons with CHB, LC, and non-HCC groups. These findings support the role of miRNA panels as complementary tools to AFP for HCC screening, particularly in HBV-infected populations. Further prospective validation and standardization are essential to facilitate their integration into clinical practice.
