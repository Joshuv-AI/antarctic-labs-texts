Virulence determinant of Senecavirus A: 3C protein functions as a protease to cleave viral polyprotein and host factors
Authors: Houcheng Zhou, Mengyao Wang, Zuo Xin, Yan Li, Fuxiao Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSenecavirus A (SVA) is an emerging virus, classified into the family Picornaviridae. It causes a severe vesicular disease in pigs. SVA 3C protein, a nonstructural protein, in itself is a protease (3Cpro), harboring the typical chymotrypsin-like fold with an S1 subsite. As a protease, 3Cpro plays a dual role in the replicative cycle of SVA. On the one hand, 3Cpro cleaves the viral polyprotein stepwise to generate different proteins. On the other hand, 3Cpro can specifically degrade a variety of host factors. The degradation of host factors may impair one or even more signaling pathways, making SVA not only efficiently propagate in cells but also ingeniously evade the host’s immune responses. Herein, we systematically reviewed SVA 3Cpro involved in its structural characteristics, enzymatic properties, and more importantly its functions in cleaving the viral polyprotein and host proteins, aiming to provide a theoretical insight into the development of SVA-related antivirals.

Introduction

Senecavirus A (SVA), formerly known as Seneca Valley virus (SVV) [1], has emerged as a notable pathogen that impacts swine populations worldwide, conferring high neonatal mortality rates in pigs [2–4]. SVA infection in swine is marked by the emergence of vesicular lesions that primarily affect the skin and mucosal areas, particularly surrounding the snout, oral cavity and hooves. Initially, these lesions present as erythematous spots or plaques, which subsequently advance to vesicles that may burst, resulting in crust formation [3–5]. The clinical signs associated with SVA infection are often indistinguishable from those caused by other vesicular diseases, such as foot-and-mouth disease and swine vesicular disease, thereby complicating accurate diagnosis and effective management [6].

To date, cases of SVA infection have been reported in at least 10 countries, including Canada [7], the United States [8], Brazil [9], China [10], Thailand [11], Colombia [12], Vietnam [13], Chile [14], Mexico [15] and India [16], consequently garnering significant interest from the global swine industry. What is even more concerning is that SVA has been undergoing the trans-species transmission. SVA had been reported to be detectable in mice and buffaloes [3,17]. It was more recently reported that minks orally infected with SVA developed pathological changes in their intestines [18]. Both houseflies and Culicoides have been demonstrated to act as mechanical vectors for SVA transmission [19,20]. Even more incredibly, SVA was recently reported to be isolated from dead stranded cetaceans, implying a major host switching to the marine animal [21].

SVA is classified into the genus Senecavirus in the family Picornaviridae. The virion is an icosahedral particle without envelope, approximately 30 nm in diameter, surrounding a naked RNA genome. Its capsid is composed of a densely packed icosahedral arrangement of 60 protomers. Each protomer consists of VP1, VP2, VP3, and VP4 (Figure 1(A and B)). Like some other picornaviruses, SVA can also generate naturally occurring empty capsids, known as procapsids, in cells [22]. SVA genome is a positive-sense, single-stranded RNA (Figure 1(C)) that spans roughly 7,300 nucleotides in length, featuring a poly(A) tail at the 3’ end, while lacking a cap structure at the 5’ end. As a substitute, a 22-aa-long VPg is covalently linked to the 5’ end, and functions as a protein primer for initiating the synthesis of nascent genome [23]. The SVA genome comprises both 5’ and 3’ untranslated regions (UTRs), as well as a single extended open reading frame that codes for a 2181-aa-long polyprotein precursor. The 5’ UTR is a 668-nt-long sequence that contains a key viral element, internal ribosome entry site (IRES), necessary for initiating the translation of viral polyprotein [24,25]. The 68-nt-long 3′ UTR is predicted to harbor either a kissing-loop structure or an H-type-like pseudoknot. The putative kissing-loop structure, even if present in the 3′ UTR, is unnecessary for SVA propagation, whereas the H-type-like pseudoknot, if structurally deformed, would exert a lethal impact on SVA recovery [26].

SVA virion uses the anthrax toxin receptor 1 as its cellular receptor [27,28] to enter a cell through endocytosis, or even micropinocytosis [29,30], and then is subjected to a series of processing steps. Figure 2 schematically reveals one single cycle of SVA replication in a cell following viral infection. With respect to the translation and processing of viral proteins, the polyprotein precursor will undergo translation, and be subsequently cleaved stepwise into 12 distinct polypeptides, i.e., L, VP4, VP2, VP3, VP1, 2A, 2B, 2C, 3A, 3B, 3C, and 3D [31]. VP1, VP2, VP3 and VP4 are four structural proteins. The other proteins are nonstructural proteins, mainly involved in viral replication, host metabolism, and immune evasion [32]. Out of these nonstructural proteins, 3C and 3D proteins have been extensively studied to elucidate their molecular mechanisms in the interaction between SVA and hosts. The 3D protein is an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp, or 3Dpol), responsible for catalyzing the replication between viral genome and antigenome [33]. The 3C protein has been identified as a significant one that possesses the protease activity, thereby also called 3C protease (3Cpro), which aids both in the processing of viral polyprotein precursors and in the modulation of host immune responses [34,35]. The involvement of 3Cpro in viral replication, along with its cleavage effects on various proteins, highlights the necessity of investigating it to further understand the overall pathogenicity of SVA. Recent studies have emphasized the diverse functions of SVA 3Cpro, which not only are involved in the cleavage of viral polyprotein, but also play multifaceted roles in resisting the host antiviral responses, therefore enabling SVA to persist and replicate effectively in a host [32,36–41]. Studies on the SVA 3Cpro are crucial for comprehending the viral replication mechanisms and the overall pathogenic processes, both of which not only expand scientific knowledge about SVA, but also guide the formulation of novel preventive and control measures. These are vital for protecting the swine industry from the rising threat.

Here, we systematically reviewed SVA 3Cpro in different aspects, concerning its phylogenetic features, protein properties, structure characteristics, and especially, proteolysis mechanisms in cleaving the viral polyprotein and cellular proteins. This review primarily aims to offer a theoretical summary for SVA 3Cpro functioning as a protease in the process of viral replication and cellular metabolism.

Characteristics of nucleotide sequence of SVA 3Cpro

Nucleotide composition

The 3Cpro sequence is composed of 633 nucleotides. The CH-LX-01–2016 (GenBank No.: KX751945.1) is a classical strain that has been isolated from China [42]. The nucleotide composition of its 3Cpro sequence includes 20.85% A, 24.96% G, 26.54% U and 27.65% C, showing no long region with high GC or AU content in its genome.

Putative secondary structure

The SVA genome harbors many high-order RNA structures, such as IRES [43,44] and cis-acting replication element [45], required for SVA replication. Using the RNAfold web server (http://rna.tbi.univie.ac.at//cgi-bin/RNAWebSuite/RNAfold.cgi) based on the fold algorithms of minimum free energy, we modeled a putative secondary structure forming within the 3Cpro region (Figure 3). However, it still remains to be clarified whether the 3Cpro region contains RNA secondary structures that affect the replication of SVA genome. Compared with other sequences, the 3Cpro fragment is susceptible to positive selection during SVA evolution [46,47], suggesting its genetic instability. Mutation profiles after serial SVA passages

Compared with the DNA viruses, most RNA viruses, including picornaviruses, are genetically unstable during viral replication. Their feature of low-fidelity replication is attributed to their RdRps with low-fidelity properties [48]. The degree of picornaviral RdRp fidelity can be improved by the genetic modification using site-directed mutagenesis [49–52]. It has been demonstrated that only one single site mutation, S460L in SVA (HLJ/CHA/2016) RdRp, can even significantly enhance the viral replication fidelity [53]. Wild-type SVA CH-LX-01–2016 has the L460 site in itself at its RdRp, implying its high-fidelity characteristics during genome replication. We carried out 200 rounds of SVA CH-LX-01–2016 passages in vitro. The passage-80 progeny had showed five sites with single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the 3Cpro sequence [54]. More recently, the passage-200 progeny was also subjected to next-generation sequencing, displaying seven SNP sites with 1.10% of mutation rate in the 3Cpro sequence. The comparative analysis indicated such a mutation rate higher than those of VP2, VP3, VP1, 2C and 3Dpol sequences at passage-200 (unpublished).

Characteristics of protein sequence of SVA 3Cpro

Amino acid composition

The full-length protein sequence of SVA 3Cpro consists of 211 amino acids with a molecular mass of 22.9 kD. The DNASTAR Lasergene (v11) was used here for the bioinformatic analysis on the 3Cpro based on the SVA isolate CH-LX-01–2016. Its isolectric point was determined to be 9.59, implying the strongly basic nature in it. All 20 kinds of amino acids were identified at the 3Cpro. The number of each amino acid was revealed in Figure 5(A). Indeed, the total number of basic amino acids (R, H and K) is more than that of acidic amino acids (D and E). Additionally, hydrophobic amino acids (94 in total) are more than hydrophilic amino acids (78 in total). Such an elevated hydrophobic-to-hydrophilic residue ratio in 3Cpro fundamentally dictates structural behavior and biological functionality with profound implications. Putative secondary structure

Further, secondary structures of 3Cpro were analyzed using the Chou & Fasman Secondary Structure Prediction (CFSSP) server (https://www.biogem.org/tool/chou-fasman/index.php) based on the Chou & Fasman algorithm [55], as schematically shown in Figure 5(B). Except R1 to R9, some other regions may be super-secondary structures, such as the motif spanning aa 25–50.

Multiple sequence alignment

For profiling each site with different amino acids at the 3Cpro among SVA isolates, we selected 50 3Cpro sequences from different SVA isolates for the multiple sequence alignment, which revealed 22 sites with single amino acid polymorphisms (SAAP), as graphically represented in Figure 5C via the WebLogo web server (https://weblogo.berkeley.edu). The overall height of the stack indicates the sequence conservation at a given site. The height of symbols within the stack designates the relative frequency of each amino acid at the site [56]. In biochemistry, a conservative substitution is an amino acid replacement in a protein that changes a given amino acid to another one but with similar biochemical properties [57]. Out of the 22 SAAP sites, 10 are conservative substitutions, such as M86V and K201R, and the others are unconservative substitutions, like A39T (Figure 5C). There are substitutions between hydrophilic and hydrophobic amino acids, such as A39T, but is no substitution between acidic and basic amino acids.

Structure characteristics of 3Cpro

General characteristics of picornaviral 3Cpro structure

Picornaviral 3Cpro is a structurally conserved chymotrypsin-like cysteine protease, derived from its precursor, 3CD polypeptide, which shows different conformational dynamics from those of 3Cpro and RdRp in functionally relevant regions [58]. Picornaviral 3Cpro has a conserved Cys-His-Asp/Glu catalytic triad within its active site [59]. Such a catalytic triad resembles those of serine proteases, whereas its catalytic nucleophile is Cys rather than Ser [60]. The X-ray crystal structure of picornaviral 3Cpro was reported as early as 1994, revealing human rhinovirus 3Cpro that, structurally similar to trypsin-like serine proteases, folded into two topologically equivalent six-stranded β-barrels [61].

Two other picornaviruses, hepatitis A virus [62] and poliovirus [63], were reported afterward to determine their individual crystal structures at the same year. Both viruses were demonstrated to have the typical feature of chymotrypsin-like cysteine protease. For example, poliovirus 3Cpro consists of two six-stranded antiparallel β-barrel domains, between which the shallow active site cleft, containing a His40-Glu71-Cys147 catalytic triad, is located. The polypeptide loop precedes the Cys147, and is flexible, perhaps undergoing a conformational alteration upon substrate binding [63]. Subsequently, other picornaviruses, including foot-and-mouth disease virus [64,65], coxsackievirus [66,67], human enterovirus [68,69] and more recently SVA [70], were separately analyzed for uncovering their individual 3Cpro structures.

Structural characteristics of SVA 3Cpro

The 3CD replicative intermediate of SVA has a linker between 3C and 3D polypeptides, which will be proteolytically cleaved to release mature products, 3Cpro and 3Dpol [71]. SVA 3Cpro possesses the typical chymotrypsin fold with the S1 subsite (T155Y156W159C160H178S179A180G181) as a conservative site. Meng et al. (2022) accurately resolved the crystal structure of SVA (SVV-001, GenBank No. DQ641257) 3Cpro at a resolution of 1.9 Å. The crystal was demonstrated to belong to the P 1 21 1 space group with two similar 3Cpro molecules per asymmetric unit. The root mean square deviation was 0.258 Å for all of the Cα atoms between both molecules. According to the study of 3D structure conducted by Meng et al. (2022), SVA 3Cpro forms two major domains, Domain I and II, adjacent to N- and C-terminus, respectively [70]. The topology diagram is exhibited in Figure 5D.

Domain I is mainly composed of a seven-stranded β-barrel structure (Figure 5D, A1 to G1). Domain II harbors one compact barrel core, displaying the eight-stranded antiparallel arrangement (Figure 5D, A2 to H2). Both domains, linked together through one 24-aa-long loop over the “rear” surface of the molecule, form one cleft, at which the His48-Asp84-Cys160 catalytic triad is located [70]. The aa site, Asp84, has been additionally recognized at a linear B-cell epitope F76THHGLPTDL85, highly conserved in different SVA strains [34]. Domain II contains one surface loop between C2 and D2, named β-ribbon. The picornaviral β-ribbon was previously described in a report concerning the 3Cpro of enterovirus 71, which contained two key amino acids, perhaps playing a role of hinges in the open-and-close flipping motion of the β-ribbon [68]. Unlike that of enterovirus 71, the β-ribbon of SVA only shows a closed state in the 3D structure [70].

Properties of SVA 3Cpro in cleaving viral polyprotein

SVA polyprotein that is mainly cleaved by 3Cpro

In an SVA-infected cell, the viral polyprotein is translated, and then cleaved gradually into polypeptides. The P1 polypeptide is cleaved into VP0, VP3, and VP1 by 3Cpro. VP0 will be myristoylated and subjected to the further proteolytic processing to generate VP4 and VP2 [22,72]. Nevertheless, how such a proteolytic event occurs between VP4 and VP2 remains to be elucidated. The P2 and P3 polypeptides are cleaved stepwise into seven nonstructural proteins: 2A, 2B, 2C, 3A, 3B, 3Cpro, and 3Dpol. This process is mediated by 3Cpro. The specific “cleavage” event can occur when the ribosome runs into a conserved TNPG↓P motif between 2A and 2B sequences, where the arrow indicates the “cleavage” site, or more precisely, the ribosome skipping site [73]. The 2A peptide is involved in the function of ribosomal skipping. In Figure 6(A), except brown and red arrowhead-marked sites, the others are cleaved by 3Cpro, as marked with blue arrowheads. Probability that amino acids arise at 3Cpro-identified motifs

The terminology of cleavage positions at a substrate was described by Schechter and Berger (1967), who designated the scissile bond between P1 and P1‘, incrementing the numbering in the N-terminal (P2, P3, and P4) and C-terminal (P2’, P3’, and P4’) directions, respectively [74]. Nine 3Cpro-identified motifs (P4 to P4‘) are marked over the schematic diagram of SVA (CH-LX-01–2016) proteins (Figure 6(A)). Blue arrowheads indicate scissile bonds. The model of 3Cpro-identified sites is schematically represented in Figure 6(B). The 3Cpro subsites (S1, S1’, etc.) are chemical and spatial complements of substrate amino acids. We collected 58 SVA isolates for uncovering the probability that amino acids arose at P4 to P4’ sites of eight 3Cpro-identified motifs by the multiple sequence alignment. The result was exhibited in Figure 6(C) via the WebLogo web server. The height of symbols within the stack designates the relative frequency of each amino acid at the site.

In Figure 6(C), glutamine is prone to arising at the P1 site, which moreover shows the highest conservation value among all eight sites. The P1’ is the second most conserved site, having a preference for four hydrophilic amino acids (G, S, N and Q). P2, P3 and P4 sites are involved in five, six and seven amino acids, respectively; P2’, P3’ and P4’ sites display six, six and eight amino acids, respectively. It is observable that the closer a given site is to the scissile bond, the higher its conservation is. The scissile bond is between P1 and P1’ sites, both of which as the primary determinants directly affect the proteolytic efficiency of chymotrypsin-like serine protease [75,76]. Besides P1 and P1’, other sites can also significantly change the proteolytic efficiency. For example, if the motif “YELQGNVQ (P4 to P4’)” of SVA L/VP0 is subjected to the N→P substitution at P2’ site, the efficiency of 3Cpro-mediated cleavage would increase approximately 9-fold [77]. Although, only through the enzymological study on L/VP0 region, such an N→P substitution significantly enhances the proteolytic efficiency of 3Cpro, this does not mean that SVA tends to undergo the N–P substitution at its L/VP0 region during viral evolution. It is possible that such a substitution would exert a negative impact on SVA replication, whereas this hypothesis needs to be clarified by means of reverse genetics technique.

Characteristics of SVA 3Cpro in degrading host proteins

SVA 3Cpro as a pair of molecular “scissors” not only cuts the viral self-polyprotein, but also can selectively cleave host proteins. Its proteolytic activity degrading a variety of cellular proteins, such as mitochondrial antiviral signaling (MAVS) protein [78] and erythropoietin-producing hepatoma receptor A2 (EphA2) [37], represents an important strategy for boosting the virulence via weakening cellular antiviral responses. Cleavage of host proteins is involved in the His48-Asp84-Cys160 catalytic triad, especially in the His48-Cys160 catalytic dyad [37,79–81]. Innate immunity-related factors

Innate immunity is the first line of the host defending against virus infections. Innate immune recognition acts through limited microbial-specific receptors to initiate rapid antiviral responses [98]. In the case of SVA infection, 3Cpro-mediated proteolysis appears to be central to immune evasion [41]. It can degrade innate immunity-related adaptor proteins, transcription factors, or even directly cleave interferons (IFNs), accordingly disrupting IFN signaling pathways [80,83,87,91,93]. It is particularly notable that 3Cpro is involved in the dual targeting of both IFN secretion and signaling pathways after SVA infection. Based on prior studies, a mechanistic diagram is drawn here for schematically displaying how 3Cpro disrupts innate immunity in an SVA-infect cell (Figure 7), as discussed as follows. Adaptor proteins

Adaptor proteins are a series of signaling molecules required for regulating the innate immunity. Pathogens can be identified by an innate immune system through intracellular or (and) surface receptors. Adaptor proteins are closely related to distinct receptor-mediated signaling pathways, playing a role of bridges in linking the receptors to other molecules [99]. There are three usual adaptor proteins, namely, MAVS, tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor (TRAF) family member-associated NF-κB activator (TANK), and Toll/interleukin 1 receptor domain-containing adaptor inducing IFN-β (TRIF). MAVS, localizing to multiple intracellular membranous compartments, is vital for the innate immunity against viruses [100]. TANK is able to interact with two members (TRAF2 and TRAF3) in the TRAF family for regulating TRAF-mediated signaling pathways [101,102]. TRIF, as an adaptor for Toll-like receptor (TLR)3 and TLR4, is associated with the MyD88-independent cascade [103]. Qian et al. (2017) demonstrated that SVA infection inhibited the type I IFN response against virus by 3Cpro-mediated cleavage for three above-mentioned adaptors. Cleavage sites, corresponding to MAVS, TANK and TRIF, were Q148, E272 and Q291, and Q159, respectively. The cleaved products lost their original abilities of regulating pattern recognition receptor (PRR)-mediated IFN secretion, suggesting that SVA interfered with IFN production to bypass the innate immune responses through cleaving adaptor molecules in hosts [78].

Signal transducers and activators of transcription (STATs)

The STAT family is composed of a series of STAT proteins, which are intracellular transcription factors that participate in a variety of regulatory progressions, e.g., cellular differentiation, proliferation, apoptosis, and immunity [104,105]. They are primarily activated by membrane receptor-associated Janus kinases (JAK). The mammalian STAT family contains seven members, out of which STAT1 and STAT2 are identified in the IFN system [106]. SVA 3Cpro is able to block the JAK-STAT signaling pathway for antagonizing the type I IFN response via a specific mechanism, whereby 3Cpro selectively cleaves and degrades STAT1 and STAT2, but independent of the interaction with JAK1, JAK2 and IFN regulatory factor (IRF)9. SVA 3Cpro is prone to cleaving human and porcine STAT1s at the same site between L693 and D694. In the case of STAT2, a consensus cleavage site, Q707, is also recognized at human and porcine STAT2s, whereas another cleavage pattern differs between them, as evidenced by aa 754–757 in human STAT2, but Q758 in porcine STAT2 [36]. Additionally, SVA 3Cpro can impair nuclear import, interfere with formation of the IFN-stimulated gene factor 3 complex, and trigger karyopherin 1 degradation to block STAT1/STAT2 nuclear localization [83].

Retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I)

RIG-I, a DExD/H-box RNA helicase, is a cytosolic PRR that induces the type I IFN response, thereby playing a crucial role in the innate immune system to identify virus-infected cells [107,108]. In the case of SVA infection, Wen et al. (2019) exhibited that both the 2C protein and 3Cpro were able to induce RIG-I degradation through the caspase pathway, hence significantly reducing the level of RIG-I-induced IFN-β, and weakening the innate immunity in hosts [87]. Some picornaviruses, like poliovirus and rhinoviruses, have the ability to cleave directly the RIG-I in virus-infected cells [109]. Wen et al. (2019) further examined whether SVA 3Cpro could also directly cleave RIG-I, but the Western blotting showed no specifically cleaved band on the membrane. Contradictorily, SVA 3Cpro could interact with RIG-I in cells, and moreover 3Cpro mutants were demonstrated to lose its protease activity for degrading the RIG-I [87]. Therefore, the mechanism SVA 3Cpro uses to induce the degradation of RIG-I remains to be further clarified.

IRFs

IRFs are responsible for regulating transcription of IFNs. After pathogen invasion, IRFs primarily regulate type I IFNs in host, whereas a variety of viruses have evolved defense mechanisms to regulate IRF functions for escaping the host immune system [110]. It had been demonstrated that SVA 3Cpro possessed the ability to interfere with both IRF3 and IRF7 concerning their expression and phosphorylation. Their degradation was dependent on the 3Cpro protease activity, and consequently blocked the transcription of IFN-β, IFN-α1, IFN-α4, and IFN-stimulated gene 54 [93]. More recently, Song et al. (2023) unraveled another IRF, IRF9, which could be also cleaved by SVA 3Cpro concerning the caspase pathway, through which STAT1, STAT2 and karyopherin α1 were degraded, leading SVA to antagonize the type I IFN response in hosts [83].

Swine pro-interleukin-1β

In addition to the above-mentioned factors, pro-interleukin-1β as a precursor form of interleukin can be also cleaved by SVA 3Cpro. Unlike monomeric human pro-interleukin-1β, swine pro-interleukin-1β exists as an oligomeric precursor. Huang et al. (2024) recently reported that SVA 3Cpro cleaved swine pro-interleukin-1β to generate a monomeric interleukin-1β with activity, but cleaved human pro-interleukin-1β to fail to generate a mature product. When the inflammasome is blocked, SVA 3Cpro continues to activate interleukin-1β via direct cleavage in swine-derived cells. Molecular modeling and mutagenesis analysis revealed that the pro-domain of swine pro-interleukin-1β acted as an “exosite” with its hydrophobic residues docking into a positively charged 3Cpro pocket, thus directing the substrate to the active site. The study conducted by Huang et al. (2024) comes to a conclusion, namely, species-specific interleukin-1β that is an inflammatory sensor of SVA 3Cpro [95].

RNA-interacting proteins

RNA helicases

DDX21, belonging to the DEAD (Asp-Glu-Ala-Asp)-box RNA helicase family, performs critical functions in multiple RNA metabolic processes, such as ribosome biogenesis, RNA unwinding and remodeling, as well as transcription regulation [111,112]. SVA infection has been demonstrated to have the ability to degrade DDX21 by means of 2B protein and 3Cpro through the caspase pathway. Nevertheless, neither of them can interact with DDX21, implying the degradation of DDX21 independent of protein–protein interaction. As for the 3Cpro, the protease activity leads it to degrade DDX21, whereas the cleavage site has not yet been defined [84].

DExH (Asp-Glu-X-His)-box helicase 30 (DHX30), a member of the RNA helicase family, possesses the capacity to unwind RNA secondary structures during RNA metabolism through the hydrolysis of adenosine triphosphate [113]. DHX30 acts as a virus-RNA binding protein for interfering with viral propagation at the early stage of SVA infection. As an intrinsic anti-SVA factor, its ability against virus relies on its helicase activity. To bypass such a DHX30-mediated interference, SVA has evolved to antagonize the antiviral effects through the 3Cpro-induced cleavage of DHX30 [85].

Poly(A) binding protein cytoplasmic 1 (PABPC1)

PABPC1 shuttles between the cellular nucleus and cytoplasm. It binds to the 3’ poly(A) tail of eukaryotic mRNAs via RNA recognition motifs, promoting ribosome recruitment to initiate protein translation [114]. Members in the genus Cardiovirus share the highest genetic homology with SVA [115]. Encephalomyocarditis virus, belonging to this genus, had been proven to be capable of cleaving PABPC1 at residue 437 through its 3Cpro activity, generating approximately 50 and 25 kD products [116]. More recently, SVA 3Cpro was also demonstrated to have the ability of cleaving PABPC1 at residue 437 in SVA-infected cells, consequently not only attenuating the antiviral response in host but also preferentially promoting the translation of viral RNA [96].

Heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPS)

The hnRNPs are a large family of RNA-binding proteins, associated with multiple aspects of nucleic acid metabolism, like alternative splicing, mRNA stabilization, as well as transcriptional and translational regulation. In the hnRNP family, some members differ in the domain composition and functional properties [117]. Even so, picornaviral 3Cpros, especially those of enteroviruses, are able to cleave different hnRNPs, such as hnRNP M, K, and A1 [118–120]. As for SVA 3Cpro, it has been also demonstrated to have the ability of degrading both hnRNP K and A1. The hnRNP K can be directly cleaved. The cleavage site is Q364, generating two fragments: the latter (aa 365–464), functionally similar to the intact hnRNP K, facilitates SVA replication, but the former (aa 1–364) inhibits its replication [82]. The hnRNP A1 can be also degraded by SVA 3Cpro through its protease activity in a dose-dependent manner, but unfortunately the cleavage site has not been clarified as yet. Such a degradation process can be attenuated by a proteasome inhibitor MG132, implying the 3Cpro-mediated event of hnRNP A1 degradation closely related to the proteasome pathway [88].

Gasdermins (GSDMs)

The GSDM family, a group of structure-related proteins, consists of GSDMA, GSDMB, GSDMC, and so forth. These members regulate multiple physiological functions, including cellular differentiation, coagulation, inflammation, and tumorigenesis [121]. SVA infection induces the cleavage of both porcine and human GSDMAs, and suppresses GSDMA-mediated cell death. SVA 3Cpro cleaves the scissile bond between Q187 and G188 at porcine GSDMA to produce two fragments. The shorter (aa 1–186) causes neither lytic cell death nor lactate dehydrogenase release, and moreover, neither localizes to the plasma membrane nor triggers cell death, therefore promoting SVA replication by suppressing immune responses in hosts [81]. In addition to GSDMA, the porcine GSDMD was also reported to undergo the 3Cpro-cleaved processing, leading to pyroptosis in SVA-infected cells. The 3Cpro-mediated cleavage is concerned with two proteolytic sites, Q193 and Q277. The fragment (aa 1–277), functionally similar to an N-terminal fragment derived from caspase-1-cleaved porcine GSDMD, can induce cell death, while other fragments have no significant inhibitory effect on cellular activity [90].

Cyclic GMP-AMP synthases (cGass)

The cGAS, belonging to the nucleotidyltransferase family, is a cytosolic DNA sensor that activates a type I IFN response. It has the capacity of binding to microbial DNA as well as self-DNA that invades the cytoplasm, and catalyzes the synthesis of cyclic guanosine monophosphate–adenosine monophosphate [122]. SVA 3Cpro is able to disrupt mitochondria DNA-mediated innate immune sensing through the proteolysis of cGAS but dependent of species specificity. Three 3Cpro‘s catalytic residues cooperatively cleave porcine cGAS, but neither human nor mouse cGAS. The fragment W137KLQT141 is a unique motif in porcine cGAS for 3Cpro-mediated proteolysis [91]. SVA 3Cpro can translocate from the cytoplasm to nucleus, where it binds to nucleosomal DNA, contributing to the cleavage of nuclear cGAS, and, in turn, suppressing the induction of type I IFN response following poly(dA:dT) stimulation [92].

Other cellular proteins

Nucleolin

Nucleolin is a major nucleolar protein, highly conserved in eukaryotic cells. It is found associated with distinct cellular aspects, like nucleic acid metabolism, chromatin organization, and cell proliferation [123]. After SVA infection in cell, nucleolin will be cleaved by the viral 3Cpro, and then translocated out of the nucleoli. The cleavage site is located at its residue Q545. The cleavage fragment (aa 1–545), functionally similar to the full-length nucleolin, contributes to SVA replication [89].

Sequestosome-1 (SQSTM1)

SQSTM1, also known as ubiquitin-binding protein p62, is encoded by the SQSTM1 gene. It is a multifunctional cytoplasmic protein, capable of noncovalently binding to ubiquitin and many signaling proteins, indicating its regulatory role in connecting to the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway [124]. SQSTM1 possesses the capacity to interact with VP1 and VP3, followed by targeting both proteins to phagophores to degrade them for inhibiting SVA replication. To counteract this effect, SVA 3Cpro cleaves SQSTM1 at its sites E355, Q392 and Q395, consequently abolishing its ability to induce the selective autophagy [86]. It has been subsequently demonstrated that SVA infection induces autophagy adopting a defined mechanism, whereby VP1, VP3 and 3Cpro contribute synergistically to the AKT-AMPK-MAPK-MTOR pathway [125].

mRNA decapping enzyme 1A (DCP1A)

DCP1A is a core component of an mRNA decapping complex, and serves as an important factor in regulating mRNA decay [126]. DCP1A negatively regulates SVA replication in cells, while 3Cpro can cleave DCP1A at its site Q343, leading it to lose the ability of restricting SVA propagation. In contrast, a mutant (DCP1A-Q343A), because devoid of the cleavage site for 3Cpro proteolysis, exhibits more robust effects against SVA than the wild-type DCP1A. Although the porcine DCP1A is an anti-SVA factor that interacts with viral RdRp for autophagic degradation, 3Cpro-mediated cleavage of DCP1A makes the SVA RdRp intact, since the cleaved DCP1A fails to interact with the RdRp.

Optineurin (OPTN)

OPTN is a ubiquitously expressed cytoplasmic protein, which is encoded by the OPTN gene and shows a multifunctional feature [127]. OPTN interacts with VP1, and targets VP1 for degradation to inhibit SVA replication. SVA infection makes viral 3Cpro cleave OPTN at its site Q513 to generate two fragments. The C-terminal fragment fails not only to degrade VP1, but also to inhibit SVA replication. The N-terminal fragment sustains its interaction with VP1, whereas its VP1-degrading ability decreases, and moreover its anti-SVA effect is dramatically impaired. Additionally, the N-terminal fragment attenuates the type I IFN pathway. The 3Cpro-mediated cleavage impairs OPTN’s functions in selective autophagy and in type I IFN secretion, implying that SVA is able to survive through avoiding autophagic machinery and type I IFN response in hosts [40].

Histone deacetylase 4 (HDAC4)

HDAC4 is a type of enzyme that removes acetyl groups from lysine residues of proteins. It contains a glutamine-rich domain, folding into a straight α-helix that assembles as a tetramer, which has neither regularly arranged apolar residues nor an extended hydrophobic core [128]. Li et al. (2025) recently investigated mechanisms involved in SVA escaping the host innate immunity, finding that SVA infection led 3Cpro to cleave HDAC4 at site Q599 in a protease activity- and caspase pathway-dependent manner. The cleaved products blocked the ability of intact HDAC4 to interfere with SVA propagation. The HDAC4 could interact with the viral RdRp, and then triggered its proteasomal degradation. The cleaved HDAC4 products were demonstrated neither to inhibit SVA replication nor to induce RdRp degradation. Moreover, the cleaved products failed not only to activate the secretion of type I IFN, but also to induce the expression of IFN-stimulated genes [39].

Tripartite motif-containing protein 32 (TRIM32)

TRIMs are a large family of proteins with the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. Almost all TRIMs have three conserved structural domains: a RING-finger domain, one or two B-box domains, and a coiled-coil domain [129]. TRIMs are well-known antiviral factors, which function in virus-infected hosts through either regulating cell-intrinsic defense pathways or directly antagonizing viral components [130]. TRIM32 was recently recognized as a host factor that interfered with SVA replication through selectively targeting VP3 protein for ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. Nonetheless, SVA 3Cpro could directly cleave TRIM32 at its site E332, causing it unable to inhibit SVA replication. TRIM32-cleaving products not only weakened the activity of E3 ubiquitin ligase, but also reduced the activation of type I IFN pathway [97].

Elements affecting proteolytic activity of SVA 3Cpro

The proteolytic activity of SVA 3Cpro can be improved, attenuated or even completely abolished by certain elements, including catalytic residue integrity, biomacromolecules, small-molecule inhibitors and so on.

Catalytic residue integrity

The integrity of key catalytic residues is a viral self-factor, with which mutations associated can even completely inactivate the SVA 3Cpro in certain functions. For instance, single amino acid mutation, H48A, C160A or combination of H48A and C160A, has been demonstrated to abolish not only the deubiquitinating activity of 3Cpro, but also the ability of 3Cpro to block IFN-β induction in cells [80].

Biomacromolecule inhibitors

Endogenous phospholipid molecules, e.g., cardiolipin, phosphoinositol-4-phosphate and sulfatide, have been proven to be able to bind to an exclusive region that neighbors the proteolytic site of SVA 3Cpro, responsible for significantly enhancing the proteolytic activity. In contrast, a decrease in phospholipid-binding capacity would interfere with the enzymatic activity of SVA 3Cpro. Moreover, if the lipid-binding ability of 3Cpro is impaired in an SVA mutant, the viral infectivity would be suppressed to some extent. It can be concluded that some endogenous phospholipids potentially serve as allosteric activators for regulating the proteolytic activity of 3Cpro in SVA-infected cells [131].

Small-molecule inhibitors

The above-mentioned biomacromolecules are characterized by the positive regulation of 3Cpro enzymic activity. Conversely, some small-molecule caspase inhibitors, like Z-VAD (OMe)-FMK, Z-FA-FMK and Z-VDVAD-FMK, can suppress the cleavage ability of SVA 3Cpro to some extent, therefore exerting a negative regulatory effect on its proteolytic activity. It was reported more recently that Z-VAD (OMe)-FMK was able to bind directly to the SVA 3Cpro through interaction with the crucial enzymatic site at residues His48 and Cys160, while Z-FA-FMK and Z-VDVAD-FMK interacted only with residue Cys160. The Z-VAD (OMe)-FMK experimentally displayed stronger inhibitory effect than the other two did [79]. Such a manner of interaction between a small-molecule inhibitor and the 3Cpro looks like the inhibitor hijacking one key active site at the 3Cpro, thus inhibiting the enzymatic activity. The direct degradation is another manner regarding how a small-molecule inhibitor functions to inhibit the activity of SVA 3Cpro. Rapamycin is a macrolide immunosuppressive drug that abrogates T-cell responses to cytokines [132,133]. It additionally is an autophagy inducer [134,135], causing autophagy that inhibits SVA replication by degrading its 3Cpro in the early stage of viral infection [136].

Other roles SVA 3Cpro plays in regulating cellular processes

Synergistic role of 3Cpro and other factors

Viral proteins exhibit sophisticated functional synergy to optimize infection efficiency, immune evasion, and host manipulation [137,138]. The synergetic effect of VP1, VP3 and 3Cpro has been briefly mentioned above, whereby the AKT-AMPK-MAPK-MTOR signaling pathway would be activated for SVA-induced autophagy. On the one hand, VP1 can trigger autophagy, as evidenced by the degradation of SQSTM1/p62 and augmented levels of microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3-II through the AKT-AMPK-MTOR pathway. On the other hand, both VP3 and 3Cpro can also trigger autophagy, through activating the ERK1/2 MAPK and p38 MAPK pathways. Therefore, these three viral proteins synergistically activate the AKT-AMPK-MAPK-MTOR pathway for SVA to induce autophagy [125].

Induction of apoptosis through 3Cpro‘s protease activity

Members of Poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) family participate in various cellular processes, e.g., DNA repair, genomic stability and apoptosis [139]. PARP1 is the most abundant of members in the PARP family, and is mostly present in the nucleus [140]. Cytochrome C, a small hemeprotein, is loosely associated with the inner membrane of the mitochondrion, playing an important role in cellular respiration. If cells are activated by an apoptotic stimulus, cytochrome C would be released into the cytosol [141]. SVA 3Cpro has the ability of leading cytochrome C to release from mitochondria into the cytoplasm, but in itself was mainly distributed still in the cytoplasm. Concurrent with the release of cytochrome C, 3Cpro induces apoptosis through its protease activity but fails to cleave directly PARP1. SVA 3Cpro can additionally activate caspase-9, -8 and -3, indicating its potential in triggering apoptosis through both the mitochondrial pathway and the death receptor pathway [142].

Disruption of interaction between other proteins

Stress granules (SGs) are cell compartments without membrane, formed in response to different stress stimuli. SGs encapsulate a variety of components, out of which the Ras GTPase-activating protein-binding protein 1 (G3BP1) plays a key role in SG formation [143]. Enterovirus 71 as a picornavirus has been elucidated to induce SG formation in cells, relying on the interaction between G3BP1 and eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4 gamma 1 (eIF4GI) [144]. In SVA-infected cells, SGs form at the early stage of infection via the double-stranded RNA-dependent protein kinase-eIF2α pathway, as evidenced by the recruitment of two markers, G3BP1 and eIF4GI. However, at the late stage of SVA infection, SG formation is inhibited, mainly attributed to the protease activity of 3Cpro. More importantly, 3Cpro blocks the SG formation through disrupting the interaction between eIF4GI and G3BP1. The mechanism regarding 3Cpro that disrupts such an interaction remains unclear [145].

Conclusions

SVA is still regarded as an emerging virus nowadays, increasingly threatening the pig industry in several countries. The viral genome encodes four structure proteins and eight nonstructural proteins. Out of them, 3Cpro has been broadly studied to uncover, to some extent, a series of its molecular mechanisms in regulating viral replication, and cellular signaling pathways involved in innate immunity, autophagy, apoptosis and so on. SVA 3Cpro is highly conserved in its protein structure. Its crystal conformation exhibits that it harbors the typical chymotrypsin-like fold with an S1 subsite as the most conservative site across picornaviral 3Cpros. However, there is still structural differentiation in 3Cpro between SVA and other picornaviruses. The main differentiation is the binding groove, perhaps affecting the cleavage specificity of degrading polyproteins [70].

SVA 3Cpro plays a dual role in cleaving proteins. On the one hand, it is required for cleaving the viral polyprotein into functional proteins. Except two specific sites, as marked with red and brown arrowheads in Figure 6(A), the others are cleaved by 3Cpro step by step to produce structural and nonstructural proteins, contributing to the assembly of viral components to generate mature virions, and facilitating the replication between viral genome and antigenome. On the other hand, its ability of cleaving host proteins underscores its involvement in disruption of antiviral signaling pathways. Cleavage-related host proteins have been listed in Table 1. As a result, by degrading host factors necessary for antiviral defense, 3Cpro effectively creates a favorable environment for SVA propagation in cells.

Despite the growing body of reports focusing on SVA 3Cpro, its precise mechanisms in regulating viral replication and immune evasion have not been fully elucidated yet. Continued interdisciplinary researches between virology and other disciplines, like structural biology, multi-omics and bioinformatics, would contribute to unraveling the unknown mechanisms. This will pave the way for developing cutting-edge agents against SVA infection in future.
