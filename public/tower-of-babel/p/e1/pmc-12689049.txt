Profound hematologic instability in consanguinity-associated familial hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis: a pediatric case report
Authors: Danyal Bakht, Rabia Yousaf, Faiza Yousaf, Syed Faqeer Hussain Bokhari, Muhammad Numan Awais, Muhammad Ahmad Qureshi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction and Background:Familial hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (FHL) is a rare, life-threatening systemic inflammatory disorder characterized by excessive immune activation. It is primarily caused by mutations affecting cytotoxic function in natural killer cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes. Early diagnosis and treatment are crucial due to the condition’s rapid progression and high mortality without intervention.Case Presentation:In August 2023, a 3-year-old Asian male patient was admitted, exhibiting symptoms of high fever, jaundice, and hepatosplenomegaly. Introduction

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a rare but severe systemic inflammatory syndrome characterized by excessive immune activation[1]. It is a potentially fatal disorder that can affect individuals of any age, with a wide range of clinical manifestations[2]. HLH can be classified into two main forms: primary (familial) and secondary (acquired). Familial hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (FHL) is an autosomal recessive disorder with an estimated incidence ranging from 1 in 50 000 to 1 in 300 000 live births[3]. However, its prevalence may vary among different populations and ethnic groups due to the influence of founder effects and consanguinity[4,5]. It is caused by underlying mutations in genes involved in the cytotoxic function of natural killer (NK) cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes. These genetic defects impair the ability of these cells to eliminate infected or abnormal cells, leading to uncontrolled immune activation and a cytokine storm. The molecular basis of FHL is well-established, with several causative genes identified. Mutations in the PRF1, UNC13D, STX11, and STXBP2 genes account for a significant proportion of FHL cases[6,7]. These genes play crucial roles in the formation, movement, and release of cytotoxic granules within NK cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes. The clinical presentation of FHL is diverse and can involve multiple organ systems. Patients typically present with prolonged fever, hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenias, and hyperferritinemia[2]. Other features may include neurological symptoms, skin rash, bleeding, and lymphadenopathy[9]. Diagnosing FHL can be challenging due to its overlap with other conditions, such as infections, malignancies, and autoimmune disorders. The HLH-2004 diagnostic criteria, which include clinical, laboratory, and histopathological findings, are widely used to aid in the diagnosis[10]. However, definitive diagnosis often requires genetic testing to identify the underlying causative mutation.

Early diagnosis and prompt treatment are crucial in FHL, as the condition can rapidly progress and become life-threatening if left untreated. Without appropriate therapy, FHL has a poor prognosis, with high mortality rates[5]. The mainstay of treatment involves immunosuppressive therapy, such as steroids, chemotherapy, and targeted biological agents, aimed at controlling the excessive immune activation. Ultimately, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is often required to provide a curative treatment option for FHL patients[11]. In this case report, we describe a 3-year-old male who was ultimately diagnosed with FHL. The case report has been prepared in accordance with the CARE guidelines and additionally acknowledges the TITAN guidelines[12,13]. The case presents a unique and complex clinical scenario marked by profound hematologic instability, with alternating episodes of normal, suppressed, and elevated cell line counts. It further highlights the challenging management dilemmas posed by this rare condition, as the patient’s treatment course was complicated by a severe septic episode, leading to a significant interruption in therapy.

Case presentation

In August 2023, an Asian male child aged 3 years and 5 months was admitted to the pediatric unit of the hospital. The patient presented with a high-grade fever, chills, rigors, body aches, and moderate to severe headaches. Additionally, the child exhibited pallor, pica, and spontaneous bruising in the absence of trauma, all of which had persisted for 1 month. There was no history of cough, urinary complaints, or drowsiness. The patient had experienced a similar episode of high-grade fever a year prior, which was managed with antipyretics. The patient was born to consanguineous parents and has three siblings from the father’s first marriage, two of whom died between the ages of 4–5 years with similar symptoms. Additionally, his maternal uncle had succumbed to blood cancer.

On examination, the patient presented with pallor, jaundiced skin, and icteric sclerae. The patient was also febrile and exhibited a bruise on the lower lip and left arm, as well as a pustule on the cheek. The abdomen was firm and distended, with the spleen being firm, non-tender, and extending 5 cm below the costal margin. The liver was also firm with a smooth surface, extending 4 cm below the costal margin. Multiple bilateral cervical and iliac lymph nodes were palpable. Preliminary blood tests revealed pancytopenia with elevated ferritin and triglyceride levels, with a normal coagulation profile, viral markers, liver function tests, and renal function tests. During the 1-month hospital stay, the patient received symptomatic management, including antipyretics, intravenous fluids, and a regimen of antibiotics comprising tazobactam with piperacillin, amikacin, and ceftriaxone. The patient also underwent transfusions of 11 units of packed red blood cells and 2 units of fresh frozen plasma, each 130 mL, administered in accordance with the patient’s weight. Post-transfusion, 13 mg of furosemide (Lasix) was given to prevent fluid overload. The patient experienced a one-time significant drop in hemoglobin levels to 3.2 g/dL, despite no identifiable macroscopic or microscopic sources of bleeding. Subsequently, the patient was referred to oncology for chemotherapy, as no infection source was detected. The patient underwent 12 sessions of chemotherapy comprising vinblastine, prednisolone, and mercaptopurine, successfully concluding in December 2023. However, the continuation therapy of 52 cycles with vinblastine, commenced in January 2024, was hindered by recurrent upper respiratory tract infections, resulting in an inability to complete the cycles as planned.

In May 2024, following completion of six cycles of continuation chemotherapy, the patient presented with an 8-day history characterized by fever, rigors, a productive cough, and a single episode of emesis. Eyelid edema without discharge manifested within 3 days of fever onset. Clinical evaluation disclosed pallor, pharyngeal erythema, and a distended abdomen with hepatosplenomegaly. Additionally, bilateral generalized cervical lymphadenopathy was noted, necessitating immediate hospital admission. Urine analysis revealed glucosuria and pyuria. Blood investigations indicated impaired liver function, elevated triglycerides (183 mg/dL), increased C-reactive protein (40.4 mg/L), elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (35 mm/1st hour), decreased albumin (2.6 g/dL), thrombocytopenia (36 × 103/µL), and leukopenia (2.2 × 106/L). Viral hepatitis serology yielded negative results, while ferritin levels were elevated (326.4 ng/mL). Despite multiple urine and blood cultures, no growth was detected. Viral hepatitis tests were nonreactive. The patient exhibited elevated serum LDH levels (809 U/L) and direct bilirubin (3.7 mg/dL). A recent chest X-ray revealed bilateral multilobar infiltrates with sharp cardio-phrenic angles (Fig. 2). Ultrasonography demonstrated an enlarged liver (14.4 cm) with mild pericholecystic edema and a 1 cm portal vein diameter. Additionally, an enlarged spleen (13.8 cm), bilaterally enlarged kidneys, and mild abdominal and pelvic free fluid suggestive of mild ascites were noted (Fig. 3). Management strategies were implemented targeting sepsis, direct hyperbilirubinemia, and FHL. The treatment regimen included antibiotics, antifungal agents, diuretics, Vitamin K supplementation, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), as well as packed cell volume and platelet-rich plasma transfusions to manage hematologic abnormalities and support the patient’s recovery. Discussion

HLH is a potentially fatal disorder characterized by excessive immune activation, leading to systemic inflammation and multi-organ dysfunction[14,15]. The disease pathophysiology involves dysregulated activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, NK cells, and macrophages, resulting in hypercytokinemia and immune-mediated tissue damage[16]. HLH encompasses two primary types: primary HLH and secondary HLH. Primary HLH, also known as familial HLH (FHL), is attributed to genetic mutations inherited in a homozygous or compound heterozygous manner, impairing the interaction between NK cells, CD8+ cytotoxic T-cells, and antigen-presenting cells[14,16]. FHL and related immune dysregulation syndromes are linked to mutations in the PRF1, UNC13D, STXBP2, STX11, RAB27A, AP3B1, and LYST genes. These genes are critical for the cytolytic activity of CD8+ T-cells and NK cells[17]. Secondary HLH, in contrast, arises from external triggers such as infections (commonly Epstein–Barr virus), malignancies, autoimmune diseases, or post-allogeneic HSCT[15].

While literature on the incidence of FHL is limited, the latest information originates from a study in Sweden, reporting an incidence of 1.8 per 100 000 births with an equal distribution between males and females[18].

The diagnosis of HLH is established by meeting specific criteria outlined in the HLH-2004 guidelines. Diagnosis can be confirmed if either criterion 1 (a molecular diagnosis consistent with HLH) or criterion 2 (fulfillment of at least five of eight clinical and laboratory criteria) is met. These criteria include prolonged fever, splenomegaly, cytopenias affecting at least two of three blood cell lineages, hypertriglyceridemia and/or hypofibrinogenemia, evidence of hemophagocytosis in bone marrow, spleen, or lymph nodes without evidence of malignancy, low or absent NK cell activity, elevated ferritin levels (>500 ng/mL), and elevated soluble CD25 (sIL-2R) levels (>2400 U/mL)[10]. Our patient met six out of the eight diagnostic criteria essential for the diagnosis of HLH, including fever, splenomegaly, hypertriglyceridemia, hyperferritinemia, and evidence of hemophagocytosis in bone marrow. Serum biomarkers play a crucial role in distinguishing HLH from infectious etiologies. Elevated levels of interferon-gamma and interleukin-10 (IL-10), coupled with moderately increased interleukin-6 (IL-6), represent critical early diagnostic markers that can effectively differentiate HLH from infectious conditions[19].

Cytopenias impacting multiple cell lines are pivotal in FHL, a hallmark as supported by the literature. A major aspect of our case involved the considerable fluctuations in the patient’s blood cell levels. In 2023, the patient’s RBC values fluctuated significantly, ranging from 2.64 to 4.57 (normal: 4.5–6.5), indicating episodes of anemia, particularly during hospital stays. Hemoglobin levels varied from 3.2 to 13.9 (normal: 12–18), with critical lows, suggesting chronic anemia and episodic acute drops. Platelet counts fluctuated drastically between 17 and 786 (normal: 150–400), indicating recurrent thrombocytopenia interspersed with episodes of reactive thrombocytosis. The WBC counts ranged from 1.4 to 11.9 (normal: 4–11), often falling below normal, which, combined with low neutrophil counts (1.10–69.8%; normal: 40–70%), highlighted persistent neutropenia and increased susceptibility to infections. Lymphocyte percentages ranged from 22.6 to 88.1% (normal: 20–45%), reflecting an immune system under significant stress. In 2024, despite ongoing treatment, the patient continued to experience instability in hematologic parameters. RBC counts ranged from 3.29 to 6.29, and hemoglobin levels from 8.3 to 15.8, suggesting some improvement but still not fully stable. Platelet counts remained erratic, from 17 to 618, with episodes of critical lows and subsequent recoveries. WBC counts ranged from 2.2 to 19.2, with DLC showing persistent fluctuations in neutrophil (12.7–78.7%) and lymphocyte (13.6–79.3%) percentages, indicating an ongoing struggle with immune dysregulation. Peripheral blood smear analysis further supported these findings, showing reactive lymphocytes, microcytosis, hypochromia, and the presence of nucleated red blood cells. These findings reflect the profound impact of FHL on hematologic stability and the ongoing challenge of effective management.

A notable finding in our case was hyperferritinemia, with ferritin levels significantly elevated to 525 ng/mL (normal range: 7–140 ng/mL). Hyperferritinemia is a hallmark of HLH and indicates excessive inflammation and immune activation, consistent with the patient’s clinical presentation. Extreme ferritin elevations in FHL result from cytokine activation, including IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-alpha. The HLH 2004 criteria consider a ferritin value ≥500 µg/L as indicative of HLH. High ferritin levels should heighten clinical suspicion of FHL, especially when accompanied by other relevant clinical and laboratory findings[10,20].

The patient’s clinical situation was made more complex by evident lung issues. In May 2024, the patient presented with respiratory symptoms, including fever, shivering, and a productive cough. Chest X-ray findings showed bilateral infiltrates with sharp cardio-phrenic angles, suggestive of lung pathology. Ultrasonography revealed left pleural effusion and right lung basal consolidation with parapneumonic effusion, indicative of pulmonary involvement. These findings are significant as they suggest secondary infection or inflammation within the lungs, which is not uncommon in HLH due to the severe immunosuppression and systemic inflammation associated with the condition.

In May 2024, the patient presented with high fever, chills, hepatosplenomegaly, and cervical lymphadenopathy. Concurrent findings of bicytopenias, elevated inflammatory markers, and imaging that showed bilateral infiltrates and fluid accumulation supported a clinical suspicion of sepsis, necessitating immediate management with antibiotics and supportive therapy. Research highlighted by Chaturvedi et al underscores that HLH patients typically exhibit elevated levels of activated T-cells (CD 38 high/HLADR+), particularly CD8+ T-cells. A threshold of >7% CD 38 high/HLADR+ cells among CD8+ T-cells shows strong positive predictive value for diagnosing HLH over sepsis. Additionally, cytokine profiling, particularly elevated levels of TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, IL-18, and interferon-γ, supports the diagnosis of HLH by indicating hypercytokinemia and persistent macrophage activation[21].

Throughout the hospital stay, the patient’s management included symptomatic treatments, antibiotics, and chemotherapy with vinblastine, mercaptopurine, and prednisolone, culminating in 12 successful sessions by December 2023. Despite these efforts, the patient experienced multiple episodes of upper respiratory tract infections, reflecting the underlying immunodeficiency. By May 2024, despite ongoing chemotherapy, the patient exhibited signs of sepsis and hypoalbuminemia, requiring an adjusted therapeutic approach. The treatment regimen was expanded to include diuretics, Vitamin K, and G-CSF. The patient’s condition stabilized, with improvements in C-reactive protein, LDH, hemoglobin, and platelet levels, although liver function tests and total white blood cell count remained abnormal. The HLH-2004 criteria guide the treatment protocol for HLH, including etoposide, dexamethasone, and cyclosporine A[10]. Our treatment differed by using vinblastine, prednisolone, and mercaptopurine. Initial chemotherapy successfully controlled the disease, but subsequent therapy was complicated by multiple respiratory infections.

Early recognition using HLH-2004 criteria and prompt initiation of immunochemotherapy are crucial to improving outcomes. However, cautious differential diagnosis is essential to avoid administering potentially harmful treatments for HLH in cases where severe infections may mimic its presentation[22]. The introduction of etoposide-based immunochemotherapy has increased FHL survival rates from less than 10 to 55 to 61% at 5 years, while patients undergoing bone marrow transplant show survival rates of 64% at 3 years and 58–66% at 5 years[11].

Genetic testing plays a pivotal role in distinguishing between genetic and nongenetic forms of HLH[4]. Genetic sequencing is regarded as the gold standard for classifying patients into various subgroups of FHL. While diagnosis was once primarily reliant on a clinical constellation of abnormalities, it is now significantly enhanced by functional flow cytometry screening, which guides subsequent genetic investigation[23]. Consanguineous marriages have been known to be associated with the pathogenesis of HLH[24,25]. Our patient was born to parents in a consanguineous union, along with a history of similar deaths in the siblings, implying a potential genetic predisposition.

Future management of this patient should emphasize advancements in genetic screening for rapid and precise diagnosis, paving the way for personalized therapies targeting specific genetic mutations. Innovations in immunosuppressive treatments, aiming to balance efficacy with reduced toxicity, are crucial. Enhanced supportive care strategies, including refined infection management and hematologic support, will mitigate treatment-related complications.

The patient’s hematologic profile, oscillating between these extremes, emphasizes the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges inherent in managing such a rare and complex condition. Additionally, the course of treatment was notably complicated by a severe septic event, which not only posed immediate life-threatening risks but also led to a substantial interruption in the ongoing therapeutic regimen. The case illustrates the multifaceted dilemmas faced in the management of rare hematologic disorders, particularly in the context of immunocompromise, where balancing efficacy and safety becomes paramount in shaping patient outcomes.

Conclusion

The case of a 3-year-old male detailed herein exemplifies the diagnostic intricacies and therapeutic hurdles encountered in FHL. Initial symptoms of prolonged fever, hepatosplenomegaly, and cytopenias were indicative of underlying immunologic dysfunction, eventually leading to a diagnosis confirmed by meeting HLH-2004 criteria. Genetic predisposition due to familial consanguinity further complicated the clinical picture, emphasizing the importance of thorough genetic testing in suspected cases. Treatment involved a multidisciplinary approach comprising chemotherapy, immunosuppressive agents, and supportive care to manage hematologic instability and recurrent infections. Despite initial success with chemotherapy, ongoing challenges such as pulmonary complications and sepsis necessitated adaptive treatment strategies, including antibiotics, antifungal agents, and specialized supportive therapies. The patient’s clinical trajectory, characterized by fluctuating hematologic parameters and persistent infectious vulnerabilities, underscores the critical need for early intervention and vigilant monitoring in FHL management. Advances in diagnostic criteria and therapeutic modalities, including HSCT, offer promising avenues for improving outcomes in FHL. Continued research and clinical vigilance are essential to refine treatment protocols and optimize patient care for this challenging and potentially devastating condition.
