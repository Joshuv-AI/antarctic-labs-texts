Mechanisms of enhanced performance in Zr4+/Ta5+ codoped rutile–TiO2 ceramics via broadband dielectric spectroscopy
Authors: Yasumin Mingmuang, Narong Chanlek, Masaki Takesada, Viyada Harnchana, Wirat Jarernboon, Pairot Moontragoon, Pornjuk Srepusharawoot, Ekaphan Swatsitang, Prasit Thongbai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aliovalent dopant codoped rutile–TiO2 materials have garnered attention due to their excellent performance properties, characterized by low loss tangent (tanδ), high dielectric permittivity (ε′), and stable ε′ over a broad temperature range. This performance is primarily due to the electron−pinned defect−dipoles (EPDDs) of the complex defects \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{2}^{{3+}}{\text{V}}_{{\text{O}}}^{{ \cdot \cdot }}$$\end{document}Ti3+–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{Nb}}_{2}^{{5+}}$$\end{document}Ti3+BTi. Notably, the excellent dielectric properties in ZrxTa2.5%Ti0.975−xO2 (Zr–TTO) ceramics can be achieved using the traditional mixed oxide method without the EPDDs, due to the absence of A3+ (acceptor doping ions). Instead, the existence of localized free electrons and oxygen vacancies (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{V}}_{{\text{O}}}^{{ \cdot \cdot }}$$\end{document}) in Zr–TTO structures, due to doping ions and the sintering process, was confirmed by X–ray photoelectron and Raman spectroscopies. These ceramics exhibited ε′~ 2 × 104 and tanδ < 0.03 at 1 kHz and 25 °C in the 2.5–10%Zr–TTO samples. Moreover, all ceramics demonstrated a maximum ε′ change (∆ε′) of less than ±15% over the temperature range suitable for X7R and X8R type ceramic capacitors. Significantly, the change in ε′ related to relative humility was calculated to be less than ±0.5% over the range of 50–95% RH, indicating the environmental stability of the dielectric properties, which is essential for capacitor applications. Introduction

A novel dielectric material, codoped rutile–TiO2 with acceptor (A2+/3+) and donor (D5+) doping, has been intensively investigated for application in ceramic capacitors as a replacement for traditional ferroelectric BaTiO3 and colossal dielectric oxide (ε′ > 103) CaCu3Ti4O12 (CCTO) ceramics1–4. The primary challenge with BaTiO3 and CCTO ceramics was their poor loss tangent (tanδ) and dielectric performance across a broad temperature range, necessitating improvements though techniques such as enhancing the microstructure, employing suitable preparation methods, and/or doping with potential ions5–7. In contrast, the excellent performance properties (ExPPs) of codoped rutile–TiO2, which including high ε′ and low tanδ over a wide temperature and frequency ranges, were more readily achieved in codoped rutile–TiO2 systems which have a simpler oxide composition compared to the complex compositions of BaTiO3 and CCTO materials1,8.

Generally, colossal dielectric oxides were easily obtained in doped TiO2–based ceramics, facilitated by the presence of D5+ ions. The reduction of Ti4+ to Ti3+ occurred when Ti4+ sited were replaced by D5+ ions, generating free electrons within the structure. However, D5+ doped TiO2 ceramics simultaneously exhibited a large tanδ, making them unsuitable for capacitors applications1,2. Conversely, A2+/3+ dopants resulted in significantly lower ε′ and tanδ values compared to single–doped rutile–TiO2 with D5+ doping1,9. These values approximated those of pure rutile–TiO2 ceramics (ε′~ 102) due to the absence of charge compensation between Ti4+ and Ti3+. However, A2+/3+ single–doped TiO2 ceramics formed defect clusters of oxygen vacancies (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{V}}_{{\text{O}}}^{{ \cdot \cdot }}$$\end{document}), significantly affecting the doped rutile–TiO2 based oxides4,10.

Hu et al. described the mechanism of the achieving ExPPs in codoped rutile–TiO2 with aliovalent dopants such as In3+ and Nb5+ (INTO) materials. This mechanism was correlated to complex defects of localized free electrons generated by Nb5+ doping and defect dipole clusters of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{V}}_{{\text{O}}}^{{ \cdot \cdot }}$$\end{document} formed by In3+ doping into rutile–TiO2 lattices, under the electron–pinned defect–dipoles (EPDDs) model9,11. Interestingly, the ExPPs were also obtained in codoped rutile–TiO2 ceramics of D5+ with I4+ systems that could not generate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{2}^{{3+}}{\text{V}}_{{\text{O}}}^{{ \cdot \cdot }}$$\end{document}Ti3+ defects like A2+/3+/ D5+ codoped within lattices. Localized free electrons were typically generated by the replacement of D5+ into Ti4+ sites, enhancing ε′ value. Meanwhile, I4+ showed similar results to A2+/3+ doping, with low dielectric properties, especially ε′ in order of 10216,17. Notably, due to the + 4 oxidation state of I4+, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{2}^{{3+}}{\text{V}}_{{\text{O}}}^{{ \cdot \cdot }}$$\end{document}Ti3+ could be ignored. However, defects of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{V}}_{{\text{O}}}^{{ \cdot \cdot }}$$\end{document} might form at high sintering temperatures16,18. Yang et al. reported the existence of defect dipoles related to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{V}}_{{\text{O}}}^{{ \cdot \cdot }}$$\end{document}, following Ti4+ · e– –\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{V}}_{{\text{O}}}^{{ \cdot \cdot }}$$\end{document}– Ti4+ · e– in Zr4+/Nb5+ codoped rutile–TiO2 (ZNTO), which might confine localized electrons, improving dielectric properties16. Moreover, our recent studies presented I4+ (i.e., Sn4+/ Zr4+) doped with Nb5+ and/or Ta5+ achieving high ε′> 104 and low tanδ < 0.05 over a broad temperature range, required for advanced X–type ceramic capacitors. The results were attributed to the interfacial polarization at the interface with a large resistance of grain boundaries (Rgb) due to both D5+ and I4+ effects2,18.

The multiple mechanisms attributing different dielectric contributions with specific activation energy (Ea) to each temperature range were discussed in codoped rutile–TiO2 materials. At least two contributions were observed over a broad temperature range at different frequencies: at lower room temperatures, intrinsic complex defects primarily dominated, while at higher temperatures, phenomena such as IBLCs, SBLCs, and the movement of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{V}}_{{\text{O}}}^{{ \cdot \cdot }}$$\end{document} or O2– ions contributed3,22. Due to the complex mechanism of codoped TiO2 ceramics, achieving simultaneous colossal dielectric oxide and low tanδ over a wide temperature range is still unclear. Especially, I4+/D5+ codoped systems remain largely unknown and require further investigation compared to A2+/3+/D5+ systems. To understand the phenomenon of how ExPPs are obtained and how I4+ performed, Ta5+–doped with several Zr4+ concentrations into rutile TiO2–based oxides, prepared using the conventional mixed oxide method, were investigated for dielectric and electrical properties over a broad temperature and frequency ranges, as well as other characterizations such as crystal structure, microstructure of polycrystalline ceramics, and oxidation state of each ion.

Experimental details

High–purity raw materials, including ≥ 99.90% rutile–TiO2 (Sigma–Aldrich), 99.00% Ta2O5 (Sigma–Aldrich), and 99.95% ZrO2 (Nanostructured & Amorphous materials, Inc.), were weighed according to the chemical equation ZrxTa2.5%Ti0.975−xO2 (Zr–TTO) at varying Zr4+ concentrations of x = 2.5%, 5%, and 10% (2.5%Zr–TTO, 5%Zr–TTO and 10%Zr–TTO). It is worth noting that due to the presence of TiO2 as a transition metal oxide, the substitution of TiO2 with Ta5+ typically results in charge compensation by reducing Ti4+ in the rutile structure to Ti3+ (Ti4+ + e–). Consequently, Ti3+ can be represented in the formula as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{Z}}{{\text{r}}_x}{\text{T}}{{\text{a}}_{0.025}}{\text{Ti}}_{{0.025}}^{{3+}}{\text{Ti}}_{{0.950 - x}}^{{4+}}{{\text{O}}_2}$$\end{document}. Subsequently, 2 mm ZrO2 balls and ethanol were used to mix all raw materials for 24 h. The ZrO2 balls were then separated from the mixtures, and the ethanol was evaporated in an oven at 80 °C overnight. The mixed powders under different conditions were achieved without calcination and then pressed uniaxially to form green bodies, using a binder consisting of 5 wt% polyvinyl alcohol solution. These green bodies were sintered in air at 1300 °C for 3 h, using a heating rate of 5 °C/min and naturally cooled to room temperature.

Several techniques were used to characterize all sintered ceramics. Crystal structure and phase compositions were observed using X–ray diffraction (XRD) with a Cu anode material, operated by PANalytical, EMPYREAN, scanning from 20 to 80 degrees at 25 °C. Moreover, Raman microscopy (Bruker, Senterra II) was used to measure Raman spectra and also confirm the phase composition in the range of 100 to 900 cm−1. Subsequently, all sintered ceramics were polished using diamond lapping film with 6 and 1 μm and then thermally etched at 1180 °C for 30 min to analyze surface morphologies as well as the distribution of elements in these polycrystalline ceramics, utilizing FESEM (Helios NanoLab, G3 CX) with energy–dispersive X–ray spectroscopy (EDS). X–ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), operated by PHI5000 VersaProbe II, ULVACePHI, Japan, was employed to detect the oxidation state on the surface of the ceramics. All ground ceramics were painted with Ag electrodes on both sides, followed by drying at 680 °C for 30 min, and then sputtered with an Au electrode for the 2.5%Zr–TTO sample. Subsequently, the dielectric properties were measured using an Agilent 4294 A and KEYSIGHTE 4990 A Impedance Analyzer over temperature ranges of -258 to -63 °C and − 60 to 210 °C, respectively. An AC oscillation voltage of 0.5 V was applied over a frequency range of 102 to 106 Hz. Humidity measurements were conducted at 25 °C by varying the relative humidity (%RH) in the range of 50–95% at different frequencies from 102 to 106 Hz using a humidity chamber (ESPEC, SH–222).

Results and discussion

The diffraction peaks of Zr–TTO ceramics are shown in Fig. 1(a). Pure rutile–TiO2 (JCPDS 21–1276) was obtained in all samples for x ≤ 5%8,13. However, at x = 10%, an impurity peak of ZrTiO4 at ~ 30.64 degrees became detectable, indicating that at x = 10%, a higher amount of ZrO2 was incorporated into TiO2. This observation was elucidated by the phase diagram and can also be described by the notably high melting temperature of ZrO2, ∼2715 °C (significantly higher than that of TiO2 and Ta2O5)23. Moreover, the larger radius of Zr4+ (r6(Zr4+) = 72.0 pm) exceeded that of Ti4+ (r6 = 60.5 pm) by up to 19.0%, surpassing Hume–Rothery’s law value by 4.0%. The influence of different dopant radii on the host not only affected the occurrence of a second phase but also impacted the shift of the main peak (110) of rutile–TiO2. As depicted in Fig. 1(b), the (110) shifted to a lower degree, resulting in an expansion in the crystal structure (i.e., lattice parameter and bond length) when Ti4+ sites were replaced by Ta5+ and, particularly, Zr4+, in accordance with Bragg’s law8,19. Rietveld refinement was employed to calculate these values, as illustrated in Fig. 1(d–f). Both lattice parameters and bond length values were summarized in Fig. 1(c) and Table 1, respectively. Therefore, both Zr4+ and Ta5+ can fully replace Ti4+ in amounts of 2.5% and 5%, while an amount exceeding 10% can lead to the formation of ZrTiO4.

The presence of rutile–TiO2 was further confirmed by the observation of four Raman peaks (B1g, Eg, A1g, and B2g), as well as the 2nd order, as shown in Fig. 2. Unfortunately, the second– phase of ZrTiO4 peak could not be clearly detected using this technique for the 10%Zr–TTO sample, which was similar to the codoped rutile–TiO2 that has been reported as impurity phase from other characterizations8,24. The results suggested that the probe size related to the limited area of the measurement points might have led to the absence of an accumulation of secondary phase and/or low–intensity measurements. However, the two primary peaks of Eg and A1g, fitted by the Lorentzian function, exhibited slight shifts depending on the wavenumber as Zr4+ concentrations increased. Specifically, Eg shifted towards lower wavenumber (439.6→438.6→435.9 cm−1), while A1g shifted towards higher wavenumbers (611.2→611.9→613.0). These shifts were attributed to the liberation of oxygen atoms along the c–axis and the bending of O–Ti–O bonds, respectively2,24. In other words, the shifting of Eg was related to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{V}}_{{\text{O}}}^{{ \cdot \cdot }}$$\end{document} resulting from the sintering process, and A1g was associated with the replacement of co–dopants into Ti4+ host sites. Therefore, the slight increase in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{V}}_{{\text{O}}}^{{ \cdot \cdot }}$$\end{document} with increasing Zr4+ at high sintering temperatures might not have resulted from the formation of defect clusters like \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{2}^{{3+}}{\text{V}}_{{\text{O}}}^{{ \cdot \cdot }}$$\end{document}Ti3+ due to the Zr4+ effect but rather from the large different in electronegativity (EN) values and ion radii between the dopant Zr4+ and host Ti4+16,18.

The highly dense ceramic was obtained in all Zr–TTO samples, as depicted in Fig. 3. These polycrystalline ceramics consisted of several grains, well–separated by grain boundaries (GBs). The average grain sizes of the 2.5%Zr–TTO, 5%Zr–TTO, and 10%Zr–TTO ceramics were calculated from measurements of 250 grains. The statistical distribution of grain sizes for each composition is presented using a Gaussian distribution function, as shown in Fig. 3(d–f). The results showed that there was no important change in grain size of each condition. The grain size slightly decreased from 6.4 to 5.6 μm at x = 2.5% and 5%, respectively. This result was attributed to the drag on GB mobility due to the Zr4+ effect, resulting from the mismatch of Zr4+, Ti4+, and Ta5+ ionic radii. Similar results were reported regarding the effect of Zr4+ on the microstructure of polycrystalline ceramics, such as Zr4+–doped CCTO, Zr4+–doped SrTiO3 and ZNTO martials16,26. However, these was no significant change in average grain size when increasing Zr4+ levels to x = 10%, compared to 2.5%. In other words, the grain size of the 10%Zr–TTO sample slightly increased when the Zr4+ level was elevated from x = 5 to 10% (5.6 to 6.2 μm). The results indicated the increased formation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{V}}_{{\text{O}}}^{{ \cdot \cdot }}$$\end{document}, which improved ion or/and atom diffusion across GBs, as mentioned in Eg mode in Raman results4,18. This was why the average grain size of the 10%Zr–TTO ceramic increased after the decrease in the 5%Zr–TTO sample. According to the Eg value, there was no significant shift for 2.5%Zr–TTO and 5%Zr–TTO ceramics, suggesting that Zr4+ dominantly influenced the properties to decrease grain sizes at x = 5%.

The dielectric properties at 25 °C in the frequency range of 102 to 106 Hz are presented in Fig. 4. The ε′ > 104 was revealed in all Zr–TTO samples and decreased when Zr4+ levels increased from x = 2.5 to 10%, primarily due to the Zr4+ effect, similar to other I4+/D5+ codoped rutile–TiO2 systems. This result suggested improvements in the electrical properties of the semiconducting part, as seen in previous works14. Meanwhile, ultra–low tanδ (< 0.05) was also obtained in the frequency ranges from 102 to ~ 3.5 × 103 Hz. The values of these properties at 1 kHz are listed in Table 1. An interesting observation was the step–like decrease in ε′ at frequencies exceeding 104 Hz, which was related to the rapid increase in tanδ. The results indicated interfacial polarization at the interface with a large Rgb, which will be discussed in detail in the following section on the origin mechanism17,27. It is worth noting that as the Zr4+ content increased from 2.5 to 10%, the dielectric response tended to continuously decrease, while the tanδ values for all samples slightly changed in the low–frequency range (< 2 × 104 Hz). Therefore, the optimized dielectric properties can be achieved at a low Zr4+ content.

Moreover, the ε′ behavior of the 2.5%Zr–TTO ceramic with an Ag electrode painting was measured over a frequency range at room temperature and compared to that with a sputtered Au electrode to investigate the electrode effect, as shown in the inset of Fig. 4. The sputtered Au electrode revealed increased ε′ responses compared to the painted Ag electrode. The rapid increase in ε′ response at frequencies below 104 Hz in the Au electrode suggested that the interface between the sample surface and the metal electrode forms a non–Ohmic contact. This phenomenon can be explained by the Schottky–Mott theory, which relates to the Schottky Barrier Height due to differences in work functions12,28.

Generally, D5+ doping, such as Nb5+ and Ta5+, in rutile–TiO2 based–oxides generated localized free electrons within the lattices, leading to a significant increase in the ε′ value in codoped TiO2 ceramics1,24. In the experimental results, the presence of these electrons was confirmed through Ti spectra obtained from XPS measurements. The Gaussian fit peaks of Ti 2p for the 10%Zr–TTO sample are shown in Fig. 5(a). This sample exhibited four peaks of Ti 2p3/2 for Ti3+ and Ti4+ at 456.94 and 458.54 eV, and Ti 2p1/2 for Ti3+ and Ti4+ at 462.70 and 464.29 eV, respectively. The results indicated that the generation of free electrons in the structure of this sample was attributed to the replacement of Ta5+ into Ti4+ sites1,16. In other words, Ta5+ carried a greater charge than Ti4+. This finding aligned with similar outcomes observed in other studies where D5+ dopants were introduced into Ti4+ host sites.

The distinctive property of codoped rutile–TiO2 materials, surpassing both BaTiO3 and colossal dielectric oxide materials, is their remarkable stability of ε′ across a broad spectrum of frequencies and, notably, temperatures1,17. This characteristic positioned these codoped systems as promising candidates for applications in microdevices operating under extreme conditions. In the context of X–type ceramic capacitors, the electronics industries alliance (EIA) established standard values for different types. For instance, X7R capacitors has to exhibit a maximum ε′ change (∆ε′) of 15% over the temperature range of − 55 to 125 °C when compared to the ε′ value at 25 °C. Similarly, X8R and X9R capacitors were defined for temperature ranges of − 55 to 150 °C and − 55 to 200 °C, respectively33. The results indicate that all Zr–TTO ceramics are suitable for application in X7R and X8R type ceramic capacitors.

Water, being a polar molecule, contributed to electronic polarization in dielectric materials, resulting in a response in the ε′. The absorption of water molecules into the structure of dielectric materials increased electronic polarization when an external electric field was applied34,35. To investigate the change in dielectric properties in a humid environment, we characterized the relative humidity (%RH) in a range of 50 to 95%, as shown in Fig. 6(b). The ε′ of 2.5%Zr–TTO remained independent of humidity at frequencies from 100 Hz to 100 kHz. The observation indicated that water molecules could not be absorbed into the structure of this sample. Furthermore, the change in ε′ at any %RH when compared with 50% RH at 1 kHz for all Zr–TTO ceramics was calculated to be less than 0.5%, as illustrated in the inset of Fig. 6(b). These results were attributed to the reduction of intrinsic defects related to the absence of the defect of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{2}^{3+}{\text{V}}_{\text{O}}^{}$$\end{document}Ti3+, along with a small amount of Ta5+ doping serving as an absorption site for water molecules36. Therefore, the application of Zr–TTO ceramics for capacitors emerged as a promising new candidate for the future.

The dielectric properties of CCTO ceramics, attributed to interfacial polarization based on the IBLC model, exhibited a dramatic step decrease in ε′ values, reaching magnitudes of 102–103, and a corresponding increase in tanδ in the MHz region. This behaviour resulted from the normal ionic polarization of CCTO materials5,38. Interestingly, the ε′ values of Zr–TTO ceramics still showed high values on the order of 104 after that relaxation, as depicted in Fig. 4. This observation indicated the presence of various polarizations within these samples. Such behaviour was discussed in previous works, exploring the complex origins in codoped rutile–TiO2, such as (Bi0.5Nb0.5)xTi1−xO2, (La0.5Nb0.5)xTi1−xO2 and (Ni1/3Nb2/3)xTi1−xO2 ceramics3,20. These ceramics revealed several thermally activated relaxations with different Ea across various temperature regions, suggesting that both intrinsic (i.e., EPDDs) or extrinsic (i.e., IBLCs) distributions, as well as other factors such as SBLCs, electron hopping, and the movement of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{V}}_{{\text{O}}}^{{ \cdot \cdot }}$$\end{document} and/or O2–, played significant roles3,22. It is noteworthy that the ε′ of the 2.5%Zr–TTO ceramic rapidly decreases to its intrinsic value due to ionic polarization at extremely low temperatures (below 15 K), particularly at high frequencies (> 1 MHz) as shown in Fig. 7(a). This decrease is not typically associated with interfacial polarization at the insulating grain boundaries (ins−GBs) via the IBLC effect, which will be further examined in the impedance and electric modulus spectroscopy analyses. In comparison, for CCTO polycrystalline ceramics, the ε′ at 1 kHz, which is related to the ins−GB effect, decreases sharply to below 102 around 120 K40. Thus, the observed R1 relaxation and the corresponding plateau−1 in the 2.5%Zr−TTO ceramic are unlikely to be attributed to the IBLC effect, given the extreme−temperature drop in ε′.

At temperatures above 120.00 K, the dielectric response from R2 mechanism corresponds to ε′ at the plateau−2 level, proving a ε′ value of ∼0.9 × 104 at 1 kHz. The relaxation peak of the imaginary part (ε′′= ε′×tanδ) of the complex dielectric permittivity (ε*) and the corresponding step−like decrease in ε′ in the temperature range of 140.00 K to 210.00 K shift to higher temperatures with increasing frequency, as shown in Fig. 7(c). The Ea for R2 was calculated using the Arrhenius equation, yielding a value of ∼0.081 eV, as depicted in Fig. 7(d). This value is in close agreement with the activation energies typically associated with dielectric relaxation and conduction within the semiconducting grains (semi−Gs) of CCTO ceramics, which are around 0.1 eV37,38. According to the IBLC model, the high dielectric response observed is primarily due to interfacial polarization at the ins−GB layer, which is situated between adjacent semi−Gs. Under these conditions, the Ea for dielectric relaxation is nearly identical to the activation energy required for conduction within the semi−Gs (Eg)41. Furthermore, the temperature range of R2 was related to the IBLCs effect in other codoped rutile–TiO2 ceramics3,42. Notably, R2 exhibited relaxation in a wider temperature range in the 2.5%Zr–TTO sample compared to other studies; specifically, between − 153.15 and 76.85 °C. There were two relaxations of IBLCs and SBLCs responses in Ni2+/Nb5+ codoped rutile–TiO23. In other codoped rutile−TiO2 ceramics, such as Ni2+/Nb5+ codoped samples, two relaxations corresponding to IBLC and SBLC responses were observed within this temperature range3,13. In other words, R2 covered the region attributed to the SBLC effect (higher temperature regions), which occurred between the outer resistive surface layer and the inner semi−Gs. This indicates the absence of the SBLC effect in these Zr−TTO ceramics, likely due to the experimental procedure. The dielectric properties were measured on polished ceramics, confirming that the enhanced dielectric properties in Zr−TTO ceramics did not result from the SBLC effect. Consequently, R2 may have been caused by the IBLC effect, which will be further confirmed by the impedance and electric modulus spectroscopy analyses.

At temperatures exceeding 400 K, only a partial step−like decrease in ε′ for R3 is observed. The exact plateau cannot be determined experimentally as it likely occurs beyond the upper temperature limit measured, as suggested by the assumed guideline of plateau−3 in Fig. 7(b). The Ea value of R3 cannot be calculated. The dielectric response from the R3 mechanism corresponds to the ε′ value at the assumed plateau−3 level, indicating a ε′ of greater than 1.3 × 104. It is plausible that R3 is caused by non−Ohmic contact between the sample surface and the metal electrode, leading to the formation of a Schottky barrier at the interface due to the difference in work functions12,28. This result correlates with the significant ε′ response observed with the sample with sputtered Au electrode, as shown in the inset of Fig. 7(b). The presence of several dielectric relaxations over a wide temperature range indicates that the origin of Zr−TTO ceramics is due to both extrinsic and intrinsic, suggesting a complex mechanism in codoped rutile−TiO2 systems.

Conclusions

Significantly improved dielectric properties were achieved in Zr–TTO ceramics prepared using the solid–state reaction method. XRD and Raman techniques confirmed the presence of the rutile–TiO2 phase in all Zr–TTO ceramics. The lattice parameters increased with higher Zr% content, indicating the substitution of dopants with larger ionic radii. A dense microstructure with only slight changes in the mean grain size was observed in all samples sintered at a low temperature of 1300 °C for 3 h. The ε′ at 25 °C and 1 kHz of the Zr–TTO ceramic decreased from ∼27,000 to 23,700 as the Zr% content increased from 2.5 to 10%, while the tanδ values remained very low, ranging from 0.013 to 0.020. Notably, the stability of ε′ in Zr–TTO ceramics was within ±15% over a broad temperature range from − 60 to 150 °C, comparable to the temperature coefficient of X7R and X8R type ceramic capacitors. Additionally, ε′ changed by less than ±0.5% relative humidity from 50 to 95% RH.
