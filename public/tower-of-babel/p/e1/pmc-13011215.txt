Infliximab-Induced Pneumonitis in a Patient With Sarcoidosis: A Case Report
Authors: Alexander Ali Jiménez Romero, Mercedes Guillamón Sánchez, María del Carmen Velasco Alcazar, Cristina Serrano Cortés
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Infliximab, a tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) inhibitor, is recommended in selected cases of pulmonary sarcoidosis. Although infectious complications are well recognized, non-infectious pulmonary toxicity is less frequently reported. We describe the case of a 52-year-old woman with atypical alveolar sarcoidosis who developed acute pneumonitis after initiation of infliximab. The patient had persistent disease despite treatment with systemic corticosteroids and methotrexate and was started on infliximab. Following the second dose, she presented with acute worsening dyspnea, tachycardia, and desaturation. High-resolution computed tomography revealed bilateral mosaic attenuation and ground-glass nodules. An infectious cause was excluded, and the temporal relationship with infliximab exposure supported the diagnosis of infliximab-induced pneumonitis. Treatment with high-dose systemic corticosteroids led to clinical and radiological improvement, and infliximab was permanently discontinued. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of infliximab-induced pneumonitis in the specific setting of pulmonary sarcoidosis.

Infliximab is a monoclonal antibody directed against tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and is widely used in the treatment of various inflammatory diseases.1 Although its use has been associated with infectious and immunological pulmonary complications, including interstitial lung disease (ILD),2 the most recent European Respiratory Society (ERS) guidelines on sarcoidosis propose infliximab as part of the management of pulmonary involvement in symptomatic sarcoidosis patients at high risk of future mortality or permanent disability who have persistent disease despite treatment with glucocorticoids or other immunosuppressive agents.3

We report the case of a 52-year-old woman with a history of childhood bronchial asthma. Baseline pulmonary function tests showed a forced vital capacity (FVC) of 2900 mL (89% predicted), a forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) of 2320 mL (89% predicted), and an FEV1/FVC ratio of 80%. She had no history of smoking and had experienced SARS-CoV-2 infection in 2022. In 2025, she was diagnosed with atypical alveolar sarcoidosis. Treatment was initiated with systemic corticosteroids at a dose of 30 mg/day. Due to steroid dependence, methotrexate was added as a steroid-sparing agent at a dose of 21 mg weekly, allowing only partial tapering of prednisone to 10–15 mg/day. Despite this, the patient developed recurrent pulmonary infiltrates, persistent dyspnea, and corticosteroid-related adverse effects.

The case was re-evaluated by a multidisciplinary interstitial lung disease board, and infliximab was initiated at a dose of 3 mg/kg following an induction regimen, with doses administered at weeks 0 and 2. The third planned dose at week 6 was not administered.

After the second dose of infliximab, the patient presented to the emergency department with worsening dyspnea on minimal to moderate exertion, resting tachycardia up to 120 beats per minute, and oxygen desaturation to 88% during the six-minute walk test (baseline SpO2 95%). She required hospital admission. An infectious etiology was ruled out, with negative serological tests and microbiological cultures for typical and atypical pathogens. The patient was receiving trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii. As the radiological findings were not consistent with sarcoidosis progression, and given the absence of microbiological evidence and the close temporal relationship between infliximab exposure and symptom onset, a diagnosis of infliximab-induced pneumonitis was considered.

Systemic corticosteroid therapy was initiated at a dose of 1 mg/kg/day, resulting in rapid clinical improvement and marked radiological resolution on follow-up HRCT after one month (Fig. 1B). The patient was discharged with outpatient follow-up. Infliximab was permanently discontinued, and a gradual corticosteroid taper was attempted, with a maintenance dose of 15 mg/day required due to persistent steroid dependence.

Among the non-infectious adverse events associated with infliximab, interstitial pneumonia has been described. Although its exact incidence is unknown, some registries estimate it to be approximately 2.9%. It appears to be more frequent in male patients, older individuals, and those with pre-existing lung disease or prior exposure to glucocorticoids.2

In Spain, a series of 122 cases of anti-TNF-α-associated ILD has been reported, predominantly in patients with rheumatoid arthritis, with infliximab involved in nearly half of the cases.2 To date, however, no cases of pneumonitis have been reported when infliximab is used specifically for the treatment of pulmonary sarcoidosis, which underscores the clinical relevance of this case.

In conclusion, although anti-TNF agents are primarily associated with infectious complications, drug-induced pulmonary toxicity should be considered in patients who develop acute respiratory symptoms, even when these agents are used to treat interstitial lung disease. While a definitive causal relationship cannot be established, close monitoring during the early phases of treatment is essential. In this case, pneumonitis developed after the second dose of infliximab during the induction phase, highlighting the importance of early vigilance.
