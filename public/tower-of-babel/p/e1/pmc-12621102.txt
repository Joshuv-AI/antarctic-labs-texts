The role of plant cell walls in pest resistance: current insights and future perspectives
Authors: Marta Marina Perez-Alonso, Lucia Talavera-Mateo, Dairon Ojeda-Martinez, Laura Barcenilla-Valcárcel, Álvaro Montesinos, Alejandro Garcia, Carlos Frey, Marta Boter, Manuel Martinez, Isabel Diaz, M Estrella Santamaria
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSignificant strides have been made by researchers aiming to understand the crucial role of the plant cell wall (PCW) in defending against pathogens. Numerous reviews have synthesized findings on plant–pathogen interactions, shedding light on the role of the PCW in defence, the generation of cell wall damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), perception, and signalling response. However, the role of the PCW in responses to herbivory remains underexplored. This review aims to fill this gap, offering a comprehensive analysis of the current knowledge in this area. For the first time, it consolidates studies focusing on the involvement of the PCW in pest response. The review examines key aspects, including the structural changes in the PCW following pest infestation, the role of cell wall-derived DAMPs, and the phenotypic traits of transgenic plants with modified cell wall components. Additionally, it delves into the genetic underpinnings of cell wall plasticity and the mechanisms of DAMP perception. Lastly, the article identifies promising research pathways to further unravel the complex role of the PCW in pest defence, encouraging advancements in this relatively nascent field. Through this effort, it aims to bridge the knowledge gap and inspire deeper investigations into the interplay between the PCW and pest resistance.

Introduction

In a dynamic and ever-changing environment, plants rely on intricate defence systems, including pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) and effector-triggered immunity (ETI), to withstand adverse conditions such as pathogens and pest attacks (Fig. 1). At the forefront of these defences lies the plant cell wall (PCW), a multifunctional structure that maintains cell shape, regulates cell growth, and serves as a crucial physical barrier between the plant and its environment (Miedes et al., 2014; Fuertes-Rabanal et al., 2025). Conversely, the survival of pathogens and pests often depends on their ability to breach this barrier and access plant tissue to secure essential nutrients for survival. As a result, many pathogens and pests have evolved cell wall-degrading enzymes (CWDEs), such as pectinases, xylanases, and xyloglucanases, that enable them to efficiently penetrate host tissues by disrupting cell wall integrity (CWI) (Gómez-Gómez et al., 2001; Wanjiru et al., 2002; Lev and Horwitz, 2003; Niture et al., 2006; van den Brink and de Vries, 2011). Remarkably, increasing evidence shows that plants can monitor CWI to detect changes caused by pathogen-derived CWDEs (Jones and Dangl, 2006; Juge, 2006; Vaahtera et al., 2019; Molina et al., 2021). Alterations in CWI, including modifications in cell wall composition and structure, can lead to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) (Bacete et al., 2020; Tanaka and Heil, 2021). The role of cell wall-derived DAMPs in plant immunity has been extensively characterized, especially in interactions with bacteria and fungi. These DAMPs, many of which are oligosaccharides, function as potent elicitors that activate PTI responses (Bacete et al., 2018; Wan et al., 2021; Ishida and Noutoshi, 2022; Swaminathan et al., 2022). The detection of DAMPs or herbivore-associated molecular patterns (HAMPs) by plant recognition receptors (PRRs) can trigger PTI responses, leading to a cascade of immune signalling events that are crucial for orchestrating effective defence against invading pathogens and pests (Miedes et al., 2014; Bacete et al., 2017; De Lorenzo et al., 2018; Guerreiro and Marhavý, 2023). However, unlike pathogens, less knowledge is available on the role of the PCW as a sensor of pest infestation, particularly by phytophagous insects and mites.

Schematic overview of pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) in plants upon pest infestation. Cell wall-degrading enzymes (CWDEs) secreted by herbivory disrupt cell wall integrity, releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). The defence response is activated by the recognition of these DAMPs, as well as herbivore-associated molecular patterns (HAMPs), by pattern recognition receptor complexes (PRRs and co-PRRs). Elicitor binding to PRRs leads to a series of signalling events, such as membrane depolarization (Vm), activation of Ca2+ channels and influx of Ca2+ into the cytosol, burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS) via activation of RBOHD, membrane depolarization, and activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade. MAPK and Ca2+-dependent kinase (CDPK) cascades trigger transcriptional reprogramming, activating phytohormone (SA, JA, and ET) signalling, transcription regulatory factors, and synthesis of defensive compounds.

In this review, we offer an in-depth analysis of the PCW components or modifications that contribute to plant defence against herbivorous pests, the interactions between the CWDEs produced by pests and the PCW, the recognition of PCW-derived DAMPs by plant receptors, and the challenges that remain in understanding the role of the PCW in the response to herbivory. By synthesizing these insights, we aim to provide a comprehensive framework that can guide future research efforts and inspire innovative strategies in sustainable agriculture.

Role of the plant cell wall in herbivory resistance

Within PCWs, primary and secondary types are distinguished based on their composition, structure, and function. In addition, a third part called the middle lamella separates neighbouring cells. The primary cell wall is a flexible and dynamic structure that surrounds cells undergoing growth, and is predominantly formed by polysaccharide-rich macromolecules, namely cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin (Cosgrove, 2005; Delmer et al., 2024). In contrast to the primary cell wall, the secondary cell wall is a rigid, specialized layer deposited after cessation of cell expansion in certain types of cells, such as tracheids, vessels, fibres, and sclereids (Cosgrove and Jarvis, 2012; Zhong et al., 2019). The secondary wall shares some components with the primary cell wall, such as cellulose and hemicellulose, but is also enriched with lignin, a complex phenolic polymer which is essential for plant support and water transport (Liu et al., 2018; Zhong et al., 2019).

In this section, we delve into the role of PCW components in plant resistance to pests and how the regulation of genes involved in cell wall remodelling can modulate plant–herbivore interactions. Furthermore, we discuss how manipulating PCW composition by suppressing or overexpressing these genes affects plant resistance to pests. Plant cellulose content influences herbivory success

Cellulose is a major structural component of PCWs, comprising 25–30% of the primary and 40–60% of the secondary cell walls. It consists of long chains of β-1,4-linked glucans (Fig. 2), which aggregate into unbranched crystalline microfibrils that are stabilized by extensive hydrogen bonding. This highly ordered structure provides tensile strength to the PCW, thereby determining the mechanical properties of plant tissue, which may play a crucial role in herbivory (Cosgrove, 2005; Vaahtera et al., 2019). For instance, a study investigating the feeding behaviour of leaf-chewing insects found that two coleopteran species, Polydrusus nothofagii and P. roseus, showed a greater preference for trees with a lower cellulose content. This behaviour was attributed to the reduced mechanical resistance of less rigid tissues. In contrast, the lepidopteran Perzelia arda exhibited a positive association with trees with a higher cellulose content, suggesting that the shelters created by these insects when feeding enable the efficient exploitation of cellulose-rich substrates more effectively (Pietrantuono et al., 2017).

Cellulose synthesis is carried out by the cellulose synthase (CeSA) complexes, which utilize cytosolic UDP-glucose as a substrate (Hernández-Blanco et al., 2007; Pedersen et al., 2023). In the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, an infestation analysis using the green peach aphid Myzus persicae showed a lower number of aphids on the constitutive expression of vegetative storage protein 1 (cev1) mutant, compared with wild-type plants (Ellis et al., 2002a). Remarkably, this mutant exhibited a recessive mutation in CeSA3, which led to significantly reduced levels of cellulose in roots (Ellis et al., 2002b). These findings indicate that in herbaceous plants there is a negative relationship between aphid tolerance and cellulose content. Furthermore, the study demonstrated that the cev1 mutant displayed constitutive activation of canonical plant defence mechanisms against pathogens and pests, including the up-regulation of the defensive gene VSP2 and elevated levels of the hormones jasmonate and ethylene (Ellis et al., 2002b). These observations underscore that perturbations in the cellulose content may result in intracellular stress signalling that triggers the initiation of the defence machinery. However, it remains unclear whether these insects directly influence CeSA gene expression and how cellulose deficiency modulates plant defence in response to herbivore predators. In this regard, a transcriptomic analysis of phloem tissue in celery plants (Apium graveolens) found that a putative CeSA gene was up-regulated in response to M. persicae feeding (Divol et al., 2005). These seemingly contradictory findings suggest that the outcome of the interaction may largely depend on the feeding strategy of the pest and the specific plant tissue affected. Nonetheless, further investigation into the role of cellulose in plant defence response is essential to better understand these dynamics.

In addition, it was hypothesized that the reinforcement of the cell wall serves a dual purpose: providing mechanical protection by impeding the penetration of the stylet into plant tissues and increasing the recalcitrance of the PCW to the action of CWDEs present in the aphid saliva (Divol et al., 2005; Mohase and Taiwe, 2015). A recent study on both susceptible (Tugela-Dn1) and resistant (Tugela-Dn5) wheat cultivars revealed that infestation with the Russian wheat aphid (Diuraphis noxia) stimulated the accumulation of cellulose and hemicellulose in the resistant cultivar at 72 h and 120 h post-infestation (Mafa et al., 2022). Supporting this hypothesis, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy detected an increase in the content of crystalline cellulose, further emphasizing the role of cell wall fortification in plant defence (Mafa et al., 2022).

Structural and chemical modification of hemicellulose are essential in regulating plant–pest interactions

Another key component of the PCW is hemicellulose, a heterogeneous class of polysaccharides characterized by a β(1→4)-linked backbone composed predominantly of glucose, mannose, and xylose, often decorated with side chains (Fig. 2) (Bacete et al., 2017; Chibrikov et al., 2024). This diversity allows hemicellulose to interact with cellulose microfibrils, contributing to the structure and function of the PCW. Interestingly, Divol et al. (2005) showed that besides CeSA and pectin-related genes, the phloem-feeding aphid M. persicae induced the expression of xyloglucan endotransglycosylases/hydrolase (XTH) genes in celery plants. XTH enzymes play a pivotal role in remodelling the structure of the hemicellulose by catalysing the cleavage and rejoining of xyloglucan molecules, essential for plant growth, development, and stress responses (Rose et al., 2002; De Caroli et al., 2021). Further analysis using northern blotting revealed that the expression of celery XTH1 and its homologous gene in Arabidopsis, XTH33, was strongly up-regulated in response to aphid attack (Divol et al., 2007). These authors also showed that the Arabidopsis xth33 knockout mutant displayed increased susceptibility to M. persicae, highlighting the importance of the structural reorganization of xyloglucans in plant defence against herbivores. In addition, the advancement of transcriptomic technologies has enabled the identification of possible candidates involved in hemicellulose remodelling during plant–herbivore interactions. For example, differential expression of XTH genes has been found in Arabidopsis plants in response to feeding by the cabbage aphid (Brevicoryne brassicae) or the two-spotted spider mite (Tetranychus urticae) (Kuśnierczyk et al., 2008; Garcia et al., 2021). It might be possible that xyloglucan rearrangement by XTH enzymes influences the association of hemicellulose with cellulose microfibrils. Interestingly, these association points, referred to as mechanical hotspots, are believed to play crucial roles in plant stress responses (Daher et al., 2024). However, direct evidence demonstrating how these connections influence plant–pest dynamics is still lacking.

In contrast to cellulose, hemicelluloses such as xylans, xyloglucans, and mannans can be O-acetylated by the action of TRICHOME-BIREFRINGENCE-LIKE enzymes, which are localized in the Golgi apparatus, where both synthesis and decoration of hemicellulose occur (Gille and Pauly, 2012). Impairment of hemicellulose acetylation in the Arabidopsis tbl37-1 mutant increased susceptibility to the chewing herbivore Spodoptera exigua, as evidenced by a higher number of larvae and an increased larval weight on mutant plants (Sun et al., 2020). Notably, acetylation modulates the interaction of hemicellulose with cellulose microfibrils, influencing the biomechanical properties of the PCW and being less accessible to enzymatic activities (Xiong et al., 2013).

Pectin composition impacts plant resistance to phytophagous predators

Pectin, a complex group of polysaccharides, is predominantly located in the middle lamella and primary walls of dicotyledons (Shahin et al., 2023). It is rich in D-galacturonic acid (GalA), and its polymers can be classified into homogalacturonans (HGs), rhamnogalacturonan-I (RG-I), and RG-II, based on their structural complexity (Fig. 2) (Mohnen, 2008). Among these, HGs are the most abundant, accounting for ∼65% of total pectin (Wolf et al., 2009). It consists of linear chains of α(1→4)-linked D-GalA residues and is synthesized in the Golgi apparatus, where it undergoes post-synthetic decorations such as acetylation and methylesterification. Once secreted into the PCW, the degree of acetylation and methylesterification is regulated by distinct families of enzymes: pectin acetylesterases (PAEs), pectin methylesterases (PMEs), and their inhibitors (PMEIs). These modifications are critical to regulating different biological processes, such as cell adhesion, plant organ development, and protection against pathogens (Pogorelko et al., 2013; Bacete et al., 2018; Barnes et al., 2021; Guo et al., 2022; De la Rubia et al., 2024). Moreover, they play a pivotal role in the plant–herbivore interaction (Sénéchal et al., 2014). A comparative transcriptional analysis conducted in Arabidopsis revealed that the PMEI13 gene was specifically up-regulated in plants attacked by M. persicae (De Vos et al., 2005). Myzus persicae feeding increased total PME activity, reduced the degree of HG methylesterification, and triggered the release of methanol, a volatile defence signal emitted during herbivory (von Dahl et al., 2006). Supporting this, transgenic tobacco and cotton plants overexpressing PME genes increased methanol content and exhibited resistance against sap-sucking insects (Dixit et al., 2013; Srivastava et al., 2023). It is well established that HGs can cross-link one to another via calcium ions (Ca2+) between demethylesterified HG groups, leading to increased cell wall rigidity and reduced matrix porosity (Obomighie et al., 2025). Using biochemical and immunolocalization assays, Silva-Sanzana et al. (2019) demonstrated that aphid infestation promoted HG demethylesterification and enhanced Ca2+ cross-linking in Arabidopsis, suggesting that cell wall strengthening may play a protective role against herbivory. In addition, they found that the pmei13 knockout mutant exhibited higher susceptibility to aphid infestation. Intriguingly, this aphid showed a marked preference for pmei13 plants, probably due to improved access to the phloem and lower methanol emissions. Notably, the authors also observed a lower degree of methylesterification in the pmei13 mutant. These findings suggest that, in addition to the overall degree of methylesterification, other factors may significantly influence aphid–plant interactions. Interestingly, the pattern of demethylesterification has been shown to play a pivotal role in determining whether HG undergoes Ca2+-mediated cross-linking or is instead targeted for degradation (Du et al., 2020). A random distribution of demethylesterifications has been associated with the pectin degradation pathway, involving enzymes such as pectin lyases and polygalacturonases (PGs), which could lead to the release of oligolacturonides (OGs) (Marín-Rodríguez et al., 2002; Guo et al., 2022; Safran et al., 2023). Remarkably, studies performed across different plant species, including Arabidopsis, soybean (Glycine max), tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), and durum wheat (Triticum turgidum subsp. durum), have shown that OGs act as potent signalling molecules. They can trigger the accumulation of defence hormones, callose deposition, and the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Denoux et al., 2008; Ferrari et al., 2013; Jiménez-Maldonado et al., 2018; Howlader et al., 2020; Bigini et al., 2024). Despite these findings, the precise role of OGs in increasing resistance to herbivores remains to be fully elucidated. A recent study found that treating Arabidopsis leaves with a galacturonan oligosaccharide of a degree of polymerization (DP) 10–15 significantly impaired M. persicae colonization (Silva-Sanzana et al., 2022). This treatment reduced the production of aphid offspring and increased the difficulty of feeding. The application of these OGs (DP 10–15) enhanced plant defence by promoting callose deposition, ROS production, and salicylic acid biosynthesis and signalling pathways. A limitation of these works is the lack of evaluation of the potential growth penalties that could arise from the continuous activation of the defence machinery (Huot et al., 2014; Benedetti et al., 2015; Garcia et al., 2024), in this case, due to OG overaccumulation.

Beyond piercing–sucking herbivores, the methylesterification status of pectins is also involved in the interaction with chewing insects. For instance, von Dahl et al. (2006) showed that Manduca sexta-associated wounding triggered the emission of methanol in Nicotiana attenuata. Increasing methanol content associated with the overexpression of PME genes also causes resistance against chewing insects (Dixit et al., 2013; Srivastava et al., 2023). Körner et al. (2009) further demonstrated that silencing of the herbivore-inducible PME1 gene in N. attenuata allowed M. sexta caterpillars to gain more body mass compared with control plants. More recently, a genome-wide analysis conducted on Arabidopsis leaves revealed significant up-regulation of several PME genes, including PME2, PME17, and PME32, as well as PMEI11, 24 h after infestation with Pieris brassicae or T. urticae (Montesinos et al., 2024). In cotton (Gossypium hirsutum), GhPME36 overexpression led to increased susceptibility to the leaf miner Liriomyza sativa. Interestingly, transgenic lines showed a decreased degree of pectin methylesterification, accompanied by a decrease in the thickness of the epidermal cell wall in their leaves (Yang et al., 2024).

Regarding acetylation, Kloth et al. (2019) revealed that higher levels of pectin acetylation in the pae9 mutant facilitated a faster phloem-feeding establishment by M. persicae than in control plants. However, this initial advantage was later mitigated by the activation of hormonal defences in the mutant plant. Notably, the transcriptional activation of PAE genes has also been documented in plant species with agronomical importance, such as apple tree (Malus domestica), in response to feeding by the rosy apple aphid (Dysaphis plantaginea) (Qubbaj et al., 2005). Interestingly, unlike methylesterification, which predominantly occurs in HG pectin polymer, acetylation appears to be highly enriched in in the RG-I fraction (de Souza et al., 2014). This could suggest that RG-I may play a role in modulating plant tolerance to phytophagous predators. Consistent with this, a genome-wide analysis performed in Arabidopsis plants infested with Pieris rapae, S. exigua, or M. persicae revealed a significant down-regulation of a subset of genes associated with cell wall-modifying enzymes including pectinases, such as β-galactosidase1 (BGAL1) and the β-D-xylosidase1 (BXL1) (Appel et al., 2014). Notably, BXL1 exhibits bifunctional xylosidase and arabinofuranosidase activities, facilitating the cleavage of xylose and arabinose residues from RG-I chains, thereby contributing to their effective release (Arsovski et al., 2009). This enzymatic activity led to the remodelling of the RG-I side chains, which can trigger the generation of DAMPs. Recently, a study using Arabidopsis demonstrated that another xylosidase/arabinosidase enzyme, BXL4, plays a crucial role in activating plant defence responses against Botrytis cinerea (Guzha et al., 2022). However, information regarding the role of these enzymes in plant–pest interaction remains limited. These findings underscore the importance of understanding the regulatory role of pectin-degrading enzymes in modulating CWI and plant defence against pests.

Lignin deposition regulates plant protection against pests

Lignin is a phenolic polymer deposited on the cell wall of the secondary xylem to provide mechanical strength and hydrophobicity. It is primarily composed of three monolignols: p-coumaryl alcohol, coniferyl alcohol, and sinapyl alcohol, which are produced via the phenylpropanoid- and monolignol-specific pathways (Fig. 2) (Liu et al., 2018). After synthesis, these monolignols undergo oxidation by peroxidases (PRXs) and laccases (LACs). Oxidized monolignols form the building block of lignin: p-hydroxyphenyl (H), guaiacyl (G), and syringyl (S) units, respectively (Liu et al., 2018; Vanholme et al., 2019). Interestingly, lignin deposition acts as a protective barrier against pathogen invasions (Miedes et al., 2014; Xie et al., 2018), and variations in lignin content have been shown to influence plant susceptibility to herbivore attacks. A higher lignin content is often associated with improved plant resistance to pests. Independent studies have shown that in chrysanthemum plants (Chrysanthemum morifolium), feeding by the aphid Macrosiphoniella sanborni triggered the expression of two transcription factor genes, CmMYB15 and CmMYB19, both of which are involved in lignin biosynthesis (Wang et al., 2017; An et al., 2019). Overexpression of these MYB genes resulted in increased lignin accumulation, which in turn hindered aphid performance by inhibiting population proliferation. Using molecular and biochemical approaches, F. Li et al. (2023) demonstrated that the lignin biosynthetic gene Cm4CL2 is directly regulated by CmMYB15. Silencing of CmMYB15 and Cm4CL2 resulted in reduced lignin content, leading to an increase in aphid number (F. Li et al., 2023). In Arabidopsis, the overexpression of the auxin biosynthetic gene YUC9 triggered the overaccumulation of lignin. The overexpressing line showed reduced damage caused by T. urticae compared with non-infected plants (Pérez-Alonso et al., 2021). In contrast, reduced lignin content has also been associated with a high plant susceptibility to pests. In rice (Oryza sativa), independent genetic inactivation of exocyst subunit EXO70 (EXO70H3) and S-adenosylmethionine synthase-like (SAMSL) resulted in a significant reduction in lignin levels. These lines showed an increased susceptibility to the brown planthopper Nilaparvata lugens (Wu et al., 2022). Similarly, silencing of MePAL6 in cassava (Manihot esculenta) plants resulted in a lower lignin content, facilitating increased damage by T. urticae (Yao et al., 2023). Likewise, an increase in the larval weight of Trichobaris mucorea was found when these stem borers infested N. attenuata NaCAD1 mutants, which exhibited a reduction in the levels of monolignols (Joo et al., 2021). Most importantly, Gallego-Giraldo et al. (2018) demonstrated that lignin composition rather than its total content modulates plant tolerance to biotic stress. Using Arabidopsis loss-of-function (fah1-2) and overexpression (F5H-OE) lines for the lignin biosynthetic gene ferulate/coniferaldehyde 5-hydroxylase (F5H), the authors showed that while the overall lignin levels remained comparable between the two lines, the F5H-OE plants, characterized by an increased proportion of S-lignin, has a significantly higher number of M. persicae nymphs than the fah1-2 line, which accumulated higher levels of G-lignin. Transcriptomic analysis further revealed that overexpression of F5H led to the up-regulation of PROPEP3, which encoded the precursor of elicitor peptide 3 (Pep3), a well-characterized DAMP involved in jasmonic acid (JA) signalling (Klauser et al., 2015).

Other plant cell wall components involved in plant–pest interaction

In addition to the mentioned components of the PCW, plants can synthesize other elements that reinforce and enhance its structural integrity. Besides proteins that connect and modify polysaccharide-rich components, other polymers, such as callose and cutin, can be deposited in association with the PCW.

Callose is a linear polysaccharide polymer composed of β-1,3-glucan (Fig. 2) and is synthesized in the PCW in response to abiotic and biotic stressors (Ellinger et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2013; N. Li et al., 2023). Callose deposition has been specifically observed at wounding sites caused by phloem-feeding aphids in wheat (Botha and Matsiliza, 2004) and rice (Hao et al., 2008), where it plays a critical role in restricting phloem transport. Various studies have shed light on the molecular mechanisms governing callose formation in response to phloem-feeding herbivory (Kuśnierczyk et al., 2008; Julius et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2019; Twayana et al., 2022). The consensus is that callose deposition is tightly regulated by complex signalling networks involving plant hormones, ROS, and calcium signalling pathways (Will et al., 2013; N. Li et al., 2023). These regulatory systems enable plants to quickly perceive phloem-feeding attacks and activate callose biosynthesis. For example, the cotton callose synthase gene GhCalS5 is induced in response to Aphis gossypii (Mbiza et al., 2022), and the Arabidopsis callose synthase gene CalS1 is involved in callose-mediated defence against Bemisia tabaci (Kempema et al., 2007). In addition, Rosa-Diaz et al. (2023) demonstrated that the defensive hormone JA is closely linked to callose deposition in response to herbivory. Arabidopsis infestation by T. urticae triggered the expression of jasmonic acid oxidase 2 (JAO2), a key gene involved in the oxidation of JA to the inactive form 12-OH-HA (Smirnova et al., 2017), thereby down-regulating plant defence mechanisms. Notably, jao2 mutants exhibited reduced leaf damage, lower mite fecundity, and decreased callose deposition after 24 h of mite infestation compared with treated control plants (Rosa-Diaz et al., 2023). In addition to sucking–piercing pests, activation of callose-related genes has also been observed in response to chewing herbivores, such as P. rapae or S. exigua, at both local and systemic levels (Appel et al., 2014). These results emphasize the dynamic role of callose deposition in reinforcing the PCW as a protective mechanism against herbivory damage. While callose itself is not a classical DAMP, there is still a knowledge gap regarding whether its deposition in response to damage can indirectly lead to the release of callose-derived oligosaccharides, which in turn would activate plant defence signalling pathways.

The cuticle is a lipid-based extracellular layer that covers the aerial organs of plants, including leaves, stems, seeds, and fruits (Serrano et al., 2014). Cuticles are primarily composed of cutin, an insoluble polymer built on esterified C16 and C18 hydroxy and epoxy fatty acids, impregnated with wax (Yeats and Rose, 2013; Philippe et al., 2022). Cuticles are physical barriers that protect plants against pathogens and pests. An example supporting this assumption is found in barley plants knocked down for papain 19, which showed increased cuticle thickness and had a greater resistance to the mite T. urticae (Gomez-Sanchez et al., 2019). However, extensive research has shown that mutants impaired in the synthesis of cutin monomers are often accompanied by an increased tolerance to bacterial and fungal pathogens (Serrano et al., 2014; Blanc et al., 2018; Aragón et al., 2021). Similarly, the mutant eca2, characterized by the constitutive expression of the early microbe-associated molecular pattern-induced gene ATL2, showed a significant reduction in the larval weight of S. littoralis compared with wild-type plants (Blanc et al., 2018). Chemical analysis on the eca2 mutant showed a 35% reduction in oxygenated cutin monomers and an increase in cuticle permeability compared with wild-type plants, indicating a possible link between cuticle integrity and pest defence mechanisms. In an elegant study that used purified cutin from tomatoes, Moreira et al. (2024) revealed that both the cutin polymer itself and cutin-derived oligomers can act as elicitors, activating plant PTI in Arabidopsis. Moreover, their spectroscopic and spectrometric analysis suggests that the active elicitor compounds are mainly dimers and trimers composed of 10,16-dihydroxyhexadecanoic acid monomers linked by ester bonds.

Pest-derived cell wall-degrading enzymes in plant cell wall degradation

As mentioned in the Introduction, herbivorous pests rely on a diverse array of CWDEs to overcome the physical and chemical barriers posed by PCWs (Fig. 3) (Calderón-Cortés et al., 2012; Tokuda, 2019). These enzymes are crucial in facilitating nutrient acquisition and establishing successful interactions with host plants. Genomic studies using omics approaches showed that arthropod herbivores had a higher CWDE content compared with other feeding groups, supporting that the PCW exerts a high selective pressure on the carbohydrate metabolism of herbivores (Ojeda-Martinez et al., 2024). During feeding, pest secretions enriched with CWDEs initiate pre-digestion, breaking down structural components and reducing friction (Ma et al., 2024). A more exhaustive digestion process occurs within the digestive system, where enzymes are secreted and more specialized body structures participate (Calderón-Cortés et al., 2012; Celorio-Mancera and Labavitch, 2016).

Cell wall-degrading enzymes in salivary secretions

Oral secretions (OS) are a critical mechanism for delivering CWDEs during pest feeding. In most arthropod herbivores, different components of the saliva affect the structure and composition of the PCW.

Cellulases are critical components of pest salivary secretions, facilitating cellulose depolymerization and nutrient acquisition. These enzymes have been identified in saliva of many pests such as the aphid Acyrtosiphum pisum, the leafhopper Empoasca fabae, the termite Reticulitermes speratus, the thrip Frankliniella occidentalis, or the beetle Mylabris pustulata (Watanabe and Tokuda, 2010; DeLay et al., 2012; Stafford-Banks et al., 2014). Likewise, β-1,4-endoglucanases, among other cell wall-digesting enzymes, have been identified in the saliva from the glassy-winged sharpshooter Homalodisca vitripennis (Backus et al., 2012) and the cricket Teleogryllus emma (Kim et al., 2008). Hemicellulases have also been described in the saliva of H. vitripennis, where they facilitate pest stylet access to xylem elements (Backus et al., 2012). Endogenous lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases have been reported in firebrats (Zygentoma), revealing a high catalytic activity on cellulose (Sabbadin et al., 2018; Vandhana et al., 2022). Direct evidence of the association of pest cellulases and the PCW was found in the interaction of the brown planthopper N. lugens and rice. Silencing of a salivary endo-β-1,4-glucanase gene, NlEG1, of the pest decreased its capacity to degrade cellulose and hampered its capacity to reach the phloem (Ji et al., 2017).

In hemipterans, salivary elements can also induce up-regulation of native plant glucanases, causing callose decomposition by plant enzymes (van Bel and Will, 2016). For instance, brown planthopper (N. lugens) feeding induces the deposition of callose on sieve plates in rice but also activates β-1,3-glucanase genes in susceptible rice plants that remove sieve tube occlusions (Hao et al., 2008). Prevention of callose deposition by salivary elements occurs via sequestration of Ca2+ ions. Since callose deposition is Ca2+ dependent, the presence of Ca2+-binding proteins such as calmodulin, angiotensin, or calreticulin reduces callose deposition, restricting sieve tube occlusion (Will et al., 2009; Hattori et al., 2012; Nicholson et al., 2012). The presence of enzymes that can degrade phenol-containing cell wall components has also been detected in saliva from multiple hemipterans. Enzymes such as polyphenoloxidases (Harmel et al., 2008; Cooper et al., 2011), peroxidases (Chaudhary et al., 2015), oxidoreductases, GMC-oxidoreductases (Carolan et al., 2009; Nicholson et al., 2012), and laccases (Hattori et al., 2005) are among them. These enzymes have the potential to interact with phenolic compounds of the PCW, such as lignin, and destabilize its structure, easing the degradation of the rest of the components of the cell wall.

In the OS of lepidopterans, multiple enzymes that interact with PCWs have also been described. This is the case for the secretions of Spodoptera littoralis, Spodoptera frugiperda, Helicoverpa armigera, and P. brassicae, which contain β-glucosidases and β-1,3-glucanases acting on the PCW (Celorio-Mancera et al., 2011; Consales et al., 2012; Acevedo et al., 2017). Most arthropod β-glucosidases accept substrates other than β-D-glucosides, including various di- and oligosaccharides, glycoproteins, and glycolipids. This creates a scenario where arthropod salivary enzymes can not only break down lignocellulosic components of the cell wall but also act on glycosylated proteins and lipids (Huber et al., 2021). A vital part of the cell wall polysaccharide networks are also glycoproteins which interconnect and strengthen the elements of the cell wall structure. Therefore, structurally altered glycoproteins weaken the general architecture of cell walls, both covalently and non-covalently, and induce the alteration of biological processes within the cells (Nguema-Ona et al., 2014).

The presence of pectinases in pest saliva helps the infestation process by degrading the pectin of the middle lamella of plant cells. Its presence in the watery saliva of Lygus hesperus produces the disintegration of parenchyma cells in their hosts, producing pectin fragments that can potentially function as DAMPs (Sharma et al., 2014). PGs are frequently found in the saliva of insect pests and are responsible for visible plant injury (Zhu et al., 2016). Significant up-regulation of PGs has been identified in the salivary glands of the true bug Apolygus lucorum and on Lygus (Allen and Mertens, 2008; Celorio-Mancera et al., 2009; Zhang et al., 2015), suggesting the relevant role of this enzyme in the extra-oral digestion of the PCW by pests. Interestingly, plants secrete polygalacturonase-inhibiting proteins (PGIPs) to inhibit exogenous PGs. The significance of PGIPs in insect–plant interactions has been demonstrated. The fitness of the leaf beetle Phaedon cochleariae was increased when larvae were fed on Arabidopsis pgip mutant plants compared with wild-type plants (Kirsch et al., 2020).

Cell wall-degrading enzymes from sources other than saliva

In addition to salivary secretions derived from mouthparts, CWDEs can originate from other pest sources, such as the stomach and the haemocoel, faeces, or microbial symbionts (Celorio-Mancera and Labavitch, 2016). Therefore, microorganism-derived enzymes may also interact with PCWs during feeding. Aphid saliva, such as that of Macrosiphum euphorbiae, contains proteins from the endosymbiont bacteria Buchnera aphidicola that influence plant responses and pest fitness (Chaudhary et al., 2015). Coleopterans secrete regurgitants rich in symbiotic bacteria that produce cell wall-modifying enzymes (Acevedo et al., 2015). Lepidopterans also release regurgitant fluids containing foregut enzymes and bacteria, which facilitate mastication and food processing (Acevedo et al., 2015). Recent studies have identified CWDE in the faeces of different pest insects. CWDEs can appear in faeces either because the insect ingested fungi and bacteria containing these enzymes, and they were not degraded during passage through the digestive tract, or because they originate from the insect’s intestinal microbiota (Martínez-Romero et al., 2021; Schiøtt and Boomsma, 2021; Zhang et al., 2024). In any case, these CWDEs in the faeces could act on the cell wall of the host plant.

Plant receptors of cell wall compounds

As mentioned above, the interaction between pests and plants may alter the composition and structure of the cell wall, releasing carbohydrates (glycans) that can act as DAMPs. These carbohydrate derivatives are mainly perceived by receptor-like kinases (RLKs)—transmembrane proteins with a variable extracellular domain plus a cytosolic kinase domain (reviewed by Narváez-Barragán et al., 2022; Molina et al., 2024). These receptors slightly differ in the kinase domain, but the main differences are in the specific carbohydrate-binding properties of the ectodomain. Once the receptor recognizes cell wall fragments and/or other pathogen/herbivore compounds, a transduction pathway is triggered to finally activate adaptive and defence responses. Romero-Hernandez and Martinez (2022) identified a set of RLKs that were up-regulated in Arabidopsis after mite infestation. Many herbivore-regulated RLKs have extracellular domains that bind carbohydrates. The RLKs of the Catharanthus roseus RLK1-like (CrRLK1L) family have a malectin motif. These RLK receptor types are required for Arabidopsis responses and triggered by β-1,4-D-xylo-oligosaccharides derived from PCWs (Fernández-Calvo et al., 2024). FERONIA (FER), an Arabidopsis RLK with a malectin-like domain, can perceive cell wall-derived DAMPs such as oligogalacturonides and polygalacturonic acid, the backbone of HG, but also acts as a mechanosensor capable of detecting mechanical or physicochemical perturbations in the cell wall, thereby triggering downstream signalling pathways independently of DAMP release (Vaahtera et al., 2019). FER also plays a role in maintaining PCW integrity during development and under stress conditions. High salinity levels weaken the cell wall, and FER mediates calcium signalling to prevent cell bursting, which is essential for the recovery of plant growth following stress (Feng et al., 2018). Lectin-RLKs contain different classes of lectins with carbohydrate-binding motifs (Bellande et al., 2017). Wall-associated kinases (WAK-RLKs) comprise epidermal growth factor (EGF) repeats with the ability to bind to cell wall pectin (Kohorn, 2016). The LysM-RLK family is involved in the perception of chito-oligosaccharides and lipochito-oligosaccharides from fungi, mycorrhiza, and rhizobia (Miya et al., 2007; Desaki et al., 2018; Mélida et al., 2018).

Many studies have reported the molecular and functional characterization of different RLKs involved in the perception of saccharide fragments in the plant–pathogen context (Bellincampi et al., 2014; Bacete et al., 2018; Narváez-Barragán et al., 2022; Molina et al., 2024), but the role of RLKs in response to phytophagous arthropods is still very elusive. In the plant–herbivore interplay, OS and fluids are deposited on wounded plant sites during feeding and oviposition. In particular, OS contain a battery of compounds (elicitors) derived from both plants and feeders, including saccharides, that are recognized by specific receptors to induce host plant defences. Bricchi et al. (2013) demonstrated that the OS of Spodoptera litura contain a putative β-galactofuranose polysaccharide, which triggered early responses to herbivory in Arabidopsis, although the receptor involved in the process was not identified. Likewise, fractionation of OS of chewing lepidopteran Spodoptera species after feeding on soybean and Arabidopsis yielded a subfraction of oligosaccharides, probably resulting from plant polysaccharide-degrading enzymes. These compounds, eventually converted into elicitors or danger signals, were recognized by RLKs (GmHAK1 and AtHAK1), acting as receptors or co-receptors that activate or amplify defence signalling (Uemura et al., 2020).

Several lectin RLKs have been associated with responses to herbivory. Arabidopsis LecRLK18 was locally up-regulated upon P. brassicae oviposition and egg extract treatment. Egg extract treatment in lecRLK18 mutant plants caused a substantial reduction of several defence-associated biological processes (Gouhier-Darimont et al., 2019). NaLecRK1 of N. attenuata was up-regulated by M. sexta infestation, promoting the JA-mediated defence response (Gilardoni et al., 2011). Likewise, several rice LecRK genes participate in the defence response against the herbivore N. lugens (Liu et al., 2015). The Poaceae-specific metabolite β-1,3:1,4-D-glucan significantly accumulates in rice upon N. lugens attack and contributes to the JA-mediated resistance against the chewing insect. This oligosaccharide binds with high affinity to the lectin RLK OsLecRK1 and modulates its activity. Rice plants overexpressing the OsCsIF6 gene, which encodes a glucan synthase involved in the biosynthesis of β-1,3:1,4-D-glucan, had enhanced cell wall thickness and rice resistance to N. lugens. Opposite effects were observed in infested OsCsIF6 knockdown plants (Dai et al., 2023).

A remarkable recent study published by Kanda et al. (2025) demonstrated the participation of a LysM-RLK, the Chitin Elicitor Receptor Kinase 1 (CERK1), in the plant–herbivore interaction. Feeding bioassays with larvae of the lepidopteran Mythimna loreyi on OsCERK1 knockdown rice plants demonstrated that immune responses depended on the presence of OsCERK1. Moreover, the Chitin Elicitor-Binding Protein (CEBiP), which binds to the chitin receptor OsCERK1, contributed to the defences triggered by OS derived from two lepidopteran species. These findings are consistent with the fact that JA-mediated resistance in wounded leaves is induced by chitin and is amplified by lepidopteran OS.

Conclusions and future perspectives

Although significant progress has been made in understanding the role of the PCW in herbivore defence, key knowledge gaps remain. Herbivory-induced cell wall remodelling influences the release of DAMPs; however, systematic studies that comprehensively link these dynamic changes with immune activation remain largely unexplored. The localized and transient nature of these modifications adds complexity, requiring site-specific and temporal analyses to fully elucidate the role of the PCW as a mediator of plant defence responses against phytophagous pests.

Cutting-edge technologies, such as FTIR spectroscopy, glycome profiling, carbohydrate microarrays, and monoclonal antibodies, serve as powerful tools for unravelling the complexities of cell wall dynamics (Sopeña-Torres et al., 2018; Molina et al., 2021). FTIR spectroscopy enables the identification of wall polymers and functional groups, while glycome profiling and microarrays provide high-throughput insights into immune-active fractions. For instance, glycome profiling combined with calcium signalling assays in transgenic plants expressing cytosolic apoaequorin has been used to characterize immune-active wall fractions (Bacete et al., 2017). In addition, BODIPY-based molecular rotors represent a promising biophysical tool to probe local changes in the mechanical properties of the cell wall, offering real-time, spatially resolved insights into viscosity and rigidity dynamics in vivo (Ryder et al., 2023). When combined with transcriptomic and proteomic approaches, these tools allow a comprehensive understanding of how herbivory-induced changes in the cell wall influence plant defence responses. However, most current methods rely on global analyses, emphasizing the need for localized approaches to resolve site-specific DAMP production during herbivory attack.

Several key questions remain unresolved. Which components of the cell wall change in response to pest attack? Are these changes conserved across different types of biotic stresses? Do these changes vary depending on the feeding mode of the pest? Are the alterations in the PCW triggered by CWDEs present in the saliva of phytophagous insects, or by the structural damage caused during the attack itself? How do plants differentiate between endogenous and foreign CWDEs? Are DAMPs released because of modifications in PCW components? Are these DAMPs produced and maintained over time following herbivore attack? How are herbivory-associated DAMPs specifically recognized by plant receptors? How do herbivores inhibit or modulate DAMPs recognized by plant receptors, and how do they interfere with DAMP signalling through salivary effectors or other strategies? Which molecular pathways are triggered by specific DAMPs?

Interdisciplinary approaches that incorporate advanced imaging, multi-omics, and synthetic DAMP mimics could offer valuable insights to develop sustainable pest management strategies enhancing plant immunity without reliance on chemical pesticides. Deepening our understanding of DAMP-mediated immunity will allow researchers to develop environmentally friendly solutions to protect crops and improve productivity in the face of ongoing biotic challenges.
