Potential profile analysis of reproductive concerns and influencing factors among testicular cancer patients of reproductive age
Authors: Ya Hu, Guolong Chen, Yong Dong, Xiangdong Li, CangMei Fu, Wanqin Hu, Xue Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo analyze the status and potential profiles of reproductive concerns in testicular cancer patients in reproductive age and explore the related factors of reproductive concerns levels in different categories of patients.MethodsA cross-sectional study was used in this study. A total of 214 patients with testicular cancer of reproductive age from the department of urology, Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center in Guangdong Province were selected by convenience sampling from January 2024 to January 2025. Data on demographic characteristics, the reproductive concerns after cancer scale-male (RCAC-M), and the fear of progression questionnaire-short form (FoP-Q-SF) were collected. Potential profile analysis of reproductive concerns levels in testicular cancer patients of reproductive age was performed, and binary logistic regression was used to analyze the related factors of reproductive concerns levels in different categories.ResultsThe reproductive concerns score of testicular cancer patients of reproductive age was (58.16 ± 10.99), and their reproductive concerns was divided into two potential profiles: a low level of reproductive concerns group (46.3%) and a high level of reproductive concerns group (53.7%). Those with fertility intention (OR = 4.208, 95% CI: 2.204–8.034) and more severe fear of disease progression (OR = 1.111, 95% CI: 1.069–1.156) were more likely to develop into the high-level reproductive concerns group (all P < 0.05).ConclusionReproductive concerns in male cancer patients of reproductive age was at a moderate level and showed a substantial heterogeneity. Healthcare professionals should recognize the different reproductive concerns conditions of patients and implement targeted interventions to reduce the reproductive concerns of patients with testicular cancer.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16240-7.Keywords: Testicular cancer, Reproductive age, Reproductive concerns, Fear of progression, Latent profile analysis

Introduction

Testicular cancer is a rare malignant tumor affecting the male urinary system, comprising 1% of all adult cancers and 5% of urological cancers [1]. According to the Global Burden of Disease database, the incidence of testicular cancer increased by 111.07% globally and by 431.48% in China in 2019 compared with 1990 [2]. Testicular cancer most commonly affects men aged 20 to 40, which coincides with their prime reproductive years [3, 4]. Despite notable advancements in treatment strategies including radical orchiectomy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, persistent issues such as varying degrees of reproductive dysfunction in patients have been reported [5, 6]. This potential impairment of reproductive function poses a significant threat to young patients during their critical family-building and life-development years, leading to severe psychosocial distress, particularly concerning fertility.

Reproductive concerns denote an individual’s distress regarding fertility, childbearing, parenting, personal health implications, offspring well-being, and their children’s care [7]. Research has demonstrated that the intensity of these concerns is closely linked to higher levels of existential distress [8]. Furthermore, such concerns can negatively influence individuals’ body image, undermine their sense of gender identity, and result in diminished hope and treatment adherence [9–11]. These psychological effects can have profound implications for patients’ long-term mental well-being and overall quality of life [12]. A qualitative study indicates that patients with testicular cancer experience multiple concerns and negative emotions related to fertility issues [13]. Therefore, exploring factors affecting reproductive concerns is crucial in devising adaptive interventions to improve the reproductive concerns and QoL in young and middle-aged patients with testicular cancer.

Fear of progression (FoP) refers to a persistent anxiety of disease recurrence or progression, which is commonly observed in individuals with chronic disorders and cancer [14]. As a non-neurotic and fully conscious response, individuals with typical levels of FoP generally display rational and appropriate reactions when confronted with potential health threats. Some quantitative studies found that FOP could increase reproductive concerns in breast cancer patients and young lymphoma patients [15, 16]. Moreover, FOP has been applied as an intervention target for cancer patients to decrease cancer-related distress [17]. These studies indicate that patients’ FOP may serve as a predictive factor of reproductive concerns.

The existing research mainly focuses on the reproductive concerns of female cancer patients, [11, 18] with less focus on male patients. This gap is particularly pronounced among young testicular cancer patients within specific cultural contexts, such as China, which deeply influenced by traditional reproductive culture, their unique experiences of fertility-related distress remain underexplored [19, 20]. In terms of methodology, even within the limited studies on male studies, the main approach has been to treat fertility concerns as a homogeneous construct, relying on aggregate scores for assessment [9]. This approach overlooks potential heterogeneity within this population and hinders the development of tailored, individualized support strategies. Latent profile analysis identifies homogeneous subgroups based on patients’ response patterns to items assessing fertility concerns. This approach overcomes the limitations of relying on sum scores by enabling a refined characterization of heterogeneity within the population [21].

Therefore, this study applied a combination of LPA and logistic regression analysis to analyze the status and potential profiles of reproductive concerns in testicular cancer patients in reproductive age and explore the related factors of reproductive concerns levels in different categories of patients.

Study

Study design, setting and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted in the department of urology of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center in Guangdong province from January 2024 and January 2025. Convenience sampling was used to recruit patients. The inclusion criteria: (1) aged 18 ~ 40 years old, (2) diagnosed with testicular cancer by pathologic examination, (3) documented informed consent, and (4) aware of the disease condition; Exclusion criteria: (1) those with severe cognitive dysfunction or previous psychiatric history, (2) communicative impairments. Research on LPA has yet to establish standard calculation formulas for sample size, generally requiring at least 50 to 100 observations per latent profile [22]. This study comprises two latent profiles, necessitating a minimum sample size of 100 to 200 cases. Besides, the sample size for multiple regression analysis is typically 5 to 10 times the number of variables [23]. This study included 15 variables. Considering a 20% inefficiency rate, the final sample size required for this study was calculated to be 90 to 180 cases. A total of 230 questionnaires were distributed, with 214 valid responses (93.04% effective recovery rate).

Data collection

Patient information was obtained using convenience sampling from the inpatient unit of a specialized oncology hospital in GuangDong Province, China. First, after obtaining consent from the head nurse, an investigator screened eligible inpatients from the hospital’s electronic medical record system according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Subsequently, trained research nurses conducted face-to-face study briefings after eligibility screening. An electronic questionnaire was administered via the “Questionnaire Star” platform to collect data. Participant consent was obtained explicitly, with the first question serving as the consent item; a positive response constituted agreement to participate. Also, paper-based questionnaires were distributed, written informed consent is obtained from patients prior to completing the questionnaire. Platform-enforced response requirements ensured complete data submission.Unqualified questionnaires such as omitted or wrong items were excluded, exclusion criteria: (1) questionnaire completion is regular, and (3) questionnaire response time is less than 10 min.

Measures

Demographic characteristics

Designed by the researchers, this study included age, education level, marital status, per capita monthly income of the family, residence, number of children, fertility desire, type of surgery, radiation or chemotherapy, tumor stage, tumor type, and fertility preservation (e.g., freezing of sperm).

Reproductive concerns

The reproductive concerns after cancer scale-male (RCAC-M) was developed by Gorman in 2020, which is used to evaluate the level of reproductive concerns in young male cancer survivors [24]. The scale includes six dimensions: fertility potential, partner disclosure, child’s health, personal health, acceptance and achieving pregnancy, contains 18 items scored and uses the Likert 5-point scale, 1 means “strongly disagree”, 5 means “strongly agree”, and all items were positively scored except for items 5, 10, and 15. The total score ranges from 18 to 90, with higher scores indicating stronger reproductive concerns. It was translated into Chinese by Liu Jie in 2023 [25], reported a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.827 for the entire scale. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of this scale was 0.874.

Fear of progression

The Fear of Progression Questionnaire-Short Form (FoP-Q-SF) is developed by Mehnert in Germany in 2006, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.870 [26]. The Chinese version was localized by Wu et al. in 2015, [27] the coefficient of the entire scale was 0.883. It is segmented into two distinct dimensions: physical health and social-family well-being. The scale consists of 12 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “never” (1) to “always” (5). The total score ranges from 12 to 60, with higher scores indicating an increased fear of disease progression among patients [27]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of this scale was 0.908.

Ethical considerations and consent to participate

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center Ethics Committee (IRB No: B2024-007-01). Although this study did not include unethical conduct or human clinical trials, and did not have any adverse effects on the physical and mental health of the participants, we performed a great deal of work to consider ethics. The following ethical principles were strictly observed: Voluntary Participation: Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Before interviews, researchers thoroughly explained the study purpose, procedures, time commitment, potential risks, confidentiality measures, and participants’ right to withdraw at any time using layperson-friendly language. All questions raised by participants were addressed conscientiously. Signed informed consent forms were collected as documentation of voluntary participation; Confidentiality Protection: All research data and findings will be used exclusively for scientific purposes. Participants’ personal privacy and identifiable information will remain strictly confidential; Equity Principle: All eligible candidates meeting the inclusion criteria were provided equal opportunities to participate in this investigation.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS25.0 and Mplus8.0 software. Q-Q plots verified a normal distribution of the studied variables. Means (standard deviation [SD]) were used to describe continuous data, frequencies and percentages were used to summarize categorical variables, comparisons between multiple groups were made by chi-square test. LPA were analyzed using the scores of each entry of the reproductive concerns scale for testicular cancer patients as explicit variables. The main evaluation metrics of the latent profile model included the log likelihood ratio test (LL), Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample size-adjusted BIC (SABIC), the mean information index (Entropy), corrected likelihood ratio (LMR) and Bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT). The smaller the absolute value of the log-likelihood ratio test, the AIC, BIC and SABIC indicate better model fitness. The Entropy takes values between zero and one; [28] a value closer to one indicates that the model classification is more accurate; 0.8 indicates that the classification accuracy is more than 90%. When the LMR and BLRT values reached a significance level, it indicated that the model with k categories significantly outperformed the model with k-1 categories [29]. The best classification model for reproductive concerns in testicular cancer patients was determined based on the results of the model fitting index. Finally, binary logistic regression was used to analyze the factors influencing the potential profile of reproductive concerns in testicular cancer patients (forward: Wald). All differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 214 testicular cancer patients were surveyed, including: 9 patients (4.2%) aged ≤ 20 years, 95 patients (44.4%) aged 21–30 years, and 110 patients (51.4%) aged 31–40 years. Most patients (67.3%) lived in urban areas, 44.9% were unmarried, over half of patients had a per capita family income of 5000 yuan or above, and 69.6% patients having college education or above. Disease characteristics included 57.5% diagnosed as non-seminomatous, 52.8% in stage I, 51.9% performed a radical orchiectomy, and 30.3% performed a retroperitoneal lymph node dissection. Most of them (60.7%) did not have child, and 62.6% had a desire of fertility, and only 36.4% performed a fertility preservation. Other details are shown in Table 1.

Latent profile analysis of reproductive concerns

Using the scores of each entry of the reproductive concerns scale as explicit variables, the optimal number of potential categories was explored, fitting 1 to 3 models. Model fit indices are presented in Table 3. As the number of latent profiles increased, AIC, BIC, and aBIC decreased. However, the 3-profile model showed non-significant LMR (P > 0.05), indicating no superiority over the 2-profile model. The 2-profile model was selected as optimal (Entropy = 0.860; LMR and BLRT: P < 0.05), with reasonable class probabilities (46.3% vs. 53.7%). Moreover, the two profiles demonstrate excellent interpretability and clinical utility. A qualitative study [13] revealed a marked dichotomy in reproductive concerns among testicular cancer patients: one group, potentially due to being unmarried, childless, or holding traditional views on parenthood, perceived loss of fertility as a significant threat, leading to heightened anxiety levels. Conversely, another group, being married with children and adopting a life-first attitude, exhibited lower anxiety levels. Moreover, categorising patients into two conceptually distinct and clearly defined profiles facilitates rapid clinical identification of high-risk individuals, enabling targeted care interventions.

Discussion

Current status of reproductive concerns among testicular cancer patients of reproductive age

This study found that reproductive concerns among testicular cancer patients of reproductive age were generally moderate (58.16 ± 10.99), consistent with the significant threat of potential fertility impairment faced by this group. This overall level is comparable to studies on young female patients with breast cancer, [11, 30] indicating that reproductive concerns represent a prevalent core psychosocial issue among young cancer survivors. Further analysis revealed that “offspring health” was the dimension of greatest concern for testicular cancer patients (11.82 ± 2.86). This finding aligns with prior research on testicular cancer patients [9] and resonates with observations among female cancer patients, [11, 30] strongly suggesting that anxiety about offspring health constitutes the most universal and profound core component of cancer-related reproductive concerns, irrespective of gender or cancer type. The potential mechanisms may be as follows: First, patients are generally aware that treatments such as radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and retroperitoneal lymph node dissection can directly impair spermatogenic function and increase sperm DNA fragmentation [4, 31]—a factor that may raise the risk of birth defects in offspring and thus intensifies their reproductive concerns. Secondly, the long-term impact of different treatment regimens on fertility in testicular cancer patients remains unclear. This uncertainty, in turn, can act as a catalyst for negative thinking regarding the health of their future offspring. Concurrently, cancer survivors often hold concerns about potentially passing on a genetic cancer risk to their children, a fear that is especially acute among younger patients. Finally, only 36.4% testicular cancer patients undertook fertility preservation measures in this study. Missed opportunities for preservation can lead to significant decision regret and may further exacerbate reproductive concerns, which is consistent with the results of previous study [32]. A lack of crucial information—regarding optimal timing, available facilities, associated costs, and procedures—serves as the primary barrier for testicular cancer patients seeking fertility preservation [33]. This suggests that healthcare professions should provide clear, detailed, operable fertility counselling to alleviate patients’ confusion and help them make the right decision.

Characteristics of reproductive concerns in testicular cancer patients

This study employed latent profile analysis (LPA) to identify two distinct profiles of reproductive concerns among testicular cancer patients: a “low reproductive concern group” (46.3%) and a “high reproductive concern group” (53.7%). This classification helps pinpoint high-risk populations for prioritized psychological intervention. The larger “high reproductive concern group” demonstrated clinically significant characteristics. It primarily consisted of unmarried, childless patients with non-seminoma tumors, who reported stronger fertility desires and significantly higher fear of disease progression scores. In contrast, the “low reproductive concern group” contained a higher proportion of married patients with ≥ 2 existing children, alongside significantly lower disease progression fear scores. This pattern resembles the “reproductive concerns” subgroup identified by Chen et al. [34] in cervical cancer patients, supporting the argument that marital status and existing number of children serve as key indicators for assessing psychological fertility risk.

The divergent characteristics of these two groups may reflect distinct psychosocial adaptation pathways. Patients in the “low reproductive concern” group likely perceive treatment-related fertility risks more moderately and demonstrate greater psychological resilience. This may be attributed to having already completed their families through existing children, notably deriving stronger social support from marriage. Conversely, those “high reproductive concern” group often faces a compound pressure of managing both survival fears and significant Reproductive concerns. Notably, higher educational attainment did not emerge as a protective factor against anxiety in this study. This may suggest that while greater health literacy enhances patients’ understanding, it can also make them more acutely aware of the complexity of treatment side effects and fertility risks, thereby leading to greater distress [34]. Furthermore, the substantial disparity between the high fertility desire (62.6%) and low fertility preservation implementation rate (36.4%) across the entire cohort strongly suggests practical factors such as inadequate clinical communication, insufficient decision support, or socioeconomic barriers [35, 36]. Therefore, medical interventions must be personalised: for the “high fertility anxiety group”, the focus should be on alleviating disease-related fears and providing clear decision support for fertility preservation; for the “low fertility anxiety group”, emphasis may be placed on reinforcing their positive mindset and adaptive coping strategies.

The influence of fertility desires and fear of disease progression on reproductive concerns

Our findings indicate that a desire for fertility significantly elevated the likelihood of patients being classified into the high reproductive concern group (OR = 4.208, P < 0.001). This result aligns with studies conducted among young female cancer patients with breast cancer, gynaecological tumours, and other conditions, [34, 37] suggesting that intense fertility aspirations are closely linked to concerns about fertility loss regardless of gender. For testicular cancer patients of reproductive age, establishing a family and raising children constitute vital components of life planning. Consequently, the conflict between strong fertility aspirations and the reality that cancer treatments may compromise reproductive function not only generates psychological distress but may also complicate clinical decision-making. Patients may struggle to choose between more aggressive therapies (potentially more damaging to fertility) and conservative approaches (potentially raising concerns about efficacy).

A heightened fear of disease progression constitutes another independent risk factor (OR = 1.111, P < 0.001). Consistent with findings in various cancer survivor cohorts, where anxiety regarding disease recurrence or progression constitutes a pervasive and enduring stressor that compromises overall quality of life, including future fertility planning [38]. In testicular cancer patients, intense fear of disease progression can drive them toward treatment options perceived as more definitive, despite higher genotoxicity. This choice may paradoxically elevate the risk of fertility impairment, creating a vicious cycle. Meanwhile, anxiety about survival can amplify the fear that future fertility may be impaired [38].

Notably, this study identified a “high reproductive concern” patient group, typically characterized by both a strong desire for future fertility and a pronounced fear of disease progression. This “dual-high-risk” psychological profile renders them a particularly vulnerable population. Beyond coping with the illness itself, they are forced into a difficult trade-off between “ensuring survival” and “preserving fertility,” which imposes a severe dual burden of psychological distress and decisional conflict [9, 11].

Based on the above analysis, target interventions to alleviate reproductive concerns in testicular cancer patients of reproductive age was strongly suggested. For “dual-high-risk” patients exhibiting both strong fertility desires and intense fear of disease progression, the healthcare system should provide integrated support. Firstly, detailed information and counseling regarding fertility preservation should be provided before treatment initiation, including aspects such as the optimal timing (e.g., sperm cryopreservation before chemotherapy), available methods, accessing specialized centers, and associated costs. This should be incorporated as a standard component of the treatment pathway [39, 40] to mitigate fears about the permanent loss of reproductive opportunity. Secondly, a multidisciplinary team approach should be activated, integrating specialized assessment and counseling from oncology, reproductive medicine, and psychology/psychiatry departments to address patients’ complex medical and psychosocial needs concurrently [41]. Additionally, cognitive-behavioral interventions are recommended. These could involve presenting evidence on the potential recovery of sperm quality after treatment and sharing narratives of successful post-treatment parenthood, thereby helping patients correct cognitive biases regarding treatment outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations: First, this study employed a cross-sectional design; thus, causal relationships among the variables cannot be established. Second, as a single-center investigation, although participants were recruited from multiple provinces across China, all were from one hospital, which may introduce selection bias. Thirdly, the lack of data on time since diagnosis/treatment represents another limitation, as this factor may influence fear of disease progression and reproductive concerns throughout the survivorship trajectory. Future research can employ a prospective design to study factors associated with reproductive concern in testicular cancer patients using a nationally representative sample of testicular cancer patients in China. In addition, future longitudinal research is needed to address these issues.

Conclusion

This study shows that patients with testicular cancer in reproductive age have a moderate level of reproductive concern, and identified two latent profiles of reproductive concern, including the low concern group and high concern group. Patients with intensive fertility desire and high levels of fear of disease progression are more likely to develop into high reproductive concern. Healthcare professionals should focus on this group of patients, understand their needs and provide targeted intervention plan to alleviate patient reproductive concern. Future research should employ a prospective design to study factors associated with reproductive concern in testicular cancer patients using a nationally representative sample of testicular cancer patients in China.
