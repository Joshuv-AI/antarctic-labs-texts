Satisfaction and preference for home delivery pharmaceutical services among patients attending primary care settings in Qatar during COVID-19 pandemic: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Muhammad Tanveer Alam, Shajitha Thekke Veettil, Jeyaram Illiayaraja Krishnan, Reena Ghazi Alsalmi, Jinsy Jacob, Aly Tamer Mousa, Samah Auni Mesbah Sakallah, Shafqat Mahmood, Ahmed Al Nuaimi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesDuring the COVID-19 epidemic, due to the high risk associated with travel and group gatherings, the Ministry of Public Health in Qatar introduced home delivery of pharmaceutical services. This study aims to investigate the satisfaction and preferences of patients who used the new home delivery services provided by health centres (HCs).Study designA cross-sectional study was conducted using an exploratory questionnaire to assess patients’ preferences for pharmaceutical delivery services.SettingsPrimary care.ParticipantsA convenient sample of 604 adults who used the pharmaceutical delivery services was recruited. The study tool measured satisfaction with a set of 11 service evaluation criteria on a Likert scale of 5 grades. The score ranged between 1 for highly dissatisfied and 5 for highly satisfied. A quantitative summary satisfaction score was calculated by summing the satisfaction rating for home delivery on the 11 service evaluation criteria. The final score was adjusted to a maximum of 100 for ease of understanding.ResultsResults showed that 45.5% of participants were highly satisfied with the overall quality of the home delivery service. Furthermore, 58.9% were willing to recommend the service to others, while only 30.9% considered the delivery cost to be reasonable. Among the factors associated with satisfaction was nationality, where Qatari nationals had a significantly higher mean satisfaction score (89%) compared with expatriates (79.5%).OutcomeThe satisfaction score exhibited a significant positive trend with age and educational level. Factors such as gender, the time required to access the HCs, frequency of requesting pharmacy service and income categories did not show a significant difference in the satisfaction score.ConclusionThe home delivery service is a promising addition to primary care. It may play a crucial role in enhancing accessibility for the beneficiaries reducing unnecessary visits to HCs in the future.Keywords: COVID-19, primary care, cross-sectional studies

Introduction

Telepharmacy which is part of telehealthcare is a resource-efficient way to deliver pharmacy service1 using information and communication technology.2 During COVID-19, governments worldwide introduced regulations on social distancing, leading to less direct contact with patients.3 Examples like China,4 Cape Town and South Africa5 adopted a pragmatic approach by introducing a virtual pharmacy service along with home delivery as a precautionary measure to control the spread of infection and protect the vulnerable population. ‘Mail order phadministrative regions: Airport armacy’ is not a new concept. In America, up to one-third of medications for chronic conditions are delivered via mail6 and account for nearly a quarter of pharmacy sales even before the COVID-19 pandemic.7 Its benefits have been shown by looking at statins with a positive association found with low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol control in new statin users7 and better medication adherence among patients with diabetes and other chronic conditions.8 Economically, it may be cheaper for health plans and patients as well.9

Despite different models of telepharmacy, the aim was to provide a full range of pharmacy services remotely including prescribing medications as well as imparting patient education, and counselling.2 It allows the pharmacist to play a distinct role in pharmacovigilance and pharmaceutical care plans especially for patients with comorbidities, and in special populations with advantages highlighted in the immunosuppressed.10 The convenience of avoiding travel, reduced exposure to infection, time and money saved, reduced stress, employment disruption better adherence to medication and better experience of health services including more autonomy by having consultations fitted around own schedule have been highlighted.11 12 However, there are disadvantages including illiteracy as well as reluctance to use computer/technology especially among the elderly, inability to fully analyse the patient’s condition and the usual misadventures with medications.2 Despite these concerns, studies have shown that the quality of care is at least as good as direct patient care.13

The Primary Health Care Corporation (PHCC) in Qatar in collaboration with the Ministry of Public Health adopted home medication delivery services to the general population in March 2020 due to the COVID-19 epidemic to reduce unnecessary visits to the health centres (HCs). Delivery of medications was done through the well-organised network of Qatar Post (Q-Post https://qatarpost.qa/home), which is a governmental agency that provides national postal services. Q-Post facilitated the implementation and provision of the service free of charge to the residents of Qatar. The new service includes medication refills, teleconsultation, virtual consultation and home healthcare. The service does not include narcotic drugs, psychotropic medications or emergency and urgent prescriptions. The service workflow consisted of different phases and PHCC implemented that successfully.5 14 This service has been a vital resource for the public throughout the pandemic and the organisation, therefore, decided to continue the service passed the end of the epidemic. It alleviates congestion and avoids unnecessary visits to HCs, ensuring uninterrupted medication access for patients during this crucial epidemic period.14

Implementing the prescribed medication home services has several significant advantages. It plays a key role in reducing the spread of COVID-19, particularly among vulnerable populations and healthcare workers. Additionally, over the long term, it has the potential to reduce the lost productivity caused by individuals taking time off work to collect their medications. This service also has the capacity to enhance patient compliance with their prescribed medications while fostering improved communication between patients and pharmacy professionals.5 14

There are certain challenges associated with the home delivery service. Expanding the service on a larger scale necessitates effective communication, support and coordination among various stakeholders, including pharmacies, primary care providers, Q-Post and other community-based services. Another challenge pertains to undelivered packages, with each failed delivery incurring an approximate cost of US$8. These undelivered packages not only increase the overall cost of the service but also patients’ inconvenience. There is also the potential for issues related to medication timeliness and completeness, stemming from miscommunication between patients and pharmacists. To mitigate these challenges, pharmacists and pharmacy staff should undergo continuous training in effective communication with patients when using virtual services.5 14

Al-Zaidan et al in their article described the development, implementation, service process metrics and the opportunities and challenges of this programme in Qatar.14 However, the experience of patients using this service in Qatar was not studied, which has been shown to vary in other studies.15 The main objective of this study is to investigate the perceptions and preferences of consumers who have used the new medication home delivery service from their HCs when compared with the traditional approach.

Methodology

Study design

A cross-sectional study using survey methodology to assess patient satisfaction and preferences. Convenient sampling was employed, with a structured questionnaire administered to consenting patients who visit/approach the pharmacy for collecting their medication in person at their designated HCs.

Study setting and population

The PHCC is the largest primary healthcare provider for the people of Qatar. At the time of the study, the PHCC was operating 27 primary healthcare centres distributed throughout the country. Qatar is administratively divided into three regions namely: Central (including the south), Western and Northern. 13 of these HCs are located in Doha city (Central Region). The PHCC through its HC provides appropriate and effective healthcare services focused on the needs of patients. Services delivered emphasise health promotion, prevention and diagnosis, as well as treatment and provision of long-term and appropriate support to patients and their families.

The patient population in the PHCC HCs varies due to the regional population dependency. All patients above 18 years requiring medications who used both types of pharmacy services (face-to-face and home delivery) were targeted in the study. The exclusion criteria would be patients with physical and mental disability, acute illnesses, who cannot read English and Arabic.

Sampling and sample size determination

A convenient sampling approach was used by the pharmacist to recruit individuals who visit the pharmacy for their medication at HCs. Three HCs were chosen to represent each of the three administrative region: Airport Health Center (Central), Mesaimeer Health Center (Western) and Al Khor Health Center (North). A structured questionnaire was administered to consenting individuals while following the privacy and confidentiality requirements. Since the study was executed during COVID-19 mobility restrictions, some individuals were approached using the phone. A verbal consent was obtained by the pharmacist and the questionnaire was completed accordingly.

The structured questionnaire was adapted from the Patient Satisfaction Questionnaire Guidance RPS Handbook for Homecare Services—Appendix 5—Version 2, 20207 with amendments made to fit with the local context (online supplemental file 1). It was delivered in Arabic or English depending on the participant’s preference. Demographic details such as gender, age, nationality and educational attainment were recorded. In addition, the distance to the HC measured in terms of time of travel, frequency of visits, income and medical condition were also included. The questionnaire included 11 questions on preference and satisfaction and 2 questions that measured the overall satisfaction.

Both the English and Arabic version of the questionnaire were initially piloted by randomly selecting 15 participants including patients, pharmacists, clinicians and nurses from one HC for their views, acceptability and feedback.

The sample size calculation was based on the formula for estimating a single population proportion: n=Z2 p(1−p)/d2, where:

n=The sample size necessary for estimating a population proportion.

Z=1.96 (standardised normal deviate for 95% confidence level).

p=0.5 (The most conservative estimate for an unknown proportion since more than one proportion is required for this study).

d=0.04 (accepted margin of error, which represents 8% of the estimated P of 0.5).

The required sample size according to the above parameters would be 601. An extra 20% will be added to the required sample size to adjust for missing answers/values on some key questions. The resulting targeted sample size is 720.

Patient and public involvement

A pilot study was conducted to ascertain the opinion of patients and other colleagues at the HC. The feedback on eight simple questions was gathered from the participants of that pilot. Based on the feedback received, the questionnaire was found to be of suitable length with acceptable timings of completion, of good clarity, structure and format and without significant perceived difficulties in completion or language (Piolet study report—online supplemental file 2).

A quantitative summary score was calculated by summing the Likert satisfaction rating for home delivery on the 11-home delivery service evaluation criteria. Each Likert score ranged between 1 for highly dissatisfied and 5 for highly satisfied. The final score was adjusted to have a maximum of 100 for ease of understanding. The overall quality of home delivery service showed a statistically significant positive linear correlation with the pharmaceutical delivery service satisfaction score (online supplemental file 3).

As shown in online supplemental file 4, gender showed no association with the mean pharmaceutical delivery service satisfaction score. Local (Qatari) nationality had a statistically significant higher mean satisfaction score of 89% compared with expatriates 79.5%. The mean satisfaction score showed a statistically significant positive trend with age and educational level. The duration of time required to access the HC from residence, frequency of requesting pharmacy services and income categories failed to show any obvious or statistically significant difference in mean satisfaction score.

Discussion

This may qualify as the first study exploring the views of patients regarding telepharmacy and home delivery during the COVID-19 epidemic in the Gulf Region. A standardised search in accessible research repositories (PubMed, Google Scholar) failed to find any publications using the keywords: COVID-19, home delivery, pharmaceutical services, telepharmacy, patient satisfaction, Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) and primary care. This combination of telepharmacy and home delivery became the most widely used model during home quarantine across many countries providing multiple benefits10 including protecting patients and staff from infection and decongesting primary care facilities.12 Our results indicated that 45.5% of participants were highly satisfied with the overall quality of the home delivery service. Furthermore, 58.9% were willing to recommend the service to others. A similar publication also showed that the participants were satisfied with the home delivery of pharmaceutical services.16

In Qatar, this initiative was implemented at a large scale without any hindrance since the basic structure and foundation of primary healthcare services were already in place. The main issues to address were logistics and communication. In addition to television, radio and daily newspapers, WhatsApp and social media through Twitter and Facebook were used to advertise and inform about the new medication delivery service.14 The results showed that the locals (Qatari nationality) were more satisfied with the service than expatriates. This seems logical given that the mobile phones are inexpensive and accessible in Qatar with around 100% of all residents having access to the internet. The development of telecommunications in Qatar is roughly equivalent to the USA with nearly 3.80 million mobile phone connections, which corresponds to an average of 1.3 per person. In the USA in comparison, this figure is 1.1 mobile phones per person.16 This may have contributed to a smoother implementation phase in Qatar when using technologies for communication and advertisement of the service.

The current study showed very little difference in preference between face-to-face and home delivery with regard to punctuality of deliveries and choice of delivery time offered during the pandemic. However, there was a clear preference for face to face when it came to receiving the drug without affecting the conditions of transportation and storage (58% vs 42%) and accuracy and completeness of supplies (59% vs 41%). Studies have shown that patients do perceive certain barriers and facilitators when using the service and have pointed out that the system is unreliable and the delivery date unpredictable.8 Al-Zaidan et al in their article describing the development of the Medication Home Delivery Service in Qatar, highlighted the importance of coordination between pharmacy, primary care, Q-Post and other community-based services.14 They noted that the return of parcels had been attributed to ‘no one answering or opening the door’, ‘the client refused to accept the parcel’, ‘the address was incorrect’, ‘incorrect mobile number’, ‘the client did not pick up the phone’ and ‘he was not at home at time of delivery’. Not answering the phone or addresses being incorrect due to incomplete existing patient records has been also shown in other studies.5 Medications not reaching on time and incomplete medications due to miscommunication with the pharmacist14 as well as asynchronised supplies of different medicines are other common issues.15 Mash et al’s study reported patients raising similar concerns like not always receiving all their medication (because something was out of stock) and the medication looked different to what they were expecting.12 The fear that medications may not be enough before a new one arrives and that outdoor exposure may also harm the prescription medication’s efficacy, especially when it comes to diabetes-specific medications like insulin has also been highlighted.8 Al-Zaidan et al reiterated that the percentage of returned packages (during a period of 5 months) ranged from 2.4% and 2.1% in the Central and Northern Region to 0.9% in the Western Region. Despite being low, in addition to the inconvenience of the return of undelivered packages during the pandemic, this also has a knockdown effect on the overall cost of the service.14 The role of pharmacists, along with other pharmacy staff, as front line should be based on clear agreements and is essential in not only maintaining the supply but also in providing counselling and information on medication.3 The recommendation is that they need constant training on effective communication with patients using virtual services to avoid these challenges.14

The cost of the home delivery service was not of concern during the pandemic as it was paid for by the hospital pharmacy or relevant department and not by the patient in some countries.10 Initially, patients had free-of-charge home delivery in Qatar as well. However, later on when COVID-19 cases dropped considerably, a delivery cost was added which has been identified as an important bugbear to many patients. Each delivery costs around QAR30 (US8$). With a wide variation in income among the expats,17 this may have resulted in patient dissatisfaction with the preference for face to face.

Despite all the above issues, almost half (45.5%) of the study participants expressed their positivity about the home delivery service. The home delivery satisfaction score was based on the personal rating of 11 service evaluation criteria. This home delivery service satisfaction score correlated positively with age and educational level. Since this is a remote service, some patients may feel that it reduces important face-to-face interactions and limits the interaction opportunities with pharmacists and primary care providers or they may have security fears over lost or stolen medications or concerns over receiving incorrect medication, as highlighted earlier.8 Studies have reported a considerable increase in medicines-related anxiety15 during COVID-19. The current study, however, did not explore the role of personal face-to-face discussions with the pharmacist over their medication in providing the required information or reassurance and a comfort blanket to the service recipients.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that most consumers had a good experience with the home delivery service during the COVID-19 pandemic. Consumers and carers prefer a convenient and easily accessible service which is people centred. As in other studies, variations in opinion can depend on patient’s own expectations, prepandemic experience of the pharmacy service and relationship with pharmacy staff. The overall positive response, however, suggests the possibility of a long-term hybrid system which would provide more options to the patient regarding managing their medications. The role of the pharmacy staff in communication, establishing good relationships, following SOPs, coordination of service provision and facilitating medicine delivery are all essential in any of the chosen processes. The logistic procedures and patient information and understanding of the system also contribute to a high-quality pharmacotherapy service.
