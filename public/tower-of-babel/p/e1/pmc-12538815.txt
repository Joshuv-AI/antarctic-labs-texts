Research characteristics about the 24-h movement behaviors in children and adolescents: a scoping review
Authors: Jaminson Raul Ricardo-Sejin, Carlos Mario Arango-Paternina, Alberto Flórez-Pregonero, Fredy Alonso Patiño-Villada
Published: 2025

AbstractBackgroundHealth-related behaviors, such as physical activity, sedentary behavior, and sleep, have variability, intensity, and intentionality within human behavior and are integrated within the 24-h movement behaviors (24-HMB). Background

During the day, the human being performs actions that can be in one of three categories of movement: moving, sitting or lying down, or sleeping. Although physical activity (PA), sedentary behaviors (SB), and sleep have been widely studied separately, an interest in studying them from an integrative perspective has recently emerged. The integration is supported by the idea that these behaviors alternate during a 24-h cycle, a phenomenon that has been termed 24-h movement behaviors (24-HMB) [1]. During a cycle of 24-HMB, time segmentation is common due to the variability, intensity, and intentionality of human behavior, and it is logical since the three behaviors are mutually exclusive [2–4]. The integrative perspective has been recognized and useful in formulating global [5] and national 24-HMB guidelines [6–10]. For instance, the Canadian Society for Exercise Physiology recommends that children and adolescents should sleep between 9–11 (5–13 years) and 8–10 (14–17 years) hours per night, engage in at least 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity each day, and limit recreational screen time to no more than two hours per day [6, 11].

Based specifically on those guidelines [6, 7, 10], previous studies have informed about the adherence to the 24-HMB guidelines (meeting the three recommendations). For instance, a meta-analysis with a sample of 387,437 participants in 23 countries found an overall adherence to the 24-HMB guidelines of 7.1%, 2.9% in South America, 7.8% in North America, 9.6% in Europa, and 10.9% in Oceania. In contrast, 19.2% of the overall sample did not meet any of the three 24-HMB guidelines [12]. In addition, it has been documented that compliance with the three recommendations decreases from preschool to adolescence, and meeting only one recommendation could not attenuate the adverse effects of the other two [13, 14]. This epidemiological panorama raises the attention of public health authorities due to the relevance of the behaviors mentioned above. Those who meet the 24-HMB guidelines report more favorable health indicators than those who do not [15]. For instance, meeting the three 24-HMB guidelines has been positively associated with body composition [16], motor and cognitive development [17], perceived health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [18], psychosocial health [19], and dietary patterns [20].

To date, some reviews have addressed the 24-HMB characteristics by examining lifetime prevalence of meeting the 24-HMB guidelines [12], the impact of COVID- 19 on 24-HMB [21, 22], the use of questionnaires for its measurements [21], and associations with health outcomes such as mental health [23, 24]. However, given the phenomenon’s scope and the methodological challenges imposed by the integration of the three behaviors [1], in terms of measurement, analysis, and intervention, it is necessary to identify the most relevant characteristics of research about it, which offer pertinent information to consolidating an epidemiological framework to better understand the 24-HMB in children and adolescents. Although the topic has received research interest, and systematic reviews have been conducted on various aspects of the 24 HMB [25, 26], the nature and purpose of scoping reviews [27, 28] enable a broader exploration and mapping of the research methods used in the study of 24-HMB. For example, this scoping review was useful in identifying the diversity of methodological approaches applied to study the 24-HMB, such as the factors influencing behaviors, the health outcomes associated with the 24-HMB, the different methods for measuring behaviors, among others. Therefore, this scoping review aimed to identify the nature and extent of the published evidence on 24-HMB in children and adolescents.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted following guidance from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) scoping review development guidelines [28] and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic review and Meta-Analysis extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [29]. The protocol for this scoping review was registered on Open Science Framework on May 24, 2022, with identifier : DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/VUKPY.

Eligibility criteria

The study’s eligibility criteria were based on the components of PCC (Population, Concept, Context) suggested by JBI to develop scoping reviews [28, 29]. Population: The review focused on school-aged children and adolescents between the ages of 6 and 18, regardless of their health status. Studies that included populations outside the 6–18 years were included in the review if results were separated by age groups and most of the sample was inside the age range. Concept: This review explored the different characteristics of the investigations concerning 24-HMB. The behaviors included all concepts related to PA (exercise), sedentary behavior (e.g., screen time, TV viewing time, sedentary lifestyle), and sleep (e.g. sleep habits, sleep patterns). Studies where behaviors were analyzed independently were excluded. Context: The review encompassed a broad spectrum of research without imposing restrictions based on geographic locations, cultural aspects, geographical factors, or specific social or gender interests. This analysis included all articles published from January 1, 2012, when the study of the 24-HMB began to be reported in the literature, until November 26, 2024.

This scoping review analyzed information derived from primary studies to understand the characteristics of the 24-HMB. The review encompassed various study designs utilizing quantitative approaches (such as observational, experimental, or quasi-experimental studies) and qualitative studies, all of which were considered for inclusion. Language restrictions were not applied, and only studies with available full text were eligible for inclusion. Reviews and abstracts were excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

A search was made in the electronic databases of MedLine (access: PubMed), EMBASE, CINHAL (access: EBSCO), Web of Science, and LILACS (VHL) using free-text keywords, used in similar evidence synthesis and primary studies, and controlled terms adapted for each database (Additional file 1). These databases were used following the recommended combination for literature searches [30]. In turn, filters were applied by age (6 to 18 years) and year of publication (2012–2024). We also checked the reference lists of all included articles for further study. The complete search strategy for each database is provided in Additional file 2.

Selection of sources of evidence

All the identified citations were imported into the Rayyan bibliographic manager [31], and after identifying the duplicates through the software, one of the reviewers (JR) manually removed them. Two reviewers (FP and JR) independently reviewed the titles and abstracts according to the eligibility criteria. Relevant sources were fully retrieved, and two reviewers (FP and JR) independently assessed the full text of the selected articles against the eligibility criteria. Reasons for exclusion from full-text sources of evidence that do not meet the eligibility criteria were recorded and reported in the scoping review. Any disagreements regarding study eligibility were resolved by consensus or with the involvement of a third reviewer (CA).

Data charting process

Each selected article was randomly assigned to two reviewers (JR, FP, CA, and AF). Each reviewer independently extracted data following the JBI template for scoping reviews [27]. The articles included for data extraction were processed using an Office Forms tool. The extraction form included the following information: identifying information (authors, year publication, continent, country of origin, and language); characteristics of the phenomenon (main objective, phenomenon/paradigm); description of the participants (total sample, setting, age group, percentage of women, health status, parental involvement); methods (approach, study design, tool measurement cycle); outcomes and stage framework [32]. Before extracting information, a pilot test (the first ten records) was carried out to evaluate the instrument. Discrepancies or doubts that arose during data extraction were resolved by consensus. Following the methodology of the scoping reviews, an assessment of the critical appraisal of the included studies was not performed.

Synthesis of results

The scoping review results are presented through summary tables, narrative synthesis, and figures (where applicable) to describe the characteristics of the studies included in the review. The figures were elaborated using the Microsoft Excel package.

Following the removal of 2244 duplicates, 3691 unique records were identified for title and abstract screening, excluding 3347 reports and 332 that proceeded to full-text reviews. Of these, 119 reports were excluded, and finally, 213 studies were included in the current review.

Discussion

This scoping review aimed to identify the nature and extent of the published literature on 24-HMB in children and adolescents. The review identified 213 studies distributed across all continents and found that most studies were carried out in high-income countries. There are a wide variety of terms to refer to the phenomenon. Half of the studies included a population aged 6 to 18 years and 70% were reported in the school settings. Most of the studies were cross-sectional and used questionnaires. About a fifth part of the studies employed an isotemporal substitution model or compositional data analysis. Compliance with 24-HMB recommendations was the most frequent outcome. Establishing links between behaviors and health has been the stage that has contributed the most to the epidemiological framework.

In this review, we have observed that North America has the highest frequency of publications (42.2%) on 24-HMB. This predominance can be attributed to Canada was the first to integrate guidelines related to 24-HMB globally [6]. Furthermore, as high-income countries, North American countries possess more financial resources, technological advancements, and governmental support, which facilitate conducting studies on this phenomenon [241]. In contrast, it is possible that the priorities of public health in low-income countries are different (communicable diseases, chronic diseases, etc.). Therefore, research efforts in these countries could be focused on structural factors, such as improving infrastructure and access to healthcare [242, 243].

While the components of the 24-HMB (PA, SB, and sleep) have been conceptualized separately for several decades, the integrated phenomenon has evolved into a polysemy. A study by Falck [244] uncovered many terms associated with this phenomenon. In turn, this review identified at least eighteen terms that hinder the conceptual definition of the phenomenon, leading to a lack of consensus in addressing it. Therefore, it is necessary to consolidate a specific term to refer to the integration of movement behaviors. In this regard, “24 Hour Movement Guidelines” was the most frequent term reported, perhaps because most of the studies were focused on verifying the guideline’s compliance. The second most used term was “24 Hour Movement behavior”, which expresses the integration of the three behaviors in a more precise way. It is worth noting that the Canadian guidelines refer to four behaviors, given that physical activity is divided into moderate-vigorous and light intensity. However, many of the studies carried out in Canada were based on compliance with the three recommendations, taking only one for physical activity. This aspect is relevant to consider in future studies and facilitates the comparability of the findings.

The review revealed that almost 50% of the studies on 24-HMB covered children and adolescents between 6 and 18 years old. This wide age range allows identifying the transition of changes in the 24-HMB from childhood to adolescence. In turn, the setting where most studies were reported was the school since this is a space where the population is captive and there is real control of the variables. It is also an environment conducive to the formation of healthy habits and that influences the well-being, physical and mental health of students [245, 246].

Quantitative methods and cross-sectional designs were most frequently used in 24-HMB research. These approaches allow for the systematic collection of numerical data and the examination of behaviors over specific time periods. As expected, the time segmentation perspective, which drives the integration of the three behaviors, shares its premises with quantitative methodologies. This explains the number of quantitative studies analyzing the phenomenon. On the other hand, qualitative approaches such as that of Faulkner et al. [51] can be useful in understanding people’s subjective experiences with movement behaviors over 24 h and how these behaviors affect their health, well-being, and quality of life.

Questionnaires emerge as the most common option for collecting information about the 24 HMB cycle. This may be attributed to questionnaires offering a practical and inexpensive means of collecting data from large and diverse populations, allowing for comprehensive assessments of movement behaviors at different time points [247]. However, it was found only one study using a single instrument evaluating the three behaviors in an integrated manner [132] reporting moderate reliability (r = 0.79, 0.61, 0.56) for sleep, PA, and SB respectively. Although accelerometers were used to a lesser extent, they capture more complete and precise information about the components of 24-HMB [248], which in turn facilitates the application of analysis methods such as compositional data analysis and isotemporal substitution model. It should be noted that these methods are considered a developing field since only about a fifth part of the studies used them in analysis and their application is mostly associated with the use of accelerometers.

In the review, compliance with the 24-HMB recommendations is the most common outcome due to the interest in knowing its prevalence. Other outcomes such as mental health [168], body composition [165], academic performance [197], and HRQoL [249] were reported as they attempt to establish relationships between these factors and 24-HMB in adolescence.

Considering the epidemiological framework proposed by Sallis et al. [32] and the mapping done in this review, it was found that most of the findings of the included studies were related to establish the connections between behaviors and health (phase 1 of the epidemiological framework). This finding may be attributed to the fact that one of the primary objectives of public health research is to identify modifiable factors influencing health outcomes [247]. Although elucidating the links between behaviors and health outcomes provides practical insights for developing effective interventions and health promotion strategies, few studies have developed Phase 4 (Evaluate interventions to change behavior) [34, 183]. In this regard, evidence recommends intervening one outcome at a time [248, 250], this suggestion raises a challenge to the integration of multiple behaviors. Consequently, the few studies about Phase 4 reflect the limited progress in the consolidation of Phase 5 (Translating research into practice).

Strength and limitations

The strengths of this review include a comprehensive search strategy across a wide range of databases without language restrictions, and it integrated all three components of 24-HMB. This scoping review presents some limitations. First, the authors did not consider including gray literature in their search. Second, despite applying a search strategy following the recommended database combination, it is possible that studies included in databases not consulted were overlooked. And third, considering the purpose of scoping reviews, which aim to provide an overview rather than an in-depth synthesis, some aspects were not analyzed, such as validity of measurement instruments and description of measurement instruments used for each behavior.

Implications and future research

Among the studies reviewed, the lack of specific standardized and validated questionnaires to measure 24-HMB and the methodological variability to analyze 24-HMB stands out. In line with the findings of Šuc et al. [26] and Rodrigues et al. [25], the results of the present review suggest the need to develop more precise instruments to measure the three behaviors in an integrated manner. Furthermore, in studies aimed at analyzing the relationship between adherence to recommendations and health outcomes, it is recommended to incorporate analytical methods that facilitate the integration of data from the three behaviors, such as compositional data analysis and isotemporal substitution, to enrich these analyses. In several studies, compliance with the Canadian guidelines has been assessed using accelerometry exclusively as a method to measure sedentary behavior. This approach introduces some discrepancies, as the Canadian guidelines specify a threshold for screen time (up to two hours per day), but not for the other domains of sedentary behavior. However, data obtained through accelerometry do not allow for differentiation between the various domains of sedentary behavior. For a more accurate assessment, it is necessary to complement these data with additional reports. This aspect should be considered in future studies that employ accelerometers as the primary measurement tool.

Another key finding in the review was the limited number of qualitative studies on the 24-HMB. For this reason, it is recommended to implement qualitative research initiatives that allow for a deep exploration of contextual factors, such as the social and cultural environment that may influence behaviors, personal perspectives on behaviors, and other elements that cannot be studied using standardized methods, and that require highlighting the participant's voice.

Given the few intervention studies and as we move into a new era of understanding 24-HMB, there is a need to develop interventions that address all three components (physical activity, sedentary behavior, and sleep) in an integrated way. More experimental or intervention studies are needed to further consolidate knowledge on the effectiveness of interventions, which is a key input for translating these findings into practical actions [32].

This review evaluated the evidence for 24-HMB in largely healthy children and adolescents, and most of the studies were conducted in high-income countries. It strongly suggests that 24-HMB compliance may be an important determinant of some health outcomes such as academic performance, mental health, body composition, and HRQoL in these age groups. However, the number of studies on populations with specific conditions (Anxiety and depression, attention-deficit/hyperactivity, autism spectrum, chronic disease, cardiovascular risk, cystic fibrosis, epilepsy, neurodevelopmental disorders, and physical and sensory disabilities), and low- and middle-income countries were limited, which suggests the need to increase research on both, the 24-HMB and the adherence to 24HMB guidelines, in the aforementioned populations.

Conclusions

It can be concluded that 24-HMB reveals a field dominated by studies in high-income countries, often focusing on children and adolescents within school settings. While diverse definitions of 24-HMB exist, research predominantly employs quantitative methods, cross-sectional designs, and questionnaires, frequently analyzing compositional data and isotemporal substitution. Studies often assess compliance with 24-HMB components and explore links between behavior and health. This overall perspective of research aspects about 24-HMB in children and adolescents could inform future research to advance in the field.
