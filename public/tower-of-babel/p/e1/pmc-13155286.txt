Strain‐Driven Surface Reconstruction During the Alkaline Oxygen Evolution Reaction: a Model Thin‐Film Study
Authors: Yong Beom Kim, Jeong Jin Lee, Jeongah Lee, Yeongtaek Hong, San Kwak, WooChul Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDynamic surface reconstruction critically governs the performance and durability of oxide‐based electrocatalysts for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER), yet controlling this process under operating conditions remains challenging. Here, we demonstrate that lattice strain regulates the extent of surface reconstruction in perovskite oxides by modulating the redox behavior of lattice nickel (Ni). Using epitaxial LaNiO3 (LNO) thin films as a model system, we show that strain‐induced changes in Ni–oxygen(O) bond length systematically tune the reducibility of Ni3+, thereby controlling the degree of surface reconstruction. Tensile strain enhances Ni reducibility, promotes Ni (oxy)hydroxide formation, and results in a nearly order‐of‐magnitude increase in reconstruction compared to compressive strain. Under OER conditions in iron (Fe)‐containing alkaline electrolytes, tensile‐strained LNO exhibits a 5.7‐fold enhancement in activity due to synergistic interactions between Fe species and the reconstructed Ni‐based surface. By extending this concept to powder‐type catalysts through isovalent doping, we demonstrate that modulation of the Ni–O bond length through Scandium (Sc) doping induces comparable surface reconstruction behavior and catalyst activity, thereby confirming the scalability of this approach. These results identify metal–oxygen bond length as a general design parameter for tuning dynamic surface reconstruction and catalytic activity in perovskite oxide electrocatalysts.Keywords: Fe impurity, lattice strain, oxygen evolution reaction, perovskite oxide, surface reconstruction, transition metal

Main Text

The oxygen evolution reaction (OER) is a critical process in numerous electrochemical energy conversion and storage systems, including hydrogen production via water electrolysis [1], fuel generation through CO2 [2] or N2 [3] reduction, and metal−air batteries [4], all of which are essential technologies for realizing a sustainable circular economy. However, the slow kinetics of OER at the anode remains a major bottleneck, limiting efficiency and hindering the widespread adoption of these technologies. In this regard, various classes of non‐precious 3d transition metal (TM) oxides have demonstrated promising OER performance [5]. Several OER activity descriptors, such as the electron occupancy of the eg orbital [6], the difference between ΔGO* and ΔGHO* [7], and the oxygen 2p band center [8], have been proposed to elucidate the correlation between catalyst performance and the bulk material properties in their ideal and static states.

In practical applications, however, OER catalysts undergo surface phase transitions, resulting in the formation of active sites distinct from those of the pristine catalyst—a phenomenon widely recognized as surface reconstruction [9, 10]. Under operational conditions, these catalysts experience a wide range of electrochemical potentials, including oxidative potentials during OER, voltage fluctuations associated with the intermittency of renewable energy sources [11], reverse currents during shutdown in zero‐gap alkaline water electrolyzers [12], and cyclic redox potentials in rechargeable metal−air batteries [13]. The applied potential, in combination with the intrinsic physicochemical properties of the catalyst, plays a critical role in determining catalyst stability and directing surface reconstruction under various operating conditions [14]. Compared to controlled laboratory environments, surface reconstruction in real devices is often more diverse, making it even difficult to uncover the underlying mechanisms or predict the catalytic behavior [15]. Furthermore, achieving precise control over these dynamic surface transformations and the in‐situ generation of active species remains a critical task, which is essential for realizing high operational activity.

In parallel, contemporary research on oxide‐based electrocatalysts for aqueous environments has highlighted the critical role of lattice strain in modulating the electronic structure to enhance catalytic performance. Petrie et al. established a direct relationship between lattice strain and the weighted d‐band center of LaNiO3 (LNO), demonstrating its effect on catalytic activity for alkaline OER and ORR (oxygen reduction reaction) [16]. Similarly, Liu et al. enhanced the OER activity of Sr‐doped LaCoO3 (LSC) by controlling lattice relaxation, which modified the oxygen vacancy concentration and band structure of LSC [17]. While these approaches represent promising advancements in modulating catalytic activity, their applicability remains limited to the modification of the pristine catalyst activity. Importantly, however, OER‐active catalysts based on 3d transition metals typically undergo surface reconstruction under operating conditions, often transforming into their (oxy)hydroxide phases due to their thermodynamic stability in alkaline media. Given that surface phases play a dominant role in architecting the catalytic reaction at the electrolyte−catalyst interface, catalytic activity during operation is highly influenced by the dynamics of surface reconstruction. In perovskite oxides, lattice strain can alter the electronic structure, thereby modulating surface reconstruction. Since surface reconstruction is intimately associated with dynamic changes in the valence states of metal centers, strain‐induced perturbations of the electronic structure are expected to exert a significant influence on reconstruction behavior. Nevertheless, a comprehensive understanding of how lattice strain regulates surface reconstruction and the resulting catalytic activity under operating conditions remains lacking.

To systematically investigate the influence of lattice strain on surface reconstruction, we employed epitaxially grown LNO thin films as a representative perovskite oxide electrode for alkaline OER. LNO is a widely studied material for the alkaline OER, which exhibits near‐metallic electronic conductivity and a pseudocubic crystal structure—features that make it an ideal platform for fabricating model thin‐film electrocatalysts and precisely applying desired strains. The degree of surface reconstruction of LNO under varying strain conditions was evaluated through cyclic voltammetry (CV) analysis in purified KOH electrolytes. Among the synthesized catalysts, LNO under tensile strain demonstrated the greatest extent of reconstruction, whereas compressive strain resulted in minimal reconstruction. The magnitude of the reduction potential during cycling was identified as the critical factor governing the degree of surface transformation, whereas the oxidation potential exhibited minimal influence on the extent of reconstruction, functioning primarily as a threshold. Notably, strain‐induced modulation of LNO's electronic structure, mediated by the change in the Ni−O bond length, altered the Ni redox behavior and facilitated the precipitation of surface Ni(OH)2 via the reduction of lattice Ni3+. Moreover, experimental analyses reveal that after 100 CV cycles of OER operation in a KOH electrolyte with Fe impurities, LNO under tensile strain exhibits a 5.7‐fold increase in activity compared to its initial state. This enhancement is linked to the interaction between iFe ions and the reconstructed Ni (oxy)hydroxide surface, establishing a direct correlation between the degree of surface reconstruction and catalytic activity. Building on these observations, we further demonstrate that controlling the Ni−O bond length through isovalent doping in powder‐type catalysts can also enhance surface reconstruction and OER activity, thereby successfully extending the scope of our study to commercial powder‐type catalysts.

Thin Film Characterization

To investigate the influence of lattice strain on the surface reconstruction behavior of LNO, atomically flat, epitaxially strained LNO thin films were synthesized via pulsed laser deposition (PLD) on a series of lattice‐mismatched single‐crystal substrates. Epitaxial growth on carefully selected substrates is a well‐established approach to systematically tune in‐plane lattice strain while preserving identical chemical composition, crystallographic orientation, and growth conditions across the film series. In this configuration, the film coherently adopts the in‐plane lattice constant of the substrate, enabling precise control of biaxial strain without introducing extrinsic defects or altering film chemistry. (001)‐oriented strontium titanate (SrTiO3, STO), lanthanum strontium aluminate (LaSrAlO4, LSAO), and lanthanum aluminate (LaAlO3, LAO) were chosen to impose tensile (+1.7%), nearly relaxed, and compressive (−0.9%) strain states on LNO, respectively, based on their close lattice matching to pseudocubic LNO (a ≈ 3.838 Å). These substrates provide sufficiently distinct lattice parameters to induce controlled strain while remaining within the coherent epitaxial growth regime, thereby avoiding misfit dislocations or film cracking that can arise from larger lattice mismatches. All films were deposited under identical PLD conditions with a uniform thickness of 6.5 nm, ensuring full strain retention and minimizing variations in morphology, crystallinity, and defect density. As a result, lattice strain is isolated as the primary variable across the sample set, enabling a reliable and systematic evaluation of its effect on surface reconstruction.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis reveals that all films exhibit a pristine, atomically smooth surface morphology, devoid of secondary phase segregates typically associated with highly strained films (Figure 1a), indicating the high structural integrity of the model system. The step‐terraced surface topology, characterized by monolayer unit cell steps, demonstrates exceptional uniformity, with sub‐nanometer root‐mean‐square (Rq) roughness values below 0.2 nm, further confirming the well‐defined and precisely controlled nature of the synthesized films (see Figure S1 for line scan results). To rigorously confirm phase purity beyond the presence of Laue fringes, full‐range 2θ–ω scans were additionally collected and compared with standard reference patterns from the International Centre for Diffraction Data (ICDD) Powder Diffraction File, revealing only the expected (00l) reflections of LNO and the single‐crystal substrates without detectable secondary phases. Reciprocal space mapping (RSM) further corroborates the strain states by directly resolving the in‐plane and out‐of‐plane lattice parameters. The LNO films deposited on STO (LNO−STO) and LAO (LNO−LAO) remain coherently strained, while the LNO film on LSAO (LNO−LSAO) undergoes strain relaxation due to the significant lattice mismatch and the limited critical thickness for maintaining epitaxial strain (see Figure S5). The extracted lattice parameters from RSM (Figure 1c) reveal the strain distribution across the films: LNO−STO experiences biaxial tensile strain (+1.7%), LNO−LSAO remaining nearly strain‐free (0%), and LNO−LAO undergoing biaxial compressive strain (−0.9%). Lattice strain was calculated as the difference in lattice parameters relative to the relaxed state, with LNO deposited on LSAO serving as the reference. Atomic‐resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) confirms the epitaxial alignment and defect‐free interfaces between the LNO films and their respective substrates, with the observed trends in lattice parameter variations across different substrates closely aligning with the XRD results (Figure 1d; Figures S2 and S6). These results establish LNO thin films as a robust platform for systematically investigating the impact of lattice strain on surface reconstruction.

Strain‐Induced Surface Reconstruction

To comprehensively quantify surface reconstruction in LNO under varying degrees of strain, we employed complementary characterization approaches that distinguish between the electrochemically active fraction of the reconstructed species and the physical extent of lattice phase transformation. Specifically, CV‐based Ni redox analysis was used to probe the electrochemically active reconstructed phase, while O K‐edge X‐ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) assessed the degree of phase transformation. On this basis, the electrochemical reconstruction behavior of LNO was first investigated using a conventional three‐electrode configuration, with graphite as the counter electrode and Hg/HgO (1.0 M KOH) as the reference electrode. Thin film electrodes were fabricated by attaching the deposited LNO films to a copper wire with conductive paste and encapsulating all components with chemically resistant epoxy resin, leaving an exposed catalyst area of approximately 0.5 cm2 to the electrolyte (further details of the experimental setup are provided in the Methods section and in Figure S7). To mitigate the influence of Fe impurities on the surface reconstruction behavior and the catalytic activity during the OER, purified 0.1 M KOH was employed as the electrolyte. The purification of the electrolyte was conducted according to previously established methods, and its purity was verified through inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry (ICP–MS) (see Table S1) [18]. Surface reconstruction was triggered via CV within a potential range of 0.9−1.8 VRHE, and the reconstruction behavior was studied by monitoring the real‐time evolution of the Ni2+/3+ redox peak, which corresponds to the redox‐active surface species formed during reconstruction. The peak area was integrated and normalized with respect to the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) of each catalyst, providing a quantitative measure of the intrinsic extent of surface reconstruction per unit surface area (see Figure S8 for ECSA calculation results) [19].

Figure 2a−c presents the CV curves for the three LNO films subjected to varying degrees of lattice strain. All films exhibited a pronounced increase in the Ni2+/3+ redox peak, indicating the progressive formation of surface‐reconstructed Ni species during continuous CV cycles. The extent of surface reconstruction was determined by integrating the total charge of the Ni oxidation peak, corresponding to approximately 2−4 monolayers (ML) of the pristine LNO catalyst after 100 CV cycles, implying a significant degree of surface reconstruction under applied experimental conditions. As shown in Figure 2d, the normalized peak areas, plotted at 10‐cycle intervals, exhibit a monotonic increase in surface reconstruction with increasing strain over 100 CV cycles. LNO under tensile strain undergoes the most significant reconstruction, with its peak area after 10 CV cycles being 6.1 times larger than that of LNO under compressive strain, while relaxed LNO shows an intermediate level of reconstruction. Notably, the observed trend in surface reconstruction does not correlate with the electronic conductivity of LNO, even though higher conductivity would generally be expected to facilitate electrochemically driven reconstruction. Four‐point probe measurements (Figure S9) show that tensile‐strained LNO exhibits the highest resistivity yet the largest extent of surface reconstruction, whereas compressively strained LNO shows the lowest resistivity and minimal reconstruction, indicating that lattice strain plays a dominant role in governing the reconstruction behavior.

To further examine the extent of surface reconstruction, O K‐edge spectra of strained LNO were analyzed before and after reconstruction. Figure 2e presents the total electron yield (TEY) O K‐edge spectra of LNO before CV cycling, after CV cycling, and reference Ni(OH)2, all of which show excellent agreement with previously reported literature data [20, 21, 22]. Prior to CV cycling, the O K‐edge spectra of pristine LNO films with different strain levels were nearly identical (see Figure S10), indicating that strain does not affect the initial spectral features. After CV cycling, however, strain‐dependent differences became evident: LNO under tensile strain showed the largest change in the pre‐edge peak intensity, whereas compressively strained LNO exhibited the least change. The pre‐edge peak at ∼528 eV is a key spectral feature, directly associated with Ni 3d−O 2p hybridization of LNO, which is absent in Ni(OH)2. Because the reconstructed nickel (oxy)hydroxide surface is unstable in air, it converts to Ni(OH)2 prior to XAS measurements. Taken together, these analyses confirm that the degree of surface reconstruction can be directly assessed by the change in the pre‐edge peak intensity, revealing a trend consistent with electrochemical data: tensile strain strongly promotes surface reconstruction, while compressive strain effectively suppresses it. This conclusion is further supported by ICP–MS analysis of the electrolyte after CV cycling, which shows no detectable Ni, indicating that Ni released during reconstruction is retained on the catalyst surface rather than dissolved into the electrolyte (see Table S2). For accurate comparison, all XAS spectra were normalized to the absorption background both below the edge and at ∼570 eV [23]. To minimize substrate interference in the spectra, the film thickness was maintained above the probing depth of soft XAS (∼5 nm) [24]. This assumption was further validated by comparing TEY (probing depth ∼5 nm) and total fluorescence yield (TFY, probing depth ∼100 nm) spectra of LNO films on STO, where the absence of the characteristic STO peak at ∼530.4 eV in the TEY spectra (Figure S11) confirmed that substrate contributions were effectively excluded.

Characterization of the Reconstructed Surface

Prior to conducting a detailed analysis of the effects of strain on the surface reconstruction in LNO, the characteristics of the surface phases that emerged during the reconstruction process were first examined. Previous studies have reported that TM‐based perovskite oxides can undergo surface reconstruction, forming (oxy)hydroxide species due to the thermodynamic stability of TMs in alkaline electrolytes. To elucidate the structure of the reconstructed surface, STEM, X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and soft XAS analyses at the Ni L‐edge were performed on (i) pristine, (ii) 50 CV‐cycled, and (iii) 100 CV‐cycled LNO. HAADF‐STEM images of LNO at various reaction stages are presented in Figure 3a−c. The pristine catalyst exhibited a highly crystalline structure in both surface and bulk regions. Following CV cycling, a porous layer with a thickness ranging from 5 to 17 nm developed on the surface, progressively increasing with continued cycling, accompanied by a consumption of the initial LNO film. The integrated charge of the Ni redox peak indicated the formation of surface Ni species equivalent to 3.4 ML of LNO after 100 CV cycles, consistent with the change in film thickness observed in the STEM images shown in Figure 3c. The apparent discrepancy between the electrochemically estimated thickness (∼3.4 ML of LNO) and the reconstructed layer thickness observed by STEM (5–17 nm) arises from the fundamentally different structural characteristics of the two phases, with pristine LNO film having a dense structure and coherently ordered lattice, whereas the reconstructed surface layer exhibits a highly porous, low‐density morphology. Notably, XRD analysis confirmed that the underlying LNO film beneath the reconstructed surface largely retains its crystalline structure and the applied strain, indicating that the reconstruction is predominantly confined to the near‐surface region under measurement conditions used in this study (see Figure S12). In the STEM images of LNO films under different degrees of epitaxial strain, the strain‐dependent nature of surface reconstruction was also further evidenced by the consistent variation in the thickness of the reconstructed layer with strain across all samples, with LNO under tensile strain exhibiting the thickest reconstructed layer, whereas LNO under compressive strain showed the thinnest (Figure S13). To determine the elemental composition of the reconstructed layer, EDS line scan was conducted across a 10 nm depth of the cycled surface. The EDS spectra (Figure 3d) revealed that the reconstructed layer was primarily composed of nickel, with a near‐surface region enriched in lanthanum (La). This compositional discrepancy can be attributed to the distinct structural stability of Ni and La hydroxides during reaction, wherein the formation of the porous structure reflects repeated dissolution and redeposition processes, as further discussed later in this section [9, 25].

X‐ray‐based analytical techniques were employed to further investigate and confirm the reconstructed phase. In the O 1s XPS spectra of pristine LNO film prior to CV cycling, peaks observed at 528.3 and 531.3 eV corresponded to La−O and Ni−O bonds, respectively, in agreement with literature data [26]. Following cycling, spectral changes revealed the emergence of a peak at ∼532.5 eV, associated with OH species, indicating the formation of surface hydroxyl species [27]. Concurrently, the decrease in the La−O bonding peak supports the decomposition of LNO and the formation of new surface phases with continued cycling. Changes in the chemical states of Ni were further investigated using XPS and Ni L‐edge XAS. As shown in the Ni 2p XPS (Figure S14), CV cycling led to a noticeable change in the Ni2+/Ni3+ peak area ratio, indicating the formation of more reduced Ni species at the catalyst surface. Furthermore, Figure 3f presents the Ni L2‐edge XAS spectra collected before and after cycling, where the spectral features and the absorption edge step were distinguished through Gaussian and arctan function fittings, respectively, to enable a clearer comparison (see Figure S15 for detailed fitting results) [28]. A shift of 0.3 eV toward lower binding energy was observed, indicating that the surface Ni transitioned to a reduced state after reconstruction [29]. This suggests that the reconstructed surface phase comprises majorly of Ni2+ species. Combined with the changes observed in the XPS spectra and EDS results, these findings imply that the reconstructed surface primarily consists of Ni (oxy)hydroxide, with near‐surface La(OH)3 species. The change in the O K‐edge spectra, as discussed in the previous section, further corroborate and confirm this conclusion (Figure 2e). The formation of Ni (oxy)hydroxide, known for its high redox activity in alkaline electrolytes, also explains the Ni2+/3+ peak observed in the CV curves, which became more pronounced after cycling. In contrast, although La(OH)3 is present, La remains electrochemically inactive under the CV measurement conditions, and thus no La‐related redox features were observed. Additionally, the porous nature of the reconstructed layer can be attributed to the continuous dissolution and redeposition of Ni (oxy)hydroxide, facilitated by the activation of lattice oxygen during OER via the lattice oxygen mechanism (LOM) [30]. The resulting morphology can be better seen in the top surface SEM image in Figure 3g which reveals a morphology similar to that of Ni (oxy)hydroxide synthesized through electrodeposition, a characteristic not observed for La‐containing phases [31].

Mechanistic Insights into Surface Reconstruction

Surface reconstruction can occur through various mechanisms, including corrosion [32], lattice oxygen participation [33, 34], precipitation [35], and elemental leaching [36]. The occurrence of these phenomena is influenced by the intrinsic properties of the catalyst, the pH of the electrolyte, and the applied potentials. Depending on the specific working conditions of the catalyst, the relative importance of each factor in driving surface reconstruction may vary. Thus, it is crucial to identify the dominant factor governing surface reconstruction under specific operating conditions relevant to desired applications, such as water electrolyzers and metal‐air batteries. In this section, we conducted a systematic analysis to elucidate the effect of applied potential on the surface reconstruction behavior of LNO. CV measurements were performed while varying the range of applied potentials to determine the specific potential range in which significant surface reconstruction occurs. All measurements were conducted in purified 0.1 M KOH electrolyte to exclude possible effects from Fe impurities. To distinguish whether reducing or oxidizing potential plays a dominant role in surface reconstruction, three distinct sets of experiments were designed. First, the effect of reducing potential was examined by maintaining the upper potential limit at 1.80 VRHE (V vs. reference hydrogen electrode, RHE) while varying the lower potential limit from 0.90 to 1.30 VRHE. Second, the effect of oxidizing potential was investigated by maintaining the lower potential limit at 0.90 VRHE while adjusting the upper potential limit from 1.45 to 1.80 VRHE. Third, the extent of surface reconstruction under purely oxidizing conditions was evaluated by maintaining the potential within the range of 1.45–1.80 VRHE for 1300 consecutive CV cycles. The extent of surface reconstruction was quantified by measuring changes in the integrated oxidation peak area of Ni (Ni2+/3+) over 100 CV cycles.

The results of the initial experiment are summarized in Figure 4a, which depicts the degree of reconstruction as a function of the lower potential limit. Here, a significant increase in surface reconstruction was observed with decreasing lower potential limits, which indicates that reducing potential played a crucial role in driving surface reconstruction (see detailed CV curves in Figure S16). Since lattice Ni cations in LNO predominantly exist in the Ni3+ oxidation state, applying reducing potential below the Ni2+/3+ redox potential strongly drives the reduction of Ni3+ to Ni2+. Given the substantial difference in ionic radii between Ni2+ (69 pm) and Ni3+ (56 pm) within the octahedral site, this reduction induces pronounced local lattice distortions in LNO, destabilizing the geometric balance of the perovskite lattice and rendering Ni2+ energetically unfavorable in the bulk, thereby promoting its expulsion toward the surface. Once released into the strongly alkaline electrolyte, Ni2+ rapidly precipitates as Ni(OH)2 owing to its extremely low solubility and thermodynamic stability in this potential–pH regime, consistent with the absence of any measurable change in Ni concentration in the electrolyte after CV analysis (Table S2). This behavior is in agreement with prior study reporting Ni exsolution and Ni(OH)2/NiO precipitation in LNO at reducing potentials as low as 0.62 VRHE, where the high Ni–O covalency of LNO was shown to enable efficient charge compensation upon Ni2+ removal [35]. Furthermore, negligible reconstruction was observed when the catalyst was only cycled down to 1.3 VRHE, a potential slightly below the Ni redox potential in LNO (1.32 VRHE, determined from the midpoint of the oxidation and reduction peak apexes). This behavior was not limited to LNO–STO (LNO under tensile strain) but was also observed for the LNO–LAO catalyst (LNO under compressive strain), albeit with a lower degree of surface reconstruction than that of LNO–STO (Figure S17). These findings suggest that the reduction of lattice Ni below the Ni2+/3+ redox potential induces surface reconstruction via Ni(OH)2 precipitation, which can be further accelerated by increasing the extent of the applied reducing potential.

In contrast, the second experiment, which evaluated the effect of oxidizing potential, revealed no significant correlation between the extent of surface reconstruction and the applied upper potential limit. Figure 4b presents the degree of surface reconstruction as a function of the upper potential limit, showing no clear trend (see detailed CV curves in Figure S18). Instead, two distinct plateaus of surface reconstruction were observed, separated by a potential range between 1.55 and 1.60 VRHE, suggesting a threshold‐like behavior for significant surface reconstruction. This finding deviates from the conventional understanding that perovskite oxides with high metal−oxygen covalency undergo lattice oxygen loss during OER via the LOM, which leads to increased surface reconstruction under highly oxidizing conditions [37]. To clarify this behavior, additional experiments were conducted to verify the occurrence of LOM and its contribution to surface reconstruction. The presence of LOM on LNO was examined using pH‐controlled experiments, where a pronounced change in catalytic activity with electrolyte pH provided evidence for LOM participation (Figure S19). Based on this finding, a third experiment was subsequently performed to specifically evaluate the extent of LOM‐induced surface reconstruction under purely oxidizing potential conditions. As shown in Figure S20, CV cycling was performed between 1.45 and 1.80 VRHE for 1300 cycles. Despite the prolonged cycling, the observed change in peak area after 1300 cycles was more than four times smaller than the change recorded after 100 CV cycles in the reducing potential region (0.90–.80 VRHE). These results indicate that surface reconstruction does occur via the LOM under oxidizing conditions; however, it also underscore the relatively limited extent of this process compared to the reconstruction induced by reducing potentials. Furthermore, these findings highlight that even brief exposure to reducing potentials in practical applications—such as reverse currents, load fluctuations in electrolyzers, and redox potentials in metal‐air batteries—can exert a substantially greater influence on the surface reconstruction of OER catalysts than continuously applied oxidizing potentials. A notable aspect of the second experiment is the threshold potential observed between 1.55 and 1.60 VRHE. Given that this potential closely aligns with the OER onset (1.56 VRHE at 0.02 mA cm−2), it is plausible that structural changes associated with OER contribute to surface reconstruction. Since lattice oxygen in LNO actively participates in the OER, oxygen vacancies are generated primarily in the near‐surface region. Although these vacancies did not serve as the dominant factor driving surface reconstruction, they can perturb the local lattice environment, facilitating the extraction of Ni2+ cations and promoting surface reorganization, which may account for the observed threshold‐like behavior.

As revealed by the results, the degree of applied reducing potentials predominantly governed the extent of surface reconstruction by modulating the reduction of lattice Ni. To further elucidate the effect of lattice strain on the reconstruction behavior, we analyzed the changes in the valence states of lattice Ni as a function of lattice strain. Soft XAS at the Ni L‐edge was employed to probe changes in the valence state of Ni (Figure 4c). Due to the overlap of the La M4 and Ni L3 edges, the Ni L2 edges of LNO were analyzed under varying degrees of strain. As depicted in Figure 4d, LNO under tensile strain exhibited a red shift in the spectrum (∼ +0.2 eV), indicative of lattice Ni reduction, whereas LNO under compressive strain showed a blue shift (∼ −0.2 eV), corresponding to Ni oxidation. This behavior can be attributed to a preferential transition from Ni3+ to Ni2+ under tensile strain, where elongation of the Ni–O bond length accommodates the larger ionic radius of Ni2+, whereas compressive strain shortens the Ni–O bond length and instead favors Ni3+ with a smaller ionic radius [38]. These results establish a direct relationship between the Ni−O bond length and the Ni valence state in LNO. Furthermore, the strain‐dependent variation in Ni valence closely matches the observed surface reconstruction behavior. The reduced valence of lattice Ni of LNO under tensile strain implies the increased tendency of Ni3+ → Ni2+ transition under external reducing potentials. Since this redox transition in Ni valence (Ni3+ → Ni2+) is a primary factor for surface reconstruction, tensile strain consequently enhances reconstruction, whereas compressive strain, which increases the Ni valence, suppresses it. Overall, these results reaffirm the proposed surface reconstruction mechanism of LNO and establish a clear correlation between lattice strain and its primary driving factor, thereby rationalizing the strain‐mediated control of surface reconstruction

OER Activity of Strained LNO

Unlike the ideal conditions of purified electrolytes typically used in controlled experiments, commercially available KOH electrolytes contain significant amounts of impurity ions, some of which can substantially influence the catalyst activity. Among these impurity species, Fe(aq) has been reported to adsorb onto the catalyst surface and form dynamically stable active sites, thereby enhancing OER activity even at trace concentrations [18, 39]. Fe (oxy)hydroxide, while being one of the most active yet unstable OER active sites in alkaline electrolytes, interacts strongly with surface TM (oxy)hydroxides, leading to the formation of highly active and stable electrocatalysts [40]. Building upon this, precise control over the amount of surface TM (oxy)hydroxides presents a rational approach to modulating catalyst activity during the OER reaction, through the regulated adsorption of Fe (oxy)hydroxide species.

To explore this concept further, additional analysis was performed to investigate the correlation between strain‐induced surface reconstruction and catalyst activity. The change in catalyst activity, along with the degree of surface reconstruction, was measured using CV analysis, as illustrated in Figure 5a−c for all catalysts tested in 0.1 M KOH electrolyte containing 0.1 ppm of Fe(aq) impurities. Measurements were conducted over 100 consecutive cycles, during which both the degree of surface reconstruction and catalyst activity steadily increased with the number of CV cycles. The relationship between strain and the degree of surface reconstruction remained consistent even after the inclusion of Fe impurities, with LNO under tensile strain exhibiting the greatest degree of reconstruction. This suggests that the Fe(aq) impurities in the electrolyte did not significantly affect the trend of strain‐induced surface reconstruction but rather had a pronounced impact on catalyst activity during the reaction (Figure 5d). The similarity in OER activity during the first CV cycle between catalysts cycled in pure KOH and those cycled in KOH electrolyte containing Fe(aq) impurities indicates that the interaction between Fe(aq) and the pristine LNO surface was minimal, consistent with previous reports that the adsorption energy of Fe(aq) on perovskite oxides is low compared to (oxy)hydroxides (Figure 5e) [41]. In contrast, after 100 cycles, catalyst activity was enhanced up to 5.7‐fold for LNO under tensile strain compared to the pristine catalyst, with notable increases also observed for relaxed LNO (2.6‐fold) and LNO under compressive strain (2.0‐fold), each exhibiting varying degrees of enhancement. This substantial improvement in activity, along with its parallel behavior to the degree of surface reconstruction, strongly suggests that the catalyst activity during operation is governed by the Fe adsorbed Ni (oxy)hydroxide species formed through the surface reconstruction of LNO. For comparison, the CV curves of all films cycled in purified KOH are shown in Figure S23, where no notable enhancement in catalytic activity is observed, in clear contrast to the behavior shown in Figure 5a−c. Further investigation of the OER activity was conducted using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) in pure and Fe‐containing electrolytes after 100 CV cycles (see Figure 5f; Figure S24). In Fe‐containing electrolytes, LNO under tensile strain exhibited the smallest charge‐transfer resistance, indicating a facilitated catalytic reaction associated with its largest degree of surface reconstruction, whereas LNO under compressive strain showed the largest arc, corresponding to the lowest activity, in agreement with the LSV results. Notably, all catalysts cycled in purified electrolytes displayed substantially higher charge‐transfer resistance than those cycled in Fe‐containing electrolytes, highlighting the pronounced influence of Fe on the reconstructed catalysts. The ohmic resistance remained comparable for all samples across different strain states and electrolyte compositions. Dose‐dependent experiments were also conducted by varying the Fe impurity concentration to evaluate its influence on catalyst activity. As shown in Figure S25, a clear saturation behavior was observed, with no further enhancement in OER activity beyond an Fe concentration of 0.1 ppm. This indicates that only a trace yet adequate amount of Fe impurity is required to activate the reconstructed catalyst surface, where the low Fe requirement (∼0.1 ppm) supports the practical relevance of the observed activity enhancement also under industrially relevant operating conditions.

The adsorption of Fe(aq) on the reconstructed Ni (oxy)hydroxide, identified as the primary origin of the observed activity enhancement, was further verified by complementary STEM–EDS, ICP–MS, and Fe L‐edge XAS analyses. As shown in Figure S26, HAADF‐STEM images and the corresponding EDS elemental maps of LNO cycled in Fe‐containing electrolyte show that Fe is homogeneously distributed across the reconstructed surface layer. The strong spatial correlation between the Fe and Ni signals within the reconstructed region indicates that Fe is uniformly adsorbed on the reconstructed Ni (oxy)hydroxide phase, rather than forming isolated Fe‐rich domains. In addition, changes in the electrolyte composition were monitored by ICP–MS, which showed a slight decrease in the Fe concentration after CV cycling, further supporting the adsorption of Fe onto the reconstructed surface (Table S3). To further verify the presence and chemical state of Fe species at the catalyst surface, Fe L‐edge XAS measurements were conducted on LNO cycled in purified and Fe‐containing electrolytes. As shown in Figure S27, no detectable Fe‐related features were observed for LNO cycled in purified KOH, whereas LNO cycled in Fe‐containing electrolyte exhibited a pronounced Fe signal closely resembling that of Fe(OH)3, indicating that Fe is adsorbed in a state analogous to Fe (oxy)hydroxide. The slight red shift of the absorption edge suggests the possible coexistence of partially reduced Fe species. The adsorption of Fe also modified the chemical state of Ni within the Ni (oxy)hydroxide, indicating a homogeneous distribution of Fe and electronic interactions between Fe and the Ni (oxy)hydroxide matrix (see Figure S28).

An important observation is the opposite trend in catalyst activity between the pristine and cycled states (see Figure 5e). Specifically, LNO under compressive strain exhibited the highest activity in its pristine state but the lowest activity after cycling (Figure 5e). This discrepancy can be attributed to several factors, including the higher electronic conductivity of LNO under compressive strain and the optimized adsorption energy of reaction intermediates, which is a widely recognized key descriptor of OER activity [16]. This variation in catalyst activity between the pristine and cycled states highlights a gap between currently rationalized catalyst activities and the changes in catalyst activity under reaction conditions. In this study, our results provide new design principles for optimizing catalyst activity under operational conditions through strain engineering. Strain‐induced modulation of surface‐electrolyte interfaces offers an effective approach for tuning catalyst activity by controlling the amount of TM (oxy)hydroxide and the subsequent adsorption of Fe(aq) in the electrolyte. This method represents a novel direction for bridging the gap between OER activity descriptors and actual catalyst performance during operation.

Scalability Towards Practical Catalysts

In a technical context, applying external strain to LNO through lattice mismatch between the thin film and substrate proves impractical under typical reaction conditions. However, a more effective and scalable approach is to control the Ni−O bond length by manipulating the size mismatch between host and dopant elements through isovalent doping [42]. This approach allows for precise structural engineering and is applicable to high‐surface‐area catalysts, such as powder‐type or supported catalysts, which are more compatible with industrial applications. To increase the Ni−O bond length of LNO, Sc3+ ions (ionic radius: 75 pm) were doped at the B‐site in place of Ni3+ ions (ionic radius: 56 pm). Scandium was chosen due to its chemical and catalytic inertness under alkaline OER conditions, its slightly larger ionic radius compared to that of Ni3+, and its isovalent 3+ charge. Rietveld refinement of the XRD patterns (Figure 6a) reveals that pristine LNO crystallizes in a hexagonal structure with space group R3̅c, featuring lattice parameters a = b = 5.453(3) Å and c = 13.147(2) Å, along with a Ni−O bond length of 1.9261(7) Å, which well agrees with the reported values [43]. Upon doping 5 at% Sc at the B‐site, the lattice expands slightly, with parameters increasing to a = b = 5.463(0) Å and c = 13.197(7) Å, and the Ni−O bond length extending to 1.9406(0) Å. SEM images of LNO and Sc‐doped LNO (Sc−LNO) shown in Figure 6b reveal similar morphologies, suggesting that Sc incorporation does not significantly alter the surface structure. To assess the effect of isovalent doping on surface reconstruction and OER activity, both undoped LNO and Sc−LNO were subjected to 100 CV cycles in 0.1 M KOH containing 0.1 ppm Fe(aq). The synthesized Sc–LNO catalyst was first evaluated by cycling in both purified and Fe‐containing electrolytes to examine its electrochemical reconstruction behavior. Under both conditions, Sc–LNO exhibited continuous surface reconstruction, as evidenced by the progressive increase in the Ni2+/3+ redox peak. Notably, while the OER activity gradually decays upon cycling in purified KOH, it is enhanced in the presence of trace Fe impurities, highlighting the critical role of Fe adsorption in activating the reconstructed Ni (oxy)hydroxide surface (see Figures S29 and S30). To enable a clear comparison of surface reconstruction and catalytic activity between LNO and Sc–LNO, independent of variations in the pristine surface area, both the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA, estimated from the double‐layer capacitance (Cdl)) and the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area were employed for normalization (see Figure S31 for the Cdl determination procedure). Because ECSA and BET probe complementary aspects of electrochemically accessible and total physical surface area, respectively, the use of both approaches allows a more robust evaluation of surface reconstruction and catalytic behavior. Figure 6c presents the CV curves before and after cycling, normalized to the ECSA, where the larger Ni2+/3+ oxidation peak area observed for Sc−LNO compared to LNO indicates a greater degree of surface reconstruction facilitated by the elongated Ni−O bond length (see detailed CV curves in Figure S29). Moreover, the OER activity of the catalysts exhibited a consistent trend, with Sc−LNO showing a 39% increase in current density compared to LNO after 100 cycles of electrochemical cycling. Given that both pristine LNO and Sc−LNO before CV cycling exhibited comparable OER activity, the enhanced performance of Sc−LNO after cycling is likely attributable to its greater degree of surface reconstruction, consistent with the observations discussed earlier. To exclude possible effects of Sc leaching on surface reconstruction during CV cycling, ICP measurements were performed, which detected no measurable Sc in the electrolyte (Table S4). The progressive enhancement with continued cycling is further demonstrated in Figure 6d and Figure S32, where both the degree of surface reconstruction and the OER current density of Sc−LNO consistently exceed those of undoped LNO over successive cycles. To elucidate the origin of this behavior, the effect of Sc doping on the Ni valence state was examined using XPS. As shown in Figure S33, a clear reduction in the average Ni valence state was observed upon Sc doping, analogous to the behavior of tensile‐strained LNO films, where elongation of the Ni–O bond length stabilizes reduced lattice Ni. Taken together, these results indicate that isovalent Sc doping effectively modulates the lattice Ni valence by increasing the Ni–O bond length in a manner functionally equivalent to tensile strain, thereby promoting surface reconstruction and enhancing catalytic activity. Notably, despite the dilution of active Ni sites in the B‐site lattice due to Sc doping, the catalytic performance was enhanced, reaffirming the central role of Ni−O bond length in governing surface reconstruction behavior and OER activity. These findings validate our design strategy and establish Sc−LNO as a new class of catalyst that leverages bond‐length engineering to surpass pristine LNO, demonstrating the scalable applicability of our approach to enhance industrial electrocatalyst performance.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrate for the first time the systematic modulation of catalyst surface structure through lattice strain engineering. Through controlled experiments, we show that the primary factor governing surface reconstruction of LNO is the reduction of lattice Ni, followed by the precipitation of Ni(OH)2. By precisely tuning lattice expansion and compression, the resulting changes in Ni−O bond lengths modulate the redox behavior of transition‐metal centers, which in turn directly determines the extent of surface evolution under operational conditions. Importantly, the observed correlation between the degree of reconstruction and catalytic activity highlights a broader principle in OER catalyst design, illustrating how strain can be used to tailor the surface reactivity under operating conditions. Extending this concept beyond epitaxial thin films, we further demonstrate its applicability to powder‐type catalysts via isovalent doping, confirming both scalability and practical relevance. Overall, these findings provide a general framework for designing high‐performance electrocatalysts through controlled surface reconstruction, thereby paving the way for rational development of catalysts for sustainable energy conversion.

Methods

Chemicals

Semiconductor‐grade potassium hydroxide (KOH, Sigma Aldrich, 99.99% purity) was utilized with further purification as detailed in the subsequent section. All precursor materials employed in this study, including lanthanum nitrate hexahydrate (La(NO3) 3·6H2O, Sigma Aldrich, 99.999% purity), nickel nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, Sigma Aldrich, 99.999% purity), iron nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, Sigma Aldrich, 99.999% purity), scandium nitrate hydrate (Sc(NO3)3·xH2O, Thermo Fisher, 99.99% purity) were used without further purification. Citric acid (JUNSEI, guaranteed reagent), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, JUNSEI, guaranteed reagent), and ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH, JUNSEI, guaranteed reagent) were employed in the EDTA–citrate complexation method for powder catalyst and PLD target synthesis. Ultrapure deionized (DI) water (18.2 MΩ·cm) was used for the preparation of electrolytes.

Preparation of Thin Films

Single‐crystalline epitaxial LaNiO3 thin films were deposited via PLD using an oxide target on single‐crystalline SrTiO3 (001), LaSrAlO4 (001), and LaAlO3 (001) substrates (CrysTec, Germany) with dimensions of 10 × 10 × 0.5 mm3. The LaNiO3 target was synthesized via the EDTA–citrate complexation method using La(NO3)3·6H2O and Ni(NO3)2·6H2O precursors. The resulting powder was initially calcined at 800°C for 5 h to achieve phase formation, followed by uniaxial pressing into the desired shape and subsequent sintering at 1200°C for 6 h to finalize target formation. PLD was performed using a coherent COMPex Pro 205 KrF excimer laser (wavelength: 248 nm) under deposition conditions of a pulsed laser energy of 300 mJ and a repetition rate of 1 Hz. During deposition, the substrate temperature was maintained at 700°C, and the oxygen working pressure was set to 35 mTorr. Post‐deposition, all samples were annealed at 700°C in 1 Torr of O2 for 20 min to ensure complete oxidation.

Synthesis of Undoped and Sc‐Doped LaNiO3

Undoped and Sc‐doped LaNiO3 catalysts were synthesized using the EDTA‐citrate complexation method with La(NO3)3·6H2O, Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, and Sc(NO3)3·xH2O as precursors. Stoichiometric amounts of these precursors were dissolved to achieve a total cation concentration of 4 mmol. Citric acid and EDTA were added for cation chelation, and NH4OH was used to adjust the solution pH to 9. The resulting sol was stirred at 180°C to induce gelation, followed by pre‐calcination at 450°C for 3 h to facilitate organic combustion. The obtained powder was then calcined at 800°C for 10 h in air to achieve phase formation.

Electrolyte Purification and Preparation

Electrolyte purification was conducted following established protocols [44]. Specifically, 2 g of Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (99.999% purity) was dissolved in 4 mL of 18.2 MΩ·cm DI water within a 50 mL centrifuge tube, and high‐purity Ni(OH)2 was precipitated via the addition of 20 mL of 1.0 M semiconductor‐grade KOH. The resulting green Ni(OH)2 precipitate was washed twice using a combination of 20 mL DI water and 2 mL of 1.0 M KOH, followed by centrifugation at 4000 rpm. After the final wash, the supernatant was removed, and the tube was refilled with semiconductor‐grade 1.0 M KOH. The tube was vigorously shaken to redisperse the precipitate and allowed to rest overnight, facilitating the adsorption of Fe impurities onto Ni(OH)2. The supernatant was collected by filtration through a 0.2 µm polyethersulfone (PES) syringe filter to eliminate any residual Ni(OH)2. A 0.1 M KOH solution was prepared by diluting purified 1.0 M KOH with 18.2 MΩ·cm DI water to attain a final pH of 12.9. Electrolytes containing 0.1 ppm Fe(aq) were prepared by introducing precise amounts of Fe(NO3)3·9H2O into the purified 0.1 M KOH solution.

Electrochemical Measurements

Thin film electrodes were prepared by connecting the film to a current collector wire using Ag paste (Elcoat). To prevent contamination from the Ag paste and wire components, the electrode was hermetically sealed with epoxy resin (Loctite, EA 9460), leaving only the desired area of the thin film catalyst exposed to the electrolyte. The active surface area of the catalyst was determined via both optical imaging and ECSA measurements. For rotating disk electrode (RDE) experiments, electrodes were fabricated by drop‐casting catalyst ink onto a 3 mm glassy carbon electrode. The ink was prepared by dispersing 5 mg of catalyst powder, 2 mg of conductive carbon (Vulcan XC 72), 50 µL of Nafion, and 500 µL of ethanol. A 3 µL aliquot was deposited onto the electrode to achieve a catalyst loading of 0.425 mg cm−2. Electrochemical measurements were conducted using a potentiostat (Bio‐Logic VMP‐300 and VSP‐300) in a three‐electrode configuration with a Hg/HgO reference electrode (1.0 M KOH) and a high‐purity graphite rod (99.99995%) as the counter electrode. To minimize contamination, all components were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water before each experiment. Graphite rods, electrochemical cells, and Teflon caps were additionally treated with dilute HCl to remove residual contaminants. All potentials were iR‐corrected, with resistance values determined via electrochemical EIS. Potentials were referenced to the reversible hydrogen electrode using the equation: ERHE = EHg/HgO + E°Hg/HgO + 0.059 × pH, where EHg/HgO is the recorded potential vs. Hg/HgO and E°Hg/HgO is 0.098 V (for 1.0 M KOH at 25°C). Electrolyte solutions were purged with N2 for 30 min before measurements. CV was performed within a potential range of 0.9 to 1.8 VRHE at a scan rate of 10 mV s−1 to induce surface reconstruction. ECSA was estimated via double‐layer capacitance measurements in an N2‐saturated 0.1 M KOH solution within a non‐Faradaic potential window (±50 mV around the open‐circuit potential) at scan rates of 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 mV s−1. For powder samples, a specific capacitance of 40 µF cm−2 was assumed for the flat electrode surface [45].

X‐Ray Diffraction (XRD) and Reciprocal Space Mapping (RSM)

Lattice constants in both the in‐plane and out‐of‐plane directions were determined via 2θ‐ω scans and RSM using high‐resolution thin‐film XRD (SmartLab, RIGAKU) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm). The system was equipped with a high‐precision goniometer (θ/θ geometry, 300 mm in radius) and a Eulerian cradle (chi, Z‐axis, phi) with an in‐plane arm (5‐axis goniometer) and a Ge (220) 2‐bounce monochromator. XRD patterns of powder type catalysts were collected using a X‐ray powder diffractometer (D8‐Advance, BRUKER) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å, 40 mA, and 40 kV). Samples were measured in the 2θ range of 10–120° using a step size of 0.01° with a scan rate of 0.2° per minute.

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)

Surface morphology was analyzed using AFM (NX10, Park Systems) equipped with an acoustic enclosure to minimize acoustic noise and an active vibration isolation system to reduce floor vibrations. Non‐contact mode imaging was performed using an SSS‐NCHR cantilever over a 2 × 2 µm2 area, with a default set point force, a scan rate of 0.5 Hz, and a resolution of 256 × 256 pixels.

Inductively Coupled Plasma‐Mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS)

The purity of the electrolytes used for all measurements was analyzed by ICP‐MS (ICP‐MS 7700S, Agilent) prior to use. Baseline correction was performed before each measurement, followed by calibration using reference sample with concentrations of 200, 500, and 1000 ppb. The detection limit was approximately on the order of 0.1 ppb for 3d transition metals. (see detailed calibration results in Table S1)

X‐Ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)

Surface chemical states were characterized using XPS (NEXSA G2, Thermo Scientific) with an Al Kα X‐ray source (1486.6 eV) under ultra‐high vacuum conditions (∼10−9 torr). An X‐ray spot size of 400 µm was used. To mitigate charge buildup, a dual‐beam low‐energy electron/Ar ion flood gun was employed. Each spectrum was obtained by averaging three consecutive scans.

X‐Ray Absorption Spectroscopy (XAS)

XAS was performed using a soft X‐ray source with a photon energy range of 35−1200 eV at the 10D‐XAS‐KIST and 10D‐HRPES I beamlines at the Pohang Accelerator Laboratory (PLS‐II). Absorption spectra were acquired for the La M, Ni L, and O K‐edges in both TEY and FEY modes with normal incidence and a beam size of 1000 × 2000 µm2. Each spectrum was normalized to the incident photon flux.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

The morphology of powder catalysts was examined using SEM (JSM‐7600F, JEOL) at an acceleration voltage of 3 kV and a probe current of 93.2 nA, achieving a resolution of approximately 1.5 nm.

Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy (STEM)

Cross‐sectional lamellae of LNO−STO, LNO−LSAO, and LNO−LAO samples (∼40 nm thick) were prepared using a focused ion beam (FIB) (Helios 5 UX, Thermo Fisher). HAADF‐STEM imaging and STEM‐based EDS mapping were performed using a double‐Cs‐corrected transmission electron microscope (Spectra Ultra, Thermo Fisher) operating at 300 kV.

Sheet Resistance Measurement

The sheet resistance of the deposited films was measured using a four‐point probe station (CMT‐SR2000N, AIT) with a probe spacing of 1 mm. The values reported correspond to the average of five measurements acquired along different in‐plane directions of the film.

Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET)

The BET surface area of the powder catalysts was measured using N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms at 77 K (TriStar II 3020, Micromeritics). Samples were degassed under vacuum at 200°C for 2 h prior to measurement and BET surface areas were calculated from the adsorption data in the P/P0 range of 0.005–0.30.
