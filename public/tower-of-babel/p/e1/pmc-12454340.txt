Development of a cognitive function assessment tool based on psychomotor function tests in patients with schizophrenia
Authors: Yuma Shimizu, Ippei Takeuchi, Masakazu Hatano, Manako Hanya, Kiyoshi Fujita, Nakao Iwata, Hiroyuki Kamei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive dysfunction has a significant impact on social functioning, such as employment, in patients with schizophrenia. However, existing cognitive assessments are time-consuming, impose a significant burden on patients, and require specialized training for evaluators, making them impractical for routine clinical use. Therefore, the present study investigated whether a simple and novel assessment tool, called Psychomotor Function Tests (PFT), correlates with existing Neuropsychological Tests (NT) and assessments with the Life Assessment Scale for the Mentally Ill (LASMI), which evaluates social functioning, including employment.MethodsCognitive function was examined in 24 patients with schizophrenia using NT (the Japanese Adult Reading Test, Trail Making Test (TMT), and word fluency test) and tablet-based PFT, while social functioning was evaluated using LASMI. Twenty-four healthy controls (HCs) underwent the same cognitive assessments.ResultsPsychomotor function, as evaluated by the choice reaction time, compensatory tracking test, and rapid visual information processing, was significantly worse in patients with schizophrenia than in HCs (p < 0.001). Furthermore, the composite score of PFT correlated with the time required for TMT (r = −0.707, −0.637) and LASMI subscales related to work, endurance & stability, self-recognition, required skills, and retention skills (r = −0.640, −0.689, −0.634, −0.420, −0.548).ConclusionPFT correlated with existing NT, which are widely used in cognitive function assessments. Cognitive function examined by PFT was closely associated with social functioning. These results suggest the potential of PFT for evaluating cognitive function in routine clinical settings for patients with schizophrenia.

Introduction

In addition to positive and negative symptoms, cognitive impairments, such as decreased attention and memory, are observed in approximately 80 % of patients with schizophrenia (Reichenberg et al., 2006). Cognitive dysfunction in patients with schizophrenia is characterized by more pronounced impairments in attention, executive function, working memory, and processing speed than in other psychiatric disorders, resulting in significantly reduced cognitive performance than that in healthy individuals (Keefe et al., 2004; Reichenberg et al., 2009). Cognitive function is associated with social functioning, including daily living skills and employment (Green et al., 2009; Evans et al., 2003). More than 80 % of patients with schizophrenia are unemployed, which is a significantly higher percentage than in patients with other psychiatric disorders (Hakulinen et al., 2019). Therefore, improving cognitive functions that hinder social improvement, such as employment, is critically important for patients with schizophrenia.

Atypical antipsychotics have been reported to improve cognitive function in patients with schizophrenia (Baldez et al., 2021). Furthermore, clozapine, the only atypical antipsychotic approved for treatment-resistant schizophrenia, improves psychomotor speed as assessed by the Digit Symbol Substitution Test and Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale-Revised, verbal fluency as evaluated through category fluency and letter fluency tasks, and verbal learning and memory as measured by the Wechsler Memory Scale-Revised, in patients with schizophrenia (Goldberg et al., 1988). On the other hand, a network meta-analysis showed that executive function and verbal learning were worse in patients with schizophrenia taking clozapine than in those receiving other antipsychotics (Baldez et al., 2021). Therefore, treatment strategies for cognitive impairment in patients with schizophrenia have yet to be fully established.

To improve cognitive impairment in patients with schizophrenia, it is essential to assess cognitive function objectively and easily in routine clinical practice. The Brief Assessment of Cognition in Schizophrenia (BACS) is one of the most frequently used cognitive assessment tools (Keefe et al., 2004). BACS comprehensively assess impairments in cognitive domains, such as verbal memory, working memory, motor speed, verbal fluency, attention, and executive functions, in patients with schizophrenia. Additionally, a Japanese version, known as BACS-J, has been developed, and its reliability and validity have been well-documented (Kaneda et al., 2007). However, BACS-J requires more than 60 min to complete, posing a heavy burden on both patients and evaluators. Moreover, its use is restricted to evaluators with specialized training, making it challenging to implement in routine clinical practice (Haddad et al., 2022). Therefore, the development of a simple and objective cognitive assessment method that is suitable for long-term use in psychiatric routine care in an urgent priority.

We previously reported the potential of Psychomotor Function Tests (PFT) using dedicated equipment as a novel cognitive assessment method for patients with schizophrenia, enabling the evaluation of higher-order cognitive function, such as attention as well as working memory, which affect social functioning, in approximately 20 min (Kamei et al., 2021).

In the present study, we developed a tablet-based version of PFT to improve their applicability to routine clinical practice and evaluated its use for assessing cognitive function in patients with schizophrenia. We also examined the relationship between cognitive assessments conducted using PFT and the Life Assessment Scale for the Mentally Ill (LASMI) (Iwasaki et al., 1994), which is used to evaluate social functioning, including employment.

Methods

Subjects

The present study included 24 outpatients aged 20 years or older who were diagnosed with based on the DSM-IV criteria for schizophrenia at Fujita Health University Hospital and Okehazama Hospital between July 2023 and July 2024 and who met the following criteria: 1) no comorbidities affecting psychomotor function other than schizophrenia, 2) no history of drug or substance abuse, and 3) no sleep/wake cycle disturbances affecting psychomotor function. Participants were clinically stable in terms of no changes in medication types or dosages within 1 month of testing.

Twenty-four healthy controls (HCs) aged 20 years or older who met the following criteria were also enrolled: 1) a Mini-Mental State Examination score ≥ 24 (Folstein et al., 1975; Sakurai et al., 2021), 2) a Full Scale Intelligence Quotient (FSIQ) score ≥ 85 as assessed by the Japanese Adult Reading Test (JART) (Matsuoka et al., 2006; Braaten and Norman, 2006), 3) no history of diseases affecting psychomotor function, 4) no use of medications that affect psychomotor function, 5) no history of drug or substance abuse, and 6) no sleep/wake cycle disturbances affecting psychomotor function. Participants provided their written informed consent. This study was approved by the Ethics Committees of the participating institutions [ethic approval numbers: 2022-31-1 (Meijo University), EN23-014 (Fujita Health University), R05-02 (Okehazama Hospital)] and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study procedures

Assessment procedures

We administered PFT using a tablet device (Nonoi Co., Ltd.), as well as Neuropsychological Tests (NT) combining JART (Matsuoka et al., 2006), the Trail Making Test (TMT) (Japan Society for Higher Brain Dysfunction and Brain Function Test Committee, 2019), and Word Fluency Test (WFT) (Saito et al., 1992). We used the Clinical Global Impressions (CGI) scale (Busner and Targum, 2007) to examine the severity of schizophrenia, the Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) to evaluate social, psychological, and occupational functions as well as (Aas, 2011), and LASMI to assess social functioning, including employment (Iwasaki et al., 1994).

We used the Drug-Induced Extrapyramidal Symptoms Scale (DIEPSS) (Inada, 1996) immediately before PFT to investigate the potential impact of drug-induced extrapyramidal symptoms on the results of PFT.

PFT and DIEPSS were conducted by pharmacists, and NT (JART, TMT, and WFT), CGI, and GAF by physicians. LASMI was performed by physicians or pharmacists based on interviews with the patient's family or other caregivers (Iwasaki et al., 1994).

PFT, JART, and WFT were conducted by the patient and HC groups. TMT, for which standardized HCs data are available (Japan Society for Higher Brain Dysfunction and Brain Function Test Committee, 2019), as well as DIEPSS, CGI, GAF, and LASMI were performed exclusively by the patient group.

Assessment scales

Psychomotor Function Tests (PFT)

A combination of the following four tasks was used as a test battery to evaluate psychomotor function (Kamei et al., 2003, Kamei et al., 2012, Kamei et al., 2021).

Critical Flicker Fusion (CFF)

CFF evaluates the integrative capacity of the central nervous system, specifically sensory ability to discern the intermittent flicking of light (Kamei et al., 2003, Kamei et al., 2012). A lower threshold indicates a decrease in the overall activity of the central nervous system. The test set up on the tablet screen consisted of four red lights arranged in a 1-cm square. Lights were flicked on and off at a constantly increasing or decreasing rate. Subjects were required to discriminate flicker from fusion, and vice versa. The CFF threshold (Hz) for each subject was selected by averaging eight measurements: four ascending (flicker to fusion) and four descending (fusion to flicker) measurements.

Choice Reaction Time (CRT)

CRT is used as an indicator of sensorimotor ability, and assesses the ability to focus on and respond to a visual stimulus (Kamei et al., 2003, Kamei et al., 2012, Kamei et al., 2021). In CRT, a higher score indicates a decline in sensorimotor function. The tablet screen consisted of a start button in the middle, as well as 6 reaction buttons aligned in a fan-shape at equal intervals. Each reaction button had red and green lights that were also aligned in a fan-shape, and the green light turned on prior to the red light. After placing the index finger onto the start button, subjects were required to use the green light as a warning to react quickly when the red light turned on. Subjects were required to press the reaction button next to the red light as soon as it lit up in order to turn it off. The number of warning green lights gradually increased (1, 3, and 6 lights in this order) and decreased (6, 3, and then 1 light). The time between the red light coming on and the finger pushing the action button was taken as CRT.

Compensatory Tracking Test (CTT)

CTT consists of the following dual tasks: a tracking task (TT) and the peripheral awareness task (PAT), which subjects were required to perform simultaneously.

Tracking Task (TT)

TT assesses the ability to react to and track an object (Kamei et al., 2003, Kamei et al., 2012, Kamei et al., 2021). In TT, a higher score indicates an impaired ability to accurately track an object. Subjects were required to track a round object that appeared on the tablet screen. The object moved along a straight line in a random manner, and subjects were asked to place the cursor in the middle of the object using a wireless mouse and track its movement. The distance between the center of the object and the cursor was measured in pixels, and performance was assessed as the mean distance between the two points for samples randomly selected during the 9-min test.

Peripheral Awareness Task (PAT)

PAT was performed at the same time as TT, ensuring that it did not interfere with TT (Kamei et al., 2003, Kamei et al., 2012, Kamei et al., 2021). A higher score in PAT indicates an impaired ability to divide attention. PAT assesses peripheral awareness and lasts for 9 min, similar to TT. In PAT, 50 stimuli (yellow objects) randomly appeared at the 4 corners of the tablet screen, and subjects were required to push the reaction button as soon as they saw the yellow object while performing TT. The average time interval between the appearance of the object and pushing the reaction button was calculated in msec.

Rapid Visual Information Processing (RVIP)

RVIP was used as a means to assess working memory, sustained attention, and concentration on cyclical events (Kamei et al., 2003, Kamei et al., 2012). A higher score in RVIP, indicates better sustained attention and concentration. Subjects were required to monitor a series of single digits (0–9) appearing on the screen at a rate of 100 digits every minute and respond to consecutive sequences of three odd or even digits using a wireless mouse button during the 9-min test period. The assessment measured the rate of correct responses.

Neuropsychological Tests (NT)

The following three NT, which are routinely used in daily clinical practice, were conducted.

Japanese Adult Reading Test (JART)

JART is an intellectual assessment test adapted into Japanese from the National Adult Reading Test, which was originally developed in the UK (Matsuoka et al., 2006). In this study, JART-25 was used to estimate FSIQ, verbal IQ (VIQ), and performance IQ (PIQ).

Trail Making Test (TMT)

TMT consists of two parts, Part A (TMT-A) and Part B (TMT-B), allowing for a comprehensive assessment of cognitive functions, such as a wide range of attention, working memory, spatial exploratory, and processing speed (Japan Society for Higher Brain Dysfunction and Brain Function Test Committee, 2019). In TMT, the completion time (s) was assessed.

Word Fluency Test (WFT)

In WFT, subjects were required to verbally generate as many nouns as possible within a 1-min time limit for each of the three specified initial letters (Saito et al., 1992). The total number generated across the three initial letters was evaluated as verbal fluency. Additionally, the repeated generation of the same nouns was recorded as preservative retention errors.

Clinical Global Impressions (CGI)

The severity of schizophrenia was evaluated by CGI-Severity (CGI-S), ranging from 1 (normal) to 7 (very severe), with a higher score indicating more severe psychiatric symptoms (Busner and Targum, 2007).

Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF)

GAF is a numerical assessed on a scale ranging from 1 to 100 comprising two components: the severity of psychiatric symptoms and the level of functioning in social and occupational contexts, with higher scores indicating better psychological well-being (Aas, 2011).

Drug-Induced Extrapyramidal Symptoms Scale (DIEPSS)

DIEPSS assesses the severity of drug-induced extrapyramidal symptoms, which may occur during antipsychotic treatment based on the results of PFT. DIPESS consists of 8 items and one global assessment, each rated on a 5-point scale, with higher scores indicating severe extrapyramidal symptoms (Inada, 1996).

Life Assessment Scale for the Mentally Ill (LASMI)

LASMI was developed to comprehensively measure life disability in social life in patients with mental illness. It consists of 40 items, divided into the following five categories: daily living, interpersonal relations, work, endurance & stability, and self-recognition. Each item is rated on a 5-point scale, with lower scores indicating better social life functioning. The score of each category was calculated as the average of the items within that category (Iwasaki et al., 1994). Endurance & stability was assessed based on the LASMI manual with E-1 rated on a 6-point scale, and E-2 rated on a 7-point scale. The average score of the two items was used as the score of this category.

By extracting only the necessary items from LASMI, it is possible to assess employment prognosis as “employment skills” (Iwasaki et al., 1998). In the analysis, nine items that patients need to acquire when aiming for employment transition were defined as “required skills”, thirteen items that need to be improved to a certain extent when aiming for employment transition were defined as “partially required skills”, and nine items that need to be acquired when aiming for employment retention were defined as “retention skills”.

Statistical analysis

Using the statistical analysis software IBM SPSS ver. 29.0 (IBM Japan, Tokyo), Spearman's rank correlation coefficient test was performed to examine the relationships between assessment scales. In addition, the χ2 test was used to compare patient background factors between groups as necessary, and the Mann-Whitney U test was employed to compare between groups. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

The degree of cognitive function in each patient was evaluated by calculating the Z-score (Curtis et al., 2016) from the data obtained from the HCs group. Z-scores were calculated to indicate a reduced ability when the Z-score decreased. In PFT, the average of the five Z-scores calculated from each task was assessed as a composite score. In the present study, the sample size was calculated to be approximately 23 cases, assuming Spearman's correlation coefficient of 0.6 (Akoglu, 2018).

Additionally, 15 patients refused to participate in this study. Discussion

In the present study, correlations were observed between PFT, which we developed, and existing NT, particularly the TMT. We investigated the clinical utility of the PFT by confirming its concurrent and criterion-related validities. Furthermore, cognitive function assessed by PFT correlated with social functioning evaluated by LASMI.

Patients with schizophrenia often have impaired cognitive function, including working memory, attention and alertness, reasoning and problem solving, and information processing, as well as diminished social functioning. In the present study, significant differences were observed in PFT scores, such as CRT, CTT, and RVIP, between patients and HCs.

CRT is used to evaluate sensorimotor ability, with higher CRT scores indicating a decline in attention. The prolonged CRT response time observed in patients compared to HCs suggests impaired selective attention (Sohlberg and Matter, 1987; Gold et al., 2018). This impairment manifests as difficulty in focusing solely on the red target lamp amid multiple green cue lamps (Kamei et al., 2003, Kamei et al., 2012, Kamei et al., 2021).

Tracking response ability (TT) and sustained attention (PAT) were simultaneously evaluated in CTT, and patients showed significantly higher TT and PAT scores than HCs, suggesting a decline in divided attention (Kamei et al., 2003, Kamei et al., 2012). Previous studies reported that divided attention in a dual task situation was significantly worse in patients with schizophrenia than in HCs (Gray et al., 2014). Since CTT involves performing two tasks at the same time, it is suggested to be related to divided attention, where attention must be directed to multiple stimuli, such as a circular target moving irregularly and yellow lamps appearing at corners (Kamei et al., 2003, Kamei et al., 2012, Kamei et al., 2021). In the present study, TT scores were significantly higher in patients than in HCs, suggesting impaired sustained attention. Sustained attention has been identified as an indicator of cognitive impairment (Liu et al., 2002) and has been associated with social cognition (Ruiz-Toca et al., 2023).

RVIP requires subjects to focus on identifying three odd or even numbers in a row among randomly appearing numbers (Kamei et al., 2003, Kamei et al., 2012). In the present study, patients with schizophrenia had significantly lower scores than HCs, indicating a potential decline in working memory (Goldberg et al., 1988; Weinberger et al., 1986).

Among several scales of cognitive function, NT are commonly used worldwide. Therefore, we examined the relationship between PFT and NT.

A meta-analysis of TMT completion times reported that patients with schizophrenia had significantly longer TMT-A and TMT-B than HCs (Laere et al., 2018). In the present study, TMT-A and TMT-B classified 70.8 and 58.3 % of patients, respectively, as “borderline” or “abnormal”. Previous studies indicated that 75–85 % of patients with schizophrenia showed cognitive impairments (Reichenberg et al., 2006), and also that TMT alone was insufficient to detect cognitive dysfunction.

In the present study, correlations were observed between PFT tasks and both TMT-A and TMT-B. TMT has been shown to sensitively detect declines in psychomotor functions in patients with schizophrenia (Laere et al., 2018). TMT may be used to assess a wide range of cognitive functions (Tombaugh, 2004), including visual searching, scanning, processing speed, mental flexibility, and executive function. TMT-A primally evaluates visual searching and motor speed skills, while TMT-B mainly assesses mental flexibility (McGurk and Meltzer, 2000). This may be the reason for the correlation observed between PFT and TMT in the present study.

In TMT, the preparation of materials required for the evaluation is cumbersome and complex (Bowie and Harvey, 2006). On the other hand, PFT is relatively simple, requiring only a tablet and computer mouse. It also does not require special training, which reduces the burden on the evaluator. Furthermore, because PFT uses a tablet, it may be performed in a game-like manner, which may help alleviate the mental burden on a patient. Additionally, correlation coefficients between most LASMI scores and PFT scores, except for interpersonal relations, were higher than those between LASMI scores and the TMT time required. Therefore, PFT may facilitate the assessment of cognitive function in patients with schizophrenia.

A previous study reported a correlation between the work subscale score in LASMI and the symbol coding task score in BACS-J for attention (Watanabe et al., 2023). In the present study, the LASMI-work score correlated with Z-scores in PFT requiring attention, such as CRT, CTT-TT, CTT-PAT, and RVIP.

Furthermore, regarding “employment skills”, CRT, CTT-TT, CTT-PAT, and RVIP correlated with required skills and partially required skills. RVIP correlated with all subscales of employment skills. RVIP reflects sustained attention and working memory (Kamei et al., 2003, Kamei et al., 2012), and these cognitive functions are closely related to employment (Tan, 2009). Therefore, PFT may be a comprehensive cognitive assessment tool that broadly reflects social functioning, including employment prognosis, in patients with schizophrenia.

In the present study, unemployed patients showed declines in sustained attention and working memory, which is consistent with previous findings (McGurk and Meltzer, 2000).

Regarding the effects of medications on cognitive function, we demonstrated that patients taking clozapine had significantly higher Z-scores for CRT and RVIP than those not taking clozapine. Previous studies suggested that clozapine improved psychomotor speed, verbal fluency, verbal learning, and memory (McGurk, 1999), which is consistent with the present results. Therefore, clozapine may have a positive impact on cognitive function in patients with schizophrenia.

The present study has several limitations that need to be considered. Age and disease duration in patients correlated with the Z-scores of PFT, which may have affected the results of PFT. The correlation coefficient for age ranged between 0.6 and 0.7 (Moderate) (Akoglu, 2018), and a previous study also indicated that age was a factor affecting assessments of cognitive function (Mosiołek et al., 2016). Therefore, the potential impact of these factors need to be carefully considered. On the other hand, since there was no correlation between PFT scores and years of education, it is likely that the effects of education history on the results obtained were minimal. In the present study, PFT scores did not correlate with motor dysfunction, such as extrapyramidal symptoms evaluated by DIEPSS. On the other hand, some motor impairments may not have been detected by DIEPSS, and since PFT may reflect motor function rather than cognitive function, these results need to be interpreted with caution. In PFT, RVIP has been shown to reflect working memory and sustained attention (Coull et al., 1996; Kamei et al., 2003, Kamei et al., 2012). However, the cognitive domains evaluated by NT in the present study were limited and, thus, it is unclear whether PFT evaluates all cognitive domains. Further studies are needed that include motor function and key areas of cognitive function, such as executive function and working memory. Another limitation is that in comparisons between the clozapine and non-clozapine groups, the sample size was limited because it was a secondary evaluation. Additionally, the clozapine group was significantly younger and had higher DIEPSS scores than the non-clozapine group. Although no relationship was observed between DIPESS scores and PFT scores, age correlated with PFT scores, which suggests that the interpretation of cognitive function in the clozapine group may be affected by these factors. In the present study, we developed a cognitive function assessment tool that reflects social functioning, such as employment, and the effects of medications on cognitive function were examined. PFT is considered to be complementary to traditional cognitive assessments of cognitive impairments in patients with schizophrenia.

Conclusion

The novel PFT using a tablet device in patients with schizophrenia correlated with neuropsychological assessments, suggesting that it is a useful cognitive function assessment tool that reflects social functioning, including employment.
