Assessment of Bryophyllum pinnatum mediated Ag and ZnO nanoparticles as efficient antimicrobial and cytotoxic agent
Authors: Huma Noor, Asma Ayub, Erum Dilshad, Tayyaba afsar, Suhail Razak, Fohad Mabood Husain, Janeen H. Trembley
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Bryophyllum pinnatum is used to cure infections worldwide. Although the flavonoids of this plant are well known, it is still unknown how much of the plant’s Ag and ZnO nanoparticles are beneficial. In the current research work, silver and zinc oxide nanoparticles were prepared using Bryophyllum pinnatum extract. The synthesized particles were characterized by UV-visible spectroscopy, SEM, EDS, XRD and FTIR. Synthesized particles were subjected to evaluation of their bactericidal and antifungal activity at various doses. Uv vis spectra at 400 nm corresponding to AgNPs confirmed their synthesis. Strong peaks in the EDS spectra of Ag and ZnO indicate the purity of the sample. The scanning electron microscopic images of ZnONPs showed a size of about 60 nm ± 3 nm, which demonstrated the presence of triangular-shaped ZnO nanoparticles. Green synthesized nanoparticles showed bactericidal activity against both Gram-positive (Micrococcus luteus, Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus subtilis) and Gram-negative (Agrobacterium tumifaciens, Salmonella setubal, Enterobacter aerogenes) strains. AgNPs proved to be more effective against Gram-negative bacterial strains compared to Gram-positive owing to MIC values (10 ppm and 20 ppm respectively). Whereas, ZnONPs were found more effective against Gram-positive bacteria with lower MIC values (10 ppm) as compared to Gram-negative ones (20 ppm). Also, the synthesized nanoparticles exhibited moderate dose-dependent antifungal activity against tested fungal strains ranging from 10 to 70%. Cytotoxicity of nanoparticles was found significant using Brine shrimp’s lethality assay with IC50 values of 4.09 ppm for AgNPs, 13.72 ppm for ZnONPs, and 24.83 ppm for plant extract. Conclusively, Ag and ZnO nanoparticles were more effective than plant extract and AgNPs had higher activities than those of ZnONPs. Further research is warranted to explore the precise mechanism of action and the potential applications of these nanoparticles in the medical field.

Background

Nanobiotechnology refers to the combination of two distinct fields i.e. biology and engineering that leads to the development of multipurpose devices and systems working at the nanoscale with enhanced sensitivity, specificity and a higher rate of recognition to improve biomedical applications. Various human diseases can be treated with the use of nanoparticles and nanosystems due to diverse properties like size, stability, catalytic activity, conductivity and optical behaviour1. Nanotechnological advances credited the most improved carrier system for the delivery of therapeutic agents at the targeted site. The carrier protects the encapsulated therapeutic agent from external degradation as well as improves its stability2. Moreover, nanoparticles provide enhanced support for the delivery of phytochemicals. The polymeric materials encapsulate phytochemicals and protect them from external degradation with enhanced stability for long-term storage. Surface modification can be possible to avoid drug leakage and enhance specificity toward specific targets by ligand attachment to the surface2.

The most recent Global Antimicrobial Surveillance System (GLASS)3 from the WHO reveals that 500,000 individuals with documented bacterial illnesses across 22 countries have widespread antimicrobial resistance. The most often found resistant bacteria were K. pneumoniae, S. aureus, E. coli, and S. pneumoniae. Penicillin resistance reached up to 51% among nations stating to GLASS, while resistance to ciprofloxacin, an antibiotic often used to treat urinary tract infections (UTIs), ranged from 8.4 to 92.9% for E. coli and from 4.1 to 79.4% for K. pneumoniae. Data on MRSA bloodstream infections from 25 countries, regions, and zones, as well as data on E. coli bloodstream infections from 49 countries, were received by GLASS in 2019. Methicillin-resistant S. aureus had a median incidence of 12.11%, while E. coli with a median rate of resistance to third-generation cephalosporins was reported4.

Nanoparticles have many characteristics that make them substitutes for traditional antibiotics which can improve the inhibitory effects of available antibiotics which are consequently regarded as next-generation antibiotics. The large surface-area-to-volume ratio increases the contact area of NPs with which they interact with bacteria with increased cell membrane penetration, and play with molecular pathways5. Silver and zinc oxide nanoparticles are widely used metallic and metal oxide nanomaterials that have various applications in the fields of medicine, environmental remediation, agriculture, and others. Both Ag and ZnO nanoparticles have been evidenced to have antimicrobial properties at very low concentrations against resistant microbial strains6. They are also found to have anticancer properties against various cancer cell lines7–10.

By using green synthesis techniques, a significant number of non-toxic nanoparticles are created. By avoiding the use of dangerous chemicals, green synthesis produces affordable and environment-friendly byproducts. Because natural components are used in the synthesis, the green synthesized nanoparticles are particularly stable11,12. When compared to conventional methods, the bio-inspired technique produces materials with lower impurity levels. Green synthesis makes it simple to achieve the required size and form with a well-defined morphology13. Furthermore, the synthesis of nanoparticles using green plant extracts has attracted the interest of more researchers than the synthetic approach since it is less expensive, eco-friendly, energy-efficient, and uses fewer hazardous chemicals14. The production of nanoparticles using extracts from green plant materials results in useful applications free from contamination. Compared to synthetic approaches, the environmental toxicity of biosynthesized nanoparticles is extremely low15.

Bryophyllum pinnatum belonging to the family Crassulaceae is an air plant also called miracle leaf or life plant which is native to Madagascar. In ethnomedicine, it is used for the treatment of burns, abscesses, ulcers, insect bites, diarrhoea and Lithiasis16. Unique Bryophilin A content of this plant shows strong anti-tumour, antioxidant and antimicrobial activity. Various properties make it a valuable health-strengthening agent including the potential to reduce blood pressure and blood sugar levels17.

Thus, in a desire to explore biologically effective and eco-friendly nanoparticles, the present study was designed with the major objective of synthesizing silver and zinc oxide nanoparticles using Bryophyllum pinnatum plant extract along with their physical characterization and biological evaluation as antimicrobial and cytotoxic agents. To the best of our knowledge, no previous finding is available describing the comparative study of silver and zinc oxide nanoparticles of B. pinnatum. This is the first study to give insights into that silver nanoparticles of this plant are more effective than zinc oxide nanoparticles. However, both have efficacy more than that of plant extract.

Materials and methods

Plant collection

Leaves of B. pinnatum were collected from the local farm area of Gujjar Khan, Pakistan following the standard national and international guidelines for conducting plant research. The plant sample was identified from the herbarium of Quaid-i-Azam University Islamabad and the sample specimen was deposited in the herbarium with accession code No. HMP-Bry-200.

Synthesis of silver nanoparticles

Experiments were conducted following the international guidelines for conducting plant research. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethical Review Committee (ethical approval No. CUST-BI-2020/5). Aqueous extract of B. pinnatum was prepared by using fresh leaves which were washed with distilled water to remove dust. Leaves (10 g) were chopped and boiled in 100 mL of distilled water for 10 to 15 min following the reported protocol18. The extract was then filtered and stored at 4 ºC for further use as a reducing and stabilizing agent. AgNO3 solution (0.05 M) was prepared by dissolving 4.25 g of silver nitrate salt in 500 mL of distilled water along with magnetic stirring at room temperature for 30 min19. Similarly, zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn (C4H6O4) 2H2O) purchased from Sigma-Aldrich was used as a zinc precursor for the synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles. For the preparation of a 0.25 M solution of zinc acetate, 22.92 g of zinc acetate salt was put into the flask that had 500 mL of distilled water in it. Subsequently, salt solutions were mixed with plant extract (9:1) and the solutions were incubated for 2–3 h at room temperature having pH 8. A change of colour indicated the completion of the reaction18.

Characterization

To determine the optical properties of synthesized Ag and ZnO nanoparticles, UV-Vis analysis was performed using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (UV 1602 BMS spectrophotometer, Spain) with spectra recorded in the range of 200–500 nm at normal temperature12. Morphological features including the size and shape of particles were determined by carrying out SEM analysis (JEOL-JSM-6490LATM) operating at 20 Kv (maximum) with a counting frequency of 2368 cps (maximum). Magnified images were taken up to 20 µM resolution to get better results. The elemental composition of the sample was determined by EDS (Oxford instruments) coupled with SEM thus giving both qualitative and quantitative analysis of data operating at an energy level of 6.04 Kv14,20. The samples for X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis were made by taking a small amount of solution from the bottle and drying it on a quartz plate (XRD D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany)8,21 The surface chemistry of synthesized nanoparticles was studied by the FTIR spectrometer. The nanoparticle’s solution was dried at the temperature of 75 °C and the dried powder of nanoparticles was subjected to characterization in the range of 4000–400 cm− 1 utilizing a KBr pellet strategy14,22.

Antimicrobial analysis

Antibacterial activity

Antibacterial activity of synthesized Ag and ZnO nanoparticles was tested against six different bacterial strains including Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 6538), Bacillus subtitles (ATCC 6633) and Micrococcus luteus (ATCC 10240) and Gram-negative bacterial strains (Agrobacterium tumifaciens (ATCC 4720T), Salmonella setubal (ATCC 19196), Enterobacter aerogenes (ATCC 13048) using the disc diffusion method18. All bacterial strains were sub-cultured in nutrient broth i.e. Luria broth and incubated at 37 ºC for 24 h. Petri plates were prepared to have media (nutrient agar) swabbed with bacterial cultures and discs for inoculation of samples. A sample including plant extract and nanoparticles was poured on discs at different concentrations of 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 100 ppm. Streptomycin and distilled water were used as positive and negative controls respectively.

Antifungal activity

Antifungal activity was calculated using the tube dilution method against five different fungal strains (Aspergillus flavus, Aspergillus fumigatus, Aspergillus niger, Mucor, Fusarium solani)23. The sabouraud dextrose agar media (4 mL) was poured into each test tube along with the sample solutions (50, 100 and 150 ppm) and was let down in a horizontal position to get the slanting region. After that, each tube was inoculated with seven days old fungal strains from the culture. Tubes were also poured with terbinafine as a positive control and distilled water as a negative control. Tubes were incubated at 28 ºC in the dark for two days. Cytotoxic analysis

Cytotoxicity of samples was tested using brine shrimp lethality assay by following the method as reported earlier8. Sea salt solution was prepared by dissolving 17 g of sea salt in 500 mL of distilled water. Brine shrimp eggs were allowed to hatch and thus hatched shrimps were used for further analysis. Glass vials were poured with different concentrations of samples and about 15 shrimps were added to each vial with the help of a Pasteur pipette. Sea salt was used as the negative control in the experiment. Vials were kept in light for 24 h at 24 ºC. After that, alive shrimps were counted. Statistical analysis

All the experiments were performed in triplicate, and the standard deviation was calculated along with applying two-way ANOVA using GraphPad Prism software.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of biosynthesized AgNPs

After the addition of plant extract in the salt solutions, the colour changed from colourless to dark brown confirming the synthesis of AgNPs8, while it changed to light brown in the case of ZnONPs (Fig. 1) indicating the formation of metallic ZnONPs in the aqueous solution24. The colour change is related to surface plasmon resonance, a major phenomenon exhibited by metallic nanoparticles due to the collective oscillation of free electrons after exposure of particles to light of a particular wavelength25. The different functional groups of phytochemicals are present in the plant extract which are responsible for the capping of ionic substances into metallic nanoparticles. The amount of plant extract in the reaction medium and the wide range of metal concentrations can change the biosynthesis reaction and change the size and shape of the nanoparticles26.Fig. 1Synthesis of nanoparticles. Similarly, in the case of ZnONPs, FTIR spectrometry was performed for the qualitative analysis of the expected reducing and capping agents/metabolites involved in the synthesis of nanoparticles (Fig. 6b). In the current study, FTIR spectra showed the disappearance of bands at 1600 cm-1, which could be the C = O, C = N, C = C stretching vibration of ethers and esters and also = C–O stretching of the carboxyl group of lipids of essential oils. Moreover, the disappearance of bands at 3400 cm-1 is the characteristic signal of the O-H stretching mode of alcohols and is a dead giveaway for the presence of an alcohol group. The breadth of this signal is a consequence of hydrogen bonding between molecules, thus representing the involvement of their role in the synthesis of silver nanoparticles18.

Antimicrobial analysis of biosynthesized silver nanoparticles

Aqueous plant extract of B. pinnatum was also used as a test sample along with Ag and ZnO nanoparticles to determine its antibacterial potential. Previously, silver nanoparticles of B. pinnatum have been found to have higher antibacterial activity against S. aureus (12 mm) and B. subtilis (12 mm) than that of plant extract31. Furthermore, the green synthesized ZnO nanoparticles of B. pinnatum, when tested against a few highly MDR uropathogenic bacteria (E. coli, E. furgusonii, K. pneumoniae, S. flexneri, and P. aeruginosa), were observed to exhibit higher antimicrobial response (zones of inhibition ranging between 22 mm and 28 mm) than that of respective plant extract18. It has been reported that secondary metabolites present in the plant extract are involved in the reduction of metal ions to metal nanoparticles and they are responsible for enhanced therapeutic potential of metallic nanoparticles8.

MIC values of AgNPs for both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria were determined and it was 20 ppm and 10 ppm respectively. Whereas, MIC values of ZnONPs were found to be 10 ppm for gram-positive and 20 ppm for gram-negative bacteria (Table 1). From the results, it was concluded that AgNPs proved to be more effective against Gram-negative bacterial strains as compared to Gram-positive bacterial strains owing to lower MIC value. The reason lies behind the fact that Gram-negative strains were most susceptible to silver nanoparticles as compared to Gram-positive strains due to the structural composition of their cell wall, which was made of peptidoglycan while the cell wall of the Gram-positive bacteria was composed chiefly of peptidoglycan with various cross-linkages between linear polysaccharides thus giving the membrane a rigid structure making it less permeable to the uptake of AgNPs32. The size and shape of the particles were important determinants of antibacterial activity. As the size changes, the surface area to volume ratio changes and the ability of a particle to get penetrated in the cell is changed33. Particles with the same surface area but different shapes may also have different efficiency due to the availability of active facets and effective surface area. Thus, this rigid structure minimized the accessibility of silver nanoparticles to penetrate the cell wall of Gram-positive bacteria and thus showed minimum inhibitory effect30. Whereas ZnONPs were found more effective against Gram-positive bacteria with lower MIC values as compared to Gram-negative bacteria (Table 1). Previously, reports have stated that Gram-positive bacteria are more sensitive to ZnO nanoparticles than Gram-negative bacteria, it seems Gram-negative bacteria showed more resistance towards ZnO nanoparticles9.

Antifungal assay

In the current study, prepared nanoparticles and plant extract were also tested against five different fungal strains to determine their antifungal potential using the tube dilution method. All samples were tested at three concentrations i.e. 50, 100 and 150 ppm. Results of the assay are summarized in Table 2 exhibiting the percentage inhibition at all concentrations. The highest percentage of inhibition was observed against the Solani strain i.e. 70% and the lowest for the Mucor strain showing 28% inhibition by AgNPs while results were different for plant extract and ZnONPs. ZnONPs showed a maximum inhibitory effect against the A. flavis with 60% inhibition, while inhibition observed against the Mucor species was 43%. The other three strains Niger, Fumigatus and Solani also showed sensitivity towards ZnONPs such as 45%, 40%, and 50% respectively. It was observed that the activity of plant extract was lower as compared to AgNPs and ZnONPs with maximum inhibition observed against the Solani strain i.e. 37% and lowest for the Mucor strain i.e. 10%. Results of the study revealed that antifungal activity was decreased in a dose-dependent manner and it increased as the concentration of the sample increased. Moreover, synthesized nanoparticles were found to be more effective in showing antifungal activity than that of plant extract (Fig. 8). The highest antifungal activity of synthesized nanoparticles was due to the unique interaction of these particles with fungal spores thus disturbing the membrane integrity of those spores and ultimately causing death34. In the case of positive control, no growth was observed while maximum growth was observed for negative control. In another report, AgNPs were synthesized including seed extracts of grapes and those particles were tested against different pathogenic fungal strains35. The major mechanism associated with the inhibition of microbial cell growth involves the interaction of AgNPs with sulfur and phosphorous-containing proteins in the membrane thus inhibiting the actions of respiratory enzymes and also denature DNA, hence causing cell death36. Mechanisms of action of the antifungal activity of ZnONPs have also been reported according to which ZnONPs penetrate the fungal cell by the process of diffusion and endocytosis. When these particles reach the cytoplasm of the cell, they can take part in mitochondrial function, resulting in the generation of ROS and Zn2+. These generated ions enter into the nuclear membrane and penetrate the DNA, leading to nuclear destruction such as irreversible chromosome damage which causes the death of the cell36. Cytotoxicity of biosynthesized ag and ZnO nanoparticles

The cytotoxic profile of synthesized nanoparticles and plant extract was evaluated using a Brine shrimp lethality assay at three different concentrations. The percentage of cell viability was measured (Table 3) using the formula discussed above. In general, silver and ZnO nanoparticles showed enhanced cytotoxicity against Brine shrimps as compared to that of plant extract. AgNps were found to exhibit greater cytotoxic potential as compared to ZnONPs and plant extract showing 5% viability at the highest concentration while the later had 17% and 33% respectively. Further results showed a lesser IC50 value i.e. 4.09 ppm for AgNPs as compared to ZnONPs (13.72 ppm) and plant extract (24.83 ppm). There are studies available describing the enhanced therapeutic potential of nanoparticles as compared to plant extract against different diseases, which is mainly due to the synergistic effect of the metal itself and the presence of important bioactive compounds of plants on the surface of these nanoparticles. For example, gold nanoparticles prepared using an extract of Withania somnifera were found to show 1.5 times higher cytotoxicity as compared to plant extract mainly because of the presence of bioactive compound withanolide-A37. Furthermore, it was reported that ZnONPs showed maximum cytotoxicity due to several factors such as dose time, small size, and large surface-to-volume ratio41. The main mechanism that is associated with the cytotoxicity of nanoparticles is the production of ROS thus causing damage to the lipid, protein and DNA components of the cell and eventually causing cell death36. These particles can act by reducing the ATP content of the cell thus ultimately damaging mitochondrion and causing the production of ROS in a dose-dependent manner. In another report, cytotoxicity was found to be associated with the synergistic action of AgNPs and phytochemicals of plant extract attached at the surface of nanoparticles36.

Conclusion

The current study utilized the medicinally important plant extract of B. pinnatum to synthesize Ag and ZnO nanoparticles quickly and effectively employing a green chemistry approach. AgNPs were found to be marginally more effective than ZnONPS, even though both Ag and ZnO nanoparticles were more effective than plant extract. These green-synthesized nanoparticles were potentially cytotoxic in addition to having antimicrobial properties. In comparison to the corresponding plant extract, they not only exhibited increased antimicrobial activity but also better cytotoxic potentials against Brine shrimps. Because of their enormous potential, in vivo investigations can be used to further investigate the underlying mechanism of action of these stable metallic nanoparticles.
