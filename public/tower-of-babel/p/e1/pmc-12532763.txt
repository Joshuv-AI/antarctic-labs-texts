PARP1 contributes to intimal hyperplasia by regulating METTL3-mediated m6A methylation of TRAIL
Authors: Shaodong Li, Fengxiang Pang, Wenhai Guo, Weibing Guan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1) promotes vascular intimal hyperplasia (IH) while contributing to N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methylation regulatory processes. The present study focuses on whether the PARP1 inhibitor PJ34 can improve vascular IH by regulating m6A methylation modification. Mice with femoral artery wire injury-induced IH and platelet-derived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB)-challenged mouse vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) were utilized in the study. PJ34 treatment significantly alleviated neointimal formation, suppressed VSMC proliferation and phenotypic switching, and reduced global m6A methylation and methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) expression in injured arteries. Dot blot, RT-qPCR, western blot, and immunohistochemistry confirmed these changes. In vitro, PJ34 impaired PDGF-BB-stimulated proliferation and migration in VSMCs, effects reversed by METTL3 overexpression but not observed in METTL3-deficient cells. Mechanistically, METTL3 regulated the m6A methylation and stability of tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) mRNA. PJ34 downregulated TRAIL expression via inhibition of METTL3-mediated m6A modification. TRAIL-knockout mice were resistant to the vascular protective effects of PJ34, highlighting the essential downstream role of TRAIL. Immunohistochemistry confirmed TRAIL localization in the neointima and media. Moreover, TRAIL deficiency did not lead to increased systemic inflammation, as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β levels in plasma remained unchanged. In conclusion, PJ34 mitigates vascular IH by modulating METTL3-mediated TRAIL m6A methylation. Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the diseases with the highest mortality rate around the world, threatening human health seriously (Goldsborough et al. 2022). One of the most important pathophysiological bases for the onset of CVDs is atherosclerosis (AS). Neointimal formation (also called intimal hyperplasia), a structural change secondary to vascular injury, is a key cause for vascular stenosis and even occlusion (Ma et al. 2022). Intimal hyperplasia (IH) hastens AS formation and postoperative restenosis (arterial bypass grafts, arteriovenous endovascular fistulas, vascular balloon angioplasty, and stent implantation) (Yamamura 2024). Currently, metal stents in combination with anti-proliferative drugs can suppress neointimal formation effectively, but it can cause complications such as delay in the process of vascular reendothelialization, thrombosis in the stent, and so on (Maccarrone et al. (Maccarrone 2019); Clare et al. (Clare 2022)). Therefore, figuring out the molecular mechanisms of IH is crucial for improving AS and restenosis.

During normal physiological conditions, vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) exist predominantly in a contractile phenotype within the vascular media (Shi et al. 2020). Impairment of the vasculature causes platelet activation and aggregation, resulting in a phenotypic shift in VSMCs from contractile to synthetic (Gardin et al. (Gardin, et al. 2022); Tang et al. (Tang, et al., 2022)). Synthetic phenotype of VSMCs exhibit an increased in migration and proliferation, as well as secrete extracellular matrix, facilitating their migration towards the intima and inducing neointimal formation, resulting in IH (Cao et al. 2022; Tang et al. (Tang, et al., 2022)). Consequently, suppression of the proliferation and migration of VSMCs is important for the improvement of IH in vessels.

RNA epigenetics, an emerging pathway for post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression, may affect variable splicing, translation, cellular localization and stability of RNAs by inducing changes in RNA pairing, thermodynamics and folding properties (Prasher et al. 2020). Characterized by methylation at the nitrogen-6 position of RNA adenosine, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) represents ~ 97.4% of documented RNA modifications (Li et al. 2022). m6A modification exists in a dynamic equilibrium, and it is primarily established by methyltransferase complexes and removed by demethylases. Altered expression profiles of m6A regulators detected via mass spectrometry strongly correlate with AS progression, suggesting that m6A modifications participate in AS progression (Quiles-Jiménez et al. (Quiles-Jiménez, et al., 2020)). Furthermore, m6A-dependent regulation of ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase like 5 through the methyltransferase-like (METTL) 14/YTH m6A RNA binding protein 1 axis promotes inflammation and vascular remodeling in AS (Yang et al. 2023). METTL3 enhances miR-375-3p expression by mediating the binding of DiGeorge syndrome critical region gene 8 to pri-miR-375, therefore regulating the phenotypic switch of VSMCs (Chen et al. 2023). Currently, the regulatory role of m6A modification in VSMC proliferation and migration remain poorly characterized.

Poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1) exerts significant roles in DNA damage repair, gene transcription, and cell differentiation (Safdar et al. 2024). Compelling experimental evidence suggests a crucial role of PARP1 in AS (Xu et al. 2014). Activation of PARP1 fosters recruitment of inflamed cells and vascular endothelial damage, therefore exacerbating AS progression (Shafiq et al. 2023). Moreover, PARP1 inhibition attenuates VSMC proliferation and inflammatory activation, consequently preserving vascular functional and structural homeostasis (Henning et al. 2018). Yet, PARP1 regulates the transcription of METTL3 and subsequent mediated m6A modifications by controlling its chromatin accessibility (Sun et al. 2023). The PARP1-inhibiting compound PJ34 demonstrates therapeutic potential for AS by promoting endothelial repair in rabbits with high-fat diet-induced AS (Zha et al. 2019). Currently, whether PJ34 can repress IH by mediating key enzymes of m6A methylation is unclear.

This study utilized both femoral artery (FA) wire injury-induced IH and platelet-derived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB)-challenged mouse VSMCs to address whether PJ34 can repress IH by mediating m6A modification.

Materials and methods

The Ethics and experimental animals

Approval for the study design was acquired from the Animal Care Committee of the Guangzhou Ruige Biological Technology Co., Ltd (Approval number 20250522–001). All experiments involving animals complied with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council Committee for the Update of the Guide for the and Use of Laboratory (National 2011)). Male C57BL/6 J mice (8–10 weeks old; Hfkbio, Beijing, China), tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) KO mice with a C57BL/6Smoc background (male, 8–10 weeks old; Guangdong Animal Medicine Center, China), and male wild-type (WT) C57BL/6Smoc mice (8–10 weeks old; Guangdong Animal Medicine Center, China) were housed in the specific-pathogen-free facility with strictly regulated environmental parameters: 22 ± 2 °C ambient temperature, 60–70% relative humidity, and standardized photoperiod (12 h light/dark).

FA wire injury model and groupings

To assess the efficacy of the PARP1 inhibitor PJ34, experimental animal studies were performed one week after acclimatization in C57BL/6 J mice according to the following groupings: control, injured, injured + vehicle, injured + PJ34-L, and injured + PJ-34-H. FA wire injury was performed following established protocols. Mice underwent FA wire injury surgery in the injured group. For this operation, anesthesia was administered to the mice by inhalation of 2% isoflurane. Microscopic dissection was conducted on the left common FA and its tributaries, followed by proximal looping of the main artery with 6–0 nylon suture and distal looping of the branching vessels. Next, the iliac artery was inserted with a spring wire (0.38 mm diameter) after the muscle branch underwent transverse arteriotomy. The wire was kept inside for 1 min, followed by ligation of the muscle branch at the bifurcation point with an 8–0 silk wire. Closure of the skin incision was performed after the restoration of blood flow. Following a 4-week recovery period, surgical mice received intraperitoneal anesthesia (80 mg/kg ketamine + 0.6 mg/kg midazolam) and were subsequently euthanized by cervical dislocation. A sham operation was conducted following the same protocol but omitting the ligation procedure. The sham group mice received intraperitoneal injections of a vehicle consisting of saline (45%), Tween-80 (5%), PEG300 (40%), and DMSO (10%) (n = 6). Injured mice were randomly allocated into 3 groups and injected intraperitoneally with vehicle, PJ34 with a low dose (PJ34-L; 5 mg/kg; Invivochem, Guangzhou, China), or PJ34 with a high dose (PJ34-H; 10 mg/kg; Invivochem), respectively (n = 6 mice/group). Mice were administered with vehicle or PJ34 twice a week for 4 weeks.

To validate whether PJ34 works via mediating TRAIL expression, TRAIL KO and WT mice were divided into the uninjured and injured groups. TRAIL KO and WT mice in the injured group were treated with vehicle or PJ34 following FA wire injury. TRAIL KO and WT mice in the uninjured group did not experience femoral arterial line injury but underwent vehicle or PJ34 treatment. The administration of vehicle or PJ34 was as described above.

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining

FA samples were collected from mice euthanized by inhalation of carbon dioxide and cervical dislocation. The collected arteries were fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde (Catalog No. E672002; Sangon, Shanghai, China) and embedded in paraffin, followed by preparation of cross-Sects. (5-μm thickness) for sectioning and morphometric analyses. HE staining was carried out as described previously. All sections were imaged digitally using a Leica microscope, followed by blinded quantification of neointimal areas and intima-to-media ratios by an independent investigator with ImageJ software. Calculation of the neointimal area involved subtraction of the luminal area from the internal elastic lamina (IEL)-bounded area. For the medial area, subtraction of the area bounded by the IEL from the area bounded by the external elastic lamina was performed. Quantification of IH employed the neointima/media area ratio. Analyses were performed on sections located ∼250 μm proximal to the ligature.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

At 4 weeks after wire injury, femoral arteries were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, processed for paraffin embedding, and cut into 5-μm sections for IHC analysis. Citrate buffer (pH 6.0) was used for antigen retrieval prior to peroxidase inactivation via 3% hydrogen peroxide incubation. Following incubation with primary antibodies against METTL3, TRAIL, α-SMA, and PCNA, the slides were subjected to sequential treatments: incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, development using DAB substrate, and final counterstaining with hematoxylin. Stained sections were imaged using a Leica microscope.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Peripheral blood was collected via cardiac puncture at 4 weeks post-injury to obtain plasma. Measurement of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β levels employed commercial ELISA kits following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Cell culture

Mouse aortic VSMCs (Catalog No. CRL-2797 ™; ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were cultured in DMEM (Catalog No. 30–2002; ATCC) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Catalog No. A5670801; Gibco, Thermo, Madison, WI, USA) and 0.2 mg/ml G-418 (Catalog No. PB180125; Procell, Wuhan, China). For drug treatment, mouse VSMCs were incubated with serum-free medium containing PJ34 (0.2 nM) for 12 h, followed by stimulation with 20 ng/ml PDGF-BB (Catalog No. HY-P7087; MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) for 12 h, with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Catalog No. A100231; Sangon) as the vehicle.

RNA interference and plasmid transfection

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting METTL3 (si-METTL3#1, 5′-GCA​AGA​AUU​CUG​UGA​CUA​UTT-3’; si-METTL3#2, 5′-GGA​UAC​CUG​CAA​GUA​UGU​UTT-3’; si-METTL3#3, 5′-GCU​ACC​UGG​ACG​UCA​GUA​UTT-3’) synthesized by Tsingke (Beijing, China) were transfected into mouse VSMCs using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Catalog No. 13778150; Invitrogen, Thermo) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For the METTL3 overexpression plasmid, the complementary DNA (cDNA) sequence of METTL3 (NM_019721.2) was cloned into the pcDNA™3.1(+) plasmid (Catalog No. V79020; Invitrogen, Thermo). The same method was used to construct the TRAIL overexpression plasmid (NM_009425.2). The plasmids were transduced into mouse VSMCs using ViaFect Transfection Reagent (Catalog No. E4982; Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to standard procedures.

Dot blot assay

Total RNA isolated from femoral arteries and cultured VSMCs using Trizol reagent (Catalog No. B610409; Sangon) was digested with DNase I (Catalog No. EN0521; Thermo) denatured at 65 °C for 5 min. RNA samples were spotted onto a nitrocellulose membrane (hybond-N +, Catalog No. RPN303B; GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) mounted in a Bio-Dot device (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), followed by cross-linking under UV irradiation for 5 min. Membranes blocked with 5% non-fat milk were hatched with anti-m6A antibody (1/5000, Catalog No. ab151230; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) overnight at 4 °C. The imaging system was used to visualize dot blots after they were incubated with a secondary antibody (1/20,000, Catalog No. ab97051; Abcam). The loading control membrane was stained with 0.02% methylene blue (Catalog No. A416886; Sangon). Input RNA content was displayed by scanning the blue dots. Assessment of dot density was carried out using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

The isolated RNA (1 μg) was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (Catalog No. R323; Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, cDNA amplification was performed using specific primers (Table 1) and the ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Catalog No. R331; Vazyme). The amplified products were separated on 2% agarose gels with TAE buffer. Calculation of relative gene expression employed the 2−ΔΔCt method with normalization to β-actin.

Cell viability analysis

Mouse VSMCs (5 × 103 cells/well) with different treatments were incubated in 96-well plates for 24 h. An additional incubation lasting about 2 h was performed after adding 10 μl of the cell counting kit (CCK)−8 reagent (Catalog No. E606335 Sangon). Optical density (OD) was read at a wave length of 450 nm and cell viability was calculated using the following formula: (ODtest-ODblank)/(ODcontrol-ODblank) × 100%.

Cell proliferation analysis

The BeyoClick™ EdU-488 cell proliferation kit (Catalog No. C0071S; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used to assess the proliferative capacity of mouse VSMCs by 5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EdU) assays. In brief, the harvested mouse VSMCs were incubated with 500 µL of the EdU reagent (10 µM) for 2 h. After washing, mouse VSMCs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with an enhanced immunostaining permeabilization solution (Catalog No. P0097; Beyotime), and then incubated with the click-reaction reagent. Staining of the cell nuclei was performed with 4’,6-diamidine-2-phenylindole (Catalog No. C1005; Beyotime). Visualization of the cells was performed using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell migration analysis

Mouse VSMCs (2 × 104 cells) suspended in serum-free medium (200 μl) was added to the upper chamber (8-μm pores; Corning, New York, USA). A complete medium (500 μl) containing the corresponding treatment was added to the lower chamber. Following 24-h incubation, migrated mouse VSMCs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min) and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Catalog No. A600331; Sangon) for 30 min. Cell counting was done using ImageJ software after capturing microscope images.

Western blot

The harvested mouse VSMCs were lysed using the radio immunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer (Catalog No. P0013; Beyotime). Then, protein samples (25 ug/lane) were resolved with 10% sodium dodecyl sulphate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Transference of protein blotting onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (PVDF, Catalog No. FFP78; Beyotime) was performed. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, the membranes were probed at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies: METTL3 (Catalog No. ab195352, at 1/1000 dilution; Abcam), matrix metalloproteinase 2 (MMP2) (Catalog No. FNab05238, at 1/1000 dilution; FineTest, Wuhan, China), matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) (Catalog No. FNab05247, at 1/1000 dilution; FineTest), TRAIL (Catalog No. A00466-1, 0.5 μg/mL; BosterBio), and β-actin (Catalog No. FNab00869, at 1/2000 dilution; FineTest). Membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and immunoblots were visualized using with a chemiluminescent substrate (Catalog No. WBKLS0500; Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) and images were taken by the ECL imager (Tanon-5200 Multi; Tanon, Shanghai, China).

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP)

Estimation of TRAIL m6A modification was undertaken using a riboMeRIP m6A transcriptome profiling kit (Catalog No. R11096.3; RiboBio). In brief, total RNA (100 μg) isolated from mouse VSMCs was randomly fragmented into approximately 100 nucleotides. Ten percent of total RNA was removed as input control. The fragmented RNA samples were then immunoprecipitated with magnetic beads pre-coated by the anti-m6A antibody (5 μg) in IP buffer for 2 h at 4 ℃. Elution of m 6A-precipitated RNA was based on m6A 5’-monophosphate sodium salt. We determined m6A modification on TRAIL mRNA through qPCR analysis.

RNA stability assay

Following a 12-h exposure to PDGF-BB, mouse VSMCs were incubated with fresh media containing 5 mg/ml actinomycin D. Total RNA was extracted at different time points (0, 2, 4, and 8 h) and then subjected to qPCR analysis.

Statistical analysis

Experiments were repeated at least for three times independently. Data analysis was conducted with the GraphPad Prism 7.0 software (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA), with results expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). We assessed data normality via the Shapiro–Wilk test. Statistical comparisons relied on one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) plus Tukey’s test, with significance established at p < 0.05 (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001).

Results

Suppressing PARP1 activity alleviates IH in mice with FA wire injury

To figure out the mechanism by which PARP1 regulates neointimal hyperplasia during AS, we induced neointimal hyperplasia in mouse models by FA wire injury and treated with the PARP1 inhibitor PJ34 for 4 weeks. The streamlined animal experimentation procedure is shown in Fig. 1A. Changes in IH of the FA vessels were analyzed by HE staining. Significantly higher neointimal area and neointimal/medial area ratio were observed in the injured group versus the control group. Whereas, injury-suffering mice showed an obvious decrease in both neointimal area and neointimal/media area ratio post-treatment with PJ34, with a better efficacy at a higher dose (Fig. 1B-D). Furthermore, there was no significant change in the relative media area among the groups versus the control group (Fig. 1E). We then performed immunohistochemical staining for α-SMA and PCNA in FA sections. Compared with the sham group, injured arteries exhibited enhanced expression of PCNA and reduced α-SMA staining, indicative of VSMC proliferation and phenotypic switching. PJ34 treatment markedly reversed these changes, as evidenced by decreased PCNA and increased α-SMA expression in the neointimal and medial layers (Fig. 1F and G). These results suggest that PJ34 mitigates neointimal hyperplasia, at least in part, by preserving VSMC contractile phenotype and limiting proliferation. Recent evidence suggested that PARP1 works by mediating “writers” in the m6A modification (Sun et al. 2023). Taking this into account, we analyzed whether PARP1 can regulate IH by mediating the m6A modification. Changes on m6A levels in intimal tissues of FA vessels were analyzed by dot blot assays. As displayed in Fig. 2A, the abundance of m6A in intimal tissues of the injured group was significantly upregulated, but PJ34 treatment reduced the elevated m6A abundance. In addition, the transcript levels of popular “writers” were subsequently estimated. RT-qPCR results showed that METTL3 mRNA levels were highly expressed in intimal tissues of the injured group, while the elevated mRNA levels of METTL3 were impaired following PJ34 treatment. Nevertheless, METTL14, WTAP, FTO, and ALKBH5 mRNA levels did not change significantly in the intimal tissues among the groups (Fig. 2B). These results manifested that may mediate IH by regulating METTL3-dependent m6A modifications. To further validate this regulatory relationship at the tissue level, we performed IHC staining for METTL3 in FA sections. METTL3 overexpression reverses PJ34-mediated repressive effects on mouse VSMC proliferation and migration

Pathological vascular stimulation or injury induces abnormal conversion of VSMCs from a contractile to synthetic phenotype, promoting VSMC proliferation and migration that ultimately leads to IH. To explore the function of METTL3, we constructed METTL3-overexpressed mouse VSMCs. Mouse VSMCs exhibited prominent upregulation of METTL3 mRNA and protein levels following transfection with the METTL3 overexpression plasmid (Fig. 3A and B). Subsequently, mouse VSMCs with or without overexpression of METTL3 were treated with PJ34 under PDGF-BB stimulation to validate that PJ34 works by mediating METTL3 expression. The results demonstrated that PDGF-BB stimulation upgraded cell viability as well as accelerated cell proliferation and migration in mouse VSMCs, but PJ34 incubation PDGF-BB-driven alterations in cell viability, proliferation, and migration. Interestingly, the action of PJ34 was reversed following METTL3 overexpression (Figs. 3C-E). Considering that MPPs facilitate cell migration by degrading the ECM, we tested MMP2 and MMP9 protein levels. Undoubtedly, PDGF-BB resulted in significant upregulation of these two proteins in mouse VSMCs, whereas PJ34 treatment attenuated the PDGF-BB-mediated alterations in these two proteins. Undoubtedly, the introduction of METTL3 neutralized the efficacy of PJ34 (Fig. 3F). In addition, mouse VSMCs showed a prominent elevation in m6A abundance after stimulation by PDGF-BB. However, the enhanced m6A abundance in PDGF-BB-challenged mouse VSMCs was eroded after PJ34 treatment. As expected, METTL3 overexpression re-raised m6A abundance in PJ34-treated mouse VSMCs under PDGF-BB stimulation (Fig. 3G). PJ34 inhibits METTL3-mediated TRAIL upregulation

Next, we further elucidated the latent mechanisms by which PJ34 exerts its efficacy through METTL3-dependent m6A modifications. Growing evidence suggested that the TRAIL signaling regulates vascular cell function and remodeling under pathological conditions, including VSMC proliferation and migration (Patil et al. 2020; Li et al. 2016). The mRNA sequence of TRAIL was predicted by SRAMP to have 47 m6A methylation sites, of which 6 m6A methylation sites have very high confidence (Fig. 5A and B). Protective effect of PJ34 on injury-induced IH is abolished in TRAIL‐deficient mice

To determine whether PJ34 erodes neointimal formation via repressing TRAIL expression, global TRAIL KO and WT mice with or without FA wire injury were treated with PJ34 or vehicle. Histopathological analyses showed no significant difference in intimal status between WT and TRAIL KO mice, regardless of whether they were treated with PJ34 in the absence of FA wire injury (Fig. 7A). Both PJ34 treatment and TRAIL knockout improved injury-induced IH, accompanying with unchanged media area, reduced neointimal/media area ratio, and shrunken neointimal area. However, PJ34 treatment failed to act on the intimal status in TRAIL KO mice subjected to injury, with no significant changes in media area, neointimal/media area ratio, and neointimal area (Fig. 7A-D). To further confirm that PJ34 regulates TRAIL expression in vivo, we assessed TRAIL levels in injured femoral arteries using RT-qPCR and western blot. Significant upregulation of TRAIL was observed in injured arteries, while PJ34 administration effectively downregulated TRAIL expression levels in WT mice. However, in TRAIL KO mice, no TRAIL expression was detected as expected (Fig. 7E-F). Additionally, IHC staining of TRAIL in vascular tissues revealed similar expression trends (Fig. 7G). TRAIL was predominantly expressed in the neointimal and medial layers following injury, and PJ34 treatment attenuated this elevation in WT mice but not in TRAIL-deficient animals. These findings provide morphological evidence that PJ34 inhibits neointimal hyperplasia at least partly by suppressing TRAIL expression. To further assess whether TRAIL deficiency affects systemic inflammatory status, we measured peripheral cytokine levels in mouse plasma. ELISA results showed that PJ34 treatment significantly reduced TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β concentrations in WT mice after vascular injury. However, cytokine levels in TRAIL KO mice did not significantly differ from WT mice under injury conditions, indicating that the observed neointimal phenotype is unlikely to be caused by TRAIL-mediated systemic immune dysregulation (Supplementary Figure S1A-C). Discussion

The current study explored the efficacy of PJ34 in the treatment of vascular IH and the associated molecular mechanisms by combining animal and cell experiments. This study discovered that the PARP1 inhibitor PJ34 ameliorates IH and impairs PDGF-BB-induced proliferation and migration of mouse VSMCs by modulating METTL3-mediated m6A methylation of TRAIL, giving insights into the molecular mechanisms of vascular IH.

Increasing evidence suggests the association of PARP1 with AS. Lin et al. revealed that inhibition of the PARP1 signaling by Geniposide can improve AS by restoring lipophagy (Lin et al. 2024). Report of Luo et al. indicated that PARP1 upregulation regulated by low shear stress-induced NRP2 promotes AS progression (Luo et al. 2022). The release of PARP1 mediated by TRIM25 encourages aortic inflammation and atherosclerotic plaque formation (Wu et al. 2024). Lack of PARP1 relieves neointimal hyperplasia induced by hyperglycemia via upregulation of the TFPI2 activity in diabetic mice (Wang et al. 2021). The PARP1 inhibitor PJ34 has been reported to boost endothelial repair in rabbits with high-fat diet-induced AS (Zha et al. 2019), but the molecular mechanism by which it eases AS needs to be elucidated.

IH development post-vascular injury is predominantly mediated by hyperproliferation and migration of VSMCs. The m6A methylation modification is engaged in regulating the activity, migration, and proliferation of VSMCs (Tan et al. (Tan, et al., 2022)). METTL3 is the sole enzyme that can bind to methyl donors and transfer the methyl group, and its mediated m6A modification is linked to the onset of multiple diseases, including AS. Li et al. uncovered that METTL3 silencing restrains atherosclerotic plaque formation and alleviates inflammation in mice models with AS (Li et al. 2023). Moreover, inhibition of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway occurs via METTL3 silencing-mediated impairment of JAK2-IGF2BP1 interaction, thereby hindering the AS process in mouse AS models (Dong et al. 2021). Similarly, METTL3-mediated hypermethylation of NPC1L1 mRNA facilitates AS progression (Zhang et al. 2023). METTL3 silencing mediates the phenotypic conversion of VSMCs via the miR-375-3p/PDK1 axis, thereby stabilizing atherosclerotic plaques (Chen et al. 2023). Also, METTL3 knockdown promotes the decay of PI3K mRNA and impairs the PI3K/AKT signaling, thus repressing the phenotypic conversion of VSMCs (Zhao et al. 2022). Here, the data demonstrated that PJ34 could lighten IH post FA wire injury in mouse models. Interestingly, the elevation of global m6A levels and METTL3 expression in intimal tissues of FA wire injury-induced mice was impaired after PJ34 treatment, manifesting that PJ34 may mediate IH by regulating METTL3-dependent m6A modifications. Cell experiments in vitro showed that METTL3 overexpression overturned PJ34-mediated suppressive effects on the proliferation and migration of PDGF-BB-induced mouse VSMCs. Yet, PJ34 did not act on METTL3-knockdown mouse VSMCs in response to PDGF-BB stimulation. In vivo, PJ34 markedly reduced PCNA expression while restoring α-SMA levels in injured arteries, further confirming its role in suppressing VSMC proliferation and maintaining the contractile phenotype. All data indicated that PJ34-mediated METTL3 suppression attenuates PDGF-BB-stimulated proliferation and migration in mouse VSMCs, ultimately ameliorating IH in AS mouse models.

TRAIL, a pleiotropic cytokine belonging to the TNF superfamily, mediates diverse cellular processes (von Karstedt et al. 2017). TRAIL significantly contributes to AS progression, as demonstrated by research evidence (Patil et al. 2020). Neointimal hyperplasia is reduced in TRAIL-knockout mice with peri-vascular cuff injury, whereas delivery of recombinant TRAIL restores neointimal thickening, suggesting that TRAIL may contribute to AS development at an early stage (Chan et al. 2010). Moreover, TRAIL upregulated by arterial injury stimulates VSMC proliferation and neointima formation in mouse models (Chan et al. 2010). BAG3 upregulates TRAIL to promote the proliferation of rat VSMCs (Fu et al. 2018). Herein, we verified that exogenous TRAIL expression overthrew the inhibitory effects of METTL3 silencing on the proliferation and migration of mouse VSMCs with PDGF-BB stimulation. PJ34 repressed TRAIL expression by controlling METTL3-mediated methylation of TRAIL mRNA. Importantly, we validated this mechanism in vivo by showing that PJ34 significantly suppressed TRAIL expression at the mRNA and protein levels, and TRAIL was predominantly localized in the neointimal and medial layers, indicating VSMC-derived expression. Furthermore, the vascular protective effect of PJ34 was abolished in TRAIL knockout mice, supporting the notion that TRAIL is a critical downstream effector of the PARP1–METTL3 axis. To rule out the possibility that global TRAIL deletion altered systemic immunity and confounded the vascular phenotype, we quantified circulating TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β levels. TRAIL knockout did not result in elevated plasma cytokines, and PJ34 failed to suppress inflammation in TRAIL-deficient mice, suggesting that the vascular changes were not driven by systemic immune modulation.

Taken together, our findings demonstrate that PJ34 alleviates vascular injury-induced IH by inhibiting METTL3-mediated m6A methylation of TRAIL mRNA and suppressing pathological activation of VSMCs. Notably, the TRAIL knockout mice used in this study were global knockouts, and we cannot fully exclude potential contributions from non-VSMC cell types. Future studies using smooth muscle cell-specific TRAIL knockout models, such as Myh11-Cre or SM22α-Cre, will be essential to dissect the precise cell-autonomous role of TRAIL. In addition, the m6A “reader” proteins that recognize and bind methylated TRAIL transcripts remain to be identified and represent an important direction for future work. Collectively, the therapeutic potential of PARP1 inhibition is highlighted in this study as a novel epigenetic strategy to prevent or treat IH.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the PARP1 inhibitor PJ34 suppresses IH by regulating METTL3-mediated m6A methylation of TRAIL, offering a rationale and potential therapeutic targets for IH prevention and treatment.
