Evaluation of plant community composition and ecological benefits based on six urban greenway plant species
Authors: Shuxin Bian, Yibing Feng, Yang Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With the acceleration of urbanization, urban greenways, as an important part of urban ecosystems, play a crucial role in maintaining ecological balance and enhancing urban environmental quality through the diversity and structure of their plant communities. This study analyzed 30 sample sites from 10 urban greenways in Minhang District, Shanghai, focusing on 4 biodiversity indices (SRI, SDI, SWDI, SE) and 5 ecological benefit indicators (microclimate adjustment, negative oxygen ion provision, PM2.5/PM10/TSP reduction, noise reduction, wind protection). The results showed that mixed vegetation types (MDEBF and MCBF) had significant advantages in biodiversity, their SRI reached 11.82 ± 1.37 and 13.00 ± 0.00, SDI reached 0.47 ± 0.05 and 0.52 ± 0.11, SWDI reached 0.81 ± 0.08 and 0.87 ± 0.20, and SE reached 0.63 ± 0.05 and 0.65 ± 0.14, all significantly higher than other types (P < 0.05). In terms of ecological benefits, MDEBF and MCBF performed more prominently: MDEBF had an average cooling rate of 22.75%, negative oxygen ion CI enhancement rate of 62.45%, and PM10 reduction rate of 13.58%; MCBF had a PM2.5 reduction rate of 14.04% and wind speed attenuation rate of 50.97%, which were better than single vegetation types. In contrast, monoculture evergreen broad-leaved forest (EBF) performed relatively poorly in both biodiversity (the lowest SRI of 5.83 ± 1.28 and SWDI of 0.20 ± 0.03) and ecological benefits. In addition, structural characteristics of plant communities, such as mean canopy width and mean sub-leaf height, were significantly correlated with their ecological benefits (P < 0.05). The results of the study provide a scientific basis for optimizing the plant configuration of urban greenways and enhancing their ecological functions, which can help promote sustainable urban development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26352-y.

Introduction

When the history of human beings entered the era of industrial society due to the industrial revolution, cities developed, expanded and expanded rapidly with the help of new production tools. Today, more than half of the world’s population lives in urban areas, and according to the World Health Organization, this proportion will exceed 68% by 20501. In addition, with the development of human society, the demand of urban residents for green space resources has increased. Yet the total amount of green space resources available in cities has been gradually eroded by urbanization. Patchy and fragmented green spaces lose their original ecological role2,3. This has led to a series of ecological and environmental problems - a dramatic increase in greenhouse gases, a decline in forest resources, fragmentation of biological habitats, accelerated extinction of species, etc4,5. China has also noted this problem and has pointed to the need to vigorously promote the construction of an ecological civilization, reverse the trend of ecological and environmental deterioration and elevate sustainable development to the level of green development6,7.

Greenways, as a means of planning and design that harmonizes the relationship between people and the land and has important landscape ecological significance, have been gaining attention in recent years8. In 1987, the President’s Commission on Americans Outdoor first officially recognized the concept of greenways. And in the 1990s, the Greenway movement took off9. Urban greenways have a variety of functions for the overall space of a city, and they can optimize and enhance the entire urban space with a small footprint. Connecting, isolating and buffering the various elements of the city, in addition to the role of a link10,11. It has ecological, economic, social, landscape and other aspects of the comprehensive benefits, from a macro point of view, the urban greenway network to form a large landscape pattern with totality and completeness. In terms of ecological and environmental functions, urban greenway networks are conducive to nature conservation, balance of surface water resources, purification of the atmosphere, water resources and soil resources, etc12,13. Therefore, urban greenway is an effective way to reshape the ecological background of the city and enhance the ecological function of the city14.

Plant communities serve as the fundamental unit of urban greenways, forming the basis of the greenway ecosystem and indicative of the quality and sophistication of roadside landscape development15. Plant communities not only provide rich and varied biodiversity, but also improve the quality of the urban environment by absorbing harmful substances in the air and releasing oxygen16–20. These plant communities also absorb noise, reduce land erosion, filter rainwater and improve water quality, providing a comfortable and pleasant recreational space for urban residents21–25. Furthermore, studies have shown that green spaces with high plant diversity can not only enhance soil biodiversity but also improve ecosystem multifunctionality26. Meanwhile, the structure of green areas and the selection of plant species influence their appeal to insect pollinators, thereby affecting the provision of pollination services. Adopting near-natural design concepts and diversifying plant species in urban green space planning can significantly increase their ecological benefits27. Therefore, studying the plant communities of urban greenways helps to deeply understand the ecological functions, environmental benefits, landscape values and social benefits of urban greenways, provides a scientific basis for urban planning and management, and promotes sustainable urban development. In addition, by constructing a suitable ecological benefit evaluation index system, the optimal urban greenway plant community collocation can be found.

To address the lack of clarity on vegetation-biodiversity-ecological benefit relationships in urban greenways, this study aims to provide targeted support for greenway ecological optimization. In this study, we selected 30 sample sites from 10 urban greenways in Minhang District, Shanghai, and analyzed four biodiversity indices (species richness index (SRI), Simpson’s diversity index (SDI), Shannon-Wiener’s diversity index (SWDI), species evenness (SE)) and five ecological benefit indicators (microclimate adjustment, negative oxygen ion provision, atmospheric suspended particulate matter (PM2.5, PM10, TSP) reduction, noise reduction, wind protection) to explore differences in biodiversity, community structure characteristics, and ecological benefits among different vegetation types. Clarifies the performance of different vegetation types in key biodiversity indices and ecological benefit indicators, providing a scientific basis for optimizing plant configuration of urban greenways and enhancing their ecological functions, and thus helping to promote sustainable urban development.

Research methods

Sample plot selection

Sample plots were selected from the east, south, west and north of Minhang District, Shanghai (31°5’ N, 121°25’ E). Taking into account the different surrounding environments, such as residential, commercial, cultural and educational, factories, and countryside, etc. Including (1) Lianhua South Road Greenway; (2) Pukang Road Greenway; (3) Jianchuan Road Greenway; (4) Puxing Highway East Greenway; (5) Zhongchun Road East Greenway; (6) Wuzhong Road Greenway; (7) Yinqiu Road Greenway; (8) Humin Highway West Greenway; (9) Bei Hengjing Greenway; and (10) Pufang Road Greenway, with a total of 30 sample plots of 10 urban greenways (Fig. 1). Sampling will be concentrated between July and September 2024. Additionally, it is noted that the greenways in Minhang District were initially piloted in 2012, with large-scale construction occurring between 2015 and 2020. All greenways in this study are artificially designed and renovated, with vegetation consisting predominantly of artificially planted species. Originally transformed from roadside green belts or forest strips, the terrain of these greenways is generally flat, and topographic variations were considered negligible given the scale of the sample plots. Furthermore, a minimum distance of 20 m was maintained between adjacent sample plots to prevent mutual interference (Fig. 2).

Plant structural features

For trees greater than 1.5 m in height in the sample plots were surveyed and plant height, diameter at breast height, height below leaf level, crown spread and depression were determined and recorded. Herbaceous layers less than 1.5 m in height within the sample plots were surveyed and the maximum height of each plant species was measured, and the degree of depression of each plant was estimated using the Braun-Blanquet visual estimation method28.

Plant diversity

Commonly used species diversity indices were calculated by counting the number of plant species occurring in the sample plots, the total number of individuals in the plants, and the number of individuals in each plant species: the species richness index (SRI), Simpson’s diversity index (SDI), Shannon-Wiener’s diversity index (SWDI), and species evenness (SE). Pearson correlation analysis was employed to examine the relationship between community structure characteristics and species diversity in the study plots. The test of significance of differences between the four indices was done using one-way ANOVA and completed on IBM SPSS Statistics 24 software. Ecological benefit

Temperature and relative humidity, negative oxygen ions (NOx), suspended particulate matter (SPM), noise level, and wind speed were measured at 30 sample sites in Minhang District at different time periods. And conduct eco-efficiency evaluations.

Temperature and relative humidity measurement

The air temperature and humidity were measured at sampling sites perpendicular to a municipal road during light wind conditions. Measurements were conducted during three time periods: from 7:00 to 9:00 (covering morning exercise and commuting to work), from 12:00 to 13:00 (lunch breaks), and from 16:00 to 18:00 (evening leisure and commuting from work). Test points were positioned along municipal roads and within adjacent greenways, aligned perpendicularly to the road. Two UNI-T UT333 Mini Thermo-Hygrometers were used to simultaneously measure air temperature and humidity. The instruments have an operating temperature range of -20 °C to 60 °C and a humidity measurement limit of < 85% RH. Each measurement was repeated three times, with each repetition lasting 5 min, and the average value for each period was taken as the final sample value. All instruments were calibrated and measurements were taken at breathing height (1.2–1.5 m above ground level). Data were recorded immediately after each measurement.

Measurement of negative oxygen ions in air

Measurements of air anion concentrations were conducted at sampling sites arranged perpendicular to municipal roads under light wind conditions. The measurements were taken during three-time intervals: 7:00–9:00 (morning exercise and commute to work), 12:00–13:00 (lunch break), and 16:00–18:00 (evening leisure and commute from work). Test points were located along municipal roads and within adjacent greenways, forming a line perpendicular to the road. An anion tester was used to perform the measurements, with each session lasting 10 min and repeated one to two times. The average value for each period was adopted as the representative sample value. All measurements were taken at a breathing height of 1.2–1.5 m above ground level. Data were recorded immediately after each measurement.

Determination of atmospheric suspended particulate matter content

Measurements of airborne particulate matter (PM) content were conducted at sampling sites arranged perpendicular to municipal roads under light wind conditions. Monitoring was carried out during three time periods: 7:00–9:00 (covering morning exercise and commuting to work), 12:00–13:00 (lunch breaks), and 16:00–18:00 (evening leisure and commuting from work). Test points were located alongside municipal roads and within greenways, aligned perpendicularly to the roads. A particle meter (Aerocet 831 particle counter) was used to simultaneously measure PM2.5, PM10, and TSP concentrations. Each measurement was repeated three times, with a duration of five minutes per repetition. The average value for each period was taken as the final sample value. The instrument was calibrated prior to the measurements, which were conducted at a breathing height of 1.2–1.5 m. Data were recorded immediately after each measurement.

Noise level measurement

Noise level measurements were conducted under light wind conditions at locations perpendicular to a municipal road. The measurements were taken during three-time intervals: 7:00–9:00 (morning exercise and commuting to work), 12:00–13:00 (lunch breaks), and 16:00–18:00 (leisure and commuting from work). Test points were positioned alongside municipal roads and within greenways, forming a line perpendicular to the road. Two Sigma AS-824 decibel testers were used simultaneously for each measurement. Readings were taken over 10-minute intervals and repeated one to two times; the average value for each period was used as the final sample. An open area was selected as a control site for comparative measurements. All measurements were conducted at a height of 1.2–1.5 m above ground level. Care was taken to minimize interference from extraneous sound sources such as human activity, birds, and cicadas. Data were recorded on site immediately after each measurement.

Measurement of wind speed values

Wind speed was measured at sampling sites perpendicular to the town road during three time periods: 7:00–9:00 (morning exercise and commuting to work), 12:00–13:00 (lunch break), and 16:00–18:00 (leisure and commuting from work), under windy conditions. The test points were monitored simultaneously using two BENETECH GM8908 portable anemometers, capable of measuring both wind speed and temperature. Measurements were repeated one to two times, with each session lasting 10 min. The maximum and minimum wind speeds during each interval were recorded, and the average value for each period was used as the final sample value. All measurements were taken at a height of 1.2–1.5 m above ground level.

Results and discussion

Characterization of plant diversity and plant community structure

This study classified the plant communities across 30 sample plots according to their physiognomy and floristic composition, following the classification framework established in Vegetation of China29. Based on these criteria, the communities were categorized into six vegetation types: evergreen broad-leaved forest (EBF)、evergreen coniferous forest (ECF)、deciduous broad-leaved forest (DBF)、deciduous broad-leaved and evergreen broad-leaved mixed forest (MDEBF)、deciduous shrub forest (DSF) and coniferous and broad-leaved mixed forest (MCBF), based on the external appearance, species composition, and other characteristics of the plant communities. Three vegetation types, MDEBF、DBF and EBF, were dominant, with a frequency of occurrence of 36.67%, 23.33% and 20%, respectively, as shown in Table 1 (The original plant classification data can be found in Supplementary Material-original data 2).

Among them, there were 13 species in 12 families and 13 genera in the tree layer, 45 species in 26 families and 35 genera in the shrub layer, and 13 species in 7 families and 13 genera in the herb layer. It can be seen that the vegetation selected for the construction of the existing urban greenway is relatively rich.

Correlation of structural characteristics of plant communities with plant diversity

Different plant community types with different community structure characteristics can form greatly different plant diversity. The height, diameter at breast height, crown width, height under leaf, density and depression of plant communities in 30 sample plots were counted and correlated with species diversity (Detailed data on plot community structure are available in Supplementary Material-original data 3). As shown in Table 3, there was a significant correlation (P < 0.05) between the species richness index and mean height and mean sub-leaf height. There was a highly significant negative correlation (P < 0.01) between Simpson’s diversity index and mean height. Shannon-Weiner diversity index had a highly significant negative correlation (P < 0.01) with mean height and a significant negative correlation (P < 0.05) with mean sub-leaf height. Species evenness had a highly significant negative correlation (P < 0.01) with mean height and a significant negative correlation (P < 0.05) with mean crown width and mean sub-leaf height. This indicated that plant diversity was negatively correlated with height, crown width, underleaf height and depression of plant communities, i.e., the larger the values of the above four items, the poorer the plant diversity.

Taken together, the plant community structural characteristics in the urban greenways in Minhang District varied significantly, with MCBF and MDEBF being prominent, followed by DBF performing better, and EBF performing poorly. The plant diversity of the greenway was negatively correlated with the mean height and mean sub-leaf height of the plants in the forest, with the greater the canopy and sub-leaf height of the plants in the forest, the poorer the diversity of the plants in the forest. It is because for MCBF and MDEBF, which are rich in plant species and have a more complex spatial structure, such a spatial structure will make the overall community less depressed and the light permeability of the canopy greater, providing a good living environment for diverse organisms. At the same time, this complex plant community structure makes it easier to regulate the microclimate of the environment, resulting in a low-temperature and high humidity space, which facilitates the decomposition of dead leaves and fruits corrosion, and provides a more fertile soil for the whole greenway, so that it can have a good environment with good moisture, light, and nutrients30–33. As for MCBF, conifers have a smaller leaf area, and their canopies are able to let in more light, which, when combined with broadleaf mixes, also produces a wider variety of plant species.

Ecological benefits of greenway plant communities

Cooling and humidification benefits

In the summer when plant growth was vigorous, urban greenways played a better role in cooling and humidification (Table 4), This is consistent with the findings of other scholars34–37. The temperature inside the greenways of different vegetation types was significantly lower than that outside the greenways, and there were significant differences in the cooling function in the summer (P < 0.05). MDEBF, ECF, and DBF were significantly better than the other three community types in cooling in summer, with an average cooling rate of 22.75%, 21.69% and 22.54%, and DSF had the smallest average cooling with an average cooling rate of 9.74%. As far as relative humidity is concerned, the relative humidity of different vegetation types of communities within the greenway was significantly higher than that of the control outside the greenway, and the differences in vegetation community types within the greenway led to highly significant differences in humidification effects among different communities (P < 0.01), with the excellent humidification effects of EBF, MDEBF, and MCBF, with the relative humidity enhancement rates of 18.87%, 18.89%, and 18.19%, respectively. Limitations and outlook

This study examines greenways in Minhang District, Shanghai, an area with a distinct elongated shape and diverse urban functions, serving as a microcosm of urban development. However, as the district is still developing, the findings may have limitations in generalizing to more established or specialized greenways. Future research should include other Shanghai areas and greenways with specific functional orientations for comparative insights. Ecological benefits were assessed primarily from a user perspective, but future evaluations should incorporate broader ecological dimensions, such as soil, water, and stormwater management, into a multi-objective framework. Moreover, as greenways integrate ecological, social, cultural, and economic functions, planning should promote their transformation into multifunctional public spaces. Evaluation systems must also include socioeconomic factors to support cost-effective and publicly satisfying greenway development.

Conclusion

With the Industrial Revolution accelerating urbanization, issues such as rising greenhouse gases and habitat fragmentation have emerged. Urban greenways help mitigate these problems through efficient land use. This study assessed plant communities and ecological benefits along 10 greenways in Minhang District, Shanghai, using four biodiversity indices and five ecological benefit indicators. Results showed that mixed vegetation types (MDEBF and MCBF) performed best. MDEBF achieved an SRI of 11.82 ± 1.37, a cooling rate of 22.75%, and a negative oxygen ion increase of 62.45%; MCBF reached an SRI of 13.00 ± 0.00, a PM2.5 reduction of 14.04%, and wind speed attenuation of 50.97%, significantly exceeding other types (P < 0.05). In contrast, evergreen broadleaf forest (EBF) had the lowest SRI (5.83 ± 1.28) and diversity (SWDI = 0.20 ± 0.03). Canopy width was positively correlated with cooling (r = 0.513) and noise reduction (r = 0.362), while sub-leaf height negatively correlated with PM2.5 reduction (r=-0.419) and wind speed attenuation (r=-0.428). These findings offer data-supported guidance for optimizing greenway design to enhance ecological services and urban sustainability.
