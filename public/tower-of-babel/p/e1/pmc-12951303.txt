To the Editor:

The bronchodilator response (BDR) test is commonly used in asthma diagnosis workflow and is typically assessed through spirometry. A positive BDR test supports the diagnosis when clinical suspicion of asthma exists. However, other methods, such as respiratory oscillometry, offer additional insights of respiratory resistance and reactance through soundwave-generated oscillations during quiet tidal breathing, thus providing valuable information about small airway function often overlooked by spirometry [1]. Spirometry primarily measures airflow limitation in larger central airways, whereas respiratory oscillometry, in addition to being sensitive to larger central airways, also captures dysfunction in smaller peripheral airways, potentially identifying early or subtle respiratory dysfunction complementary to spirometry. Nevertheless, the combined clinical value of spirometry and respiratory oscillometry in evaluating bronchodilator response in airway function remains to be fully elucidated.

We have previously defined the normal range of BDR in impulse oscillometry (IOS) in the Swedish CArdioPulmonary bioImage Study (SCAPIS) [2]. Using these recently established BDR thresholds for IOS, this study aimed to assess firstly the associations between BDR measured by 1) spirometry, 2) IOS, and 3) both methods combined, secondly clinical outcomes, including respiratory symptoms (wheeze, cough), respiratory diseases (asthma, COPD) and inflammatory biomarkers (blood eosinophil counts (B-Eos), fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FENO)). We hypothesised that there is an additive role of assessing airway responsiveness with both IOS and spirometry in relation to presence of respiratory symptoms, and inflammatory profiles compared to either method alone.

This cross-sectional analysis included 3465 participants from the Uppsala cohort of the population-based SCAPIS. IOS was assessed 15 min after inhalation of 400 µg salbutamol, followed by spirometry, using the Jaeger MasterScreen IOS and PFT systems (CareFusion, Höchberg, Germany). Spirometry and IOS were performed according to European Respiratory Society/American Thoracic Society technical standards, with exception of performing only two IOS acquisitions (30 s each) [3, 4]. The mean value of the two IOS acquisitions was reported. BDR by spirometry was defined as an increase of ≥12% and 200 mL in forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), while BDR by IOS was defined as a decrease of ≥29% in resistance at 5 Hz (R5) or an improvement of ≥45% in reactance at 5 Hz (X5) [2]. Data on self-reported respiratory symptoms, respiratory diseases and biomarkers were collected at enrolment. FEV1 was selected as the primary spirometric measure of interest due to its established clinical relevance in asthma diagnostics.

Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata/IC 16.1 software. Comparisons utilised Chi-squared and Kruskal–Wallis tests, with associations assessed via multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, and smoking status (p<0.05).

Descriptive baseline characteristics of the study population (n=3465) consisted of 51.1% women and the participants had a mean age of 57.6 years. Current smoking was reported by 6.8% of participants. The prevalence of respiratory symptoms was: 18.0% for cough, 9.5% for sputum production, 6.5% for dyspnoea, and 6.5% for wheeze. Self-reported physician diagnosed asthma was found by 6.3% and COPD and/or chronic bronchitis and/or emphysema in 4.9%.

Four BDR groups were identified: 1) no BDR in R5, X5 or FEV1 (n=2899); 2) BDR only in R5 and/or X5 (n=303); 3) BDR only in FEV1 (n=171); and 4) BDR in both R5 and/or X5 and FEV1 (n=92). Participants with BDR in both R5, X5 and/or FEV1 were more often male, had a higher body mass index and were more likely to be smokers. The prevalence of asthma, wheeze, cough, dyspnoea as well as levels of FENO and B-Eos, were higher among participants with BDR only in FEV1 and those with BDR in both R5 and/or X5 and FEV1, with the largest differences observed in the combined group. For instance, asthma prevalence was lowest among participants without BDR (5.2%), increased in those with BDR only in FEV1 (13.7%, p<0.001), and was highest in participants exhibiting combined BDR (23.6%, p<0.001). Similarly, median FENO levels were higher in participants with combined BDR (19 ppb, p<0.001) and in those with BDR only in FEV1 (17 ppb, p<0.001) compared to participants without BDR (16.0 ppb). In contrast, COPD and/or chronic bronchitis/emphysema prevalence was significantly higher only in the combined BDR group (11.1%, p<0.005) versus participants without BDR (4.7%).

BDR only in R5 and/or X5 showed no associations with respiratory symptoms, pulmonary diseases, or biomarker levels after adjustment for age, sex, smoking status, and body mass index >30 kg·m−2. In contrast, when BDR was present only in FEV1, significant associations were observed with cough, wheeze, and asthma; adjusted odds ratio (OR) ranging from 1.54 to 3.13. Notably, individuals with combined BDR (R5 and/or X5 and FEV1) had the highest likelihood of respiratory symptoms, pulmonary disease, and elevated biomarker levels, with OR ranging from 1.83 to 7.21 (table 1).

It was somewhat unexpected that BDR only in R5 and/or X5 did not relate to the studied outcomes, given previous single time-point oscillometry studies linking abnormal IOS to respiratory burden, even when spirometry was normal [5]. A possible explanation is that isolated IOS-defined BDR may occur very early in the disease process, before the development of clinical symptoms or disease appear, or that such responses are of lower clinical relevance. By contrast, when BDR was present in both IOS and spirometry indices, indicating involvement of both peripheral [6] and central airways, the bronchodilator response appears to be of broader clinical importance, resulting in stronger associations with symptoms, disease, and inflammatory biomarkers. This supports the complementary roles of the methods in characterising BDR, in a similar way with the combined value of the methods to characterise airway obstruction [5]. Prior studies support this approach, for example, Sheen et al. [1] found that children with small airway hyperresponsiveness and positive BDR in both FEV1 and IOS were more likely to have asthma, wheezing, and atopic dermatitis (n=575). Similarly, Cottee et al. [7] reported that IOS was more sensitive than spirometry in detecting bronchodilator response, which was associated with poorly controlled asthma in adults (n=52).

We could not find any relationship between BDR only in R5 and/or X5 and respiratory symptoms, as opposed to Lu et al. [8] (n=466), who reported that positive BDR by IOS alone was associated with wheeze and cough. These differences might be due to population differences; our cohort comprised healthy, middle-aged Swedish adults, while Lu and colleagues studied Chinese adults (>40 years) with early COPD. The relationships between BDR assessed by FEV1 align with previous results. For example, Tan et al. [9] reported in a large population of adult subjects (n=4669) that BDR in FEV1 related with higher likelihood of asthma and that BDR in FEV1 was linked to wheeze in subjects without asthma or COPD diagnosis (n=3508). Regarding inflammatory biomarkers, participants with positive BDR by both spirometry and IOS exhibited significantly higher B-Eos counts and FENO levels, indicating a higher degree of type-2 inflammation that could be associated with asthma and airway hyperresponsiveness [10].

Strengths include the large, population-based design and the fact that the normal range of BDR in IOS indices was defined in the present population. Limitations include the narrow age range, one site-study design, and cross-sectional approach that only allows to study concurrent associations. Additionally, lack of longitudinal clinical data limits validation of predictive and diagnostic implications. Another limitation is that only two repeated IOS measurements were performed, fewer than current guideline recommendations [11]. However, differences between a single measurement and the mean of three measurements were found to be minimal in another Swedish, population-based study, suggesting one measurement may be sufficient [12].

In conclusion, participants demonstrating a positive BDR in both IOS and FEV1 exhibited the highest prevalence of respiratory symptoms, asthma, COPD and elevated inflammatory biomarkers, emphasising the potential value of combining these methods for a comprehensive evaluation of bronchodilator responsiveness, particularly involving peripheral airway function. Future longitudinal studies should investigate whether the combined spirometry-IOS assessment of BDR provides clinically useful information in regard to treatment response in patients that receive asthma diagnosis and study the value of the methods for respiratory outcomes in population-based studies.
