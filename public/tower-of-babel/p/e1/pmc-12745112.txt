Safety, efficacy, and patient satisfaction after esophagectomy for esophageal neuromotor disease: A single-institution experience
Authors: Samantha L Savitch, Jonathan E Williams, Andrew C Chang, Rishindra Reddy, Jules Lin, Mark B Orringer, Kiran H Lagisetty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEsophagectomy remains the mainstay of treatment for patients with end-stage esophageal neuromotor diseases, yet there are limited data on outcomes after esophagectomy in this population. We sought to characterize the safety and efficacy of esophagectomy for esophageal neuromotor disease.MethodsWe retrospectively queried a prospectively collected database of all esophagectomies performed at our institution from 1975 to 2023 for patients without cancer diagnosed with end-stage esophageal neuromotor disease. Operative characteristics, perioperative and functional outcomes, and mortality were evaluated. Patients with and without a previous esophageal operation were compared using χ2 for categorical variables and t test for continuous variables.ResultsWe identified 237 patients for analysis. The majority were female (57.0%), White (82.7%), and nonsmokers (83.7%). Mean age was 52.4 years. In total, 185 (78.1%) patients had a diagnosis of achalasia, and 72.2% of patients underwent a previous esophageal operation. The majority of esophagectomies (91.6%) were transhiatal, and a gastric conduit was used in 228 patients (96.2%). Anastomotic leak rate was 9.7% (23/237). One-year mortality was 2.5% (6/237). There were no differences in outcomes between patients who had and had not had a previous esophageal operation. Postoperative functional symptoms were reported in 69.7% of patients at first follow-up, and 97 (40.9%) patients required postoperative dilation for symptom management. The majority of patients reported that they felt better (72%) and would undergo esophagectomy again (72%).ConclusionsEsophagectomy for esophageal neuromotor disease is safe and feasible with a gastric conduit, regardless of prior esophageal operations. Despite persistent postoperative functional symptoms, patients have high satisfaction after the procedure.Key Words: esophageal dysmotility, achalasia, esophagectomy, mega esophagus

Central Message

Esophagectomy for end-stage neuromotor disease is safe, feasible with a gastric conduit, and associated with a high level of patient satisfaction.

Perspective

Esophagectomy is the mainstay of treatment for end-stage esophageal neuromotor disease, yet patients frequently endure years of symptoms before referral for definitive treatment due to concerns regarding the safety of esophagectomy for this population. In a large single-institution study, we found esophagectomy to be safe and feasible for these patients with high levels of patient satisfaction.

Esophageal neuromotor disease is prevalent in the United States, with approximately 5% of these patients eventually developing end-stage disease.1 The mainstay of treatment for these patients remains esophagectomy,2 and despite being the definitive management for end-stage neuromotor disease, limited data are available on outcomes after the operation in this patient population.

It has been previously shown that patients undergoing esophagectomy for end-stage achalasia have relatively low complication rates, with a recent meta-analysis demonstrating a combined morbidity and mortality rate of less than 10%.3 Despite this, patients are often not referred to a thoracic surgeon for esophagectomy until late in the progression to end-stage disease, largely attributable to ongoing concerns regarding the safety and efficacy of the operation. Previous studies have relied on either very small case series1,4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 or large, retrospective datasets that lack granularity or follow-up data to elucidate long-term outcomes or quality of life.10,11 Furthermore, these studies only report on achalasia and do not include other esophageal neuromotor diseases.

In this context, we sought to characterize the safety and efficacy of esophagectomy for esophageal neuromotor disease at a large, high-volume institution. We hypothesized that patients would have good long-term outcomes and improved function after esophagectomy for end-stage esophageal neuromotor disease. The data contained herein comprise the largest single-institution cohort of esophagectomy for esophageal neuromotor disease to date, to our knowledge, and allow for enhanced granularity and breadth of information compared with previously published reports.

Methods

Patient Sample and Data Collection

The University of Michigan Department of Thoracic Surgery maintains a prospective database of all esophagectomies performed at the institution since 1975. Patient demographics, operative parameters, and postoperative outcomes are collected prospectively, initially by paper form and now by electronic form. All patients undergo a barium esophagram before hospital discharge to assess for anastomotic leak. Follow-up data, including patient symptoms, long-term complications, need for reintervention, and mortality, are recorded at each follow-up visit. A survey regarding patient satisfaction with the operation is administered in person or by phone at each follow-up visit. Patients are asked whether they feel better than preoperatively, whether they are pleased with the results of the operation, and whether, in retrospect, they would choose to undergo esophagectomy again. For the purposes of this study, the most recent responses to these follow-up surveys were used for analysis.

We retrospectively queried this prospective database for all patients between 1975 and 2023 who underwent esophagectomy with a diagnosis of end-stage esophageal neuromotor disease, including achalasia, diffuse esophageal spasm, mega-esophagus, or other dysmotility. Patients were excluded if they had a diagnosis of malignancy. Additional retrospective manual chart review was performed as needed to fill in missing or incomplete data, including comorbidities and preoperative functional symptoms, as these were not collected prospectively. Because of the retrospective nature of this study, no patient consent was required. Ethical approval was given by the University of Michigan institutional review board HUM00012731, approved April 23, 2007.

Outcomes

Preoperative demographics, operative characteristics, perioperative and functional outcomes, postoperative interventions, and mortality were evaluated. Patients with and without a previous esophageal operation were compared using χ2 for categorical variables and t test for continuous variables. All statistical analysis was performed using Stata, version 18.0 (StataCorp LLC).

Results

A total of 237 patients underwent esophagectomy for esophageal neuromotor disease during the study period, 3 of whom had a concomitant recurrent hiatal hernia. The majority of patients were female (135, 57.0%), White (196, 82.7%), and nonsmokers (128, 83.7%). Mean age was 52.4 years (standard deviation [SD] 16.2 years). In total, 185 (78.1%) patients had a primary diagnosis of achalasia, and 106 patients (44.7%) had a primary or secondary diagnosis of mega-esophagus. 72.2% (171/273) of patients had undergone a previous esophageal operation, of which 129 (75.4%) were a myotomy. Comorbidity data were available for 158 of 237 patients. Of those, 86 (54.4%) had at least 1 major comorbidity, the most common being hypertension (41/158, 26.0%) and diabetes (41/158, 26.0%) (Table 1).

The majority of esophagectomies performed were transhiatal (217/237, 91.6%), with a gastric conduit used in 231 patients (97.5%). In the 6 patients for whom a gastric conduit was not used, a colon interposition was performed. A pyloromyotomy was performed in 94.5% of cases (224/237), and a jejunostomy tube was placed for feeding access in 96.6% of patients (229/237). The number of cases performed per year over the study period ranged from 1 to 10 cases (mean 5.3 cases/year). The majority of cases were via an open approach (227/237, 95.8%); minimally invasive techniques were first undertaken in 2015 and were used for 10 of the 26 cases performed between 2015 and 2023. The overall complication rate was 49.0% (116/237), with the most common nonleak complications being pneumonia (19/237, 8.0%), respiratory failure (17/237, 7.2%), atrial fibrillation (12/237, 5.1%), and ileus (13/237, 5.5%). Rate of anastomotic leak was 9.7% (23/237), with 6 (31.6%) requiring reoperation to address the leak before discharge. Median length of stay was 10 days (interquartile range, 7-12 days). There were 2 in-hospital deaths, one of which occurred on postoperative day 1 because of cardiac arrest and the other occurred on postoperative day 32 because of pneumonia, sepsis, and multisystem organ failure. There were 4 additional deaths within 1 year postoperatively, none of which were the result of operative complications. There was no significant difference in hospital length of stay, anastomotic leak, intraoperative blood loss, operative approach, intensive care unit admissions, or 1-year mortality between patients who had and had not had a previous esophageal operation (Table 2).

Preoperative symptoms were available for 149 patients. Before the operation, 86.6% of patients (129/149) reported dysphagia and 54.3% of patients (81/149) endorsed regurgitation. Sixteen of 237 patients were lost to follow-up. Postoperative functional symptoms (dysphagia, cramping, diarrhea, regurgitation, and nausea) were reported in 69.7% of the remaining 221 patients at first follow-up (Figure 1). Mean time to first follow-up was 82.7 days (SD 461.2 days). In total, 176 of 221 patients had more than 1 follow-up visit. The percentage of patients with persistent symptoms did not significantly change at last follow-up visit (72.7% at last follow-up visit, P = .507), and follow-up data were available beyond 1 year for 149 of 237 patients (62.9%). Average time to last follow-up was 2077.6 days, and average time between first and last follow-up was 1992.4 days. 2.7% of patients reported severe symptoms at first follow-up, and 27.2% of patients reported moderate-to-severe symptoms at first follow-up. In total, 97 (40.9%) patients required at least 1 postoperative dilation for symptom management, with 54 of those (55.7%) requiring serial dilations. Average time to first dilation was 195.8 days (SD, 468.6 days). Only 2 patients required anastomotic revision over the mean follow-up period of 2033 days, one in 1979 and one in 1986. In total, 213 patients responded to at least 1 survey question regarding satisfaction with the operation. The majority of patients stated that they felt better (yes: 154/213, 72.3%; no: 7/213, 3.3%; undecided: 52/213, 24.4%), were pleased with the operation (yes: 154/212, 72.6%; no: 9/212, 4.3%; undecided: 49/212, 23.1%), and would undergo esophagectomy again (yes: 150/209, 71.8%; no: 5/209, 2.4%; undecided: 54/209, 25.8%). Patient satisfaction was significantly greater in the latter half of the study period (2000 and later) as compared with the first half of the study period, with 89.8% responding “yes” to feeling better (vs 57.4% before 2000, P < .001), 92.8% responding “yes” to being pleased with the operation (vs 55.7% before 2000, P < .001), and 91.6% responding “yes” to undergoing esophagectomy again (vs 55.3% before 2000, P < .001).

Discussion

In this single-institution experience, esophagectomy for end-stage esophageal neuromotor disease is associated with low risk of anastomotic leak and very low postoperative mortality (Figure 2). Although patients report persistent functional symptoms postoperatively, overall satisfaction with the operation is high, with most patients reporting that they would undergo esophagectomy again. Given these findings, esophagectomy is a safe operation for patients with end-stage esophageal neuromotor disease and should be considered as a definitive operation earlier after the onset of refractory end-stage disease. This work expands our previously published cohort of 22 patients from 198212 to 273 patients, and to our knowledge is the largest single-institution cohort of patients undergoing esophagectomy for end-stage esophageal neuromotor disease.

At our institution, any patient with neuromotor disease who has had a previous surgical intervention and has evidence of end-stage disease, such as mega-esophagus, sink trap deformity, or severe oral intolerance with or without anatomic changes, is offered definitive esophagectomy. Additional factors, such as chronic aspiration, that were not captured in our study may also lead to the pursuit of esophagectomy. Patients with end-stage esophageal dysfunction who have never undergone a surgical intervention and do not have any gross anatomic changes may be considered for less-invasive interventions. Although not the focus of this study, repeat myotomy is usually reserved for patients with ongoing symptoms but without end-stage disease. Our practice is to place preoperative feeding access for any patient with end-stage neuromotor disease undergoing esophagectomy who is chronically malnourished, usually via a nasogastric feeding tube. We find that feeding the stomach is easier for the patient and avoids an additional operation for jejunostomy-tube placement. Patients are maintained on tube feeds until their nutrition is optimized. Patients with chronic aspiration present an additional challenge, because there is a narrow window of opportunity for intervention as a result of frequent pneumonias and pulmonary insufficiency. In these cases, we will also often place a feeding tube and make patients nil per os to enhance nutrition and decrease the risk of aspiration. However, we may intervene on these patients sooner because of the ongoing risk of pneumonia and prolonged hospitalizations. This treatment algorithm has remained consistent at our institution over time; however, in earlier years of the study period, the threshold to offer esophagectomy was lower, and some patients may have undergone the procedure without any of the hard indications discussed herein.

Esophagectomy has long been considered a highly morbid procedure, the risks of which may be increased in patients with previous esophageal operations (including myotomy and hiatal hernia repair), mainly because of adhesive disease compromising the integrity of the gastric conduit leading to increased risk of complications such as anastomotic leak.13 However, most research regarding the safety of esophagectomy has been performed in patients undergoing esophagectomy for malignant disease, who often have additional risk factors for postoperative complications, such as compromised tissue from neoadjuvant chemoradiation, immunosuppression, and malnutrition. In contrast, the patients in our study with esophageal neuromotor disease are younger and healthier than the average patient with esophageal cancer, and the leak rate in our study is expectedly lower than the 15% to 20% reported in the literature on cancer.14, 15, 16, 17 Furthermore, the majority of the leaks in our cohort were either subclinical or managed nonoperatively, with less than one third of the patients who experienced a leak requiring operative intervention. It should be noted that our cohort contained a large percentage of patients who had previously undergone an esophageal operation, which is reflective of the majority of patients with end-stage esophageal neuromotor disease.1,8,18 Importantly, we did not find any differences in outcomes in patients who had and had not had a previous esophageal operation, highlighting the safety of esophagectomy for these patients regardless of their surgical history.

It is estimated that 10% to 20% of patients with a dysmotility disorder will require more than one intervention in their lifetime,19,20 with 1 study showing that more than 70% of patients with end-stage disease have previously undergone multiple previous surgical interventions.5 Although esophagectomy is considered the only definitive management for end-stage neuromotor dysfunction, with the growing availability of peroral endoscopic myotomy, which has been shown to have lower adverse events and shorter operative times compared with surgical myotomy,21 some would argue for repeat myotomy for recurrence of disease. Notably, research has demonstrated poor results with repeat interventions, leading to delays in esophagectomy and consequently delays in symptom relief.4 A study by Glatz and Richardson4 showed that patients will experience symptoms for an average of 12.5 years from time of initial diagnosis until esophagectomy. These delays may be attributable to concerns over the safety of esophagectomy and therefore the decision to pursue alternate treatment modalities. That said, the progression to end-stage disease and mega-esophagus may be unavoidable regardless of improving treatment modalities, as this pathophysiologic esophageal dilation is not reversible. Patients with any signs of mega-esophagus with “sink trap” deformity, wherein the dependent portion of the esophagus is inferior to the lower esophageal sphincter and cannot empty by gravity, are likely to have an unsuccessful myotomy. This can often be seen on preoperative upper gastrointestinal series and endoscopy; however, it is sometimes discovered intraoperatively during myotomy. Given the high failure rates of myotomy for patients who have progressed to mega-esophagus, and based on the findings in our study, these patients should be readily referred for esophagectomy, regardless of time since initial diagnosis or number of previous esophageal interventions. This provides the best chance at definitive management and symptomatic improvement for these patients.

There are many misconceptions regarding esophagectomy, mainly that it is a morbid procedure with low patient satisfaction. Although some studies have shown that quality of life after esophagectomy is poor,22, 23, 24 there are others who have shown improved quality of life at longer time points.25,26 Importantly, much of the quality-of-life work in esophagectomy centers on patients with malignant disease, who typically had normal swallowing function before their cancer diagnosis and operation. Patients with end-stage esophageal neuromotor disease, in contrast, are distinctly different given the natural history of their disease and resultant preoperative symptoms. The restrictive postoperative diet and lifestyle, while a burden to patients with normal preoperative function, may be an improvement for patients with dysmotility. Our findings support this, with high satisfaction ratings from patients despite ongoing functional symptoms. There have been few quality-of-life analyses performed in patients undergoing esophagectomy for benign indications. A study by Jones and colleagues18 showed persistent symptoms impacting quality of life after esophagectomy for achalasia, which is consistent with our findings of persistent functional symptoms in more than two thirds of our study population. In contrast, the patients in our study expressed extremely high levels of satisfaction with the operation, which is in keeping with another small cohort study that found both improved symptom scores and satisfaction with the operation.27

There are some limitations to this study that should be addressed. First, patients in our study were treated at a single institution, thus limiting the generalizability of the results. Second, although we included patients with all types of esophageal neuromotor disease, the majority of our patients had achalasia, and our results may therefore not apply to all patients with other forms of esophageal dysmotility. Third, despite this being a relatively large single-institution cohort, the sample size is still small, and we may have been underpowered to detect differences in patients who did and did not undergo prior esophageal operations. Finally, the survey questions that we ask patients at follow-up are not part of a standardized score or validated questionnaire. Future work using validated, standardized patient satisfaction instruments would be of benefit in this patient population.

Conclusions

Our findings have several key implications for the operative management of end-stage esophageal neuromotor disorders. First, esophagectomy for esophageal neuromotor disease appears safe and feasible with a gastric conduit, regardless of prior esophageal operations. In addition, despite persistent postoperative functional symptoms, patients who undergo esophagectomy for neuromotor disease report high satisfaction following the procedure. On the basis of these findings, esophagectomy should be considered for patients with end-stage esophageal dysfunction for definitive management of symptoms. Further prospective studies are needed to determine the optimal timing for referral for esophagectomy for patients with persistent symptoms after initial surgical intervention.
