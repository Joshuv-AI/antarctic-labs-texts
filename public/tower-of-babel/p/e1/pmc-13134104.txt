Evaluation of the resilience status of children with inherited metabolic disorders and the levels of their mothers’ burnout and resilience
Authors: Ekin Özsaydi Aktaşoğlu, Ayşe Akyüz, Sabire Gökalp, Ayşe Nur Altun, Fevzi Demi̇r, Elif Gönen, Azime Şebnem Soysal Acar, Aslı İnci̇, İlyas Okur, Fatih Süheyl Ezgü, Leyla Tümer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe resilience status of patients with metabolic diseases and their mothers’ resilience and burnout were evaluated. The relationship between resilience and burnout scores was further investigated. The effects of other related factors, such as sociodemographic status, on these parameters.MethodsThe study was conducted at the Gazi University Faculty of Medicine Pediatric Nutrition and Metabolism Outpatient Clinic. Patients’ mothers were asked to fill out the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) and the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), while only the CD-RISC was administered to patients aged 10–25.ResultsThe mean CD-RISC scores of mothers and children were 71.65 ± 14.98 and 67.89 ± 19.35, respectively, and the mean total score of the mothers received from MBI was 17.36 ± 10.00. A positive relationship was detected between mothers and their children’s resilience. The burnout level of working mothers was lower than that of the non-working group, and the difference was statistically significant, but working status did not affect the CD-RISC score.ConclusionsThis is the first study to evaluate the resilience and burnout levels of mothers of children suffering from inherited metabolic disorders. It emphasizes the importance of assessing the psychosocial status of patients who suffer from chronic diseases and their families.

Background

Chronic diseases, onset in childhood, affect not only the children suffering from the disease but also their caregivers and the entire family [1, 2]. Inherited metabolic disorders (IMDs) are genetic conditions that affect metabolic pathways in the body and cause chronic conditions [3], and some are treatable [4, 5]. The patients’ survival has increased due to the availability of appropriate treatment options. However, in addition to the primary treatment, the patients and families facing the burden of these diseases also need to be supported psychologically.

Chronic diseases may have a detrimental effect on the quality of life, and it can be predicted that patients suffering from chronic diseases or their caregivers may lose their resilience and become more exhausted. Resilience is a person’s ability to cope with stress. With increasing interest in positive psychology, research started to be published to determine the resilience status of patients [6] and their families [7]. Also, existing studies investigated psychological factors such as burnout and depression symptom levels of parents of children with diagnoses of chronic conditions such as hypoplastic left heart syndrome [8], autism spectrum disorders [9], cystic fibrosis, and primary ciliary dyskinesia [10]. Studies focusing on IMD revealed that anxiety or depression was observed in the parents of patients followed up with phenylketonuria [11]. In another study, it was observed that the depression scores of the parents of children who experienced a metabolic crisis were higher than those of the parents of children who did not [12]. In another study conducted with the parents of children followed up with mucopolysaccharidosis (MPS) type III, it was found that the anxiety and depression scores of the parents were above the clinical cut-off [13]. However, there are limited studies on individuals with IMD and their families.

To the best of our knowledge, no studies have evaluated resilience and burnout among patients with IMDs and their families. This study aims to evaluate the resilience status of mothers of patients with IMDs, the burnout status of mothers, and the links between these parameters. It also investigates the effects of sociodemographic status, duration of exposure to the disease, dietary intake status, and the presence of comorbidities on the resilience and burnout status of mothers.

Methods

Study participants

The mothers of patients with confirmed IMDs who applied to the Gazi University Faculty of Medicine pediatric nutrition and metabolism outpatient clinic and agreed to participate in the study were included. Patients aged between 10 and 25 whose mothers were also included in the survey, participated. Informed consent to participate was obtained from the parents of participants under 16.

Since some IMDs are accompanied by mental-motor retardation, patients who were literate, able to continue their education, and volunteered to participate in the study were asked to fill out the CD-RISC. Illiterate individuals with severe neurological problems and their mothers were not included in the study. Since IMDs have chronic conditions, mothers were considered to have mild depression. A depression scale was not used, and therefore, depression in mothers was not determined as an exclusion criterion. The study was planned in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki between September and November 2021; informed consent forms were obtained from the participants and approved by the Gazi University Ethics Commission (No: 2021 − 837).

A form was created to evaluate patients’ and their families’ demographic and socioeconomic status. Parents’ ages, living status, education level, working status, family income, divorce status, the number of children, family type (number of people living together), presence of chronic disease in another family member, and presence of the same IMDs in another child were collected. Only the working status, not the parents’ occupations, was questioned. In addition, in the same form, data regarding the patient’s age, diagnosis, age at diagnosis, diet/medication status, presence of comorbidities, presence of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) or specific learning disability (SLD), need for special education programme and attendance of the school level were learned.

Study instruments

The Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) and the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) were used in the study. Permission was obtained to use the CD-RISC and MBI. Since the patients’ primary caregivers are the mothers of most patients, the research instruments were administered only to the patients’ mothers. CD-RISC and MBI were administered to the patients’ mothers, but only the patients were administered CD-RISC. The study’s sample size was calculated separately for both CD-RISC and MBI. Using G*Power 3.1.9.4 software, when evaluated for MBI, it was seen that at least 90 patients and evaluated for CD-RISC, at least 25 patients should be included. When determining the sample size, it was assumed that there could be a 10% data loss, and the sample size was calculated against the data loss, and the required sample size differed according to the measurement tools used in the study. Because the minimum sample size for statistical analysis of the MBI was larger, 99 mothers were included, and both the MBI and the CD-RISC were administered. In contrast, the minimum sample size required for statistical analysis of the CD-RISC was smaller; therefore, this scale was administered to both children and their mothers, and 28 children were included in the study. The number of children was determined to ensure statistical compatibility with the CD-RISC analysis performed in mothers.

MBI was developed in 1981 [14] and has been used since the Turkish adaptation [15], demonstrating its validity and reliability [16]. MBI is a Likert-type scale with 22 items divided into three subscales: ' emotional exhaustion’, ‘depersonalization’, and ‘personal accomplishment’. Emotional exhaustion and depersonalization subscales are scored as never = 0, very rarely = 1, sometimes = 2, often = 3, and always = 4. In this study, the personal accomplishment subscale was reverse-coded and scored because it contained positive judgments and was termed as ‘low personal accomplishment’. The scores obtained from the subscales range from 0–36 for emotional exhaustion, 0–20 for depersonalization, and 0–32 for low personal accomplishment. The scores obtained from the 3 subscales were evaluated separately, and the MBI total score was computed by summing the subscale scores. The MBI total score was used as the primary outcome for the main comparative analyses to provide a single summary measure and limit additional multiple testing. High scores on three subscales and the total score indicate severe burnout [17].

CD-RISC was developed by Connor and Davidson [18], and a Turkish validation study by Karaırmak showed that it is a usable scale for the Turkish population [19]. CD-RISC is a Likert-type scale which contains 25 items and each of them rated on a 5-point scale: not true at all = 0, rarely true = 1, sometimes true = 2, often true = 3, true nearly all of time = 4. The total score ranges from 0 to 100, and a high score predicts high resilience [18].

Statistical analyses

Analyses were conducted using SPSS Version 23 (IBM SPSS Statistics, IBM Corporation). Mean, standard deviation, median, and minimum and maximum values are presented as descriptive statistics. Mother’s education level, working status, presence of the same metabolic disease in another child, presence of chronic disease in another family member, children’s diet status, the presence of ADHD or SLD, the need for special education, or the presence of comorbidities were treated as binary variables. Normal distribution evaluation of quantitative data was made with objectives such as Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, coefficient of variation, skewness, kurtosis values, and subjective criteria such as histogram, box-plot graph, etc. The Spearman correlation test was used to assess correlations between quantitative data. Given the exploratory nature of the analyses, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied.

Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate independent factors associated with the maternal Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) total score. Variables included in the regression models were selected based on clinical relevance and supported by findings from univariate analyses. All selected variables were entered simultaneously into the models using the enter method. Model assumptions were assessed by examining residual distributions and model fit statistics. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factor (VIF) values. In addition, a secondary linear regression analysis was performed to explore factors associated with maternal resilience, as measured by the Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), using similar covariates to those included in the burnout models. p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Main findings

Ninety-nine mothers and 28 children between the ages of 10–25 were enrolled in the study. Considering 99 patients, the mean age of the children was 109.80 ± 68.72 months, and the mean age at diagnosis was 18.96 ± 34.76 months. The large standard deviation reflects the wide age range at diagnosis. The median age at diagnosis was 3 (0-144) months. Fifty-eight (58.6%) patients were receiving diet therapy, and 82 (82.8%) patients were using medication such as drug therapy or enzyme replacement therapy (ERT). Five (5.1%) patients had SLD, 11 (11.1%) patients had ADHD, 14 (14.1%) patients had both of them, and 32 (32.3%) patients were attending special education programs. Twenty-six (26.3%) of the patients had comorbidities, while 73 (73.7%) had no additional disease. The largest diagnostic group comprised phenyalanine metabolism disorders (26.3%). The mean CD-RISC score of mothers and children was 71.65 ± 14.98 and 67.89 ± 19.35, respectively (Fig. 2). The mean total scores the mothers received from the MBI were 17.36 ± 10.00 (Fig. 2), and the scores for emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and low personal accomplishment were 8.68 ± 6.12, 2.65 ± 2.47, and 6.02 ± 4.30, respectively. The CD-RISC score of the patient and her mother, who was followed up with cobalamin C deficiency and had another sibling with the same disease, was found to be the lowest score. The child of the mother with the highest MBI score was being followed up with phenylketonuria. When the relationship between mothers’ CD-RISC scores and other scores was evaluated, mothers’ CD-RISC scores were negatively correlated with their MBI scores (r= -0.468, p = < 0.001) and positively correlated with children’s CD-RISC scores (r = 0.509, p = 0.006). It was found that as mothers’ resilience increases, their burnout decreases. Also, a positive relationship was observed between mothers’ and children’s resilience (Table 3).

Subgroup analyses

The relationship between age at diagnosis and the time of scale completion and scores was examined to examine the effect of the lifetime spent with the disease on mothers’ burnout and resilience, and on their children’s resilience. Although a negative correlation was found between the mothers’ MBI low personal accomplishment subscale score and the lifetime spent with the disease to date, this association was not statistically significant (r= -0.061, p = 0.547). Likewise, a statistically non-significant negative correlation exists between children’s CD-RISC score and the lifetime spent with the disease to date (r = -0.051, p = 0.796) (Table 4).

Mother’s education level, working status, presence of the same metabolic disease in another child, and presence of chronic disease in another family member were treated as binary variables in the analysis. No statistically significant differences were observed between mothers’ CD-RISC scores and these parameters (p = 0.073, p = 0.768, p = 0.417, p = 0.772, respectively). Also, there were no significant differences in mothers’ CD-RISC scores according to children’s diet status, the presence of ADHD or SLD, the need for special education, or the presence of comorbidities (p = 0.418, p = 0.976, p = 0.817, p = 0.784, respectively) (Table 5). When mothers’ MBI scores were compared across these variables, only working status showed a statistically significant difference (p = 0.023) (Table 5). In other words, the burnout level of non-working mothers was higher than that of the working group. Disease duration was not significantly associated with MBI (Table 6) or CD-RISC (Table 7) scores in either the unadjusted or adjusted models (p > 0.05). In contrast, mothers’ working status remained independently associated with the outcome after adjustment for mothers’ education and caregiving-related variables, whereas mothers’ education level, presence of special education, and presence of comorbidity were not significant predictors. Although the income levels of most families were around the hunger threshold during that period, no statistically significant relationships were found between income level and CD-RISC and MBI scores (p = 0.634, p = 0.928, respectively).

Discussion

Although there is a large number of studies determining the psychological status of children with chronic diseases and their families, followed up with different diagnoses, there were limited studies on individuals with IMDs. In this study, we showed the burnout status of mothers of children with different IMDs. Instead of focusing entirely on the negative effects of the diseases, we also investigated the resiliency of mothers and their children. In our study, we observed that mothers’ resilience is high and their burnout is relatively low. This is closely related to the value given to children. In societies like ours, caring for a patient is not something to complain about or handle; it can enhance their resilience.

IMDs are chronic diseases that often have no satisfactory treatment or require long-term, difficult-to-apply dietary regimens. In most families, mothers cover daily follow-up and care for these patients. In our study, the scales were administered to mothers because the primary caregiver of most patients was their mothers. In a study in which the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) and the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory S and T (STAI S and T) were used, the scores of mothers of children with phenylketonuria were found to be higher than those of mothers of healthy children, but burnout status was not evaluated. In the same study, low education level was found to be an independent factor for depression [11]. Given that educational status affects job status, the authors who developed the MBI stated that the relationship between burnout and educational level is difficult to interpret [14]. In our study, although education level did not affect burnout and resilience scores, mothers with less than a high school degree had higher CD-RISC scores and lower MBI scores. Based on this data, these mothers appear to be more resilient and less exhausted. Individuals with less contact with the outside world and daily routines have higher resilience. Because they only complete their ‘to-do list’. If they have strong social support (husband, neighbors, relatives, etc.) and are in their ‘comfort zone’ where they can express themselves and feel safe, burnout decreases even more. Those with higher levels of education have higher expectations of themselves. Especially if they are not working and do not have an area outside the home where they can show themselves, their burnout can be expected to be lower.

Given the working situation, burnout scores among working mothers were significantly lower than those among non-working mothers. In a study investigating the effects of social support on the burnout status of mothers of patients suffering from complex diseases, it was found that actively participating in social support programs and helping others positively reduced burnout [20]. Working mothers are more involved in their social environment, leave home for even short periods, and strive not only for their children and families but also for their personal development; it can be predicted that they will be less exhausted. It can be thought that mothers with a certain level of education and who work in areas where they can use their education, positively affect the psychological status of both mothers and the individuals they are responsible for caring for.

In a previous study conducted with mothers of children followed up with IMD, BDI, and STAI-S and STAI-T were used. Ninety-three mothers were included in this study, and their children’s diagnoses were urea cycle defect, organic acidemia, maple syrup urine disease, hereditary fructose intolerance, phenylketonuria, galactosemia, and non-ketotic hyperglycinemia. Depression scores were found to be higher in mothers of patients whose child had the same diet and had previously experienced or were at risk of experiencing a metabolic crisis [12]. In our study, the dietary treatment of the patients had no effect on the resilience or burnout status of the mothers; however, the mothers’ depression or anxiety conditions were not examined. Furthermore, the CD-RISC scores of mothers of children receiving diet therapy were higher. In a more comprehensive study, evaluating mothers’ burnout and resilience status with depression or anxiety scores will be useful to understand the psychosocial status of mothers and, therefore, their families.

In a study in which the families of 23 patients with MPS type III were compared with the families of the same number of children with intellectual disability, parents of children with MPS III stated that they experienced fewer behavioral difficulties as their children got older, although it was found that both parent groups scored above the clinical limit for anxiety and depression [13]. However, families of children with MPS were not compared with families of healthy children, but compared with families of children with similarly poor intellectual capacity. Therefore, it can be said that the negative effects of MPS on families cannot be fully evaluated. In the current study, five children had MPS type IIIB, two had MPS type IIIA, three had MPS type VI, and one child had MPS type IVA. However, due to the wide variety of diseases and the limited number of patients in some disease groups, the effects of the diseases and/or the intellectual capacity of the patients on the resilience or burnout status of the mothers have not been compared.

In a study conducted by Kütük et al. in which they evaluated the depression and burnout levels in the families of children with autism by using BDI and MBI, it was found that the burnout and depression levels of these families increased significantly compared to the control group. In addition, it was found that mothers’ burnout levels were higher than fathers’ [9]. In another study in which the relationship between mothers’ depression and burnout status and treatment of children suffering from ADHD was evaluated, BDI and MBI were applied to the mothers before and after the treatment. After the treatment, significant decreases were observed in BDI, MBI-emotional exhaustion, and MBI-personal accomplishment scores [21]. In our study, we did not find any statistically significant effect of having ADHD or SLD in children on mothers’ burnout or resilience. This may be because the children are already suffering from a chronic disease that is difficult to treat and handle, or because the mothers’ burnout and resilience levels are not compared to those of mothers of healthy children. The intellectual levels of the patients whose mothers were included in the study differed, and these levels were not assessed with a precise scale. Therefore, the MBI or CD-RISC scores of the patients’ mothers could not be evaluated based on their children’s intellectual levels. Additionally, neither the children’s burnout status nor the difference between the patients’ mothers’ and fathers’ resilience and burnout could be evaluated. These topics can also be considered limitations of this study.

Edraki et al. evaluated the resilience, depression, anxiety, and stress levels of mothers and fathers of children with insulin dependence by using CD-RISC and Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS-21). The presence of more than one child with diabetes and the length of diagnosis of diabetes had no statistically significant effect on the resilience of mothers [22]. Similarly, in our study, the presence of another child with the same IMD did not affect mothers’ resilience or burnout. Furthermore, in the study by Edraki et al., maternal education level, maternal employment status, and income level did not affect resilience, but the number of hospital admissions did [22]. In a study using the CD-RISC, low resilience was reported among the parents of children and adolescents in the end stages of life [23]. Since we did not focus on a specific diagnosis, we did not directly assess the impact of the number of hospital admissions and disease stage on resilience and burnout. However, the number of hospital visits may serve as an indirect indicator of disease severity. Future studies could more precisely examine resilience across different stages of illness.

Lau et al. found that high distress in the child made the mother more resilient, and conversely, high distress in the mother made child resilience worse, where they evaluated the resilience of adolescents and young adults who were followed up with cancer and their mothers [24]. Our study found a positive correlation between mothers’ and children’s resilience. In light of these data, it is evident that psychosocial support for primary caregivers (i.e., mothers) plays a vital role in children’s compliance with treatment and family cooperation.

This study has some limitations, including the lack of a healthy control group, the failure to evaluate mothers’ depression, a heterogeneous sample with varied patient needs, and the inclusion of only literate cases with relatively high intelligence, without comparing cognitive levels. Also, small sample sizes and subgroup heterogeneity may have limited statistical power, which could be considered a limitation. Although we provide subscale means, relying primarily on the total score may mask dimension-specific differences across MBI domains; therefore, subscale-level inferences were not treated as primary findings and should be interpreted cautiously. No multiplicity adjustment was applied; therefore, borderline p-values should be interpreted with caution due to increased Type I error risk. These factors may have influenced the results and should be considered in future studies.

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, it is the first study investigating the resilience and burnout levels of mothers of children with IMDs. In the current study, not only burnout but also resilience status, which has become increasingly important in recent years for evaluating people’s psychological status and stress-coping skills, were evaluated. It could be inferred to evaluation of children’s and their families’ psychosocial status and supporting them when necessary is very important. In summary, given advancements in medical interventions, including gene therapies, and increased survival rates, it is imperative to consider expanding support networks for pediatric patients with IMD and their caregivers.
