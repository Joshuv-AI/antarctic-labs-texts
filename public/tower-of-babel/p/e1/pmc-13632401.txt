Association and predictive value of preoperative high-sensitivity C-reactive protein for postoperative atrial fibrillation after video-assisted thoracoscopic lobectomy: a cohort study using the INSPIRE database
Authors: Han Wu, Zhipeng Zhou, Long Huang, Xiyuan Xie, Liangcheng Qiu, Xiaodan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative atrial fibrillation (POAF) is a common and serious complication following video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), which affects patients’ medical outcomes and quality of life. The predictive value of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) as an inflammatory marker for POAF in non-cardiac surgeries remains unclear. This study aims to investigate the association and predictive capability of preoperative hs-CRP levels with postoperative new-onset atrial fibrillation.MethodsThis study employed a retrospective cohort design, analyzing data from the INSPIRE database, which included 3,219 patients who underwent thoracoscopic lobectomy. Patients were classified into low-risk, moderate-risk, and high-risk groups based on preoperative hs-CRP levels. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to assess the independent association of hs-CRP with the occurrence of POAF in different risk groups. Additionally, the incremental predictive utility of hs-CRP for POAF was evaluated using C-statistics, continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), in order to quantify the improvement in predictive accuracy when including hs-CRP in existing risk models. Feature importance calculations and predictive models were generated using the Boruta algorithm and machine learning methods.ResultsThe incidence of clinically recognized POAF increased progressively across hs-CRP categories (2.9% in the low-risk group, 6.0% in the intermediate-risk group, and 9.3% in the high-risk group; P for trend < 0.001). After multivariable adjustment using preoperatively available covariates, the high hs-CRP group remained significantly associated with clinically recognized POAF compared with the low hs-CRP group (OR 2.33, 95% CI 1.31–4.14; P = 0.004). Adding hs-CRP to a baseline clinical model including age, sex, BMI, RCRI score, and extent of resection improved the C-statistic from 0.758 to 0.845 (P < 0.001), with significant improvements in continuous NRI and IDI. However, these reclassification metrics should be interpreted as statistical risk refinement rather than evidence of clinically actionable reclassification.ConclusionPreoperative high-sensitivity C-reactive protein is associated with an increased risk of clinically recognized POAF after VATS lobectomy. Its inclusion in baseline models improves statistical risk stratification, pending further prospective clinical validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04035-z.Keywords: High-sensitivity C-reactive protein, Postoperative atrial fibrillation, Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, Machine learning, Risk assessment

Introduction

Postoperative atrial fibrillation (POAF) is a common arrhythmic complication of thoracic surgery, with an incidence rate of 10% to 30% following lobectomy [1]. It severely impacts patient prognosis and medical resource consumption. POAF not only leads to prolonged hospital stays and increased healthcare costs but is also closely associated with a heightened risk of stroke, an increased incidence of long-term cardiovascular events, and a rise in overall mortality [2–10].

Current research suggests that the occurrence of postoperative atrial fibrillation is the result of multiple factors, including intraoperative cardiac manipulation, autonomic nervous system dysfunction, electrolyte imbalances, hypoxemia, and systemic inflammatory responses [11, 12]. Among these, the role of inflammatory responses in the development of POAF has received increasing attention. The systemic inflammatory response triggered by surgical trauma can promote atrial electrical and structural remodeling through various pathways [11–16]. Therefore, evaluating the inflammatory status preoperatively or during the perioperative period may provide important evidence for risk stratification and prevention of POAF.

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) is one of the most widely used systemic inflammatory markers in clinical practice, offering advantages such as ease of detection, low cost, and high standardization. In the cardiovascular field, hs-CRP has been shown to predict the risk of various cardiovascular events, including coronary artery disease and heart failure [17–21]. Numerous studies have found that elevated hs-CRP levels in patients undergoing cardiac surgery, either preoperatively or early postoperatively, are associated with an increased incidence of POAF [11, 22–26]. However, the value of hs-CRP in predicting postoperative atrial fibrillation following non-cardiac thoracic surgery, especially thoracoscopic surgery, has been relatively underexplored.

Consequently, the clinical significance of assessing preoperative hs-CRP as an easily accessible laboratory index for POAF risk evaluation in patients undergoing thoracoscopic surgery is substantial. This study utilizes the INSPIRE database, a large perioperative cohort, to investigate the association between preoperative hs-CRP levels and postoperative atrial fibrillation, aiming to quantitatively assess the predictive capability of hs-CRP for POAF.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study utilized perioperative data obtained from INSPIRE (version 1.3), a comprehensive perioperative medical database from academic institutions in Korea. INSPIRE covers the period from 2011 to 2020 and contains high-quality medical records for approximately 130,000 patients who underwent surgery under anesthesia. These records include detailed patient demographics, vital signs, comorbidities, laboratory results from pre-admission to six months after discharge, and in-hospital medication data. Access to and extraction of data from the database were managed by one of the authors, Han Wu, in compliance with all required data access protocols.

The study population consisted of patients aged ≥ 18 years who underwent elective VATS lobectomy, identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Procedure Coding System (ICD-10-PCS). The study was reported in accordance with the STROCSS (Strengthening the Reporting of Cohort Studies in Surgery) guidelines [27].

The exclusion criteria were as follows: a history of atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or other sustained supraventricular tachyarrhythmias prior to surgery; previous cardiac surgery or the presence of moderate to severe valvular heart disease; emergency surgery or concomitant major non-thoracic surgery; intraoperative conversion to pneumonectomy; and missing data on hs-CRP or postoperative atrial fibrillation outcomes. After applying these criteria, a total of 3,219 patients were included in the final analysis. Based on preoperative hs-CRP levels, patients were categorized into three risk groups: low-risk (n = 2,703), intermediate-risk (n = 235), and high-risk (n = 281), with the following cut-off values: low risk < 1 mg/L, intermediate risk 1–3 mg/L, and high risk ≥ 3 mg/L(Figure S1). This stratification was predefined according to previous literature and the distribution characteristics of hs-CRP in this cohort [17, 28, 29]. Furthermore, to exclude the potential confounding effect of acute infection or active systemic inflammatory diseases, patients with preoperative hs-CRP levels > 10 mg/L were excluded from the analysis cohort.

Ethics statement

This retrospective study utilized the publicly available, de-identified INSPIRE database. It was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. H-2210-078-1368) and the Institutional Data Review Board (DRB No. BD-R-2022-11-02), with a waiver of informed consent. The study exclusively enrolled adults aged ≥ 18 years, involving no participants under 16. All procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection and definitions

Data extraction was performed using PostgreSQL (version 13.8), which employs Structured Query Language (SQL) for precise information retrieval. The extracted candidate variables were grouped into five categories: (1) demographic details, such as age, sex, and body mass index (BMI); (2) comorbidities, including diabetes, hypertension, carotid artery stenosis, prior cerebral infarction, coronary heart disease (CHD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and chronic kidney disease (CKD); (3) cardiovascular medication use, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), β-blockers, calcium channel blockers (CCBs), dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors, metformin, sulfonylureas, thiazolidinediones, insulin, antiplatelet agents, anticoagulants, and statins; (4) laboratory tests, including preoperative hemoglobin, hematocrit, serum albumin, white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet count, serum creatinine, activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), fibrinogen, blood glucose, lymphocyte percentage, and neutrophil percentage; and (5) surgical details, including the resected lobe location, side of resection, number of lobes resected, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, operative time, anesthesia time, intraoperative hypotension, intraoperative bradycardia, total intraoperative fluid volume, estimated blood loss, and urine output. Operative time was defined as the interval from skin incision to completion of skin closure; anesthesia time was defined as the interval from the start of anesthesia induction to completion of extubation. Intraoperative hypotension (IOH) was defined as a mean arterial pressure below 65 mmHg or a decrease of more than 20% from the preoperative value on two consecutive measurements. Intraoperative bradycardia was defined as a heart rate below 50 bpm on two consecutive measurements [30, 31]. All laboratory variables were extracted from the first tests performed after hospital admission.

Clinical outcomes

The primary clinical outcome was clinically recognized postoperative atrial fibrillation (POAF) during the index hospitalization. POAF events were identified from postoperative in-hospital diagnostic records using ICD-10 codes in the INSPIRE database. Patients with a preoperative history of atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or other persistent supraventricular tachyarrhythmias were excluded during cohort construction and were therefore not counted as incident POAF cases. Because POAF ascertainment relied on diagnostic coding rather than systematic continuous rhythm monitoring, the outcome should be interpreted as clinically recognized or clinically significant POAF rather than the total incidence of postoperative atrial arrhythmias.

Statistical analysis

Patients were stratified by preoperative hs-CRP levels. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). Baseline comparisons utilized one-way ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis, Mann-Whitney U, chi-square, or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Variables with > 15% missing data were excluded. Under the Missing at Random (MAR) assumption, missing values were handled using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) with predictive mean matching (m = 5, 50 iterations). Diagnostic trace and density plots confirmed robust algorithm convergence and distributional consistency between the imputed and observed data, respectively (Figure S2).

The primary outcome was postoperative atrial fibrillation (POAF) during the index hospitalization. POAF incidence trends across hs-CRP groups were evaluated using the trend chi-square test. The association between preoperative hs-CRP categories and clinically recognized POAF was evaluated using multivariable logistic regression. Three models with progressive adjustment were constructed, culminating in Model 3, which comprehensively adjusted for demographics, comorbidities, medications, ASA status, RCRI score, preoperative laboratories, and resection extent. Crucially, to preserve a strictly preoperative predictive framework and avoid overadjusting for downstream mediators, all intraoperative variables were rigorously excluded from these multivariable models. Linear trends (P for trend) were assessed by treating hs-CRP categories as a continuous variable. To evaluate hs-CRP’s incremental predictive value, we compared C-statistics using the DeLong test and calculated the continuous (category-free) net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), with 95% CIs derived from 1,000 bootstrap resamples.

For machine learning (ML) analysis, the Boruta algorithm was utilized for feature selection, classifying preoperative variables as confirmed, tentative, or rejected based on random forest variable importance. The cohort was then randomly split into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets. Ten supervised ML algorithms (CatBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest, XGBoost, Extra Trees, Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes, Decision Tree, SVM, and KNN) were trained exclusively on preoperative features. To address the low POAF incidence without inducing data leakage, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied exclusively within the training folds during 5-fold cross-validation combined with grid search hyperparameter tuning.

Model performance on the independent test set was primarily evaluated using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC AUC). Given the highly imbalanced dataset, Precision-Recall (PR) AUC was computed as a complementary metric to reflect model robustness. Calibration plots assessed the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcome frequencies.

All statistical tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered significant. Analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.2.

Intraoperatively, the High Risk group experienced longer operative and anesthesia times, greater blood loss, and higher urine output (all P < 0.001). Preoperative laboratory findings in this group demonstrated lower albumin and hemoglobin, alongside elevated white blood cell, neutrophil, and fibrinogen levels (all P < 0.001), consistent with a heightened baseline inflammatory state. Notably, the incidence of POAF increased in a stepwise manner across the risk strata (2.9% vs. 6.0% vs. 9.3%, P < 0.001). The High Risk group also required significantly prolonged postoperative hospital stays (P < 0.001).

Association between preoperative hs-CRP risk categories and POAF

The crude incidence of POAF according to hs-CRP risk strata is illustrated in Fig. 1A. The frequency of POAF increased stepwise from the Low through the Medium to the High Risk group (2.9% vs. 6.0% vs. 9.3%; (P for trend < 0.001)).

Figure 1. Association between preoperative hs-CRP risk stratification and POAF. (A) Incidence of POAF across hs-CRP risk groups (Low, Medium, and High). (B) Odds Ratios (ORs) and 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) for POAF across three regression models (Model 1: unadjusted; Model 2: adjusted for demographics; Model 3: fully adjusted model incorporating clinically relevant preoperative baseline covariates), with the Low Risk group as the reference.

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were then performed to evaluate the independent association between hs-CRP risk categories and POAF (Fig. 1B). In the unadjusted model (Model 1), both the Medium Risk group (OR 2.13, 95% CI 1.19–3.83; (P = 0.011)) and the High Risk group (OR 3.43, 95% CI 2.16–5.45; (P < 0.001)) were associated with a higher risk of POAF compared with the Low Risk group. After adjustment for potential confounders in Model 2, the associations were slightly attenuated but remained significant for both the Medium Risk group (OR 1.83, 95% CI 1.01–3.30; (P = 0.046)) and the High Risk group (OR 3.01, 95% CI 1.87–4.80; (P < 0.001)).

In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), which included preoperative clinical covariates, the High Risk group remained an independently associated factor of POAF with an approximately twofold higher risk than the Low Risk group (OR 2.33, 95% CI 1.31–4.14; (P = 0.004)). In contrast, the association for the Medium Risk group was further attenuated and no longer statistically significant (OR 1.38, 95% CI 0.72–2.66; (P = 0.332)).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses demonstrated a consistent positive association between preoperative hs-CRP and POAF risk across most clinically relevant strata (Fig. 2). For every one-unit increase in hs-CRP, the odds of POAF increased significantly regardless of age (< 65 years: OR 1.16, 95% CI 1.09–1.23; ≥65 years: OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.04–1.15; P for interaction = 0.182), sex (women: OR 1.17, 95% CI 1.09–1.25; men: OR 1.08, 95% CI 1.03–1.13; P for interaction = 0.055), or BMI (< 30 kg/m²: OR 1.11, 95% CI 1.06–1.15; ≥30 kg/m²: OR 1.22, 95% CI 1.01–1.48; P for interaction = 0.323). Furthermore, no significant interactions were observed across ASA classes, RCRI scores, surgical sites, or the extent of resection (all P for interaction > 0.05). Although the association attenuated to non-significance in specific high-risk subsets, the absence of statistically significant interactions suggested no clear evidence of effect modification.

Incremental predictive value of hs-CRP beyond clinical risk models

To assess the incremental prognostic value of preoperative hs-CRP, we evaluated its addition to established clinical risk models (Table 2). Incorporating hs-CRP into the RCRI score significantly improved the C-statistic from 0.753 (95% CI 0.706–0.799) to 0.796 (95% CI 0.749–0.843; P = 0.003) (Fig. 3A). Reclassification analyses demonstrated enhancements, yielding a continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) of 0.371 (95% CI 0.198–0.544; P < 0.001) and an integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) of 0.015 (95% CI 0.003–0.027; P = 0.017).

Similarly, adding hs-CRP to the Baseline Risk Model (comprising age, sex, BMI, RCRI, and resection extent) increased the C-statistic from 0.758 (95% CI 0.706–0.788) to 0.845 (95% CI 0.808–0.882; P < 0.001) (Fig. 3B). The reclassification improvements were pronounced, with an NRI of 0.894 (95% CI 0.715–1.073; P < 0.001) and an IDI of 0.172 (95% CI 0.125–0.219; P < 0.001). Taken together, these findings suggest that hs-CRP improved statistical discrimination and risk redistribution. However, because the NRI was category-free, these reclassification results should be interpreted as statistical risk refinement rather than evidence of clinically actionable patient reclassification.

Machine learning–based feature selection and model performance

Feature selection using the Boruta algorithm identified several clinically relevant variables, including preoperative hs-CRP, as important predictors of clinically recognized POAF (Fig. 4A), further supporting the relevance of systemic inflammation in POAF risk stratification.

Figure 4. Machine learning-based feature importance and predictive performance for POAF. (A) Variable importance ranked by the Boruta algorithm. Green, yellow, and red boxplots indicate confirmed, tentative, and rejected features, respectively, compared to shadow attributes (blue). (B) Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves comparing the performance of the evaluated machine learning models.

ROC curve analysis comparing multiple machine learning models revealed that the Random Forest classifier achieved the highest discriminative performance (AUC = 0.85), outperforming other robust algorithms such as CatBoost (0.84) and Extra Trees (0.83) (Fig. 4B). Figure 5 summarizes the calibration and clinical utility assessment of the final Random Forest model. The model showed a calibration intercept of − 0.89 (95% CI, − 1.27 to − 0.51) and a calibration slope of 0.76 (95% CI, 0.55 to 0.97). The PR-AUC was 0.292, higher than the baseline event rate of 0.036, indicating some ability to identify clinically recognized POAF in this imbalanced cohort. Clinical impact and decision curve analyses suggested potential net benefit mainly at lower threshold probabilities. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov curve showed moderate separation between patients with and without POAF (KS statistic = 0.545, P = 0.099). These findings suggest that the model may be useful for exploratory relative risk stratification, but not for definitive absolute risk estimation without external validation.

Sensitivity analysis

Full data analysis indicated that the high-risk hs-CRP group was associated with the risk of POAF occurrence (Table S3). A similar trend was observed in the propensity score-matched cohort. The high-risk hs-CRP group remained significantly associated with an increased risk of POAF occurrence, further confirming the aforementioned findings (Table S4). Sensitivity analysis restricted to patients undergoing standard single-lobe anatomical resection (excluding bilobectomy and pneumonectomy) yielded consistent results, supporting the consistency of the association between hs-CRP and POAF independent of greater surgical extent (Table S5).

Discussion

This study, based on the INSPIRE database of thoracoscopic surgery cohorts, demonstrates an independent association between preoperative high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) levels and postoperative atrial fibrillation (POAF). This association remains robust even after adjusting for demographic characteristics, comorbidities, cardiovascular medication use, and preoperatively available covariates. Furthermore, incorporating hs-CRP into the base clinical model enhances the model’s discriminative ability and reclassification capacity, although calibration assessment indicated a tendency toward overprediction of absolute risk. These findings suggest that preoperative inflammatory burden has quantifiable predictive value for clinically recognized POAF and may serve as an accessible biomarker for preoperative risk refinement, pending prospective validation.

The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying hs-CRP as an inflammatory marker predicting POAF are multifaceted. Firstly, elevated preoperative hs-CRP levels indicate a state of chronic, low-grade systemic inflammation [32]. This condition may already have potential impacts on the atrial myocardium, leading to structural and electrophysiological remodeling [6, 33, 34]. Inflammation can promote atrial fibrosis and disrupt electrical conduction coupling between cardiomyocytes, providing a structural basis for the formation of reentry circuits [34]. Secondly, patients with high baseline inflammation may trigger a more severe acute inflammatory cascade response when faced with surgical trauma, referred to as the “second hit.” This response results in the massive release of inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6 and TNF-α), which can directly or indirectly affect the ion channel function of atrial myocytes (particularly potassium and calcium channels), leading to shorter action potential durations and increased dispersion of effective refractory periods, thereby lowering the threshold for atrial fibrillation [35, 36]. The magnitude of improvement in discrimination after adding hs-CRP to the baseline clinical model warrants cautious interpretation. Although hs-CRP was evaluated as a single biomarker, it is a non-specific marker that may integrate multiple dimensions of systemic vulnerability. Elevated hs-CRP may reflect not only inflammatory activation but also unmeasured pulmonary function impairment, frailty, nutritional compromise, tumor-related inflammation, occult infection, cardiovascular comorbidity burden, or greater surgical complexity. Therefore, the observed improvement in C-statistic should not be interpreted as evidence that hs-CRP alone has direct mechanistic or stand-alone clinical decision-making value. Rather, hs-CRP may function as a surrogate marker that captures broader preoperative illness burden not fully represented by conventional clinical variables.

In coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and valve surgeries, the association between CRP and postoperative atrial fibrillation has been widely and deeply studied, with relatively solid evidence supporting this link. Numerous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have confirmed that both preoperative and early postoperative CRP levels are significantly associated with the incidence of atrial fibrillation [33, 37]. Studies have shown that elevated preoperative CRP is an independent predictive factor for POAF following cardiac surgery, suggesting that an existing chronic inflammatory state renders the atrial substrate more vulnerable and reduces its tolerance to surgical stress [38, 39]. Moreover, peak postoperative CRP levels more effectively reflect an individual’s inflammatory response intensity to surgical trauma, as patients experiencing POAF have significantly higher postoperative CRP levels compared to those without atrial fibrillation [33]. Unlike in cardiac procedures, high-quality evidence linking preoperative CRP to POAF in non-cardiac thoracic surgeries remains scarce. Because thoracic patients possess distinct baseline characteristics and face unique postoperative arrhythmic triggers—such as hypoxemia and altered pulmonary hemodynamics—findings from cardiac cohorts cannot be directly extrapolated. Thus, robust evidence is still required to establish CRP as a routine predictive tool for POAF following lung surgery.

The observed effect size should be interpreted cautiously. In the fully adjusted model, high preoperative hs-CRP remained associated with clinically recognized POAF (OR = 2.33, 95% CI: 1.31–4.14). Given the multifactorial nature of POAF, this association suggests that hs-CRP may reflect broader systemic vulnerability rather than a solitary deterministic factor. Although potentially relevant for risk refinement, the finding should be interpreted in light of the low absolute event rate, ICD-10–based outcome ascertainment, and residual confounding. Moreover, quantitative calibration metrics (intercept = − 0.89, slope = 0.76) indicated imperfect calibration and possible overestimation of absolute risk. Therefore, the model is more appropriate for exploratory relative risk ranking than for precise absolute risk estimation, and external validation with potential recalibration is required before clinical use.

From a clinical perspective, our findings may help generate hypotheses for future risk-stratification studies. Rather than proposing immediate implementation, we outline a preliminary framework in which hs-CRP could be evaluated as an adjunct to established clinical risk assessment tools such as the RCRI. In this framework, patients with elevated baseline clinical risk and hs-CRP > 3 mg/L may represent a subgroup worthy of closer evaluation in prospective studies. Future prospective cohorts or trials are needed to determine whether enhanced postoperative rhythm surveillance or targeted preventive strategies improve outcomes in this subgroup. Until such validation is available, hs-CRP should be regarded as a potential risk-refinement biomarker rather than a stand-alone trigger for clinical intervention.

Several limitations inherent to this retrospective, single-center study warrant consideration. First, fundamental thoracic predictors, specifically detailed pulmonary function test results (e.g., FEV1, DLCO) and the precise extent of mediastinal lymph node dissection, were not systematically captured in the database. Although we adjusted for composite surrogate variables like the RCRI and the extent of anatomical resection, the absence of these specific variables inherently introduces the risk of residual confounding. Consequently, we must be cautious in drawing absolute causal inferences, as preoperative hs-CRP might partially capture the unmeasured burden of poorer baseline pulmonary reserve or greater surgical trauma. Second, our reliance on in-hospital ICD-10 diagnostic codes rather than continuous postoperative telemetry monitoring inevitably underestimates the true absolute incidence of POAF (3.6% in our cohort vs. 10–30% typically reported in prospective series). As a result, our detection method preferentially captures clinically overt, symptomatic, or severe episodes that necessitate medical intervention, while likely missing transient or asymptomatic events. This introduces a potential misclassification bias, meaning our findings and predictive models are inherently weighted toward predicting severe, clinically significant POAF rather than the overall incidence. Nevertheless, from a clinical decision-making standpoint, identifying this specific subset of severe cases is arguably of greatest relevance for allocating targeted preventative strategies and intensive care resources. Finally, while our SMOTE-balanced models achieved high ROC AUC scores (~ 0.84–0.85), this overestimates clinical utility given the highly imbalanced dataset (3.6% POAF incidence). With low sensitivity (Recall ≈ 0.23), the model misses most cases and cannot serve as a broad “rule-out” screening tool. However, Because the ICD-10-based outcome likely captured clinically overt POAF events, the model may be more suitable for exploratory relative risk ranking than for ruling out POAF or guiding treatment decisions. Furthermore, including hs-CRP improved risk reclassification, as evidenced by the continuous Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI). However, this metric requires cautious interpretation, as it mainly reflects statistical redistribution of predicted probabilities and may be sensitive to small changes that do not necessarily translate into clinically actionable reclassification. Therefore, the observed NRI improvement supports incremental statistical risk refinement rather than direct evidence for treatment-changing patient stratification. In addition, the hs-CRP thresholds used in this study were adapted from established cardiovascular risk categories. These cutoffs may not be optimal for patients undergoing thoracic surgery, and future studies should determine whether thoracic surgery–specific hs-CRP thresholds provide better risk stratification for clinically significant POAF.

Conclusion

Preoperative hs-CRP is associated with an increased risk of clinically recognized POAF following VATS lobectomy. While incorporating hs-CRP into baseline models improves statistical discrimination, this enhancement may partly reflect its role as a surrogate for broader systemic vulnerability and unmeasured illness burden. Furthermore, given our reliance on ICD-10 codes, these findings apply specifically to clinically significant POAF rather than transient, asymptomatic episodes. Prospective validation remains essential before integrating hs-CRP-based strategies into routine clinical decision-making.
