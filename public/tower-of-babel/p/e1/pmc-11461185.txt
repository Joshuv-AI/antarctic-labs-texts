Bacterial Meningitis Following Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage and Its Association with Cerebral Vasospasm
Authors: Kunimasa TERANISHI, Masanori GOTO, Tadashi SUNOHARA, Masaomi KOYANAGI, Junichi TAKEDA, Ryu FUKUMITSU, Nobuyuki FUKUI, Yuki TAKANO, Kota NAKAJIMA, Yuji NARAMOTO, Yasuhiro YAMAMOTO, Rikuo NISHII, Satohiro KAWADE, Takateru TAKAMATSU, Masanori TOKUDA, Hikari TOMITA, Mai YOSHIMOTO, Hirotoshi IMAMURA, Nobuyuki SAKAI, Tsuyoshi OHTA
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is a critical condition with high in-hospital mortality rates. Delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI), a secondary complication associated with aSAH, can also contribute to morbidity and mortality. Although draining the hematoma from the subarachnoid space has been considered effective in preventing DCI, the placement of a drainage system could increase the risk of bacterial meningitis and ventriculitis. This study aimed to examine the association between meningitis following aSAH and the occurrence of DCI, focusing on the role of cerebral vasospasm. Patients who underwent endovascular coiling or surgical clipping for aSAH from April 2001 to March 2022 were included in this study, while those who did not undergo postoperative drainage were excluded. The patient's clinical characteristics, treatment modalities, and outcomes were then analyzed, after which logistic regression was used to assess the odds ratios (OR) for DCI. A total of 810 patients with aSAH were included in this study. Meningitis following aSAH was identified as an independent factor associated with DCI (odds ratio 5.0 [95% confidence intervals (CI) 2.3-11]). Other significant factors were female sex (odds ratio 1.5 [95% CI 0.89-2.5]) and surgical clipping (odds ratio 2.1 [95% CI 1.3-3.4]). This study demonstrated a significant association between meningitis following aSAH and the development of DCI, suggesting that the inflammatory environment associated with meningitis may contribute to cerebral vasospasm. Early recognition and treatment of meningitis in patients with aSAH could reduce the risk of DCI and improve patient outcomes.

Introduction

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is a significant life-threatening medical condition. It has a reported acute in-hospital mortality rate of 14.1%. The leading causes of this condition include initial hemorrhagic damage, rebleeding, and refractory cerebral edema.1) Even after surviving the initial bleeding, affected patients remain at risk for morbidity and mortality due to secondary complications, such as hydrocephalus and delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI). Evidence suggests that the amount of intracranial bleeding could be the strongest determinant of DCI.2) The placement of an external cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage system after aSAH helps drain the hematoma from the subarachnoid space, thereby effectively preventing DCI.3,4) However, the placement of a CSF drainage increases the risk of intracranial infections, such as bacterial meningitis and ventriculitis.5) Several studies have reported that bacterial meningitis causes severe cerebral vasospasm and subsequent ischemia.6,7) However, it remains unclear whether bacterial meningitis associated with aSAH induces cerebral vasospasm. Therefore, this study aimed to determine whether bacterial meningitis following aSAH was associated with DCI.

Materials and Methods

Study cohort and patient selection

This single-center, retrospective, case-control study used an electronic database containing information on patients who underwent endovascular coiling or surgical clipping for aSAH from April 2001 to March 2022. Patients who underwent craniotomy and who received endovascular treatment were included in this study. Therefore, when assessing the risk of meningitis, it is necessary to consider factors related to invasive procedures such as drainage other than craniotomy. We focused on cases of meningitis associated with CSF drainage; those who did not undergo postoperative ventricular drainage, cisternal drainage, or lumbar drainage due to the small amount of SAH or severe condition were excluded. This study was approved by the Ethics Institutional Review Board of Kobe City Medical Center General Hospital with a waiver of informed consent (#zn240214). There was no funding source for this study.

Treatment protocol for aSAH

After confirming the presence of SAH via computed tomography (CT), digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was performed under general anesthesia.

Based on the angiographic anatomy, the neurosurgeons used the endovascular or surgical approach to treat the responsible intracranial aneurysm. Although endovascular treatment was the first treatment choice for a ruptured aneurysm, surgical treatment was performed for aneurysms accompanied by hematoma or those whose shape was unsuitable for coil embolization.8)

Management of drainage and postoperative evaluation

After completing radical treatment, a ventricular drainage was usually placed in cases with acute hydrocephalus. In other cases, we placed a lumbar drainage after coil embolization and a cisternal drainage after clipping to remove the hematoma from the subarachnoid space, except for those with only a small amount of hematoma. In cases where drainage was placed, antibiotics treatment (cefazolin) was administered to prevent bacterial infection at the site of drain insertion.

All patients underwent early posttreatment head CT within the first 24 h after radical treatment.

Moreover, we performed DSA within 14 days of SAH onset to assess whether the aneurysm had been secured and whether angiographic vasospasm was present.

Definition of vasospasm and DCI

Angiographic vasospasm was defined as reversible and temporary moderate to severe arterial narrowing on DSA with the presence of flow stagnation beyond the stenosis area within 14 days of aSAH onset, including cerebral and peripheral arteries that was not attributable to atherosclerosis, catheter-induced spasm, or vessel hypoplasia as evaluated by one neurosurgeon.9)

DCI was defined as transient or permanent new focal neurological deterioration, with angiographical vasospasm in the related territory, except for other causes, such as symptomatic epilepsy.10) Patients with angiographic vasospasm or DCI were treated with intraarterial administration of fasudil hydrochloride or percutaneous transluminal angioplasty.11)

Diagnosis of meningitis associated with CSF drainage

Meningitis associated with CSF drainage was diagnosed based on clinical findings such as fever, headache, peripheral leukocyte counts, C-reactive protein in blood samples, increased cell counts, leukocyte counts, and decreased glucose level on CSF examination in the presence of ventricular/cisternal/lumbar drains.12) Leukocyte counts in the CSF may be elevated due to inflammation under the presence of SAH,13) which can complicate the definitive diagnosis of meningitis. Therefore, in cases with elevated leukocyte counts in the CSF, meningitis was diagnosed based on a CSF/serum glucose ratio of less than 0.4,13,14) along with a comprehensive evaluation of clinical symptoms.

Meningitis was classified as bacterial or aseptic based on the results of CSF culture tests.15)

Empirical antibiotic therapy (monotherapy or combination therapy with ceftriaxone, vancomycin, and meropenem) was started after diagnosing meningitis. Antibiotic treatment was then appropriately adjusted based on the CSF culture tests. Any drains were removed except in cases with severe hydrocephalus.

Data collection

Data including patient characteristics (age, sex, and medical history [hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, chronic renal failure]), smoking history, aSAH grading according to the World Federation of Neurological Surgeons, modified Fisher scale, concurrent intracerebral hematoma and intraventricular hematoma), aneurysm treatment modality (surgical or endovascular), development of meningitis after aSAH onset, and drainage duration were reviewed.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using JMP software version 15.1.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, United States). A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. A P value of <0.001 was written as <0.001. Categorical and continuous variables were presented as number (percentage) and median (interquartile range [IQR]), respectively. The patient characteristics and clinical outcomes of groups with and without symptomatic vasospasm were then compared using Pearson's chi-squared analysis and Mann-Whitney U test for categorical and continuous variables, respectively.

To examine the association between DCI and meningitis following aSAH, logistic regression models were developed to estimate the odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% CIs for the occurrence of DCI. Adjusted ORs were calculated using multivariate logistic regression models that adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, smoking history, aneurysm treatment modality, and intracerebral hematoma. To maintain model integrity and manage the risk of error due to multiple comparisons, explanatory variables for the multivariable logistic model were restricted to 10% of the patients in the less frequent category16) and were selected based on preexisting studies on their significant association with the onset of DCI.10,17-19)

Results

A total of 1,194 aSAH patients were hospitalized from April 2001 to March 2022, 125 of whom with severe conditions who did not receive radical treatment and 259 who did not receive CSF drainages were excluded. Finally, 810 patients (median [IQR] age, 63 (53-74) years; 65% women [n = 528]) were ultimately included in our study cohort (Fig. 1). Several cases involved the placement of different types of drainage, making it challenging to retrospectively assess each case individually. Therefore, we did not conduct an evaluation based on the type of drainage.

Flowchart of patients included in the study.

These patients underwent surgical clipping (n = 364, 45%) or endovascular therapy (n = 446, 55%) for aSAH. The median (IQR) time of performing DSA was 8 (7-9) days from the SAH onset.

Among the analyzed aSAH patients, angiographic vasospasms were found in 24% (n = 191) of patients, and 12% (n = 96) developed DCI. Of these patients, 71 cases were treated with intraarterial administration of fasudil hydrochloride and 12 cases were treated with percutaneous transluminal angioplasty.

Characteristics of patients with DCI

Table 1 compares the characteristics of aSAH patients with and without DCI. Notably, the group with DCI consisted of more females (71 [74%] vs. 457 [64%]; P = 0.054), underwent significantly more surgical clipping (58 [60%] vs. 306 [43%]; P = 0.0012), and had significantly more cases of meningitis (12 [13%] vs. 24 [3.4%]; P < 0.001) than the group without DCI. Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that the presence of meningitis was the most significant independent factor of DCI (OR, 5.0 [95% CI 2.3-11]), followed by female sex (OR, 1.5 [95% CI 0.89-2.5]) and surgical clipping (OR, 2.1 [95% CI 1.3-3.4]) (Table 2).

Risk factors for the meningitis group

We analyzed the characteristics of meningitis patients (36, 4.4%) to identify their risk factors. The median time to meningitis onset was 9 days (IQR, 6-18) after aSAH onset. Seven cases tested positive on CSF culture and were diagnosed with bacterial meningitis (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus [MRSA]: 6 cases; Streptococcus agalactiae: 1 case), whereas 29 tested negative on CSF culture and were diagnosed with aseptic meningitis.

Patients with meningitis were younger (57 [46-65] vs. 63 [53-74] years; P = 0.0034) and had a longer drainage duration (14 [8-16] vs. 9 [5-13] days; P = 0.0017) than those without meningitis (Table 3). There was significant no difference in the incidence of meningitis between those who underwent surgical and endovascular treatment (16 [44%] vs. 20 [56%]; P = 0.95).

Regarding clinical characteristics, meningitis patients had significantly more angiographic vasospasms (16 [44%] vs. 175 [23%], P = 0.0026) and required more intraarterial administration of fasudil hydrochloride (11 [31%] vs. 60 [7.8%], P < 0.001) than those without meningitis.

The median days diagnosed with angiographic vasospasm was 8 days (IQR, 6-10), which was nearly concurrent with the meningitis onset. Angiographic vasospasm occurred in 4 out of the 7 cases where bacteria were detectable on CSF culture and in 12 out of the 29 cases where bacteria were not detected. There was no significant difference between these groups (P = 0.45). Moreover, significantly more patients with meningitis than without meningitis had shunt-dependent hydrocephalus (17 [47%] vs. 162 [21%], P < 0.001) and poor 90-day outcomes with a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 4-6 (20 [56%] vs. 248 [32%]; P = 0.0037).

In cases of meningitis that developed DCI, there was no significant difference in leukocytes and CRP levels in the peripheral blood at the time of meningitis diagnosis compared to cases that did not (CRP: 2.7 [0.67-5.5] vs. 2.2 [0.95-7.5] mg/L; P = 0.85, WBC: 9300 [5500-12275] vs. 8900 [7400-11600] cells/μL; P = 0.88). In addition, there was no significant difference in the CSF cell count between the two groups (1300 [284-5571] vs. 684 [264-2500] cells/mm3; P = 0.96).

Discussion

This retrospective study on 810 patients with aSAH found a significant association between the development of meningitis and DCI. Available studies have reported that the presence of ICH and IVH, aSAH volume, density, clearance rate, aneurysm location, neurological deficit on admission, surgical treatment, smoking, preexisting hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and female sex are factors contributing to DCI.10,17-22) However, to our knowledge, no study has yet reported on the association between meningitis and DCI.

DCI is usually associated with cerebral vasospasm, which is caused by a spasmodic substance produced after hemoglobin degradation in the subarachnoid space. This condition can lead to poor neurological outcomes and even mortality.23,24) Previous studies have shown that draining the hematoma from the subarachnoid space can effectively prevent DCI.3,4) However, our findings showed that meningitis was a potent risk factor for the development of DCI. Therefore, close attention must be paid to the onset of meningitis when placing a CSF drainage.

Meningitis is the fourth most common infection after pneumonia, urinary tract infections, and bloodstream infections following aSAH.25) In fact, only 4.4% of the cases included herein developed meningitis, which was lower than the incidence rates reported in prior studies.26-30) Previous studies have identified several risk factors for the onset of postoperative meningitis, such as the length of surgery, immediate reoperation, coincident incision infection, smoking, and diabetes mellitus.26,31-35) Similar to our study, several other studies have reported an association between CSF drainage duration and infection.36-38) In a recent retrospective matched case-control study, placement of a CSF drainage for more than 10 days increased the risk of bacterial meningitis,39) which was similar to 14 days observed in our study. Other risk factors included diabetes mellitus, CSF leakage, and drain opening for CSF examination.40) Therefore, strict blood sugar control, surgical site infection control, limited cerebrospinal fluid examinations, and earlier removal of a CSF drainage could reduce the risk of bacterial meningitis in patients with aSAH.

In this case series, significantly more patients with meningitis had shunt-dependent hydrocephalus than those without meningitis. This may be attributed to the fact that the pathophysiology of meningitis may increase the risk of hydrocephalus by increasing the CSF protein levels and promoting arachnoid granulation fibrosis.41-43)

Our study suggests that meningitis may be associated with the development of DCI after aSAH and the inflammatory environment associated with meningitis may contribute to the pathophysiology of vasospasm.44) This pathophysiology leading to vasospasm could induce DCI and potentially reduce functional outcomes.

In cases of bacterial meningitis, several studies45-47) revealed that inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β and tumor necrosis factor-α are significantly elevated in the CSF. These cytokines trigger a series of events that result in the movement of leukocytes into the subarachnoid spaces, in which their phagocytosis of infectious particles leads to cell death and degranulation, releasing nitric oxide (NO) and oxygen free radicals.48)

NO, in peripheral arteries, increases the endothelial growth factor levels within endothelial cells and inhibits platelet adhesion and leukocyte chemotaxis.49) This exerts a tonic effect on the vascular endothelium, creating an anti-inflammatory and antiatherogenic environment.50)

Conversely, NO, in cerebral blood flow regulation, plays a significant role in endothelium-mediated cerebral vasodilation, dilation from local application of excitatory amino acids, and perivascular nerve stimulation.51)

NO normally prevents smooth muscle vasoconstriction. However, in patients with aSAH, excessive production of NO in the subarachnoid space leads to the induction of free radicals, such as hydroxyl radicals and peroxynitrite.52) These free radicals contribute to the development of vascular injury associated with cerebral vasospasm after SAH.53,54)

Meanwhile, studies on patients with meningitis have shown that bacterial and viral products, such as bacterial lipopolysaccharide and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), produce massive amounts of NO.55-57) Therefore, the massive amount of NO produced during the onset of meningitis, combined with the NO already present at the onset of aSAH, may potentially accelerate vasospasm.

Thus, aSAH and meningitis might share similar pathophysiological processes in the subarachnoid space, potentially contributing to cerebral vasospasm.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, given that bacterial and aseptic meningitis can present similar CSF test findings,58) we evaluated both patients despite their distinct pathologies. Second, patients who developed meningitis in this study had a longer duration of drain placement, which might reflect the need for more time to drain a high volume of subarachnoid hematoma from the arachnoid space. This could be a confounding factor in the development of meningitis. Third, owing to the retrospective nature of our study, we could not investigate factors such as the duration and severity of meningitis and demonstrate whether early diagnosis and treatment of meningitis could reduce DCI and improve clinical outcomes. However, considering that withholding treatment to patients with meningitis poses ethical concerns, prospective studies may not be able to address this matter.

Conclusions

Meningitis after aSAH was associated with angiographic vasospasm and sequential DCI. Our study suggests that reducing meningitis could prevent DCI and improve outcomes of aSAH patients.
