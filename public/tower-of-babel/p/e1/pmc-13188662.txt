Association between femoral notch area and patient-reported outcomes after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction
Authors: Hilal Yağar, Süleyman Ekiz, Alp Yekta Gökalp, Selim Çınaroğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone morphology is a key intrinsic factor in anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of femoral notch area (FNA) on functional outcomes after ACL reconstruction (ACLR).MethodsThis single-center retrospective study was conducted on 68 patients (62 men and 6 women). Data on age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and follow-up duration of patients were recorded. FNA and morphologic measurements were obtained. Patient-reported outcome measures included the International Knee Documentation Committee score (IKDC), the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS), and Tegner Activity Scale (TAS) assessed at a minimum of 24 months postoperatively. The correlation between variables was examined using Spearman correlation, linear, and ordinal regression analyses. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe mean age of the patients was 28.9 ± 7.8 years, and the mean follow-up period was 32.2 ± 5.0 (min–max: 25–39) months. A negative correlation was found between BMI and IKDC (r = − 0.363; p = 0.002) and TAS (r = − 0.436; p = 0.001). Statistically significant correlations were found between FNA and IKDC (p = 0.001), KOOS (p = 0.001), and TAS (p = 0.021). ROC analysis identified an FNA cut-off value of 42.2 mm² associated with a favourable IKDC outcome (≥ 75.9), although discrimination was modest (AUC = 0.635).ConclusionsFemoral notch area was associated with patient-reported outcomes after ACL reconstruction; however, its predictive value was limited. Additionally, an increase in BMI scores negatively impacted patients’ IKDC and TAS scores after ACLR.Level of evidenceIII, retrospective cohort study.Keywords: Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, Body mass index, Bone morphometry, Femoral notch area, Patient-reported outcome measures

Background

The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is one of the most important stabilizers of the knee joint and the most frequently injured knee ligament [1]. ACL injuries occur in 85 of every 100,000 people aged 16–39 years [2]. Many extrinsic and intrinsic risk factors for ACL injury in athletes have been reported. The extrinsic factors include contact sports, footwear, playing surfaces, and environmental factors, whereas the intrinsic factors include bone morphology and hormonal and neuromuscular factors [3]. Bone morphology is also reported to be an important intrinsic predisposing risk factor for anterior cruciate ligament injuries [4]. A decrease in notch volume and width, an increase in tibial slope, and a decrease in tibiofemoral congruity have been associated with increased risk of ACL injuries [5]. The existence of a narrow femoral notch can cause impingement with thick tendon grafts, whereas a high tibial slope can lead to early graft failure [6]. Based on these differences in bone morphology, incorporating surgical procedures, such as osteotomy, notchplasty, and lateral extra-articular tenodesis, into ACL reconstruction (ACLR) in ACL injuries may positively impact postoperative outcomes [7]. Examination of these bone morphology parameters in detail may guide the implementation of personalized ACLR. However, while femoral notch morphology has been widely studied as a risk factor for ACL injury, its association with patient-reported outcomes after ACL reconstruction remains insufficiently understood. Accordingly, our hypothesis is that a smaller femoral notch area is associated with poorer patient-reported outcomes after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effect of bone morphology on patient-reported outcomes in patients undergoing ACL reconstruction.

Methods

Study design and inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study was conducted at the Orthopedics and Traumatology Clinic of Niğde Ömer Halisdemir University Education and Research Hospital. The study protocol was approved by the Niğde Ömer Halisdemir University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Date: 05.05.2025, Decision No: 2025/48) and and the requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design of the study. The ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki were observed throughout the study.

All patients undergoing arthroscopic ACLR during the study period were screened. Patients who underwent arthroscopic ACLR due to ACL rupture between January 2019 and April 2023 were retrospectively evaluated. The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: (a) age 18–50 years; (b) at least 2 years of follow-up after surgery; (c) an isolated ACL rupture; and (d) availability of high-quality preoperative magnetic resonance (MR) images.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) incomplete or insufficient clinical data; (b) presence of multiple ligament injuries; (c) presence of concomitant meniscal or chondral pathology; (d) additional surgical procedures such as osteotomy, lateral extra-articular tenodesis, or notchplasty; (e) postoperative follow-up period shorter than 2 years; and (f) patients undergoing revision ACLR.

The patients’ demographic data, including age, sex, and body mass index (BMI), were obtained. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) were used to evaluate functional outcomes. The PROMs included the International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) Score [8] assessing symptoms, functional capacity, and sports performance after knee injury, the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) [9] evaluating pain, symptoms, daily living activities, sports, and quality of life after knee injuries or osteoarthritis, and the Tegner Activity Scale (TAS) [10] assessing patients’ physical and sports activity levels after ligament injuries. PROMs (IKDC, KOOS, and TAS) were collected at the latest follow-up visit (minimum 24 months after surgery). Failure endpoints such as graft re-rupture, revision ACL reconstruction, or tunnel widening were not evaluated, and only PROMs were evaluated because of the retrospective design of the study and the potential for information bias due to incomplete records.

Surgical technique and rehabilitation

All patients underwent arthroscopic, anatomically positioned, single-tunnel ACLR. The ipsilateral hamstring tendon was used as the graft. Femoral fixation was achieved using the fixed-length Endobutton system. Tibial fixation was performed using a bioabsorbable screw and a U staple. Arthroscopy during graft placement revealed that the graft did not impinge within the intercondylar notch in all cases. Patients who underwent notchplasty at this stage were excluded from the study.

No postoperative brace was used, and early weight-bearing was permitted. Exercises to support the joint range of motion and muscle activation were performed for the first 4 weeks based on the standard protocol. Patients were assessed by a physical therapist and referred to an individualized rehabilitation program starting from the fourth week.

Radiological measurements

The patients’ imaging was performed using the same imaging protocol with a 1.5-Tesla MR system (Siemens Magnetom Aera; Siemens Medical Systems, Erlangen, Germany). All evaluations were performed on the Sisoft Picture Archiving and Communication System imaging platform.

Measurements related to femoral notch morphology were performed using axial Proton Density Fat Suppressed (PDFS) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Axial images clearly showing the femoral condyles and the popliteus tendon groove at its most prominent point were used for each patient. Measurements of femoral morphology are shown in Fig. 1.

Measurement of femoral morphology (a) posterior condylar axis (PCA)(yellow line), condylar fossa height (CFH)(red line), condylar fossa width (CFW)(blue line) (b) Bicondylar width (BCW)(green line), c Intercondylar notch angle (ICNA)(blue angle), d femoral notch area (FNA, green-shaded area)

Condylar fossa height

The line tangential to the medial and lateral femoral condyles posteriorly was defined as the posterior femoral condylar axis (PCA) [11]. The shortest distance between the deepest point of the femoral notch and the PCA was defined as the condylar fossa height (CFH; analogous to notch height) [12]. CFH measurement is shown in Fig. 1a.

Condylar fossa width

The distance between the bone cortices parallel to the PCA, passing through the upper two-thirds of the CFH, was defined as the condylar fossa width (CFW; analogous to notch width) [12]. CFW measurement is shown in Fig. 1a.

Bicondylar width

The widest distance between the lateral bone cortices of the medial and lateral condyles, parallel to the PCA at the level where the popliteal fossa was the most prominent, was defined as the bicondylar width (BCW) [12, 13]. BCW measurement is shown in Fig. 1b.

Notch width index

The ratio of CFW to BCW was calculated as the notch width index (NWI); in other words, NWI = CFW/BCW [13].

Intercondylar notch angle

The angle between the lines extending from the deepest point of the femoral notch to the most inferior and inner aspects of the both femoral condyles was defined as the intercondylar notch angle (ICNA) [14]. ICNA measurement is shown in Fig. 1c.

Femoral notch area

A reference line was first drawn by connecting the posterior cartilage margins of the medial and lateral femoral condyles, followed by tracing the bony cortex of the intercondylar notch. FNA was defined as the enclosed area between these boundaries. The femoral notch area (FNA) was defined by Iriuchishima et al. using computed tomography slices [15]. It was measured for the first time using axial PDFS MRI images in the present study. FNA measurement is shown in Fig. 1d.

Sagittal T1-weighted images were used for measuring posterior tibial slope angles. The medial and lateral tibial slope angles were measured separately during the measurements. PDFS sequences were used in angle measurements to distinguish between the bone cortex and the cartilage [16]. Measurements of tibial morphology are shown in Fig. 2.

Measurement of tibial morphology (a) Determination of tibial anatomic axis (b) Measurement of lateral posterior tibial slope (LPTS) (c) Measurement of medial posterior tibial slope (MPTS)

Lateral posterior tibial slope

The angle between the reference line perpendicular to the anatomical axis of the tibia and the medial plateau surface of the tibia was described as lateral posterior tibial slope (LPTS) [17]. Anatomical axis of tibia is shown in Fig. 2a, LPTS is shown in Fig. 2b.

Medial posterior tibial slope

The angle between the reference line perpendicular to the anatomical axis of the tibia and the medial plateau surface of the tibia was defined as medial posterior tibial slope (MPTS) [17]. MPTS is shown in Fig. 2b.

Inter- and intraobserver reliability analysis

All radiological measurements were performed independently by an orthopedic specialist (H.Y.) and a radiology specialist (S.E.). Each observer repeated the measurements twice on the same MRI scans, with a 1-week interval between measurements. This ensured both intra- and interobserver reliability.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas categorical data were presented as frequency (n) and percentage (%) during data analysis.

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine whether all continuous variables were normally distributed, revealing that these variables were not normally distributed. TAS is an ordinal outcome measure, whereas IKDC and KOOS are continuous outcome measures. Ordinal logistic regression was used to evaluate the relationship between FNA and TAS, while simple linear regression analysis was used to evaluate the relationships FNA and KOOS, IKDC scores. Multivariable regression models were not performed because of the relatively small sample size and the large number of potential confounders. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the correlations between BMI and morphometric measurements and patient-reported outcomes (IKDC, KOOS, and TAS).

Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were used to determine the inter- and intraobserver reliabilities for radiographic measurements. The inter- and intraobserver reliability of BCW, CFW, CFH, ICNA, MPTS and FNA measurements was good to excellent, with ICC values ranging from 0.72 to 0.99.

The sample size was determined using G*Power (Version 3.1.9.4) software. Based on calculations assuming a type I error (α) of 0.05, a type II error (β) of 0.20 (power = 1 – β = 0.80), and a moderate effect size (f² = 0.15), the minimum sample size required for the study was determined to be 68 patients.

The optimal threshold value of FNA for predicting IKDC outcome was determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the cut-off selected by maximizing the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). An IKDC score threshold of 75.9 was used to define a favorable outcome, consistent with a previously reported threshold for a satisfactory symptom state after primary ACL reconstruction [18].

All data were entered into a Microsoft Excel (Office 2010; Microsoft Corp., WA, USA) database. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., NY, USA) software. A p value less than 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

Results

The study was conducted on 68 patients meeting the inclusion criteria out of 146 patients who underwent ACLR. The flowchart of the patient selection criteria is shown in Fig. 3. Of the 68 patients included in the study, 91.2% (n = 62) were male and 8.8% (n = 6) were female. The mean age of the patients was 28.9 ± 7.8 years. Surgery was performed on the left side in 52.9% (n = 36) and on the right side in 47.1% (n = 32) of the patients. The mean BMI of the patients was 26.6 ± 3.2 kg/m².

Simple linear regression analysis showed a significant correlation between FNA and KOOS (β = 6.48, p < 0.001, R² = 0.17) and IKDC (β = 8.72, p < 0.001, R² = 0.27) scores. Ordinal logistic regression analysis revealed a significant correlation between FNA and TAS (odds ratio = 2.62; 95% confidence interval = 1.36–5.07; p = 0.0047) scores. Thus, an increase in FNA was associated with a significant increase in functional outcomes.

The exploratory cut-off point for favorable outcome ( IKDC score ≥ 75.9) was determined to be 42.2 mm² (AUC = 0.635; sensitivity 86.7%, specificity 36.8%); however, its discriminatory ability was limited.

Discussion

Previous studies have investigated the effect of bone morphology on ACL injuries in recent years [19]. However, the present study evaluated the functional outcomes following ACLR based on femur and tibia morphology. This study also contributes to the literature by showing that FNA measurement is associated with functional outcomes after ACLR. Previous studies have primarily defined FNA on CT imaging [15]. In this study, we provide a standardized and reproducible method to measure FNA using MRI.

Female athletes are two to three times more prone to ACL injuries than male athletes [20]. However, Devana et al. reported that, although women were more prone to ACL injuries due to differences in knee morphology, ACLR surgery was performed more in men [21]. Some studies also demonstrated a higher number of male patients undergoing ACLR [22, 23]. A majority of participants in the present study were also men (91.2%). This might be due to the sporting habits in the region where the study was conducted.

Functional scores following ACLR and BMI are important parameters examined frequently. A systematic review of eight cohorts reported no correlation between the Lysholm score, KOOS score, and BMI, whereas patients with a BMI above 25 had lower IKDC scores [24]. Harput et al. performed a prospective study involving 91 patients and found that patients with a BMI above 25 had lower IKDC scores and quadriceps capacity after 6 months [25]. Kowalchuk et al. demonstrated that BMI was a modifiable risk factor associated with reduced function and medial chondrosis after ACLR [26]. The present study also found that BMI negatively impacted IKDC and TAS scores but did not influence KOOS scores.

Few studies have examined the functional outcomes of ACLR based on femur and femoral notch morphology. Overall, smaller notch measurements (e.g., decreased ICNA, lower NWI or notch width < 15 mm) have been associated with worse functional scores and a higher risk of graft failure, although some findings are contradictory [27–31]. The present study found that CFW increased the KOOS score, whereas BCW had a positive correlation with the IKDC score. The present study also did not find any correlation between other morphometric measurements of the femur and functional outcomes. However, the notch width was greater than 16 mm in all patients in the present study, resulting in selection bias. Therefore, our results should not be interpreted as demonstrating the effect of true notch stenosis or definite mechanical impingement. Rather, the observed association between FNA and patient-reported outcomes may reflect morphologic variation across a non-stenotic, normal-to-wide femoral notch configuration. Given the scarcity and heterogeneity of data, new and comprehensive studies should be conducted to examine the correlations between the femoral morphology and functional outcomes.

Previous studies have frequently investigated the correlation between posterior tibial slope (PTS) measurements and functional outcomes. Several studies showed that steeper PTS particularly greater than 12 degrees is associated with increased graft failure, tunnel expansion, tibial translation, and poorer long-term functional results [32–35]. A study examining 10-year functional outcomes after ACLR demonstrated that LPTS greater than 3.8 degrees and MPTS greater than 5.6 degrees negatively influenced functional outcomes [36]. In addition, reducing PTS in revision ACL surgeries has been reported to positively impact functional outcomes [37] However, in some studies, the association between increased PTS and functional outcomes could not be clearly demonstrated, and inconsistent findings have been reported [27, 35]. In the present study, PTS angles did not impact functional outcomes; however, all PTS measurements were below 12 degrees. Therefore, the effect of PTS values above 12 degrees on functional outcomes could not be evaluated, resulting in selection bias.

FNA was examined in this study due to several reasons. First, no previous study showed a significant correlation between notch volume and morphological measurements [38]. Second, Oshima et al. claimed that notch volume could be assessed alone, and the PCL volume in their study also influenced functional outcomes [39]. Third, Tashmann et al. stated that three-dimensional notch volume measurement might not show the true space for the ACL and the graft, and, therefore, two-dimensional measurements might provide more accurate information [40]. Further, a retrospective study with 140 patients found no significant correlation between notch volume and functional scores [41]. Consistent with previous findings, this study involved two-dimensional FNA measurements instead of three-dimensional measurements and aimed to evaluate the effect of these measurements on functional outcomes. We believe that the notch width is an important anatomical parameter in terms of ACLR, and including not only notch width but also notch height in the assessment more accurately reflects the notch area. Based on this approach, the present study revealed that FNA may be an important intrinsic factor in predicting functional outcomes. In addition, our cut-off value of 42.2 mm² should be interpreted with caution because our cohort was predominantly male. Further studies are needed to determine gender-specific FNA cut-off values.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. First, it evaluated short- to medium-term outcomes in a limited number of patients. Second, it did not examine complications such as revision rates, re-rupture rates, graft size and tunnel enlargement. Third, excluding patients with concomitant meniscal or chondral pathology may have resulted in a selection bias toward relatively healthier knees, thereby potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. Fourth, a majority of patients were men, resulting in bias. Fifth, the exclusion of the PCL area while evaluating the notch area could also be considered an important limitation. Finally, multiple statistical comparisons were performed across several morphometric parameters and patient-reported outcome measures. The possibility of type I error must be taken into account when interpreting statistically significant findings, particularly those with borderline p values.

Conclusions

Femoral notch area was associated with patient-reported outcomes after ACL reconstruction; however, its predictive value was limited. An increase in BMI negatively impacted patients’ IKDC and TAS scores after ACLR. Finally, bone morphology partially aided in predicting functional outcomes. However, comprehensive studies should be conducted to evaluate the effect of bone morphology on functional outcomes.
