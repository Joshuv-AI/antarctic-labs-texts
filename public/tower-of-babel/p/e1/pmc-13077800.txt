Anesthetic management of a patient with McCune-Albright syndrome complicated by pathological cervical fracture: a case report
Authors: Zhenghui Li, Hongli Yue, Weihua Cui
Published: 2026

Background

McCune-Albright syndrome is characterized by a variable combination of polyostotic fibrous dysplasia, café-au-lait macules, and hyperfunctioning endocrinopathies. From an anesthetic perspective, craniofacial involvement and prior growth hormone excess may increase the likelihood of difficult mask ventilation and tracheal intubation; spinal deformity and pathological fractures increase the risk of neurological deterioration and positioning-related injury; and associated endocrinopathies (e.g., hyperthyroidism or cortisol abnormalities) may contribute to perioperative hemodynamic instability [1–5, 12–16]. We report the anesthetic management of an adult patient with MAS and severe upper cervical spine involvement requiring occipito-cervical fusion.

Case presentation

A 40-year-old woman (height 164 cm; weight 61.5 kg) was admitted with chronic neck pain for 3 years, which worsened over 3 weeks, followed by progressive neck weakness for 1 week resulting in difficulty maintaining an upright head posture without manual support. She reported bilateral occipital numbness without upper-limb radicular symptoms or bowel/bladder dysfunction (Table 1).

Disease progression

Past history was notable for clinically diagnosed MAS with craniofacial fibrous dysplasia and café-au-lait macules (Fig. 1A, B). Endocrine manifestations included precocious puberty and a previously resected growth hormone-secreting pituitary macroadenoma (age 27). At this admission, she had no clinical features suggestive of uncontrolled endocrine hyperfunction, and preoperative hormone tests (thyroid function, cortisol, growth hormone/insulin-like growth factor-1, and prolactin) were within reference ranges.

On examination, vital signs were stable (noninvasive blood pressure 111/76 mmHg; heart rate 76 beats/min). Airway assessment revealed markedly restricted neck motion (rigid cervical collar in place), mouth opening < 2 fingerbreadths, and a Modified Mallampati grade III view(Fig. 1C). Tenderness and percussion pain were present over the C2-C3 spinous processes and paravertebral areas, accompanied by occipital numbness. The patient was alert with a normal gait. Muscle tone and strength were normal in all extremities, with no pathological reflexes. There was no bowel or bladder dysfunction.

Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated polyostotic fibrous dysplasia involving the craniofacial skeleton and upper cervical vertebrae, with destructive lesions of C1-C3 and a pathological fracture of C2 causing atlantoaxial instability (Fig. 2).

The C1–C3 vertebrae are fused and occupied by cystic and fibrous lesions with fluid–fluid levels, consistent with fibrous dysplasia. There is evidence of a pathological fracture through C2 with atlantoaxial instability

The final diagnosis was MAS with a pathological fracture of the C2 vertebra causing atlantoaxial instability. Given the risk of neurological deterioration with neck manipulation, meticulous cervical spine protection was required during airway management and positioning. The patient was scheduled for posterior cervical decompression and occipito-cervical fusion. The surgical team planned a posterior occipito-cervical fusion spanning the occiput to C3 with internal fixation using screws and rods.

Anesthetic management

Given the anticipated difficult airway and the unstable upper cervical spine, awake flexible fiberoptic bronchoscope-guided oral intubation under light sedation with preserved spontaneous ventilation was planned as the primary technique. A difficult airway cart was prepared in advance, including a video laryngoscope, supraglottic airway devices, and an emergency cricothyrotomy kit as backup options.

Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was established, including non-invasive blood pressure, electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, and the Bispectral Index (BIS). An intravenous line was secured in the right lower limb. Intravenous midazolam (2 mg) and sufentanil (5 µg) were administered for light sedation and analgesia. With the rigid cervical collar in place, manual inline stabilization was maintained throughout airway topicalization and intubation to minimize cervical spine motion. Under local anesthesia, the left radial artery was cannulated for continuous blood pressure monitoring.

Subsequently, the pharynx was topically anesthetized using a lidocaine aerosol spray. A translaryngeal block was performed via cricothyroid membrane puncture, instilling 5 mL of 2% lidocaine into the trachea. A 7.5-mm internal diameter endotracheal tube was successfully inserted using a flexible fiberoptic bronchoscope with an outer diameter of 4.8 mm via the oral route.

Following confirmation of correct endotracheal tube placement, general anesthesia was induced with sufentanil (20 µg), cisatracurium (14 mg), and etomidate (12 mg). A right femoral venous catheter was inserted for central venous access and rapid fluid administration. After the induction dose, no additional neuromuscular blocking agents were administered to facilitate intraoperative motor evoked potential (MEP) monitoring.

Anesthesia was maintained with low-dose sevoflurane (< 0.5 minimum alveolar concentration [MAC]) combined with continuous infusions of propofol (3 mg/kg/h) and remifentanil (0.1 µg/kg/min), supplemented with intermittent boluses of sufentanil (HR and/or MAP > 20% above baseline), to ensure adequate anesthesia while preserving SSEP/MEP signal quality. Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was targeted at 85–90 mmHg to optimize spinal cord perfusion, and norepinephrine was titrated in the approximate range of 0.03–0.10 µg/kg/min as needed.

After induction, the patient was positioned prone using a team-assisted log-roll maneuver with axial stabilization of the head and neck to maintain cervical alignment. Erythromycin ophthalmic ointment was applied and the eyelids were covered with protective eye pads and taped closed; the eyes were checked periodically to avoid external pressure. After positioning, the endotracheal tube position was immediately reconfirmed. Ventilatory parameters (airway pressure, tidal volume) and hemodynamic indices were closely monitored throughout the procedure to ensure adequate ventilation and perfusion.

Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring, including somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs) and motor evoked potentials (MEPs), was employed. Anesthetic depth was titrated to maintain a BIS value between 40 and 60. Mechanical ventilation was provided in volume-controlled mode with a tidal volume of approximately 6–8 mL/kg, a positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 5 cmH₂O, and a respiratory rate adjusted to maintain an end-tidal carbon dioxide (PETCO₂) between 35 and 45 mmHg. Peak airway pressures were kept below 30 cmH₂O.

The total surgical duration was 585 min. Intraoperative fluid administration included 4500 mL of Lactated Ringer’s solution, 100 mL of normal saline, 500 mL of 6% hydroxyethyl starch, 520 mL of allogeneic suspended red blood cells, and 400 mL of fresh frozen plasma. The estimated blood loss was 1600 mL, and urine output was 2800 mL. Body temperature was monitored using an axillary probe and remained ≥ 36 °C throughout the procedure; therefore, no active warming measures (e.g., forced-air warming blanket) were implemented.

Allogeneic blood products were crossmatched and prepared preoperatively; due to ongoing blood loss and a hemoglobin level of 8 g/dL on arterial blood gas analysis, transfusion was performed and administered via a standard blood administration set with an in-line filter.

For postoperative analgesia, a multimodal regimen was used. At the end of surgery, the orthopedic surgeons infiltrated the wound with local anesthetic. An intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) pump was prepared (sufentanil 100 µg, flurbiprofen axetil 100 mg, ondansetron 8 mg, diluted with normal saline). The PCA pump was programmed with a background infusion of 2 mL/h, a 2 mL bolus dose, a 10-minute lockout interval, and a maximum dose limit of 20 mL per 4 hours.

At the conclusion of the surgery, anesthetic agents were discontinued. After the return of adequate spontaneous ventilation, the patient was observed breathing room air for 5 min, during which SpO2 remained above 95%. The patient was able to follow simple commands. She was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) with the endotracheal tube secured in place for planned postoperative airway observation. In the ICU, she was managed without sedation or neuromuscular blockade and did not require continued mechanical ventilation; routine airway care and humidified oxygen/nebulized therapy were provided. Extubation readiness was assessed by stable hemodynamics, adequate oxygenation/ventilation on minimal support, effective cough and secretion clearance, absence of clinical airway edema, and a satisfactory cuff-leak test. The tracheal tube was successfully extubated on the following day. The patient remained hemodynamically and respiratorily stable and reported no complaints of hoarseness or other airway-related discomfort. No new neurological deficits were noted postoperatively, and the patient was mobilized with a cervical brace during recovery.

Discussion

Preoperative evaluation and assessment

In addition to routine examinations, special attention should be paid during the preoperative evaluation of these patients to the involved endocrine systems and the extent of their dysfunction. Key considerations include screening for hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, Cushing’s syndrome, acromegaly, and fibrous dysplasia [1]. Endocrine abnormalities that may compromise anesthetic safety should be actively managed. In this case, the patient had previously undergone endoscopic transsphenoidal resection of a growth hormone-secreting macroadenoma, and all preoperative hormone levels were within normal range.

Additionally, a thorough airway assessment is essential, with particular attention to the impact of various endocrine abnormalities on the airway. In MAS patients with acromegaly, enlargement of the lips, tongue, and oropharyngeal soft tissues, along with relative mandibular hypoplasia, may impair laryngoscopic view and increase the risk of difficult intubation. Beyond endocrine disorders, maxillary or mandibular enlargement due to fibrous dysplasia, as well as trismus caused by temporomandibular joint fibrosis and restricted mobility, can complicate mask ventilation [1–3]. Although this patient currently has normal growth hormone levels, her history of a growth hormone-secreting macroadenoma has resulted in facial changes that increase the possibility of difficult intubation. Furthermore, the patient exhibits significantly limited neck mobility due to upper cervical vertebral destruction, multiple cervical space-occupying lesions, kyphotic deformity, and pathological fracture. This is compounded by difficulty in head extension and a mouth opening of less than two fingerbreadths, further increasing the intubation challenge [5, 6, 9, 11, 12, 14].

Anesthesia plan

According to the 2022 ASA Practice Guidelines for Management of the Difficult Airway, awake intubation should be considered when a difficult airway is anticipated. In this patient with marked atlantoaxial instability, severely limited neck mobility, restricted mouth opening, and craniofacial changes related to fibrous dysplasia/acromegaly, awake flexible fiberoptic bronchoscope-guided oral intubation with manual inline stabilization was selected to minimize cervical spine motion while preserving spontaneous ventilation. To facilitate tolerance while maintaining airway reflexes and spontaneous respiration, minimal, titrated sedation (small doses of midazolam and sufentanil) was combined with airway topical anesthesia, including lidocaine spray for mucosal surface anesthesia and a transtracheal injection via cricothyroid puncture to further blunt airway reflexes. This multimodal approach achieved adequate patient comfort and cooperation with preserved spontaneous breathing. Therefore, dexmedetomidine was not used because satisfactory awake-intubation conditions had already been achieved with brief low-dose sedation plus topicalization, while avoiding the need for continuous infusion and potential hemodynamic effects [5, 6]. The nasal route was avoided because the patient had previously undergone endoscopic transnasal pituitary surgery with skull base reconstruction. Backup options included video laryngoscopy, supraglottic airway devices, and an emergency cricothyrotomy kit.

Peripheral intravenous access was established in the lower limb to maintain reliable access after prone positioning and extensive draping. A femoral central venous catheter was selected to obtain central access while avoiding neck manipulation in the setting of marked atlantoaxial instability and limited cervical mobility [5, 11].

Given the need for intraoperative somatosensory evoked potential (SSEP) and motor evoked potential (MEP) monitoring, we used a balanced intravenous-inhalational technique (propofol and remifentanil infusions with sevoflurane maintained below 0.5 MAC and intermittent sufentanil) to provide stable anesthesia while minimizing depression of evoked potential signals. Neuromuscular blockade was limited to a single induction dose to facilitate intubation; no additional muscle relaxant was administered to allow reliable MEP monitoring [7].

Because spinal cord perfusion may be compromised in the setting of cervical spinal cord compression/instability and prolonged prone positioning, we targeted a MAP of 85–90 mmHg and used norepinephrine when necessary to counteract anesthetic- and position-related hypotension, thereby optimizing spinal cord perfusion during instrumentation [8].

The patient was placed in the prone position. During positioning, special attention was paid to maintain axial alignment to prevent exacerbation of cervical spine injury, including padding of pressure points and a team-assisted log-roll to prone position while maintaining spinal alignment. After turning prone, proper positioning was confirmed, including endotracheal tube placement, patency of the breathing circuit, and avoidance of abdominal organ and major vascular compression. Eye protection was applied throughout prolonged prone positioning, with care taken to avoid orbital compression. Invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring was implemented to ensure adequate perfusion of the spinal cord and vital organs. Fluid intake and output, arterial blood gas parameters, electrolyte and acid-base balance, and hemoglobin levels were closely monitored throughout the procedure.

Postoperative management plan

An elective strategy of postoperative airway observation with the endotracheal tube in place was adopted, with extubation performed on postoperative day 1 after objective assessment confirmed readiness.

Given the expected severity of pain after occipito-cervical fusion and the adverse effects of high-dose opioids, a multimodal analgesic strategy was adopted, including surgical wound infiltration and intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (PCA). Regional techniques (e.g., cervical paraspinal or plexus blocks) were not performed because extensive fibrous dysplasia and planned instrumentation may distort local anatomy and increase procedural risk.

In this case, tracheal intubation was performed smoothly, intraoperative vital signs remained stable, neurophysiological monitoring was satisfactory, and postoperative analgesia was effective, facilitating a rapid recovery. This demonstrates the rationality and effectiveness of the perioperative anesthesia and analgesic strategy.

Published experience with anesthesia in MAS is limited and largely consists of case reports and rare-disease recommendations. Kang and Jeong reported awake fiberoptic intubation using a spray-as-you-go technique for anticipated difficult airway during endoscopic nasal surgery in a patient with craniofacial fibrous dysplasia, which is consistent with our decision to prioritize an awake approach; in our case, the additional risk of atlantoaxial instability required strict cervical immobilization. In contrast, Kumar et al. described successful mask ventilation and tracheal intubation after conventional intravenous induction for a neurointerventional procedure, emphasizing the importance of preparing a difficult airway cart and anticipating endocrine and skeletal comorbidities. In obstetric anesthesia, Varvarousi et al. used a combined spinal–epidural technique for cesarean delivery to mitigate the risk of an unexpectedly high spinal block in the setting of short stature and scoliosis. Compared with these reports, the distinguishing feature of our case was the combination of severe upper cervical spine involvement and the requirement for prolonged prone occipito-cervical fusion with SSEP/MEP monitoring, which increased the importance of cervical immobilization and a planned postoperative airway strategy [5, 9–11].

Conclusion

This case highlights the complexities involved in anesthetizing a patient with McCune-Albright syndrome and cervical spine pathological fractures. Preoperative planning, including airway assessment, personalized anesthesia techniques, and careful postoperative management, is essential for ensuring patient safety and improving clinical outcomes. The use of awake fiberoptic bronchoscopy for intubation in MAS patients with difficult airways provides a reliable and effective method of managing such complex cases.
