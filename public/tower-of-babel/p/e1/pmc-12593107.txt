Molecular Insights into the Protic Organic Ionic Plastic Crystal (POIPC): Effect of Dopants and Vacancies
Authors: Sanjeet Kumar Singh, Abdessamia Rhazaoui, Sadollah Ebrahimi, Yasmine Benabed, Jean-Christophe Daigle, Armand Soldera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In recent years, significant research effort has been dedicated to the development of robust, high-performance solid electrolyte batteries. A wide range of materials, including polymers, ceramics, and glasses, etc., have been tested and characterized as potential solid electrolyte candidates. In this respect, organic ionic plastic crystals (OIPCs) have attracted significant research attention owing to their promising properties. However, the OIPCs from different crystal classes, composed of distinct cations and anions, may display widely different phase behaviors and properties. Herein, following our previous work on pure POIPC, we conduct extensive molecular dynamics simulations, in conjunction with experimental methods, to investigate the effect of dopants and vacancies on the structural, thermodynamic, and dynamical properties of POIPC [DBUH]-[FSI], consisting of the protic [DBUH]+ cation with labile proton (H+) on the nitrogen atom. Simulations have been performed for the LiFSI doping fractions of 1.31%, 2.52%, 5.39%, and 10.55%. Additionally, the effect of Schottky vacancies was examined at concentrations of 0.23% and 0.46% for the 1.31% and 2.52% doped systems. The simulated solid–solid and the solid–liquid phase transition temperatures are in very good agreement with the DSC data. Our findings indicate that the effect of the vacancy is more pronounced at the lower doping levels. Hydrogen bonding analysis reveals that the protic N2–H1 site forms the strongest hydrogen bond in the system, with N2–H1--O being the dominant hydrogen bond. Using rotational autocorrelation functions (RACFs), we identify distinct solid phases associated with different rotational modes in the systems, confirming the presence of at least three distinct solid phases across the doping range. Translational dynamics analysis affirms that the [FSI] anion is the most mobile component of the system. The activation energy (E a) computed using the Arrhenius plot, is in considerable agreement with the experimental data. The lithium cation transference number (t Li+ ) agrees very well with the experimental analysis.

Introduction

The use of solid electrolyte batteries offers significant advantages over the conventional liquid electrolyte such as improved safety, higher energy density, longer lifespan, and lesser environmental impact. Among the solid-state electrolyte materials, the organic ionic plastic crystals (OIPCs) are new promising candidates with several advantages over the electrolytes based on ceramics, glasses and polymers. , Owing to their superior properties such as high chemical/electrochemical stability, negligible vapor pressure, high thermal stability, and wider plastic crystal temperature range, the potential application of these materials is evolving in various energy storage devices such as solar cells, , fuel cells, ,, capacitors, and lithium batteries. −

The OIPCs are characterized by a short-range disordered structure and a long-range ordered lattice in the plastic phases. With rise in temperature, the different rotational/translational or reorientational motions of the cations and anions occur within these OIPCs, giving rise to the short-range disordered structure and one or several solid–solid phase transitions before the melting point. The local disordered structure is also responsible for boosting the ionic conductivity and mechanical plasticity of these materials by the creation of defects such as slip planes and vacancies. , Although there is a complex interplay of short-range molecular motions with an increase in temperature, the long-range crystalline structure of these materials is essentially maintained in the plastic phases. However, in the high-temperature plastic phases, subtle changes in the long-range order: such as transformations in lattice symmetry or unit cell parameters can occur, for example in plastic phase I just before melting. − This is evident in phase transitions where enhanced molecular mobility leads to higher symmetry structures without the loss of long-range order. In OIPCs, the complexity of the phase transition behavior depends largely on the chemistry of the cations and anions present in the system, ,− which in-turn dictates their microscopic/macroscopic properties and their subsequent applications.

The ionic conductivity of neat OIPCs at ambient temperature is relatively lower compared to that of the ionic liquids and organic solvent electrolytes. ,− However, the lithium (Li) doped OIPCs have been found to exhibit improved ionic conductivity and electrochemical performance using different combinations of cation–anion and composites. − ,,− Computational modeling in conjunction with the experimental techniques has been widely utilized to explore new materials and has greatly aided understanding of their potential for battery applications. ,,− In this respect, molecular dynamics (MD) is a compelling tool to characterize the molecular structural and dynamical behavior of these novel materials for diverse applications. First, MD simulation of Li-doped OIPC was performed by Jin et al. to corroborate the effects of doping the OIPC tetra­methyl­ammonium dicyanamide ([TMA]­[DCA]) with the metal ion. The crystal structure, as given by the X-ray structural analysis, was used to construct the unit cell, and NPT ensemble simulation was performed for temperatures ranging from 200 to 600 K. Results suggested the cluster formation between the anion and the Li ion in the plastic crystal phase. These clusters restrict the rotational mobility of the anion on a shorter time scale but lead to creation of more free volume in the remaining systems at longer time scales, thus enhancing the diffusion of all the ions present in the system. Chen et al. carried out the MD simulation of the lithium and sodium ion doped OIPC diethyl­(methyl)­(isobutyl)­phosphonium hexafluorophosphate ([P1,2,2,4]-[PF6]) to reveal the molecular insights into the structure and transport properties of both the ions in the plastic crystal phase. Simulations were performed using the CHARMM force field for doping concentrations of 0.93 and 8.3 mol% respectively. Their analysis revealed the higher mobility of the Li ion compared with the Na ion. The reason for this is attributed to the different coordination geometries in the first solvation shell and the corresponding potential energy barrier changes linked to the solvation structure of the alkali metal ions.

Detailed MD simulations were conducted to investigate the effect on the translational, rotational, and thermal stability of the OIPC 1-methyl-3-methyl­imidazolium hexafluoro­phosphate ([MMIM]­[PF6]) doped with sodium, potassium and lithium ions, the study revealed that the smallest ion (Li+) prompts the highest degree of distortion of the host OIPC matrix by creating larger free volumes. Furthermore, different alkali ions follow different modes of translational diffusion in the OIPC matrix, and a similar effect is observed for the rotational mobility of the host ions also. All-atom molecular dynamics simulation performed to study the effects of defects (vacancies) on the heterogeneous dynamics of OIPCs led to the conclusion that the motion of vacancies is dependent not only on temperature of the system but also on the distance between the vacancies. Their study further confirms that the presence of vacancies in the system enhances the dynamic heterogeneity, which, in turn, facilitates the mobility of ions. Additionally, it was also noticed that the vacancies in the OIPCs promote the coupled translational motion of the cations and anions, in agreement with the experimental findings. In another related study by the same group, MD simulation was used to analyze the impact of defects such as Schottky vacancies and grain boundaries in OIPCs on the structure and transport mechanism of both the matrix and the dopant ions. The simulated system consisted of the OIPC 1,3-dimethyl­imidazolium hexafluoro­phosphate ([MMIM]­[PF6]) doped with a lithium ion. It was observed that the introduction of defects led to the changes in the local structure around the metal ion, with a high concentration of the Schottky vacancies increasing the overall ion conduction but simultaneously decreasing the transference number. It was further concluded that the rotational mobility of the matrix cation is more pronounced within the grain boundaries.

In this study, building on our previous work on the pure protic OIPC 1,7-diazabicyclo[5.4.0]­undec-7-ene - bis­(fluorosulfonyl)­imide ([DBUH]-[FSI]), herein, we conduct detailed MD simulations to examine the effects of dopants and vacancies on the [DBUH]-[FSI] system. Previously, using an innovative synthesis method, the [DBUH]­[FSI] compound reported by Benabed et al. was obtained with a purity of 99.8%. This experimental crystalline structure was used to gain molecular insight into the pure POIPC by using MD simulation. Experimental results exhibit a melting point above 318.15 K and multiple solid–solid phase transitions occurring at ∼285 and 305 K before melting. Within this temperature range, the anions display liquid-like diffusion behavior while the compound remains in a solid state. The experimental density of the sample measured using an AccuPyc 1330 instrument (Micromeritics) is 1.440 g/cm3 at 298 K. At room temperature, the ionic conductivity of DBUH+/FSI– reaches approximately 1.4 × 10–5 S·cm–1, a value considered suitable for energy-storage applications. Due to its promising conductivity and phase behavior, it is noteworthy to analyze the effect of dopants and vacancies on this material.

Computational Methods

The atomistic simulations have been performed to analyze the effect of doping (LiFSI) and vacancies on the structural, thermodynamic, and dynamical properties of [DBUH]-[FSI] plastic crystal. The initial pure crystalline samples were generated using the experimental structure from Benabed et al. The initial systems, generated using MedeA, contained 432 ion pairs with the following triclinic unit cell parameters: a = 11.29 Å, b = 8.96 Å, c = 9.45 Å, and angles α = 69.09°, β = 112.85°, γ = 102.69°. The details of the force field parameters used can be found in. The structural formula of [DBUH]-[FSI], with atom names as those used in this work, is shown in Figure . The initially prepared pure crystalline systems were doped with LiFSI at four different level of doping (f): f = 1.31%, 2.52%, 5.39% and 10.55% (weight%). The doped systems were set up by randomly substituting the required number of DBUH cation with Li ion (cationic substitution) at each of the doping percentage, respectively. The doping levels: 1.31%, 2.52%, 5.39%, and 10.55%, were chosen to span from dilute to relatively concentrated regimes while remaining computationally feasible and comparable with experimental DSC/NMR conditions. Furthermore, this enables us to observe the gradual effects of doping on the phase behavior and ion dynamics of the system.

Structural formula of [DBUH]-[FSI] with atom labels.

We have considered three different concentrations of Schottky vacancies (C v: C v = 0%, 0.23%, and 0.46%. In order to introduce Schottky vacancies into the systems, single and double pairs of [DBUH] and [FSI] ions are selected randomly and removed from the supercell. A single pair of ions removed from the system out of 432 pairs is equivalent to a vacancy concentration of 0.23 mol%, while the vacancies fraction in experiments usually ranges from 0.1 to 0.2 mol%. , The doped systems of 1.31% and 2.52% were selected to investigate the effects of Schottky defects on the samples. These concentrations were selected for defect studies because these systems have the most crystalline orders, and vacancies are most impactful at lower dopant concentrations, where the lattice is less disrupted by the lithium salt. At higher doping levels, vacancy effects become masked by disorder and hence were not considered. Doping of the purely crystalline samples and creation of vacancies were done in such a way that the system remained overall charge neutral. For all of the different levels of doping and vacancy percentage, we have generated five different initial configurations. In the present work, we mostly use the nomenclature: 1.31-1V, 1.3-–2V, 2.52-1V, and 2.52-2V, to represent the one vacancy and two vacancy samples of 1.31% and 2.52% doped system, respectively. Alternatively, we have also used the abbreviation C v to indicate the vacancies concentrations. Figure shows a snapshot of the systems with and without vacancies for a doping percentage of 2.32%. Details of the various simulated systems are shown in Table S1 of the Supporting Information (SI) section.

Sample snapshot for the systems (a) without vacancy (b) with one vacancy (c) with two vacancies.

In this study, the various systems were subjected to simulations for temperatures ranging from 80 to 500 K. Temperatures were varied in 20-K increments from 80 to 500 K, with each temperature point undergoing separate equilibration and production runs, instead of a continuous heating process. At each temperature, the samples were first equilibrated by a 1 ns NVT run, followed by a 1 ns NPT run. The production runs were executed in an NPT ensemble for 5 ns. Thus, the total simulation duration per temperature was 7 ns, which included 1 ns of NVT equilibration, 1 ns of NPT equilibration, and an NPT production run of 5 ns. The equations of motion were evolved using the velocity-Verlet integrator with a time step size of δt = 1.0 fs, and the systems were restricted to a specific temperature and pressure using the Nose-Hoover thermostat and barostat. − The coupling constants for the thermostat and barostat were 0.1 ps (100 δt) and 1 ps (1000 δt), respectively. The lj9_6 functional form, with a cutoff of 1.4 nm, was used to compute the van der Waals interactions, with tail corrections. The pairwise electrostatic interactions were estimated using a cutoff of 1.4 nm, while beyond this distance the particle–particle particle-mesh solver (pppm), with relative error in forces set to 10–5, was used to account for the long-range Coulombic forces. The LAMMPS simulation package was utilized to perform all the MD simulations, while TRAVIS was used to carry out the analyses of the systems. ,

Experimental Methods

[DBUH]­[FSI] was synthesized following the procedure reported by Benabed et al. The resulting salt was dried under vacuum at 50 °C for 48 h. Samples (5–10 mg) were hermetically sealed in aluminum pans inside a glovebox (H2O < 0.5 ppm, O2 < 0.5 ppm) and analyzed for melting point determination using a TA Instruments DSC2500. Measurements were conducted under nitrogen atmosphere from −90 to 150 °C at a heating rate of 2 °C·min–1. The second heating cycle was used for data analysis. Thermal transitions across the doping levels were identified as the temperature corresponding to the endothermic peak. The uncertainty in transition temperatures is estimated at ±1 °C, based on instrument calibration and thermal lag. The uncertainty in enthalpy values is approximately ±3%, considering calibration, baseline selection, and sample mass accuracy.

Diffusion coefficients (D) were measured using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, conducted on a 500 MHz WB Bruker Avance Neo NMR spectrometer equipped with a double resonance diffusion probe (Diff50). Gradient pulse durations ranged from 1.0 to 1.5 ms, with diffusion times in the range of 100 to 800 ms. The gradient strength was incrementally varied in 16 steps from 100 to 2400 G/cm. The self-diffusion coefficient of the ions were obtained by measuring the nuclei 1H (cation), 19F (anion), and 7Li (metal ion). Diffusion coefficients extracted from NMR measurements were used to calculate the activation energy (E a) and the lithium cation transference number (t Li+ ).

Results and Discussion

Thermodynamic Properties

In order to depict the thermodynamic properties of the systems, the density (ρ), melting point (T m), and specific heat capacity (C p) have been computed. Variation of density and specific heat capacity with respect to the change in the doping percentage, vacancy concentration, and temperature is displayed in Figure and respectively. As should be the trend, the density of the samples decreases gradually with increase in temperature, a sharp fall in density is observed around the melting temperature. All crystalline samples experiences the first order phase transition at the melting temperature. Had the simulated system been infinitely large, we would have observed a discontinuous first order phase transition at the melting point. A comparative plot evincing the variation of density with respect to the doping percentage at all of the simulated temperatures is shown in Figure (a). It is evident from Figure (a) that the density of the samples increases with an increase in the doping percentage at a specific temperature, specifically in the high-temperature regime. The density of 10.55% sample is always greater than the remaining doping fractions at all temperatures. This pinpoints that the addition of LiFSI leads to improved packing efficiency of ions in the system. In the low-temperature region, except in the phase transition zone, the density of 5.39 samples is slightly higher than the 1.31 and 2.52 systems, while it remains the same for the 1.31 and 2.52 samples. It is clear in the phase transition zone that the sample with the lowest doping fraction maintains the crystal order in the system to a maximum extent. The effect of vacancy on the system’s density is elucidated in Figure S1 of the SI section. It is seen that for the pure and vacancy system of 1.31, the density of the one vacancy and without vacancy system is the same at all temperatures, whereas the density for the two-vacancy system is only narrowly lower than the other two until a temperature of 280 K and from 320 K all three systems share the same density. An interesting trend is observed in the transition regime, where we do observe a marginally larger difference in density between the two-vacancy system and the remaining two. Lower density signals the availability of higher free volume in the system, which might expedite the dynamics of the system. The densities of the 2.52% doped system and its vacancy variant are almost alike at all temperatures. Thus, we conclude that the presence of a vacancy has little and no impact on the density of the 1.31% and 2.52% doped system, respectively.

Structural Properties

Radial Distribution Function (RDF)

RDF is a widely used tool to gain insight into the structural details of simulated microstructures. It measures the variation in the particle density of the specified atom or molecule as a function of distance around the reference particle, thus quantitatively characterizing the arrangement of atoms in the systems. To inspect the evolution of the structure of the POIPC as a function of temperature, doping percentage, and vacancies, we have computed the RDF between the center-of-mass (COM) of the various atoms/molecules present in the system. The RDF was calculated for the pairs: DBUH-DBUH, DBUH-FSI, DBUH-LI, FSI-FSI, FSI-LI, and LI-LI within the temperature range of interest. RDFs between each pair for the doping fraction of 1.31 and 10.55 is shown in Figure and , RDFs among the remaining pairs is reported in Figures S2–S7 of the SI section. At the lowest simulated temperature of 80 K, the RDFs display multiple sharp peaks for the various pairs, especially for the doping percentage of 1.31, 2.52, and 5.39, representing a highly ordered rigid crystal structure of the systems. Moving on from the doping percentage of 1.31 to 10.55, it is noted that for 10.55% the RDF takes on a relatively simpler structure with several peaks in the RDF, both at the short- and long-range, vanishing. This is especially evident for the pairs DBUH-DBUH, DBUH-FSI, FSI-FSI, and LI-LI. Therefore, the system with a doping of 10.55% LiFSI more likely resembles the amorphous phase. This is also confirmed from the DSC analysis, where the magnitude of the melting transition peak is minimum for the 10.55% doped fraction, which means that the above-mentioned doped fraction possesses the lowest percentage of crystallinity. The RDF for the pair FSI-LI presents two sharp peaks at 3 and 5 Å, indicating a strong interaction between the pairs at these distances. The intensity of the second peak is higher than the first peak for the doping percentage of 1.31, 2.52, and 5.39, in the low temperature regime. To decipher the origin of the greater peak intensity for the second peak, we obtain the RDF at 80 K between the metal cation and different atoms of FSI, i.e. N, F, and O, and the same is shown in Figure S22. From Figure S22, it is evident that the first peak in LI-FSI RDF in Figure correlates to the strongest interaction between the LI atom and oxygen of FSI, while at a distance of ∼5 Å, there is significant secondary interaction between the nitrogen, fluorine, and oxygen atom. Thus, the second peak in LI-FSI RDF in Figure corresponds to this secondary interaction; the intensity of this peak is higher than the first peak primarily because of the greater number of atomic pairs in this shell (N, F, and O) compared to the first shell. Also, there might be the substantial contribution to the second peak coming from the sulfur atom which is directly bonded to the oxygen atom to which LI primarily coordinates. Similar coordination behavior have also been reported in the literature for Na-FSI and LI-FSI. , While for the 10.55%, the trend reverses with intensity of the first peak being greater than that of the second peak. However, with rise in temperature, the magnitude of the peak intensity for both peaks becomes equal. The RDF between different pairs takes on a liquid like appearance at temperatures of 320 K for 1.31%, 280 K for 2.52%, 260 K for 5.59%, and 240 K for 10.55%, confirming the transition to a highly disordered phase.

RDF between the various pairs for the doping of 1.31%.

RDF between the various pairs for the doping of 10.55%.

There is negligible variation in the structure of the system with and without vacancy for the doping percentage of 1.31 and the vacancy concentrations of 0.23% (1.31-1V) and 0.46% (1.31-2V) in the low temperature regime, except for the LI-LI pair. For the LI-LI pair in 1.31-2V system, we do observe a new peak at ∼7 Å as observed in Figure S3, in addition to the first peak at ∼5.4 Å for all three systems. This additional peak in the 1.31-2V system persist until a temperature of 200 K. Furthermore, the intensity of the first peak for the 1.31-2V system is higher compared to the 1.31 and 1.31-1V system. This demonstrates the vacancy induced enhanced probability of metal ion clustering for the 1.31-2V system, principally because of the additional local free volume in the sample. At 300 K, the RDF between various pairs for the 1.31-2V system exhibits significant variations relative to the samples without vacancy. The RDF for the system with two vacancies displays liquid like structure at 300 K, while the system without vacancy manifests the presence of the short-range and long-range order in the samples at the same temperature, the changes are more prominent for the pairs DBUH-DBUH, DBUH-FSI, and FSI-FSI. On comparing the RDFs for the doping of 2.52% with that of the systems with one and two vacancies, it is to be noted that the effect of vacancy on the structure of the system is very less evident for 2.52% than 1.31%. At 2.52% doping, the increased number of Li ions already introduces sufficient local disorder in the crystalline lattice, reducing the relative effect of additional vacancies in the system. At 1.31%, the lattice is more ordered, so vacancies play a stronger role. For 2.52% also, the effect of vacancy is particularly apparent for the LI-LI pair in the low temperature region of <280 K, but here again the changes are less marked than 1.31%. Altogether, the RDF confirms the trend discovered in the thermodynamic properties section that the crystal structure hardly changes by the introduction of the vacancy defects for the 2.52% samples, and the 1.31% and 1.31-1V samples. The effect of vacancy is more dominant in the melting transition regime for the 1.31-2V samples.

The RDF analysis reveals two important temperature ranges where the likely changes in the structure of the crystals takes place. The first one, although a minor one, is in the temperature range of 180–200 K. It is observed that for the doping percentage of 1.31, minor peaks in the RDF pair DBUH-DBUH at about 10 and 16 Å disappears at 200 K, similarly for the pair DBUH-LI minor peak at 7.5 Å is merged with the first principal peak at the temperature of 180 K, similar changes are also observed in the LI-LI pair. It is also noticed that several miniature peaks at a distance greater than 10 Å are completely overlapped for the pairs DBUH-LI, FSI-LI, and LI-LI. Analogous changes in the RDFs are also detected for the other doping percentage in the aforementioned temperature range. Overall, in the temperature segment of 180–200 K, apart from the small changes in the RDF, suggesting that some motion begins to occur in the system, the short- and long-range order of the system remain essentially preserved. Thus, the RDF analysis already points out the beginning of the local structural rearrangements occurring in the system in the temperature range of 180–200 K. These subtle features are not captured in the DSC analysis, which reveals bulk thermal transitions only at higher temperatures (above 273 K). However, the major changes in the crystal structure take place in the temperature range of 280–320 K for 1.31%, 260–280 K for 2.52%, 220–260 K for 5.39%, and 200–240 K for 10.55%. The temperature range for the substantial changes in the crystal structure does not vary much for the system with and without vacancies. The characteristic changes in the RDF, in the above temperature zone, for specific doping percentage and vacancies is primarily accompanied by merging, broadening, and shifting of peaks, indicating the solid–solid phase transition characteristic of the plastic crystal phase, and the melting transition. The simulated temperature range representing the solid–solid phase transition is in considerable agreement with the DSC data (first set of peaks), which shows peaks in the range of 280–298 K for different doping percentage. We will correlate the changes in the RDF to the rotational and translational dynamics of the samples in a later section. The enhanced rotational dynamics of the different moieties of the system is a dominant feature associated with the plastic crystal phase. Lastly, it is worth noting that for the doping concentration of 1.31%, 2.52%, and 5.39%, the effect of doping is not significantly relevant until the samples reach the plastic crystal phase temperature. As pointed out before, the doping effect becomes critical at all temperature ranges only when the doping concentration reaches 10.55%.

Hydrogen Bond (HB)

Hydrogen bonding is an important intermolecular interaction that plays a key role in determining the structure and dynamics of a system, and its importance has already been realized for ionic liquids (ILs). − Plastic crystals, being a structural analogue of ILs, will exhibit a similar tendency for hydrogen bonds depending on the cation and anion involved. Herein, to ascertain the effect of hydrogen bonding on the structure and dynamics of the doped [DBUH]­[FSI] POIPC, we analyze the hydrogen bond between the various donor and acceptor pairs. Figure presents the combined distribution function (CDF) at different temperatures, highlighting the probability to form hydrogen bonds, for the doping fraction of 1.31% and 10.55% for the donor–acceptor pair N2–H1--N3. The color intensity scale in Figure depicts that the tendency to form hydrogen bonds decreases with an increase in temperature and an increase in the LiFSI doping fraction. This decline in intensity is more marked at the lowest simulated temperature of 80 K.

CDF depicting the intensity of hydrogen bonding for the pair: cation N2–H1 and anion N3 at different temperatures, upper panel is for 1.31% and lower panel is for 10.55%.

The RDFs between the various hydrogen bonding pairs have also been computed and are shown in Figure S8 of the SI section. The decline in principal peak intensity combined with the shifting of peaks to larger distances with increasing doping fractions for various pairs, specifically at temperatures of 80 and 200 K, signifies the reduction in the number and strength of hydrogen bonds, thus authenticating the findings of Figure . Figure S8 further unveils that the presence of vacancies in the system also leads to the decline in the number of hydrogen bonds as corroborated by the reduction in peak intensity for the HB pairs N2–H--N3, N2–H1--O, C4–H6--O, and C7–H12--O, mostly at the lowest simulated temperature of 80 K. It is interesting to note that for the 1.31% doping fraction and its vacancy equivalents at 300 K, the 1.31-2V system displays a significant reduction in the number and strength of the hydrogen bond compared to the other two. This might be the reason for the enhanced rotational and translational dynamics (later section) exhibited by the 1.31-2V system notably at 300 K.

To get a quantitative estimate and compare the strength of the hydrogen bond between the hydrogen bonding pairs, the hydrogen bond length as extracted from the first peak position of RDFs at the lowest simulated temperature of 80 K is displayed in Table . The temperature of 80 K was selected to nullify the effect of temperature and focus more on the chemistry of the ions. In a simplistic picture, it is assumed that shorter distances between the hydrogen bonding atoms implies stronger bonds. The distance between the bonding atoms follows the sequence: r­(H1--O) < r­(H1--N3) < r­(H6--O) < r­(H12--O) < r­(H12--N3) < r­(H6--N3). Thus, a stronger hydrogen bond forms at H1 site compared to the other hydrogen sites. It is witnessed from Table that bonding with the oxygen atom of FSI leads to a comparatively stronger hydrogen bond than the nitrogen atom, attributed principally to the higher electronegativity and smaller atomic radii of the oxygen atom. In general, for DBUH, Table also reveals that the hydrogen bond formed by the C–H group is weaker than that of the N–H group, as ascertained by the larger distances. The higher electronegativity of nitrogen compared to carbon is responsible for this behavior. The presence of vacancies has negligible impact on the strength of hydrogen bond for both the 1.31% and 2.52% doped system, except for the pairs H12--N3 for the doping fraction of 1.31%. The HB distance falls by 5.7% for the two vacancies system of 1.31% in comparison to its pure system counterpart. The increase in LiFSI concentration does not leave a considerable mark on distances for the bonding pairs H1--N3, H1--O, H6--N3, H6--O, and H12--O, at-least until a doping fraction of 5.39%, signifying that the strength of HB does not vary much until this fraction. However, we do observe an increase in the HB distances for 10.55% doping fraction for the pairs H1--N3, H1--O, and H6--N3. The maximum increase in distances is 9.74% for the pair H1--N3 compared to the doping fraction of 1.31%. The rise in HB distances points out to the weaking of hydrogen bonds for 10.55%. The HB pair H12--N3 reveals a reverse trend, and the distance decreases with increase in the doping fraction. A fall of 7.6% is noticed on moving from the doping fraction of 1.31% to 10.55%. Overall, N2–H1--O forms the strongest HB in the system.

Dynamics

Rotational Dynamics

Ions in plastic crystals possess diverse rotational and conformational relaxation modes depending on the temperature and the system’s constituents. It has been known that the rotational dynamics of plastic crystals are highly heterogeneous: at a specific temperature, some rotational and relaxation modes of ions may be activated while the other ions of the same kind may not exhibit the similar rotational/relaxation mode. The onset of the solid–solid phase transition in these systems is marked by the activation of certain conformational and rotational modes. We investigated the various types of rotational motions of the ions in our system by computing the rotational autocorrelation functions (RACF) of the different selected vectors to track their evolution with respect to time and temperature. The selected vectors for the cation includes: N2–H1, N1–C1, C2–C5–C6, C7–C9–C8, and N1–C1–N2, while for the anions, these vectors are S1–F1, S2–O4, S1–S2, and S1–N3–S2. The selection of vectors was done to completely characterize the rotational dynamics of both the rings of the cation, to investigate the rotation of FSI along its longest axis (S1–S2), to analyze the rotational dynamic heterogeneity of the ions, and to study the rotation of the whole cation and anion. The vector reorientation autocorrelation functions (P 2), which describes the rotational dynamics of the ions in the systems, was calculated using the second Legendre polynomial of cos(θ): 2 ⟨P2(cos(θ))⟩=12⟨3cos2(θ)−1⟩ where θ is the angle between the orientation of the vector at time t and its initial orientation. This function indicates how rapidly a specific vector in the molecule loses its original orientation over time, offering insight into the rotational mobility of the molecules. The decline of P 2 to a nonzero value reflects the rotation of the selected vector by a certain degree. The RACFs of the selected vectors for the cation and anion are shown in Figures S9–S16 of the SI section. In order to inspect the rotational mobility of the ions more explicitly, we have also calculated α (alpha): the mean value of RACFs of the various vectors averaged over the last 4 ns of the simulation time at each temperature. Thus, α quantifies the average change in the orientation of a particular vector over the simulation time. Figure displays the variation of α as a function of temperature for the different vectors and the various systems of interest. It is observed that among the several vectors considered for the anion, vector S1–F1 possesses the fastest rotational mode followed by S2–O4 and S1–N3–S2, while long vector S1–S2 corresponds to the slowest rotational mode. For the cation, the vectors N2–H1, N1–C1, C7–C9–C8, and N1–C1–C2 share similar rotational mobility, representing the slowest rotational mode. The normal vector C2–C5–C6 renders the fastest rotational mode for the cation. Also, for all of the systems, the dissimilar mobility of the vectors C2–C5–C6, and the vectors C7–C9–C8, reveals the different rotational dynamics of the two rings of the cation, pointing out the rotational heterogeneity of the bigger ion. The smaller ring consisting of C2–C5–C6 atoms rotates with greater ease compared to the sluggish rotational mobility of the larger ring constituting the atoms C7–C9–C8. The analogous rotational mobility of the vectors N1–C1–N2, N2–H1, and C7–C9–C8 reflects that the lower mobility of C7–C9–C8 has greater influence on the rotational dynamics of these neighboring vectors compared to the faster dynamics of C2–C5–C6. Figures S9–S16 further unveils that except at lower temperatures, the RACFs of the respective vectors decay in a stretched-exponential fashion, a classical signature of the rotational dynamic heterogeneity subsisting in the system. The RACFs for various vectors, as expected for solid phases at low temperatures, do not decline to zero but instead converge to a nonzero plateau value (Figures S9–S16 in the Supporting Information). This represents constrained rotational motion and suggests that the simulations are nonergodic in these settings, as the initial vector orientations remain coupled throughout the production run. While extensive simulations were carried out, the finite simulation time does limit full ergodic exploration, especially in ordered phases. In order to overcome this constraint, all provided results are averaged over five independent initial configurations for each doping percentage and vacancy concentration. This gives us confidence that the observed nondecaying correlations are intrinsic to the system’s slow dynamics rather than artifacts of insufficient sampling. Additionally, as interpreted from Figure , the gap between the slowest and the faster relaxational mode gradually diminishes as the fraction of LiFSI is increased from 1.31% to 10.55%. This reflects the decline in the rotational heterogeneity of the samples with a jump in the doping %.

Rotational Dynamics and Solid Phases

Depending on the variation of α values with respect to temperature associated with the vectors undergoing the slowest rotational mode for the various doping fractions, distinct solid phases can be identified in the samples. It is important to note from Figures and S17, Figure S17 shows the comparative plot of the chosen vectors as a function of temperature, indicating that the other vectors C2–C5–C6, S1–N3–S2, S1–F1 etc. also demonstrate the synonymous behavior in proximity of the transition temperature, but the change in α is not as pronounced as the vectors with the slowest rotational mode. Herein, we group the vectors: N2–H1, C7–C9–C8, N1–C1–N2, S1–S2, and N1–C1, undergoing similar slowest rotational dynamics into group 1, while the other more mobile vectors are grouped into group 2. We see that for the doping fraction of 1.31 at temperatures below 280 K, the variation in α values is not that significant, and the group 1 vectors are relatively rigid. On the other hand, the α values of the group 1 vectors fall swiftly as the temperature is increased from 280 to 300 K. A rather steeper fall in the α values is evident in the temperature regime of 300–320 K compared to the earlier regime of 280–300 K. Starting at 320 K, the α values of the group 1 vectors reaches 0 and is unchanged. Thus, it is apparent that for the doping fraction of 1.31, we have four distinct phases in the system corresponding to the four distinctive temperature regimes of: <280 K, 280–300 K, 300–320 K, and >320 K. We identify these phases as < 280 K: solid III, 280–300 K: solid II, 300–320 K: solid I, and >320 K: melt phase. The temperature 280 K marking the onset of the solid–solid phase transition from solid III to solid II phase in the plastic phase regime, in confirmation with the RDF analysis, is in excellent agreement with the thermal transition temperature of 280 K observed for a similar doping fraction of 1.73% using DSC. The second solid–solid phase transition from solid II to solid I phase at 300 K is not detected by DSC, possibly because the magnitude of the energy change involved here is much weaker than the first transition. The melting transition at 320 K, supplementing the RDF analysis, is again in excellent agreement with the melting temperature of 318 K for 1.73% using DSC. The thermal transition behavior of the one vacancy system of 1.31 is similar to the system without vacancy. Figure displays the distinct solid phases for the various samples.

Distinct solid phases for the various samples.

The change in α with temperature for the two-vacancy variant of 1.31 and 2.52 and its vacancy counterpart is very much alike. Similar to the other systems of 1.31, the first solid–solid transition is observed at 280 K for the 1.31-2V system. The rotational mobility of the group 1 vectors is relatively rigid below this temperature. On the other hand, a significant drop in α takes place in the 20 K temperature window of 280 to 300 K (T m). At T m, it is noticed that the group 2 vectors decorrelate completely, while the group 1 vectors decorrelate by more than 90%. The remaining decorrelation of the group 1 vectors takes place in the temperature range of 300–320 K, where a very narrow fall in α is observed. This is anticipated from the RDF analysis as at 300 K the 2V model of 1.31 doped system shows structure very much similar to liquid, but the system still possesses very minor orders both at the short-range and long-range. This is evident from the very smooth RDF at 320 K, more representative of the liquid structure, compared to the one at 300 K. On the whole, it is inferred that the presence of two vacancies in the system for 1.31% accelerates the slowest rotational relaxation mode especially in the transition zone, in-line with what we expected in the thermodynamic properties section. Overall, for the 1.31-2V system, we have the following phases: < 280 K: solid II, 280–300 K: solid I phase, and >300 K: melt phase. For the 2.52% doped system and its vacancy model, the principal solid–solid transition, although a minor one, occurs at 240 K; the second solid–solid transition, a major one, occurs at 260 K, and melting takes place at 280 K (T m). Analogous to 1.31-2V system, α declines to more than about 85% of its initial value in the temperature range of 260–280 K for the group 1 vectors, and henceforth a limited fall is observed above T m in between the temperature of 280–300 K. The group 2 vectors almost losses completely their initial memory at 280 K for all the three 2.52% doped samples. Here again, similar to the 1.31-2V system, for the three 2.52% doped systems as corroborated by the RDF analysis the structure pretty much resembles the disordered liquid phase at 280 K and the outright melting occurs only at 300 K. Therefore, the sequential phases for the 2.52% doped system along with its vacancies model are < 240 K: solid III, 240–260 K: solid II, 260–280 K: solid I and >280 K: melt. Corroborating again the RDF revelation, Figure , and S17 reveal that the system with doping of 2.52% and its vacancy equivalent shares very similar rotational motility. DSC analysis of the 2.52% doped system reveals solid–solid phase transition at around 280 K, with the onset temperature at around 275 K, in excellent agreement with the current work.

The variation in α for 5.39 and 10.55% doped samples is a bit different from the remaining samples. For 5.39%, the group 1 vectors are comparably rigid below 220 K, and the first sharp fall in α is observed in between 220 and 240 K. This is in agreement with the RDF results where we do observe a major change in the crystalline structure in this temperature range. The second solid–solid transition, the major one, is characterized by a drastic fall in α within the temperature stretch of 240–260 K. At 260 K, as confirmed from the RDF analysis, the structure melts. However, at this temperature, only the group 2 vectors undergo complete decorrelation (α≅0) and the group 1 vectors decorrelates completely only at temperature of about 300 K. The 10.55 doping percentage, exhibits the first minor solid–solid transition in between 200 and 220 K. The second solid–solid transition, a major one, occurs in the range of 220–240 K. The RDF reveals that the structure melts at 240 K, but the complete decorrelation of the vectors occurs at approximately 300 K. For 10.55%, the temperature range of 240–260 K also reveals a sharp decline in α, we attribute this fall to the continuous evolution of the short-ranged DBUH-FSI correlation, as indicated by RDF, even beyond the melt temperature of 240 K. The stronger short-range correlation between FSI-Li may also play a role here. The same holds true for 5.39% in the temperature range of 260–280 K.

Translational Diffusion

The diffusive dynamics of the ions in the system has been investigated using mean square displacement (MSD), which quantifies the change in the spatial extent of the translational motion of a particle at time t in comparison to its initial time. The function formulation is described below: 3 MSD=1N∑j=1N|ri(t)−ri(0)|2 here N is the number of atoms, r i (t) and r i (0) are the positional vector of species i at time t and 0 respectively. The analysis has been carried out comprehensibly by computing the MSD of the center-of-mass (COM) of the cation, anion, and that of the lithium atom at the selected temperatures of interest. Figure S18 depicts the MSD of DBUH and FSI for the system studied in this work at distinct temperatures for various samples. Figures , and depict the comparative MSD of DBUH, FSI and LI at chosen temperatures of significance. The MSD curves for the various systems reach plateaus in the low temperature regime, for example at temperature below 300 K for DBUH and FSI for the 1.31% doped system. The presence of plateaus in the MSD plot signals to the in-cage dynamics of ions, where the motion of the ions are constrained in proximity around the lattice site.

It is observed from Figure S18 that the translational diffusion of FSI is always greater than that of DBUH, principally because of the FSI’s smaller size and symmetry. It is also noticed from Figure and Figure , especially at 240 K, that the translational mobility of both the DBUH and FSI increases with an increase in the doping concentration of LiFSI in the system. Above 240 K, the time-dependent MSD of the doped system, 5.39% and 10.55%, remains nearly identical. Also, it is clear that with an increase in temperature, the effect of vacancy defects on the system becomes more pronounced for the doping fraction of 1.31%. At 300 K for the systems with and without vacancies of 1.31%, the MSD of DBUH and FSI for the sample with two vacancies is significantly greater than that of the systems with one vacancy and the system without a vacancy. In-fact, at the temperatures of 240, 260, and 280 K also the motility of the system with C v = 0.46% is slightly higher than the pure 1.31 and 1.31-1V system, with latter systems displaying the similar dynamics. The effect of vacancy for the 2.52% system is not as dominant as that of the 1.31% counterpart, three of the 2.52% systems share almost the similar dynamics. The MSD curve for the various systems becomes alike beyond the melting transition temperature. Overall, trends in the dynamics of DBUH and FSI with respect to the variation in temperature, doping fraction, and vacancy is similar, and commensurate with the findings of the RDF, density, and rotational dynamics.

Figure presents the comparative dynamics of Li for the systems with varied doping fraction and vacancy at the temperatures of 280, 300, and 320 K. At 280 K, it is seen that after the initial relaxation period of about 20 ps the system with f = 2.52% and its vacancy equivalent, 5.39%, and 10.55%, manifests akin to translational motion of the metal ion. In contrast, the 1.31% doped system and its corresponding vacancies’ samples displays comparatively slower dynamics than the remaining systems studied here. The trends in the MSD of the metal ion at 300 K is very similar to that of the DBUH and FSI. For the 1.31% doped system, the MSD increases with an increase in the vacancy concentration, while for the 2.52% samples as noted earlier, the presence of vacancies does not have much impact on the dynamics. Beyond the melting temperature, at 320 K, the time-dependent dynamics of the different systems converges, as for the DBUH and FSI. To gain further insights into the dynamics of the three ions, we have also compared the MSDs of the three ions at 280 and 300 K, and the same is shown in Figures S19 and S20 of the SI section. Figure S19 reveals that at 280 K the motility of the metal ion is substantially greater than that of the DBUH and FSI ion at the LiFSI concentration of 1.31% and its vacancy equivalents. This means that the metal ion is the major contributor to the ionic conductivity of the 1.31% systems at 280 K. While for the remaining systems, at 280 and 300 K, after the initially short relaxation time lasting about 100 ps, it is the matrix anion (FSI) that contributes most to the conductivity of the system.

The diffusion coefficients (D) have been deduced from the slopes of the MSDs as a function of time using the relation: D = ⟨Δr 2⟩/6t. Figure S21 unveils the diffusion coefficient of the matrix ions as a function of temperature for the various doping and vacancy concentration. For computing D we make sure that we are in the diffusive regime (slope = 1 for the MSD vs Time plot). For the simulation time used in this work, the diffusive behavior for the ions were observed from 320 K for the 1.31% doped system and its one vacancy analogue, and at 300 K for the remaining systems. Figure S21 demonstrates that D for FSI is higher than DBUH at all temperatures, and the difference grows with an increase in temperature. D for the 10.55% doped system is comparatively lower than those of the other samples for both DBUH and FSI, with minimal difference between the remaining samples. The experimentally observed diffusion coefficient for 5.39% at 298 K is 5.83 × 10–12 m2/s for DBUH, 6.94 × 10–12 m2/s for FSI, and 5.97 × 10–12 m2/s for Li. The corresponding values from simulations at 300 K are 5.121 × 10–11 m2/s for DBUH, 5.985 × 10–11 m2/s for FSI, and 4.374 × 10–11 m2/s for Li. D expt. for 10.55% samples at 298 K is 5.41 × 10–12 m2/s for DBUH, 6.55 × 10–12 m2/s for FSI, and 5.59 × 10–12 m2/s for Li, while the simulated values at 300 K are 4.201 × 10–11 m2/s for DBUH, 5.137 × 10–11 m2/s for FSI, and 3.897 × 10–11 m2/s for Li. Thus, the simulated values are an order of magnitude higher than the experimental data, but the simulation results do match the experimental trend qualitatively i.e. FSI is the most mobile ion, and there is a reduction in D for all the ions on raising the doping fraction of LiFSI from 5.39% to 10.55%. The simulated diffusion coefficient of the FSI anion at 400 K is 4.5 × 10–10 m2/s for the doping fraction of 10.55%, in excellent agreement with the D value of 4.0 × 10–10 m2/s reported for [DCA]− anion (in similar study) at 400 K for Li+ doping of 8%. In another MD simulation study, the diffusion coefficient of the matrix ion doped with 10 mol % of the Na+ ion was reported to be 1.37 × 10–11 m2/s (anion) and 9.75 × 10–12 m2/s (cation), while that of the metal ion is 1.33 × 10–11 m2/s at 298 K. These values are in considerable agreement with the diffusion coefficient of the ions reported earlier in this study at 298 K for 5.39% doping. The simulated diffusion coefficient of the ions at 330 K for the ionic liquid pyr14TFSI doped with 20 mol % of LiTFSI salt was reported to be 0.2 × 10–10 m2/s (pyr14), 0.15 × 10–10 m2/s (TFSI) and 0.1 × 10–10 m2/s (Li), in line with the diffusion coefficient of 0.820 × 10–10 m2/s (DBUH), 0.94 × 10–10 m2/s (FSI), and 0.74 × 10–10 m2/s (Li) at 320 K obtained in the current work for 10.55% doping. Thus, the computed D for different ions using MD simulation in our work is in a similar range to that of the simulation studies reported in the literature.

Overview and Perspective

The current study sheds light on the design of solid electrolytes, showing the interplay of structural, thermodynamic, and dynamic features that influence their performance. Our analysis reveals that doping [DBUH]­[FSI] with LiFSI (at concentrations of 1.31%–10.55%) enhances ion mobility, particularly for the [FSI] anion, which is the most mobile species. However, higher doping levels (e.g., 10.55%) reduce the melting temperature and crystallinity, promoting a more amorphous, conductive phase. Careful selection of dopant ions (e.g., Li+, Na+, K+) is critical, as smaller ions like Li+ create larger distortions in the lattice, increasing free volume and ion diffusion. However, excessive doping (e.g., 10.55%) may weaken hydrogen bonding and reduce structural stability, necessitating a balance between the conductivity and mechanical integrity. Dopants should be chosen to maximize the transference number (e.g., t Li+ ), which the study confirms increases with higher LiFSI concentrations, aligning with experimental trends.

The work shows that Schottky vacancies (0.23% and 0.46%) have a considerable influence on the dynamics of POIPCs, especially at lower doping levels (1.31%). Vacancies enhance dynamic heterogeneity, accelerate rotational and translational motions, especially in the phase transition regime (280–300 K), and reduce activation energy barriers for ion diffusion. Controlled introduction of vacancies can enhance the ionic conductivity by creating pathways for ion transport. However, the study shows that vacancies have a negligible impact on thermodynamic properties (e.g., density and melting point) at higher doping levels (2.52%), suggesting that defect engineering is most effective in systems with low dopant concentrations. Defect engineering should focus on optimizing the vacancy concentration to balance conductivity and structural stability, as excessive vacancies may compromise long-range order.

Using rotational autocorrelation functions (RACFs) and radial distribution functions (RDFs), distinct solid phases (solid III, II, I, and melt) are identified, with solid–solid phase transitions (e.g., at 280–300 K for 1.31% doping) marking the onset of enhanced rotational and translational dynamics. These transitions are critical for enabling the plasticity and conductivity in the plastic crystal phase. Materials should be designed to exhibit solid–solid phase transitions at or near operational temperatures (e.g., ambient or slightly elevated) to maximize the ionic mobility. Finally, the study also demonstrates that hydrogen bonding (e.g., N2–H1--O) plays a key role and significantly influences the structure and dynamics of the POIPCs. Stronger hydrogen bonds at lower doping levels stabilize the crystal lattice, while increased doping weakens these interactions, promoting disorder and conductivity. The strength and distribution of hydrogen bonds should be tailored to balance the structural stability and ion mobility. For instance, the study shows that oxygen atoms in [FSI] form stronger hydrogen bonds than nitrogen atoms due to higher electronegativity, which can be exploited to design more stable POIPCs. Cations and anions with specific hydrogen-bonding capabilities (e.g., protic groups such as N–H) should be selected to optimize the interplay between lattice stability and dynamic heterogeneity.

Conclusions

MD simulations, complemented by experimental methods, have been used to comprehensibly characterize the structural, thermodynamic, and dynamic properties of LiFSI doped POIPC [DBUH]-[FSI]. Simulations have been performed for the doping fraction of: 1.31%, 2.52%, 5.39%, and 10.55%. The effect of vacancies has also been realized for the Schottky vacancies concentrations of 0.23% and 0.46% and for the doping fractions of 1.31% and 2.52%. All the systems are allowed to evolve for the temperature ranging from 80 to 500 K. The computed T m for the various doping fractions is in very good agreement with the DSC data. The presence of vacancies does not alter the T m for the 1.31% doped system and its one vacancy analogue; the melting temperature also remains the same for the doped fraction of 2.52% and its vacancies’ counterpart. However, we do observe a decline of 20 K in T m for the two vacancy samples corresponding to the doping intensity of 1.31%. The structural evolution of the system discerned using RDF successfully captures the temperature ranges where the important changes in the structure of the system takes place. The RDF indicates the first signs of mobility in the samples at the temperature of 180–200 K for the lowest doping fraction. Hydrogen bond analysis reveals that the tendency to form hydrogen bond decreases with increase in doping fraction and temperature.

Investigation into the rotational mobility of the samples confirms the presence of rotational dynamics heterogeneity prevalent in the systems. The evolution of the RACFs for the various normal and bond vectors with temperature signals the presence of multiple solid phases existing in the system. The RACFs analysis validates that the first set of thermal transitions observed in DSC analysis for the various doping fractions corresponds to the solid–solid phase transition temperature. Analysis of the translational motion of the three ions reveals that the [FSI] anion is the most mobile ion in the system. The impact of doping fraction on MSD is more dominant at the lower temperature, while the effect of vacancy is more evident at 280–300 K. The extracted E a using the diffusion coefficients plot for the doping fraction of 5.39% and 10.55% is in good agreement with the experimental data. The accuracy of the t Li+ calculated using MD simulation reaffirms the robustness of the technique in characterizing these materials.
