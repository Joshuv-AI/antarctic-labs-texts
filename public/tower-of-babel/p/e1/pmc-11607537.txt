Understanding Enterohepatic Bile Acid Pools and Their Impact on Intestinal Physiology
Authors: Shogo Takahashi, Frank J Gonzalez
Published: 2024

Bile acids (BAs) are essential metabolites within the gastrointestinal system, performing digestive and signaling functions critical to maintaining health. Traditionally, BAs are recognized for their role in facilitating the digestion and absorption of dietary lipids, fat-soluble vitamins, and other hydrophobic nutrients, as well as in promoting the excretion of cholesterol. However, beyond these conventional roles, BAs are also significant signaling molecules that activate and inhibit a variety of receptors, including the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and G protein-coupled bile acid receptor 1 (also known as TGR5), primarily expressed in the liver and small intestine.1,2 Through these interactions, BAs regulate numerous physiological processes, including glucose and lipid metabolism, energy homeostasis,3 immune responses,4,5 and cancer.6 These diverse functions highlight the multifaceted roles of BAs, underscoring their importance in maintaining gut and systemic health.

In this issue of Cellular and Molecular Gastroenterology and Hepatology, the article titled "Quantifying forms and functions of enterohepatic BA pools in mice"7 provides a comprehensive analysis and valuable insights into the dynamics of BA pools in the small intestine, focusing specifically on the ileum, the main site for BA absorption. The ileum is also the site where FXR and TGR5 are expressed. Using mass spectrometry, the authors quantified 88 distinct BA species and metabolites in wild-type mice and mice deficient in the apical sodium-dependent BA transporter (ASBT; SLC10A2). This study revealed that active transport mediated by ASBT accounts for about 80% of the BA pool transferred across ileal epithelial cells into the bloodstream; the other 20% of BAs are passively absorbed. This active absorption not only amplifies the size of the ileal BA pool but also influences its composition as revealed in ASBT-deficient mice, where the ileal BA pool was approximately 5 times smaller and was enriched in secondary BAs and metabolites, produced by the microbiota, that are usually predominant in the colon. These compositional shifts can influence FXR and TGR5 signaling in epithelial cells, emphasizing the direct impact of altered BA metabolism on physiology.

The findings of this study highlight the critical role of ASBT in maintaining BA homeostasis and point to the complex interplay between active and passive absorption in shaping the enterohepatic circulation of BAs. The shift toward secondary BAs in ASBT-deficient mice suggests that under normal conditions, active ileal absorption by ASBT helps to maintain a specific BA profile, by selectively transporting primary BAs while allowing secondary BAs to pass into the large intestine. This selective absorption could be crucial for the regulation of BA signaling pathways, which are known to influence metabolic processes and immune responses.

Despite the significant insights provided by this study, several important questions remain. Recent findings by Gentry et al8 suggest that the number of BA metabolites is markedly greater than previously thought. In public metabolomics data, 145 conjugated BAs were identified, 139 of which were previously undescribed in humans and mice. Most of these new metabolites are primarily generated by the gut microbiota and exist in very low abundances, detectable only by high-resolution mass spectrometry.9 It is still unclear whether these low-abundance metabolites have biological significance, given that their concentrations may be insufficient to activate nuclear receptors or G-protein-coupled receptors. Nevertheless, their presence suggests a need for these metabolites to be incorporated into any comprehensive BA modeling system. The translational relevance of the current findings to human physiology also needs attention. One of the major challenges in BA research is the species-specific differences in BA metabolism between humans and mice.10,11 Humans and mice exhibit distinct BA profiles, conjugation patterns, and receptor affinities. These differences could affect the extrapolation of data from mice to humans. Therefore, future research must address these species-specific differences to ensure that findings from mouse models can be effectively translated to humans.12 Investigating how human-specific BA metabolites influence signaling pathways and metabolic processes will be critical for advancing the understanding of BA biology and its relevance to human health. Moreover, the role of the gut microbiota in shaping BA profiles and its broader implications for diseases, such as inflammatory bowel disease, cancer, metabolic syndrome, and liver diseases, such as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, remains an area ripe for exploration.3,13 The gut bacteria’s ability to modify BAs into secondary and tertiary forms adds another layer of complexity to BA metabolism. Understanding how these microbially modified BAs interact with host receptors and contribute to health and disease could open new avenues for therapeutic interventions. Future studies should also investigate how dietary factors, antibiotics, probiotics, and other interventions influence the microbiota-BA axis and its impact on host physiology.

In conclusion, this study significantly advances the understanding of BA dynamics, particularly in the context of disrupted ASBT function. The insights gained into the size, composition, and function of ileal BA pools provide a foundation for future research aimed at understanding the complexities of BA metabolism. However, translating these findings from mice to humans will require careful consideration of species differences and the interplay between diet, microbiota, and host factors. As the understanding of BA biology continues to expand, so does the potential for novel therapeutic strategies targeting BA pathways to treat metabolic and inflammatory diseases.
