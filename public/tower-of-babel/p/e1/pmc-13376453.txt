How to Use Full-Length Standing Radiographs to Measure Posterior Tibial Slope and Anterior Tibial Translation
Authors: Abid M. Khan, Adam V. Daniel, Alexander K. Bishop, Mario Hevesi, Aaron J. Krych, Adam J. Tagliero, Bruce A. Levy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Increased posterior tibial slope (PTS) and anterior tibial translation (ATT) result in a higher risk of anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction failure. Accurate measurement of PTS and ATT is essential to guide surgical planning in these patients. Full-length weightbearing lateral tibial radiographs yield the most accurate measurement of PTS and ATT compared with short-film tibial radiographs, computed tomography scans, and magnetic resonance imaging.Indications:We aim to demonstrate a validated, reproducible technique in a stepwise fashion to accurately measure PTS and ATT using full-length weightbearing lateral tibial radiographs. This presentation adds to the field by offering a precise measurement technique for surgical planning purposes.Technique Description:To precisely measure PTS using a full-length weightbearing lateral tibial radiograph, first, perpendicular lines are drawn 5 cm proximal to the ankle joint and 5 cm distal to the knee joint. A line is then extended proximally from the center of the perpendicular line near the ankle through the center of the perpendicular line near the knee, and then extended up to the tibial articular surface. Next, a line is drawn along the medial tibial plateau. The angle measured between these 2 lines is subtracted from 90° to yield the PTS.To precisely measure ATT, a line is drawn along the diaphysis of the tibia and approximated to the posterior aspect of the proximal tibia. Then, another parallel line is drawn and approximated to the posterior aspect of the distal femur. The distance between these lines is measured to yield anterior tibial translation.Results:We measured a PTS of 14.2° and an ATT of 12.6 mm using the technique described in this presentation.Discussion/Conclusion:This technique demonstrates an accurate and reproducible method to precisely measure PTS and ATT using full-length weightbearing lateral tibial radiographs. This technique yields greater accuracy in PTS and ATT than other imaging modalities. As a result, there is a direct benefit for surgical planning when using this technique.Patient Consent Disclosure Statement:The author(s) attests that consent has been obtained from any patient(s) appearing in this publication. In this video, we will demonstrate how to use full-length standing radiographs to precisely measure posterior tibial slope and anterior tibial translation.

Background

Increased posterior tibial slope (PTS) and anterior tibial translation (ATT) can increase the risk of anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) failure.1,3,8 Full-length weightbearing lateral tibial radiographs should be used to precisely measure PTS and ATT over short-film tibial radiographs, computed tomography (CT) scans, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).2,4,5,12,13 This presentation aims to demonstrate a validated, reproducible technique for measuring PTS and ATT using full-length weightbearing lateral tibial radiographs.

Case Presentation

The patient in this case is a 31-year-old male with a history of 3 previous failed ACLRs.

On the patient's sagittal MRI, the PTS looks essentially normal, as both the medial and lateral tibial plateaus look flat. The sagittal CT scan shows some mild increase in slope. However, it is still hard to appreciate compared with the radiographs on the right, which provide a much more accurate visualization of the patient's increased PTS.

Technique Description

How to Measure PTS

This presentation will demonstrate a validated method, as reported by Dean et al, 2 in which they have measurements originating from 5 cm distal and proximal to the knee and ankle joints, respectively, to precisely measure PTS.

First, we measure 5 cm proximal to the ankle joint and draw a perpendicular line across the distal tibia. 2 Next, we measure 5 cm distal to the knee joint and draw a similar perpendicular line across the proximal tibia. 2 From here, the initial 2 lines used to measure 5 cm are deleted to have a cleaner field of view. 2 Then, starting at the center of the line near the ankle joint, a line is carefully drawn up and through the center of the line near the knee joint, where it is then extended up to the articular surface of the tibia. 2 Lastly, another line is created that follows the tibial plateau.2,13 The angle between the tibial plateau line and the line along the tibial axis is then measured, which we found to be 75.8°. This value is then subtracted from 90° to yield the PTS, which we found to be about 14.2°.

Next, we zoom in slightly here to demonstrate that careful attention must be made when drawing the line along the tibial plateau. In this presentation, we are determining the slope using the medial tibial plateau, which is supported by an international Delphi consensus statement in which a majority of experts recommended using the medial plateau. 13 However, this is still controversial as some authors choose to measure using the lateral plateau. 13

How to Measure ATT

Now that we have measured the PTS, we will proceed to measure the ATT. We reference the technique described by Macchiarola et al, 10 in which they demonstrate that first, a line must be drawn along the posterior tibial cortex, labelled “a.” 10 Then, lines parallel to “a” are drawn and approximated to the posterior tibia and the posterior femur, labelled “b” and “c,” respectively. 10 Lastly, measurements are taken between the initial line “a,” and the posterior lines “b” and “c,” and the difference between the 2 is the ATT, which is represented by the red line in this image. 10

First, we place a line along the posterior aspect of the diaphysis of the tibia. 10 Next, we move this line to the posterior aspect of the proximal tibia. 10 We then create a parallel line and align it with the posterior aspect of the femur. 10 From here, we zoom in and measure the space between the 2 lines, which we found to be about 12.6 mm. 10 Although not universally accepted, most authors consider >10 mm of ATT as significant.2,13 This really demonstrates how the increase in PTS drives the tibia forward, placing increased strain on the ACL.

Pre- Versus Post Slope-Correcting Osteotomy

Being able to accurately measure PTS and ATT is essential because it informs the surgeon as to how much correction is needed when performing a slope-reducing osteotomy.3,8 Here, we can see the patient's pre- and postoperative radiographs in which the PTS was reduced from 14.2° to 4.5°. Furthermore, the ATT was reduced from 12.6 mm to about 0 mm. Notice how the tibia now sits directly beneath the femur, symmetrically, demonstrating a significant improvement in the PTS and ATT.

Discussion

Our discussion will focus on why full-length weightbearing lateral tibial radiographs should be used over short-film tibial radiographs, CT scans, and MRIs.

Once again, here is a comparison of this patient's preoperative imaging modalities that show how PTS appears essentially normal on sagittal MRI, and only slightly increased on CT scan. This is because both MRI and CT scans are taken with the patient in a supine position. 14 Therefore, without loading the knee joint in a weightbearing fashion, it is difficult to truly appreciate the effect of that increased PTS and thus accurately measure the ATT.

The Hudek group compared PTS measurements between radiographs and MRIs. 7 The solid line represents radiographic PTS, and the dotted line represents MRI PTS, and you can see here how the PTS was consistently underestimated using MRI. 7 A multitude of authors reported similar findings not just for using MRIs, but also CT scans as well.1,6,7,9,11,13,14 In addition, these authors reported that PTS measurements varied widely when using either MRI or CT scans. This again underscores the benefit of using radiographs instead of MRI and CT scans when trying to precisely measure PTS.

Decide between short-film and full-length radiographs for measuring PTS must now be undertaken. Multiple groups have reported that increased tibial bone length, when measured, increases the accuracy of PTS.5,12,13 Here, you can see in figures B through D that PTS increased with every additional 5 cm of tibial bone length. 5 These results really highlight the importance of using full-length standing lateral tibial radiographs to most accurately measure PTS.5,12,13

Conclusion

Overall, based on the previous studies mentioned, MRI and CT scans have been shown to underestimate the PTS. As well, since they are performed supine and nonweightbearing, they will also underestimate ATT. Radiographs with short tibial bone lengths also underestimate PTS, and studies clearly show that an increase in tibial bone length will thus improve the accuracy of PTS measurements. Therefore, we recommend using full-length weightbearing lateral tibial radiographs, not just for accurate measurements of PTS, but also to accurately measure ATT so surgeons can appreciate the effect that an increase in PTS has on ATT. This allows surgeons to appropriately correct PTS and neutralize ATT, as shown in this case presentation.
