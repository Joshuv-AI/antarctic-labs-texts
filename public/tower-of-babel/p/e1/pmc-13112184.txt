State Expansions in Medicaid Financial Assistance for Low‐Income Medicare Beneficiaries: Changes in Enrollment and Use
Authors: Vicki Fung, David Cheng, Mary Price, Kobi Khong, Zhiyou Yang, Felippe O Marcondes, J Wyatt Koma, John Hsu, Margarita Alegria, Joseph P Newhouse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo examine the association between state increases in income or asset eligibility limits for Medicare Savings Programs (MSP), which provide assistance with Medicare cost‐sharing and/or premiums, and MSP enrollment and health care use.Study Setting and DesignWe used interrupted time series analysis to evaluate changes in MSP enrollment rates before and after expansion. We estimated associations between post‐expansion enrollment in MSPs with outpatient visits, prescription drug fills, emergency department (ED) visits, and hospitalizations using a matched difference‐in‐differences approach. Matching variables included individual and area‐level sociodemographic characteristics, comorbidity scores, and MSP eligibility group.Data Sources and Analytic SampleWe used Medicare data from four states that met the inclusion criteria of having at least 24 months of data before and after their expansion between 2006 and 2019: Connecticut (income and assets), Indiana (income), New York (assets), and Oregon (assets).Principal FindingsRelative to predicted enrollment in the absence of MSP expansion, actual enrollment over 24 months increased by varying degrees: 65.3% in Connecticut and 34.1% in Indiana, but only 3.9% and 3.7% in New York and Oregon, respectively. Introduction

Medicare Savings Programs (MSPs) provide low‐income Medicare beneficiaries with financial assistance with their Medicare cost‐sharing and/or premiums. State Medicaid programs administer MSPs and are required to offer them to beneficiaries whose income and assets are below federally set amounts. As of 2025, the federal asset limit for most MSP beneficiaries is $9660 for an individual and $14,470 for a married couple. States can, however, increase income or asset limits above this federal standard, which could reduce financial barriers to care for beneficiaries who do not qualify for benefits under the federal standards or make it easier for eligible beneficiaries to enroll [1, 2, 3]. To date, 15 states plus Washington D.C., have opted to do so, but evidence on the effects of such expansions on enrollment and health care use is limited.

There are three primary MSP programs that provide different benefits depending on income. The majority (8.0 of 10.0 million in 2021) of MSP enrollees are Qualified Medicare Beneficiaries (QMBs) whose federal income test is 100% of the Federal Poverty Level (FPL). QMBs receive coverage for Parts A (inpatient) and B (outpatient) cost‐sharing and premiums [4]. Beneficiaries with incomes between 100%–135% FPL qualify for two other MSP eligibility groups: Specified Low Income Beneficiaries (SLMBs) and Qualifying Individuals (QIs), which cover Medicare Part B premiums, but not Parts A or B cost‐sharing. In 2021, there were 1.3 million SLMB and 0.6 million QI enrollees [4, 5].

The most common approach states have taken to expanding MSP eligibility is eliminating asset tests for QMBs, SLMBs, and QIs (14 states plus D.C.). Five of these states also raised income limits, and one other state has raised income limits, but did not eliminate asset tests. State increases in income limits for MSPs range from 165% FPL to 300% FPL for QIs (with corresponding increases in limits for QMBs and SLMBs). Interviews with state officials who expanded MSP eligibility prior to 2001 indicate that eliminating asset tests reduced administrative barriers to MSP enrollment for beneficiaries as well as state administrative costs to verify assets [6]. A recent report finds raising income limits may be associated with more immediate increases in enrollment compared with eliminating asset tests [7]. Income limit changes may allow states to shift beneficiaries who qualify for other low‐income assistance programs into MSPs without requiring additional information from beneficiaries, particularly if asset tests are similar or waived [8].

If MSP eligibility expansions increase enrollment in MSPs, such policies could also increase the receipt and continuity of Part D Low Income Subsidies (LIS) for prescription drug coverage [9, 10]. All dual‐eligible beneficiaries, including those in MSPs, automatically receive the Part D LIS administered by the Social Security Administration. Importantly, the loss of LIS benefits has been associated with greater mortality, particularly for those using chronic medications to treat cardiovascular disease, chronic lung disease, and human immunodeficiency virus [11]. Additionally, prior studies find that beneficiaries with incomes just above federal income limits for MSPs and the Part D LIS have higher out‐of‐pocket spending, fewer outpatient visits, less prescription drug use, and are more likely to report affordability barriers to care [2, 3]. To the extent that MSP eligibility expansions improve affordability of care, including through Part D LIS, access to timely outpatient care and chronic care management could improve.

Using Medicare data between 2006 and 2019, this study (1) examined changes in MSP enrollment levels and trends following MSP eligibility expansions, and (2) examined changes in outpatient visits, prescription drug use, and acute care use for beneficiaries who enrolled in MSPs following state eligibility expansions. We hypothesized that MSP enrollment would increase after eligibility expansions, and that enrolling in MSP following expansions would be associated with increases in outpatient visits and prescription drug use and decreases in acute care use.

Methods

Study Population and Data Sources

This study used Medicare enrollment and claims data for beneficiaries eligible for Medicare based on age or disability in four states that met the eligibility criteria for this study: Connecticut, Indiana, New York, and Oregon. A list of all states that have expanded MSP eligibility is available in the Appendix (Table A1). The four study states were chosen based on having: (1) at least 24 months of pre‐expansion policy data that did not cross the introduction of Medicare Part D in January 2006; and (2) at least 24 months of post‐expansion policy data prior to the Medicaid continuous enrollment provision that was implemented in March 2020 as part of the Families First Coronavirus Response Act of 2020. These two policy changes could have influenced MSP enrollment separately from state eligibility expansions.

Connecticut eliminated the asset test and raised its income test to 232% FPL in October 2009. It further increased its income test in July 2011 (249% FPL), March 2012 (248%), and March 2013 (246%); we focus on the first change. Indiana raised its income test to 185% FPL in June 2014. New York eliminated its asset test in April 2008, and in January 2023, increased its income test to 186% FPL. We did not evaluate this latter change because we lacked a 24‐month follow‐up period. Oregon eliminated the asset test in January 2016.

Changes in MSP Enrollment After Eligibility Expansion

We assessed changes in the number of MSP enrollees per month per 100,000 Medicare beneficiaries in each state in the 24 months before and after each state's MSP eligibility expansion, including beneficiaries in Traditional Medicare and Medicare Advantage. MSP enrollees can qualify for MSPs only (i.e., partial benefit dual‐eligibility) or MSPs plus full Medicaid (i.e., full benefit dual‐eligibility) if they qualify for Medicaid through additional eligibility pathways, such as Supplemental Security Income (SSI)‐related pathways for the elderly and disabled (Appendix Table A2). States typically cover Part B premiums and cost‐sharing for full‐benefit dual‐eligibles, even if they are not enrolled in any MSPs [12]. Thus, in secondary analyses, we examined changes in total dual‐eligible enrollment to assess how frequently beneficiaries newly gained Medicare premium and cost‐sharing assistance following MSP eligibility expansion. Data on individual income and asset levels were not available in Medicare enrollment files to identify beneficiaries who were eligible for benefits but did not enroll.

We conducted interrupted time series (ITS) analysis separately by state to examine changes in enrollment in MSPs associated with each state's eligibility expansion. Given wide variation across state Medicaid programs and enrollment patterns, we used ITS to estimate the effects of eligibility expansions on MSP enrollment without relying on comparisons across states, under the assumption that historical enrollment within each state can be used to project counterfactual trends had eligibility not been expanded. We fit segmented linear regression models to estimate immediate changes in the month after eligibility expansion (level change) and changes in linear time trends for MSP enrollment rates in the 24‐month post‐expansion versus 24‐month pre‐expansion period (slope change). We then calculated the percentage change in enrollment attributable to eligibility expansion at 12 and 24 months by comparing predicted enrollment with and without eligibility expansion based on the ITS models.

To assess the extent to which beneficiaries newly gained assistance with Medicare cost‐sharing, we examined the distribution of types of assistance in the month prior to MSP enrollment among those who newly enrolled in MSPs in the 6 months after state expansion. Specifically, we examined the percentage who: (1) were full benefit dual‐eligibles without enrollment in MSPs; (2) were receiving the Part D LIS without Medicaid (MSPs or other dual‐eligibility); (3) had no prior assistance from Medicaid or Part D LIS; or (4) were new to Medicare in the month of MSP enrollment.

Post‐Expansion Enrollment and Subsequent Health Care Utilization

We applied difference‐in‐differences in a matched population to estimate associations between post‐expansion MSP enrollment and subsequent utilization outcomes. We restricted the study population in this analysis to beneficiaries living in Connecticut and Indiana based on changes in enrollment in MSPs associated with eligibility expansion estimated in the ITS analysis. We also limited Traditional Medicare enrollees to those with 12 months of Medicare Parts A and B prior to MSP expansion since both states expanded MSP eligibility criteria before the first available Medicare Advantage Encounter data in 2015.

Differences in utilization before and after the first month of MSP enrollment post‐expansion (index month) were compared between newly enrolled MSP beneficiaries and existing MSP beneficiaries. Newly enrolled beneficiaries were identified as those who enrolled in MSPs in a 6‐month enrollment window after the MSP eligibility expansion and were not enrolled in MSPs in the prior 12 months. We assessed MSP enrollment gains specifically after eligibility expansion to include enrollments induced by expansion policies, rather than enrollments due to qualifying changes in household income and assets. Existing MSP beneficiaries were identified as those who were enrolled in MSPs for at least 1 month in both the 12 months prior to and 6 months after the policy change.

Matched Enrollment Groups

To improve comparability, we matched newly enrolled MSP beneficiaries to existing MSP beneficiaries by propensity score matching via a greedy nearest neighbor matching algorithm at a 1:1 ratio (SAS 9.4 PSMATCH procedure). Propensity scores were estimated by logistic regression with the following pre‐expansion policy characteristics as covariates: age group (< 65, 65–74, 75–84, 85+ years old), sex (male, female), original reason for Medicare entitlement (age vs. disability), race/ethnicity (American Indian/Alaska Native, Asian, Black, Hispanic, Other or Unknown, White); Hospital Service Area defined by ZIP codes of residence, CMS‐HCC comorbidity scores, and ZIP code characteristics, including rurality defined using USDA rurality codes and quintiles of ZIP code poverty based on the proportion of residents with income at or below the poverty line.

Given differences in the coverage for Medicare cost‐sharing across MSP eligibility groups, we also matched new MSP enrollees to existing enrollees by exact matching on the eligibility group (QMB only, QMB plus, SLMB only, SLMB plus, and QI) associated with MSP enrollment in the index month alongside the propensity score matching.

Utilization Outcomes

In each study month, we assessed monthly rates of outpatient visits, emergency department (ED) visits, and hospitalizations per 1000 beneficiaries. Additionally, we assessed monthly rates of 30‐day prescription drug fills overall and for three commonly used, guideline‐recommended chronic drug classes (antidiabetes, antilipid, and antidepressant drugs) per 1000 beneficiaries. In secondary analyses, we examined changes in monthly out‐of‐pocket Part D drug costs. Because out‐of‐pocket costs in Part A and B claims do not reflect the portion paid by Medicaid, we did not examine changes in inpatient or outpatient out‐of‐pocket costs.

Analysis

Because post‐expansion MSP enrollment was staggered over the 6‐month enrollment window and there was likely heterogeneity in treatment effects across beneficiaries and over time, we applied difference‐in‐differences for multiple time periods that aggregate individual 2 × 2 difference‐in‐differences, estimating the associations between enrollment and utilization outcomes across different months [13].

To assess associations between enrolling in MSP and utilization over time, we used event plots that summarized estimates by month relative to the month prior to the index month of MSP enrollment. We also estimated the average association across all 12 pre‐enrollment and 12 post‐enrollment months. Under a parallel trends assumption that differences in mean counterfactual outcomes among the newly enrolled beneficiaries, had they not enrolled, coincide with those of existing MSP beneficiaries, these associations estimate the average treatment effect on the treated (ATET) among the newly enrolled group. The estimated percentage change in monthly outcomes associated with enrolling in MSP post‐expansion was calculated by dividing the ATET estimates by the unadjusted mean monthly utilization in the pre period among beneficiaries newly enrolled in MSP.

Due to concerns that ED visits or hospitalizations occurring in pre‐enrollment months might precipitate MSP enrollment [14], we also conducted a sensitivity analysis that included a wash‐out period that excluded the 3 months before and after the index month of MSP enrollment from the analysis.

Results

Changes in MSP Enrollment

Pre‐expansion policy MSP enrollment varied across states. At the start of each state's observation period (i.e., 24 months before their policy change), MSP enrollment was 66,440 in Connecticut, 120,753 in Indiana, 339,920 in New York, and 94,621 in Oregon, representing a range of 11,369 MSP enrollees per 100,000 Medicare beneficiaries in Indiana to 13,222 per 100,000 beneficiaries in Oregon (Figure 1).

Secondary analyses examining changes in total dual‐eligible enrollment following MSP eligibility expansions were largely directionally consistent with the primary finding on MSP enrollment but with a smaller magnitude (Table A3, Appendix Figure S1). Consistent with these findings, between 19% (Connecticut) and 46% (New York) of beneficiaries who newly enrolled in MSPs in the first 6 months after state eligibility expansions were full benefit dual‐eligibles in the month prior to enrolling in MSPs (Appendix Table A4). Additionally, between 8% (Connecticut) and 32% (Indiana) of post‐expansion MSP enrollees were enrolled in the Part D LIS without Medicaid in the month prior to enrolling in MSPs.

Characteristics of Post‐Expansion MSP Enrollees in Connecticut and Indiana

Among the 12,080 and 21,915 traditional Medicare beneficiaries in Connecticut and Indiana who were newly enrolled in the 6‐month period post‐expansion, we identified successful matches with beneficiaries with existing MSP enrollment for 9393 (78%) and 18,910 (86%) of them. The newly eligible beneficiaries whom we could not match mostly consisted of those who enrolled in the SLMB and QI eligibility groups, because beneficiaries with existing MSP enrollment were primarily QMBs. These analyses, thus, largely represent the effect of gaining both Parts A and B cost‐sharing and premium subsidies following eligibility expansions.

Table 2 presents the characteristics of beneficiaries who newly gained MSP coverage in the first 6 months post‐expansion compared with those already enrolled in MSPs, before and after matching. Newly enrolled beneficiaries in both Connecticut and Indiana were less likely to be younger than age 65 (19% vs. 45% in Connecticut and 43% vs. 63% in Indiana), live in ZIP codes with higher levels of poverty, and be enrolled in the QMB eligibility group; they were more likely to be of White race or ethnicity. After matching, standardized differences for nearly all observable characteristics were < 0.1 (Appendix Figure A2), and the distribution of propensity scores became more comparable (Appendix Figure A3).

Post‐expansion enrollment in MSPs was associated with a decrease of seven hospitalizations per 1000 beneficiary‐months in Connecticut (95% CI: −11, −4; 18% decrease) and four hospitalizations per 1000 beneficiary‐months in Indiana (95% CI: −7, −2; 11% decrease). There was also a decrease of 8 ED visits per 1000 beneficiary‐months associated with post‐expansion MSP enrollment in Indiana (95% CI: −12, −4; 8% decrease); in Connecticut, the estimated change in ED visits was negative but not statistically significant.

In Connecticut, post‐expansion MSP enrollment was associated with an increase of 380 prescription drug fills per 1000 beneficiary‐months (95% CI: 333, 427; 9% increase relative to the pre‐enrollment mean), and in Indiana, 384 fills per 1000 beneficiary‐months (95% CI: 341, 426; 7% increase). Post‐expansion MSP enrollment was also associated with increases in fills for antidepressants, antidiabetes, and antilipid drugs (e.g., 19 fills per 1000 beneficiary‐months for antidiabetes drugs in Connecticut, 95% CI: 12, 27; 12% increase).

In secondary analyses, MSP enrollment was associated with significant relative reductions in monthly out‐of‐pocket drug costs in Connecticut of 88% and Indiana of 51% (Appendix Table A5). In sensitivity analyses that excluded the 3 months before and after the index month of MSP enrollment from the analysis, results were consistent with the main findings (Appendix Table A6).

Discussion

Although states have been expanding MSP eligibility for decades, data on the impact of these policies on MSP enrollment and health care use are scarce. Across the four states included in this study, MSP eligibility expansions had variable associations with enrollment increases, with much larger increases in the two states that expanded eligibility by raising income limits (Connecticut and Indiana) compared with the two states that only eliminated asset tests (New York and Oregon). The largest increase was in Connecticut, which raised income limits and eliminated asset tests. Beneficiaries who were newly enrolled in MSP after eligibility expansions in Connecticut and Indiana experienced relative increases in prescription drug use and decreases in hospitalization and, in Indiana, ED visit rates in the first year after gaining benefits compared with matched controls who were enrolled in MSP prior to eligibility expansion.

States are currently continuing to expand MSP eligibility criteria in the absence of evidence. Since 2020, Maine, Massachusetts, and New York have increased income limits, and Maine, Massachusetts, New Mexico, California, and Washington have eliminated asset tests. Our findings on enrollment largely agree with one prior report that examined enrollment for four states—two states that overlap with our study (Indiana and Oregon) and two different states, Louisiana, which eliminated asset tests in 2019, and Massachusetts, which increased income and asset tests in 2020. This prior study used a similar analytic approach but included years during the temporary COVID‐19 Medicaid Continuous Enrollment provision in its sample, which would have increased MSP enrollment rates independent of the eligibility expansion policies [7].

The more limited change in MSP enrollment following only asset test eliminations in New York and Oregon was unexpected given prior reports that the asset test presents a major administrative barrier to beneficiaries enrolling in MSPs [15, 16]. It is possible that asset test eliminations affect a smaller share of enrollees or are less salient to beneficiaries compared with income limit increases. For example, state officials in Arizona, which eliminated the MSP asset test before 2002, noted that few MSP applicants failed to qualify for MSP due to failing asset tests [16]. Thus, if the MSP applicant pool did not substantially change because of eliminating the asset test—perhaps because few beneficiaries knew of the policy change or were unsure about their eligibility for MSP—eliminating the asset test alone without outreach and education efforts may not result in substantial increases in MSP enrollment. Additionally, it may be easier to identify potentially eligible individuals enrolled in other state assistance programs following income‐based expansions. Reports from Maine, which first eliminated the asset test for MSPs in January 2007 and then increased income limits in April 2007, note that MSP enrollment increased by the largest amount in the month of the income expansion when the state deemed State Pharmacy Assistance program recipients eligible for MSPs, highlighting the potential importance of automatically enrolling individuals participating in other assistance programs, when possible, into MSPs following eligibility expansions [8].

Pairing enrollment changes with detailed information from states on outreach activities and enrollment processes is needed to inform approaches to increase the take‐up of MSP assistance among eligible beneficiaries following state policy changes. Indeed, the proportion of eligible beneficiaries who take up MSP benefits has been found to range from only about half to two‐thirds, with similar take‐up rates among states that have and have not expanded MSP eligibility [17, 18]. We cannot assess the extent to which increases in enrollment following state eligibility expansions were among those newly eligible for MSP benefits or those previously eligible but unenrolled since we do not have individual‐level data on income and assets. Such information would be valuable for state policymakers for tailoring outreach strategies.

Surprisingly, we did not find evidence of increases in outpatient visit rates associated with gaining MSP benefits, in contrast to prior findings that the Medicare cost‐sharing cliff at 100% FPL is associated with decreased visit rates [3]. In both Connecticut and Indiana, some beneficiaries who enrolled in MSP after the eligibility expansion were previously full‐benefit dual‐eligibles without MSP. This may partially explain our finding of the limited effect of gaining MSP on office visit rates. Because full‐benefit duals typically receive coverage for Medicare A and B cost‐sharing, whether or not they are enrolled in MSP, gaining MSP likely conferred relatively fewer benefits in terms of reduced cost‐sharing compared with those who were not previously dually eligible. Another possibility is that limited or declining availability of outpatient providers willing to see dual‐eligible beneficiaries due to differential reimbursement contributed to the observed lack of change in visit rates [19, 20].

Newly enrolling in MSPs was associated, however, with significant increases in prescription drug fills, including for three clinically important chronic drug classes. As noted previously, MSP enrollees are automatically enrolled in the Part D LIS, and numerous studies have found that receiving the Part D LIS is associated with reductions in cost‐related barriers to prescription drug use [9, 21, 22]. Some new MSP enrollees in both states, however, previously had Part D LIS benefits and Connecticut also had a state pharmacy assistance program that limited drug cost‐sharing for beneficiaries with incomes at or below 228% FPL [23]. We lacked information on previous enrollment in state pharmacy assistance programs, and so did not assess variation in the potential effects of MSP on drug use for those with and without prior drug subsidies, which would be valuable to investigate in future studies.

We found that MSP enrollment following state eligibility expansions was associated with decreases in hospitalizations and ED visits, including in sensitivity analyses that included a wash‐out period. However, we view these findings with caution, given the elevated rates of acute care events in the month prior to gaining MSP among those who newly enrolled in MSP compared with controls who were already enrolled in MSPs. Notably, ED visits and hospitalizations have been identified as important precipitating events for MSP enrollment. For instance, beneficiaries may receive assistance applying for Medicaid from hospital staff, particularly in the face of high‐cost events [14]. Taken together, these patterns suggest that MSPs serve a role in responding to and, potentially, mitigating acute care events.

This study has other limitations. Concurrent policy changes or secular trends could bias our estimates of enrollment changes, particularly longer‐term changes, following MSP eligibility expansions. Because Medicare data do not include individual‐level information on income or assets, we are unable to determine which beneficiaries were eligible for MSP before or after eligibility expansions. Consequently, we conducted matched difference‐in‐differences analyses using those who were already enrolled in MSPs as a control group who could have lower incomes, on average, compared with those newly enrolled. Nevertheless, inspection of pre‐enrollment trends in utilization among the matched sample of those who newly enrolled into MSP post‐expansion and those already enrolled demonstrated parallel trends in the preperiod. We had limited ability to match beneficiaries who gained MSP through eligibility groups that provide premium assistance only (e.g., SLMB, QI) or beneficiaries of American Indian/Alaska Native race/ethnicity, and we lacked encounter data for Medicare Advantage enrollees, so our estimates of changes in use may not extend to these groups. Similarly, we examined only a subset of states that expanded MSPs, and findings may differ in other states. Lastly, due to concerns about differential primary nonadherence in the pre‐period, we focused on 30‐day prescription fills as an indicator of medication use, which does not allow us to determine how MSP expansions may have influenced individual‐level adherence to chronic medications.

In conclusion, in this study of four states with MSP eligibility expansions occurring between 2006 and 2020, we found variable impacts on MSP enrollment across states, with larger increases in states that expanded eligibility through raised income tests compared with eliminating asset tests alone. In Connecticut and Indiana, which had significant immediate increases in enrollment, gaining MSP benefits was associated with increases in prescription drug fills, including for three commonly used chronic drugs, underscoring the potential clinical value of comprehensive financial assistance for low‐income beneficiaries.
