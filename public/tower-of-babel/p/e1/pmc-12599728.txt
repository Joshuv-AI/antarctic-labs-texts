Causal association between sepsis and acute respiratory distress syndrome: Evidence from a 2-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Pei Zhang, Wenbin Tian, Ye Tian, Ning Yu, Chao Liu, Shuxian Ma, Xueqing Li, Xiaoyu Zhou, Huaihai Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a life-threatening respiratory failure syndrome, and sepsis is a dysregulated host response to infection. Although sepsis is a major ARDS trigger, the causal relationship at the genetic level remains unestablished. This study assesses the causal effect of sepsis on the risk of ARDS using 2-sample Mendelian randomization (MR). We performed a 2-sample MR analysis using summary statistics from genome-wide association studies. Sepsis-associated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were derived from the UK Biobank (11,643 cases; 474,841 controls). ARDS outcome data were obtained from the FinnGen project (165 cases; 216,363 controls). Analyses utilized the “TwoSampleMR” package in R, with primary causal estimation via the inverse-variance-weighted method, supplemented by sensitivity analyses including weighted median regression, MR Egger regression (MR-Egger), and MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier for horizontal pleiotropy assessment. Quality control encompassed SNP harmonization for allele alignment, exclusion of ambiguous palindromic SNPs, and Bonferroni correction. Inverse-variance-weighted analysis showed no significant causal effect of genetically predicted sepsis on ARDS risk (odds ratio [OR] = 0.514, 95% confidence interval: 0.195–1.359, P = .943). Sensitivity analyses confirmed null associations (MR-Egger test: OR = 0.309, P = .276; weighted median: OR = 0.950, P = .943). No directional pleiotropy was detected (MR-Egger intercept, P = .172; MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier global test, P = .527). The leave-one-out analysis revealed no significant SNPs. Exploratory analysis identified 5 pleiotropic loci (succinyl-CoA glutarate-CoA transferase, Ras-related protein Rab-38, glutamate ionotropic receptor kainate type subunit 4, adrenoceptor alpha-1 B, and axis inhibition protein 1). This MR study found no genetic evidence supporting a causal relationship between sepsis and ARDS. The identified pleiotropic loci warrant further investigation of shared biological pathways.

1. Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is an acute and severe pulmonary disease, that typically results from acute lung injury caused by infections, trauma, burns, chemical stimuli, or systemic inflammatory reactions.[1] ARDS impairs lung function and prevents sufficient oxygen from entering the bloodstream, thereby leading to low oxygen levels. Its features include diffuse inflammatory reactions in the lungs, increased permeability of alveoli and microvessels, triggering inflammation that results in the leakage of plasma proteins and fluids into the alveolar interstitium, causing pulmonary edema and exudation, ultimately compromising respiratory function and potentially leading to respiratory failure.[2] ARDS not only affects the lungs but can also result in multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, particularly damaging organs such as the cardiovascular system, kidneys, and liver.[3] Although ARDS can occur at any age, younger adults are more susceptible to factors such as trauma, surgery, or infection. Older individuals may be more prone to developing ARDS because of their underlying health conditions. In most cases, there is no significant sex difference in the incidence of ARDS between males and females.[4] Lung imaging through X-rays or computed tomography scans typically reveals lung infiltrates and exudation. Treatment of ARDS primarily involves addressing underlying causes, providing mechanical ventilatory support, fluid management, nutritional support, and other supportive therapies. Intensive care is crucial, because patients may require close monitoring and comprehensive care.[5] The pathogenesis of ARDS involves inflammatory reactions, increased alveolar membrane permeability, alveolar collapse, and increased negative pressure in the alveolar space, leading to inadequate oxygen exchange in the lungs and resulting in symptoms such as rapid breathing, hypoxemia, and respiratory distress.[6,7]

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a novel epidemiological method based on whole-genome sequencing data that utilizes single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) to reveal causal relationships. MR leverages the random allocation of genes in nature to infer the impact of phenotypes on diseases.[8]

Sepsis is a systemic inflammatory response syndrome caused by the invasion of pathogenic microorganisms, including bacteria, which leads to organ dysfunction. Based on its severity and progression, sepsis can be categorized into sepsis, severe sepsis, and septic shock. Sepsis can result in organ dysfunction and circulatory disturbances and is a common complication of serious traumas, burns, shocks, infections, and major surgeries.[9,10]

The regulation of ARDS risk factors plays a crucial role in reducing the risk of ARDS. In this study, we used a 2-sample MR analysis to reexamine whether there is a causal relationship between sepsis and an increased risk of ARDS. We used information from sepsis genome-wide association studies (GWAS) to explore the causal relationship between sepsis and ARDS. We assessed the association of 14 sepsis-associated SNPs with ARDS in a study that included 165 patients and 216,363 control participants from the FinnGen project.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Overall study design

The abstract data for this study were derived from published research that has obtained approval from the institutional review board. Therefore, further sanctions were not required. A 2-sample MR design was used to investigate the causal relationship between sepsis and ARDS (Fig. 1).

A 2-sample MR design was employed to investigate the causal relationship between sepsis and ARDS. ARDS = acute respiratory distress syndrome, MR = Mendelian randomization.

2.2. Ethical approval

This study utilized publicly available, de-identified summary statistics from previously published GWAS. The original GWAS projects (UK Biobank and FinnGen) received ethical approval from their respective institutional review boards. De novo ethics review was not required for this secondary analysis of aggregated genetic data, as it utilized anonymized, publicly accessible datasets in accordance with the national guidelines for research involving preexisting de-identified data. Individual informed consent was obtained from the original studies. This analysis used summary-level data without access to personal identifiers.

2.3. Data sources

2.3.1. Identification of SNPs related to sepsis

From the latest GWAS database, we used data from the UK Biobank to obtain 14 SNPs associated with sepsis. These SNPs were identified in 11,643 sepsis cases and 474,841 controls. In our study, the selection of IVs was based on SNPs that were independent in the European population (r2 < 0.001, window size = 10,000 kb) and significant (P < 5 × 10-6, F-statistic > 10).

2.3.2. ARDS

ARDS data were obtained from the FinnGen project and are available at https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets/finn-b-J10_ARDS/. The dataset included 165 ARDS patients and 216,363 control participants. The study was approved by the institutional review board, and all participants provided informed consent for the original study.

2.4. Statistical analysis

We performed 2-sample MR analysis using the “TwoSampleMR” package in the R software (version 0.5.8) (R version 4.2.2, http://www.r-project.org). Bonferroni-corrected analyses were conducted for all statistical tests, with a 2-tailed significance level of P < .05. The inverse-variance-weighted (IVW) method was used to estimate the causal relationship between sepsis and ARDS, and sensitivity analyses were conducted using the weighted median and MR Egger regression (MR-Egger) methods. The MR-Egger intercept estimated the presence of pleiotropy, and whether the intercept differed significantly from zero was statistically assessed.

In addition, SNP harmonization between the exposure (sepsis) and outcome (ARDS) datasets was conducted using the harmonize data() function in the TwoSampleMR package to ensure proper allele alignment. Ambiguous palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies were excluded to avoid strand mismatch. We also calculated the F-statistics for each IV using the formula F = β²/SE². All selected SNPs had F-statistics >10, indicating strong IVs and suggesting that weak instrument bias is unlikely to influence the results.

The GWAS summary statistics used for sepsis and ARDS were derived from 2 independent cohorts, the UK Biobank and FinnGen. As these datasets originate from distinct European populations with no participant overlap, the risk of sample overlap bias in the MR estimates was minimal.

To further evaluate horizontal pleiotropy and identify potential outlier SNPs, we conducted the MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO) global test.

3.2. MR analysis of ARDS

We used IVW, MR-Egger, and weighted median regression to estimate the causal relationship between genetically predicted sepsis and ARDS. IVW is a commonly used statistical method for MR analysis. In IVW, multiple genetic variant loci are considered and their effect estimates are weighted averages, with weights determined by the inverse variance of each locus. The MR-Egger test is a statistical method used in MR analysis to assess causal relationships between exposure and outcome, addressing potential unbalanced pleiotropy weighted median regression is a statistical method commonly used in regression analysis that provided better estimates of the regression coefficients in the presence of outliers or anomalies. According to the results of the 3 MR methods (MR-Egger: OR = 0.309, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.042–2.300, P = .276; weighted median: OR = 0.950, 95% CI = 0.228–3.951, P = .943; IVW: OR = 0.514, 95% CI = 0.195–1.359, P = .943), we could not confirm a significant causal relationship between sepsis and ARDS (Fig. 4).

3.3. Horizontal pleiotropy analysis

Horizontal pleiotropy analysis is often used in genetic epidemiology and causal inference, particularly in MR. Horizontal pleiotropy refers to the influence of genetic variation on a target-specific trait without involving intermediate factors, thereby ensuring the accuracy and reliability of MR results, especially when dealing with genetic variations that may impact multiple intermediate factors. In our study, the MR-Egger intercept showed no evidence of significant directional pleiotropy (P = .172) (Table 2).

3.4. Individual genetic instruments’ impact on ARDS

To validate the impact of each SNP on the overall causal estimate, omission analysis was conducted. Omission analysis aims to predict the likelihood of future events by observing patterns in the past data. When individual SNPs were systematically removed and the MR analysis was repeated, the estimated causal effects did not exhibit substantial differences (Fig. 5). Therefore, no single genetic instrument led to estimated effects, reinforcing the robustness and reliability of our study results.

3.5. MR-PRESSO analysis

To further assess horizontal pleiotropy and detect potential outlier SNPs that could bias causal estimates, we performed the MR-PRESSO global test. The analysis did not identify any outlier SNPs, and the distortion test was not applicable because no correction was required. The global test yielded a non-significant result (P = .527), indicating no evidence of significant horizontal pleiotropy. 4. Discussion

In this study, we used a 2-sample MR approach to explore the potential causal association between sepsis and ARDS in individuals of European ancestry. MR utilizes genetic variants as IVs to mimic randomized controlled trial conditions, helping to reduce the confounding and reverse causation common in observational studies.[11–13] Summary-level genetic data were obtained from 2 independent GWAS cohorts: the UK Biobank for Sepsis (n = 474,841) and the FinnGen project for ARDS (165 cases, 216,363 controls). Three complementary MR methods were applied: IVW, MR-Egger, and weighted median regression.

Across all approaches, we found no statistically significant evidence supporting the causal effect of genetically predicted sepsis on the risk of ARDS. MR-Egger test suggested a potential protective effect, but this estimate lacked statistical significance. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our findings with no evidence of directional pleiotropy. The MR-PRESSO global test detected no outliers (P = .527), and leave-one-out analysis indicated that no single SNP disproportionately influenced the results. Additionally, all the selected SNPs had F-statistics > 10, suggesting strong instrument strength and a low likelihood of weak instrument bias.

Several methodological considerations are essential for interpreting our findings. MR relies on critical assumptions, including the relevance of genetic instruments, absence of horizontal pleiotropy, and independence of instruments from confounders. To assess potential violations, we conducted sensitivity analyses using the MR-Egger regression, leave-one-out testing, and MR-PRESSO. The MR-Egger intercept did not indicate directional pleiotropy, and the MR-PRESSO global test also showed no significant outliers, suggesting that our estimates were not substantially biased by horizontal pleiotropy.

Nonetheless, this study has some limitations. The relatively small number of ARDS cases may have reduced the power to detect modest causal effects, and we were unable to perform a reverse MR analysis due to the lack of sufficiently powered ARDS GWAS data for exposure. Additionally, our analysis was restricted to European ancestry populations and summary-level data, limiting subgroup exploration (e.g., sex or comorbidities).

Previous MR studies[14] have typically relied on a single analytical approach and identified biomarkers, such as plasma angiopoietin-2 (ANG2), as potential causal mediators in sepsis-associated ARDS. Elevated plasma ANG2 levels may contribute to ARDS pathogenesis, and targeting ANG2 could offer novel therapeutic avenues. In contrast, our study employed 3 complementary MR methods (IVW, MR-Egger, and weighted median regression) to minimize the potential pleiotropic bias. Consistency across these approaches strengthens the robustness of our findings.

ARDS is a severe acute respiratory syndrome with high mortality rate.[15] Previous MR studies[16,17] have focused on individual biomarkers such as plasma sRAGE, suggesting a potential causal mediation pathway in sepsis-associated ARDS. However, our study evaluated the overall genetic liability for sepsis and did not support a direct causal relationship with ARDS risk, thereby providing a broader perspective on the genetic architecture linking the 2 conditions.

ARDS is a severe acute respiratory disease typically caused by direct lung injury or a systemic inflammatory response.[18] The development of ARDS involves a surge in inflammatory cells (such as neutrophils and macrophages), which release inflammatory mediators, such as cytokines and chemokines. The inflammatory response leads to local tissue damage and pathological changes, increasing alveolar surface tension owing to inflammation and fluid leakage, resulting in alveolar collapse and restricted lung ventilation, leading to respiratory distress. ARDS often involves diffuse injury and inflammation of the lungs. The early stages of ARDS involve increased alveolar membrane permeability, which causes protein and fluid leakage into the alveolar interstitium. This leads to alveolar edema, which affects the gas exchange between oxygen and carbon dioxide. Patients may experience rapid breathing, respiratory distress, and hypoxemia, which can potentially progress to respiratory failure. In the later stages of ARDS, a transparent membrane may form within the alveoli, composed of fibrin, cell debris, and inflammatory cells, further hindering gas exchange and potentially having long-term effects on patient recovery.[19] The main goals of ARDS treatment are to control the inflammatory response, maintain adequate oxygenation and ventilation, and address the primary causes of ARDS. Even for survivors of ARDS, long-term recovery challenges may include chronic respiratory issues, lung function impairment, cardiovascular problems, and psychological and cognitive challenges.[20]

There is a close association between sepsis and ARDS,[21,22] but this is not always a causal relationship. Sepsis is a syndrome of the systemic inflammatory response caused by infection and is characterized by an exaggerated inflammatory response to infection. The dissemination of bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites during infection may lead to a systemic inflammatory response, ultimately causing multiorgan dysfunction.[23] ARDS is characterized by rapid deterioration in lung function, and increased alveolar permeability leading to pulmonary edema and severe respiratory distress. ARDS is often caused by direct lung injury or non-pulmonary infections.[24,25] Sepsis and ARDS often occur in the context of infection or severe inflammatory states, and they may mutually influence each other in a complex and intertwined manner.[26–28] Sepsis can lead to ARDS, because the systemic inflammatory response induced by sepsis may cause inflammation and damage to the lungs, ultimately triggering the development of ARDS. The release of inflammatory and cytokine factors may increase alveolar permeability and initiate ARDS development. ARDS, in turn, exacerbates sepsis, as respiratory impairment caused by ARDS may necessitate mechanical ventilatory support, potentially increasing the risk of infection, especially in hospital settings. Additionally, lung damage may make patients more susceptible to infections.

The succinyl-CoA glutarate-CoA transferase (SUGCT) gene encodes a protein associated with succinate metabolism. SUGCT-AS1 is a nuclear lncRNA enriched in human macrophages that regulates pro-inflammatory responses in macrophages.[29] SUGCT is a mitochondrial enzyme involved in succinyl-CoA synthesis from glutarate-CoA during tryptophan and lysine catabolism. Loss of SUGCT function can lead to dysbiosis of the gut microbiota, causing age-dependent pathological changes in the kidney, liver, and adipose tissue. SUGCT gene mutations associated with type III glutaric aciduria result in multiple transcript variants due to alternative splicing.[30] The outcome of SUGCT mutations is modulated by the microbiome, age, and diet. Knockout of the non-essential gene SUGCT leads to diet-related microbial imbalance, resulting in a phenotype resembling diabetes-like metabolic syndrome.[31]

The Ras-related protein Rab-38 (RAB38) encodes a small guanosine triphosphatase belonging to the Ras family and is typically involved in organelle transport and secretion pathways. It plays a role in the biogenesis of lysosome-related organelles and in defense against certain microbial infections.[32,33] RAB38 is located in various cellular compartments including cytoplasmic vesicles, lysosomes, and mitochondria-associated endoplasmic reticulum membranes. It participates in nuclear-to-melanosome transport, melanosome assembly, and phagosome acidification.[34] Endogenous Rab38 regulates membrane recruitment of LRRK2 in melanocytes.[35] The interaction between myosin Va and cell type-specific Rab32 and Rab38 proteins mediates the transport of substances to mature melanosomes, thereby facilitating organelle secretion.[36] Combined deficiency of RAB32 and RAB38 can simulate Hermansky–Pudlak syndrome and severely impair thrombosis.[37] RAB32 and RAB38 play crucial roles in macrophages and osteoclasts,[38] in maintaining bone homeostasis by regulating intracellular transport in osteoclasts.[39]

Glutamate ionotropic receptor kainate type subunit 4 (GRIK4) encodes an ionotropic glutamate receptor that regulates glutamate neurotransmitter signaling in the nervous system.[40] Polymorphisms in GRIK4 influence cognitive impairments in patients.[41] Increased GRIK4 gene dosage leads to an imbalanced circuit output and behaviors associated with human diseases.[42] GRIK4 gene variations affect the response to electroconvulsive therapy and play a role in treatment-resistant depression.[43,44]

Adrenoceptor alpha-1 B (ADRA1B) is an adrenergic receptor subtype representing α1B-adrenergic receptors that is involved in fine-tuning the signaling of the neurotransmitter adrenaline. ADRA1B gene variations are associated with susceptibility to psoriasis in the Chinese population.[45] Alpha-1-adrenergic receptors (alpha-1-ARs) are members of the G protein-coupled receptor superfamily that activates mitotic reactions and regulates the growth and proliferation of many cells.[46] Adrenergic receptors play a crucial role in regulating neurotransmitter adrenaline signaling, impacting various physiological processes, including cardiovascular, immune, and nervous system regulation.[47]

Axis inhibition protein 1 (AXIN1) is a protein playing a crucial role in cell signaling, with a G protein-signaling (rgs) domain and an irregular axin (dix) domain. It primarily participates in the regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which is critical for cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival and is closely associated with inflammation and immune responses.[48] AXIN1 protects against the development of colorectal cancer through immune-mediated mechanisms.[49] AXIN1 plays a key role in embryonic development by preventing excessive axial and organ growth. Additionally, AXIN1 regulates essential cellular processes such as maintaining stemness, proliferation, differentiation, and programmed cell death.[50] In infectious diseases, AXIN1 prevents bacterial invasion by Salmonella and modulates inflammatory responses during infection.[51]

Sepsis and ARDS are 2 inflammatory and immune-related diseases that can originate from infections in various parts of the body, most commonly the lungs, abdomen, or urinary tract. Therefore, it is speculated that the abnormal expression of SUGCT, RAB38, GRIK4, ADRA1B, and AXIN1 may be associated with sepsis and ARDS.

To meet the assumptions of the MR analysis, we exclusively selected SNPs significantly associated with sepsis in individuals of European ancestry to minimize population stratification and confounding. Allele harmonization was carefully conducted using the harmonize_data() function in the TwoSampleMR package, and palindromic variants with intermediate allele frequencies were excluded to prevent strand mismatches.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, our analysis relied on summary-level data, precluding stratified analyses by sex, age, comorbidities, and clinical severity. Second, although ARDS and sepsis are known to interact in a bidirectional clinical context, our study aimed to assess the unidirectional causal effect of sepsis on ARDS. Owing to the lack of adequately powered GWAS summary data for ARDS as an exposure, reverse MR analysis could not be performed. Future studies with larger ARDS cohorts and expanded genetic coverage are needed to enable such analyses and to more fully clarify the temporal and causal dynamics between the 2 conditions.

Third, the relatively small number of ARDS cases (n = 165) may have limited the statistical power to detect subtle causal effects. Although our MR methods are robust, the possibility of false-negative results due to underpowering cannot be excluded. A formal power analysis, currently constrained by the available data, should be incorporated in future studies to strengthen the interpretation of the null findings.

In summary, this MR study found no evidence of a causal relationship between sepsis and ARDS risk among individuals of European ancestry. Although these findings do not support a direct genetic causal link, they highlight the need for further research incorporating larger sample sizes, more diverse populations, and complementary study designs. Such efforts may help to uncover specific pathways or intermediate phenotypes that connect systemic infection and acute lung injury, ultimately guiding more targeted prevention or intervention strategies.

5. Conclusions

MR algorithm provided genetic loci (SUGCT, RAB38, GRIK4, ADRA1B, and AXIN1) that may play a role in sepsis and ARDS.
