Left atrial strain tracks abnormal ventricular mechanics in Fabry disease
Authors: Chosita Cheepvasarach, Michael Gribble, Martin Ugander, Ravi Vijapurapu, Sabrina Nordin, Joao Augusto, Richard Paul Steeds, Michel Tchan, James C Moon, Faraz Pathan, Rebecca Kozor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFabry disease (FD) is an X linked lysosomal disorder with ventricular myocardial involvement that drives morbidity and mortality. Early diagnosis of cardiac involvement can be difficult. This study explored whether abnormal left atrial (LA) strain by cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) may be an early sign of ventricular involvement in FD.MethodsA multicentre, multinational cohort of patients with FD was assembled with images centralised for core lab analysis. Adult patients with gene-positive FD and healthy volunteers (HV) underwent CMR. LA strain analyses included manually contouring the left atrium in end-diastole and end-systole to calculate LA volumes and ejection fraction, then semiautomatic analysis for LA reservoir strain.ResultsThere were n=214 patients with FD (mean age 45±15 years, 39% males) and n=76 HV (49±15 years, 53% males). CMR results in FD: left ventricular ejection fraction 73% (IQR=9), left ventricular mass index (LVMi) 89±39 g/m2, 99 (46%) had left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), 36% had late gadolinium enhancement. In FD, LA strain correlated with LVMi (r=−0.52, p<0.01), left ventricular (LV) global longitudinal strain (GLS) (r=−0.61, p<0.01) and native myocardial T1 (r=0.34, p<0.01). FD had abnormal LA strain in overt disease (LVH positive) compared with HV (p<0.01). LVH-negative FD did not differ in LA strain compared with HV (p>0.5). FD with low T1+LVH negative did not differ in LA strain compared with normal T1/LVH-negative FD or HV (p>0.3).ConclusionsLA strain is abnormal in FD with LVH (overt disease) and correlates with LVMi, native T1 and GLS. LA strain is normal in FD with early disease (LVH negative+low T1) and normal in FD with no myocardial disease (LVH negative+normal T1). These findings indicate that LA strain is a consequence of abnormal LV mechanics such as LVH and abnormal GLS, rather than isolated myocardial sphingolipid deposition.Trial registration numberNCT03199001.Keywords: Cardiac Imaging Techniques, Magnetic Resonance Angiography, Magnetic Resonance Imaging

Introduction

Fabry disease (FD) is a rare condition caused by a mutation in the GLA gene resulting in deficiency of the enzyme alpha-galactosidase A.1 Although FD is X linked, it affects both men and women.2 Cardiac involvement drives mortality and morbidity 3 4 5 6and is thought to be due to sphingolipid deposition in cardiac myocytes and vascular endothelium, with associated inflammation, hypertrophy and fibrosis, leading over time to arrhythmias, heart failure and sudden death.3 4 However, all components of the heart may be affected, including the atria and valves.7

Left atrial (LA) function, assessed as LA strain, reflects reservoir, conduit and contractile phases during the cardiac cycle.8 9 Abnormal LA strain in FD has been associated with cardiac complications including atrial fibrillation and stroke, as well as ventricular structural changes such as left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH).410,12 Abnormal atrial strain has also been documented using echocardiography prior to the development of LVH.12

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) is the preferred modality in FD because it combines accurate assessment of ventricular volumes, mass and function with tissue characterisation. Unlike echocardiography, CMR can detect early myocardial sphingolipid deposition and fibrosis through T1 mapping and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE), often before hypertrophy develops. Low T1 is a surrogate marker of myocardial sphingolipid deposition, indicative of early disease.13 CMR has also been used to propose different stages of FD, including the prehypertrophy stage.14 15

Our hypothesis was that abnormal LA strain is an early sign of cardiac involvement in FD. Thus, the aim of this study was to investigate LA strain by CMR in FD and its association with low myocardial native T1, LVH and other CMR markers of FD.

Methods

This study is a secondary analysis of a prospective, multicentre, international, observational study (Fabry400, NCT03199001), recruiting patients with FD from three sites (London, Birmingham, Sydney). Ethics approval was obtained. Written informed consent was provided by all participants. Inclusion criteria were adults ≥18 years and with gene-positive FD. Healthy volunteers (HV) were recruited at a single site with no cardiovascular disease. Exclusion criteria included contraindications to CMR, poor image quality, incomplete datasets or arrhythmias preventing reliable strain analysis.

CMR imaging

CMR was performed at 1.5 T (Avanto, UK; Aera, Australia) using standard protocols, including cine imaging, T1 mapping (Modified Look-Locker Sequence) and LGE.16

CMR analysis

Conventional CMR parameters were previously analysed (left ventricular (LV) volumes, function, global longitudinal strain (GLS), mass, LGE, native T1).16 LA strain analysis was performed using CVI42 software, including manual contouring of the left atrium in end-diastole and end-systole (four-chamber and two-chamber views) to derive volumes, LA ejection fraction and reservoir strain. Analyses were repeated five times per patient, and the mean was used. Intraobserver and interobserver repeatability were assessed (n=10).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using SPSS Version 29. Continuous variables are mean±SD; categorical variables are frequency or %. Normality was assessed with Shapiro-Wilk test. Group comparisons: t-test or Mann-Whitney U test; analysis of variance for multiple groups; regression models assessed relationships between LA strain, T1 and LVH. P<0.05 was significant. Bland-Altman analysis assessed observer variability.

Results

Participant characteristics

There were 290 participants in total (214 FD and 76 HV), all in sinus rhythm. Of note, eight patients were excluded from the LA strain statistical analysis prior to the total population count, due to either incomplete phase scans or poor image quality. Adequate tracking quality was obtained for all other study participants (n=290). Baseline participant demographics and CMR findings are demonstrated in table 1. The mean Fabry age was 45±15 years with 83 (39%) males and 131 (61%) females. The HV population was slightly older (mean age 49±14 years, p=0.01; females 48% and males 52%).

LA function

Compared with HV, FD had similar LA end-systolic volumes (FD 79.2±26.9 mL vs HV 74.6±18.9 mL, p=0.18), but larger LA end-diastolic volumes (FD 37.6±20.0 mL vs HV 32.9±10.9 mL, p=0.05) and lower LA ejection fraction (FD 53.8±10.2 vs HV 56.4±6.2, p=0.04).

LA strain and LVM

LA strain correlated with LVMi in the total Fabry cohort (as LVMi increased LA strain worsened) (r=−0.52, p<0.01). Table 2 shows the LA reservoir strain measurements in HV and FD divided into males and females and also those with and without LVH. FD with LVH (overt disease) had worse LA strain compared with FD without LVH and HV (29.2±9.6 vs 40.3±11.5 vs 37.6±8.3, p<0.001), and this was also seen in both males and females. FD without LVH had similar LA strain compared with HV both in males (34±8.6 vs 34.8±7.1, p=0.82) and females (41.7±11.6 vs 40.6±8.3, p=0.55).

LA strain and LV function

LA strain did not correlate with LVEF but showed a strong correlation with LV GLS (r=–0.61, p<0.01), such that worsening GLS was associated with reduced LA strain. To address the primary objective, participants were stratified according to LV involvement (LVH present vs absent; abnormal vs normal GLS; low vs normal T1).

LVH status: Patients with FD with LVH had significantly lower LA reservoir strain than those without LVH and HV (29.2±9.6% vs 40.3±11.5% vs 37.6±8.3%, p<0.001). Even when GLS was normal, LA strain remained impaired in FD with LVH (32.6±8.2%)(table 3).

LVH status: Patients with FD with LVH had significantly lower LA reservoir strain than those without LVH and HV (29.2±9.6% vs 40.3±11.5% vs 37.6±8.3%, p<0.001). Even when GLS was normal, LA strain remained impaired in FD with LVH (32.6±8.2%)(table 3).

GLS status: Using the HV reference (19.3±2.0%), abnormal GLS was associated with marked LA strain reduction. In patients with abnormal GLS, LA strain was significantly lower in those with LVH compared with those without LVH (27.1±9.5% vs 41.6±12.3%, p<0.001; normal LA strain in HV 37.6±8.3%).

Discussion

The main finding of this CMR study is that LA strain is abnormal in patients with FD with LVH but normal in patients with FD with no LVH. Additionally, LA strain correlates with increased LVMi, low native T1 and abnormal GLS in FD. In LVH-negative patients with Fabry, that is, those with ‘early disease’, LA strain does not correlate with native T1. These findings indicate that LA strain is not influenced solely by sphingolipid deposition, but rather is a consequence of structural changes such as LVH and LV GLS. Therefore, abnormal LA strain by CMR can be used as an imaging marker of cardiac involvement.

Regarding how these results compare to the existing literature, there is a notable lack of consensus and ongoing debate about whether LA dysfunction in FD is caused by the progression of ventricular dysfunction or by sphingolipid deposition in the left atrium itself.10 One theory states that the build-up of sphingolipids in the LA myocardium results in interstitial fibrosis, which affects LA size and causes mechanical dysfunction, ultimately putting the patient at risk of Atrial Fibrilation.10 The alternate theory suggests that deposition of sphingolipids in the left ventricle may cause decreased LV compliance, diastolic dysfunction and higher LV filling pressures. The thin LA wall is particularly sensitive to changes in LV filling pressures, and so this dysfunction is then passively transmitted to the left atrium.10

A recent study showed that in LVH-negative FD, impairment of LV GLS by CMR was associated with a reduction in native T1.16 These findings suggest that LV mechanical dysfunction may occur before evidence of LVH, when there is evidence of low T1 (sphingolipid deposition).16 However, to date, most studies of LA strain in patients with Fabry have been conducted on small populations using echocardiography speckle-tracking imaging rather than CMR.17 18 Phasic LA function, particularly conduit and reservoir LA strain measured using MRI, is independently predictive of heart failure hospitalisations and death in other (non-Fabry) patient populations.18 Thus, there is an important gap in the current literature regarding CMR investigation of LA function in FD, which this study addresses.

Our findings support the hypothesis that LA dysfunction in FD is predominantly secondary to LV involvement. Saccheri et al reported that LA dysfunction and volume changes in FD are closely related to LV remodelling, rather than intrinsic atrial myocardial pathology.10 Similarly, Nordin et al proposed staged myocardial phenotypes in FD, suggesting that atrial dysfunction manifests primarily in association with overt hypertrophy and myocardial fibrosis.14 15 Our study adds to this by demonstrating, using CMR, that LA strain is preserved in early disease stages (LVH negative, low T1), indicating that atrial mechanical impairment does not occur before overt ventricular changes.

Recent studies have further elucidated the role of CMR in assessing cardiac involvement in FD. Lanzillo et al highlighted that CMR with tissue characterisation capabilities, such as native T1 mapping and LGE, is an accurate technique for differential diagnosis of LVH and staging of FD cardiomyopathy.19 They emphasised that a decreased myocardial T1 value is specific to FD and precedes the development of LVH.

Li et al conducted a prospective cohort study involving 602 patients with a hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) phenotype. They found that decreased native T1 mapping and a unique LGE pattern by CMR imaging are specific imaging markers for FD, aiding in distinguishing FD from HCM.20 This underscores the importance of incorporating CMR in the diagnostic workup of patients with suspected FD.

Furthermore, a 2024 study published in the Journal of CMR examined the contribution of LA strain in differentiating FD from HCM. The study demonstrated that LA strain derived from CMR feature tracking can effectively discriminate between these two phenocopy diseases, providing valuable information in the diagnostic process.21

Recent studies by Boyd et al have argued that LA dysfunction occurs before the development of LVH in patients with Fabry.12 Their study used echocardiography speckle tracking to show that increased LA volume and reduced atrial compliance occurred in patients with Fabry in the absence of LVH. Boyd et al therefore argued that FD alters atrial myocardial properties early in the disease process, and that LA dysfunction is largely due to atrial myocardial fibrosis. Similarly, further echocardiography investigation from Pichette et al suggests that sphingolipids also accumulate in the left atrium, and that LA dysfunction is therefore not just due to passive transmission of LV dysfunction.11 Conversely, Saccheri et al found that LA dysfunction and changes to atrial volume in FD are likely associated with the progression of LV dysfunction rather than the deposition of sphingolipids in the LA myocardium.10 The results of our study support the latter: that LA dysfunction is largely a consequence of LV changes, such as LVH and abnormal GLS, and sits on the continuum of ventricular storage to LVH.

There are several limitations identified in this project. First, FD is very rare, and as such, any studies on the subject are inherently limited in their sample sizes and, therefore, the statistical power of any results obtained. Considering this, this study represents one of the largest Fabry datasets to date (n=214). However, it is a retrospective study that included patients already diagnosed with FD, potentially introducing bias, and there are small numbers when comparing subgroups, particularly males with no LVH. Second, there are difficulties in standardising the analyses of LA strain using CMR, as there are differences depending on the software used (CVI42 in this study) and differences when compared with echocardiography analyses. There are other challenges associated with strain imaging of the left atrium that may undermine its diagnostic accuracy,22 such as the thin LA wall and the confounding presence of the atrial appendage and pulmonary veins, which complicate the measurement of LA strain parameters using the current technology.8 9 Third, this study did not examine the confounding effects of comorbidities, such as renal disease, nor disease-specific therapy (eg, enzyme replacement therapy). Fourth, it is difficult to comment on sex differences of LA strain in patients with FD. In our cohort, females had smaller left ventricular end-diastolic volume, lower LVMi and normal mean wall thickness, that is, less LVH (75% in males and 28% in females). The results of our study suggest that LA strain becomes abnormal when LVH occurs; hence, it was not surprising that females had normal mean LA strain compared with males and healthy controls because they had less LVH. In order to better explore sex differences in LA strain in FD, future studies need to include more females with LVH. Finally, this study is limited by the absence of histological confirmation of the extent of sphingolipid deposition and fibrosis within the left ventricle and left atrium. Consequently, asserting that the subgroup displaying no LVH and normal T1 values represents individuals with no cardiac involvement of FD, and that patients with no LVH and low T1 values represent very early disease, although plausible, cannot be definitively stated.

Conclusions

LA strain is abnormal in FD with LVH (overt disease) and correlates with LVMi, native T1 and GLS. LA strain is normal in FD with early disease (LVH negative+low T1) and normal in FD with no myocardial disease (LVH negative+normal T1). These findings indicate that LA strain is a consequence of abnormal LV mechanics such as LVH and abnormal LV GLS rather than isolated myocardial sphingolipid deposition.
