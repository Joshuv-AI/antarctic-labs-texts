Association between current cigarette prices and cessation behaviors among male adult smokers: findings from 2018 to 2020 ITC Vietnam surveys
Authors: Thi Phuong Thao Tran, Thi My Linh Nguyen, Thi Ngoc Phuong Nguyen, Thi Phung Tran, Bao Ngoc Nguyen, Tu Anh Duong, Thi Hai Phan, Ngoc Khue Luong, Son Dao The, Min Kyung Lim, Hoang Van Minh, Jin-Kyoung Oh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study evaluated the impact of the tax increase in January 2019 on changes in intention to quit and the effect of cigarette prices on quit attempts and successful quitting among male cigarette smokers in Vietnam.MethodsData were derived from the ITC project in Vietnam, which included 1585 adult smokers at baseline (Wave 1, Aug-Oct 2018) followed up to waves 2 (Sep-Nov 2019) and 3 (Sep-Dec 2020). Generalized estimating equations regression was performed to estimate changes in the intention to quit. Multiple logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the cigarette price of a cigarette pack in relation to quit attempts and successful quitting.ResultsThe increase in cigarette tax in 2019 did not significantly increase the likelihood of the intention to quit. After the tax increase, 63.6% of participants who smoked made a quit attempt, and 27.6% successfully quit smoking in the follow-up waves. However, the price of a cigarette pack was not significantly associated with quit attempts and successful quitting. The study did not observe a significant impact of cigarette prices on quit attempts and successful quitting in all subgroups of household income. Factors associated with quit attempts included the number of cigarettes smoked and the intention to quit, while those associated with successful quitting included age, dual use of cigarettes and other tobacco products, and the intention to quit.ConclusionCurrent cigarette prices were not associated with cessation behaviors even within the lowest household income group. Therefore, a sharp rise in cigarette tax is required to incentivize smokers to quit smoking.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-19689-y.Keywords: Cigarette price, Cigarette tax, Cessation behaviors, Quit intention, Quit attempt, Successful quitting

Introduction

Extensive research from around the world indicates that increasing tobacco taxes and prices is an effective way to reduce tobacco consumption and encourage people to quit smoking [1]. On average, a 10% price increase will lead to a consumption drop of 4% in high-income countries and 5% in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [2]. Despite the effectiveness of tax policies in reducing tobacco consumption, their implementation has been lacking [1]. As of 2023, only 41 countries have raised taxes to account for at least 75% of the retail price of a pack of 20-stick cigarettes [3]. Unfortunately, most LMICs still have insufficient tobacco tax rates or do not levy any tobacco taxes at all, thereby undermining their potential to reduce tobacco use and promote quitting behavior in these countries. Vietnam is among these countries, and there is no evidence to support the impact of cigarette price on smoking behaviors in the country.

In Vietnam, 15.6 million people currently smoke different types of tobacco products. Of which, most of them (80.6%) smoked cigarettes, much higher than bamboo water pipes (29.8%) and shisha (0.4%). Additionally, only 1.1% of adults had ever used an electronic cigarette and 0.2% of adults currently use electronic cigarettes [4]. Vietnam’s tax system for cigarettes is solely based on ad valorem, which is determined by the pre-tax factory price [5]. The excise rate on cigarettes was set at a uniform rate of 55% in 2006 and was subsequently increased to 65% in 2008, 70% in 2016, and 75% in 2019 [6]. As no longitudinal study had been conducted previously to evaluate the impact of the policy changes, we conducted an International Tobacco Control Policy Evaluation Project (ITC) Vietnam survey to analyze changes in smoking behaviors before and after the tax increase in 2019. Therefore, we evaluated the impact of cigarette price on intention to quit, quit attempt and successful quitting. ITC Vietnam survey focused on only adult male smokers because the smoking prevalence in women was extremely low (1.1% current tobacco smokers, and 0.9% daily smokers), compared to 45.3% for men.

Methods

Study design

The ITC Project conducts longitudinal surveys in representative cohorts in 30 countries, with Vietnam being the 31st ITC country. The ITC Survey is a longitudinal study on a representative sample of adult male smokers in Hanoi and involves contacting the same respondents for subsequent survey waves. The ITC surveys were conducted in two districts of Hanoi: Cau Giay (representing urban areas) and Quoc Oai (representing rural zones), with a total of 1,988 smoekrs. This sample size is robust enough to detect statistically significant changes and ensure sufficient sample size in case of lost-to-follow-up or refusal. This approach is also applied to 30 countries implementing the ITC Project. The protocols and quality control measures for the ITC Vietnam survey follow the standard procedures of ITC Projects. The methodologies, sampling techniques, and quality control measures for the ITC Vietnam surveys are described in more detail in technical reports [7–9].

Study population

This study was a longitudinal study using data from ITC Vietnam Wave 1 in 2018, before increasing cigarette excise tax from 70 to 75% since January 2019, and follow up to Wave 3 in 2020. Among 1988 smokers at baseline (Wave 1, Aug-Oct 2018), we excluded subjects who did not answer any of the follow-up surveys (Waves 2, Sep-Nov 2019 and Wave 3, Sep-Dec 2020), resulting in the final sample size of 1585. The loss-to-follow-up rate in this study was 20.3%, which was lower than other countries including South Korea [10], and similar to that of China [11].

Measure

Outcome

Intention to quit. All tobacco users who planned to quit “within the next month”, “within the next 6 months”, were classified as having the intention to quit. Those who answered “not interested in quitting” or “someday in the future” were classified as not having the intention to quit.

Quit attempt. Quit attempt was defined as quitting more than 24 h in the past 12 months measured, or being a quitter at any follow-up waves.

Successful quitting. Smokers who were classified as quitter (i.e., smokers who reported not to smoke any cigarette within the last 30 days) at both Wave 2 and Wave 3, or classified as quitter at Wave 3.

Covariates

Covariates were extracted from baseline survey. Demographics information was measured, including age, residential areas (urban, and rural), the highest education attainment (primary school or lower, secondary school, high school, and college/university or higher), and household income. The household income was divided into four quartile groups: Q1 – lowest income (0 to 7 million VND), Q2 (7.2 to 10 million VND), Q3 (10.5 to 16 million VND), Q4 – highest income (16.2 to 500 million VND). Tobacco use-related variables were collected, which consisted of the number of cigarettes smoked per day and the cigarette price at last purchase. The cigarette price was measured by two questions (1) “In the last time you purchased cigarettes, did you buy them by the single, the pack or the carton of cigarettes?” and (2) “How much did you pay (by single/pack/carton of cigarettes)?”. Then, we calculated the price for a cigarette pack. Dual users were defined as persons who used both cigarettes and other tobacco product types, such as bamboo tobacco waterpipe or electronic cigarettes.

Statistical analysis

Characteristics of study participants were presented as frequency and percentage while price of cigarette pack of 20 cigarettes and age was displayed in mean, standard error (se).

We compared the proportion of smokers intending to quit smoking at the time of Wave 1 with the proportions of smokers intending to quit at the time of Wave 2 and Wave 3. The GEE logistic regression of intending to quit as a function of wave and covariates was performed (binomial distribution, logit link function, exchangeable correlation structure). Additionally, multiple logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the cigarette price of a cigarette pack in relation to quit attempt and successful quitting, adjusted for other covariates. To test whether increasing cigarette taxes could make any impact on quit attempt and successful quitting for lower-income smokers, subgroup analysis by household income was proceeded.

We used longitudinal Wave 1-Wave 2-Wave 3 sampling weights to account for uneven representation in given age groups and regions, including attrition between recruitment and the primary survey. All the analyses were performed by STATA Version 17.

Results

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of study population. The study population had a mean age of 46.11 years (se = 0.63), and approximately 60% of smokers lived in urban areas. A high proportion of respondents obtained a high school education or lower. On average, they smoked 13.4 cigarettes per day. More than 70% of cigarette smokers did not additionally use other tobacco products, such as bamboo waterpipe. The mean price of a cigarette pack at the last purchase was 13.05 thousand VND (US$ 0.52). Approximately 22% of smokers intended to quit smoking within the next 6 months.

Discussion

Undoubtedly, higher cigarette taxes/prices can promote and encourage smoking cessation. In the US, the intention to quit smoking in the next six months was positively associated with cigarette price (OR = 1.63; 95%CI = 1.38–1.92) among non-Hispanic African American smokers [12]. Additionally, ITC in the US supported the finding that smokers living in areas with higher cigarette prices are significantly more motivated to quit, and higher cigarette prices increase the likelihood of quitting [13]. In Korea, smoking intensity decreased significantly after raising the cigarette price by 80% [14]. However, decreased consumption appeared limited to heavy Mexican smokers only after increasing taxes [15]. In New Zealand, after the tax increase in 2010, smokers were 1.5 times more likely to attempt to quit smoking [16]. It was observed in a study of International Tobacco Control (ITC) in four countries (the US, Canada, the UK, and Australia) that smokers who employed strategies to avoid or reduce prices and taxes were 28% less likely to quit successful cessation, and individuals who obtained cigarette from sources with lower or no taxation had a reduced likelihood of quitting [17]. However, in our study, we did not see the impact of the current cigarette price on intention to quit, quit attempts, and successful quitting among Vietnamese male smokers. Moreover, the study did not find a significant impact of cigarette prices on either quit attempts or successful quitting even within the lowest household income group. No significant effects of the cigarette price on cessation behaviors were due to the inadequate increase in cigarette tax. The current tax rate of 75% accounted for only 36% of the retail price, which was much lower than the global average of 56% and the levels in neighboring Southeast Asia countries [18]. After a limited increase of 5%, the cigarette price remained low and stable. Additionally, a 5% increase in the cigarette tax results in only a small increase of approximately 300 VND (equivalent to 1.29 cents) in retail prices. This change is extremely small compared to the income of even the lowest quartile income group. Specifically, the weighted mean price of a cigarette pack in 2018, 2019, and 2020 stood at VND 12,330 (US$ 0.54), VND 12,700 (US$ 0.55), and VND 12,120 (US$ 0.53) (p-value > 0.5, data from ITC surveys). These figures are notably lower when compared to other countries in the Western Pacific Region, such as Laos (US$ 0.70), China (US$ 0.74), Malaysia (US$ 1.98), and Singapore (US$ 3.26) [19]. The relatively modest increases in price and taxes were compensated for by higher income growth. The average price of a pack of cigarettes during the period of 2018 to 2020 in our study remained low and similar to GATS 2015 survey (11,800 VND, equivalent to US$ 0.54) [4]. Furthermore, during the period between 2005 and 2016, nominal income per capita increased by 4.7 times, while the price of a popular brand of cigarettes increased only 2.2 times [5], which means that cigarettes is now more than twice as affordable. Therefore, we emphasized the urgent need to significantly increase cigarette prices by levying specific tobacco taxes to achieve tax policy effectiveness and public health goals. While we await a significant government decision on cigarette taxes, which may take several years, immediate actions such as public health campaigns promoting the economic benefits of quitting and providing cessation services like Quitline—especially for low-income individuals—are crucial to increasing smoking cessation rates.

The challenge in implementing tobacco tax policy in Vietnam lies in the current tax system. Vietnam has applied a pure ad valorem tax, which has proven difficult to implement effectively and diminishes the impact of the tax policy [20]. To improve effectiveness, Vietnam should consider aligning with the practices of other countries by transitioning from a pure ad valorem tax system to a specific tax system. This shift could enhance the effectiveness of tax increases in reducing smoking rates. Therefore, one section of our evaluation in the ITC Vietnam survey will investigate the level of specific tax increase necessary to encourage Vietnamese smokers to quit. his section will also assess the combined effects of other tobacco control measures—such as smoke-free policies, cessation support, and public awareness campaigns—on smoking cessation behaviors. The findings from this section will be published in the coming months. As a result, this paper is focused on the primary issue of how current prices are insufficient to prompt smokers to quit.

In this study, dual use primarily referred to individuals using both cigarettes and waterpipe tobacco, given the low prevalence of e-cigarette use in Vietnam, as mentioned in the introduction. Notably, waterpipe tobacco has higher nicotine levels (9%) compared to cigarettes (1-3%) [21], which may facilitate an easier transition for smokers looking to quit cigarettes and opt for waterpipe tobacco as an alternative with equal or even superior nicotine intake. Therefore, this could explain the findings that dual use was associated with successful quitting in our study. However, additional research is imperative to confirm this association between quitting behaviors and smoking waterpipe tobacco. Furthermore, the scientific evidence cautions against the adverse impacts of using various tobacco products when enacting tobacco tax policies. The Guidelines for implementing Article 6 of the WHO FCTC emphasize the need for countries to create tax systems that discourage consumers from switching to less expensive products within the same category or to cheaper types of tobacco products in response to tax or retail price hikes, as well as other market-related influences.3 The ITC Bangladesh study also highlighted this lesson, demonstrating a notable decrease in the overall prevalence of bidi smoking, dropping from 12.4% in 2009 to 5.7% in 2012 [22]. This shift was attributed to smokers’ changing preferences, moving away from bidi and towards cigarettes [22]. In Vietnam, the prevalence of cigarette smoking among men stands at 36.7%, while 13.7% use bamboo waterpipes, marking the highest prevalence of waterpipe use in Asia [23]. Bamboo waterpipe tobacco is significantly more affordable than factory-made cigarettes, with prices ranging from VND 70,000 to VND 150,000 (approximately US$ 3.01 to US$ 6.44) for 100 g of Nicotiana rustica (i.e., tobacco leaves). Importantly, waterpipe tobacco remains untaxed in Vietnam [24]. Even though the findings showed that current price was not sufficiently high to motivate quit behaviors, it is important to note that dual users may be less affected by higher cigarette tax policies, based on the lessons from other countries. Therefore, it is advisable to implement a specific tax on waterpipe tobacco in Vietnam, thereby preventing smokers from switching to different tobacco or nicotine products in response to higher cigarette price.

This study is the first longitudinal study to use the standardized study design of the ITC project for analyzing the effects of cigarette tax policies in Vietnam. However, there are limitations in this study. First, given that this study was not representative of the entire Vietnamese population, nationally representative longitudinal study is needed to evaluate the potential impact of cigarette tax increase. This study is limited to male adult smokers, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to the broader smoking population in Vietnam, including women and youth smokers. Second, the self-reported prices of cigarettes in the study are different depending on the location of purchase in Vietnam. This variation may lead to potential methodological limitations, such as measurement errors or residual confounding, which could contribute to the lack of a significant association between cigarette prices and cessation behaviors. Third, smoking intensity was measured inconsistently between Wave 1 and the subsequent Waves. In Wave 1, for dual users of cigarettes and waterpipe tobacco, the number of waterpipe sessions was converted to number of cigarettes that 5 sessions equal 1 cigarette. In Wave 2 and 3, smoking intensity was asked for cigarette users and waterpipe tobacco users separately. Thus, the number of cigarettes participants smoked per day in Wave 1 could be slightly higher than follow-up waves in general. Therefore, it is a measurement bias in that we did not measure changes in smoking intensity after increasing cigarette taxes. Last, loss to follow-up could lead to attrition bias. Therefore, we examined the statistically significant differences between participants successfully followed up and lost to follow up (Table S1). No statistical differences were found in terms of sociodemographic characteristics and smoking-related variables between the two groups, except for age group, even though the attrition rate was higher than desired (20.3%), which suggests that attrition bias may be minimal.

Conclusion

Current cigarette prices were not associated with cessation behaviors even within the lowest household income group. This can be attributed to the affordable price of cigarettes in Vietnam. Therefore, a sharp rise in cigarette tax is required to incentivize smokers to quit smoking.
