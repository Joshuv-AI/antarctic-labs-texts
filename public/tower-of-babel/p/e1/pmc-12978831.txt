Heterogeneity of treatment effects in transcranial direct current stimulation for knee osteoarthritis pain and symptoms
Authors: Chiyoung Lee, Xiaoxiao Sun, Juyoung Park, Chen X. Chen, Christine Pellegrini, Nan-kuei Chen, David O. Garcia, Heewon Kim, C. Kent Kwoh, Hyochol Ahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.The characteristics associated with heterogeneity of treatment effects in transcranial direct current stimulation here may provide insights to inform the development of personalized stimulation protocols.

1. Introduction

The public health burden of osteoarthritis in the United States is expected to rise substantially due to the aging population.14 Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is the most prevalent form of arthritis, affecting more than 14 million older adults in the United States and leading to chronic pain and reduced quality of life.17,21 Since KOA is currently incurable, its management primarily focuses on pain relief and functional improvement. Although pharmacological treatments for KOA show promise, they often cause side effects, highlighting the need for novel nonpharmacological approaches.

Emerging research suggests that pain-related brain mechanisms are altered in individuals with KOA pain and that altered central pain processing is a key driver of joint pain and dysfunction.13,27 This has led to growing interest in nonpharmacological strategies targeting central pain mechanisms, particularly transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS).37 The pain-modulating effects of tDCS are believed to result from a complex interplay of mechanisms, including the modulation of cortical excitability, activation of endogenous pain control systems, induction of neural plasticity, and regulation of neurotransmitters.37

The efficacy of tDCS in alleviating KOA pain and symptoms has been demonstrated in several clinical trials, with the primary motor cortex (M1) being the most commonly targeted brain area for stimulation.1–3,28 Conventionally, intervention efficacy is assessed based on the average treatment response. However, differential responses to treatments, often referred to as the “heterogeneity of treatment effects” (HTEs), are inherent in intervention outcomes.22 Not all patients in treatment groups respond in the same way or to the same extent, with some showing no meaningful improvement—an outcome typically classified as “low responders” (low efficacy) compared to “high responders” (high efficacy). These findings raise concerns, particularly when certain individuals fail to benefit from active treatment, as the implications for clinical decision making could be substantial. In addition, some patients in the sham control groups may exhibit a “treatment response” despite receiving no active intervention, underscoring the difficulty in interpreting tDCS outcomes.

Several factors may contribute to the HTE of tDCS. Pain perception and response to treatment vary among individuals, making personalized care particularly relevant. According to de Rooij et al.,16 HTE in multidisciplinary treatment for chronic widespread pain was associated with gender and baseline levels of pain and anxiety. Similarly, Boonstra et al.10 reported that HTE in multidisciplinary pain rehabilitation was linked to baseline levels of pain, disability, and depression in patients with chronic musculoskeletal pain. Given the considerable phenotypic heterogeneity among individuals with KOA, a one-size-fits-all treatment approach is unlikely to be effective. Treatment planning must account for individual variability in contributing factors and symptomatic burden. Such insights may help inform personalized brain stimulation protocols, ensuring that interventions are tailored to each patient's specific characteristics, ultimately maximizing therapeutic benefits and minimizing ineffective treatments.48

To date, HTE in active and sham tDCS have rarely been empirically examined in tDCS for KOA. To the best of our knowledge, only 1 study has investigated the HTE of active tDCS and its associated factors in participants with KOA.38 However, that study considered a limited range of baseline variables, focusing primarily on sociodemographic factors such as age, race, gender, marital status, and education, along with a few clinical characteristics including body mass index (BMI) and duration of osteoarthritis. The authors reported that individuals with higher BMI and body weight were more likely to respond to the tDCS intervention.38 These preliminary findings underscore the need for additional empirical research to clarify the factors that contribute to differential treatment responses and to guide the refinement of personalized tDCS approaches for KOA.

This study aimed to identify the presence of HTE in tDCS treatment among participants with symptomatic KOA and to examine participant characteristics associated with this heterogeneity. First, we classified individuals and assessed HTE based on their longitudinal KOA pain and symptom patterns from baseline to postintervention follow-up; this approach accounted for differences in the time course of symptomatic change to determine differential responses to tDCS. Associations between the identified trajectory groups and baseline demographic, clinical, and quantitative sensory testing (QST) characteristics were then examined. The sham tDCS group was analyzed separately to assess potential HTE, providing insights into subgroup susceptibility to placebo effects and strategies to mitigate them.

2. Methods

2.1. Design

This secondary analysis utilized data from a double-blind, randomized, sham-controlled phase II pilot clinical trial with a parallel group design. The trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04016272). It involved a 3-week home-based tDCS program, monitored in real time via secure videoconferencing. Of the 129 individuals assessed for eligibility, 6 were not interested in the study or declined to participate. The remaining 123 were randomized to either active (n = 61) or sham (n = 62) tDCS. Three participants withdrew during the study, and 120 completed all sessions (Fig. 1). Randomization was conducted in the order of study enrollment using a pregenerated randomization list created with SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC) by a statistician uninvolved in the clinical aspects of the trial.

Participant flow diagram (n = 120). “Declined” meant that participants came to baseline visit (signed informed consent) but did not start any intervention. For this study, among 6 declined subjects, 4 subjects signed informed consent at the baseline but declined to study participation; 2 subjects did not show up at the baseline. tDCS, transcranial direct current stimulation.

2.2. Participants

Participants were recruited from Southeast Texas through advertisements at local institutions, including the University of Texas Health Science Center and University of Texas Physicians Orthopedics Clinic. Participants aged 50 to 85 were eligible if they (1) have unilateral or bilateral symptomatic KOA based on American College of Rheumatology Clinical criteria,4 (2) have had KOA pain in the past 3 months with an average of at least 30 on a 0 to 100 Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) for pain,44 (3) can speak and read English, and (4) have no plans to alter their pain medication regimen during the study. Specific exclusion criteria are (1) history of brain surgery, brain tumor, seizure, stroke, or intracranial metal implantation; (2) systemic rheumatic disorders; (3) alcohol/substance abuse; (4) diminished cognitive function (ie, Mini-Mental Status Exam score ≤23); (5) pregnancy or lactation; (6) prosthetic knee replacement or nonarthroscopic surgery to the affected knee; and (7) hospitalization within the preceding year for psychiatric illness.

2.3. Intervention: transcranial direct current stimulation

2.3.1. Active transcranial direct current stimulation

For chronic pain treatment, tDCS has been recognized to be successful when delivered with the anode electrode placed over the primary motor cortex (M1) and with the cathode electrode placed over the supraorbital region.5,25,33 For active stimulation, tDCS with a constant current intensity of 2 mA was applied for 20 minutes per session via the Soterix 1 × 1 tDCS mini-CT Stimulator device (Soterix Medical Inc, New York, NY) with headgear and 5 × 7-cm saline-soaked surface sponge electrodes. Participants can only administer a stimulation session via the device after being provided a single-use unlock code by the research staff once proper placement and contact are achieved. After the participant entered the unlock code, the screen on the device shows a timer that counts down the minutes until the end of the session. At 20 minutes, the device turns off automatically.

2.3.2. Sham transcranial direct current stimulation

The sham stimulation setup was identical to that of active tDCS to maintain blinding. The stimulator was only activated for 30 seconds at the beginning and end of each session, stimulating the initial and concluding sensory experience of active tDCS without delivering sustained current. This method effectively preserved blinding by ensuring participants could not distinguish between active and sham stimulation.29 Additional details on tDCS intervention are provided in Supplementary Material S1 (available at http://links.lww.com/PR9/A384).

2.4. Measurements: knee osteoarthritis pain and symptoms

Knee osteoarthritis pain and symptoms were assessed at baseline, after 5 intervention sessions (day 5), after an additional 5 sessions (day 10), and after the final set of 5 sessions (day 15). Follow-up evaluations occurred at 1 month (day 45), 2 months (day 75), and 3 months (day 105) postintervention.

2.4.1. Numeric Rating Scale

Participants rated their average knee pain over the past 24 hours using the NRS, a scale ranging from 0 (no pain) to 100 (worst pain imaginable).

2.4.2. The Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index

We measured key joint symptoms using the WOMAC,7 which consists of 3 subscales relating to pain during activities (range 0-20), stiffness during the day (range 0-8), and impairments of physical function (range 0-68), with higher scores indicating worse pain, stiffness, and impairments of physical function. Total scores range from 0 to 96, with higher scores indicating worse osteoarthritis pain–related symptoms. The WOMAC is a disease-specific, valid, and reliable instrument.

2.5. Measurements: baseline characteristics

2.5.1. Demographic and clinical characteristics

Demographic characteristics, including age, gender, BMI (kg/m2), race, education, and marital status, as well as clinical characteristics, such as the index knee (the most affected knee), Kellgren–Lawrence score, and the average duration of osteoarthritis (months), were collected. Pain catastrophizing was measured via the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS, ranging from 0 to 52), which consists of 13 items.43 It has excellent reliability with Cronbach alpha coefficient of ≥ 0.9 and is a well-validated measure.47

2.5.2. Quantitative sensory testing procedures

Quantitative sensory testing is a research and clinical method used to measure responses to sensory and painful stimulations, providing insight into altered pain sensitivity or modulation.45 A multimodal QST battery is designed to capture a range of pain stimuli and responses across different domains, including threshold, intensity, tolerance, and modulation.34 The assessment incorporates mechanical, thermal, and chemical stimuli.34 In this study, we included heat pain threshold, heat pain tolerance, pressure pain threshold (PPTh), punctate mechanical pain, temporal summation of pain (TSP), conditioned pain modulation (CPM), and cold pain. Temporal summation of pain, which involves the repeated administration of noxious stimuli at a fixed intensity, induces a gradual increase in pain sensation and is believed to reflect endogenous pain-excitatory mechanisms.6 Conditioned pain modulation assesses the function of the endogenous pain-inhibitory pathway, commonly referred to as the “pain inhibits pain” mechanism.6 More details on QST procedures are provided in Supplementary Material S2 (available at http://links.lww.com/PR9/A384).

2.6. Statistical analysis

Multitrajectory latent class growth analysis (Supplementary Material S3, available at http://links.lww.com/PR9/A384)30 was conducted using the SAS procedure Proc Traj to identify distinct latent groups with similar trajectories based on jointly modeled KOA pain and symptom parameters from baseline to the 3-month postintervention follow-up. The optimal number of multitrajectory groups was determined using several fit criteria, including the Akaike information criterion, Bayesian information criterion, homogeneity, latent class separation, interpretability, and group proportion, with the latter 2 being the most critical factors. Subsequently, the relationship between the identified trajectory groups and (1) demographic, (2) clinical, and (3) QST features was evaluated. Categorical variables were analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test, whereas continuous variables were assessed using the t test or Mann–Whitney U test, depending on data normality. We calculated both unadjusted and Bonferroni-adjusted significance levels to account for multiple group comparisons.9 Unadjusted estimates are also presented and discussed, given the exploratory nature of this study and its relatively small sample size. All analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

3. Results

The active tDCS group had a mean age of 65.32 ± 8.41 years, with 66.7% female participants, whereas the sham tDCS group had a mean age of 66.60 ± 8.43 years, with 70.0% female participants. White participants comprised 43.3% of the active tDCS group and 53.3% of the sham tDCS group. Additional details on the baseline characteristics of each treatment group are provided in Supplementary Table 1 (available at http://links.lww.com/PR9/A384). The best-fitting models for most longitudinal latent classes were characterized by linear slopes (Supplementary Table 2, available at http://links.lww.com/PR9/A384). The intervention was well tolerated by participants, with no significant side effects reported.

3.1. Active transcranial direct current stimulation

3.1.1. Trajectory model results

All fit criteria favored models with a greater number of classes (Table 1 and Fig. 2). However, we selected the 2-group multitrajectory model (Akaike information criterion = −5312.39, Bayesian information criterion = −5342.76) for several reasons. Although the three-class model demonstrated slightly better fit statistics than the 2-class model, it merely added a class reflecting the average levels of the other 2 without revealing substantively distinct trajectory patterns. Similarly, the four-class model included a marginally distinct class with a relatively small number of participants, making it less interpretable. Consequently, the 2-class model most effectively captured substantially differentiated and meaningful trajectory patterns. Group 1 comprised 46.9% of participants (n = 28), whereas group 2 included the remaining 53.1% (n = 32).

Group 2 exhibited higher baseline levels for KOA pain and symptoms compared to group 1. Both groups demonstrated an overall linear decrease in symptoms from baseline to the 3-month follow-up, with all measures generally declining over the 15-day period (+15 active tDCS sessions), despite slight increases during the monthly follow-ups. 3.1.2. Characteristics of the participants by trajectory group

Group 2 had a significantly higher BMI than group 1 (P = 0.040), with BMI values of 34.93 ± 10.00 kg/m2 and 30.09 ± 6.23 kg/m2, respectively (Table 2). In addition, a greater proportion of participants in group 1 had at least a 2-year college degree compared to group 2 (P = 0.013), with nearly 93% of group 1 achieving this level of education or higher. Group 2 exhibited significantly greater pain catastrophizing levels than group 1, with PCS scores of 22.16 ± 14.73 and 8.21 ± 8.45, respectively (P < 0.000). Regarding QST measures, significant baseline differences were observed between the groups in PPTh at the medial knee (P = 0.009) and trapezius (P = 0.002), punctate mechanical pain at the patella (P = 0.013) and hand (P = 0.016), CPM at 30 seconds (P = 0.008) and 60 seconds (P < 0.000), and cold pain intensity at 30 seconds (P = 0.003). Compared to group 1, group 2 demonstrated lower PPTh at both sites, higher punctate mechanical pain at both sites, lower CPM, and higher cold pain intensity. No significant differences were found between the groups (P > 0.05) in age, gender, race, marital status, Kellgren–Lawrence score, average duration of osteoarthritis, heat pain threshold, heat pain tolerance, or TSP. After applying the Bonferroni correction, only the group differences in PCS, PPTh at the trapezius, and CPM at 60 seconds remained statistically significant.

3.2. Sham transcranial direct current stimulation

3.2.1. Trajectory model results

The 2-group multitrajectory model was selected as the final model based on the distinctiveness of the groups and their clinical relevance (Table 1 and Fig. 3). The three-class model did not yield any meaningful additional insights, whereas the four-class model was more complex and less interpretable compared to the more parsimonious 2-class solution. Group 1 comprised 55.4% of participants (n = 33), whereas the remaining 44.6% were in group 2 (n = 27).

Group 2 exhibited higher baseline values for all KOA pain and symptom measures compared to group 1. Throughout the study, significant linear decreases were observed in WOMAC pain and stiffness in both groups (all P < 0.050), with symptom reductions occurring over the 15-day period (+15 sham tDCS sessions). In group 2, NRS pain showed an increasing trend; however, these changes were not statistically significant (β = 0.05, P = 0.213). Group 1 was designated as “low initial symptoms with a partial sham effect,” whereas group 2 was classified as “high initial symptoms with a partial sham effect.”

3.2.2. Characteristics of the participants by trajectory group

Group 2 had a higher BMI than group 1 (P = 0.048), with values of 34.93 ± 9.38 and 30.55 ± 6.83 kg/m2, respectively (Table 2). A greater proportion of participants in group 1 had completed at least a 2-year college degree compared to group 2 (P = 0.013), with nearly 82% of individuals in group 1 achieving this level of education or higher. Group 2 exhibited significantly higher pain catastrophizing than group 1 (P = 0.002), with PCS scores of 19.78 ± 12.77 in group 2 and 10.39 ± 9.78 in group 1. Regarding QST measures, a significant baseline difference was observed in PPTh at the medial knee (P = 0.021), with group 2 demonstrating lower PPTh than group 1 (group 1: 2.87 ± 1.14 kgf; group 2: 2.20 ± 1.12 kgf). However, none of these differences remained statistically significant after applying the Bonferroni correction.

4. Discussion

The study identified HTE of tDCS in participants with symptomatic KOA, as well as key participant characteristics associated with this heterogeneity. In the active tDCS group, 2 distinct response trajectories emerged: “low initial symptoms with significant improvement” and “high initial symptoms with minimal improvement.” The former group can be classified as “high responders,” whereas the latter represents “low responders.” Several baseline characteristics distinguished high responders from low responders, including BMI, education, pain catastrophizing, PPTh at both the medial knee and trapezius, punctate mechanical pain at both the patella and hand, cold pain, and CPM. Some of these findings should be interpreted with caution, as they did not meet the conservative Bonferroni-adjusted significance thresholds. Nonetheless, the results indicate that, although the relationships may appear straightforward at first, they involve considerable nuance. We discuss all relevant findings, including those that were significant before adjustment and those that remained significant after Bonferroni adjustment, particularly given the exploratory nature of our analysis.

In this study, low responders had a higher BMI than high responders. The precise mechanism underlying this relationship remains unclear; however, comorbid health conditions associated with KOA may exacerbate pain and functional limitations, potentially hindering an individual's ability to fully engage in the intervention. In addition, evidence suggests that obesity is linked to structural and functional brain alterations, as well as cognitive deficits, which may stem from altered neuroplasticity and could contribute to an impaired response to brain stimulation.42 Furthermore, biomechanical factors, such as increased joint loading, may influence the analgesic response to tDCS. Notably, the only study to date that has investigated HTE in active tDCS reported that participants with higher BMI and body weight were more likely to respond to the intervention.38 These findings contrast with ours. This discrepancy may partly reflect methodological differences. The previous study clustered participants based on change scores (eg, change in NRS or WOMAC), an approach that could identify subgroups showing significant or nonsignificant improvements regardless of baseline severity. Although the methods are not directly comparable, further research is clearly warranted.

Low responders had lower educational attainment than high responders. Previous tDCS studies have shown that education moderates its effects on memory performance and cognitive outcomes, with more years of education associated with greater benefits—underscoring the role of education in personalized stimulation protocols.8,23,24 Similarly, de Rooij et al.16 found that higher educational attainment contributed to greater improvements in pain interference among individuals undergoing multidisciplinary treatment for chronic widespread pain. The present findings may be attributed to differences in health literacy, which could influence participants' comprehension of tDCS-related materials and adherence to stimulation procedures. Even with remote clinical supervision, these factors may have affected the response to tDCS.

Low responders exhibit higher levels of pain catastrophizing at baseline compared to high responders. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that pain catastrophizing is one of the strongest predictors of pain treatment outcomes across various chronic pain conditions, including chronic neuropathic pain, musculoskeletal pain, low back pain, and fibromyalgia.18,19,36,40 According to the biopsychosocial model, pain perception and response are shaped by the complex interplay of physiological, psychological, and social factors.20 For individuals with high pain catastrophizing, addressing this factor should be a key consideration in future tDCS trials. One potential approach is to combine tDCS with other active interventions that specifically target pain catastrophizing, such as cognitive behavioral therapy.12

Low responders exhibited greater sensitivity to pressure, mechanical pain (at both the affected knee and remote body sites), and cold pain compared to high responders. Increased hypersensitivity at the affected site suggests peripheral sensitization, whereas hypersensitivity at an unaffected site indicates central sensitization.35 Although no prior tDCS studies have specifically examined this relationship, a systematic review and meta-analysis found that pretreatment QST profiles indicative of both peripheral and central sensitization are associated with reduced pain relief after total knee arthroplasty, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug treatment, and exercise-based therapies.39 Peripheral pain mechanisms in KOA primarily originate in the joint structures, particularly the synovium and subchondral bone.46 Neogi et al.31 reported that lower PPTh at the affected joint in KOA correlates with the degree of synovitis observed on functional magnetic resonance imaging. Although the present study did not assess structural joint changes via magnetic resonance imaging, joint inflammation and other pathological factors may have contributed to the limited reduction in KOA pain and symptoms among study participants. In case of central sensitization, various alterations in somatosensory input processing mechanisms and central nervous system neurons become overstimulated, and this heightened pain sensitization may contribute to a diminished response to pain treatment. Future tDCS trials could benefit from personalized approaches that account for pain sensitization, such as pain neuroscience education,26 to optimize treatment outcomes in individuals with KOA experiencing central sensitization.

In addition, low responders exhibited lower CPM than high responders, suggesting that a less efficient endogenous pain inhibitory system may contribute to an inadequate response to tDCS. The previously mentioned systematic review and meta-analysis identified CPM as the second most frequently reported QST parameter (40.0%) for pharmacological treatment after TSP (60.0%).39 However, no prior studies have specifically examined the relationship between CPM and responses to tDCS.

No HTE was observed in the sham tDCS group; both trajectory groups demonstrated a partial placebo response, characterized by significant reductions in WOMAC pain and stiffness, but differed in their initial symptom severity—categorized as low or high. The findings on the partial sham effect suggest that a sham protocol previously considered inactive may still exert minor neuromodulatory effects on certain outcomes.11 This aligns with prior studies,15,32 which suggest that such effects may arise from skin sensations initially induced in the sham condition (eg, ramp-up/down procedures) or cortical modulation by micro-ampere-scale currents. However, caution is warranted when interpreting these findings. Several alternative explanations remain possible, including the natural fluctuation of symptoms over time rather than genuine neuromodulatory activity. The modes sample size also raises concern, as 1 or 2 outliers could substantially alter statistical significance.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, these analyses were exploratory and not prespecified in the trial, necessitating cautious interpretation of the conclusions. Second, the modest sample size resulted in small subgroup sizes, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Third, in the active tDCS arm, participants were provisionally categorized as “high” or “low” responders for interpretability. However, this binary distinction may oversimplify a continuum of responses. Classifications could vary depending on follow-up density, baseline symptom severity, and distributional characteristics. Most importantly, the small class sizes increase the influence of outliers. Therefore, these labels should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger, prospective studies. Fourth, the observed baseline differences between trajectory groups were, at least partly, influenced by the limited set of baseline variables included in our analysis. Future studies should consider incorporating a broader range of participant characteristics for a more comprehensive characterization of trajectory group differences. In particular, incorporating multimodal neuroimaging and biomarker data may enhance the identification of high and low responders, thereby facilitating greater personalization in future research. These factors could provide early insights to guide the development of tailored, multimodal stimulation paradigms grounded in mechanistic understanding. Last, a single pain measurement obtained within 24 hours before assessment may not accurately represent the patient's overall pain experience. Pain thresholds can fluctuate daily, and even within the same day.41 Moreover, pain ratings at any given time may be influenced by various environmental and cognitive factors, including depression, anxiety, and distress.41 Consequently, our pain measurement approach may have yielded an imprecise representation of pain, constituting another limitation of our study.

5. Conclusion

Participants with symptomatic KOA exhibited varying responses to tDCS. The characteristics associated with HTE, which distinguished high responders from low responders in this study, provide preliminary insights that may influence the development of personalized stimulation protocols. Continued efforts in this area could ultimately improve the efficacy and applicability of neuromodulatory interventions for chronic pain and symptom management in KOA, potentially informing the development of more appropriate brain stimulation models. In addition, further research is needed to explore potential HTE in sham tDCS within sham-controlled tDCS trials.
