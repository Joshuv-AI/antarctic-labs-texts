Association between the diabetes risk reduction diet and polycystic ovary syndrome
Authors: Maryam Jamalvandi, Hawal Lateef Fateh, Touraj Ahmadi Jouybari, Jalal Moludi, Mehnoosh Samadi, Mehdi Moradinazar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPolycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is a common endocrine disorder in reproductive-age women. Dietary patterns may influence its development through inflammatory and metabolic pathways.ObjectiveTo evaluate the association between adherence to the Diabetes Risk Reduction Diet (DRRD) and the risk and severity of PCOS.MethodsThis case-control study included 232 women with newly diagnosed PCOS and 206 healthy controls aged 19–45 years. Dietary intake was assessed using a validated food frequency questionnaire (FFQ), and DRRD scores were calculated based on nine components. Anthropometric, biochemical, and psychological parameters were measured. Multivariate logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for PCOS across DRRD tertiles.ResultsParticipants in the highest DRRD tertile had significantly lower odds of PCOS PCOS (OR: 0.14, 95% CI: 0.08–0.25, p < 0.001; p-trend < 0.001) compared to the lowest tertile, even after adjusting for confounders. Higher DRRD adherence was associated with better metabolic and psychological profiles, though findings on specific biochemical parameters were mixed.ConclusionHigher adherence to the DRRD is associated with a reduced likelihood of PCOS. These findings support the role of anti-inflammatory dietary patterns in PCOS prevention.

Introduction

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is a multifaceted endocrine disorder characterized by ovulatory dysfunction [1], clinical or biochemical hyper androgenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology [2]. It affects approximately 8–13% of women of reproductive age worldwide and represents one of the leading causes of female infertility [3]. Beyond reproductive disturbances, PCOS is closely associated with a spectrum of metabolic abnormalities, including insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and obesity [4], which collectively increase the risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), cardiovascular diseases, and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [5]. Additionally, women with PCOS frequently experience psychological comorbidities such as anxiety, depression, and impaired quality of life [6].

The pathophysiology of PCOS is complex and multifactorial, involving genetic predisposition, hormonal imbalances, and environmental factors [3], among which diet plays a pivotal role [7]. Emerging evidence highlights the importance of dietary patterns rich in antioxidants, anti-inflammatory nutrients, and low glycemic index foods in ameliorating PCOS symptoms and improving metabolic outcomes [1]. Various dietary indices, such as the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII), Healthy Eating Index (HEI), and Dietary Diversity Score (DDS), have been employed to evaluate diet quality and its association with PCOS [8, 9]. However, most studies have focused on individual nutrients or isolated food groups, which may not fully capture the synergistic effects of overall dietary patterns [10, 11].

The Diabetes Risk Reduction Diet (DRRD) is a composite dietary score originally developed to predict and reduce the odds of T2DM by approximately 40% [11]. This dietary pattern emphasizes high intake of cereal fiber, nuts, coffee, whole fruits, and a favorable ratio of polyunsaturated to saturated fats, while minimizing consumption of trans fats, sugar-sweetened beverages, and red and processed meats [12]. Given the shared pathophysiological features of insulin resistance and chronic low-grade inflammation in both T2DM and PCOS [4, 11], adherence to the DRRD may offer protective effects against the development and progression of PCOS.

To date, limited research has examined the relationship between adherence to the DRRD and PCOS odds or severity. Therefore, the present case-control study aims to investigate the association between DRRD adherence and the risk of PCOS, as well as its impact on metabolic parameters and clinical features of the syndrome in an Iranian population.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This case-control study was conducted to investigate the association between adherence to the Diabetes Risk Reduction Diet (DRRD) and Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) among women aged 19 to 45 years. A total of 232 women with newly diagnosed PCOS and 206 age-matched healthy controls were recruited in Kermanshah, Iran. The sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7), assuming an odds ratio of 1.8, a power of 80%, and a two-sided alpha of 0.05, which yielded a minimum of 200 participants per group. The final enrollment exceeded this number to enhance the robustness of the analysis.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Kermanshah university of medical science [IR.KUMS.REC.1402.416], in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Anthropometric and clinical measurements

Anthropometric data including weight, height, and waist circumference were collected following standardized procedures. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). Blood pressure was measured using a calibrated sphygmomanometer after participants rested for at least 10 min. Weight was measured using a calibrated digital scale (Omron HN-286, Kyoto, Japan), and the same model was used for all participants to ensure consistency across measurements.

Dietary assessment and DRRD score calculation

Dietary intake was evaluated using a validated semi-quantitative Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) designed to capture habitual consumption over the preceding 12 months. The FFQ included 149 food items representative of the typical Iranian diet [13]. Participants reported frequency of consumption (daily, weekly, monthly, yearly) and portion sizes, which were converted to average daily intakes using standard serving sizes and nutrient composition tables specific to the Iranian population. The Diabetes Risk Reduction Diet (DRRD) adherence score was computed according to the method described by Rhee et al. [14]. Nine dietary components were included, comprising: cereal fiber, nuts, coffee, polyunsaturated to saturated fat ratio, whole fruits, trans fats, sugar-sweetened beverages and fruit juices, red and processed meats, and glycemic index. For each component, participants’ intakes were categorized into quintiles and assigned a score ranging from 1 (lowest adherence) to 5 (highest adherence), except for detrimental components (e.g., trans fats, sugar-sweetened beverages, red/processed meats), which were reverse scored. The total DRRD score was calculated by summing the scores across all components, resulting in a range from 9 to 45, with higher scores indicating better adherence to the DRRD pattern [13].

Physical activity assessment

Physical activity levels were quantified using the validated Persian version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) and expressed as metabolic equivalent task (MET)-minutes per week [15].

Biochemical and hormonal measurements

Serum glucose and lipid profile (total cholesterol, HDL-C, LDL-C, triglycerides) were analyzed using enzymatic colorimetric methods with commercial kits (Pars Azmoon, Tehran, Iran) on an autoanalyzer (Hitachi 902, Tokyo, Japan). Hormonal parameters including SHBG, DHEA-S, LH, FSH, and prolactin were measured by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) using a Roche Cobas e411 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). The intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation were < 3.5% and < 5.0% for total cholesterol, < 4.0% and < 6.0% for HDL-C, and < 3.8% and < 5.2% for triglycerides, respectively.

Psychological and quality of life evaluation

Psychological distress was assessed using the 21-item Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS-21). The Persian version of the DASS-21 has been previously validated and demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.79–0.91) among Iranian adults. PCOS-specific quality of life was evaluated with the Polycystic Ovary Syndrome Questionnaire (PCOSQ) [16]. Hirsutism severity was graded using the Ferriman-Gallwey scoring system.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using Stata version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to adjust for potential confounders including age, BMI, physical activity, total energy intake, educational level, medication use, and family history of PCOS. Logistic regression models were employed to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the presence of PCOS across tertiles of the DRRD score. Multivariable models adjusted for potential confounders including age, BMI, physical activity, waist circumference total energy intake. Differences in continuous variables between the case and control groups were assessed using independent samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate, while categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 232 women diagnosed with PCOS and 206 age-matched healthy controls were included in the final analysis. The overall mean ± SD age of participants was 30.1 ± 8.7 years, and the mean ± SD BMI was 28.6 ± 3.7 kg/m². The mean age of participants was comparable across groups, though a significantly greater proportion of cases fell into the < 25 years category (p < 0.001). No statistically significant differences were observed in anthropometric measures including height, weight, BMI, and waist circumference (WC) between the case and control groups. In terms of socio-demographic characteristics, education level and occupational status significantly differed between the two groups (p < 0.001). Physical activity levels, as assessed by the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), were significantly higher in the PCOS group compared to controls (p < 0.001). (Table 1)

Mental health and hirsutism assessment

Mental health parameters, including depression, anxiety, and stress (measured using the DASS-21), were significantly worse among women with PCOS (p < 0.001 for all domains). Severe or very severe psychological symptoms were markedly more prevalent in the PCOS group. Hirsutism, measured via the Ferriman-Gallwey scoring system, was documented only in the PCOS group, with 31.03% classified as having mild hirsutism and 1.72% with severe hirsutism. (Table 1)

Psychological and quality of life outcomes

Psychological distress, assessed using the DASS-21, was significantly more pronounced in the PCOS group compared to controls. A higher proportion of women with PCOS reported moderate to very severe levels of depression (51.72%), anxiety (58.19%), and stress (58.2%). In addition, PCOSQ scores revealed diminished quality of life among cases, especially in domains related to emotional health, menstrual irregularity, and infertility. (Table 1)

Dietary intake comparison between groups

Total energy intake was significantly higher in PCOS patients compared to controls (2796.17 ± 255 kcal/day vs. 2193.65 ± 284.82 kcal/day, p = 0.015). Additionally, macronutrient intakes, including protein (97.45 ± 84.74 g vs. 77.61 ± 17.39 g, p = 0.001), carbohydrate (377.21 ± 581.11 g vs. 296.8 ± 58.03 g, p = 0.024), and fat (103.82 ± 164.91 g vs. 77.33 ± 16.48 g, p = 0.011), were all significantly higher in the case group.

Intake of specific lipid components, such as cholesterol, saturated fatty acids (SFA), monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA), and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), was significantly elevated in the PCOS group (all p-values < 0.001). Fiber (41.58 ± 11.78 g vs. 35.68 ± 14.75 g, p = 0.008), sugar (130.95 ± 18.86 g vs. 99.9 ± 14.85 g, p = 0.01), and caffeine (70.25 ± 12.46 mg vs. 56.41 ± 12.46 mg, p = 0.005) intakes were also significantly higher among PCOS patients. No significant differences were observed in dietary glycemic load or trans-fat intake between groups. (Table 2)

Biochemical and hormonal profiles

Although total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), LDL cholesterol, and blood pressure values were higher in the PCOS group, only fasting blood sugar (FBS), TC, and systolic blood pressure (SBP) showed statistically significant differences (p < 0.05). In the hormonal assessment, luteinizing hormone (LH) levels were significantly higher among PCOS participants (p < 0.05), whereas FSH levels were lower but not statistically significant. Other endocrine markers such as prolactin and SHBG did not show significant group differences. (Table 2)

DRRD tertiles and clinical outcomes

Within the PCOS group, participants in the highest tertile of DRRD adherence demonstrated more favorable levels of LDL cholesterol, FSH, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, yet showed unexpectedly higher levels of FBS, WC, TC, and HDL cholesterol. These paradoxical findings may reflect behavioral adaptation post-diagnosis or residual confounding. Among the control group, women in the highest tertile of DRRD adherence had lower weight, WC, and FSH, along with slightly elevated LH and LDL cholesterol. (Table 3).

Dietary intake trends across DRRD tertiles

Among PCOS cases, higher DRRD adherence was associated with significantly higher intake of protein and caffeine (p < 0.05), whereas no significant trends were observed for other macronutrients. Among controls, participants in the highest DRRD tertile had significantly higher intake of protein, fiber, and caffeine, and lower intake of saturated fats, cholesterol, MUFA, and PUFA compared to the lowest tertile. Association between DRRD score and PCOS risk

These findings collectively highlight the multifaceted burden of PCOS, encompassing dietary, metabolic, endocrine, and psychological dimensions, and reinforce the potential protective role of a DRRD dietary pattern.

Discussion

This case-control study, conducted in Kermanshah, Iran, from December 2022 to August 2023, investigated the association between adherence to the Diabetes Risk Reduction Diet (DRRD) and hormonal, metabolic, anthropometric, and psychological outcomes in 232 women aged 19–45 years newly diagnosed with Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) compared to 206 age-matched healthy controls.

The primary finding of this study was a robust and statistically significant inverse association between higher adherence to the DRRD and the odds of PCOS (OR: 0.14, 95% CI: 0.08–0.25). This association remained significant after adjustment for key confounders, including age, BMI, physical activity, total energy intake, educational level, medication use, and family history of PCOS. Although several important confounders were adjusted for, residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be entirely excluded.

The biological plausibility of this protective association is supported by the pathophysiological overlap between PCOS and type 2 diabetes, particularly the central roles of insulin resistance and chronic low-grade inflammation [10, 17, 18]. The DRRD emphasizes dietary components with documented beneficial effects on these mechanisms, including high intakes of cereal fiber, whole fruits, nuts, coffee, and a favorable ratio of polyunsaturated to saturated fats, while discouraging consumption of red and processed meats, sugar-sweetened beverages, and trans fats [12]. Individually, these components have been linked to improved insulin sensitivity, attenuation of systemic inflammation, and oxidative stress reduction [13]—all crucial factors implicated in the development and progression of PCOS.

Our findings are congruent with prior literature that has examined similar dietary patterns in relation to PCOS. For instance, systematic reviews and meta-analyses have demonstrated that adherence to the Mediterranean diet—some pattern rich in fiber, unsaturated fats, and antioxidants—correlates with reduced inflammatory markers and improved hormonal profiles in women with PCOS [6]. Moreover, studies by Kazemi et al. reported that adherence to the alternate Mediterranean diet (aMED) and Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diets is associated with improved ovarian morphology, decreased insulin resistance, and reduced hyperandrogenism [19]. A meta-analysis by Shang et al. further confirmed that low-carbohydrate and Mediterranean diets significantly enhance fertility outcomes, including increased pregnancy and ovulation rates, while reducing hyperandrogenism, suggesting substantial clinical benefits of dietary pattern modification in PCOS management [20].

The DRRD’s protective effect likely operates via multiple intertwined mechanisms. The high dietary fiber content slows glucose absorption, stabilizes postprandial blood sugar, and promotes satiety, collectively enhancing insulin sensitivity, a pivotal factor in PCOS pathogenesis [21]. The diet’s emphasis on low-glycemic-index foods further mitigates insulin demand, reducing hyperinsulinemia-driven ovarian androgen production and ovulatory dysfunction [22]. Unsaturated fats from nuts and olive oil contribute anti-inflammatory and cardioprotective effects, counteracting chronic inflammation common in PCOS [23]. Coffee’s antioxidant properties and positive effects on glucose metabolism have been linked to a decreased risk of type 2 diabetes and may modulate hormonal balance [24]. Conversely, limiting red and processed meats, sugar-sweetened beverages, and trans fats reduces exposure to pro-inflammatory and metabolic stressors [25].

Given that insulin resistance is a central feature of PCOS pathophysiology, the DRRD’s protective effect may largely stem from its ability to improve insulin sensitivity and modulate glucose–insulin homeostasis [26]. Diets rich in cereal fiber, whole grains, and unsaturated fats enhance peripheral glucose uptake and reduce hepatic gluconeogenesis, thereby lowering circulating insulin levels [27]. Reduced hyperinsulinemia, in turn, attenuates ovarian androgen production and restores normal follicular maturation, which may explain part of the inverse association observed between DRRD adherence and PCOS odds in this study [28]. Additionally, the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant components of the DRRD can suppress cytokine-mediated impairment of insulin receptor signaling, further improving insulin action in metabolic tissues [29].

Importantly, the DRRD’s efficacy appears to arise from the synergistic effects of its components rather than isolated nutrients, consistent with contemporary paradigms in nutritional epidemiology that emphasize holistic dietary patterns over single nutrient effects [30]. This may explain the robust inverse association with PCOS risk despite the absence of clear dose-response trends for many individual dietary components across tertiles.

Despite this strong protective association, our study observed limited and somewhat inconsistent significant trends in anthropometric and biochemical parameters across DRRD adherence tertiles within the PCOS group. Women with PCOS in the highest tertile exhibited lower LDL cholesterol, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and blood pressure, yet paradoxically higher fasting blood sugar (FBS), total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, and waist circumference compared to those in the lowest tertile. The elevated FBS and waist circumference are unexpected given the DRRD’s purported metabolic benefits and may reflect residual confounding, reverse causality, or the complexity of PCOS phenotypes. For example, higher energy intake observed in the highest tertile might contribute to increased adiposity if not offset by physical activity, despite statistical adjustment for these factors. Alternatively, newly diagnosed women with more severe metabolic disturbances might have adopted healthier diets prior to diagnosis, potentially biasing associations. Among controls, higher DRRD adherence was associated with lower body weight and waist circumference but higher luteinizing hormone (LH) and LDL levels, suggesting differential effects of diet on metabolic and hormonal parameters in healthy versus PCOS populations.

These heterogeneous findings parallel previous studies reporting variable metabolic responses to dietary interventions in PCOS. For instance, Moran et al. documented lipid profile improvements with DASH diet adherence but inconsistent effects on glucose metabolism [31]. It is plausible that the DRRD’s primary influence pertains to PCOS risk reduction rather than marked metabolic improvements once the syndrome is established or that the sample size limited detection of subtle metabolic effects.

Notably, the absence of significant trends in gonadotropin levels (LH, FSH) across DRRD tertiles—except for lower FSH in the highest tertile among PCOS women—suggests a limited direct effect on hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis regulation. The modest reduction in FSH could reflect improved insulin sensitivity influencing hormonal feedback loops [6]. The lack of data on androgen levels, such as total testosterone or free androgen index, represents a significant limitation, as hyperandrogenism is a core diagnostic and pathogenic feature of PCOS. Prior dietary intervention studies have demonstrated reductions in androgen levels concomitant with clinical improvements [20]. Furthermore, psychological outcomes such as hirsutism severity, quality of life, and depression/anxiety scores were not reported, despite evidence supporting dietary improvements in PCOS-related psychological well-being [31]. Future investigations should incorporate validated instruments, such as the PCOS Quality of Life (PCOSQ) questionnaire, to comprehensively evaluate the psychological impact of dietary patterns.

The magnitude of risk reduction observed—an 86% lower odds of PCOS among women with high DRRD adherence—underscores the potential of diet-based strategies as non-pharmacological interventions in PCOS prevention and management. The DRRD’s reliance on widely accessible foods like whole grains, fruits, nuts, and healthy fats makes it an attractive, culturally adaptable dietary model for resource-limited settings such as Kermanshah. Integrating DRRD principles into clinical nutritional counseling may empower healthcare providers to offer evidence-based dietary recommendations that address metabolic, hormonal, and reproductive abnormalities in PCOS, potentially reducing long-term complications including type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease [32]. Public health initiatives promoting these dietary patterns could enhance population-level PCOS risk reduction.

Although the observed odds ratio indicates a strong protective association between higher DRRD adherence and lower odds of PCOS, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the inherent limitations of the case-control design. The temporal sequence between dietary intake and disease onset cannot be established, and the possibility of recall bias or residual confounding cannot be excluded. Therefore, prospective cohort studies and randomized controlled trials are warranted to confirm the directionality and causality of this relationship.

Nevertheless, several study limitations merit acknowledgment. The case-control design limits causal inference and cannot establish temporality between DRRD adherence and PCOS onset. Self-reported dietary intake is susceptible to recall bias and measurement error, which may attenuate observed associations. The study’s single-center design in Kermanshah may limit generalizability to other ethnicities or populations with distinct genetic and environmental factors. The absence of androgen profiles and psychological outcome data restricts a holistic understanding of the DRRD’s effects on PCOS symptomatology. Finally, limited significant trends in anthropometric and biochemical parameters suggest that larger, prospective studies are necessary to delineate the DRRD’s impact on metabolic phenotypes.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal cohort studies and randomized controlled trials to validate causal relationships and investigate mechanisms by which the DRRD influences insulin signaling, inflammatory pathways, and gut microbiota in PCOS. The incorporation of objective dietary biomarkers could mitigate self-report biases. Moreover, assessing comprehensive hormonal panels, inflammatory markers, and psychological well-being will elucidate the multifaceted benefits of the DRRD. Multi-center studies encompassing diverse populations will enhance external validity and inform culturally tailored dietary guidelines.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study provides compelling evidence that higher adherence to the Diabetes Risk Reduction Diet is associated with substantially lower odds of PCOS in Iranian women. The DRRD’s alignment with established beneficial dietary patterns such as the Mediterranean and DASH diets supports its potential integration into clinical and public health frameworks aimed at PCOS prevention and management. Although mixed findings in metabolic and anthropometric outcomes underscore the complexity of PCOS pathophysiology and dietary influences, the strong inverse association observed highlights the importance of holistic dietary interventions. Further rigorous studies are warranted to confirm these results, elucidate underlying biological mechanisms, and assess the DRRD’s broader effects on PCOS-related clinical and psychological outcomes.
