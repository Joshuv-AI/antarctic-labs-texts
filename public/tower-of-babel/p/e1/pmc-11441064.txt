Development of a novel nomogram for predicting prognosis of North Chinese with autoimmune cerebellar ataxia
Authors: Chunxia Zhao, Cao Jiang, Decai Tian, Yajun Yao, Tian Song, Huabing Wang, Wangshu Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe aim of this study was to develop a prognostic nomogram which could predict the prognosis of north Chinese patients with autoimmune cerebellar ataxia (ACA) after immunotherapy.MethodsPatients with an initial diagnosis of ACA who accepted first-line immunotherapy at our hospital from March 2018 to May 2023 were retrospectively reviewed. Modified Rankin Scale (mRS) was used to evaluate neurological outcomes. According to the mRS scores after immunotherapy, patients with ACA were divided into good prognosis group (mRS 0–2) and poor prognosis group (mRS 3–6). The nomogram for poor prognosis of ACA patients were built based on logistic regression analysis. The validation of the prognostic model was evaluated by concordance index (C-index), calibration curves, and decision curve analyses (DCAs).ResultsA total of 86 patients with ACA who received immunotherapy at our hospital were included in this study. They were randomly divided into a training cohort (n = 60) and a validation cohort (n = 26) at a ratio of 7:3. Multivariate analyses revealed that that prognostic variables significantly related to the poor prognosis of ACA were age, elevated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) albumin (ALB) and abnormal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The nomogram was constructed based on above 3 factors. The C-index of the nomogram was 0.935 (95% CI: 0.884-0.991) in the training set and 0.933 (95% CI: 0.763-0.994) in the validation set. The calibration plots for the nomogram showed that predictions of risk of poor prognosis were almost consistent with actual observations. The DCAs showed great clinical usefulness of the nomograms.ConclusionWe successfully developed a nomogram to predict poor prognosis for ACA patients using risk factors of age, elevated CSF-ALB and abnormal MRI.Keywords: Autoimmune cerebellar ataxia, nomogram, prognosis, modified Rankin Scale, prediction

Introduction

Autoimmune cerebellitis (AC) is also known as autoimmune cerebellar ataxia (ACA), which refers to the cerebellum dysfunction caused by various autoimmunity disorders, including several subtypes such as paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration, primary autoimmune cerebellar ataxia, gluten ataxia, and anti-GAD associated cerebellar ataxia [1,2]. An etiological study has showed that ACA accounted for more than 50% of sporadic cerebellar ataxia [3]. It is usually with rapid onset and progression, and can be divided into paraneoplastic and non-paraneoplastic disorders [4].

The ACA is mainly characterized by the cerebellum with or without brainstem syndrome including dysarthria, disorders of gait and balance, and limb ataxia, but some patients can even present extracerebellar manifestations such as seizures, sleep disturbance, peripheral neuropathy, and encephalopathy [1]. Most of the patients with ACA can recover well after the treatment of immunotherapy including corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIG) and mycophenolate [5]. However, there is still a small group of patients who do not respond to such treatment. Thus, it is important to pursue early evaluation of prognosis for patients with ACA following immunotherapy. Patients with poor prognosis should receive early and personalized intervention [6].

Previously, several factors have been demonstrated to affect the response to immunotherapy in ACA. For example, Liu et al. [7] conducted a prospective cohort study which included patients with primary ACA. They found that onset age, presence of peripheral neuropathy and elevated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) protein concentration were closely associated with poor prognosis of primary ACA [7]. An increasing number of antibodies, including Purkinje cell cytoplasmic antibody type 1 (PCA-1), glutamic acid decarboxylase 65-kDa isoform (GAD65), and antineuronal nuclear antibody type 1 (ANNA-1), have been confirmed to be related to ACA. A study conducted at Mayo Clinic reported that neurologic improvements following immunotherapy were significantly more common among ACA patients with plasma membrane protein (PMP) antibody positivity [4]. Similarly, a study conducted in Japanese patients with ACA found that patients with anti-gliadin antibody-positive, anti-GAD antibody-positive patients and anti-thyroid antibody-positive responded well to immunotherapy [8].

Although risk factors associated with prognosis in ACA have been evaluated in several studies, no straightforward prognosis model has been clearly characterized. The nomogram is a simple-to-implement visualization tool for implementation of individual predictions. In patients with autoimmune encephalitis, constructing a nomogram to predict positive immunotherapeutic response has been demonstrated to be beneficial to autoimmune encephalitis treatment [6]. Thus, we conducted this study to develop and validate a prognostic nomogram which could predict the prognosis of patients with ACA after immunotherapy.

Methods

Patients

This single-center retrospective study was approved by the ethics committee of Beijing Tiantan Hospital in accordance with Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective and de-identified nature of this study, the ethics committee waived the obligation to obtain informed consent.

From March 2018 to May 2023, patients with an initial diagnosis of ACA who accepted first-line immunotherapy (including corticosteroids, IVIG, or plasma exchange) during the inpatient stay in our hospital were included in this study. The ACA subtypes included in this study were paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration, primary autoimmune cerebellar ataxia, gluten ataxia, and anti-GAD associated cerebellar ataxia. The diagnostic criteria for gluten ataxia were based on the presence of characteristic cerebellar symptoms, positive serum antigliadin antibodies, and a positive response to a gluten-free diet. The diagnostic criteria for paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration were based on established clinical guidelines [9], including specific antibody testing, clinical presentation, and the exclusion criteria were as followed: (1) patients with another autoimmune disease; (2) patients with incomplete clinical data; (3) patients with an age less than 18 years old.

The enrolled patients were randomly separated into the training and validation sets in a 7:3 ratio. The training set was applied to construct the prognostic nomogram and the validation set was used to validate the prognostic nomogram.

Data collection

We used modified Rankin Scale (mRS) to evaluate neurological outcomes. The mRS is an ordinal 7-point scale ranging from no residual symptoms (0), to severely disabled (5) or death (6) [10]. The mRS scores were evaluated at the last follow-up, which was conducted at a standardized time point of six months after the completion of first-line immunotherapy. According to the mRS scores after immunotherapy, patients with ACA were divided into 2 groups: good prognosis group (functional independence, mRS 0–2) and poor prognosis group (functional dependency or death, mRS 3–6). The selected factors for analysis included age, gender, diagnosis of tumor, presence of cognitive disorder, presence of dysarthria, behavioral changes, seizures, CSF albumin (ALB), CSF-IgG, CSF white blood cell (WBC) count, CSF tumor necrosis factor (TNF) concentration, CSF IL-6 concentration, CSF IL-8 concentration, CSF IL-1 concentration, positivity of tumor markers, C-reactive protein (CRP) in serum, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings, and neuronal antibodies.

The cutoff levels of each laboratory parameter defining elevation were as follows: CSF-ALB > 0.15 mg/mL, CSF-IgG > 0.019 mg/mL, CSF WBC count > 20 cell/μL, CSF TNF concentration > 8.1 pg/mL, CSF IL-6 concentration > 3.4 pg/mL, CSF IL-8 concentration > 62 pg/mL, CSF IL-1 concentration > 5 pg/mL, CRP > 2.87 mg/L, and ESR > 20 mm/h.

Nomogram construction and validation

The training set was used for nomogram building based on logistic regression analysis [11]. The univariate analysis was conducted to assess independent predictors of poor prognosis in ACA. Then, the selected variables obtained from the univariate analysis were analyzed in the multivariate logistic regression model. The nomogram was constructed by the independent predictors extracted from the multivariable logistic regression. The nomogram could help estimate the total score and then predict the probability of poor prognosis. The higher scores indicated the poorer prognosis.

The validation set was used to validate the prognostic nomogram. The calibration curve was used to evaluate the predictive accuracy of the nomogram. Model discrimination was evaluated by receiver operating characteristics curve (ROC). The total clinical benefit of the nomogram was evaluated using decision curve analyses (DCAs).

Anti-neuronal antibody testing

Anti-neuronal antibody testing was performed using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The PCA-1 antibody was detected by assessing serum samples for the presence of anti-PCA-1 antibodies. The sensitivity and specificity of the ELISA test for PCA-1 were validated by comparing with established reference standards.

Malignancy screening

Malignancy screening included a combination of routine clinical examinations, imaging studies, and laboratory tests. Patients with ACA underwent a comprehensive evaluation to identify potential underlying malignancies. This included: ① Clinical Examinations: Detailed medical history and physical examination to identify symptoms suggestive of malignancy; ② Imaging Studies: Chest X-ray, abdominal ultrasound, or CT scans as appropriate based on the patient’s clinical presentation and risk factors; ③ Laboratory Tests: Blood tests including complete blood count (CBC), liver function tests, and tumor markers relevant to the types of malignancies being screened for; ④ Biopsy: If imaging or laboratory tests indicated potential malignancy, biopsy was performed to confirm the presence of cancer.

MRI definition and abnormalities

MRI scans were assessed for abnormalities by an experienced radiologist. The definition of ‘abnormal’ MRI in this study included the following criteria: White Matter Hyperintensities: Scattered white matter hyperintensities were considered abnormal if they were extensive or unusual in distribution, and not typical for the patient’s age or clinical presentation. Small, isolated white matter hyperintensities in elderly patients were not classified as abnormal unless accompanied by other findings. Other Abnormal Findings: This included the presence of: Atrophy: ① Significant cerebellar or cortical atrophy beyond what is expected for the patient’s age; ② Enhancement: Abnormal enhancement of cerebellar or cortical areas after contrast administration, indicating possible inflammation or malignancy; ③ Lesions: Presence of focal lesions, especially in areas commonly associated with ACA, such as the cerebellum; ④ Signal Abnormalities: Any unusual signal intensities or patterns that deviated from normal MRI findings for the cerebellum or related structures.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using R software version 3.6.2 and the SPSS 23.0. Between the training and validation sets, categorical factors were assessed using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. The nomogram was derived from the results of multivariate logistic analysis using R software. A p value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

During the study period, a total of 86 patients with ACA who received immunotherapy at our hospital were included in this study. These patients encompassed various subtypes of ACA, including paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration (n = 20), primary autoimmune cerebellar ataxia (n = 30), gluten ataxia (n = 18), and anti-GAD associated cerebellar ataxia (n = 18). Of the 86 patients, 43 (50.00%) were males. 58.14% of patients were older than 45 years. Of the 86 patients, 43 (50.00%) were males. 58.14% of pa­tients had an age more than 45 years. The number of patients who received corticosteroids, IVIG, and plasma exchange were 45, 25, and 16, respectively. The enrolled patients were randomly divided into a training cohort (n = 60) and a validation cohort (n = 26) at a ratio of 7:3 (Figure 1). The mRS scores for the training and validation sets were as follows: Training Set (n = 60): mRS 0–2: 36 patients (60%), mRS 3–6: 24 patients (40%); Validation Set (n = 26): mRS 0–2: 15 patients (57.7%), mRS 3–6: 11 patients (42.3%), A p-value of 0.78 indicates no significant difference in the mRS score distribution between the two sets; In addition, the training set exhibited 2.85 ± 3.33, while the validation set had a median mRS score of 2.75 ± 3.68. To assess the statistical significance of the differences in mRS scores between the two sets, we performed a Wilcoxon test (for non-parametric data)/t-test (for parametric data), and the results indicated no statistically significant difference (p = 0.747). In this study, a total of 18 patients were diagnosed with gluten ataxia. All of these patients underwent a gluten-free diet following diagnosis. Post dietary intervention, 12 patients (66.7%) showed improvement in their mRS scores, with 9 patients (50%) achieving mRS scores of 0-2, indicating functional independence, while 3 patients (16.7%) showed no change, indicating ongoing functional dependency or death. Three patients (16.7%) did not show significant improvement following dietary intervention and may require further immunotherapy or alternative treatment strategies. The regimen of first-line immunotherapy and other subsequent immunotherapy treatments were comparable between the training set and validation set. Additionally, the composition of ACA subtypes was comparable between the training set and validation set. The baseline characteristics of the enrolled patients are shown in Table 1. The chi-square test results showed that the baseline characteristics in the training and validation cohorts were balanced (all p > 0.05). The proportion of patients with poor prognosis in the training and validation datasets was 35.00% and 42.31%, respectively.

The study analyzed the time intervals from the onset of symptoms to the confirmation of diagnosis and from diagnosis to the start of treatment. The results showed that older patients had significantly longer delays in both intervals compared to younger patients. Specifically, the median time to diagnosis was 15 weeks for patients over 65 years old compared to 8 weeks for those 45 years old or younger (p-value = 0.03, using Mann-Whitney U test). The median time from diagnosis to treatment initiation was 5 weeks for older patients compared to 2 weeks for younger patients (p-value = 0.04, using Mann-Whitney U test).

The univariate analysis using the training set showed that age (p = 0.001), elevated CSF-ALB (p < 0.001), positivity of tumor markers (p = 0.029), elevated ESR (p = 0.018), and abnormal MRI (p = 0.001) were associated with the prognosis of ACA. Then, the above variables obtained from the univariate analysis were analyzed in the multivariate logistic regression model. In the multivariate analysis, the factors significantly related to the prognosis of ACA were: age [≥ 45 years vs. < 45 years, odds ratio (OR) = 41.281, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 3.356-507.780, p = 0.004], elevated CSF-ALB (Yes vs. no, OR = 14.192, 95% CI = 2.332-86.370, p = 0.004), and abnormal MRI (Yes vs. no, OR = 10.526, 95% CI = 1.030-107.620, p = 0.047). The results of the univariate and multivariate analyses are shown in Table 2. After variable selection, only three factors were identified in the final multivariable prediction model, including age, elevated CSF-ALB and abnormal MRI. A nomogram for predicting prognosis of ACA patients that integrated the above 3 independent prognostic factors is shown in Figure 2. The nomogram score of age ranged from 0 to 100.0, making the largest contribution to the prognosis of ACA.

Discussion

In the present study, we attempted to construct a nomogram for ACA patients, with the aim to provide a relatively accurate estimate of the prognosis following immunotherapy. As expected, we successfully developed a nomogram to predict poor prognosis for ACA patients using risk factors of age, elevated CSF-ALB and abnormal MRI. We found that our nomogram presented both favorable discrimination and adequate calibration.

ACA is an important and potentially treatable cause of sporadic cerebellar syndrome [11]. Immunotherapy is the first line of therapy for ACA [4]. Many patients with ACA can recover well after the treatment of immunotherapy. However, there is still a group of patients who do not respond to such treatment, presenting a poor prognosis. Several risk factors may affect the prognosis of ACA patients after immunotherapy. In the present study, we firstly found that ACA patients with an age more than 45 years old were more likely to have poor prognosis than younger patients. It has been reported that the median onset age of ACA in Chinese patients ranged from 42.9 years to 47 years [7,11]. Thus, we chose the cut off value of 45 years to define the different age groups. In a previous study, Sun et al. found that age was an important factor to predict prognosis for patients with autoimmune encephalitis including ACA [6]. Consistent with our findings, they also found that elderly patients were more likely to have poor prognosis [6]. It has been reported that elderly patients were more likely to combine with other systemic diseases, presenting poor improvement in symptoms of autoimmune encephalitis after a course of treatment [12,13]. Review of clinical records indicated that older patients were more likely to experience delays in seeking medical attention due to a higher likelihood of attributing symptoms to age-related changes or comorbidities. Additionally, diagnostic and treatment processes were often prolonged due to the complexity of their presentations and the need for comprehensive evaluation to differentiate between age-related conditions and ACA. Thus, older patients often respond less well to immunotherapy.

ACA is an inflammatory disease of the central nervous system. Large cohort studies showed that CSF findings like CSF pleocytosis and increased protein could support the diagnosis of ACA and trigger early treatment [14]. In a previous study, Liu et al. reported that elevated CSF protein concentration was an important relapsing factor for primary ACA patients [7]. Similarly, our results indicated that elevated CSF-ALB was linked with worse prognosis of ACA. Elevated protein concentration in CSF could reflect the disruption of the blood-CSF barrier [15]. It has been demonstrated that autoimmune encephalitis patients with normal blood-CSF barrier had good prognosis compared to those with damaged blood-CSF barrier [16]. Due to this phenomenon, we observed the relationship between elevated CSF-ALB and poor prognosis of ACA.

A Case Series from China reported that brain MRI showed atrophy or abnormal signal intensity in 44.3% of ACA patients [11]. Wu et al. [17] found that abnormal brain MRI was a key risk factor for poor prognosis of autoimmune encephalitis. The results from the study conducted by Neo et al. [18] indicated that autoimmune encephalitis patients with abnormal MRI were more likely to have poor outcomes compared to those with normal imaging. Consistent with above studies, abnormal MRI was regarded as an important prognostic factor for ACA patients and included in our nomogram.

In several previously reported studies, elevated CSF-WBC and positivity of neuronal antibodies have been shown to portend of poor prognosis. For example, Broadley et al. [15] found that evaluated CSF-WBC was correlated with worse outcomes in patients with autoimmune encephalitis. Similar results were obtained from studies conducted by Balu et al. [19] and Montmollin et al. [20]. Nanri et al. [8] found that patients with ataxia-associated antibody-positive cerebellar ataxia responded well to immunotherapy. Jones et al. [4] reported that ACA patients harboring autoantibodies directly against PCA-1 had a poor prognosis than those with GAD65 autoimmunity. In our study, however, we did not find such a relationship. The rejection of these 2 factors in the nomogram may be the result of the small sample size or confounding by other predictors. In addition, in this cohort, 38 patients tested positive for PCA-1 antibodies, but only 5 were diagnosed with malignancy. This significant discrepancy can be attributed to the fact that PCA-1 antibody positivity is associated with paraneoplastic cerebellar ataxia but does not necessarily indicate the presence of a malignancy in all cases. PCA-1 antibodies can be present in patients with autoimmune cerebellar ataxia without an underlying tumor, reflecting a broader spectrum of PCA-1 associated disorders. The low rate of malignancy in our cohort suggests that while PCA-1 antibodies are a marker for ACA, their presence alone does not always correlate with the presence of a tumor. This finding underscores the importance of distinguishing between paraneoplastic and primary autoimmune forms of cerebellar ataxia and highlights the need for careful evaluation of each patient for malignancy.

Based on the risk factors of age, elevated CSF-ALB and abnormal MRI. we successfully built a nomogram for ACA prognosis after immunotherapy. Using the validation cohort, we validated that our nomogram had high accuracies and provided great positive net clinical benefits for predicting poor prognosis of ACA.

The present study has several limitations. Firstly, the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) was used to evaluate neurological outcomes in this study. While the mRS is a widely accepted tool for assessing functional outcomes in various neurological disorders, its use in cerebellar ataxia may have limitations: ① The mRS primarily evaluates overall functionality and independence, which may not accurately reflect the specific neurological impairments seen in cerebellar ataxia; ② mRS does not specifically measure ataxia-related symptoms such as coordination, balance, and gait, which are crucial for assessing the severity and progression of cerebellar ataxia; ③ While mRS provides a general assessment of disability, it may not capture the nuances of cerebellar ataxia, potentially leading to an underestimation of the disease’s impact on patients’ lives; ④ Disease-specific scales like the Scale for the Assessment and Rating of Ataxia (SARA) or the International Cooperative Ataxia Rating Scale (ICARS) are more tailored to the symptoms and challenges faced by patients with cerebellar ataxia, providing a more comprehensive and relevant evaluation. Future studies should consider using disease-specific scales such as SARA or ICARS for a more precise assessment of neurological outcomes in patients with cerebellar ataxia. These scales can offer a detailed evaluation of ataxia-related symptoms and provide more clinically relevant information for managing and treating this condition. Secondly, this study was a retrospective analysis with inherent biases. All patients were from a single center. This may cause selection bias. All clinical data were collected from medical records, which may cause information bias. Finally, the sample size of this study was small. Due to the limited sample size, several factors that were extracted from univariate analysis were not included in the final nomogram.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a nomogram to predict poor prognosis for ACA patients after immunotherapy using risk factors of age, elevated CSF-ALB and abnormal MRI. The nomogram we constructed exhibited good predictive performance, which may assist clinicians in their decision-making process.
