A rare entity in salivary gland pathology: A case report of an epidermoid cyst in the parotid gland

Introduction

Cystic lesions in the parotid gland are relatively uncommon, comprising approximately 5 % of all salivary gland tumours [1,2]. Epidermoid cysts are benign developmental cysts derived from the ectoderm. They are typically found in the skin of the scalp, face, and neck, with less frequent occurrences on the trunk. These cysts constitute between 1.6 and 6.9 % of all cysts in the head and neck region [3] and can appear in various locations, including the orbital area, buccal and submental regions, and the junction of the hard and soft palate [4]. Their occurrence in the parotid gland is exceedingly rare, with only a few case reports documented [5].

The aim of this case report is to present a case of an epidermoid cyst of the parotid gland that was treated surgically in our ENT department, and to highlight its diagnostic characteristics and perioperative management.

The work was written in line with the SCARE criteria [6].

Patient and observation

We present the case of a 12-year-old boy, who presented with a swelling in the lateral cervical region. The swelling was insidious in onset and slowly progressive with a duration of one year. There was no history of trauma or surgery in the designated area, and no other co-morbidities. The physical examination revealed the presence of a 3 cm cystic mass in the right parotid gland, situate sub-lobulary and moveable towards the skin. The underlying skin was observed to normal. No fistula was observed in the external auditory canal, and the patient showed no signs of facial or trigeminal dysfunctions. Furthermore, there was no evidence of palpable adenopathy.

An MRI was conducted and revealed a round, well-defined multilobulated cystic formation in the retro-parotid region, extending over 4 cm. The mass was not observed to infiltrate the surrounding tissue. The specimen demonstrated a high signal intensity on T2- weighted imaging, and a low signal intensity on T1- weighted imaging. No enhancement was observed and no signs of malignancy were evident. The patient was managed surgically via an external approach with a Redon incision, which revealed a bilobed cystic mass in the superficial lobe of the parotid gland. Subsequently, a superficial parotidectomy was performed following the identification of the facial nerve (Fig. 2). The postoperative course was uneventful, in particular, no facial palsy was detected. The histopathological examination of the excised mass yielded results consistent with those of an epidermoid cyst.

Discussion

Epidermal cyst of the parotid gland is a rare, benign cystic lesion that typically affects individuals in their youth to middle-aged adults. Nevertheless, as illustrated in our case report of a 12-year-old boy, these cysts can also manifest in paediatric patients, emphasizing the necessity of considering this diagnosis even in younger age groups. There is no gender predilection [2]. They represent 1.6–6.9 % of all cysts in the head and neck region [3]. The precise histogenesis of salivary epidermal cysts remains uncertain. However, it is postulated that they may have originated from developmental branchial pouch analogue epithelium, which can occur in salivary glands [7]. Alternatively, they may have resulted from obstruction in the salivary duct within the substance of the gland leading to an epithelial lining cavity filled with viscous, semisolid epithelial degradation product [3].

Epidermoid cysts are classified by the World Health Organization as part of the group of non-neoplastic cysts.

Such lesion may manifest as isolated or multifocal lesions, as in case of Gardner's syndrome [4].

The clinical and radiological characteristics of this condition can be ambiguous. The diagnosis of cystic lesions is challenging due to the difficulty in determining whether the process is benign or malignant. Indeed, the differential diagnosis of an epidermoid cyst in terms of malignancy includes a number of conditions, including parotid gland tumours, which can be either benign or malignant. The most common malignant tumours are mucoepidermoid carcinoma and adenoid cystic carcinoma. These tumours are susceptible to cystic degeneration, which presents a diagnosis challenge as they may manifest with characteristics analogous to epidermoid cysts [4].

Epidermoid cysts are clinically defined as painless swellings of the parotid region, that manifest insidiously and gradually increase in size without attachment to the overlying skin or involvement of facial nerve [2] as seen in our patient. The absence of the facial nerve involvement and the slow, insidious onset of the swelling in the 12-year-old boy highlight the classic presentation of these lesions. A similar clinical presentation may be observed in other swellings, including parotid duct cysts, cystic lesions of the parotid gland, and intraglandular vascular (haemangiomas) and non-vascular benign tumours. These lesions contribute to the diagnostic challenge and necessitate diverse surgical strategies for management, including parotidectomy if indicated, which can result in significant morbidity. It is therefore essential to plan meticulously in order to manage these lesions appropriately [2,4,8].

Malignant lesions are often suspected when there is a rapid enlargement associated with lymphadenopathy or facial nerve paresis. This distinction is of significant importance in determining the most appropriate treatment modality. The preoperative diagnosis of the lesion is of significant importance. Some authors post that Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology (FNAC) is the most reliable and least expensive method for making a preoperative diagnosis [2,7]. Nevertheless, other authors have proposed that FNAC in cases of parotid malignancies with cystic degeneration may potentially result in tumour seeding, thereby contributing to tumour recurrence [4,9]. In the present case, a FNAC was not performed.

Preoperative imaging studies, including ultrasound and MRI, are fundamental for the assessment of cyst size and anatomical location, as well as for the differential diagnosis [10].

On ultrasound, epidermal cysts typically present as well-defined, relatively homogeneous, hypoechoic lesions with no evidence of vascularity [10].

MRI, which is more sensitive than computed tomography (CT), plays an essential role in the evaluation of cystic lesions in the parotid gland. In this case, the MRI findings were consistent with those of a non-neoplastic cyst. The lesion exhibited well-defined margins, with a low signal intensity on T1-weighted images and a markedly high signal intensity on T2-weighted images, like cerebrospinal fluid, and demonstrated restricted diffusion. These imaging characteristics are typical of non-neoplastic cysts, such as epidermoid cysts, which are lined by squamous epithelium that is typically too thin to be visualized on MRI. Furthermore, the absence of enhancement and calcification provided additional support for the diagnosis [11].

Our case is consistent with previous reports that describe cystic lesions with well-defined margins and no solid components as indicative of non-neoplastic cysts on MRI. It is, however, important to note that benign tumours with extensive cystic degeneration can present similarly, but often include mural nodules within the cystic lesions. In this case, the absence of such features. In conjunction with the typical MRI characteristics, served to reinforce the diagnosis of an epidermoid cyst rather than a cystic degeneration of a benign tumour [5].

A histopathological examination of the cyst is necessary for confirmation of the diagnosis. Histologically, epidermal cyst has a stratified squamous epithelial lining and is typically filled with cheesy material or keratin [2,5,7]. This finding is great of significance as it enables the differentiation of epidermoid cysts from other cystic lesions and solid tumours of the parotid gland, which can occasionally manifest with similar clinical and radiological features. Histopathology provided confirmation of the diagnosis of an epidermoid cyst in our patient, revealing a stratified squamous epithelial lining without evidence of malignancy.

This entity mimics malignant tumours of the parotid, the differential diagnoses for such lesions are numerous, but are not limited to malignancy, abscess and non-acinar tumours include that arise from vascular, lymphatic, or neural structures, such as hemangiomas, lymphomas, and neurofibromas. Warthin's tumour is another parotid lesion with a similar clinical presentation [4].

Surgical excision remains the definitive treatment for epidermoid cysts. The decision as to what type of surgical approach, incision and extent is based on five factors and remain without consensus: histology, size, location of tumour, as well as expertise of surgeon. The management of epidermoid cysts of the parotid gland typically favors superficial parotidectomy over cystectomy, as it reduces the risk of recurrence and protects the facial nerve. While cystectomy may be considered for superficial cysts, it carries a higher risk of incomplete excision and recurrence. In contrast, superficial parotidectomy ensures the removal of both the cyst and a margin of surrounding tissue, minimizing recurrence and safeguarding nearby structures [12].

In our case, a superficial parotidectomy was performed, ensuring complete removal of the cyst while preserving the facial nerve. This approach aligns with most cases reported in the literature [2,4,5], emphasizing the importance of careful surgical planning to avoid complications such as facial paralysis or cyst rupture. Care should be taken not to rupture the cyst which can lead to postoperative inflammation and recurrences. Additionally, it is crucial to preserve vital structures during the surgery [5].

Conclusion

Epidermoid cysts of the parotid gland are an exceptionally rare entity, with only a few documented cases in the medical literature. This study illustrates the difficulties inherent in diagnosing such lesions given their potential similarity to other cystic and malignant conditions. Precise imaging techniques, particularly MRI, are great of importance in distinguishing benign cysts from malignancies by revealing characteristic features. The successful surgical management of the epidermoid cyst demonstrates the value of tailored surgical approaches to avoid complications and ensure effective treatment. Overall, this case contributes valuable insights into the management of rare parotid gland lesions and reinforces the necessity for careful diagnostic and surgical planning in cases of unusual parotid cystic formations.
