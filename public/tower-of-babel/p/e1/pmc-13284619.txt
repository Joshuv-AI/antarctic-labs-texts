Risk stratification and anal cancer screening in immunocompetent women with genital HPV: Value of multicentric HSIL and performance of HPV‐based screening
Authors: Isabel Matas, Aureli Torné, Adela Saco, Cristina Martí, Ada Cardiel, Natalia Rakislova, Lorena Marimon, Ariel Glickman, Ingrid Victoria, Silvia Alós, Jesús Lopez‐Diaz, Pere Fusté, Jaume Ordi, Marta del Pino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe incidence of anal squamous cell carcinoma (ASCC) is rising, and women with genital human papillomavirus (HPV) infection or HPV‐associated high‐grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL) are at high risk of developing ASCC. We conducted a cross‐sectional study including 354 immunocompetent women referred for anal evaluation because of HPV infection or HSIL of the lower genital tract between 2019 and 2024. Participants were categorized according to the genital site affected by HSIL (uterine cervix, vagina, vulva/perineum/perianal region, or multicentric disease), and a control group of 99 immunocompetent women with genital HPV infection and/or low‐grade SIL was included. All patients underwent anal high‐risk HPV testing, liquid‐based cytology, and high‐resolution anoscopy when indicated. Overall, high‐risk HPV infection and HSIL were identified in the anal canal in 62.5% and 5.6% of women, respectively. Women with multicentric disease showed a higher prevalence of anal HSIL compared with the control group (6/29, 20.7%, vs. 2/99, 2.0%; p = .031), and the other genital HSIL groups (11/182, 6.0% for cervical; 0/13, 0%, for vaginal; 1/31, 3.2% for vulvar HSIL; p = .003). Genital HSIL, particularly multicentric disease, was a strong marker of anal involvement. No HSIL/AIN occurred among women with a negative anal high‐risk HPV test. Anal high‐risk HPV testing alone showed optimal sensitivity (100%) and negative predictive value (100%) for the diagnosis of HSIL/AIN, whereas cytology alone had lower sensitivity (68.4%). Combining both tests did not improve accuracy and resulted in excessive referrals. High‐risk HPV testing alone appears to be the most efficient approach for anal screening in women with genital HSIL.Keywords: anal intraepithelial neoplasia, human papillomavirus, immunocompetent women

INTRODUCTION

Anal squamous cell carcinoma (ASCC) is an emerging public health concern, and epidemiological studies have identified a steadily increasing incidence of this tumor over the recent decades. 1 Remarkably, approximately two‐thirds of all patients with ASCC are immunocompetent women, which challenges the prevailing notion that this malignancy predominantly affects immunocompromised individuals and men who have sex with men.2, 3

ASCC has many analogies with cancer of the uterine cervix: the vast majority are associated with high‐risk human papillomavirus (HPV), and nearly all are preceded by high‐grade squamous intraepithelial lesion/anal intraepithelial neoplasia (HSIL/AIN), which shows identical morphological features to HSIL/cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (HSIL/CIN). Due to its strong association with HPV, the rising incidence of ASCC has been mainly attributed to an increasing exposure to the virus. 4 Interestingly, recent studies have reported a significantly higher prevalence of anal HPV infection in women with HPV infection or HSIL involving other sites of the anogenital tract such as the perineum, the vulva, the vagina or the uterine cervix, compared to the general population. 5 In a recent study from our group, 42.6% of women undergoing treatment for HSIL/CIN, including women with multicentric disease, tested positive for anal HPV, and 14.7% developed HSIL/AIN. 6 A systematic review reported a 23‐fold increased risk of HSIL/AIN among women treated for HPV‐associated lesions in the genital tract. 7 Similarly, a recent meta‐analysis found that women with genital HSIL have a substantially higher incidence of ASCC compared with the general population. 8 This high prevalence is thought to be related to the anatomical proximity between the anal canal and the genital region.9, 10 Interestingly, it has been suggested that the risk of HSIL/AIN may differ depending on the site affected by HSIL, that is, uterine cervix, vagina, vulva, or perineum, 7 and an inverse association has been reported between the anatomical distance of the genital area involved by the HPV‐related lesion to the anus and the risk of anal lesions. 11

Despite this accumulating evidence, until very recently, no guidelines for ASCC screening in immunocompetent women at high risk were available, mainly due to the limited knowledge of the natural history of the disease in this population.12, 13, 14, 15 In 2024, the International Anal Neoplasia Society (IANS) issued the new consensus guidelines for ASCC screening which, for the first time, included recommendations for immunocompetent women with HPV‐associated genital lesions. Notably, these guidelines proposed different screening approaches depending on the anatomical site of the HSIL, but it was acknowledged that the supporting evidence was limited and emphasized the need for research in the field. 16 The document of the IANS also suggests that HSIL/AIN detection should be feasible using screening methodologies analogous to those employed for cervical cancer, such as anal cytology and HPV testing. However, the performance of these tools in screening immunocompetent women for ASCC remains insufficiently validated.17, 18 These knowledge gaps underscore the urgent need for robust data on risk stratification and screening accuracy in this recently recognized high‐risk population.

In our institution the screening of ASCC started in 2019 and all women with abnormal cervical cancer screening test result or suspicious of HPV‐associated genital lesion are referred to the Lower Anogenital Tract Disease Unit. The present study includes all immunocompetent women who underwent ASCC screening in our Unit from January 2019 to July 2024. The aim of the present study is to evaluate the risk of HSIL/AIN among women with HSIL involving the lower genital tract (uterine cervix, vagina, vulva, or perineum) and to assess the diagnostic performance of the currently used screening tools in this population in order to determine the best strategies for screening of ASCC in immunocompetent women.

METHODS

Selection criteria

From January 2019 to July 2024, we offered ASCC screening to all women who met the following criteria: (1) immunocompetent women older than 35 years with histologically confirmed multicentric HSIL (i.e., genital HSIL involving more than one anatomical site of the genital tract: cervix, vagina, vulva, perineum or perineum/perianal region), and (2) immunocompetent women older than 40 years with histologically confirmed unifocal HSIL in any anatomical site of the genital tract, that is, cervix (HSIL/CIN), vagina (HSIL/VaIN), vulva (HSIL/VIN) or perineum/perianal region (HSIL/PaIN). Exclusion criteria were: (a) pregnancy, (b) history of anal cancer or treatment for HSIL/AIN within the three previous years and, (c) women with known diagnosis of HIV infection, solid organ transplantation, hematologic malignancy, autoimmune diseases requiring systemic immunosuppressive treatment or long‐term systemic corticosteroid use.

In the period July 2023–July 2024, we enrolled as control group immunocompetent women with genital HPV infection and/or low‐grade SIL (LSIL), including genital warts.

Initial evaluation of the lower genital tract

Our Lower Anogenital Tract Disease Unit receives women referred for abnormal cervical cytology, positive HPV testing result, or clinically suspected of HPV‐associated lesions of the lower genital tract. In the first visit a sample of the uterine cervix is obtained for HPV testing and liquid‐based cytology (LBC) in all women referred to the Unit. In addition, all women undergo a thorough evaluation of the entire lower genital tract including the cervix, vagina, vulva, perineum, and perianal area. In the presence of any visible lesion in the vulva, perineum, or perianal area, a 3‐ or 4‐mm punch biopsy is performed.

Following the national guidelines, all women with cervical infection by HPV16/18 in a single sample, women with persistent cervical infection by high‐risk HPV types other than 16/18 (i.e., positive results in at least two tests separated by more than 1 year) regardless of cytology, or abnormal cervical cytology (ASC‐US, with positive HPV test results, or higher) are referred for colposcopy evaluation. A thorough colposcopy examination of the cervix and the vagina is performed, and biopsies are obtained in case of suspected lesions.

Treatment of genital lesions

Women with confirmed HSIL/CIN underwent large‐loop excision of the transformation zone (LLETZ) under colposcopy guidance as previously described. 19 Women with vaginal HSIL (HSIL/VaIN) underwent colposcopy‐guided thermocoagulation or laser vaporization, whereas those with HSIL/VIN or with perineal/perianal lesions were treated either with excision or with topical Imiquimod or ablative therapy, after exclusion of invasion. 20

ASCC screening, high‐resolution anoscopy, and anal HSIL diagnosis and management

The anal sample was collected using a cytobrush. The brush was inserted approximately 3.0–4.0 cm into the anal canal and rotated 360° clockwise for 1 minute, followed by a 360° counterclockwise rotation for an additional minute. The material was used for HPV testing and LBC. Women with an unsatisfactory anal LBC result and a positive HPV test were referred directly for HRA. On the other hand, women with an unsatisfactory anal LBC result and a negative HPV test, anal cytology was repeated, and those with persistently unsatisfactory cytology were referred for HRA.

High‐resolution anoscopy (HRA) was indicated for women with positive anal HPV (any genotype) or abnormal anal cytology. HRA was conducted by a gynecologist with experience in the procedure. Prior to HRA, a digital anorectal examination was systematically performed to rule out any palpable abnormalities suggestive of anal cancer. 16 HRA was carried out using an Olympus Evis Exera II CV‐180 anoscope (Olympus, Barcelona, Spain). Acetic acid at 5% was applied for 2 min to enhance the visualization of the epithelium, followed by iodine‐based solution to delineate abnormal areas. When suspicious lesions were detected, targeted biopsies were obtained using biopsy forceps. In the absence of visible lesions, the HRA was considered negative for disease.

All women with anal biopsy results showing HSIL/AIN were referred for topical treatment with 80% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) or imiquimod. 6

HPV testing and liquid‐based cytology

Cervical and anal samples were preserved in PreservCyt solution (Hologic, Marlborough, MA, USA) for subsequent HPV testing and LBC. HPV detection and genotyping were performed using the Cobas HPV test (Roche Molecular Diagnostics, GmbH, Mannheim, Germany), a real‐time polymerase chain‐reaction (PCR) assay that identifies 14 high‐risk HPV types and provides individual genotyping for HPV16 and HPV18. This technique has been specifically validated for cervical HPV. 21

Cytology slides were prepared using the ThinPrep T2000 slide processor (Hologic, Inc., Marlborough, MA, USA) and stained following the Papanicolaou method. Cytological evaluation was carried out according to the Bethesda system. 22

Histological processing and diagnosis

All biopsy samples and surgical specimens were submitted for histological assessment. Specimens were fixed in 10% formalin and routinely embedded in paraffin. Three‐micron sections were stained with hematoxylin & eosin (H&E). p16 immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed in all cases using the Roche platform with CINtec Histology Kit (clone E6H4; Roche‐Mtm Laboratories, Heidelberg, Germany). Only cases showing continuous “block” staining of the basal and parabasal layers were considered positive for p16.23, 24 In all vulvar, perineal/perianal, and anal biopsies, p53 IHC staining (clone DO‐7; Dako, Carpinteria, CA) was also performed. p53 IHC was evaluated following the pattern‐based criteria. 25

All biopsies were initially evaluated using pure morphological criteria on H&E stained sections. p16 immunohistochemistry was mainly applied to distinguish HSIL from its mimickers (atrophy, reparative changes), as recommended by the WHO. 23 The diagnosis of HSIL required the unequivocal HSIL morphology and block‐positive p16 staining. The diagnosis of LSIL was also based on morphological criteria. p16 staining was only used as an adjunct in the diagnosis of LSIL versus HSIL in morphologically equivocal cases, but did not result in systematic upgrading of LSIL lesions. p53 IHC was used to exclude HPV‐independent lesions mimicking HSIL, which show mutation type p53 IHC pattern in addition to p16 negativity in contrast with the p53 wild type pattern of staining typical of HSIL. 26 Histological samples were classified as negative for dysplasia, low grade SIL (LSIL) or HSIL. 22 The anatomical area of the lesion and the grades of abnormality were specified following the World Health Organization (WHO) terminology. 27

Patient categorization

Patients were categorized into different groups according to the genital area involved by the HPV‐associated lesion: (1) Control group, comprising women with genital warts, and/or cervical HPV infection without associated histological genital HSIL; (2) HSIL/CIN, which included women with unicentric histologically confirmed HSIL involving the uterine cervix; (3) HSIL/VaIN, which included women with unicentric histologically confirmed HSIL involving the vagina; (4) HSIL/VIN, comprising women with unicentric histologically confirmed HSIL involving the vulva, perineum, or perianal region; and (5) multicentric HSIL, which included women with confirmed HSIL involving ≥2 anogenital sites (cervix, vagina, vulva, and/or perineum/perianal region).

For analytical purposes, HPV genotypes detected in cervical and anal samples were classified into three mutually exclusive, hierarchical categories: (1) HPV16 (alone or with any other type, including HPV18); (2) HPV18 (alone or with non‐16/18 types; co‐infections including HPV16 were classified as HPV16); (3) HPV types other than 16/18.

Atypical squamous cells of uncertain significance (ASC‐US) in cervical cytology were grouped with LSIL for analysis. Conversely, anal ASC‐US was considered as a separate category due to its relatively high prevalence among women with negative anal HPV testing. Cytological results of ASC‐cannot exclude a high‐grade lesion (ASC‐H) were grouped with HSIL given their significant risk of underlying histological HSIL.28, 29

Data analysis

The STATA/BE 18.0 software (College Station, TX, USA) was used for data analyses. Epidemiological and clinical variables including age, smoking habit, and HPV vaccination status (defined as having received at least one HPV vaccine dose prior to the referral visit) were recorded.

Categorical variables are presented as absolute numbers and percentages and compared using the Chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate. Continuous variables are reported as means and standard deviations (SD) and compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA).

Univariate logistic regression was used to estimate the risk of HSIL/AIN according to the genital area affected by HSIL, the genital and anal HPV genotype, and the anal cytological result. Risk estimates are reported as odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Multivariate logistic regression models were fitted to assess the association between the above‐mentioned variables while adjusting for potential confounders. The genital HPV infection group served as the reference group in all regression analyses. A p value of .05 was considered statistically significant.

For diagnostic performance, we calculated the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) for the detection of HSIL/AIN as well as the proportion of women referred to HRA under each screening strategy.

RESULTS

A total of 354 women were included in the study. The study group comprised 255 women and the control group 99 women. In the study group, 182 (51.4%) women had HSIL/CIN, 13 (3.7%) HSIL/VaIN, 31 (8.8%) HSIL/VIN, and 29 (8.2%) had multicentric HSIL. Sixteen out of the 29 women (55.2%) with multicentric HSIL, had involvement of the vulva, perineum or perianal region. Table 1 summarizes the epidemiological characteristics (age, smoking habit, vaccination status), HPV testing and genotyping results, and the cervical LBC findings across the groups. Overall, 313/354 (88.4%) women had a positive HPV testing result in the uterine cervix. HPV16 was the most prevalent genotype identified in the uterine cervix (158/354, 44.6%). Cervical HPV16 infection was significantly more frequent in women with multicentric HSIL and HSIL/CIN compared with controls (p = .002 and p < .001, respectively).

Anal HPV testing and anal cytological results

Table 2 summarizes the anal HPV testing and genotyping and the anal LBC results in the different groups of study. Overall, only 2/354 (0.6%) anal HPV tests showed invalid results. Among the 352 women with a valid test, 220 (62.5%) tested positive for anal HPV. The percentage of women with anal HPV infection was over 60% in all groups, with no significant differences between them (p = .578). When genotype was considered, the distribution of anal HPV types differed significantly among groups, with a higher prevalence of HPV16 in women with vulvar and multicentric disease (p = .031). Anal HPV16 prevalence ranged from 19.2% (19/99) in controls to 51.7% (15/29) in multicentric HSIL (p = .001). Genotype‐specific concordance between cervical and anal HPV was high. Of women with concurrent cervical and anal HPV infection, 75/82 (91.5%) of those with cervical HPV16 also had anal HPV16; 11/14 (78.6%) of those with cervical HPV18 had anal HPV18 and 63/80 (78.8%) of the women with HPV other than 16/18 in the cervical sample also had HPV other than 16/18 in the anal sample.

Among the 352 women with valid anal HPV test, 31 (8.8%) showed unsatisfactory anal LBC, with no significant differences between the groups (p = .220). Of the 322 women with satisfactory anal cytology, 125 (38.8%) had an abnormal result: 73 (58.4%) had ASC‐US, 36 (28.8%) LSIL, and 16 (12.8%) ASC‐H/HSIL. Anal HSIL cytology was significantly more frequent in women with HSIL/CIN (12/182, 6.6%; p = .002) and in those with multicentric HSIL (2/29, 6.9%; p = .039) compared with controls (0/99, 0%). No significant differences were observed between controls and the HSIL/VIN (p = .093) or HSIL/VaIN (p = 1) groups. The proportions of LSIL and ASC‐US cytology results did not differ significantly among groups.

High resolution anoscopy and histological results

Two hundred and twenty‐seven out of the 354 (64.1%) women included in the study were referred to HRA. 213 (93.8%) accepted and underwent the procedure, and 14 (6.2%) declined. From them, seven were positive for HPV16 in the anus, two for HPV18, and five for HPV other than 16/18.

HSIL/AIN was diagnosed in 20/213 (9.4%) of the patients who underwent HRA (20/354; 5.6% of the overall group). All HSIL/AIN lesions showed clear morphological criteria, block type p16 positivity, and wild type p53 IHC staining. No case of mutation type p53 staining was identified. Interestingly, histologically confirmed HSIL/AIN varied markedly between the different groups (control: 2/99, 2.0%; HSIL/CIN: 11/182, 6.0%; HSIL/VaIN: 0/13, 0%; HSIL/VIN: 1/31, 3.2%; multicentric HSIL: 6/29, 20.7%; p = .003). Women with multicentric HSIL had a significantly higher prevalence of HSIL/AIN compared with all other groups (p = .003).

Within the multicentric HSIL group, 13 women (44.8%) showed vulvar involvement. When considering all women with vulvar HSIL, either isolated (HSIL/VIN group) or as part of a multicentric disease (n = 44), anal HSIL/AIN was detected in 5/44 (11.4%). This prevalence was significantly higher than in the control group (p = .029). Among women with multicentric HSIL, those with concurrent vulvar involvement showed a higher prevalence of HSIL/AIN (4/13, 30.8%) than those without vulvar lesions (2/16, 12.5%), although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = .360). Among the 158 women with cervical HPV16 infection, 10 (6.3%) were diagnosed with anal HSIL/AIN.

Table 3 shows the histological result of the anal biopsy stratified by the results of HPV testing and LBC in the anal samples. Among women with positive anal HPV testing results, the prevalence of HSIL/AIN increased with the severity of the LBC result: 6/116 (5.2%) with negative cytology, 5/42 (11.9%) with ASC‐US, 4/30 (13.3%) with LSIL, and 4/15 (26.7%) with HSIL (p < .001). None of the women with a negative HPV testing had SIL/AIN (one woman had an ASC‐H result in the LBC, but the biopsy result was negative).

Accuracy of the anal screening test

Table 5 shows the diagnostic accuracy of different anal screening strategies including HPV testing alone, LBC result alone and combinations between HPV testing and LBC results for the diagnosis of HSIL/AIN. Among the strategies assessed, anal HPV testing alone provided the most appropriate balance, with 100% sensitivity, 41.0% specificity and referred to HRA 45.8% of the women. The strategy used in this study (HPV‐positive testing OR LBC result ≥ASC‐US) had also 100% sensitivity, but reduced specificity (29.6%) and referred to HRA 64.1% of the women. The strategy of positive anal HPV testing AND LBC result ≥ ASC‐US reduced referrals to 19.8% but at the expense of a significant number of missed HSIL/AIN. When missed cases were explicitly considered, the strategy based on positive anal HPV testing combined with abnormal cytology (≥ASC‐US) failed to detect 7 of 20 histologically confirmed HSIL/AIN cases (35.0%). In contrast, anal HPV testing alone and the strategy based on HPV OR cytology did not miss any HSIL/AIN. Notably, abnormal cytology in HPV‐negative women did not result in the detection of additional HSIL/AIN cases, despite increasing referrals to HRA.

DISCUSSION

The present study shows that more than half of the immunocompetent women with genital HPV and/or genital HSIL also have anal HPV infection, and nearly 10% of the patients with anal HPV infection harbor HSIL/AIN. These findings are consistent with a previous study from our group restricted to women with HSIL/CIN. 6 Notably, in the present series, the risk of anal lesion showed marked differences depending on the lower genital tract site affected by the HPV, with significantly higher risk in women with multicentric HPV infection.

Until recently, few studies had quantified the risk of HSIL/AIN in immunocompetent women with HPV infection and/or HPV‐associated lesions of the lower genital tract. A recent review reported that these women, particularly those with vulvar HSIL or HPV‐associated cancer, have a significantly higher risk of ASCC than the general population. 8 The review also found increased ASCC risk among women with HSIL/CIN or persistent genital HPV16 infection.

In our study, anal HPV infection was highly prevalent across all groups of women with HPV infection involving the lower genital tract (ranging from 59.9% to 75.9%). These findings are consistent with the few published studies evaluating immunocompetent women with HPV infection or HPV‐associated disease involving the lower genital tract,30, 31, 32 and reinforce the need of targeted ASCC screening this population. Recently, the IANS has incorporated immunocompetent women with genital HPV‐associated lesions among the risk groups for ASCC in their guidelines, and has recommended risk‐based targeted screening strategies. 16 Following these recommendations, a few European scientific societies have also adopted similar guidelines in recent years.33, 34

Importantly, the prevalence of histologically confirmed HSIL/AIN varied significantly by the location in the genital tract of the HPV‐associated lesion: it was substantially higher in women with multicentric HSIL compared not only with controls but also with all other study groups. In our cohort, this subgroup showed a disproportionately high prevalence of HSIL/AIN (20.7%), clearly exceeding that observed in women with unifocal lesions (0%–6.2%). Prior studies in immunocompetent women have suggested an increased risk of HSIL/AIN among patients with vulvar HSIL. 8 In our series, women with lesions confined to the vulva did not show an increased risk of anal lesions. Notably, nearly half of the women with multicentric disease had concurrent involvement of the vulva (13/29; 44.8%). When considering all women with vulvar HSIL, either isolated or as part of a multicentric disease, the prevalence of HSIL/AIN remained high (11.4%) and significantly higher than in controls. To our knowledge, none of the previous reports have explicitly analyzed multicentric HSIL as a separate category. Moreover, without a thorough examination of the lower genital tract, many women with concurrent vaginal and/or cervical HSIL would have been classified as HSIL/VIN. Thus, our data refine the risk stratification beyond single‐site HSIL and identify multicentric disease as a marker of high risk of anal involvement.

In our study, different age cut‐offs were selected for women with multicentric disease (>35 years) and unifocal disease (>40 years) based on the known differences in HPV persistence and the cumulative risk of HPV‐related disease according to disease extent. 6 These thresholds align with the risk‐stratification principles outlined in the recently published IANS Consensus Guidelines. 16 Our findings support this risk‐stratified approach, demonstrating a lower HSIL/AIN prevalence in our unifocal HSIL/CIN group (6.0%) but a high prevalence (20.7%) among women with multicentric disease.

Anal HPV16 infection has been considered an independent risk factor for HSIL/AIN and ASCC.8, 16 In the present study, anal HPV16 infection was strongly associated with HSIL/AIN (adjusted OR 20.3; 95% CI 2.0–590.4; p = .040), supporting the role of HPV16 as the most oncogenic genotype in the anal canal and highlighting its relevance as a potential biomarker for surveillance. However, although the prevalence of HSIL/AIN was higher in women with anal HPV16 infection (11.7%) than in those with anal HPV18 or with HPV other than 16/18 (5.9% and 7.0%, respectively), this difference did not reach statistical significance. It should be noted that a non‐negligible proportion of women with anal HPV18 and HPV other than 16/18 had HSIL/AIN.

Remarkably, the concordance between the HPV type identified in the cervical and in the anal samples was high. Specifically, 91.5% of the women with cervical HPV16 also had anal HPV16, 78.6% of the women with cervical HPV18 showed anal HPV18, and 78.8% of the women with HPV other than 16/18 in the cervical sample had also anal HPV other than 16/18. These findings are in keeping with previous reports describing strong genotype concordance between genital and anal regions. 34 The high level of genotype correlation suggests that HPV autoinoculation across adjacent anogenital sites may play a relevant role in HPV transmission to the anal canal.34, 35 The 6.3% prevalence of anal HSIL/AIN observed among women with cervical HPV16 infection further supports the clinical relevance of HPV16‐specific risk stratification and is consistent with current IANS recommendations. 16

This study included women who received HPV vaccination after the diagnosis of HPV infection and/or HPV‐related disease. Thus, vaccination represents an adjuvant rather than a primary prophylactic vaccination. The role of adjuvant HPV vaccination in women treated for HSIL remains controversial. While several studies suggest a reduced risk of recurrent disease at the treated cervical site, the available evidence is heterogeneous and largely limited to cervical outcomes. 36 Moreover, the potential impact of adjuvant vaccination on HPV‐related lesions at other anatomical sites, such as the anal canal, possibly mediated by reduced autoinoculation or cross‐site viral persistence, has not been specifically evaluated.

Our data support the central role of anal HPV testing in immunocompetent women for risk stratification of HSIL/AIN. In the present series, no HSIL/AIN cases were detected among women testing negative for anal HPV, underscoring the high negative predictive value of anal HPV testing. Notably, although 28% of HPV‐negative women had abnormal anal cytology, none of these women had histologically proven HSIL/AIN. These findings suggest that the consideration of cytological results in anal HPV‐negative women increases HRA referrals without increasing the yield of HSIL/AIN, thereby reducing screening efficiency. Accordingly, the results of our study support a screening strategy based on HPV testing alone. Interestingly, among women with positive anal HPV tests, the prevalence of HSIL/AIN increased with cytological severity. However, in the present series, women with low‐grade abnormalities on the anal LBC had an HSIL/AIN prevalence >10%, a level that supports referral to HRA for any abnormal cytology result in HPV‐positive women.16, 33 The proportion of unsatisfactory samples observed in our study (8.8%) suggests that additional training in anal cytology sampling may be beneficial. However, this rate is within the range reported in studies of immunocompetent women (approximately 3%–16%), and therefore is likely to have only minimally contributed to a lower sensitivity of cytology observed in our study. 37 Nonetheless, the risk stratification afforded by cytology can be leveraged to prioritize the order of HRA referrals, with ASC‐H/HSIL scheduled ahead of ASC‐US/LSIL when resources are constrained.

This study has several strengths. First, it is one of the few to evaluate anal HPV infection and associated lesions in immunocompetent women with genital HPV infection, and, to our knowledge, the first to categorize patients with multicentric HSIL as a distinct group of risk. Another strength is that all women were prospectively recruited, and very well characterized through cervical and anal HPV testing/genotyping and LBC. Finally, an additional strength of the present study is the inclusion of a control group which, despite its heterogeneity, provides a pragmatic comparator, as routine ASCC screening is not justified in women without genital HPV infection. This study also has some limitations that should be noted.

The limited number of HSIL/AIN cases reduces statistical power for subgroup analyses, particularly within the multicentric HSIL category. The heterogeneity of the control group may introduce variability, although it mirrors real‐world clinical populations. Finally, as a single‐cohort study with cross‐sectional assessment, generalizability and causal inference are constrained.

In conclusion, this study shows that immunocompetent women with HPV infection of the lower genital tract, particularly those with multicentric HSIL, are at increased risk of anal HPV infection and HSIL/AIN. While most prior work has focused on high‐risk populations (e.g., men who have sex with men or people living with HIV), our findings provide evidence supporting expanding screening to include immunocompetent women with HSIL of the lower genital tract. Anal HPV testing effectively identifies women at risk of HSIL/AIN and supports the adoption of an HPV‐based anal screening strategy in this population. Larger, prospective studies with longitudinal follow‐up are warranted to quantify progression risk and develop cost‐effective, risk‐adapted screening algorithms for this growing at‐risk group.
