Effects of dustbathing environment on gut microbiota and expression of intestinal barrier and immune-related genes of adult laying hens housed individually in modified traditional cage
Authors: Hanlin Yu, Ye Wang, Jiaqi Zhang, Xiaoxu Wang, Rui Wang, Jun Bao, Runxiang Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractLitters, the composition of sand and peat rich in microbiota, are essential to trigger the dustbathing behavior of chickens. To investigate the effects of a dustbathing environment (DE) on the intestinal health, gut microbiota, and immune responses of laying hens, a total of 72 healthy Hy-Line Brown laying hens at 69 wk of age (WOA) were housed individually in modified traditional cages and randomly divided into 2 groups: one group had free access to litters (CT), while the other one was restricted from litters (CC). The experiment lasted for 42 d. At the end of the experiment, the intestinal histomorphology and immune status of laying hens were determined, and the 16S rRNA sequencing method was used to assess the composition of the intestinal microbial community of birds. Intestinal histomorphology changed, including villus height and villus-to-crypt ratio significantly increased in the CT group (P < 0.01). DE reshaped the microbial community and increased the microbial richness with the higher indicators of Chao1 and observed species and the comparatively abundant beta diversity (P < 0.05). Ten genera, including Faecalibacterium and Coprococcus, declined in laying hens from the CT group (P < 0.05), while Alistipes increased in CT hens (P < 0.05) compared to those hens from the CC group. The expression levels of intestinal barrier-related genes of claudin-1, claudin-4, occludin, ZO-1, and ZO-2 and immune-related genes of IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IFN-γ, IgA, TLR-2, and TLR-4 were significantly upregulated in the intestine of laying hens in CT group (P < 0.05). DE also increased the serum levels of IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IFN-γ, and IgA (P < 0.01). The alteration of the gut microbiota by DE is closely related to host immune responses, including Lactobacillus positively correlated with IL-4 and IgA. Thus, a dustbathing environment can improve the welfare of laying hens by changing the intestinal histomorphology, immune response, and the gut microbial community.Key words: dustbathing environment, intestinal microbiota, immune response, modified traditional cage, laying hen

INTRODUCTION

With the promulgations of policies in various countries, the housing system of laying hens has begun to reform from the traditional battery cage to the furnished cage or cage-free system (Lay Jr et al., 2011). The new alternative system has more horizontal and vertical spaces and provides a litter area for laying hens to promote the expression of natural behaviors of scratching, pecking, and dustbathing. Dustbathing is a crucial native behavior regarded as having rewarding properties in poultry (Olsson and Keeling, 2005). The effects of dustbathing environment (DE) on the health and immunity of laying hens have been understudied until now.

The intestine is an essential digestive organ of the body and is vital for the healthy growth and development of animals and humans. Intestinal health includes the functional integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier, stable intestinal microbiota, and certain intestinal immune level that are affected by age (Bosco and Noti, 2021), genetics (Org et al., 2015), feed (Geng et al., 2021), and environmental exposure (Wu et al., 2021). In the commercial egg industry, the hatching of chicks is carried out in separate incubators, in which the contact between adult hens and chicks is isolated. Recent studies have emphasized the imperative role of the environment in host health status and microbial community structure in mammal animals and poultry (Kers et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2019; Kamel et al., 2021). The alteration of the microbial community structure can affect the immunity of the intestinal region (Zhou et al., 2020). The production of acidic mucin, villus height, and crypt depth was decreased in broilers housed in isolators compared to conventionally housed broilers (Forder et al., 2007). This alteration of the intestinal morphology was subjected to the differences in microbiota structure (Forder et al., 2007). The structure of intestinal microbiota tended to be stable and located in a dynamic equilibrium with the increasing age of hens (Rychlik, 2020). Natural sand and peat are rich in microorganisms, and their texture is soft and dry, and easy to access and are the preferred litters for birds expressing dustbathing behavior (Scholz et al., 2011; Martin et al., 2012; Monckton et al., 2020). Although many studies have explored the alteration of behavior and other indicators caused by a dustbathing environment, the effects of DE on the intestines of adult laying hens remain unclear.

The main aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of a dustbathing environment on the intestinal health of adult hens. In modified traditional cages with and without litters (equal proportions of industrial sand and peat) exposure, the morphology of the intestine, microbial community, and immunological parameters of laying hens were compared, which deepens our understanding of the positive effect of the dustbathing environment for the poultry health and welfare.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics Statement

All experiments were approved by and conducted according to the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Northeast Agriculture University (NEAU-[2011]-9). And the sampling procedures complied with the “Guidelines on Ethical Treatment of Experimental Animals” (2006) No. 398 set by the Ministry of Science and Technology, China.

Experimental Animals and Management

In this study, 72 healthy Hy-Line Brown hens of 69 wk of age (WOA) were selected from a commercial layer farm, where laying hens were housed in traditional cages from 18 to 69 WOA. Laying hens used in this study were individually housed in modified traditional cages (Figure 1), each of size 48 cm length × 35 cm width × 45 cm height. The cages furnished with a dustbathing tray (30 cm length × 35 cm width × 3 cm height, installed on the left and rear of the sidewalls) were regarded as environmental enrichment (CT). The cages without the dustbathing tray were defined as the standard cage (CC). The litters placed in the dustbathing tray were comprised equal proportions of industrial sand and peat, and the litter was replaced every 2 d. Thus, 36 laying hens in the CT group had free access to litters, while the other 36 hens in the CC group were restricted from litters. A rectangular feeding trough and waterline with 1 nipple drinker were installed outside of front wire-mesh sidewall of CT and CC cages. The laying hens’ house was enclosed with an artificial light for 16 h from 05:00 to 21:00 h. The light intensity was 20 to 24 lux. The ambient temperature and relative humidity of house were 21°C to 24°C and 50 to 70%, respectively. The commercial corn–soybean meal egg-laying diet with 17.8% of crude protein, 6.3% ether extract content, and 2,800 kcal/kg metabolic energy was provided ad libitum for laying hens throughout the entire experiment period from 69 WOA to 75 WOA.

A front view of a modified traditional cage.

Serum and Tissue Samples

Fifteen laying hens from per group were randomly selected to take serum samples from the wing vein. After selecting serum, these birds were euthanized at 08:00 am on the first day of 76 WOA. Serum was harvested at the supernatant following centrifugation of the collected blood (3,000 rpm, 15 min at 4°C) and stored at −80°C for further use. The ileum was aseptically excised and washed 3 times with deionized water, circumscribed 1 cm and fixed in 4% poly formaldehyde solution for morphologic examination. Cecum contents per hen were collected by 1.5 mL freezing tubes to analyze microbial community structure. The portions of ileum were freed of residual food, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C for further analysis.

Light Microscopic Examination

The fixed intestinal samples were dehydrated in ascending grades of alcohol and embedded in paraffin for hematoxylin and eosin staining. The histomorphometric analysis of the small intestine was assessed using CaseViewer 2.4 (3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary) in low microscopic fields (50×). The morphometric variables analyzed including villus height using the lamina propria as the base (from the tip of the villus to the villus-crypt junction), crypt depth as the depth of the invagination between adjacent villi, and villus-to-crypt ratio according to Uni et al. (1999).

DNA Extraction, 16S rRNA Gene Amplicon Sequencing and Sequence Analysis

Total genomic DNA (gDNA) of cecum contents was isolated by the OMEGA Soil DNA Kit (M5635-02) (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. The gene of 16SrRNA (V3–V4 region) was amplified with primers (F: ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG and R: GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT). The PCR mixture including 5 μL of buffer (5×), 0.25 μL of Fast pfu DNA Polymerase (5 U/μL), 2 μL (2.5 mM) of dNTPs, 1 μL (10 uM) of each forward and reverse primer, 1 μL of DNA template, and 14.75 μL of ddH2O in a 25 μL reaction volume was prepared. Each of the 25 cycles consists of 98°C for 30 s, 53°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 45 s, after an initial denaturation at 98°C for 5 min and 72°C for 5 min. PCR amplicons were purified with Vazyme VAHTSTM DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and quantified using the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Amplicons were pooled in equal amounts, and pair-end 2 × 250 bp sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq platform with NovaSeq 6000 SP Reagent Kit (500 cycles) at Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Raw sequence data were demultiplexed using the demux plugin, followed by primers cutting with the cutadapt plugin (Martin, 2011). Sequences were filtered, denoised, and merged of quality and the chimera was removed using the DADA2 plugin (Callahan et al., 2016). Sequence data analyses were performed using QIIME2 2019.4 (Bolyen et al., 2019) and R packages (v3.2.0). ASV-level alpha diversity indices such as Chao1, observed species, Shannon diversity index, Simpson index, and Good's coverage were calculated using the ASV table in QIIME2. Beta diversity analysis was performed to investigate the structural variation of microbial communities across samples using Jaccard metrics (Jaccard, 1908) and Bray-Curtis metrics (Bray and Curtis, 1957) and visualized via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS). Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted based on the genus-level compositional profiles (Ramette, 2007). The significance of the differentiation of microbiota structure among groups was assessed by permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) (McArdle and Anderson, 2001) and analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) (Warton et al., 2012).

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real-Time PCR Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the ileum tissues of the CC and CT groups (n = 15) employing RNAiso Plus Kit (Takara, Dalian, China) in accordance with manufacturer's specification. Isolated RNA was quantified utilizing a NANO-400 (ALLSHENG, Hangzhou, China) at 260 nm and 260/280 nm. Total RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA with ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Kit (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). The final cDNA solution was stored at −20°C for further use.

Primer Premier Software 5.0 (PREMIER Biosoft International, Palo Alto, CA) was used to synthesize the specific primers for intestinal barrier-related genes including claudin-1, claudin-3, claudin-4, E-cadherin, occludin, zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), zonula occludens-2 (ZO-2), zonula occludens-3 (ZO-3), and mucin; and immune-related genes of interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-2 (IL-2), interleukin-4 (IL-4), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), interleukin-10 (IL-10), interleukin-17 (IL-17), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interferon-gamma-γ (IFN-γ), immunoglobulin A (IgA), toll-like receptor 2 (TLR-2), toll-like receptor 4 (TLR-4), toll-like receptor 5 (TLR-5), toll-like receptor 7 (TLR-7), and toll-like receptor 15 (TLR-15). The primers used for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) were shown in Supplementary Table S1 and qRT-PCR with Roche 480 LightCycler (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) was used to measure the mRNA expression of genes. Each 10 µL reaction mixture contained 5 µL 2X NovoStrart SYBR qPCR SuperMix Plus (Shanghai, China), 1 µL diluted cDNA, 0.3 µL of each primer (10 µM) and 3.4 µL RNase-free water. The qPCR conditions were as follows: initial heating to 95°C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 20 s and 60°C for 1 min and relative gene expression levels were evaluated using the 2−ΔΔCT method with β-actin (NM_205518.1).

Determination of Immune Indicators in Blood

Chicken ELISA kits (Shanghai Xinle Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) were used to measure the levels of IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IFN-γ, and IgA in serum of laying hens from CC and CT groups (15 serum samples per group) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The ELISA procedure is as fellows. Briefly, the capture antibodies to the detected antigen were coated to the wells of 96 ELISA plates (Corning). The 50 μL serum samples were added into the micropore and incubated, then washed 5 times to remove nonspecific binding. Lastly, the detecting antibodies crosslinking with horseradish peroxidase were incubated. The optical density values were measured at a wavelength of 450 nm by an ELISA reader (TECAN GENios, Grödig, Austria). A standard curve was obtained with the concentration and absorbance of standards to produce a linear equation to quantify experimental samples.

Statistical Analysis

The data were analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences 23 (IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0, Armonk, NY). Parameters were tested for normal distribution before analysis. Independent samples tests were used to analyze the differences between groups. The relationships between luminal bacteria (the top 30 genus level) and the immune indicators in serum were analyzed by the Pearson correlation test. The bacterial genera served as explanatory variables, and the immune indicators in serum were used as response variables. The P < 0.05 was considered to be a statistically significant value.

Microbial Community Diversities of Laying Hens

The alpha diversity of gut microbiota in the hens was estimated by the Chao1, observed species, Simpson, Shannon, and coverage indices. The Chao1 and observed species indies of CC were significantly lower than that of CT groups, while there was no significant between CT and CC groups hens in Simpson and Shannon indices (Figure 4A–E). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and NMDS, based on the Bray–Curtis distances, were used to assess the differences in bacterial community structure between the hen microbiome samples (Figure 4F and G). The PCoA (PERMANOVA, F = 1.8265, P = 0.005) and NMDS (ANOSIM, R = 0.1428, P = 0.006) revealed that the beta diversity indexes were significantly altered by DE (P < 0.05).

The Microbial Community Structure in Different Levels of Laying Hens From 2 Groups

The composition and abundance of bacterial taxa in the microbiota of laying hens were statistically analyzed. The compositions of the gut microbiota are shown in Figure 5. At the phylum level, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes could dominate the microbial community in all the birds, with an abundance of over 80% of the population. The remaining phyla identified were Proteobacteria, Synergistetes, Actinobacteria, WSP-2, and Spirochaetes. At the phylum level (Figure 5A), the composition of bacteria was the same in 2 groups; the abundance of Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, Synergistetes, Actinobacteria, WSP-2, and Spirochaetes showed no significant difference (P > 0.05). At the genus level (Figure 5B), Bacteroides and Phascolarctobacterium were the most prevalent genera in both groups. Alistipes (P = 0.029) was higher in the CT hens, In contrast, Faecalibacterium (P ≤ 0.02), [Ruminococcus] (P = 0.002), Ruminococcaceae_Ruminococcus (P = 0.031), Blautia (P = 0.017), Coprococcus (P = 0.033), Megasphaera (P = 0.007)), Peptococcus (P = 0.005), Sutterella (P = 0.023), Odoribacter (P = 0.049), and Megamonas (P = 0.001) were significantly lower in CT birds. Linear discriminant analysis effect size analysis (LEfSe) (P < 0.05, LDA > 2) identified representative gut bacterial taxa for the birds in CC groups and the CT group (Figure 6). The relative expression levels of intestinal barrier-related genes in the intestine of laying hens were analyzed to determine the effects of a dustbathing environment on the intestinal barrier function of laying hens (Figure 7A). Compared to the CC groups, the relative expression levels of claudin-1, claudin-4, occludin, ZO-1, and ZO-2 were significantly higher in laying hens from the CT groups (P < 0.05). However, there was no difference between the relative expression levels of claudin-3, E-cadherin, ZO-3, and mucin2 (P > 0.05).

Effects of Dustbathing Environment on Intestinal Immunity

The expression levels of the immune-related genes were affected by the dustbathing environment (Figures 7B and C). IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IFN-γ, IgA, TLR-2, and TLR-4 expression levels were significantly upregulated in the intestine of laying hens in CT groups compared with CC groups (P < 0.05). There was no difference in the 2 groups of the other cytokine mRNAs (IL-1β, IL-2, IL-10, IL-17, IFN-γ, TLR-5, TLR-7, and TLR-15, P > 0.05). As shown in Figure 6D, the serum concentrations of IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IFN-γ, and IgA were significantly enhanced in dustbathing environment treatments group compared to the CC group (P < 0.01). The results of immune indicators in serum were consistent with relative expression levels of the immune-related genes in the ileum.

DISCUSSION

The intestine is the main site for digestion and absorption of nutrients crucial for animal health status and growth performance. Characteristic features of a bird's digestive tract for optimal functions include a large surface area covered with long healthy villi having shallow crypts (Ahsan et al., 2016). The single layer of epithelial cells lining the intestinal lumen is renewed from the pool of crypt-based stem cells. The newly formed cells migrate the villus, to enter anoikis and exfoliate from the villus tip (Ducatelle et al., 2018). Long villi and shallow crypts provide a larger surface area for the absorption of nutrients and a low renewal rate, allowing efficient enzyme production and maturation of the intestinal cells. In this study, there were alterations in the morphology of the ileum in the CT group of hens compared to the CC group, with an increase in villus height and villus/crypt ratio. The improved intestinal morphology observed in the DE-supplemented hens suggests an enhancement in the digestive and absorptive capacities of the intestinal tract, which aids in replenishing the nutritional deficits associated with egg production.

As a crucial immunological barrier within the chicken gut, the mucosal immune system of the intestine plays a pivotal role in defending against the invasion of harmful microorganisms. The establishment of this barrier relies on the formation of tight junctions (TJs) located at the apical portion of polarized epithelial cells (Chelakkot et al., 2018). By sealing the intercellular gaps and regulating paracellular ion solute transport, the formation of the cellular barrier is achieved. The key to this process is claudin proteins responsible for intercellular gap sealing and modulation of trans-epithelial/trans-endothelial transport. Claudin-1 is the “sealer” of tight junctions, while claudin-4 is intricately associated with transepithelial resistance. Furuse et al. (2002) reported that claudin-1-deficient mice died within 1 d of birth because of significant transepidermal water loss. Moreover, in vitro and in vivo data suggest transgenic claudin-4 expression in vitro increased transepithelial resistance and reduced paracellular cation conductance to inhibit channel function (Shashikanth et al., 2022). Occludin is a critical component of the TJ barrier in cellular adhesion to maintain intact TJ complexes. The scaffold protein conula occludins (ZO-1, ZO-2, and ZO-3) is involved in anchoring claudin to the cytoskeleton within the cell. We recorded a notable upregulation of claudin-1, claudin-4, occludin, ZO-1, and ZO-2 in the ileum of laying hens housed in DE cages compared to that in the CC group. Song et al. (2017) showed that microencapsulated sodium butyrate could reverse the adverse effects of necrotic enteritis by regulating the gene expressions levels of TJ proteins, including claudin-1, claudin-4, occludin, and ZO-1. DE may enhance the ability of intestinal barrier tissue to resist invasion by harmful microorganisms by promoting its improvement and increasing the gene expression levels of TJ proteins.

We observed that the expression levels of immune indicators of IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IFN-γ, IgA, TLR-2, and TLR-4 were increased in the intestine and serum from DE-housed laying hens in the present study. IL-4 is secreted by activated T lymphocytes, eosinophils, and mast cells. Exerts effects as an immune modulator influence the differentiation of T lymphocytes and B cells. IL-6 can induce the proliferation and maturation of T cells and maintain the balance between T-cell subsets (Th1 vs. Th2) (Dodge et al., 2003). The upregulation of IL-4 and IL-6 levels of laying hens from a dustbathing environment enhances the immune response and plays a regulatory role in maintaining the balance of T-cell subsets. IL-8 plays a significant role as a crucial chemotactic factor in immune responses (Menger and Vollmar, 2004). The IFN-γ enhances NK cell activity and plays a role in immunoglobulin production and class switching in B cells (Jorgovanovic et al., 2020). B cells in the intestinal mucosa produce IgA, serving as the primary defense line to protect the intestinal mucosa from harm. The high expression levels of IL-8, IFN-γ, and IgA indicate that the CT hens have a strong immune regulatory response against both internal and external infection and stresses. Toll-like receptors (TLR) activate various signaling pathways by sensing pathogen-associated molecular patterns and modulating cytokines to participate in immune responses (Kawai and Akira, 2007). The expression levels of TLR-2 and TLR-4 were higher in the intestine of DE-housed laying hens. The results of immunology responses indicate that chickens can enhance their immune capacity in the dustbathing environment by regulating the production of cytokines and TLRs, improving the adaptability of laying hens to the complex environment.

Environmental factors are important for intestinal microbiota assembly, affecting the growth performance, immunity, behavior, and cognitive abilities of the host (Bi et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022). Studies have reported that litters and water affect the composition of birds' intestinal microbiota (Wang et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2019). In the present study, a high richness of microbial flora was found in the CT group following the previously found that taking the birds out of water increased the richness of the microbial community (Zhao et al., 2019). However, no difference was observed in the Shannon and Simpson indices of birds, which may be attributed to the fact that the chickens in this study were adult laying hens in the late stage of the laying period. The chicken microbiota was more plastic earlier and reached an adult microbial equilibrium above 25 wk (Ngunjiri et al., 2019). The dialog between microbiota and intestinal histomorphology has a crucial role and contributes to the global well-being of the host (Houshmand et al., 2011).

As an animal host ages, its influence on microbial selection in the development of the intestinal microbiota increases, and the alternation of diversities of microbial community was dampened by the adults' host homeostatic responses (Björk et al., 2019). The outcomes of this study suggest that under the conditions of DE supplement, the intestinal microbial community of hens may become more diverse at the phylum level, we observed similarities between the microbiotas of layers housed in a dustbathing and standard environment, and no significant difference in the relative abundances of the 10 most abundant phyla were found. At the genus level, 11 genera in CT birds were observed significantly in CC layers. Among 11 genera, 10 genera (Faecalibacterium, [Ruminococcus], Ruminococcaceae_Ruminococcus, Blautia, Coprococcus, Megasphaera, Sutterella, Peptococcus, Megamonas, Odoribacter) were discovered at a much lower level in CT group, while 1 genus (Alistipes) were shown to be higher in CT layers, where Firmicutes (Faecalibacterium, [Ruminococcus], Ruminococcaceae_Ruminococcus, Coprococcus, and Megasphaer) expressed enzymes required for butyrate production while Megamonas and Blautia expressed enzymes for propionate production pathways (Polansky et al., 2016). Odoribacter and Peptococcus genera are related to the expression levels of proteins involved in energy metabolism (Zhao et al., 2018, 2019). Alistipes is a new genus of Bacteroidetes, expressed methylmalonyl-CoA epimerase in driving propionate production. Alistipes can also metabolize glutamate into γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) by expressing glutamate decarboxyl and generating a Na+ gradient across the cytoplasmic membrane (Björk et al., 2019). The findings of this study imply that DE can enhance the diversity of the intestinal microbial community, alter the fermentation products of the gut microbiota, and influence the pH level of the intestine. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are a major class of bacterial metabolites, composed of acetate, propionate, and butyrate, as signaling molecules affect host physiology and immune regulation and can be consumed by enterocytes as energy sources (Liao et al., 2020; Ruiz-Aceituno et al., 2020). Xu et al. (2020) found that L-theanine affected intestinal mucosal histology by increasing the total SCFAs contents in Sprague-Dawley rats, which might be due to the responses of the microbiota to the external environmental stimuli on the interaction influence of the requirement of the host for products of microbiota and sustained existence. The balance of genera is modulated by changing the relative abundance like the gradual succession of Firmicutes and Bacteroides coinciding with sexual maturity.

The composition of gut microbiota may be related to host immunity (Ducatelle et al., 2018). The results of Pearson correlation analysis revealed that only the Lactobacillus genus exhibited a positive correlation with immune indicators in the serum. Lactobacillus could produce Indole derivatives (indolelactic acid, indoxylsulfuric acid, and 3-indoleacetic acid) to reduce proinflammatory cytokines and inhibit intestinal inflammation, maintaining intestinal homeostasis (Zhao et al., 2022). Megasphaera and Megamonas are considered beneficial bacteria, while both relate to human diseases (Llorente et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2019). The rest of the discovered genera of this study displayed a negative correlation with immune indicators. Among these genera, Blautia and Peptococcus are recognized as potentially harmful bacteria, and both types of bacteria can promote the development of inflammation (Polansky et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2022). Subdoligranulum is not a major bacterium acting on health but is rather an opportunistic bacterium (Van Hul et al., 2020). Because of the lack of information on immunity regarding [Ruminococcus] and Ruminococcaceae_Ruminococcus, the relevance of its relationship with immune response cannot be speculated. Hence, the sand bathing environment might modify the structure and composition of the chicken gut microbiota, contributing to the enhancement of immune function in the chickens.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, the dustbathing environment improved intestinal histomorphology by increasing villus height and villus/crypt ratio. The dustbathing environment could alter the microbial community and increase the microbial richness of the hen gut, improving the gut barrier and immune function of laying hens. The dustbathing environment may be an important environmental enrichment tool in the commercial laying hen industry due to its positive effect on gut health.
