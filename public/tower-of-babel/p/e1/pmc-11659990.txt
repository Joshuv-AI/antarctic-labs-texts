Closing the Knowledge Gap of Post-Acquisition Sample Normalization in Untargeted Metabolomics
Authors: Brian Low, Yukai Wang, Tingting Zhao, Huaxu Yu, Tao Huan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSample normalization is a crucial step in metabolomics for fair quantitative comparisons. It aims to minimize sample-to-sample variations due to differences in the total metabolite amount. When samples lack a specific metabolic quantity to accurately represent their total metabolite amounts, post-acquisition sample normalization becomes essential. Despite many proposed normalization algorithms, understanding remains limited of their differences, hindering the selection of the most suitable one for a given metabolomics study. This study bridges this knowledge gap by employing data simulation, experimental simulation, and real experiments to elucidate the differences in the mechanism and performance among common post-acquisition sample normalization methods. Using public datasets, we first demonstrated the dramatic discrepancies between the outcomes of different sample normalization methods. Then, we benchmarked six normalization methods: sum, median, probabilistic quotient normalization (PQN), maximal density fold change (MDFC), quantile, and class-specific quantile. Our results show that most normalization methods are biased when there is unbalanced data, a phenomenon where the percentages of up- and downregulated metabolites are unequal. Notably, unbalanced data can be sourced from the underlying biological differences, experimental perturbations, and metabolic interference. Beyond normalization algorithms and data structure, our study also emphasizes the importance of considering additional factors contributed by data quality, such as background noise, signal saturation, and missingness. Based on these findings, we propose an evidence-based normalization strategy to maximize sample normalization outcomes, providing a robust bioinformatic solution for advancing metabolomics research with a fair quantitative comparison.Keywords: post-acquisition sample normalization, metabolomics, mass spectrometry, bioinformatics, maximal density fold change, quantitative comparison

Introduction

Metabolomics is a powerful bioanalytical technology that has been routinely used to gain a global view of the metabolic status in various biological systems. Metabolomics enabled by high-resolution mass spectrometry (MS) can deliver the detection of thousands of metabolic features from biological samples. The accurate detection of these metabolites has allowed quantitative comparison between two or more groups of samples to discover statistically significant metabolites for biomarkers and the elucidation of underlying biological mechanisms.1,2 From an analytical perspective, a fair quantitative comparison relies on the fact that all samples should be analyzed using the same total metabolite amount. However, such conditions are usually not met for various biological sample types, such as urine, feces, and sweat, among others, due to inherent sample-to-sample variation. These variations include differences in water content, initial sample quantities, and metabolic interferences.

To ensure that the same total sample metabolite amount is compared, sample normalization is needed. Sample normalization in metabolomics can be done either before or after data acquisition, known respectively as pre- and post-acquisition sample normalization.3 Pre-acquisition requires a quantity that reflects the total metabolite amount in the sample. In this approach, sample metabolite amounts are adjusted based on the measured quantity prior to LC-MS analysis.3 For instance, creatinine level is often used to normalize urine metabolomics results as it is excreted by kidney at a constant rate. Similarly, cell count is commonly used to normalize cell metabolomics data. However, a significant limitation of pre-acquisition sample normalization is that many sample types do not have a well-established quantity that reflects their total metabolite amount. Such samples include breath and sweat. Furthermore, strong metabolic interference poses a sample normalization problem for many biological sample types. For instance, dietary metabolites remaining in the oral cavity might interfere with the saliva metabolome. In addition, there is growing concern about how accurately well-established quantities reflect metabolite amounts. Many established pre-acquisition sample normalization methods can be inaccurate if their assumptions are not met. For example, the accuracy of using creatinine levels for normalization in urine metabolomics may be affected by renal impairment.4 Despite these challenges, pre-acquisition sample normalization is generally preferred over post-acquisition normalization when a valid quantity that reflects total metabolite amount exists. This is because pre-acquisition sample normalization can also help to optimize sample loading amounts prior to data acquisition, ensuring maximum detectability without overloading the LC-MS system.

To deal with sample normalization in which pre-acquisition sample normalization does not work, post-acquisition sample normalization becomes the only other option. The key idea of post-acquisition sample normalization is to adjust MS signal intensities after data acquisition for a fair comparison. Several post-acquisition sample normalization algorithms have been developed. Each makes different assumptions on the data structure; thus, performance is greatly dependent on how close their assumptions are to reality. Common post-acquisition sample normalization methods include the sum,5 median,6 PQN,7 MDFC,8 quantile,9 and class-specific quantile10 methods. A summary of these strategies can be found in Table 1.

Although there have been studies evaluating the performance of post-acquisition sample normalization methods, there is still no clear understanding of which method is the best fit for a given metabolomics dataset.11−13 To address these knowledge gaps, we studied the commonly used sum, median, PQN, MDFC, quantile, and class-specific quantile normalization methods. Notably, we applied data simulation, experimental simulation, and real experimental analysis to gain a mechanistic understanding of post-acquisition sample normalization. We also highlighted the importance of data quality for the normalization outcome. Based on these findings, we proposed an evidence-based workflow to facilitate better post-acquisition sample normalization that considers both data quality and the choice of normalization method.

Methods

Choice of Normalization Algorithms

This work studied and compared six post-acquisition sample normalization methods, including five commonly used methods: sum, median, quantile, class-specific quantile, probabilistic quotient normalization (PQN), and the recently developed maximal density fold change (MDFC).8 In the algorithms of sum, median, PQN, and MDFC, samples were normalized against a reference sample, which is the sample with the fewest missing values. Notably, for the MDFC method, the bandwidth was optimized to be 0.3 (Text S1). All data processing was done in R (ver. 4.1.3).

Public Metabolomics Datasets

To elucidate the discrepancies between normalization methods, we used four public metabolomics datasets from the studies of HIV,14 juice,15 opium,16 and sepsis.17 The details of these datasets can be found in Texts S2 and S3. Statistically significant metabolic features were extracted from each normalized feature table using the two-tailed Wilcoxon rank-sum test and fold change (FC) (p-value <0.05, FC > 1.5, or FC < 0.67). For the juice dataset of paired samples, the two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. The unique and common statistically significant features from each normalization method were compared and visualized using the “UpSetR” R package (ver.1.4.0).

Data Simulation and Benchmarking

To simulate a two-sample dataset (i.e., one reference and one test sample), a vector of 5000 intensity values was randomly sampled from an experimental metabolomics data file and served as the reference. To simulate metabolic features for the test sample, we multiplied the reference intensities with Gaussian noise (noise ∼ N(1, 0.2)) to represent metabolic fluctuations between samples. A certain percentage of features in the test sample were then up- or downregulated to simulate metabolic dysregulation caused by significant biological differences. These significant metabolic dysregulations can be caused by experimental and/or biological factors such as age, gender, diet, and lifestyle differences between individuals. The intensity of each dysregulated feature was up- or downregulated using the following uniform distributions: upregulation ∼ U(1.5, 10) and downregulation ∼ U(0.1, 0.67). Finally, to simulate different sample metabolite amounts, all intensities in the test sample were multiplied by a dilution factor of 5. We also varied the percentages of non-dysregulated features from 0–100% in steps of 1% to comprehensively evaluate a wide range of data structures. In addition, dysregulated features were simulated to have upregulation from 0–100% in steps of 1%. Normalization factors were obtained using the sum, median, PQN, and MDFC methods. To improve the robustness of statistical comparison, each simulation was repeated 100 times.

Multi-sample simulations were performed to represent more realistic metabolomics studies composed of multiple samples in two experimental groups. We first simulated 20 samples from the reference sample by adding Gaussian noise (noise ∼ N(1, 0.2)). The samples were then divided equally into two groups: control and experimental treatment. Each sample had 25% of its metabolic features randomly chosen and dysregulated using factors from the uniform distributions described in the two-sample simulation. For these dysregulated features, the percentage of features with upregulation was randomly chosen to be between 25 and 75%. Next, to represent metabolic dysregulation from experimental treatment (e.g., disease and drug perturbation), 50% of the metabolic features in the treatment group were selected and further dysregulated. Finally, each sample was chosen to have a metabolite amount 4.5-fold more or less than the reference sample, representing a maximum difference of approximately 20-fold. Two simulations were conducted: the dysregulated features in the treatment group were simulated to have 50 or 75% upregulation. To better align the simulated data with reality, we injected missing values into the multi-sample datasets (Text S4). To improve the robustness of statistical comparison, each data simulation was repeated 100 times.

To benchmark the results of the two-sample simulation, we calculated the average log-ratio between the calculated normalization factor (k′) and the true dilution factor (k). To benchmark the multi-sample simulations, statistically significant metabolic features from each normalized feature table were identified using the two-tailed Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The resulting p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg correction method. Statistically significant features had an adjusted p-value of <0.05. The statistically significant features extracted from the reference feature table served as the ground truth. The true positive rate (TPR) and false positive rate (FPR) were calculated by comparing the significant features extracted from each normalization method and the ground truth. The TPR was defined as the proportion of true positives over the total actual positives, and the FPR was defined as the proportion of false positives over the total actual negatives.

Experimental Simulation of Metabolic Interference

To investigate the impact of metabolic interference on the normalization performance, we spiked urine samples with plasma. The percentages of plasma spiked into urine were 20, 40, 60, 80, and 90%. As a negative control, we spiked another set of urine samples with water at the same ratios. The mixtures were analyzed with a 1290 Infinity II UHPLC system (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) coupled to an Impact II ultrahigh-resolution Qq-time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) in positive ESI mode. Hydrophilic interaction chromatography (HILIC) separation was achieved on a SeQuant ZIC-pHILIC column (150 × 2.1 mm, 5 μm, 200 Å). Samples were normalized to the unspiked urine sample with the sum, median, PQN, and MDFC methods. The log-ratios between the normalization factors (k′) and the experimental dilution factors (k) were calculated. The details of sample preparation, experimental conditions, and data preprocessing can be found in Text S5.

Impact of Data Quality on Post-Acquisition Sample Normalization

To study the impact of data quality on sample normalization, we collected four urine samples from healthy volunteers. We diluted each sample with water for 10 urine concentrations ranging from 10–100% in increments of 10%. Together, there were four sets (i.e., urine datasets A–D) with 10 concentrations in each set. The urine samples were analyzed with the same LC-MS platform as described previously but in negative ESI mode. The raw data files were preprocessed in the same manner as described in Text S5. Normalization factors were compared to the experimental dilution factors using the root-mean-square error (RMSE) in the R package “Metrics” (ver. 0.1.4). The reference sample in each dataset was the 100% urine sample. The detailed sample preparation and experimental conditions can be found in Text S6.

Symmetry Factors

Symmetry factors (SFs) were proposed to guide the choice of normalization method in evidence-based sample normalization. SFs show the percentage of samples with unbalanced data in a dataset. It was defined to be the ratio between the right and left areas in the FC density plot, and it was calculated in the following way. First, the FC distribution between each sample and the reference sample was calculated. The Anderson-Darling test from the R package “nortest” (ver. 1.0–4) was used to check for normality to prevent unnecessary data transformations. If the distribution was not normal, the data was log2 transformed. A Gaussian kernel with a bandwidth of 0.3 was used for kernel density estimations. The region to the left and right of the FC with maximal density was integrated using the trapezoidal rule in the R package “pracma” (ver. 2.4.2). Samples with SFs between 0.80–1.25 were considered balanced. Otherwise, the samples were classified as unbalanced.

Results and Discussion

Discrepancies among Normalization Outcomes

We first highlighted that the chosen normalization method can significantly affect the outcome of downstream quantitative comparison and statistical analysis. To illustrate this, we employed a public dataset from an HIV saliva study.14 We extracted and compared the statistically significant features that distinguish between healthy controls and HIV patients before and after post-acquisition sample normalization. For this comparison, in addition to pre-normalized data, we tested six normalization methods that were either raw intensity-, fold change-, or quantile-based (Figure 1A). As shown in Figure 1B, a great discrepancy in the statistical output was observed. For example, only 48% of the significant features were common between the outputs of sum and quantile normalization. After expanding the comparison to all normalization methods, we found that many statistically significant metabolic features were specific to the normalization method (Figure S1A). For instance, normalizing with the class-specific quantile method resulted in 69 unique statistically significant features that would not be detected in any of the pre-normalized or normalized datasets. Moreover, of all 804 statistically significant features that were extracted by all seven tested methods, only 171 were shared (Figure 1C) while 200 were unique to one of the normalization methods. Interestingly for the 171 shared significant metabolic features, their p-values from statistical testing were vastly different. To demonstrate this, we calculated the ratio between the largest and smallest p-values obtained from all normalization methods for each metabolic feature (Figure S1B). Of the 171, 53.8% had a ratio larger than 10, underscoring the critical influence of the normalization method on statistical outcomes. Unfortunately, the root cause for the substantial disparities in statistical outcomes observed across various normalization methods remains unclear, prompting the demand for a deeper mechanistic understanding.

Understanding the Metabolic Differences between Samples

Fundamentally, sample normalization aims to minimize the metabolic difference arising from different metabolite amounts between samples. This process allows for the extraction of significantly changed metabolic features that differ due to the experimental treatments, such as cancer vs normal or treatment vs control. However, metabolic differences between samples can have multiple sources. It is crucial to first dissect all possible sources. From there, we can assess whether a certain normalization method could accurately recognize the portion of metabolic differences caused by differing metabolite amounts, thereby ensuring precise sample normalization.

Based on our understanding of metabolomics data structure, this work categorizes metabolic differences into three types, excluding those differences caused by varying sample metabolite amounts. These types include: (1) metabolic fluctuations, (2) metabolic dysregulations, and (3) metabolic interferences (Table 2). Metabolic fluctuations are small-scale metabolic changes due to analytical and inherent biological variations. These fluctuations are random and expected to follow a Gaussian distribution, resulting in relatively minor metabolic differences between biological samples. Metabolic dysregulations, on the other hand, represent more systematic and significant changes in metabolic abundances between samples. For example, gene knockdown and drug perturbation can cause notable metabolic changes in biological samples. These dysregulated metabolites often hold significant biological meaning and can serve as diagnostic biomarkers or be used to illustrate biological mechanisms. Lastly, metabolic interferences are introduced through contamination during the sample collection and analysis stages. For example, the saliva metabolome may be contaminated by residual food metabolites in the mouth. Similarly, feces and urine samples collected from mice in metabolic cages can be cross-contaminated, leading to inaccurate metabolome analyses.18 Metabolic interference can cause variations in metabolic content and may artificially increase metabolic intensities if such interference is present in the original samples. In this study, both experimental data and simulations were employed to comprehensively assess the effectiveness of post-acquisition sample normalization in mitigating the impact of metabolic fluctuations, dysregulations, and interferences. This will allow for correct estimations on the metabolic difference arising from varying sample metabolite amounts.

Data Simulation to Understand the Impact of Metabolic Fluctuation and Dysregulation

With the understanding of factors that influence normalization performance, we first benchmarked the normalization methods on simulated data structures with metabolic fluctuations and dysregulations. An important advantage of data simulation is the ability to define and apply the true dilution factors that represent the varying metabolite amounts between samples. This is critical because the true dilution factor is usually unknown in metabolomics studies. In contrast, data simulation conveniently provides the true dilution factor. This allows for a direct comparison between true dilution factors and the normalization factors calculated by the normalization algorithms. Another advantage of data simulation is to generate a broad spectrum of data structures that covers various experimental scenarios. This approach mitigates the time and cost burden associated with generating all these scenarios through real LC-MS analysis. Importantly, data simulation enables us to explore the impact of unbalanced metabolite dysregulation, a common phenomenon where the number of up- and downregulated metabolic features are unequal. For example, plasma samples from patients with acute kidney injury after cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass have been reported to have unbalanced metabolite dysregulation.19

Given the diverse nature of experimental metabolomics datasets, a well-performing normalization method should accurately estimate dilution factors over a wide range of data structures. We first set up two-sample simulations (Figure 2A) and compared the algorithm-calculated normalization factors against the true dilution factor. The heatmaps in Figure 2B show the results for the sum, median, PQN, and MDFC normalization methods. We did not include the quantile and class-specific quantile methods as they do not provide normalization factors. Each grid in the heatmap represents the log-ratio between the normalization factor (k′) and the true dilution factor (k) under a certain data simulation condition. A heatmap with more white area represents a method that can more accurately estimate the true dilution factor over a wide range of data structures. Our results show that when the percentages of non-dysregulated features were high (i.e., near 100%), all tested normalization methods performed relatively well. This finding suggests that metabolic fluctuations alone do not significantly impact normalization performance. Next, the sum method did not perform well across most data structures. This occurs because sum normalization factors primarily reflect the intensities of highly abundant metabolic features rather than the metabolite amount.7 Moreover, when the percentages of upregulated metabolic features were significantly higher and lower than 50%, both the median and PQN methods performed poorly. This is because the median intensity and the median fold change are biased by an abundance of up- and downregulated features. For example, with unbalanced upregulation (with more upregulated features than downregulated ones), PQN normalization factors will be overestimated. In contrast, the maximal density fold change is not impacted by this positive bias, resulting in consistently accurate normalization factors. However, in the extreme cases of low percentages of non-dysregulated features (i.e., <25%), MDFC produced severe errors in the normalization factors. In these situations, the maximal density will represent the dysregulated features rather than the features that represent sample amounts. More simulations on other conditions are presented in Figure S2, highlighting that MDFC performed consistently well regardless of the simulated dilution factor.

We further set up and studied multi-sample simulations (Figure 3A). In these simulations, we evaluated the true positive rate (TPR) and false positive rate (FPR) of the statistically significant features extracted from normalized datasets compared to the ground truth dilution factors. In comparative metabolomics, both the TPR and FPR are crucial indicators of data quality. A low TPR is problematic because it means real statistically significant features may be overlooked, which is especially concerning if these features are potential disease biomarkers or essential for understanding underlying biological mechanisms. Conversely, a high FPR is problematic because it leads to false statistically significant features, wasting resources on interpreting biologically irrelevant data. If a feature is falsely deemed to be statistically significant, it could also be a waste of effort trying to interpret its biological meanings. Figure 3B shows the results of multi-sample simulations. For balanced data, all normalization methods performed reasonably well with a high TPR and low FPR. However, with unbalanced data, MDFC and class-specific quantile maintained good performance while other methods showed a decrease in TPR and an increase in FPR. As consistent with a previous study, our results show that the class-specific quantile method outperformed the conventional quantile method.10 Importantly, the results of multi-sample simulations agree with the results of two-sample simulations where MDFC outperformed the other tested normalization methods. Interestingly, our results suggest that conducting statistical analysis on pre-normalized data may be preferable to using improperly normalized data. Further discussion on this observation can be found in Text S7. In short, choosing a normalization algorithm that fits the given metabolomics data structure would lead to the best data quality for downstream quantitative comparisons and statistical analysis. Among all the tested normalization algorithms, MDFC proved to be the most robust algorithm.

Experimental Simulation to Understand the Impact of Metabolic Interference

Metabolic interference from sample contamination adds a layer of complexity to post-acquisition sample normalization. If the contaminant does not overlap with metabolic features in the sample, then sample normalization performance will be less affected (Text S8). However, it is more common for biological matrices, which share many metabolic features, to contaminate each other.18 Thus, we designed an experiment where we spiked urine with either water or plasma (Figure 4A). Urine samples spiked with plasma represent a scenario in which urine is metabolically contaminated with blood during sample collection or due to hematuria.20 Because the urine samples were spiked with known amounts, normalization factors can be directly compared to the experimental dilution factors.

(A) Experimental design for investigating the impact of metabolic interference on normalization performance. The calculated normalization factors (k′) were benchmarked against the experimental dilution factors (k). (B) Results for the metabolic interference experiments for urine samples spiked with water and plasma. The black dashed line represents a perfectly accurate normalization factor.

As shown in Figure 4B, when urine samples were spiked with water, all normalization methods performed relatively well as the log-ratios between k and k′ were close to 0. This is because spiking with water represents a simple dilution of urine samples. Conversely, when urine samples were spiked with plasma, the magnitude of the log-ratio increased significantly. Since urine and plasma share many metabolic features (Figure S3A), increasing the ratio of plasma will result in upregulated urine metabolic features with respect to the unspiked urine sample (Figure S3B). This metabolic interference results in unbalanced upregulation. The sum, median, and PQN methods did not perform well with this data structure, leading to large differences between the normalization factors and experimental dilution factors. Importantly, the experimental results here also validated the findings from the two- and multi-sample simulations as MDFC was the only normalization method that performed well when the data was unbalanced. In extreme cases, where more than 80% of the sample consisted of plasma, no tested normalization method was able to accurately recover the true dilution factor. Therefore, besides the choice of normalization algorithm, strict control of sample collection to minimize metabolic interference is also essential to ensure high normalization performance.

The Impact of Data Quality

Given that post-acquisition sample normalizations calculate normalization factors using the collected metabolomics data, it is reasonable to believe that the quality of the data plays an important role in normalization performance. Unfortunately, limited studies discuss the importance of data quality in the context of post-acquisition sample normalization. Based on our understanding of the metabolomics data, we proposed four aspects of the data quality that are important to the performance of post-acquisition sample normalization, including the existence of (1) non-biological features, (2) features with poor quantitative performance, (3) fold change bias, and (4) data missingness and imputation. We investigated the impact of each of these aspects on normalization outcomes by comparing normalization factors to true dilution factors using both experimental and simulated data (Figure 5). In brief, using only non-biological features and features with poor quantitative performance for normalization calculations consistently resulted in inaccurate normalization factors. However, even after removing metabolic features with these two issues, normalization outcomes can still be poor due to fold change biases in electrospray ionization (ESI)-based MS analysis.21,22 Additionally, we show that missing values and subsequent imputation can result in biased normalization factors as the imputation strategy can introduce artifacts and biases into the data structure. Thus, normalization should better be performed prior to missing value imputation. Further discussion on each of these data quality aspects can be found in Texts S9–S13. Taken together, it is critical to address the data quality issues to ensure accurate normalization results. We also illustrate that normalization performance can be significantly improved by addressing these data quality issues (Text S14).

Evidence-Based Sample Normalization

From what has been discussed, the performance of post-acquisition sample normalization is strongly affected by the choice of the normalization algorithm, data structure, and data quality. Therefore, we propose an evidence-based post-acquisition sample normalization workflow to achieve high-quality sample normalization (Figure 6). First, metabolic interference should be considered to ensure that there is minimal risk of sample contamination in the experimental design. After data collection and instrument analysis, the next step is to manually inspect the raw chromatograms. There should be an adequate signal, minimal signal saturation, and no severe retention time drift. Notably, signal saturation should be addressed immediately as it is difficult to correct and will negatively impact normalization performance. Thus, samples should be reanalyzed again at a lower injection volume. Next, non-biological features and features with poor quantitative performance should be removed prior to downstream calculations. Intensities should then be converted to QC loading amounts to correct fold change bias and facilitate reliable quantitative comparisons. After ensuring optimal data quality, the dataset is ready to be normalized. When choosing a normalization method, the severity of unbalanced data should be considered as poor normalization performance is primarily a result of this data structure. In particular, we propose the calculation of symmetry factors (SFs), which is detailed in Text S15, to guide the choice of a normalization method. In an evidence-based normalization workflow, SFs are critical for choosing a normalization method as they can detect unbalanced data (Text S16). If a dataset has a large percentage of samples with unbalanced data, it is a good indicator that MDFC should be used as it can best handle this data structure. Importantly, using three public metabolomics datasets15−17 as case studies, we show that hundreds of significantly changed metabolic features can be false positives or never be discovered if a suboptimal normalization method is used (Text S17). Thus, unless the dataset has a low percentage of samples with unbalanced data, we strongly recommend using MDFC. The R markdown protocol for performing evidence-based sample normalization is available on GitHub for public use (https://github.com/HuanLab/sample-normalization).

Conclusions

This work provides a comprehensive study to mechanistically understand post-acquisition sample normalization methods. To begin, we showcased the significant performance discrepancy between the various normalization methods. We then dissected the possible sources of sample-to-sample metabolic differences with metabolic fluctuation, dysregulation, and interference. These metabolic differences convolute post-acquisition sample normalization, resulting in poor quantitative results. With data and experimental simulations, we explained why certain normalization methods are better than others for dealing with the various data structures. Furthermore, we highlighted that a good normalization outcome is more than just the choice of the normalization algorithm. In fact, the quality of metabolomics data is equally important. Finally, this work proposed an evidence-based normalization workflow that considers all of the above-mentioned factors. We hope that this study will greatly benefit the metabolomics community with a better understanding of sample normalization. It serves to guide the future development of more robust and accurate sample normalization strategies for better quantitative comparisons in metabolomics on various biological sample types.
