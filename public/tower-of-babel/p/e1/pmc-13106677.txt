Reversible H2 storage at moderate temperature by a trilayered lithium borohydride nanocomposite
Authors: Wenxuan Zhang, Xin Zhang, Chaoqun Li, Lingchao Zhang, Huifeng Liu, Zichen Lou, Zhenguo Huang, Guanglin Xia, Mingxia Gao, Wenping Sun, Yongfeng Liu, Hongge Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydrogen storage in lithium borohydride (LiBH4) with high gravimetric and volumetric hydrogen densities has attracted intensive research interest. However, the high working temperatures and poor reversibility due to the high thermodynamic stability and kinetic barriers limits its practical applications. Herein, we fabricate a trilayered nanostructure composed of graphene, Ni nanoclusters, and LiBH4 nanoparticles in sequence, through a layer-by-layer assembly approach, where LiBH4 is mostly partitioned from graphene by the Ni nanoclusters. The use of graphene plays important role on uniformly dispersing Ni nanoclusters and forming LiBH4 nanoparticles. The direct contact of Ni nanoclusters with LiBH4 significantly promotes the cleaving of the H-H bonds and B clusters during hydrogenation, creating additional hydrogen absorption sites and reducing the H adsorption energy of B. As a result, a high reversible storage of 12.3 wt% H2 with respect to LiBH4 commencing from 70 °C to 350 °C under 100 bar H2 is achieved. This finding guides the design and fabrication of light-metal hydride nanostructures for practical on-board hydrogen storage applications.Subject terms: Chemical hydrogen storage, Synthesis and processing

Introduction

To power a sustainable civilization, hydrogen is a desirable alternative to conventional fossil fuels1–3. However, the hydrogen storage remains a roadblock to the wide use of hydrogen as a fuel as lack of safe, efficient, and economical methods4–6. Special attention has been paid to materials-based hydrogen storage, and among the choices, LiBH4 with extremely high gravimetric (18.5 wt%) and volumetric (121 kg m−3) hydrogen densities has received intensive research interests7–9. However, its high thermodynamic stability and high kinetic barrier lead to high temperatures for reversible hydrogen storage by LiBH410,11. A variety of strategies have been proposed to improve the hydrogen storage performance of LiBH4, including substitution of anions or cations12–15, construction of reactive composites16–19, catalysis20–24 and nanoconfinement25–39, etc.

Nanoconfinement is the most frequently used strategy for nanostructured LiBH4, which are generally performed by the infiltration of LiBH4 solution in the pores of the scaffolds followed by removing the solvents25,26 or by melting infiltration LiBH4 to the pores of the scaffolds27,28, where the frameworks are mostly porous carbonaceous materials. The hydrogen storage kinetics can be effectively improved by the increased specific surface areas, the formation of active interface and the shortened hydrogen diffusion path. As reported, 33 wt% LiBH4 is confined into a mesoporous carbon with pores in size of ca. 4 nm by a solution method, the system shows enhanced desorption kinetics compared with the pristine, where 3.4 wt% H2 is released at 300 °C within 90 min26. The confinement of LiBH4 into carbon cages via melt infiltration shows reductions in hydrogenation temperatures, where hydrogenation commences from 200 °C under 50 bar H2, and hydrogenation is almost completed at 400 °C27. The desorption kinetics can be further improved by coupling with metal/metal oxide catalysts, especially for Ni decorated scaffolds29–36. The onset temperature for dehydrogenation is decreased to 250 °C by confining LiBH4 into a porous carbon scaffold decorated with Ni nanoparticles31. Another confined system with porous hollow carbon microspheres composed of carbon-coated Ni nanoparticles shows a dehydrogenation capacity of 4 wt% H2 at 300 °C within 30 min32. Hydrogenation is also significantly enhanced by the introduction of Ni into the nanoporous carbon-confined LiBH436. Moreover, graphene with high specific surface area are reported can loaded LiBH4 up to 70 wt%, which still show superior hydrogen storage properties by different research grops37,38, where the peak dehydrogenation temperatures are reduced to 346–359 °C. In a recent work of our group39, Ni is introduced to a graphene-supported LiBH4 as catalyst by a one-pot chemical method, where the Ni nanoparticles in size of 2–4 nm are enabled in LiBH4. The peak dehydrogenation temperature is further decreased to 285 °C for the loading of LiBH4 of 70 wt%. However, the overall hydrogen storage properties still remain challenge for practical application, such as the onset hydrogenation temperature of 130 °C is still too high for using the waste heat from hydrogen fuel cells on board40.

In the present work, based on a density functional theory (DFT) calculation, it is found that the structural breakdown of the B icosahedron on the Ni surface increases the availability of adsorption sites for H, while C tends to occupy the adsorption sites, hence probably hinder the migration of H on the B surface. In inspired by this, for graphene supported Ni catalyzed LiBH4 system, it should be helpful if to avoid the direct contact of LiBH4 and graphene and leave the direct contact of LiBH4 with Ni to enhance the catalytic effect. Therefore, a LiBH4-based hydrogen storage material with a trilayered nanostructure composed of graphene, Ni nanoclusters, and LiBH4 nanoparticles in sequence is prepared. The composite shows superior hydrogen storage property, especially a low hydrogenation temperature of 70 °C.

The trilayered LiBH4 composite is prepared through a layer-by-layer assembly approach as illustrated in Fig. 2a. A Ni nanocluster-decorated graphene (denoted as Grs-Ni) is synthesized via heating the mixture of Ni(NO3)2·6H2O and few-layer graphene at 400 °C, under a mixture atmosphere of Ar+10 vol%H2. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis demonstrates the formation of cubic metallic Ni (PDF: 04-0850) (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Further X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis also demonstrates the presence of metallic Ni (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observation (Fig. 2b–f) shows that Ni particles with size smaller than 10 nm disperse uniformly on the graphene. The amount and the size of the Ni particles are all gradually increased with the increased amount Ni. Taking Grs-10 wt% Ni (labeled as Grs-10Ni) as a representative, Ni particles in size of 2–5 nm are spread on the graphene surface uniformly (Supplementary Fig. 1c).

The graphene-Ni-LiBH4 nanostructure (denoted as Grs-Ni-LiBH4) is prepared by an in-situ reaction of n-butyl lithium (C4H9Li, n-BuLi) and triethylamine borane ((C2H5)3NBH3, Et3N∙BH3) in n-hexane under 50 bar H2 using the prepared Grs-Ni as supports. The reacted product is rinsed by n-hexane to remove the by-products, followed by dynamic vacuum drying. Thereafter, solid Grs-Ni-LiBH4 powders are obtained. Here, Grs-Ni as the support is set to be 30 wt% of the resultant product. The characteristic diffraction peaks of LiBH4 (PDF: 01-081-9182) are clearly identified by XRD analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) examination indicates the B-H vibrations of LiBH4 at 2390, 2291, 2221 and 1122 cm−1 (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations of Grs-xNi-LiBH4 (Fig. 2g–k) show that plenty of nanoparticles, which should be LiBH4, uniformly dispersed on the Grs-xNi supports. The amount and size of the LiBH4 nanoparticles gradually increase from 33.4 nm to 98.3 nm with the Ni amounts to 6 wt% in the Grs-Ni composites (Fig. 2g–k, Supplementary Fig. 3a–c). When the Ni amount is further increased to 10 wt%, the size of the LiBH4 nanoparticles decreases to 66.1 nm (Supplementary Fig. 3d), demonstrating that the presence of nanoparticulate Ni facilitates the nucleation and growth of LiBH4 nanoparticles. Further increasing the Ni amount to 20 wt%, the LiBH4 nanoparticles coarsen to 98.8 nm. The variation may be attributed to the nucleation competition between the amount and sized of Ni on the graphene (Supplementary Fig. 3e). The graphene loaded with 10 wt% Ni nanoclusters is an optimum support in facilitating the smallest size of LiBH4 particles. Thus, the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite, corresponding a composition of 70 wt% LiBH4, 27 wt% Grs, and 3 wt% Ni, achieves a balanced coordination between relatively small size of LiBH4 particle and uniform distribution.

The microstructure of Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 is further studied by TEM, energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 4), by comparing the Grs-10Ni and the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4. The dark-field TEM image and corresponding EDS mapping of the Grs-10Ni confirm that metallic Ni particles disperse uniformly on the surface of the graphene (Fig. 3a). Figure 3b–e shows that for the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite, a LiBH4 particle layer spreads on the nanocluster Ni layer, the latter of which is dispersed on the graphene support layer. HRTEM and SAED results indicate the significant difference between area 1 and 2 (Fig. 3c). The interplanar spacings of the (311) facet of Ni, the (020) facet of LiBH4 and the (11¯00) facet of graphene are clearly detected in area 1. However, only Ni and graphene are identified in area 2. The synthesized LiBH4 particles are measured to be 40–80 nm in size (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 4). For a more exact structure analysis of the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite, FIB-TEM (Focused Ion Beam Transmission Electron Microscopy) analysis is performed (Fig. 3d, e, Supplementary Fig. 5). HRTEM and HAADF-STEM images reveal a distinct interlayer between the graphene support and the LiBH4 nanoparticle layer (Fig. 3d, e). STEM-EDS (Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy-Energy Dispersive Spectrometer) mapping clearly demonstrates that the interlayer consists of Ni nanoparticles (Fig. 3e). Therefore, it is concluded that the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite has a trilayered nanostructure, composed of a graphene support layer, a nanocluster Ni layer and a nanoparticulate LiBH4 layer in sequence.

Hydrogen storage properties of the trilayered lithium borohydride nanocomposite

The resultant trilayered nanocomposites are subjected to hydrogen desorption/absorption measurements in isothermal and non-isothermal modes. As shown in Fig. 4a–c, the presence of Ni nanoclusters contributed a noticeable decrease in the dehydrogenation temperature of LiBH4. The dehydrogenation peak temperature of the composite is lowered from 360 °C to 330 °C when increasing x in the Grs-xNi from 0 to 10 wt%, the latter of which is 105 °C lower than that of pristine LiBH4 (435 °C) (Fig. 4a). The desorption curve maintains almost unchanged when further increasing x to 20 wt%. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) examination shows the same tendency on the reduction of the desorption temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 6) with the variation of the amounts of Ni in the composites. Different from the pristine LiBH4, no obvious melting endothermic peak is observed for the Grs-xNi-combined composite, especially for the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite. The dehydrogenation capacity of the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 sample composite upon heating to 368 °C is 7.4 wt% with respect to the total contents of the nanocomposite, with an onset temperature of 100 °C (Fig. 4b), corresponding to ca.10.5 wt% of H2 with respect to sole LiBH4, which is nearly 4 times that of the pristine LiBH4 (~2.7 wt%) under the same condition. Isothermal examination reveals a much higher dehydrogenation rate for the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite than the pristine LiBH4 and the LiBH4-Grs composite. There is ca. 8 wt% H2 released within 35 min at 350 °C, while the pristine LiBH4 only liberates ca.1.7 wt% H2 (Fig. 4c). For the isothermal temperature of 300 °C, the average dehydrogenation rate of the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite is twice that of the LiBH4-Grs composite. There is ~6.5 wt% H2 released within 100 min for the former, and there is only 3.2 wt% H2 released for the latter (Fig. 4d). Moreover, in the case that the isothermal temperature is lower than the melting point of the LiBH4, i.e., dehydrogenation takes place in the solid state of the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite. Significantly enhanced dehydrogenation kinetics is obtained for the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite. There is ca. 6.0 wt% H2 released at 275 °C with 300 min, which is more than 4 times of that of the pristine LiBH4 (1.4 wt%) under the same condition (Supplementary Fig. 7). The desorption activation energy is calculated to be 108 ± 6 kJ mol−1 for the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite (Fig. 4e), reduced by ~40% and 7% with respect to the pristine LiBH4 (182 ± 4 kJ mol−1) (Supplementary Fig. 8) and the Grs-LiBH4 composite (116 ± 3 kJ mol−1) (Fig. 4f), respectively. These results indicate unambiguously the important role of Ni in the catalysis on the hydrogen desorption of LiBH4.

Hydrogenation of the dehydrogenation products at different temperature conducted under 100 bar H2 shows that the Ni nanoclusters reduce significantly the onset hydrogenation temperature of the dehydrogenated LiBH4 (Fig. 5a). The optimal composition is Grs-10Ni-LiBH4, which shows an extremely low onset hydrogenation temperature of 70 °C, lowered by ca. 245 °C compared with that of the dehydrogenated pristine LiBH4 (315 °C). To the best of our knowledge, this is the lowest onset hydrogenation temperature for LiBH4-based hydrogen storage materials (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Table 2). There is ca. 8.2 wt% H2 absorbed upon heating to 350 °C and dwelling for 126 min (Supplementary Fig. 9), corresponding to ca. 12.3 wt% H2 with respect to LiBH4. The hydrogenation is completed within 300 min at 350 °C under 100 bar H2 for the dehydrogenated Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite (Fig. 5c). Moreover, the amount of hydrogen absorbed reaches 7.5 wt% H2 within 500 min at 300 °C, in contrast to 5.0 wt% H2 for the LiBH4-Grs composite (Supplementary Fig. 10) and no hydrogen is absorbed for the pristine LiBH4 (Fig. 5c). More encouragingly, the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite can reabsorb ca. 8.1 wt% H2 at a considerably low temperature of 150 °C with a sufficient period of dwelling, which is nearly full hydrogenation (Fig. 5d). All these results indicate that the Ni nanoclusters favor significantly the reversible dehydrogenation and hydrogenation of LiBH4. It is worth highlighting that the onset hydrogenation temperature of 70 °C is lower than that of the composite of graphene supported LiBH4 nanoparticles (5–10 nm) decorated with nano-sized Ni particles (130 °C) reported previously39, where LiBH4 directly grow on graphene. In the present work, the interlayer of LiBH4 nanoparticles in the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite are mostly nucleate on the Ni nanoclusters, therefore, there is a physical separation between LiBH4 and graphene by the Ni nanocluster layer. The direct contact of Ni and LiBH4 facilitate the cleavage of H-H bonds and facilitate the formation B-H bonds, thus improves the reversibility of dehydrogenation and hydrogenation.

The hydrogen cycling performance of the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite is evaluated in an isothermal mode (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Fig. 11). In contrast to the pristine LiBH4, which shows a rapid decay in the dehydrogenation capacity with only 40% remaining after 5 cycles, the dehydrogenation capacity of the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite maintains 8.1 wt% after 30 cycles with negligible decay. As shown in Fig. 5f, the pile of the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite maintains almost stable after hydrogenation, whereas obvious foam eruption generates in the pristine LiBH4 (Fig. 5g). The stable pile of the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite during cycling is believed to be an important factor for its superior cycling ability. The dehydrogenation kinetics somewhat slows down (Supplementary Fig. 12), possibly due to the slight aggregation and growth in size (Supplementary Fig. 13). Nevertheless, the decrease in dehydrogenation kinetics is still slower than that of the LiBH4-Grs composite (Supplementary Fig. 12), further confirming the important role of the catalytic Ni nanoclusters and nanoconfinement of LiBH4 by the graphene during cycling. Moreover, the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite delivers the highest desorption kinetics for the 2nd cycle of dehydrogenation (Fig. 5h), which confirms that this composition achieves the optimal synergistic effect of catalysis and nanoconfinement to LiBH4. Supplementary Table 2 lists the hydrogen storage properties of the present work and of the representative Ni-based or nanostructured LiBH4 reported in literatures. The present work is among the best in terms of dehydrogenation temperature, hydrogenation temperature and cyclic performance.

Mechanism investigation

The influence of the factors of the morphology, nanostructure and composition on the hydrogen storage reversibility is further investigated. For the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite, SEM observation reveals that its morphology featuring as nanoparticles dispersed on the support are mostly stable after either dehydrogenation and hydrogenation (Supplementary Fig. 14a, b). Whereas for the Grs-6Ni-LiBH4 and Grs-20Ni-LiBH4 composites, nanorods, which are identified as B species, generate after dehydrogenation and hydrogenation (Supplementary Figs. 15, 16), particularly in their hydrogenated samples (Supplementary Fig. 15b, d). The formation of the nanorod-shaped B species results in a separation of the regenerated LiBH4 from the Ni nanoclusters, consequently losing catalytic effect from Ni. Further FTIR spectra demonstrate the formation of the highly thermal stable intermediate of Li2B12H12 in both Grs-6Ni-Grs and Grs-20Ni-Grs composites after dehydrogenation and hydrogenation, which lowers the reversibility (Supplementary Fig. 17). Moreover, metallic Ni is still identified by XPS after dehydrogenation and hydrogenation at 300 °C (Supplementary Fig. 14c). FTIR results reveal the disappearance and reappearance of [BH4]- group in LiBH4 without any signs of [B12H12]2- (Supplementary Fig. 14d), which are further confirmed by solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (ss-NMR) spectra of 11B and high-resolution B 1s XPS spectra (Supplementary Fig. 14e, f). In addition, Ni-B species are not detected in the dehydrogenated product at 300 °C by XRD, XPS and NMR (Supplementary Figs. 14c, e, 18a), but are observed in the dehydrogenated product at 350 °C (Supplementary Fig. 18a, b). So far, it is concluded that the stable trilayered nanostructure, small particle size, high catalytic activity of Ni, and effective separation of LiBH4 from graphene response for the significantly lowered operation temperatures and improved reversibility of the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite prepared by the layer-by-layer assembly.

Further electronic analysis of Ni-B adsorption in Fig. 6a shows a significant accumulation of electrons at the Ni-B interface, which mostly surround the B atoms. This electronic redistribution effectively compensates the electron deficiency of the B clusters. Meanwhile, the electron transfer induced by Ni and the structural changes in B significantly affect the strength of the B-B bond. The analysis by the crystal orbital Hamiltonian population method (COHP) (Fig. 6b, c) indicates that the distribution of the strength of B-B bonding orbitals changes notably. With the action of Ni, the distribution of the strength of the B-B bond more approaches Gaussian feature, where the average and minimum bond strengths all decrease, which facilitate the adsorption of H atoms onto B, and eventually lead to the formation of the [BH4]- group. Further partial density of state (PDOS) calculations (Fig. 6d–f) show that the adsorption of C on B leads to significant p-orbital hybridization, which causes a shift of the B valence band away from the Fermi level. This shift is unfavorable for the electronic transfer during H atom adsorption. In contrast, the Ni d-electrons reduce the electronic localization of B, enhancing the metallic character and electron-donating ability of the B atoms on the Ni surface. The above results provide more evidences for what obtained in Fig. 1d, that the adsorption of B on Ni results in a reduction in the adsorption energy of the B atoms, in comparison with those of the B-B bond and B on C.

To understand the hydrogen adsorption behavior of B clusters on the Ni surface, ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations are performed at 500 K. Structural changes (Supplementary Fig. 19) clearly reveal four distinct behaviors of H₂ within the system: H dissociation, hydrogen migration on the Ni surface, hydrogen migration from the Ni surface to the B surface, and H migration on the B surface (including migration of the B-H group). Transition state searches and kinetic energy barrier calculations for hydrogen adsorption are performed using the climbing-image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) and improved dimer method (IDM) to quantitatively describe the enhanced hydrogen adsorption kinetics of B clusters on Ni. As shown in Fig. 6g, the maximum thermodynamic energy change for hydrogen dissociation and migration on the Ni-B surface is −0.42 eV (H₂ dissociation on the Ni surface), with the highest reaction energy barrier being only 0.42 eV (H migration on the B surface). In contrast, for pure B cluster (Fig. 6h), the H₂ dissociation and H migration processes exhibit a relatively low dissociation barrier of 0.48 eV, but involve a significant thermodynamic energy change of 3.18 eV. Furthermore, the H migration barrier for pure B clusters is as high as 2.90 eV, whereas for Ni-B, it is only 0.42 eV. The result suggests that H atoms migrate more easily on the B surface after dissociation, creating a chemical potential gradient that facilitates subsequent phase transition processes. Further FTIR analysis indicates the appearance of Ni-H bonds41 in the Grs-10Ni support after hydrogenation under 100 bar H2 at 350 °C, proving the presence of hydrogen overflow in the Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 nanocomposite during hydrogenation (Supplementary Fig. 20). Thus, the hydrogen overflow resulting from the direct contact between the Ni surface and B clusters, along with the separation of graphene and B clusters in the layered structure, leads to a reduction in the energy barriers for hydrogen dissociation and migration. This reduction in energy barriers improves the hydrogen adsorption performance of the system.

Discussion

In summary, a trilayered nanostructured composite composed of graphene support layer, Ni nanocluster layer and LiBH4 nanoparticle layer in sequence is fabricated by a layer-by-layer assembly strategy with LiBH4 nanoparticles growing on Ni nanoclusters, which are deposited on the surface of the graphene support. Such unique trilayered nanostructure enables a reversible storage of 12.3 wt% H2 with respect to LiBH4 initiating from 100 °C for desorption and 70 °C for absorption. The dehydrogenated LiBH4 can be hydrogenated almost fully at 150 °C under 100 bar H2. The Ni nanocluster interlayer acts as nucleation sites for LiBH4 nanoparticles and also avoids the physical contact of LiBH4 and graphene, favoring the regeneration of B-H bonds and effectively eliminating the foaming of the material upon dehydrogenation. Moreover, the Ni nanoclusters facilitate the cleavage of B clusters and the H-H bonds, thereby creating more H-atom adsorption sites on the cleaved B clusters. Theses cleaved B clusters have weaker hydrogen adsorption energies compared with pure ones, which significantly demonstrates a superior action of Ni in facilitating the splitting, adsorption and migration of hydrogen in the hydrogenation process. As a result, a dehydrogenation capacity of 8.1 wt% is maintained after 30 cycles with negligible decay in a moderate cyclic regime. This breakthrough sheds light on how to design light-metal borohydrides for practical hydrogen storage applications.

Methods

Materials and preparation

Raw materials

Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (99.99%, Aladin), n-butyl lithium (C4H9Li, n-BuLi, 2.0 M in cyclohexane, Sigma-Aldrich), triethylamine borane ((C2H5)3NBH3, 97%, Xiya), n-hexane (99%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co.), few-layer graphene (99%, XFNano Inc.) and absolute ethanol (≥99.7, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co.) were purchased.

Preparation of Grs-xNi

248 mg Ni(NO3)2·6H2O and 50 mg few-layer graphene were ultrasonicated in 20 mL absolute ethanol for 20 min, and the mixture was then continuously stirred at 500 rpm for 8 h at 60 °C. After stirring, the dry black powders were placed into a tubular furnace and heated at 400 °C for 2 h with a ramp of 5 °C min−1 under 10% H2-90% Ar gas mixture at a flow rate of 30 mL min−1. After natural cooling to room temperature, the final products named as graphene-10 wt% Ni (Grs-10Ni) were obtained. By adjusting the mass ratio of few-layer graphene to Ni(NO3)2·6H2O while keeping the mass of few-layer graphene constant (50 mg), the products named as graphene-x wt% Ni (Grs-xNi) can be successfully obtained through the aforementioned preparation process, where the x values are 0, 2, 6, 10 and 20.

Preparation of Grs-xNi-LiBH4

The composites of Grs-xNi-LiBH4 were synthesized via a hydrogen-assisted solvothermal reaction. Firstly, 30 mg of the as-prepared Grs-xNi was mixed with 50 mL n-hexane. Then, 1.6 mL of 2.7 M C4H9Li solution (in hexane) and 645 μL of (C2H5)3NBH3 solution were added dropwise to the above solution under stirring. The mixture was exposed to ultrasonic waves generated by an ultrasonic horn with an output power of 600 W (20 kHz, FS600-N, SXSONIC Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) in a Rosset cell. Ultrasonic waves were applied in the pulsed mode with cycles including 5 s pulses and 5 s relaxation, each lasting 30 min. After 4 cycles of ultrasound treatment, the mixture was then transferred into a 150 mL custom-made stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 100 °C for 3 h under 50 bar H2 with constant stirring. After cooling to room temperature, the obtained products were collected by vacuum filtration, washed with n-hexane for several times, and dried at 90 °C through dynamic vacuum to remove triethylamine. The obtained samples were named as Grs-xNi-LiBH4, x represents the mass ratio of Ni nanoparticles in the Grs-Ni support.

Materials characterizations

The crystal structures of the samples were identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD) MiniFlex 600 (Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm, 40 kV and 15 mA). The data were collected in a 0.05° increment between scattering angle of 10–90° (2θ) at room temperature. A custom-designed sample holder with a window covered by Scotch tape for transmission of X-ray was used to prevent the exposure to air. The FTIR spectra were recorded with a Bruker Tensor 27 unit (Germany) in the transmission mode. The test pellets were prepared by first mixing dried KBr with sample powders at a weight ratio of 200:1 and then cold pressing under 10 MPa. The spectra were created after 16 scans with 4 cm−1 of resolution. The morphologies of the samples were observed by SEM(Hitachi SU8010). The sample powders were dispersed on electrically conducting adhesive tapes in a glove box and transferred into the SEM chamber under protective Ar using a custom holder. More details of the microstructures of the samples were observed and analyzed by TEM (HT-770, Hitachi), HRTEM, SAED and EDS. The sample powders were dispersed on Cu grids which were then loaded in a double-tilt vacuum transfer holder (Gatan 648, USA and FEI Tecnai G2 F20 S-TWIN, USA). FIB-TEM was employed to examine the trilayer structure of Grs-10Ni-LiBH4 composite. The sample was imaged first by SEM (Scios 2 DualBeam, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). A carbon deposition layer was applied to protect the regions of interest on the sample surface within the SEM/FIB system. Then, a thin foil was formed through a process of stripping and final thinning. The thickness of the thinned foil was about 100 nm. TEM, (FEI Tecnai G2 F20 S-TWIN, FEI) was employed to characterize the thinned foils at an operating voltage of 200 kV. The valance states of Ni and B of the samples were analyzed by XPS (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha) with a monochromatic Al Kα (hν: 1486.6 eV) X-ray source and calibrated with the adventitious C peak at 284.8 eV as the reference. Solid-state 11B and 7Li magic angle spinning NMR analysis was performed on a Bruker AVANCE NEO 600WB spectrometer. The sample powders were packed into a SiO2 NMR tube and sealed with a tightly fitted cap inside an Ar-filled glove box. The operating frequencies for 7Li and 11B were 233.34 and 192.64 MHz, respectively. The NMR shifts were reported in parts per million (ppm) referenced to BF3OEt2 and LiClO4, respectively.

All the operations that need to avoid the air and moisture are performed in an Etelux glove box (Lab 2000, P. R. China).

Hydrogen cycling measurements

A home-built temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) system attached to a mass spectrometer (MS) (Hiden QIC-20, England) was used for qualitative measurements of dehydrogenation, where pure Ar with a flow rate of 20 mL min−1 was used as a carrier gas. The sample was heated from room temperature to 550 °C at 2 °C min–1 for TPD testing. A custom-made Sievert’s-type equipment was used for the quantitative measurement under isothermal or non-isothermal conditions. Approximately 30 mg of the sample was used for each measurement. The non-isothermal volumetric hydrogen release was conducted under primary vacuum (ca. 10−3 Torr) with the temperature ramping at 2 °C min–1. The dehydrogenated sample was hydrogenated from room temperature to 350 °C at a heating rate of 1 °C min–1 under 100 bar H2. The isothermal measurements were conducted by rapidly heating (10 °C min−1) the sample to a desired temperature, maintaining for a certain duration at this temperature. Thermal analysis was performed by DSC measurement (NETZSCH DSC 200F3). Approximately 2 mg of sample is loaded into an Al2O3 crucible and heated from 30 °C to 550 °C at 2 °C min–1.

Theoretical calculations

First-principles calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)42. The electronic and ionic interactions were described within the framework of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional43. Before selecting kinetic cut off energy and k-points grid, the extensive convergence test has been performed. The planewave basis-set cutoff was set to 500 eV, with convergence criteria of 10⁻⁵ eV for energy and 0.02 eV/Å for structural optimization forces. The Brillouin zones were sampled using 2 × 2 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack k-point meshes.

A small B cluster was modeled using an octahedral cluster built from six B₁₂ units, and a Ni(110) surface with a 6 × 6 cubic cell containing four atomic layers was used as the slab model. A pristine 6 × 6 graphene supercell containing 72 carbon atoms was selected as the support layer, with ~15 Å vacuum added in the z-direction to avoid interlayer interactions. All atoms were fully relaxed during structural optimizations. To determine the optimal adsorption configuration of the B cluster on graphene, multiple initial geometries were constructed, where the cluster was placed at a distance of ca. 2.5–3.0 Å above the graphene surface, in line with typical van der Waals physisorption distances. These initial placements were designed in terms of maximizing the contact area between the cluster and the graphene sheet, allowing multiple atoms in the cluster to interact with the π-conjugated surface, which represents natural anchoring modes. Geometry optimizations were performed for all candidate configurations, and the most stable adsorption structure was selected based on the calculated binding energies. The results are summarized in Supplementary Fig. 21, which shows the relative binding energies of different adsorption modes, confirming that the chosen model corresponds to the most thermodynamically favorable configuration. AIMD simulations embedded in the VASP code were conducted using a time step of 1 fs in an NVT ensemble with a Nosé-Hoover thermostat. Simulations were run at 500 K to observe the dynamics of H atoms, with energy and force convergence set to 10–5 eV and 0.02 eV/Å, respectively.

Transition states for hydrogen dissociation, adsorption, and migration were located using the CI-NEB and IDM44,45. Bonding interactions were analyzed via COHP method in the LOBSTER software package46,47.
