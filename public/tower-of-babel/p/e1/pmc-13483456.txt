Efficiency-weighted cooling degree days reveal opposing temperature and humidity effects on energy demand
Authors: Jake W. Casselman, Christina Karamperidou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cooling degree days (CDD) are widely used to estimate air-conditioning energy demand, yet they implicitly assume constant refrigeration efficiency, neglecting its dependence on temperature and humidity. We introduce an efficiency-aware cooling metric –effective cooling degree days (eCDD)– that links ambient temperature and humidity to the physical work required for cooling. Here we show that, across North America, cooling efficiency has declined by 2–4% per decade since 1971, and that regionally opposing trends in temperature and humidity cause CDD to misrepresent cooling demand. During hot extremes, efficiency losses are amplified under humid-heat but partially offset under dry-heat. Projections reveal a continent-scale shift in humidity regimes, with an eastward extension of dry heat that enhances cooling efficiency during extremes, while eCDD increases by 10–80%. These results demonstrate that temperature-based metrics alone are insufficient and efficiency-aware metrics are essential for accurately assessing cooling demand, especially considering differing electricity generation mixes.

Introduction

Cooling infrastructure underpins public health, economic activity, and global cold-chain logistics, and its importance is intensifying as exposure to heat and humidity increases1–3. The air-conditioning (AC) sector is now a nearly $100 billion (USD) market with about 100 million units sold each year, and household AC penetration is projected to rise from 27% to 41%, increasing residential cooling electricity demand from 1220 to 1940 terawatt-hours (TWh) year−14–6, trajectories that threaten power-system reliability and amplify household energy burdens.

Cooling degree days (CDD), defined as the cumulative temperature exceedance above a base threshold, are widely used as a proxy for cooling demand, implicitly assuming equal energy requirement per degree. The metric was originally developed as an analog to heating degree days, rather than from first-principles cooling physics7. This requires system efficiency to remain constant, even though ambient temperature and humidity jointly control the vapor-compression cycle efficiency. Recent extensions that use wet-bulb temperature, humidity, or population weighting8–10 improve how CDD is represented but still treat all degrees of exceedance as thermodynamically equivalent. By ignoring efficiency changes, CDD conflates thermal forcing with required work, introducing a state-dependent bias in energy demand estimates.

Cooling efficiency is governed by vapor-compression thermodynamics and by the partitioning of heat between sensible and latent components. Rising temperatures increase the thermodynamic lift required for heat rejection, reducing system performance, while higher humidity further degrades efficiency by diverting capacity toward latent heat removal. As a result, hot-dry conditions primarily penalize cooling through increased compressor work, whereas hot-humid conditions impose an additional loss by suppressing sensible cooling. Cooling demand metrics that ignore these efficiency variations provide an incomplete representation of cooling demand. Consistent with this framework, experiments show efficiency losses of 40–54% across operating conditions, compounding electricity use and peak load stress11–13.

Therefore, projecting cooling demand requires metrics that reflect the physical work performed by cooling systems, rather than temperature exceedance alone. Here we introduce a thermodynamically-informed reframing of cooling demand metrics that links ambient thermal conditions directly to the thermodynamic effort required for cooling. By accounting for how temperature and humidity jointly govern cooling-system efficiency, we replace the implicit constant-efficiency assumption embedded in conventional cooling degree days. This framework yields an efficiency-aware cooling demand metric – effective cooling degree days (eCDD) – that transforms cooling degree days from a purely temperature-based index into a thermodynamically meaningful measure of cooling work. Using this approach across North America, we show that conventional temperature-based metrics systematically mischaracterize cooling demand, motivating a shift from empirical degree-day proxies to physics-based cooling demand indicators.

Results

Climatic controls of cooling efficiency

Cooling efficiency is fundamentally state-dependent, rather than constant, with the Coefficient of Performance (COP, the ratio of heat removed to work input) reflecting temperature-dependent compressor performance, and the Sensible Heat Ratio (SHR) describing the partitioning between sensible temperature reduction and latent moisture removal. This interaction produces a “tug-of-war” between the impacts of refrigeration load on the compressor and the balance between latent and sensible heat removal, where efficiency loss from COP can be amplified or offset by decreases or increases in SHR (Fig. 1c). To capture the interaction between SHR and COP, we define an effective coefficient of performance (eCOP, COP multiplied by SHR, see Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). eCOP has declined by approximately 1.6–2.4% per decade across much of the United States, with some regions experiencing declines of over 8% per decade (e.g., western Canada, Fig. 1d). Surprisingly, other areas, such as the Pacific Southwest, show negligible or even positive trends (though statistically insignificant) when accounting for the offsetting by SHR.

To capture the relationship of cooling energy demand with efficiency, we create the efficiency-weighted cooling degree days (eCDD), defined as CDD divided by eCOP (Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. 2). Summertime eCDD has increased by 4–12% per decade over most of North America, with certain regions over the Rockies exceeding 20% per decade (Fig. 2a). Conversely, across much of the Southeast and central United States, CDD underestimates trends by up to 3% per decade (Fig. 2b). Regions of significant over- and underestimation align with long-term trends in SHR (Fig. 1b), indicating that CDD performs poorly where SHR trends are substantial. The shortcoming of CDD is further shown near anomalous regional climate dynamics, such as with central U.S. warming hole, where daily maximum temperature have remained constant or decreased15. Here, CDD trends are weak, as temperature alone is considered, yet produces significantly different eCDD trends. Overall, these results demonstrate that conventional CDD fails to capture key regional differences in cooling demand, and that efficiency weighting is required for regions where temperature and inlet moisture stress evolve jointly.

During extreme events, defined here as days with temperature exceeding the 95th percentile, many areas southeast of the Central Plains have a lower eCDD anomaly when compared to much of Canada, U.S. Pacific Northwest, Great Basin, and Northern Rockies, which have elevated eCDD anomalies that are up to 3 standard deviations (Fig. 2c). The differences with efficiency weighting CDD (Fig. 2d) closely tracks with the response of both SHR and eCOP (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Where extreme heat coincides with positive SHR anomalies (drier conditions), CDD tends to overestimate cooling demand, whereas in regions with accompanying negative SHR anomalies (wetter conditions) the discrepancy reverses sign. This asymmetry reflects CDD’s assumption of fixed cooling efficiency: for eCDD, reduced inlet moisture stress increases SHR and lowers the effective cooling penalty, whereas increased inlet moisture stress reduces SHR and raises it. Here, dry and humid heat extremes arise from differences in land-atmosphere coupling, with radiation-driven forcing producing dry events under soil-moisture limitation and humid events where evaporation remains active16.

The importance of distinguishing inlet moisture stress (i.e., vapor pressure) from the relative humidity in shaping the SHR anomaly is exemplified by the 1995 Chicago and 2012 Central U.S. heat waves (Supplementary Fig. 5 and 6). For both events, anomalously high saturation vapor pressure indicated a thermodynamic capacity for vapor pressure to increase, provided sufficient moisture was available in the surrounding environment. During the 1995 event, vapor pressure rose at approximately the same rate as the saturation vapor pressure, resulting in increased inlet moisture stress, while the relative humidity anomaly was essentially flat. This produced a negative SHR anomaly and anomalously high eCDD compared with CDD. Conversely, in 2012 vapor pressure was anomalously low resulting in decreased inlet moisture stress and anomalously high SHR, as well as a negative relative humidity, leading to anomalously low eCDD compared to CDD. Thus, these two cases show that relying solely on the relative humidity to understand how eCDD will evolve is insufficient and that one must consider the role of inlet moisture stress in determining SHR and, consequently, eCDD.

Projections of effective cooling demand

Model simulations show that the response of eCDD to a high emissions scenario by mid-century (2015-2050) is projected to increase 10–80% across most of the continent and particularly large increases of up to 150% in northern regions (Fig. 3a). These regions of extreme increase occur in areas where there have historically been minimal cooling degree days (Supplementary Fig. 7), consistent with past studies17,18. Over the Central U.S. regions, eCDD outpaces CDD less strongly than in surrounding regions, although it remains statistically significant in the multi-model analysis. This region of reduced divergence likely reflects compensating effects between SHR trends and declining compressor efficiency (COP), which occur over similar areas (Supplementary Fig. 7c), consistent with the tug-of-war identified in reanalysis. It should be noted that this analysis should be treated as a comparative case-study world rather than a predictive benchmark, also considering demonstrated temperature-humidity biases in models19,20.

Projected eCDD anomalies during hot extremes are significantly lower than in the present, indicating that for the same level of extreme heat (i.e., 95th percentile), the relative cooling-energy requirement is reduced compared to present-day conditions (Fig. 3c). Interestingly, differences in eCDD and CDD response during extremes are approximately the same to those found in reanalysis (i.e., Fig. 2d) for both present and future climates. This indicates that climate models can successfully capture the dry-heat versus humid-heat dichotomy over North America (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9). In future projections, the central North America region of CDD overestimation (i.e., negative regions in Fig. 2d, and Supplementary Fig. 10) expands eastward, consistent with an increasing spatial extent of dry-heat extremes (Fig. 3d). However, central North America drying contrasts with the wetting trend observed in reanalysis, likely reflecting a common climate-model bias20. This error persists despite present and future simulations accurately reproducing the background SHR climatology (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b).

Regions facing the largest projected increases in effective cooling demand tend to coincide with electricity systems that already rely less on fossil fuels and more on hydroelectric, nuclear, or other non-fossil sources (e.g., northeast and northwest U.S.; Fig. 4b, c). This association may partly arise from large-scale geographic structure, whereby northern grid regions most exposed to rising cooling demand often overlap with areas rich in hydropower along the eastern and western margins21, or with wind-dominated (but hydro-limited) systems across the central United States22. In contrast, projected eCDD growth is generally smaller in the southeastern U.S., where both hydropower and wind resources are comparatively limited (i.e., bottom right in Fig. 4c).

A notable exception occurs in grid regions west of the Great Lakes where increases in cooling demand are more likely to translate into additional fossil-fuel generation. These regions combine relatively large eCDD increases with limited low-carbon generation potential, owing to scarce hydropower, suboptimal wind resources, and northern latitudes that constrain solar output23. Importantly, these same regions are also those where conventional CDD more closely tracks eCDD, suggesting that earlier temperature-based analyses have likely captured future cooling demand growth reasonably well. Conversely, regions in southern central United States tend to exhibit smaller eCDD increases for their given emissions profile, often coinciding with abundant wind resources. Nonetheless, the observed inverse association between fossil-fuel share and projected eCDD change does not imply a causal relationship, i.e., that cleaner electricity systems reduce cooling-demand; rather, it reflects that regions facing the largest increases in cooling demand are, in many cases, already comparatively decarbonized.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that incorporating refrigeration thermodynamics and sensible-latent heat partitioning into cooling demand metrics fundamentally alters regional projections of energy needs. We introduce an efficiency-weighted cooling degree day (eCDD) metric, which accounts for temperature-dependent compressor performance and the humidity-sensitive fraction of cooling associated with sensible heat removal. Our results show that conventional temperature-only metrics, such as cooling degree days (CDD), miss important spatial and temporal patterns in efficiency-weighted cooling demand, particularly in regions where moisture trends alter the sensible-latent partitioning of cooling.

Historical data indicate that while cooling efficiency generally declines as temperatures rise, regional moisture trends can mitigate or exacerbate temperature-driven efficiency losses. As a result, eCDD anomalies during extreme heat events, and also their long term trends, can differ significantly from their corresponding CDD counterparts. Notably, during extreme heat events, humid extremes lower SHR and amplify efficiency losses, increasing the energy burden, while dry extremes raise SHR and can partially offset the penalties imposed by temperature. This humidity-dependence mirrors a broader recognition that co-occurring temperature and moisture anomalies can produce disproportionate impacts relative to either driver alone24. Recent extensions of the CDD framework have proposed wet-bulb temperature or enthalpy as the ambient forcing variable to better capture the full thermal burden on occupants9,25,26. Our results extend this logic to the mechanical side: humidity plays an equally critical role in governing cooling system efficiency, a compounding that no temperature- or wet-bulb-based metric alone can resolve.

Future climate projections show that effective cooling demand, as quantified here by eCDD, is expected to outpace the growth in temperature-driven demand, as captured by CDD, by 7.5–15.0% over much of the United States, while over 30% in areas in the far north. However, during hot extremes, the additional effective cooling demand is projected to be lower in the future for the same relative temperature anomaly, consistent with the tendency for relative humidity to decline on the hottest days under warming27, which raises SHR and partially offsets the compressor efficiency penalty. Efficiency dynamics mediated by both vapor-compression thermodynamics and humidity effects on SHR also introduce important regional structure that cannot be captured with simple temperature exceedance. Yet accurately characterizing this structure depends on climate models robustly reproducing the partitioning between dry and humid heat regimes, a known difficulty, as coarser-resolution models systematically misrepresent the moisture conditions associated with extreme heat20. Resolving this remains essential not only for human heat stress projections, but for accurate estimates of the mechanical cooling burden that depends on the same thermodynamic drivers28.

Aggregating effective cooling demand (eCDD) change at the grid level reveals spatial disparities in risk across the U.S. power system, with direct implications for demand forecasting, energy infrastructure planning, and climate adaptation. Regions with the largest projected increases in cooling demand tend to have lower shares of fossil-fuel generation. Treating CDD as a proxy for energy use introduces region- and state-dependent biases that can lead to misallocation of resources and misrepresentation of risk, particularly in regions where efficiency changes are greatest. By accounting for temperature-dependent compressor performance and SHR-linked moisture effects, eCDD provides a thermodynamically-informed basis for comparing regional efficiency-weighted cooling demand and for integrating demand projections with supply-side constraints (e.g., energy mix and infrastructure) and mitigation strategies. However, eCDD should be interpreted as an efficiency-weighted CDD proxy, rather than a complete estimate of total cooling load, because it captures temperature-dependent compressor performance and sensible–latent partitioning but does not represent all building-, system-, and behavior-specific drivers of cooling demand.

While this study employs a simplified refrigeration model, the framework is intended as an initial step to move beyond temperature-only CDD, demonstrating how explicitly accounting for efficiency is essential for diagnosing regional differences in efficiency-weighted cooling demand and for articulating the climatic requirements that future cooling technologies must meet. Future work should seek to integrate eCDD with detailed building and grid simulations (e.g., building energy models and management systems), and should account for evolving technologies and standards (e.g., alternative refrigerants, dehumidification, and more physically detailed system representations). Future work should also seek to combine the efficiency-weighting approach developed here with emerging efforts to improve the demand side of CDD itself, including wet-bulb-, enthalpy-, or psychrometric-load-based exposure metrics, thereby accounting for both the climatic drivers of cooling load and the efficiency with which that load is met. Furthermore, we focus primarily on long-term trends and extremes, while interannual variability is reserved for future investigation. Uncertainties in climate projections remain an area of concern, particularly regarding humidity trends and land-atmosphere interactions, as noted above. For example, model-derived humidity diagnostics may be indirectly affected by known biases in land-surface evapotranspiration partitioning over North America, which could potentially alter relative humidity as well as the demarcation between regions where humid-heat or dry-heat events occur29.

In summary, an efficiency-aware cooling degree day metric, eCDD, is important for projecting future efficiency-weighted cooling demand in regions where temperature and humidity co-evolve. This work presents a framework connecting climate and the thermodynamic principles of refrigeration, clearly demonstrating where the implicit assumptions underlying cooling degree days fall short. Viewed through this lens, cooling efficiency is governed by climate, not merely engineered into machines.

Idealized refrigeration model

We create an idealized refrigeration model to capture the thermodynamic dependence of cooling efficiency on environmental conditions and use a single refrigerant, R134a, as a representative example of how efficiency responds to changes in the climate. The model calculates the change in enthalpy (h) between the four principal stages of the vapor-compression cycle (h1, h2, h3, h4). In this framework, h1 → h2 represents isentropic compression, h2 → h3 condensation at high pressure, h3 → h4 isenthalpic expansion, and h4 → h1 evaporation at low pressure.

Several key assumptions are applied in these calculations. First, the evaporator temperature is fixed at 0∘C, which provides a midpoint between typical operating conditions for refrigerators (−50 to −25∘C)31 and air conditioners (approximately 6.85∘C)32. This choice establishes an idealized baseline for comparing system performance without biasing the analysis toward either application type, while avoiding effects associated with sub-freezing evaporator temperatures (e.g., frost accumulation, reduced effective heat exchange). Second, we assume an isentropic efficiency of 0.8 as a representative mid-range value33. Finally, we assume a constant temperature difference (TΔ) of 10∘C between the condenser temperature and outside ambient temperature34. The use of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\max ({w}_{{{\rm{in}}}}-{w}_{{{\rm{out}}}},0)$$\end{document}max(win−wout,0) in the latent heat term ensures that the latent cooling component is only considered when moisture is actually removed from the air, i.e., when the inlet humidity ratio exceeds the outlet humidity ratio. This prevents unphysical negative latent heat values that would otherwise imply condensation occurring in reverse (moisture addition), which does not contribute to cooling.

The humidity ratio (w) (i.e., the mass of water vapor per unit mass of dry air) is computed using the following psychrometric relationship in below equation: 5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w=0.622\,\frac{{p}_{v}}{{p}_{{{\rm{atm}}}}-{p}_{v}},$$\end{document}w=0.622pvpatm−pv,where pv is the partial vapor pressure of water vapor (Pa), and patm is the total atmospheric pressure (Pa). The partial vapor pressure is obtained from the product of the relative humidity (RH) and the saturation vapor pressure (psat) at a given air temperature TK, as given in below equation: 6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{v}={{\rm{RH}}}\times {p}_{{{\rm{sat}}}}({T}_{K}).$$\end{document}pv=RH×psat(TK).

The saturation vapor pressure is estimated using the Magnus approximation, as given in below equation: 7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{{{\rm{sat}}}}({T}_{K})=610.78\,\exp \left[\frac{17.27\,({T}_{K}-273.15)}{({T}_{K}-273.15)+237.3}\right].$$\end{document}psat(TK)=610.78exp17.27(TK−273.15)(TK−273.15)+237.3.

We assume a constant atmospheric pressure of patm = 101, 325Pa and set the evaporator saturation temperature to Tevap = 273.15K, matching the evaporator temperature itself. Additional model parameters are held fixed to typical reference values: approach temperature = 1.0K; specific heat capacity of air Cp = 1020.05J kg−1 K−1; latent heat of vaporization Hfg = 2.501 × 106J kg−1; and outlet relative humidity RHout = 1.0 (saturated).

This framework allows us to define the effective coefficient of performance (eCOP), as given in below equation: 8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{eCOP}}}={{\rm{COP}}}\times {{\rm{SHR}}},$$\end{document}eCOP=COP×SHR,thereby incorporating the effect of humidity on sensible-latent load partitioning, rather than treating all cooling as useful sensible temperature reduction. In this formulation, COP captures the temperature-dependent performance of the vapor-compression cycle, while SHR captures the fraction of total cooling delivered as sensible cooling. The eCOP therefore reflects two distinct effects: the reduction in compressor performance under higher temperature lift, and the reduction in useful sensible cooling when part of the cooling load is diverted toward latent moisture removal. Together, these terms ensure that eCOP reflects a more realistic measure of system efficiency under varying thermal and moisture conditions.

Index definitions and diagnostics

The cooling degree days (CDD) index is calculated relative to a threshold of 18 ∘C, as given in below equation: 9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{CDD}}}={\sum }_{i=1}^{n}\max \left(0,{T}_{{{\rm{amb}}},i}-{T}_{{{\rm{thresh}}}}\right),$$\end{document}CDD=∑i=1nmax0,Tamb,i−Tthresh,where Tamb,i is the hourly t2m. Leap-day data (February 29) are removed to maintain consistent year lengths across the record. The index is presented in degree-hours, and normalization by the constant factor of 24 to convert to degree-days was omitted, as it does not affect the derived relative measures (percentage change, z-scores, etc.) central to this analysis. Conversion to degree-days can be achieved by dividing by 24. We chose 18 ∘C threshold (Tthresh), as this was the most commonly used value across disciplines, including climate science8, futures markets36, and energy demand analysis37.

To incorporate the thermodynamic efficiency of cooling systems into cooling degree days, we define an effective cooling degree day (eCDD) index. The eCDD index is therefore expressed as: 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{eCDD}}}={\sum }_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\max \left(0,\,{T}_{{{\rm{amb}}},i}-{T}_{{{\rm{thresh}}}}\right)}{{{\rm{eCOP}}}({T}_{{{\rm{amb}}},i})}.$$\end{document}eCDD=∑i=1nmax0,Tamb,i−TthresheCOP(Tamb,i).

As CMIP6 fields are daily rather than hourly resolution, we approximate cooling degree days using the UK Met Office method38 which uses the minimum, maximum, and mean daily temperature to approximate the diurnal cycle. To validate this approximation, we compared daily estimates with values computed directly from hourly data and found close agreement for temperature, efficiency, and eCDD metrics on days exceeding the 18∘C cooling threshold (see Supplementary Fig. 12).

Extreme-temperature analyses for both the historical reanalysis and CMIP6 projections follow a consistent procedure. First, we remove February 29 from all datasets to obtain a uniform 365-day calendar. At each grid point, we then detrend daily temperature and all other diagnostic fields using a linear least-squares fit over the full analysis period (1971–2020 for ERA5; 1979–2014 and 2015–2050 for the CMIP present and future segments, respectively). From the detrended fields, we construct a day-of-year climatology and corresponding standard deviation for each calendar day by averaging over all available years. Daily standardized anomalies (z-scores) are then computed as the detrended anomaly divided by the day-of-year standard deviation at each grid point. Hot extremes are defined on a pointwise basis as all days between May and September (MJJAS) for which the t2m z-score exceeds +1.645 (95th percentile). Composites for each field (e.g., SHR, eCOP, eCDD) are formed by averaging the corresponding standardized anomalies over all hot days at each latitude and longitude location, such that the number of contributing events varies spatially according to the local frequency of extremes. For the CMIP analyses, the standardization (climatology and variance) is performed separately for the present and future periods, ensuring that z-scores and composites are defined relative to each period’s own internal variability.

Statistical significance is assessed using a nonparametric bootstrap with 1000 resamples, drawn with replacement. For each bootstrap iteration, n years are randomly selected, where n is the length of the given sample, and resampling is applied on a point-wise basis (i.e., at each latitude-longitude grid cell). Confidence intervals are determined from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. A signal is considered significant at the 95% level if the interval excludes zero (i.e., upper < 0 or lower > 0). The null hypothesis for the slope analyses is that the trend equals zero, and resampling is performed from the entire record. Field significance was additionally assessed following the approach of Wilks39 using a false discovery rate approach with an alpha of 0.1 to account for multiple testing across spatially correlated grid cells.

To assess the relative effect of including COP, we paired each pointwise eCDD slope with the corresponding CDD slope and computed the error slope field, as given in below equation: 11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Delta }_{{{\rm{slope}}}}={{{\rm{slope}}}}_{{{\rm{eCDD}}}}-{{{\rm{slope}}}}_{{{\rm{CDD}}}}$$\end{document}Δslope=slopeeCDD−slopeCDD

To assess whether the CDD and eCDD trends were significantly different, we performed a paired bootstrap with 1000 resamples, recomputing both indices’ slopes simultaneously using the same random years. The distribution of slope differences provided 95% bootstrap percentile intervals, and grid cells where these intervals excluded zero were classified as regions where the CDD and eCDD trends differ significantly. To account for spatially correlated grid cells, we additionally assessed field significance with an alpha of 0.1 following approach of Wilks39, ensuring that mapped trends are interpreted only where the spatial pattern exceeds what would be expected by chance.

Finally, to compute spatial averages of energy grids, we use the U.S. EPA’s eGRID 2023 subregion boundaries. For simple regional means (e.g., area-averaged CDD or eCDD trends), grid cells are weighted by the cosine of latitude to approximate their relative surface area. To quantify changes in population-weighted cooling demand, we instead combine eCDD with gridded population datasets. For the historical period (1979-2014), we use year 2000 population counts from the Gridded Population of the World, Version 4 (GPWv440) Population Count product, which provides a temporal midpoint. For the future period (2015-2050), we use year 2030 population projections from the Global One-Eighth Degree Urban Land Extent Projection and Base Year Grids under the SSP5 demographic projections41. Where these fields are provided as densities (persons km−2), they are converted to counts by multiplying by the corresponding grid-cell area inferred from the spherical grid geometry.

Population-weighted eCDD is then constructed on a grid-cell basis as the product of population and period-mean eCDD and summed within each eGRID region.
