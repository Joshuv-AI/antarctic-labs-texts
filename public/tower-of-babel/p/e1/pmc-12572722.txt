Perceptions Regarding mHealth for Self‐Care Among Older Adults With Chronic Heart Failure, Family Caregivers and Health Care Professionals: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Yu Chen, Yuxin Lin, Yue Yu, Xing Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Chronic heart failure (CHF) is a severe and expensive condition with a high mortality rate, frequent exacerbations, and a reduced quality of life (Trivedi et al. 2019). Prevalent among older adults, approximately 80.0% of the 37.7 million patients with CHF worldwide are older (Brennan 2018). In China, CHF affects 23.5%–30.8% of adults aged 60 years and older (Hu et al. 2019). Effective self‐care, including symptom monitoring, medication adherence, dietary management and activity adjustment, is crucial for disease management (Heart Failure Group of Chinese Society of Cardiology et al. 2018). Older adults with CHF often have other comorbid conditions and face more economic and social problems, which lead to the complexity and difficulty of self‐care. Mobile health (mHealth) is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO 2011) as a medical practice supported by mobile devices, such as mobile phones, patient monitoring devices and other wireless devices. Its incorporation into patient care has become a prominent focus in the field of health care. Chinese guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of HF also recommend mHealth as a way to improve self‐care (Heart Failure Group of Chinese Society of Cardiology et al. 2018).

With the rapid development of technological innovation, mHealth has been used in the management of many chronic diseases such as CHF, diabetes, asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (Chen and Lu 2022; Li et al. 2019; Kouri et al. 2023). It has the potential to be useful for self‐care because it can include behavioural prompts, reminders and illness monitoring (Foster et al. 2022). mHealth may be more cost‐effective than traditional face‐to‐face treatment, as it can reduce barriers to face‐to‐face treatment, improve access to treatment and reduce waiting time (Liu et al. 2022). A scoping review of 84 studies regarding mHealth among patients with CHF (Chen and Lu 2022) indicated that patients could be provided with health monitoring, health education, social support, self‐care follow‐up and reminders through mHealth. The main platforms include remote monitoring systems, applications (APPs), websites and social platforms. A large amount of research has investigated the effectiveness of mHealth in patients with CHF. Some systematic reviews have demonstrated that mHealth is beneficial in reducing all‐cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, all‐cause readmission and HF‐related readmission, improving HF knowledge and self‐care and increasing quality of life (Ding et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2022).

Background

While mHealth has become increasingly available and effective, older adults tend to adopt technology at a slower pace compared to younger generations (Han et al. 2019). Moreover, mHealth in China started late, and services specifically designed for older patients are rare (Gong et al. 2018). One study of 181 older adults with CHF in Shanghai, China, found that less than half of the patients were willing to use mHealth (Chen and Xu 2022). Knowledge about mHealth, mHealth usage, self‐rated health, perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness and self‐efficacy are important influencing factors (Chen and Xu 2022). However, perceptions of mHealth could not be obtained in this quantitative study. Chen and Lu's (2022) scoping review also noted that few qualitative studies were exploring older patients' perceptions of mHealth in China.

The CHF care system involves patients, family caregivers and health care professionals. Many older patients live with their families, and health care professionals use mHealth for health education, support and follow‐up reminders. This care system dynamic means that family caregivers and health care professionals are also important users of mHealth. Understanding their perceptions of mHealth is necessary for the development and implementation of effective mHealth‐based interventions to improve self‐care in older adults with CHF. However, to the best of our knowledge, few qualitative studies have explored the perceptions of family caregivers and health care professionals regarding mHealth in China.

The Study

Aim

This study aimed to explore the perceptions of mHealth regarding self‐care for CHF in China among older patients, family caregivers and health care professionals.

Methods

Design

This was a descriptive qualitative study. Data was collected via one‐to‐one, face‐to‐face, semi‐structured, in‐depth interviews and analyzed via content analysis. This method enables researchers to obtain information directly from participants and engage deeply with the data in the analysis and presentation processes.

Sampling and Recruitment

A purposive sampling procedure was adopted to select participants, including older adults with CHF, family caregivers, and health care professionals. The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: age > 60 years, diagnosis of CHF, ability to communicate in Mandarin, use of mHealth and willingness to participate in the study. The inclusion criteria for family caregivers were as follows: family members aged 18 years and above, involvement in patient care, awareness of mHealth and the ability to communicate in Mandarin. The exclusion criteria for patients and family caregivers included a history of schizophrenia, malignancy, or other serious diseases.

The inclusion criteria for health care professionals were doctors and nurses who were involved in managing CHF for at least 1 year and were willing to participate in the study. A maximum variation approach was used to emphasise heterogeneity across professionals' disciplines and qualifications.

The criterion for sample size determination was data saturation to show the patterns, categories and diversity of the phenomena under study (Moser and Korstjens 2018).

Data Collection

One‐to‐one face‐to‐face, semi‐structured, in‐depth interviews were conducted to collect data owing to their suitability for addressing specific issues when the research already had a clear focus (Bryman 2016). The interview guides were developed based on literature reviews and researchers' interests. The interview questions for patients were as follows: How do you feel about using mHealth to conduct CHF self‐care? Did you encounter/anticipate any difficulties or concerns when using mHealth? What platforms do you think can be used in mHealth? What functions do you think mHealth services should perform? What are your needs for mHealth services?

The interview questions for family caregivers were as follows: Have you helped [the patient] conduct CHF self‐care using mHealth? How do you feel about using mHealth services? Did you encounter/Did you anticipate any difficulties or concerns with using mHealth to help [the patient] conduct self‐care? What platforms do you think can be used for mHealth? What functions do you think mHealth services should perform? What are your needs regarding the use of mHealth in older adults with CHF?

The interview questions for health care professionals were as follows: What do you think of the status of the clinical management of CHF? How do you feel about using mHealth in older adults with CHF? What platforms do you think can be used for mHealth? What functions do you think mHealth services should perform? What are your needs [as a health care professional] regarding the use of mHealth in older adults with CHF?

Interviews were conducted by the first researcher (YC) with assistance from two other researchers (YL and YY) between March 2021 and January 2022 in the cardiology wards of two hospitals in Shanghai, China. The researchers received training for the qualitative study. They were unaware of the participants before they participated in the study. The participants determined the duration of the interviews. Before conducting the interviews, the purpose of the study was explained, definitions of mHealth and self‐care were clarified, and written informed consent was obtained. The interviews lasted between 30 min and 1 h and were conducted in a small meeting room for patients and family caregivers, and office rooms for health care professionals. Field notes were collected during interviews. Interviews were audio‐recorded with the participant's permission and transcribed immediately after that, and the interviews continued until thematic saturation was achieved.

Data Analysis

The content analysis method was used for data analysis in accordance with Graneheim and Lundman (2004). Audio recordings of the interviews were carefully listened to and transcribed verbatim by three researchers (YL, YY and XL). The transcripts were uploaded to NVivo 10.0. Three researchers compared the transcripts with the original records, combined them with field notes to ensure accuracy, and read several times to obtain a sense of the entire picture. The interview data were condensed into meaning units labeled with a code. The codes were compared based on similarities and differences and sorted into subthemes. The subthemes were then formulated into themes. The first researcher (YC) comprehensively reviewed the results. Any differences in opinions were discussed among all researchers until a consensus was reached.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of X University and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent before participating and were informed that they could withdraw at any time without any consequences. All identifying information was removed from the interview records, and only the codes were used in the final analysis.

Research Rigour

To ensure the validity of this study, credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability were applied using rigorous evaluation criteria suggested by Lincoln and Guba (1985). To ensure credibility, all researchers received training in qualitative studies and had some experience with interviewing. In addition, the results of the analysis were sent to five participants to check whether the content described by the researchers was consistent with their experiences. All the participants agreed that the findings fully reflected their experiences. To ensure transferability, detailed information on the data collection method, duration, and participants was recorded. In addition, health care professionals were selected using the maximum variation approach. For dependability, audio recordings were transcribed by two researchers (YL and YY) and checked by another researcher (XL). The first researcher (YC), who had experience in qualitative studies, confirmed the analysis process and results. To meet confirmability requirements, the researchers practiced bracketing so that their perceptions of mHealth did not affect their understanding of their experiences.

Participants' Perceptions Regarding mHealth for Self‐Care of CHF

MHealth Is a Promising Way to Improve the Self‐Care of Older Adults With CHF

CHF is a chronic, complex, debilitating syndrome. Although self‐care is an important component of CHF management, its complexity makes at‐home care management difficult for older patients. There is a gap between the health care services offered to inpatients and those discharged under the current health care system in China. Effective supervision of patient self‐care is challenging for health care professionals. MHealth is a promising approach for addressing this gap.

The management of CHF includes treatment of inpatients and follow‐up of discharged patients. Follow‐up is probably more important because patients spend most time at home. We should teach them how to take medicine, how to recognize symptoms, and what they should pay attention to……MHealth is a good way. (Professional #7, doctor, aged 35 years)

MHealth may be helpful. I use a mobile phone to read messages regarding self‐care (from WeChat public accounts and WeChat groups). It is very convenient for me to get this information online. (Patient #1, female, aged 62 years)

My father likes to watch short videos via mobile phone. There are more and more educational videos now. If self‐care information can be presented in the form of short videos, I think it will be good for him. He can easily know how to conduct self‐care (just via mobile phone). (Caregiver #5, female, aged 30 years)

MHealth Should Have Multiple Functions

The participants reported multiple functions that they thought mHealth should have, including monitoring and analyzing physical indicators, communicating with health care professionals and providing health education.

Monitoring and Analysing Physical Indicators

Patients with CHF were required to record daily measurements such as blood pressure, heart rate and body weight. If mHealth could monitor, record, and analyze these indicators automatically, it would have helped patients better understand their conditions.

Patients are asked to measure body weight, abdominal circumference, blood pressure, and heart rate weekly……(MHealth) is able to convert the numbers into graphs, which makes it easy for patients to understand. (Professional #2, nurse, 29 years old)

(I think) mHealth should monitor and record the patient's weight, vital signs, and medications. Doctors can easily get access to this information at each visit so that they have a clearer reference for the diagnosis. (Professional #8, nurse, aged 31 years)

We can record physical indicators (using mHealth). It is better to automatically demonstrate the normal and abnormal (indicators). (Caregiver #3, female, aged 30 years)

Communicating With Health Care Professionals

Older patients encounter various problems during long‐term self‐care. Communication can overcome time and space barriers to a certain extent and help patients communicate with doctors and nurses in a timely manner.

To us, it would be nice to have 24‐hour contact with doctors. Sometimes, she (my mum) suddenly feels ill, I can consult a doctor about how to deal with it. If there is a shortcut key that connects to doctors the first time, the survival rate might be a little higher. (Caregiver #3, female, aged 30 years)

(MHealth) allows patients to communicate with doctors, either by text or voice or even by video……It is better that doctors proactively contact patients. (Professional #7, doctor, aged 35 years)

Health Education

Health education is an important aspect of managing CHF. Patients care about their conditions and may have collected disease‐related knowledge from various sources. Many patients and caregivers hope to receive trustworthy health information.

I rarely believe the internet information because I cannot distinguish between true and false information. I hope doctors provide health education (using mHealth). (Patient #4, male, aged 62 years)

(MHealth) can teach us how to eat, how to exercise, how to take medicine…… (Caregiver #2, female, aged 59 years)

All health care professionals believed that mHealth should have a scientific health education function.

We should regularly push health‐related information, such as cardiac structure……Of course, we can also teach (patients) how to prevent complications, how to establish a healthy lifestyle. (Professional #3, nurse, aged 34 years)

It is unrealistic to spend a lot of time on providing health education in the clinic. These information should be pushed (using mHealth) continuously. (Professional #10, doctor, aged 40 years)

MHealth Platforms Could Be Diverse

When using mHealth, it is necessary to consider the characteristics of older patients. These platforms are diverse. Most participants mentioned the WeChat and CHF management apps.

(Some articles and videos) preferably can be read/watched via WeChat public account. APP can also be used. Some APPs have the function of consultation; I can ask questions (about CHF). (Caregiver #1, male, aged 65 years)

We set up a WeChat public account which has several functions, such as receiving messages, pushing guidelines and assessment tools. It is managed and weekly maintained by nurses. (Professional #6, nurse, aged 43 years)

It is best to establish a WeChat group for CHF patients. Patients can communicate with each other in this group. Doctors can give us instructions. (Patient #4, male, aged 62 years)

It is completely possible to develop a specialized APP. The APP developed by a hospital is relatively reliable and (patients) will be willing to use it. (Professional #8, nurse, aged 31 years)

Some participants perceived television and telephones as important for the older population.

Television can also be (a platform). Some elderly patients do not use smartphones, but they always watch TV. The self‐care information can be popularized in TV programs, which may attract (patients') attention. (Professional #5, doctor, aged 51 years)

I use geriatric cell phone……I prefer TV (to receive self‐care instruction). (Patient #1, female, aged 62 years)

We used telephone follow‐up in the past. In fact, the telephone is very good. The communications with patients are complete and direct……However, telephone follow‐up requires a lot of time and labor. (Professional #6, nurse, aged 43 years)

There Are Some Barriers to Using mHealth

While participants perceived the importance of using mHealth, they expressed some concerns.

Burdensome to Use

As older participants perceived the use of mHealth as complex, they faced specific challenges while using it.

I need someone to teach me how to use it. I might not even understand (even if someone taught me). (Patient #1, male, aged 65 years)

I cannot see clearly. My eyes will be uncomfortable if I look at (the mobile phone) for a long time. So, I rarely use it. (Patient #4, male, aged 62 years)

We are both illiterate……We can only accept recordings and videos. Using it (mHealth) is complicated. We need someone's help. (Caregiver #2, female, aged 59 years)

Some health care professionals have also raised concerns regarding the difficulties in using mHealth for older patients.

Most older adults don't use mobile phones as smoothly as younger people. Sometimes they can't even read the words on the screen clearly, let alone open the software, input information, and upload data. It may not be very convenient for them to use (mHealth). (Professional #7, doctor, aged 35 years)

Older patients may not be able to use (mHealth). They need the assistance of their children…… (The use of mHealth) should depend on the patients' educational levels and their abilities. If the patient is illiterate, it is difficult to use (mHealth). (Professional #9, nurse, aged 38 years)

Shortage of Human and Financial Resources

Adequate human and financial resources are required for the long‐term management of mHealth. If patients do not have good experiences using mHealth, their willingness to use it will inevitably be affected. Many health care professionals consider this to be time‐ and resource‐intensive.

There are few health care professionals. It is impossible to spend so much time on managing (mHealth) or communicate with patients online all the time. (Professional #5, doctor, aged 51 years)

The main concern about mHealth is that we don't have time……We can only answer patients' online questions in the evening (after work)…… It is difficult to respond to them in a timely manner. (Professional #7, doctor, aged 35 years)

It takes a lot of money to develop an APP or maintain a WeChat public account. (We) do not have so much money. (Professional #6, nurse, aged 43 years)

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the few studies to explore the perceptions of older patients, family caregivers, and health care professionals regarding mHealth for CHF self‐care in China. Because CHF is a progressive and debilitating disease, self‐care is crucial. The nature of the disease is regarded as one of the key points in the management of patients with CHF (Chen et al. 2023). The Chinese guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of HF suggest that patients should be followed up with health education, medication adherence and psychological support to improve self‐care (Heart Failure Group of Chinese Society of Cardiology et al. 2018). One such approach is the use of new information technologies. This finding was confirmed in the present study. Participants perceived mHealth as a promising way to improve self‐care in older adults with CHF. Many expressed great expectations and stated that mHealth might be helpful in providing self‐care information and instructions. The characteristics of real‐time, continuous, long‐term and extensive health services provided by mHealth compensated for the deficiencies in the current CHF management in China and created conditions for real‐time disease monitoring, personalised health assessment, self‐care supervision and regular health education. According to a previous study conducted in Southeast America, elderly patients with CHF are highly willing to use mHealth services (Tarte and Amirehsani 2019). Therefore, the use of mHealth to improve self‐care in older patients is a trend for CHF management in China.

The functions of mHealth in China are limited to reminders of medical visits, registration appointments, online payments and health education (Shen et al. 2019). Other functions, such as the monitoring of physical indicators, real‐time communication with health care professionals, and personalised management, have not been realised. The participants in this study described multiple functions related to mHealth among elderly patients with CHF, notably with respect to the automatic monitoring, recording and analysis of physical indicators and communication with health care professionals. From the perspective of patients, caregivers and health care professionals, promoting patient health is the ultimate goal. Thus, monitoring and analysing physical indicators is very important. These actions would help patients understand their condition better and effectively undertake self‐care tasks. Patients may also receive early warnings based on the results of the analysis, which could remind them to seek medical advice as soon as possible. In addition, health care professionals can easily and in a timely manner understand the conditions of patients via mHealth and better undertake disease management. Communication is an important function of mHealth. The patients wanted an interactive feature rather than unidirectional delivery of information. Ineffective communication has been identified as a barrier to high‐quality self‐care and interferes with the implementation of medical recommendations at home (Trivedi et al. 2019). Through communication, health care professionals can provide personalised health education and guidance, which helps older patients distinguish between true and false health information. Communication can also improve patients' social support and trust in health care professionals, which makes them more willing to use mHealth services.

The functions of mHealth mentioned above can be realised via various platforms, such as WeChat, APP, television, and telephone. Regardless of the platform used, the characteristics of older patients should be considered. Participants perceived the use of mHealth as a significant obstacle. The usability and operability of mHealth influence the use of older adults (Tarte and Amirehsani 2019). The decline in body function makes it challenging for older patients to use mHealth services. They are less skilled and self‐efficient when using new technologies. Therefore, a simplified design must be used. The simplicity of the interface, language accessibility, convenience of operation and use of pictures and short videos should also be considered (Chen and Xu 2022). In addition, it is better to provide training and education for older patients at the beginning of device usage and guarantee support for them after the implementation of new technology (Tadayon et al. 2021).

In addition, the cost of mHealth services cannot be ignored. The development and maintenance of mHealth incur high costs. Hospitals in China have insufficient human resources. Health care professionals may use their time off to undertake mHealth‐related work, such as answering patients' online questions and creating health education materials. The interviewed health care professionals repeatedly stressed that they did not have sufficient time. Therefore, to ensure the long‐term use of mHealth, the government, relevant enterprises and hospitals should jointly strengthen investment in human and financial resources and establish a specialised mHealth management team. Economic analyses are required to understand better the financial costs of mHealth (Fairbrother et al. 2014).

Strengths and Limitations

This is one of the few studies to explore the perceptions of older patients, family caregivers and health care professionals regarding mHealth for CHF self‐care in China. The results have implications for professional or patient care. Perceptions should be considered when developing and implementing mHealth‐based interventions to improve self‐care. This study had some limitations. The study was conducted in Shanghai, an economically developed city in China. The perceptions of patients, family caregivers and health care professionals regarding mHealth may differ from those in other cities. Although qualitative studies do not produce generalizable knowledge in a manner similar to quantitative studies, it is important to recognise that the results reported herein were based on a specific group of respondents (Trivedi et al. 2019). Additionally, this study was based on the perceptions of patients, family caregivers, and health care professionals at the individual level (Song et al. 2020). Another potential source of bias is the relatively small sample size of patients and family caregivers owing to the COVID‐19 pandemic.

Recommendations for Future Research

Future studies should be conducted with a relatively large sample size in other cities, including economically developed and undeveloped cities in China. Additionally, future studies should include policymakers and stakeholders to explore their perceptions of health system‐related issues.

Conclusion

Long‐term adherence to self‐care is important for older patients with CHF. A trend toward improving patients' self‐care through mHealth was evident. This study emphasises the importance of and need to consider the perceptions of older patients, family caregivers and health care professionals when developing and implementing mHealth‐based interventions as effective self‐care strategies among older patients.
