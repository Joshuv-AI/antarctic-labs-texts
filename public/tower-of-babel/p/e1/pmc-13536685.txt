An interpretable machine learning model integrating peripheral blood lncRNAs and clinical variables for phase classification in chronic myeloid leukemia
Authors: Yuanyuan Bai, Mengting Zheng, Hongshuang Li, Chenlei Song, Bingxin Huang, Meiyun Cai, Zhanguo Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate disease phase assessment remains clinically important in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), as progression to accelerated or blast phase (AP/BP) is associated with therapeutic resistance and poor prognosis. Introduction

Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) is a myeloproliferative neoplasm characterized by the BCR-ABL1 fusion gene and constitutive tyrosine kinase activation [1, 2]. The introduction of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) has dramatically improved the prognosis of CML, transforming the disease from a once-fatal malignancy into a manageable chronic condition for most patients [3, 4]. Nevertheless, progression from chronic phase (CP) to accelerated phase or blast phase (AP/BP) remains a major clinical challenge, as advanced disease stages are associated with therapeutic resistance, disease instability, and markedly inferior survival outcomes [5–7]. Accurate assessment of disease phase therefore remains essential for therapeutic decision-making and clinical management in CML.

Current clinical assessment of CML phase primarily relies on hematologic parameters, bone marrow morphology, cytogenetic abnormalities, and BCR-ABL1 transcript monitoring according to European LeukemiaNet (ELN) and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) recommendations [8–10]. Although these approaches remain indispensable in routine practice, disease phase assessment can still be challenging in certain patients due to biological heterogeneity and overlapping clinical features. Consequently, there is increasing interest in identifying additional molecular biomarkers that may complement conventional clinical indicators and improve disease phase assessment in CML.

Liquid biopsy approaches based on peripheral blood biomarkers have emerged as promising tools for molecular characterization in hematologic malignancies [11]. Among these, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides with limited protein-coding potential, have attracted increasing attention because of their regulatory roles in epigenetic modulation, transcriptional control, and post-transcriptional signaling pathways [12, 13]. In CML, dysregulation of specific lncRNAs such as HOTAIR, H19, and SNHG5 has been linked to enhanced proliferation, genomic instability, and TKI resistance [14–16]. A growing body of evidence underscores the pivotal role of a broader spectrum of lncRNAs in driving disease phase transition and therapeutic resistance [17, 18]. Importantly, lncRNAs can be stably detected in peripheral blood using real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), supporting their potential utility as minimally invasive biomarkers for disease monitoring and molecular assessment [19].

Machine learning (ML) methods provide effective tools for integrating complex molecular and clinical datasets and have shown increasing applications in oncology and hematology research [20, 21]. However, the clinical implementation of ML models remains limited by concerns regarding interpretability and transparency, particularly for complex algorithms frequently regarded as “black-box” systems [22–24]. Therefore, integrating multimodal data within interpretable ML frameworks is essential for translating predictive performance into clinically actionable insights [25]. Explainable artificial intelligence approaches, such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), can improve model interpretability by quantifying the contribution of individual variables to model predictions and thereby facilitate clinical understanding of ML-assisted decision-making [26, 27].

In the present study, we developed and validated an interpretable machine learning framework integrating peripheral blood lncRNA expression and clinical variables for disease phase assessment in CML. Notably, SNHG5, one of the lncRNAs previously implicated in TKI resistance, was retained in the final feature set identified in the present study. Using feature selection strategies and multiple machine learning algorithms, we identified a compact set of molecular and clinical predictors associated with disease phase. We further applied SHAP analysis to improve model transparency and interpretability. This study aims to provide a supportive framework for disease phase assessment in CML and to establish a basis for future multicenter and longitudinal validation studies.

Methods

Study design and participants

Between April 2018 and October 2024, we prospectively enrolled 305 treatment-naïve patients newly diagnosed with CML prior to any therapy and 90 age- and sex-matched healthy controls at The Second Affiliated Hospital and Yuying Children’s Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. Disease phase classification, including CP and AP/BP, was determined according to the ELN recommendations [8] and the NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology for CML [9]. The cohort comprised 220 CML-CP and 85 CML-AP/BP patients. Exclusion criteria included concurrent hematologic malignancies, active infection, or incomplete clinical data. All participants provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital and Yuying Children’s Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Approval No. 2025-K-59-01).

lncRNA screening and validation

To identify lncRNAs associated with CML disease phase, we first selected 12 candidate lncRNAs implicated in leukemogenesis through systematic literature review and database annotation. The lncRNA expression profile was analyzed using a commercial lncRNA PCR array (WcGene Biotech, Shanghai, China) in peripheral blood samples from eight randomly selected individuals per group (CML-CP, CML-AP/BP, and healthy controls). Healthy controls were included solely for lncRNA discovery and biological validation and were not incorporated into machine learning model development because the objective was disease phase assessment among patients with CML. Ten lncRNAs exhibiting significant differential expression across disease phases were prioritized for validation. Their expression levels were quantified by RT-qPCR in an expanded cohort comprising 212 CML-CP, 77 CML-AP/BP, and 82 healthy controls. GAPDH was used as the endogenous control, and relative expression was calculated using the comparative 2−ΔΔCt method [28]. The sequences of all primers used are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Feature selection

A total of 29 baseline variables—including 10 validated lncRNAs and 19 clinical parameters (e.g., white blood cell count, blast percentage, platelet count, splenomegaly)—were considered for model development. To mitigate overfitting and improve model stability, we applied a two-stage feature selection strategy. First, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation was used to penalize model complexity and select features with non-zero coefficients at the optimal regularization parameter (λ) [29, 30]. These candidate variables were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model with backward stepwise elimination to identify independent variables associated with CML disease phase. Multicollinearity among the final selected features was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with a VIF < 5 considered acceptable.

Machine learning model development and evaluation

The dataset comprising 305 CML patients was randomly divided into a training cohort (70%, n = 214) and an independent holdout test cohort (30%, n = 91) using stratified sampling according to disease phase. The training cohort was used for machine learning model training, hyperparameter optimization, and internal cross-validation. Feature selection was conducted prior to machine learning model construction using the available study dataset.

Five machine learning algorithms, including logistic regression (LR), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), were implemented using R software (version 4.2.2). Hyperparameter optimization was performed exclusively within the training cohort using grid search combined with five-fold cross-validation. The optimal model was selected according to the highest cross-validated area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).

Model performance was assessed in the training cohort and subsequently validated in the independent holdout test cohort. No explicit resampling techniques were applied to address class imbalance during model training. Discriminative ability was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and precision-recall (PR) curves, with corresponding AUC values reported [31]. The optimal classification threshold (0.374) was determined in the training cohort using the Youden Index and subsequently applied to the independent holdout test cohort for calculating sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, precision, and F1 score. Calibration performance was evaluated using calibration curves and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test [32]. Clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA) [33]. To improve interpretability and transparency, SHAP analysis was performed to quantify the contribution of each variable to model predictions at both global and individual levels [34].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test; categorical variables as counts (%) and compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. All statistical tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R (v4.2.2) and SPSS (v27.0).

Missing data handling

No missing values were observed among variables included in the final analysis.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

Between April 2018 and October 2024, a total of 305 CML patients and 90 healthy controls were enrolled from The Second Affiliated Hospital and Yuying Children’s Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Fig. 1). The cohort comprised 85 patients with CML-AP/BP and 220 with CML-CP. For the initial PCR array screening, eight samples were randomly selected from each of the following groups: CML-CP, CML-AP/BP, and healthy controls. The clinical and demographic characteristics of this screening cohort were well-balanced and are detailed in Supplementary Table S2. The overall study design, outlining the five-phase workflow from cohort establishment to model interpretation, is schematically summarized in Fig. 1.

Selection of independent variables associated with disease phase

To improve model performance and reduce overfitting, we performed feature selection on 29 initial variables (10 lncRNAs and 19 clinical characteristics) using LASSO regression. The model error was minimized at the optimal λ value (log(λ) = − 4.56), which selected 17 features with non-zero coefficients (Fig. 4A, B). These 17 features were subsequently subjected to multivariable logistic regression analysis to control for confounding factors and identify variables associated with disease phase. This refined the model to eight independent factors strongly associated with CML disease phase: three lncRNAs (CCDC26, SNHG5, FENDRR) and five clinical variables (Age, Neutrophil Percentage (NEUT%), Blast Percentage, Red Blood Cell Distribution Width-Coefficient of Variation (RDW-CV), Splenomegaly) (Table 2). To ensure feature independence for stable model construction, Spearman correlation analysis was conducted on the eight final selected features; the resulting heatmap showed no significant pairwise correlations (Fig. 4C). Collinearity diagnostics on the final eight features confirmed the absence of multicollinearity (all VIF < 5).

Performance of machine learning models

The eight selected features were used to construct five machine learning models—including a conventional logistic regression and four machine learning algorithms (KNN, SVM, RF, XGBoost)—on the training set. Their performance was evaluated on the independent holdout test cohort. ROC curve analysis revealed that all models except the basic Logistic Regression achieved favorable discrimination, with AUC values exceeding 0.95 in the independent holdout test cohort (Fig. 5A, B). A comprehensive evaluation, incorporating calibration curves and performance metrics (Table 3 and Supplementary Table S3), was conducted. While Random Forest achieved the highest AUC among the evaluated models, the XGBoost model demonstrated a more favorable balance of discrimination, calibration, and clinical net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 5C, D). Therefore, XGBoost was selected as the final model for subsequent analyses.

Validation and in-depth evaluation of the XGBoost model

The XGBoost model was optimized via grid search, with the optimal probability cutoff determined to be 0.374 using the Youden Index, yielding a sensitivity of 98.3% and a specificity of 92.2% (Supplementary Table S3). We first evaluated its discriminative performance using ROC analysis. In the independent holdout test cohort, the model achieved an AUC of 0.9656 (95% CI: 0.9317–0.9995) (Fig. 6A). Five-fold cross-validation further demonstrated the robustness of the model, with AUC values ranging from 0.8629 to 0.9758 across folds (Fig. 6B). The observed variation may reflect differences in sample composition and the relatively limited number of AP/BP cases within individual folds. The optimal hyperparameter combination identified through grid-search optimization is summarized in Supplementary Table S5.

(A) ROC curve showing the model’s performance on the independent holdout test cohort (n = 91), with the area under the curve (AUC) and its 95% confidence interval. (B) ROC curves and corresponding AUC values from the 5-fold cross-validation performed on the training set (n = 214), demonstrating the stability and robustness of the model across different data splits

To provide a comprehensive assessment, we next examined multiple performance dimensions using Fig. 7. The side-by-side ROC curves for training and independent holdout test cohort showed nearly identical AUCs (0.9658 vs. 0.9656), indicating minimal overfitting (Fig. 7A). Given the imbalanced class distribution, PR curves demonstrated strong classification capability, with AUC-PR exceeding 0.9 in both datasets (Fig. 7B). Calibration curves suggested reasonable agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes (Fig. 7C). In addition, the XGBoost model achieved a low Brier score of 0.016, indicating good overall predictive accuracy. Additional quantitative calibration metrics, including calibration intercept and calibration slope, are provided in Supplementary Table S6. Learning curves indicated stable model performance as sample size increased, reflecting good generalizability (Fig. 7D). Finally, DCA confirmed that the model delivers superior clinical net benefit across a wide range of risk thresholds in both the training and independent holdout test cohort (Fig. 7E, F).

Ablation analysis excluding phase-defining clinical variables

To assess the influence of phase-defining clinical variables, an ablation analysis was performed by excluding blast percentage and splenomegaly from the final feature set. The reduced model included three lncRNAs (CCDC26, SNHG5, and FENDRR) and three clinical variables (Age, NEUT%, and RDW-CV).

Despite removal of these variables, the ablated XGBoost model maintained strong discriminatory performance in the independent holdout test cohort, achieving an AUC of 0.9606, sensitivity of 0.8033, specificity of 0.9539, accuracy of 0.9108, precision of 0.8750, and F1 score of 0.8376. Compared with the full model (AUC = 0.9797), only a modest reduction in performance was observed (Supplementary Figure S1 and Supplementary Table S4). These findings suggest that model performance was not solely driven by phase-defining clinical variables and that the retained lncRNA signature contributed substantially to disease phase assessment.

Interpretation of the XGBoost model using SHAP

To elucidate the decision-making process of the model and ensure its transparency, we employed SHAP analysis. The SHAP summary plot visually ranked the features by their global importance (Fig. 8A), which was further quantified by the mean absolute SHAP values (Fig. 8B). This analysis revealed that lower NEUT%, higher RDW-CV, a higher blast percentage, high expression of SNHG5, low expression of FENDRR, and splenomegaly were the major contributors to advanced disease phase classification. Age and CCDC26 expression had comparatively smaller impacts. For local interpretation, SHAP waterfall plots illustrated individual predictions for two typical cases: a representative CML-CP patient (predicted probability = 0.034), for whom RDW-CV contributed the largest negative SHAP value (reducing predicted probability); and a representative CML-AP/BP patient (predicted probability = 0.764), whose very low NEUT% yielded the largest positive SHAP value, with additional substantial contributions from blast percentage, SNHG5, FENDRR, and splenomegaly (Fig. 8C, D). These findings provide insight into the model’s decision-making process at both the population and individual levels and enhance the interpretability of the proposed framework.

Discussion

Accurate disease phase assessment in CML remains clinically important in the post-TKI era [35]. In this study, we developed and validated an interpretable machine learning framework integrating peripheral blood lncRNA expression and clinical variables for disease phase assessment in CML. Among the evaluated algorithms, the XGBoost model demonstrated favorable discrimination and calibration within this cohort while maintaining model interpretability through SHAP analysis. These findings suggest that integrating molecular biomarkers with clinical variables may provide supportive information for disease phase assessment in CML. Importantly, this study should be interpreted as a cross-sectional disease phase classification study rather than a longitudinal prognostic prediction model. Therefore, future multicenter longitudinal studies are warranted to further evaluate the clinical utility and generalizability of the identified lncRNA signature.

Our findings support the potential utility of a peripheral blood-derived ten-lncRNA signature as an informative molecular biomarker for CML disease phase assessment. The observed expression pattern, characterized by upregulation of oncogenic lncRNAs (e.g., SNHG5, HOTAIR, and HULC) and downregulation of the tumor suppressor FENDRR in advanced disease phases, is consistent with previous reports. These findings suggest that integrating multiple lncRNAs may better capture disease-related biological heterogeneity than individual markers alone [5, 17]. Notably, SNHG5 has been implicated in imatinib resistance through sequestration of miR-205-5p [36], whereas reduced FENDRR expression has been associated with tumor progression [37]. Furthermore, detection of these lncRNAs using routine RT-qPCR platforms supports their feasibility as minimally invasive peripheral blood biomarkers [11].

Feature selection identified a compact set of eight predictors integrating molecular and clinical variables. The inclusion of age, blast percentage, and splenomegaly is consistent with established clinical staging systems [8, 9], whereas lower NEUT% may reflect impaired myeloid differentiation associated with disease progression. RDW-CV also emerged as an important predictor. Beyond its traditional association with anemia, elevated RDW has been linked to systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, both of which have been associated with cancer progression and poorer TKI responses [38]. Its contribution to the model suggests that RDW-CV may serve as an indirect indicator of disease-related biological dysregulation, although its relationship with anemia-related variables requires further investigation [39].

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of explainable artificial intelligence, human-centered clinical decision support systems, and multimodal data integration for successful clinical implementation of machine learning models [40–42]. XGBoost has been widely applied in biomedical research because of its ability to model complex nonlinear relationships and manage heterogeneous datasets [43]. In our study, the model demonstrated stable performance across internal cross-validation and independent holdout testing. Although Random Forest achieved a slightly higher AUC, XGBoost demonstrated a more favorable balance of discrimination, calibration performance, clinical net benefit, and interpretability. Interpretable machine learning approaches have increasingly been applied in biomedical studies to improve model transparency and facilitate clinical translation, highlighting the growing importance of explainability in medical artificial intelligence applications [44, 45]. SHAP analysis further improved model transparency by quantifying the contribution of individual variables to model predictions. At the global level, disease phase classification reflected the combined effects of molecular biomarkers and clinical variables. At the individual level, SHAP illustrated how elevated SNHG5 expression could increase the predicted probability of advanced disease phase, whereas favorable hematologic indicators such as higher NEUT% and lower RDW-CV could partially offset this effect. Importantly, VIF and Spearman correlation analyses revealed no substantial multicollinearity among the final selected variables, supporting the independent contribution of NEUT% to the model. Such interpretability may facilitate clinical understanding of model outputs and increase confidence in ML-assisted decision-making [27, 46].

To address potential circularity, we performed an ablation analysis excluding blast percentage and splenomegaly. The reduced XGBoost model retained strong performance (AUC = 0.9606), supporting the contribution of the lncRNA signature and non-definitional clinical variables to disease phase assessment. The modest decline in performance further indicates that phase-defining variables still contribute to overall model discrimination.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study with a relatively limited sample size, particularly for AP/BP patients, which may affect generalizability. Second, the initial lncRNA screening phase included only a small number of samples per group, potentially increasing the risk of false-positive candidate selection. Third, feature selection was performed using the available study dataset prior to machine learning model development, which may have introduced information leakage and resulted in optimistic performance estimates. Fourth, the study employed a cross-sectional design rather than longitudinal follow-up and lacked external validation. Therefore, larger multicenter cohorts, external validation studies, and prospective investigations are required before broader clinical application.

In conclusion, we developed an interpretable machine learning framework integrating peripheral blood lncRNAs and clinical variables for supportive disease phase assessment in CML. The proposed model demonstrated favorable discrimination, calibration, and interpretability within this cohort and may provide complementary molecular information for clinical evaluation. Further multicenter and longitudinal studies are needed to determine its broader clinical applicability.
