Crosslinked F-actin networks regulate load-dependent energy conversion
Authors: Ryota Sakamoto, Zachary Gao Sun, Michael P Murrell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCellular energy conversion from chemical energy to mechanical work underlies essential processes ranging from single-cell division to embryonic development. This energy is primarily derived from the hydrolysis of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), which powers motor proteins to generate forces on the filamentous cytoskeleton. While the load-dependent behaviors of individual motor proteins and ordered muscle fibers are well studied, how motor proteins collectively respond to mechanical load within cellular disordered cytoskeletal networks remains poorly understood. Here, we investigate this by reconstituting purified actomyosin networks crosslinked with various actin crosslinking proteins to mimic cellular environments. We find that structural and mechanical properties of the crosslinked networks, including inter-filament spacing, filament polarity, and network stiffness, modulate load-dependent myosin ATP consumption and inferred mechanical power generation. These findings reveal the intricate role of actin crosslinkers in regulating the mechano-chemical behavior of myosin, offering insight into how cells control energy conversion and force generation through cytoskeletal architecture.Subject terms: Bioenergetics, Synthetic biology

Introduction

Living systems continuously convert chemical energy into mechanical work. From nanometer-scale motor proteins1 to millimeter-scale embryonic development2, energy conversion underlies the essential processes that allow organisms to maintain their structure and adopt diverse morphologies. One of the primary pathways for generating the free energy required for dynamic behavior is the hydrolysis of adenosine triphosphate (ATP)3, which powers motor proteins such as myosin II to induce power strokes along actin filaments (F-actin)4,5. This fundamental mechanism is integrated into the actin cytoskeleton, a fiber-like network spanning inside the cell, where it generates forces that drive mechanical behaviors across scales, including cell division6,7, migration8–10, and oocyte polarization11,12.

One of the most intriguing properties of myosin motor proteins is their mechanosensitive behavior: myosin force generation is enhanced under applied forces, exhibiting load-dependence13–16. This occurs because the lifetime of the actin-bound state increases under opposing loads. From single myosin molecules13 to muscle fibers17, load-dependent force generation has been characterized, revealing the mechanosensitive regulation of force generation in the actomyosin complex. However, the biomechanical roles of myosin load dependence within the cellular-scale environment remain unclear. In living cells, numerous myosin motors generate force collectively and influence one another through disorganized actin network, which differs substantially from the highly ordered sarcomere-like structures found in muscle4. Importantly, intracellular force transmission is mediated by various actin-crosslinking proteins18, which regulate both the spatial organization of mechanical forces and structural formation19. Therefore, understanding how different types of actin crosslinkers influence force transmission and modulate myosin load-dependent behavior is essential for uncovering the mechano-chemical principles that govern cellular energy consumption and force generation.

Crosslinked F-actin networks exhibit distinct structural and mechanical properties depending on the type of crosslinker, including inter-filament spacing20, filament polarity21,22, and overall network stiffness23. These physical characteristics have been suggested to regulate the load-dependent force generation of myosin thick filaments24–26. However, unlike previous in vitro studies that examined the motion of individual myosin thick filaments along actin bundles21,26, myosin motors in living cells operate collectively within crosslinked F-actin networks during contraction processes, such as actin flow in living cells19,27. Thus, the role of load-dependent behavior of myosin in collectively converting chemical energy into mechanical work during actomyosin network contraction remains largely unexplored in disordered assemblies.

Here, we explore myosin ATP consumption within crosslinked F-actin networks using an in vitro reconstituted system28–38, encapsulated in water-in-oil droplets to mimic cellular actomyosin contraction5,39–41. ATP consumption rate is measured using the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH)-coupled assay, while mechanical power is simultaneously estimated through fluoresence microscopy of the actin network5,42–44. This system enables quantification of energy input (ATP consumption) and mechanical output (work performed by myosin), thereby elucidating the conversion of chemical energy into mechanical work. We found that crosslinked F-actin networks regulate load-dependent myosin energy consumption and inferred mechanical power based on their structural and mechanical properties. Our results underscore the pivotal role of crosslinked F-actin network architecture in modulating the load-dependent behavior of myosin, offering insights into the regulatory mechanisms of cellular energetics and force generation via actin crosslinkers.

Results

Actin-activated myosin ATP consumption rate at varying actin concentrations

To estimate adenosine-triphosphate (ATP) consumption rate of the actomyosin network, we employed the NADH-coupled assay5,42–44. This system measures the actin-activated myosin ATPase activity (ATP consumption rate) through the measurement of the decay of the fluoresence of NADH (Fig. 1a). Briefly, in the actin-activated myosin ATPase cycle, one ATP is hydrolyzed to ADP, then the ADP is regenerated into ATP via pyruvate kinase (PK) by converting phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) into pyruvate (Pyr.). One pyruvate molecule is subsequently converted into lactate (Lac.) via lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), during which one NADH molecule is converted into NAD+. Since NADH fluoresence is excited at 340 nm but NAD+ is not, we can estimate how much ATP molecules are consumed through measuring the decay of NADH fluoresence.

We encapsulate purified actomyosin networks and the NADH-coupled assay within water-in-oil droplets, serving as pico-litter reaction chambers for the quantitative measurement of ATP consumption rate (Fig. 1b, c and Supplementary Movie 1). The mean NADH fluoresence intensity, INADH(t), inside the droplets was analyzed over time and exhibited an approximately linear decrease (Fig. 1d). The linear decay phase above ~0.2 was fitted with a linear function, INADHt=−at+b, and the ATP consumption rate (Fig. 1e) was determined by the slope a using a calibrated relationship between NADH fluorescence intensity and NADH concentration (Methods). We analyzed the comparable sizes of droplets with radius ~20–35 µm to reduce the variability as shown in our previous study5. Additionally, we estimated the myosin ATPase activity, defined as the number of ATP molecules consumed per myosin molecule. The myosin ATPase activity was calculated by dividing the ATP consumption rate by the myosin concentration (Fig. 1f). We confirmed that myosin ATPase activity among different myosin concentrations are comparable (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Movie 2), consistent with our previous study5, ensuring the validity of myosin ATPase activity measurement in the present setup.

To validate if the actin-activated myosin ATPase activity follows a simple catalytic reaction in our experimental condition, we measured the ATP consumption rate (Δ[ATP]/Δt) at varying actin concentration from [Actin] = 0.24 µM to [Actin] = 24 µM, with the fixed myosin concentration at [Myosin] = 80 nM. The higher actin concentration induced faster myosin ATP consumption as expected for actin-activated myosin ATP consumption45 (Fig. 1d, e, Δ[ATP]/Δt=3.0±1.7 µM min−1 at [Actin] = 0.24 µM; Δ[ATP]/Δt=61±16 µM min−1 at [Actin] = 24 µM). We confirmed that the ATP consumption rate of actin without myosin (Δ[ATP]/Δt=1.6±1.3 µM min−1 at [Actin] = 6 µM) was negligibly smaller than that of myosin ATP consumption rate (Δ[ATP]/Δt=38.8±6.3 µM min−1) (Supplementary Fig. 2). We fitted the dependence of myosin ATPase activity on actin concentration using the Michaelis-Menten kinetic equation, kmyo0+kcat[Actin]/(KA+[Actin]), where kmyo0=0.36 s−1 is the myosin ATPase activity without actin, kcat=13.8 s−1 is the maximum actin-activated myosin ATPase activity, and KA=3.1 µM is the concentration of actin at 50% of maximum ATPase activity (Fig. 1f). The dependence of myosin ATPase activity on actin concentration follows Michaelis-Menten kinetics, confirming the catalytic nature of actin-activated myosin ATPase activity45.

The advantage of using the NADH-coupled assay alongside fluorescence intensity measurements of the actin network is the simultaneous assessment of ATP consumption rate and actomyosin contraction dynamics. Using the time-lapse images of the contracting actomyosin network, we computed the apparent strain (ϵ) and inferred mechanical power (P) (see “Methods”)5 (Fig. 1g). We refer to these quantities as apparent and inferred, noting that they are approximate measures. They may reflect not only the actual change in volume of the contracting network but also filament deformations and structural rearrangements within the network, as detailed in the following paragraphs. Since apparent strain is defined as the relative volume change of a material, it is necessary to quantify the volume change of the contracting actomyosin network. To this end, we performed particle image velocimetry (PIV) analysis to calculate the local displacement field of the network at each time frame, ur,t from which the local apparent strain at time t was estimated as, ∇⋅ur,t. The mean cumulative apparent strain of the network was then computed as ε(t)=−∫0tdt′∫dr[∇⋅ur,t′]/∫dr (Fig. 1h). Next, we define the inferred mechanical power generated by myosin is defined as the volume change of the network multiplied by the myosin-induced stress, dWmyo(t)=−σmyo(t)dV(t), where σmyo is the stress generated by myosin, dV(t)=V(t+dt)−V(t), and dt is the time step of the timelapse image. To calculate the inferred mechanical power, it is necessary to estimate the volume change of the network at each time frame. We therefore estimated the net change in the cross-sectional area of the network from the local displacement field as ΔA(t)=A0+∫0tdt′∫dr[∇⋅ur,t′] where A0 is the initial cross-sectional area of the network. The network radius R(t) was then approximately estimated by equating the cross-sectional area to πR(t)2, which was used to estimate the network volume as V(t)~4/3πR(t)3. The instantaneous work performed by myosin was therefore calculated as dWmyo(t)=−σmyo(t)dV(t) and the instantaneous inferred mechanical power was calculated as P=dWmyo/dt.

The stress induced by myosin acting on the actin network is estimated using the total myosin-generated force within a droplet: Fmyotot~fmyocmyoV0d, where fmyo = 3.4 pN is the force generated by single motors46, cmyo is the myosin concentration, V0 is the droplet volume, and d = 0.05 is the duty ratio of skeletal muscle myosin II47. For simplicity, here we assumed (i) myosin motors act independently, and (ii) load-dependence of myosin is not considered. The first assumption is validated by the results of the linear relationship between energy consumption rate and inferred mechanical power under varying myosin concentration (Supplementary Fig. 1). The second consideration is revisited in the later section. The myosin-induced stress is defined as σmyo(t)=Fmyotot/(4πR(t)2) and is used for the calculation of inferred mechanical power, and inferred mechanical power is divided by the droplet volume V0.

It should be noted that our estimates of myosin-based mechanical work do not encompass: (1) volume-conserving deformation modes, (2) dissipative effects, or (3) load- and architecture-dependent myosin force generation, as we use changes in network volume as a first-order readout of the mechanical work generated by myosin-induced stress5. We refer to the energy stored in deformation modes associated with changes in network volume as EΔV=Wmyo. However, additional contributions to the energetic cost may arise during network contraction. Accordingly, the total energy cost of deformation could be expressed as Etotal=EΔV+EΔV=0+Edissipative+Eload/architecture, where EΔV=0 represents the energy stored in volume-conserving deformation modes (e.g., filament buckling48–50 and stretching51–54), Edissipative denotes the energy dissipated during contraction (e.g., filament breakage48,55–57), and Eload/architecture accounts for load-dependent and architecture-specific stresses generated by myosin motors26,58. These contributions may differentially influence myosin dynamics under distinct crosslinking conditions. Consequently, the current estimate should be interpreted as a first-order readout, and the resulting quantities are therefore referred to as inferred mechanical power, and apparent efficiency, serving as a lower bound. These methods can be complemented with others, such as calorimetry in similar assays (Supplementary Fig. 3).

It is also worth noting that, as an alternative approach to estimating strain and mechanical work, the fluorescence intensity of labeled proteins could be used. However, fluorescence intensity may overlook contributions to mechanical work generated by myosin through network displacement without a substantial increase in network density, for example, during the coalescence of small clusters. In such cases, displacement-field analysis to estimate strain and mechanical work can complement intensity-based measurements. PIV-based strain and stress analysis has previously been demonstrated to quantify actomyosin network contraction profiles in circular regions, where the observed behavior was quantitatively reproduced by a theoretical model59.

The apparent strain in the contracting actomyosin network increases over time, and the maximum apparent strain is greater at higher actin concentrations (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 4). The inferred mechanical power varies over time (Fig. 1i), and the inferred mechanical power averaged over 10 min, 〈P〉, is greater at higher actin concentrations (Fig. 1j, P=3.2±0.8×10−4 J m−3 s−1 at [Actin] = 0.24 µM; P=5.8±2.3×10−1 J m−3 s−1 at [Actin] = 24 µM). To correlate the inferred mechanical power (⟨P⟩) with the energy consumption rate (ΔGATP/Δt), we estimate the energy consumption rate from the ATP consumption rate by multiplying it with the free energy released from ATP hydrolysis under physiological conditions, ~50–70 kJ mol−1 60,61. We found that the inferred mechanical power increases with the energy consumption rate, revealing the relationship between energy input and output in the actomyosin system (Fig. 1k). We observed that the apparent efficiency of converting chemical energy into inferred mechanical power, η=⟨P⟩/(ΔGATP/Δt), increases with actin concentration (Supplementary Fig. 4). We interpret the observed relatively sharp increase in mechanical power at [Actin] = 24 µM (Fig. 1k) as potentially reflecting the contribution of actin network elasticity, as higher actin concentrations may result in increased network stiffness and more effective global transmission of contractile forces. For example, the plateau modulus of the actin network has been reported to increase approximately 3-fold, from ~0.07 Pa at [Actin] = 12 µM to ~0.21 Pa at [Actin] = 24 µM62. Although investigating the concentration dependence of actin network mechanics is beyond the scope of the present study, it represents an interesting future direction, particularly in combination with agent-based modeling that explicitly accounts for the concentrations of actin, myosin, and crosslinker proteins63.

The α-actinin-crosslinked network increases ATP consumption rate and mechanical power

Having established estimates of the ATP consumption rate and inferred mechanical power, we next explored the impact of actin crosslinking on these properties. Previous studies have reported that the architecture of crosslinked F-actin networks alters myosin force generation and the motion of myosin thick filaments along actin bundles21,26, although the underlying energetic costs remain unclear. In the following, we fixed the concentrations of actin and myosin at [Actin] = 6 µM and [Myosin] = 80 nM, and then varied the concentrations of actin crosslinkers. First, we used α-actinin64, a crosslinker with a contour length of ~35 nm, which forms crosslinked F-actin networks where the F-actin polarity is randomly distributed (i.e., mixed polarity) (Fig. 2a). We varied the α-actinin concentration and measured the contraction dynamics of the actomyosin network and the ATP consumption rate (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Movie 3). It is notable that the ATP consumption rate increases with α-actinin concentration (Fig. 2c, d, Δ[ATP]/Δt=57±25 µM min−1 at [α-actinin]/[Actin] = 0.01; Δ[ATP]/Δt=106±29 µM min−1 at [α-actinin]/[Actin] = 0.4). The ATP consumption rate drops at [α-actinin]/[Actin] = 1, although it remains higher than the control condition (Fig. 2d). We confirmed that crosslinking via α-actinin does not alter the ATP consumption rate of the actin in the absence of myosin (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Movie 4). The structure of the actin network in the presence of various crosslinkers was quantitatively characterized by image analysis (Supplementary Fig. 6).

The inferred mechanical power and apparent strain increase with α-actinin concentration (Fig. 2e–h and Supplementary Fig. 7, P=3.4±1.9×10−2 J m−3 s−1 at [α-actinin]/[Actin] = 0.01; P=9.2±5.3×10−2 J m−3 s−1 at [α-actinin]/[Actin] = 0.4). The inferred mechanical power increases up to [α-actinin]/[Actin] = 0.1, along with the energy consumption rate, above which it does not change significantly (Fig. 2i). We found that the apparent efficiency of energy conversion remained unchanged at ~1.0×10−3 across varying α-actinin concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 7). Together, these results suggest that α-actinin enhances both the ATP consumption rate and inferred mechanical power, acting as a crosslinker that maintains the apparent efficiency of energy conversion even as the extent of crosslinking varies.

The fascin-crosslinked network decreases ATP consumption rate while enhancing mechanical power

To examine how the structure of crosslinked F-actin networks influences myosin ATP consumption rate, we next used fascin65, a small (~6 nm) actin crosslinking protein that forms F-actin bundles with filaments aligned in the same polarity (i.e., unipolar) (Fig. 3a). Notably, a higher fascin fraction slowed down the ATP consumption rate (Fig. 3b–d and Supplementary Movie 5, [ATP]/Δt=36±10 µM min−1 at [Fascin]/[Actin] = 0.01; Δ[ATP]/Δt=12±2.2 µM min−1 at [Fascin]/[Actin] = 1), which is the opposite behavior compared to the α-actinin crosslinked network. The inferred mechanical power and apparent strain increase with fascin concentration (P=1.8±1.3×10−2 J m−3 s−1 at [Fascin]/[Actin] = 0.01; P=1.1±0.2×10−1 J m−3 s−1 at [Fascin]/[Actin] = 1) (Fig. 3e–h, Supplementary Fig. 8). As a result, the inferred mechanical power vs energy consumption rate plot shows a trajectory from right to left, which is the opposite dependence compared to the α-actinin crosslinked network (Fig. 3i). The apparent efficiency of energy conversion increases with fascin concentration (Supplementary Fig. 8). These results indicate that the crosslinker-dependent properties of the actin network, including filament polarity and spacing, may regulate myosin-based energy consumption rate and inferred mechanical power.

The fimbrin and filamin-crosslinked networks decrease ATP consumption rate at higher concentrations

To gain further insights into the impact of crosslinked networks on myosin energy consumption, we next examined two other representative crosslinkers, fimbrin and filamin. Fimbrin is a small protein (~7 nm) that forms F-actin bundles, where the F-actin polarity is randomly distributed (i.e., mixed polarity), in contrast to fascin66 (Fig. 4a). Notably, the ATP consumption rate of the fimbrin-crosslinked network increased at higher crosslinker concentrations up to [Fimbrin]/[Actin] = 0.1, and then decreased (Fig. 4b–d, Supplementary Movie 6). Filamin is a crosslinker that forms a wide inter-filament distance (>100 nm) with mixed polarity67 (Fig. 4e). The ATP consumption rate of the filamin-crosslinked network exhibited a similar trend to that of fimbrin, but with a minor decrease followed by a sharp drop nearly to 0 at [Filamin]/[Actin] = 0.1 (Fig. 4f–h and Supplementary Movie 7). The inferred mechanical power and apparent strain of the fimbrin- and filamin-crosslinked networks exhibited an increase at lower concentration, followed by a decrease at higher concentrations (Fig. 4i–n and Supplementary Fig. 9). Although the inferred mechanical power decreased at high crosslinker concentrations for fimbrin and filamin, the resulting apparent efficiency of energy conversion increased at higher crosslinker concentrations due to the slower ATP consumption rate (Supplementary Fig. 9).

The observed sharp drop in ATP consumption with modest change in mechanical power at [Filamin]/[Actin] = 0.1 could be due to the coexistence of sparse and dense actomyosin networks in the droplet. In the time-lapse images of actin fluoresence, the network structures differ between these conditions: at [Filamin]/[Actin] = 0.04, the network consists of contractile clusters (Supplementary Fig. 10), whereas at [Filamin]/[Actin] = 0.1, dense bundles are formed and distributed in the droplet (Supplementary Fig. 10). We interpret this behavior as resulting from a majority of myosin molecules being incorporated into dense filamin bundles and becoming stalled as reported previously26, leading to a decreased ATP consumption rate, while global contraction remains possible due to the presence of a sparse contractile network. Further increasing the filamin concentration completely stalls contraction of the actin network at [Filamin]/[Actin] = 1 (Supplementary Fig. 10)26. Because the apparent mechanical power is calculated based on global network contraction, the presence of the contracting sparse network maintains a relatively high mechanical power value, even though the majority of myosin molecules are stalled within dense bundles. This mechanism may underlie the relatively high mechanical power observed despite low energy consumption at [Filamin]/[Actin] = 0.1.

To further interpret the results in fimbrin- and filamin-crosslinked networks, we first compared the fascin and fimbrin networks. At low crosslinker concentrations, the ATP consumption rate of the fimbrin network increased, opposite to the fascin network. This suggests that the polarity of the network plays a role at lower crosslinking fractions, where the mixed-polar network may facilitate the local engagement of myosin thick filaments. This is also likely the case for the α-actinin and filamin networks at lower crosslinking fractions, which exhibit enhanced ATP consumption. By contrast, fascin may form polar asters where myosin thick filaments could be stalled, slowing down the ATP consumption rate35,68,69. At high crosslinker concentrations, both fascin and fimbrin networks exhibited slower ATP consumption rates, suggesting that this behavior originates from the common properties of these crosslinkers. One possibility could be the narrow inter-filament spacing (~6–7 nm), which has been theoretically predicted to prolong the strongly bound state of myosin due to its load-dependent behavior24–26, which may have caused the significant reduction in ATP consumption rate. On the other hand, the significant reduction in the ATP consumption rate of the filamin network could be due to the significantly stiffened filamin-crosslinked network with a high elastic modulus70.

As an orthogonal approach to support the interpretation that the observed behavior stems from the load-dependence of myosin, we altered myosin concentrations from 80 nM to 20 nM while maintaining a fixed crosslinker fraction at [Crosslinker]/[Actin] = 0.1, assuming that lower myosin concentrations induce lower loads (Supplementary Fig. 11). Indeed, we observed that the impact of the crosslinker on ATPase activity is less significant at [Myosin] = 20 nM compared to [Myosin] = 80 nM. This result suggests that the crosslinker mediates the load-dependent behavior of myosin force generation.

It is worth noting that the dynamics of small contractile structure formation and coalescence vary depending on the crosslinker type at an intermediate crosslinker fraction of [Crosslinker]/[Actin] = 0.1 (Supplementary Fig. 12). In the α-actinin case, we observed that tiny clusters form initially and gradually merge to become larger, with multiple clusters remaining after 10 min ([α-actinin]/[Actin] = 0.1; Supplementary Fig. 12). In the case of the fascin network, long bundle structures are formed primarily, leading to rapid contraction into a single large cluster, which is distinct from the multiple clusters formed in the α-actinin network ([Fascin]/[Actin] = 0.1; Supplementary Fig. 12). In the fimbrin network, small clusters immediately merge to form a large cluster at intermediate concentrations under contractile conditions, similarly to the fascin network ([Fimbrin]/[Actin] = 0.1; Supplementary Fig. 12), while filamin-crosslinked networks show global contraction dynamics accompanied by long bundle formation ([Filamin]/[Actin] = 0.1; Supplementary Fig. 12). These results suggest that different actin crosslinking proteins differentially regulate the formation of small contractile structures and their subsequent merging.

Notably, the dependence of ATP consumption rate on crosslinker concentration highlights the distinct impacts of different crosslinkers (Fig. 4o). The ATP consumption rate of the fascin-crosslinked unipolar network decreases monotonically with crosslinker concentration, while that of the other crosslinkers forming mixed-polar networks increases or remains unchanged within 0.01 < [Crosslinker]/[Actin] < 0.1. However, at higher crosslinker concentrations ([Crosslinker]/[Actin] > 0.1), the ATP consumption rate of the fimbrin- and filamin-crosslinked networks drops significantly, while the α-actinin-crosslinked network exhibits only a minor reduction, with an ATP consumption rate still higher than the control condition without crosslinkers. These results indicate distinct roles of actin crosslinkers in controlling myosin ATP consumption rate. The concentration dependence of apparent strain and inferred mechanical power in the fascin- and α-actinin-crosslinked networks follows a similar increasing trend, whereas that in the fimbrin- and filamin-crosslinked networks decreases at higher concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 13).

Notably, these distinct energetic and mechanical dependencies on crosslinkers can be represented as separate trajectories within the phase space of inferred mechanical power vs energy consumption (Fig. 4p). The apparent strain dependence on energy consumption follows similar trajectories to those of inferred mechanical power (Supplementary Fig. 13). Together, these results reveal distinct roles of actin crosslinkers in regulating the conversion of chemical energy into mechanical work within cellular-scale contracting actomyosin networks.

Crosslinker-mediated regulation of load-dependent mechanical power

To gain further quantitative insights into crosslinker-mediated myosin ATP consumption, we examine the load-dependence of the actin-activated myosin ATPase cycle. The myosin ATPase cycle consists of weakly bound and strongly bound states (Fig. 1a)71. Under load, the strongly bound state is prolonged due to the mechanosensitive catch-bond nature of myosin motors, resulting in greater force generation24–26. The duty ratio, d, is a useful parameter for characterizing myosin load-dependence71. It is defined as the ratio of the duration of the strongly bound state (ts) to the total ATPase cycle time (tc), d=ts/tc, where tc=ts+tw and tw is the duration of the weakly bound state71. Since ATP consumption rates reflect the microscopic myosin ATPase cycle, we can approximately estimate the effective duty ratio, deff, from the actin-activated myosin ATPase activity, Amyo (s-1). The inverse ATPase activity, Amyo−1 (s), represents the duration of one actin-activated ATP hydrolysis event by myosin, corresponding to the timescale a single ATPase cycle. Given the typical unloaded duty ratio of d~0.05 for skeletal muscle myosin II47, we have ts≪tw, and therefore d~ts/tw. Because myosin ATPase activity slows under load due to a prolonged strongly bound state, the effective duty ratio approximately scales with ATPase activity as deff~Amyo−1. Thus, we estimate the effective duty ratio from ATPase activity by using the non-crosslinked network condition as a reference, such that deffxlinked/⟨deffno−xlink⟩. We found that the effective duty ratio slightly decreased in the α-actinin-crosslinked network (Fig. 5a), whereas the fascin-crosslinked networks exhibited a monotonic increase (Fig. 5b). Fimbrin-crosslinked networks exhibited a non-monotonic trend with a decrease followed by a stronger increase (Fig. 5c). Filamin-crosslinked networks exhibited no increase at [Filamin]/[Actin] = 0.01 and 0.04, followed by a significant increase at [Filamin]/[Actin] = 0.1 and 1 (Fig. 5d).

Using the effective duty ratio, we estimate the inferred mechanical power under load, Ploadxlinked, relative to the inferred power of non-crosslinked network, Ploadno−xlink, as a reference condition: Ploadxlinked/Ploadno−xlink~Pxlinked/Pno−xlink×deffxlinked/⟨deffno−xlink⟩) (Fig. 5e). We observed crosslinker-based regulation of load-dependent inferred mechanical power, with the fascin- and filamin-crosslinked networks exhibiting strong load dependence, while the fimbrin- and α-actinin-crosslinked networks showed minor effects. This suggests that, among bundle-forming small crosslinkers, fascin and fimbrin have distinct impacts on myosin load dependence, and among network-forming large crosslinkers, filamin and α-actinin exhibit different load-dependent effects on myosin as well (Fig. 5f). These behaviors may reflect the distinct roles of actin crosslinkers in cellular structures. Fascin and filamin are enriched in dynamic intracellular structures such as filopodia72 and lamellipodia73, respectively, where efficient force generation is essential for cell migration and intracellular transport. In contrast, α-actinin and fimbrin are predominantly associated with more stable structures, including stress fibers74 and microvilli75. Our findings suggest that cells may selectively employ different crosslinkers to support specific functional demands by tuning load-dependent myosin force generation. Finally, the relationship between inferred mechanical power under load and relative energy consumption rate is summarized in a single plot (Fig. 5g). Notably, different types of crosslinkers exhibit distinct trajectories that depend on crosslinker concentrations (Fig. 5g, h). Together, these results reveal the pivotal role of actin crosslinkers in regulating load-dependent energy consumption and inferred mechanical power.

Discussion

Previous studies have explored the load-dependent properties of myosin II motors at the single-molecule scale using optical tweezers13, or in macroscopically organized muscle fibers17. However, how such load-dependent myosin behaviors impact the cellular-scale, disorganized F-actin network remains unclear. The effect of crosslinked F-actin networks on load-dependent myosin behavior was previously studied by observing the motion of individual myosin thick filaments, inferring load-dependent myosin force generation in the absence of contraction in the underlying crosslinked F-actin network26. Yet, how disordered, crosslinked F-actin networks regulate energy conversion, force production, and the contraction of the actomyosin network at the cellular scale in the presence of an ensemble of myosin motors remains unknown.

We explored this question by monitoring myosin energy consumption as a readout of myosin behavior, alongside the estimation of inferred mechanical power in the actin network. We found that energy consumption can be significantly modulated by the structure of the F-actin network formed through actin crosslinking. The fascin-crosslinked unipolar network reduced the ATP consumption rate, a signature of the load applied to myosin due to enhanced force transmission. Previous studies suggested that myosin thick filaments walk along immobile, crosslinked actin bundles with no load in two-dimensional (2D) actin networks26, while we observed that the three-dimensional (3D) actin network exhibited behavior characteristic of being under load. This may suggest that in a 3D crosslinked actin network, where many myosin motors collectively produce forces, contraction could be facilitated in the unipolar network, imposing higher load on myosin. Moreover, unlike the non-contractile actin bundles used in previous studies21,26, a contractile actin network in the presence of an ensemble of myosin motors could form polar asters35,68,69, which may stall myosin thick filaments and induce higher load. A more detailed understanding would be gained through future agent-based simulations considering 3D crosslinked actin networks63,76.

Another possible contribution is the stiffness of the crosslinked F-actin network. For example, it has been suggested that higher stiffness induces a stronger load on myosin motors, resulting in greater force generation24–26. Given the several times higher bending rigidity of fascin-crosslinked bundles compared to α-actinin-crosslinked bundles77, the fascin-crosslinked network may play a more significant role in inducing load-dependent force generation on myosin at the scale of individual bundles, which could result in a slower ATP consumption rate. Inter-filament spacing could also contribute, with narrower filament spacing leading to a more effectively stiffer network24–26. The distinct ATP consumption rates observed in highly crosslinked networks between α-actinin and filamin may also be explained by the stiffness of the network, as the filamin-crosslinked network exhibits several times larger elastic modulus than the α-actinin-crosslinked network70. The detailed mechanism remains as a future work along with three-dimensional agent-based modeling.

It could be possible that catch-bond behavior contributes to the α-actinin (catch-bond linker) crosslinked network at lower crosslinker concentrations. Catch-bond linkers decelerate their unbinding rate under load78,79. Given a fixed myosin concentration, the presence of additional crosslinkers may help distribute myosin-generated stress across the network, potentially reducing the load on individual links, thereby accelerating the unbinding rate of catch-bond linkers like α-actinin. At high crosslinker concentrations, the ATP consumption rate in the α-actinin-crosslinked network is faster than in the condition without crosslinkers, whereas in the filamin-crosslinked network, it is slower, despite both α-actinin and filamin being considered catch-bond linkers80. This discrepancy suggests that the increased ATP consumption rate at higher α-actinin concentrations is unlikely to be solely attributable to its catch-bond properties. Instead, the stiffness of the crosslinked network may play a role24–26. Indeed, filamin significantly slows ATP consumption at high concentrations, which can be explained by the much higher elastic modulus of the filamin-crosslinked network compared to the α-actinin-crosslinked network70.

We identified how different crosslinkers modulate load-dependent inferred mechanical power. Interestingly, fascin and filamin contribute to highly load-dependent force generation, while fimbrin and α-actinin induce minor load dependence. These behaviors may reflect their roles in cellular structures: fascin and filamin are abundant in dynamic intracellular structures such as filopodia72 and lamellipodia73, respectively, where force generation is critical for cell migration and intracellular transport. In contrast, α-actinin and fimbrin are found in more stable structures such as stress fibers74 and microvilli75, respectively. Our findings suggest that cells may selectively employ different crosslinkers to achieve specific functions, leveraging their distinct effects on the regulation of load-dependent myosin force generation.

It is worth noting that the NADH-coupled assay is limited in spatial resolution; therefore, correlating local actomyosin dynamics with local changes in the NADH signal is not feasible in the present system. Addressing this limitation will require future approaches capable of resolving local dynamics together with local energy consumption. For example, FRET-based sensors represent a promising strategy for measuring localized ATP consumption, as demonstrated in studies of mitochondrial ATP metabolism in living cells81,82. Such future work, enabling parallel quantification of local actomyosin reorganization and local energy consumption, would provide valuable insight into how small and large crosslinkers differentially modulate myosin accessibility and recruitment to contractile assemblies, and the resulting spatial patterns of energy consumption.

It should be noted that our estimates of myosin-based mechanical work do not fully encompass: (1) volume-conserving deformation modes, (2) dissipative effects, or (3) load- and architecture-dependent myosin force generation, since we use changes in network volume as a first-order readout of the mechanical work generated by myosin-induced stress. Future work is therefore needed to obtain more accurate and direct measurements of mechanical work. Potential approaches include: (1) adopting a two-dimensional assay to more directly resolve the motion of individual myosin thick filaments and thereby estimate the mechanical work generated by myosin motors; (2) incorporating theoretical modeling to gain insight into how individual myosin motors consume energy, perform mechanical work, and dissipate heat; and (3) exploring the possibility of directly addressing these issues through heat-dissipation measurements of the system. Specifically, the mechanical work generated by individual myosin motors could be more directly and quantitatively addressed using two-dimensional assay systems, as demonstrated in previous studies26,35,48,57,58,83. Clear visualization of myosin motion in such systems would enable estimation of myosin-generated forces and mechanical work, as described in mathematical models of load-dependent myosin force generation in crosslinked networks26, and in agent-based numerical simulations that quantify energy dissipation during critical phenomena in actomyosin contraction83. Furthermore, an integrative approach that simultaneously measures energy consumption and dissipated heat would provide valuable insights, for example, by incorporating heat-dissipation measurements84.

Our system is transient, as an initially spread actomyosin network coalesces into a single accumulated structure without notable oscillations, likely because the present system does not include actin-severing proteins such as gelsolin or cofilin. In our previous work, we showed that the inclusion of gelsolin increased ATP consumption through enhanced stress relaxation5, whereas cofilin could either decrease ATP consumption when severing was inefficient, or stabilize actin filaments via filament decoration by cofilin85. Therefore, an important future direction will be to investigate how the interplay between actin crosslinking and severing gives rise to changes in ATP consumption dynamics.

To conclude, our findings highlight the importance of exploring energy conversion within actomyosin networks at the cellular scale, in the context of network contraction, beyond single-motor-scale behaviors. Our results emphasize the critical role of actin crosslinking proteins in modulating the load-dependent properties of crosslinked actomyosin networks, unveiling how cellular energy conversion is regulated by the architecture of crosslinked F-actin.

Methods

Lipid compositions

Lipids are a combination of L-α-phosphatidylcholine from egg yolk at 61% (EPC) (840051C; Avanti), cholesterol at 36% (ovine wool) (110796; Avanti), and 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[methoxy(polyethylene glycol)-2000] at 2% (18:0 PEG2000 PE) (880120; Avanti) was used to prevent non-specific adsorption of proteins at the water-oil interface, Oregon Green 1,2-dihexadecanoyl-snGlycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DHPE) (O12650; Invitrogen) at 1%. Lipids were dissolved in chloroform solution and stored at −20 °C. The lipids were combined in a glass vial and dried under Ar gas. The chloroform was dried, and the lipids were dissolved in mineral oil (Sigma-Aldrich) at 2 mg ml−1. The oil mixture was then sonicated in a bath sonicator for 2 h at room temperature. The mixture was stored at 4 °C up to a week.

Buffers

Buffer concentrations were referenced from previously developed actomyosin assays5,86,87. Actin polymerization buffer (IP-buffer) contains 2 mM CaCl2, 6 mM MgCl2, 50 mM KCl, 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.6), 0.8 mM DTT, 5 mM ATP, 80 mg ml−1 dextran, and 280 mM sucrose. The inclusion of dextran and sucrose is a result of previously optimized conditions in liposome systems43,86,87. The storage buffer for actin (G-buffer) contains 2 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 0.1 mM CaCl2, 0.2 mM ATP, 0.5 mM DTT, and 22 µM actin. The storage buffer for myosin (MSB) contains 4.5 M KCl, 0.1 M HEPES (pH 7.0), and 21 µM myosin. The storage buffer for NADH contains 100 mM NaHCO3 and 14 mM NADH. The storage buffer for PEP contains 100 mM NaHCO3 and 48.5 mM PEP. The storage buffer for PK contains 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 100 mM KCl, and 2 KU mL−1 PK. The storage buffer for LDH contains 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 100 mM KCl, and 2.6 KU mL−1 LDH. The spin-down buffer (SDB) contains 2 µL phalloidin, 4.2 µL 238 µM actin, 40 µL IP buffer, 15 µL myosin (in MSB), 4.3 µL of 4.6 M KCl. Myosin is centrifuged in the presence of polymerized actin to isolate the catalytically active myosin dimers before use. Briefly, actin is polymerized for 1 h at 4 °C in high salt (IP-buffer +4.5 M KCl) and stabilized with phalloidin. The actin network is spiked with ATP to 1 mM, and freshly thawed myosin is added. The actin-myosin mix is then centrifuged at 4 °C for 30 min at 128,360 × g. The supernatant is collected, and optical absorbance is measured at 280 nm to determine the concentration. All the regents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

NADH-coupled assay compositions

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH)-coupled assay consists of pyruvate kinase (PK) and phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP)-based ATP regeneration system, together with lactic dehydrogenase (LDH) that oxidize NADH5,42,43,88. The final concentration of NADH (10128023001; Roche), PEP (10108294001; Roche), PK (P9136; Sigma-Aldrich) and LDH (L1254; Sigma-Aldrich) is 0.5 mM, 1 mM, 10 U mL−1, and 16 U mL−1, respectively. The choice of NADH concentration is based on technical considerations. First, we selected a sufficiently low NADH concentration to guarantee the complete decay of NADH fluorescence in ~10 min at [Myosin] = 80 nM. This enhances the visibility and clarity in identifying ATP consumption under different conditions. Moreover, as the conversion of NADH requires a comparable amount of PEP and the associated concentrations of PK and LDH, using a larger NADH concentration would significantly increase the overall concentration of the additional enzymatic system. To minimize the impact of NADH assay-related enzymatic systems on the conventional actin polymerization buffer, we selected a low enough NADH concentration for our measurements.

Protein compositions

The final concentration of non-fluorescent actin (AKL99-D; Cytoskeleton Inc.) rhodamine-actin (AR05-C; Cytoskeleton Inc.) and skeletal muscle myosin II (MY02; Cytoskeleton Inc.) were 5.3 µM, 0.7 µM, and 80 nM, respectively in the experiments with crosslinkers. In the experiments assembling crosslinked actin network, fascin (CS-FSC01; Cytoskeleton Inc.), Fimbrin (Methods), α-actinin (AT01; Cytoskeleton Inc.), and Filamin (8312-01; Hypermol) were used at varied concentrations.

Protein purification

Purified proteins were prepared by following the method described in previous studies5,58. Briefly, Fimbrin (full-length; pET-21a-Fim1p-His plasmid was a gift from David Kovar lab) was expressed in E. coli (BL21-Codon Plus (DE3)-RP strain). The cells were induced with 1 mM IPTG for 2 h at 37 °C and purified using Ni-NTA resin by following the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by another purification using the size exclusion chromatography.

Preparation of water-in-oil droplets

Water-in-oil droplets were prepared by following the previously described method39–41. First, the mixture of the protein-NADH-coupled assay was prepared in a 0.65 mL tube. Separately, 10 µL of lipid-oil mixture was prepared in another 0.65 mL tube. Next, 0.5 μL of the protein-NADH-coupled assay mixture was added to the 10 μL of lipid-oil mixture in the same tube. The tube was gently tapped or syringed 2–3 times to generate water-in-oil emulsion droplets. Immediately after emulsification, the water-in-oil droplets were carefully transferred onto a coverslip mounted on the confocal microscope. All actin-associated proteins and enzymatic systems of NADH-coupled assay were kept on ice until use.

Microscopy

Images were acquired by Leica DMi8 inverted microscope equipped with a 63× 1.4-NA oil immersion lens (Leica Microsystems), a spinning-disk confocal (CSU-X1; Yokagawa), and sCMOS camera (Zyla; Andor Technology) controlled by Andor iQ3 (Andor Technology). The NADH fluorescence was imaged using a filter set consisting of 350 ± 50 nm excitation filter, 415 nm long-pass dichroic, and 460 ± 50 nm emission filter (Leica). Illumination from a mercury halide light source (11504120; Leica EL6000 Metal Halide) was used to excite the NADH in droplets. The excitation source was shuttered after every acquisition to minimize the photobleaching. The number of droplets recorded in each experiment is constrained by several factors. Firstly, we need to capture the time course of NADH fluorescence decay to calculate the ATP consumption rate. Consequently, within a single experiment involving the preparation of one tube of water-in-oil emulsion, the focus is limited to the region of interest (ROI), despite having several hundred droplets outside the ROI. Secondly, we employ a relatively low ratio of the reaction solution to oil (1:20, solution:oil), ensuring that droplets are adequately separated to prevent coalescence during measurements. Thirdly, we use high magnification objectives (63×), further constraining the size of the ROI in our confocal microscope. Due to these constraints, the measurement of the NADH fluorescence decay time course is limited to 1–3 droplets in a single experiment.

Image analysis

Fluorescence intensity of NADH within a droplet was analyzed using Fiji/ImageJ (NIH), Microsoft Excel, and MATLAB. The mean intensity within a circle with a half-radius of a droplet was determined and subtracted by the mean intensity of the background region. The photobleaching was corrected using the exponential fitting method89 for the droplets encapsulating NADH without any proteins. To relate the fluorescence intensity to the NADH concentration, calibration measurements were performed by adding known amounts of NADH5. Because of the 1:1 stoichiometric ratio between ADP and NADH, the slope of the NADH fluorescence decay gives ATP consumption rate.

Quantitative image analysis on contracting actomyosin network was performed by using a custom code written in MATLAB. We measure the displacement vectors between individual frames at every spatial location ur,t to using a public domain PIV program implemented as a Fiji/ImageJ plugin. Mean compressive apparent strain ε of the actin network is calculated from the divergence of the cumulative displacement field, ε(t)=−∫0tdt′∫dr[∇⋅ur,t′]/∫dr. The spatial integral is taken over the negative divergence within a droplet to calculate the compressive apparent strain. The subsequent change in cross-sectional area is calculated as ΔA(t)=A0+∫0tdt′∫dr[∇⋅ur,t′], where A0 is the initial cross-sectional area of the network. The network radius R was estimated by approximating the spherical symmetry of the contracting network and the cross-sectional area to be πR2. Inferred mechanical power under load was estimated as Ploadxlinked/Ploadno−xlink~Pxlinked/Pno−xlink×deffxlinked/⟨deffno−xlink⟩).

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical tests comparing distributions are done with the Wilcoxon rank sum test. All data displayed as a single value with an error bar is quoting the mean ± SD. Data are presented as boxplots where the interquartile range (IQR) is between Q1 (25th percentile) and Q3 (75th percentile), the center line indicates the median, whiskers are extended to Q3 + 1.5 × IQR and Q1 − 1.5 × IQR. The symbols *, **, and *** represent p < 0.05, 0.01, and 0.001, respectively. The sample size was determined by experimental feasibility and adjusted when it was deemed sufficient, ensuring that sample statistics were not affected by changes in sample size. Sample sizes (n) are indicated at the relevant locations in the manuscript. Experiments were independently replicated at least two times, and all replications were successful. Independent experiments (N) are indicated at the relevant locations in the manuscript.
