Reduced Expression of MTSS1 Increases Sarcomere Number and Improves Contractility in Select Forms of Monogenic DCM
Authors: Hannah Kleppe, Anastasia Budan, Luke Zhang, Marie Majetic, Reva Shenwai, Alan R. Levinson, Olga Cisne-Thompson, Bernardo Zepeda, Charles E. MacKay, Farshad Farshidfar, Jonathan H. Tsui, Sylwia M. Figarska, Timothy Hoey, James R. Priest, Rebecca E. Slater
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a form of heart failure typically characterized by reduced cardiac output and thinning of ventricular chambers affecting up to 1 in 250 adults.1 A heritable cause of DCM is present in 50% of individuals, and titin (TTN) is the most common DCM locus with truncating mutations found in approximately 25% of familial DCM and 18% of sporadic DCM cases,2 and mutations in a variety of sarcomere and non-sarcomere genes such as BAG3, TPM1, and CSRP3 are less common genetic forms of DCM.3 There are no approved therapies that directly address the decreased contractility and sarcomere dysfunction that underlies DCM.

Genome-wide association studies have identified MTSS1 as a modifier of cardiac function; genetic variation in a nearby regulatory region appears to lower cardiac expression of MTSS1 and is strongly and reproducibly associated with increased contractility and decreased risk for heart failure.4, 5, 6, 7, 8 The I-BAR protein MTSS1 was first identified as a metastasis suppressor missing in metastatic bladder carcinoma cell lines and is known to have several functions including a role in cell motility along with actin assembly and integrity.9,10 A mouse knockout of Mtss1 shows decreased left ventricular (LV) end-diastolic dimensions and LV end-systolic dimension, as well as trends towards increased LV fractional shortening,4 and appeared to have a modest beneficial effect upon contractility when crossed with a Tpm1 D230N mouse model of DCM.11

Here, we examine the relationship of a human genetic modifier of cardiovascular function and disease risk MTSS1, to uncover a new potential treatment for specific genetic forms of DCM. We demonstrate the specific effect of naturally occurring genetic variation upon MTSS1 expression in a group of individuals affected with TTN DCM and demonstrate a beneficial effect of MTSS1 knockdown in induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) models of TTN DCM. Furthermore, we additionally characterize the impact of MTSS1 upon the subcellular architecture of induced pluripotent stem cell cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CM) and propose a previously unrecognized role for MTSS1 modulating cardiomyocyte biology.

Methods

Survival Analyses and Human Genetics

Genetic and clinical data in the UKB (UK Biobank) cohort were obtained from the UKB. The UKB was approved by the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent to participate in the study. DCM was defined as ICD-10 diagnosis code I42.0 or ICD-9 code 425.4. Statistical analysis was performed with R software using the survival package (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Kaplan-Meier curves were produced with survfit function, and HRs were obtained from Cox regression model adjusted for sex and age at DCM diagnosis.

iPSC Culture and Maintenance

Wild-type (WT) iPSCs used in this study were derived from the WTC-11 line that was modified with a blasticidin resistance gene at the MYH6 locus. These cells were further modified with CRISPR to introduce a TTNA22353X+/− mutation to create the DCM mutant line. iPSCs were cultured in E8 medium (Life Technologies) on Matrigel (Corning) coated plates at 37 °C and 5% CO2, and medium was changed daily until cells achieved a confluency of approximately 80%. Cells were then passaged using 0.5 mmol/L EDTA in phosphate buffered saline (Invitrogen) as needed until differentiations.

iPSC Differentiation into iPSC-CMs

iPSCs were differentiated into ventricular cardiomyocytes using a Wnt-modulating protocol12. In brief, iPSCs were first induced with 7 μmol/L CHIR990921 (LC Labs) in RPMI+B27(−insulin) (Invitrogen). Two days postinduction, cells were placed in RPMI+B27(−insulin) for 24 hours, followed by RPMI+B27(−insulin) supplemented with 5 μmol/L IWP-2 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 48 hours. At 7 days postinduction, cells are then fed with RPMI+B27. At 9 days post-induction, cells are cultured in RPMI+B27 + 1 μmol/L blasticidin (Gibco) for 9 days to select for differentiated cardiomyocytes. Purified cells were frozen down in CryoStor10 (StemCell Technologies) 19 to 21 days postinduction. Only cardiomyocytes that were >90% cTnT+ as verified by flow cytometry were used in this study.

iPSC-CM Cell Culture

iPSC-CMs were thawed and added to a 384-well plate at a density of 10,000 cells/well. Wells were coated with 1:100 ratio of Matrigel up to 24 hours in advance. Edge wells were avoided to minimize edge effects, and cells were allowed to recover for 7 days with medium changes every 2 to 3 days with RPMI+B27. After 7 days, cells were transfected with 5 nmol/L small interfering RNA (siRNA) using Opti-MEM and maturation media. Medium with the siRNA was removed after 2 days, and medium changes occurred every 2 to 3 days post-siRNA treatment for 7 days. After 7 days, medium was removed from the plate, and wells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde. Wells were then stained for α-actinin (1:200 anti-ACTN2 rabbit monoclonal, Invitrogen 701914) and cMyBPc (1:200 anti-mybpc3 mouse monoclonal, Santa Cruz Biotechnology sc137237) and Hoechst nuclear dye.

Quantitative Microscopy Analyses

As described in the preceding text, sarcomere proteins (α-actinin and cMyBPc) were fluorescently tagged with AF+594 and AF+488 (Alexa Fluor, ThermoFisher), respectively, allowing for high-throughput imaging of sarcomeres using an IXM confocal microscope (Molecular Devices). Five nonoverlapping images were taken per well in a cross pattern avoiding edges and visualized using the FITC, TxRed, and DAPI channels. Images were read into a custom MATLAB script Tamarack. Learning from our previous sarcomere image analysis where we used a 2-class deep learning model based on healthy and diseased iPSC-CMs, Tamarack is a modified program incorporating aspects of SarcTrack,13 Tamarack employs 3 parallel Morlet wavelets with a set distance range, d, between peaks to represent the pattern of a sarcomere. The use of a 3 parallel wavelets reduced the incident of false-positive sarcomeres compared with the traditional double wavelet pattern. Images were initially processed by masking for sarcomeres using either channel to remove unstained space and improve analysis time. Masked images are contrast corrected and binarized. The wavelet is then convolved over the image and then rotated and resized to quantify sarcomere presence, orientation, and length. Tamarack reports sarcomere count, length, orientation, and pattern strength, as well as variation of all these metrics. Sarcomere count is reported as both an absolute count as well as normalized to nuclear count to account for variation in the number of cells per well. Outputs were averaged from each of the 5 images per well and reported as an average. Successful siRNA knockdown of both background genes and target gene (MTSS1) were confirmed in separate experiments.

Analysis of Tamarack Outputs

Random Forest/Sarcomere scoring methods

Analysis of Tamarack outputs were completed in R environment for statistical computing using the randomForest package version 4.7-1 (https://journal.r-project.org/articles/RN-2002-022/RN-2002-022.pdf). Raw data were cleaned by excluding conditions that resulted in 0 counts as well as the top and bottom 1% of all measured parameters. To inspect the data, Quantile-Quantile plots were used to visualize the distribution of data and to determine whether exclusion of additional outliers was necessary. Boxplots were used to visualize the quantile distribution, outliers, and mean of individual conditions, and compare them among treatment groups.

Data were normalized by plate and split into training and validation datasets in a 0.7 to 0.3 ratio across multiple plates using the untreated (WT/CDI) to siRNA-knockdown and/or isogenic background for each Mendelian cardiomyopathy gene tested (TTN, MLP/CSRP3, BAG3, RBM20, TPM1). The random forest was constructed and trained upon the training dataset, and the ntree, classwt, and nodesize parameters were manually optimized to maximize the negative predictive value of the final model to 95% or better. The best performing model was then applied to the data comprising the treatment groups of interest, with the output set to estimate the probability of each condition (untreated WT/CDI or siRNA/isogenic background). To assess the impact of a particular treatment group, the untreated WT/CDI probabilities were analyzed with either a Student t-test or standard linear model (lm function in R) and visualized with a beeswarm plot (ggplot2 v.3.4.2, ggbeeswarm 0.7.2). An example dataset along with an analysis and visualization script are publicly available at the Tenaya Therapeutics GitHub page: https://github.com/tenayatherapeutics/MTSS1_manuscript.

Engineered Heart Tissues

Engineered heart tissues (EHTs) were fabricated using the commercially available Mantarray kit (Curi Bio), and manufacturer’s protocols were used. In brief, casting wells arrayed in a 24-well plate format were prefilled with 50 μL of a 6.4 U/mL thrombin solution, and the post-lattice was inserted into these wells. iPSC-CMs and human cardiac fibroblasts (HCFs) were then mixed with 5 mg/mL of fibrinogen at a per tissue composition of 5 × 105 cardiomyocytes and 7.5 × 104 HCFs. This cell and fibrinogen solution was then added to the casting wells containing thrombin and thoroughly mixed around the inserted posts. Tissues were allowed to crosslink for 80 to 90 minutes at 37 °C before being lifted out of the casting wells and placed into a new 24-well plate filled with fresh RPMI+B27 medium. EHTs typically began beating within 1 week of casting. In experiments in which EHTs were transfected with siRNA, transfection occurred 18 days after tissue fabrication using the same timing and methods used in this study with 2-dimensional cardiomyocyte cultures.

Confocal Microscopy

CDI cells were thawed and added to a 6-plate at a density of 1 mole/L cells/well. Wells were coated with 1:100 ratio of Matrigel up to 24 hours in advance. After 96 hours, cells were transfected with 2.5 μg of MTSS1-GFP construct using ViaFect (Promega E4981) reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Medium with ViaFect and DNA was removed after 24 hours, and medium changes occurred every 2 days post-treatment for 4 days. After 4 days, medium was removed from the plate, and wells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde. Wells were then costained for α-actinin (1:200 dilution; Invitrogen anti-ACTN2 rabbit monoclonal antibody) and for nuclei with DAPI, and then imaged on a Leica Stellaris 5 confocal microscope using a 63× oil immersion objective.

Mouse Modeling of Ttn DCM

Applied Stemcell Inc. was contracted for creation of Ttn exon274(c.63116insAT) mice, using the CRISPR/Cas9 system with the oligo donor CAAGAACACAGTCAGCCTGGCGTGGCCTAAGCCAAGGCATGATGGTGGGAGCAAGATATCACTGGCTATGTGATTGAAGCCCAGAGAAAAGGGTCTGACCAGTGGACCCACATC on a C57BL/6 background. Animal experiments were preapproved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tenaya Therapeutics and were performed in compliance with internal animal use protocols (AUP: 2023.005 and 2023.007) and with all national requirements for the care and use of laboratory animals. Mouse genotyping was performed using standard approaches.

For the in vivo phenotyping, Ang II at 1,000 ng/kg/min was supplied by subcutaneous osmotic minipump starting at 12 weeks of age for 1 month. Echocardiography and body weight measurements were performed every 2 weeks. The L-NAME (N-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester) model was trialed as a stressor with 0.5 g/L L-NAME provided in the drinking water starting at 12 weeks of age. Echocardiography and body weight measurements were performed every 4 weeks for 16 weeks. Isoproterenol at 100 mg/kg was dosed subcutaneously for 5 days starting at 12 weeks of age. Echocardiography was performed 2 weeks and 4 weeks postinjection. Cardiac function was assessed by transthoracic echocardiography using high-resolution micro-imaging systems (Vevo 3100 Preclinical Imaging System, VisualSonics). Briefly, anesthetized spontaneously breathing mice (1% to 3% isoflurane and 98.5% to 99% O2) were placed in the supine position on a temperature-controlled heating platform to maintain their body temperature at ∼37 °C. Nair (Church & Dwight Co.) was used to remove hair and expose the skin to the probe. Parasternal short-axis M-mode tracings of the LV were recorded for LV mass and LV ejection fraction calculations. Vevo Lab software 5.6.1 was used for analyses.

Results

Human Genetics

The genomic mechanism for relationship of MTSS1 to cardiac phenotypes is hypothesized to be a disruption of a cardiac enhancer by a common genetic variant, confirmed by expressed quantitative trait analyses of multiple datasets where the rs35006907-C allele was associated with higher risk of DCM and increased MTSS1 expression in European and Han Chinese populations.4,14 Data from the Genotype Tissue Expression Consortium confirms this finding where the T allele of rs12541595 is strongly associated with lower left ventricular expression of MTSS1 (Figure 1A) and is in strong linkage with rs35006907 (R2 0.9799). Within the UK Biobank, a large prospective cohort of participants enrolled at age 40 years or later, 99 individuals carrying a pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutation in TTN developed a diagnosis of DCM.15 Individuals carrying both TTN pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants and the MTSS1 expression-lowering T allele of rs12541595 showed significantly improved event-free survival from cardiovascular death or heart transplantation (HR: 0.29; P = 0.0016) relative to individuals homozygous for the G allele (Figure 1B), suggesting that lower levels of MTSS1 may also be specifically protective from the contractile dysfunction that underlies TTN DCM.Figure 1Lower Cardiac Expression of MTSS1 Is Associated With Improved Survival in TTN DCM(A) The T allele of rs12541595 is strongly associated with lower expression of MTSS1 in left ventricular cardiac tissue and to a lesser extent within atrial tissue (−0.28 normalized expression per T allele; P = 9 × 10−08). iPSC Models of DCM

To explore the impact of MTSS1 knockdown on cardiomyocyte biology, we performed high-throughput, high-content confocal microscopy to quantify aspects of cardiomyocyte and sarcomere biology (number of sarcomeres, sarcomere length, sarcomere angle, and sarcomere score/quality) based on previously described modifications of deep learning approach.16, 17, 13 Using the imaging-derived parameters, we developed a set of classification algorithms to incorporate the entirety of the sarcomere imaging data to distinguish untreated WT iPSC-CMs from DCM models of disease induced by siRNA knockdown. For each DCM model, a random forest classifier was constructed that incorporated and weighted the sarcomere biology data to output the probability that an image was derived from WT cells (treated with a scrambled siRNA [SCR]) or a DCM disease gene (Figure 2A). The prediction models displayed good or excellent discriminatory performance when evaluated on a held-out test dataset separate from the training data (Supplemental Figure S1). We observed that siRNA knockdown of MTSS1 shifted the classification of cells treated with siRNA-TTN (P = 2.9 × 10−6) and in a dose-dependent effect for siRNA-CSRP3 (PLM_dose = 1.34 × 10−14) and a dose-dependent effect for RBM20 (P = 5.7 × 10−4) (Figures 2B, 2C, and 2E). By contrast, treatment with siRNA-MTSS1 only modestly shifted the model classifications of the WT iPSCs treated with siRNA for TPM1 with a much smaller dose-dependent effect (P = 0.07) (Figure 2D). Importantly, we observed that the siRNA models of DCM mildly impact iPSC-CM survival (Supplemental Figure S2). The primary clinical deficit in DCM is decreased cardiac contractility. Therefore, to further explore the in silico observations and hypotheses generated from high-throughput imaging, we examined the effect of MTSS1 knockdown in a variety of iPSC-CM models of DCM using a commercially available EHT system. EHTs seeded with cells carrying a known pathogenic mutation in TTNA22353X display impaired contractility relative to isogenic controls (Supplemental Figure S4) and when TTNA22353X EHTs were treated with siRNA for MTSS1 (siMTSS1) we observed an improvement of 147% in twitch force (P = 3.1 × 10−13) (Figure 3A) and similar improvements in contraction and relaxation velocities (Supplemental Figure S5A). Additional experiments confirmed improvements in twitch force were correlated with the degree of siRNA-mediated reduction in MTSS1 levels (Supplemental Figure S6). Next, we surveyed the impact of siRNA-MTSS1 on other forms of genetic DCM. Improved contractility was observed in EHTs with siRNA knockdown of CSRP3/MLP treated with siMTSS1 (Figure 3B), as well as with EHTs consisting of an isogenic line of RBM20 carrying the known pathogenic mutation S635F19 where we observed a 284% improvement in twitch force (P < 2 × 10-16) (Figure 3C). Again, significant improvements in twitch kinetics were also observed in both DCM backgrounds when treated with siMTSS1 (Supplemental Figures S5B and S5C). Notably, there was no improvement in sarcomere appearance in quantitative microscopy and no corresponding improvement in contractility in BAG3 iPSC-CM models of DCM treated with siRNA MTSS1 (Supplemental Figure S7). Mechanistic Investigations

To gain a better understanding of the role of MTSS1 within the cardiomyocytes and sarcomere biology, we examined the quantitative microscopy data for all siRNA and genetic iPSC-CM backgrounds tested. Notably with siRNA knockdown of MTSS1 we observed a reproducible increase in the total number of sarcomeres and the number of sarcomeres per cardiomyocyte across different genetic backgrounds (Figure 4, Supplemental Figure S9). Given the observation that siRNA knockdown of MTSS1 appeared to increase the number of sarcomeres within an iPSC-CM and improved measures of contractility in select iPSC-CM models of DCM, we sought to further investigate the molecular mechanism within cardiomyocytes by which MTSS1 exerts effects on sarcomeres and contractility. Formation of new sarcomeres in mature cardiomyocytes appears to arise from actin stress fibers within the perinuclear region of cardiomyocytes.20 Existing single-cell data suggest that as a cytoskeletal-related actin binding protein, MTSS1 is known to have relatively ubiquitous expression including cardiomyocytes (Supplemental Figure S10A), and other than actin, is not previously known to have binding partners that modulate contractility or sarcomere function in cardiomyocytes (Supplemental Figure S10B). To interrogate the protein binding partners of MTSS1 specific to cardiomyocytes, we performed adeno-associated virus (AAV) infection of a wild-type iPSC-CM cell line with a FLAG-tagged MTSS1 construct followed by a pull-down and mass spectrometry (Figure 5B). Relative to an AAV transfection of FLAG-tagged GFP control, the MTSS1 construct appeared to interact with high-affinity to previously known binding partners21,22 (Supplemental Table S1), multiple members of the protein phosphatase 2A complex, and notably, the unconventional myosin MYO18A, a protein known to potentiate the formation of actin-stress fibers23,24 (Figure 5C). To assess these findings, individual coimmunoprecipitation assays confirmed a protein-protein interaction in iPSC-CMs between MTSS1 and PPP2R2A and MYO18A alongside ACTN2 and ACTA2, suggesting a direct interaction between MTSS1 and the key components of actin-stress fibers involved in formation of sarcomeres (Figure 5D).

To better understand the performed RNA sequencing of EHTs seeded with TTNA22353X, iPSC-CMs comparing the transcriptional response of EHTs treated with siRNA-MTSS1 were compared with siRNA scrambled control. Unbiased analyses uncovered up-regulation of genes that are known to be involved in cardiac hypertrophy and calcium signaling such as TMEM64 and CCDC4725, 26, 27 (Figure 5D), as well as the mechanical response of cardiac fibroblasts to hypertrophy.28 Importantly, we found a relative up-regulation in some contractile sarcomere proteins (Figure 5D), most notably siRNA MTSS1–mediated increases in TTN (0.41 log2FC; P = 4.9 × 10−4) and MYH7 (0.29 log2FC; P = 2.9 × 10−6), alongside a relative decrease in components of the sarcomere including TNNC1 the calcium sensing subunit of the cardiac troponin complex (−0.16 log2FC; P = 0.004) and the contractile modulatory protein MYBPC3 (−0.11 log2FC; P = 0.01) compared with iPSC-CMs treated with a SCR control. Additionally, we observed relative up-regulation in key calcium handling, desmosome, and Wnt/Hippo signaling genes and relative down-regulation of apoptosis genes, as well as down-regulation of heart failure marker NPPA (−2.5 log2FC; P = 0.05) (Figure 5E and Supplemental Figure S11). Relative to SCR-treated controls, the transcriptional response of increased Z-disk components, calcium, and hypertrophy -related genes in MTSS1 knockdown–treated cells is consistent with the observation of a greater number of sarcomeres (Figure 5F) and increased twitch force.

Discussion

In a multimodal set of analyses combining human genetics with human model systems of cardiac disease, we provide evidence supporting a relationship of lower cardiac MTSS1 levels to beneficial outcomes in 3 Mendelian forms of DCM. Our findings suggest the outlines of a cardiomyocyte-specific mechanism by which MTSS1 is involved in regulating sarcomere production or turnover via interactions with actin stress fiber-related protein MYO18A and ACTN2 and ACTA.23 Consistent with our observation of perinuclear cytoplasmic localization of MTSS1, new sarcomere formation from actin stress fibers is a cytoplasmic process localized away from the periphery of the cell.20 Earlier exploration of MTSS1 as a potential therapeutic target did not show a clear beneficial effect in a mouse model of Tpm1 DCM,11 which is consistent with our findings in vitro where a beneficial effect was only observed for specific genetic forms of DCM. Importantly, the in vitro findings of improved contractility in EHTs modeling TTN DCM observed with siRNA knockdown of MTSS1 are consistent with the novel observation of improved survival from cardiovascular death or heart transplantation within individuals affected with TTN DCM who carry common alleles clearly demonstrated to reduce the cardiac expression of MTSS1 in other research. Overall, these data support the potential for therapeutic benefits of reducing MTSS1 levels in select forms of DCM defined by a specific Mendelian genetic etiology and may suggest a therapeutic mechanism whereby MTSS1 modulates the number of sarcomeres in a cardiomyocyte.

Current treatment for Mendelian DCM falls within standard of care for heart failure including beta-blockade, afterload reduction, and SGLT2 inhibition, but importantly, does include therapies that address the underlying causes of reduced contractility.29 The repeated observation in genome-wide association studies of improved contractility and heart failure outcomes with the T allele of rs12541595 that confers lower cardiac MTSS1 levels highlights the importance of using human genetics to uncover novel targets for heart failure. However, it is worth noting that in addition to supporting target selection, our results also suggest that human genetics can support the first steps of clinical development enabling hypotheses about patient populations where a therapeutic target may or may not be effective.30 Therefore, an increased understanding of the genetic determinants and modifiers of disease and phenotypes in humans is likely to provide strong hypotheses for both target selection as well as matching the specific targets to select patient populations in order to reduce the time and costs of creating new therapies.31

Our approach employed machine learning to capture data from high-content microscopy followed by quantitative analyses, which enabled the detection of changes in the cardiomyocyte and sarcomere—this exemplifies a robust and reproducible analytical approach for assessing the validity of potential targets. In addition to identifying MTSS1 as a target for knockdown to ameliorate DCM, this study contributes to the growing understanding of genetic targets for heart failure and paves the way for future therapeutic development using similar strategies.32

Study limitations

Our findings are not without limitations. Although a potential mechanism for relating MTSS1 levels to sarcomere turnover or production in cardiomyocytes is suggested by the findings of increased sarcomeres and interactions with key proteins related to sarcomere assembly, a direct mechanistic link to these processes is not yet revealed by our data. In general, iPSC-CMs have proven to be useful models of contractile phenotypes of cardiomyocytes; however, even the most mature in vitro systems do not replicate the complexities of cardiovascular maturation, physiology, and multisystem pathophysiology of the adult human heart.33 The attempts to create a mouse model of TTNtv-DCM with 4 different stressors did not result in a reproducible cardiovascular phenotype, and thus, an in vivo validation of the therapeutic hypothesis is provided here (Supplemental Figure S8). The findings also suggest interactions of MTSS1 with the serine/threonine protein phosphatase 2A complex, which has a variety of well-described roles in regulating cardiac physiology34—therefore, it is possible that MTSS1 may modulate sarcomere structure and function in multiple ways. Our observation relating an increased number of sarcomeres to increased contractility may suggest that sarcomere production and/or turnover could be relevant clinical and translational phenotypes for heart failure and cardiomyopathy in general. Further work on the relationship of MTSS1 to sarcomere structure and cardiomyocyte function is necessary.

Additional limitations are inherent to the nature of the findings from human genetics; the observation of protective effects of lower MTSS1 in human TTN DCM is enabled by a large number of individuals in the UK Biobank with pathogenic mutations in TTN, the most prevalent Mendelian genetic form of DCM. Although the observation of protective effect is striking and supported by the experimental data reported here, the protective allele for MTSS1 is present since conception and does not represent an intervention, though the effect on DCM risk is assumed to be outweighed by the Mendelian TTN allele. This observation of a protective effect requires replication in another longitudinal dataset of individuals affected with TTN DCM, and unfortunately no evidence derived from human genetics is yet available for RBM20 or CSRP3, which are much more rare causes of monogenic DCM than TTN.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings illustrate the power of human genetics to select specific targets for cardiovascular disease, as well as to identify patient populations affected with Mendelian DCM that are likely to benefit from manipulation of a specific targets. These data suggest that careful quantitative approaches to cardiomyocyte biology can predict meaningful phenotypic responses of EHTs, which are in turn representative of observations from human genetics. Together these data link human genetics with in vitro human model systems to illustrate the potential for quantitative approaches to cardiovascular discovery.
