Protocol for analyzing shoot apical meristem development and heat stress responses in tomato
Authors: Nan Xiao, Cao Xu, Xiaozhen Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryShoot apical meristem (SAM) development is critical for plant aerial architecture and reproduction, but its spatiotemporal specificity and phenotyping challenges hinder mechanistic studies, particularly under stresses. Here, we present a protocol for molecular and cellular analysis of SAM development and stress response in tomato. We describe steps for imaging, developmental stage-specific heat treatment, and recovery growth. We then detail procedures for microdissection and tissue sectioning. This protocol enables high-resolution investigation of SAM development in response to heat stress.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Huang et al.1Subject areas: Genetics, Plant sciences, Molecular Biology

Before you begin

Shoot apical meristem (SAM) serves as the developmental foundation for all aerial organs in plants and plays a central role in determining flowering time, inflorescence architecture, and ultimately, reproductive success and crop yield.2,3,4,5,6 In tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), SAM development follows a tightly regulated progression through five well-defined stages: early vegetative meristem (EVM), middle vegetative meristem (MVM), late vegetative meristem (LVM), transition meristem (TM), and flower meristem (FM).3 This developmental trajectory determines flowering time and inflorescence architecture of tomato, thereby influencing fruit harvest time and yield.

This protocol integrates the following key procedures: developmental stage identification and imaging, developmental stage-specific heat treatment and recovery growth, microdissection, RNA extraction from low-input samples and transcriptional profiling, agarose-embedded sectioning, reactive oxygen species (ROS) staining. It enables systematic analysis of physiological and molecular changes in the SAM of tomato in response to heat stress.

Originally developed for tomato cultivar Ailsa Craig (AC) in our associated research, this protocol is also adaptable to SAM studies in other plant species. Notably, the microdissection technique also facilitates low-input RNA transcriptomes analysis of specific tissues, while the agarose-embedded sectioning method supports cytological and biochemical studies of fresh tissues in broader biological contexts.

Innovation

SAM development exhibits pronounced spatiotemporal specificity, with distinct adaptive mechanisms to heat stress across vegetative, transitional, and reproductive stages. Phenotypic observation requires extensive microscopic manipulation, but until now, a systematic and reliable workflow for dissecting SAM development and stress responses has been lacking. To address this gap, our protocol establishes an integrated research system using tomato as a model, combining strategic heat treatment, high-resolution SAM imaging, microdissection and tissue sectioning, as well as transcriptional level analysis. This unified workflow enables simultaneous investigation of structural, molecular, and physiological aspects of SAM, overcoming limitations of previous approaches that examined these components separately. By providing a comprehensive toolkit, the protocol allows high-resolution spatiotemporal analysis of SAM stem cell dynamics and stress adaptation mechanisms, facilitating breakthroughs in understanding plant developmental plasticity and environmental responses.

Preparation of buffer

Timing: 1 day

Paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution (4%, w/v): Dissolve 4 g of PFA powder in 100 mL of ddH2O. Adjust the pH to approximately 11.0 using solid KOH pellets while stirring to aid dissolution. Once fully dissolved, slowly adjust the pH to 7.0 using concentrated sulfuric acid. Store the solution at 4°C in the dark, for up to one week.

CRITICAL: PFA is toxic, and both KOH and concentrated sulfuric acid are highly corrosive. Wear gloves, lab coat, and protective eyewear. A face mask is recommended.

Note: For optimal fixation quality, PFA solutions should be prepared on the day of use. Better results are obtained when it is freshly prepared.

Step-by-step method details

Identification of developmental stage and imaging of SAM

Timing: 10–22 days

This step describes how to identify the developmental stages of the tomato SAM and perform imaging for documentation. Accurate identification of developmental stage is critical for timing the subsequent heat treatment.

1.Identification of SAM developmental stages.a.Remove all visible leaves from the tomato seedling using fine-tipped forceps.b.Under a stereomicroscope, carefully dissect the remaining young leaves using a 1 mL syringe needle, retaining two leaf primordia on either side of the SAM to aid in orientation.c.Observe the exposed SAM morphology and determine the developmental stage based on the size and morphology of dome.3d.Record the number of days after sowing and the total number of leaves present at the time of SAM stage identification (see Figure 2 for representative SAM stages of cultivar AC).

A representative timeline for the development of cultivar AC under our experimental conditions is shown in Figure 2.

Note: In our study, the developmental stages of the tomato SAM are categorized as EVM (early vegetative meristem), MVM (middle vegetative meristem), LVM (late vegetative meristem), TM (transition meristem), and FM (flower meristem). CRITICAL: Avoid damaging the meristem dome during dissection, as this is essential for accurate morphological staging of the SAM. This can be achieved by using the needle of 1 mL syringe to carefully remove surrounding leaf primordium without touching the dome.

CRITICAL: Operators are advised to develop proficiency through repeated practice on representative samples prior to formal morphological assessment, ensuring reliable and consistent staging.

2.Imaging of the SAM.a.Dissect the shoot apex under a stereomicroscope, retaining one intact leaf primordium on each side of the SAM to aid in orientation (see Figure 3A).b.Once the front is confirmed, gently press the stem with your fingers to flatten the cylindrical surface.i.Stabilize the sample on the stereomicroscope stage.ii.Adjust exposure, white balance, and other camera settings as needed to optimize image clarity.c.Immediately capture images of the dissected apex using a stereomicroscope equipped with an integrated digital camera.d.Acquire a z-stack of images by gradually adjusting the focal plane from top to bottom.Note: Each layer corresponds to an optical section. Once the upper and lower boundaries of the focal plane are defined and the total number of images is specified, the step size is automatically calculated by the software. Because the thickness of the SAM varies among genotypes and developmental stages, the number of optical sections should be carefully adjusted.Note: We generally recommend acquiring 15–25 layers per sample. Using fewer than 15 sections may result in blurred composite images, while acquiring more than 25 sections may cause software instability or slow performance. Users should evaluate the tissue thickness of their own samples to determine the appropriate number of sections.e.Save the images in ZVI format and perform multi-dimensional image merging.f.Add annotations, including leaf number and scale bar, and save the final image in TIFF format for documentation and publication.Note: The software-guided imaging workflow, including camera parameter adjustment, z-stack acquisition, and image export, is illustrated in Figure 3B.

Developmental stage-specific heat treatment and recovery growth

Timing: variable (depending on heat treatment duration and the developmental stage of application)

This step describes how to perform developmental stage-specific heat treatments on tomato plants and monitor their recovery growth. Two types of heat treatment regime were used in our associated research article to investigate the impact of heat stress on meristem development and reproductive outcomes (Figure 4).1

3.Transfer plants to heat stress conditions.a.Select tomato seedlings grown under normal conditions and identify their SAM stage according to the method described above.b.For continuous heat treatment: Select plants at the early vegetative meristem (EVM) stage and transfer them into a growth chamber set to heat stress conditions (16 h light at 35°C / 8 h dark at 28°C, 50% relative humidity, 80–100 μmol m−2 s−1 light intensity). Allow plants to grow continuously under these conditions until they reach the middle vegetative meristem (MVM), late vegetative meristem (LVM), transition meristem (TM), and flower meristem (FM) stages.c.For stage-specific heat wave treatment: Select plants at the EVM, MVM, LVM, TM, and FM stages grown under normal conditions. Simultaneously transfer all selected plants to the growth chamber set to heat stress conditions (16 h light at 35°C / 8 h dark at 28°C, 50% relative humidity, 80–100 μmol m−2 s−1 light intensity) and maintain them under heat conditions for 14 days.d.For both treatments, retain a matched group of plants under normal growth conditions as untreated controls.

Agarose-embedded sectioning and imaging of SAM

Timing: 1 day

This step describes the agarose embedding and sectioning of tomato SAMs to obtain high-quality, viable tissue sections for microscopic analysis. These sections can be used for the downstream applications, such as subcellular protein localization (Figure 6A) or ROS detection. In this protocol, we use hydroxyphenyl fluorescein (HPF) staining as an example to visualize ROS level in SAM cells (Figure 6B).

11.Agarose embedding and sectioning of fresh SAM tissue.a.Prepare 6% agarose solution in ddH2O by heating in a water bath at 100°C until fully dissolved.CRITICAL: Remove any bubbles from the agarose solution to ensure even embedding and proper sectioning of the samples.Pause point: If the agarose solution is not used immediately, place the tube in a container of boiling water and transfer it to a 65°C oven. For optimal performance, the solution can be kept at this temperature for up to 3 h.b.Select seedlings based on developmental stage.c.Remove any large visible leaves from the shoot apex.d.Carefully cut approximately 2 mm of the shoot apex.e.Tissue fixation.i.Immerse the samples in 4% PFA solution.ii.Perform vacuum infiltration at a pressure range of 0.8–0.9 MPa for continuous 30 min.CRITICAL: Ensure that the tissue is fully submerged during vacuum infiltration to achieve uniform fixation.iii.Wash the fixed samples three times with PBS (5 min per wash).f.Cool agarose to about 50°C and embed the sample in a plastic embedding bottom mold, positioning it horizontally at the center.Pause point: Place the mold at 4°C for 15 min to solidify the agarose.g.Trim the agarose block to an appropriate size for sectioning.Note: Trim the agarose block into a four-sided trapezoidal shape, with the base wider than the top. This increases the contact area between the block and the vibratome stage, providing better stability during sectioning.h.Stick trimmed agarose blocks on the vibratome stage with a wettable paper tape using super glue.i.Adjust the vibratome settings to produce 50 μm-thick sections, then start the program.j.Immediately collect slices using fine-tipped forceps and transfer them into PBS in a culture dish for further analysis.

Expected outcomes

This integrated protocol enables detailed molecular and cellular analysis of the SAM in plants, providing a framework to investigate SAM development, transcriptional regulation, protein behavior and physiological responses.

By staging and imaging the SAM, researchers can generate a genotype- and condition-specific developmental timeline, including leaf number, day after sowing, and meristem morphology (such as size and shape) at defined stages. These data facilitate the elucidation of gene functions and the developmental impact of stress treatments, such as heat.1

Developmental stage-specific heat treatments followed by recovery growth allow precise assessment of short-term heat wave effects on crop development and reproductive outcomes. For example, 14-day heat stress applied during the EVM stage delays SAM progression and floral transition. Upon return to normal growth conditions, plants generally exhibit normal inflorescence development and stable fruit yield (Figure 4B).1 In contrast, heat stress during later stages (MVM, LVM, TM, FM) often results in inflorescence defects such as branching and floral reversion, leading to significant yield penalties (Figure 4B).1 This approach thus supports mechanistic studies of reproductive thermotolerance and developmental plasticity.

Microdissected SAM tissues yield high-quality RNA suitable for low-input transcriptional profiling. Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) or RNA-seq can reveal gene expression dynamics associated with developmental transitions and stress responses. For example, expression of the floral identity gene ANATHA (AN) is delayed under heat stress, consistent with the postponed floral transition.1 The protocol supports the analysis of regulatory networks integrating developmental programs and environmental signals.

Vibratome sectioning of agarose-embedded SAMs yields high-quality, intact tissue slices suitable for imaging. These fresh slices can be used to monitor changes in protein behavior in vivo, such as phase separation, or perform physiological assays such as ROS detection in vivo. For instance, heat stress promotes punctate localization of TMF proteins within nuclei and increases ROS accumulation in the SAM.1 This method facilitates the visualization of molecular and physiological responses within meristematic tissues and is broadly applicable to related studies of other young plant tissues.

Collectively, this protocol provides a comprehensive strategy for investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying SAM developmental programming and stress-responsive reprogramming at the phenotypic, transcriptional, translational, and physiological levels in tomato. The techniques are adaptable to other plant species with accessible SAMs and offer a versatile platform for exploring developmental plasticity and environmental acclimation in plants.

Limitations

Although SAM developmental stages are defined based on meristem morphology, the number of leaves and days after germination required to reach each stage can vary significantly across genotypes and environmental conditions. Genetic background and growth environment affect the pace of meristem maturation and its response to heat. Therefore, developmental stage boundaries should be empirically determined when applying this protocol to different cultivars or growth conditions.

This protocol relies on growth chambers with precisely controlled environmental parameters to ensure reproducible heat treatment. In field or semi-natural conditions, temperature fluctuations often co-occur with variations in light, humidity, soil conditions and other weather changes. Therefore, the protocol is best suited for controlled environments and may not fully capture plant responses in complex natural conditions.

Additionally, the use of agarose embedding and vibratome sectioning enables the preparation of viable tissue slices suitable for imaging and physiological assays but limits achievable resolution. The section thickness is typically in the range of 30-100 μm, which may not be sufficient for studies requiring ultrathin sections or high-resolution imaging of individual cell layers.

Potential solution

•Minimize the time between leaf primordia dissection and imaging, as the SAM rapidly dehydrates and collapses when exposed to air.

Minimize the time between leaf primordia dissection and imaging, as the SAM rapidly dehydrates and collapses when exposed to air.

•Shorten the imaging process itself to reduce the time the tissue remains uncovered and limit the number of Z-stack layers to avoid prolonged exposure. We recommend capturing no more than 25 layers per sample.

Shorten the imaging process itself to reduce the time the tissue remains uncovered and limit the number of Z-stack layers to avoid prolonged exposure. We recommend capturing no more than 25 layers per sample.

•Retain one intact leaf primordium on each side of the SAM to help maintain orientation during imaging.

Retain one intact leaf primordium on each side of the SAM to help maintain orientation during imaging.

•Ensure that the SAM is fully exposed and not obscured by surrounding tissue; gently reposition the leaf primordia using a fine needle under the stereomicroscope if necessary.

Ensure that the SAM is fully exposed and not obscured by surrounding tissue; gently reposition the leaf primordia using a fine needle under the stereomicroscope if necessary.

Ensure that brown glass vials are stored upright during freezing; never place them upside down.
