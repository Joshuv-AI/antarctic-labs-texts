A Phase 2 study of CPX‐351 in combination with venetoclax in patients with newly diagnosed high‐risk acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Wei‐Ying Jen, Jennifer Croden, Emmanuel Almanza‐Huante, Courtney DiNardo, Kelly Chien, Danielle Hammond, Wei Qiao, Yesid Alvarado, Lucia Masarova, Andres E Quesada, Sherry Pierce, Alex Bataller, Guillermo Garcia‐Manero, Amin Alousi, Nicholas Short, Naval Daver, Farhad Ravandi, Hagop Kantarjian, Tapan M Kadia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVenetoclax has been combined with intensive chemotherapy regimens in the treatment of acute myeloid leukemia (AML). We aimed to investigate the safety and efficacy of venetoclax combined with full‐dose CPX‐351 (CPX + VEN) in newly diagnosed (ND) AML. Seventeen patients with a median age of 59 years (range, 43–69) were treated; 71% had secondary AML, 47% had prior hypomethylating agent (HMA) exposure, 59% had myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS)‐related (MR) mutations, 47% had complex karyotype, and 29% were TP53 mutated. The overall response rate (ORR) was 82% (95% CI, 57–96) with a composite complete remission rate (CRc) of 71% (95% CI, 50–93). Patients with MR mutations had an ORR of 100% (95% CI, 69–100), including a CRc of 90% (95% CI, 55–100). Patients with prior HMA exposure had a CRc of 63% (95% CI, 24–91). With a median follow‐up time of 11.8 months, the median overall survival (OS) was 12.8 months (95% CI, 5–NE) with a 2‐year OS of 34% (95% CI, 10–61). Patients with MR mutations had a median OS of 17.9 months versus 5.1 months in patients without MR mutations (P = 0.039). Twelve (86%) of 14 responding patients proceeded to stem cell transplant (SCT); the median recurrence‐free survival and OS landmarked from date of SCT were 14.7 months (95% CI, 1–32) and 14.7 months (95% CI, 4–25), respectively. The 4‐week mortality was 0% and the 8‐week mortality was 17%. The most common adverse events were related to myelosuppression. CPX + VEN resulted in high remission rates and enabled progression to allogenic SCT for the majority of a highly adverse group of ND AML patients.

INTRODUCTION

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a heterogeneous disease characterized by the expansion of malignant myeloid precursors in the bone marrow. Despite advances in the field over the last decade, the “7 + 3” combination of cytarabine and an anthracycline has remained the standard induction chemotherapy regimen in most centers for younger, fit patients, producing complete remission (CR) rates from approximately 60%–85%. 1 , 2 Modifications to this regimen, including the addition of purine nucleoside analogs3, 4, 5 or venetoclax, 6 have been investigated with the goal of improving response rate, deepening remissions, and prolonging survival. To date, the optimal induction regimen for newly diagnosed (ND) AML patients has not been determined.

Venetoclax is a BCL2 inhibitor that has previously shown synergism with lower intensity chemotherapy regimens, including 5‐azacitidine 7 and low‐dose cytarabine (LDAC), 8 resulting in improved CR rates and overall survival (OS). Based on Phase III randomized trials, the combination of venetoclax with lower intensity therapy, such as LDAC or hypomethylating agents (HMAs), is approved for patients with ND AML aged ≥75 years or those deemed unfit for intensive chemotherapy. Following this approval, the addition of venetoclax to intensive induction regimens, including cladribine, idarubicin, cytarabine (CLIA), 9 , 10 fludarabine, cytarabine, idarubicin (FLAG‐IDA),11, 12, 13, 14 and “7 + 3” 6 has been investigated. The addition of venetoclax to intensive chemotherapy, regardless of backbone, has consistently resulted in high rates of composite CR and measurable residual disease (MRD) negative CR.

CPX‐351 is a nanoscale liposome that includes a fixed 5:1 molar ratio of cytarabine and daunorubicin. It is hypothesized that the liposomal formulation may facilitate a controlled release of daunorubicin and cytarabine concentrating within leukemic cells in the bone marrow with reduced systemic drug distribution. 15 Clinically, this results in comparatively more myelosuppression than conventional “7 + 3” but is associated with less extramedullary toxicity and is theorized to overcome mechanisms of chemotherapy resistance. 16 In patients with ND high‐risk or secondary AML, CPX‐351 has demonstrated superior response rates and OS compared to “7 + 3”. 17 Specifically, in the pivotal Phase III trial of 309 patients, CPX‐351 had a CR/CR with incomplete hematologic recovery (CRi) rate of 48% and a median OS of 9.3 months, compared to 33% and 5.6 months in the 7 + 3 control arm, respectively. The rate of all‐cause mortality was also lower with CPX‐351 than with 7 + 3 (81% vs. 93%). 17 Based on these data, CPX‐351 is approved as frontline therapy in patients with ND therapy‐related AML and AML with myelodysplasia‐related changes (AML‐MRC).

Drawing from encouraging data from ongoing studies of intensive chemotherapy with venetoclax for patients with ND AML, we hypothesized that the combination of CPX‐351 and venetoclax (CPX + VEN) could be highly synergistic, clinically effective, and associated with lower rates of extramedullary toxicity in ND AML patients eligible for intensive chemotherapy.

METHODS

Participants

Patients between the ages of 18 and 69 with ND AML who were fit for intensive chemotherapy were eligible for enrollment. ND AML was defined as AML in patients who had not received prior treatment for AML; patients with a preceding myeloid neoplasm who had received treatment for that disorder (e.g., HMA for prior myelodysplastic syndrome [MDS]) and/or patients with therapy‐related AML could be included. Patients who had previously received venetoclax could be included. Additional inclusion criteria included: Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS) ≤ 2, creatinine ≤ 1.5× upper limit of normal (ULN), bilirubin ≤ 2 mg/dL, aspartate transaminase (AST) or alanine transaminase (ALT) ≤ 3× ULN (or ≤5× ULN if related to leukemic involvement), and an ejection fraction of ≥45%.

Patients who had received prior treatment with CPX‐351, had uncontrolled intercurrent illness, had active central nervous system (CNS) involvement of leukemia, or had New York Heart Association (NYHA) Class III/IV congestive heart failure or clinically significant cardiac arrhythmias were excluded. The AML subtypes of acute promyelocytic leukemia and core binding factor AML were also excluded.

Study design

This was a Phase Ib/II trial that was conducted at The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center to evaluate the safety and efficacy of CPX + VEN. The trial consisted of a lead‐in dose finding portion, followed by two expansion cohorts: one in patients with ND AML and a second in patients with relapsed/refractory AML. In this manuscript, we summarize the characteristics and outcomes of ND patients.

In the lead‐in dose finding portion, dose level −2 was chosen as the recommended Phase 2 dose (RP2D) for the expansion phase (Table S1). The expansion phase implemented the RP2D as follows: (induction) CPX‐351 (44 mg/m2 of daunorubicin and 100 mg/m2 of cytarabine) on Days 1, 3, and 5, plus venetoclax 300 mg on Days 2–8. To mitigate the risk of tumor lysis syndrome, venetoclax was started on Day 2 and was ramped up from 100 mg on Day 2 to the target dose of 300 mg on Day 4. Venetoclax dosing was adjusted for concomitant use of cytochrome P450 Family 3 Subfamily A (CYP3A) inhibitors, including azole antifungals; moderate inhibitors required a dose reduction to 150 mg daily, and strong inhibitors required a dose reduction to 50 mg daily.

Patients who did not respond to the first induction could receive an additional re‐induction regimen of CPX‐351 (44 mg/m2 of daunorubicin and 100 mg/m2 of cytarabine) on Days 1 and 3 and venetoclax 300 mg daily on Days 2–8. Patients who did not have a response to either induction cycle were taken off study. Patients who responded to induction could receive up to four cycles of consolidation chemotherapy with the following regimen: (consolidation) CPX‐351 (22 mg/m2 of daunorubicin and 50 mg/m2 of cytarabine) on Days 1 and 3 and venetoclax 300 mg daily on Days 2–8. Treatment was continued until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, patient/physician preference, or stem cell transplant (SCT).

Study procedures

Before starting treatment, AML diagnosis was confirmed by bone marrow aspiration and biopsy in all patients. Cytogenetic analysis by conventional karyotyping, next‐generation sequencing (NGS) using a targeted 81‐gene panel, 18 , 19 and multiparameter flow‐cytometry (MFC) were performed at study enrollment. MRD was assessed via MFC with a sensitivity of 10−4 on each remission bone marrow sample.

All patients received antiviral, antibacterial, and antifungal prophylaxis while on study. Tumor lysis syndrome prophylaxis included intravenous hydration starting the day before venetoclax initiation (Day 1) and treatment with either allopurinol or rasburicase. Filgrastim or pegfilgrastim use was permitted on‐protocol at the discretion of the treating physician.

All patients provided written informed consent before participation. The trial adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the Good Clinical Practice standards established by the International Council for Harmonization. The trial is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov under the identifier NCT03629171.

Outcome measures

The study's primary outcome was composite complete remission rate (CRc), which is comprised of patients achieving CR, CR without platelet recovery (CRp), and CRi, as defined by the International Working Group. 20 Overall response rate (ORR) included CRc plus morphologic leukemia‐free state (MLFS). Secondary outcomes included OS (time from Cycle 1, Day 1 to death from any cause) and event‐free survival (EFS; time from Cycle 1, Day 1 to death, relapse, or nonresponse, whichever occurred first). Recurrence‐free survival (RFS) after SCT was defined as the time from SCT to relapse or death from any cause. Adverse events were monitored continuously and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0.

Statistical analysis

A Bayesian model 21 was used to monitor efficacy and toxicity. Stopping rules were designed to halt the trial if there was a >90% chance that the grade ≥ 3 therapy‐related non‐hematologic toxicity rate exceeded 30% or if there was a <2.5% chance that the average CRc rate was larger than 35%.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) software. Ninety‐five percent confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated for CRc rates. Time‐to‐event analyses were estimated with the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log‐rank test.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Eighteen patients with ND AML were enrolled and treated between November 2018 and January 2022; one patient was taken off the trial on Day 2 of treatment after he was discovered to have a FLT3‐ITD mutation. Therefore, 17 patients were available for analysis. Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. The median age of the cohort was 59 years (range, 43–69), and 65% of patients were male. Notably, 12 patients (71%) had an antecedent hematologic disorder (AHD), including 9 (53%) with MDS and 2 (12%) with chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML). Approximately half of the patients (47%) had treated secondary AML (ts‐AML), having had prior treatment with HMA for their MDS or CMML before transformation to AML. 22 , 23 One patient (6%) had previously received both venetoclax and an allogeneic SCT for a prior diagnosis of high‐risk MDS.

Responses

Response rates are summarized in Table 2. Patients received a median of two cycles (range, 1–5) of CPX + VEN, and required a median of one induction cycle (range, 1–2) to achieve the best response. The ORR was 82% (95% CI, 57–96). The CRc rate was 71% (95% CI, 50–93), comprising 5 patients (29%) with a CR and 8 patients (47%) with a CRi. One patient (6%) achieved an MLFS. Of the patients who responded, 79% (95% CI, 35–87) had undetectable MRD by MFC. Patients' MRD status after each cycle of therapy is available in the supplementary materials (Table S2).

Patients with MR gene mutations had an ORR of 100% (95% CI, 69–100) including 90% (95% CI, 55–100) CRc and undetectable MRD in 70% (95% CI, 35–93). Patients with de novo AML had a CRc rate of 100% (95% CI, 54–100) with undetectable MRD in 50% (95% CI, 12–88). Those with wildtype TP53 had a CRc rate of 92% (95% CI, 62–100) and undetectable MRD in 67% (95% CI, 35–90). Eight patients (47%) with ts‐AML had prior HMA exposure; these patients had a CRc rate of 63% (95% CI, 24–91). The three non‐responding patients in the study had highly adverse disease features: all three had ts‐AML with previous HMA failure, were TP53 mutated, and had complex cytogenetics.

Time‐to‐event outcomes

The median follow‐up time for surviving patients was 11.8 months (range, 3.9–35.5). In this adverse‐risk cohort, the median OS was 12.8 months (95% CI, 5–NE), with a 1‐year OS of 57% (95% CI, 38–87) and a 2‐year OS of 34% (95% CI, 15–79). Patients with MR gene mutations had a median OS of 17.9 months versus a median OS of 5.1 months in patients without MR gene mutations (P = 0.039). Those who had previously received an HMA had a median OS of 10.5 months, whereas patients who were HMA‐naïve had a median OS of 12.8 months (P = 0.260). Patients with wildtype TP53 had a median OS of 17.9 months; in comparison, patients with mutated TP53 had a median OS of 3.1 months (P = 0.001). The median EFS was 17.9 months (95% CI, 4–NE), with a 1‐year EFS of 51% (95% CI, 32–83) and 2‐year EFS of 39% (95% CI, 18–81). Among responding patients, the 2‐year cumulative incidence of relapse was 19% (95% CI, 5–58).

Twelve (86%) of 14 responding patients proceeded to SCT. The median age of patients who received SCT was 61 years (range, 44–69). Of these patients, 9 (75%) were classified as adverse risk by ELN 2022 stratification: 7 had MR mutations, 4 had complex cytogenetics, and 2 were TP53 mutated. The median RFS and OS landmarked from the date of SCT were 14.7 months (95% CI, 1–32) and 14.7 months (95% CI, 4–25), respectively.

Of the 12 transplanted patients, 6 remain alive and in remission. Three patients deceased within 100 days of SCT (Days 15, 41, and 53): 2 of presumed infection‐related respiratory failure and 1 of sepsis with multiorgan failure. Of the remaining three patients who deceased post‐SCT, two relapsed (6.2 and 25.3 months after SCT) and subsequently deceased, and one deceased while in remission (14.6 months after SCT). Of the two responding patients who did not undergo SCT, one relapsed after two cycles of treatment and subsequently deceased, and one completed five cycles of treatment on‐protocol followed by azacitidine and venetoclax maintenance and remains alive.

Eleven (92%) of the 12 transplanted patients were MRD negative before SCT. Among the six patients who remain alive and in remission post‐SCT, five were MRD negative and one was MRD positive before transplantation. Both patients who relapsed after SCT, as well as the patient who deceased in remission, were MRD negative before transplantation.

Safety

Adverse events that occurred during induction and consolidation cycles are summarized in Table 3. There were two Grade 5 events during induction, both from infection: one patient developed Streptococcus mitis bacteremia with pulmonary infiltrates and declined intubation despite progressive respiratory failure; the second patient developed Staphylococcus epidermidis bacteremia with progressive hypotension refractory to vasopressors. Both patients had persistent disease at the time of death. Overall, the 4‐week mortality was 0% and the 8‐week mortality was 17%. Of the three deaths that occurred within 8 weeks of treatment, two were due to infection as previously outlined, and one was due to disease progression. There were no deaths during consolidation therapy for the remainder of the study period.

Infection‐related events of any grade occurred in 65% of patients (11/17) during induction. The most common grade ≥ 3 infection‐related event was febrile neutropenia, which occurred in 53% of patients, followed by bacteremia (24%), pneumonia (18%), and cellulitis (12%). Bleeding‐related events of any grade occurred in 18% of patients (3/17) and included grade ≥ 3 events of gastrointestinal bleeding (12%) and hemorrhagic stroke (6%). Additional grade ≥ 3 extramedullary toxicities were rare and included diarrhea (6%), edema (6%), and pleural effusion (6%). No grade ≥ 3 cardiac toxicities were observed, including no occurrences of cardiac failure or cardiomyopathy.

In patients who achieved a CR with induction therapy, the median number of days to platelet recovery (≥50,000/μL) was 37 (range, 28–60), and the median number of days to neutrophil recovery (≥1000/μL) was 37 (range, 30–60). DISCUSSION

Introduction of the BCL2 inhibitor, venetoclax, has transformed the effectiveness and toxicity profiles of existing chemotherapy backbones in the treatment of ND AML. The combination of venetoclax with lower intensity therapy has significantly improved remission rates and OS among older or unfit patients, and ongoing studies of venetoclax in combination with intensive chemotherapy have dramatically improved rates of remission and MRD negativity, with retrospective studies suggesting improvements in OS among high‐risk groups compared to historical controls. 23 However, along with higher efficacy, the addition of venetoclax has also brought higher rates of toxicity, mostly in the form of myelosuppression, infection, and some extramedullary toxicity. CPX‐351 is a unique liposomal formulation of cytarabine and daunorubicin containing significantly lower cumulative doses of cytarabine and daunorubicin than traditional “7 + 3”. In the pivotal Phase III study of CPX‐351 versus “7 + 3” in patients with ND secondary AML, CPX‐351 was associated with higher rates of remission and improved OS compared to “7 + 3”, with lower rates of early mortality and similar rates of infection. Based on this unique profile, we investigated the combination of venetoclax with CPX‐351 in patients with ND AML. In a highly adverse‐risk population of patients, including 71% with secondary AML and 47% with ts‐AML with prior HMA exposure, the ORR was 82%, with 86% of responding patients proceeding to allogeneic SCT.

In addition to high rates of secondary and ts‐AML, our patient cohort had highly adverse cytomolecular features, including 59% of patients with MR mutations by NGS, 47% patients with a complex karyotype, and 29% of patients with TP53 mutated AML. Intriguingly, patients with MR mutations had improved OS when compared to patients who did not (P = 0.039), although this analysis is limited by small numbers. This may support the assertion that CPX‐351 is associated with improved outcomes in this patient population; Lancet et al. previously showed that CPX‐351 was associated with a decreased hazard of death compared to “7 + 3” in patients with antecedent untreated MDS (HR 0.45). 17 While there are limited data available regarding the outcomes of ELN adverse‐risk patients treated with “7 + 3” plus venetoclax (7 + 3 + VEN), Wang et al. reported on eight patients with de novo AML who were treated with this regimen. 6 The ORR was 75%, relatively similar to what was observed in our study; however, the proportion of patients who did not respond was 25% (vs. 12% in our cohort), and only 67% of patients who received 7 + 3 + VEN were able to proceed to transplant. Notably, this study only enrolled patients under the age of 60 and excluded secondary AML or ts‐AML.

While the patients in our study were considered “frontline”, 71% had an AHD and 47% had been previously treated with an HMA. Historically, patients previously exposed to HMA have performed very poorly, with an ORR of 32% and an OS of 6 months in a large retrospective review. 24 More recently, Senapati et al. published a large analysis of 497 evaluable patients with ts‐AML; this cohort had an ORR of 37% with a median OS of 4.8 months. 25 Notably, while venetoclax was utilized in a minority of patients in that analysis (27%), its use was associated with an increased odds of response in patients treated with low‐intensity AML therapy (OR 2.5). In our study, those with prior HMA exposure still had a reasonable CRc rate of 63% with a median OS that was not significantly different than those who were HMA‐naïve, suggesting that CPX + VEN may be of benefit to this challenging group of patients.

Additionally, Uy et al. recently published their experience with a lower intensity combination of CPX + VEN in ND AML patients who were unfit to receive intensive chemotherapy. 26 This Phase 1b trial used approximately 30% of the standard dose of CPX‐351 used in our study (daunorubicin 13.2 mg/m2 and cytarabine 30 mg/m2 administered on Days 1 and 3), but with a longer course of venetoclax (400 mg daily from Days 2 to 31). Importantly, this study excluded patients who had been previously treated with HMA, and only 14% of included patients had an AHD. Among 35 patients with a median age of 74 years, they observed a CR/CRi rate of 54.8% with a median OS of 9.1 months. Compared to our study, their patient population had a lower proportion of ELN adverse‐risk (54% vs. 88%) and secondary AML (23% vs. 71%). Two patients (6%) proceeded to SCT; this low proportion is likely due to the unfit baseline patient population. When comparing these data to other lower intensity regimens, such as azacitidine and venetoclax in the VIALE‐A study, lower intensity CPX + VEN was associated with a higher rate of MRD‐negative CR, establishing the regimen as a possible alternate to HMA plus venetoclax in older patients not eligible for higher dose intensive chemotherapy.

Both CPX‐351 and venetoclax results are associated with myelosuppression. To help mitigate this, after initial dose finding, the duration of venetoclax used in this study was 7 days. With this dose and schedule, our cohort had a time‐to‐platelet and time‐to‐neutrophil recovery of 37 and 37 days after Cycle 1, respectively, similar to what was observed with single‐agent CPX‐351. Overall, the myelosuppression of this regimen was manageable with transfusions, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and administration of growth factors as needed. We did note Grade 3 or more febrile neutropenia in 10 (59%), bacteremia in 4 (24%), pneumonia in 3 (18%), and cellulitis in 2 (12%); however, these rates are similar to what has been reported for other intensive chemotherapy‐plus‐venetoclax combinations. 6 , 9 , 12 Importantly, no mortality was seen within the first 4 weeks of treatment, and there were no deaths during subsequent consolidation cycles. Overall, we feel that observed toxicities were within the limits of what would be expected for patients receiving frontline intensive AML therapy, and especially for patients with adverse‐risk disease including secondary and ts‐AML.

In conclusion, CPX + VEN resulted in high remission rates and enabled progression to allogenic SCT for the majority of a highly adverse group of ND AML patients. The results of this study suggest that CPX + VEN is a well‐tolerated and effective regimen in patients with high‐risk disease, particularly in AML with MR cytomolecular features and in patients with previous HMA failure.
