Food Poverty and Early Childhood Development Across Food Insecurity Levels in Brazil
Authors: Micaela Rabelo Quadra, Fernanda Oliveira Meller, Antônio Augusto Schäfer, Leonardo Pozza Santos, André Demboski Dalpont, Gabriela Evaldt Espíndola, Gabriela Buccini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis cross‐sectional study investigated associations between food insecurity, food poverty, and early childhood development (ECD) among Brazilian children under 5 years of age, and explored whether the association between food poverty and ECD varied across food insecurity levels. Data were drawn from 14,062 participants from the Brazilian National Survey on Child Nutrition. ECD was assessed using milestone scores from the Survey of Well‐Being of Young Children—Brazilian Version (SWYC‐BR). Exposures included food insecurity levels (secure, mild, or moderate/severe) and food poverty (lack of minimum dietary diversity). Crude and adjusted linear regression models estimated associations and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with stratified and interaction analyses by food insecurity levels. The mean SWYC‐BR score was 14.14; 46.2% of children experienced moderate food poverty, 16.0% severe food poverty, 38.0% mild food insecurity, and 10.2% moderate or severe food insecurity. Mild food insecurity (adjusted β = −0.41; 95% CI: −0.74, − 0.09) and severe food poverty (adjusted β = −0.91; 95% CI: −1.37, − 0.44) were associated with lower SWYC‐BR scores. In stratified analyses, children from food‐secure (fully adjusted β = −0.87; 95% CI: −1.50, − 0.24) and mildly food‐insecure (fully adjusted β = −1.17; 95% CI: −1.93, − 0.41) households experiencing severe food poverty had lower SWYC‐BR scores. Findings indicate that food poverty is associated with ECD in Brazil and this association varies across food insecurity levels, underscoring the need for public health policies that address both nutritional quality and household food security among vulnerable populations.

Introduction

In Brazil, 13% of children under age 5 were at risk of poor development in 2015 (United Nations Children's Fund 2025). Early childhood development (ECD) is the period during which cognitive, emotional, and social skills are constructed, and social determinants of health such as environment, community, society, family aspects, health condition, as well as inadequate nutrition, are responsible for shaping this outcome (Akram et al. 2024; Black et al. 2017; Likhar et al. 2022; United Nations Children's Fund 2025).

Children's inadequate nutrition can be characterized by both food insecurity and food poverty. The lack of continuous access to a sufficient, safe, and nutritionally adequate diet is defined as food insecurity. It can be classified into mild, moderate, and severe levels, according to the difficulty of access (Food and Agriculture Organization 2008, 2016; Brazil 2006). Conversely, food poverty is characterized by the failure to achieve the minimum dietary diversity (MDD) requirements, i.e., the intake of at least five out of eight food groups in the previous 24 h. Its severity is classified as moderate or severe according to the number of food groups consumed per day (United Nations Children's Fund 2024).

In Brazil, the prevalence of food insecurity reached 24.2% of the population in 2024, rising to 32.1% in households with children less than 5 years of age (Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics 2025). In 2019, 38.5% of Brazilian children were classified as experiencing moderate or severe food poverty levels (Carneiro et al. 2024).

Food insecurity is an important social determinant of health. A systematic review and meta‐analysis of 33 studies reported associations of food insecurity with delays in cognitive and vocabulary skills (Oliveira et al. 2020), and a systematic review of 10 studies found an association with adverse childhood experiences (Royer et al. 2022). Likewise, food poverty can hinder physical growth, immune system, concentration, and brain and cognitive development (United Nations Children's Fund 2024). However, while the influence of food insecurity on ECD is well established, the effects of food poverty on this outcome remain less well understood (Casey and Winsler 2025; Oliveira et al. 2020; Qin et al. 2024; United Nations Children's Fund 2024).

Food insecurity and food poverty are complementary aspects of inadequate nutrition. Food insecurity reflects a lack of food availability (quantity), leading to malnutrition, poorer overall health, and reduced energy reserves. On the other hand, food poverty refers to nutritional deficits in the diet (quality), which may impair ECD due to insufficient intake of essential nutrients (Casey and Winsler 2025; Food and Agriculture Organization 2016; United Nations Children's Fund 2024). Understanding the role of inadequate nutrition in ECD outcomes is essential; therefore, this study had two‐fold aim (1) to investigate associations between food insecurity, food poverty and ECD scores, and (2) to explore whether the association between food poverty and ECD outcome varies across food insecurity levels among Brazilian children.

Methods

Study Design

This cross‐sectional analysis utilized data from the Brazilian National Survey on Child Nutrition (ENANI‐2019), which was a nationally representative household survey conducted between February 2019 and March 2020. The ENANI‐2019 protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Clementino Fraga Filho University Hospital of the Federal University of Rio de Janeiro (Protocol CAAE 89798718.7.0000.5257), and participation was voluntary. Before the interview, an informed consent form was signed by the individual responsible for the child. For this study, we followed the reporting guidelines of Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) for cross‐sectional studies (Supplementary Material S1).

Study Setting

Brazil has a population of 203,080,756 inhabitants, with almost 13 million children under 5 years of age, according to the last census from 2022. The gross domestic product per capita is R$47,802.02 (approximately U$8900.00), and the Human Development Index is 0.760 (ranked 89th in the world) (Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics 2024; United Nations Development Programme 2024). The ENANI‐2019 included a representative sample from the five geographic regions of Brazil (North, Northeast, Central‐West, Southeast, and South).

Participants

The eligible population for ENANI‐2019 was children under 5 years old residing in private households in the Brazilian territory. Children were not eligible if they lived in collective or improvised households, in villages (indigenous children), in households where the Portuguese language was not spoken (foreign children), or with a condition that prevented anthropometric measurement (Vasconcellos et al. 2021).

Sampling

Stratification and clustering were employed in the ENANI‐19 sampling process to ensure a robust and representative data collection. The process was divided into two blocks.

In the first, all Brazilian capitals (27 strata) and 20 municipalities with more than 500,000 inhabitants (20 strata) were included, totaling 47 strata. In these, a two‐stage selection was carried out: (1) the census sector was defined as the primary sampling unit (PSU), and (2) eligible households were defined as the secondary sampling unit (SSU). In the second block, municipalities for each major geographic region (5 strata) were systematically selected, resulting in 76 municipalities. For these, a three‐stage selection was carried out: (1) the municipality was defined as the PSU, (2) the census sector as the SSU, and (3) eligible households as the tertiary sampling unit (TSU). Altogether, the ENANI‐2019 sampling comprised 52 strata and 123 municipalities across Brazil. More details of the sampling process were previously described (Vasconcellos et al. 2021).

A total of 1,500 census sectors were selected—300 in each of Brazil's five regions—resulting in 15,000 households (10 households per census sector). All children under 5 years of age residing in the selected households were eligible for the study. Data were collected from 12,524 households (83.5% of the sample), totaling 14,558 children studied (97% response rate) (Vasconcellos et al. 2021).

For this study, children with a diagnosis of Down syndrome, phenylketonuria, autism, or missing ECD data were excluded, and only children 1 month or older were included in the analytical sample, as the Survey of Well‐Being of Young Children Brazilian Version (SWYC‐BR) does not assess ECD for very young infants. Therefore, the analytical sample for this study includes 14,062 children (Figure 1).

Data Collection

Data collection was conducted in the family's household. The questionnaire included information about the child's birth, food intake, and supplement use, anthropometric data, and blood collection, ECD; the mother's sociodemographic, pregnancy, and prenatal characteristics; the household's benefits and income, food insecurity; and the cooking and environment in the home.

Variables

Outcome: Early Childhood Development (ECD)

ECD was evaluated using the Survey of Well‐Being of Young Children Brazilian Version (SWYC‐BR) developmental milestones, which is a screening tool translated, adapted, and validated for Brazilian children. This questionnaire can be applied for children aged 1–65 months, to evaluate neuro‐psychomotor development (Guimarães et al. 2022; Moreira et al. 2019; Sheldrick and Perrin 2013).

The SWYC‐BR encompasses 54 items to assess motor, language, and cognitive developmental milestones relevant to the 12 age‐specific groups (1–3, 4–5, 6–8, 9–11, 12–14, 15–17, 18–22, 23–28, 29–34, 35–46, 47–58, 59–65 months). Each age group includes 10 items to be evaluated using a 3‐point scale to report how the child performs that skill: 0 points for “not yet,” 1 point for “somewhat,” and 2 points for “very much.” The scores from each question are added together to produce the final score, between 0 (minimum) and 20 points (maximum), in which the higher scores indicate better developmental performance (Guimarães et al. 2022).

For data analysis, we applied the methodology previously used in studies employing the ENANI‐19 dataset, correcting the age of preterm children (< 37 weeks of gestation under 2 years (Freitas‐Costa et al. 2023; Padilha et al. 2025). For that, the gestational age at birth was subtracted from 40 weeks and then the resulting difference was subtracted from the child's chronological age at the time of the interview.

The SWYC‐BR score was treated as a continuous variable; however, for descriptive purposes, it was also classified according to the age cutoff points established for Brazilian children to identify ECD delays (Guimarães et al. 2022). These cutoffs ranged from 11 to 15 points across the 42 age categories; for instance, the cutoff for the youngest children (1–3 months) was 13 points, while the cutoff for the oldest children (63–65 months) was 12 points.

Additionally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using the Developmental Quotient (DQ). The DQ variable was created using an estimate of developmental age derived from item response theory (IRT) and the graded response model (GRM), implemented in Stata version 17.0 (commands: irt and grm) with sampling weights (svy: peso_crianca_calib) to account for the complex survey design. Previous studies with ENANI‐19 already used this methodology to evaluate the DQ, but with the Mplus software V.7 (Freitas‐Costa et al. 2023; Padilha et al. 2025).

In this process, each set of items was analyzed separately by age groups to ensure comparability across different ages. In the GRM approach (svy: irt grm), the item characteristic curves (ICC) can be estimated for each milestone, representing the probability of achieving a given milestone according to chronological age. Each ICC is defined by two parameters: alpha (α), representing the most likely age at which the milestone is achieved, and beta (β), indicating the discriminative power of the milestone (i.e., its ability to differentiate children across developmental levels). In Stata, the latent scores estimated by the GRM correspond to the child's position in the development curve, analogous to ICC‐based scoring in Mplus (Freitas‐Costa et al. 2023; Padilha et al. 2025).

Using these parameters, it was possible to estimate each child's developmental age based on the milestones they had achieved. The DQ was then calculated by dividing development age by chronological age. This variable was analyzed both as a continuous measure and, for descriptive purposes, categorized as follows: 1 = milestones expected attained; < 1 = milestones below expectations, > = 1 milestones above expectations (Freitas‐Costa et al. 2023; Padilha et al. 2025).

Exposures: Food Insecurity and Food Poverty

Food insecurity was assessed using the Brazilian Food Insecurity Scale (EBIA), which was applied to mothers or legal guardians to evaluate the household's food security status. The EBIA instrument comprises 14 items relating to the last 3 months, with “no” (0 points) and “yes” (1 point) answers. The total EBIA score is obtained by summing the answers to classify households into four levels: food security (0 points), mild food insecurity (1–5 points), moderate food insecurity (6–9 points), and severe food insecurity (10–14 points). In this study, food security status was classified in three categories: food security (0 points), mild food insecurity (1–5 points), and moderate and severe food insecurity (6–14 points) (Pérez‐Escamilla et al. 2004). We combine moderate and severe food insecurity because they reflect a serious condition, in which the quality and variety of food intake are compromised, leading to hunger (Food and Agriculture Organization 2016).

Food poverty was assessed using the score of MDD created by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF) (2021). The parents or legal guardians reported all foods and drinks the children from 6 months of age consume 24 h prior to the interview. The answers were categorized in 8 food groups: (1) breast milk; (2) grains, roots and tubers (rice, potato, yam, cassava/manioc, or instant flours made from rice, corn, wheat, or oat); (3) legumes (beans or other types of pulses, such as lentils, peas, and chickpeas); (4) dairy products (powdered or liquid cow's milk, infant formula, porridge or mash food with milk, or yogurt); (5) meat products (beef, chicken, pork, fish, liver, hamburger, ham, bologna, salami, nuggets, sausage, or hot dogs); (6) eggs; (7) vitamin‐A rich fruits and vegetables (carrot, pumpkin or sweet potato, kale, spinach, taioba, broccoli, caruru leaves, purslane, bertalha or mustard greens, mango, papaya, or guava); (8) other fruits and vegetables (fruits and vegetables other than those listed above) (Federal University of Rio de Janeiro 2021; World Health Organization and United Nations Children's Fund 2021). The consumption of each group scores 1 point, while the non‐consumption scores 0 points. Therefore, the food poverty score corresponds to the total number of food groups consumed by the child in the previous 24 h. Intake of 0–2 food groups is classified as severe food poverty, 3–4 food groups as moderate food poverty, and 5 or more food groups as without food poverty (World Health Organization and United Nations Children's Fund 2021).

Covariates

The covariates were selected using a conceptual hierarchical framework, according to previous literature (Bliznashka et al. 2022; Cunha‐Rodrigues et al. 2023; Milner et al. 2018; Oliveira et al. 2020; Weigel and Armijos 2023; Zhao et al. 2021). They were grouped into three levels (distal, intermediate, and proximal), as illustrated in Supporting Information S1: Figure 1. These levels encompass children's, maternal, and household characteristics, which are detailed in Supporting Information S1: Chart 1.

Data Analysis

All analyses were conducted using Stata software, version 17.0, accounting for the complex sampling design of the ENANI‐19 survey by employing the “svy” command with the recommended weighting variable (peso_crianca_calib). The use of Stata‐specific algorithms such as svy, irt, and grm may result in slight differences in the outcomes compared to those reported in previous studies using the ENANI‐19 database, which were conducted in R and Mplus (Freitas‐Costa et al. 2023; Padilha et al. 2025).

Analyses were limited to participants with complete ECD outcome data. All covariates were included in the analyses despite minimal missingness, with about 1% of covariate data missing. This limitation is acknowledged in the discussion.

Descriptive Analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed for all variables. For categorical variables, absolute (n) and relative (%) frequencies are presented along with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). For numerical variables, the mean and standard deviation are presented.

Analyses for SWYC‐BR Scores

Linear Regression

Crude linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the association between food insecurity, food poverty and SWYC‐BR scores. Subsequently, adjusted linear regression analyses were carried out following a hierarchical modeling approach to explore whether the associations between food insecurity, food poverty, and ECD were independent of confounding factors.

Variables were structured into three conceptual levels based on a theoretical framework (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1): (1) Distal – demographic variables, including children's sex, age, and skin color, and mothers' age and skin color; (2) Intermediate—socioeconomic variables, including mothers' education and employment, receipt of government benefits, household income, and children's enrollment in daycare or school; (3) Proximal, divided in three blocks—behavioral and health variables, including children's nutritional status and type of delivery (block 1), food insecurity (block 2), and food poverty (block 3). Level 1 was entered first in the analysis and remained to control for more proximal levels. Level 2 entered second, adjusted for the distal level, and retained control of the subsequent level. Level 3 was included last and was adjusted for covariates from both distal and intermediate levels. Importantly, food insecurity was included as a covariate in the analysis of the association between food poverty and ECD. All covariates with a significance level of 20% were retained in the final model as confounders.

Prior to hierarchical modeling, multicollinearity among the numerical covariates (children's age, mothers' age and education, household income, children's nutritional status, and food insecurity) was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) in linear regression with no concerning multicollinearity detected.

The regression coefficient (β) was used as the effect measure and reported with a 95% CI. Joint significance was assessed using the adjusted Wald test, with the corresponding p‐value.

Stratified Analysis

Therefore, we explored whether the association between food poverty and ECD varied across food insecurity levels. The analysis included crude, minimum adjusted (including only covariates from level 1 of the theoretical framework), and fully adjusted model (adding covariates from levels 2 and 3) of linear regression, in order to allow the comparability across food insecurity levels. Results were presented as regression coefficient (β) with 95% CI and p‐values were calculated using the Wald test.

Interaction Analysis

Finally, to identify whether the significant stratified associations reflect an effect modification, we conducted interaction analysis. The analysis followed the same approach of the stratified analysis, including crude, minimum and fully adjusted models of linear regression. Results were presented as regression coefficient (β) with 95% CI and p‐values were calculated using the Wald test.

Sensitivity Analysis Using DQ Scores

Additionally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using the DQ scores as an alternative measure of ECD. The same analytical approach described above was applied, and the results are presented in the Supplementary Material S1.

Ethics Statement

ENANI–2019 protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board ofthe Clementino Fraga Filho University Hospital of the Federal Universityof Rio de Janeiro (Protocol CAAE 89798718.7.0000.5257).

Results

Descriptive Analysis

The analytical sample consisted of 14,062 children. Among them, 31.7% were classified as having developmental delays, with a mean SWYC‐BR score of 14.14 points (range: 0–20). Around 42.5% of the children exhibited developmental milestones below expectations, with a mean DQ score of 1.02 (range: 0.67–2.00). More than one‐third of the households experienced mild food insecurity (38.0%), while 10.2% had moderate and severe status. Additionally, 46.2% and 16.0% of the children were, respectively, in moderate and severe food poverty (Table 1).

The children's sample was evenly distributed across age groups, 51.0% of them were male, and 52.2% were mixed‐race. Almost half of the mothers were between 20 and 29 years old (48.0%), had more than 12 years of education (51.2%), and 33.0% were outside the labor market. Government benefits were received by 43.0% of households, and 34.6% of them were in the 1st income tertile (Table 1).

Linear Regression Analysis

In the crude analysis, a linear trend was observed in both associations with the SWYC‐BR score. Children living in households with mild (β = −0.74, 95% CI [−1.06;−0.42]) and moderate‐severe (β = −1.21, 95% CI [−1.70;−0.73]) food insecurity had lower SWYC‐BR scores. After adjusted analysis, only children in mild food insecurity had a significant reduction in the SWYC‐BR scores (β = −0.41, 95% CI [−0.74;−0.09]). Regarding food poverty, the crude analysis indicated that children living in moderate (β = −0.53, 95% CI [−0.86;−0.20]) and severe (β = −0.64, 95% CI [−1.10;−0.18]) food poverty had lower SWYC‐BR scores. After adjusting, only children in severe food poverty showed a significant reduction in the SWYC‐BR score by 1.04 points (β = −0.91, 95% CI [−1.37;−0.44]) (Table 2).

Stratified Analysis

Children from food‐secure households with severe food poverty had lower SWYC‐BR scores in the crude (β = −0.65, 95% CI [−1.27;−0.03]), minimum (β = −1.05, 95% CI [−1.69;−0.41]), and fully (β = −0.87, 95% CI [−1.50;−0.24]) adjusted model. Similarly, in children from households with mild food insecurity, both moderate (β = −0.58, 95% CI [−1.14;−0.03]) and severe food poverty (β = −0.81, 95% CI [−1.56;−0.05]) were associated with lower SWYC‐BR scores in the crude analysis, but only the association with severe food poverty remained in the minimum (β = −1.25, 95% CI [−2.03;−0.48]), and fully (β = −1.17, 95% CI [−1.93;−0.41]) adjusted models. No associations were observed among children from moderate and severe food insecurity households.

Interaction Analysis

No statistical significance was found in the interaction term of food poverty and food insecurity on ECD, in crude, minimum or fully adjusted analyses.

Sensitivity Analysis

Analyses of the DQ score using linear regression, stratified models, and interaction tests yielded results that were generally consistent with those observed for the SWYC‐BR score. However, unlike the SWYC‐BR findings, children living in food poverty exhibited a slight increase in DQ scores. For more details, see Supporting Information S1: Tables 1–3.

Discussion

Our study analyzed the association between food insecurity, food poverty, and ECD; while investigating whether the association between food poverty and ECD varies across food insecurity levels. Mild food insecurity and severe food poverty were associated with decreases in SWYC‐BR scores, increasing the likelihood of developmental delay. Moreover, in the stratified analysis, significant associations were observed between food poverty and ECD among children from food‐secure and mildly food‐insecure households. However, interactions were not significant, suggesting differences only across levels. These findings highlight the role of social, economic, and political factors on ECD and the potential of targeted interventions to mitigate the effects of food poverty.

Nearly one‐third of children showed developmental delay on the SWYC‐BR, and over 40% had below‐expected milestones on the DQ. Globally, about 25% of children seem to present developmental delays. However, this frequency ranges across regions: from 10% in Europe and Central Asia to 42% in West and Central Africa (Gil et al. 2020). In middle‐ and low‐income countries, 9.7% of the children 3–4 years old had cognitive delay (Emerson and Llewellyn 2023). These children are at greater long‐term risk for health problems, academic difficulties, and social and emotional challenges throughout life (Palipung et al. 2024). Moreover, developmental delays can lead to chronic conditions and disabilities that persist in adulthood, impacting their overall quality of life (Aldharman et al. 2023).

In our study, half of households experienced food insecurity, and almost two‐thirds of children faced food poverty. Variations in the prevalence of food insecurity and food poverty are also observed across the world. In Ethiopia, 52.1% of children under 5 years old were not in food poverty, with higher adherence of MDD linked to food security (Moga Lencha et al. 2022). In Nigeria, 19.0% of households with children under 5 years of age suffered from food insecurity (Oderinde et al. 2023), while in Brazil, the prevalence was 40% in households with children under 2 years old (Oliveira et al. 2020). Moreover, the Northern and Southern regions of Brazil have a higher risk of food insecurity in households with three or more children under 18 years old, ranging from 2.8 (South) to 3.3 (North) times higher, reflecting regional and socioeconomic disparities (Cherol et al. 2021).

Children experiencing severe food poverty had poorer ECD according to the SWYC‐BR score (about 1 point lower). Similarly, children living in mildly food‐insecure households also had poorer ECD outcomes, although the reduction in SWYC‐BR scores was even more modest (about half a point lower). Moreover, the association between severe food poverty and ECD varied across food insecurity levels: reductions of about 1 point were observed among food‐secure and mildly food‐insecure children, with the largest decrease in the latter. Interestingly, no associations between food poverty and ECD were found in the moderate or severe food insecurity levels.

The lack of association between moderate and severe food insecurity and SWYC‐BR scores, as well as between food poverty and this outcome in the moderate and severe food insecurity levels, may be explained by the small number of observations in these analyses, which likely reduced statistical power and contributed to non‐significant estimates. Additionally, EBIA and MDD differ in their assessment timeframes and in what they measure (food insecurity focus in quantity, whereas food poverty primarily assesses quality) (Casey and Winsler 2025; Food and Agriculture Organization 2016; United Nations Children's Fund 2024), which may also explain the lack of association, as a child may live in a food‐secure household yet experience a day of food poverty. Cultural dietary patterns may contribute, as diet quality and, consequently, food poverty, can be affected even in socioeconomically advantaged households, given that associations between socioeconomic factors and ultra‐processed food consumption vary across countries (Dicken et al. 2024). It is also possible that children in this study were receiving social support and public health assistance through government programs, which could mitigate the negative effects of food insecurity on ECD. Agreeing with this, a study from Iran indicated that households' food security status increased with higher perceived social support (Mokari‐Yamchi et al. 2020). In Northeastern Brazil, social food programs were associated with a decrease in severe food insecurity status in rural and urban areas (Palmeira et al. 2022).

Brazil has a history of public health and nutrition policies and interventions, such as the National Food and Nutrition Program, National Breastfeeding Incentive Program, Program to Combat Specific Nutritional Deficiencies, Food and Nutrition Surveillance System, Strategy on Breastfeeding and Feeding Brazil, School‐based Health Program, and National Supplementation Programs. In addition, Brazil has a cash transfer program, called Bolsa Família, which covers approximately 5.65 million children. These efforts have fostered articulation between ECD and food and nutrition in the country (Brazil 2025; Claro et al. 2022). While these hypotheses help to contextualize null findings, the modest effect observed between mild food insecurity and severe food poverty and ECD (≈0.5‑ and 1‑point decreases, respectively, in SWYC‑BR scores), including within the stratified analysis, should be noted. It is important to consider that SWYC‐BR is a screening instrument subject to individual‐level variability, meaning that not all small differences may reflect real variation (De Vet et al. 2011; Perrin et al. 2016). Likewise, interaction analyses following the stratified findings were not statistically significant, indicating that the observed differences reflect descriptive heterogeneity rather than true effect modification, particularly given the small number of observations. Nonetheless, the stratified findings remain relevant, highlighting subgroups of food‐insecure children who may be more vulnerable to the association between food poverty and ECD. Although modest at the individual level, such effects can still be meaningful at the population level, reflecting structural inequalities and social vulnerability.

Consistent with our result, studies in China and Portugal reported that achieving MDD was associated with a lower risk of development delay (Cunha‐Rodrigues et al. 2023; Qin et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2021). In Ethiopia, a cross‐sectional survey observed that children between 12 and 36 months old who were stunting and underweight presented delays in different domains of ECD (Delbiso et al. 2024). Similar results were also found in 36‐ to 59‐month‐old children from Nepal (Shrestha et al. 2022). In Brazil, a study pointed out that among children 36–59 months of age, the DQ was lower in those with vitamin B12 deficiency, while higher levels of vitamin B6 were related to a better DQ score in 24‐to 59‐month‐olds (Freitas‐Costa et al. 2023).

Over the past decade, Brazil has faced growing economic and political crises, with regional disparities (Claro et al. 2022). In line with this, a study demonstrated that low early childhood friendly municipal index (IMAPI) scores were more common in the North and Northeast, as well as in small‐ and medium‐sized cities, while high scores were more common in the Southern and Southeastern regions and metropolitan areas (Buccini et al. 2022). Understanding this is essential for developing nurturing care environments for ECD appropriately tailored to their time and context (Pedroso et al. 2022). Our findings may reflect these discrepancies in Brazil, underscoring the need to rethink current policies. For instance, a recent study on food insecurity in the Criança Feliz Program reported insufficient protocols to guide and engage families experiencing food insecurity, unmet expectations for stipends or food baskets, and bureaucratic barriers to government support, all of which affect early responsive care. The study recommended staff training, protocol development, provision of food assistance resources, and establishment of partnerships to better support families (Buccini et al. 2025).

We must underscore the findings regarding the DQ. Unlike the SWYC‐BR score, severe food poverty was associated with higher DQ, including in the stratified analysis. This may reflect methodological aspects, since the DQ (weighted by IRT and GRM models) can overestimate scores in some age groups. The DQ also tends to decrease with age according to the study by Freitas‐Costa et al. (2025), who reported lower values among children between 36 and 59 months compared with those 1–35 months old. Another possibility is parental report bias, as limited knowledge of ECD may lead parents to overestimate milestones (Awad et al. 2025; Kumar et al. 2024). These results indicate the need for caution when using the DQ to assess food poverty.

It is important to consider both the limitations and strengths of our study. Its cross‐sectional design prevents causal inference, and the stratified analyses and interaction terms involved few observations, potentially reducing statistical power and contributed to non‐significant estimates. Combining moderate and severe food insecurity into a single category may have prevented differences but was necessary due to small cell sizes. The ENANI‑19 dataset had missing information for some covariates, which may have exacerbated or underestimated exposures and outcomes, although missingness in all covariates was below 1% of the sample. Additionally, since ENANI‐19 excluded children from villages or non‐Portuguese‐speaking households, our analysis may have omitted vulnerable families. Another limitation regarding using an existing national dataset, such as ENANI‐19 is that we could not adjust for confounders such as maternal mental health, household sanitation, and caregiving practices, because these variables were not available in the dataset. Key measures available in the dataset were parent‐reported, which may have inflated DQ scores, and DQ interpretation is limited by IRT/GRM scaling challenges

Despite these limitations, strengths include the use of nationally representative ENANI‐19 data, covering children under 5 years across all regions; complementary ECD measures; validated, reliable instruments, which rely on short recall periods, as well as the novel use of WHO/UNICEF MDD to assess food poverty. Future research should explore causal mechanisms on how socioeconomic factors, including race/skin color, shape these associations to inform interventions to mitigate disparities in infancy (Tables 3 and 4).

Conclusion

Food poverty and food insecurity were associated with ECD in Brazilian children under 5 years of age, with severe food poverty and mild food insecurity, respectively, related to lower SWYC‐BR milestones scores. The association between food poverty and ECD varied across household food security levels, although no significant interactions were observed. The findings reflect the influence of structural determinants, including income, race, employment opportunities, and household conditions, on food access and child development. Our findings underscore the importance of targeted public health policies, nutritional education, and social protection measures, such as improved income transfer programs, increased access to minimally processed foods through food banks, and expanded poverty reduction initiatives implemented through integrated primary health care and nutrition actions to support ECD.
