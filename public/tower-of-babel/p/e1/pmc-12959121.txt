Fluid Resuscitation Choice and its Effect on Plasma Chloride and Acute Kidney Injury in Severe Acute Pancreatitis: A Randomized Study of Normal Saline vs. Balanced Crystalloids (Physiomax)
Authors: Mandip S Bhatia, Ritu Attri, Ashok K Pannu, Atul Saroch, Saurabh C Sharda, Neeraj Singla, Sakshi Mehta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute pancreatitis (AP) is a potentially fatal gastrointestinal condition, often associated with organ failure and acute kidney injury (AKI). Early fluid resuscitation with Normal saline (NS) may lead to hyperchloremia and AKI, which prompted interest in using balanced crystalloids. However, the optimal choice of crystalloid fluid remains uncertain. This study aimed to compare the effects of NS versus Physiomax on plasma chloride levels and AKI incidence during early resuscitation in patients with predicted severe AP (pSAP).MethodsA single-center, prospective, randomized study was conducted between November 2022 and December 2023 at PGIMER, Chandigarh, India. 112 patients with pSAP were enrolled and randomized (1:1) to receive either NS or Physiomax for fluid resuscitation over the first 48 h. Primary outcome was plasma chloride concentration at 48 h. Secondary outcomes included AKI incidence, MAKE-30 events, ICU admissions, and mortality.ResultsBaseline and clinical parameters were comparable between groups. At 48 h, plasma chloride levels were significantly higher in NS group (114.1 ± 4.3 mmol/L) versus Physiomax group (108.1 ± 3.2 mmol/L; p < 0.001). AKI occurred in 41 % of NS group while 21.4 % in Physiomax group (p = 0.03). MAKE-30 events were more common in NS group (17.9 % vs. 5.4 %; p = 0.008). ICU admission rates were also significantly higher with NS infusion (28.6 % vs. 10.7 %; p = 0.03). No significant differences in hospital length of stay, mortality, or other complications were observed in between groups (p > 0.05).ConclusionPhysiomax significantly reduced plasma chloride levels and AKI incidence in patients with pSAP, suggesting a potential clinical benefit of balanced crystalloids in early AP resuscitation.Keywords: Acute pancreatitis, Normal saline, Balanced crystalloids, Physiomax, Acute kidney injury

Introduction

A cute pancreatitis (AP) is a prevalent and potentially lethal gastrointestinal disorder, with a growing incidence and rising hospital admissions worldwide.1 Studies in the literature have reported a wide range of incidence rate of AP, varying between 4.9 and 73.4 cases per 100,000 people annually.2 The Atlanta classification categorized the severity of AP as mild, moderate, or severe.2 Approximately 20 % of patients are affected by moderate to severe AP, resulting in a mortality rate of 20–40 %.3 Despite advances in critical care, severe AP (SAP) leads to organ failure (20 % of patients), acute kidney injury (AKI) (25–59 % of patients), necrosis, and systemic complications.4,5 The multifactorial pathophysiology of AP involves hypovolemia, pancreatic proteolytic enzyme activation, renal microcirculation impairment, and intra-abdominal hypertension.6 To maintain normovolemia and preserve organ perfusion, early and adequate fluid resuscitation is considered the cornerstone of AP management However, the optimal type and volume of fluid remain debatable.7

Normal saline (NS, 0.9 % sodium chloride) is the most widely used isotonic crystalloid for emergency resuscitation globally.9 However, its high chloride content (154 mmol/L), which exceeds normal plasma levels (94–111 mmol/L), can lead to hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis.8,9 This electrolyte imbalance has been associated with systemic inflammation, decreased renal perfusion, and a higher risk of AKI and the need for renal replacement therapy (RRT) in critically ill patients.10–12 Conversely, balanced crystalloid fluids, such as Lactated Ringer's solution, Plasma-Lyte A or Physiomax, are alternative resuscitation fluids with more physiologic electrolyte compositions, resulting in reducing inflammatory markers.13 The American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) guidelines also recommend the use of balanced crystalloids over NS for early fluid resuscitation in AP patients due to their favorable effects on acid-base balance.14 Several clinical trials and meta-analysis have shown that balanced crystalloids lead to lower rates of systemic inflammation, reduced ICU admission and hospital stays, and overall improved outcomes compared to NS.15–17 However, there is limited data on the impact of Physiomax resuscitation in AP patients specifically and this has prompted investigations into whether balanced crystalloids could mitigate AKI risk in AP patients.18

Due to the uncertainty regarding the best crystalloid fluid for resuscitation in AP, this study aims to compare the effects of NS and a balanced crystalloid (Physiomax) on plasma chloride concentration and AKI incidence in patients with predicted SAP (pSAP) during the first 48 h of resuscitation.

Methods

2.1. Study design

This was a single-center, prospective, randomized study conducted in the Medicine Emergency and Emergency Ward, Department of Internal Medicine, Postgraduate Institute of Medical Education and Research (PGIMER), Chandigarh, India. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of PGIMER.

2.2. Study population

The inclusion criteria comprised adults aged 18–70 years diagnosed with AP based on abdominal pain, serum amylase or lipase levels at least three times the upper limit of normal, and/or characteristic findings on computed tomography. Patients with predicted severe acute pancreatitis were included if they had a systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) score of ≥2 and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels >150 mg/L. Recruitment was completed within 72 h of symptom onset. Patients were included only if they were willing and able to comply with study procedures and provided written informed consent.

Patients were excluded if they had chronic kidney disease (eGFR <60 ml/min/1.73 m2 for three months), required emergent RRT at admission, had undergone RRT before admission, or required percutaneous/transmural drainage or emergency surgery due to complications such as abdominal compartment syndrome or bowel ischemia. Additional exclusion criteria included pregnancy or lactation, fulminant multiple organ failure with a predicted death within 24–48 h, and severe cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, hepatic, hematologic, or immunologic disease.

2.3. Study intervention

Patients with AP were randomized into the following two groups:

Physiomax group: Patients who received Physiomax (Otsuka Pharmaceutical) for isotonic intravenous fluid resuscitation.

Physiomax group: Patients who received Physiomax (Otsuka Pharmaceutical) for isotonic intravenous fluid resuscitation.

NS group: Patients who received 0.9 % normal saline for isotonic intravenous fluid resuscitation.

NS group: Patients who received 0.9 % normal saline for isotonic intravenous fluid resuscitation.

2.4. Randomization

Patients with AP who met the inclusion criteria were screened, enrolled and randomized. Randomization was performed using computer-generated sequences to ensure blinding. Patients were randomly allocated to either the NS or Physiomax group in the ratio of 1:1, through sealed opaque envelopes.

2.5. Procedure

Fluid resuscitation in both groups followed the 2013 IAP/APA guidelines.19 Initial fluid administration was set at 5–10 ml/kg/h until resuscitation goals were met including hemodynamic targets such as a heart rate <120/min, mean arterial pressure between 65 and 85 mmHg, and urinary output >0.5 ml/kg/h. Invasive clinical targets such as stroke volume variation and intrathoracic blood volume determination, along with biochemical targets such as hematocrit levels of 35–44 %, were also monitored. Patients who had already received resuscitation before enrollment were managed according to these predefined resuscitation goals. Fluids were administered whenever indicated for up to seven days, or until discharge or death, whichever occurred first.

All patients received standard medical management, including early enteral nutrition (within 48 h), antibiotics and sedatives as needed, and organ support interventions such as mechanical ventilation and vasopressors at the discretion of the treating physicians. Patients who developed AKI (≥1.5 times the baseline creatinine level) and met predefined criteria for RRT were considered for treatment. Suspected infections of pancreatic necrosis were managed using a step-up approach, starting with percutaneous or transmural drainage followed by debridement as required.

2.6. Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the plasma chloride concentration after 48 h. This time point was chosen as early aggressive hydration is recommended in AP, and fluid administration is highest within the first 24–48 h.

Secondary outcomes included major adverse kidney events (MAKE-30), which comprised death, new RRT initiation, or persistent renal dysfunction at hospital discharge or by day 30. Additional clinical outcomes included intensive care unit (ICU) admission rates, ICU-free and hospital-free days, ventilator-free days, vasopressor-free days, and RRT-free days within 30 days of enrollment. Other secondary outcomes included new-onset organ dysfunction (as assessed by the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score), daily SOFA and SIRS scores for the first seven days, daily assessment of abdominal pain using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), intravenous morphine equivalent dosage, and the incidence of complications such as infected pancreatic necrosis, sepsis, intra-abdominal bleeding, and gastrointestinal fistula. Mortality and the need for open surgery during the index admission were also recorded.

2.7. Biochemical endpoints

Biochemical endpoints included creatinine, daily serum chloride and bicarbonate measurements from day 1 to day 7, and plasma neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) concentrations on days 1, 2, 3, and 5.

2.8. Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated for a 95 % two-sided confidence interval and 80 % power, assuming an AKI incidence of 40.4 % in the NS group and 7.1 % in the balanced crystalloid group. Using the Kelsey method, the required sample size was determined to be 112, with 56 patients in each group (1:1 ratio).

2.9. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses was conducted using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median, and analyzed using Mann–Whitney U test, based on normality (assessed using the KS test). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the Fisher's exact test. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 123 patients with SAP were screened between November 2022 and December 2023 in a single-center setting. Of these, 11 patients did not meet the inclusion criteria. Remaining 112 patients were enrolled and randomized into two groups to receive either NS or Physiomax for intravenous fluid resuscitation (n = 56 each). Discussion

In the present study, we compared the clinical impact of NS and Physiomax on plasma chloride and AKI in patients with pSAP. Our findings demonstrated that fluid resuscitation with Physiomax during first 48 h was associated with significantly lower plasma chloride levels, reduced incidence of AKI, lesser ICU admission rates, and fewer MAKE-30 events as compared to NS fluid resuscitation in patients with pSAP. These results suggest that balanced crystalloids resuscitation may offer renal and metabolic advantages in the management of SAP.

Experimental studies have shown that large-volume NS administration has the tendency to induce hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis.20,21 Acidosis then may activate pancreatic zymogens and exacerbate cellular injury, potentially worsening the severity of AP.19 In contrast, Physiomax may better preserve acid-base balance, leading to improved microcirculatory perfusion and cellular function.22 These physiological benefits align with our findings, as plasma chloride levels increased significantly after 48 h in the NS group compared to the Physiomax group.

Substantial evidence in the literature suggests that buffered crystalloids are associated with a lower risk of AKI.23 In our study, AKI incidence was significantly higher in NS group (41 %) compared to Physiomax group (21.4 %; p = 0.03). In a sequential-period pilot study conducted by Yonis et al. (2012), the authors compared chloride-restricted and chloride-liberal resuscitation strategies in patients admitted to ICU and reported a significant reduction in AKI with a chloride-restrictive strategy.24 This study aligns with our data, suggesting buffered crystalloids may influence renal outcomes. However, other large trials such as SPLIT Randomized Clinical Trial comparing Saline vs Plasma-Lyte 148 for ICU therapy, Isotonic Solutions and Major Adverse Renal Events Trial (SMART), Saline Against Lactated Ringer's or Plasma-Lyte in the Emergency Department (SALT-ED) pragmatic trials, and meta-analysis of 9 RCTs23,25–27 did not find significant differences in AKI between saline and balanced crystalloids. We also observed a significantly lower rate of MAKE-30 events in the Physiomax group (5.4 %) compared to the NS group (17.9 %; p = 0.008). This is comparable to MAKE-30 incidence reported in the saline group of the SMART trial (15.4 %)16 however, the difference between balanced-crystalloids and saline groups was not statistically significant. These discrepancies in data may reflect differences in the choice of fluid used for resuscitation, severity of AP, and differential hospital settings.28

Our findings also showed lower ICU admission rates in Physiomax groups compared to NS. However, no significant differences were observed in ventilator-free, vasopressor-free days, ICU length of stay, hospital length of stay, and mortality between the two groups. In a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Ocskay et al. (2023) analyzing eight RCTs, intravenous fluid resuscitation with lactated ringer's solution and NS in adult or pediatric patients with AP was compared. The authors found a significant lower risk of need of ICU and no risk reduction for length of hospitalization and mortality, which is in concordance to our findings. 29 Other meta-analyses by Aziz et al. (2021) and Zhou et al. (2021) similarly reported significant lower ICU admissions but no difference in mortality in lactated ringer's solution group versus NS group.30,31 The BaSICS Randomized Clinical Trial (2021) also found no significant differences in vasopressor-free days, mortality, and length of stay in ICU and hospital in balanced solution vs saline solution in critically ill patients.32 These studies are in concordance with our findings.

Early fluid resuscitation using balanced crystalloids may also mitigate the inflammatory response i.e., reduction in SIRS scores in the early phases of treatment.20,33 In our study, Physiomax group had significantly lower SIRS and SOFA scores on Days 2 and 3. This is in accordance with the results observed by Choosakul et al. (2018), Iqbal et al. (2018), and Wu et al. (2011), where lactated Ringer's solution is associated with reduced SIRS score in early hours of fluid resuscitation.20,34,35 However, these differences were not sustained beyond 48 h, reflecting multifactorial pathophysiology of AP.

4.1. Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. It is among the first randomized controlled trials to evaluate Physiomax, a balanced crystalloid, in the early resuscitation of pSAP, whereas most prior studies have focused on Lactated Ringer's or Plasma-Lyte. The study also contributes important data from a South Asian tertiary care center, helping to address regional evidence gaps in the management of AP. Additionally, this study has focussed on chloride burden and early renal biomarkers such as NGAL over the first 7 days that added valuable mechanistic insights, which were not explored in most prior studies.

However, the study also has limitations. First, the sample size is smaller, which may be insufficient to detect differences in clinical endpoints and patient-centered outcomes like MAKE 30 and mortality. The single-center trial may also limit external validity. Second, we focussed solely on patients with SAP in our study and excluded mild and moderate severity of AP, which may limit generalizability. Third, although both Physiomax and Lactated Ringer's are balanced solutions, compositional differences may impact direct comparability to earlier such studies.36

Conclusion

In conclusion, Physiomax as balanced crystalloid was found to be safe and effective in the early resuscitation and management of patients with SAP. Compared to NS, early resuscitation with Physiomax was associated with significantly lower plasma chloride levels, a reduced incidence of AKI, lower ICU admission rate, and fewer MAKE-30 events. Overall, our results support the preferential use of balanced crystalloids in early fluid resuscitation to reduce renal and metabolic complications. Further large-scale studies are needed to confirm these benefits and to better define optimal fluid strategies in this SAP patient population.
