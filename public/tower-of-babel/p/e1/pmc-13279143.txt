Derivation and Validation of Risk Prediction Models for Cardiovascular and Kidney Outcomes of Non‐ST‐Segment–Elevation Acute Coronary Syndrome in Patients With Chronic Kidney Disease
Authors: Todd A. Wilson, Lee Er, Tolulope T. Sajobi, Stephen B. Wilton, Glen S. Hazlewood, Peter Birks, Adeera Levin, Michelle M. Graham, Bryan Har, Matthew T. James
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with chronic kidney disease are at high risk of adverse outcomes after acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and need optimized treatment decisions. We derived and validated a series of risk models for predicting 1‐year mortality, readmission for myocardial infarction, and progression to kidney failure following ACS.MethodsThe development cohort included adults with chronic kidney disease who had an admission for non‐ST‐segment–elevation ACS, in Alberta, Canada between April 1, 2004 and March 31, 2017. Cox proportional hazard and Fine and Gray competing risk models were externally validated and updated in a temporally distinct cohort of patients in Alberta and a geographically distinct cohort of patients with chronic kidney disease and ACS in British Columbia, Canada.ResultsThe derivation cohort included 11 980 patients, the temporal validation cohort 4204, and the geographic validation cohort 1787. All models showed comparable discrimination and calibration in the temporal validation cohort; comparable model performance was achieved in the geographic validation cohort after updating. In the temporal and geographic validation cohorts, respectively, discrimination was very good for kidney failure (C‐indexes, 0.93 [95% CI, 0.89–0.97] and 0.80 [95% CI, 0.78–0.82]), and modest for mortality (0.77 [95% CI, 0.75–0.78] and 0.71 [95% CI, 0.69–0.73]), and readmission for myocardial infarction (0.65 [95% CI, 0.62–0.67] and 0.57 [95% CI, 0.53–0.62]). All models were well calibrated after updating.ConclusionsWe have developed, validated, and updated risk models for cardiac and renal outcomes in patients with chronic kidney disease and ACS, which can help facilitate shared decision‐making surrounding diagnostic testing, treatment, and monitoring of these patients.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is associated with a high co‐occurrence of coronary artery disease and poor outcomes following acute coronary syndrome (ACS). 1 , 2 Patients with CKD are 2 to 5 times more likely to die following an ACS event than patients without CKD, 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 even when adjusting for comorbidities that commonly accompany CKD that can adversely affect survival. 9 , 10 This greater patient mortality risk in those with CKD may be due to more severe coronary disease at the time of presentation 9 , 11 , 12 and lower use of specific treatment strategies. 5 , 13 , 14 Recognizing this risk profile when managing cardiovascular disease in people with CKD could potentially improve patient outcomes.

Patients with CKD who receive invasive management for ACS have lower risks of mortality and recurrent myocardial infarction (MI) than those who do not, as suggested by observational studies and subgroup analyses from randomized controlled trials. 11 , 14 , 15 , 16 However, people with CKD are between 20% and 50% less likely to receive invasive management of non‐ST‐segment–elevation ACS, as shown in prior research, which highlights a risk‐treatment paradox in the management of ACS in this patient population. 1 Fears of worsening kidney function via contrast‐associated kidney injury (leading to kidney failure), and an underappreciation of the heightened risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in patients with CKD are 2 barriers to use of these treatment strategies. 1 , 2

Accurate risk stratification is key for the management of ACS, including selecting patients for invasive management, suggesting risk prediction models for important cardiovascular and kidney outcomes could help optimize ACS management for patients with CKD. However, existing risk prediction models are limited when applied to patients with CKD, including their focus on short‐term kidney risks of invasive management strategies. For example, currently available risk models predict risk of acute kidney injury following invasive management fail to consider long‐term kidney outcomes such as permanent kidney failure. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Models such as the thrombolysis in MI and GRACE (Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events) scores, which predict mortality and recurrent MI following ACS, do not include key markers of CKD (including estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] and albuminuria, both of which are important predictors of mortality, cardiovascular events, and kidney failure in people with CKD). Further, their derivation cohorts excluded people with advanced CKD, 19 which limits their generalizability to people with CKD.

The limitations of existing ACS risk prediction approaches that are available for patients with CKD highlights the need for the creation of new prognostic models. Thus, we aimed to develop and externally validate a set of risk prediction models for long‐term cardiovascular and kidney outcomes following ACS, including mortality, readmission for MI, the composite outcome of readmission for MI or mortality, and kidney failure for patients with CKD.

METHODS

Data Sharing

Data cannot be made available to other researchers due to contractual arrangements with government agencies who are the data custodians. Information on how researchers may make requests to obtain a representative data set from these custodians may be obtained from https://absporu.ca/research‐services/, and https://www.popdata.bc.ca/data_access#:~:text=In%20order%20to%20access%20provincial,complete%20and%20submit%20your%20DAR.

The study was approved by the Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board at the University of Calgary, Alberta and the Human Ethics Board of Providence Health Care Research Institute, British Columbia, Canada. This was a retrospective cohort study using routinely collected health data; therefore, the Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board at the University of Calgary, Alberta and the Human Ethics Board of Providence Health Care Research Institute, British Columbia, Canada granted a waiver of patient consent for the study. We adhered to the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis statement for reporting multivariable prediction model development and validation. 22

Data Sources for Cohort Formation

We assembled the model development and temporal validation cohorts using a population‐based cohort created from the AKDN (Alberta Kidney Disease Network) repository of provincial laboratory and administrative health data from the province of Alberta, Canada. Using each patient's unique provincial health number we linked this with the APPROACH (Alberta Provincial Project for Outcome Assessment in Coronary Heart Disease) prospective clinical registry. 23 , 24 Included in the AKDN database are patient age, sex, laboratory measures, and dialysis and kidney transplant records for all residents of the province of Alberta. 25 The APPROACH database is a prospective registry which includes clinical details on all patients undergoing coronary angiography, percutaneous coronary intervention, or coronary artery bypass graft in Alberta. 23 Data extracted from the AKDN and APPROACH were linked with Alberta Health administrative data to obtain vital statistics, sociodemographic information, physician claims, and diagnosis and procedure codes from hospital and ambulatory health care encounters. To develop the models, we used hospital admissions with diagnosis codes for non‐ST‐segment–elevation ACS from April 1, 2004, to March 31, 2017. We used hospital admissions with codes for non‐ST‐segment–elevation ACS from April 1, 2016, to March 31, 2020, to generate the temporal validation cohort.

We assembled the geographic validation cohort using the Patient Records and Outcome Management Information System database linked to province wide administrative health data from Population Data BC using patient's unique provincial health numbers. The Patient Records and Outcome Management Information System captures laboratory and clinical data, including records for dialysis and kidney transplant, on patients referred to nephrologists in the province of British Columbia, Canada. 26 , 27 This cohort was linked to the Cardiac Services BC HEARTis (Heart Information System) registry to identify coronary angiography, percutaneous coronary intervention, and coronary artery bypass graft procedures. 28 For geographic validation, we used hospital admissions with codes for non‐ST‐segment–elevation ACS from April 1, 2004 to March 31, 2019.

Study Population

The derivation and validation cohorts included patients 18 years and older with a first admission for non‐ST‐segment–elevation ACS, with a serum creatinine measurement within 1 year before admission, and excluded patients who, as of the date of the ACS admission, had kidney failure as determined by receipt of maintenance dialysis, a kidney transplant, or 2 prior eGFR measurements <10 mL/min/1.73 m2 separated by at least 90 days up to 2 years before the index admission date. We identified patients with CKD as those with at least 2 eGFR measurements <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or urine albumin to creatinine ratio (ACR) measurements >30 mg/g, separated by at least 90 days before hospital admission.

Outcome Variables

We assessed long‐term outcomes up to 1 year following hospital admission, which included all‐cause mortality (ascertained from provincial vital statistics), hospital readmission for myocardial infarction (identified based on validated International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification [ICD‐9 CM] and ICD, Tenth Revision, Canadian Modification [ICD‐10‐CA] coding approaches) (Table S1), and kidney failure. Kidney failure was defined as initiation of maintenance dialysis or kidney transplantation, identified through Alberta Kidney Care program records 29 , 30 (development and temporal validation cohorts) and the BC Patient Records and Outcome Management Information System database 31 , 32 , 33 (geographic validation cohort), or as a sustained eGFR <10 mL/min/1.73 m2, based on at least 2 measurements taken at least 90 days after the index hospital admission.

Candidate Predictor Variables

Candidate predictor variables were selected based on a review of published prognostic models for ACS and CKD. 20 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 To enhance the applicability of our models in routine clinical settings, we prioritized variables that could be reliably identified from standard clinical information available at the time of ACS hospitalization. Fourteen candidate predictors were included: age; sex; ACS type (non‐ST‐segment–elevation MI versus unstable angina); whether an invasive procedure was performed during index hospitalization; baseline kidney measures (eGFR and urine ACR); hemoglobin level; comorbid conditions including congestive heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, liver disease, peripheral vascular disease, and diabetes; and prior coronary interventions (angiography, percutaneous coronary intervention, or coronary artery bypass graft) before the index admission.

Estimated glomerular filtration rate was calculated from serum creatinine using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation without inclusion of race. 38 For each patient, we used the measurement closest to the index hospital admission date within the preceding year. Hemoglobin values were taken from the laboratory test closest to each patient's hospital admission date within the preceding year. Albuminuria was primarily determined from urine ACR tests and supplemented by urine dipstick tests when ACR was not available. Albuminuria was categorized as normal (ACR <30 mg/g or dipstick negative), mild (ACR 30–300 mg/g or dipstick trace/1+), or heavy (ACR >300 mg/g or dipstick ≥2+) based on the median value within 1 year before the index admission. 39 Comorbidities were identified up to 3 years before hospitalization for ACS using hospital discharge abstracts and physician claims, applying validated coding algorithms for diabetes and Charlson comorbidities 40 , 41 (Table S1).

Statistical Analysis

Prediction Model Development

We selected variables for inclusion in each multivariable model if they were significant at P<0.05 in stepwise backward selection or we deemed a priori to be clinically important for a specific outcome. We assessed for linearity between continuous variables (age, eGFR, and hemoglobin) and outcomes using locally weighted scatterplot smoothing. 42 We identified departures from linearity for the eGFR relationship with mortality and the composite outcome. We developed models with eGFR modeled as a 3‐, 4‐, and 5‐knot restricted cubic and chose the model with the lowest Akaike information criterion. A spline restricted to linearity in both tails was chosen because of few observations in the regions of low and high eGFR and we therefore used a 3‐knot restricted cubic spline with knots at 28, 51, and 76 mL/min/1.73 m2 in these models. 43 Albuminuria was missing for 5788 (48.3%) patients in the development cohort. We compared the characteristics of patients with and without albuminuria measurements to assess if albuminuria could reasonably be imputed from measured values of other patient characteristics under the assumption of missing at random and concluded it was reasonable to do so. As recommended by Steyerberg, we performed multiple imputation with 5 imputed data sets, 42 using an ordinal logistic regression model that included diagnosis (unstable angina or non‐ST‐segment–elevation MI), outcomes, age, sex, eGFR, hemoglobin, and comorbidities to impute missing albuminuria. Models were developed separately on each of the 5 imputed data sets and the resulting coefficients were averaged to produce the final models. Using Rubin's rule, we combined the model coefficients by calculating their mean across the 5 imputations and derived coefficient standard errors by summing the within‐imputation between‐imputation variances. 44

We fit Cox proportional hazards models evaluate 1‐year mortality and the composite outcome of mortality and readmission for MI. For readmission for MI and kidney failure, Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard models were used, treating mortality as a competing risk. 45 , 46 The proportional hazards assumption was assessed by regressing Schoenfeld residuals against time to test for nonzero slopes and by visually inspecting plots of Schoenfeld residuals against failure time to assess for evidence of major deviations. 47 For each model we estimated a functional form of the baseline survival function using fractional polynomial regression of the log of the estimated baseline cumulative hazard (baseline cumulative subdistribution hazard from Fine and Gray competing risk models) against the time to event. 48

Prediction Model Validation and Updating

We assessed the discrimination of each model using Harrell's C‐index (C‐index) for the models for the outcomes of mortality and the composite of mortality or readmission for MI. We compared Harrell's C‐index to Uno's C‐index to assess for any impact on model performance due to heavy censoring. 49 For the outcomes of readmission for MI and kidney failure, we used a time‐dependent, cause specific C‐index for the competing risk models. We calculated calibration using the observed to expected ratio, calibration slope, and plots of observed versus predicted risks at 1 year. 43 , 50 , 51 , 52 We used bootstrap resampling to estimate 95% CIs for the Harrell's and cause‐specific C‐indices and for testing the performance of the original models in the development cohort and the updated models in the validation cohorts to account for optimism. 43 , 51 , 53

We compared the C‐index between the validation and development cohorts to evaluate changes in discrimination. 43 We estimated the calibration slope for each model in the validation data sets by regressing the linear predictor (the sum of the beta coefficients from the model, calculated for each patient) from each model against the outcome. The resulting model coefficient was taken as the estimate of the calibration slope. We applied the baseline survival functions, determined in the development data set, to the validation data sets and compared these to an empirically derived baseline survival function in the validation data by running the Cox proportional hazards or Fine and Gray subdistribution hazards models with the linear predictor (LP) as an offset. The 2 survival curves for each model were plotted for comparison to assess fit of the baseline survival functions in the validation data. In addition, we plotted model estimated and observed risks to visually assess each model's ability to separate patients into risk groups based on the 16th, 50th, and 84th percentiles of the linear predictor. 48 Further, we calculated the observed to expected ratio, integrated calibration index, and E50 and E90, which are estimates of the mean, median, and 90th percentile, respectively, of the weighted absolute difference between predicted and observed probabilities based on a smoothed calibration curve derived from using the flexible adaptive hazard regression model or Cox regression with 3‐knot restricted cubic spline, where appropriate. 54 , 55

Patients with CKD referred to nephrologists typically have higher risk of kidney failure and lower risks of mortality than nonreferred patients, thus the need for model updating to account for differences in risk was anticipated. When model calibration in the geographic validation cohort was poor, we updated the models, in a stepwise manner, and reassessed the fit after each step to determine whether further updating was required. 42 , 56 We deemed model fit to be poor if the calibration slope was statistically significantly different from 1, or when substantial deviation was seen in the plots of model versus baseline hazard functions or plots of observed versus predicted risks by risk groups based on percentiles of the linear predictor. When calibration was deemed poor, the first step was to update the baseline hazard function or subdistribution hazard function (Fine and Gray models). Next, a global recalibration of the LP by the calibration slope, followed by reestimating the coefficients of individual predictors in the model, if necessary. The final step in updating, if necessary, was to reestimate all regression coefficients and baseline (subdistribution) hazard function using only the validation data. We did not consider new predictors for the updated models.

We assessed calibration by CKD stage in the temporal and geographic validation cohorts for the models for death and kidney failure, using the following strata: CKD stage G3a (eGFR 45–59 mL/min/1.73 m2), G3b (eGFR 30–44 mL/min/1.73 m2), and G4 or G5 (eGFR <29 mL/min/1.73 m2). 57

The predictor variable invasive management may be susceptible to treatment selection bias and as such, we performed a sensitivity analysis in which we excluded this predictor from the models for mortality and the composite of mortality and readmission for MI and assessed model performance in the development cohort.

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 17, (StataCorp. 2021. Stata Statistical Software: Release 17.0. College Station, TX: StataCorp LP.), SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R (R Core Team, 2020). 58 , 59 , 60

RESULTS

Cohort Characteristics

The development cohort included 11 980 patients and the temporal validation cohort included 4204 patients with CKD and an ACS hospitalization (Figure 1). The average (SD) age of patients was 77 (11) years in both cohorts, 35% and 39% were female, the mean±SD baseline eGFR was 52 (19) and 48 (18) mL/min/1.73m2, and 39% and 47% received invasive management during the index hospitalization in the development and temporal validation cohorts, respectively. The geographic validation cohort included 1787 patients with CKD and an ACS hospitalization (Figure 1B). The average (SD) age of patients was 76 (10) years, 39% were female, the mean±SD baseline eGFR was 29 (13) mL/min/1.73 m2, and 631 (35%) received invasive management during the index hospitalization. Risk Prediction Models

Multivariable predictors of mortality, readmission for MI, and the composite outcome included age, sex, eGFR, hemoglobin, albuminuria, ACS presentation (non‐ST‐segment–elevation MI versus unstable angina), use of invasive management, and comorbidities (heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, peripheral vascular disease, prior invasive management and liver disease [mortality and composite outcome only]) (Table 2). The models for mortality and the composite outcome used eGFR as a restricted cubic spline. The models with 3 knots produced the lowest Akaike information criterion and as such, eGFR was modeled with knots at 28, 51, and 76 mL/min/1.73 m2. Multivariable predictors of kidney failure included age, sex, eGFR, hemoglobin, albuminuria, use of invasive management, and history of prior invasive management. Model coefficients were consistent between models using imputed albuminuria and using complete case analysis. (Table S3).

Model Performance and Updating

Discrimination and calibration statistics on internal validation for all models in the development cohort are shown in Table 3. In the development cohort, discrimination ranged from modest for the model for readmission for MI (C‐index, 0.60 [95% CI, 0.58–0.63]) to excellent for the Fine and Gray model for kidney failure (C index, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.92–0.98]). All models were well calibrated in the development cohort, and there was little difference between apparent and optimism corrected c‐statistics, demonstrating the models were not overly optimistic. Comparisons of observed versus predicted risks of each of the outcomes in the development cohort, across risk groups based on percentiles of the LP, demonstrated good calibration of each of the models and illustrated graded increases in risk of each outcome with greater predicted risks with each model except for the model for kidney failure which only showed separation between the highest risk group and the 3 lower risk groups (Figure S1).

DISCUSSION

We developed and validated risk prediction models for all‐cause mortality, readmission for MI, the composite of mortality or readmission for MI, and progression to kidney failure in a population‐based cohort of adults with CKD hospitalized for non‐ST‐segment–elevation MI. We further validated and updated these models in a geographically distinct cohort of patients with CKD and non‐ST‐segment–elevation ACS who had been referred to nephrology. Using a competing risk framework, this set of models allow simultaneous prediction of important cardiovascular and kidney outcomes of ACS for patients with CKD based on readily available clinical variables. This prognostic information could assist with ACS risk stratification to help guide individualized treatment decisions and support shared decision‐making between patients with CKD and their health care providers.

We found model performance varied for the outcomes, ranging from excellent discrimination for predicting those at high risk of kidney failure to more modest discrimination for predicting death and readmission for MI. Combining laboratory measures including eGFR and albuminuria with age and sex are known to provide excellent prediction of kidney failure in the community setting. 61 Predicting readmission for MI has been shown to be challenging in other studies, with the addition of biomarkers such as C‐reactive protein, cardiac troponins, NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide), or imaging findings shown to improve prediction. 62 , 63 , 64 However these biomarkers and imaging findings are not routinely performed in all patients. Currently, the clinical utility of our model for readmission for MI is limited due to its modest discrimination. This model may serve as a base model for updating with additional predictors using imaging, biomarkers, or ECG variables to improve its performance. Upon validation in a temporally distinct cohort of patients, we found little to no drop in the ability of the models to discriminate between those who experienced an adverse outcome and those who did not, suggesting these models do not show signs of optimism and are suitable for further evaluation in external validation studies. Furthermore, we found all models were well calibrated and did not deteriorate upon internal validation in bootstrapped samples of the development cohort, suggesting that absolute risks predicted by the models were accurate with low likelihood of model overfitting. When applied to a geographically distinct cohort of patients with CKD and ACS who had been referred to a nephrologist, we found that model performance was poorer. This was expected, as patients with CKD who are referred to nephrologists typically have different causes of CKD, more proteinuria, higher risk of kidney failure, and lower competing risk of death than unreferred patients. 65 , 66 We observed similar differences between the population‐based and referred cohorts included in our study, reinforcing the importance of model updating, similar to the need for stratification of analyses in population‐based and high‐risk cohorts defined by CKD risk factors in prognostic research in CKD. 67 , 68

Our models found that invasive management was associated with lower risk of mortality and the composite of mortality or readmission for MI but higher risk of kidney failure. These opposite directions of effect were as expected based on prior evidence for the effects of invasive management on cardiovascular and kidney outcomes. 15 , 69 , 70 Although information on treatment was useful for predicting outcomes within our multivariable models, it is important to note these estimates are vulnerable to treatment selection bias within an observational study, and should not be used to estimate treatment efficacy for patients with CKD. Data from randomized trials should be used to quantify the actual benefits versus harms of invasive versus conservative management of ACS. The models we have created can be used to personalize the absolute risks of cardiovascular and kidney outcomes for people with CKD and ACS, upon which the relative effects of invasive or conservative treatment approaches could be applied.

Strengths of this study include the development and external validation of the models in large, population‐based cohorts and the use of predictor variables that are readily available in clinical practice at the time of hospitalization with ACS. In addition, in our competing risk analysis there were a large number of competing events (mortality) relative to other events of interest (readmission for MI or kidney failure). Therefore, we used Fine and Gray modeling methods to model the incidence of outcomes within a competing risk framework to avoid overestimating the risk of nonfatal outcomes. Further, we externally validated and updated these models in a cohort of patients referred to nephrologists who had a distinct risk profile from the population‐based cohort in which the models were developed, increasing the applicability of the updated models to referred patients with CKD.

There are limitations to this study. The candidate predictor variables used in the models were obtained from routinely collected clinical data from a cardiac registry and administrative health system databases, which do not include all potential predictors of the outcomes of interest following ACS. For this reason, we were unable to include variables including specific physical examination findings, ECG changes, or cardiac imaging findings that have been associated with outcomes. 71 However, these risk prediction models based on commonly available variables can serve as a base model upon which to evaluate additional risk markers for patients with CKD in future studies. Furthermore, there were many patients without measurements for albuminuria, which required imputation for just under half of the cohort. However, we addressed this using multiple imputation after assessing that the mechanism of missingness could reasonably be assumed to be missing at random. Prior research has shown that multiple imputation remains reasonable even with a large percentage of missingness for a predictor variable when the imputation model is correctly specified and relevant auxiliary variables are included. 72 , 73 We found that complete case analyses produced consistent models and model performance, suggesting our results were robust to the use of imputation.

Our models compare favorably with other prognostic models for patients with CKD and address gaps in the risk prediction tools available to inform decisions for patients with CKD in the setting of non‐ST‐segment–elevation MI. Several prior models have been developed for risk prediction for kidney failure, 20 however, none have been developed and validated for use in the setting of ACS where invasive treatment approaches can increase the risk of kidney injury. Few models have been developed to predict fatal or nonfatal cardiovascular events in patients with CKD, and none have been developed specially for use in a high‐risk cardiovascular population such as those with ACS, where they could be helpful to inform clinical decision making for management. Limitations of prior models have included modest discrimination, lack of reporting of calibration, and infrequent inclusion of eGFR as a predictor despite its known relationship with cardiovascular outcomes in CKD. Our model for the composite of mortality and readmission for MI had better discrimination than has been reported in other studies of the thrombolysis in MI risk score for longer term outcomes, and the discrimination of our model for mortality was only slightly lower than the GRACE risk score for in‐hospital mortality. 19 , 34 , 35 However, our models were developed specifically for patients with CKD and ACS, a population of patients in whom risk prediction has not been specifically studied despite the known high risks of adverse cardiovascular and kidney outcomes.

This study has several implications for patients with CKD, their care providers, and decision makers. Providing individualized information on the competing risks of these cardiovascular and kidney outcomes for patients with CKD in the setting of non‐ST‐segment–elevation ACS may help inform treatment decisions or help better inform patients with CKD about potential adverse outcomes. Risk prediction could assist care providers in identifying high‐risk patients and tailoring their initial management and subsequent follow‐up based on their cardiac and kidney risks. Such information would also assist with patient counseling and treatment decision‐making. Individualized predictions from these models could be used to help support decision‐making related to the selection of invasive treatment approaches for patients with CKD, which may be more favorably considered for individuals whose risks of mortality or MI outweigh their risks of kidney failure. Furthermore, they could be useful for identifying patients requiring closer follow‐up for renal monitoring or nephrology care due to their high risk of progressing to kidney failure following an ACS. These models can be used as a base for further work to individualize risk estimates for informing treatment decisions as they allow for estimation of absolute risks of outcomes under different treatment approaches. Finally, as new predictors and biomarkers become more readily available, these models could serve as a base clinical model for model updating to include this new information and potentially improve cardiovascular and kidney failure risk prediction for patients with CKD.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, we have developed, externally validated, and updated a series of risk models for important cardiovascular and kidney outcomes for use following hospitalization for ACS in patients with CKD. These models use readily available clinical data that facilitates their immediate clinical use within routine care. Future work is required to synthesize individual patient estimates of risk with effects of clinical interventions and to evaluate the impact of their use when implemented in clinical practice for ACS care of patients with CKD.
