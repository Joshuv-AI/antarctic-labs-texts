De novo twig-like middle cerebral artery after suspected M1 dissection in early childhood with coexisting KRAS-mutated brain arteriovenous malformation: illustrative case

Twig-like middle cerebral artery (T-MCA), also referred to as aplastic/twig-like middle cerebral artery (MCA) or rete MCA, is an uncommon vascular configuration in which the proximal M1 segment is absent and replaced by a plexiform network of fine arterial channels that reconstitute a relatively normal distal MCA tree.1 This pattern has traditionally been regarded as a congenital anomaly, reflecting the persistence of the primitive MCA plexus, which normally fuses into a single M1 trunk during embryonic development.2 In this classic view, the twig-like network represents an unfused remnant of that plexus rather than an acquired lesion.

More recently, however, some case reports and small series have challenged the notion that all T-MCA lesions are purely congenital. Longitudinal imaging studies in adults have documented de novo development of a twig-like configuration from an initially normal or mildly stenotic M1 segment, often in the context of isolated MCA disease or chronic atherosclerotic change.3–5 In these patients, progressive focal steno-occlusion of M1 is followed by the formation of a plexiform collateral network, leading some authors to reframe T-MCA as an acquired secondary anomaly rather than a uniform embryological persistence.6 These observations suggest that this angiographic pattern may arise through different mechanisms, with both congenital and acquired pathways contributing in individual patients.1

Despite this emerging concept, almost all reported de novo or acquired T-MCA cases involve adults, and the precipitating vascular insult is often inferred rather than directly documented. Pediatric cases are rare, and to our knowledge, there are no prior reports of a T-MCA evolving in early childhood after a documented intracranial vascular insult. We describe a young child in whom the left M1 segment was angiographically normal in the neonatal period; the segment subsequently developed a dissection, which in the following years progressed to complete occlusion with formation of a T-MCA configuration. The lesion arose contralateral to a molecularly confirmed, KRAS-mutated right frontal arteriovenous malformation (AVM). This case provides direct evidence that a T-MCA pattern can represent post-dissection remodeling in early childhood and illustrates how, in the absence of longitudinal imaging, such a lesion could easily be misinterpreted as a congenital T-MCA.

Illustrative Case

A male infant was delivered by cesarean section at 36 weeks’ gestation after acute placental abruption and suspected prenatal asphyxia (Apgar scores 1, 6, and 8 at 1, 5, and 10 minutes, respectively). The neonate required resuscitation, neuroprotective hypothermia, and catecholamine-dependent circulatory support. Cranial ultrasound suggested a right intracranial arteriovenous shunt. The child had no dysmorphic features, and the remainder of the neonatal examination was unremarkable.

At 6 days of life, transfemoral digital subtraction angiography (DSA) confirmed a high-flow Spetzler-Martin grade II right frontal AVM, supplied by the right MCA (dilated superior trunk) and right anterior cerebral artery (ACA) branches, draining into a dilated frontal cortical vein. The infant had catecholamine-dependent circulatory instability consistent with high-output shunt physiology (approximately 40% of cardiac output); echocardiography showed a hyperdynamic circulation with pulmonary arterial hypertension. The lesion was treated with coil-assisted embolization (Glubran 2/Lipiodol), resulting in significant angiographic shunt occlusion and rapid hemodynamic improvement. Contralateral left internal carotid artery (ICA) injection showed normal anatomy, including a well-formed M1 segment and distal MCA branches, without stenosis, dissection, or collateral networks (Fig. 1). The patient’s development during infancy was age-appropriate, with no focal neurological deficits, seizures, or developmental delay. At approximately 1 year of age, the AVM was reassessed by DSA. On the right, there was a recurrent arteriovenous shunt arising from the precentral frontal region, dorsal to the previously embolized feeders (Fig. 2A), which resulted in a second embolization with coils and Glubran 2/Lipiodol being indicated. On the left, the MCA—normal on the neonatal study—showed subtle luminal irregularity and proximal M1 caliber reduction, most consistent with an early intracranial arterial dissection (Fig. 2B). Retrospectively, this was attributed to left ICA/A1 catheterization during the initial procedure, as the right A1 origin could not be directly accessed and the right ACA feeder had to be approached via the anterior communicating artery. There was no evidence of pseudoaneurysm or contrast stagnation. Brain MRI and MR angiography performed in the same period showed no ischemic lesions (Fig. 2C and D) on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), indicating that the dissection had not yet resulted in cerebral infarction. The child remained clinically well and continued to meet developmental milestones. No further treatment was undertaken during this interval. At approximately 2.5 years, DSA demonstrated a recurrent right frontal arteriovenous shunt. After multidisciplinary discussion, the patient underwent a right frontal craniotomy and microsurgical AVM resection as part of a staged hybrid strategy.7,8 The nidus and the markedly dilated cortical draining vein were removed en bloc without intraoperative complications, and tissue was submitted for histopathological and molecular analysis. The early postoperative course was uneventful, and postoperative DSA confirmed complete obliteration of the arteriovenous shunt, with no residual early venous filling (Fig. 2E). Interestingly, on the left, the plexiform network replacing the proximal MCA showed clear progression of the previously noted M1 abnormality: the proximal M1 segment was severely narrowed to nearly occlusive, and a cluster of fine, tortuous collateral vessels was beginning to appear along the expected course of M1, connecting the carotid terminus and ACA branches to preserved distal MCA trunks (Fig. 2F). There were no signs of ICA or ACA stenoses. The angiographic appearance at this stage was indistinguishable from that of a classic T-MCA. Contemporaneous MRI again showed no DWI or FLAIR evidence of infarction, and MR angiography confirmed preserved distal MCA flow via the emerging collateral network.

During the microsurgical resection of the right frontal AVM, part of the nidus was submitted for histopathological and molecular analysis as part of an institutional workup for complex pediatric vascular malformations and to explore potential eligibility for targeted therapies. Targeted ultra-deep next-generation sequencing was performed using libraries prepared with unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) to reduce amplification artifacts.9 In the DNA of a native sample from the resected tissue, a somatic, likely pathogenic KRAS c.197_223dup (p.Ala66_Thr74dup) in-frame duplication could be identified at low variant allele frequency (1.2%, 30/2565 UMI-based consensus reads), consistent with a focal mosaic RASopathy confined to the malformation.10 In view of this result, the case was discussed in an interdisciplinary board meeting and in consultation with clinical geneticists to evaluate the indication for off-label treatment with a MEK inhibitor in the event of further progression. Given that the patient was clinically well, with no neurological deficits and no angiographic evidence of a residual arteriovenous shunt after surgery, and considering the very limited experience with MEK inhibition in young children with intracranial AVMs,11 a conservative watchful-waiting strategy was recommended. No germline testing was pursued, and no additional vascular malformations have been identified on systemic imaging to date.

Subsequent surveillance imaging showed a minimal but definite recurrent arteriovenous shunt in the right frontal region, for which further management was planned (Fig. 3A). The left-sided lesion remained stable. The last available DSA study, performed at approximately 4.5 years of age, showed a fully established twig-like configuration of the left MCA: the M1 segment was absent, and a compact plexiform network reconstituted normal-caliber distal MCA branches (Fig. 3B and C). Throughout the entire follow-up, no signs of cerebral ischemia were observed in the affected left-sided territory. The child’s modified Rankin Scale score was 0 at this final follow-up, with normal neurological examination and age-appropriate development. Discussion

Observations

T-MCA is classically defined as replacement of the M1 segment by a plexiform network of small vessels, with relatively preserved caliber and anterograde flow in the distal MCA branches.12–14 It is rare, with reported angiographic prevalence around 0.1%–1.2% and a clear predilection for adults.1 Most symptom-driven series emphasize hemorrhagic and ischemic presentations and frequent association with aneurysms.2,13–15 The present case adds a different perspective: a de novo T-MCA configuration developing in early childhood after a documented M1 dissection, in the setting of a KRAS-mutated brain AVM.

Congenital Versus Acquired Pathogenesis

From a developmental standpoint, many authors initially postulated that T-MCA was primarily a congenital anomaly of the proximal MCA. During normal embryogenesis, the MCA arises from a plexiform arterial network sprouting from the ACA, which subsequently fuses and regresses into a single M1 trunk. A failure of this fusion would leave a persistent network of small vessels in place of a normal M1, resulting in the T-MCA configuration.1,16,17 This interpretation was supported by case reports and series in which the lesion was strictly unilateral, confined to the M1 segment, and accompanied by a well-developed distal MCA tree without hemispheric maldevelopment, features that were initially interpreted as compatible with a focal developmental arrest rather than with diffuse occlusive vasculopathy.12–14 In the 15-patient cohort of Seo et al., 80% of patients had additional cerebrovascular anomalies (including aneurysms and other arterial variants), suggesting a broader congenital arterial dysplasia rather than a purely acquired process.14 Moreover, the identification of moyamoya-associated RNF213 variants in a subset of patients with rete/T-MCA anomalies, together with the recognized spectrum of RNF213-related large-artery vasculopathies, further supported the idea that in at least some cases, T-MCA may reflect an underlying congenital predisposition to abnormal arterial development and remodeling.18,19

More recently, however, several groups have challenged a purely congenital model, arguing that isolated involvement of the proximal M1 segment, despite a well-formed distal MCA and otherwise normal brain parenchyma, is difficult to reconcile with a global embryological failure of MCA formation. Ota and Komiyama instead suggested that twig-like networks may develop as collateral channels in response to focal M1 steno-occlusion.6 The most compelling evidence for an acquired component comes from de novo T-MCA reports and from systematic natural history data. Kajita et al. described an adult whose MCA was angiographically normal and then showed progressive M1 narrowing and ultimately a twig-like plexiform network over a 12-year interval.3 Nakajima et al. reported the case of a 40-year-old man with moderate M1 stenosis on screening MRA who, 1 year later, had complete M1 occlusion with a typical twig-like network and preserved distal MCA branches; they interpreted this as de novo T-MCA and proposed redefining T-MCA as a variety of “isolated MCA disease with new vessel formation.”4 Park et al., in the largest series to date (51 cases), identified 3 patients in whom a previously normal MCA evolved into a T-MCA configuration, and concluded that many T-MCAs likely represent an acquired secondary anomaly after focal steno-occlusion, with contributions from both moyamoya angiopathy and chronic atherosclerosis in some patients.5 These data support the view that T-MCA might be understood as a radiographic phenotype that can arise through at least two distinct pathophysiological pathways: 1) embryological persistence of the primitive MCA plexus and 2) postnatal focal M1 steno-occlusive disease with secondary collateral network formation. Notably, evidence supporting the congenital pathway remains largely inferential. In the present case, the imaging and clinical features are most consistent with an acquired process, extending the spectrum of this mechanism into early childhood.

Sequential Evolution From M1 Dissection to a Twig-Like Network

Our patient had a normal left MCA on DSA shortly after birth, at a time when the right frontal AVM was already fully formed and hemodynamically relevant. Follow-up DSA 1 year later showed subtle dissecting changes in the left M1 segment, progressing over months to high-grade stenosis and then complete occlusion. After occlusion, a plexiform collateral network matured into a classic twig-like configuration with normal-caliber distal MCA branches, potentially indistinguishable from congenital T-MCA on cross-sectional DSA. The contralateral ICA, ACA, and distal left MCA branches remained normal, and there was no progression toward bilateral disease or basal collateral formation suggestive of moyamoya angiopathy.13 This stepwise evolution—normal M1, angiographic dissection, progressive narrowing, occlusion, and plexiform collateralization—mirrors de novo cases described by Kajita et al. and Nakajima et al., but in a much younger patient.3,4 Furthermore, it strongly argues against a purely congenital persistence of a primitive plexus and instead supports an acquired remodeling process triggered by a focal arterial injury (dissection). The absence of ischemic lesions on serial MRI suggests that collateralization occurred in parallel with luminal compromise, maintaining tissue perfusion and allowing the twig network to mature without overt infarction. This is in line with reports that many T-MCA patients, including those with de novo formation, remain asymptomatic or have only transient ischemic events despite critical proximal occlusion.3,4,13

T-MCA is exceptionally uncommon in pediatric patients, with most reported cases described in adults. This age distribution is often cited in support of interpreting T-MCA as a congenital vascular anomaly rather than an acquired condition.1,20–22 Moreover, to our knowledge, no previously published pediatric case has provided serial angiographic documentation of de novo T-MCA formation from a normal-appearing M1 segment. Our case therefore provides unusually direct evidence that a twig-like configuration can be generated entirely postnatally—within the first years of life—in response to a focal arterial injury. The imaging pattern also aligns with reports of isolated unilateral M1 arteriopathy in young patients, in which focal nonatherosclerotic stenosis or occlusion is accompanied by secondary collateral neovascularization, without the bilateral, basal, and progressive features of moyamoya disease.23 Viewed through this lens, T-MCA is less a fixed embryological pattern and more a morphological endpoint of specific localized MCA lesions, including arterial dissection.

Relationship to Moyamoya Spectrum and Other Occlusive Vasculopathies

Distinguishing T-MCA from unilateral moyamoya angiopathy is clinically relevant because management strategies and risk profiles differ. T-MCA is typically defined by a unilateral plexiform network centered on the M1 segment, with a normal ICA bifurcation, preserved distal MCA with anterograde flow, and absence of classic basal collateral vessels. In contrast, moyamoya disease primarily affects the ICA terminus, is often bilateral and progressive, and features prominent basal “puff-of-smoke” collateral vessels.13,24 In our case, the abnormality remained strictly confined to the left M1 segment with a normal ICA terminus/A1, and no contralateral or posterior circulation involvement over years of follow-up—features that favor isolated MCA arteriopathy with twig-like remodeling rather than early/unilateral moyamoya.1,4,19

Possible Contribution of Somatic KRAS Mosaicism and Known Genetics in T-MCA

Although the T-MCA segment itself was not sampled, the coexistence of a brain AVM harboring a somatic KRAS in-frame duplication raises a biologically plausible, hypothesis-generating possibility: RAS-MAPK activation could have modulated endothelial responses to focal arterial injury and thereby influenced both lesions. Somatic activating KRAS variants are well supported as drivers in sporadic brain AVMs, with human and experimental data implicating constitutive MAPK/ERK signaling and pro-angiogenic endothelial phenotypes (enhanced proliferation, migration, and sprouting).10,25,26 Consistent with this, a broader spectrum of “mosaic RASopathies” caused by postzygotic variants in RAS/MAPK signaling pathway genes (including KRAS) is increasingly recognized, in which even low-level mosaicism can produce regionally restricted but clinically significant vascular malformations and abnormal remodeling responses.27–29

In contrast, the genetic landscape reported for T-MCA remains limited. Most available studies have focused on RNF213—a major moyamoya susceptibility gene—with occasional overlap in variant status between T-MCA and moyamoya spectrum arteriopathies, while systematic evaluation of non-moyamoya pathways has been sparse.5,30,31 Nevertheless, the identification of KRAS c.197_223dup p.(Ala66_Thr74dup) at a low allelic fraction in AVM tissue is notable. This variant is cataloged in ClinVar in association with venous malformation based on a single clinical-laboratory submission; however, the ClinVar record indicates that it has not been previously reported in the peer-reviewed literature and that its functional effect has not been experimentally tested. Importantly, the duplication lies within the KRAS switch II region, and in-frame insertions/duplications affecting switch regions in RAS genes have been linked to vascular malformation phenotypes and are generally thought to act through enhanced RAS-MAPK signaling, supporting the biological plausibility of an activating effect in this domain even if the precise consequence of this specific duplication remains uncertain.27–29 If a low-level mosaic KRAS clone were present more broadly in cerebrovascular endothelium, it could, at least conceptually, bias the response to focal arterial injury toward exaggerated angiogenic sprouting and collateral network formation, rather than a quiescent occlusion phenotype.25 Evidence linking RASopathies to intracranial steno-occlusive arteriopathy is limited, but suggests a rare association. Moyamoya, a proliferative vasculopathy, is an uncommon complication of neurofibromatosis type 1 and has been reported in small numbers of patients with Noonan syndrome and Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair, and more rarely with cardio-facio-cutaneous and Costello syndromes.32 While speculative, this framing raises the possibility that, in selected patients, a T-MCA phenotype may not reflect an isolated, purely mechanical consequence of focal M1 disease alone, but instead may emerge on a background of genetically “primed” vasculature with an exaggerated angiogenic or reparative response to injury. In this context, the twig-like network could be viewed as a phenotypic endpoint shaped by both the inciting lesion (e.g., dissection) and underlying somatic vascular genetics, rather than as remodeling within an otherwise normal arterial tree.

Limitations

This report is constrained by its single-patient design and by incomplete molecular sampling: we did not perform targeted sequencing of the affected MCA or other arterial segment, and the low variant allele fraction in AVM tissue cannot be extrapolated robustly to other vascular territories. In addition, deep endothelial sequencing beyond RNF213-focused analyses has not been routinely applied in existing T-MCA cohorts, limiting genotype-phenotype comparisons. Second, no preembolization contralateral left ICA injection or vessel wall MRI was performed; thus, the suspected dissection is inferred from serial luminal angiographic evolution.

Lessons

A T-MCA configuration can be an acquired remodeling endpoint. Progressive focal M1 injury, including dissection, may evolve into a plexiform network indistinguishable from presumed congenital T-MCA on a single late angiogram. Accurate etiological classification therefore requires—even in children—prior vascular imaging or early adjunctive assessment.

The present case also illustrates that, while RNF213 has been the primary gene evaluated in T-MCA cohorts and may overlap with moyamoya susceptibility, other molecular pathways may be relevant and remain underexplored. The coexistence of a KRAS-mutated AVM raises the possibility that RAS-MAPK mosaicism modulates vascular remodeling after arterial injury, with a potential propensity toward collateral network formation; however, future studies integrating high-depth endothelial sequencing across lesions are needed to clarify whether somatic vascular genetics contributes to susceptibility for twig-like remodeling.
