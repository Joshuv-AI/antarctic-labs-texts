Tomato SlERF26 enhances drought tolerance by regulating SlPIP2;4-mediated ROS homeostasis
Authors: Yaling Wang, Xiaomeng Hu, Jiayan Yang, Yongjie Mo, Huifang Hou, Yin Ding, Wenrui Gong, Pan Zeng, Chunyu Shang, Xingguo Zhang, Yu Pan, Lang Wu, Hongyuan Song, Nan Hu, Dongyang Dai, Md Mahmudul Hasan, Jinhua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdverse environmental conditions severely limit plant growth and productivity, especially under an increasingly frequent and severe drought. Cultivated tomato is highly sensitive to drought, while wild tomato species exhibit remarkable drought tolerance. In this study, we identified SlERF26 as a drought-responsive gene that was differentially expressed between drought-tolerant Solanum pennellii introgression lines and the cultivated tomato background through combined introgression-line and transcriptomic analyses. SlERF26 encodes an AP2/ERF (APETALA2/Ethylene Response Factor) transcription factor. Under drought stress, overexpression of SlERF26 reduced the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including H₂O₂ and O₂−, which is consistent with its positive role in regulating drought tolerance. Transcriptome profiling further indicated that SlERF26 regulated a series of drought-responsive genes involved in membrane transport and ROS scavenging, such as aquaporin and peroxidases. Specifically, SlERF26 directly bound to the promoter of SlPIP2;4, an aquaporin gene, and activated its transcription. The slpip2;4 mutant accumulated higher ROS levels and exhibited significantly reduced drought tolerance. Moreover, analysis of SlERF26 OE × slpip2;4 hybrid plants confirms that SlERF26 acts upstream of SlPIP2;4 in drought tolerance. Consistent with this genetic relationship, biochemical quantification and histochemical staining demonstrated that SlERF26 and SlPIP2;4 act together to maintain ROS homeostasis during drought stress. Collectively, our findings define a SlERF26–SlPIP2;4 regulatory module that enhances drought tolerance in tomato by modulating aquaporin expression and ROS homeostasis.

Introduction

Plants are stationary and constantly threatened by various environmental stresses, such as drought, salinity, extreme temperatures, metal toxicity, and light stress. Among them, drought is considered a major limiting factor that severely affects plant development, crop yield, and global food security [1–3]. More critically, drought stress disrupts cellular osmotic balance, compromises cell membrane integrity, and inhibits the activities of metabolic enzymes [4]. As a result, prolonged or severe drought can substantially restrict plant growth and development, leading to significant yield reductions or even plant mortality. To enhance drought tolerance, plants have developed sophisticated physiological and biochemical adaptive strategies, including stomatal regulation, root architecture remodeling, antioxidant defense systems, and phytohormone-mediated signaling pathways [5–10]. Nevertheless, enhancing plant adaptability to drought remains a major challenge, especially under ongoing climate change and rising demands for agricultural water resources. Genetic engineering and advanced breeding strategies offer promising approaches for rapidly and precisely improving drought tolerance, thereby minimizing crop yield losses. Thus, identifying key genes involved in drought tolerance and deciphering their regulatory networks are crucial steps toward developing drought-resilient tomato varieties.

Transcription factors (TFs) are key regulators of gene transcription, modulating the expression of downstream genes through sequence-specific DNA binding and interactions with regulatory cofactors. Extensive research has demonstrated that TFs serve as central regulators in plant responses to drought stress, with key contributions from the APETALA2/ethylene response factor (AP2/ERF) [11], NAC [12], and BBX [13] families. The AP2/ERF family represents one of the largest TF groups in plants [14]. AP2/ERF proteins possess one or two AP2/ERF domains, each comprising ~60–70 amino acids, which are essential for DNA binding. This family comprises: ERF, AP2, RAV (related to ABI3/VP1), and DREB (dehydration-responsive element-binding protein) [15, 16] The ERF subfamily contains a single AP2/ERF domain and can be further divided into two major groups: the CBF/DREB and the ERF subfamilies [14, 15]. CBF/DREB proteins bind to the dehydration-responsive element/C-repeat (DRE/CRT), featuring a core A/GCCGAC sequence, thereby enhancing tolerance to abiotic stresses. In contrast, ERF proteins specifically bind to the AGCCGCC motif, also referred to as the GCC-box or ethylene-responsive element (ERE), and contribute primarily to resistance against biotic stresses [15, 17]. Notably, many AP2/ERF TFs are capable of recognizing both DRE/CRT and ERE motifs [18–23].

Consistent with their broad roles in stress signaling, AP2/ERF-mediated transcriptional regulation is frequently implicated in both biotic and abiotic stress responses [18–26]. For instance, Arabidopsis ERF6 positively contributes to defense against Botrytis cinerea, which is associated with reduced hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) accumulation [27]. In contrast, OsERF922 negatively regulates resistance to Magnaporthe oryzae while impairing salt stress tolerance [28].

Moreover, numerous ERF genes participate in abiotic stress adaptation, including drought and oxidative stress-related pathways [29–33]. ERF74 overexpression upregulates reactive oxygen species (ROS)-detoxifying enzymes and key genes involved in drought response, thereby enhancing drought tolerance [30]. Overexpression of SpERF.B7 increases salt tolerance in both tomato and Arabidopsis [31]. Ectopic expression of pitaya ERF1 in Arabidopsis was found to increase salt tolerance while reducing the accumulation of H2O2 and O2− [32]. Similarly, TERF2/LeERF2 improves freezing tolerance in both tomato and tobacco [34]. In addition, overexpression of SlERF5 improves tomato tolerance to both drought and salinity [29]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate the important roles of ERF proteins in abiotic stress responses. However, the molecular mechanisms by which ERF proteins regulate drought tolerance remain poorly understood.

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) is a widely cultivated vegetable crop and serves as an important model plant species. Given the high sensitivity of cultivated tomato to water deficit, understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying drought stress tolerance is essential. In our previous work, we identified SlERF26 as a drought-responsive transcription factor (TF) that showed differential expressed between introgression lines and cultivated tomato under drought conditions [35]. However, the molecular mechanism by which SlERF26 regulates drought tolerance remains largely unclear. In this study, we investigated the functional role of SlERF26 in drought tolerance and delved into its downstream regulatory network in tomato. We further examined whether SlERF26 regulates drought responses through modulating the aquaporin gene SlPIP2;4, providing new insights into the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms underlying drought tolerance in tomato.

Results

Gene features of ERF26 gene

SlERF26 (SGN: Solyc06g035700) was initially identified by tomato microarray–based gene expression profiling. The analysis revealed that SlERF26 was differentially expressed in two drought-tolerant S. pennellii introgression lines (ILs) compared with the drought-sensitive recurrent parent S. lycopersicum cv. M82 under drought stress conditions [35, 36] (Fig. 1a). Compared with well-watered conditions, SlERF26 expression did not significantly change in M82 following drought treatment but was markedly upregulated in IL2-5 and IL9-1, two drought-tolerant near-isogenic lines [35] (Fig. 1b), implying that SlERF26 is potentially involved in drought resistance.

To explore sequence variation potentially associated with SlERF26 regulation and function, the promoter and coding sequences of SlERF26 were compared between cultivated tomato (cv. M82) and S. pennellii. The promoter and coding region exhibited 92.91% and 94.82% identity, respectively, suggesting that both transcriptional regulation and functional roles in drought response are likely conserved between the two species (Fig. S1). Subsequently, we cloned the gene and found 537 base pairs (bp) coding sequence (CDS) sequence encoding a predicted protein of 178 amino acids. Sequence analysis revealed that SlERF26 harbors a conserved 63-amino acid AP2 domain, including the conserved alanine (A) and aspartic acid (D) residues typical of AP2 family proteins (Fig. S2). Phylogenetic analysis further indicated that SlERF26 clusters closely with StERF26, SvERF26, SsERF26, and CbERF26 (Fig. S3).

To determine the subcellular localization of SlERF26, a SlERF26-GFP fusion protein was transiently expressed in epidermal cells of Nicotiana benthamiana. The fluorescence from the SlERF26-GFP was exclusively detected in the nucleus, whereas free GFP was diffusely distributed throughout the cytoplasm and nucleus, indicating that SlERF26 is a nuclear-localized protein (Fig. 1c).

Furthermore, a 3.0-kb sequence upstream of the SlERF26 translation start site was retrieved and analyzed for putative cis-regulatory elements using PlantCARE database (Fig. S4). Multiple putative elements associated with plant hormone responsiveness [ABRE, TGA, and trichloroacetic acid (TCA)], light signaling (G-BOX and BOX4), and abiotic stress responses (MBS and LTR) were identified. These predictions suggest that SlERF26 expression may be regulated through the integration of external environmental stimuli and internal hormonal signaling pathways. To test whether SlERF26 responds to abiotic stress, we analyzed the transcript levels in plants exposed to salt (NaCl) and osmotic (PEG) stresses. Under salt stress, the SlERF26 transcripts were rapidly induced within 0.5 h and remained elevated for at least 6 h (Fig. 1d). Similarly, PEG treatment led to a rapid upregulation of SlERF26 transcription in leaves (Fig. 1d). These findings support that SlERF26 is responsive to both salt and osmotic stress, consistent with the presence of stress-responsive cis-elements in its promoter region.

SlERF26 overexpression improves drought tolerance in transgenic plants

To investigate the function of SlERF26 in drought tolerance, four independent homozygous T2 SlERF26-overexpressing (OE) lines were generated using the CaMV 35S promoter. Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis confirmed that SlERF26 transcript levels in lines OE-18, OE-15, OE-6, and OE-4 were markedly higher than those in the wild type (WT), and these four lines were selected for further experiments (Fig. 2a).

To evaluate drought tolerance, OE and WT plants were subjected to drought stress by withholding irrigation at the five-leaf stage. At the pre-stress stage (Day 1), no discernible phenotypic differences were observed between the SlERF26 OE lines and WT plants. After 5 days without watering, WT plants were severely wilted, whereas OE lines only showed mild leaf curling. After 10 days of water deprivation, all plants manifested dehydration symptoms. Nevertheless, following 4 days of re-watering, OE lines recovered significantly, while the WT plants were entirely shriveled (Fig. 2b). To further validate the necessity of SlERF26 for drought resilience, SlERF26 knockout lines (CR-1, CR-2, and CR-3) generated using CRISPR/Cas9 were subjected to the same drought treatment. Compared with WT plants, the slerf26 mutant plants showed increased sensitivity to water deficit, with earlier and more severe leaf dehydration and chlorosis (Fig. S5b).

Plants typically generate elevated levels of ROS in response to environmental stress. To assess oxidative stress levels, nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) and 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining were conducted to visualize O₂− and H₂O₂ accumulation, respectively. Under control conditions, WT and SlERF26 OE lines showed similar staining intensity. After exposure to drought stress, WT leaves accumulated a substantially larger amount of dark-blue and reddish-brown staining, whereas OE leaves exhibited much weaker staining, which indicated reduced ROS accumulation in the transgenic lines (Fig. 2c and d). Consistently, NBT and DAB staining showed that slerf26 mutant leaves accumulated higher levels of ROS under drought stress than WT leaves, as evidenced by more intense and widespread precipitates (Fig. S5c and d). Quantitative measurements revealed similar H₂O₂ and O₂− content in WT and SlERF26 OE lines under control conditions. Under drought stress, H₂O₂ and O₂− levels increased significantly in WT plants, while their accumulation was markedly lower in OE lines (Fig. 2e and f). Although the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and peroxidase (POD) were similar in both genotypes under control condition, both activities were significantly higher in OE lines following drought stress, suggesting enhanced antioxidant capacity in SlERF26 OE lines (Fig. 2g and h).

Malondialdehyde (MDA), a marker of lipid peroxidation [35], showed no significant difference between the WT and SlERF26 OE lines under control conditions. After 5 days of drought stress, MDA levels were significantly higher in WT plants than in SlERF26 OE lines (Fig. 2i). Drought stress increased proline content and electrolyte leakage in both WT and OE plants, but both parameters were notably lower in the OE lines than in WT plants under drought conditions (Fig. 3a and b). Similarly, the chlorophyll content was decreased under drought in both groups of plants; however, these reductions were significantly smaller in the SlERF26 OE lines compared with WT plants (Fig. 3c). Consistently, SlERF26 OE lines maintained higher relative water content (RWC) and exhibited significantly lower stomatal conductance (gs) under drought conditions compared with WT (Fig. 3d and e), indicating improved water retention capacity and reduced transpirational water loss. We further quantified stomatal density on the abaxial leaf surface and found that OE lines displayed a significantly lower stomatal density than WT (Fig. 3f and g), which may partially contribute to the reduced gs observed under drought conditions.

Collectively, these results illustrate that SlERF26 overexpression endows enhanced drought tolerance in tomato by reducing the MDA content, proline accumulation, electrolyte leakage, gs, and stomatal density while maintaining higher chlorophyll content, RWC, and SOD/POD activities, thereby promoting efficient ROS scavenging and improved cellular protection under water deficit.

SlERF26 regulates the expression of a series of drought response genes under drought stress

To gain deeper insight of the molecular mechanisms underlying the SlERF26 mediated improved drought tolerance, RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) was performed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between WT and SlERF26 OE lines (OE15 and OE18). A total of 40.41–46.67 million clean reads were obtained for each sample, of which 93.99%–96.14% were successfully mapped to tomato reference genome. The Q30 values ranged from 91.79% to 92.28%, and the average GC content ranged from 41.53% to 42.75% (Tables S1 and S2), indicating that the RNA-seq data were of high quality and suitable for downstream analyses. Correlation analysis and principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated high reproducibility among biological replicates and clear separation of samples by treatment and genotype (Fig. S6), supporting the reliability of subsequent differential expression analysis.

Global transcriptome profiling revealed distinct transcriptional differences between drought treatment (DT) and control (CK) conditions, as well as between the WT and OE plants. This indicates that overexpression of the SlERF26 alone can modify the expression levels of numerous downstream genes (Fig. 4a). Under drought stress, 326 DEGs were identified in OE-15 compared to WT, including 96 upregulated and 230 downregulated genes, whereas 124 DEGs (27 upregulated and 97 downregulated) were identified in OE-18 (Fig. S7). In addition, comparison of DT versus CK within each OE line revealed 2162 DEGs shared by OE-15 and OE-18, representing a core set of drought-responsive genes consistently affected in the SlERF26 OE background (Fig. 4b). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis demonstrated these DEGs were mainly involved in carbon metabolism, glutathione metabolism, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, and peroxisome function (Fig. 4c), suggesting that SlERF26 participates in multiple stress-responsive pathways.

Promoter analysis of the DEGs identified 32 genes containing DRE or GCC cis-elements, suggesting that these genes may be transcriptionally regulated by SlERF26 (Fig. 4d). To clarify the potential regulatory pathways associated with SlERF26-mediated drought tolerance, we focused on identifying functional genes that could directly involve in plant protection and drought stress mitigation. Among them, SlPIP2;4 was downregulated by drought stress in WT plants while upregulated in SlERF26 OE lines under the same conditions (Fig. 4e). Given that aquaporin genes play important roles in drought tolerance and antioxidation in plants [37], SlPIP2;4 was selected for further investigation. In addition to SlPIP2;4, a POD family gene containing a DRE motif in its promoter was also identified from the RNA-seq dataset (Fig. 4d). RT-qPCR analysis confirmed the differential expression of this POD gene in SlERF26 OE lines, suggesting that it may function as a downstream target of SlERF26 (Fig. S12c). These results indicate that SlERF26 may regulate ROS homeostasis through transcriptional regulation of ROS-related genes. Furthermore, RT-qPCR validation of 12 representative genes was consistent with the RNA-seq results, which confirmed the reliability of the transcriptomic data (Fig. S8).

SlERF26 directly binds to the SlPIP2;4 promoter

We analyzed the promoter region of SlPIP2;4 and identified a DRE motif located ~741 bp upstream of the transcription start site. Dual-luciferase assays showed that SlERF26 significantly activated a 2000-bp promoter reporter (SlPIP2;4pro-2 K: LUC). Consistently, SlERF26 also enhanced the activity of a 161-bp fragment containing DRE motif (SlPIP2;4pro-DRE: LUC), whereas mutation of the DRE motif markedly attenuated this activation, indicating that the DRE element is required for SlERF26-mediated transactivation (Fig. 5a–c). To directly assess binding, we carried out a Y1H assay. SlERF26 specifically interacted with the DRE-containing promoter fragment, while no interaction was detected when the DRE motif was mutated (Fig. 5d). Moreover, EMSA confirmed that the purified HIS-SlERF26 protein directly bound to DNA probes containing the 5′-TGTCGG-3′ DRE motif, but not to probes with the mutated sequence (Fig. 5e). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SlERF26 directly targets the DRE motif in the SlPIP2;4 promoter to regulate its transcription.

SlPIP2;4 positively regulates drought tolerance in tomato

SlPIP2;4 was silenced using a virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) system (pTRV—SlPIP2;4) as a preliminary assay of its role in drought tolerance. After 7 days of drought stress, SlPIP2;4 silenced plants exhibited higher sensitivity to water deficit compared with pTRV control plants. Consistently, silenced plants also displayed stronger NBT and DAB staining, indicating increased accumulation of ROS (Fig. S9a–d). To corroborate these findings, two independent CRISPR/Cas9 knockout lines of slpip2;4 were generated in tomato, with 1-bp and 3-bp deletions respectively. The 1-bp deletion caused a frameshift leading to a premature stop codon, whereas the 3-bp deletion resulted in loss of Leu79 without changing the reading frame (Fig. 6a). To assess the potential consequence of the loss of Leu79, we performed sequence and structural analyses. Multiple sequence alignment revealed that the C–G–X–V–G–L–L region is highly conserved among plant PIP aquaporins (Fig. S10d). Transmembrane topology prediction using TMHMM indicated that Leu79 is located near the N-terminal region of Loop A, close to the second transmembrane helix. Consistent with this, structural comparison based on AlphaFold modeling suggested local conformational changes in Loop A and adjacent regions in the mutant protein, suggesting a potential influence on channel architecture (Fig. S10e and f).

Phenotypic analysis showed that after 15 days of drought stress, slpip2;4 mutant plants displayed increased sensitivity to drought stress than WT plants (Fig. 6b). The activities of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD, and catalase (CAT)) were comparable between WT and slpip2;4 mutant plants under control conditions. However, under drought stress, activities of all three enzymes were significantly reduced in slpip2;4 mutant plants relative to WT (Fig. 6c–e). After 25 days of drought stress and subsequent rehydration, survival rates were markedly lower in slpip2;4 mutant plants (46% for CR-1 and 49% for CR-2) than in WT plants (Fig. 6f). Histochemical staining further showed stronger dark-blue (NBT) and brown (DAB) precipitates in leaves of slpip2;4 mutant plants than in WT plants under drought conditions, suggesting increased ROS accumulation (Fig. 6g and h). Quantitative measurements confirmed that H₂O₂ and O₂− levels were similar between genotypes under control conditions but were significantly higher in slpip2;4 mutant plants after drought treatment (Fig. 6i and j). Although slpip2;4 mutant plants exhibited smaller stomatal apertures under drought conditions (Fig. S11), they showed lower RWC and higher gs than WT plants (Fig. 6k and l), indicating impaired water status regulation and altered gas-exchange capacity.

SlERF26 acts upstream of SlPIP2;4 to regulate drought tolerance

To investigate the genetic relationship between SlERF26 and SlPIP2;4 in drought tolerance, hybrid plants were generated by crossing SlERF26 OE lines with slpip2;4 mutant plants (OE6 × CR1 and OE18 × CR2). Subsequently, the expression patterns of SlERF26 and SlPIP2;4 in these hybrid plants were subsequently examined by RT-qPCR. SlERF26 transcript levels were significantly higher in OE6/CR1 and OE18/CR2 plants, whereas SlPIP2;4 expression was reduced relative to WT plants (Fig. 7a and b). The resulting plants were then exposed to drought stress. After 18 and 23 days of drought treatment, OE6/CR1 and OE18/CR2 plants displayed reduced drought tolerance compared with SlERF26 OE lines but still showed higher tolerance than WT and slpip2;4 mutant plants (CR1 and CR2) (Fig. 7c). Consistently, OE6/CR1 and OE18/CR2 exhibited intermediate survival rates (Fig. S12a), which were lower than those of SlERF26 OE lines but higher than those of WT and slpip2;4 mutant plants. Consistent with these phenotypic differences, RWC showed a similar trend. SlERF26 OE lines maintained the highest water status, whereas slpip2;4 mutant plants exhibited the most severe dehydration under drought stress (Fig. S12b). These intermediate phenotypes suggest that the contribution of SlERF26 to drought tolerance is at least partially dependent on SlPIP2;4.

To elucidate the physiological basis underlying these phenotypic differences, ROS accumulation was assessed using multiple complementary approaches. Under drought conditions, SlERF26 OE lines accumulated significantly lower levels of ROS, whereas the highest ROS accumulation was detected in slpip2;4 mutant plants, with hybrid plants and WT displaying intermediate levels, as shown by H₂DCFDA fluorescence analysis (Fig. 7d and e). Consistently, H₂O₂ and O₂− contents were elevated in OE6/CR1, OE18/CR2, and WT plants than in SlERF26 OE lines but remained lower than those in slpip2;4 mutant plants (Fig. 7f and g). These patterns were further supported by NBT and DAB staining (Fig. S12e and f).

Consistent with the ROS accumulation patterns, the transcript levels of genes encoding antioxidant enzymes (SOD and POD) were significantly upregulated in SlERF26 OE plants but progressively declined in hybrid lines, WT, and slpip2;4 mutant plants (Fig. S12c and d). Correspondingly, OE6/CR1 and OE18/CR2 exhibited intermediate antioxidant enzyme activities, including SOD (Fig. 7h), POD (Fig. 7i), and CAT (Fig. 7j), which were higher than those of WT and slpip2;4 mutant plants but lower than those of SlERF26 OE lines.

Collectively, these findings support a model in which SlERF26 acts upstream of SlPIP2;4 to enhance drought tolerance, likely by coordinating ROS homeostasis, antioxidant capacity, and water retention to mitigate oxidative damage under drought stress.

Discussion

ERF TFs belong to a large and highly versatile family of plant transcriptional regulators that mediate various responses to developmental cues and environmental stresses. In tomato, 85 ERF genes have been identified, and a phylogenetic analysis has been performed together with their Arabidopsis thaliana orthologs [16]. SlERF26 was originally identified from drought-tolerant S. pennellii introgression lines and exhibited markedly stronger drought-induced expression in IL2-5 and IL9-1 than in M82 [35] (Fig. 1a). Sequence and subcellular localization analyses verified that SlERF26 encodes a nucleus-localized AP2/ERF TF possessing a conserved 63-amino-acid AP2 domain (Figs 1c and S1b). The evolutionary conservation of ERF26 orthologs across various species suggests that SlERF26 may execute conserved regulatory functions in plant adaptation to abiotic stresses (Fig. S1c), akin to other ERFs that govern stress responses in Arabidopsis, maize, and soybean [25, 38, 39]. Expression profiling in our study further revealed that SlERF26 was strongly induced by drought and salt stress, underscoring its participation in abiotic stress signaling (Fig. 1d). This inducible expression pattern is consistent with previous reports indicating that AP2/ERF TFs integrate hormonal signals (e.g. abscisic acid (ABA,) ethylene) and environmental cues to coordinate adaptive responses [23, 30, 33, 40, 41]. Together, these observations suggest that SlERF26 is a functionally relevant ERF regulator in tomato with the potential to regulate downstream stress-responsive gene networks.

SlERF26 enhances drought tolerance in tomato

Drought stress is among the most devastating environmental factors that negatively impact agricultural production [42]. Plants have developed multiple defense strategies, such as morphological adaptations, osmotic adjustment, maintenance of membrane integrity, and enhancement of ROS-scavenging capacity. Extensive studies have demonstrated that ERF TFs are involved in regulating ROS signaling and proline metabolism [22, 23, 25]. In tomato, Overexpression of LeERF1 and LeERF2 increases proline accumulation and reduces the levels of MDA, an indicator of lipid peroxidation [43]. Consistent with this, SlERF26 OE lines accumulated lower levels of MDA, H2O2, and O2− under drought stress while maintaining higher chlorophyll content and enhanced activities of antioxidant enzymes, including SOD and POD (Figs 2 and 3). These findings suggest that SlERF26 strengthens cellular antioxidant defense system, thereby alleviating oxidative damage and maintaining photosynthetic efficiency under water-deficit conditions. Furthermore, the transcript levels of genes encoding antioxidant enzymes, including POD and SOD, were significantly upregulated in SlERF26 OE lines under drought stress, consistent with the increased enzymatic activities. This suggests that SlERF26 may enhance antioxidant capacity at both the transcriptional and enzymatic levels, contributing to more efficient ROS detoxification. In addition to enhanced antioxidant capacity, SlERF26 OE lines maintained significantly higher RWC and exhibited reduced gs under drought stress, indicating improved water retention and decreased transpirational water loss. These physiological traits are likely associated with the enhanced ROS homeostasis observed in OE lines, as reduced oxidative damage may help sustain membrane integrity and cellular water balance during dehydration stress. Interestingly, SlERF26 OE lines also displayed reduced stomatal density (Fig. 3f), suggesting a potential role in stomatal development. A lower stomatal density may contribute to reduced gs and improved water conservation. However, because stomatal density is largely determined during leaf development, this difference likely represents a developmental trait rather than an immediate drought-induced response. Therefore, while altered stomatal density may partially contribute to drought tolerance, it is unlikely to fully account for the observed physiological and stress-resistance phenotypes.

Notably, SlERF36, another ERF TF containing an EAR motif, has been reported to negatively regulate stomatal density in tomato [44]. This indicates that modulation of stomatal development may represent a conserved function in ERF TFs.

Collectively, our results indicate that SlERF26 enhances drought tolerance through coordinated regulation of ROS homeostasis, antioxidant capacity at both transcriptional and enzymatic levels, and water retention, thereby improving plant resilience under water-deficit conditions.

Transcriptomic profiling reveals SlERF26-regulated pathways under drought stress

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying SlERF26-mediated drought tolerance, we performed comparative transcriptomic analysis was conducted between OE and WT plants under drought conditions. Two thousand one hundred sixty-two DEGs were identified between the plants under drought conditions and the control group due to SlERF26 overexpression (Fig. 4b). KEGG pathway analysis suggested that these DEGs were mainly associated with carbon metabolism, MAPK signaling, and peroxisome-related pathways (Fig. 4c), highlighting broad transcriptional reprogramming in OE plants during drought stress. Carbon metabolism is essential for plant growth, development, and yield formation. It involves key processes including photosynthetic CO2 assimilation, carbohydrate biosynthesis, catabolism, and interconversion [45]. Drought stress typically induces oxidative damage, triggers stomatal closure, and restricts shoot growth [46]. ERF110 enhances cold tolerance in citrus by modulating sugar and sterol accumulation [26]. The SlERF26 OE lines exhibited higher chlorophyll content and a reduced stomatal density, which may help preserve photosynthetic capacity and improve water conservation under drought stress. Therefore, SlERF26 might precisely adjust carbon allocation to maintain cellular homeostasis, thereby improving drought tolerance. The MAPK signaling pathway plays a central role in plant responses to drought stress, actively engaging in stomatal regulation, ROS metabolism, and the activation of stress-responsive TFs [1, 2]. In both tomato and Arabidopsis, multiple MAPKs are promptly activated under drought or osmotic stress. Subsequently, they also regulate downstream TFs and stress-inducible genes to enhance drought tolerance [47, 48]. To gain further insight into the downstream regulatory network associated with SlERF26, we analyzed the expression patterns of 32 selected genes and performed functional annotation based on the Solanaceae genome database. Functional categorization revealed that these genes could be broadly grouped into four major classes: membrane transport, hormone signaling, ROS scavenging/defense, and primary/secondary metabolism. Notably, genes involved in membrane transport included an aquaporin and a solute transporter, which are potentially associated with transmembrane movement of water and small molecules movement. Given previous evidence that certain aquaporins facilitate H₂O₂ diffusion across membranes [49, 50], this observation supports a mechanistic connection between SlERF26-mediated transcriptional regulation and intracellular ROS distribution.

In addition, several genes related to hormone signaling pathways were identified, including gibberellin 2-oxidase, auxin response factor 9, and a histidine phosphotransfer protein. The presence of these genes suggests that SlERF26 may participate in crosstalk between stress responses and phytohormone signaling, potentially modulating plant growth and adaptation under stress conditions.

Importantly, a substantial proportion of the selected genes were associated with ROS scavenging and stress defense, such as peroxidases, chitinases, and osmotin-like proteins. These proteins are well known to function in ROS detoxification and enhancing stress tolerance, indicating that the SlERF26 regulatory network may contribute to maintaining ROS homeostasis [51–55]. This is consistent with the physiological phenotypes observed in SlERF26 OE plants. Taken together, these results suggest that SlERF26 functions as an upstream regulator coordinating multiple biological processes, including membrane transport, hormone signaling, ROS detoxification, and metabolic reprogramming. Rather than acting through a single downstream target, SlERF26 likely modulates a broader transcriptional network, with SlPIP2;4 serving as a key node linking H₂O₂ transport to ROS homeostasis. This integrated regulatory framework provides a mechanistic basis for understanding how SlERF26 enhances stress tolerance in tomato.

SlERF26 directly regulates SlPIP2;4 to modulate redox homeostasis

ERF proteins are TFs that regulate stress-responsive genes by binding to their promoters, thereby enhancing stress tolerance. The GCC-box (AGCCGCC) is a well-characterized cis-element that is bound by several ERF proteins, whereas others ERFs preferentially bind the DRE (A/GCCGAC). Some ERFs display dual DNA-binding specificity and are capable of interacting with both motifs. For example, ERF1 was demonstrated to specifically bind either the GCC-box or DRE/CRT elements in a stress-dependent manner, facilitating responses to distinct abiotic stresses [38]. In tobacco, overexpression of NtERF172 increases drought tolerance by controlling NtCAT expression, potentially through DRE element binding in the promoter region [40]. Therefore, identifying the downstream targets of ERFs in tomato will offer crucial insights into their functional roles and the molecular mechanisms underlying drought tolerance regulation. In this study, we identified SlPIP2;4, a plasma membrane aquaporin, as a key downstream component of the SlERF26 regulatory network (Fig. 4d and e). Dual-luciferase reporter assays and EMSA verified that SlERF26 directly binds to the DRE motifs in the SlPIP2;4 promoter, thereby transcriptionally activating this gene (Fig. 5).

Aquaporins (AQPs) are major membrane proteins mediating transport of water and various small neutral solutes across plant cellular membranes. Phylogenetic and sequence analyses further divide PIPs into two subfamilies, PIP1 and PIP2. PIP2 aquaporins are generally regarded as having higher intrinsic water channel activity than PIP1 isoforms [56]. ROS can induce cellular damage by promoting lipid peroxidation, nucleic acid oxidation, and the loss of enzymatic activity [57]. Increasing evidence suggests that certain aquaporins act as peroxiporins, facilitating transmembrane transport of H₂O₂, thus modulating redox signaling and influencing the activity of downstream enzymes [6, 58–60]. The kinase OST1 phosphorylates Ser121 of aquaporin PIP2;1, a post-translational modification participates in ABA-and ROS-mediated stomatal closure [59]. In addition to redox signaling, AQPs are crucial for maintaining cellular water homeostasis under abiotic stress [61, 62]. SlPIP2;1, and SlPIP2;5 have been reported to improve drought tolerance by achieving higher hydraulic conductivity levels [63]. Overexpression of SiPIP2;4 promotes stomatal closure and reduces water evaporation, which leads to enhanced drought tolerance [64]. Consistent with these reports, slpip2;4 mutant plants in our study exhibited elevated ROS levels, decreased activities of SOD, CAT, and POD (Fig. 6), supporting a positive role of SlPIP2;4 in both redox balance and drought adaptation. Notably, although slpip2;4 mutant plants displayed smaller stomatal apertures under drought conditions (Fig. S11), they exhibited higher gs and lower RWC than WT (Fig. 6k and l). These observations suggest that gs is not solely determined by aperture size and may also reflect other factors, such as whole-leaf hydraulic properties and plant water status. Future studies employing time-resolved gs measurements and ABA/ROS-induced stomatal closure kinetics would help further dissect the contribution of SlPIP2;4 to stomatal regulation and drought responses.

Genetic evidence supports a hierarchical relationship between SlERF26 and SlPIP2;4

Genetic analysis using SlERF26 OE × slpip2;4 hybrid lines provided important insight into the relationship between SlERF26 and SlPIP2;4 in drought tolerance. The intermediate phenotypes observed in the hybrid plants, including survival rates, RWC, and ROS accumulation, indicate that the contribution of SlERF26 to drought tolerance is at least partially dependent on SlPIP2;4. Mechanistically, coordinated changes in ROS accumulation, antioxidant gene expression, and antioxidant enzyme activities across the different genotypes support a model in which SlERF26 functions upstream to modulate ROS homeostasis. On the one hand, SlERF26 activates SlPIP2;4 expression, which may influence H₂O₂ distribution and cellular redox balance. On the other hand, SlERF26 enhances antioxidant capacity at both the transcriptional and enzymatic levels, thereby promoting efficient ROS detoxification. Together, these findings support a framework in which SlERF26 integrates SlPIP2;4-mediated processes to fine-tune ROS homeostasis and improve drought tolerance.

The SlERF26-SlPIP2;4 regulatory module identified in this study complements current models of ERF-mediated drought responses by offering a more nuanced perspective on cellular redox homeostasis. Earlier studies have predominantly focused on ERF TFs directly regulating antioxidant enzyme genes [40] or modulating hormone signaling pathways, such as ABA-dependent responses [23, 33, 65, 66], to enhance stress tolerance. In these models, ERFs primarily function as transcriptional regulators that control downstream stress-responsive gene expression. In contrast, our findings reveal that SlERF26 not only participates in transcriptional regulation but also contributes to the regulation of intracellular H₂O₂ level, potentially through SlPIP2;4. This introduces an additional regulatory layer involving ROS transport, which is largely absent from conventional ERF-centered models. Notably, the partial retention of drought tolerance in the SlERF26 OE × slpip2;4 background further suggests that SlERF26 integrates multiple downstream pathways, including both ROS redistribution and ROS scavenging. Therefore, unlike the canonical ERF-antioxidant enzyme or ERF-hormone signaling modules, the SlERF26–SlPIP2;4 module represents a dual regulatory system that coordinates ROS transport and detoxification. Collectively, these observations extend current ERF-centered models by indicating a potential contribution of ROS transport, in addition to ROS detoxification, to drought-associated redox regulation.

Based on the molecular and genetic evidence, we propose that drought-induced SlERF26 acts upstream of SlPIP2;4 to coordinate redox homeostasis and drought adaptation in tomato. Under drought stress, the SlERF26 protein directly binds the DRE motif in the SlPIP2;4 promoter and activates its transcription, thereby increasing SlPIP2;4 abundance at the plasma membrane. Elevated SlPIP2;4 may contribute to the regulation of intracellular H₂O₂ distribution and water status, while SlERF26 also enhances antioxidant capacity by upregulating ROS-detoxifying components at both the transcriptional and enzymatic levels. As a result, ROS accumulation and oxidative damage are restrained, and plants maintain improved water retention and survival under drought stress (Fig. 8).

Proposed model of the SlERF26–SlPIP2;4 module in drought tolerance. Under well-watered (normal) conditions, basal expression of SlERF26 maintains SlPIP2;4 and redox status at homeostatic levels. Under drought stress, SlERF26 is rapidly induced and accumulates in the nucleus, where it directly binds the DRE motif in the promoter of SlPIP2;4 and activates its transcription. Increased SlPIP2;4 at the plasma membrane may facilitate H₂O₂ movement across membranes and contribute to cellular redox balance and water status regulation. In parallel, SlERF26 enhances antioxidant capacity by promoting the expression (SlPOD, SlSOD) and activities of ROS-scavenging enzymes (SOD, POD, and CAT). Together, these coordinated processes reduce ROS accumulation and oxidative damage, improve water retention, and ultimately enhance drought tolerance.

Materials and methods

Gene isolation, vector construction, and generation of transgenic tomato

The SlERF26 (SGN: Solyc06g035700) coding sequence was amplified from S. lycopersicum cv M82 using polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The full-length SlERF26 PCR product was cloned into the pHellsgate8 vector [66], containing CaMV 35S promoter using homologous recombination following the manufacturer’s instructions (Hieff Clone® Plus, Yisheng, Shanghai, China). The recombinant plasmid was subsequently transformed into A. tumefaciens LBA4404 following the freeze–thaw technique for stable plant transformation.

The SlERF26 OE tomato plants were generated using Agrobacterium-mediated transformation of tomato cotyledon explants as previously described [5]. Then, the transgenic lines were confirmed by PCR using gene-specific primers (Table S3), and their further expression was verified by RT-qPCR. To select for transgenic lines, plants were grown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium containing 50 mg/L kanamycin. To generate CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of the SlPIP2;4, two guide RNAs (gRNAs) targeting the coding sequence were designed using the CRISPR-p v2.0 platform. The designed gRNAs were inserted into the pTX vector via homologous recombination [67]. The resulting CRISPR/Cas9 constructs, which utilize the U6 promoter for efficient expression in plants, were introduced into A. tumefaciens LBA4404 to mediate tomato transformation.

To evaluate the drought resistance of SlERF26 OE lines and SlPIP2;4 knockout lines in a single plant, we crossed SlERF26 OE6 with slpip2;4-CR1 plants and SlERF26-OE18 with slpip2;4-CR2 plants to generate the OE6/CR1, and OE18/CR2 double mutants. PCR with primers specific for the SlERF26 overexpression construct and SlPIP2;4 CRISPR loci (Table S3) confirmed the presence of the target mutations. RT-qPCR was conducted to verify the transcription levels of SlERF26 and SlPIP2;4 in the hybrid plants.

Bioinformatics analysis

The SlERF26 gene sequence, along with the corresponding sequences of ERF26 homologs in 25 plant species were obtained from the NCBI database. These sequences were selected based on their homology to SlERF26 and the availability of full-length coding sequences. The conserved protein motifs of SlERF26 and other ERF26 proteins were identified using the SMART tool, which predicts functional domains and conserved regions within protein sequences.

The amino acid sequences were aligned with GeneDoc (version 2.7), followed by manual inspection to ensure correct alignment of conserved regions. Regions with poor alignment were excluded from the analysis.

Phylogenetic relationships among ERF26 proteins were inferred in MEGA (version 7) using the Neighbor-Joining approach. The analysis was based on aligned protein sequences, evolutionary distances were calculated using the Poisson correction model, and branch support was evaluated with 1000 bootstrap replicates.

The transmembrane topology of SlPIP2;4 was predicted using the TMHMM server based on a hidden Markov model. Structural models of SlPIP2;4 WT and the CR2 mutant were generated using the AlphaFold server. The model with the highest predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) score was selected for subsequent analyses. Structural visualization and comparison were performed using PyMOL (version 3.0).

Subcellular localization

The SlERF26 ORF was PCR-amplified using primers bearing BamHI and KpnI restriction sites to enable subsequent cloning into the expression vector. Then, the PCR product having the mentioned restriction site was digested and ligated into the pCAMBIA2300 vector. The recombinant construct was verified by sequencing to verify the correct insertion of the SlERF26 sequence. The construct pCAMBIA2300-35S-EGFP-SlERF26 was designed to express SlERF26 fused to enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) driven by the CaMV 35S promoter.

An empty 35S-EGFP construct was prepared in parallel as a control. Both the SlERF26-EGFP fusion construct and the control 35S-EGFP were co-transfected into N. benthamiana epidermal cells via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation following standard protocol.

After 24 h, epidermal cells were stained with 1 mM 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 1 h to label nuclei. EGFP and DAPI fluorescence was detected using a Zeiss laser scanning microscope (LSM) 780 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Göttingen, Germany) with excitation at 515 and 405 nm, respectively, to investigate the subcellular localization of the SlERF26–EGFP fusion protein.

RNA extraction and real time RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the tomato tissues using RNAiso Plus reagent (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of DNase I-treated RNA using the HiFiScript gDNA Removal RT MasterMix (CoWin, Taizhou, China). RT-qPCR was performed on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) with SYBR Green-based 2xSP qPCR Mix (Bioground, Chongqing, China). All reactions followed the manufacturer’s protocols.

SlActin gene was used as an internal control, and relative transcript levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Drought stress and physiological assays

Tomato plants were grown in a greenhouse under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod at 25 ± 2°C. At the five-leaf stage, drought stress was imposed by completely withholding irrigation. Seedlings were transplanted into individual plastic pots (9 cm diameter) containing a homogeneous substrate mixture of peat:vermiculite:perlite (3:1:1, v/v/v). Prior to drought treatment, all pots were saturated with water until drainage occurred, ensuring uniform soil water content at the start of the experiment. The soil weight of each pot was standardized before stress imposition to minimize variation in initial water availability among genotypes. After the drought treatment, plants were rewatered and allowed to recover for 3–4 days. Phenotypic responses were recorded, including visual symptoms such as wilting and leaf curling.

The MDA and proline contents were determined using methods described [68, 69]. For MDA quantification, plant samples were homogenized in TCA, and the resulting supernatant was reacted with thiobarbituric acid to form a color complex, which was measured spectrophotometrically. Proline levels were measured using acid–ninhydrin reaction and quantified by absorbance at 520 nm.

Leaf conductivity was measured according to the method [70]. Leaf disks were incubated in distilled water at 100°C for 30 min. The electrical conductivity was recorded before and after boiling the samples, and relative conductivity was calculated as a measure of cell membrane integrity under stress. For ROS and biochemical measurements, samples were collected when drought-sensitive plants first exhibited visible wilting symptoms but before irreversible tissue collapse. For survival assays, drought was extended until severe wilting was observed in most plants, followed by rewatering to assess recovery.

Chlorophyll concentration was assessed by measuring the absorption spectra of leaf extracts at 470, 649, and 665 nm using a spectrophotometer. The leaves (0.1 g) were soaked in 10 ml of 95% ethanol for 24 h in the dark.

POD, CAT, and SOD activities were determined following standard protocol [71]. Enzyme activities were measured by spectrophotometry, where POD activity was evaluated by the oxidation of guaiacol, CAT activity was determined based on the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide, and SOD activity was assessed by the inhibition of NBT reduction.

The stomatal density and quantity were measured on the abaxial (lower) epidermis of the third or fourth fully expanded leaf using a photomicroscope. Stomatal density was expressed as the number of stomata per square millimeter. To investigate the stomatal microstructure, mature, fully expanded leaves were harvested from drought-stressed plants on Day 15, and the leaf surface was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SU3500, Hitachi High-Technologies Corp., Tokyo, Japan). The analysis was conducted in triplicate, and six representative fields of view were captured per replicate to ensure consistency in the data.

Leaf RWC was determined following a standard protocol with minor modifications. Fresh weight (FW) of fully expanded leaves was measured immediately after sampling. The leaves were then floated on distilled water in the dark for 12 h at room temperature to obtain the turgid weight (TW). Subsequently, the samples were oven-dried at 80°C for 24 h to determine the dry weight (DW). RWC was calculated using the following formula: RWC = (FW − DW)/(TW − DW) * 100.

Stomatal conductance was measured using a portable porometer (LI-6400XTR, LI-COR, USA). Measurements were performed on the abaxial surface of fully expanded leaves between 9:00 and 11:00 a.m. under stable environmental conditions. For each plant, at least three leaves were measured, and three independent biological replicates were included. At least three biological replicates were included for each treatment.

Intracellular H₂O₂ levels were detected using the fluorescent probe H₂DCFDA (2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate). Leaf samples were incubated in 10 μM H₂DCFDA solution prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). To facilitate dye penetration, samples were subjected to vacuum infiltration for 10 min, followed by incubation in the dark at room temperature for a total of 30 min.

After incubation, the samples were washed three times with PBS (5 min each) to remove excess dye. Fluorescence signals were observed using a fluorescence microscope (excitation: 488 nm, emission: 525 nm). Images were captured under identical settings for all samples. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

For each genotype and treatment, three independent biological replicates were used. Each biological replicate consisted of fully expanded leaves collected from three individual plants and pooled as one sample. All collected samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until further analysis.

RNA-seq analysis

Leaves of WT and two representative SlERF26 overexpression lines (OE15 and OE18), selected based on their expression levels and drought tolerance phenotypes, were subjected to 5-day drought stress, harvested, and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent RNA extraction. Well-watered plants served as a control. Total RNA was extracted using a standard RNA isolation protocol. Next-generation sequencing was performed by Novogene (Beijing, China) using the Illumina HiSeq2500 platform to obtain high-quality RNA-seq data.

Raw reads were filtered to remove adaptor sequences, poly-N reads, and low-quality reads. Clean reads were aligned to the tomato reference genome (SL2.50) using HISAT2 (v2.0.5) with default parameters, and only uniquely mapped reads were retained for downstream analysis.

Gene-level read counts were generated using featureCounts (v1.5.0-p3). Differential expression analysis was conducted in R using DESeq2 (v1.20.0) based on raw count matrices and a negative binomial distribution model. Two independent biological replicates were included for each condition. Data normalization was performed using the DESeq2 median-of-ratios method, and P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 were considered differentially expressed.

PCA and sample-to-sample correlation analyses were performed using normalized count data to assess replicate consistency. Fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) values were calculated for visualization purposes only.

For functional annotation, the identified genes were compared against several public databases, including COG, KEGG, GO, NR, Swiss-Prot, and Pfam. Enriched pathways and functional terms were considered to be associated with drought stress responses in the transgenic lines.

To validate the RNA-seq results, the expression patterns of selected genes were further confirmed by qRT-PCR.

Yeast one-hybrid and dual-luciferase reporter assays

The full-length SlERF26 CDS was amplified and inserted into the pGADT7 to produce the effector construct for assessing binding to the DRE element. The promoter of SlPIP2;4, containing three tandem DRE element and their mutants, was synthesized and cloned into the pAbAi vector as the reporter construct. Both constructs were co-introduced into Saccharomyces cerevisiae Y1HGold using the LiAc/SS carrier DNA/PEG method. Yeast transformants were selected on SD/-Ura medium and then transferred to SD/-Leu medium containing aureobasidin A (AbA; Cat. CA2332G; Coolaber, Beijing, China) and incubated for 5 days to screen for protein–DNA interactions based on growth inhibition.

For the dual-luciferase assay, the SlERF26 full-length CDS was cloned into pGADT7 under the CaMV35S promoter, and the SlPIP2;4 promoter was cloned into pGreen II 0800-LUC as the reporter. Agrobacterium GV3101 carrying the effector and reporter constructs was infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. After 48 h, fluorescence imaging was performed using a Fusion FX system (Vilber Lourmat, France). Fluorescence intensity was captured and quantified using ImageJ2 in the Fiji platform to assess the relative fluorescence signal [72]. Each assay was repeated three times, with at least three independent plants per replicate.

Recombinant proteins and electrophoretic mobility shift assay

The full-length SlERF26 CDS was amplified and inserted into the pET-30a, containing a 6x His tag for protein purification, and transformed into Escherichia coli Rosetta BL21 (DE3). Protein expression was induced with 0.1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 37°C for 12 h. After induction, cells were collected by centrifugation and sonicated in lysis buffer. The His-tagged SlERF26 protein was then purified using the His-tag Protein Purification Kit (Cat. P2226; Beyotime, Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol for affinity chromatography. Purity and concentration were confirmed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). For the EMSA, a probe containing the DRE box from the SlPIP2;4 promoter was synthesized by Qingke Company (Beijing, China) and labeled with biotin. The probe was annealed to form double-stranded oligonucleotides. The binding assay was conducted using the Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (Cat. GS009; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the reaction mixture contained 10 μg of the recombinant His-SlERF26 protein and the biotin-labeled DRE probe, incubated for 30 min at 25°C. For competition assays, a 100-fold excess of unlabeled competitor probe was added before the labeled probe. The DNA–protein complexes were resolved on a 6% native polyacrylamide gel and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The biotin-labeled probes were detected using a Chemiluminescence Detection System (OI600, Bio-OI Technology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) and the results were visualized using a chemiluminescent imaging system. Each assay was repeated three times to ensure reproducibility.

Virus-induced gene silencing

SlPIP2;4 was silenced in tomato using TRV-based VIGS vectors (pTRV1/2) [73]. Gene-specific cDNA fragments (Table S3) were amplified and cloned into pTRV2, sequence-verified, and then transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101.

For VIGS assays, Agrobacterium cultures harboring pTRV1 and pTRV2 constructs were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and infiltrated into the cotyledons of 2-week-old tomato seedlings using a needleless syringe. Plants infiltrated with empty pTRV2 vector were used as negative controls.

After infiltration, plants were maintained in the dark for 24 h and then transferred to normal growth conditions (25°C, 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod). Gene silencing efficiency was assessed by RT-qPCR analysis of SlPIP2;4 expression levels at 2–3 weeks postinfiltration. Only plants showing significant reduction in transcript levels were used for subsequent experiments. The effectiveness of the VIGS system was confirmed using a TRV-PDS construct, which resulted in a characteristic photobleaching phenotype.

To minimize potential off-target effects, the silencing fragment was designed based on a gene-specific region with low sequence similarity to other tomato genes, as determined by BLAST analysis against the tomato genome available at the Sol Genomics Network.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For multiple group comparisons, one-way or two-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used as appropriate. Statistical significance in figures is indicated as *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001. The number of biological replicates (n) is indicated in the figure legends. Biological replicates refer to independent plants unless otherwise stated.
