Profiling intimate partner violence in booked pregnant women at Alex Ekwueme Federal University Teaching Hospital Abakaliki and associated puerperal depressive sequelae: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Amuchechukwu V. Nwafor, Odidika Ugochukwu J Umeora, Arinze C Ikeotuonye, Bobby C Iwe, Justus N Eze, Leonard O Ajah, Christian C Anikwe, Chidi O. U Esike, Francis C Okoroafor, Okey Mbanefo, Uchenna N Nwaedu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntimate partner violence (IPV) in pregnancy is a physical, sexual, psychological or economic harm by a current or former partner or spouse on a pregnant woman. It is a global public health problem that is common but underreported. Women are at increased risk of psychiatric illness in pregnancy and after delivery with the risk of major depressive disorder being highest during the postpartum period. Intimate partner violence in pregnancy may worsen this problem.ObjectivesThe objectives of the study were to determine the prevalence of intimate partner violence (IPV) in pregnancy, incidence of postpartum depression and the relationship between intimate partner violence, delivery outcomes and postpartum depression among booked pregnant women at Alex Ekwueme Federal University Teaching Hospital Abakaliki, Ebonyi state, Nigeria (AEFUTHA).Study designThis study was a prospective cohort study.SettingThe antenatal clinic, antenatal ward, labour ward, postpartum clinic and under five clinic of Alex Ekwueme Federal University Teaching Hospital Abakaliki, Ebonyi state, Nigeria were used for the study.Method One hundred and thirty-seven booked pregnant women that received antenatal care at AEFUTHA who met the inclusion criteria and consented to the study, were recruited from 37 weeks to 41 weeks gestation and screened for intimate partner violence and depression. Those with depression were referred for treatment while those that met the inclusion criteria were followed up to delivery and the delivery outcomes documented. They were also followed up to six weeks postpartum when they were screened for postpartum depression. Data were collated, tabulated and then statistically analysed using Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) software (version 25, Chicago II, USA). Numerical variables including participant’s age, parity and gestational age were presented as mean, median, frequencies and standard deviation (Mean ± S.D), while categorical variables including occupation, level of education, social class and family type were presented as numbers and percentages. Chi-square test (X2) and relative risk was used for comparison between groups for categorical variables while Fisher’s exact test was used when Chi-square test (X2) was not suitable. Binary regression analysis was used to determine the relationship between intimate partner violence and postpartum depression. A P value of ˂0.05 is considered statistically significant.ResultsThe prevalence of intimate partner violence was 52.6%, as 72 out of the 137 women recruited endured intimate partner violence. The major risk factors for intimate partner violence in the study were low level of education, low social class, polygamy and unemployment. The general incidence of postpartum depression was 29.93% while the incidence among women with intimate partner violence was 56.94%. Women with emotional violence and verbal abuse had a five-fold increase in the incidence of postpartum depression. Sexual violence and physical violence were not statistically significant risk factors for postpartum depression.ConclusionIntimate partner violence is common as seen from the study. It is a significant risk factor for postpartum depression. Women that are emotionally and verbal abused are more likely to have postpartum depression. Screening pregnant women for intimate violence may identify those at risk and allow for proper interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-024-06869-2.

Introduction

Intimate partner violence (IPV) is defined as a physical, sexual, psychological or economic harm by a current or former partner or spouse [1, 2]. It is any behaviour or act within the intimate relationship by an intimate partner that results to either physical, psychological or sexual harm to the person in that relationship [2, 3]. The act of IPV is mostly men towards women [3]. It has been reported that two out of every three victims of intimate partner or family related homicide are women [3]. It is currently recognized globally to be of public health importance as it impacts negatively on maternal mental health [1–4].

According to World Health Organisation (WHO), the prevalence of intimate partner violence is 15–71% globally in women of reproductive age group [4]. Annually, up to 1.5 to 4 million women in the United States of America are victims of intimate partner violence [1, 5]. The prevalence of intimate partner violence in pregnancy varies from place to place. Prevalence of IPV in pregnancy was reported to be 3–30% in Europe and Asia, 4.2% in Cambodia and Philippines, 10% in Zimbabwe, 7% in South Africa, 13.5% in Uganda, 4.7% in Vietnam and 3–8% in East Flanders, Belgium [2, 5–7]. The prevalence of intimate partner violence in pregnancy in Abakaliki, Southeast Nigeria was 44.6% [8].

In most cases of intimate partner violence, the perpetrator is usually the husband or intimate male partner [6]. Intimate partner violence takes various forms including acts of physical aggression like slaps, punches, kicks and assaults with a weapon that can lead to homicides [1]. It can also be in the form of sexual violence like forced intercourse, sexual coercion and other controlling behaviours [1, 9]. Intimate partner violence is usually associated with emotional abuse, economic restrictions and other controlling behaviour on the victim [6]. There is no predilection for background, age, ethnicity, socio-economic status, sexual orientation or religion [10].

Evidence has shown that intimate partner violence in pregnancy is even commoner than most other maternal health conditions including hypertensive disorders, and therefore requires more attention [6]. Pregnancy can make some women vulnerable to intimate partner violence possibly because of the physiological changes that alter the woman’s physical, emotional, social and economic needs which may lead to aggression and violence [3, 10]. It could be a continuation of pre-pregnancy abuse or a new violence in that pregnancy. Women who have a partner with a previous history of IPV, substance abuse problem or unemployment when pregnant, have an exacerbation of IPV as the pregnancy usually trigger or exacerbate existing problems [4]. An unplanned pregnancy, financial stress of supporting the pregnancy and shift of attention from the partner to the pregnancy are often trigger factors [4, 10].

Intimate partner violence in pregnancy is a preventable public health problem [11, 12]. Many authorities believe that the perinatal period creates an excellent opportunity for intimate partner violence in pregnancy to be addressed as this is the only time couples are most likely to make several visits to the health facility together [10]. Intimates partner violence in pregnancy is of particular interest to the Obstetrician since it impacts negatively on women’s and children’s health and finally becomes a medical liability in caring for them [2]. It affects all aspects of a woman’s health, and can complicate her reproductive health and that of her offsprings [2]. Obstetricians have a special role in women’s health as it concerns IPV in pregnancy [13].

Studies show that intimate partner violence in pregnancy is associated with both maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality [1, 3]. Victims are at risk of injury and/or death [2]. The victims of IPV in pregnancy are at risk of several health consequences resulting from poor attendance to both antenatal and postnatal clinics, predisposing them to poor pregnancy outcomes [3]. Some of the health consequences of intimate partner violence in pregnancy include fetal complications like spontaneous miscarriage, preterm delivery, low birth weight, small for gestational age and neonatal death [1, 2, 13]. Clients suffering from intimate partner violence are also at risk of placenta abruption, uterine rupture, antepartum haemorrhage and fetal distress [2]. Other maternal complications of IPV include physical, psychological and emotional trauma. This can eventually lead to fatal effects including homicide and other negative health consequences both in pregnancy and during the puerperal period [9]. Femicide is an important underreported sequelae of IPV [2]. Women who are victims of IPV are also at increased risk of sexually transmitted diseases including HIV/AIDS [3]. IPV in pregnancy also predisposes victims to mental adverse outcomes including depression, anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorders, suicide attempt, adaptation challenges, and poor nutrition, as well as use of tobacco, alcohol and illicit drugs, eating disorder, substance dependence, non-affective psychosis and antisocial personality disorder [2, 4, 6]. These in turn, increases the stress levels and impacts negatively on maternal behaviour and nutrition [1]. The aforementioned eventually leads to poor pregnancy outcome and postpartum depression.

Depression is a very serious and life-threatening mental disorder that affects women of child bearing age significantly [9]. Early identification of women exposed to IPV in pregnancy at risk of postpartum depression during the antenatal period will provide the rationale for appropriate intervention in preventing postpartum depression and its sequelae, among other numerous benefits of such study [7]. Depression can be so devastating in a woman’s life whenever it occurs; it is even worse when it occurs in the perinatal period as it has numerous negative implications on the woman, her family and the child [10].

Most of those studies available on this topic were done in the postpartum period thereby obtaining information about the relationship between intimate partner violence and postpartum depression in retrospection [9, 14–17]. These studies did not screen for pre-existing depression in pregnancy as this might continue in the postpartum period thereby confounding the outcomes. Routine screening of antenatal women for intimate partner violence in pregnancy has been recommended and practiced in developed countries, but it is yet to be implemented in third world countries including Nigeria. This study screened pregnant women for depression at the antenatal period to exclude those with pre-existing depression and determine the true incidence of postpartum depression resulting from IPV. The other objectives were to determine the prevalence of intimate partner violence (IPV) among booked pregnant women at Alex Ekwueme Federal University Teaching Hospital, Abakaliki and to study the relationship between IPV in pregnancy and postpartum depression (PPD) and associated pregnancy outcomes.

Methods

This was a prospective cohort study on intimate partner violence (IPV) conducted at the antenatal clinic, Labour ward, postnatal ward and postnatal clinic of AEFUTHA. Consenting participants from 37 weeks to 41 weeks, who met the inclusion criteria were recruited. The exclusion criteria were history of psychiatric illness in previous pregnancy including puerperal blues, women diagnosed with depression, unmarried pregnant women, patients on antipsychotic medications, medical disorders (diagnosed with hypertension, diabetes mellitus, thyroid disorders, self- reported sleep disorder, history of alcoholics, history of substance/drug abuse, Smoking, multiple pregnancy).

The sample size was calculated using the formula for a clinical cohort study according to Charan et al. [18], at power of 80%, 5% significance and this gave a total of 136. Adding an attrition rate of 20% attrition (20% of 136 = 28), this gave a sample size of 164. Recruitment was spread out over a period of six months (120 working days) from 1st April, 2020 to 31st March, 2021 from gestational age of 37 weeks to 41 weeks at the antenatal clinic. Follow up was done at the labour room, postnatal ward and infant welfare clinic at 6 weeks postpartum. The sampling method used was systematic sampling technique. Two participants were recruited each day to allow time for follow up. Every fifth pregnant woman that came for antenatal each day was recruited as they were routinely given numbers serially. When the participant did not meet the inclusion criteria, the next on the list replaced her. Recruited participants were made to understand the study objectives, to enable them give an informed consent. They were told that the process would involve asking some intimate questions concerning their relationship with their spouses which might be sensitive. They were assured of the confidentiality of the information given and the fact that it would be read only for the purpose of this study. They were also told that they were free to withdraw their participation at any point without consequences. Informed consent was then obtained at this point by the researcher or the assistants from consenting participants. The inclusion criteria were pregnant women receiving antenatal care at AEFUTHA, women at gestational age of 37 weeks to 41 weeks and women who consented to the study. The exclusion criteria include women with background psychiatric illness, history of psychiatric illness in previous pregnancy including puerperal blues, women with depression, unmarried pregnant women, those on antipsychotic medications, medical disorders such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, thyroid disorders, Self-reported sleep disorder, history of alcoholics, history of substance/drug abuse, smoking and multiple gestation.

The questionnaire

The questionnaire was in both English and Igbo, such that it could be understood by all the participants. The validation of the Igbo questionnaire was done by first translating the questionnaire into Igbo language by a Professor of Igbo language at the Alex Ekwueme Federal University, Ndufu-Alike, Ikwo, Nigeria. The translated and original versions of the questionnaires were then pre-tested among a selection of the study population and then analysed per participant response to note for uniformity in the answers and any ambiguity in the questions. This was also tested to ensure it fit into the study objectives. The questionnaire had three parts, including; socio-demographic data, assessment of intimate partner violence and postpartum depression assessment using the Edinburg Postpartum Depression Scale. The essence of assessing for depression at the point of recruitment was to exclude women with depressive symptoms which could interfere with the postpartum depression scores. The first part which is the socio-demographic included; the participant’s age, gestational age, parity, occupation, highest educational qualification, husband’s occupation, social class, family type and booking parameters including height, weight and blood pressure. The social class of the participants were filled by the researcher using her level of education and the occupation of the husband. Participants were classified into social classes I to V according to Olusanya et al., 1985 [19, 20]. They were further classified into low, middle and high social classes; 1 and II as high, III as middle, and IV and V as low social class.

The second part of the questionnaire screened for intimate partner violence. The various types of intimate partner violence were assessed including; Physical violence (slapping, pushing, shoving or throwing of an object, punches, kicks and assaults with a weapon, hitting that could hurt, kicking, dragging, beating; choking or threatening with a weapon), Emotional/psychological violence (emotional abuse, economic restrictions and other controlling behaviour) and Sexual violence (forced intercourse, sexual coercion). It also assessed verbal abuse which may also be a form of psychological violence. (Adapted from the Abuse Assessment screen form- AAS by Soeken KL et al. [20]. See appendix).

The third part of the questionnaire screened for depression using the Edinburg postpartum depression scale. Edinburg postnatal depression scale, EPDS is a 10-item self-administered questionnaire that is scored from 0 to 30. The scale contains statements describing depressive symptoms. The depressive symptoms assessed include; inability to laugh and look forward to things with enjoyment, unnecessary blaming of oneself, anxious or worried, scared or panicky, inability to cope, difficulty sleeping, feeling sad or miserable, crying and thoughts of harming oneself. Each of the questions have four possible answers depending on depressive symptom severity and duration ranging from 0 to 3. The responses are scored 0, 1, 2 and 3. Items 3, 5 to 10 were reversely scored (i.e., 3, 2, 1, and 0). All the 10 items were to be completed. The total score was obtained by addition of all the scores. The participants were expected to tick the options that were closest to their experience in the past seven days. Total scores of 15 to 30 were considered significant to define PPD, for the purpose of this study.

A pro forma was attached that assessed the delivery outcome. The parameters recorded were; route of delivery, if episiotomy was given, sex of the baby, birth weight, APGAR scores in the fifth minute, new born admission and duration of hospital stay. These were confounding factors that can predispose to postpartum depression. They were subjected to regression analysis.

Study procedure

After obtaining an informed consent, the self-administered questionnaire with three parts was given to the participants to fill. The participants were expected to complete the questionnaire themselves. The researcher or any of the assistants was present but maintained a distance as the participants filled the questionnaires, to make clarification when required and to ensure that the questionnaires were completed before submission. The participants also answered questions on intimate partner violence including physical, psychological and sexual violence as well as verbal abuse indicating if they have had any in the index pregnancy. This helped to determine the prevalence of intimate partner violence in pregnancy. The participants then completed the Edinburg postpartum depression scale. This helped to determine incidence of PPD as well as exclude prenatal depression that might continue to the postpartum period. Participants who had depression during screening at point of recruitment, were referred to the Clinical Psychologist and Mental Health Physician for treatment and were excluded from the study.

The participants were recognised in labour because the researcher’s phone number was written with a small plaster measuring about 1.5 by 4 centimetres on the participant’s case note. This enabled the doctors on call to contact the researcher or the assistant. The delivery outcomes were recorded after delivery, including; birth weight, APGAR score in the fifth minute, sex of the baby, mode of delivery, admission into new born intensive care unit and duration of hospital stay. Before discharge, the contact of the researcher or any of the assistant was made available to the participants for easy communication. They were subsequently counselled on the need for six weeks postpartum visit and sixth week infant immunisation at the facility, so as to complete the study. The date for the sixth week visit were calculated according to their routine clinic days and written on the participant’s case questionnaire by the researcher for easy follow up. Participants were called to remind them of the date of their appointment.

Follow-up

Follow up continued up to six weeks postpartum. The participants were identified with the researcher’s contact written on their case notes. This enabled the team to contact the researcher. Some of the participants were seen at the under-five clinic for six weeks immunization. They were then assessed for postpartum depression using the self-administered Edinburg universal postpartum depression scale. Patients with postpartum depression were referred first to the managing Obstetrician, who assessed and decided whether to refer to the Neuropsychiatric team or to invite them for proper patient’s management.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequency tables. Comparison of the categorical variables were done using Chi-square test and Fischer’s exact where applicable. Logistic regression analysis was done to determine the effect of confounding variables on the study outcomes. Model 1 was adjusted for sociodemographic characteristic: age, occupation, religion, level of education, social class, gestational age at delivery, parity and family type. Model 2 was adjusted for delivery outcomes such as mode of delivery, birthweight, APGAR score, new born admission and duration of hospital stay. P-value of < 0.05 was taken to be statistically significant.

Results

One hundred and sixty four (164) participants that met the inclusion criteria were recruited, out of which 137 participants continued to the end of the study (giving a completion rate of 83.5%). Seven participants were noted to have symptoms of depression after initial screening and were managed, nine delivered elsewhere, while 11 participants were lost to follow-up at six weeks postpartum.

In Table 1 the mean ages of the participants in IPV group was 29.2 ± 5.4 years when compared with those with no IPV (30.4 ± 4.6 years), showed no statistically significant difference (P = 0.33). Most of them were aged 30–34 years. The employment status was significantly different in both groups, with the IPV victims more likely to be self-employed (P = 0.01). Significantly too, the victims belonged to the lower strata of socio-economic ladder in the society (P < 0.001). Again, IPV was significantly higher in participants with lower education (P < 0.001) and those in polygamous marriages (P = 0.01). The comparison of outcome measure in relation to PPD in Table 3 showed that physical violence, emotional violence and verbal abuse constituted significant risk to postpartum depression, while sexual violence was not statistically significant as a risk factor for postpartum depression (P<0.05). Also, birth weight, route of delivery, sex of the baby, APGAR scores in the fifth minute and duration of hospital stay were not statistically significant between the groups.

Logistic regression analysis showed that only emotional violence and verbal abuse were significantly associated with postpartum depression (P<0.05). Those who endured emotional violence were 56-times more likely to have postpartum depression than those that did not have it (RR=56.159, 95% C.I. of RR = 10.827-291.293). Also, those that had verbal abuse were 5.3 times more likely to have postpartum depression than those that did not (RR=5.305, 95% C.I. of RR = 1.525-18.463). Models 1 and 2 were adjusted for the sociodemographic and delivery outcomes.

Discussion

The prevalence of intimate partner violence from this study was 52.6%. This was higher than the prevalence reported worldwide from other studies including; 3-30% in Europe and Asia [2], 7-14% in other African countries [6], 7.4% in Northern Nigeria [16], 37.2% in Enugu, Northeast Nigeria [14] and from an earlier study 44.6% in Abakaliki [8]. It is instructive to note that the prevalence here is closer to those reported at Abakaliki and Enugu, and might be attributed to the similarity in the population’s socio-demographic characteristics. This could be explained by the high level of poverty and illiteracy, as those abused were mostly self-employed and in the low socio-economic class and these parameters were statistically significant. The studies at Enugu and Abakaliki also noted polygamy as a risk factor for intimate partner violence while other socio-demographic variables including age, religion, gestational age, parity and family type were not statistically significant. Other studies also reported similar findings [10].

The incidence of postpartum depression from this study was 29.9% of the participants studied and 56.9% among participants with intimate partner violence. These women with postpartum depression did not present for consultation even at the point of screening, possibly because postpartum women are hardly recognized due to good family support. Family members will always visit after childbirth as is customary in our environment. This 29.9% is higher than 10-20% reported in USA [18], but within the range reported in other countries such as 4.3-43.9% in Vietnam [10], 20.7-32.7% in Malaysia [7, 16], and 21.8% in Northern Nigeria [21, 22]. This is possibly because women in United States of America are more likely to report intimate partner violence and are also more likely to walk out of the relationship instead of developing depression. They are also more likely to seek mental health help early enough. Surprisingly, it is higher than the prevalence of 15.5% reported in Enugu by Ukaegbe et al possibly because women over there are more educated and in the higher socio-economic class than in our setting [23]. Postpartum depression was noted to be high in women with intimate partner violence. Similar findings were reported by other studies [14–17]. All the literatures reviewed showed a range of 2 to 5 folds increase in risk of postpartum depression in women with intimate partner violence. Delivery outcomes were not statistically significant among the participants that had postpartum depression when compared with women without postpartum depression. The mean Edinburg postnatal depression scores among the participants at recruitment was 8.51± 4.12688.

Physical violence, emotional violence and verbal abuse are risk factors for postpartum depression from Table 3. This is possibly because verbal abuse most times occur with emotional violence. But when regressed in Table 4, it was observed that emotional violence carries the greatest risk of PPD up to 56 times more than in those who did not have it, while verbal abuse increased the risk about 5.3 times more than those who were not exposed. Sexual violence was noted to increase the risk of postpartum depression up to 1.6 times, but this was not statistically significant while those with physical violence are less likely to have PPD. This is possibly because physical violence is usually followed by regret, apology and reconciliation and rarely causes PPD when it occurs in isolation. Other delivery outcome measures did not pose an additional risk for postpartum depression. The finding above about the relationship between physical violence and PPD was at variance with others who found that physical abuse posed more risk for PPD than verbal abuse [14–16]. It is important to note than in most cases verbal abuse precedes physical abuse and may more frequently occur than physical abuse or may co-exist. The perpetrators may also stop at the level of verbal abuse without going further. In our study there were more cases of verbal abuse than physical abuse. In this study also the dose dependent effect of IPV on PPD was not explored as in the other studies. These may account for the finding above. Causes of IPV reported by the participants interviewed were low social class, low income, wrong choice of partner, occupations that kept men out of home, economic dependence, keeping late night and alcohol intake as well as lack of love. However, one of the participants thought the cause in her own case was her husband’s personality or upbringing. This finding is similar to the causes identified by other studies including studies by Ezeudu et al [24] in Enugu and Onoh et al [8] in Abakaliki, South-east Nigeria which identified illiteracy and poverty as the main triggers of intimate partner violence [8, 23].

In an attempt to proffer possible solutions to IPV, the participants suggested that government should create policies that will protect women such as legislating punishments and/or fines for perpetrators, to serve as deterrent to the offenders. They suggested special rehabilitation programme for victims and enculturation of individuals from abusive homes prior to marriage, as well as public awareness/sensitization on dangers of IPV and most importantly, women empowerment. They advocated that churches teach against violence in marriage classes and parents should teach their sons how to care for women and not just teaching only the female children how to be good wives, as religion plays an important role in the upbringing of individuals in our environment. This may help since some studies identified that religion and family play key roles to intimate partner violence [8, 22–25]. Both families should call the couple to caution and reconcile them. The woman on her own side should report to the agency or institutions in charge to caution the man. They also posited that the woman should quit the marriage rather than sacrifice her life. In their words, “run for your dear life”. In our environment, conflict resolution measures include the families of both couple and instituted authorities. These are usually first utilized before exploring divorce. There are laws that have been enacted at the national level and adapted to the states to protect individuals exposed to intimate partner violence. There are also family courts that attend to these issues when they occur. All these are geared toward the protection of the women folk who bear the brunt of IPV and its sequelae.

Conclusion

Intimate partner violence is common as seen from the study. It is a significant risk factor for postpartum depression. Women that had emotional violence and verbal abuse are more likely to have postpartum depression. Most women see verbal abuse as normal and do not report it as violence. All the participants had some knowledge of intimate partner violence and were eager to talk about it. Screening pregnant women for intimate partner violence may identify those at risk of postpartum depression for proper interventions.

Recommendations

It is important that routine screening of antenatal women for intimate partner violence be incorporated into the practice protocol of OBGYN departments of institutions across the country and the world over, where this has not been implemented. The Society of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists of Nigeria (SOGON) can lead advocacy on the above. There should be close co-operation between relevant agencies saddled with IPV and all Health institutions to ensure seamless care for these women, improve perinatal and maternal outcomes, and ensure prosecution of perpetrators according to the laws of the land. Individuals who unfortunately are diagnosed with postpartum depression should be referred appropriately for treatment.

Limitations of the study

The study was hospital-based, and the findings may not be easily generalized. Reporting bias may also not be ruled out. The Edinburg postpartum depression scale used in this study is not the gold standard in assessing PPD.
