Regenerative agriculture practices and multidimensional poverty in eastern rural India
Authors: Sunil Khosla, Raja Rajendra Timilsina, Pradyot Ranjan Jena, Dil Bahadur Rahut
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRural households in developing countries face multidimensional poverty (MDP), i.e., deprivations beyond income, including health, education, empowerment, and living standards, while they are also being highly vulnerable to climatic risks. Regenerative agriculture (RA), a set of practices aimed at restoring soil health, enhancing biodiversity, and improving long-term farm resilience, has emerged as a promising strategy to boost productivity, diversify incomes, and promote sustainability. However, little is known about the impact of RA practices on MDP, and empirical evidence linking the two remains scarce. To this end, the present study examines the relationship between RA practices, such as crop rotation, agroforestry and crop diversification, and MDP reduction in eastern rural India. We administer household survey data from 917 households to construct an MDP index based on Alkire and Foster’s counting method and estimate the impact of RA adoption through Propensity Score Matching (PSM). The results show that RA practices significantly reduce MDP by improving access to education, healthcare, and living standards. These findings underscore the potential of RA as a pathway for sustainable rural development and call for targeted policy interventions to support its broader adoption.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-18461-5.Keywords: Regenerative agriculture practices (RA), Multidimensional poverty (MDP), Poverty alleviation, Sustainable agriculture, Environmental sustainability, Rural developmentSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Efforts to eradicate poverty in all its forms remain central to global development agendas, particularly under the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)1,2. The experience of overlapping deprivations across various dimensions, such as human well-being, health, education, and living standards beyond income poverty, is known as multidimensional poverty (MDP)3–5. This concept of MDP has been introduced to capture the broader reality of poverty faced by millions, particularly in developing countries, where systemic inequalities and limited access to essential services persist6–8. In recognition of this complexity, global development frameworks such as SDGs have emphasized the need for inclusive approaches that address poverty in all its forms. According to the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP), over 1.1 billion people in 112 countries currently live in MDP, with more than 83% residing in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia9. Within this global context, India stands out because it is home to the highest number of MDP individuals, approximately 234 million10,Government of India11. Overall, MDP in itself is one important issue that warrants further exploration and discussion.

Many developing countries have made great strides in poverty alleviation through targeted policies and social protection programs. India has also exerted great efforts in poverty reduction, but large segments of the rural population remain deprived across multiple dimensions10. These deprivations are deeply intertwined with rural livelihoods, particularly in agriculture, which continues to be the primary source of income for most rural households. Farmers, already among the poorest and most deprived groups, face increasing threats from climate-induced shocks such as erratic rainfall, prolonged droughts, and extreme weather events12. These climate risks not only undermine agricultural productivity but also exacerbate existing inequalities and deprivation, trapping households in cycles of vulnerability. Given this state of affairs, global attention has turned toward sustainable agricultural strategies that build resilience while enhancing productivity1,13,14. Among these strategies, regenerative agriculture (RA) that emphasizes crop rotation, agroforestry, and diversification has emerged as a promising model for restoring ecological balance and improving farmer well-being15–17. However, despite its growing popularity, no empirical evidence exists on whether regenerative agriculture (RA) can directly contribute to reducing MDP. This study addresses this gap by examining the relationship between RA practices and MDP outcomes among rural households in eastern India.

Poverty research over the past three decades has moved beyond income-based metrics to recognize the multidimensional nature of deprivation. Early studies often focused on monetary indicators and poverty lines, but this approach overlooked the broader well-being dimensions that define human development4,18–21. To address this gap, the Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI), introduced by Alkire and Foster3,4, provided a robust methodology for capturing deprivations in education, health, and living standards. This framework has since been widely applied to assess poverty dynamics across regions and populations. A growing body of empirical work has applied MDP frameworks in rural settings, where poverty is often most acute and intertwined with agricultural livelihoods. For instance, Azeem et al.18 compared alternative methods for measuring vulnerability to MDP and found that outcomes are highly sensitive to indicator selection and weighting. Fransman and Yu22, using data from South Africa, showed persistent multidimensional deprivation despite income growth, highlighting the limits of income-only approaches. In the Indian context, Khosla and Jena23 demonstrated that universal public works and food security programs significantly improved household wellbeing, particularly in rural regions. These studies highlight that addressing MDP requires interventions that go beyond financial transfers to target the root causes of multiple deprivations.

An emerging body of research now explores sustainable agricultural practices as it has gained attention for their potential to improve household well-being beyond income, particularly in the face of climate variability. Regenerative agriculture (RA) is one such approach, aiming to restore soil health, improve biodiversity, and enhance long-term farm productivity24. To be more precise, climate-smart agriculture (CSA) techniques are believed to be centered on the enhancement of production, the enhancement of resilience to climate change, and the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions. On the other hand, RA practices go farther by focusing on natural systems and nature-based solutions for the long-term ecological process. Studies across diverse contexts have highlighted RA’s positive impact on various household outcomes, including increased agricultural income, greater food security, and enhanced resilience to climate shocks25–29.

Despite the established benefits of RA, previous studies have not directly investigated the relationship between RA practices and MDP. The existing literature predominantly addresses CSA. However, there is little literature dealing with MDP and its determinants to eradicate, such as30 demonstrated that the adoption of CSA resulted in a 41% decrease in MDP among coastal farming households in Bangladesh. Ali et al.31 found that Ethiopian households implementing CSA had notably reduced MDP levels in comparison to non-adopters. In the case of RA practices, Belay et al.32 showed that households in Southern Ethiopia practicing RA reported higher incomes and better nutritional outcomes. Teklewold et al.28,33 demonstrated that diversified farming systems improved food security and nutritional adequacy in Ethiopia, while Danso-Abbeam and Baiyegunhi34 found that improved pesticide management under RA raised food consumption and productivity in Ghana.

These studies suggest a promising link, but the evidence remains sparse, particularly in the poor and climatic risky state of the Indian context. However, Ma and Rahut35 analyzed the adoption and impact of CSA practices and found that they contributed to income diversification, reduced input dependency, and enhanced household resilience to climate variability in the context of India. Kapoor and Pal36 emphasized that water-efficient practices in India improved yields and enhanced access to health and education by stabilizing household income. Arslan et al.37 found that improved seed use and crop rotation significantly enhanced maize yields in Zambia, while Jamil et al.13 reported that integrated irrigation and soil management increased cotton yields in Pakistan. Tanti et al.38 further demonstrated that crop diversification and agroforestry are positively associated with household wellbeing, particularly through improvements in food security and education. Feliciano1, in a global review, confirmed that crop diversification supports SDG 1 (No Poverty) by smoothing income flows and improving dietary diversity. Midingoyi et al.39 showed that integrated pest management not only raised Mango yields in Kenya but also contributed to improved health outcomes. Agroforestry systems that include fruit and nut trees increase access to nutrient-dense foods and reduce market dependency40,41. Egeru et al.42 found that these practices significantly improved food consumption scores and dietary diversity.

Indian agriculture is dominated by marginal and smallholder farmers, who may face unique barriers and opportunities in adopting RA practices and this gap is especially important to explore14,29. As RA is increasingly promoted by Indian policymakers to enhance food security and climate resilience, rigorous empirical evidence is important to understand its broader welfare implications. Thus, the present study contributes to this emerging literature by analyzing household-level data from 917 rural households in eastern India. Specifically, we examine the effects of three RA practices: crop rotation, agroforestry, and crop diversification on MDP. In doing so, we offer one of the first empirical assessments of the RA, MDP nexus in the Indian context, with implications for both academic research and rural development policy.

Conceptual framework: the nexus between SDGs, regenerative agriculture, and multidimensional poverty

The relationship between regenerative agriculture (RA) practices and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) has earned growing interest in development economics35,43. In particular, RA is increasingly recognized as a pathway for promoting environmental sustainability and agricultural resilience and reducing various forms of deprivation that extend beyond income. The conventional poverty assessments only focus on monetary metrics and often fail to capture household’ complex and overlapping disadvantages. The multidimensional poverty framework, pioneered by Alkire and Foster3, addresses this gap by incorporating indicators across education, health, and living standards, thus offering a consolidated approach to poverty assessment. Recent studies such as Khosla and Jena12 and Jena et al.44 have highlighted the importance of integrating MDP indices into development planning, particularly in rural and agriculturally dependent regions where deprivations are more acute.

Embedding MDP within agricultural and environmental research helps identify how interventions like RA can enhance human well-being. RA practices such as crop rotation, agroforestry, and crop diversification have been empirically linked to outcomes that align with the SDGs, including improved productivity, climate resilience, and ecological sustainability. Agroforestry, for instance, has been shown to improve access to clean energy and drinking water, two key dimensions of living standards14,30,31. It also reduces soil erosion, enhances water retention, and mitigates climate shocks1,45, while generating additional income through non-timber forest products and improving soil fertility and biodiversity40,41,43. Similarly, crop diversification boosts nutritional outcomes, health, and income by allowing farmers to grow a mix of staple and high-value crops1,32,43,46, also spreading risk and reducing susceptibility to climatic variability26. Crop rotation further contributes to income stability and long-term economic resilience47. Beyond these benefits through productivity gains RA practices support the development of human capital, such that stable farm incomes enable greater investment in education and reduce child labor33,48. Additionally, RA enhances nutrition and health through improved access to diverse, nutrient-rich food sources and reduced reliance on harmful agricultural inputs49–53.

Despite this evidence, no studies have explicitly investigated the link between RA practices and MDP. This study contributes to the emerging literature by empirically examining the role of RA practices in reducing MDP among smallholder farmers in eastern rural India. This focus is timely, as most Indian farmers are marginal or smallholders, and existing policies increasingly promote RA for both food security and environmental sustainability14,29. Using survey data from 917 rural households, we analyze the effects of three widely practiced RA methods crop rotation, agroforestry, and crop diversification on MDP.

Materials and methods

This section outlines the underlying theoretical concepts and frameworks guiding the study. It also details the research approach, including the methodological design, data collection strategies, and analytical tools employed to ensure rigorous and reliable results. The estimation process begins with the identification of MDP Indicators, which are categorized into three core dimensions: Education, Health, and Standard of Living (Fig. 2). These dimensions are foundational in the estimation of MDP, as they represent the key areas where deprivation might occur within a household. The data for these indicators is collected and summarized through descriptive statistics to understand the overall status of poverty in the study area. Following the collection and summary of the data, the next step involves the MDP estimation process. Here, the MDP indicators are used to calculate the level of MDP within households. This step sets the stage for examining the influence of external factors specifically RA practices on MDP levels. To assess the impact of RA practices on MDP, the PSM method is used to control for selection bias by matching households that have adopted RA practices with those that have not, based on observable characteristics. The final output of this analysis is presented as estimation results, which highlight the relationship between RA practices and MDP.

Analytical framework.

Study area

Odisha has 30 districts situated in the eastern region of India. The districts are categorised into three geographical divisions: southern Odisha has 12 districts, northern Odisha includes 9 districts, and coastal Odisha consists of 9 districts55. Coastal Odisha surpasses northern and southern Odisha in economic growth56. The coastal region of Odisha, where the state capital is situated, is well-connected to the rest of India by an extensive transportation network. Northern Odisha, situated near the border of Jharkhand and Bihar, hosts a limited number of enterprises, including an aluminium and steel plant. The southern region of Odisha is economically disadvantaged, inhabited predominantly by tribal populations. The state is globally recognised for its elevated poverty rate, with 35.7% of the rural population existing below the poverty line57. A majority (83%) of individuals reside in rural regions characterised by inadequate infrastructure, a low human development index, and a deficient education system. The poverty rate differs throughout the state, with the most impoverished districts being Kandhamal, Koraput, Malkangiri, Boudh, and Balangir57.

This study utilises comprehensive household survey data from 917 households in the Koraput district of Odisha. This district was purposely selected for the analysis. The district exhibits elevated levels of poverty, food insecurity, and unemployment23. The district ranks among the top five most extremely vulnerable districts58. The district was formerly part of the undivided Koraput districts, which included Rayagada, Nabarangpur, and Malkangiri, until it was designated as a separate district in October 1992. The district is bordered to the extreme North by Nabarangpur district, to the West by Bastar district of Chhattisgarh State, to the South by Malkangiri district, and to the East by the districts of Vizianagaram and Srikakulam in Andhra Pradesh State57. The district has a population of 1,379,647, comprising 2028 villages and 239 Gramme Panchayats, as reported in the 2011 Census for Koraput. The average literacy rate in Koraput district, according to the 2011 census, is 81.80%, with male literacy at 88.48% and female literacy at 74.90%. The economy of Koraput district encompasses crop and livestock production, formal employment, fishing, and the sale of wood and wild fruits. Agriculture serves as the primary economic activity, with approximately 83% of the population reliant on it. Figure 3 illustrates the geographical location of the study area.

Study area.

The average rainfall in Koraput district during the rainy season is 1567 mm. The district features three significant rivers: Kolab, Indravati, and Machkund, which are home to the Kolab reservoir, Muran dam, and Jalaput reservoir. The 2011 census indicates that 53.12% of households are classified as marginal farmers, 25.96% as small farmers, and 20.92% as big farmers55. The primary field crops in Koraput district include Paddy, Finger millet, Niger, Maize, and Arhar. The district faces significant risks from flooding, fire incidents, drought, heat-related illnesses, and adverse events.

Sample selection

A multistage sampling technique was utilized to gather household survey data. First, Koraput, was purposively selected from Odisha’s 30 districts. This selection was based on the pronounced poverty dynamics and vulnerability23,58. In the second stage, blocks were selected. The final stage involved the random selection of 917 farming households from the district. Sample representativeness was ensured by allocating household interviews proportionally based on village populations. The research team personally conducted the surveys using a thoroughly pre-tested questionnaire. The survey captured a wide range of demographic and socio-economic information, including gender, age, marital status, income, expenditure, and assets. It also assessed household livelihoods through access to social, physical, financial, human, and natural capital.

Analytical framework and econometric specification

This section explains the econometric specification for MDP estimation and impact evaluation approaches that are used for the analysis. We begin with a presentation of the counting approach used in the study.

Assessment of multidimensional poverty: counting approach

This study adopts the methodology pioneered by Alkire and Foster3,4 to assess MDP. The estimation follows a three-step process designed to capture the MDP beyond mere income measures.

Step One: The first phase involves the identification of primary dimensions of household well-being and the selection of appropriate indicators for these dimensions. In alignment with recent research12 (Jena et al. 2024), this study focuses on three core dimensions: education, health, and living standards. These dimensions are frequently emphasized in MDP poverty studies for their comprehensive reflection of household welfare. The choice of specific indicators within these dimensions, however, may vary across studies, influenced by the nature and availability of the data (see12). A detailed description of the dimensions and indicators employed in this study is presented in Table 1.

Step Two: The next step assigns weights to each dimension and its respective indicators, while also establishing cutoff points to determine deprivation within each indicator. Following standard practices in the literature, equal weights are assigned to the three core dimensions—education, health, and living standards—each receiving a weight of 1/3 (33.33%). Additionally, equal weights are assigned to the indicators within the dimensions. Within the education dimension, for instance, the indicators ‘years of schooling’ and ‘school attendance’ are each assigned a weight of 1/6. Similarly, the indicators within the health dimension are weighted at 1/6, and those within the living standards dimension are each weighted at 1/18 (see Table 1). This approach ensures a balanced representation of all aspects of well-being in the overall poverty measure.

Following the recent literature (see12, Jena et al. 2024), this study employs two critical cutoffs: the deprivation cutoff and the poverty cutoff. The deprivation cutoff is used to ascertain whether a household is deprived with respect to a specific indicator. The poverty cutoff, on the other hand, identifies the threshold number of deprivations across indicators that classify a household as multidimensionally poor. The total deprivation score for each household is computed as the weighted sum of its deprivations. Following the past studies framework, we adopt a poverty cutoff of 33%, whereby a household is deemed multidimensionally poor if its deprivation score exceeds this threshold. We have also conducted additional robustness checks using an alternative weighting scheme. Specifically, we applied a uniform cutoff point of 10 for all indicators, thereby equally distributing 100% weight across the 10 indicators, consistent with prior literature18.

Dimensions, indicators, deprivation cut-offs, and weights

This study employs a Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) grounded in the framework established by the 2014 Human Development Report by UNDP. Since household members generally share common resources and living conditions, this analysis measures deprivation and poverty at the household level. If a household is identified as deprived in a particular indicator, all members are considered deprived in that domain. Similarly, if a household is classified as multidimensionally poor, every member is deemed to share that poverty status3,4.

In the educational dimension, the indicators and deprivation thresholds are consistent with those used in the 2014 Human Development Report. A household is classified as deprived in years of schooling if no member has completed at least five years of education. Additionally, deprivation in child enrolment is identified if any child aged 6–14 in the household is not attending school at the primary level (grades 1–8) (Table 1). These indicators are widely recognized in the literature18 (Jena et al. 2024).

The health dimension presents unique challenges due to the variability in data availability across different contexts. As a result, studies often adopt different health indicators based on the data at hand. Common indicators include nutritional deficiency, child mortality, health insurance coverage, Body Mass Index (BMI) of children, and overall health functioning18 (Jena et al. 2024). This study specifically focuses on child mortality and health functioning. A household is considered deprived in child mortality if it has experienced the death of a child within the last five years. Deprivation in health status is identified if any household member suffers from a chronic illness or disability (Table 1). The thresholds for these indicators are aligned with those applied in other empirical studies22,60.

For the living standards dimension, the six indicators and their respective deprivation thresholds are also consistent with the 2014 Human Development Report. A household is considered deprived in cooking fuel if it primarily relies on dung, wood, or charcoal. Deprivation in sanitation occurs if the household lacks access to improved or private sanitation facilities. A household is considered deprived in drinking water if it sources its water from rivers or ponds. The absence of electricity defines energy deprivation. This study replaces the standard flooring indicator with a focus on housing conditions, utilizing more detailed survey data on housing types. A household is classified as deprived in housing if it resides in a kutcha or tiled house. Finally, asset deprivation is defined if a household owns no more than one of the following: radio, television, telephone, bicycle, motorbike, or refrigerator, and lacks ownership of a car or tractor. Each of the three dimensions education, health, and living standards is weighted equally at 33.33%, with equal weights assigned to indicators within each dimension (Table 1).

Propensity score matching (PSM) approach

In order to investigate the impact of RA practices on MDP households, we employed PSM, a method that has been widely used in the impact evaluation literature61 (Jena et al. 2024). In summary, PSM seeks to create a control group by selecting non-adopters who share the same observable features as adopters. The main challenge of a reliable impact assessment is to create a counterfactual outcome; that is, identifying what would have happened to the MDP households that practiced RA in the absence of RA practices. The cross-sectional data usually has non-experimental biases, such as selection bias62,63. PSM has been developed to help design and analyse non-randomized observational studies in order to mimic some of the features of a randomized control trial64.

The PSM corrects the selection bias caused by observables by matching a sub-sample who practiced RA with those who did not practice RA but have similar observable characteristics and by making comparisons in the region of common support65. Beta fit regression analysis

The outcome variable for this study ranges between 0 and 1, in accordance with the study’s objective. Beta fit regression was employed as a robustness check to evaluate our PSM results, with treatment variables including RA practices such as crop diversification, agroforestry, and crop rotation. Previous research has employed this method to verify the empirical findings66. Beta fit regression is utilized when the dependent variable represents a proportion or index restricted to the interval (0, 1). This framework offers a statistically valid and robust modeling approach that effectively addresses the unique distributional characteristics of bounded continuous data, which traditional linear models fail to manage adequately.

Results and discussion

Table 2 provides a detailed overview of the demographic, economic, and geographic characteristics of the 917 sampled households, offering critical context for evaluating the potential of RA practices to alleviate MDP. Household heads are predominantly male, with a mean age of 44.4 years and an average of just 3.7 years of formal schooling, suggesting limited educational attainment. Despite an average farming experience of 28 years, the sample reflects substantial variation in landownership (mean = 3.2 acres) and asset accumulation (mean = 2.9), pointing to pronounced economic disparities within the rural population. Household size averages 5.1 members, further underscoring the social and economic heterogeneity across the sample.

Geographic factors further highlight the challenges faced by rural households, particularly in terms of market and infrastructure accessibility. On average, households are located 9.3 km from the nearest market and 15.6 km from extension services, with substantial variability, as indicated by large standard deviations. These distances likely constrain access to key agricultural inputs and services, which are vital for the adoption of RA practices. Additionally, only 16.6% of households report awareness of crop insurance, signaling low engagement with risk management mechanisms critical for climate resilience. Together, these statistics reveal the multifaceted constraints households face, emphasizing the need for context-specific interventions to enhance the effectiveness of RA practices in reducing MDP in this region.

Figure 4 presents the distribution of households according to farming status, classified by landholding size, offering important insights into the structural composition of the agricultural sector in the study area. Most households are classified as marginal (34.24%) or small (33.81%) farmers, suggesting that approximately 68% of the sample manages small landholdings, generally under 2 hectares. This highlights the commonality of small-scale farming, typically marked by subsistence-level agriculture, restricted resource access, and increased susceptibility to climate shocks. Semi-medium farmers, possessing marginally larger landholdings, account for 25.85% of the sample, whereas medium and large farmers comprise only 5.46% and 0.65%, respectively. The low percentage of large farmers underscores the concentration of land in smaller plots, indicating that strategies to promote RA in this region should be specifically designed to address the needs of smallholders.

Large farmers exhibit a minimal MDP rate of 0.52%. The limited adaptive capacity of smaller farmers may contribute to their challenges in adopting RA practices, including barriers related to access to credit, technical knowledge, and climate-resilient inputs. Larger landholders gain advantages from economies of scale, increased resources, and improved access to RA technologies, enabling them to more effectively mitigate the effects of environmental and economic shocks.

The results from these approaches confirm that RA practices (crop diversification, crop rotation, and agroforestry) significantly reduce MDP. More specifically, these findings suggest that households that adopted RA practices have a lower level of MDP than their counterfactuals. The study used three matching methods, namely nearest neighbor, radius, and Kernel, for the robustness check, and the results from the matching methods show that all three estimators yield similar results (Table 3 and Supplementary Table S2). The propensity score graphs for three matching methods are shown in Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2.

The study checked the satisfaction of the balancing test, and the balancing tests show that the differences after matching are statistically insignificant, which is a desired property for a good matching algorithm and which shows that the results are reliable. After verifying that the treatment and control group are properly balanced and ensuring common support (Table S1), we estimated the impact as the average treatment effect on the treated. The PSM results imply that RA negatively and significantly impacts reducing MDP. More importantly, depending on the specific algorithm used for the estimated impact of RA practices on the MDP, it is estimated that the MDP decrease ranges are from 2 to 4% (Table 3 and Table S2).

Among the RA practices analyzed, crop rotation demonstrates the most pronounced impact, with an ATT of − 0.031 (t = − 2.16), reflecting a 3.1 percentage point reduction in MDP scores for adopters compared to non-adopters. This significant reduction underscores the ability of crop rotation to enhance agricultural productivity and environmental sustainability (Feliciano 2018). Crop rotation alleviates income poverty by improving soil health, stabilizing yields, and reducing dependence on chemical inputs while fostering better health and educational outcomes. Crop diversification follows closely, with an ATT of -0.028 (t = − 2.64), indicating a 2.8 percentage point reduction in MDP scores among adopters. The significant impact of diversification highlights its capacity to mitigate household vulnerabilities by spreading agricultural risks across multiple crops, enhancing dietary diversity, and stabilizing incomes43. By buffering against market fluctuations and climate shocks, crop diversification enables households to allocate resources toward critical areas such as education, healthcare, and infrastructure, leading to a holistic reduction in multidimensional deprivation. These findings validate the long-standing argument that diversified cropping systems strengthen resilience and generate co-benefits that directly enhance household well-being.

In the case of agroforestry, the result shows a statistically significant ATT of -0.021 (t = − 1.97), corresponding to a 2.1 percentage point reduction in MDP scores. The more modest impact of agroforestry likely reflects its longer gestation period, as benefits such as income from non-timber forest products, improved soil quality, and enhanced ecosystem services accrue over time. Despite this, agroforestry remains critical for fostering environmental resilience and economic diversification. By integrating trees into farming systems, agroforestry offers households a sustainable pathway to improve living standards through renewable resources such as timber, fruits, and fodder. Its long-term potential to address MDP underscores its importance as part of a comprehensive rural development strategy. Research conducted in eastern India for the use of diversification of higher-value crops on agricultural growth67 noted that the shift towards diversification of higher-value crops has been a significant factor contributing to the rapid growth of agriculture.

We also performed a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the results’ sensitivity to unobserved bias, utilising Rosenbaum’s bounding method (Table S3)64. This study examined the sensitivity analysis at the 0.1 scale margin. The sensitivity analysis results indicate that the treatment effect remains significant at a gamma level of 1.5. The lower bound is statistically significant at the 10% levels for all three RA practices: crop diversification, crop rotation, and agroforestry. The sensitivity analysis indicates that unobserved heterogeneity moderately affects the qualitative interpretation of our findings.

In the health domain, significant differences are observed for specific indicators. Child death rates demonstrate the most striking disparity among crop rotation adopters (3.5%) compared to non-adopters (9.0%), suggesting a strong link between this practice and improved child survival. This reduction could stem from enhanced household income stability, improved maternal health, and better food security facilitated by crop rotation (Feliciano 2018). Similarly, adopters of agroforestry and crop diversification report lower child death rates (2.5% and 2.7%, respectively) compared to non-adopters (3.0% and 3.2%), indicating that these practices may indirectly contribute to reducing child mortality through pathways like improved nutritional access and economic resilience43. Conversely, health functioning shows minimal differences, with crop rotation adopters having a deprivation rate of 18.3% compared to 19.6% for non-adopters. Agroforestry adopters, however, report a slightly higher deprivation rate (19.5%) than non-adopters (18.3%), possibly reflecting transitional challenges, such as labor-intensive demands during adoption periods.

Indicators related to education consistently favor RA adopters, suggesting improved access to schooling and reduced opportunity costs. Crop diversification shows the most significant improvement in years of schooling deprivation, with adopters exhibiting a rate of 16.0% compared to 20.8% for non-adopters. This result implies that income stabilization associated with crop diversification enables households to prioritize educational investments. Additionally, significant improvements in school attendance are observed among crop rotation and agroforestry adopters, with deprivation rates of 23.9% and 22.0%, respectively, compared to 32.1% and 27.7% for non-adopters. These findings suggest that the economic and food security benefits of adopting RA practices reduce school absenteeism, particularly in households previously constrained by financial instability or food insecurity43.

The standard of living indicators also reveal similar results. Deprivation in cooking fuel usage is significantly lower among crop rotation and crop diversification adopters, with 41.3% and 40.9%, respectively, compared to 49.5% and 48.6% among non-adopters. These reductions suggest that improved financial resources associated with RA practices enable households to invest in cleaner and more efficient energy sources. Housing deprivation rates also show slight improvements, particularly among agroforestry adopters, with a deprivation rate of 28.8% for non-adopters and 25.9% for adopters. Significant differences are also observed in sanitation deprivation, where crop diversification, crop rotation, and agroforestry practices show lower deprivations. Crop diversification adopters demonstrate the most substantial improvement, with a deprivation rate of 69.8% compared to 80.6% for non-adopters. This indicates that households adopting RA practices may have greater capacity to invest in sanitation infrastructure, reducing health risks and improving living conditions. However, indicators like drinking water and electricity reveal smaller or inconsistent differences. For example, electricity deprivation is notably lower among agroforestry adopters (2.1% compared to 4.1% for non-adopters), potentially reflecting its integration with renewable energy initiatives.

The study also performed a mechanism analysis utilising both theoretical and empirical literature to elucidate the impact of RA practices on MDP. The study examined intermediate outcomes such as household income, educational attainment, asset accumulation, and living standards. The findings presented in Table S4 indicate that RA practices significantly enhance these dimensions, thereby strengthening their contribution to alleviating MDP through various interconnected channels.

Conclusion and policy implications

The findings of this study underscore the significant role that RA practices can play in reducing multidimensional poverty (MDP) in rural farming communities in eastern rural India. By adopting practices such as crop diversification, crop rotation, and agroforestry, households in the region directly experience improvements in food security, income stability, and resilience to environmental shocks. Therefore, these practices significantly reduce deprivations in key dimensions of MDP, including health, education, and living standards, thus enhancing household well-being.

Although this study offers significant insights into the impacts of RA practices on MDP in eastern rural India, it also has limitations. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences from the findings, as it captures the impacts of RA practices at one point of time, neglecting the potential longitudinal effects. A longitudinal study could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the long-term impacts of RA practices on the alleviation of MDP. Secondly, the scope of the study is limited to the Koraput district, and as such, the findings may not be applicable to other regions characterised by distinct socio-economic and environmental conditions. Furthermore, although the PSM method effectively accounts for observable biases, there remains the possibility of unobservable variables that may affect both the adoption of RA practices and MDP outcomes. The study also used self-reported variables such as health functioning and adaptations, which may carry measurement biases. Further, future studies should examine heterogeneity in the RA–MDP nexus across agro-ecological zones, social groups, gender, and institutional support mechanisms such as women’s self-help groups. Finally, although we assert that RA practices reduce MDP, we also acknowledge the reduction of the MDP may be attributable to various economic advancements, including the implementation of social protection policies in the study area.

These findings highlight that investment to scale up RA practices enhances agricultural and environmental sustainability, improving household well-being and reducing MDP. Government and development agencies should enhance agricultural extension systems to deliver practical training and localised demonstrations on RA methods to maximise these benefits. The incorporation of resource augmentation practices, such as vermicomposting pits, water harvesting, and afforestation, into the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA) can enhance cost-effectiveness for the government. This approach will offer labor-based support while concurrently enhancing land productivity and food security. Most rural populations continue to rely on subsidised food grains for food security and frequently utilise the public distribution system. Therefore, the government should emphasise these practices within the public distribution system to effectively reach the maximum number of targeted individuals, as awareness is essential for addressing existing gaps. Furthermore, the government should establish value chains and premium markets for regenerative practices via cooperatives, Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs), and public procurement to improve these practices.
