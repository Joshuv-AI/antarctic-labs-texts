QuLF-CT
Authors: Gourav Modanwal, Rohan Dhamdhere, Amit Khera, James A de Lemos, Ronald Peshock, Jeffrey Browning, Parag Joshi, Ian Neeland, Sadeer Al-Kindi, Anant Madabhushi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Corresponding author

Hepatic steatosis (HS) is a common consequence of metabolic derangements related to obesity and insulin resistance. It is now the leading cause of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma in the United States. Liver fat is also linked to an increased risk of cardiac or pulmonary complications.1 Recent studies suggest that emerging therapies show promise in reducing liver fat.2 Therefore, estimating liver fat fraction through noninvasive imaging techniques can help detect HS and guide treatment and lifestyle interventions such as weight loss, exercise, and dietary changes to prevent further liver damage and its consequences. Liver biopsy is the gold standard for comprehensive liver assessment, while 1H-magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-MRS) has emerged as a noninvasive gold standard for quantifying liver fat content. However, the broader clinical adoption of these methods is hindered either by the invasiveness and associated risks of liver biopsy, or by limited accessibility and resources. Consequently, alternative approaches like liver attenuation and liver-to-spleen ratio on low-dose Computed Tomography (CT) are often used in clinical practice to identify the presence or absence of HS. Despite widespread use, these methods lack precision in quantifying liver fat fraction, focusing solely on intensity. In this work, we also leverage subvisual textural changes in the liver to develop and validate the model for the Quantification of Liver Fat fraction on cardiac CT (QuLF-CT) against 1H-MRS and liver biopsy, providing complementary insights into liver health for cardiovascular risk and lung cancer screening.

We analyzed images from 2 distinct cohorts: C1 comprised 744 participants from the Dallas Heart Study 2 (NCT00344903) while C2 included 159 participants from University Hospitals Cleveland Medical Center, Cleveland, Ohio (UHMC). C1 is a multiethnic (non-Hispanic Black: 350, non-Hispanic White: 259, Hispanic: 116, other: 14, unknown: 5), population-based study of adults (age 18-65 years, male: female = 269:475, age 49.36 ± 10.75 years) who underwent low-dose cardiac CT scan for coronary artery calcium scoring and 1H-MRS of the liver. Meanwhile, C2 comprised individuals who underwent both cardiac CT scan and liver biopsy (grade 0 = 38, grade 1 = 83, grade 2 = 22, grade 3 = 16) as part of their routine clinical care. UT Southwestern Medical Center and UHMC IRB approved this study, and all participants provided informed consent before enrollment. The liver was segmented using a pretrained U-Net model.3 We utilized the liver region of interest to extract 954 radiomics features, including shape features (2D and 3D), first-order features, and various texture features using PyRadiomics software package (version, 3.0.1).

A Boruta feature selection method was used to select top features, and a supervised multivariate regression (extra trees regressor) model (QuLF-CT) was trained and optimized using 10-fold cross-validation on the training set (N = 372) from cohort C1. QuLF-CT was subsequently validated on the remaining holdout set (N = 372) of C1 cohort as well as on an external holdout set (C2) with liver biopsy-based HS grades. The QuLF-CT model demonstrated a high correlation with 1H-MRS liver fat fraction on the holdout set (R2 = 0.81 [95% CI: 0.74-0.85]; P < 0·001) (Figure 1CI). The liver attenuation and liver-to-spleen ratio obtained with a previously developed deep-learning based hepatic fat assessment pipeline (DeHFt) 3 exhibited R2 of 0.68 and 0.46, respectively. QuLF-CT performed equitably among racial groups (non-Hispanic Black: 0.75 [95% CI: 0.63-0.83], non-Hispanic White: 0.8 [95% CI: 0.72-0.87], Hispanic: 0.8 [95% CI: 0.74-0.88]), and gender (males: 0.81 [95% CI: 0.73-0.85], females: 0.77 [95% CI: 0.66-0.84]). On the external holdout set (C2), the liver fat quantification using QuLF-CT was significantly different (P < 0.05, Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test) between grades of HS, except grade 1 vs grade 2) (Figure 1CII). This study demonstrates that combining selected shape, intensity, and texture radiomics features explains 81% of 1H-MRS liver fat fraction variation. These features captured the intensity and subvisual spatial alterations of fat-infiltrated liver tissue (Figure 1CIII). A SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) feature importance plot, reveals how selected features—especially wavelet-LL_firstorder_Median, a key indicator of lipid changes in CT alongside other texture features—contribute to liver fat estimates.

This study explores the potential of cardiac CT for noninvasive liver fat quantification, building on previous investigations.4,5 While traditional liver attenuation demonstrated modest performance (R2 = 0.68), our radiomics-based approach significantly enhances quantification by extracting subvisual textural features related to fatty infiltration. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to include a larger and ethnically diverse patient population, showcasing the potential clinical applicability of a CT radiomics for noninvasive liver fat quantification validated against 1H-MRS and liver biopsy. The ability to quantify liver fat noninvasively on routine low-dose CT scans could have important clinical implications. QuLF-CT could help identify patients with elevated liver fat who may benefit from interventions, without needing specialized magnetic resonance imaging or biopsy. It also allows longitudinal tracking of liver fat changes in response to lifestyle or medical therapies. As low-dose CT screening becomes more widespread for cardiovascular risk assessment and lung cancer screening, liver fat quantification using QuLF-CT could impart additional clinical information to guide prevention and management of fatty liver disease and related complications. The equitable performance of QuLF-CT across race and gender groups represents a significant stride toward achieving health equity. It drives fair diagnosis, improved cardiometabolic health, and fosters inclusivity by mitigating disparities in health care. With further validation on large multi-institutional cohorts, QuLF-CT has the potential to become a practical tool that extracts additional value from existing CT scans to improve cardiometabolic health.
