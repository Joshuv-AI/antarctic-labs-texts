Daidzein-betaine cocrystal alleviates high-energy and low-protein diet induced fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome in laying hens through gut-liver axis
Authors: Yanling Sun, Tiantian Wang, Yu Zhang, Ning Ma, Xinghua Zhao, Xin He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome (FLHS) severely endangers the health and productivity of laying hens, marked by hepatic steatosis and impaired lipid metabolism. Daidzein-betaine cocrystal improves lipid metabolism in the diet-induced obesity mice. We investigated the effects of daidzein-betaine cocrystal diet on production performance, egg quality, intestinal injury, cecal microbiota, the metabolites of intestinal microbiota and hepatic lipid metabolism in FLHS laying hens. A total of 108 twenty-eight-week-old laying hens were allocated to 6 treatments: the control group (normal diet), model group (high-energy and low-protein (HELP) diet), daidzein group (200 mg/kg daidzein + HELP diet), betaine group (184.62 mg/kg betaine + HELP diet), physical mixture group (384.62 mg/kg physical mixture + HELP diet) and daidzein-betaine cocrystal group (384.62 mg/kg daidzein-betaine cocrystal + HELP diet). The findings showed that intake of daidzein, betaine, physical mixture and daidzein-betaine cocrystal effectively alleviated the disordered lipid metabolism in laying hens fed a HELP diet. Moreover, daidzein-betaine cocrystal was best supplement in the improvement of the lipid metabolism. The daidzein-betaine cocrystal modulated the dysbiosis induced by HELP-diet through reducing the abundance of Desulfovibrio and increasing the abundance of Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group and Unclassified_f_Lachnospiraceae, whereas enhancing β-diversity of the gut microbiome, to restore equilibrium. Fecal metabolomics analysis showed that the indicators related to the 6 differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) of lipid metabolism were improved. Transcriptome analysis identified 12 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) related to lipid metabolism. DAMs and DEGs were mainly co-enriched in peroxisome proliferators-activated receptor (PPAR) signaling pathways. To sum up, our results showed that dietary daidzein-betaine cocrystal could alleviate hepatic metabolic disorders associated with FLHS in laying hens by regulating the "gut-liver axis". This highlights the significant potential of the daidzein-betaine cocrystal as a therapeutic agent for counteracting the impacts of a HELP diet.Keywords: Daidzein, Betaine, Fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome, Cocrystal, Laying hen

Introduction

Fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome (FLHS) is an economically significant disease in the poultry industry, predominantly affecting caged high-production laying hens. It is marked by liver hemorrhage, along with hepatomegaly, friability, paleness, and excessive hepatic fat deposition (Lv et al., 2024). FLHS-affected laying hens generally exhibit reduced egg production and a shortened laying peak, causing great economic losses to the egg industry (Sharma and Leishangthem, 2024). In 1998, Marshall was the first to propose the "gut-liver axis" concept, emphasizing the significance of gut microbiota status in liver diseases (Yan et al., 2023). Dysbiosis can compromise intestinal barrier function, enabling bacterial metabolites and endotoxins (e.g., lipopolysaccharide) to translocate to the liver, thereby contributing to the development of liver disorder (Di Vincenzo et al., 2024). Additionally, alterations in the metabolites produced by gut microorganisms may also contribute to the development of liver disease (Hsu and Schnabl, 2023). Dietary traditional Chinese medicines and their active components have been proven to improve liver function in chickens through different pathways of the "gut-liver axis"(Pan et al., 2025).

Daidzein (DAI) is a natural isoflavone compound found in leguminous plants such as Pueraria lobata, Trifolium pratense, and soybean (Fang et al., 2023). DAI can enhance fatty acid oxidation and suppress fatty acid synthesis by modulating the expression of liver genes linked to lipid metabolism, such as the estrogen-related receptor gene and adipocyte fatty acid-binding protein (Sakamoto et al., 2014; He et al., 2016; Kitamura et al., 2020). Betaine (BTN) occurs naturally in beets, goji berries, wheat germ, and grains (Dobrijević et al., 2023). BTN mainly engages in the methionine cycle by providing methyl groups, enhancing hepatic carnitine synthesis, and promoting fatty acid transport into mitochondria for β-oxidation, thus ameliorating lipid metabolism (Arumugam et al., 2021; Ba et al., 2024). Meanwhile, recent studies demonstrated that BTN could exert an indirect regulatory effect on hepatic lipid metabolism by modifying the composition and functional properties of the gut microbiota (Alqudah and Claesen, 2024). BTN may also strengthen intestinal barrier integrity, mitigate endotoxin translocation, and attenuate hepatic inflammation, thus facilitating the maintenance of normal lipid metabolism (Du et al., 2021).

Although the DAI and BTN have promising potential in regulating lipid metabolism, the synergistic effects of DAI and BTN have been scarcely explored. DAI is classified as biopharmaceutics classification system Class IV, exhibiting poor solubility (3.01 µg/mL at 35 °C in water) and permeability (Peff = 1.41), significantly limiting its absorption and efficacy in vivo (Waldmann et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2019; Bolus et al., 2020). BTN exhibits high hygroscopicity, with a strong tendency to convert into betaine monohydrate, thereby posing great challenges for its industrial production and storage (Nunes et al., 2004). The daidzein-betaine cocrystal (DAI-BTN) is a new pre-formulation prepared by our laboratory using crystal engineering strategy. As a novel pharmaceutical preparation technology, it provides a new approach to address these inherent shortcomings (Aitipamula and Bolla, 2024; Meng et al., 2024). Cocrystals incorporating two therapeutic agents represent a widely utilized and effective strategy for development into fixed-dose combination products (Thipparaboina et al., 2016). The fixed-dose combination products demonstrate significant advantages in terms of cost-efficiency, safety, multi-targeted therapy, and drug stability (Frantz, 2006). Our previous studies had successfully prepared the DAI-BTN cocrystal with a 1:2 molar ratio via cocrystallization (Fig. 1). These studies had shown that the DAI-BTN cocrystal could improve the inherent defects of daidzein and betaine, and had preliminarily confirmed its potential in regulating lipid metabolism(Sun et al., 2025). However, whether the DAI-BTN cocrystal can alleviate high-energy and low-protein (HELP)-diet laying hens remains unclear.

In the present study, laying hens with the HELP diet-induced FLHS were used to examine the impact of the DAI-BTN cocrystal, and to uncover its potential mechanisms for regulating lipid metabolism based on the "gut-liver axis".

Materials and methods

Animals

A total of 108 laying hens (aged 28 weeks and weighted 1.97 ± 0.07 kg) of Peking Pink were purchased from Huanshan Agricultural Development Co., Ltd. (Hebei, China). All laying hens were caged in enclosures (Height 0.45 m × Width 0.4 m × Depth 0.4 m) and maintained under standard feeding conditions: temperature 22 ± 2°C and a 16/8 h light/dark cycle. Animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Ethical Committee of Hebei Agricultural University (number: 2024121). After a one-week acclimatization period, the formal experiment was carried out.

Experimental design

DAI (purity > 90%) was obtained from Biopurify Phytochemicals Ltd (Sichuan, China) and anhydrous BTN (purity > 90%) was obtained from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The preparation methods of physical mixture (PM) and DAI-BTN cocrystal were previously described in our previous literature (Sun et al., 2025). A total of 108 laying hens were divided into six groups (6 replicates per group and 3 hens per replicate). The control (ND) group received a standard diet and the model (HL) group was fed a HELP diet (Miao et al., 2024). Other four groups received HELP diet supplemented with 200 mg/kg DAI(Lu et al., 2017), 184.62 mg/kg BTN, 384.62 mg/kg PM and 384.62 mg/kg DAI-BTN, respectively. All diets were formulated to meet the NY/T33.2004 standards for laying hens (Hu et al., 2024) with detailed formulation presented in Table 1. The induction of FLHS via the HELP diet has been confirmed in a recent study(Zhang et al., 2025).

Measurement of serum analysis

In accordance with the manufacturer's instructions, commercial kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Jiangsu, China) were employed to measure the TG, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, ALT and AST in serum. The contents of diamine oxidase (DAO, JLT0829) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS, JL52811) in serum were measured by ELISA kits (Jianglai industrial Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Histopathological analysis

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining of the jejunum and liver and Oil Red O (ORO) staining of liver were used to evaluate the tissue morphology following established protocols (Chen et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024). Briefly, intestinal sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by embedding, sectioning, and staining. For liver tissues, 5 μm slices were prepared by frozen sectioning and stained with ORO. Subsequently, images of jejunum and liver morphologies were captured. Metrics such as villus height and crypt depth were measured and quantified using Case Viewer software (version 2.4). Samples were stained with Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining to count goblet cells, as previously described (Hou et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The RT-qPCR were performed referring to standard protocols of previous article(Peng et al., 2024; Qiao et al., 2024). Approximately 50 mg of jejunum tissue was taken for total RNA extraction (BFMP08M, Bigflybio Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China), and the extracted total RNA was then used to carry out reverse transcription following the instructions of the cDNA synthesis kit (RR092A, Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, China). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on the Applied Bio-Rad Real-Time PCR System with the SYBR Green Master Mix (CN830A, Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, China). Target gene mRNA expression was normalized to β-actin via the comparative 2-△△Ct method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). Primer sequences for qRT-PCR are provided in Table S1.

Gut microbiota analysis

Cecal microbial community DNA was extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The V3-V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified with primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). Amplicons were then purified and sequenced on an Illumina Nextseq2000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA) per standard protocols from Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The sequencing results were analyzed using the Majorbio Bio Cloud Platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com). Specifically, the mothur software (http://www.mothur.org/wiki/Calculators) was used to calculate Alpha diversity. Beta diversity was analyzed via PCoA (Principal Coordinates Analysis) based on the Bray-Curtis distance algorithm. Meanwhile, PERMANOVA (Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance) non-parametric test was conducted to determine whether the differences in microbial community structure between sample groups were significant. Additionally, Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis (http://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/LEfSe) was performed to identify bacterial taxa with significantly different abundances from phylum to genus levels among different groups, with the criteria of LDA > 2 and P < 0.05.

Untargeted metabolomic analysis for cecum contents

Cecal sample extraction was performed as the previous method (Geng et al., 2018). The extracted samples were analyzed on a Thermo UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system equipped with an ACQUITY HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm; Waters) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd., (Shanghai, China). Raw peak extraction, database filtering, baseline calibration, peak alignment, deconvolution, peak identification, and area integration were done with Progenesis QI software (Waters, Milford). Metabolites were identified via the Majorbio Database, and the resulting data matrix was uploaded to Majorbio Cloud (https://cloud.majorbio.com) for comprehensive analysis.

Next, orthogonal least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was conducted using the R package “ropls” (Version 1.6.2), with a seven-cycle interactive validation to assess model stability. Differential metabolites were defined as those with Variable Importance in the Projection (VIP) > 2 and P < 0.05 (via OPLS-DA and Student's t-test). Metabolic enrichment and pathway analysis based on the KEGG database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) was used to map differential metabolites between ND vs HL groups and HL vs DB groups to their biochemical pathways.

Hepatic transcriptomic sequencing analysis

Transcriptomic sequencing analysis of liver tissue was performed as the previous method(Zheng et al., 2024). Briefly, RNA extraction, reverse transcription, library construction and sequencing were done by Shanghai Majorbio Co., Ltd. Differential expression genes (DEGs) with log2FC| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05 (DESeq2) were significantly DEGs. In addition, KEGG functional enrichment analysis was performed to identify DEGs significantly enriched in metabolic pathways (Bonferroni-corrected P-value < 0.05). KEGG pathway analysis was carried out by Python SciPy software. Spearman's rank analysis was performed on the blood lipid indicators, gut microbiomes, DAMs and DEGs (|r| > 0.6, P < 0.05).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 19.0.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD test was used to assess significant differences among multiple groups, with P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

As Table 2 shows, the HL group had a significantly lower laying rate (P < 0.05) but a higher feed conversion ratio (P < 0.05) compared with the ND group. After DAI, BTN, PM and DB treatment, compared with the HL group, the laying rate was enhanced and the feed conversion ratio was reduced. Among them, the DB group had the best effect (P < 0.05).

DB supplementation alleviates liver lipid accumulation and liver damage in FLHS laying hens

A set of experiments was carried out to assess the therapeutic potential of DB (Fig. 2A). As shown in Fig. 2B, the liver index in the DAI, BTN, PM and DB groups were significantly decreased (P < 0.05), and there was no significant difference between the DB group and the ND group (P > 0.05). H&E staining of liver and phenotypic images showed that the laying hens in the HL group had enlarged, yellowish livers with abundant fat vacuoles and inflammatory cell infiltration, signs of hepatic steatosis. Lipid accumulation in ORO staining further confirmed this. It is worth noting that the DAI, BTN, PM and DB treatment improved the condition of hepatic steatosis (Fig. 2C).

Compared with the HL group, the DB group significantly reduced the contents of ALT and AST (P < 0.05), while the decreases in the laying hens within the DAI, BTN, and PM groups were not statistically significant (P > 0.05, Fig. 2D-E). As shown in Figs. 2F-K, compared to HL group, the concentrations of TG and TC in the DAI, BTN, PM and DB groups were all reduced, and the concentrations of DB group were the lowest (P < 0.05). In addition, the addition of the four drugs in the feed led to a decrease in LDL-C levels (P < 0.05) and an increase in HDL-C levels. Compared with the HL group, the HDL-C level of the DB group was significantly increased (P < 0.05). Thus, DB synergistically regulates lipid metabolism in laying hens.

DB ameliorated the impairment of intestinal morphology and barrier function in FLHS laying hens

The histological morphology was depicted in Fig. 3A. The results reveal that the ND group presented a normal appearance, characterized by regular, intact and closely packed intestinal villi and crypts, along with an intact mucosal structure. In the HL group, the intestinal villi exhibited breakage and shedding, and the crypts were irregular in shape with a reduced number. The intervention groups repaired the morphology of the intestinal tissue to varying degrees. Among them, the villus length and crypt depth of the PM and DB groups were significantly increased, and the DB group demonstrated a more pronounced effect (P < 0.05, Fig. 3B-C). In addition, compared with the HL group, DAI, BTN, PM, and DB treatments significantly increased the count of PAS+ goblet cells in the ileum of laying hens (Fig. 3D). The mRNA expression of Zonula Occludens-1 (ZO-1), Claudin-1 (CLDN1) and Occludin (OCLN) of intervention groups was upregulated in the jejunum to different extents (Fig. 3E-G). Moreover, DB group was superior to other groups in upregulating the mRNA expression of the genes. DB reduced intestinal permeability more effectively than the DAI, BTN, and PM groups compared to the HL group (Figs. 3H-I). This reduction was reflected in lower serum DAO activity and LPS levels (P < 0.05). All results showed that more positive repair effect on intestinal injury caused by the HELP diet is exerted by DB.

DB reshaped the gut microbiota in FLHS laying hens

No significant differences were observed in α diversity among the intervention groups (P > 0.05, Fig. 4A-D). In addition, the unweighted UniFrac PCoA plot showed obvious separation among the ND, HL and DB groups, indicating structural changes of gut microbiota after DB supplement (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, the impacts of DB on the gut microbiota composition were investigated. As shown in Fig. 4F, Bacteroidota and Bacillota were overwhelmingly dominant, accounting for approximately 90% at phylum level. They were followed by the phylum of unclassified_k_norank_d_Bacteria and Thermodesulfobacteriota. At the genus level, it was mainly composed of Bacteroides, Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, unclassified_o_Bacteroidales, and Mediterraneibacter, which together account for about 50%. The HELP diet led to an increase in the relative abundance of Desulfovibrio, while the relative abundances of Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, Unclassified_f_Lachnospiraceae and Unclassified_k_norank_d_Bacteria were decreased (Fig. 4H-K). However, this trend was alleviated by DB supplement. The distribution of OTUs in each group was depicted in Fig. 4L. The result showed that 1008 overlapping OTUs were identified across ND, HL and DB group. Subsequently, LEfSe analysis (with an LDA > 2) was employed to identify the biomarkers with significant differences among ND, HL and DB group on genus level. As shown in Fig. 4M, 10 taxa biomarkers in the ND, HL and DB group were identified. 6 taxa biomarkers were identified in the ND group and 4 taxa biomarkers were identified in the DB group. This indicated that g__Frisingicoccus, g__Lachnoclostridium, g__Anaerobutyricum and g__Succinatimonas may be the key bacteria.

DB altered the caecal metabolome induced in FLHS laying hens

To gain in-depth insights into the regulatory effect of DB on the caecal metabolic profile, an untargeted metabolomics study was further carried out. After raw data processing, 5975 features were detected in both positive and negative ion modes for all samples. The features in QC samples showed correlation coefficients (R²) of 0.758 and 0.732, with relative standard deviations (RSD) < 0.3.

PCA score plots revealed clear separation and clustering of samples between the ND and HL groups in both positive and negative ion modes (Figs. 5A-B). Notably, the metabolic profile of the DB group was altered to some extent, bringing it closer to that of the ND group. OPLS-DA identified key differential metabolites between the ND and HL groups as well as between the HL and DB groups, categorizing the metabolites into two groups (Figs. 5C-F, Figure S1). A volcano plot was generated based on VIP values. Compared to the ND group, the HL group had 94 upregulated and 47 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 5E). Similarly, the DB group showed 52 significantly upregulated and 84 downregulated metabolites compared to the HL group (P < 0.05, Fig. 5F). To explore the metabolic pathways involved in the regulation of these metabolites by the DB diet, we conducted KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. Compared with the ND group, the metabolic pathways that were significantly altered by the HELP diet included Linoleic acid metabolism, Sphingolipid metabolism, Primary bile acid biosynthesis, and Phenylalanine metabolism. Compared with the HL group, the pathways that were significantly regulated after the DB diet treatment included Nucleotide metabolism (P = 0.0023), Pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis (P = 0.0083), beta-Alanine metabolism (P = 0.0094), Primary bile acid biosynthesis (P = 0.020) and PPAR signaling pathway (P = 0.0231, Fig. 5I). Six lipid metabolism-related differential metabolites (Estrone Glucuronide, 4α-Carboxy-5α-Cholesta-8,24-Dien-3β-Ol, Testosterone, Adrenic Acid, Aldosterone, and 13(S)-Hode) were significantly elevated.

Transcriptome analysis revealed the effects of DB on the expression of lipid-related gene

PCA showed that the variance among the three groups accounted for 22.38%, 11.24%, and 9.20% for PC1, PC2, and PC3, respectively, indicating significant differences in gene expression levels among the groups (Fig. 6A). The number of uniquely expressed genes was 373 in the ND group, 191 in the HL group, and 103 in the DB group (Fig. 6B). The volcano plot of DEGs revealed that 23 genes were significantly upregulated and 42 genes downregulated in the HL group compared to the ND group (Figure S3). Subsequently, 282 upregulated and 273 downregulated genes of the DB group were identified compared to those in the HL group (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, the KEGG analysis demonstrated that the DB supplement influenced the lipid metabolism pathways (fatty acid elongation, fatty acid degradation, glycerolipid metabolism), amino acid metabolism pathways (tryptophan metabolism, lysine degradation, beta-alanine metabolism), as well as the signaling pathways related to intestinal barrier and liver functions in laying hens (Fig. 6D). It was worth noting that there were 14 genes associated with lipid metabolism (Fig. 6E), which were primarily involved in primary bile acid biosynthesis (STOML2, ACAA2), sphingolipid metabolism (SMPD3, B4GALT7), fatty acid elongation (THEM4), steroid biosynthesis (FAXDC2), fatty acid degradation (ALDH6A1, CDYL2, GCDH), arachidonic acid metabolism (PTGES3L), and glycerolipid metabolism (LPGAT1, LIPH) (Fig. 6F-L).

Roles of the microbiota-gut‑liver axis

Based on significant correlations, potential key factors related to lipid metabolism disorders were identified, including 7 liver lipid-related indicators, 12 DEGs, 6 DAMs, and 4 gut microbiota (Fig. 7A-D). Further KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the pathways enriched for DAMs and DEGs included the PPAR signaling pathway, tryptophan metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, beta-alanine metabolism, primary bile acid biosynthesis, cysteine and methionine metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, and ABC transporters (Fig. 7E). Based on these functions and significant correlations, we deduced the underlying mechanism of lipid metabolism regulation in DB-fed laying hens (Fig. 7F). DB diet enriched beneficial bacteria and reduced harmful bacteria in cecum of the laying hens, thereby improving intestinal structure and alleviating intestinal barrier dysfunction. Meanwhile, the produced metabolites are transported to the liver through the bloodstream and act as ligands to activate PPARγ, regulate the expression of genes related to lipid metabolism (ACAA2, LXR, FABP4) in the liver, resulting in the breakdown of lipids.

Discussion

FLHS is closely linked to reduced egg production in laying hens, leading to substantial economic losses for the poultry sector (Gao et al., 2019). Thus, the formulation of therapeutic strategies to prevent these adverse outcomes is important. Notably, the HELP diet has been connected to a higher incidence of FLHS (Rozenboim et al., 2016). As an alternative and complementary therapeutic approach, active ingredients derived from traditional Chinese medicine have been reported to exert a vital impact on regulating lipid metabolism and ameliorating metabolic disorders in laying hens triggered by a HELP diet (Lv et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Among potential bioactive compounds, DAI and BTN are two natural compounds that hold potential for regulating lipid metabolism. For instance, Liu et al. showed that adding 30 mg/kg DAI to the diet of 385-day-old laying hens led to a significant reduction in serum MDA, TC, and TG levels, thereby demonstrating DAI's ability to exert both antioxidant effects and lipid metabolism-regulating functions (Liu et al., 2023a). Supplementing the diet of laying hens with 8 g/kg betaine significantly relieved the dexamethasone-induced elevation in total bilirubin, total bile acids, and TC levels. Transcriptomic results showed that lipid metabolism-related genes (ACC, FAS, SCD1, ELOVL6, MTTP) were significantly upregulated at the mRNA level (Wang et al., 2024a).

A wealth of research has shown that metabolic balance is mainly regulated by the gut microbiota via three key pathways: modifying microbial composition, regulating microbial metabolites, and strengthening intestinal barrier function (Fan and Pedersen, 2021). Additionally, previous reports have also found that the two drugs are more effective than a single drug alone in improving overweight by targeting the gut microbiota(Zhu et al., 2021). Actually, In our previous study, the results of the regulation of gut microbiota in obese mice by DAI, BTN, PM, and DAI-BTN cocrystal showed that, compared with the other groups, the DB group synergistically promoted the growth of Proteobacteria and Akkermansia and inhibited the growth of Desulfovibrio and Flexispira(Sun et al., 2025). Consistent with these earlier observations, DB significantly altered the structure of the intestinal microbiota in laying hens. Specifically, DB increased the abundance of beneficial bacteria (Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, Unclassified_f_Lachnospiraceae) while reducing the abundance of harmful bacteria (Desulfovibrio). as a member of the Firmicutes phylum, the Lachnospiraceae family has been documented to convert cholesterol into coprostanol, thereby reducing total cholesterol levels (Vacca et al., 2020). Moreover, other studies have revealed a positive correlation between the relative abundance of Lachnospiraceae and the proliferation of multiple butyrate-producing bacteria (Telle-Hansen et al., 2022). Butyrate, a type of short-chain fatty acid, is capable of upregulating the transcriptional expression of colonic mucins (MUC3, MUC5B, MUC2, and MUC5AC) by inhibiting histone deacetylase (Gaudier et al., 2004). It also helped maintain balance the overgrowth and differentiation of crypts, thus safeguarding intestinal health (Li et al., 2025). Disruption of the intestinal barrier can result in intestinal absorption disorders, and consistent with this, the present study observed disrupted intestinal structural integrity in laying hens receiving a HELP diet(Zheng et al., 2024). HELP treatment induced villous atrophy and rupture through reducing the depth of crypt epithelial cells. Additionally, significant changes in the expression of tight junction and adherens junction-related molecules, such as OCLN, CLDN1, and ZO-1, were observed. Villous atrophy and tight junction protein loss were regarded as factors that increased intestinal permeability (Jeurissen et al., 2002). As expected, in this study, it was found that the levels of intestinal permeability markers (e.g., DAO and LPS) were increased in HELP-diet laying hens. It was noted that these abnormalities were significantly reversed by DB intervention. Given that 16S rDNA sequencing results showed that DB significantly altered the gut microbiota structure in laying hens, increasing the abundance of Unclassified_f_Lachnospiraceae. Therefore, we speculate that Unclassified_f_Lachnospiraceae may be a type of butyrice-producing bacteria that reduces intestinal permeability and increases villus length and crypt depth by enhancing the function of mucin.

Desulfovibrio is an opportunistic pathogen known to produce LPS endotoxin, which triggers the release of inflammatory cytokines from various host cells, thereby inducing inflammatory responses (Zhou et al., 2024). Research indicated that intestinal LPS levels in obese patients were higher than in healthy individuals. Similarly, our results demonstrated that the addition of DB supplementation reduce the serum LPS levels in HELP diet-fed laying hens by decreasing the abundance of Desulfovibrio in the gut microbiota. Additionally, changes in the intestinal barrier's integrity and functionality were viewed as early events affecting the progression of chronic liver disease (Liu et al., 2023b) and high levels of Desulfovibrio disrupt the integrity of intestinal epithelial cells and damage the intestinal barrier. (Singh et al., 2023). A prior study demonstrated that Desulfovibrio heightened the barrier permeability of polarized intestinal Caco-2 cells by disrupting the localization of OCLN (Singh et al., 2022), and this effect was mediated by Desulfovibrio through upregulating the Snail1 transcription factor. Our results showed that the relative abundances of Unclassified_f_Lachnospiraceae and Desulfovibrio in the DB group were regulated, which reversed the imbalance of gut microbiota and thereby synergistically maintained a complete intestinal structure. Furthermore, earlier research showed a correlation between Desulfovibrio dysregulation and fatty acid levels, indicating that adjusting the amount of Desulfovibrio could be a potential approach to treating lipid metabolism disorders (Lin et al., 2022).

The abundance of Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, which belongs to the Bacteroidetes phylum was enhanced in the DB group compared to those of the HL group. Moreover, the abundance of Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group was significantly negatively correlated with the levels of the serum lipid parameters. The Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group is thought to contribute to gut homeostasis as a potential SCFA-producing bacterium (Teng et al., 2023). In a word, our study highlights the potential roles of Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, Unclassified_f_Lachnospiraceae, and Desulfovibrio in the cecal microbiome of laying hens in alleviating lipid metabolism disturbances are highlighted in our study.

Subsequently, the specific mechanisms of intestinal-liver crosstalk mediated by changes in beneficial and harmful bacteria in the gut were explored using untargeted metabolomics and transcriptomics. 13(S)-HODE is the primary metabolite generated when 15-lipoxygenase-1 metabolizes linoleic acid. As an endogenous ligand, it has the ability to activate PPARγ(Zuo et al., 2006; Yuan et al., 2010). Our study confirmed that HELP diet decreased 13(S)-HODE and DB supplementation significantly increased 13(S)-HODE in gut of laying hens. PPARs belong to the nuclear hormone receptor superfamily, which includes PPARα, PPARβ/δ, and PPARγ. These receptors act as indicators of fatty acids and fatty acid derivatives, and play a crucial role in lipid and energy metabolism processes (Li et al., 2018). In particular, PPARγ was mainly expressed in adipose tissue, liver, skeletal muscle, gut, and immune cells, where it regulated adipogenesis, insulin sensitivity, and anti-inflammatory responses (Hernandez-Quiles et al., 2021). A study indicated that FABP4, the most distinctive member of the FABP family, was specifically expressed in adipose cells and macrophages (Wang and Wu, 2025). In terms of lipogenesis, PPARγ promoted an increase in hepatic TG by facilitating FABP4-mediated free fatty acid uptake, which was consistent with our results (Chen et al., 2023). ACAA2 encoded acetyl-CoA transferase, which catalyzed the final step of the mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation spiral: the formation of acetyl-CoA, which could enter the TCA cycle for ATP production (Dunning et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2016). This process decreased lipogenesis and triglyceride levels, thus mitigating liver damage. In this study, notable reductions in liver indices (AST, ALT, and TG levels) were seen with DB supplementation. Moreover, a significant reduction in hepatic steatosis and lipid droplet formation was demonstrated with DB supplementation in H&E and ORO staining. In addition, cholesterol metabolism was also tightly regulated by certain feedback mechanisms, such as SREBP2 and LXR (Sussy et al., 2016). PPARγ is an upstream factor in LXRs-induced signaling and played a crucial role in regulating hepatic cholesterol homeostasis (Han et al., 2019). In a word, we proposed that PPARγ and target genes (ACAA2, FABP4, LXR) could may potentially serve as a novel therapeutic target for FLHS.

Conclusion

The present study explained the mitigating effect and underlying mechanism of DB on HELP-diet induced hepatic lipid metabolism disorder based on “gut-liver axis”. Specifically, diary DB supplementation changed the abundance and diversity of the gut microbiota and increased the production of 13(S)-HODE. Further analysis revealed that it regulated the expression of key genes, including PPARγ, ACAA2, FABP4 and LXR, thereby alleviating hepatic damage of FLHS laying hens. In conclusion, this study identifies DB as a functional feed additive, and provides a novel strategy for preventing liver diseases in poultry.
