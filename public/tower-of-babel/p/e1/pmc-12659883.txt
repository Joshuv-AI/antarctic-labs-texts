The auxin gatekeepers: Evolution and diversification of the YUCCA family
Authors: Mallika Vijayanathan, Amna Faryad, Thanusha D Abeywickrama, Joachim Møller Christensen, Elizabeth H Jakobsen Neilson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThe critically important YUCCA (YUC) gene family is highly conserved and specific to the plant kingdom, primarily responsible for the final and rate‐limiting step for indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA) biosynthesis. IAA is an essential phytohormone, involved in virtually all aspects of plant growth and development. In addition, IAA is involved in fine‐tuning plant responses to biotic and abiotic interactions and stresses. While the YUC gene family has significantly expanded throughout the plant kingdom, a detailed analysis of the evolutionary patterns driving this diversification has not been performed. Here, we present a comprehensive phylogenetic analysis of the YUC family, combining YUCs from species representing key evolutionary plant lineages. The evolutionary history of YUCs is complex and suggests multiple recruitment events via horizontal gene transfer from bacteria. We identify and hierarchically classify the YUC family into an early diverging grade, five distinct classes and 41 subclasses. Angiosperm YUC diversity and expansion are explained in the context of protein sequence conservation, as well as spatial and gene expression patterns. The presented YUC gene landscape offers new perspectives on the distribution and evolutionary trends of this crucial family, which facilitates further YUC characterization within plant development and response to environmental change.Keywords: auxin, evolution, flavin‐containing monooxygenase, FMO, YUCCA

INTRODUCTION

Auxins are essential phytohormones that orchestrate almost every facet of plant growth and development (Friml, 2022; Sauer et al., 2013; Zhao, 2010) such as cell elongation, vascular bundle differentiation, embryonic polarization, phototrophic and gravity response, and apical dominance (Biedroń & Banasiak, 2018; Han et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022; Muday, 2001; Mueller‐Roeber & Balazadeh, 2014; Pan et al., 2015). In addition, auxins are critically important in how plants respond and survive in response to biotic and abiotic stresses (Cohen & Strader, 2024; Korver et al., 2018; Salehin, 2024). At least five endogenous plant auxins are reported (Figure 1A) (Ludwig‐Müller, 2020; Simon & Petrášek, 2011). Tryptophan (Trp)‐derived indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA) is the most established of the auxins. In plants, IAA is widely and interchangeably referred to as “auxin,” a term derived from the Greek word “auxein,” meaning “to grow/increase” (Kögl & Haagen‐Smit, 1931). IAA is also present in algae, bacteria, fungi, and animals and considered an important cross‐kingdom signaling molecule (Cheng et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2015; Miyata et al., 2021; Morffy & Strader, 2020; Takei et al., 2019). Other auxins include 4‐chloroindole‐3‐acetic acid (4‐Cl‐IAA), a bioactive form present in many Fabaceae species (Reinecke, 1999; Tivendale et al., 2012), indole‐3‐butyric acid (IBA), and indole‐3‐propionic acid (IPPA), as well as the Phenylalanine (Phe)‐derived auxin phenylacetic acid (PAA) (Cook et al., 2016; Sugawara et al., 2015). A tyrosine‐derived auxin‐like molecule (hydroxy‐PAA or OH‐PAA) has also been identified in algae (Abe et al., 1974; Fries & Iwasaki, 1976), but an analogous role in plants has not been established.

Tryptophan‐dependent biosynthesis of IAA is well established (Figure 1A). The predominant route follows conversion of Trp into indole‐3‐pyruvate (IPA) through the action of tryptophan aminotransferase proteins (TAA/TARs; EC 2.6.1.27) (Tao et al., 2008; Stepanova et al., 2008; Zhao, 2010). This reaction is reversible, with IPA recently shown to be the preferred substrate for the Arabidopsis TAA1 enzyme, implicating TAA1 as a key regulator for IPA accumulation (Sato et al., 2022). The conversion of IPA to IAA is then catalyzed by a class B flavin‐containing monooxygenase (FMO) belonging to the large YUCCA (YUC) gene family (EC 1.14.13.168). YUCs perform an irreversible oxidative decarboxylation reaction (Mashiguchi et al., 2011; Won et al., 2011); the rate‐limiting and gate‐keeping step for IAA biosynthesis (Dai et al., 2013). The establishment of the YUC pathway as the dominant route for IAA production is strongly supported by significant in vitro, in vivo and genetic studies, including the use of inhibitors such as Yucasin, which binds tightly within the YUC binding pocket (Figure 1B,C). A parallel YUC pathway for 4‐Cl‐IAA biosynthesis has also been established in pea (Pisum sativum) (Tivendale et al., 2012), where Trp stands at the point of chlorination. The YUC pathway for IAA biosynthesis is unique to plants, as other pathways are in operation for IAA production in bacteria, algae, and fungi. While TAA gene members are also present in green algae, the evolutionary origin of YUCs is likely derived from bacteria via horizontal gene transfer (Bowman et al., 2021; Turnaev et al., 2020; Yue et al., 2012). A detailed comparison of what differentiates YUCs from other plant major class B FMOs (Nicoll & Mascotti, 2023) has not been performed. Cross‐kingdom phylogenetic analysis of FMOs identified three major clades containing plant FMOs [i.e., clade II (S‐OX), clade III (YUCs), and clade IV (N‐OX) contain plant FMO members], and supports the monophyletic origin of YUCs within plants (Nicoll & Mascotti, 2023). Recent analysis also proposed a class of FMOs sister to the YUCs (designated “sister YUCCAs” or sYUCs) (Carrillo‐Carrasco et al., 2023), but detailed comparisons between this clade and the YUCs have not yet been addressed.

Biosynthesis of the other plant auxins IBA and PAA may also occur via the YUC pathway (Ludwig‐Müller, 2020; Sugawara et al., 2015) (Figure 1A). IBA biosynthesis from IAA is evidenced by enzymatic assays derived from maize (Zea mays), but corresponding genes remain unidentified (Ludwig‐Müller et al., 1995). YUC involvement in PAA biosynthesis is supported by overexpressing YUC lines (Sugawara et al., 2015) and in vitro enzyme activity studies (Dai et al., 2013) (Figure 1B). AtYUC6 is capable of forming both IAA and PAA from their respective indole and phenyl pyruvic acids (Figure 1A). Other routes for the biosynthesis of different auxins have also been proposed (Cook et al., 2016; Günther et al., 2023; Zhao, 2012), but to date, only one other alternative pathway for IAA and PAA biosynthesis has been firmly established in mono‐ and di‐cotylendous species. This alternative pathway operates via cytochrome P450 from the CYP79 family, which perform two successive N‐hydroxylations of the precursor amino acids Trp and Phe to form their respective aldoxime intermediates (Irmisch et al., 2015; Perez et al., 2021, 2023). Given that CYP79s are not present in bryophytes, lycophytes and ferns, this pathway is likely an evolutionary later adaptation for auxin biosynthesis compared to the YUC pathway.

In general, the YUC gene family has widely expanded in plants, with species possessing multiple gene copies. For example, Arabidopsis has 11, rice (Oryza sativa) has 14, the Norwegian Spruce (Picea abies) has seven, the moss Physcomitrium patens has six while the liverwort Marchantia polymorpha has two (Cao et al., 2019) (Figure 2). The diversification of YUCs has been linked to their gate‐keeping role in auxin biosynthesis, whereby YUCs control production in a highly fine‐tuned spatial and temporal manner (Brumos et al., 2018). At present, the evolutionary patterns of YUC diversification remain highly unclear with previous phylogenetic analyses separating this large and critical plant family anywhere between four and nine different classes (Hao et al., 2022; Poulet & Kriechbaumer, 2017; Shao et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2021) without a uniform naming system. A clear resolution of phylogenetic positioning in these studies is likely due to a restricted number of input gene sequences and/or a bias toward angiosperms and model plants. Without a common representation of the YUC evolutionary landscape, it remains a highly challenging task for the community to compare cross‐species gene diversification events, evaluate and assign lineage‐specific YUC structure, identify specific evolutionary clades potentially holding functional diversification beyond auxin biosynthesis, and perform comparative analyses with other gene families. It is therefore highly relevant to realize the evolutionary diversity of the YUC family and establish a common classification that encompasses this rich diversity.

Here, we present a comprehensive phylogenetic analysis of the YUC gene family, combining YUCs from 54 different plant species, representing key evolutionary plant lineages. We identify and hierarchically classify the YUC family into an early diverging grade, five distinct classes and 41 subclasses based on evolutionary position and amino acid sequence similarity, following an expandable naming system comparable to other gene families (i.e., UDP‐glucosyltransferases and cytochromes P450; Nelson, 2006; Ross et al., 2001). We show that the YUC family is highly diverse, having undergone multiple gene duplication and retention events throughout plant evolution, especially in angiosperms. The rich diversity of YUCs is discussed in the context of protein structure and sequence conservation, as well as localization and gene expression patterns. The presented YUC landscape offers new perspectives on the distribution and evolutionary trends that facilitate further functional characterization of this crucial gene family across the green lineage.

RESULTS

TAA/TAR and YUC distribution in Viridiplantae

The main route for auxin biosynthesis in plants operates via the TAA/TAR and YUC pathway (Figure 1A). To determine distribution for both enzyme families, homologs were acquired from 59 species representing major lineages throughout the plant kingdom and green algae (Figure 2). All identified and retrieved sequences were subsequently verified and manually inspected to eliminate redundant sequences or pseudogenes, including pseudogenes known from literature (e.g., Marchantia polymorpha). In total, 253 TAA/TARs and 469 YUCs were identified as potential homologs from these 59 species, and they are evolutionarily conserved in all plant species (Figure 2; Data S1). Chlorophyte and streptophyte algae also possess TAA/TARs in line with previous reports (Bowman et al., 2021; Carrillo‐Carrasco et al., 2023; Romani, 2017). Gene copy numbers ranged between 1 and 13 for the TAA/TAR family and between 1 and 23 for the YUC family. Angiosperms typically possess a higher ratio of YUCs compared to TAA/TARs, but similar copy numbers are present in bryophyte, fern and gymnosperm genomes (Figure 2). Phaeoceros carolinianus (hornwort) and Gnetum montanum (gymnosperm) stand out as exceptions for possessing a high TAA/TAR to YUC ratio.

Phylogenetic distribution and classification of the YUC and TAA/TAR gene families

To infer the molecular basis of YUC evolution and diversity, phylogenetic analysis was performed using IQ‐TREE based on MAFFT‐aligned sequences of 469 YUCs identified from 54 plant species (Figure 3). The unrooted tree positions non‐vascular plant YUCs (i.e., bryophytes and lycophytes) at the center, with vascular plant YUCs [ferns (monilophytes), gymnosperms and angiosperms] radiating in two major directions (Figure 3A). Further large evolutionary duplication and diversification events are apparent, with separation of spermatophyte homologs into five additional and distinct groups. To compare evolutionary trajectories, phylogenetic analysis of 253 TAA/TARs from the same 54 plant species plus five algal TAA/TARs was also performed (Figure 4; Data S1). YUCs show significantly more divergence and gene retention compared to TAA/TARs, especially among angiosperms. TAA/TARs diversity only occurs along two major axes common across all plant lineages, with algal TAA/TARs positioned at the center, consistent with previous studies (Carrillo‐Carrasco et al., 2023; Matthes et al., 2019).

To assess whether the difference in YUC and TAA/TAR evolutionary diversification was due to the presence of a known outgroup (i.e., the known algal TAA/TAR outgroup), the phylogenetic analysis was also performed with the addition of four bacterial Clade III FMOs (accessions: MBX2822162, WP_020597914, WP_247236107, and WP_184177050), selected due to their close position to the YUC family in the Nicoll and Mascotti (2023) analysis (Figure S1). The topography of the resultant tree remained largely unchanged, with the major groups of plant YUCs observed (Figure S1): bryophyte and lycophyte members remain positioned at the center of the tree, with five major arms of radiation for spermatophytes. Notably, the bacterial FMOs position closely to a subclade of monilophyte YUCs, which may suggest a more recent, independent acquisition of YUCs in ferns by horizontal gene transfer (HGT). As the evolutionary position of the bacterial FMOs does not follow plant phylogeny, these FMOs were not used as a formal outgroup for further analysis. Selection of different bryophyte YUCs to root the tree did not alter topography (data not shown), so the rooted tree presented here is rooted with a moss YUC (P. patens; Pp3c11; Figure 3B). Comparison of YUCs with other plant class B FMOs (sYUC, N‐OX, and S‐OX) showed a maximum sequence identity of 23% with sYUCs (E‐value 8e‐031) and < 20% sequence identity with N‐OXs and S‐OXs (E‐value 7e‐014, 2e‐011; respectively). The average pairwise distances between YUCs and sYUCs, N‐OXs, and S‐OXs are 0.58, 0.64, and 0.52, respectively (Data S2, Figure S2). A phylogenetic analysis of selected YUCs and sYUCs was also performed, but the evolutionary distance was significantly greater compared with the four selected bacterial FMOs (Figure S1).

Using the phylogenetic tree topology and amino acid sequence identity, we propose a formal classification system to denote different groups of the plant YUC family: an early diverging (ED) paraphyletic grade that encompasses YUCs present in Bryophyta and Lycopodiophyta and five YUC classes (1–5) that are present in spermatophytes (Figure 3). The classification between the different classes coincides with a sequence identity cutoff of ~40% between each group, following established hierarchical classification systems of other large protein families such as UDP‐glucosyltransferases (Ross et al., 2001), cytochromes P450 (Nelson, 2006) and N‐OX FMOs (Jiang et al., 2025). Significant diversification within each major YUC class also occurs, so our hierarchical classification system includes 41 subclasses based on bootstrap support and a sequence identity cutoff of ~50% within the subclasses (Figure S3; Data S2; Figure 3B). The YUC grade/classes, and their diversification, reflect major ancient and lineage‐specific whole genome duplication events across the plant kingdom. For example, within the ED YUC grade, moss YUCs separate into four moss‐specific subclasses (EDa, b, f and g), suggesting lineage‐specific duplication events. YUC diversification across seed plants occurs after the zeta duplication event (Figure 2). Here, this event is evidenced by the radiation of YUCs in the two major directions (class 1 vs. classes 2–5), although it is anomalous that class 2 also contains monilophyte YUCs. The epsilon duplication in angiosperms is evidenced by divergence patterns within classes 1, 3, and 5. Overall, the high copy number of angiosperm YUCs is primarily attributed to paleoploidy genome duplication and triplication events (denoted as γ, α, β, π, Ψ, ω, etc., in Figure 2) and subsequent gene retention. Few dicot species retain representative YUCs from these duplications, with only Papaver californicum, Populus trichocarpa, Vitis vinifera, and Hydrangea querifolia possessing YUCs from Class 3b, and no dicot species retained members from Class 5d. Classes 3b and 5d are instead retained by a greater number of monocot species. Tandem gene duplications also contribute to the high gene copy number in some species, such as Hordeum vulgare, Medicago truncatula, Oryza sativa, Populus trichocarpa and Vitis vinifera, but not all (e.g., A. thaliana; Figure 6).

Sequence‐structure conservation, motif analysis and predicted subcellular localization

To investigate the structural variation across the different YUC classes, we performed multiple sequence alignment and motif analysis. To broadly compare the YUC motif patterns with other plant class B FMO families, 20 S‐OX, N‐OX and sYUC FMO representatives were also included (Data S3 and S4). Motif analysis using MEME suite (Bailey et al., 2015) identified 22 motifs (a–v), including canonical signature motifs and several shorter motifs (Figure 5A, Figure S4, Data S4 and S5). Five motifs were conserved across YUCs and other class B FMOs (sYUC, N‐OX, S‐OX): two canonical Rossmann folds (GXGXXG) for FAD and NADPH binding (motifs f and k, respectively), the FMO‐specific FXGXXXHXXXY/F motif (j), the F/LATGY motif (q), and a fifth motif PFYP‐X(3)‐QFXXYLEX (h) located between the FAD‐binding and FMO‐identifying motifs. These shared motifs map around the cofactor and substrate‐binding pocket common to class B FMOs (Figure 5B).

The analysis identified four motifs unique to the YUC family: a CELP motif (g), a PREXXG motif (m) [or its divergent GAXTSIVVRX(3)H (l) in ~70% of class I YUCs], a leucine‐rich KWLP motif (n), and a glycine‐rich GX(5)GXXRPXXGP motif (o) (Figure 5A). The CELP motif is positioned between the FAD‐binding (f) and PFYP‐X(3)‐QFXXYLEX (h) motifs in the substrate binding pocket, while the other three YUC‐specific motifs are localized in proximity around the substrate channel (Figure 5B) and may contribute to the positively charged environment of this region (Figure S5b). Two motifs are also shared between YUCs and sYUCs: an ATG‐containing motif (i) and a VGT/AX(3)IXXG motif (p) (Figure 5A).

Superposition of the 11 AtYUCs reveals a highly conserved structural fold (RMSD 0.33–0.47 Å), characterized by a positively charged substrate pocket (Figure S5). Notably, AtYUC10 and AtYUC11 (Class 1) carry motif “l” instead of “m” around the substrate pocket, a region predicted to be under negative selection (Figure S7, Data S7 and S8). To assess if this sequence divergence altered the binding pocket surface area (SA) and volume (V), these parameters were calculated. While AtYUC10 was predicted to be the smallest of the AtYUCs (SA: 1001.7 Å; V: 758.6 Å; Figure S6), it cannot be fully attributed to Class I sequence divergence as AtYUC11 (Class I) is similar to other AtYUCs, with SA and V of 1102 Å and 854 Å, respectively. Class 3 YUCs (i.e., AtYUC2 and AtYUC6) possess the highest surface area and cavity volume parameters of the AtYUCs (Figure S5).

Motif organization across the YUC family showed some degree of variability occurring at the C and N terminals (Figures S5 and S6). Motif distributions were evaluated in the context of YUC classes to assess for any class‐specific divergence, where classes 3–5 contain YUC members with an N‐terminal RRCVWV motif (e), and a C‐terminal K/QK/QKKX(5)HRR motif (u) was identified in class 4, contributing to a long α‐helix tail (Figure S6). The absence of conserved motifs in some members [e.g., lack of PREXXG and KWLP in a P. sativum YUC (class 4f) and lack of CELP motif in Musa acuminata YUC (class 4e)] suggests potential pseudogenization. Details on species‐specific motifs and their corresponding sequences are available in Tables S1 and S2; Data S5 and S6.

Subcellular localization predictions based on the DeepLoc server (Ødum et al., 2024) indicated that most YUCs are cytoplasmic (~80%), a smaller fraction targeting the cytoplasm/ER (~15%) or ER (~4%), and few predictions for plastid, peroxisome, or nucleus (~1%) (Figures S8 and S9; Data S9). PROSITE predicted a C‐terminal ER retention motif (KDEL) (Schouten et al., 1996) in HqYUC6 (Class 3e); however, SignalP failed to detect any signal peptide. Instances of nuclear and peroxisomal target signals as well as chloroplast transit peptides were observed. Peroxisomal target signals are dispersed across all YUC classes except class 2. Chloroplast target peptides and nuclear localization signals are prevalent in classes ED, 1 and 3. DeepLoc analysis predicted that the majority (~91%) of YUCs are soluble, with approximately 9% predicted to be transmembrane proteins. In contrast, TAA/TAR proteins showed broad localization, with ~25% each in the ER, cytoplasm, and ER/Golgi, and smaller fractions in the Golgi (~15%) or extracellular space (~6%). Most of them (~73%) contain signal peptides or transmembrane domains, with ~50% predicted as transmembrane and ~35% as soluble (Figures S8 and S9; Data S9).

Multiscale coordination of YUC gene expression; a focus on Arabidopsis

To examine patterns defining YUC classes, the 11 A. thaliana YUCs were broadly compared across different scales: genomic and cellular localization, as well as gene expression and co‐expression at different developmental stages, tissues and in response to environmental cues. Genomic mapping observations from the ePlant database (Waese et al., 2017) show that At YUCs are dispersed across chromosomes 1, 2, 4, and 5 (Figure 6A). At YUC3 and At YUC9 (class 4) lie ~200 kb apart on Chromosome 1, but microsynteny analysis indicates they are WGD‐derived paleologs rather than true tandem duplicates, with multi‐gene collinear blocks flanking both loci (Figure 3B, Figure S10). YUC copy number in Arabidopsis reflects multiple paleo WGD events, including the α and β duplications that occurred in Brassicaceae, mirroring evolutionary patterns of the auxin transporter family PIN3/4/7 (Figure S11). Subcellular localization predictions suggest ER targeting for AtYUC6, AtYUC10, and the splice variant AtYUC4.2, while the remaining AtYUCs are predicted to localize to the cytoplasm (Figure 3B). In planta studies, however, only partially support the predictions, with all class 4 AtYUCs (AtYUC3, AtYUC5, AtYUC7‐9) and AtYUC4.2 (class 5) localized to ER while the other AtYUC classes localized in the cytoplasm (Kriechbaumer et al., 2012, 2016) (Figure 6B). In a separate in planta study, AtYUC6 (class 3) was reported to localize in both the cytoplasm and an unidentified endomembrane compartment (Kim et al., 2007). While DeepLoc 2.0 achieves ~87–88% accuracy overall (Ødum et al., 2024), these predictions remain provisional until experimentally validated.

At YUC gene expression patterns across various tissues and developmental stages were summarized according to the Plant Expression Omnibus (PEO) database (Koh et al., 2024). At YUCs from classes 3, 4 and 5 show broad gene expression patterns, expressed across different ontogenetic stages and tissues (Figure 6C, Data S10). This is exemplified by AtYUC6 which is expressed in the seed, stem, leaves, flower, and fruit tissue, and at different developmental stages. In contrast, class 1 At YUCs (AtYUC10, AtYUC11) show a relatively narrow expression pattern, with AtYUC10 expressed in the endosperm and AtYUC11 expressed in the epidermis of cotyledons. Differential gene expression data in response to diverse biotic and abiotic stress factors were summarized from the TAIR database generating a heat map based on temporal leaf‐specific average expression values across various environmental treatments. The expression of individual At YUCs appears highly variable and fine‐tuned in response to different environmental stressors, with no obvious class‐specific gene expression patterns identified (Figure 6D). For example, there is a trend where At YUC1 (class 5) is consistently downregulated in response to abiotic stresses, but shows variable expression upon different biotic stress treatments. In contrast, AtYUC6 (class 3) exhibits a variable gene expression response upon different abiotic stresses.

DISCUSSION

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of the YUC family

The YUC family plays a fundamental role in plant auxin biosynthesis and has attracted significant attention since their role in auxin metabolism was initially identified (Zhao et al., 2001). To date, all characterized YUCs catalyze the conversion of IPA into IAA (Cohen & Strader, 2024), a critical plant hormone. In addition, YUCs are involved in the biosynthesis of other auxins, such as 4‐Cl‐IAA, and possibly PAA and IBA (Figure 1). Thus, YUCs represent a plant enzyme family that is extremely important for growth, development, and response to environmental stimuli (Cao et al., 2019; Martin‐Arevalillo & Vernoux, 2023). The YUC family is relatively large, showcasing significant diversification in angiosperms, represented by the higher ratio of YUCs compared to the precursor biosynthetic enzyme for IAA production, the TAA/TARs family (Figure 2). With an average of 11 gene copies per angiosperm species, the YUC family is in a similar range to other denoted large gene families, such as the transcription factor families Aux/IAA and ARF (Hernández‐García et al., 2024; Li et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2017) whereby high copy number is attributable to environmental stress response (more details below).

To aid the classification and interpretation of YUC diversification and evolution, we established an expandable nomenclature system for the YUC family consisting of a paraphyletic ED grade, five distinct classes (Classes 1–5), and 41 subclasses (a, b, c, etc.). This expandable, hierarchical naming system is based upon phylogenetic positioning and sequence identity cutoffs following established naming systems of other large gene families (e.g., cytochromes P450 and UDP‐glucuronosyltransferases; Nelson, 2006; Ross et al., 2001), and a system recently applied to the N‐OX family of class B FMOs (Jiang et al., 2025). This system does pose the limitation that classification does not necessarily translate to evolutionary history. For example, the monilophyte classes 1a and 1b are classified together with other class 1 YUCs, despite phylogenetic analysis supporting a more recent acquisition via HGT from bacteria (Figure S1). Nonetheless, the presented YUC classification provides a valuable and systematic framework that builds upon previous attempts to organize the YUC family, whereby YUCs were grouped anywhere between four and nine different clades (Poulet & Kriechbaumer, 2017; Shao et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2021). Here, the inclusion of diverse plant lineages improves delineation between phylogenetic clades and aids the interpretation of how the family has expanded through evolutionary time. For example, Class 2 YUCs appear unique to ferns and gymnosperms, and given their evolutionary position, may possibly indicate it arose following an ancestral genome duplication event prior to the zeta (ζ) WGD event (Li et al., 2015; Qiao et al., 2022) in seed plants (Figure 2), or may possibly represent an independent HGT event. Our analysis did not resolve a clear evolutionary pattern among early diverging plant lineages, so here we classify bryophyte and lycophyte YUC members as a polyphyletic grade rather than a monophyletic class. Our study therefore emphasizes the importance of genomic sequencing projects within phylogenetically important lineages to better understand how important traits (i.e., auxin biosynthesis) evolved in plants.

In a few cases, phylogenetic subclasses did not fall within the expected evolutionary position, such as subclasses 1j and 1l. This might suggest lineage‐specific diversification, exemplified by an unusual substitution of serine (S) and alanine (A) in the otherwise conserved and YUC‐specific CELP motif of the Poales‐specific subclass 1j (Data S4). Further characterization of 1j clade could illuminate possible functional diversification or neo‐functionalization. Overall, the comprehensive phylogenetic analysis of the YUC family presented here shows that many YUC genes are retained following paleoduplication events. In contrast to YUCs, much fewer TAA/TARs have been retained throughout plant evolution, with these genes only diversifying in two major directions in line with other TAA/TAR‐specific studies (Figure 4) (Carrillo‐Carrasco et al., 2023; Matthes et al., 2019; Poulet & Kriechbaumer, 2017).

Origin, expansion and diversification of the YUC family

Our homology‐based searches did not retrieve any potential YUCs from core chlorophyte and streptophyte algae, in line with the current consensus that YUCs have originated from bacteria via HGT, then neo‐functionalized to acquire IAA functionality and expanded throughout the Plantae kingdom (Bowman et al., 2021; Turnaev et al., 2020; Yue et al., 2012). Recent classification of class B FMOs across different kingdoms supports that the closest FMOs to the YUC family belong to bacteria (Nicoll & Mascotti, 2023), and inclusion of selected bacterial sequences shows close proximity to a clade of YUCs from monilophytes (Figure S1). A sister clade of YUCs has also been proposed (Carrillo‐Carrasco et al., 2023) but our analysis here, and expanded classification of class B FMOs in plants (Christensen & Neilson, 2025) does not support this family to represent a true sister clade to the YUCCAs. In comparison, the TAA/TAR family has a different evolutionary origin, arising in green algae (Figures 2 and 4; Wang et al., 2014).

Following the shift from algae to pioneer land plants, a whole genome duplication (WGD) event occurred (Leebens‐Mack et al., 2019; Qiao et al., 2022). This evolutionary event is apparent in the TAA/TAR phylogeny, with two clear directions of evolutionary expansion, but is not evidenced in the YUC evolutionary tree. Instead, bryophyte and lycophyte YUCs cluster in a central Early Diverging (ED) paraphyletic YUC grade, with YUC radiation observed in ferns, gymnosperms, and angiosperms. The YUC family shows great expansion and high copy number variation (CNV), especially within angiosperms. Whole genome duplication (WGD), whole genome triplication (WGT), as well as local or small‐scale duplications underlie this expansion (Defoort et al., 2019; Soltis & Soltis, 2021). Arabidopsis, for example, possesses > 60% of genes with at least one paralog corresponding to a syntenic block derived from one of the three α, β, or γ WGD events (Bowers et al., 2003; Panchy et al., 2016) (Figure 2). Here, we show that YUC gene expansion across monocots and dicots is largely attributed to both WGD and WGT events (Figure 2), but also local, segmental and small‐scale duplications. For example, in Medicago species, YUC copy number variation arises from tandem and segmental duplications in addition to paleoduplication events (Shao et al., 2023). Indeed, most duplicated genes are constrained by negative (purifying) selection (Lye & Purugganan, 2019), and often become inactive through non‐functionalization, pseudogenization, and gene loss. In Medicago and Isatis indigotica, YUCs show evidence of strong negative selection (Qin et al., 2020; Shao et al., 2023), limiting the spread of deleterious mutations. Evidence of pseudogenization is also present in banana (Musa acuminata). In M. acuminata, three WGDs after divergence from other monocots likely contributes to the presence of 22 YUCs (Figure 2; D'hont et al., 2012) but motif analysis indicates that several homologs have lost the YUC‐specific CELP motif, consistent with pseudogenization (Figure 3, Figure S4, Data S5).

Beyond gene loss, many YUC duplicates are retained by sub‐functionalization or neo‐functionalization (Kondrashov & Kondrashov, 2006; Kuzmin et al., 2022; Nowak et al., 1997), and are often acquiring complementing, tissue‐specific functions. Considering the lineage‐specific duplications and purifying selection described above, YUC gene expression pattern suggest a typical canalization of functions whereby YUC duplicates subfunctionalize and acquire complementary, tissue‐biased roles. For example, Arabidopsis YUC paralogs possess different expression domains (reproductive vs. vegetative) (Figure 6), and in the liverwort M. polymorpha, MpYUC1 is sporophyte‐specific, whereas MpYUC2 is expressed in both the gametophyte thallus and the sporophyte (Eklund et al., 2015; Kawamura et al., 2022).

YUC functional diversification is closely linked to varied spatial and temporal regulation of auxin synthesis, as reflected in the diverse expression patterns of Arabidopsis YUC genes across developmental stages and tissues (Figure 6C). For example, AtYUC1 and YUC4 are specifically expressed at the boundary layer between floral whorl 3 and 4, regulated by the transcription factor SUPERMAN, which increases local auxin accumulation and leads to the formation of extra primordia of reproductive organs (Xu et al., 2018). In maize (Zea mays), loss of the spi1 YUC (class 5B) results in severe vegetative and reproductive tissue phenotypic defects (Gallavotti et al., 2008). The relatively high CNV of angiosperm YUCs may suggest a higher order of auxin biosynthetic regulation in flowering plants, providing the ability to generate novel plant morphologies.

The high CNV of YUCs, relative to TAA/TARs, may be linked to their specific biochemical functionalities within auxin production. The TAA/TARs catalyze the conversion of Trp to IPA (Tao et al., 2008; Stepanova et al., 2008; Zhao, 2010, 2012), a reversible reaction. Recent characterization of the Arabidopsis TAA1 enzyme shows IPA as the preferred substrate compared to Trp, implicating TAA1 as a key regulator for IPA accumulation (Sato et al., 2022). In contrast, the YUC‐catalyzed conversion of IPA to IAA is irreversible and rate‐limiting (Dai et al., 2013). Accordingly, YUCs stand as the critical gatekeepers for auxin production, where YUC sub‐functionalization is strongly linked with gene expression and the tightly controlled, highly fine‐tuned production of auxin across time, space and environmental conditions (Figure 6). High YUC CNV and the complex regulation of auxin production in response to environmental change, mirrors other instances in angiosperms, where CNV of other gene families (e.g., nucleotide‐binding leucine‐rich repeat (NB‐LRR) genes) is associated with elevated protein expression, enzymatic activity, and environmental tolerance in plants (Panchy et al., 2016; Zmieńko et al., 2014), providing a mechanism to facilitate rapid adaptation (Lian et al., 2004; Sakurai et al., 2005). Notably, in a recent population genomics assessment of the endangered dipterocarp Hopea chinensis, a YUC was identified as a key gene under positive selection, associated with cold and drought tolerance (Xiang et al., 2025).

Functional diversification is further supported by biochemical data. Beyond auxin biosynthesis, a small handful of YUCs and so‐called “YUC‐like” proteins have also been reported to possess extended or alternative functionalities and may contribute to a high degree of YUC gene retention and diversification via neo‐functionalization. For example, in vitro biochemical analysis of AtYUC6 reports some substrate promiscuity such that AtYUC6 can catalyze the conversion of both IPA and PPA to their respective auxins IAA and PAA, respectively (Dai et al., 2013; Figure 1). While a role in PAA biosynthesis via the YUC pathway has not been confirmed in vivo, Arabidopsis lines overexpressing either YUC1, 2, or 6, all show increased levels of PAA conjugates compared to control lines (Sugawara et al., 2015). Arabidopsis YUCs display variability in predicted substrate‐binding surface area and volume (Figure S5), and in the case of pea (P. sativum), YUCs can successfully take the larger halogenated 4‐Cl‐IPA substrate to form the highly bioactive 4‐Cl‐IAA (Figure 1; Tivendale et al., 2012). This may suggest that YUCs may act on a wider range of substrates but to date, no large screens of potential YUC substrates have been performed, likely hindered by challenges relating to FMO expression and purification (Thodberg & Neilson, 2020).

Dual YUC functionality has been reported for AtYUC6 (Class 3E) and two poplar (Populus trichocarpa) YUCs, PtYUC4 (Class 5B) and PtYUC5 (Class 3B). These YUCs can produce IAA, but also moonlight as NADPH‐dependent thiol‐reductase enzymes, increasing plant resistance under different abiotic stress conditions (Cha et al., 2015, 2022; Wang et al., 2022). Lastly, a neo‐functionalized “YUC‐like” Bs3 gene is important for plant immunity. Specifically, the pepper (Capsicum annuum) Bs3 gene (Class 4) lacks approximately 70 aa in the region between the NADPH‐binding and F/LATGY motifs (Krönauer et al., 2019). This neo‐functionalized Bs3 gene encodes an enzyme without auxin capabilities but instead exhibits NADPH oxidase activity (Krönauer & Lahaye, 2021) which aids effector protein recognition against the pathogenic bacterium Xanthomonas campestris pv. Vesicatoria (Römer et al., 2007).

Together, these findings show that the YUC family follows a distinct evolutionary trajectory compared with the TAA/TARs family, where the YUCs originate from bacteria via HGT, and have especially expanded in angiosperms through WGD, WGT and lineage‐specific duplication events. Many duplicated YUCs have been retained, with exhibiting sub‐ and neo‐functionalization, unpinning the role of YUCs as auxin “gatekeepers” and important drivers of development and environmental adaptation in plants.

Diversification of YUCCA structure and function

Structural and motif analysis shows large conservation of the YUC family, with many common motifs. As class B FMOs, YUCs share five canonical sequence motifs with other members of the S‐OX, N‐OX, and “sYUC” families (Figure 5A). YUCs also possess four family‐specific motifs, with the CELP motif positioned between the NADPH‐binding and F/LATGY motifs, and the remaining “PREXXG,” “Leucine‐rich KWLP” and “Glycine‐rich GX(5)GXXRPXXGP” motifs positioned at the substrate channel entrance (Figure 5B). The substrate channel is positively charged and would therefore favor sustainable attraction, orientation, and binding of IPA and other anionic α‐keto acids (Dai et al., 2013).

Overall, Arabidopsis YUCs possess highly similar structural folds, with many sites under negative selection (Figures S6 and S7, Data S7 and S8). Motif pattern variation, however, does exist (Figure S4), which could influence factors such as protein localization, protein–protein interaction partners, and oligomeric state, or may contribute to extended or dual protein functionality. Further biochemical analysis of the YUC family is required to pinpoint critical residues responsible for specific functions. For example, examination of different YUCs would identify how prevalent thiol‐reductase moonlighting extends, and determine specific residues or circumstances of if, where and when this function is prioritized over IAA biosynthesis.

Sequence variation that differentially drives YUC subcellular localization is likely important for specific protein–protein interactions based on compartmentalization (Figures S8 and S9, Data S10), especially given that plants TAA/TARs predicted localization is evenly distributed in the endoplasmic reticulum, cytoplasm and also the Golgi (Figure 4). Comprehensive YUC localization analysis is well documented within Arabidopsis, showing localization to the cytoplasm and ER (Kriechbaumer et al., 2012, 2016) (Figure 6B). An earlier study also identified AtYUC6 to occur in an unknown endomembrane compartment (Jeong et al., 2007), which here we speculate may be the peroxisome given AtYUC6 (Cha et al., 2015, 2016) is predicted to have a peroxisomal targeting signal (Figure 3B). The lack of conventional signal peptides in the YUC family indicates that they are not transported into the ER lumen, despite their anticipated localization to the ER. Rather, it is likely that YUCs operate as ER‐associated enzymes on the cytosolic face where they have continuous access to their FAD and NADPH cofactors. The presence of ER‐retention KDEL in HqYUC6 is most likely incidental and non‐functional as ER retention necessitates initial ER entry. Conclusions on YUC localization, however, require empirical support, as the AtYUC in silico predictions do not align with experimental outcomes. Alternative gene splicing may also contribute to YUC sub‐functionalization, diversity and evolution. A key example is AtYUC4 which occurs as two splice variants, experimentally shown to have distinct intracellular localization (Kriechbaumer et al., 2012): AtYUC4A is localized in cytoplasm and AtYUC4B to the endoplasmic reticulum (Figure 6B). Computational studies also reveal a nuclear localization signal for AtYUC4A, consistent with experimental support showing AtYUC4A in the nucleoplasm (Kriechbaumer et al., 2012), and common for cytosolic proteins.

CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

The critically important YUC family plays a key gate‐keeping role in auxin biosynthesis. Here, we perform a comprehensive phylogenetic analysis of the YUC family, representing diverse members across the plant kingdom. YUCs are ubiquitous across all plant species, with diversification especially evident in vascular plants. In contrast to the other auxin biosynthetic family TAA/TAR which have evolved in two major directions, vascular plant YUCs have evolved in multiple. To facilitate classification and interpretation of YUC diversification and evolution, we establish and present an expandable nomenclature system for the YUC family, consisting of an ED grade, five classes, and 41 subclasses (a, b, c, etc.). This expandable naming system is based upon phylogenetic positioning and sequence identity cut‐offs following established naming systems of other large gene families. YUC expansion in angiosperms can be attributed to many factors, including the fine‐tuned control of auxin biosynthesis in time, space and environmental conditions, possible catalytic promiscuity and novel functionalities (i.e., thiol reductase moonlighting capabilities). Experimental evidence of extended functionalities is currently limited and further investigations into the multifunctionality of this family will provide important insights into how and why these genes are retained. Our analysis of YUC sequence diversity revealed variable motif regions which may affect protein folding, stability, localization, and interaction with other proteins. Biochemical characterization of YUCs is extremely limited and a major bottleneck and hinders a more complete understanding of the YUC catalysis mechanisms and substrate selectivity. The evolutionary and structural perspective presented here offers new perspectives on the distribution and evolutionary trends of this crucial family, and facilitates future YUC characterization to better understand their role within plant development and environmental change.

METHODOLOGY

Homolog identification of YUCs and TAA/TARs in plants

To identify potential plant YUCs and TAA/TAR homologs, did a BLAST search against databases including NCBI (Sayers et al., 2023), OneKP (Leebens‐Mack et al., 2019), Phytozome v13 (Goodstein et al., 2012), Phycocosm (Nordberg et al., 2014), CNGBdb (Chen et al., 2020), PlantGenIE (Sundell et al., 2015), Hornwortbase (https://www.hornwortbase.org/), FernBase (https://fernbase.org/), and Takakia database (Hu et al., 2023). Arabidopsis YUC and TAA were used as queries (accessions: AtYUC6‐AT5G25620.1; AtTAA‐AT1G70560.1). We also used the plaBi database's cladogram view (https://www.plabipd.de/plant_genomes_pa.ep) to get genomic data availability details and primary insights into their phylogenetic relationship. Putative homologs were selected for further analysis based on the E‐value cutoff 1e‐50 and query coverage >50%, and cross‐checked with known YUCs from the literature (e.g., two YUCs identified in M. polymorpha; Eklund et al., 2015). Partial or truncated sequences were omitted in this study (i.e., <340 cutoff amino acid length). All retrieval details are provided in Supplementary Data S1. To provide a more comprehensive picture of the evolutionary position of YUC within the green lineage, we included several species from angiosperms, bryophytes, lycophytes, hornworts, liverworts, gymnosperms, and green algae. Furthermore, to classify as a prospective hit and to avoid the chance of any false positives, each retrieved putative hit was further validated by reciprocal blast. A selected number of other class B FMOs (S‐OX, N‐OX, and sYUCs) were also retrieved for comparative study. Selected plant species, homologs sequence data, and their respective source database details are given in Data S1. Ploidy levels were checked and confirmed using PloiDB_web (https://taux.evolseq.net/PloiDB_web) and PhyloGenes (Zhang et al., 2020). The species tree was generated using Lifemap (de Vienne, 2016) using taxonomy identifier (TaxId) as input. All databases and tools used in this study are listed in Table 1.

Sequence analysis and phylogenetic studies

The sequence identity matrix for 469 YUC sequences was constructed using Sequence Demarcation Tool Version 1.3 (SDTv1.3) (Muhire et al., 2014) by employing the MAFFT (Katoh & Standley, 2013) aligning algorithm with the Neighbor‐joining (NJ) tree function enabled. Percent identity matrix for each YUC class and subclass was done in Clustal Omega (Sievers & Higgins, 2018). All the matrix details are provided in Data S2. The retrieved and validated YUC and TAA/TAR homologs were aligned in MAFFT v.7 using the auto alignment strategy (Katoh et al., 2019; Katoh & Standley, 2013) and visualized in ESpript 3.0 (Robert & Gouet, 2014). ClustalW from MEGA11 (Tamura et al., 2021) was also used to check the percentage level‐based sequence conservation. Furthermore, to investigate the evolutionary relationships of YUCs and TAA/TARs, phylogenetic trees were constructed in IQ‐TREE using the maximum likelihood (ML) model finder method, and ultrafast bootstrap (1000 bootstraps) (Hoang et al., 2018; Minh et al., 2020; Trifinopoulos et al., 2016). The MAFFT‐aligned sequences were used as input. Very short/partial fragments were omitted based on the sequence length. Full‐length alignment (Data S3) was used for creating the phylogram. In addition, a truncated version of alignment by omitting the super‐variable region (using trimAl) was also used to confirm the phylogram topology. IQ‐TREE‐generated trees were visualized using iTOL v.6 (Letunic & Bork, 2024). The YUC phylogram includes 469 sequences retrieved from 54 diverse plant species, and the TAA/TAR tree includes 253 sequences from the same species. The YUC tree was rooted using bryophytes (early diverging (ED) clade) (Harris et al., 2022; Qiu & Mishler, 2024). Furthermore, YUCs were classified into specific classes based on the clustering pattern in the phylogram and sequence identity matrix. The DIVEIN server was also used to calculate and confirm the diversity of different YUC classes and subclasses (and between different plant groups) using the Pairwise Hamming distance (Deng et al., 2010). For class‐specific sequence conservation/motif studies, class‐based YUCs were extracted from the phylogram using the fasta extractor program in the FaBox (1.61) toolbox (Villesen, 2007). Selection pressure and diversification analysis were conducted on the Datamonkey server with the MEME (Mixed Effects Model of Evolution) and SLAC (Single‐Likelihood Ancestor Counting) modules (Kosakovsky Pond & Frost, 2005; Murrell et al., 2012), using AtYUCs (both amino acid and nucleotide sequences) as the models. Codon alignment was generated in PAL2NAL program (Suyama et al., 2006). MEME analysis (v4.0) was executed on the AtYUC codon alignment (excluding stop codons) using HyPhy v2.5.64 (Kosakovsky Pond et al., 2020).

Structural conservation pattern, motif analysis and subcellular localization

To examine the pattern of sequence conservation in the structure, ConSurf analysis (Yariv et al., 2023) was done using all the chosen YUC sequences. Results were subsequently mapped into the AtYUC6 representative model generated by AlphaFold 3 (Jumper et al., 2021). Besides, to check the domain architecture of YUC/TAA sequences, a SMART search (Letunic et al., 2021) was done. Furthermore, MEME Suite 5.5.5 (Bailey et al., 2015) was employed to identify and examine the conserved sequence‐specific motifs of YUCs with 30 cut‐offs. DeepLoc 2.1 (Ødum et al., 2024) was used for predicting the subcellular localization and all localization details were mapped into the phylogram.

Gene localization and expression analysis

The chromosomal locations of all Arabidopsis YUC genes were obtained from the ePlant database (Waese et al., 2017). To explore the organ‐specific expression of Arabidopsis YUCs, the Plant Expression Omnibus web‐based tool (PEO) (Koh et al., 2024) was used. The expression pattern of all 11 AtYUCs was estimated by analyzing the mean value of TPM values (transcripts per million) of distinctively expressed samples in different stages of Arabidopsis development. Gene expression data for various environmental stresses were obtained from the eFP browser (Winter et al., 2007). The expression levels of YUC genes under different abiotic and biotic stresses were analyzed using relative values of Log2 Ratios. Expression levels were examined under multiple abiotic stress conditions, including heat, cold, drought, salt, genotoxic, and oxidative stress. For biotic stress analysis, the Log2 Ratios of one representative treatment were selected for each stress type to ensure consistent and comparable data across all AtYUC genes. For Botrytis cinerea, Phytophthora infestans, and Erysiphe orontii, gene expression data were analyzed using the only representative treatment available for each pathogen. For Pseudomonas syringae, Log2 ratios derived from triplicate infiltrated leaf samples treated with a virulent strain were estimated. Elicitor stress was assessed using Log2 ratios from a 10 μm HrpZ treatment, a bacterial‐derived elicitor. The overall expression level was determined as the mean of Log2 ratios for both abiotic and biotic stress conditions, based on visual inspection of the data.

Molecular modeling and docking

To investigate structural features and probable variations of YUCs between phylogenetic classes, protein models were generated for representative AtYUCs (AtYUCs) using AlphaFold (Jumper et al., 2021). Structural refinement was done in GalaxyWEB using the GalaxyRefine option (Ko et al., 2012), and all models were validated using Verify 3D, PROCHECK, ERRAT, and ProSA‐web (Dym et al., 2006; Eisenberg et al., 1997; Laskowski et al., 1993; Wiederstein & Sippl, 2007). Molecular docking was done in AutoDock 4.2.6 and PyRX software (Dallakyan & Olson, 2015; Morris, 2010). FAD, NADPH, IPyA, PPA, and the auxin inhibitor yucasin were used as ligands. Energy minimization was done using UFF (United Force Field). Furthermore, the cavity parameters of AtYUC models were computed in CASTpFold (Ye et al., 2024), and the electrostatic surface potential (ESP) was calculated using the ASPB electrostatics plugin (version 2.1) in PyMOL 2.5.4 (Academic licensed version) (DeLano, 2002; Jurrus et al., 2018; Schrodinger, 2015).
