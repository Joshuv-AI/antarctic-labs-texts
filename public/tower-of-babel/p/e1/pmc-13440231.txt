Implementation evaluation of nurse-initiated care in 29 Australian emergency departments: a RE-AIM multi-method evaluation
Authors: Sarah Kourouche, Julie Considine, Ling Li, Margaret Murphy, Saartje Berendsen Russell, Louise Casey, Rebecca Richardson, Ramon Z. Shaban, Christina Aggar, Michael M. Dinh, Amith Shetty, Timothy Shaw, Radhika V. Seimon, Nanda R. Aryal, Kate Curtis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNurse-initiated care improves timely treatment access, but adoption varies. The Ministry of Health introduced 73 Emergency Care Assessment and Treatment (ECAT) protocols for public emergency departments (EDs) to standardise nurse-initiated care. A behaviour change strategy was used to implement ECAT in 29 EDs as part of a trial, using implementation strategies like education, clinical champions, videos, and audit and feedback. The aim of this study was to evaluate the implementation of the ECAT protocol and the strategies used for reach, effectiveness, adoption, quality, and maintenance (REAIM).MethodsThis multi-method implementation evaluation used the RE-AIM framework. Methods comprised: i) emergency nurse surveys (Intervention and Control groups); ii) scoring by site implementation nurses of fidelity, adaptation and effectiveness of implementation strategies out of 5; iii) audits determining appropriateness of ECAT protocol use at 6–12 weeks and iv) implementation tracking logs during all study periods. Descriptive statistics were used for quantitative data, and content analysis for qualitative data (surveys/logs).ResultsData from surveys (n = 787), audits (1375 audits, 509 nurses), fidelity scores (n = 11), and implementation tracking logs (>600 entries) were used to evaluate implementation of ECAT protocols in the 29 EDs. Emergency nurses reported high use of ECAT protocols in their daily practice (median (IQR) 9.0 (8.0, 10.0), and audits demonstrated 92.9% of eligible nurses used at least one ECAT protocol in their shift (509/548). High effectiveness was demonstrated through 93.7% selection of the fully appropriate ECAT protocol (1288/1375). Implementation strategy fidelity was high (4.6/5) and effective (3.9/5), particularly the Education program (885 nurses, 97 courses), Audit and Feedback (1375 audits, 509 nurses), stakeholder engagement (>450 meetings) and electronic medical record update (all sites). The strategy with the lowest fidelity and effectiveness was Clinical Champions. Maintenance was demonstrated by training, use and monitoring embedded in practice at 12 months.ConclusionBehaviour-change informed implementation strategies successfully enabled large-scale successful adoption of nurse-initiated care at 12 months. Relying on clinical champions already operating at full capacity in an overcrowded ED to assist with implementation was impractical. Ongoing investment in facilitation, education, and adaptive support structures is essential to maintain gains and realise the full potential of nurse-initiated emergency care.Trial RegistrationAustralian and New Zealand Clinical Trial, ACTRN12622001480774p. Background

Global demand for emergency care has grown over the past decade and continues to exceed resource supply [1, 2], posing serious risks to patient safety [3]. Emergency department (ED) visits are at an all-time high in the United Kingdom [4], and the United States of America (29.4 and 42.7 per 100 people, respectively) [5]. Aging populations and poor primary health care access contribute to ED overcrowding and access block [6, 7]. Overcrowding and access block result in delays in treatment and longer ED length of stay [8], associated with suboptimal care, and negative patient experiences [9].

Likewise, Australia’s 300 public hospital EDs are plagued by overcrowding, access block and treatment delays affecting the nine million annual episodes of care [10]. These free, primary emergency care services are overwhelmed by rising presentations—up from 31.0 to 33.3 per 100 in a decade [11]. Australia’s EDs are unable to keep up with demand, with only 63% of “urgent” patients (42% of total) attended to within 30 minutes, risking severe consequences from delayed assessment and treatment [12, 13]. Australians of all ages seek emergency care with varying urgency, requiring prompt assessment and treatment [14].

Nurse-initiated care has been suggested as a potential solution to overcrowding and ED demand issues by empowering experienced emergency nurses to commence investigations and care. Nurse-initiated care includes clinical treatments, such as analgesia, or investigations such as imaging or pathology, for eligible patients under protocol, based on a nurse assessment and prior to physician review, beyond their usual scope of practice [15]. EDs use a team-based care model; however, emergency nurses are the first clinicians a patient encounters. After triage, nurses are solely responsible for patients until medical review. In regional areas, emergency nurses may be the only clinicians patients encounter [16]. Nurse-initiated care has been shown to reduce ED length of stay [17], and reduce time to analgesia [18–21], imaging [17], treatment [22], in both adult and paediatric populations [23–25].

Despite the evidence supporting nurse-initiated care, its implementation and use have lacked consistency and standardisation. In New South Wales (NSW), Australia, a large, geographically diverse state, nurse-initiated care was used variably at emergency sites and nurses needed medical sign-off. To reduce unwarranted clinical variation in the 180 state, public EDs, the NSW Agency for Clinical Innovation (ACI) released 73 standardised Emergency Care Assessment and Treatment (ECAT) protocols for nurse-initiated care for common and time-critical adult and paediatric emergency presentations [26]. The ECAT protocols provided nurses with expanded authority and guidance to assess patients and initiate investigations and treatments without medical sign-off, supported by structured, condition-specific clinical guidance. The ECAT protocols were implemented with support from NSW Health for policy and online education modules. Alongside the state implementation, the ECAT protocols were implemented in 29 sites as part of the EPIC-START Type II Hybrid implementation effectiveness trial [27]. The implementation at the trial sites was supported by a targeted strategy informed by implementation science and behaviour-change theories [28].

Evaluation of the implementation strategy for ECAT protocols was essential to understand the success or failure, including the effectiveness of strategies used to support it [29]. In the ED, implementation of evidence-based practice is often negatively impacted by high workflow, patient turnover, noise levels [30] and staff attrition [31], with frequent examples in the literature of unsuccessful implementation [32, 33]. Implementation fidelity is the extent to which an intervention is delivered per the intended implementation plan [34]. In this study, an evaluation of a comprehensive behaviour change strategy was needed to assess implementation fidelity and inform the validity and reliability of the overall trial’s results, and to better understand which strategies were effective in implementation. The aim of this study was to evaluate the implementation of the ECAT protocols and the strategies used to achieve reach, effectiveness, adoption, quality, and maintenance at the 29 study sites [27].

Methods

The Intervention: ECAT protocols

The ECAT protocols were developed in a statewide, co-designed project by NSW Health based on previous work by study authors. The initial work at one local jurisdiction included 56 stakeholder meetings with 60+ senior emergency nurses, doctors and pharmacists, resulting in 54 guidelines for nurse-initiated care. NSW Health’s 2022–2023 consultation built on this initial work with previous nurse-initiated care models, evidence checks and extensive consultations with committees of 20–40 stakeholders from medical, nursing, allied health, radiology, pathology, and pharmacy.

The final endorsed set included 73 ECAT protocols − 41 for Adult and 32 for Paediatrics, covering a wide spectrum of presentations such as wounds and burns, mild head injuries and traumatic injuries, allergic reactions, shortness of breath, chest pain, and suspected sepsis. Emergency nurses who have completed the required training take a patient history and perform an assessment, select and use the ECAT protocols, and then initiate investigations and treatments if the patient meets specific criteria, without physician sign-off. It is important to note, ECAT protocols guide care initiation but do not include diagnosis or definitive treatment. Once a healthcare provider assumes care, nurses should stop using the ECAT protocol or follow standard escalation procedures if the patient is too unwell (https://aci.health.nsw.gov.au/ecat/).

Implementation strategies

A multi-faceted plan for the ECAT protocol implementation was developed based on behavioural diagnostic analysis from 847 nursing and medical staff surveys, using the Behaviour Change Wheel and Theoretical Domains Framework and refined with implementation experts and stakeholders [28]. The resulting plan included six implementation strategies consisting of 18 behaviour change techniques [35], with delivery through face-to-face Educational Programs, local clinical champions, individualised audit and feedback, medical staff engagement, environmental strategies, steering committees, leadership and stakeholder outreach [28] (Box 1).

Each site had a tailored delivery of the strategies to meet their needs. The structured education program supported staff attending during work hours, and included an Instructor Course (train-the-trainer design) and two Provider Courses tailored for experienced emergency nurses with recognised prior learning of nurse-initiated care previously (EPIC-START CREDIT) and less experienced emergency nurses without recognised prior learning (EPIC-START CIN) (see Box 1). All permanent and casual nurses were eligible and were allocated in consultation with the site managers and educators to the appropriate course. The Education Program aligned with the statewide ECAT policy, which contained 12 policies (456 pages) that linked to a further 58 policies (1964 pages) to comply with clinical governance, and was grounded in the pedagogical principles of scaffolded learning, constructive alignment [36] and backwards design [37], which have been successfully used in other large-scale trials in emergency nursing [38].

Clinical champions included senior clinical nurses who expressed interest in contributing to supporting the change. In addition, ‘implementation nurses’ were employed for the duration of the study to support implementation processes and data collection. Individualised audit and feedback were provided by the implementation nurses to nurses using the ECAT protocols. Medical staff were engaged through consultation at meetings by the implementation nurses. Environmental strategies included statewide policy and modifications to the electronic medical record system to streamline access to ECAT resources. The implementation process was managed with locally led steering committees that included local leaders.

The included sites were emergency services or departments, defined as hospital-based facilities that are equipped to provide 24-hour urgent care for life-threatening injuries or illnesses within a health system, and included triage service [39]. Study design

This pre-post observational implementation evaluation used surveys, audits, fidelity scoring and implementation tracking log data. The methods were guided by the RE-AIM framework (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation (fidelity/adaptation) and Maintenance/Sustainability) [40]. Quantitative data were collected to measure proportions and determine the level of achievement for each component of RE-AIM. Qualitative data were used to understand the “how” and “why” of quantitative results, and any deviations or adaptations to the proposed implementation strategy. The REAIM definitions, related study outcomes and sources are presented in Table 2. Reach was measured by the proportion of eligible nurses using the ECAT protocols regularly, aiming for over 80% [41]. Effectiveness was assessed based on increased nurse confidence scores after implementation and appropriate ECAT protocol use. Adoption was measured by the proportion of eligible sites that implemented ECAT protocols, including if this was by the required date and as intended. Implementation quality was determined by the consistency with which individual components and the overarching implementation strategy were delivered, in terms of fidelity, adaptations and quality. Maintenance was assessed through demonstrating the integration of ECAT protocols into routine operations at 80% of sites 12 months post-implementation. Data sources and participants

Four data sources were used in this multi-method evaluation. Each source had different participants and methods, explained below, and linked to the RE-AIM framework (Table 2).

Data source 1: nurse surveys

Population and process: All nursing staff employed at the 29 study sites were invited to participate in an electronic survey before (control) and after (intervention) the implementation of ECAT protocols. Surveys were administered through REDCap® (Research Electronic Data Capture) [44], a secure web-based data management and survey delivery application. Invitations were sent via email by nurse managers or study implementation nurses every two weeks, and advertisements featuring QR codes were placed in staff meal rooms to encourage their participation. Participants received a $20 AUD voucher as a token of appreciation for their time. Consent was obtained electronically through REDCap®, and responses were anonymous.

Survey Instruments: Surveys were adapted from similar questionnaires developed using the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) [45] and other validated change management tools [46] (Supplementary file 3). The surveys included closed and open-ended questions, enabling elaboration and capture of potentially overlooked perspectives. The surveys comprised six sections and were conducted to evaluate different aspects of implementation of ECAT protocols:i.Participant characteristics (Intervention and Control groups): position, years worked as a nurse and in the ED, highest qualificationii.Confidence in ECAT protocol use (Intervention and Control groups): nurses indicated their confidence in ECAT protocol use (e.g. protocol selection, ordering of pathology, radiology, medications) on a scale of 0 (no confidence) to 10 (complete confidence) for adult and paediatric presentations.iii.ECAT protocol usability (Intervention group): statements related to usability and feasibility of protocols scored on a scale of 0 (strongly disagree) to 10 (strongly agree).iv.Utility of implementation strategies (Intervention group) scored on scale of 0 (strongly disagree) to 10 (strongly agree)v.Education program evaluation (Intervention group)vi.Free-text questions for elaboration and general comments (Intervention and Control groups)

An advisory group of emergency nursing and medical researchers and clinicians with content expertise assessed both surveys for face and content validity. Items were phrased both positively and negatively to counter response set bias. The advisory group completed the survey on desktop computers and mobile devices, providing feedback on the functionality of the survey, completion time, typographical errors, questions irrelevant to their allocated role, and the clarity of the instructions. Comments were collated and only minor editorial changes were needed.

Data source 2: audits of emergency nurse ECAT use

Population and process: Site implementation nurses conducted up to three audits for each eligible emergency nurse 6–12 weeks after implementation. Emergency nurses included in the audit had participated in EPIC-START Education Programs and were approved to use ECAT protocol in practice. Lists of eligible staff were uploaded into a purpose-built audit tool in REDCap® [44], with pre-entered nurse names, sites, and attended courses. Implementation nurses randomly selected patient presentations in which an ECAT was used, this was the only criteria for patient selection.

Instrument: The audit tool was purpose-built to assess ECAT use (quantity) and appropriateness of use (quality). The audit tool was developed by investigators based on the ECAT protocol requirements and structure: History, Identify Red flags, Assessment, Interventions, Diagnostics, communication and reassessment [47]. The audit tool was reviewed and tested by implementation nurses, who gave feedback on usability and accuracy. Their knowledge of ECAT protocols and ED practices was vital in development. Implementation nurses received training and a quick reference guide. Investigators regularly met with auditors to address inconsistencies. Since auditors were also implementation nurses involved from the start, they understood ECAT protocol requirements. They were experienced emergency nurses familiar with assessment and documentation. The audit tool also served as an implementation strategy, allowing nurses to record and provide feedback.

In the REDCAP audit tool, auditors recorded the total number of ECAT protocols used by each nurse. If ECAT protocols were used fewer than five times, a reason or a follow-up note was documented. Nurses who had not used ECAT at all for reasons such as retired or on long leave were not counted in the proportions for reach. Then, audits were conducted using available clinical information from the electronic medical record, ECAT protocols and relevant guidelines. ECAT use was audited for the appropriateness of nurse-initiated protocol selection, specifically whether the nurse selected the correct ECAT, and the appropriateness of radiology, pathology, and/or medication selection and use. Items were scored as yes (appropriate), no (not appropriate), not applicable (e.g. not indicated or not available on site), or not sure/partial (insufficient information to decide). Auditors were subsequently able to select the reason for their choice and add comments to explain their choice as needed. The quality of nurse documentation for patient history, red flags, assessment, and reassessment was also audited, as these are essential elements of emergency nursing care and would affect nurse ECAT selection. These items were scored as Yes, No (no documentation available), Partial, or Not Applicable.

Data source 3: implementation nurse fidelity scoring

Population and process: Participants included a convenience sample of site implementation nurses, principal investigators, and individuals who were significantly involved in the implementation process at each cluster. Participants were asked to score the implementation strategies in an evaluation scoring sheet during an online meeting with an author (SK) and two to three participants from each cluster. Participants were asked questions such as “Do you think the education programs were delivered as intended?”, “Do you think the education programs were effective as a strategy at your sites?”, and “Were changes made to the education program? What changes and why?”. Participants were asked to score each strategy and then discuss as a group to reach consensus on their scores. After the meeting, the final decisions were shared with participants for feedback. Final scores were collated and compared across clusters.

Instrument: The implementation score sheet was developed similarly to previous evaluations, with scoring for fidelity, adaptations and effectiveness of ECAT implementation, scoring strategies on a 0–5 scale [48]. Each strategy was scored on an implementation fidelity scale and an adaptation scale (Supplementary file 3). The implementation fidelity score was defined as the extent to which the strategy was implemented as intended in the initial plan, scored from 0 (not implemented) to 5 (fully implemented). The adaptation score indicated the level of adaptations made to implement the plan, scored from 0 (no adaptations) to 5 (many changes). The effectiveness score was how effective the item was as a strategy, scored from 0 (not effective) to 5 (very effective). Results are presented with a mean score out of five and range. Site implementation nurses provided comments on the reasons for their choice.

Data source 4: site implementation tracking logs

Detailed implementation tracking logs were kept by site implementation nurses in an ExcelTM template. Implementation nurses were asked to record education events, issues, actions, and meetings related to the implementation of ECAT. Site implementation tracking logs were collated. Personal identifiers were removed, and data synthesised and summarised into categories (site visits, meetings, risks, education events) by author (RR) and checked by second author (SK). A summary of events with counts is presented (Supplementary file 1).

Data analysis

Where quantitative data were used, they were analysed by statisticians in the investigator team (LL, NRA) using the Statistical Analysis System (SAS 9.4), SAS Institute Inc, North Carolina, USA. Participant characteristics were summarised using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages [n (%)]. Mean and standard deviation (SD) were presented for normally distributed numerical variables, and median and interquartile range (IQR) were presented for not normally distributed numerical variables. If any measures were missing, the percentages for categorical variables and summary statistics for numerical variables were calculated based on the available values. Comparisons of Intervention and Control group confidence scores from the nurse surveys were conducted using Wilcoxon rank test and the Kruskal-Wallis test for comparison between clusters.

Qualitative data from free text responses were imported into NVivo™ v14 (QSR International, Burlington, MA) and analysed collectively using the Graneheim, Lindgren [49] method of inductive content analysis. The text was read several times to obtain a sense of the whole; the content was divided into meaningful units (codes) for analysis. The codes were abstracted to sub-categories and categories at the highest level. Abstraction and interpretation of the latent content were kept low as the content was brief. This process was completed by one author (SK); increasing the reliability of the interpretation by limiting the number of coders. Analysis was checked by a second author (KC). Any queries were resolved through discussion among authors (SK, KC). Quotes have been edited for simple spelling and grammatical errors.

Results

Data sources and their characteristics

Surveys were completed by 787 nursing staff whose characteristics are presented in Supplementary file 1. Fidelity scores were completed by 11 implementation nurse participants representing each of the four clusters. Audit data was collected by six implementation nurses for 628 staff who used 1288 ECAT protocols. Implementation tracking logs contained over 900 entries from all sites. Appropriateness of use of ECATs, results from audit data

ECAT - Emergency Care Assessment and Treatment

Nurse confidence

Nurse confidence significantly increased in the Intervention compared to the Control group in all aspects of nurse-initiated care. In particular, confidence increased for identifying the need to initiate nurse-led care, as well as clinical treatments, such as pathology, radiology, opioids, simple analgesia, bronchodilators, antiemetics, and IV therapy (Table 5). In subgroup analysis comparing years of emergency nursing experience, a significant increase was still observed in most groups, except for simpler clinical treatments (e.g., simple analgesia, pathology) that had already reached maximum confidence scores (Supplementary file 1). In comments, nurses expressed they felt confident to use ECAT protocols but were less confident with more complex or uncommon clinical treatments. They noted that opportunities to practice were variable and sometimes unavailable, such as when doctors started treating patients before the nurse. One participant described challenges with confidence due to reduced exposure or opportunity:With less common presentations- there is reduced confidence initiating medications due to uncertainty of treatment pathway. Overall implementation at study sites

All 29 sites implemented ECAT protocols (100%) using our implementation strategy. However, this was not without challenges. Once the ECAT protocols and policies were released, and to ensure the education program aligned with policy, we needed to train over 800 nurses in 5 months. Audit and feedback

Implementation quality for the Audit and Feedback was high in fidelity (4.3/5), with low adaptations (1.0/5) and high effectiveness (5.0/5). This was supported by audit completion numbers, with a total 1375 audits with at least one completion for 509 nurses and all three for 419 of those staff (73%) (Supplementary file 1). Feedback was recommended for 674 (45.1%) of audits and 361 (57.5%) of eligible staff. In the nurse survey, participants responded that individual and departmental feedback was helpful (Table 4).

Medical staff engagement

The medical engagement strategy had moderate fidelity (3.8/5) and was partly effective (2.5/5). Six medical-specific briefings were recorded, as well as monthly steering committee meetings. However, despite the invitation to these meetings, medical staff attendance was poor. Nurses reported they felt doctors were mostly supportive (median (IQR) 7.0 (5.0, 9.0)).

Environmental strategies - policies and electronic medical record (eMR)

Environmental strategies, specifically changes to the eMR for an easier ordering process, scored highly for fidelity (5.0/5), with some adaptations primarily in one cluster (Cluster 4), and demonstrated high effectiveness (4.3/5). All sites used the statewide policy to guide practice, and one site initiated a local policy to further inform local practice. All clusters used the state-recommended eMR build, with some local adaptations. Nurses reported that the enablers to use ECAT protocols were that they were easy to locate electronically, easy to follow and record, and quick to order medication (Table 3). Steering committees, leadership and stakeholder outreach

Fidelity to leadership and stakeholder strategies was high (5.0/5) with low adaptations (1.0/5) and moderate effectiveness (3.8/5). Over 450 site meetings were conducted with stakeholders, leadership, and allied health professionals. Maintenance

Site implementation tracking logs confirmed that all sites have embedded the ECAT protocols as ‘business as usual’ twelve months post-implementation. This was achieved through the inclusion of ECAT training in onboarding processes, staff training, and integration into policy. Planning for maintenance commenced pre-implementation. Each site prepared and endorsed its own maintenance plan, involving its steering committees and key stakeholders. At the time of writing, audit and feedback have been integrated into site systems through the development of eMR reports and embedding into staff roles, and education programs have been integrated into existing career development pathways with site adaptations.

Several risks, unplanned events, and disruptions occurred and were recorded over the 12-month study period. These included industrial action (nursing and medical), where nurses were instructed not to use ECAT by local union representatives, eMR downtime (which required staff to access paper versions of protocols), and significant natural weather events. One cluster was impacted by a Category 4 tropical cyclone and flooding, which impacted the ability of staff to visit sites, and impacted staff’s use of ECAT on-site. Mitigation strategies were established through consultation and pre-arranged mechanisms.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the effective implementation of ECAT protocols through a behaviour change-informed strategy, as evidenced by high reach, effectiveness, adoption, quality, and maintenance across 29 emergency sites in NSW, Australia. Implementation of ECAT protocols was successful, with high appropriateness of use, high nurse confidence and high patient reach evident in quantitative and qualitative measures. In this study, the implementation strategies were used with high quality, fidelity, and effectiveness, with minimal adaptation of strategy components.

Effective implementation of ECAT protocols

The implementation of ECAT protocols was effective using the strategies. The reach for ECAT protocol use was over 90%, which is relatively high for implementation studies in EDs [50]. Further, nurses used ECAT protocols appropriately, with 94% correctly using ECAT and ordering the most appropriate medication or pathology test. This supports research demonstrating that nurses, when enabled, can select and use ECAT protocols without incident [18, 19].

Increased confidence may have contributed to the effectiveness of the implementation, as confidence is a mediator of barriers to implementation [51]. Nurse confidence in initiating care increased significantly across nearly all care deliverables within the ECAT protocols, independent of years of emergency experience. The greatest gains in confidence were observed among less-experienced nurses and for more complex clinical treatments, suggesting our bespoke education program reduced professional hesitancy and provided a safety net for the use of ECAT by addressing staff concerns and learning needs [52]. The Education program implementation strategy was developed and delivered by highly experienced emergency nurses and educators using robust educational principles and intended learning outcomes, and providing protected face-to-face learning as requested by staff during pre-implementation consultation and surveys [28].

Challenges of ECAT implementation

Contextual and organisational factors resulted in variation in uptake [53]. Some sites had medical-led care models or medical hierarchy, which constrained ECAT use, despite initial organisational readiness and enthusiasm. This affected the nurse’s capacity to begin care, as some medical officers assigned themselves to patients too early, before they were ready. This meant ECAT protocols could not be used, and patient care was delayed.

Other challenges arose from unplanned disruptions, which posed real risks to implementation continuity, such as industrial action, government delays in releasing the protocols, government mandates to implement earlier than sites had originally intended, and catastrophic weather events. The ability of sites to adapt and sustain ECAT use in the face of these challenges attests to the resilience of the implementation design, similar to our previous work [54], though such events should be systematically considered in future scalability planning.

Inappropriate ECAT selection was rare but occurred, especially with a mismatched medical history or clinical assessment. Nurses should conduct a thorough history and assessment before choosing ECAT, in line with NSW ECAT policy [55]. It is strongly recommended that nurses perform a comprehensive assessment of their patients prior to ECAT selection to aid ECAT selection through use of the evidence-based and validated HIRAID® emergency nursing framework [56].

The majority of the implementation strategies were used successfully. However, unexpectedly, and in contrast to other literature [57, 58], clinical champions scored low on fidelity and effectiveness in this study. This was despite the use of supports known to improve champion effectiveness [59]. Champions self-nominated to the role and received resources and support from the implementation nurses. We hypothesise that, because most champions hold clinical positions with considerable workloads, they were unable to dedicate the necessary time. Relying on clinical champions as an implementation strategy in acute healthcare is becoming increasingly impractical and challenging. Adding to clinicians’ workload only exacerbates existing pressures, negatively impacting their well-being. The implementation nurses, employed by the research team solely to support implementation, were relied upon as the default champions. This prompts a discussion regarding the role of clinical nurses, their participation in the translation of evidence-based practice, and the need to remunerate champions [60]. Another issue may have been that the champions were not yet confident and needed time to develop their skills before assisting others. Further research is required to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the success or failure of these strategies.

Research and practical implications

The application of the RE-AIM proved invaluable in structuring evaluation, and frameworks should be considered a standard in implementation research. However, a lack of consistency remains in evaluation assessments across the literature [61], which complicates both internal and cross-study comparisons. Further, deeper exploration by implementation researchers into the mechanisms underpinning effective facilitation and champions, the role of interprofessional collaboration, and the contextual factors that shape fidelity and sustainability is warranted.

The challenges highlight the importance of pragmatism, flexibility, and adaptation in implementing change and conducting health services research, where investigators have limited control over highly influential variables. Policy makers and health system leaders should recognise the need for both top-down mandates and local adaptability. Although feasible and often related to political cycles, accelerated rollouts require a significant investment in training, support, and change management to avoid superficial compliance and achieve meaningful clinical change. It is essential to support implementation with ongoing practice opportunities and adaptable supports to sustain confidence and competence in the specific knowledge and skills for patient assessment and critical thinking [62].

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this research is the use of the RE-AIM framework, which is useful in evaluating pragmatic trials as it emphasises external validity and generalisability across diverse, real-world settings [63]. Other strengths lie in the use of a multi-faceted and rigorous research approach to the evaluation.

There are limitations to this study. Firstly, while the evaluation benefits from the application of the RE-AIM framework, the reliance on self-reported fidelity and reach metrics may introduce reporting bias or overestimate true levels of adoption [40], multi-methods were used to negate these concerns. Additionally, the possibility of response bias exists, as nursing staff who used the ECAT protocols may have been more likely to participate in the survey, potentially skewing the results [64]. The clinical impact of the ECAT protocols, including patient impact, safety and satisfaction, ED length of stay, and the proportion of eligible patients who received ECAT protocols will be addressed in a separate study, which is essential for a complete understanding of overall ECAT protocol impact on patient and health service outcomes. Secondly, we do not address the potential impact of concurrent initiatives or external policy changes that may have influenced the results beyond our control. To our knowledge, there were no major policy changes. Thirdly, implementation tracking logs relied on the implementation nurses entering data regularly and accurately, and the audits relied on the nurses documenting accurately, which may have resulted in inaccuracies due to missing data. Unfortunately, because the implementation timing was very close between sites, analysis to compare clusters could not be conducted, and there was no access to data from sites not part of the research to compare findings. Finally, comparisons were not presented in this study because they were beyond the scope.

Conclusion

This study reinforces the potential for behaviour-change informed implementation strategies to drive large-scale adoption of evidence-based ECAT protocols with high fidelity and reach, in diverse and resource-variable ED contexts. Relying on clinical champions as an implementation strategy in acute healthcare is becoming increasingly untenable because it imposes additional roles on clinicians already operating at full capacity. Ongoing investment in facilitation, education, and adaptive support structures is essential to maintain gains and realise the full potential of nurse-initiated emergency care.
