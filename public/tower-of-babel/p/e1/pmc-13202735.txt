Red blood cell distribution width-albumin ratio as predictor of mortality in patients with cardiac disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Lin Ye, Wei Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the utility of red blood cell distribution width-albumin ratio (RAR) for predicting mortality in patients with cardiac diseases.MethodsPubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Web of Science databases were systematically searched for studies reporting the association between RAR and mortality in cardiac patients. The search limits were from inception of databases to 20th December 2024. Meta-analysis was conducted for short-term (up to 30 days) and long-term (> 30 days) mortality.ResultsThe analysis of the eight eligible studies showed that per unit increase in RAR [odds ratio (OR): 1.31 95% confidence intervals (CI): 1.16, 1.49 I2 = 68%] as well as higher cut-offs of RAR (OR: 1.82 95% CI: 1.51, 2.19 I2 = 17%) were associated with a significant increase in the risk of short-term mortality in cardiac disease patients. On the pooled analysis, per unit increase in RAR (OR: 1.68 95% CI: 1.18, 2.38 I2 = 99%) and higher levels of RAR (OR: 2.07 95% CI: 1.80, 2.37 I2 = 64%) were associated with a significant increase in the risk of long-term mortality. Meta-regression using sample size, age, male gender, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, pulmonary disease, red blood cell distribution width, albumin levels, and RAR cut-off as moderators did not reveal significant associations. GRADE assessment showed that certainty of evidence was ‘very low’.ConclusionsRAR can be a potential biomarker for predicting mortality in patients with cardiac disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05783-z.Keywords: Cardiac illness, Death, Biomarker, Nutrition, Blood count, Survival

Introduction

Cardiac diseases are the leading cause of death in the USA, irrespective of gender and ethnicity, accounting for about 702,880 deaths in 2022 alone [1]. Cardiac diseases are significant contributors to the disease burden and mortality in China. A report shows that in 2019, there were 11.39 million patients with coronary artery disease, 2 million patients with congenital heart disease, 4.87 million patients with atrial fibrillation, and 8.9 million patients with heart failure in China [2]. The main risk factors of cardiac diseases include lifestyle, obesity, smoking, physical inactivity, and dyslipidemia [3]. Currently, the rising incidence of cardiac diseases has led to a higher impact on the healthcare setups and increasing expenditure [4]. Therefore, it is crucial to identify patients at risk of worse outcomes so that they can be prioritized for treatment, ensuring optimal utilization of scarce resources. However, the discovery of new biomarkers for the early detection of mortality remains an unmet need.

Recent studies identified several novel cardiac biomarkers, such as N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide, cardiac troponins, galectin-3, soluble ST2, growth differentiation factor 15, neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin, fibroblast growth factor 23, tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase-2, syndecan-1, and interleukin-6 [5, 6]. However, these biomarkers may not be suitable for smaller healthcare setups and low-income countries. Therefore, there is a great need to identify inexpensive, reliable, accurate, and simple predictors of outcomes in patients with cardiac diseases that may be obtained from routine laboratory tests [7–9]. Several such hematological markers have been linked with adverse outcomes in cardiac patients. Zhang et al. [7] in their meta-analysis have shown that low prognostic nutritional index is an independent predictor of mortality and major adverse cardiac events in patients with coronary artery disease. Meta-analysis studies have shown that neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio are predictors of mortality in patients with heart failure [10, 11]. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein has been found to predict major adverse cardiac events, major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention for coronary artery disease [12]. Although numerous markers exist, none have been deemed to be gold standard, and research is persistently advancing in the discovery of new markers [7–9].

One such marker, the red blood cell distribution width (RDW), is reported with routine blood cell counts and reflects the variation in the size of erythrocytes in a blood sample. It is expressed in percentage, with higher values indicating more significant variability in cell size [13]. Higher RDW, which signifies impaired erythropoiesis and reduced cell was identified as a marker of worse survival [14–16]. Additionally, low serum albumin levels, which are indicative of the nutritional and inflammatory state [17, 18], demonstrated a significant association with worse outcomes in different diseases [19–21]. Recent studies demonstrated that the RDW-albumin ratio (RAR) can predict outcomes of acute pulmonary thromboembolism, abdominal aneurysm, and stroke [22–24]. However, the value of RAR in predicting mortality in cardiac disease patients remains unclear, as no systematic review has attempted to synthesize evidence. This study aims to address this knowledge gap and examine the prognostic ability of RAR in cardiac disease patients.

Methods

Inclusion criteria

The review protocol was uploaded on PROSPERO before beginning the review (CRD42024624197), according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [25]. PRISMA Checklist is uploaded as Supplementary File 1.

The eligibility criteria were formulated before starting the review. The review included all types of studies conducted on patients with any heart disease. Studies examining the association between RAR and the mortality of heart disease patients and reporting outcomes as multivariate-adjusted effect sizes were included, with no restrictions on the follow-up time. The use of RAR as a continuous or categorical variable was acceptable. Studies that did not report separate data on heart diseases, those that were not on RAR, those that reported duplicate data, and those that did not report on mortality were excluded. Editorials, non-peer-reviewed articles, and abstracts were also excluded.

Search strategy

Two reviewers formulated a search strategy using free and MeSH keywords. A search query combined all possible keywords for heart diseases and RAR, namely: ((((((((((((coronary artery disease) OR (heart disease)) OR (cardiac disease)) OR (myocardial infarction)) OR (percutaneous coronary intervention)) OR (PCI)) OR (Coronary artery bypass grafting)) OR (CABG)) OR (valvular heart disease)) OR (heart failure)) OR (cardiac failure)) OR (atrial fibrillation)) AND (((Red blood cell distribution width albumin) OR (RDW-albumin ratio)) OR (RAR)). This query was used on the literature repositories of PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Web of Science to retrieve relevant articles published from inception of databases to 20th December 2024. No language filter was applied. A supplementary search was also run on Google Scholar. The reference lists of included articles were also searched. Both reviewers were involved in the search and final selection of studies. Titles and abstracts of the retrieved studies were first assessed. Based on the likelihood of relevance, the reviewers selected studies for full-text analysis. Any study deemed suitable by any one reviewer was selected at this stage. After the final reading of the selected articles, both reviewers finalized the list of included studies. Disagreements were resolved after discussion.

Data extraction

An Excel spreadsheet was used to extract relevant information, such as the author of the study, country, study design, type of heart disease, sample size, demographic details, comorbidities, mean RDW & albumin levels, follow-up, the timing of RAR assessment, use of RAR as a continuous or categorical variable, RAR cut-off, and mortality data. Mortality was the only outcome assessed. For this meta-analysis, studies reporting in-hospital and 30-day mortality were used in the meta-analysis of short-term mortality. In contrast, longer follow-up data were used for long-term mortality. Studies with missing data were not included, and no assumptions were made for any outcome data.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias in the available evidence was quantified using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS) for cohort studies [26]. NOS assesses study quality based on the selection of the cohort, comparability of groups, and outcomes. The total score for each article was given after independent assessments by the reviewers. Scores ranged from 0 to 9, with 0 indicating high risk of bias and 9 indicating low risk of bias. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Data synthesis

Meta-analysis and meta-regression were performed using “Review Manager” (RevMan, version 5.3) and Meta-essentials (https://www.erim.eur.nl/research-support/meta-essentials/), respectively. Adjusted outcome data were pooled to generate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). If studies reported multiple covariate adjustment models, the model with the maximum variables was used. OR values > 1 indicated a higher risk of mortality with a higher RAR. A separate analysis was conducted for continuous and categorical data. The random-effects model was used for the meta-analysis. The inter-study heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 index of the software, with values over 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. The number of studies in each meta-analysis was limited. Therefore, the publication bias could not be assessed with funnel plots. A meta-regression analysis was done to explore inter-study heterogeneity. This was done only for RAR as a categorical variable due to the small number of studies for the meta-analysis of RAR as a continuous variable. Moderators for meta-regression included sample size, age, male gender, diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension (HT), pulmonary disease, RDW, albumin levels, and RAR cut-off. Subgroup analysis was also conducted for atrial fibrillation but only for short-term mortality due to limited data. Certainty of evidence was examined using GRADE approach.

Results

Search results

Details of the search results and screening process can be found in Fig. 1. The initial search identified 1038 studies. Of them, 618 articles were excluded as duplicates. Of the remaining 427 studies, 13 were selected by the reviewers for the full-text analysis. Of these, eight were eligible for inclusion in the review [27–34]. No additional studies were retrieved from Google Scholar or the reference list of included studies. There was no disagreement between reviewers for the selection of studies.

PRISMA diagram indicating the screening process of studies

Baseline details of studies

All eight included studies were retrospective cohorts in design (Table 1). One of the studies reported two cohorts. All were recent publications, available between the years 2022 and 2024. Included populations were: patients with all types of heart diseases, atrial fibrillation, acute myocardial infarction, coronary artery disease, and heart failure. The total sample size of all studies was 486,989. Most patients were elderly. The percentage of patients with comorbidities, namely DM, HT, and pulmonary disease, was variable. Two studies reported in-hospital mortality only, while one reported only 28-day mortality data. Longer follow-up data were available from the remaining studies. All studies calculated RAR based on admission or pre-procedural blood counts. Most studies used RAR as both a continuous and categorical variable. Only two studies used RAR as a categorical variable. The cut-off of RAR varied from 3.14 to 5.44. Four studies received a NOS score of 7, while another four received a score of 8 (Supplementary Table 1).

Short-term mortality

Four studies used RAR as a continuous variable for short-term mortality, while five used it as a categorical variable. Meta-analysis revealed a statistically significant association between RAR and short-term mortality. Per unit increase in RAR (OR: 1.31 95% CI: 1.16, 1.49 I2 = 68%) as well as higher cut-offs (OR: 1.82 95% CI: 1.51, 2.19 I2 = 17%) were associated with a significant increase in the risk of short-term mortality in heart disease patients (Fig. 2). No change in the significance of the OR was found on the exclusion of any study during sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table 2). The certainty of evidence based on GRADE was found to be ‘very low’ (Supplementary Table 3). Details of the meta-regression analysis are shown in Table 1. Sample size, age, male gender, DM, HT, pulmonary disease, RDW, albumin levels, and RAR cut-off were analyzed for their effect on the meta-analysis of RAR as a categorical variable. None of the moderators was found to significantly impact the effect size. Subgroup analysis for studies only on atrial fibrillation also showed that per unit increase in RAR (OR: 1.49 95% CI: 1.24, 1.79 I2 = 22%) as well as higher cut-offs (OR: 1.91 95% CI: 1.35, 2.69 I2 = 0%) were associated with a significant increase in the risk of short-term mortality (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Long-term mortality

Three studies with four cohorts used RAR as a continuous variable, and five studies with six cohorts used RAR as a categorical variable while reporting data on long-term mortality. The pooled analysis found that per unit increase (OR: 1.68 95% CI: 1.18, 2.38 I2 = 99%) and higher levels of RAR (OR: 2.07 95% CI: 1.80, 2.37 I2 = 64%) were associated with a significant increase in the risk of long-term mortality in heart disease patients (Fig. 3). The certainty of evidence based on GRADE was ‘very low’ (Supplementary Table 3). During sensitivity analysis, the exclusion of the study of Hao et al. [28] changed the significance of the association between a per-unit increase of RAR and long-term mortality (OR: 1.61, 95% CI: 0.95, 2.75, I2 = 99%). However, the high inter-study heterogeneity persisted on exclusion of individual studies. Meta-analysis of RAR as a categorical variable remained stable on sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table 2). Meta-regression also failed to reveal any significant effect of sample size, age, male gender, DM, HT, pulmonary disease, RDW, albumin levels, and RAR cut-off on the meta-analysis of RAR and long-term mortality (Table 2).

Discussion

The present study is the first systematic review and meta-analysis that examines the utility of RAR as a prognostic marker in cardiac disease patients. Data from eight studies with nine cohorts (486,989 patients), reporting RAR as a continuous or a categorical variable, were incorporated in the analysis to examine the association of RAR with short and long-term mortality. The analysis of the continuous data showed that a per-unit increase in RAR was associated with worse short-term and long-term survival in cardiac disease patients. The effect size for long-term mortality was higher than for short-term mortality. The incremental increase in RAR led to a 68% and 31% increase in the risk of long and short-term mortality, respectively. The analysis of a categorical set of data segregated the cohort into two, three, or four groups based on RAR and compared the risk of mortality between the highest and lowest levels. Combined analysis of such data revealed a statistically significant association between high RAR cut-offs and short- and long-term mortality. The effect size with RAR as a categorical variable was much higher than that of RAR as a continuous variable, indicating that very high levels of RAR could be a better predictor of mortality. Indeed, the meta-analysis demonstrated that the high RAR cut-offs were associated with a two-fold increased risk of long-term mortality. The consistent direction of ORs of the individual studies further confirmed the results. The forest plot for both meta-analyses showed that all studies reported a statistically significant association between high RAR and mortality. Sensitivity analysis failed to change the significance of the pooled OR except for the association between high RAR cut-offs and long-term mortality. These results suggest that RAR can be a potentially valuable biomarker in predicting mortality in cardiac disease patients.

While the current review focused only on cardiac patients, it confirms previous observations that RAR is an independent prognostic factor for various critical illnesses. A recent study demonstrated that RAR is a strong potential biomarker for sepsis-associated nonthyroidal Illness syndrome, predicting both in-hospital and 30-day mortality [35]. A retrospective study by Ersalan et al. [22] included 190 patients with acute thromboembolism and showed that RAR values of ≥ 5.294 were associated with significantly shorter survival. A cohort study by Weng et al. [23] that included patients with abdominal aortic aneurysm has found that high RAR was associated with an increased risk of in-hospital mortality as well as prolonged in-hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) stay. A study by Shan et al. [36] investigated data of ICU patients with community-acquired bacteremia and found that high levels of RAR (≥ 5.824) were associated with a four-fold increase in the risk of mortality. Similar results were noted when RAR was used as a continuous variable as well as for 28-day and 90-day mortality. A retrospective cohort study of 1906 acute ischemic stroke patients by Ding et al. [37] found that high RAR was linked with poor functional outcomes and a high risk of 30-day mortality. RAR was also found to have a dose-response relationship with the probability of poor functional prognosis.

The value of RAR in predicting outcomes in such a large number of different diseases may be attributed to the fact that RDW and albumin are known biomarkers for detecting abnormal physiological status, especially regarding nutritional levels, inflammation, and oxidative stress. A proinflammatory milieu that is seen in cardiovascular disease plays a significant role in the development and potentiation of the condition [38], and RDW is considered to be a marker of systemic inflammation, which is known to play a role in mortality. The chronic inflammatory state can diminish the maturation of erythrocytes, inhibit the metabolism of iron, and also cause reduced cell survival, which in turn increases RDW [13]. Research has shown a direct association between pro-inflammatory molecules like interleukin-6 and C-reactive protein and higher RDW levels [39]. Evidence indicate that hypoferremia with higher levels of tumor necrosis factor, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, is associated with to erythrophagocytosis and higher RDW. Pro-inflammatory cytokines can also lead to membrane deformities in the red blood cells, causing suppression of cell maturation, which increases the circulation of larger reticulocytes and therefore, RDW [39, 40].

Serum albumin is considered to be a marker of both inflammatory response and malnutrition [17, 18]. Malnutrition per se has been independently associated with heart dysfunction, disease progression, and mortality in heart failure patients [41]. Higher levels of moderate-severe calcifications are seen in malnourished patients with coronary artery disease as compared to those with regular nourishment [42]. Malnourished patients with coronary artery disease have an almost two-fold risk of contrast-induced acute kidney injury [43]. Moreover, both RDW [15] and albumin [20], have been associated with worse outcomes in cardiac diseases. Together, these observations may explain a consistent positive association between RAR and mortality after cardiac disease in this study, despite a heterogeneous patient population.

Strength and limitations

The strength of the study is that it is the first review in literature pooling data on the prognostic ability of RAR for cardiac disease. Separate analysis was conducted for short-term and long-term mortality to provide a better overview of evidence. We also pooled data on RAR as a continuous and categorical variable separately to assess the differential impact of higher RAR on mortality. Detailed sensitivity analysis and meta-regression was conducted to examine the impact of outlier studies and important covariates.

This study has some limitations. The study examined the value of RAR in a wide range of cardiac diseases, which accounts for the high inter-study heterogeneity. A detailed subgroup analysis was impossible due to the limited number of studies and the inclusion of all heart diseases in some cohorts. A subgroup analysis only for short-term mortality was possible for studies on atrial fibrillation which demonstrated significant results. However, the small number of studies in the subgroup analysis remains a major limitation. Nevertheless, a meta-regression analysis was conducted considering different moderators like sample size, age, male gender, DM, HT, pulmonary disease, RDW, albumin levels, and RAR cut-off. None of these factors significantly affected the results of the meta-analysis, indicating that the heterogeneity may be due to other reasons, like patient population, ethnicity, and disease severity. The covariates adjusted in the studies differed based on the available data. Therefore, there is a high risk of unknown and unmeasured residual confounders influencing the results. Secondly, all studies measured RAR at a single time-point: at patient admission or before treatment. Changes in trajectories of RAR were not accounted for in the included studies and hence could not be examined in this meta-analysis. Thirdly, a diagnostic accuracy analysis for the sensitivity and specificity of RAR in predicting mortality was not conducted due to a lack of sufficient data. Additionally, no information was available on the optimal cut-off of RAR in predicting mortality. Lastly, most studies used data from the USA, with just one cohort each from the UK and China, which may impact the generalization of the present results.

RAR is a cost-effective marker due to its accessibility in all healthcare facilities, as it can be determined through routine lab tests. Our results suggest that RAR has the potential to be employed in clinical settings as a rapid, low-cost, bedside marker for mortality in patients with cardiac disease. Physicians may use this marker during patient discussions to gauge the risk of both short and long-term mortality. Early prognostication providing a realistic understanding of mortality can facilitate conversations about palliative care and hospice options, allowing patients to focus on comfort and quality of life during their remaining time. Moreover, high-risk patients can be prioritized for intensive therapies to improve prognosis.

Conclusions

Very low quality of evidence suggests that RAR can be a potential biomarker for predicting mortality in patients with cardiac diseases. Future studies are required to validate the current findings and to establish the optimal cut-off of RAR in predicting mortality.
