Perceptions of black women with systemic lupus erythematosus on continuity in care coordination: a mixed methods pilot study conducted in the USA
Authors: I J Sumpter, S M Phillips, E M Williams, D L Kamen, G S Magwood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContinuity in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) care coordination is imperative to achieving positive outcomes in Black women with SLE. However, continuity is not well defined or understood in SLE care coordination for this population. Thus, exploring the perspectives of Black women with SLE to understand barriers and facilitators to continuity in care coordination is a critical first step.MethodsA convergent parallel mixed methods study was conducted in the southern region of the USA between November 2021 and April 2022 using survey data and semi-structured interviews. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, and qualitative data using constant comparison and thematic analysis. Data sets were analyzed separately and then merged for data interpretation.ResultsFacilitators such as trust, access to resources, and information sharing were the most critical facilitators for achieving continuity. Conversely, negative provider behaviors such as dismissive or demeaning language were the most profound barriers. Finally, relational continuity was found to be the most important of the three areas of continuity and determined the uptake of informational and management continuity.ConclusionDetermining continuity in care coordination for a multimorbid condition such as SLE can be complex. Relational continuity plays a critical role in all areas of continuity and should be weighed considerably in achieving care coordination. Future research should assess the impact of relational continuity at the provider and system levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09686-0.Keywords: Lupus erythematosus, Systemic, Continuity of patient care, Health status disparities, Case management

Background

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a multimorbid condition with variability in disease presentation and clinical outcomes [1–3]. There have been substantial improvements in treatment options and a greater understanding of disease etiology, resulting in increased life expectancy (40% increase between years 5–10 following diagnosis) [4, 5]. However, Black women with SLE have higher rates of premature death and morbidity and remain inexplicably burdened by SLE than other races [6–11].

SLE disease burden is thought to be multifaceted, stemming from the relationship between genetic expression and socioenvironmental factors [12–14]. Genetic expression among subgroups of Black women establishes a disease susceptibility baseline and varying manifestations [12, 13].While socioenvironmental factors specific to the United States are the triggering factors that negatively impact SLE outcomes across all groups of Black women with SLE [12–14].

Research has identified these social factors as racism, discrimination, low socioeconomic status, and being a member of a historically marginalized group as substantial contributors to increased disease activity and damage [15–18].Similarly, researchers have identified environmental factors such as pesticides, air pollution, and ultraviolet light as potential exacerbating factors of SLE disease activity [19].

SLE has also been determined to be one of the leading causes of death for Black women between the ages of 15–25 [8]. Furthermore, SLE diagnosis typically occurs between 15 and 44, when social engagement, emotional connections, work productivity, and reproductivity are the primary focus [20–23]. Collectively managing these biological and psychosocial factors requires involvement across specialties and disciplines, often resulting in separate and competing treatment plans that places this population at a greater disadvantage and increased likelihood of fragmented care [24, 25].

The aggregate impact of these factors can influence behaviors such as patient receptiveness, adherence, and engagement in care management. To address the interplay between biological and psychosocial factors, research emphasizes the importance of a comprehensive approach when managing and coordinating care for multimorbid conditions such as SLE to achieve optimal outcomes and potentially reduce disparities [3, 25, 26].

An influential element supporting comprehensive care coordination is continuity [27–30]. Schultz and McDonald describe care coordination as the purposeful structuring of patient care activities to ensure adequate healthcare delivery [31]. The authors state that care coordination activities are achieved through sufficient information exchange between the patient and relevant healthcare participants and by allocating necessary resources [31]. Conversely, continuity is described as the “enabler” that facilitates care coordination and a “measurable component” of care coordination used to understand consistency and coherence in the utilization of healthcare services and their impact on clinical outcomes [28, 30].

Three concepts of continuity

Continuity is characterized by consistency, uninterruptedness, and interconnectedness [27, 29, 30]. Manifestations of continuity in healthcare are seen when services are comprehensive, informed, and consistently address patients’ needs over time [27, 29, 30]. This research focuses on three concepts of continuity. Relational Continuity is described as a positive, constructive, and beneficial relationship between patients and providers, and between providers that influences the behavioral outcomes of receiving or implementing prescribed care [27, 29, 30]. Management continuity refers to a proactive, collaborative process of planning and monitoring suggested or prescribed care to facilitate efficient and appropriate interventions and responses [27, 29, 30]. Informational continuity is described as effective sharing and collection of critical information (past and present) that informs the comprehensive plan designed to address patient needs [27, 29, 30].

Hence, understanding the role of continuity and its relationship to care coordination can support effective, efficient, and organized care delivery, particularly for individuals impacted by prominent disparities in disease outcomes, such as Black women with SLE.

Research on this topic has contributed great insight; however, it is often unidimensional and focused on one aspect of care coordination interventions (e.g., patient autonomy, disease self-management, communication, community support) or highlighting fragmented care and the need for care coordination [24–26, 32]. There are no studies that directly study continuity in SLE care coordination, particularly engaging Black women with SLE.

Research on continuity in SLE care coordination needs to be more expansive. Hence, our study sought to understand the primary factor influencing continuity in SLE care coordination. We completed this by integrating findings from a validated survey examining continuity of care, in addition to clinical and health disparities questions, and semi-structured interview questions developed to capture the lived experience of disease management and care delivery for Black women with SLE. By using this qualitative and quantitative approach, we sought to acquire more substantive and complete findings. Thus, the following research question was developed: What do Black women with SLE perceive to be facilitators of and barriers to achieving continuity in SLE care coordination?

This paper reports on the measurements of continuity, perceptions on how it is maintained, and its role in the successful delivery and implementation of coordinated care in this population.

Methods

Conceptual framework

Barr et al.’s [33] Expanded Chronic Care Model (ECCM) was applied as the overarching framework to guide this work. The ECCM considers social determinants of health at the individual, community, and system levels for care delivery and disease management. Additionally, the constructs of this model support burden reduction and align with suggested approaches for SLE management focused on addressing the unmet biological and psychosocial needs that increase risk [34, 35]. In addition to ECCM, two more models were incorporated to determine interpersonal factors and the relationship between continuity and care coordination, which influence SLE care (Fig. 1) [34–36]. These models included MacColl’s Care Coordination Model, which focuses on the patient-centered medical home and the elements that support information exchange, care transitions, and supportive services; the Capability-Opportunity-Motivation- Behavior Model (COM-B), which is commonly used to explore the specific behavioral outcomes based on interactions between capability, opportunity, and motivation that influence the implementation of interventions; and three types of continuity: relational, management, and informational (Fig. 1) [36–38]. The integration of these models and concepts supports the examination of complex disease management while considering the key stakeholders and factors that will eventually influence implementation, uptake, and delivery of care coordination services.

Integration of concepts and models

Study design

This study utilized a mixed methods design, giving equal priority to survey and semi-structured interview data. The goal was to understand the barriers and facilitators to continuity in coordinated care for Black women with SLE. This study aimed to provide a comprehensive view of this multidimensional problem by collecting data from two sources. This approach allowed data analysis and integration to determine inconsistencies, new concepts, and confirmatory findings between data sets [39]. This approach supported generalizability and context through adequate data collection and differing perspectives [39]. Previous research on this topic was limited to a single approach, making this study an essential contribution. Survey data were collected electronically via a four-part survey comprised of questions concerning: demographics, continuity of care, clinical characteristics (disease damage), and disparate health care quality. Quantitative (survey) and qualitative (interview) data were collected concurrently and analyzed separately. Findings were later merged for side-by-side comparison in a joint display to assess for coherence or discordance and to determine inferences.

Setting and study population

Setting

The setting for the study was the Medical University of South Carolina (MUSC), an academic medical center in the southeastern region of the USA.

Study population

Survey

The target sample population included all enrolled study participants in a randomized controlled trial, Peer Approaches to Lupus Self-Management (PALS) (mentees and mentors, n= 225) who received rheumatology and/or primary care at MUSC [40]. Additionally, MUSC PALS participants associated with the academic medical center primarily resided in the southeast, and thus, participants may have included members of the Gullah population. The Gullah population is a subgroup of Black people that has been studied in SLE research. They are recognized for their West African lineage and unique genetic expression due to decades of physical separation from other races upon arriving in the US from the Transatlantic Slave Trade [12, 13]. This isolation from others resulted in a “low genetic admixture” influencing their SLE clinical manifestations and disease progression ([12–14] p11). This group’s characteristics serve as a comparison group for those of non-Gullah Black individuals [12, 13]. Additional inclusion criteria included being 18 years and older and being able to speak and understand the English language.

Semi-structured interviews

Purposive sampling was used to identify potential interview participants. This sampling strategy was based on mentee/mentor status, geographical location (rural vs. non-rural), age (categories: 18–30, 31–50, 51 and older), and clinical characteristics (i.e., self-reported lupus damage for two or more organs) to support variation.

Ethics and consent

The Institutional Review Board I (IRB I) at the Medical University of South Carolina approved all study activities [Pro00111201]. Clinical trial number: not applicable. The IRB-I - Medical University of South Carolina approved the study as exempt under 45 CFR 46.104(d) and approved a waiver of signed consent. A written statement concerning participation and consent was provided at the beginning of the study survey and read aloud before the interview began. Participation in study procedures served as informed consent.

Data collection

QUANTITATIVE (QUAN): Potential survey participants were invited by EW, the principal investigator (PI) of the PALS study, via Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) email distribution to complete a four-part survey [41]. Participants received an automated REDCap email reminder, and potential participants who expressed direct interest to the study coordinator received a reminder SMS or phone call referencing the survey link and QR from the email. Data collection occurred between November 2021 and February 2022. The survey sections consisted of (1) demographic questions: age, marital status, employment status, health insurance status, educational attainment, annual household income, zip code, and PALS study role), (2) the Continuity of Care Between Care Levels (CCAENA) questionnaire, consisting of three section (a) patient - primary care provider relationship, (b) patient - rheumatology provider relationship, and (c) continuity across care levels (i.e., information exchange and care coherence), (3) the Self-Administered Brief Index of Lupus Damage (SA-BILD) survey which provides patient reported organ damage by organ system, (4) and a disparate health quality question, which examined race as a factor for healthcare services recieved [42–44]. The CCAENA survey was the primary source of quantitative data for examining how care continuity was perceived. In addition, a single disparate healthcare quality was also used against qualitative data to confirm results. The demographic and SA-BILD provided characteristic data of the study population and comparison for management continuity. To ensure outcome measures/variables were current and data-driven, tools were selected based on their use in the target population, tool pretesting sessions with members of the target population, and use of a standardized web-based catalog for social determinants of health outcome measures/variables and other measurement protocols (Sumpter IJ, Phillips SM, Williams EM, Kamen DL, Magwood GS: Continuity in care coordination for Black women with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE): A feasibility study, unpublished). See Additional File 1 for psychometric properties of instruments/items.

QUALITATIVE (QUAL): Identified survey respondents who met sampling criteria based on mentor or mentee status in PALS, lupus related disease damage, zip code data, and age were invited via email to schedule a date and time for an interview. Interviews were offered via Microsoft Teams (with or without video by participant preference) or by telephone [45]. Interviews were conducted by an experienced interviewer (IS). Interview questions were developed based on concepts of continuity and constructs of MacColl’s Care Coordination and COM-B models [36, 37]. The COM-B model was used to evaluate the behaviors that occur because of specific conditions (receiving connected care) and interactions between an individual’s physical or psychological capability, automatic or reflective motivation, and social or physical opportunity [37]. This framework, along with the constructs of MacColl’s Care Coordination Model (i.e., relationships and agreements, patient support, accountability, and connectivity), were used to understand the behavioral and system-level factors that influence continuity in care coordination [36].

Prompts were included in the interview guide. Interview questions were pretested for validation and applicability with individuals who are similar to the target population. Pretest participants (n = 4) found questions to be applicable, relevant, and relatable (Sumpter IJ, Phillips SM, Williams EM, Kamen DL, Magwood GS: Continuity in care for Black women with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE): A feasibility study, unpublished). Interviews occurred from January 2022 to April 2022 by phone or Microsoft Teams [45]. Field notes were recorded during the interview to capture the emotions expressed in the interviews, personal thoughts of the interviewer, and emerging themes. Interviews lasted between 19 and 96 min. See Additional File 2 for the interview guide.

Participants who completed a survey received a $10.00 electronic gift card. Survey respondents who completed a semi-structured interview received an additional $20.00 electronic gift card.

Data analysis

QUAN: Following data cleaning and removal of duplicate survey submissions, REDCap survey data were analyzed using SPSS v.28 [41, 46]. Quantitative data were presented in the form of means, standard deviations, and frequency.

QUAL: Interviews were transcribed verbatim using Microsoft Teams transcription. Audio content was captured via a digital recorder. Transcripts were reviewed for accuracy using audio content and coded using Max Weber Qualitative Data Analysis (MAXQDA) by the first author (IJS) [47]. The analysis consisted of an integrative approach (inductive and deductive) with several steps. A codebook was organized by area of continuity, then categorized by constructs of the supporting models (1) MacColl’s Care Coordination Model and (2) COM-B. Predetermined codes were established from a literature review of relevant studies, model constructs, and previously conducted coded mock interviews examining the content of this study’s interview guides. To examine code and thematic agreement, IJS, SMP, and GSM held an initial session to review a coded transcript and the codebook. The remaining transcripts were reviewed and coded by IJS, followed by a second review of all remaining transcripts and codes by SMP. The coding process occurred over multiple steps, beginning with concept coding using priori codes. Relevant emergent concepts that emerged were assigned a code. To ensure alignment with the research question and to prevent redundancy between emergent and predetermined codes, codes were assessed for coherence, relevance, and similarity and assigned as subcodes or removed when necessary [48]. Coded data were assessed for patterns and organized into categories based on constructs of the care coordination and COM-B models. Themes were then generated and organized by constructs of continuity for further interpretation. SMP and GSM reviewed thematic analysis results and interpretation.

Operational definitions by concept of continuity

The operational definitions developed to provide a measurement method for the constructs of continuity were informed by relevant previously conducted research, standard dictionary definitions, and findings from our preliminary work (Sumpter IJ, Phillips SM, Williams EM, Kamen DL, Magwood GS: Continuity in care coordination for Black women with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE): A feasibility study, unpublished). This multifaceted approach considered Slife et al.’s best practice principles (i.e., clarification, specification, and justification) for operational definitions to ensure the generalizability of study procedures [49].

Relational continuity

Within relational continuity, responses focused on patient-provider relationships. Four themes crossed data sets: trust, knowledge, open and honest communication, and time. Trust was operationalized based on responses and perceptions concentrated on comfort and care. Responses related to knowledge were classified based on participants’ perceived confidence in and expertise of their provider. Open and honest communication was captured in survey content focused on understanding information shared and sufficient information sharing, or interview responses related to active listening and receptiveness. Time was categorized as sequential events across data sets.

Management continuity

Three themes within management continuity crossed data sets: social support, care management, and race. Data specific to social support centered around the accessibility and use of resources. Survey data focused on income, insurance, education, marital status, employment, and geographical location. Participant demographics showcased the variation within the participant sample and reflected the lupus population at large. Care management was categorized based on participant perceptions of agreement, role clarity, and collaboration between providers. Race was categorized by responses focused on relatability, bias/discrimination, and race concordance.

Informational continuity

Information exchange was the theme that crossed data sets for informational continuity. Data were specific to sharing medical information between healthcare providers/teams and systems.

Data integration

A joint display was used to present a side-by-side comparison of qualitative and quantitative findings guided by Gutterman et al. and Fetter et al.’s research to ensure adequate interpretation and structure of the display (Additional File 3) [50, 51]. The quantitative column of the joint display consisted of scores equaling 50% or more of the total response per survey item. Exceptions to this rule were “annual income” and “Rheumatologist sends me to PCP for follow-ups,” which fell slightly below 50% (43.5%). Items were also marked with an asterisk (*) to indicate areas where a third response was equal to another response category. The second column consisted of qualitative data, which comprised summarized themes within the associated area of continuity, that were linked to a corresponding quantitative item. The third and final column consisted of inferences made across data sets that demonstrated confirmation, discordance, or expansion of concepts. Notably, quantitative data were underrepresented in some categories and did not always have a qualitative comparator. The steps of data integration are illustrated in Fig. 2.

Integration quantitative and qualitative results

This report only addresses findings of the joint display regarding coherence, divergence, and expansion of concepts, because the study concerns the concepts of continuity and the patient perceived facilitators and barriers to achieving continuity in care coordination. Full quantitative results can be found in Additional File 4, and excerpts from themes explored can be found in Additional File 5.

Results

Recruitment outcomes and participant demographics

Of 225 potential surveys, we received 117 survey submissions. After removing duplicates and assessing for missing data, 108 (48% survey response rate) submitted surveys were used for quantitative analysis, including two with 95% or greater survey items complete. The average age for survey respondents was 45. Most participants were between ages 35 and 44 (36.1%), never married (45.5%), permanently or temporarily disabled (46.3%), and made $15,000 or less (28.7%) Medicaid insured (38.9%), college graduates (39.9%), residing in non-rural settings (57.4%) and were mentees (57.4%) in the PALS study. A subset of survey respondents who agreed to be contacted and completed an interview (n = 21) had an average age of 47, most lived in rural settings (61.9%), and reported having more than two categories of lupus-related disease damage (76.2%). Full results of participant characteristics can be found in Table 1. The sample population was a subpopulation of Black women residing in the Southeastern United States. While this population was specific to a particular geographic area, we achieved “reach,” which concerns access, generalizability, and impact within the subpopulation by including participants with a variety of social and clinical characteristics that can be encountered in the larger population [52].

Relational continuity

Trust

The following survey items had combined scores for “totally agree” and “agree” greater than 50%: “Comfort consulting Primary Care Provider (PCP)/Rheumatologist (Rheum) about doubts or health problems,” “PCP/Rheum cares about me,” and “Recommend PCP/Rheum family/friends.” Percentages for PCP scores ranged from 72.2 to 84.4%, whereas Rheumatologist percentages ranged from 87.9 to 94.4%. Participant (n = 13) interview responses concerning comfort and care centered around the rheumatologist. These responses emphasized the importance of comforting experiences, intimate bonds, feeling secure, complete reliance, and relatability in patient-provider relationships. Trust was consistently mentioned across interview data (n = 13), emphasizing its importance in facilitating patient-provider relationships. Merged data showcases the relationship between patients and their rheumatologists and patients and their PCP. Higher scores for rheumatologists versus PCPs are congruent with interview findings, in which participants reported rheumatologists as the primary manager of their care and PCPs in a supportive role.

Knowledge

For the survey item “Confidence in the professional ability of my PCP/Rheum”, the combined scores for “totally agree” and “agree” were 82.4% for PCP and 94.4% for Rheum. Participant interview responses regarding knowledge (n = 18) highlighted patient perceptions of their provider’s experience managing lupus patients and prescribed care plans. Participants also referenced the differences in diagnostic practices and care outcomes between specialties. Across interviews, rheumatologists were identified as the leader in lupus care and were described as responsive, attentive, invested, supportive, informed, and experienced. Again, findings were congruent, as participant responses and scores confirm the leading role of the rheumatologist.

Open and honest communication

For the survey items “PCP/Rheum understands what I tell them,” “Information about my plan of care that PCP/Rheum gives me is easy to understand,” and “Information about my plan of care that PCP/Rheum gives me is sufficient,” the combined scores for “totally agree” and “agree” accounted for 72.3 to 89.3% of the PCP scores and 91.6 to 96.3% of the rheumatology scores. Interview data (n = 10) captured this theme through responses that emphasized the importance of constructive dialogue, transparency, how information is delivered, and “being heard.” Merged data do not negate or confirm each other. However, merged findings expanded understanding of what patients perceive as facilitators in open and honest communication and emphasized the importance of trust and delivery style.

Time

For the survey item “Always seen by the same PCP/Rheum,” the combined values for totally agree and agree accounted for 87.1% of responses for PCPs and 96.3% of responses for rheumatologists. “Time” in interviews (n = 16) extended beyond seeing the same provider for visits and included the duration of time having the same provider and time spent during office visits. Merged findings show some congruence, as there was alignment between scores and interview responses for seeing the same provider. However, expansion occurred as a few participants discussed a different aspect of time by highlighting the use of residents in rheumatology care. Some participants, however, viewed residents as an interruption in care management because of unfamiliarity with lupus and their personal needs, poor bedside manners, and short rotation periods. Some participants found residents to be a useful second pair of eyes in complex disease management. Despite variation in perspectives concerning residents, those who mentioned their use understood this care structure to be organization-specific and not within the control of residents or students engaged in learning. Provider-patient relationship duration was mostly discussed around rheumatology, where most participants had the same provider for at least three years, and others for closer to thirty years.

In summary, identified barriers to relational continuity were those that hindered patient-provider relationships. These include judgmental tones, dismissive or disregarding behavior, and clinical incompetence. Facilitators of relational continuity centered around trust. Trust within a relationship supported patient-provider engagement, productive dialogue, and receptiveness. Facilitators for establishing trust were the length of the patient-provider relationship, consistent behavior and interactions between patients and providers, honesty, and transparency.

Management continuity

Social support

Most participants made $15,000 or less (28.7%); however, the category with the second-highest representation was those making $60,000 or more (14.8%). Most participants’ primary insurance was Medicare (36.1%) or Medicaid (38.9%). They were mostly disabled (46.3%) or working (34.3%), had never been married (45.5%), had at least a year of college completed (76.9%), and mainly lived in non-rural settings (57.4%). All interviewees (n = 21) mentioned a source of support, whether familial or community-based. Social support was identified as those things that assisted with managing day-to-day activities, improving disease outcomes, or combating feelings such as depression/isolation. Although data across sets were specific to social support, merged data did not confirm or refute each other.

Care management (n = 18) The combined scores for “always” and “often” accounted for greater than 50% of scores for survey items: “PCP agrees with Rheum’s instructions” (74.1%), and “Rheum agrees with PCP’s instructions” (71.3%). Survey items for collaborative care were: “PCP and Rheum communicate” [always (21.3%)/often (22.2%)/do not know (21.3%)]; “Rheum sends me to PCP for follow-ups” (often 21.3%, rarely 22.2%); “PCP and Rheum care is coordinated” (always 25.9%, often 19.4%). The following survey items were specific to provider roles: “Rheum repeats test PCP has already done” (always 16.7%, rarely 37%) and “Rheum orders first prescription of prescribed care” (always 65.7%, often 16.7%). A few interviewees described the correspondence between the rheumatologist and PCP. Usually, this relationship was described as non-existent, or a PCP-rheumatologist contact was only made when there was a lupus concern from the PCP’s perspective.

However, when correspondence between the two occurred, participants often described the interaction as necessary, collaborative, and led by the rheumatologist. The qualitative strand extended beyond the PCP and rheumatologist interactions and provided insight into collaborative care between other disciplines and specialties. Factors impacting care management between providers were perceived to be determined by personal beliefs, role ambiguity, and departmental norms. However, rheumatologists were always described as the primary consultants for care decisions and initiation of treatments or interventions. Additionally, in the qualitative strand, one participant mentioned redundancy in care and stated that it resulted from transitioning from one rheumatologist to another in a different health system.

High scores for patient-perceived agreement between the PCP and rheumatologist were consistent with descriptions of PCP and rheumatologist interactions. Although scores in both areas, “PCP agrees with rheumatologist” and “rheumatologist agrees with PCP,” were high, the value for “PCP agrees” denotes the supportive role of PCPs in lupus care. High scores for first prescriber responsibilities confirm interview content focused on the primary role and responsibility of the rheumatologist. Lower scores for PCP - rheumatologist communication and care coordination were consistent with participant views concerning the frequency or non-existence of a PCP - rheumatologist relationship. This data showed congruency, emphasized the current practice of contact only when warranted, and illustrated the lack of consistent and organized care planning between the two specialties.

Race (n = 18)

The survey item for this theme was: “Better medical care if belonged to a different race or ethnic group;” 34.3% responded “No,” 42.6% selected “Don’t know,” 21.3% marked “Yes,” and 1.9% chose “Refused.” All interviewees reported being pleased with the care provided by their rheumatologist, despite their race, and did not associate race with differences in care received. This is consistent with the 34.3% “No” response. Additionally, most participants stated not knowing others with lupus of a different race to make comparisons, validating the 42.6% “Don’t know” responses. Although shared experiences were specific to participant interactions with their rheumatologist, few shared their overall healthcare experience concerning racial factors. These participants stated their awareness of racial disparities and shared experiences outside of rheumatology. Additionally, a few mentioned how the presence of providers of the same race provided additional comfort and relatability.

In summary, facilitators to management continuity largely depended on available resources to support patient needs (physical, social, and psychological). From the patient’s perspective, facilitators to care management included opportunities for consistent care planning across specialties and effective provider-to-provider engagement. Understanding client needs and delivering adequate care negated racial differences among patients and providers. Identified barriers to management continuity were insufficient planning and a lack of collaboration between providers and patients and providers.

Informational continuity

Information exchange

There were four survey items for this section. Combined scores for “agree” and “often” were above 50% for each item: “Healthcare professionals know past medical history” (63% agree, 38% often), “PCP discusses rheumatology visit” (45.5% agree, 20.4% often), “PCP aware of rheumatologist instruction” (34.3% agree, 22.2% often), “Rheumatologist aware of PCP instruction” (30.6% agree, 25.9% often). Interviewee responses revealed that information sharing varied based on the setting and situation. A few participants stated that in an outpatient setting, information was shared by the patient from personal records or accessed via an electronic patient portal. A couple of interviewees with providers in different health systems expressed a personal responsibility to share clinical information with their various providers.

In comparison, others mentioned hospitalization as a time when information is shared between the healthcare teams, particularly among providers in the same network. Most interviewees did not perceive a standard for sharing information among providers. Factors discussed in interviews concerning information exchange included provider limitations, access, and setting. High scores around awareness of past medical history (PMH) in the healthcare team are consistent with perceived information exchange in the hospital setting. Conversely, lower scores around instruction awareness were congruent with information transfer in outpatient services, as information was requested if necessary, and the patients stated they were more involved in the information exchange process.

In summary, the identified facilitator of informational continuity was the act of sharing. Participants perceived provider-related barriers to include a lack of communication among providers because they were unaware of or lacked out-of-network access to records.

Discussion

This study sought to identify the barriers and facilitators to continuity in care coordination for Black women with SLE. It did so by examining behavioral and care coordination elements that influence continuity in care coordination from a chronic disease and health disparity perspective. Two distinct data sets were used to support the collection and analysis of data specific to the research problem. The data visualization occurred in the form of a joint display to showcase data confirmation, divergence, and concept expansion.

In this study, we found that relational continuity was critical in the care coordination of Black women with SLE. Relational continuity was the precursor to successful information delivery and uptake and influenced the trajectory of overall outcomes. The weightiest facilitator of relational continuity was trust, and this aligned with other previously conducted research examining healthcare interaction and decision-making, which also found that trust increased adherence and receptiveness [53].

However, in other related previous research, behavioral factors such as depression and anxiety, communication style, and compassion heavily influenced adherence in Black women with SLE [54, 55]. It is important to note the variation among Black women with SLE and to consider the geographical, social, and historical contexts that influence their health care experience. This is critical as trust was a primary factor for our population in the southeast region of the US, where historical context may have influenced responses. Another important finding was the concept of race (i.e., race concordance, racism, and discrimination), which was captured in relational and management continuity. This was a noteworthy finding because previously conducted research has determined racism and discrimination to have substantial clinical impacts on Black women with SLE. Our study found that participants were satisfied with their care and did not state occurrences of racism or the desire for race concordance, particularly with their rheumatologist. Findings highlighted the importance of an involved rheumatologist and how their actions centered around patient support and community surpass racial factors. Again, this highlights the variation in experiences and care structures that influence care management and patient-provider relationships.

We also found the secondary role of the PCP to be critical, emphasizing previously conducted research that highlights the PCP as necessary for follow-up and presumptive diagnosis [56–58]. However, our study highlighted the lack of PCP involvement and insufficient knowledge regarding SLE care. To maintain management continuity between rheumatology and primary care, there needs to be role clarity, sufficient provider-to-provider communication and planning, and clear expectations of expected outcomes. However, a substantial barrier is the lack of standardization in SLE care and evolving guidelines which may hinder successful coordination and management continuity between the two specialties [59, 60].

Another finding of our study was that information sharing was vital to informational continuity in care coordination. Previously conducted research highlighted the various avenues for information sharing and the essential factors, such as quality, content, and delivery method [61]. These are all factors to consider regarding how they enhance or inhibit informational continuity and what this means when role ambiguity and inconsistency already exist in SLE care [61].

Given the need for enhanced continuity in care coordination for Black women with SLE, it is crucial that future research builds on the concepts uncovered in this study. Research on clinical care guidelines for treatment and monitoring is sound, systematic, consensus-based, and continuing to evolve. However, research on health care delivery in SLE, specifically the structure of how care is delivered, the involvement and roles of healthcare team members, the use and integration of technology in care planning and information exchange, understanding concepts of transitional care, and the behaviors that influence uptake and implementation of change, is needed. Exploring and evaluating current practices to determine best practices from various perspectives can yield sustainable and modifiable solutions that enhance care and facilitate continuity in care coordination for this population and others with multimorbid conditions.

Lastly, most participants were never married, had some college or graduated from college, were considered permanently or temporarily disabled, and had an income of $15,000.00 or less. Income inequality is linked to social mobility, which in turn negatively affects educational attainment, access, and health outcomes [62, 63]. Traditionally, low socioeconomic status (SES) has been associated with lower life expectancy and poorer health outcomes [62, 63]. However, this population has higher percentages in educational status, but because of disease activity and progression, they experience income inequality due to work limitations and also lack the potential for additional financial support from a spouse. Based on these findings, an area for further study is how SES indicators change throughout the disease course and whether educational attainment enhances health literacy or changes in marital status contribute to improved overall health outcomes. Providing insight into SES through the disease course and its relationship to continuity.

The authors acknowledge the variation in interview length. We do not see this as a weakness because duration does not determine content quality; interviewee responses can reflect uncontrollable factors. Previously reported factors included mood, comprehension, perception, fatigue, and the ability to answer specific questions [64]. Steps were taken to assess the practicality and acceptability of the interview format, question structure, and readability, and the relevance of questions to the target audience. Modifications were made based on the findings of the feasibility assessment. A strength of the study was that it used two data sets to examine one problem, allowing for the confirmation and expansion of concepts. A weakness of this study was that the quantitative strand lacked data comparable to that of the qualitative strand. The quantitative data from this study focused on four specific areas. The part of the survey that assessed continuity concentrated on the patient perspective regarding three concepts related to care providers (rheumatology and primary care), transitions of care, care coherence, and information exchange. The interview guide aimed to explore these concepts included in the survey. While we observed an expansion of ideas around these concepts, some qualitative data emerged that lacked a quantitative counterpart for comparison. For example, patient-reported disease-tracking practices, including whether and how tracking occurred (written or digital), were mentioned in the qualitative data and offered valuable insights into patient agency and mechanisms patients use to support continuity. This finding pertains to both management and informational continuity; however, no specific survey question was designed to assess this aspect. As a result, some emergent qualitative concepts lacked a corresponding quantitative comparison strand. Hence, this study supports the need to further examine the patient’s role in achieving or maintaining continuity in care coordination and the multidisciplinary team’s role.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study has revealed that continuity in care coordination for Black women with SLE is a complex issue. Relational continuity emerges as the facilitator of management and informational continuity in coordinated care. Due to the existing behavioral and social variability, tailored and targeted plans are necessary to improve continuity in care coordination for this population. Successful continuity will also require active engagement from all individuals involved in the care decision process.
