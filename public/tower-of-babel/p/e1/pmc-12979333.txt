Socioeconomic disparities in IDH-wildtype glioblastoma survival: interactions between age and household income in a SEER cohort
Authors: Ying Wang, Kang Xie, Kang Yang, Aihong Luo, Longbo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlioblastoma multiforme (GBM) remains an aggressive brain tumor with a poor prognosis. The financial burden of GBM have raised concerns about accessibility and equity in care. We performed a cohort study to evaluate the impact of household income on survival outcomes in patients with IDH-wildtype GBM.MethodsPatients diagnosed with IDH-wildtype GBM from 2018 to 2021 were retrieved from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database and categorized into low-, middle-, and high-income groups based on household income. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression models were used to analyze survival data and evaluated by hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Further analyses of interaction effects were also performed.ResultsIn total, 6190 patients with IDH-wildtype GBM were included with a median survival of 12.52 months. Multivariate analysis revealed that middle- and high-income patients had significantly better survival outcomes compared to low-income patients (overall survival: HR 0.881 (0.816–0.951), p = 0.001, HR 0.860 (0.792–0.934), p < 0.001; GBM-specific survival: HR 0.866 (0.799–0.937), p < 0.001, HR 0.854 (0.784–0.929), p < 0.001). Such disparities in survival widened over time, particularly for GBM-specific survival. An interaction effect was also found between household income and age, with older, low-income patients with IDH-wildtype GBM showing significantly worse outcomes.ConclusionHousehold income is an independent predictor of survival in patients with IDH-wildtype GBM. Disparities in outcomes are widening over time, and targeted interventions are needed to address the socioeconomic barriers to treatment, particularly for older, low-income patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11060-026-05504-8.

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most common and aggressive primary malignant brain tumor in adults, with a dismal prognosis despite advances in treatment modalities [1, 2]. Over the past decade, the therapeutic landscape for GBM has evolved significantly, incorporating novel approaches such as carmustine wafer implantation, tumor-treating fields (TTFields), anti-angiogenetic agents (bevacizumab), immunotherapy, and oncolytic virus therapy [1, 3–6]. While these innovations have shown promise in improving patient outcomes, their associated high cost presents a significant barrier to access, raising important concerns about the affordability and equity of advanced treatments [7, 8]. Since 2021, the new diagnostic criterion for glioma requires more molecular biomarkers, such as the mutation status of IDH, 1p19q, H3K27m, and CDKN2A/B, further increasing the medical costs [9]. GBM is strictly defined as IDH-wildtype. The continuous improvement in the diagnosis and treatment of GBM has extended patient survival times, but it has also led to an increase in medical costs [7]. A previous study reported that healthcare spending on tumors of the central nervous system increased approximately 2.5 times from 1996 to 2016 [10]. Among them, costs for IDH-wildtype GBM were much higher than for diffuse low-grade glioma [11]. But the interplay between socioeconomic factors—particularly household income—and patient outcomes in IDH-wildtype GBM remains underexplored.

Currently, GBM remains incurable and needs multimodal treatments, which involves significant financial costs. In the United States, treatment costs can exceed $162,550 in the first year (2016 USD) [11]. The financial burden on patients varies due to factors such as insurance coverage, out-of-pocket expenses, and access to care. Globally, healthcare disparities for cancer patients are significant, with variations in access to advanced treatments and medical expertise resulting in inequalities in cancer care. Large-scale studies consistently show that patients in the highest income or socioeconomic quintiles have significantly longer survival, which is linked to more access to high-volume neuro-oncology centers, standard-of-care, and novel treatments [12, 13]. Patients with lower incomes often face a lack of health literacy resulting in delayed care, have a lower chance of having a tumor resection or biopsy, and have higher out-of-pocket costs for medications due to inadequate insurance coverage, which can lead to significant financial hardship and non-adherence to treatment plans [14–16]. Studies in other malignancies, such as thyroid cancer, lung adenocarcinoma, and anal cancer, have demonstrated that patients from lower socioeconomic backgrounds also face worse survival outcomes, often due to limited access to optimal treatments [17–19]. Additionally, breast cancer survivors in lower-income/rural counties had a significantly higher risk for a second primary cancer [20]. These disparities are exacerbated by differences in healthcare policies, insurance coverage, and regional healthcare infrastructure [16]. Earlier studies have identified a positive relationship between good socioeconomic status and survival of patients with GBM. However, Gorenflo et al. found that higher area-level socioeconomic status was not linked to improved survival in studies with small sample size [12]. A systemic review including 230,601 GBM patients further demonstrated no relationship between median household income and survival outcomes [21]. Notably, most data collection in these studies was performed before 2019, when molecular testing was rarely used for diagnosis of GBM.

Age is a significant factor influencing the poor prognosis of GBM, with increasing attention during the process of aging [22]. Older patients often have more comorbidities and functional status, limiting their ability to tolerate surgery and radio-chemotherapy [23]. Additionally, older individuals in the U.S. generally have lower median incomes and are more likely to experience financial vulnerability or economic insecurity, which may increase socioeconomic disparities over time [24].

Socioeconomic status is usually assessed using household income and broken into low, middle and high levels. To better understand the role of socioeconomic status in IDH-wildtype GBM patient outcomes in the molecular era, we conducted a study using data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Our study focuses on patients who underwent surgical resection for IDH-wildtype GBM, with the primary objective of evaluating the impact of household income on both all-cause and tumor-specific mortality, as well as its interaction with demographic factors such as age. By exploring these outcomes, we aim to highlight the critical role of household income and its relationship with age in shaping the prognosis of IDH-wildtype GBM patients and advocate for more equitable access to advanced therapies.

Materials and methods

Search strategy and inclusion criteria

We conducted a registry-based cohort analysis using the SEER database (from 2000 to 2021, 17 registries’ data). SEER is a population-based cancer registry system established by the National Cancer Institute of the National Institutes of Health, with the aim of reducing the cancer burden among the U.S. population [25]. Based on the 2021 World Health Organization classification, GBM is defined as a grade 4, highly aggressive, diffuse astrocytic glioma that is IDH-wildtype. We identified patients with GBM (IDH-wildtype) and downloaded related data (see variables below) using NCI’s SEER*Stat software (version 8.4.5) [26]. Inclusion criteria were histology code 9440/3 with brain molecular marker IDH wild. All patients received GBM-directed surgery and followed radio-chemotherapy. Exclusion criteria were patients with unknown or missing information on age at diagnosis, survival months, status at follow-up, marital status at diagnosis, and household income. Those reported by autopsy only were also excluded. The patient selection process was shown in the flow chart (Fig. 1).

Variables

Variables including age, sex, race, marital status, household income, radiation order, systemic treatment order, survival months, vital status, and specific death classification were extracted. Finally, 6190 records were used for further analyses. Because IDH status was available after 2018, these patients with GBM were all diagnosed between 2018 and 2021. Age was reclassified as below 39, 40–59, 60–79, and above 80 years. The household income in the SEER database represents the median income of the county where the patient resided at the time of diagnosis and has been adjusted to 2022 levels. According to the distribution of household income in the cohort and the real median household income in 2022 ($74,580) announced by the U.S. Census Bureau, we initially classified household income less than $80,000 as the low-income group, $80,000-$99,999 as the middle-income group, and equal to or more than $100,000 as the high-income group.

As the SEER database is freely publicly available to researchers and all data were de-identified with informed consent, the requirement for ethical approval and further patient informed consent was not necessary.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using RStudio version 31.7.7 and R version 4.4.3. The graphical visualizations were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.0. Categorical data are presented as numbers and percentages, while continuous data are presented as means with standard deviation (SD). One-way ANOVA analyses are used to compare the baseline characteristics across low-, middle-, and high-income household groups. Univariate Cox regression analyses were performed to determine the hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) of variables affecting survival of patients. Variables with p < 0.05 were subsequently included in multivariate Cox regression models to identify independent risk factors for survival. Interaction analyses were performed using the “interactionR” package [27]. The relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion (AP), and synergy index (SI) were calculated to assess additive interaction compared to the sum of two individual effects. Specifically, RERI = 0, AP = 0, and SI = 1 indicate no interaction. When additive interaction was confirmed, the multiplicative model was used to estimate whether the interaction was multiplicative (odds ratio, OR = 1) or more than multiplicative (OR > 1) [28, 29]. The age was classified into two groups with the cutoff at 60 years old. The household income was refined as a binary variable based on the cutoff of $80,000. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the GBM patients

A total of 6190 patients diagnosed with GBM (IDH-wildtype) between 2018 and 2021 were assessed from the SEER database. The overall characteristics of these patients were summarized in Table 1. There were 1947 (31.4%) patients in the low-income group, 2413 (39%) patients in the middle-income group, and 1830 (29.6%) in the high-income group. We found that race, marital status, overall survival, and GBM-specific death were significantly different among the three groups (p < 0.001, p = 0.039, p = 0.040, and p = 0.003, respectively, Supplementary Table 1). The low-income group had a higher proportion of Black individuals, the middle-income group had the highest proportion of unmarried individuals, the high-income group exhibited the highest average survival, and the low-income group had the highest rate of GBM-specific death.

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression on overall survival

Univariate analyses showed that increased age and male sex were significantly associated with worse overall survival, while married status and higher household income were significantly associated with better overall survival (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 1A-D). Then we included the significant variables into the multivariate regression model. Multivariate analyses identified age, male sex, marital status, and household income as independent factors for overall survival. Compared to the low-income group, the middle-income group and high-income group showed significantly better survival (HR 0.881 [0.816–0.951], p = 0.001; HR 0.860 [0.792–0.934], p < 0.001; Fig. 2A; Table 2). The nomogram shows how age, sex, marital status, and household income contribute to a patient’s survival (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression on GBM-specific survival

Tumor-specific death remained the predominant cause of mortality with worse survival. We further investigated whether these factors affect GBM-specific death in the cohort. The univariate analyses exhibited that increased age and male sex significantly increased the risk of GBM-specific survival; however, married status and higher household income significantly decreased the risk of GBM-specific survival (Table 3, Supplementary Fig. 1E-H). In multivariate analyses, old age, male sex, and unmarried status were significantly independent risk factors for GBM-specific survival. Furthermore, the middle- and high-income groups exhibited better GBM-specific survival compared to the low-income group (HR 0.866 [0.799–0.937], p < 0.001; HR 0.854 [0.784–0.929], p < 0.001; Fig. 2B; Table 3).

Subgroup analyses among different years

As advancements continue in the diagnosis and treatment of GBM, we delved into how household income impacts GBM survival over time. The proportions of the three income groups remained relatively stable from 2018 to 2021, and the age, sex, and marital status distribution of patients did not show significant changes. However, there was an increasing trend in the proportion of patients aged over 60 years, along with a decreasing trend in both overall survival and GBM-specific death (Supplementary Table 2). Our analysis revealed that, when compared with the low-income group, the HRs (all < 1) for overall survival and GBM-specific death in the middle- and high-income groups showed a decreasing trend over the years, indicating an increasing disparity across household income groups. The risk for overall survival in the middle-income group decreased below the level of significance after 2020, which is one year later than in the high-income group (after 2019). However, the HRs for both groups decreased below the level of significance for GBM-specific survival since 2019 (Supplementary Fig. 3A-B).

Interaction effect between household income and other variables

To explore the interaction effect, household income and age were reclassified as binary variables based on the cutoffs of $80,000 and 60 years old. Patients were stratified into four groups: age < 60 & household income ≥ $80,000, age < 60 & household income < $80,000, age ≥ 60 & household income ≥ $80,000, and age ≥ 60 & household income < $80,000. The Kaplan-Meier survival curves based on the Cox model demonstrated a more obvious overall survival and GBM-specific survival difference across age groups than household income groups. Compared to the age < 60 & household income ≥ $80,000 subgroup, the other three subgroups exhibited worse overall survival (OS: HR 1.06 [0.94, 1.20], p = 0.3426; HR 1.73 [1.59, 1.88], p < 0.001; HR 2.06 [1.87, 2.26], p < 0.001) and GBM-specific survival (HR 1.06 [0.93, 1.20], p = 0.3828; HR 1.71 [1.57, 1.87], p < 0.001; HR 2.07 [1.88, 2.29], p < 0.001, Fig. 3A-B). The effect of household income on survival was observed in the age < 60 subgroup (OS: HR 1.19 [1.09, 1.29], p < 0.001; GBM-specific survival: HR 1.21 [1.11, 1.32], p < 0.0001). While the effect of age on survival generally existed within strata of household income (OS: HR 1.73 [1.59, 1.88], p < 0.001; HR 1.94 [1.72, 2.19], p < 0.0001; GBM-specific survival: HR 1.71 [1.57, 1.87], p < 0.001; HR 1.96 [1.73, 2.22], p < 0.0001; Tables 4 and 5).

We found evidence of a synergistic additive interaction between household income and age on OS and GBM-specific survival. Compared with the referenced group, the RERI was 0.26 ([0.06, 0.46], p = 0.0051) and 0.31 ([0.10, 0.51], p = 0.0019), indicating that the interaction contributed to 26% of the total effect on OS and 31% on GBM-specific survival. The AP was 0.13 ([0.03, 0.22], p = 0.0040) for OS and 0.15 ([0.05, 0.24], p = 0.0013) for GBM-specific survival. The corresponding SI was 1.33 ([1.05, 1.70], p = 0.0099) and 1.40 ([1.08, 1.81], p = 0.0050), suggesting that the joint effect exceeded the sum of their independent effects by 33% and 40%. Multiplicative scales for the interaction between household income and age were not significant for OS and GBM-specific survival (OR 1.15, 95% CI [0.98, 1.34], p = 0.1340; OR 1.15, 95% CI [0.98, 1.34], p = 0.0777, respectively, Tables 4 and 5).

Furthermore, household income showed a significant effect on OS and GBM-specific survival within the strata of sex and marital status; however, no multiplicative or additive interaction effect was identified in the interaction analyses (Supplementary Tables 3–6).

Discussion

In this large, contemporary cohort of 6190 patients with IDH-wildtype GBM from the SEER database (2018–2021), we found that household income is an independent and clinically meaningful predictor of overall survival and GBM-specific survival. After adjusting for age, sex, and marital status, multivariate analyses showed that patients from middle- and high-income households had significantly lower mortality risk compared with those from low-income households. Importantly, income-related disparities not only persisted but also appeared to widen over time, with hazard ratios for both overall and GBM-specific survival in higher-income groups decreasing steadily across diagnostic years. These findings underscore socioeconomic status—specifically household income—as an evolving and increasingly important determinant of outcomes in GBM.

Prior studies have suggested that socioeconomic factors influence outcomes in multiple cancers [30–32], but evidence specific to GBM in the modern therapeutic era has been inconsistent [12, 13, 33, 34]. Some earlier analyses reported income, treatment access, education effects, or social support networks, whereas others found little to no association after adjustment for treatment variables. Our study extends this literature by using a recent, 2021 WHO–classification cohort restricted to IDH-wildtype GBM, including 6190 subjects, thereby reducing molecular heterogeneity. The consistent protective effect observed for both middle- and high-income groups—across overall and GBM-specific survival—supports the premise that socioeconomic factor-related differences remain highly relevant even in a disease dominated by biological aggressiveness.

Several mechanisms may explain the observed association. Higher-income households may have better access to specialized neuro-oncologic care, including high-volume neurosurgical centers, advanced radiation modalities, and timely chemoradiation [35]. They may also experience fewer delays between symptom onset and diagnosis, improving postoperative performance status and eligibility for adjuvant therapy. Financial stability also allows for long-term chemoradiation and may support improved treatment adherence, the ability to manage supportive care needs, and the capacity to pursue and benefit from clinical trials or diverse de novo treatments. Though a large number of effective drugs and treatments have emerged, they are expensive and inconsistent with the level of socioeconomic development. Treatment affordability is a significant barrier for low- and middle-income families; however, access to care is also influenced by insurance status. In some cases, low-income patients covered by Medicaid may encounter fewer barriers to treatment than middle- and high-income patients with private insurance, whose coverage can be more variable. Additionally, income likely correlates with broader social determinants—including health literacy, comorbid burden, and environmental exposures—that collectively influence long-term outcomes. The parallel effects observed for overall and GBM-specific survival suggest these factors relate primarily to cancer-directed care rather than competing risks.

An important novel observation is that income-related survival disparities became more pronounced over the study period from 2018 to 2021. The hazard ratios favoring middle- and high-income groups strengthened after 2019–2020, particularly for GBM-specific mortality. Along with the evolution of diagnosis and classification of glioma, molecular biomarkers and sequencing become necessary during clinical practice [9]. This trend may reflect increasing complexity of GBM management—including molecular diagnostics, genetic sequencing, novel treatment vs. standardized treatment, and supportive care—where higher socioeconomic resources provide increasing advantage. Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic disproportionately affected medically vulnerable and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations such as patients suffering from cancer, with advanced age, or with lower-middle household income, contributing to the observed decline in both overall survival and GBM-specific death from 2019 to 2021. Disruption in access to surgery, radiotherapy, comorbidity treatment, or follow-up care may have exacerbated preexisting disparities [36–39]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, patients showed a more pronounced and earlier decrease in performance status, and respiratory disease became a crucial cause of death for gliomas [36, 37]. However, Neff C. et al. reported that the impact of COVID-19 on GBM treatment was minimal compared to head and neck cancer [40]. Our findings highlight the dynamic nature of socioeconomic risk and the need for continuous monitoring as costly novel therapies redefine clinical practice.

Our interaction analyses provide further insight into how socioeconomic factors intersect with biological vulnerability. Although age remained the dominant prognostic factor, we observed significant additive synergistic interaction between age ≥ 60 and income <$80,000 for both overall and GBM-specific survival. The RERI, AP, and SI values indicate that approximately one-quarter to one-third of the excess mortality in older, low-income patients is attributable to the joint rather than independent effects of age and income. This suggests that older patients may be particularly sensitive to socioeconomic barriers—potentially due to greater dependence on support systems, higher frailty, more complications and chronic disease challenging tumor-specific treatment, or reduced expectation and ability to access or tolerate aggressive therapy [36, 38]. In contrast, no significant interaction was detected between income and sex or marital status, indicating that income exerts a relatively consistent effect across these demographic groups.

Taken together, these findings reinforce that socioeconomic disadvantage—specifically lower household income—remains a powerful and modifiable determinant of GBM outcomes in the modern era. Interventions to reduce inequities should include improved access to high-volume neuro-oncology centers, patient-navigation programs, financial counseling, transportation support, and targeted outreach to older, low-income patients who appear particularly vulnerable. As novel therapeutics, molecular profiling tools, and clinical trials increasingly shape GBM care, ensuring equitable access will be critical to avoid widening survival gaps.

This study includes a large, contemporary, population-based cohort, restricted to IDH-wildtype GBM. We estimated both overall and tumor-specific survival through univariate and multivariate models and interaction effects using both multiplicative and additive scales. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. SEER lacks granular clinical information such as tumor sites, Karnofsky performance status, extent of resection, whether functional areas are involved, adjuvant therapy details, clinical trial participation, and comorbidity burden, which may confound income–survival associations. Patients were selected by pathological diagnosis post tumor resection or biopsy, while the proportion of patients who were suspected of glioma but without pathological confirmation was not reported. In the SEER program, household income was defined as the median household income for the patient’s county of residence rather than individually, potentially introducing misclassification. Not everyone living in a “low-income” county is poor, nor is everyone in a “high-income” county affluent. If a patient moves to a different county, the income classification may be wrong. Moreover, misclassification is particularly high in rural areas. Residual confounding from unmeasured socioeconomic or clinical variables cannot be excluded. Finally, SEER does not provide data on treatment adherence or delays, which may mediate income-related disparities.

Conclusions

In summary, household income is a strong and independent predictor of survival in patients with IDH-wildtype GBM, with disparities that appear to be widening in recent years. Income interacts synergistically with age—particularly affecting older, lower-income patients—highlighting the need for targeted strategies to ensure equitable access to high-quality neuro-oncologic care. Addressing socioeconomic inequities may represent one of the most actionable pathways to improving outcomes in this highly lethal disease.
