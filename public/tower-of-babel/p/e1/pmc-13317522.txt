Retrieval of an atherosclerotic plaque–vessel wall complex during mechanical thrombectomy: illustrative case
Authors: Shohei Yoshida, Hiroyuki Ikeda, Natsuki Akaike, Minami Uezato, Makoto Wada, Masanori Kinosada, Yoshitaka Kurosaki, Masaki Chin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDRetrieval of an atherosclerotic plaque–vessel wall complex as a single specimen from the middle cerebral artery during mechanical thrombectomy (MT) is exceedingly rare.OBSERVATIONSA 74-year-old man with atrial fibrillation underwent MT for right M1 occlusion. A red thrombus and a yellowish-white specimen were retrieved, and successful reperfusion was achieved. Histopathological examination of the retrieved specimen revealed an atherosclerotic plaque attached to the arterial wall, consistent with retrieval of the plaque–vessel wall complex as a single specimen. Postoperatively, a subarachnoid hemorrhage was observed around the M2 inferior trunk, where vessel wall retrieval likely induced arterial dissection. Antithrombotic therapy and strict blood pressure control were implemented. Although progressive stenosis of the M2 inferior trunk was noted during follow-up, the vessel diameter eventually normalized without aneurysmal transformation.LESSONSMT with a stent retriever in vessels harboring atherosclerotic plaque may cause intracranial arterial dissection. Histopathological evaluation of retrieved specimens can help identify iatrogenic dissection. Mechanical thrombectomy (MT) is the standard treatment for acute ischemic stroke due to large-vessel occlusion.1,2 However, procedure-related complications, such as arterial dissection, vessel perforation, and subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), may occur.3 In particular, deployment and retraction of a stent retriever (SR) can impose mechanical stress on the vascular endothelium and atherosclerotic plaque, potentially leading to arterial injury and dissection.4 In recent years, histopathological evaluation of retrieved specimens has been reported to provide valuable insights into the embolic source and to inform therapeutic decision-making.5 Here, we report a case of middle cerebral artery occlusion associated with underlying atherosclerotic plaque in which MT resulted in retrieval of a plaque–vessel wall complex together with a red thrombus. The procedure was complicated by intracranial arterial dissection and a subsequent SAH. Based on the histopathological findings of the retrieved specimen, we discuss the mechanism of dissection and considerations regarding the initiation of antithrombotic therapy.

Illustrative Case

History and Examination

A 74-year-old man with a premorbid modified Rankin Scale score of 0 presented with sudden-onset left hemiparesis. He had a history of atrial fibrillation but was not receiving anticoagulation therapy. He arrived at our hospital 93 minutes after symptom onset. On arrival, he exhibited right conjugate gaze deviation, left hemiparesis, and dysarthria. The National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score was 8. Plain head CT showed no early ischemic changes (Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score [ASPECTS] 10). A hyperdense artery sign was observed from the distal M1 segment to the proximal M2 segment of the right middle cerebral artery, whereas no hyperdense artery sign was observed in the M2 inferior trunk (Fig. 1A–C). Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) demonstrated faint hyperintense lesions in the right lentiform nucleus and right parietal lobe (DWI-ASPECTS score 9) (Fig. 1D). MR angiography (MRA) revealed occlusion of the right M1 segment (Fig. 1E). On fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) imaging, the right M2 inferior trunk appeared isointense. Intravenous thrombolysis was initiated 173 minutes after symptom onset, and MT was started 187 minutes after onset.

Endovascular Surgery

Under local anesthesia, the right femoral artery was punctured, and a 9-Fr balloon guide catheter (Optimo EPD, Tokai Medical Products) was advanced into the right internal carotid artery (ICA). Right ICA angiography demonstrated distal M1 occlusion (Fig. 2A). An aspiration catheter (AC) (React 71, Medtronic) was advanced into the right ICA. A microcatheter (Phenom 21, Medtronic) and a microguidewire (Traxcess, Terumo) were navigated into the right M2 inferior trunk. Contrast injection through the microcatheter positioned distal to the thrombus and through the AC demonstrated a thrombus in the distal M2 inferior trunk with partial recanalization of the right M2 superior trunk (Fig. 2B and C). An SR (Solitaire X 4 × 40 mm, Medtronic) was deployed from the right M2 inferior trunk to the distal M1 segment. Angiography through the AC demonstrated antegrade flow in the M2 inferior trunk (Fig. 2D). The AC was then advanced to the proximal M2 inferior trunk (Fig. 2E). The SR was withdrawn into the AC under continuous suction, and both devices were retracted into the balloon guide catheter. A red thrombus was retrieved (Fig. 2F). Follow-up right ICA angiography showed recanalization of the proximal M2 inferior trunk with persistent distal occlusion (Fig. 2G). The AC was exchanged for a Vecta 46 (Stryker). Angiography through the microcatheter positioned at the proximal M2 inferior trunk confirmed the origin of the M2 inferior trunk (Fig. 2H). The lesion was successfully crossed using a J-shaped microguidewire (Fig. 2I). Although resistance was encountered during device navigation, the microcatheter was successfully advanced into the M2 inferior trunk. Distal angiography through the microcatheter demonstrated no apparent abnormalities beyond the lesion (Fig. 2J). An SR (Solitaire X 4 × 40 mm) was redeployed in the M2 inferior trunk. The AC was advanced to the proximal M2 inferior trunk (Fig. 2K). Both the SR and AC were retracted into the balloon guide catheter. A yellowish-white specimen was retrieved (Fig. 2L). Subsequent right ICA angiography demonstrated recanalization of the M2 inferior trunk with vessel wall irregularity and luminal narrowing (Fig. 2M). Successful reperfusion (Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction grade 2b) was achieved 48 minutes after groin puncture. Ten minutes after reperfusion, angiography showed progression of luminal narrowing in the M2 inferior trunk (Fig. 2N), which improved 20 minutes after reperfusion (Fig. 2O). These findings were considered consistent with vasospasm, and the procedure was concluded. Right common carotid angiography revealed no significant stenosis at the origin of the right ICA (Fig. 2P).

Postoperative Course

Neurological symptoms improved immediately after MT. Plain CT performed immediately after the procedure demonstrated hyperdense areas in the right sylvian fissure and convexity (Fig. 3A). SAH was diagnosed, and strict blood pressure control was implemented. On postoperative day (POD) 2, CT showed persistent SAH around the right M2 inferior trunk (Fig. 3B). CT angiography (CTA) demonstrated good patency of the M2 inferior trunk (Fig. 3C). No atherosclerotic plaques considered to be potential embolic sources were identified in the aortic arch or right carotid artery (Fig. 3D and E). Transthoracic echocardiography performed on POD 2 revealed no definite findings suggestive of a cardioembolic source. However, as atrial fibrillation had been present since admission, the etiology was considered cardioembolic, and anticoagulation therapy was initiated. DWI revealed scattered hyperintense lesions in the right middle cerebral artery territory (Fig. 3F). Volumetric isotropic turbo spin echo acquisition (VISTA) imaging demonstrated mild hyperintensity in the vessel wall of the M2 inferior trunk (Fig. 3G). Anticoagulation therapy with a direct oral anticoagulant was initiated on POD 3. However, the patient developed headache and vomiting on POD 4. CT demonstrated enlargement of the SAH around the M2 inferior trunk (Fig. 3H), and anticoagulation therapy was discontinued. CTA on POD 4 revealed no identifiable bleeding source in the M2 inferior trunk (Fig. 3I). Digital subtraction angiography performed on POD 5 demonstrated vessel wall irregularity in the M2 inferior trunk (Fig. 3J). T1-weighted MRI of the aortic arch on POD 7 showed no hyperintense atherosclerotic plaque (Fig. 3K). On POD 8, VISTA imaging demonstrated a reduction in signal intensity within the previously noted vessel wall hyperintensity of the M2 inferior trunk (Fig. 3L). T1-weighted carotid MRI on POD 9 revealed no hyperintense atherosclerotic plaque in the carotid arteries (Fig. 3M). Histopathological examination of the retrieved specimens was available on POD 9. The red thrombus consisted predominantly of aggregated erythrocytes in the center, with platelet aggregates on the surface. No vessel wall or plaque components were identified (Fig. 4A). In contrast, the yellowish-white specimen demonstrated fibrotically thickened intima extending to the media, along with clustered cholesterol clefts consistent with atherosclerotic plaque, forming a plaque–vessel wall complex (Fig. 4B–E). These findings suggested retrieval of the vessel wall and plaque from the M2 inferior trunk. Right ICA angiography on POD 11 demonstrated preserved distal flow with severe stenosis of the M2 inferior trunk (Fig. 5A). CTA on POD 14 and MRA on POD 15 confirmed persistent severe stenosis (Fig. 5B and C). Intracranial arterial dissection of the M2 inferior trunk was diagnosed, and cilostazol (200 mg/day) was initiated. CTA on POD 21 demonstrated residual but partially dilated stenosis (Fig. 5D). CTA on POD 29 showed further dilated stenosis (Fig. 5E). Cilostazol was discontinued, and anticoagulation therapy with a direct oral anticoagulant was resumed for secondary prevention of cardioembolic stroke. CTA on POD 36 demonstrated no restenosis (Fig. 5F). The patient was discharged home on POD 40 with a modified Rankin Scale score of 0. CTA performed 2 months after onset demonstrated proximal dilatation of the M2 inferior trunk (Fig. 5G). Serial MRA at 3, 6, and 7 months showed gradual improvement of the stenosis (Fig. 5H–J). VISTA imaging at 7 months demonstrated no residual vessel wall hyperintensity (Fig. 5K). Right ICA angiography at 7 months showed good patency and flow in the M2 inferior trunk without aneurysmal transformation (Fig. 5L). Subsequent periodic MRI examinations showed no recurrence of infarction or vascular changes in the M2 inferior trunk.

Discussion

Observations

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case in which a plaque–vessel wall complex was retrieved together with a thrombus during SR retraction at the occlusion site. Retrieval of the plaque–vessel wall complex was likely associated with intracranial arterial dissection and subsequent postoperative SAH. In this case, anticoagulation therapy was initiated postoperatively without recognition of the arterial dissection as the underlying cause of SAH, which may have contributed to hemorrhage progression. Hemorrhagic complications after MT are well-established predictors of poor outcomes,6 and early initiation of antithrombotic therapy in the presence of intracranial bleeding carries a risk of exacerbating hemorrhage.7 The reported incidence of iatrogenic arterial dissection during endovascular procedures ranges from 0.6% to 3.9%,8,9 and diagnosis may be challenging when based solely on angiographic findings. In this case, no typical angiographic signs of dissection were observed during MT. However, careful evaluation of the retrieved specimen could have led to earlier recognition of arterial injury, which may have influenced postoperative management decisions.

MT is not merely a revascularization procedure but also provides an opportunity for etiological diagnosis through evaluation of retrieved specimens. Macroscopic and histopathological evaluation of thrombi has been reported to help identify the embolic source.5 In addition to thrombotic material, tumor tissue or plaques of aortic origin may be present, which may require treatment strategies distinct from those for simple thromboembolism.5,10 Therefore, careful evaluation of retrieved specimens can complement etiological assessment and assist in determining postoperative antithrombotic therapy.

Histopathologically, atherosclerotic plaques are characterized by features such as cholesterol clefts, foam cells, and a fibrous cap.11 In this case, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining demonstrated intimal thickening extending from the intima to the media, accompanied by cholesterol clefts, as well as degeneration and thinning of the media. These findings were consistent with atherosclerotic changes, supporting the conclusion that an atherosclerotic plaque was retrieved.

Although it is difficult to completely exclude the possibility that the retrieved plaque originated from the aortic arch or carotid artery,11 several findings suggest a local origin. The pathological specimen demonstrated a plaque attached to the vessel wall retrieved as a single specimen, and MRI of the aortic arch and carotid arteries revealed no evident hyperintense plaques. Taken together, these findings suggest that the plaque was already present in the vessel wall of the M2 inferior trunk and was retrieved during MT.

In this case, the mechanism of intracranial arterial dissection may have involved mechanical stress exerted by the SR on a vulnerable vessel wall associated with underlying intracranial atherosclerotic disease (ICAD). Vessel wall injury caused by SRs has been demonstrated in multiple experimental animal studies.12 In vessels with atherosclerotic changes, thinning of the fibrous cap and endothelial fragility increase susceptibility to iatrogenic dissection during traction maneuvers with an SR.13 In this case, repeated SR passes in a small-caliber, plaque-bearing vessel may have contributed to the development of arterial dissection.14,15 In the treatment of large-vessel occlusion associated with ICAD, there is no consensus on the optimal thrombectomy strategy. Analyses focusing on ICAD-related large-vessel occlusion have suggested that AC and SR are broadly comparable in final recanalization rates, 90-day functional outcomes, and hemorrhagic complications (Supplemental Table 1).16–18 In the studies by Yoo et al.16 and Li et al.,17 no significant differences in first-pass recanalization rates were observed between AC and SR. Results were inconsistent across the three studies,16–18 possibly reflecting differences in study design and procedural strategy. Similar findings were reported in a study comparing first-line thrombectomy strategies for ICAD-related large-vessel occlusion, which also demonstrated that neither AC nor SR was clearly superior.19 No significant differences in hemorrhagic complications were observed between AC and SR across the three studies.16–18 These findings suggest that selecting the appropriate device alone is not enough to fully mitigate the risk of vessel injury in cases of ICAD-related large-vessel occlusion.

From a mechanistic perspective, repeated deployment and retrieval of an SR may increase mechanical stress on the vessel wall, whereas AC may be less directly injurious and could be advantageous for endothelial preservation. Therefore, an aspiration-first strategy may be considered from a safety standpoint, particularly in cases in which ICAD is suspected. However, aspiration techniques can still pose a risk of vascular injury if multiple passes are performed or excessive suction is applied, highlighting the importance of flexible intraoperative decision-making based on procedural findings.

In ICAD-related lesions, the primary cause of occlusion is usually not the thrombus itself, but rather the underlying atherosclerotic stenotic lesion, which is often more susceptible to device-related injury. A meta-analysis of a general population with large-vessel occlusion has shown that direct endovascular thrombectomy and bridging therapy are broadly comparable in efficacy and safety; however, the noninferiority of omitting intravenous recombinant tissue plasminogen activator remains controversial.20 Furthermore, in ICAD-related lesions, not only the thrombus but also the underlying pathology should be considered. Adjunctive thrombolytic therapy may help dissolve microthrombi and improve distal perfusion. Therefore, in cases in which ICAD is suspected, an individualized treatment strategy should be considered, including an aspiration-first approach, the potential use of bridging therapy, and optimization of device selection.

In this case, atrial fibrillation was present prior to the procedure. The occlusion was relatively long and was accompanied by a hyperdense sign, leading to the assumption of a cardioembolic etiology, resulting in the selection of a combined technique using an SR. Once the yellowish-white specimen had been retrieved, the possibility of vessel wall injury associated with underlying ICAD should have been considered, with conversion to an aspiration-only strategy. Although the relationship between anticoagulation therapy and progression of hemorrhagic complications remains unclear, its involvement cannot be completely ruled out, and it may have been necessary to reconsider the treatment strategy, including antiplatelet therapy. This case highlights the importance of making flexible treatment decisions based on a pathophysiological assessment of the lesion when selecting thrombectomy strategies for ICAD.

SAH after MT is a well-recognized complication. The proposed mechanisms include microperforation of the vessel wall, disruption of the blood-brain barrier due to endothelial injury, and reperfusion injury resulting from recanalization.21 In contrast, most intracranial arterial dissections present with ischemic symptoms; however, rupture of a false lumen or intramural hematoma toward the adventitial side may result in SAH, occasionally leading to a severe clinical course.22 In this case, anticoagulation therapy was initiated postoperatively without recognition of arterial dissection as the underlying cause of SAH, which may have been associated with progression of hemorrhagic complications. Although prompt discontinuation of anticoagulation therapy and strict blood pressure control prevented further progression, the possibility of a fatal outcome cannot be ruled out. If arterial injury had been identified earlier through careful evaluation of the retrieved specimen, more cautious consideration might have been given to the initiation of antithrombotic therapy. Intracranial arterial dissection may also lead to delayed stenosis or occlusion, which has been attributed to endothelial injury caused by SR manipulation and subsequent intimal hyperplasia.23 In this case, cilostazol, which has been reported to have endothelial protective effects,24 was administered during the acute phase. After confirmation of a gradual improvement in dissection-related stenosis, therapy was changed to anticoagulation alone, taking into account the risk of hemorrhage and the need for secondary prevention of cardioembolic stroke. Follow-up imaging was continued throughout the chronic phase after the resumption of anticoagulation therapy.

Stent placement for intracranial arterial dissection is still a controversial topic, with no consensus on its indications or the optimal timing for its performance. Previous reports have primarily focused on major proximal arteries, including the ICA, M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery, proximal vertebral artery, and basilar artery.25,26 These major arteries supply large perfusion territories and have a significant hemodynamic impact. Therefore, stent placement may be considered in cases of progressive ischemia or resistance to medical therapy. When determining the indication for stent placement, multiple factors should be comprehensively evaluated, including the severity and progression of neurological symptoms; vascular characteristics such as vessel diameter and lesion location; hemodynamic status, including collateral circulation and the presence of perfusion deficits; technical considerations such as device accessibility; and the risk of hemorrhage in the acute phase and the feasibility of initiating dual antiplatelet therapy. In contrast, distal vessels, including the M2 segment, are generally not considered typical candidates for acute-phase stenting.25 This is due to their smaller caliber and the technical difficulty of manipulating the device, their relatively limited perfusion territory and the potential for compensation via collateral circulation, and the need to balance the risks of branch occlusion and hemorrhagic complications. In this case, the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score was relatively low. Antegrade flow had been restored in the proximal vessel (M1), and the responsible lesion was in the M2 segment. In addition, serial intraoperative angiography demonstrated a gradual improvement over time. Based on these findings, conservative management without stent placement was selected. This case provides valuable insights into selecting initial thrombectomy strategies and managing complications arising from large-vessel occlusion associated with ICAD.

Lessons

MT with an SR in vessels harboring atherosclerotic plaque may cause intracranial arterial dissection. Histopathological evaluation of retrieved specimens can help identify iatrogenic arterial injury. Appropriate selection of antithrombotic therapy, strict blood pressure control, and serial vascular imaging may help prevent progression of intracranial hemorrhage.
