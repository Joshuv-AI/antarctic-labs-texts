Key resistance-associated mutations in multidrug-resistant tuberculosis: a genomic study from Shanghai, China, with a focus on aminoglycosides
Authors: Yinjuan Guo, Jinghui Yang, Hongxiu Wang, Wei Sha, Fangyou Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The aminoglycosides are essential drugs in the treatment of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis. Antibiotic resistance of Mycobacterium tuberculosis is a major public health concern worldwide. Therefore, it is of great significance to characterize the mutations by which susceptible M. tuberculosis evolves into drug resistance. A total of 110 clinical isolates of MDR-TB were used for Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and phenotypic drug susceptibility testing to aminoglycosides, including amikacin, kanamycin, and streptomycin. Among 110 clinical MDR-TB strains, 7 (6.36%) were cross-resistant to amikacin and kanamycin, and 81 (73.64%) were resistant to streptomycin. The resistance rates to rifabutin, streptomycin, moxifloxacin, ofloxacin, and ethambutol were observed in 79.09%, 73.64%,35.45%, 34.55%, and 30.91%, respectively. Among the 81 MDR-TB strains resistant to streptomycin, the MIC values of streptomycin against MDR-TB showed the following distribution: 61 strains (75.31%) demonstrated resistance with MICs ≥ 32 µg/ml. The rrs gene mutations were detected in 6 (85.71%; 6/7) of the amikacin/kanamycin-resistant isolates, with the most frequent mutation being A1401G. Mutations in the eis promoter were identified at two positions in 3 amikacin/kanamycin-susceptible and streptomycin-resistant isolates (-10G > A and − 37G > T). In 80.25% (65 out of 81) of streptomycin-resistant MDR-TB isolates, two types of amino acid substitutions associated with rpsL mutations were identified. K43R was observed in 86.15% of isolates with mutations in rpsL, followed by K88R at 13.85%. In conclusion, current critical concentration methods and the design of molecular diagnostics need to be revisited to provide more accurate assessments of streptomycin resistance for rspL mutation-bearing isolates.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) was among the leading causes of death worldwide, with an estimated 13.4 million new cases and 1.25 million deaths in 2023 [1]. Eight countries, including China, account for two-thirds of the total number of new TB cases [1]. The emergence of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) poses a significant challenge to our endeavors in effectively managing and eradicating the disease. MDR strains of Mycobacterium tuberculosis are defined as those that are resistant to the key first-line drugs, rifampin (RIF) and isoniazid (INH). In Hangzhou, eastern China, the overall multiple drug resistance (MDR) rate was 5.3% between 2012 and 2022, declining to 2.2% in 2022, which is lower than rates reported in other studies from different parts of China and other countries [2]. A study from Shanghai, China, had reported that the prevalence of fluoroquinolone resistance among MDR-TB patients was high, and at least one-third were transmitted [3]. All forms of drug-resistant TB are more challenging to diagnose, associated with higher mortality and morbidity, and higher rates of post-TB lung damage [4].

Aminoglycosides, second-line antituberculosis drugs, and strong antibacterial activity against M. tuberculosis have played a dominant role in the treatment of MDR-TB. According to the 2019 guidelines provided by the World Health Organization (WHO), amikacin continues to retain its status as a recommended drug in the treatment of MDR-TB. Still, it is not included in the latest guidelines [1].

Streptomycin, the first antimicrobial agent with proven activity against M. tuberculosis in the 1940 s, was discontinued because of the hazards associated with giving injections in regions where HIV is prevalent and due to growing antibiotic resistance [5]. Kanamycin was identified and developed in the 1950 s due to its broad-spectrum antibacterial properties, which also extended to strains of M. tuberculosis that were resistant to streptomycin [6]. Although XDR strains often exhibit cross-resistance to other second-line injectable drugs, there might be unutilized opportunities for employing streptomycin against low-level resistant bacteria [7]. Although amikacin is classified as one of the class C adjunct drugs recommended by the WHO for the long-term treatment of MDR-TB in 2019, aminoglycosides still play an important role currently, where MDR-TB prevention and second-line anti-TB drugs are limited, especially in developing countries.

Aminoglycosides exert their action on bacterial ribosomes and inhibit the process of protein synthesis, consequently inhibiting bacterial viability. Furthermore, they possess the ability to enhance the permeability of bacterial cell membranes, thereby facilitating the ingress of drugs into bacterial cells [8]. The resistance mechanism of action for aminoglycosides in M. tuberculosis is mainly related to gene mutations, including rpsL, rrs, eis, and gid [9–11]. The presence of a mutation-carrier gene called rrs, which codes for the 16 S rRNA, has been correlated with high-level resistance to amikacin in isolated cases of M. tuberculosis [12]. The increased expression of the eis gene, which encodes the aminoglycoside acetyltransferase, is associated with a lower resistance to both amikacin and kanamycin [13]. The mechanisms behind this elevated expression include point mutations in the eis promoter region as well as mutations in the 50 untranslated regions of the whiB7 gene [14]. Streptomycin induces the mistranslation of mRNA to protein by tightly binding to 16 S rRNA and ribosomal protein S12 encoded by rrs and rpsL, respectively. Mutations in rpsL are associated with high-level streptomycin resistance, while mutations in the 530 loop and 912 regions of rrs are associated with intermediate streptomycin resistance [15]. In addition, mutations in gidB encoding a methyltransferase enzyme responsible for methylating a G nucleotide at position 518 of the rrs are known to confer low-level streptomycin resistance [16].

In China, TB diagnosis uses both phenotypic and genotypic methods. Phenotypic methods, such as culture-based drug susceptibility testing (DST), determine drug resistance by observing bacterial growth in the presence of anti-TB drugs. Genotypic methods, such as line probe assays (LPA) and whole genome sequencing (WGS), detect specific gene mutations associated with drug resistance. The WHO has emphasized the critical role of early, rapid, and accurate identification of M. tuberculosis and determination of drug susceptibility in the treatment and management of the disease. Although phenotypic drug susceptibility testing remains essential for confirming resistance to second-line drugs not covered by initial molecular testing and resolving discrepancies with genotypic results, it takes longer time. Rapid molecular testing can be used as an initial diagnostic tool.

Therefore, the objective of this study is to carefully assess and describe mutations in rpsL, rrs, eis, and gid that lead to aminoglycoside resistance, as well as evaluate their correlation with the prevailing genotypes in China. Such investigations offer valuable insight for the advancement and implementation of swift diagnostic tools. As a result, they contribute significantly to the management of MDR-TB.

Methods and materials

Samples

The 110 multidrug-resistant M. tuberculosis clinical strains were screened from culture-positive M. tuberculosis in Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital in eastern China from January 2022 to December 2023. Duplicate M. tuberculosis clinical isolates were excluded. Among 110 M. tuberculosis isolates, 83 (75.45%) patients were male, and 27 (24.55%) were female. All isolates were taken from human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-negative patients with TB in Shanghai who were diagnosed and treated at SPH.

Drug susceptibility testing

The broth microdilution method for determining the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of M. tuberculosis was developed based on traditional drug susceptibility testing, following the guidelines set forth by the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) in document M24-A2. MIC is the lowest concentration of an antibacterial agent expressed in mg/L (µg/mL), which, under strictly controlled in vitro conditions, completely prevents visible growth of the test strain of an organism. The lowest concentration of the drug that did not show visible growth was recorded as the MIC for the respective drug. MIC determination for three aminoglycosides (amikacin, kanamycin, and streptomycin) and 9 drugs (RIF, INH, rifabutin[RFB], cycloserine[CYC], ethambutol[EMB], ethionamide[ETH], moxifloxacin[MXF], ofloxacin[OFL], pyrazinamide[PYA]) was performed using Myco TB Sensititre plates (TREK Diagnostic Systems), per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, colonies on Middlebrook 7H10 agar in log-phase growth were then mixed with a saline-tween solution and glass beads, adjusted to a McFarland standard of 0.5 turbidity, and allowed to settle for 15 min. A portion of this mixture was transferred to Middlebrook 7H9 broth with OADC and vortexed. Subsequently, 100 µl of this solution was inoculated into each well of the MYCOTB plate. The plates were sealed with permanent plastic seals provided with the test kit and incubated at 37 °C in an environment with 5% carbon dioxide. Contamination of the plates was checked at 24 and 48 h. Thereafter, bacterial growth was monitored at specific intervals by using a plate reader.

Whole genome resequencing (WGS)

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and analysis were performed as previously described [17]. The frozen isolates were cultured on Löwenstein-Jensen Medium, and the genomic DNA was extracted using the CTAB method of DNA purification. Each extracted DNA was quantified using a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen). Sequencing libraries were prepared following the manufacturer’s protocol using the Illumina TruSeq DNA LT library preparation kit. Sequencing was performed using an Illumina HiSeq instrument with a 2 × 150 paired-end sequencing method. In data quality control, SOAPnuke was used to remove adapter sequences and reads less than 150 bp, and low-quality data (Phred < 20) were filtered out to obtain clean reads. Samples with a sequencing depth of less than 100× or a genome coverage of less than 90% were considered to have unqualified sequencing quality and were not included in the study. All resistance gene-related mutations were identified using TB-Profiler software (https://github.com/jodyphelan/TBProfiler) under default parameters. Then, the whole genome data of 110 strains of MDR-TB were analyzed using Snippy software (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy, version 4.6.0), and core single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) sites were identified using H37Rv (NC_000962.3) as the reference strain.

Results

Drug resistance profiles

Of the 110 MDR-TB isolates that were included in this study, 98 (89.1%) belonged to lineage 2, and 12 (10.9%) to lineage 4. Drug susceptibility profiles are shown in Fig. 1A. Among 110 MDR-TB cases, the resistance rates to RFB, streptomycin, MXF, OFL and EMB were observed to be79.09%, 73.64%, 35.45%, 34.55%, and 30.91%, respectively. As shown in Fig. 1B, among the 7 amikacin-resistant isolates, the resistance rates to MXF, OFL, and streptomycin reached 71.43%, 71.43%, and 85.71%, respectively. Among the 81 streptomycin-resistant isolates, the resistance rates (Fig. 1C) to the same antimicrobial drugs described above were similar to those of the 110 MDR-TB strains. As shown in Fig. 1D, the MIC values of RIF, a first-line anti-tuberculosis drug, against the 7 amikacin-resistant MDR-TB strains were almost all greater than 16 µg/ml, and the MIC values of INH were distributed between 0.5 and 4 µg/ml. Among the 7 amikacin-resistant MDR-TB strains, 3, 4, 5, and 5 were resistant to EMB, RFB, MXF, and OFL, respectively, but were sensitive to ETH, PAS, and CYC. Among 81 streptomycin-resistant MDR-TB isolates, the resistance rates to CYC, EMB, ETH, MXF, OFL, PAS, and RFB were 45.9%, 45.6%, 12.1%, 39.2%, 38.1%, 9.8%, and 80.4%, respectively. Among the 82 aminoglycoside-resistant MDR-TB strains, we detected mutations associated with other anti-TB drugs. Among the RIF-resistant strains, 74 bacteria had mutations in rpoB, and the three most frequently occurring mutations were listed in Table 1. The MIC values of RIF resistance in bacteria carrying rpoB mutations at positions 450 and 435 were both greater than 16 µg/ml, which is closely related to high-level resistance. The most common mutations conferring resistance to INH, EMB, MXF, and OFL occurred at amino acid position 315 in the katG gene (for INH), position 306 in the embB gene (for EMB), and positions 94 or 90 in the gyrA gene (for MXF and OFL).

Resistance rate and MIC value of aminoglycosides

Among 110 clinical MDR-TB strains, 7 (6.36%) were cross-resistant to amikacin and kanamycin, and 81 (73.64%) were resistant to streptomycin. The MIC values of amikacin against M. tuberculosis were all ≥ 16 µg/ml. Of the 7 strains of bacteria resistant to amikacin and kanamycin, except for one strain with MIC values of 16 µg/ml and 20 µg/ml, the MIC values of amikacin and kanamycin for the remaining 6 strains of M. tuberculosis were ≥ 16 µg/ml and 40 µg/ml, respectively. Additionally, the seven bacterial strains were sensitive to CYC, PAS, and ETH. However, resistance was observed in five out of seven strains toward OFL and MXF, four out of seven strains toward RFB, and three out of seven strains toward EMB. On the other hand, streptomycin demonstrated sensitivity in six out of seven strains.

Among the 81 MDR-TB strains resistant to streptomycin, the MIC values of streptomycin against MDR-TB showed the following distribution: 61 strains (75.31%) demonstrated resistant to streptomycin with MICs ≥ 32 µg/ml, 19 strains (23.46%) exhibited intermediate susceptibility with MICs ranging between 4 and 8 µg/ml, and a single isolate (1.23%) displayed a MIC of 16 µg/ml.

Additionally, no mutation in whiB7 was detected in 110 MDR-TB clinical isolates.

Mutations associated with drug resistance

The consistency of drug resistance phenotype and genotype of aminoglycoside drugs is as follows. Only one of the seven bacteria resistant to amikacin/kanamycin does not align with the expected drug resistance phenotype and genotype. Genomic analysis revealed mutations in rpsL, rrs, and gidB loci in 92.59% (75/81) of streptomycin-resistant MDR-TB clinical strains.

Correlation between gene mutation and aminoglycoside resistance

The MIC of amikacin against MDR-TB clinical strains carrying the rrs A1401G mutation was ≥ 16 µg/ml, and the MIC of kanamycin against the above strains was ≥ 40 µg/ml. The MIC of amikacin against 10 MDR-TB clinical strains carrying only rrs A514C and A514T mutations was ≤ 0.12–0.5 µg/ml, and the MIC of kanamycin against the above strains was ≤ 0.6–5 µg/ml. The MIC range for amikacin against 2 clinical strains of MDR-TB carrying the rrs A906G mutation, 3 strains carrying the eis mutation, and 8 strains carrying the gid mutation was found to be less than or equal to 0.5 µg/ml. Similarly, the MIC range for kanamycin against the same strains was also less than or equal to 5 µg/ml.

Regardless of the presence of other mutations, all K43R mutations of the rspL gene induced MICs of > 32 µg/ml for streptomycin; the MICs of streptomycin against 9 MDR-TB strains with the K88R mutation of the rspL gene were 1–2 µg/ml for 2 strains and 4–32 µg/ml for 7 strains.

Discussion

One of the major problems with TB treatment is the emergence of MDR-TB. Analyzing MICs and mutations of anti-TB drugs across 110 MDR-TB isolates, this study demonstrated that WGS may predict resistance levels to key agents, including first-line anti-TB drugs, fluoroquinolones, and aminoglycosides. Mutations associated with high-level resistance include KatG S315T, RpoB S450L, H445X, and D435X, GyrA D94X, RspL K43R, and Rrs A1401G. Low-level resistance is typically conferred by GyrA mutations D94A, S91P, and A90V in this study. These findings align with descriptions in the WHO catalogue of M. tuberculosis complex mutations associated with drug resistance [18, 19]. Although vast genomic data on drug-resistant M. tuberculosis have emerged in recent years, most datasets lack MIC values. Even in studies with quantitative drug susceptibility testing, correlations between specific resistance-associated mutations and MICs remain inconsistent.

Among aminoglycosides, amikacin has become the preferred agent, though its use is restricted to adult patients undergoing extended treatment regimens [1]. Administration is recommended only when drug susceptibility testing confirms susceptibility, and when reliable audiometric monitoring for potential hearing loss can be performed. In this study, the resistance rate to amikacin was 6.36% (the MIC values were greater than 16 µg/ml), which is between the resistance rates of previous studies (2.24%–33.0%) [20–24]. There was literature reported that in the patient cohort, patients who received low-dose amikacin treatment and cooperated with regular clinical monitoring had a lower adverse reaction rate than those who received standard-dose treatment reported in the literature [25]. It should be noted that the standard-dose treatment reported in the literature may be based on different patient populations, treatment backgrounds, or monitoring methods, so that direct comparison may have certain limitations. Additionally, another report found that amikacin was associated with better outcomes than noninjectable drugs, kanamycin, or capreomycin [26]. Amikacin may be better than streptomycin, but the associations with cure vs. mortality were in opposite directions, leading to uncertainty as to which is better [26].

The present study revealed that 85.71% of amikacin-resistant M. tuberculosis isolates from Shanghai harbored mutations in the rrs gene, while no such mutations were detected in amikacin-susceptible isolates. It highlights a strong association between the rrs gene mutations and amikacin resistance in A1401G. And it was higher than other findings from southern, China (82%) [24], Jiangxi, China (78.9%) [27], Thailand (72.4%) [20], Lisbon, Portugal (50.0%) [28], Guangdong, China (18.89%) [29], and Korea (11.11%) [30]. The resistance mechanisms of amikacin and kanamycin were also believed to be related to the following gene mutations, including the eis promoter and whiB7 5′ UTR mutations [13, 31]. In this study, we identified only three eis mutations in amikacin/kanamycin-susceptible MDR-TB isolates, and we did not detect whiB7 or C1402T mutations. Wang et al. discovered that mutations in the whiB7 5′-UTR result in reduced resistance to kanamycin and capreomycin and were infrequently observed in clinical isolates [29]. Additionally, the rrs C1402T mutation causes decreased resistance to amikacin and kanamycin but significantly increases resistance to capreomycin [29]. Whether these novel genes are related to resistance requires further investigation.

A meta-analysis showed that amikacin may be better than streptomycin. Still, the correlation between cure rate and mortality was the opposite, so it is impossible to determine which drug is better [26]. The present study showed that the streptomycin resistance rate of MDR-TB was high (73.64%), higher than that in southern China (68.35%) [24], South Africa (66.35%) [32], and Zambia (65.90%) [33], and lower than that in Uganda (83.9%) [33]. Genomic analysis revealed mutations in rpsL, rrs, and gidB loci in 92.59% (75/81) of streptomycin-resistant MDR-TB clinical strains. In the present study, 80.25% of streptomycin-resistant MDR-TB had mutations in the rspL genes, higher than those studies from 78.26% in Beijing, China, 72.48% in southern China, and 64.71% in Korea [24, 30, 34]. The proportion of mutations in rspL in other countries, including Thailand, Iran, Spain, and Poland, was even lower, ranging from 24.64% to 63.63% [20, 35–37]. The most common rspL mutations include K43R and K88R. The K43R mutation of the rspL gene is generally associated with high drug resistance, while the MIC values ​​corresponding to the K88R mutation of the rspL were between 2 µg/ml and 16 µg/ml. The detection rate of the K43R mutation is generally higher than that of the K88R, but in a study in Poland, the mutation rate of K88R was higher than that of K43R [35]. The high level of drug resistance caused by the K88R mutation requires vigilance. In a clinical setting, monitoring the K43R mutation can be considered to evaluate high resistance to streptomycin.

In the United States, a randomized trial in the 1960 s comparing capreomycin (1 g) + PAS with streptomycin (1 g) + PAS in 138 patients (only about half of whom completed the six-month treatment) showed no difference in radiographic improvement, but a small, consistent increase in sputum conversion rate with the streptomycin group (95% vs. 82% at 6 months) [38]. A meta-analysis concluded that although the therapeutic effect of amikacin and streptomycin was better than that of no injection, the therapeutic effect of patients treated with kanamycin or capreomycin was worse than that of patients who did not receive injections [26]. In the comprehensive treatment effect analysis, the effect of patients treated with kanamycin or capreomycin was worse than that of patients treated with amikacin or streptomycin [26], suggesting that we should be cautious when choosing aminoglycosides.

The profile of mutated genes and the concordance rate of phenotype and genotype that we reported in this study have a limited impact on the effectiveness and coverage of currently used molecular diagnostic tools and rapid tests. The WHO 2022 report shows good progress in the use of new diagnostic technologies, new drugs, and new treatment regimens, especially in treatment. Progress in promoting new rapid diagnostic technologies is unsatisfactory, with promotion remaining slow. Of the 6.4 million newly diagnosed tuberculosis patients in 2021, only 38% were diagnosed through rapid molecular diagnosis, up from 33% in 2020 and 28% in 2019 [39]. The WHO recommends universal testing for RIF and INH resistance in all tuberculosis patients. Patients identified with multidrug-resistant or RIF-resistant TB should also be tested for fluoroquinolone resistance. While commercial molecular kits exist to detect these specific resistances, rapid molecular diagnostics for resistance to newer and repurposed TB drugs are currently lacking. Addressing this gap, the WHO released the guideline in July 2023, which recommended several tNGS-based molecular diagnostics for drug-resistant TB. tNGS offers the advantage of rapidly determining resistance to over a dozen TB drugs directly from clinical specimens. However, incomplete understanding of the resistance mechanisms for some second-line drugs, particularly newer ones, currently limits the detection efficiency of tNGS-based resistance testing [40].

This study has important limitations. The most important is that phenotyped isolates derived only from Eastern China. The second is that the number of amikacin-resistant MDR-TB cases is small, and the analysis of genotype and phenotype is limited.

In conclusion, MDR-TB isolates exhibited a high proportion of resistance to streptomycin, whereas excellent activity against MDR-TB was observed in amikacin and kanamycin in Shanghai, China. Strains with mutations in codons 43 and 88 of the rspL gene were more likely to be associated with high-level streptomycin resistance. In addition, mutations in the rrs gene were related to amikacin/kanamycin resistance in M. tuberculosis isolates.
