Signaling networks governing skeletal muscle growth, atrophy, and cachexia
Authors: Aniket S Joshi, Meiricris Tomaz da Silva, Savita Kumar, Ashok Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaintaining skeletal muscle mass and strength is essential for healthy aging and overall longevity. Muscle mass is governed by a complex network of signaling pathways that coordinate proteostasis and regulate the expression of anabolic and catabolic genes. Recent research has uncovered several new signaling proteins that modulate skeletal muscle mass through distinct molecular mechanisms. In this review, we summarize current understanding of the signaling cascades that control skeletal muscle mass under both physiological and pathophysiological conditions, including cancer-induced cachexia. We also discuss newly identified anabolic and catabolic factors that regulate specific intracellular pathways involved in myofiber growth and atrophy. Additionally, we highlight key knowledge gaps in the field and suggest future research directions aimed at targeting distinct signaling pathways to improve skeletal muscle mass and function in various catabolic conditions and disease states.Keywords: Skeletal muscle atrophy, Hypertrophy, Protein synthesis, Protein degradation, Cachexia, Signaling

Introduction

Skeletal muscle, the most abundant tissue in the human body, has multiple functions, which include providing structural support and maintaining posture, and ensures basic functions, such as locomotion, respiration, energy storage, and controlling whole body metabolism. It releases several growth factors, cytokines, and myokines that regulate the functioning of many vital organs in the body such as the liver, heart, and even brain [1, 2]. Skeletal muscle also possesses a high level of plasticity. For example, resistance exercise and nutritional supplements improve the myofiber diameter, energy metabolism, and performance of skeletal muscle. By contrast, aging or prolonged inactivity leads to the loss of muscle mass, commonly known as skeletal muscle atrophy or wasting [1, 3]. Skeletal muscle wasting is frequently a common denominator in many chronic disease states, such as cancer, chronic heart failure, chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and type II diabetes [2, 4]. Indeed, about two-thirds of patients with advanced cancer develop cachexia, which involves the loss of skeletal muscle mass with or without fat loss [5, 6]. Cachexia drastically reduces the quality of life, increases resistance to antineoplastic agents, and decreases the overall survival rates of cancer patients [7].

Skeletal muscle mass is predominantly determined by its protein content. Anabolic stimuli activate translational machinery to trigger protein synthesis within myofiber [8–11]. The translation process begins with the binding of eukaryotic initiating factor (eIF)4E to the 5’ Cap of mRNA strand followed by the recruitment of eIF4G, eIF4A, and eIF4B resulting in the formation of eIF4F complex, also termed as the translation preinitiation complex. Subsequent recruitment of the 40 S subunit of ribosome, containing the eIF2-GTP-Met-transfer RNA complex, forms the 43 S initiation complex, which is the rate-limiting step for the initiation of cap-dependent translation [12]. In addition to the increase in the rate of protein synthesis, the fusion of myogenic progenitor cells (i.e. myoblasts) to myofibers also contributes to the accretion of skeletal muscle mass, especially in response to functional overload [13–15].

In many catabolic conditions and disease states, the rate of myofibril protein degradation is drastically increased compared to the rate of protein synthesis, which ultimately leads to skeletal muscle wasting [16, 17]. The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) is the most important mechanism that causes degradation of many myofibril proteins in skeletal muscle in various atrophy conditions [1, 3]. Among other molecules, the E3 ubiquitin ligases are critical components of the UPS, which mediate polyubiquitination of proteins and target them for degradation by the 26 S proteasome [3, 18–20]. Several muscle-specific E3 ubiquitin ligases, such as MAFbx (also known as Atrogin-1), MuRF1, MUSA1, and UBR2 (Ubiquitin Protein Ligase E3 Component N-Recognin 2), have now been identified which mediate muscle proteolysis in distinct catabolic conditions. In addition, other E3 ubiquitin ligases, including TRAF6 and Nedd4 contributes to the loss of skeletal muscle mass in response to various catabolic stimuli, such as denervation, starvation, and cancer growth [3, 18–22]. Autophagy is another important proteolytic system through which protein aggregates and defunct organelles are cleared in mammalian cells. While certain basal levels of autophagy are required for the maintenance of skeletal muscle health, its spurious activation contributes to the loss of skeletal muscle mass in many conditions, including cancer cachexia [3, 23–26]. In addition, other proteolytic enzymes, including caspases and calpains also contribute to protein degradation in conjunction with the UPS and autophagy in skeletal muscle in some catabolic conditions [3, 27].

The activation of translational machinery and proteolytic systems in skeletal muscle is tightly regulated through the coordinated activation of multiple signaling pathways. Most of the signaling pathways regulate muscle mass through modulating the activity of various transcription factors, such as FoxOs, HDAC4-myogenin, nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB), Smads, Kruppel-like factor 10 (KLF10), and Stat3 and various components of translational initiation machinery [18, 24, 28–31]. Although there are a few published review articles discussing molecular mechanisms involved in the regulation of skeletal muscle mass [3, 32–35], there is no comprehensive review article highlighting the recent advancements about the role of various intracellular signaling pathways in the regulation of skeletal muscle mass. Here, we review the emerging role and mechanisms of action of major signaling pathways that regulate skeletal muscle mass in different conditions.

IGF1/Akt/mTOR signaling

The PI3K/Akt signaling pathway is a crucial intracellular signaling mechanism that stimulates protein synthesis and myofiber hypertrophy (Fig. 1). Binding of IGF1 to its receptor triggers the activation of multiple intracellular kinases, including phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase (PI3K), which phosphorylates phospholipid phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) to phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-trisphosphate (PIP3) leading to translocation of Akt to the membrane where it is phosphorylated by phosphoinositide-dependent kinase-1 (PDK-1). Akt indirectly activates mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) through phosphorylation of the Tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) 2 protein at S939 and T1462. TSC2 forms a heterodimeric complex with TSC1. Akt-mediated phosphorylation of TSC2 inhibits the GTPase-activating protein (GAP) activity of this complex (Fig. 1). Since TSC2 suppresses the activity of the Rheb, a selective activator of mTORC1, inhibition of TSC2 by Akt results in activation of mTORC1 [36]. FoxO family transcription factors have been implicated in the regulation of gene expression of several components of UPS and autophagy [3, 24]. Akt phosphorylates FoxO transcription factors on multiple sites, which results in the exclusion of phosphorylated FoxO proteins from the nucleus and hence inhibition of gene expression of various atrogenes, including MAFbx and MuRF1 [24, 36].

Earlier studies demonstrated that overexpression of IGF1 or constitutively active Akt is sufficient to induce myofiber hypertrophy and prevent denervation-induced skeletal muscle atrophy [37, 38]. The Akt kinase regulates muscle growth through the activation of mTOR. The mTOR kinase forms two different protein complexes, raptor-bound rapamycin-sensitive mTORC1 and Rictor-bound rapamycin-insensitive mTORC2 complex (Fig. 1). While mTORC1 is the primary target of rapamycin, administration of rapamycin over a prolonged period can also disrupt mTORC2 [39]. Once activated, the mTORC1 complex phosphorylates 4E-BP1 protein, leading to its release from the translation initiation factor, eIF4E. As such, eIF4E interacts with eIF4G and promotes the formation of eIF4F complex and the concomitant cap-dependent initiation of translation [18, 36]. Additionally, mTORC1 phosphorylates p70S6K, which stimulates protein synthesis through regulating the activity of multiple proteins involved in translation. For example, p70S6K phosphorylates rpS6 protein, a component of the 40 S ribosomal subunit [36, 40–44]. Activated p70S6K also inhibits the activity of eukaryotic elongation factor 2 kinase (eEF2K) which results in the dephosphorylation of eEF2, a process that activates the factor, promoting protein translation elongation [45]. The p70S6K can phosphorylate eIF4B, a component of eIF4F to increase the rate of translation. Programmed cell death protein 4 (PDCD4) is a tumor suppressor protein that inhibits translation and cell growth. The p70S6K-mediated phosphorylation of PDCD4 protein leads to its degradation, and consequently increased protein translation [46]. Moreover, p70S6K directly phosphorylates CAD (carbamoyl-phosphate synthetase 2, aspartate transcarbamoylase, and dihydroorotase), a multi-enzyme protein involved in pyrimidine synthesis, which also stimulate protein synthesis [47]. Growth factors and resistance exercise activate rapamycin-sensitive mTORC1 signaling, which leads to translation initiation and a net increase in protein synthesis in skeletal muscle [48–52]. Furthermore, it has been shown that forced activation of mTORC1 is sufficient to induce the phosphorylation of p70S6K and stimulation of protein synthesis in skeletal muscle [51, 52].

Even though the IGF1/Akt signaling axis is considered as a canonical mechanism regulating mTORC1 activity [36, 37, 53], there are published reports demonstrating Akt-independent regulation of mTORC1 activity in various cell types, including skeletal muscle [51, 54–57]. For example, IGF1 induces the activation of mTORC1/p70S6K in cultured myotubes even in the absence of PI3K-p110α catalytic subunit of Akt [58]. Similarly, initial activation of mTORC1 in response to mechanical loading is Akt-independent and potentially involves MEK/ERK-mediated regulation of TSC2 phosphorylation [59]. Studies using Akt1-knockout mice have also shown that the initial rapid activation of mTORC1 and gene expression of IGF1 and IGF receptor are not affected by the absence of Akt1 further suggesting that upstream IGF1/Akt1 regulation is dispensable for the activation of mTORC1 signaling following mechanical load [60]. Indeed, both Akt-dependent and -independent signaling pathways induce protein synthesis during reloading of disused slow-twitch muscle [61]. There are also reports suggesting the role of Akt-independent mechanisms in the regulation of muscle hypertrophy and growth through the modulation of positive and negative regulators of mTOR, including Rheb, AMPK, and calcium signaling [51, 62, 63].

While mTORC1 has been considered as the main regulator of protein synthesis in skeletal muscle, recent studies have also provided evidence that protein synthesis and muscle hypertrophy is also mediated through mechanisms that do not involve signaling from mTORC1 [35, 50, 64–67]. Independent studies have shown that muscle protein synthesis that occurs at late time points in response to a bout of resistance exercise is rapamycin-insensitive [64, 66]. Moreover, targeted inhibition of mTORC1 through ablation of Raptor protein in skeletal muscle does not affect the protein synthesis that occurs in response to passive stretching or mechanical loading [35, 68]. Another study also demonstrated that muscle-specific deletion of Raptor protein in adult mice does not affect muscle mass, myofiber cross-sectional area, or muscle contractile force production [69]. Alves et al. reported that overexpression of miR-29c increases protein synthesis and induces skeletal muscle hypertrophy, despite suppression of Akt/mTOR activity [70]. However, mTORC1-independent signaling that controls protein synthesis and muscle growth remains largely unknown.

Intriguingly, recent studies employing genetic mouse models have provided evidence that constitutive activation of mTORC1 results in muscle wasting and myopathy without having any significant effect on protein synthesis [71, 72]. Forced activation of mTORC1 through deletion of its inhibitor TSC1 worsens denervation-induced muscle wasting in mice [73]. Activation of mTORC1 in skeletal muscle following denervation inhibits Akt kinase through a feedback mechanism, which leads to the activation and nuclear translocation of FoxO transcription factors and increased gene expression of E3 ubiquitin ligases, MAFbx and MuRF1 [73]. While it has not been experimentally verified, it is possible that mTORC1 causes an initial increase in protein synthesis; however, due to negative feedback mechanism of inhibition of Akt and potential activation of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress-induced unfolded protein response (UPR), this response is lost at later time points. Spurious activation of mTORC1 also blocks autophagy-lysosomal system, which ultimately results in the onset of myopathy [74]. Other studies have also demonstrated that acute activation or suppression of mTORC1 dysregulates autophagy and impairs skeletal muscle mass homeostasis in skeletal muscle following denervation [75]. Sarcopenia is the age-related loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength that diminishes mobility and quality of life in the elderly [76]. Activation of mTORC1 contributes to the loss of muscle mass during aging potentially through augmenting the phosphorylation of STAT3 transcription factor [72]. Phosphorylated STAT3 stimulates the gene expression of growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15), which causes skeletal muscle wasting [72].

Interestingly, overexpression of follistatin, which inactivates myostatin and Activin A, leads to increased protein synthesis and skeletal muscle hypertrophy through the activation of Akt/mTORC1/p70S6K pathway [77]. This effect of follistatin overexpression on muscle growth is independent of myostatin-driven mechanisms suggesting that follistatin can regulate mTORC1 signaling through mechanisms that do not exclusively involve myostatin inhibition [77]. Surprisingly, follistatin overexpression-induced sustained activation of mTORC1 did not cause muscle wasting. One possibility is that the follistatin overexpression causes mTORC1 activation through upstream activation of Akt and that the level of activation of mTORC1 may not be sufficient to inhibit Akt activation through feedback mechanisms. Altogether, it is now increasingly evidenced that while transient activation of mTORC1 stimulates muscle protein synthesis and growth, chronic activation of mTORC1 can lead to muscle wasting.

TGF-β-activated kinase 1 (TAK1) signaling

TAK1 is an important signaling protein, which mediates the activation of downstream intracellular signaling pathways, including the MKK4/7-JNK, MKK3/6-p38 MAPK, and IκB kinase β (IKKβ)-NF-κB in response to various growth factors, cytokines and microbial products. For activation, TAK1 forms a heterotrimeric complex with TAK1-binding protein (TAB)1 and either TAB2 or TAB3 [78, 79]. Recent studies have shown that TAK1 plays important roles in regenerative myogenesis and growth and maintenance of skeletal muscle mass [80]. TAK1 is essential for the proliferation and myogenic differentiation of cultured myoblasts [81, 82]. Moreover, TAK1 is required for the survival, self-renewal, and proliferation of satellite cells during skeletal muscle regeneration in adult mice. Attempts to generate germline muscle-specific Tak1-knockout mice were not successful because the pups could not survive beyond 1–2 days after birth. Skeletal muscle-specific inducible inactivation of TAK1 in post-natal mice led to severe muscle wasting and development of kyphosis. Inactivation of TAK1 also reduced the gene expression of many growth factors and thick and thin filament proteins suggesting that TAK1 is required for post-natal muscle growth and maintenance of skeletal muscle mass in adults [83]. The role of TAK1 is muscle growth in adults is supported by the findings that phosphorylation of TAK1 within its kinase domain is increased in skeletal muscle of adult mice in response to chronic mechanical loading [83, 84]. Importantly, targeted inducible inactivation of TAK1 blunts myofiber hypertrophy following mechanical loading in adult mice [83]. In addition, forced activation of TAK1 through intramuscular co-injection of TAK1- and TAB1-expressing adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) causes noticeable myofiber hypertrophy in adult mice further supporting a role for TAK1 in skeletal muscle growth [84]. The role of TAK1 in muscle growth is also supported by a recently published report that demonstrates that forced activation of MKK3b, a direct phosphorylation target of TAK1, is sufficient to increase protein synthesis and muscle fiber size in adult mice [85].

One of the mechanisms by which TAK1 improves skeletal muscle growth through augmenting the rate of protein synthesis [83, 84]. In addition to mTORC1-mediated signaling, protein synthesis in mammalian cells is also regulated through the activation of MAPKs. The ERK1/2 and p38 MAPK promote phosphorylation and activation of the p90 ribosomal S6 kinases (RSKs) and the MAPK-interacting kinases (MNKs), respectively [12]. ERK1/2 also directly interacts and phosphorylates RSKs. In turn, RSKs phosphorylate rpS6, eIF4B, Raptor, TSC2, PDCD4, and eEF2K, connecting ERK1/2 signaling to the regulation of mRNA translation [12]. Interestingly, forced activation of TAK1 increases the phosphorylation of ERK1/2, p38 MAPK, MNK1, and RSK1 protein in skeletal muscle (Fig. 1). In addition, forced activation of TAK1 reduces the phosphorylation of eIF2α which is known to inhibit protein synthesis [84]. While more investigations are needed to delineate intracellular signaling, it is possible that TAK1 stimulates protein synthesis through the activation of MAPKs. Since TAK1 is an important serine/threonine kinase, there is also a possibility that TAK1 directly phosphorylates the components of translational machinery.

Mitochondria play a key role in the maintenance of skeletal muscle mass and metabolic function. Disruption in mitochondrial dynamics or function leads to the loss of muscle mass in many conditions [86]. Mitochondria are also a major source of oxidative stress in mammalian cells and oxidative stress alone is sufficient to activate proteolytic systems leading to muscle wasting and weakness [1, 3, 87]. Interestingly, inactivation of TAK1 results in the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria and increased oxidative stress in skeletal muscle of adult mice [83, 88]. Inactivation of TAK1 in skeletal muscle also leads to the derangement of neuromuscular junctions (NMJs) at the post-synaptic region [84]. It is possible that NMJ disruption in Tak1-deficient skeletal muscle occurs due to oxidative stress which is also supported by the findings that inhibition of oxidative stress using Trolox, an antioxidant vitamin E derivative, improves skeletal muscle mass and contractile function in muscle-specific Tak1-knockout mice [88].

TAK1 is also an important signaling protein that mediates the activation of proinflammatory signaling pathways in response to catabolic cytokines, such as TNFα, TWEAK, and IL-1β [79, 80] (Fig. 2). Therefore, it is not surprising that spurious activation of TAK1 can cause muscle inflammation and atrophy. Indeed, TWEAK and TNF-α augment the gene expression of various proinflammatory molecules and matrix degrading enzymes through the activation of TAK1 [89–91]. Recent studies have also demonstrated that TAK1 is highly activated in the skeletal muscle of Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) patients and in dystrophic muscle of mdx (a mouse model of DMD) mice and that inhibition of TAK1 improves dystrophic phenotype in mdx mice [92, 93]. Similarly, targeted inducible inactivation of TAK1 in young mdx mice reduces myofiber injury, inflammation, and accumulation of macrophages [94].

To further discern the role of TAK1 in skeletal muscle, we recently investigated the effect of AAV-mediated forced activation of low and high levels of TAK1 in skeletal muscle of adult mice. Consistent with its role in myofiber growth, low levels of TAK1 activation led to myofiber hypertrophy. By contrast, hyperactivation of TAK1 manifested pathological features, such as chronic myofiber degeneration and regeneration, increased expression of various inflammatory molecules, and development of interstitial fibrosis. Hyperstimulation of TAK1 also upregulated proteolytic systems and catabolic pathways in skeletal muscle [95]. This study emphasized that while physiological activation of TAK1 improves myofiber size, its uncontrolled high levels of activation results in myofiber damage and other pathological features resembling inflammatory myopathies (Fig. 3). Certainly, future investigations are needed to determine how the activity of TAK1 is regulated in skeletal muscle in various conditions, such as aging, immobilization, and chronic diseases, including cancer and whether molecular or pharmacological modulation of TAK1 activity can be used as a therapeutic approach to improve muscle mass in various catabolic conditions and diseases.

Myostatin/Activin/TGF-β signaling

Members of TGF-β superfamily, which comprise the myostatin/activin/TGF-β subfamily and the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) subfamily, play a crucial role in the regulation of muscle mass in different conditions. Binding of myostatin/activin/TGF-β group of ligands to activin type IIB and type IIA receptors (ActRIIB/IIA) and TGF-β receptors (TGFβRII) leads to the recruitment and activation of type 1 receptor, activin receptor-like kinase (ALK)-4, -7, and − 5. ALK4/7 and ALK5 phosphorylate Smad2/3 protein to promote the formation of a multiprotein complex with co-Smad, Smad4. The Smad2/3-Smad4 complex then translocates to the nucleus to regulate the gene expression of Smad2/3 target molecules involved in the regulation of skeletal muscle mass [3] (Fig. 2).

Myostatin, also known as growth and differentiation factor 8 (GDF8), is a negative regulator of skeletal muscle mass [96]. Several studies have shown that genetic or pharmacological inhibition of myostatin leads to skeletal muscle hypertrophy [97, 98]. By contrast, transgenic overexpression of myostatin reduces skeletal muscle mass and increases fat mass in male mice [99]. Myostatin levels are increased in multiple conditions involving skeletal muscle wasting, including cancer cachexia. Inhibition of myostatin/activin signaling using pharmacological or molecular approaches attenuates the loss of muscle mass in animal models of cancer cachexia [100, 101]. Follistatin binds to and acts as a potent inhibitor of myostatin and some other members of TGF-β family (Fig. 2). Several studies have shown that transgenic overexpression of follistatin in mice causes a dramatic increase in skeletal muscle mass further suggesting that neutralization of myostatin can improve muscle mass in various conditions [77, 102, 103]. Unfortunately, myostatin inhibition was not effective in improving muscle mass and displayed significant side effects in humans [104–106]. Moreover, myostatin inhibition did not significantly improve the survival rate of pancreatic cancer patients with cachexia [107]. This could be attributed to the fact that myostatin is not the only negative regulator of skeletal muscle mass functioning via the activin receptor type IIB (ACVR2B; ActRIIB). ActRIIB binds to many other ligands, including the activins (activin A, B, C and E), GDF11, bone morphogenetic protein 9 (BMP9) and BMP10 which are also involved in the regulation of skeletal muscle mass [3, 108–110]. A soluble form of ActRIIB fused to human IgG Fc fragment (ActRIIB.hFc) improved muscle growth in myostatin-null mice, further suggesting the presence of additional negative regulators of muscle mass acting through ActRIIB receptor. Indeed, Activin A is another key negative regulator of skeletal muscle growth confirmed by the findings that antibody-mediated inhibition of GDF8 and Activin A leads to a synergistic improvement in muscle mass in mice and monkeys [111]. In addition to ActRIIB, stimulation of ActRIIA also contributes to the loss of skeletal muscle mass. Bimagrumab (BYM338), a humanized antibody that inhibits both ActRIIA/ActRIIB causes a more dramatic increase in muscle mass in adult mice compared to selective inhibition of either of the two receptors [112].

A few earlier studies showed that levels of GDF11 decline with age and that restoration of systemic GDF11 provides beneficial effects for several age-related conditions [113, 114]. However, this study was contradicted by several independent reports, which demonstrated that GDF11 exerts inhibitory effects on muscle regeneration and that higher doses cause skeletal muscle atrophy and cachexia [110, 115]. Interestingly, GDF11 induces muscle wasting, at least in part, by augmenting the levels of GDF15, another member of TGFβ superfamily [109]. GDF15 expression is increased in various conditions, including aging and cancer-induced muscle wasting and is associated with the loss of lean mass and strength in both patients and animal models [116–118]. Indeed, a recent phase II clinical trial at Pfizer has reported that treatment with ponsegromab, a humanized monoclonal antibody inhibiting GDF-15, causes significant improvement in the body weight of patients with non-small cell lung cancer, pancreatic cancer, and colorectal cancer, further supporting the role of GDF15 as a driver of cancer cachexia [119].

TGF-β has been widely implicated in the regulation of fibrosis during skeletal muscle development and adult muscle regeneration. Following muscle damage or denervation, TGF-β1 is upregulated in skeletal muscle, an effect mimicked by the injection of myostatin resulting in the formation of connective tissue and the loss of skeletal muscle mass and function [120, 121]. TGF-β signaling also regulates muscle mass in pathophysiological conditions, including cancer cachexia. Treatment with TGF-β decreases myofiber size, contractile function, and increases fibrosis in wild-type and tumor-bearing mice [122]. A more recent study has demonstrated that TGF-β-induced activation of KLF10 transcription factor mediates skeletal muscle wasting in pancreatic cancer cachexia. KLF10 directly augments the gene expression of MAFbx and MuRF1 and other molecules related to autophagy and calcium signaling during cancer cachexia [29].

A common mechanism by which Myostatin/Activin/GDF11 induces muscle wasting is through the activation of Smad2/3 transcription factors evidenced by the findings that the inhibition of Smad2/3 is sufficient to promote muscle hypertrophy [123]. Furthermore, the activation of mTORC1 appears to be another mechanism responsible for the hypertrophic effect of myostatin blockade [77]. In addition to the canonical signaling involving Smad2/3, TGF-β activates non-canonical signaling through the activation of JNK and p38 MAPKs which are also involved in muscle catabolism [124]. Finally, myostatin and other members of this family also reduce skeletal muscle mass through inhibiting the proliferation and differentiation of myogenic cells [110, 125].

BMP-Smad1/5/8 signaling

BMPs are another group of growth factors and cytokines belonging to the TGF-β superfamily. In contrast to myostatin/activin/TGF-β signaling, BMP signaling positively regulates skeletal muscle mass [77, 108, 126]. The BMP/GDF subfamily ligands bind to a combination of type II receptors that include BMP type II receptor (BMPRII), ActRIIA, and ActRIIB, which then leads to the recruitment of type I receptors (i.e., ALK3, ALK6, and ALK2). Activated BMP type I receptors phosphorylate intracellular mediators of BMP signaling, Smad1/5/8, which in turn form an oligomeric complex with Smad4 (Fig. 1). The Smad complex then translocates into the nucleus to regulate transcription of BMP target genes, such as inhibitor of DNA binding (Id) genes, which are involved in the regulation of cell growth and differentiation [98, 108–110]. It is notable that in addition to Smad4, ligands from both the myostatin/activin/TGF-β and BMP/GDF subgroups also compete for some type II receptors. Furthermore, downstream of the receptor-mediated signaling, the activation of Smad1/5/8 and Smad2/3 transcription factors can be inhibited by Smad6 and Smad7 protein [101].

Accumulating evidence suggests that skeletal muscle mass in adults is regulated by a delicate balance between myostatin and BMP signaling [3, 108]. To study the relationship between myostatin and BMP signaling, Sartori et al. [108] showed that deletion of Smad4 results in muscle atrophy in adult mice, which is exacerbated following denervation. This phenotype was noticeably different from that of the myostatin (Mstn)-null mice, suggesting that Smad4 plays a critical role in the regulation of muscle mass, through mechanisms distinct from relaying myostatin signaling. Further studies using the BMP antagonist, Noggin, revealed that BMP signaling regulates muscle hypertrophy in Mstn-knockout mice [108]. In parallel, Winbanks et al. also presented evidence that forced activation of BMP signaling through overexpression of BMP7 or constitutively active BMP receptor ALK3 promotes muscle hypertrophy. BMP signaling also prevents excessive loss of muscle mass in response to functional denervation [126]. Mechanistically, BMP signaling-induced activation of Smad 1/5/8 represses the expression of E3 ubiquitin ligases, including MUSA1, which promote muscle atrophy. Finally, the BMP-Smad1/5/8 signaling also negatively regulates the gene expression of E3 ubiquitin ligases through the repression of HDAC4-myogenin axis [108, 126].

Recently, it was reported that BMP-Smad1/5/8 signaling is repressed in the skeletal muscle of animal models of cancer cachexia. Importantly, forced activation of BMP-Smad1/5/8 pathway attenuated muscle wasting and improved the integrity of NMJs in tumor-bearing mice [127]. Mina et al., recently reported that the levels of the BMP scavenger erythroferrone (ERFE) and the intracellular inhibitor FKBP12 are up-regulated in muscle biopsies of cachectic cancer patients and in skeletal muscle of mouse models of cancer cachexia [128]. Knockdown of either Erfe or Fkbp12 significantly reduced muscle wasting in cachexia. Furthermore, the inhibition of FKBP12 with low-dose FK506 improved BMP-Smad1/5/8 signaling, protein synthesis, integrity of NMJs, and skeletal muscle mass in cachectic tumor-bearing mice [128]. While the BMP receptors directly phosphorylate Smad1/5/8, it was recently reported that TAK1, another positive regulator of muscle mass, is also involved in BMP signaling. Pharmacological inhibition or knockdown of TAK1 reduces the phosphorylation of Smad1/5/8 protein in cultured myotubes following treatment with recombinant BMP7 or BMP13 proteins [84]. It has also been reported that BMP signaling promotes postnatal muscle growth by regulating the proliferation of satellite cells and their fusion with myofibers. Inhibition of BMP signaling through overexpression of noggin or inhibitory SMAD6 or satellite cell-specific ablation of Alk3 diminishes satellite cell proliferation and myonuclear accretion, thus blunting muscle growth [129].

Hippo-YAP signaling

The Hippo signaling plays a critical role in the regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation and organ size [130]. The components of Hippo signaling pathway include the kinases LATS1/2 and MST1/2, the transcriptional coactivators Yes-associated protein (YAP) and WW domain containing protein 1 (TAZ/WWTR1), and the transcriptional enhancer factor domain (TEAD) transcription factors (Fig. 1). When Hippo signaling is inactive, non-phosphorylated YAP and TAZ are active and localized in the nucleus, where they physically associate with the TEAD factors to induce transcription of target genes. When Hippo signaling is activated, phosphorylated YAP and TAZ are inactive and shuttle back to the cytoplasm, thereby disrupting transcriptional regulation by TEAD factors [130].

Several components of Hippo signaling, including YAP and TAZ, have been found to be increased in skeletal muscle in various conditions. While an increase in the levels of phosphorylated YAP leading to its degradation indicates activation of tumor-suppressor Hippo signaling, accumulation of unphosphorylated YAP occurs when the Hippo pathway is inactivated [130]. An earlier study showed that the levels of both phosphorylated and total YAP protein, along with mTORC1 signaling and protein synthesis, are increased in skeletal muscle in response to myostatin and activin blocking. Furthermore, YAP phosphorylation is induced in skeletal muscle in response to exercise and in dystrophic muscle of mdx mice [131]. However, the physiological significance of increased levels of phosphorylated or total YAP protein in the regulation of skeletal muscle mass was unknown. Like YAP, activated Hippo pathway phosphorylates TAZ, leading to its cytoplasmic retention and degradation, thus inhibiting its activity. In contrast, when the Hippo pathway is inactivated, TAZ becomes stabilized and translocates to nucleus to induce the gene expression of various molecules involved in cellular proliferation and growth [130]. Interestingly, Yang et al. showed that stabilization of TAZ protein using a pharmacological compound, IBS008738, improves skeletal muscle regeneration through enhancing MyoD-dependent gene transcription. Furthermore, IBS008738 represses the gene expression of MAFbx and MuRF1 and myofiber atrophy in adult mice following treatment with high dosages of dexamethasone [132]. Mechanical signals increase the nuclear levels of YAP and TAZ which is dependent on Rho GTPase activity and tension of the actomyosin cytoskeleton, but independent of the Hippo/LATS cascade [133]. Consistent with its involvement in mechanotransduction, an increase in YAP levels was observed in skeletal muscle following mechanical loading. Furthermore, overexpression of YAP was sufficient to induce myofiber hypertrophy [134]. Another study demonstrated that YAP, via interaction with TEAD transcription factors, positively regulates skeletal muscle mass and protein synthesis and prevents excessive loss of muscle mass following denervation [135]. More recently, studies on Drosophila flight muscle and in vitro model using chick muscle cells have also showed that YAP positively regulates muscle growth during development [136, 137].

Organ size and cell growth are determined by both the mTOR and YAP signaling mechanisms, the former by increasing protein synthesis and the latter by controlling cell proliferation. The link between mTOR and YAP is evidenced through YAP-mediated downregulation of PTEN, a critical inhibitor of PI3K/Akt signaling [138]. Another study demonstrated that YAP increases the expression of miR-29 leading to the repression of PTEN [139]. There are also reports suggesting that Akt regulates YAP phosphorylation, potentially through an interaction with MST1/2 implying that mTOR and YAP signaling may work in concert to induce protein synthesis [140, 141]. Interestingly, YAP induces myofiber hypertrophy through augmenting protein synthesis without activating mTORC1 signaling [135]. Similarly, Goodman et al. showed that YAP overexpression-induced myofiber hypertrophy is insensitive to rapamycin and that the YAP-induced hypertrophy following synergistic ablation could be attributed to the increased expression of MyoD and c-Myc, reduced MuRF1 expression, and the inhibition of Smad 2/3 transcription factors [134] (Fig. 1).

Another study demonstrated that Hippo pathway controls myofibrillar assembly and post-mitotic muscle fiber growth through the regulation of sarcomere gene expression [136]. These findings suggest that Hippo-YAP signaling plays a role in the maintenance of skeletal muscle mass through the regulation of protein-protein interactions and transcriptional reprogramming. Intriguingly, overexpression of constitutively active (c.a.) YAP mutant leads to severe muscle wasting in addition to the presence of centrally nucleated myofibers resembling chronic myofiber degeneration-regeneration, a phenotype that was reversible with the cessation of YAP mutant expression [142]. The contrasting outcomes regarding the effects of YAP overexpression in skeletal muscle [134, 142] can be partly explained by different YAP mutants (c.a. vs. wild type) and different endpoints used for these studies. The ca. YAP mutant may result in the formation of protein aggregates, leading to the observed myopathy phenotype. There is also a possibility that the opposing effect is a result of mutant YAP not being subject to negative feedback as opposed to wild type YAP protein. Collectively, these studies suggest that stabilization of YAP and TAZ protein, which could occur through the inhibition of Hippo signaling or through alternative mechanisms, promotes myofiber growth and prevents atrophy (Fig. 1). Further investigations are needed to understand the temporal regulation and the mechanisms by which YAP/TAZ/TEAD complex induces protein synthesis in skeletal muscle. Moreover, upstream signaling mechanisms that lead to the stabilization of YAP and TAZ protein in skeletal muscle in the conditions of muscle growth and atrophy remain to be identified.

ER stress and unfolded protein response

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is the major organelle involved in protein folding as well as in the maintenance of calcium homeostasis in mammalian cells. An overload of unfolded or misfolded proteins can trigger stress in the ER, which is resolved through the activation of multiple signaling cascades known as unfolded protein response (UPR). Three ER transmembrane sensors: Protein kinase R-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK), inositol-requiring protein 1α (IRE1α), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) trigger the activation of the UPR during ER stress (Fig. 2). The main function of the UPR is to restore ER homeostasis through augmenting the protein folding capacity within ER, transient suppression of translation, mRNA degradation to reduce translational load, and protein degradation in case of chronic increase in the levels of misfolded proteins to restrict the formation of protein aggregates. While the activation of UPR is a physiological mechanism, unmitigated ER stress leads to “maladaptive” UPR that can lead to deleterious consequences such as inflammation, insulin resistance, and cell death [143, 144].

Recent studies have shown that the markers of ER stress are increased in skeletal muscle under multiple conditions [17, 145]. Accumulating evidence also suggests that individual arms of the UPR play distinct roles in the regulation of skeletal muscle mass in both physiological and pathological conditions (Fig. 4). Some levels of ER stress/UPR is beneficial for skeletal muscle health supported by the findings that chronic treatment of mice with 4-phenylbutyrate (4-PBA), a chemical chaperon and a potent inhibitor of ER stress, reduces skeletal muscle mass in adult mice. Mechanistically, 4-PBA reduces the phosphorylation of mTOR and the rate of protein synthesis but increases the activity of the UPS in skeletal muscle [146]. However, skeletal muscle outcomes can also be dependent on the dosages and approaches employed for attenuating ER stress. For example, a recent study showed that oral administration of ER stress inhibitor Tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) attenuates muscle wasting in an animal model of hind limb unloading [147].

Transcriptional coactivator peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1 alpha (PGC1α) is a major regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis following aerobic exercise [148]. Interestingly, PGC1α mediates the activation of the UPR by interacting with cleaved ATF6α protein to induce adaptive UPR following pharmacologically induced ER stress and exercise. ATF6α also regulates inflammation and recovery of skeletal muscle following acute exercise-induced muscle damage. ATF6α knockout mice exhibit exercise intolerance, further emphasizing the physiological role of ATF6 in skeletal muscle adaptation following endurance exercise [149]. In response to ER stress, PERK phosphorylates eukaryotic translation initiation factor (eIF)2α on its Ser51 residue, which leads to the repression of general translation while selectively enhancing the translation of ATF4 and gene expression of stress-responsive genes, such as CHOP and glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78). Similar to chronic treatment with pan-ER stress inhibitor 4-PBA, inducible muscle-specific deletion of PERK in adult mice disrupts proteostasis leading to reduced muscle mass, grip strength, muscle contractile force production during isometric contractions, and exacerbation of muscle atrophy in Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) tumor-bearing mice suggesting that PERK is important for maintaining skeletal muscle mass in adult animals [150].

While physiological levels of PERK are important for maintaining muscle mass and strength, its spurious activation for prolonged periods can lead to skeletal muscle wasting (Fig. 4). In fact, muscle-specific overexpression of a constitutive active mutant of PERK causes progressive muscle atrophy in adult mice that could be attributed to the repression of protein synthesis [151]. Interestingly, forced activation of PERK in skeletal muscle of mice also improves amino acid metabolism, antioxidant response, and increases the gene expression and secretion of fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), an antiobesity myokine/hepatokine that stimulates energy expenditure in brown adipose tissue suggesting that despite causing atrophy, activation of PERK improves whole-body metabolic adaptation [151]. We have also recently reported that one of the mechanisms by which TWEAK-Fn14 signaling induces muscle wasting during cancer cachexia is through the activation of PERK/eIF2α arm of the UPR. Knockdown or pharmacological inhibition of PERK improves protein synthesis and myotube diameter in TWEAK-treated cultured myotubes [152]. The ATF4 transcription factor, which is also activated through the PERK-eIF2α arm of the UPR, mediates skeletal muscle wasting in multiple conditions, including aging, fasting, and limb immobilization. Forced expression of ATF4 is sufficient to induce muscle atrophy in mice. ATF4 increases the gene expression of Gadd45α (Growth Arrest and DNA Damage Inducible Alpha) protein, which promotes muscle fiber atrophy by activating the protein kinase MEKK4 [153, 154]. By contrast, CHOP, another downstream target of the PERK/eIF2α pathway, prevents denervation-induced muscle atrophy potentially through suppressing autophagy [155].

The IRE1α is the evolutionally conserved arm of the UPR that plays a major role in resolving ER stress. The IRE1α cytosolic domain possesses a kinase domain that autophosphorylates, leading to the stimulation of its endoribonuclease activity, which catalyzes unconventional processing of the mRNA encoding X-box-binding protein 1 (XBP1) and creates a transcriptionally active XBP1, known as spliced XBP1 (sXBP1). The sXBP1 protein induces the gene expression of several molecules to enhance the protein folding capacity, as well as to increase protein degradation and transport pathways, which mitigates the burden of unfolded proteins within the ER. IRE1 activation can also lead to promiscuous endoribonuclease activity that causes mRNA decay at the ER membrane, a process named regulated IRE1-dependent decay (RIDD), thus reducing the protein load. ER-associated degradation (ERAD) is another crucial process responsible for eliminating misfolded or terminally unfolded proteins through UPS and autophagy. During ER stress, the IRE1α/XBP1 axis augments the gene expression of various molecules involved in the ERAD pathway. Finally, activated IRE1α also mediates alternate signaling by recruiting TRAF2 followed by phosphorylation of JNK, which ultimately regulates cellular proliferation and apoptosis [143, 144, 156].

The physiological role of IRE1α arm of the UPR in the regulation of skeletal muscle mass remains less understood. However, it was recently discovered that IRE1α signaling through sXBP1 drives myoblast fusion, an essential process of muscle hypertrophy in response to functional overload [157]. Another recent study showed that SEL1L-HRD1 ERAD complex is essential for postnatal muscle growth and systemic metabolism, which is mediated, in part, through the secretion of FGF21 myokine [158]. Despite this initial evidence, there is very limited knowledge of how different components of the ERAD pathway regulate skeletal muscle mass.

Like PERK, spurious activation of the IRE1α arm of the UPR can lead to deleterious consequences. Multiple markers of ER stress, including IRE1α and sXBP1, are upregulated in skeletal muscles of mouse models of cancer cachexia [146, 159]. Furthermore, it was found that tumor-derived factors stimulate the IRE1α/XBP1 signaling through the recruitment of toll like receptors (TLRs) and myeloid differentiating primary response gene 88 (MyD88) protein (Fig. 2). Targeted deletion of MyD88 or XBP1 attenuated the loss of skeletal muscle mass in LLC tumor-bearing mice [159]. Importantly, forced expression of IRE1α or sXBP1 was sufficient to induce atrophy, whereas knockdown of sXBP1 attenuated myotube atrophy in response to LLC conditioned medium. This study also showed that sXBP1 regulates the gene expression of several proinflammatory cytokines and the components of the UPS and autophagy [159]. A more recent study from our group has demonstrated that the IRE1/XBP1 signaling axis stimulates skeletal muscle wasting during pancreatic cancer cachexia through the activation of proteolytic systems, STAT3 signaling, and fatty acid oxidation and that sXBP1 protein binds to promoter region of multiple genes whose products are involved muscle proteolysis and metabolism [160]. In addition to cancer cachexia, the Myd88/IRE1α/XBP1 axis also causes skeletal muscle atrophy in response to functional denervation [161].

Collectively, there is now ample evidence suggesting that the components of UPR are dynamically regulated in skeletal muscle in response to different cues. Moreover, activation of distinct arms of the UPR during muscle growth and atrophy is dependent on the duration and stimuli (Fig. 4). Future studies will elucidate the signaling crosstalk between the UPR and other anabolic and catabolic pathways during skeletal muscle hypertrophy and atrophy.

Nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling

Inflammation plays a major role in muscle wasting in almost all chronic disease states [3]. NF-κB is a transcription factor that induces the gene expression of many proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and matrix-degrading enzymes [162]. NF-κB can be activated through either a canonical or a non-canonical signaling pathway (Fig. 2). The canonical pathway involves NF-κB dimers composed of RelA (p65), c-Rel, and p50, whereas the non-canonical pathway involves dimers of RelB and p52. The canonical NF-κB signaling is activated through upstream activation of IκB kinase (IKK) β that phosphorylates IκB proteins leading to their degradation, followed by nuclear translocation of p65/p50 dimers. The non-canonical NF-κB pathway involves the stabilization of NF-κB inducing kinase (NIK) which activates IKKα. Activated IKKα phosphorylates p100 protein, which in turn undergo limited proteolytic processing to form p52 protein, followed by nuclear translocation of p52/RelB dimers [28, 162].

Both canonical and non-canonical NF-κB pathways have been found to be activated in skeletal muscle in different conditions, including functional denervation, tumor growth, and genetic muscle disorders [28, 163, 164]. Many proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, TWEAK, IFN-γ, and IL-1β cause degradation of muscle proteins and induce muscle wasting through the activation of canonical NF-κB signaling pathway [28, 165–167]. Direct role of canonical NF-κB signaling in muscle atrophy is supported by a seminal study by Cai et al. which demonstrated that targeted transgenic overexpression of IKKβ in skeletal muscle of mice leads to a profound loss of skeletal muscle mass, whereas genetic or pharmacological inhibition of canonical NF-κB signaling attenuates muscle wasting in response to denervation or tumor growth [168]. Similarly, non-canonical NF-κB signaling has also been linked to disuse-related atrophy [169] supported by the findings that genetic deletion of Nfkb1 or Bcl3 attenuates the loss of muscle mass in response to unloading [170]. Fasting also increases the expression and acetylation of the lysine 310 residue of the p65 subunit of NF-κB in skeletal muscle. Remarkably, inhibition of NF-κB through overexpression of IκBα super-repressor mutant or mutated p65-K310 protein prevents muscle atrophy in response to starvation [171]. Although the molecular underpinning remains unknown, constitutive activation of NF-κB is also a potential contributor of age-associated muscle atrophy [172, 173].

Cachexia, a devastating wasting syndrome, is responsible for reduced tolerance to therapy, longevity, and functional capability of cancer patients. Accumulating evidence suggests that multiple signaling pathways, including NF-κB mediate the cancer-induced muscle wasting [174, 175]. Hyperactivation of NF-κB in satellite cells inhibits their differentiation and fusion to myofibers in cancer-bearing host [176]. Moreover, a recent study has shown that NF-κB plays an important role in multiple cell types (e.g., satellite cells, macrophages, myofibers, and fibro-adipogenic progenitors) in the muscle microenvironment to regulate skeletal muscle mass in cancer-bearing host [177]. Mechanistically, NF-κB regulates muscle proteolysis and activation of UPS in response to tumor-derived cachectic factors, including proteolysis-inducing factor (PIF) [178]. The role of NF-κB in muscle wasting during cancer-induced cachexia is supported by the findings that overexpression of IκBα super-repressor protein leads to inhibition of NF-κB and attenuates muscle wasting in LLC tumor-bearing mice [168]. In recent years, several drugs (e.g., megestrol acetate, dronabinol, eicosapentaenoic-acid-enriched oral supplements, ghrelin, pentoxifylline, thalidomide, melatonin, nandrolone decanoate) have shown potential for the treatment of cachexia/anorexia to some extent in randomized clinical trials in cancer patients [179, 180]. Although the mechanisms of action of these drugs remain less understood, some of these drugs such as dexanabinol, eicosapentaenoic acid, ghrelin, pentoxifylline, thalidomide, and melatonin inhibit NF-κB activity in different cell types [181–186]. Recently, it was reported that the activation of ectodysplasin A2 receptor (EDA2R) by its ligand EDA-A2 promotes muscle atrophy through the activation of non-canonical NF-κB pathway via NIK [32]. Interestingly, non-canonical NF-κB signaling has also been found to induce muscle wasting through inducing the gene expression of MAFbx and MuRF1 in animal models of cancer cachexia [31, 187] further suggesting that both canonical and non-canonical arms of NF-κB are involved in muscle wasting during cancer-induced cachexia.

TWEAK cytokine and its receptor fibroblast growth factor-inducible 14 (Fn14) constitute a major signaling system that regulates skeletal muscle mass in many catabolic conditions [188]. Transgenic overexpression of TWEAK in mice causes muscle wasting whereas TWEAK-knockout mice resist denervation-induced loss of muscle mass and function [165, 189]. Myofiber-specific deletion of Fn14 receptor inhibits the activation of non-canonical NF-κB signaling, reduces the gene expression of molecules involved in UPS and autophagy, and attenuates denervation-induced muscle atrophy [190]. Recent studies have also highlighted the role of the TWEAK-Fn14 system and the canonical and non-canonical NF-κB signaling in muscle wasting during cancer cachexia [152, 191]. Interestingly, there are also reports suggesting that muscle atrophy in certain conditions is independent of NF-κB activity. For instance, Meyer et al. showed that muscle atrophy in response to rotator cuff tenotomy is NF-κB independent [192]. Similarly, muscle wasting in C26 tumor-bearing mice, a model of colorectal cancer cachexia, is IKKβ-dependent but NF-κB-independent [193] suggesting that NF-κB-independent mechanisms also contribute to muscle atrophy in different conditions.

JAK-STAT signaling

The Janus kinase (JAK)-signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) is an evolutionarily conserved signaling module, which induces the gene expression of molecules involved in inflammation and cell proliferation. The binding of specific cytokines to their receptors induces transphosphorylation of JAK. Activated JAK causes tyrosine phosphorylation of the bound receptor, which results in the formation of a docking site for STATs. Finally, JAK phosphorylates STATs, which then form homodimers or heterodimers and translocate to the nucleus to induce the transcription of the target genes [194].

Pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 and IFNγ elicit the activation of STAT3 signaling in skeletal muscle, especially in cancer patients and tumor-bearing mice [195, 196]. IL-6, a pleiotropic cytokine, exerts its biological effects by binding to its receptors, IL-6α receptors (glycoprotein 80, gp80) and IL-6β receptors (glycoprotein 130, gp130) which activates JAK2-STAT3 signaling pathway. An earlier study showed that targeted infusion of IL-6 resulted in muscle atrophy, along with an increase in STAT3 expression [197]. TNFα and IFNγ also activate STAT3 signaling, however, unlike IL-6, muscle wasting through this mechanism involves cooperation between STAT3 and NF-κB signaling. Oncostatin M (OSM), a recently identified potent inducer of muscle atrophy, also functions through the activation of the JAK-STAT signaling [31].

The mechanisms by which STAT3 induce muscle wasting, especially during cancer-induced cachexia, have also been somewhat elucidated. IL-6-mediated activation of STAT3 promotes muscle wasting in tumor-bearing mice through augmenting the activity of caspases and UPS [198]. Indeed, overexpression of STAT3 is sufficient to induce muscle atrophy and stimulate the gene expression of MAFbx and MuRF1. Conversely, treatment with JAK or STAT3 inhibitors attenuate skeletal muscle loss in tumor-bearing mice [199]. Similar to its role during cancer cachexia, a few studies have demonstrated that genetic or pharmacological inhibition of JAK-STAT signaling attenuates the loss of skeletal muscle mass following denervation or sepsis [200, 201]. Interestingly, endurance training alleviates muscle wasting during aging through the inhibition of JAK2/STAT3 signaling [202]. Furthermore, constitutive STAT3 phosphorylation leads to enhanced fatty acid metabolism and insulin resistance in skeletal muscles of diabetic patients [203] suggesting distinct mechanisms downstream of STAT3 are involved in the regulation of skeletal muscle mass in different conditions.

While the role of STAT3 transcription factor has been somewhat elucidated, there is limited knowledge of how other STAT molecules, including STAT1, STAT2, STAT5, and STAT6, regulate skeletal muscle mass in various conditions. Goldberg et al. [204] showed that genetic ablation of STAT1 in mice leads to the increased expression of ULK1 and autophagy flux in normal skeletal muscle, which is further increased in a model of LPS-induced septic inflammation. While the study did not determine the effect of LPS on muscle mass or function, it can be presumed that STAT1 may play a protective role in sepsis-induced muscle atrophy through limiting autophagy response [204]. Klover et al. suggested a link between postnatal muscle growth and the transcription factors STAT5a/b. It was shown that muscle-specific ablation of Stat5a and Stat5b leads to retardation of postnatal body growth, potentially through reduced local IGF1 expression [205]. Moreover, STAT5a/b regulates skeletal muscle growth and fiber composition in adult mice through direct transcriptional regulation of the androgen receptor [206]. Another study demonstrated that STAT5b mediates the regulation of growth hormone (GH) signaling, a critical regulator of muscle mass, in an age- and sex-dependent manner during skeletal muscle growth in mice [207]. Recently, Wang et al. revealed that B cell lymphoma 6 (BCL6) plays a vital role in maintaining muscle mass through potentiating GH signaling. However, in conditions of nutrient deprivation, GH itself transcriptionally represses BCL6 through the JAK/STAT5 pathway, suggesting a feedback mechanism during fasting-induced muscle atrophy [208]. Finally, Sheng et al. demonstrated that the inhibition of STAT6 significantly attenuates muscle atrophy and improves muscle function in an animal model of sepsis [209]. While these studies provide initial evidence, further investigations are needed to identify the mechanisms by which JAK-STAT signaling regulates skeletal muscle mass in distinct conditions.

MAPK signaling

The MAPK signaling is a major mechanism through which extracellular stimuli regulate cell proliferation, growth, differentiation and stress responses. Extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2), c-Jun N-terminal kinase 1/2 (JNK1/2), and p38 are major MAPKs in mammalian cells. The p38 MAPK family comprises four isoforms, namely p38α, p38β, p38γ, and p38δ, which are distinctly expressed in different tissues [210]. MAPKs, especially p38 MAPK, are activated in the skeletal muscle in response to endurance and resistance exercise and nutritional uptake [211, 212]. Recently, a novel muscle-brain interaction involving p38 MAPK signaling in muscle in response to exercise has been identified. It was shown that exercise induces the activation of p38γ signaling, leading to the secretion of IL-15 myokine that increases locomotor activity [213]. Another recent study showed that resistance exercise in humans and mice leads to the activation of a signaling pathway involving MKK3b/4/6, p38, MAPK-activated protein kinase 2 (MK2), and mTORC1 to increase protein synthesis and stimulate muscle hypertrophy. The role of MKK3b/6- p38 MAPK axis in skeletal muscle growth is supported by the findings that forced activation of MKK3b is sufficient to augment protein synthesis and myofiber diameter in skeletal muscle of mice [85]. Collectively, the available literature suggests that p38 activity mediates skeletal muscle growth and adaptation following exercise.

While transient activation of p38 MAPK promotes muscle growth, spurious activation of p38 MAPK-mediated signaling generally leads to the loss of skeletal muscle mass in various catabolic conditions. Tumor-derived factors augment p38β MAPK-mediated phosphorylation of p300 protein at Ser12 residue leading to the acetylation of C/EBPβ transcription factor, which itself is sufficient to induce muscle wasting. Moreover, treatment with Nilotinib, a selective p38β MAPK inhibitor, ameliorates muscle loss and improves survival of KPC tumor-bearing mice, a mouse model of pancreatic cancer cachexia [214]. The p38β-mediated activation of the C/EBPβ transcription factor increases the gene expression of autophagy-related molecules, including LC3B, Gabarapl1, and Ulk1 in skeletal muscle of tumor-bearing mice [215]. The p38 MAPK signaling also acts as a communication link between oxidative stress and autophagy to mediate muscle wasting during cachexia. Treatment of cultured myotubes with hydrogen peroxide induced the activation of p38 MAPK along with increased expression of autophagy-associated genes, including Atg6, Atg7, and Atg12, which was abrogated by blocking p38α/β signaling using the SB202190 inhibitor [216]. Another study demonstrated that treatment with Corylifol A preserves skeletal muscle mass during cancer cachexia through inhibition of the thousand-and-one amino acid kinase 1 (TAOK1)/p38/FoxO3 axis [217] further suggesting that p38 MAPK-mediated signaling is a critical driver of muscle wasting during cancer cachexia.

Compared to p38 MAPK, there is limited knowledge about the role of ERK1/2 and JNK1/2 MAPKs in the regulation of skeletal muscle mass. Lessard et al. showed that the activation of JNK signaling in response to resistance exercise leads to muscle growth via inhibition of myostatin/SMAD signaling. The study also showed that inactivation of JNK leads to skeletal muscle remodeling and results in smaller myofibers with enhanced oxidative capacity [218]. Interestingly, a recent study demonstrated that JNK signaling promotes muscle wasting during pancreatic cancer cachexia, and that treatment with SP600125, an inhibitor of JNK signaling, attenuates the gene expression of MAFbx and MuRF1 in skeletal muscle leading to reduced muscle wasting in tumor-bearing mice [219]. In addition, JNK signaling has also been shown to induce the activation of FOXO3a, which is known to augment the gene expression of MAFbx and MuRF1 and various autophagy-related modecules [220–223]. Like JNK, pharmacological inhibition of ERK1/2 attenuates the loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength in the C26 mouse model of cancer cachexia [224]. Other reports also demonstrate that treatment with distinct pharmacological compounds ameliorates muscle wasting through direct or indirect inhibition of ERK-FOXO or JNK-FOXO signaling pathways [222, 225].

Glucocorticoid signaling

Glucocorticoids are essential for maintaining homeostasis during acute stress. However, chronic exposure or high dose glucocorticoid therapy is strongly associated with skeletal muscle wasting [226]. Glucocorticoids augment the gene expression of multiple atrophy-related molecules, including MAFbx and MuRF1 through augmenting the activity of FoxO transcription factors [227, 228] (Fig. 2). Intriguingly, glucocorticoid-induced muscle atrophy is partially mediated through the activation of Notch signaling pathway [229]. Further emphasizing their pathological role, a study revealed that glucocorticoid receptor (GR) signaling is essential for muscle wasting in inflammatory states, such as endotoxemia and cancer cachexia. Skeletal muscle-specific ablation of GR in mice attenuated lipopolysaccharide-induced muscle atrophy. Moreover, ablation of GR in skeletal muscle significantly attenuated the loss of muscle mass in the LLC model of cancer cachexia emphasizing that glucocorticoids act as important mediators of catabolic responses in skeletal muscle [230].

Recent studies have also identified several modulators of glucocorticoid-induced muscle atrophy, offering potential therapeutic avenues. Sirtuin 6 (SIRT6) is a chromatin-associated NAD+-dependent enzyme with deacetylase, deacylase, and mono-ADP-ribosylase activities, which exacerbates glucocorticoid-induced muscle atrophy by suppressing IGF1/PI3K/Akt signaling and enhancing FoxO activity. Pharmacological inhibition of SIRT6 alleviated dexamethasone-induced muscle wasting, suggesting that SIRT6 is a promising target to counteract glucocorticoid myopathy [231]. Similarly, Kim et al. reported that TAZ, a transcriptional coactivator and downstream effector of the Hippo pathway, protects against dexamethasone-induced muscle atrophy by promoting mTORC1-mediated protein synthesis. Dexamethasone treatment suppressed TAZ expression, facilitating FoxO-dependent activation of MAFbx and MuRF1. Restoring TAZ activity rescued muscle mass and strength in response to treatment with high doses of glucocorticoids [232].

Beyond these canonical pathways, alternative signaling cascades also modulate glucocorticoid-driven muscle atrophy. Dexamethasone upregulates DUSP22, a dual-specificity phosphatase that promotes FoxO3a activation via JNK signaling independently of Akt. Pharmacological inhibition of DUSP22 prevented dexamethasone-induced myofiber atrophy, revealing a novel therapeutic axis outside of the traditional PI3K/Akt/FoxO signaling [225]. Interestingly, differential responses to glucocorticoids are observed among distinct muscle groups. In a model of sepsis, the gastrocnemius muscle exhibited higher activation of glucocorticoid signaling and associated atrophy program compared to the diaphragm, which was relatively protected [233]. These findings highlight the importance of muscle-specific regulatory environments in determining susceptibility to catabolic stimuli.

Finally, inflammatory mediators appear to intersect with glucocorticoid signaling to influence skeletal muscle mass. The 5-lipoxygenase enzyme (Alox5) was recently identified as a key contributor to dexamethasone-induced muscle atrophy. Pharmacological inhibition of Alox5 with malotilate preserved skeletal muscle mass and strength, suggesting that targeting inflammatory lipid mediators may offer an additional strategy to mitigate glucocorticoid-related muscle loss [234]. Collectively, these findings underscore the multifaceted and context-dependent roles of glucocorticoid signaling in skeletal muscle physiology by demonstrating that while glucocorticoids are indispensable for acute stress responses, they also drive atrophic responses via multiple converging pathways (Fig. 2).

Other signaling mechanisms regulating muscle mass

In addition to the major signaling pathways discussed above, several auxiliary mechanisms also play important roles in regulating skeletal muscle mass under diverse physiological and pathological conditions. Pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin, Notch, estrogen receptor, estrogen-related receptors (ERRs), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling are critically involved in muscle development, regeneration, growth, and maintenance. Elucidating how these signaling cascades interact with primary regulatory pathways will offer a more comprehensive understanding of the complex molecular networks that govern skeletal muscle mass. In this section, we briefly discuss the role of some of these additional signaling mechanisms in the regulation of skeletal muscle mass.

Wnt signaling

Wnt signaling pathways are broadly classified into canonical and non-canonical branches. The canonical pathway is characterized by the stabilization and nuclear translocation of β-catenin, which subsequently regulates the expression of target genes. In contrast, non-canonical Wnt pathways operate independently of β-catenin and typically involve planar cell polarity (PCP) signaling or calcium-dependent mechanisms. The canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays a crucial, context-dependent role in the regulation of skeletal muscle mass [235]. Under physiological conditions, its activation promotes muscle regeneration and growth. Specifically, Wnt/β-catenin signaling enhances muscle hypertrophy by stimulating the differentiation and fusion of muscle progenitor cells and by inhibiting myostatin activity [236, 237]. In a synergist ablation model of mechanical overload, activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling has been shown to upregulate growth-related genes, such as c-Myc and Cyclin D1 [238]. Furthermore, the importance of this pathway in muscle hypertrophy is highlighted by the findings that targeted deletion of β-catenin completely abrogates myofiber growth in response to functional overload [239].

A recent study demonstrated that chloride intracellular channel 5 (CLIC5) enhances myoblast differentiation and skeletal muscle regeneration through biglycan (BGN)-mediated activation of canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling [240]. Loss of CLIC5 impaired regeneration and reduced satellite cell function, ultimately leading to reduced muscle mass. Although CLIC5 does not directly regulate muscle atrophy, its absence impairs regenerative capacity and compromises long-term muscle maintenance. In contrast, aberrant activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in pathological conditions can lead to muscle wasting. Kajabadi et al. showed that β-catenin activation in mesenchymal progenitors promotes their expansion and induces the expression of catabolic factors, such as Noggin and Activin A, driving myofiber atrophy in a model of cancer cachexia [241]. These findings highlight the dual role of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in skeletal muscle, where it supports regeneration and growth under physiological or mechanically stimulated conditions but contributes to muscle wasting when dysregulated in disease states.

In addition, non-canonical Wnt signaling can promote myofiber hypertrophy through distinct mechanisms [242]. Wnt7a, for example, signals through its receptor Fzd7 to activate the planar cell polarity (PCP) pathway. In differentiated myofibers, Wnt7a induces hypertrophy by activating the Akt/mTOR anabolic signaling cascade via recruitment of Gαs and PI3. Remarkably, this effect occurs independently of IGF receptor-mediated signaling [242]. These findings point to a unique role of non-canonical Wnt signaling in muscle growth.

Further investigations are needed to determine whether and how canonical and non-canonical Wnt pathways interact with other signaling networks involved in the regulation of skeletal muscle mass.

Notch signaling

Notch signaling is another critical pathway involved in the regulation of skeletal muscle mass, playing dual roles depending on the physiological or pathological context. Under normal conditions, Notch signaling is essential for muscle stem cell maintenance and proliferation, thereby supporting regeneration and hypertrophy [243]. However, aberrant or sustained activation of this pathway can contribute to muscle atrophy. Recent studies have uncovered novel mechanisms linking mitochondrial dynamics to Notch signaling during muscle atrophy. The disruption of mitochondrial-associated membranes (MAMs) through deletion of the fusion protein Mitofusin 2 (MFN2) or microgravity exposure leads to the impairment of myogenic differentiation and increased susceptibility to muscle atrophy. Notably, MAM disruption led to the pathological activation of the Notch pathway, exacerbating muscle degeneration [244]. Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of Notch signaling ameliorated myofiber atrophy, highlighting the interplay between mitochondrial organization and Notch activation as a crucial determinant of muscle mass maintenance. In contrast, under conditions of mechanical overload, Notch signaling supports muscle growth by regulating satellite cell behavior. Fukuda et al. showed that in hypertrophic muscle, satellite cells sustain the expression of HeyL, a downstream Notch effector, which suppresses MyoD expression and maintains muscle stem cell proliferation [245]. This mechanism differs from the classical regeneration process, where MyoD is rapidly upregulated. Loss of HeyL impairs muscle stem cell proliferation specifically during hypertrophy, resulting in reduced myonuclear accretion and blunted muscle growth.

Moreover, Fujimaki et al. identified an endothelial-muscular Notch axis critical for regulating muscle mass during atrophy [246]. They demonstrated that during disuse or diabetes-induced atrophy, endothelial cells upregulate the Notch ligand Dll4, which in turn activates Notch2 signaling in myofibers, even in the absence of direct cell-cell contact. Activation of this endothelial Dll4-muscular Notch2 axis promoted catabolic responses leading to muscle wasting. Strikingly, the inhibition of the Dll4-Notch2 axis prevented muscle atrophy and enhanced hypertrophy in response to mechanical overload, indicating that modulation of vascular-derived Notch signals could represent a novel therapeutic strategy for muscle-wasting conditions. Another study showed that Notch signaling is highly activated in cachectic muscle of an osteosarcoma-bearing mice and pharmacological inhibition of Notch signaling attenuated myofiber atrophy in this model [247].

Collectively, these studies reveal that Notch signaling exerts context-specific effects in skeletal muscle, promoting regenerative proliferation and hypertrophy under physiological stimulation, while contributing to atrophy when aberrantly activated through mitochondrial dysfunction or vascular signaling. A deeper understanding of the temporal and spatial regulation of Notch activity will be essential for developing targeted interventions to preserve or restore muscle mass in diverse clinical settings.

Estrogen receptor signaling

Estrogen receptors play a significant role in skeletal muscle physiology, particularly in the regulation of muscle mass, regeneration, and mitochondrial function in a sex-specific manner. Estrogens exert anabolic effects on skeletal muscle through receptor-mediated mechanisms that influence satellite cell activation, immune responses, and cellular metabolism. Activation of estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) has been shown to enhance the regenerative capacity of skeletal muscle and confer protection against atrophy, particularly in females [248]. Similarly, estrogen receptor beta (ERβ) contributes to muscle growth and repair, because its deletion in young female mice impairs satellite cell activation and myofiber hypertrophy [249]. Growing evidence suggests that estrogen supports skeletal muscle homeostasis by preserving membrane integrity, reducing oxidative stress, modulating apoptotic pathways, and maintaining satellite cell function [250].

ERR signaling. ERR is a family of orphan nuclear receptors, which share structural similarities with estrogen receptors, but lack a known endogenous ligand [251]. ERR family members, ERRα, ERRβ, and ERRγ have been found to play crucial roles in various biological processes, particularly in energy metabolism and development. ERRα is expressed abundantly in all the skeletal muscles. ERRγ is also expressed in all skeletal muscles, but its expression is much higher in oxidative slow-twitch than glycolytic fast-twitch myofibers [251]. Recent studies using genetic mouse models have demonstrated that ERRs play an important role in skeletal muscle adaptation in response to endurance exercise and in the regulation of skeletal muscle mass and function in catabolic conditions [251–253]. Muscle-specific ERRγ activation attenuates muscle atrophy, reduces fibrosis, and improves muscle function following anterior cruciate ligament injury, supporting a protective role of ERRγ in skeletal muscle homeostasis [254]. Future studies will determine whether forced expression of ERRs can prevent skeletal muscle wasting in other catabolic conditions, such as functional denervation and cancer cachexia.

Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling. Traditionally recognized for its role in angiogenesis, VEGF signaling directly influences skeletal muscle growth and regeneration. VEGF promotes the formation of new capillaries within skeletal muscle, enhancing oxygen and nutrient delivery that is critical for hypertrophy and repair. Moreover, VEGF contributes to the activation and survival of satellite cells. Genetic or pharmacological disruption of VEGF signaling impairs muscle regeneration and increases susceptibility to muscle atrophy under stress conditions. There is also a published report suggesting that VEGF-A expression by satellite cells is essential to maintain proximity to capillaries, a spatial relationship that supports their regenerative potential [255]. Additionally, recent studies have examined the mRNA-miRNA network of VEGF in a mouse model of denervation-induced muscle atrophy, further emphasizing the relevance of VEGF-mediated signaling in the regulation of skeletal muscle mass [256].

Taken together, these additional signaling mechanisms underscore the intricate and highly coordinated network of signaling and molecular mechanisms that regulate skeletal muscle mass. Elucidating the interplay among these pathways and their crosstalk with major regulators such as the IGF-1/Akt/mTOR and other pathways is essential for developing targeted therapies aimed at preserving or restoring muscle mass in various clinical contexts.

Conclusions

The discussion above underscores that skeletal muscle mass and function are regulated by a diverse array of signaling pathways across various physiological and pathological conditions. It is now well established that transient activation of certain pathways is essential for muscle growth and homeostasis; however, their chronic or dysregulated activation can contribute to muscle wasting and myopathy.

Over the past decade, multiple new signaling mechanisms have been identified in catabolic conditions affecting skeletal muscle. Most of these catabolic pathways act by enhancing the expression of components of UPS and autophagy machinery, processes that are tightly regulated by various transcription factors. Additionally, recent studies have uncovered new regulators that suppress protein synthesis during muscle atrophy. One notable example is the PERK arm of the UPR, which downregulates protein synthesis in response to catabolic stimuli such as TWEAK, a key muscle-wasting cytokine.

Further research is needed to elucidate the extrinsic and intrinsic factors that activate specific signaling pathways during muscle growth and atrophy. A deeper understanding of the crosstalk between these pathways in distinct physiological and disease contexts is critical for the development of targeted therapies for muscle-related disorders.

It is note worthy that much of our current knowledge regarding various signaling mechanisms is derived from studies in cultured mammalian cells or preclinical animal models. While these models are invaluable for dissecting molecular pathways, complementary studies in human subjects are necessary to validate these findings and assess their clinical relevance. Despite these limitations, an expanding body of evidence supports the therapeutic potential of targeting specific signaling pathways to enhance or preserve skeletal muscle mass across a range of conditions.
