Bioinspired electrochemical artificial muscles with low voltage redox triggering and spontaneous contraction
Authors: Xiaobo Wang, Ming Ren, Yuxuan Liu, Lizhong Dong, Guang Yang, Yuxin Li, Jiaxi Wang, Mingyang Gao, Xiaona Wang, Jiangtao Di
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Skeletal muscle operates through a mechanism in which calcium ions act as a trigger at low electric potentials, followed by contraction powered by adenosine triphosphate hydrolysis. Inspired by this process, we have developed an electrochemical artificial muscle with low-voltage redox triggering and spontaneous contraction. Triggering is achieved by electrochemically charging the muscles at voltages below 0.4 V, which enables I3⁻ injection and subsequent oxidation to I2. Spontaneous muscle contraction then occurs via the reaction I2 + I⁻ → I3⁻, analogous to the role of adenosine triphosphate in skeletal muscle contraction, yet fundamentally distinct from conventional electrochemical artificial muscles. Following this mechanism, pristine carbon nanotube yarn muscles and carbon nanotube yarn muscles modified by quaternary ammonium cations generated contractile strokes of 5.2% and 15.5%, respectively. Moreover, the contracted state can be stably maintained without electrical energy input, and full recovery to the original length is achieved by applying a reverse voltage. Such mechanisms present a promising strategy for the development of energy-efficient artificial muscles.

Introduction

Artificial muscles, as a type of biomimetic actuator capable of reproducing the movement modes of biological muscles, have attracted increasing attention in fields such as soft robotics and biomimetic devices, owing to their advantages of direct, efficient actuation, ease of operation, and silent operation1–3. Artificial muscles have been developed in a variety of morphologies, including membranous4,5 and fibrous6–8 forms, with stimulus-response modes tailored to specific application scenarios. For instance, humidity-responsive artificial muscles can be integrated into smart textiles for humidity-regulated thermal management9,10, while magneto-responsive artificial muscles can be remotely controlled for precise and efficient medical surgeries11–13. In contrast, scenarios requiring high controllability more commonly employ electric control strategies, such as electrothermal or electrochemical actuation. The former relies on temperature-dependent volume changes induced by joule heating8,14, while the latter generates actuation through ion intercalation/deintercalation or redox reactions15–18. A broad range of materials, including highly aligned polymer fibers19,20, shape memory alloys and polymers8,21–23, and liquid crystal elastomers24,25, have been explored for electrothermal artificial muscles. Among electrochemical systems, coiled carbon nanotube (CNT) yarns electrochemically driven in organic electrolytes have demonstrated a contractile stroke of 62.4%17,26,27.

Despite these advances, electronically controlled artificial muscles face a fundamental limitation: they require continuous electrical energy input during actuation, leading to inefficient energy utilization. In contrast, the actuation logic of biological muscles presents a strategy. The contraction of natural muscles follows a two-step trigger-contraction mechanism, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. Calcium ions are first released under neural regulation to expose specific binding sites for the sliding of thick and thin myofilaments, after which contraction proceeds through the hydrolysis of adenosine triphosphate (ATP)28,29. This mechanism ensures both precise control and highly efficient energy utilization. In this work, we present an artificial muscle that achieves low-voltage-triggered spontaneous contraction inspired by the biological mechanism. This mechanism is realized by coupling the triiodide/iodide (I3−/I−) redox pair in the aqueous electrolyte with coiled CNT yarn. Actuation is triggered by the electrochemical oxidation of I3− to I2 at applied voltages below 0.4 V, after which spontaneous contraction of the CNT yarn muscle occurs without the need for further external electrical input, enabled by the in situ regeneration of I3−. Further modifying CNT yarns using quaternary ammonium ions achieved better actuation performance due to the enhanced binding between I3− ions and the muscle.

Results

Actuation system design

Figure 1a illustrates the actuation process of skeletal muscle. Upon neural stimulation, Ca2+ ions released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum bind to troponin, which exposes the binding sites on actin (Fig. 1a, I–II). Myosin then attaches to actin and pulls it along its length, powered by ATP hydrolysis (Fig. 1a, III), resulting in muscle contraction28.

A logically similar process of low-voltage triggering (Fig. 1b, I–II) and subsequent reaction-driven contraction (Fig. 1b, II–III) is schematically depicted for a coiled CNT artificial muscle utilizing the I3−/I− redox couple as the guest material. A two-electrode system was employed with the coiled CNT yarn serving as the working electrode and a platinum sheet as the counter electrode. The triggering process was carried out by positively charging the CNT yarn relative to the Pt counter electrode at a voltage above the oxidation potential of I3−, using either cyclic voltammetry (CV) or chronoamperometry. After the triggering step, the CNT yarn continued to contract even without any further external electricity input. This is in contrast to the actuation behavior of conventional electrochemical artificial muscles, whose contraction relies on continuous voltage application for the injection of ions7,15,16.

Figure 1c illustrates the actuation profile during both the process of low-voltage triggering (I–II) and the spontaneous actuation (II–III). The voltage was swept positively from 0 to 0.4 V at 20 mV s−1, resulting in a 0.7% contraction at 0.4 V. Afterward, the power supply was turned off, yet the yarn impressively continued to contract and reached a contractile stroke of 5.2%. Importantly, the contractile stroke was well maintained for over 5 h (Fig. 1c). This suggests an energy-free catch-state feature, which much surpasses the catch-state performance of the previously reported CNT yarn muscle driven by tetrachloroaluminate ions30, and the CNT yarn muscle working in a Na2SO4 aqueous electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 1). This energy-free catch state could be due to the enhanced interactions between the injected guest ions and CNTs, which can reduce the self-discharging of CNT yarns15,31.

Characterization of the actuation mechanism

We investigated the trigger-actuation coupling mechanism for the CNT yarn muscles in the I3−/I− redox couple (Fig. 2). The electrolyte was prepared by dissolving I2 in a potassium iodide (KI) aqueous solution, with the I2 concentration set at 0.05 mol L−1. The dissolution of I2 is driven by complexation with iodide ions to form soluble triiodide ions (I2 + I− → I3−)32,33. A pair of peaks, located at −0.39 and 0.22 V, is observed in the electrolyte with a KI/I2 ratio of 2 when CV scanning is performed between −0.5 and 0.5 V at 20 mV s−1 using a two-electrode configuration (Fig. 2a), which are attributed to the redox conversion between I3− and I234–36. Similar redox peaks were observed in the electrolytes with KI/I2 ratios of 10, 16, and 30, and all of these electrolytes supported the actuation of the coiled CNT yarn in this small voltage range (Supplementary Fig. 2). However, when a 0.5 mol L−1 KI aqueous solution without I2 was used as the electrolyte, the coiled CNT yarn did not contract during the CV scanning, even between −0.8 and 0.8 V (Supplementary Fig. 3a), while asymmetric redox pairs were observed (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Figure 2b compares the contractile actuation profiles of the coiled CNT yarn in the above-mentioned KI/I2 electrolytes. When the electrolyte with a KI/I2 molar ratio of 2 was utilized, the coiled CNT yarn began to contract immediately upon the onset of current, which could be due to the electrochemical injection of I3− ions into the CNT yarn. The CNT yarn continued contracting during the current rising that corresponds to the oxidation of I3− to I2 and reached 1.2% contraction at the current peak (step ①, Supplementary Fig. 2). Unexpectedly, the yarn gradually elongated, with the contraction decreasing to 1.0%, when the current started to decrease, but remained positive, and the applied voltage was still positively approaching its maximum (step ②). The current decrease observed in step ② is due to the consumption of the I3− ions in the double layer surrounding the CNT yarn (Supplementary Figs. 4 and S5)37. Interestingly, after this step, the yarn contracted again, reaching a maximum stroke of 4.3%, even though the current had reversed to negative and the voltage had swept into the negative range (step ③). Further scanning to the maximum negative voltage resulted in the recovery of the yarn muscle to its original elongated length (step ④). Similar contractile behavior was observed when CV was carried out using a three-electrode configuration (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Comparison of this actuation profile with that in Fig. 1c reveals that steps ② and ③ are not directly regulated by external voltage, suggesting that additional mechanisms are involved. Since the length change of an electrochemically driven coiled CNT yarn muscle results from the volume changes of guest species inside the yarn17, this unusual behavior can be understood in terms of ionic species transformations within the muscle.

Injection of I3− together with I− ions causes volume expansion of the yarn, whereas further oxidation of I3− to I2 reduces the volume of the injected species due to the loss of water molecules accompanying I3−. This interplay can explain the slight decrease in contractile stroke observed in step ② when the volume decrease due to I2 formation exceeds the volume increase caused by I3− injection. After this, the I2 formed inside the CNT yarn reacts with surrounding solvated I− ions, which regenerates I3− via the reaction I2 + I− → I3− (K ≈ 723 ± 10, 25 °C)32. Owing to the strong interactions between I2 and CNTs, this endothermic reaction can proceed spontaneously under the present conditions, enabling surrounding solvated I− ions to be injected into the CNT yarn muscle. This secondary formation of I3− accounts for the renewed contraction observed in step ③ due to the increased volume of guest materials. Completion of an actuation cycle can be achieved by applying a negative voltage, which reduces I3− to I− and facilitates ion extraction from the CNT yarn, thereby returning the muscle to its original length (Fig. 1b, III–I, Fig. 2b, step ④).

We observed this trigger-actuation coupling phenomenon only at low KI/I2 molar ratios (typically below 10, Fig. 2b). As the KI/I2 molar ratio increased to 10, the first contractile stroke peak increased to 4.92%, approximately 3.2 times the result obtained using the KI/I2 molar ratio of 2, and the subsequent trough contractile stroke was 3.4%. It is possibly due to the increase in ionic conductivity of the electrolyte, which rose from 13.9 to 61.7 mS/cm (Supplementary Fig. 6). We did not observe trigger-actuation coupling phenomenon when testing electrolytes with higher KI/I2 molar ratios, such as 16 and 30 or even higher ratios (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Figs. 2 and 7). The absence of the decrease in contractile stroke during I3− oxidation under these high KI/I2 ratio conditions could be attributed to the immediate reaction of the generated I2 with the abundant I− to form I3−. This rapid regeneration of I3− can offset the volume loss caused by I2 formation, preventing the mid-cycle elongation observed at lower KI/I2 ratios. Moreover, operating in these electrolytes with high KI/I2 ratios did not lead to even larger contractile strokes. This may be attributed to the altered hydration state of ions in the system that does not well match the pore structure of the artificial muscle fibers.

To further validate this mechanism hypothesis, we monitored the evolution of polyiodide ions during the contraction process by in situ Raman spectroscopy. Spectra were collected from both the CNT yarn surface (Fig. 2c, d) and the bulk electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 8, KI/I2 = 2) during CV scanning between −0.3 and 0.3 V at 1 mV s−1, and the corresponding contraction and current curves are shown in Fig. 2e. The peak at 113 cm−1 corresponds to the symmetric stretching vibration mode of I3−, while the peak near 163 cm−1 is assigned to I5− 38–40. Under the open-circuit condition, only I3− was detected in the bulk electrolyte. This is consistent with the dissolution of I2 in KI solution via the reaction I2 + I− → I3−. In contrast, both I3− and I5− signals were observed on the CNT yarn surface (Fig. 2f), which can be ascribed to the strong charge interactions between CNTs and iodine species41,42. As the voltage scanned positively from −0.3 to 0.3 V (Fig. 2e), the yarn muscle first contracted as a result of I3− injection (step ①), accompanied by a rise in current above 0 mA. Subsequently, a transient muscle elongation was observed due to the I3− oxidation. Correspondingly, the Raman signals of I3− on the CNT yarn decreased and eventually disappeared at this elongation stage (step ②) (Fig. 2c). This indicates the conversion of I3− to I2 via the reaction 2I3− − 2e− → 3I239. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images acquired at this stage revealed that the gaps on the surface of the CNT yarn were gradually filled due to the formation and accumulation of I2 (Fig. 2g)43,44. The presence of I2 in the yarn also enhanced the Raman signal of I5−, likely due to re-equilibration among iodine species (I2 + I− → I3−; I2 + I3− → I5−)32,45–47. Since I5− does not exhibit a significant volume difference compared to the combined volumes of I3− and I2, we did not attribute the volume change of yarn to I5− (Table S1). Shortly thereafter, the signal intensity of I3− increased, corresponding to the second contraction. This secondary contraction was attributed to the in situ regeneration of I3− through binding the solvated I− ions in the electrolyte to the I2 in the CNT yarn via I2 + I− → I3−. Concurrently, the consumption of I2 drove the dissociation of I5− via I5− → I2 + I3−, leading to a decrease in the I5− signal intensity.

The ex situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) showed that I3− was dominant on the CNT yarn muscle at the open-circuit condition (Supplementary Fig. 9), and it declined gradually as the I3−/I2 conversion occurred (Fig. 2h). With the voltage scanning back, the I3− signal was enhanced again (Supplementary Fig. 10). These results are consistent with the Raman spectroscopy observation. Furthermore, we investigated the evolution of the iodine species after the removal of energy input via in situ Raman spectroscopy. The intensity of I3− and I5− on the CNT yarn surface was almost constant within 180 s (Supplementary Fig. 11), after the scanning voltage was turned off at 0.3 V. This can well explain the observed energy-free catch state (Fig. 1c).

Actuation performance characterization

We investigated the effect of the triggering voltage on the spontaneous actuation of coiled CNT yarn muscles in the KI/I2 aqueous electrolyte system. Figure 3a illustrates the actuation process when the yarn was swept from −0.5 V to the triggering voltages ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 V in a 0.1 mol L−1 KI and 0.05 mol L−1 I2 aqueous electrolyte, as well as the self-contraction profiles after turning off the voltage. As the triggering voltage increased from 0.1 to 0.3 V, the pre-cutoff contraction slightly improved from 0.17 to 0.63%, reflecting the enhancement of I3− insertion with increasing voltage. However, further increasing the triggering voltage to 0.5 V did not result in improved yarn contraction, which could be caused by the increased charge transfer resistance (Rct) due to the consumption of I3− and the formation of I2 during the anodic oxidation (Supplementary Fig. 5). After voltage cutoff, significantly improved self-contractions compared to the pre-cutoff contraction were observed, with values ranging from 4 to 5.2% as the triggering voltage increased from 0.1 to 0.5 V (Fig. 3b). While the contractile stroke of 5.2% is smaller than that of previously reported electrochemical artificial muscles driven in organic electrolytes15,27,30,48, it is larger than the results for the artificial muscles driven in aqueous electrolytes, even at much higher voltages (Table S2). More importantly, the contractile stroke was achieved with a low-power trigger input of only 17.4 mJ. When an even lower triggering voltage of 50 mV was utilized, the corresponding triggering energy consumption was merely 0.105 mJ, but the yarn muscle achieved 0.0125 mJ of contractile work. If only the triggering energy is considered as the input energy, the energy conversion efficiency during muscle contraction reached 11.9%, which is much higher than previously reported electrochemical artificial muscle fibers15,16,48. When chemical energy from the reaction I2 + I− → I3− is included in the total input energy, the efficiency was approximately 1.1% (Supplementary Fig. 13).

The pre-cutoff contraction and self-contraction of the coiled CNT yarn at different applied loads are shown in Fig. 3c, with the triggering voltage set to 0.4 V. In most cases, the self-contraction exceeded the pre-cutoff contraction. The corresponding work capacity during the self-contraction can reach up to 243 J kg−1, approximately 1.8 times that during the pre-cutoff stage (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Although the CNT yarn muscle can generate spontaneous actuation at relatively low triggering voltages, we found that restoring the contracted fiber to its original length requires the application of a reverse voltage. We further characterized the actuation performance of the artificial muscle fiber under CV. The influence of applied stress on the contractile stroke and work capacity during a full CV cycle is shown in Fig. 3d. The maximum contraction of 5.5% was achieved under an applied tension of 3.5 MPa, after which the contraction decreased with further increases in stress. In contrast, the work capacity increased with stress, reaching a maximum of 270 J kg−1 at 53 MPa, approximately 6.7 times higher than that of mammalian skeletal muscle (40 J kg−1)30. The maximum load capacity of the fiber reached 83 MPa in this study.

The scan rate during CV scanning significantly influences the full-cycle actuation behavior of the yarn muscle, as shown in Fig. 3e. As the scan rate decreased from 500 to 0.5 mV s−1, the contraction gradually increased, reaching a maximum of 6.8%. This trend can be attributed to the fact that ions have sufficient time to diffuse into the CNT yarn and induce volume change at lower scan rates. Notably, at a low scan rate of 0.5 mV s−1, the contraction decreases following the oxidation current peak became much less pronounced. This can be explained by the in situ generated I2 having enough time to react with I− to form I3−, thereby sustaining fiber contraction. In contrast, at higher scan rates (e.g., >20 mV s−1), the I3− and I− reaction cannot proceed fully, resulting in the observed contraction decrease after the oxidation process. Figure 3f demonstrates the cyclic stability tested with voltage scanning between −0.5 and 0.5 V at 20 mV s−1, and the contractile stroke only decreased by 7% after 1160 cycles, demonstrating the operational durability of the yarn muscle.

The isometric stress at different triggering voltages was investigated in Supplementary Fig. 15. The maximum generated isometric contractile stress after triggering can be up to ~1.6 MPa. The self-sustained contraction behavior in different KI/I2 electrolytes with the triggering voltage of 0.4 V was compared in Supplementary Fig. 16. With the concentration of KI increasing, the stroke during the triggering process rises while that during the spontaneous contraction decreases. The self-contraction effect is more significant in the electrolyte with less content of KI.

Actuation performance optimization

As indicated above, the actuation performance of CNT yarn muscles based on this mechanism remains limited. To address this, we introduce an electrochemical modification strategy using quaternary ammonium (QA+) cations. QA+ cations are known to interact with polyiodide anions via weak Lewis acid-base interactions49 and can be effectively intercalated into CNT yarns15. As illustrated in Fig. 4a, pre-intercalation of QA+ into the CNT structure introduces binding sites for polyiodides, enabling increased I3− insertion and thereby promoting greater volumetric expansion during actuation. During this process, a pristine CNT ribbon is used as the precursor. A negative bias drives the electrochemical insertion of QA+ cations, followed by twisting the modified CNT ribbon into a coiled yarn for actuation. The influence of QA+ molecular structure was investigated by varying the alkyl chain length (Fig. 4b). With increasing carbon chain length (tetraethylammonium ion < tetrapentylammonium ion < tetraoctylammonium ion, TEA+ < TPA+ < TOA+), the contractile stroke progressively improved. Among these, tetraoctylammonium (TOA+), with the longest alkyl chains, exhibited the most pronounced enhancement, increasing the maximum contractile stroke from 6 to 9.7%.

To further elucidate this trend, we evaluated the interaction between QA+ and I3− using chemical hardness derived from HOMO-LUMO energy calculations (Fig. 4c)49. As shown in Fig. 4d, chemical hardness decreases with increasing alkyl chain length, indicating a stronger affinity for I3− ions. This enhanced interaction promotes greater ion retention within the CNT yarn, thereby accounting for the improved actuation performance observed with larger QA+ cations.

The actuation behavior of pristine and TOA+-modified CNT yarns was compared under CV between −0.5 and 0.5 V at a scan rate of 20 mV s−1 (Fig. 4e). Notably, the elongation observed after oxidation in pristine yarns was eliminated after TOA+ modification, indicating more stable ion insertion and sustained volumetric expansion. The dependence of contractile stroke and work capacity on applied stress is shown in Fig. 4f. As the stress increased from 3.5 to 19.5 MPa, the contractile stroke decreased from 11.3 to 4.7%, while the work capacity increased from 0.17 to 0.54 J g−1.

Chronoamperometry measurements further compared the dynamic actuation responses under 3.5 MPa across frequencies from 0.005 to 0.1 Hz (Fig. 4g, h). The TOA+-modified yarn consistently exhibited higher contractile stroke and faster actuation rates than the pristine yarn. While the pristine CNT yarn showed a limited maximum contraction of 2.8%, the TOA+-modified yarn reached 11.2%, nearly threefold higher. At a representative frequency of 0.02 Hz, the modified yarn continued contracting throughout the charging process, whereas the pristine yarn reached a plateau (~2.2%) after 15 s (Fig. 4i).

Finally, the TOA+ concentration during electrochemical modification was optimized to further enhance performance. As the concentration increased from 0.01 to 0.15 mol L−1, the contractile stroke first increased and then decreased, reaching a maximum of 15.5% at 0.1 mol L−1. Within a low voltage window of −0.5 to 0.5 V, this corresponds to an improvement in contraction from 6 to 15.5% after TOA+ modification, representing a significant advance for low-voltage-driven artificial muscles operating in aqueous electrolytes (Table S2).

Demonstrations of application

Figure 5 illustrates an application-oriented exploration of this class of artificial muscle yarns. To enable practical operation outside of a liquid electrolyte environment, we developed a gel-encapsulated artificial muscle fiber. Inspired by popping boba, a product of the spherification technique in molecular gastronomy, in which juice droplets are encapsulated within a thin hydrogel elastic film, we adopted a similar strategy for encapsulating yarn muscle. Following this concept, we fabricated a gel-state artificial muscle yarn using a mold-based method (Fig. 5a). The electrolyte, prepared by dissolving 0.5 mol L−1 KI and 0.05 mol L−1 I2, was mixed with sodium alginate at a mass-to-volume ratio of 10 mg mL−1. A CNT yarn muscle was suspended inside the mold, which was then filled with the mixed electrolyte. Spraying calcium lactate solution onto the surface induced Ca2+-mediated cross-linking of alginate, forming a gel shell. After complete gelation, the mold was removed, yielding an encapsulated electrochemical artificial muscle. The mechanical tensile strength of the encapsulation gel was demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 17. CV curves confirmed efficient ion migration between the electrodes (Fig. 5c), demonstrating the successful construction of the system. Under applied voltage, the artificial muscle pair contracted by 3.5% (Supplementary Fig. 18). Importantly, the actuation performance was well maintained even after multiple cycles of separation and reattachment of the gel-encapsulated yarns (Supplementary Fig. 19).

Figure 5d illustrates an application in which the encapsulated electrochemical yarn muscle pair drives the bending of a bionic arm. At −1 V, the muscle pair remained elongated. When 0.5 V was applied, it lifted the arm by 5 mm (Movie S1). We further demonstrated its use as a switch in Supplementary Fig. 20, which can trigger the target process with a very low voltage. In the elongated state, the circuit remained open. Upon contraction, the muscle pair lifted the conductive object to close the circuit, thereby lighting the bulb. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 20, the bulb was initially off at −1 V due to the circuit being open. When the voltage was scanned positively and cut off at 0.5 V, the muscle was activated. Its spontaneous contraction lifted the conductive object, completing the circuit and turning on the bulb (Movie S2).

Given its low-voltage operation and energy-free catch-state capability, this artificial muscle is well suited for in vivo biomedical applications that require sustained contraction, such as sphincter-like functions in the urinary system. The electrochemical artificial muscle can function as an intelligent valve capable of maintaining closure without continuous power input. Analogous to a biological sphincter, the device constricts to prevent fluid leakage and remains closed even after the external voltage is removed until a reverse voltage is applied. This feature enables highly energy-efficient operation in physiological environments, as illustrated in Fig. 5e.

As a proof of concept, an intelligent valve driven by the artificial muscle is initially in an open state (Fig. 5f) and is closed by applying a positive voltage of 0.5 V. After the voltage is removed, the closed state is stably maintained. During this state, liquid introduced into the device is effectively retained without leakage. Upon application of a reverse voltage, the valve reopens, enabling controlled release of the stored liquid (Movie S3).

This demonstration highlights a promising strategy for developing intelligent, energy-efficient valve systems for biomedical applications.

Discussion

In summary, a mechanism for electrochemical artificial muscle is developed, which endows the yarn muscle with low operating voltage, triggered spontaneous contraction, and a catch state. The introduction of I3−/I− redox couple enables the in situ generation of active ions thanks to the strong bonding capacity between the I2 generated during the oxidation reaction and the free I− in the electrolyte. Electrochemical modification with QA+ ions effectively enhances the actuation of CNT yarn muscles in the I3−/I− redox couple, providing a strategy for actuation performance enhancement. A quasi-solid electrochemical artificial muscle was fabricated utilizing Ca2+ as the cross-linker. Such a simple encapsulation method shows great promise in overcoming the limitations of the traditional electrolyte environment. Further enhancement of the currently low actuation performance by screening more suitable redox couples is expected to expand the applications of these artificial muscles in devices requiring low external energy consumption.

Methods

Materials

Anhydrous ethanol (A.R.) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Hydrogen (99.99%) and argon (99.99%) were purchased from Linde Gas Co., Ltd. Thiophene (A.R.), ferrocene (A.R.), potassium iodide (KI, A.R.), iodine (I2, A.R.), tetraethylammonium bromide (TEAB, A.R.), tetrapentylammonium bromide (TPAB, A.R.), tetraoctylammonium bromide (TOAB, A.R.), and propylene carbonate (PC) were purchased from Aladdin Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Sodium alginate and calcium lactate were commercially acquired.

Fabrication of CNT yarn

The CNT ribbons used in this work were fabricated by floating catalytic chemical vapor deposition17,30. The anhydrous ethanol was used as a carbon source. The contents of thiophene and ferrocene were 2–5 wt% and 1–3 wt%, respectively. The carrier gas mixture of hydrogen and argon was employed in 2000 and 2200 sccm, respectively. The reaction furnace was set at 1300 °C, and the CNT ribbon was collected at a speed of 5 m min−1. The linear density of the collected CNT ribbon is 0.04 mg cm−1, and the coiled CNT yarn was obtained under a 2.5 g load with a twist density of 25,100 turns m−1. The weight of the CNT yarn muscle used for actuation performance characterization is 0.4 mg as measured.

Characterization

The electrochemical actuation was tested by a CHI 660E electrochemical workstation from CH Instruments, Inc. The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy tests in the three-electrode system were carried out on a VSP-3e potentiostat electrochemical workstation (BioLogic). The ionic conductivity of the electrolytes was characterized using a conductivity meter (Leici, DDSJ-308F). The linear density of the CNT ribbon was measured by a microbalance (XP2U, METTLER TOLEDO). The surface morphology of the CNT yarn muscle was characterized by a cold-field SEM (S-4800). All the Raman spectroscopy was characterized by using a HeNe laser with a wavelength of 532 nm (Renishaw in Via Qantor). The XPS was collected by ESCALAB XI+ (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to study the element valence.

Electrochemical actuation measurements

A two-electrode system was applied in all electrochemical actuation measurements by CHI 660E if not specifically mentioned. The CNT yarn muscle and Platinum sheet worked as the working electrode and counter electrode, respectively. The size of the Pt sheet used was 10 mm × 20 mm × 0.1 mm, and it was placed facing the CNT yarn and parallel to it. The distance between the CNT yarn and the Pt sheet was 1.5 cm. I3−/I− aqueous solution with deionized water as the solvent, and various mixing ratios were used as the electrolytes in this work. The contractile strokes were measured using a contactless inductive displacement sensor (M30, Shanghai Muxi Electronic Technology Co., Ltd.), which was calibrated before use. During the actuation process, an iron sheet was connected to the fiber via a thin wire, and its distance from the sensor was recorded as a voltage signal, which could be converted into an actuation signal based on the linear relationship between distance and voltage signal.

TOA+ modification of CNT yarn

The quaternary ammonium salts (TEAB, TPAB, and TOAB) were dissolved in PC at certain concentrations. A two-electrode system was employed for electrochemical modification, using the CNT ribbon as the working electrode and a graphite rod as the counter electrode. The CA method was adopted using a −4 ~ 0 V square wave voltage at 0.05 Hz for 1 h, during which no load was applied to the CNT ribbon. Significant volume expansion could be observed in the modification process. The CNT ribbon was then twisted into a coiled yarn under a 2.5 g load once the modification was completed.
