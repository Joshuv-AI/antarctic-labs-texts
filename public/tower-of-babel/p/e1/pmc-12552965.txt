Targeted digital intervention boosts endocrine therapy adherence in breast cancer patients with low health literacy
Authors: Lingling Meng, Yupeng Di
Published: 2025

Dear Editor:

We read with great interest the study by Graetz [1] examining how remote monitoring interventions (App ± tailored messages) improved 12-month adjuvant endocrine therapy (AET) adherence among breast cancer patients with lower health literacy. The authors demonstrated a striking 37.9-percentage-point increase in adherence for this subgroup using an App with weekly feedback—a promising strategy to address health inequities.

However, a critical methodological limitation warrants discussion: the oversimplified measurement of health literacy. The study relied solely on a single-item screener ("confidence filling medical forms"), dichotomized as "higher" (always confident) versus "lower" (any lesser frequency). While practical, this approach risks substantial measurement error and conceptual misclassification. First, health literacy is a multidimensional construct encompassing functional (basic comprehension), interactive (communication skills), and critical (evaluative judgment) domains. A form-filling question primarily captures functional literacy, neglecting critical dimensions like understanding AET's survival benefits or evaluating side-effect severity—factors directly influencing adherence. Second, collapsing "never," "rarely," "sometimes," and "often" into one "lower literacy" category obscures meaningful gradients. A participant answering "sometimes" likely possesses distinct capabilities and barriers compared to one answering "never," yet both received identical classification. This crude dichotomy may mask nuanced intervention effects and dilute statistical power. Third, the tool's validity for oncology contexts is unproven. Chew's original validation focused on general VA populations—not cancer patients navigating complex, long-term therapies with evolving toxicities. Consequently, the observed effect of "App + Feedback" might partially reflect unmeasured literacy facets (e.g., improved medication problem-solving via messages) rather than the construct narrowly defined by the instrument. Future studies should adopt multidimensional, cancer-specific health literacy assessments (e.g., Cancer Health Literacy Test) or at least stratify "lower literacy" subgroups to better identify who benefits most from tailored interventions. Addressing this limitation is vital to ensure equitable precision in adherence support.
