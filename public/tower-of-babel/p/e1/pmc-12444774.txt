Bronchoscopic Excision of Endobronchial Leiomyoma: Evidence for Successful Long‐Term Outcome
Authors: Elaine Yap
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary leiomyomas are rare benign tumours of bronchial smooth muscle origin. They can present either in the lung parenchyma or tracheobronchial tree, with 45% being located endobronchially. Endobronchial leiomyomas typically present with airways symptoms such as dyspnoea, cough, and obstructive pneumonia. Traditionally, endobronchial leiomyomas were managed surgically with lobectomy or pneumonectomy but, increasingly, management has shifted to bronchoscopic resection with different endoscopic techniques described in the case report literature. Most reports include outcomes ranging from 3 to 6 months following bronchoscopic resection. We present a case of a 40‐year‐old female with a left upper lobe (LUL) leiomyoma who presented with complete LUL collapse consolidation. Bronchoscopic resection was performed using a combination of diathermy snare, cryo‐recanalisation, and argon plasma coagulation (APC) which resulted in excellent restoration of the LUL patency. Annual bronchoscopy surveillance over 7 years confirmed durability of the procedure with no recurrence detected.

Introduction

Benign tumours of the tracheobronchial tree are uncommon entities. Of these, endobronchial leiomyoma accounts for 2% to 3% which makes them one of the rarest benign endoluminal tumours [1, 2]. Primary pulmonary leiomyoma is a tumour of smooth muscle origin and can present either as pulmonary or endobronchial lesions. Traditionally, endobronchial leiomyomas were managed with surgical resection but increasingly, bronchoscopic approaches have been employed as evidenced by case reports and series describing successful bronchoscopic resection of leiomyomas [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Most, however, do not provide long term outcome data beyond 6 months of the acute relief of the endobronchial tumour obstruction.

Case Report

We present a case of a 40‐year‐old female who presented to our institution with a productive cough and fevers for a month. Salient investigations such as her raised white cell count 17.7 × 109/L, C reactive protein 144 mg/L, and a chest x‐ray revealing left upper lobe (LUL) collapse suggested bronchial obstruction with resultant lobar infection. Computed tomography (CT) imaging confirmed an endobronchial lesion at the left upper lobe (Figure 1) with associated post‐obstructive atelectasis/consolidation of the anterior and apicoposterior segments. Her positron emission tomography (PET) CT showed moderate fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) avidity of the LUL tumour (standardised uptake value, SUV max 6.11) and marked avidity (SUV max 10.3) of the LUL lung parenchyma. Elsewhere, FDG avidity was noted as a linear focus within her retroflexed uterus. Uterine ultrasound showed no uterine fibroids, and endometrial pipelle biopsy was normal.

Initial axial CT and PET images (A, B) respectively showing the left upper lobe endobronchial lesion, denoted by red arrow.

The patient underwent flexible bronchoscopy under conscious sedation, where a well‐defined, pale, and smooth ovoid tumour causing complete obstruction of the LUL was encountered (Figure 2). Using forceps, the bronchoscopist was able to determine the tumour was mobile and not adherent to the mucosal wall except for the stalk originating from the medial wall of the LUL orifice. A separate small nodule was seen on the lateral wall proximal to the tumour (Figure 2 arrow). Endobronchial forceps biopsies of the tumour revealed bland spindle cells consistent with leiomyoma. Immunohistochemistry stains were further confirmatory when the tumour stained positive for SMA, MSA, and desmin, and negative for EMA, CD34, S100, DOG1, and panck. The Ki‐67 index was < 1.

Endobronchial images of the leiomyoma, pre and post bronchoscopic resection. (A) A smooth, pale lesion causing complete obstruction of the left upper lobe (LUL) orifice. Arrow denotes a separate unrelated nodule proximal to the leiomyoma. (B) Diathermy snare used to resect the leiomyoma. (C) LUL orifice completely patent with no evidence of recurrence at bronchoscopy 7 years later.

The multidisciplinary recommendation was for lung‐sparing bronchoscopic resection as the CT and PET images suggested a discrete LUL endoluminal leiomyoma without pulmonary extension, causing post‐obstructive atelectasis and infection. The second bronchoscopy was performed under general anaesthesia with rigid bronchoscopy. The tumour was successfully resected using a combination of diathermy snare, cryo‐recanalisation, and cryo‐extraction whilst argon plasma coagulation (APC) was applied to remove residual tumour and control bleeding. Forceps biopsy of the separate nodule revealed inflamed respiratory epithelium with no evidence of leiomyoma. A large volume of pus was released from beyond the tumour obstruction necessitating antibiotics. There were no procedural complications and CXR several weeks post resection showed resolution of the LUL collapse consolidation.

Inspection bronchoscopy at 2 weeks, 6 months, then yearly to 7 years showed no evidence of leiomyoma recurrence at the original site or elsewhere. The mucosal nodule which was previously demonstrated to not be related to the leiomyoma was removed in its entirety at one of the bronchoscopies which again showed benign respiratory epithelial cells.

Discussion

Endobronchial leiomyoma is one of the rarest benign tumours of the tracheobronchial tree [1, 2]. Approximately 45% of leiomyomas are endobronchial; the rest occur in the lung parenchyma or trachea [8]. Whilst pulmonary leiomyoma can be clinically silent, manifestations of endobronchial leiomyoma such as cough, wheeze, recurrent or refractory pneumonia are dependent on the location, size, and degree of airways obstruction. Because of the rarity of these tumours, evidence‐based management is not always possible.

In the last decade, case reports and series suggest bronchoscopic resection may be superseding traditional surgical resection in leiomyomas that are completely endoluminal and are not wide based [3, 4, 5, 6, 7], as wide based tumours are technically difficult to resect bronchoscopically and run the risk of incomplete resection [9]. This development is in part driven by the advancement in navigational and therapeutic bronchoscopy presenting an array of techniques to manage malignant and benign endobronchial tumours [10]. Bronchoscopic intervention provides several important benefits over conventional surgical approaches such as lobectomy or pneumonectomy [11] which can be complicated by chronic thoracotomy pain, prolonged air leak and extended hospital stay. In contrast, the bronchoscopic approach is minimally invasive, allows lung preservation and facilitates direct visualisation of airways mucosa. The latter provides an excellent baseline for bronchoscopic surveillance post resection for residual disease, early recurrence or transformation, not appreciable on imaging. Whilst malignant transformation of leiomyoma has not been reported, there have been rare instances of malignant transformations of benign airways tumours such as harmatoma [12] and papilloma [13]. A case of endobronchial leiomyoma associated with carcinoma in situ (CIS) was reported by Hans et al. [14].

To the best of my knowledge, this is the first report providing long‐term outcomes via bronchoscopy surveillance (no recurrence detected at 7 years) following successful bronchoscopic resection of a large obstructing endobronchial leiomyoma. Previous case reports have reported outcomes at 3 months [6] to 6 months [3, 7, 13] post bronchoscopic resection. Sugarman et al. detected a small residual stalk at 3 months follow up which they managed with cryotherapy [6]. Bawaadam et al. detected two small nodules at the site of the resected leiomyoma at 6 months bronchoscopy follow up, which they managed with cryoablation and electrocautery [3]. However, tissue was not sent for histology, so confirmation of recurrent leiomyoma was not possible. One series which included leiomyoma of the trachea, bronchus and pulmonary parenchyma, managed either surgically or bronchoscopically, performed regular CT surveillance and at least one bronchoscopy to a median of 43.2 months (range 5.6–125.7 months) [5]. In this series, nine of 16 patients received bronchoscopy intervention, of whom one whose procedure was complicated by a bronchomediastinal fistula had a recurrence of the endobronchial leiomyoma at 17 months, requiring surgery. It was unclear whether the follow up beyond the median period was on surgical or bronchoscopy patients. Kwon et al. reported one case of recurrence at 16 months and eight patients with no recurrence following endobronchial resection of leiomyomas after a median radiological follow up of 24.5 months (range 2–88 months) [9]. As interventional pulmonology centres proliferate globally, there will undoubtedly be an increase in expertise and desire to manage these tumours bronchoscopically due to the aforementioned advantages. Successful management is not defined by simply removal of the acute bronchial obstruction but also the durability of the intervention. The potential for late recurrence in many benign endobronchial tumours is unknown due to their rarity, specifically leiomyomas managed via bronchoscopic intervention, although older reports following surgical resection have been favourable [5, 11].

Case selection is crucial as leiomyomas with iceberg appearance on CT (intraluminal and extraluminal components) are not appropriate for sole bronchoscopic intervention [8]. This case provides clinicians with some confidence that bronchoscopic resection of endobronchial leiomyomas can be associated with long‐lasting benefits. Bronchoscopic surveillance was performed in this case as it was the patient's preference and she tolerated flexible bronchoscopy very well. Clinical and radiological surveillance are alternatives, but CT surveillance is associated with radiation risk, and it is possible that these measures may not detect early endobronchial recurrence until resultant airways obstruction has occurred.

The optimal bronchoscopic technique for resecting endobronchial leiomyoma is unknown. Practically, the intervention employed will most likely depend on the size and location of the tumour and local expertise, which can be extrapolated from endobronchial management of malignant tumours. Electrocauterization has been reported in a right middle lobe non‐obstructing leiomyoma with success [15]. A near‐obstructing right main bronchus leiomyoma was successfully debulked with the use of a microdebrider [16]. Others, like this case, have employed a combination of diathermy snare, cryotherapy, and APC [3, 4, 7, 14]. Outside of interventional pulmonology units, the knowledge of these interventions may not be widespread. The availability and benefits of nonsurgical approaches should be conveyed to non‐interventional clinicians who may otherwise opt for surgery or unwittingly discount curative treatment for the elderly, frail, or patients with poor cardiorespiratory reserves.

In conclusion, endobronchial leiomyomas are extremely rare entities that can cause significant airway obstruction. These benign airway tumours can be successfully managed with bronchoscopic resection, which is less invasive than traditional surgical approaches, carries less procedural morbidity, provides acute relief of the bronchial obstruction, and is a durable solution. Post‐resection surveillance is important to ensure recurrence is detected in a timely fashion. Although there is no evidence‐based recommendation for the appropriate surveillance period currently, existing reports of occasional recurrences suggest follow‐up beyond 1 to 2 years is not inappropriate.
