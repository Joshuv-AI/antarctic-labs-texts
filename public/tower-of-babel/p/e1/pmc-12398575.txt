A cohort study on the association of dietary inflammatory index and a posteriori dietary patterns with lung cancer risk
Authors: Qing Zhou, Zhongwen Gong, Yongjia LI, Lilong Pang, Yangjiao Bai, Ting Chen, Xuemei Lian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to explore the relationship between dietary patterns and the risk of lung cancer by combining the a priori Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) with a posteriori dietary patterns identified through factor analysis using a prospective cohort study. A total of 189,561 participants from the UK Biobank were included. Dietary intake was assessed using a 24-hour dietary recall questionnaire. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to assess the correlation between the DII and dietary pattern scores. Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to quantify the relationships between the DII, dietary pattern scores, and lung cancer risk, adjusting for various confounding factors. During a median follow-up of 9.45 years, 1041 participants developed lung cancer. A moderate negative correlation was observed between the DII and the fruits and vegetables dietary pattern score, with a Spearman’s rho of − 0.59 (P < 0.01). In the fully adjusted models, a higher DII was associated with a higher risk of lung cancer (HR T3 vs. T1: 1.17; 95%CI: 1.00,1.36; P for trend = 0.035). The fruits and vegetables dietary pattern was associated with a lower risk of lung cancer (HR T3 vs. T1: 0.78; 95%CI: 0.67, 0.91; P for trend = 0.002). Notably, the cereals and processed foods dietary pattern showed no significant association with lung cancer risk (HR T3 vs. T1: 0.97; 95%CI: 0.83, 1.12; P for trend = 0.658). Meanwhile, the highest tertile of the meat dietary pattern score was associated with a 18% higher risk of lung cancer than the lowest (HR T3 vs. T1: 1.18; 95%CI: 1.02, 1.37; P for trend = 0.046). A higher dietary inflammatory index is associated with a higher risk of lung cancer. Introduction

Lung cancer is the most prevalent malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with an estimated 2.5 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths in 20221. Most lung cancer patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage and have a five-year survival rate of less than 20% 2. Therefore, controlling the risk factors for lung cancer, and thus preventing its occurrence, is an important way to reduce its harm.

The risk factors for lung cancer are numerous, including both unmodifiable factors such as age, ethnicity, and genetic predisposition, as well as modifiable factors such as smoking, occupational exposure, and dietary factors. Among these, smoking is the most significant risk factor for lung cancer. It is estimated that approximately 90% of lung cancer cases worldwide are directly or indirectly related to smoking3. Nevertheless, around 10% of lung cancer cases are attributed to other risk factors. It is worth noting that dietary factors have garnered increasing attention in recent years, primarily concentrating on three aspects: foods, nutrients, and dietary patterns. Compared to studies that focus solely on individual foods or nutrients, dietary pattern analysis considers the intake of various foods or nutrients and their interactions, providing a more comprehensive approach to examining the relationship between overall dietary habits and lung cancer risk.

Generally, dietary patterns could be generated by two methods: a priori and a posteriori. A priori dietary patterns are developed using scoring criteria derived from existing dietary guidelines or scientific evidence4. The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) is a typical example of an a priori dietary pattern. Previous studies have shown that diet can affect the body’s inflammatory state5furthermore, inflammation plays a crucial role in the initiation and progression of cancer by promoting genetic mutations, cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and other pathways6. As a tool for assessing the inflammatory potential of diet, the DII can quantify the impact of diet on the body’s inflammatory state, thereby evaluating the association between diet and cancer risk. Although the DII has been associated with the risk of various cancers, including colorectal cancer7,8 breast cancer9,10 and prostate cancer11,12 studies on its correlation with lung cancer risk are relatively few, and the results lack consistency. Two case-control studies and two prospective cohort studies have shown a positive correlation between the DII and lung cancer risk13–16 whereas other studies have failed to find a significant association17–19.

Unlike a priori dietary patterns, a posteriori dietary patterns are determined using statistical techniques such as factor analysis and cluster analysis4. Factor analysis, a dimensionality reduction method, identifies dietary patterns by examining the correlation matrix of food variables. A posteriori dietary patterns can reveal unique dietary habits of specific populations, characterized by high diversity and flexibility. Integrating these patterns with the DII can enhance our understanding of the features of high-DII diets and provide a more comprehensive assessment of the relationship between dietary patterns and lung cancer risk. To date, no studies have combined the DII with a posteriori dietary patterns to investigate this relationship.

Therefore, this study, based on the large prospective cohort data from the UK Biobank, explores the relationship between dietary patterns and lung cancer risk by combining a priori DII with a posteriori dietary patterns identified through factor analysis, providing clues for the research of risk factors for lung cancer and its prevention strategies.

During a median follow-up of 9.45 years, a total of 1041 newly diagnosed lung cancer cases were observed. Compared to participants who did not develop lung cancer, those with incident lung cancer were significantly older, comprised a higher proportion of males, were less educated and more socially deprived, and had a higher percentage of retirees and obesity cases. A posteriori dietary patterns and their correlation with DII

The rotated factor-loading matrices for a posteriori dietary patterns and Spearman’s correlation coefficients between DII and each dietary pattern score are shown in Table 2. Both the KMO (0.509) and the Bartlett’s test (P < 0.001) confirm the suitability of the data for factor analysis. Three posterior dietary patterns were identified, which explained 28.54% of the variance of 15 food groups. The first pattern was characterized by high intakes of fruits, vegetables, and non-alcoholic beverages, which was named the fruits and vegetables dietary pattern. The second pattern had high factor loadings for cereals, cereal products, fat, spreads, sugar, preserves, snacks, cakes and confectionery, which was labeled the cereals and processed foods dietary pattern. The third pattern exhibited a diet high in meat and meat products, which was identified as the meat dietary pattern. A moderate negative correlation was observed between the DII and the fruits and vegetables dietary pattern score, with a Spearman’s rho of − 0.59 (P < 0.01). In contrast, the correlation between the DII and the cereals and processed foods dietary pattern score, although statistically significant, was notably weak, as evidenced by a Spearman’s rho of − 0.09 (P < 0.01). Similarly, the relationship between the DII and the meat dietary pattern score was statistically significant but also relatively weak, with a Spearman’s rho of − 0.19 (P < 0.01).

Associations between the DII, dietary pattern scores, and the risk of lung cancer

The results of Cox proportional hazards regression analysis on the associations between the DII, dietary pattern scores, and the risk of lung cancer are presented in Table 3. In the fully adjusted Model 2, compared with the lowest tertile of DII, the highest tertile had a 17% higher risk of lung cancer (HR T3 vs. T1: 1.17; 95%CI: 1.00, 1.36; P for trend = 0.035). In contrast, the highest tertile of the fruits and vegetables dietary pattern score was associated with a 22% lower risk of lung cancer than the lowest tertile (HR T3 vs. T1: 0.78; 95%CI: 0.67, 0.91; P for trend = 0.002). Notably, no significant association was observed between the cereals and processed foods dietary pattern and the risk of lung cancer (HR T3 vs. T1: 0.97; 95%CI: 0.83,1.12; P for trend = 0.658). The highest tertile of the meat dietary pattern score also demonstrated a 18% higher risk of lung cancer compared to the lowest tertile (HR T3 vs. T1: 1.18; 95%CI: 1.02,1.37; P for trend = 0.046).

Figure 1 depicts the relationships between DII, dietary pattern scores, and lung cancer risk using restricted cubic splines. A significant non-linear association was observed between DII and lung cancer risk (P for overall < 0.001; P for non-linearity < 0.001). Specifically, the hazard ratio (HR) remained relatively stable at lower DII values but increased sharply once the DII exceeded the threshold of 0.07. Regarding the fruits and vegetables dietary pattern score, there was a significant inverse association with lung cancer risk (P for overall < 0.001; P for non-linearity = 0.096). Lung cancer risk decreased significantly as the dietary pattern score increased beyond − 3.54, and this downward trend plateaued when the score reached approximately 0.49. In contrast, a significant linear positive association was found between the meat dietary pattern score and lung cancer risk (P for overall < 0.001; P for non-linearity = 0.999). Notably, lung cancer risk increased markedly when the meat dietary pattern score ranged from − 0.27 to 1.35. However, no significant association was detected between the cereals and processed foods dietary pattern score and lung cancer risk across the entire range of scores (P for overall = 0.998; P for non-linearity = 0.995).

In addition, we analyzed the relationship between the DII, dietary pattern scores, and lung cancer risk across different smoking statuses, with results shown in Table 4. A higher DII was found to be associated with a higher lung cancer risk only among former smokers (HR T3 vs. T1: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.02, 1.55; P for trend = 0.031). The interaction between DII and smoking status on lung cancer risk was not statistically significant (P for interaction = 0.620). A higher fruits and vegetables dietary pattern score was associated with a lower lung cancer risk in both former smokers (HR T3 vs. T1: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.63, 0.97; P for trend = 0.027) and current smokers (HR T3 vs. T1: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.54, 0.99; P for trend = 0.024). For the cereals and processed foods dietary pattern score and the meat dietary pattern score, no significant associations with lung cancer risk were observed in stratified analyses by smoking status; furthermore, none of the interactions between the three dietary pattern scores and smoking status on lung cancer risk were statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study based on prospective data from the UK Biobank, we found that (1) a higher DII was associated with a higher risk of lung cancer; (2) a moderate negative correlation existed between the DII and the fruits and vegetables dietary pattern; (3) the fruits and vegetables dietary pattern was associated with a lower risk of lung cancer; (4) the meat dietary pattern was associated with a higher risk of lung cancer, and (5) no significant association was observed between the cereals and processed foods dietary pattern and lung cancer risk.

In recent years, an increasing number of studies have indicated that diet plays a pivotal role in modulating the inflammatory state of the body20–22 which is closely associated with the initiation and progression of cancer23. Therefore, investigating the relationship between dietary inflammatory potential and cancer risk is of great significance in elucidating how dietary factors influence cancer risk. This study employed DII to assess the inflammatory potential of diets and explored its association with lung cancer risk. It was found that a higher DII is associated with a higher risk of lung cancer, consistent with several previous studies13–16. A case-control study in Iran showed that individuals in the highest tertile of the DII had a 38% higher risk of lung cancer compared with those in the lowest tertile (OR: 1.38; 95% CI: 1.08,1.77)13. Another prospective cohort study in Sweden reported that each one-tertile decrease in the DII was associated with a 14% lower risk of lung cancer (HR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.75, 0.98)16. Furthermore, a study based on the Multiethnic Cohort (MEC) revealed that, compared with the highest quintile of the DII, the lowest quintile had a 10% lower risk of lung cancer (HR: 0.90; 95% CI: 0.82,0.99; P for trend = 0.008)15.The mechanism by which a higher DII may enhance the risk of lung cancer is likely related to the elevation of various inflammatory biomarkers in circulation. Previous studies have identified positive correlations between DII and various inflammatory biomarkers, including CRP24IL-625, and TNF-α26. Among these, CRP is an acute-phase reactant protein, which dramatically increases in plasma when the body is subjected to inflammatory stimuli such as infection or tissue damage. Multiple studies have indicated a positive correlation between CRP levels and lung cancer risk27,28. IL-6 is a multifunctional cytokine that plays a crucial role in diverse pathophysiological processes, including immune regulation, inflammatory responses, and hematopoiesis. Previous research has revealed that IL-6 can enhance tumorigenesis and lung cancer stem cell (CSC) proliferation by upregulating DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) and downregulating p53 and p21 through the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway29. Additionally, TNF-α, an inflammatory cytokine, also possesses pro-tumorigenic properties. By activating the MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways, TNF-α triggers inflammatory responses that contribute to the survival, proliferation, invasion, and angiogenesis of tumor cells23. Therefore, a higher DII may be associated with a higher lung cancer risk by elevating the levels of inflammatory biomarkers in circulation.

To reveal which dietary patterns are associated with higher or lower DII, we utilized factor analysis to identify three predominant dietary patterns and analyzed their correlations with the DII. The results indicated a moderate negative correlation between the fruits and vegetables dietary pattern and the DII, suggesting that individuals with a higher adherence to the fruits and vegetables dietary pattern tend to have lower DII scores. Conversely, those with higher DII scores are more likely to have a lower adherence to the fruits and vegetables dietary pattern. The fruits and vegetables dietary pattern may be associated with lower levels of inflammation. This hypothesis is supported by previous research, such as a cross-sectional study by Piccand et al.30 in Switzerland, which found a negative correlation between the fruits and vegetables dietary pattern and CRP levels. Neale et al.31 conducted a meta-analysis of 18 articles and discovered a negative correlation between a plant-based healthy dietary pattern (mostly fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, with little red meat) and CRP levels. Additionally, a meta-analysis also revealed negative correlations between vegetable- and fruit-based dietary patterns and inflammatory biomarkers32. These studies collectively support the anti-inflammatory effects of the fruits and vegetables dietary pattern, which may be attributed to the abundance of nutrients such as vitamin C, vitamin E, carotenoids, and flavonoids in these foods. These nutrients possess anti-inflammatory properties and can modulate inflammatory signaling pathways and immune functions, thus exerting their effects33–36.

Furthermore, we analyzed the relationship between the fruits and vegetables dietary pattern and the risk of lung cancer, the results indicated that this dietary pattern was associated with a lower risk of lung cancer, aligning with the results of previous research37,38. A large case-control study in the United States showed that individuals in the highest quintile of the fruits and vegetables dietary pattern score had a 32% lower risk of lung cancer, compared with those in the lowest quintile (OR: 0.68; 95% CI: 0.55,0.85; P for trend = 0.001)37. A similar result was observed in a case-control study conducted in southeastern China38. Combining the above results, we reasonably hypothesize that the fruits and vegetables dietary pattern may reduce the risk of lung cancer by lowering dietary inflammation levels. Conversely, individuals with higher DII may have poorer adherence to the fruits and vegetables dietary pattern, potentiallycorresponding to a higher risk of lung cancer.

Additionally, we found that the meat dietary pattern was associated with a higher risk of lung cancer, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies39,40. A case-control study conducted among men in Uruguay revealed that the high-meat dietary pattern was associated with a higher risk of lung cancer (OR = 2.90; 95% CI: 1.91,4.40)39. A meta-analysis conducted by Fabiani et al. demonstrated that individuals with high adherence to the “Western/meat” dietary pattern had a significantly increased risk of lung cancer (OR = 1.39; 95% CI: 1.17,1.65)40. However, surprisingly, we did not find a positive correlation between this dietary pattern and DII. This indicates that, in addition to dietary inflammation levels, there may be other more complex mechanisms or factors—such as meat cooking methods, the frequency of processed meat consumption, or other unconsidered components of the dietary pattern—that influence the relationship between the meat dietary pattern and lung cancer risk. Therefore, further research is needed to delve deeper into these potential mechanisms. Finally, we observed no significant association between the cereals and processed foods dietary pattern and lung cancer risk. This could in part be attributed to this dietary pattern including whole grains, which have been shown to have a protective effect against lung cancer41 as well as ultra-processed foods, which may be associated with a higher risk of lung cancer42.

This study has several strengths. Firstly, we utilized prospective cohort data from the UK Biobank, which is characterized by a large sample size, long years of follow-up, and standardized data collection. Secondly, the study innovatively combined a priori DII with a posteriori dietary pattern analysis to delve into the relationship between dietary patterns and the risk of lung cancer. This approach not only deepens our understanding of the link between diet and lung cancer but also aids in identifying specific dietary patterns that may enhance or mitigate dietary inflammatory potential, thereby increasing the practical applicability of the DII.

Nonetheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the 24-hour dietary recall method was used to assess the dietary intake of the participants, which may not fully or accurately reflect individuals’ long-term dietary habits. In addition, not all participants completed all five rounds of the survey; those who completed fewer rounds (e.g., 1–2) may have less reliable estimates of their dietary patterns. It is also worth noting that participants who completed more rounds may have healthier diets, which could introduce selection bias and thus affect the study results. Secondly, due to limitations in data collection, the calculation of the DII only included 28 food parameters, potentially undermining its predictive power. Yet, existing research has shown that the predictive ability of the DII remains stable when the number of food parameters for DII calculation dropped from 44 to 2843. Meanwhile, as a relatively traditional tool for assessing dietary inflammatory potential, the DII has certain limitations: its calculation does not fully account for differences in protein sources. However, existing studies have clearly demonstrated that proteins from different sources (such as plant-based vs. animal-based proteins, red meat proteins vs. white meat proteins) have significantly different effects on inflammatory responses. This may result in the DII being less accurate in evaluating dietary inflammatory potential. Thirdly, inflammatory biomarkers were not measured in this study, hence we could not further validate our hypotheses. Lastly, despite the study’s efforts to consider and adjust for potential confounding factors, there may still be unobserved confounding factors that could have influenced the study results.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study indicates that a higher DII was associated with a higher risk of lung cancer, while adherence to the fruits and vegetables dietary pattern was linked to a lower risk. In addition, the meat dietary pattern was associated with a higher risk of lung cancer, and no significant association was observed between the cereals and processed foods dietary pattern and lung cancer risk. These findings suggest that the dietary inflammatory potential, as reflected by the DII, may play a role in lung cancer development. Promoting anti-inflammatory dietary patterns, particularly those rich in fruits and vegetables, could be a potential strategy for lung cancer prevention.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This study is based on data collected from UK Biobank, details of which can be found in previous research44. In brief, the UK Biobank is a large prospective cohort study that recruited over 500,000 participants aged 39–72 years between 2006 and 2010. At baseline, the participants were required to complete a touchscreen questionnaire, undergo physical measurements and provide biological samples. UK Biobank data has approval from the North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee (REC reference: 21/NW/0157). All participants provided written informed consent at recruitment. All methods are carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

From 502,411 participants in the UK Biobank, we initially excluded those who did not complete a 24-hour dietary assessment even once (n = 291,449), as well as those with incomplete dietary data, specifically lacking the completion date, food or nutrient data from the 24-hour dietary assessment (n = 83). Furthermore, we excluded participants who had prevalent cancer (excluding non-melanoma skin cancer, ICD-10:C44; ICD-9:173) before completion of their last 24-hour dietary assessment(n = 18,893). Lastly, we excluded those with implausible energy intakes (< 600 or > 3500 Kcal/day for females, and < 800 or > 4200 Kcal/day for males; n = 2425)45. Ultimately, a total of 189,561 participants were included in our analysis (Supplementary Figure S1).

Dietary intake assessment

In the UK Biobank, dietary intake was assessed using the Oxford Web Q, a web-based 24-hour dietary recall questionnaire, which has been validated against an interviewer administered 24-hour dietary recall46. The questionnaire involved 206 foods and 32 beverages, with specified portion sizes for each item. Participants were asked to select how many portions of each item they had consumed in the previous 24 h. The daily intakes of each food and beverage for each participant were calculated by multiplying the assigned portion size by the number of portions consumed. The daily intake of a food group was calculated by summing the daily intakes of all individual foods within that group. Finally, the nutrient intakes for each participant were calculated by multiplying the quantity consumed of each food and beverage by its nutrient composition, as obtained from the UK food composition database, McCance and Widdowson’s ‘The Composition of Foods’ and its supplements46.Towards the end of the baseline assessment period (April 2009-September 2010), the UK Biobank started collecting detailed dietary intake data using the Oxford Web Q, and a total of 70,724 participants completed the Oxford Web Q questionnaire at baseline. Additionally, between February 2011 and June 2012, participants who provided a working email address were invited via email to complete the Oxford Web Q questionnaire on four separate occasions, spanning approximately one year47. The participants in our study completed the Oxford Web Q questionnaire at least once, and the mean values were calculated from the available data.

Dietary inflammatory index (DII)

The dietary inflammatory index (DII) is a population-based epidemiological investigation tool designed to assess the inflammatory potential of the diet. DII was constructed based on a review of 1943 articles that analyzed the effects of 45 food parameters on six inflammatory biomarkers (IL-1, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and CRP)48. Each food parameter was assigned a specific overall inflammatory effect score based on the number of articles, the type of study, and the association between the food parameter and the six inflammatory biomarkers. Meanwhile, to avoid the arbitrariness stemming from the use of raw consumption amounts, a world composite database was developed using data from 11 countries, wherein the global mean and standard deviation for 45 food parameters were calculated. Z-score for each food parameter was calculated by subtracting the ‘global mean’ from the reported amount and dividing this value by its standard deviation. And to minimize the effect of ‘right skewing,’ the z-score was converted to a percentile. Each percentile was then multiplied by 2 and subtracted 1 to achieve a symmetrical distribution with values centered on 0 and bounded between − 1 and + 1. The centered percentile score for each food parameter was multiplied by the corresponding specific overall inflammatory effect score to obtain the food parameter-specific DII score. All the food parameter-specific DII scores were summed to create the overall DII score for each subject. A higher DII score indicated a stronger pro-inflammatory effect of the diet, while a lower DII score reflected a stronger anti-inflammatory effect. In this study, a total of 28 food parameters were obtained from the Oxford Web Q and used in the calculation of DII, including energy, carbohydrate, protein, fat, alcohol, fiber, cholesterol, saturated fatty acid, mono-unsaturated fatty acid, poly-unsaturated fatty acid, omega 3 fatty acid, omega 6 fatty acid, trans fat, niacin, thiamin, riboflavin, vitamin B6, vitamin B12, vitamin A, vitamin C, vitamin D, vitamin E, folic acid, beta carotene, iron, magnesium, zinc and selenium.

Identification of a posteriori dietary patterns

According to the nutrient and culinary similarities, the 206 foods and 32 beverages reported in the Oxford Web Q were classified into 15 food groups (Supplementary Table S1)49. A posteriori dietary patterns were identified using factor analysis based on these food groups. Before performing the factor analysis, the suitability of the data was evaluated through the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) index and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. If the KMO index is greater than 0.5 and the Bartlett’s test is statistically significant (P < 0.05), it indicates that the data is appropriate for factor analysis. The number of a posteriori dietary patterns retained was based on the eigenvalues (> 1.0), the scree plot, and the total variance explained. The factors were extracted using varimax rotation to maintain an uncorrelated state and improve interpretability. Food groups with rotated factor loadings >0.40 were considered as the key components of a posteriori dietary patterns, and each dietary pattern was named based on these food groups50. For each participant, the dietary pattern scores were calculated by multiplying the standard intakes of food groups by their corresponding rotated factor loadings. These dietary pattern scores were then standardized and expressed as z-scores. A higher dietary pattern score indicated greater adherence to a posteriori dietary pattern.

Outcome assessment

Data on cancer diagnoses in the UK Biobank were obtained through linkages to the national cancer and death registries. The outcome of this study was incident lung cancer, which was defined according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes (C33 and C34) and 9th Revision (ICD-9) codes (162)51. We calculated the follow-up time, starting from the date of last completed Oxford Web Q, to the date of cancer diagnosis (excluding non-melanoma skin cancer, ICD-10:C44; ICD-9:173), death, withdrawal from the UK Biobank cohort, or the last date of follow-up (September 30, 2021, for England and Wales, and October 31, 2021, for Scotland), whichever came first52.

Statistical analysis

For the variables with < 5% missing values, the mode and median were used to impute missing values of categorical and continuous variables, respectively; for the variable ‘pack-years of smoking’ with 15.1% missing values, multiple imputation was adopted. The distribution of variables with missing values before and after imputation is shown in Supplementary Table S2.

The normality of the continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Baseline characteristics of participants by incident lung cancer status were presented as mean (standard deviation [SD]) for normally distributed variables, median (interquartile range) for skewed variables and number (percentage) for categorical variables. The differences between participants with and without lung cancer were examined using t-test for normally distributed variables, Mann-Whitney U test for skewed variables, and chi-squared test for categorical variables.

To explore which a posteriori dietary patterns are associated with higher or lower DII, we employed Spearman’s correlation analysis to assess the correlation between the DII and each dietary pattern score. Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to quantify the associations between the DII, dietary pattern scores, and the risk of lung cancer. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals, and no violations were identified. In Cox regression models, the DII and dietary pattern scores were classified into tertiles, with the lowest tertile as the reference group. To examine linear trends across tertiles of the DII and dietary pattern scores, the median of each tertile was assigned to each participant within that tertile, and then treated it as a continuous variable in regression models. Three models were fitted for the outcomes. The minimally adjusted model (crude model) was not adjusted for any covariates. One multivariable model (Model 1) was adjusted for age at recruitment (< 60 and ≥ 60 years), sex (female and male), ethnicity (white and non-white), education level (college or university degree, secondary education, some professional qualification, and primary education or below), Townsend deprivation index(continuous), and occupation status (working, retired, homemaker or other). Another multivariable model (Model 2) was additionally adjusted for body mass index (< 25, 25–29.9, and ≥ 30 kg/m2), smoking status (never smokers, former smokers, and current smokers), pack-years of smoking, passive smoke (yes/no)53, drinking status (never drinkers, former drinkers, and current drinkers), family history of lung cancer (yes/no), personal medical history (diabetes, asthma, emphysema or chronic bronchitis, yes/no), use of aspirin or non-aspirin NSAIDs (including ibuprofen, paracetamol, yes/no), use of vitamin supplementation (including vitamin A, vitamin B, vitamin C, vitamin D, vitamin E, and multivitamins, yes/no), use of minerals and other dietary supplementation(including zinc, iron, selenium, fish oil, and glucosamine, yes/no)51. Subsequently, restricted cubic spline regression was employed to evaluate the continuous variation in hazard ratios for the associations between the DII, dietary pattern scores, and lung cancer risk in Model 2, with three knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles54.

Considering that smoking is the most significant risk factor for lung cancer and may confound the relationship between diet and lung cancer risk, we conducted stratified analyses by smoking status. Additionally, to further evaluate the potential modifying effect of smoking on the associations between the DII, dietary pattern scores, and lung cancer risk, we included interaction terms (product terms) between DII and dietary pattern scores with smoking status in the fully adjusted models. The significance of these interactions was assessed by comparing models with and without the interaction terms using likelihood ratio tests.

Finally, based on Model 2, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding lung cancer cases diagnosed within the first 2 years of follow-up to eliminate the confounding effect of reverse causation.

All analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0, and R (version 4.4.0), and a two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Restricted cubic spline regression was performed using R rms, and ggplot2 packages.
