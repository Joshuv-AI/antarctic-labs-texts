The association between serum HbA1c and infected pancreatic necrosis in acute necrotizing pancreatitis
Authors: Xinyu Wang, Yiwei Luo, Gang Li, Dadong Liu, Jingzhu Zhang, Jing Zhou, Bo Ye, Wenjian Mao, Lu Ke, Yuxiu Liu, Weiqin Li, Zhihui Tong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGiven the evidence that poor glycemic control is associated with immunological dysfunction, serum glycosylated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), as a useful indicator reflecting blood glucose homeostasis, has potential to predict infectious complications. In this study, we aimed to explore the association between poor glycemic control (HbA1c ≥ 6.5 as an indicator) and infected pancreatic necrosis (IPN) in patients with acute necrotizing pancreatitis (ANP).MethodsThis is a secondary analysis of pooled data from the TRACE trial and a retrospective single-center cohort, involving ANP patients admitted within 72 h after onset of abdominal pain between March 2017 and October 2022. Patients had no HbA1c data at enrollment/admission were excluded. The Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression (MCPHR) model was used to assess the association between poor glycemic control and 90-day IPN. Mediation analyses were used to define the relationships between the independent variables and 90-day IPN.ResultsA total of 153 patients were enrolled, of whom 20.3% (31/153) patients developed IPN within 90 days. Most patients with IPN had HbA1c ≥ 6.5% at admission (25/31, 80.6%), compared to 38.5% (47/122) in the non-IPN patients. In the MCPHR model, HbA1c ≥ 6.5% [HR (95%CI) = 4.10 (1.42–11.90); p = 0.009], SOFA score on admission [HR (95%CI) = 1.47 (1.26–1.70); p < 0.001] and pancreatic necrosis extent > 50% [HR (95%CI) = 3.15 (1.06–9.35); p = 0.039] were independent risk factors for 90-day IPN. In addition, patients with HbA1c ≥ 6.5% experienced a higher incidence of hyperglycemia during the first week of enrollment [RR (95%CI) = 9.19 (4.23–19.95); p < 0.001]. Mediation analyses showed that the presence of hyperglycemia completely mediated the association between HbA1c ≥ 6.5% and 90-day IPN.ConclusionAmong ANP patients, HbA1c ≥ 6.5% was associated with the development of IPN. The mediation effect of hyperglycemia may explain it.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04324-w.

Introduction

The global incidence of acute pancreatitis (AP) is 34 cases per 100,000 population per year [1]. Though the majority of AP patients were mild to moderately severe, the mortality rate dramatically rises to 30% when complicated with infected pancreatic necrosis (IPN) [2–4]. Early identification of patients with a substantial risk of IPN would help key decision-making, including early transfer to intensive care and/or receiving surgical interventions. Since there is a lack of tools for predicting IPN, it is necessary to identify some novel risk factors associated with the mechanism of IPN development.

Hyperglycemia can lead to functional damage to the innate immune system, which may potentially increase the risk of infection in AP [5, 6]. AP patients with admission and persistent hyperglycemia during the first week of admission have a higher incidence of major infection [7]. However, blood glucose levels fluctuate over time and were heterogeneous during AP due to a variety of factors such as fasting, endocrine function disorder, insulin injection, etc. Thus, blood glucose or hyperglycemia at a specific time are not the ideal candidates for IPN prediction.

As a non-enzymatic condensate of glucose and hemoglobin, the level of serum glycosylated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) is a useful indicator for assessing glycemic status over the previous 2–3 months, which may be a good indicator reflecting blood glucose homeostasis [8]. In previous studies, poor glycemic control (increased HbA1c) has been reported to be associated with complement system activation pathways, accelerated immunosenescence, and immunological dysfunction [9–12]. Previous studies have shown the excellent predictive value of increased HbA1c in various infectious complications [13–15]. However, whether it can act as a biomarker in predicting IPN is still unknown.

In this regard, we conducted this retrospective study to explore the association between poor glycemic control (HbA1c ≥ 6.5% as an indicator) and IPN occurrence in patients with acute necrotizing pancreatitis [8].

Methods

Study design and participants

This study is a secondary analysis of pooled data from a multicenter, randomized controlled trial (TRACE trial) [16] and a single-center retrospective data. Briefly, the TRACE is a large multicenter randomized controlled trial that investigated the effect of immune-enhancing treatment with Thymosin α1 (Tα1) on the occurrence of IPN. Patients were enrolled in 16 hospitals between March 18, 2017, and December 10, 2020, within seven days of the onset of abdominal pain. On March 10, 2021, the follow-up was completed. Before enrollment, the trial protocol was approved by the ethics committees of the participating sites and registered at the ClinicalTrials.gov registry (NCT02473406). The other retrospective cohort study screened patients with a primary diagnosis of AP admitted to the Center of Acute Pancreatitis, Jinling Hospital, from January 1, 2021, to October 31, 2022. All data were extracted from an electronic database (AP database) with the approval of the database management committee (No.: 2019 JLAPDMC-011). Data were collected prospectively during hospitalization with the consent of the patients or their next of kin for academic use. This secondary analysis is performed in a cohort of participants from the two databases mentioned above.

All adult AP patients admitted within 3 days of the advent of symptoms were included. Patients who met the following criteria were excluded: (1) without pancreatic necrosis, (2) younger than 18 years or older than 70 years, (3) AP in pregnancy, (4) recurrent pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, or pancreatic cancer, (5) received surgical interventions before admission, (6) received immunomodulatory drugs, or (7) missing data of the HbA1c. Pancreatic necrosis was diagnosed according to the characteristic findings of AP on contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT): lack of pancreatic parenchymal and/or presence of peripancreatic necrosis [17].

Data collection

All the data required in this study were extracted from the electronic database of the TRACE trial and the AP database. The baseline characteristics included demographics of age, gender, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, etiology of AP, and the interval between abdominal pain and admission. The following data were recorded at admission: laboratory indicators (HbA1c, C-reactive protein (CRP)) and clinical severity scores (Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE)-II Score, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA)). The highest daily random blood glucose level during the first week was also collected. The median time interval between performing CECT and AP onset was 6 days. All CECT images were re-read by one experienced radiologist to assess the extent of parenchymal necrosis and determine the extent of pancreatic necrosis area [18].

Clinical outcomes

The primary outcome was the development of IPN during the 90 days after enrollment (TRACE trial) or admission (the retrospective cohort). The diagnostic criteria of IPN were: gas bubbles within pancreatic and/or peripancreatic necrosis on CT; a positive culture from pancreatic and/or peripancreatic necrosis obtained by fine-needle aspiration, drainage, or necrosectomy [17].

The secondary outcomes included hyperglycemia during the first week of admission, 90-day mortality, length of hospital stay (LOS), intensive care unit stay, and incidence of systemic and local complications during the index hospitalization. The 90-day follow-up was implemented through telephone calls or mobile applications by the TRACE investigators (TRACE trial) or by the AP database coordinator.

Definitions

The diagnosis and severity classification of AP were according to the Revised Atlanta classification (RAC) [18]. Hyperglycemia was defined as a random blood glucose >200 mg/dL (11.1mmol/L), regardless of insulin treatment [19, 20]. Poor glycemic control was defined as HbA1c ≥ 6.5% at admission, according to American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria [8].

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and IQR, and analyzed by the student t-test or Mann-Whitney’s test, determined by the normality. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to determine the normality of quantitative data. Categorical data are expressed as frequencies and percentages. The comparison of categorical data between groups was performed using Fisher’s exact test.

The relationship between poor glycemic control and 90-day IPN was evaluated by the Kaplan-Meier method and was compared using the log-rank test. The Cox proportional-hazards regression model was employed to analyze potential risk factors for 90-day readmission (p < 0.05 in univariate analysis) with five priori variables [age, sex, BMI, hyperlipidemic etiology, and APACHE II score at admission] as covariates. Multi-collinearity was additionally tested to ensure the independence of each variable. The hazard ratio (HR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated.

As for the mediation analysis, we used a bootstrapping resampling with 1000 resamples to assess the potential indirect effects on 90-day IPN and the independent variable. Point estimates and 95% CIs were estimated for indirect and direct effects. The proportion of the effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable that went through the mediator was estimated for mediation.

Statistical tests were two-sided, and P values < 0.05 were deemed significant unless otherwise stated. All statistical analyses were done in the SPSS 26.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and the R 4.2.3 software (Lucent Technologies, Inc., Murray Hill, New Jersey, USA).

Results

Characteristics of the study population

As shown in Fig. 1, 508 patients with ANP from the TRACE trial and 1035 patients with AP from the other cohort were assessed for eligibility, and a total of 153 patients were enrolled for further analysis. The development of IPN occurred in 31 of 153 ANP patients (20.3%) during the 90-day follow-up period. The baseline characteristics of patients with and without 90-day IPN are presented in Table 1. Among the study population, the most frequent etiology was hypertriglyceridemia (69.3%), with a median age of 40 (IQR: 32–49) and 113 (73.9%) males.

Compared with the non-90-day IPN group, patients in the 90-day IPN group had a higher proportion of HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, higher clinical severity scores at admission, and greater pancreatic necrosis extent (all p < 0.05). There were no statistical differences in age, gender, BMI, comorbidities, etiology of AP, and level of CRP at admission between the two groups.

Association between HbA1c ≥ 6.5% and clinical outcomes

As shown in Table 2, compared patients with HbA1c < 6.5%, patients with HbA1c ≥ 6.5% had a statistically significant higher rate of 90-day IPN (HR: 5.56, 95%CI: 2.28–13.56, p < 0.001), hyperglycemia during the first week [Risk ratio (RR): 9.19, 95%CI: 4.23–19.95, p < 0.001], as well as other clinical outcomes including an increased 90-day mortality, increased incidence of gastrointestinal fistula, intra-abdominal hemorrhage, and increased LOS (all p < 0.05).

Association between HbA1c ≥ 6.5% and 90-day IPN

The cumulative incidence of IPN during 90 days after randomization between patients with HbA1c ≥ 6.5% or not was shown in Fig. 2. The cumulative probability of developing IPN within 90 days of enrollment was significantly higher in patients with HbA1c ≥ 6.5% (Log-Rank p < 0.0001). After adjustment for age, gender, BMI, hyperlipidemic etiology, APACHE II score, SOFA score, and pancreatic necrosis extent, HbA1c ≥ 6.5% was still a significant risk factor for 90-day IPN (HR: 4.10, 95%CI: 1.42–11.90, P = 0.009) (Table 3). In addition, both the SOFA score (HR: 1.47, 95%CI: 1.26–1.70, p < 0.001) and pancreatic necrosis extent > 50% (HR: 3.15, 95%CI: 1.06–9.35, p = 0.039) were independent risk factors for 90-day IPN in the multivariate Cox proportional-hazards regression model.

The mediation effect of hyperglycemia

As shown in Fig. 3, mediation analysis showed that the presence of hyperglycemia completely mediated the causal relationship between HbA1c ≥ 6.5% and 90-day IPN (mediation effect = 0.21; 95% CI: 0.09 to 0.29, P < 0.001). In addition, the presence of hyperglycemia was also found to partly mediated the causal relationship between pancreatic necrosis extent > 50% and 90-day IPN (mediation effect = 0.18; 95% CI: 0.11 to 0.28, P < 0.001) (Figure S1).

Discussion

Among ANP patients, we found a statistically significant association between poor glycemic control (HbA1c ≥ 6.5% at admission) and 90-day IPN. Moreover, patients’ poor glycemic control was associated with a higher proportion of hyperglycemia within the first week, and worse clinical outcomes during hospitalization. Mediation analysis showed that hyperglycemia within the first week completely mediated the causal relationship between HbA1c ≥ 6.5% and 90-day IPN.

It is known that diabetes can increase the prevalence of AP and is an independent risk factor for poor AP prognosis [21–24]. Furthermore, it is reported that diabetes can increase the risk of renal failure in AP patients and is associated with a higher mortality [25, 26]. One of the specific explanations may be that hyperglycemia may attenuate cytokine production, leukocyte recruitment, antibodies and complement effectors, and pathogen recognition, resulting in neutrophil, macrophage, and natural killer cell dysfunction [6]. Since DM patients can control their blood glucose levels by using a hypoglycemic agent or insulin, HbA1c is a better indicator to represent the glycemic state in the last 2–3 months [8]. Recently, Han et al. found that serum HbA1c level was positively correlated with the severity of AP, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), local complication, and organ failure [27]. Similar results were also found in other studies [28, 29]. However, the limitation of these studies was the inclusion of patients with mild AP with a very low incidence of IPN, making it very difficult to evaluate the predictive performance of HbA1c for IPN.

Previous study showed that patients with an increased HbA1c were affected by the chronic hyperglycemia state, and the endothelial glycocalyx damage was more serious [30]. Under the impact of “inflammatory storm” caused by AP, it is more likely to cause intestinal microcirculation dysfunction and injuries to the intestinal mucosal barrier, thereby increasing the risk of translocation of gut flora and promoting the occurrence of IPN [31]. Furthermore, Rector et al. found that elevated HbA1c levels were associated with accelerated immunosenescence, reflected by increased numbers of circulating effector memory and CD45RA re-expressing effector memory T cells [10]. However, immune biomarkers were unavailable in the present study. Furthermore, in the mediation analysis, the presence of hyperglycemia was found to completely mediate the effect of HbA1c ≥ 6.5% on 90-day IPN, implying that the persistent effect of hyperglycemia was to blame.

This study also showed that the pancreatic necrosis extent >50% is another independent risk factor for 90-day IPN and was associated with a higher incidence of hyperglycemia within the first week of admission and worse clinical outcomes, which are highly consistent with the results of previous studies [32, 33]. It is known that large-scale pancreatic necrosis could result in a significant decrease in pancreatic -cells and insulin secretion [34–36]. Many studies have found that extensive pancreatic necrosis in AP patients is associated with an increased risk of pancreatic endocrine insufficiency [37–39], which was similar to our findings, despite the fact that the oral glucose tolerance test was not routinely performed. Interestingly, we found that hyperglycemia mediated 40.1% of the extensive pancreatic necrosis-90-day IPN causality, implying that hyperglycemia during hospitalization may be a consequence of extensive pancreatic necrosis and partly contribute to the IPN development. Apart from the extent of pancreatic necrosis, a study by Tu et al. also found that in comparison to necrosis in the head and body of the pancreas, necrosis in the tail was associated with a greater risk of developing diabetes [40]. However, data on the necrosis position was not available in the current study. More research on this population is needed, and early pancreatic endocrine function monitoring and/or early tight blood-glucose control may help improve their prognosis.

The main strength of our study was that the data was partly derived from a large multicenter randomized controlled trial that included standardized data collection and central monitoring. In addition, we only included patients with ANP, who are at high risk for IPN. There are also several limitations. First, more than half of the enrolled patients are hypertriglyceridemia-associated AP, which may influence the generality of the findings. The rise in hypertriglyceridemia-induced AP in the Chinese population may be attributed to dietary changes in the past 30 years and genetic factors [41, 42]. Second, immune-related biomarkers were not detected in this study. Third, the purpose of the TRACE trial did not involve glucose homeostasis monitoring, HbA1c was not measured in many patients. Meanwhile, due to the strict screening criteria of this study, only 153 patients were included. However, it highlights the importance of measuring HbA1c at the ANP admission. Prospective studies with a larger sample size are needed to validate the results in the future.

In conclusion, among ANP patients, HbA1c ≥ 6.5% was an independent risk factor for 90-day IPN. The mediation effect of hyperglycemia may explain their casual relationships.
