Higher intensity care for patients with multiple chronic conditions after hospital-physician integration
Authors: Alexandra Harris, Brady Post, Cynthia Barnard, Jeffrey A Linder, Neil Jordan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHospital-physician vertical integration—namely, hospitals acquiring physician practices, directly employing physicians, or having significant financial ties to physician practices—has accelerated in recent years. Yet, integration's implications for healthcare delivery for patients with multiple chronic conditions (MCCs) remain poorly understood.MethodsThis study examined the effect of hospital-physician integration on health services utilization among patients with MCCs using an all-payer claims database.ResultsWe found that hospital-physician integration generally led to higher use of inpatient and ambulatory care, though these findings were heterogeneous across physician specialties and outcomes. Introduction

Vertical integration between hospitals and physician practices has increased substantially in recent years, reshaping the organization of US health care delivery. The most prevalent form of vertical integration has been hospitals’ acquisition of physician practice or direct employment of physicians (“hospital-physician vertical integration”). Between July 2012 and January 2018, the share of physician practices owned by hospitals increased from 14% to 31%.1 By 2024, more than 46% of physicians were hospital-owned or directly employed by hospitals.2 Proponents argue that integration may enhance communication across care settings and support more efficient care. For example, an integrated health system may improve access to specialist care and adherence to standardized clinical guidelines, which may result in improved quality and better alignment of health services utilization with the value of that care.3 However, the current evidence linking hospital-physician vertical integration to health services utilization is somewhat limited and ambiguous in nature, with few studies demonstrating consistent patterns of improvement.4

While some research suggests that integration may reduce fragmented care and increase follow-up rates after hospitalization or emergency department (ED) visits, other studies have reported increases in utilization, particularly of hospital-affiliated outpatient services and potentially unnecessary care, such as lab testing and diagnostic imaging.4-8 Evidence on key outcomes remains sparse and inconclusive—particularly for patients with multiple chronic conditions (MCCs), who are most likely to benefit from coordinated care.4 This population is particularly complex due to challenges such as polypharmacy, interactions between chronic conditions, coordinated care management, and barriers to finding accessible, effective treatments.9-11 These patients often interact with multiple clinicians and multiple care settings, making them especially sensitive to organizational change. While it may influence how and where patients access care, vertical integration's effect on utilization patterns for patients with MCCs remains poorly understood. Understanding how vertical integration influences health services utilization for this complex and costly patient panel is critical to informing policies that promote efficient healthcare delivery models.

Using a staggered difference-in-differences (DiD) approach, we assessed whether hospital-physician vertical integration was associated with changes in the probability and intensity of health services utilization for patients with MCCs. Our findings provide novel evidence to support ongoing policy discussions about the consequences of vertical integration and its impact on health care delivery for the growing population of patients with MCCs.

Data and methods

Study design and population

Our study cohort included 77 248 patients (attributed to 4664 unique physicians) aged 45–99, with Medicare, Medicaid, and/or commercial insurance, and at least two qualifying chronic conditions at the beginning of the study period. Qualifying conditions included acute myocardial infarction (AMI), Alzheimer's disease and related disorders or senile dementia, atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder (COPD) and asthma, depression, diabetes, heart failure, and stroke and transient ischemic attack (see Appendix A for diagnosis codes). These conditions are often co-occurring, carry disproportionately high hospitalization risk, and require coordinated ambulatory care to prevent adverse events. This definition is closely aligned with the CMS MCC admission measure cohort definition that has been externally validated and used for Medicare value-based quality reporting. Patients were required to have at least one inpatient or two outpatient claims annually to meet criteria.12,13 Additional information about the study cohort is included in Appendix B.

Aligned with other research,14 we attributed each patient-year to the qualifying physician with the plurality of the patient's evaluation and management claims in that year (denoted as the majority physician in a given patient-year). Qualifying physicians were defined as specialties that may reasonably manage care for a patient with two or more of the conditions noted above, including primary care, cardiology, neurology, endocrinology, nephrology, pulmonology, psychology, and psychiatry. Moreover, if a patient had different majority physicians across their respective patient-years, we identified a “dominant” physician who accounted for at least three of six study years. In the few instances where a patient was attributed to two majority physicians for three patient-years each, we selected the first identified dominant physician. Patients without a dominant physician or with missing covariate data were excluded to ensure stable attribution (Appendix Figure BS1).

Data sources

We used 2016–2021 data from the Virginia All-Payer Claims Database (APCD) and Medicare Data on Provider Practice and Specialty (MD-PPAS). The APCD is a longitudinally linked administrative claims database consisting of de-identified, patient-level medical and pharmacy data for roughly 5.5 million Virginia residents with commercial, Medicaid, and/or Medicare coverage. These claims data included inpatient, outpatient, professional, and pharmacy claims and allowed us to determine patient diagnoses and procedures, payer information, patient demographic characteristics, and the treating physicians. MD-PPAS provided information about physicians, including medical specialties, employers, and integration status. In addition, we used the publicly available U.S. Department of Agriculture Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) Codes to define patient-level rurality based on 5-digit ZIP codes in each patient-year.15 Finally, we used 2016–2021 American Hospital Association (AHA) Annual Survey data to create a hospital-level market concentration measure.

Variables

Integration

Our independent variable was hospital-physician vertical integration, measured at the physician-level, which we defined by building upon a published algorithm16,17 that we enhanced to improve accuracy through additional validation (see Appendix C for detailed methodology). We describe our method for defining integration below. Our first step exploits the fact that physician practices vertically integrated with a hospital can, in many cases, legally bill Medicare at a higher hospital outpatient department (HOPD) rate rather than an office visit rate, even if the physician's practice is not located on the hospital's campus. Hospital-integrated physicians have had a strong incentive to bill at an HOPD rate, which requires a change in place of service code from office to HOPD on professional service claims. As such, we designated physicians as integrated if at least 75% of their claims for outpatient care were billed with a HOPD place of service code (accounted for 81% of identified integrated clinicians). Second, to mitigate possible claims-based measurement error, we then supplemented this definition by identifying physicians whose taxpayer legal names in MD-PPAS were likely to correspond to hospital employment—specifically, names that include keywords like “medical center,” “hospital,” or “system.” Third, we further enhanced this definition with manual validation of more than 6000 hospital-TIN names in MD-PPAS with TIN names in the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality Compendium of Health Systems and the AHA annual survey (see Appendix B for detailed methodology). We acknowledge that there are alternatives to our integration definition that rely on hospital-level survey data; however, these data do not allow for integration to be defined granularly at the physician-level. This would have limited our knowledge of which physicians were integrating, which was a key objective of this study.

The control group was the set of patients treated by and attributed to independent physicians who were never integrated during the study period (2016–2021). Our treatment group was the set of patients attributed to physicians who were independent at baseline (at minimum, in 2016 and 2017) and integrated beginning in 2018 or later. To account for staggered adoption of integration, we created four integration cohorts (2018, 2019, 2020, and 2021) based on the physician's first year of integration. We excluded patients attributed to physicians who were already integrated before 2018 to ensure two baseline pre-integration years. We also excluded patients attributed to physicians who became integrated and then because independent again, since model parameters do not allow for switching between the treatment and control groups.

Outcome

Our primary outcomes were indicators of healthcare utilization. For each patient-year, we constructed separate indicators for whether the patient had: (1) at least one inpatient admission, (2) at least one ED visit, (3) at least one outpatient follow-up visit within 7 days of an ED discharge, (4) at least one visit to a PCP, and (5) at least one specialist visit. Each variable was coded as 1 if the event occurred at least once during the year and 0 otherwise. As secondary outcomes, we assessed the intensity of service use by creating count variables for inpatient admissions, ED visits, PCP visits, and specialist visits per patient-year. Our binary utilization outcomes captured changes in the likelihood of use over time, conditional on baseline attribution, while our count outcomes assessed changes in the intensity of service use among patients utilizing specific services.

Inpatient admissions and ED visits were identified from claims using bill type, place of service, and revenue codes. We constructed separate binary indicators and corresponding count variables for each patient-year. Outpatient follow-up visits within 7 days of an ED discharge were defined using the HEDIS follow-up after ED visit for people with multiple high-risk chronic conditions measure (see Appendix Table DS1 for full specifications).

Aligned with published research,18 PCP visits were defined using outpatient claims that included a CPT code in the range 99202–99499 and a clinician primary specialty coded as general practice, family practice, internal medicine, geriatric medicine, preventive medicine, nurse practitioner, or physician assistant. Specialist visits were similarly identified from outpatient claims with CPT codes in the 99202–99499 range and provider primary specialties including cardiology, interventional cardiology, cardiac electrophysiology, neurology, psychiatry, psychology, clinical psychology, pulmonary disease, nephrology, or endocrinology.

Covariates

We accounted for patient-, physician-, and market-level covariates that may influence the relationship between integration and our utilization outcomes. Patient-level factors included age, sex, race, payer, Medicare-Medicaid dual eligibility, number of MCCs, binary indicator variables for each of the qualifying MCC conditions, and Charlson comorbidity score.19 Physician-level covariates included whether the dominant physician was a PCP or a specialist. Market-level factors included rurality and hospital Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) at the HSA-level.20 We categorized HSA-level HHI values into market concentration tiers following recently updated Department of Justic and Federal Trade Commission thresholds: markets with HHI <1000 were classified as unconcentrated, 1000–1799 as moderately concentrated, and ≥1800 as highly concentrated.20

Statistical analysis

Using STATA 19, we first compared baseline demographic and clinical characteristics between patients attributed to hospital-integrated vs independent physicians using standardized mean differences (SMDs). The level of statistical significance was set at alpha ≤ .05. We also compared the physician characteristics across these two groups.

Then, we used the Callaway and Sant’Anna with multiple time periods DiD (CSDiD) approach to estimate the effect of becoming integrated on our study outcomes. Unlike traditional two-way fixed effect DiD models, this method leverages a staggered treatment adoption event study design, because physicians integrated in different years. Rather than assume homogeneous treatment effects, we estimated cohort-time average treatment effects on the treated (ATTs) comparing integrated physicians in a given period to never-integrated physicians (control group). This model specification technically does allow for not-yet-treated physicians to serve as controls; however, we chose to restrict our control group to never-treated physicians, since we had ample sample size. To account for selection bias and reduce covariate imbalance between the integrated and independent groups in the Callaway and Sant’Anna DiD (CSDiD) models, we employed a doubly robust approach using stabilized inverse probability weights (IPW).21-23 Our regression models account for unobservable, time-invariant characteristics, such as calendar-year shocks and physician characteristics. Because integration timing is staggered, effects are normalized relative to each integration cohort's own pre-treatment period; as a result, the pooled event-study plot may display nonzero estimates at t = −1, even though all cohort-specific effects are defined relative to that period. The event study plots show the ATT at each event time, aggregated across treatment cohorts.

Limitations

Our study had several limitations. First, although we used rigorous methods and satisfied parallel trends assumptions in most cases, residual unobserved confounding is possible. Second, we relied on administrative data, which may not fully capture nuanced quality of care or referral appropriateness. Third, the study period overlapped with the early years of the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have introduced disruptions in care patterns, particularly for later integration cohorts, though our results were robust to the exclusion of 2020. Fourth, we applied fairly restrictive criteria (e.g., MCC definition, continuous insurance coverage) in defining our study cohort. While these criteria were important for methodological considerations, we acknowledge that the generalizability of our findings is more limited as a result. Fifth, due to existing Virginia state law, large, self-insured employers often do but are not required to submit employees’ data to Virginia Health Information, the organization that manages the APCD; therefore, the commercially insured population may be underrepresented and therefore our findings may not be fully generalizable. Sixth, selecting patients with MCCs raises the possibility of regression to the mean; however, this concern is likely limited in our setting because chronic condition counts are relatively stable over time, the inclusion threshold (two or more conditions) is not extreme, and our doubly robust DiD methodology helps reduce the influence of natural mean reversion on the estimated effects. Finally, our study was focused on patients with established chronic conditions; therefore, our findings may not generalize to patients earlier in their disease trajectories.

Study population

The study sample included 77 248 insured patients from 2016 to 2021 (Table 1). Of these patients, 43 905 were female (56.8%), with a mean (SD) age of 72.6 (10.3) years and an average of 2.80 MCCs (Table 1). The most common qualifying conditions in this population were diabetes (73.7%), CKD (63.6%), COPD (53.0%), and depression (40.1%). Baseline differences between integrated and independent groups were generally modest with respect to SMDs (Table 1), and covariate balance improved after IPW (Appendix Table ES1). Admission and admission count

The overall ATT indicated that integration was associated with a 1.6% point increase in the probability of an inpatient admission per patient-year (ATT = 0.016; 95% CI: 0.004–0.028), a roughly 2.3% increase from the 68.7% baseline probability of admission for the independent group (Figure 2). The integration group-average ATT was also statistically significant (0.012; 95% CI: 0.001–0.023), suggesting that the ATT across all treated cohorts was positive, albeit not always individually statistically significant (Appendix Table ES6).

Integration was also associated with more admissions per patient-year (ATT = 0.201; 95% CI: 0.142–0.260), a 34% increase from the 0.590 mean baseline admissions (Figure 3). The group-average ATT was similarly positive and statistically significant (0.098; 95% CI: 0.054–0.143), although smaller in magnitude. The largest cohort-specific ATT estimates were observed among the patients of physicians who integrated in 2018 and 2019, who respectively experienced 0.540 (95% CI: 0.378–0.701) and 0.186 (95% CI: 0.063–0.309) more admissions per patient-year (Appendix Table ES10).

ED visit and ED visit count

We found no evidence that integration altered the probability of having an annual ED visit (Figure 2). In contrast, the number of ED visits per patient-year declined modestly following integration (−0.050, 95% CI: −0.100 to 0.000), a 4.6% decline from the 1.09 mean baseline ED visits (Figure 3). The cohort-average ATT was slightly larger (−0.068, 95% CI: −0.111 to −0.025), driven by fewer ED visits after integration in the 2019 and 2021 cohorts (Appendix Table ES18).

Follow-up within 7 days of an ED visit

We did not find compelling evidence that integration improved the probability of receiving timely follow-up care after an ED visit (Figure 2). In the first year of integration, there was a 1.8% point reduction in the probability of follow-up (P = 0.017), but no significant differences were observed in later years (Appendix Table ES23). While these results suggest a potential short-term decline in the probability of follow-up care, this decline did not persist over time. We did find the cohort-average ATT was slightly larger in magnitude than the overall ATT and reached statistical significance (−0.016, 95% CI: −0.030 to −0.002); these findings were driven largely by the 2021 integration cohort, which experienced a 3.5% point decrease in the probability of follow-up within 7 days (95% CI: −0.056 to −0.014), a 6.1% decrease from the 57.2% baseline probability (Appendix Table ES22).

PCP visit and PCP visit count

We found no evidence that integration led to a meaningful change in the likelihood of having an annual PCP visit (Figure 2). However, cohort-specific estimates were mixed: the 2018 cohort exhibited a small but statistically significant increase in the probability of an annual PCP visit (0.029, 95% CI: 0.012–0.046), while the 2019 and 2021 cohorts showed small but significant declines (−0.023, 95% CI: −0.040 to −0.005, and −0.014, 95% CI: −0.023 to −0.004, respectively) (Appendix Table ES26). The 2020 cohort demonstrated no significant effect. In the first year of integration, there was a slight reduction in PCP visit probability (−0.008; 95% CI: −0.015 to −0.001), followed by a modest increase in later years—reaching statistical significance in the second-year post-integration (0.015; 95% CI: 0.002–0.029) (Appendix Table ES27).

Although the probability of a PCP visit did not change, integration was associated with 0.66 more PCP visits per patient-year (a 7% increase from the 9.34 mean PCP visits at baseline), possibly indicating higher intensity care among those who do see a PCP (Figure 3). Among the integration cohorts, the largest ATT was observed among the 2018 cohort, who experienced 2.03 more visits per patient-year (95% CI: 1.301–2.765), a 22% increase from baseline. The 2021 cohort had fewer PCP visits per patient-year (−0.380; 95% CI: −0.684 to −0.068). Event-time estimates show a clear and sustained increase in primary care use following integration (Figure 1).

Specialist visit and specialist visit count

We found no consistent evidence that integration changed the overall probability of having a specialist visit in a given year (Figure 2). However, the group-average ATT was statistically significant, suggesting heterogeneous cohort-level differences (Appendix Table ES42). The 2018 cohort experienced a 4.9% point (or 5.4%) reduction in the probability of a specialist visit compared with the 90.5% baseline probability (95% CI: −0.076 to −0.022), while the 2021 cohort experienced a 3.8% point (or 4.2%) increase (95% CI: 0.023–0.054). The remaining cohorts showed small, non-significant ATTs. In the first year following integration, there was a small but statistically significant increase in specialist visit probability (0.017, 95% CI: 0.007–0.028), followed by significant declines in subsequent years (Appendix Table ES43).

Additionally, we found that, on average, patients of integrated physicians had 0.38 more specialist visits per year, a 4.3% increase from the 8.9 mean baseline visits (95% CI: 0.161–0.589). The largest ATT estimates were observed among early integration cohorts (Figure 3). Specifically, the 2018 cohort experienced 1.34 more visits (15.1% increase from baseline) per patient-year (95% CI: 0.821–1.865), while other cohorts showed smaller, insignificant differences (Appendix Table ES50). Event-time analyses revealed meaningful and growing increases in specialist visits in the years following integration (Appendix Table ES51). These post-treatment increases followed a trend of fewer specialist visits in the four years before integration; the sharp reversal in the trend of post-integration estimates strengthens the interpretation of a treatment effect (Appendix Figure ES4).

Sensitivity/robustness checks

To assess whether differences in the probability and frequency of outpatient visits differed by physician specialty, we compared baseline characteristics of PCP- and specialist-attributed patients (Appendix Table ES4). Differences were generally modest, although PCP-attributed patients had more PCP and specialist visits in the baseline period, compared with specialist-attributed patients. We then re-estimated doubly robust CSDiD models, which revealed meaningful heterogeneity.

Aligned with our main analyses, there was no significant difference in the probability of having a PCP visit following integration among patients attributed to PCPs or specialists. However, the number of PCP visits increased significantly following integration among PCP-attributed patients, with an estimated 0.84 (9%) more visits per patient-year compared with baseline (95% CI: 0.501–1.183), while no change was observed for specialist-attributed patients (Figure 2).

In contrast, there was no difference in the probability of having an annual specialist visit or the number of specialist visits after integration among specialist-attributed patients. However, when PCPs integrated, their patients experienced a statistically significant increase in the probability of having a specialist visit, as well as the number of visits per year. Specifically, patients of integrated PCPs had 0.49 (5.5%) more specialist visits per year on average compared with baseline, driven primarily by a large and significant increase in specialist visits for the 2018 integration cohort (1.80, 95% CI: 1.203–2.406) (Appendix Table ES52).

Finally, to assess the robustness of our MCC definition, we conducted a sensitivity analysis that limited the study cohort to patients with at least three qualifying conditions. Our results were very similar to the main findings, with one exception. Using the modified cohort, integration was associated with a 2.7% point decrease in the probability of an ED visit (95% CI: −0.052 to −0.002).

Discussion

This study used a staggered DiD approach to examine differences in health services utilization after hospital-physician integration. We found that integration resulted in increased use of both primary and specialist care services, alongside modest increases in the probability of and number of hospital admissions. However, these findings were heterogeneous across physician specialties.

The most consistent and policy-relevant finding was a significant increase in the use of ambulatory care following PCP integration. At baseline, PCP-attributed patients had more PCP and specialist visits, suggesting that PCP-attributed patients may engage more consistently in primary and specialty ambulatory care than specialist-attributed patients of comparable clinical complexity. These patients experienced an increase in the number of PCP visits, rising steadily with each post-integration year. We also found evidence that patients of integrated PCPs experienced both a higher likelihood of seeing a specialist annually and an increase in the number of specialist visits, particularly among the 2018 integration cohort. In contrast, integration among specialists did not lead to changes in specialist utilization, pointing to a potentially important referral-driven mechanism: hospital-PCP integration may expand access to specialist services indirectly, through increased referral volume from the newly integrated PCPs. This is aligned with another recent study, which found that vertical relationships between PCPs and health systems were associated with 0.69 (22.6%) additional specialist visits and 0.80 (29.4%) additional within-system specialist visits.14

In terms of acute care utilization, all-cause hospital admissions increased modestly following integration. The extent of this difference grew in magnitude over time, suggesting that as integrated providers engage with complex patients, they may be increasing the intensity of services or increasing self-referrals within their integrated system. When it comes to ED utilization, however, we found no significant difference in the probability of a visit post-integration. Moreover, while the number of ED visits decreased slightly overall, the ATT was modest and limited to later years post-integration. These findings differ from published literature, which has generally found integration to be associated with more ED visits.14,24-27 One reason our results may deviate from other research is because we are focused on an MCC population that is known to have more ED visits at baseline; if most of our study cohort has an ED visit, we may not observe a change in the probability or number of ED visits as a result of integration.

Finally, while results showed no improvement in timely follow-up after an ED visit, consistent with other published studies,25,27 the 2021 integration cohort exhibited a small but statistically significant decline in follow-up. This may reflect transitional disruptions or pandemic-related effects, though further exploration is warranted.

Taken together, our findings suggest that hospital-physician integration influences care delivery patterns for patients with MCCs in nuanced ways. Among patients of integrated PCPs, integration was associated with more ambulatory care, including both primary care and specialty visits, while the probability of having any visit remained largely unchanged. Hospital utilization modestly increased, whereas ED use did not change, raising questions about whether these patterns reflect improved care coordination or simply higher service intensity. The increase in ambulatory visits may partly reflect changes in referral patterns within integrated systems. Notably, our study highlights the need to account for physician specialty in integration evaluations, as results may differ substantially across provider types.

Conclusion

Overall, hospital-physician integration was generally associated with slightly higher hospital use and moderately more non-emergent ambulatory care, driven by PCP integration. It remains unclear whether our findings represent improved care coordination, referral-driven increases, or simply higher service intensity, highlighting the need for further research to determine the value of these utilization changes for patients with MCCs.
