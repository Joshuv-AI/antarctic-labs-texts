How Soon Is Now?
Authors: Shokouh Arjmand, Carl M Sellgren
Published: 2026

When you say it’s gonna happen now. Well, when exactly do you mean?

—Steven Patrick Morrissey, “How soon is now?” by the Smiths (released Jan 28, 1984).

Large-scale longitudinal studies have reinvigorated interest in puberty as a critical developmental window for psychosis risk. Two recent contributions demonstrated that early pubertal timing predicts psychotic-like experiences (PLEs) in both boys and girls (1), and that later (age-adjusted) pubertal status and higher estradiol levels are linked to PLEs in female adolescents (2). These studies provide timely and methodologically robust evidence that pubertal maturation is not merely a background correlate of psychopathology but rather an active developmental process shaping vulnerability.

Using longitudinal data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, in a recently published article in Biological Psychiatry: Global Open Science, Larson et al. (1) demonstrate that early pubertal timing, defined as developing physically ahead of same-age, same-sex peers, is a robust risk factor for PLEs and associated distress in both boys and girls, although the rate of development, or tempo, manifests sex-specific effects. Specifically, a slower pubertal tempo in boys is associated with more PLEs, whereas tempo does not significantly influence risk in girls. These patterns are consistent across both the adrenal and gonadal axes, as indexed by the respective subscales of the Pubertal Development Scale, highlighting earlier adrenarche as a particularly strong predictor of PLEs in girls.

Complementing these findings, Pries et al. (2) also use data from the ABCD Study but focus on female adolescents. Similar to Larson et al. (1), they show that earlier age at menarche is associated with increased PLE likelihood, along with later perceived age-adjusted pubertal stage (mid- or late puberty vs. prepubertal) and higher salivary estradiol concentrations. During the active phase of pubertal maturation, estradiol may therefore represent a specific vulnerability factor, a finding that at least initially may seem to contradict the suggested protective effects of estrogens (3). Combined, these studies indicate that the biological and social processes of puberty exert a substantial, and possibly independent, influence on the emergence of PLEs during the transition to reproductive maturity and raise important conceptual and interpretative questions that warrant closer scrutiny.

What about schizophrenia, then? A major strength of both studies is their focus on PLEs prior to illness onset, consistent with contemporary developmental and preventive psychiatry frameworks. However, while these studies compellingly demonstrate associations between puberty timing and PLEs, they also underscore a persistent conceptual challenge in psychosis research: translating subthreshold PLEs to psychosis risk, particularly within the schizophrenia-spectrum liability.

A population-level meta-analysis indicates that PLEs are strongly associated with schizophrenia risk (odds ratio ∼ 3), but this risk is largely concentrated in the top decile of persistent symptoms (4). The PLE distribution in the ABCD cohort, with most of the self-reported scores being mild and few participants within the high-risk tail (5), limits direct schizophrenia inference. In the ABCD Study, Karcher et al. (5) reported schizophrenia polygenic risk score (PRS) links only to their significantly distressed PLE subgroup (%ΔR2 ≈ 0.1%–0.2%; ≥1 PLE with distress ≥ 3/5), while total PLEs associated more strongly with educational attainment and cross-disorder PRS (%ΔR2 = 0.2%–0.7%).

This suggests that PLEs in this context foremost mark a general vulnerability to psychopathology rather than schizophrenia specificity, and longer-term ABCD follow-up remains critical. In agreement with this, prior work has also suggested that early menarche is linked more broadly to psychopathology (6), underscoring the need for domain-specific markers and illness-course trajectories.

As summarized by Larson et al. (1), several hypotheses have been formulated to explain associations between age at menarche and mental health outcomes. Psychosocial models either focus specifically on an early puberty, emphasizing a mismatch of cognitive, social, and emotional immaturity to environmental demands, while off-time models stress heightened social comparisons, whether early or late relative to peers, as the causative factor. As mentioned above, sex steroid timing/exposure to the developing brain, either explaining early puberty as a risk factor dependent on exposure in an early sensitive time window or by cumulative dose, has also been lifted as possible mediators. The findings from Larson et al. (1) and Pries et al. (2) then effectively underscore the difficulties in isolating such effects in observational studies. In fact, both studies can be interpreted as supporting maturational disparity as well as hormonal mechanisms, although Pries et al. (2) contradicts simple off-time models via heightened social comparisons. Notably, while Pries et al. (2) found no significant interaction effects between age at menarche/puberty status and schizophrenia PRSs, educational attainment PRSs inversely associate with both age at menarche (7) and PLEs (5). This introduces overlapping genetic factors as another plausible mechanism, and longer-term follow-up should suggestively be combined with a more comprehensive temporal mapping of hormones, social processes, and genotypes to isolate risk constructs for experimental testing.

Sex matters, but how? Larson et al. (1) extend prior female-focused work by showing that early pubertal timing predicts PLEs in both sexes, with a striking male-specific effect: a slower pubertal tempo associates with more PLEs. This suggests that sex-specific hormonal dynamics (not just stage) drive vulnerability. While this needs to be studied longitudinally and broadly across sex hormones, it raises a more fundamental question: What drives clinical sex differences in schizophrenia? Surprisingly few high-quality, large-scale longitudinal studies have directly dissected sex hormone effects on clinical differences in psychotic disorders across sexes. Integrating such studies with mechanistic data on how sex hormones impact neurodevelopmental processes could dissect sex-specific neurodevelopmental pathways. Genetic risk factors could then also be taken into consideration. For example, schizophrenia risk attributed to copy numbers of C4A—the most robustly associated genetic risk factor in genome-wide association studies—is more pronounced in males (8), and postmortem studies indicate larger synapse loss effect sizes in male patients with schizophrenia (9). The proposed mechanism linking these associations—excessive microglia-mediated and complement-dependent synapse uptake—also derives from male patient induced pluripotent stem cell models (10).

What’s next? Together, these studies convincingly consolidate links between pubertal timing and the emergence of PLEs. Although the relevance for schizophrenia risk remains to be fully established, the findings underscore puberty as a sensitive developmental period during which biological, genetic, and psychosocial factors converge to shape vulnerability. As outlined above, these findings also revive the fundamental question of the biological basis for sex differences in nonaffective psychotic disorders and highlight sex hormones as a plausible contributor to sex-specific trajectories. Future work will require longer-term follow-up to establish illness trajectories, as well as more granular temporal mapping of hormonal changes, metabolic factors, social experiences, and environmental adversity. Greater integration of biological measures into large-scale longitudinal studies, complemented by experimental approaches, will also be essential to refine mechanistic hypotheses and to clarify the role of sex and development in psychotic disorders.
