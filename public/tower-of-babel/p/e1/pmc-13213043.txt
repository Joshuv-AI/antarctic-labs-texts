Upregulated TRPM1 is associated with apoptosis in Rs1 knockout mice and in ARPE19 cells through increased intracellular calcium
Authors: Weiping Wang, Jingyang Liu, Xiuxiu Jin, Ruiqi Qiu, Mingzhu Yang, Shun Yao, Guangming Liu, Mingyang Qin, Bo Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe pathological mechanism underlying retinal apoptosis in X-linked retinoschisis (XLRS), a disease caused by retinoschisin 1 (RS1) deficiency, remains incompletely understood. This study aimed to investigate the role of transient receptor potential melastatin 1 (TRPM1) in retinal tissues and cells. Retinal function and structure were assessed by electroretinography (ERG) and optical coherence tomography (OCT). Protein expression was evaluated by immunofluorescence staining and western blotting (WB). Retinal morphology was examined by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Apoptotic retinal cells were detected by TUNEL staining. Key proteins were screened using proteomics data obtained by mass spectrometry. Intracellular calcium levels were measured using Rhod-2 AM. TRPM1 expression in Rs1-KO mice was 1.3-fold higher than that in wild-type mice (p < 0.05), whereas TRPM1-overexpressing ARPE19 cells exhibited approximately twofold higher expression than the empty vector control group. Mechanistically, TRPM1-mediated calcium influx promoted calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CAMKII) phosphorylation. Concomitantly, the accumulation of the autophagy-related proteins P62 and LC3B, increased BAX expression, and decreased BCL2 expression were observed in both Rs1-KO retinal tissues and TRPM1-overexpressing ARPE19 cells. These findings collectively suggest that TRPM1 may contribute to cell’s apoptosis. Introduction

Retinoschisin (RS1) plays a crucial role in the retina, primarily through maintaining cell adhesion and structural integrity1,2. RS1 is a secreted cell adhesion protein that is expressed predominantly in photoreceptors and bipolar cells3–5. Loss of RS1 function results in X-linked retinoschisis (XLRS), a common inherited retinal disorder in young males characterized by retinal layer splitting and marked retinal dysfunction6–9. In Rs1 knockout models, retinal photoreceptors exhibit shortened outer segments and impaired electrophysiological responses, highlighting that RS1 plays a critical role in maintaining normal photoreceptors function10–15. Although restoring RS1 function through gene therapy can significantly improve retinal structure and function16–18, retinal function in Rs1 knockout mice still fails to fully recover to wild-type levels, possibly because of ongoing retinal cell apoptosis19.

The TRPM1 gene encodes a member of the transient receptor potential melastatin (TRPM) subfamily of ion channels. TRPM1 primarily functions in signal transduction in retinal ON bipolar cells20–22. TRPM1 is also involved in calcium (Ca2+) homeostasis in retinal cells. Studies have demonstrated that TRPM1 activity is positively coupled to metabotropic glutamate receptor 6 (mGluR6/GRM6) signaling in retinal cells23,24. This coupling is critical for normal retinal bipolar cell function and is disrupted in disorders such as congenital stationary night blindness, in which TRPM1 mutations lead to impaired responsiveness to mGluR6 signaling25,26. Overexpression of TRPM1 has been shown to increase cytosolic Ca2+ concentrations in vitro27. Knockdown of Trpm1 leads to reduced intracellular Ca2+ levels, decreased tyrosinase activity, and lower intracellular melanin content, supporting the role of TRPM1 in Ca2+ homeostasis. However, the mechanisms by which Ca2+ signaling mediates apoptosis in Rs1-KO mice remain to be elucidated. Ca2⁺/calmodulin-dependent kinase II (CAMKII) is a serine/threonine kinase. It is recognized as a key effector in Ca2⁺ signaling pathways and is activated by Ca2⁺ surges, which induce autophosphorylation28. Knockdown of TRPM1 protein expression reduces the phosphorylation of CAMKII29. Excessive calcium ion influx induces the phosphorylation of CAMKII, which inhibits autophagy and triggers apoptosis30,31.

Previous studies have shown increased phosphorylation of the CAMKII protein in Rs1 knockout mouse models32, although its regulatory mechanism remains unclear. Our study revealed a positive correlation between CAMKII phosphorylation and TRPM1 expression. CAMKII phosphorylation was associated with autophagy dysregulation and apoptosis. High TRPM1 expression promoted CAMKII activation, increased accumulation of the autophagy-related protein P62, and significantly reduced cell viability. These findings suggest that TRPM1 plays a critical role in retinal apoptosis, thus providing a rationale for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.

Results

Characteristics of retinal function, morphology, and apoptosis in Rs1-knockout mice

To verify successful Rs1 knockout in mice, retinal function, morphology, and RS1 protein expression were characterized at P21. A typical ERG feature of Rs1-KO mice is a disproportionate reduction in the bipolar cell-derived b-wave amplitude relative to the photoreceptor-derived a-wave amplitude (Fig. 1A). Under dark-adapted conditions at light intensities of −2, −1, 0, and 1 log cd·s/m², the mean a-wave amplitudes in wild-type mice were 8.43 ± 2.80 µV, 92.38 ± 14.76 µV, 149.70 ± 15.11 µV, and 240.67 ± 19.35 µV, respectively. The mean b-wave amplitudes in wild-type mice were 211.43 ± 6.19 µV, 321.48 ± 25.44 µV, 404.35 ± 11.68 µV, and 515.33 ± 55.82 µV, respectively. In Rs1-KO mice, the mean a-wave amplitudes were 9.73 ± 4.39 µV, 90.90 ± 6.35 µV, 133.33 ± 11.37 µV, and 141.50 ± 10.31 µV. The b-wave amplitudes were significantly reduced to 108.20 ± 5.80 µV, 168.87 ± 7.07 µV, 204.93 ± 12.74 µV, and 172.23 ± 20.38 µV (Fig. 1B). Under light-adapted conditions, at light intensities of 0 and 1 log cd·s/m², the mean b-wave amplitudes for wild-type mice were 37.23 ± 11.50 µV, and 136.25 ± 30.30 µV. The b-wave amplitudes for Rs1-KO mice were reduced to 9.43 ± 5.20 µV, and 14.73 ± 8.52 µV, respectively (Fig. 1C). Compared with WT mice, Rs1-KO mice exhibited markedly attenuated ERG responses under both dark- and light-adapted conditions, particularly in the b-wave. Normal retinal structure was observed via OCT imaging in WT mice, whereas prominent schisis-like cavities were observed in the inner nuclear layer of retinas from Rs1-KO mice (Fig. 1D). To confirm loss of RS1 expression, immunofluorescence staining and western blotting were performed to examine RS1 protein expression in the retina. As shown in Fig. 1E, robust green fluorescent signals for the RS1 protein were observed in the inner and outer plexiform layers of WT mouse retinas, whereas such signals were largely undetectable in Rs1-KO mice. The western blot results also confirmed that RS1 protein was barely detectable in the retinas of Rs1-KO mice (Fig. 1F).

To investigate whether retinal cells undergo apoptosis in Rs1-KO mice, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assays were performed. Compared with wild-type mice, H&E staining revealed nuclear loss and disorganization of tissue architecture in the inner nuclear layer (INL) of Rs1-KO retinas (Fig. 1G). TUNEL staining further confirmed prominent apoptotic signals in the INL of retinal cells from Rs1-KO mice (Fig. 1H). A marked increase in TUNEL positivity was observed in the INL of Rs1-KO mice compared with WT mice, as quantified by both the percentage of positive cells and the mean fluorescence intensity (Fig. 1I and J). Together, these results demonstrate significantly increased apoptosis in the inner nuclear layer of Rs1-KO retinas. In summary, retinal cell apoptosis occurs following RS1 deficiency, although the mechanism underlying apoptosis remains unclear.

Proteomic analysis of retinas from Rs1-knockout mice and identification of TRPM1-related pathways

To explore the pathway alterations associated with apoptosis following RS1 deficiency, we extracted retinal tissue proteins from 3-month-old Rs1-KO mice, using age-matched wild-type mice as controls, and performed mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis. Proteomic data identified 197 differentially expressed proteins from Rs1-KO mouse retinas, including 96 upregulated proteins and 101 downregulated proteins. The screening criteria for differentially expressed proteins were as follows: |log2 fold change| > 0.5 and adjusted p value less than 0.05. A volcano plot illustrating these differentially expressed proteins is shown in Fig. 2A. Next, we examined the expression of the top 14 most prominently dysregulated proteins in the radar plot (Fig. 2B). The most significantly downregulated protein was RS1. Among the upregulated proteins, TRPM1, a bipolar cell-associated protein, was notably increased.

The cnet plot showed the results of Gene Ontology biological process (GO-BP) analysis, indicating that TRPM1 was involved in biological processes such as visual perception and phototransduction (Fig. 2C). The three major Gene Ontology categories, namely, biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF), further illustrated the overall functional characteristics of the differentially expressed proteins identified in this study (Fig. 2D). KEGG analysis revealed several significantly altered signaling pathways, including the calcium signaling pathway (Fig. 2E). To further investigate changes in calcium signaling-related proteins in Rs1-KO mice, we downloaded the mouse gene set for the calcium signaling pathway from the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) database. Subsequent overlap analysis between our proteomic profiling data and the GSEA-derived gene set identified 24 overlapping proteins. Among these 24 overlapping proteins, those with significant differential expression were CAMK2a, GNOA1, GNG5, GNB5, GNGT2, PDE6B, GNB1, and GNAT2 (Fig. 2F). Specifically, CAMK2a and GNOA1 were significantly upregulated, whereas the other proteins were downregulated. Moreover, we downloaded the mouse gene set for the GOBP_REGULATION_OF_AUTOPHAGY from the GSEA database. Overlap analysis between our proteomic profiling data and the GSEA-derived gene set identified 110 overlapping proteins. Among these, four proteins (STUB1, PIK3C3, SLC7A5, and ZMPSTE24) were differentially expressed, and all of them were upregulated (Fig. 2G).

Changes in the expression of key synapse-related molecules in the retinas of Rs1 knockout mice and CAMKII phosphorylation, autophagy, and apoptosis.

We performed both RT-qPCR and western blot analyses. Retinal tissues were collected from Rs1-KO and age-matched wild-type mice at 3 months of age. Analysis of mRNA expression showed that Trpm1, Grm6, Nyx, and Goɑ were significantly upregulated (Fig. 3A). Additionally, we conducted western blot analyses to detect the protein expression of GRM6, TRPM1, GOα, protein kinase Cα (PKCα), a marker of rod bipolar cells, and postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD95). The results revealed the increased expression of TRPM1 and PSD95 in Rs1-KO mice, whereas no significant differences in the protein levels of GRM6, NYX, GOα, and PKCα were detected between the two groups (Fig. 3B and C).

To further investigate the effects of TRPM1 upregulation on cells, we examined calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CAMKII), phosphorylated CAMKII (p-CAMKII), autophagy-related proteins (P62 and LC3B), and apoptosis-related proteins (BAX and BCL2). Total CAMKII protein expression did not differ significantly between the two groups, whereas p-CAMKII was increased in Rs1-KO mice. In the retinas of Rs1-KO mice, the protein expression of LC3B-II, P62, and the pro-apoptotic protein BAX was significantly elevated, whereas that of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2 was decreased (Fig. 3D and E).

Successful transfection of TRPM1 plasmid into ARPE19 cells

To determine whether TRPM1 overexpression directly perturbs calcium homeostasis and induces cell death, a well-controlled in vitro experimental system was required. For this purpose, we employed ARPE19 cells, a retinal pigment epithelial cell line characterized by minimal endogenous TRPM1 expression.

To evaluate the transfection efficiency of the TRPM1 plasmid in ARPE19 cells, we performed immunofluorescence staining to detect TRPM1 protein expression 48 h after transfection. TRPM1 protein signal was detectable in ARPE19 cells transfected with the TRPM1 plasmid, whereas little to no signal was observed in control cells transfected with the empty vector (Fig. S1A). The transfection efficiency, as determined by quantification of TRPM1-positive cells, was approximately 62% in the pTRPM1 group (Fig. S1B).

Transcriptome analysis of TRPM1-overexpressing ARPE19 cells.

To comprehensively investigate the effects of TRPM1 overexpression at the transcriptome level, global gene expression profiles in ARPE19 cells transfected with either an empty vector or a TRPM1 plasmid were obtained by RNA-seq.

The Pearson correlation coefficients among samples in the pTRPM1 group and vector group ranged from 0.938 to 1.00, indicating an exceptionally high degree of linear correlation among the samples (Fig. 4A). This suggests excellent data consistency and stability, with no markedly aberrant or outlier samples, thereby demonstrating strong reproducibility among the experimental samples. Principal component analysis (PCA) further demonstrated clear clustering patterns between the groups in principal component space (Fig. 4B). These results confirm that the transcriptome dataset is suitable for subsequent analyses. The volcano plot presented in Fig. 4C depicts the results of differential gene expression analysis between the pTRPM1 group and the vector control group. The screening criteria for differentially expressed genes were as follows: |log2 fold change| > 1 and adjusted p < 0.05. There were 218 DEGs upregulated and 229 DEGs downregulated in the pTRPM1 group compared with the vector group. Notably, TRPM1 was among the most significantly upregulated genes, with a log2 fold change of 9.32, far exceeding the applied threshold of |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 (adjusted p < 0.05), further confirming that plasmid transfection was successful.

To explore the biological implications of TRPM1 overexpression in ARPE19 cells, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes identified by transcriptome sequencing. A Circos plot (Fig. 4D) visualized the enrichment of TRPM1-associated differentially expressed genes (DEGs), integrating terms across BP, CC, and MF ontologies, alongside DEG counts (upregulated vs. downregulated) and enrichment significance (-log10(p value)). Genes potentially regulated by TRPM1 were enriched in BP terms related to positive regulation of cell migration and ECM organization; CC terms related to cell-cell junctions, extracellular matrix (ECM) components, intermediate filament cytoskeleton, and myofibrils; and MF terms related to signaling receptor activity, cytokine activity, semaphorin receptor activity, and actin binding. A Circos plot further illustrated KEGG pathway enrichment for TRPM1-associated DEGs (Fig. 4E). The IL-17 signaling pathway was predominantly characterized by upregulation, as evidenced by the longer pink bars, whereas the focal adhesion pathway showed a bias toward downregulation. Notably, the MAPK signaling pathway displayed a balanced expression profile, with 11 genes upregulated and 11 downregulated.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to identify biological pathways systematically altered by TRPM1 overexpression. The apoptosis gene set was significantly enriched (normalized enrichment score [NES] = 1.68, nominal p = 0.0, false discovery rate [FDR] q = 0.032) (Fig. 4F), indicating a transcriptional shift toward pro-apoptotic signaling. In parallel, we also observed significant enrichment of the necroptosis pathway (NES = 1.60, nominal p = 0.0, FDR q = 0.045) (Fig. 4G), suggesting that TRPM1 overexpression may simultaneously engage multiple, distinct cell death mechanisms. Furthermore, the specific genes driving these pathway enrichments were identified and visualized in Fig. 4H and I, offering a detailed view of the key regulators involved in apoptosis and necroptosis.

TRPM1 overexpression promotes intracellular Ca²⁺ accumulation and apoptosis in vitro

To determine whether overexpression of TRPM1 enhances intracellular calcium levels, the indicator Rhod-2 AM was used to assess intracellular calcium distribution. Compared with the empty vector group, ARPE19 cells transfected with the TRPM1 plasmid for 48 h exhibited markedly increased red fluorescence, indicating elevated intracellular calcium levels (Fig. 5A and B). Treatment with 2-APB did not significantly reduce calcium fluorescence in TRPM1-overexpressing cells (Fig. S4). Cell viability was assessed using the trypan blue exclusion assay. TRPM1 plasmid transfection reduced cell viability by approximately 60% (Fig. 5C). Western blotting results showed that total CAMKII expression did not differ significantly between the two groups, whereas phosphorylated CAMKII was increased in ARPE19 cells transfected with the TRPM1 plasmid. Furthermore, in the pTRPM1 group, there was a significant increase in the protein levels of P62, LC3B-II, and the pro-apoptotic protein BAX, whereas the level of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2 was reduced (Fig. 5D and E). These results indicate that TRPM1 overexpression elevates intracellular calcium, enhances CAMKII phosphorylation, and shifts cellular signaling toward autophagy dysregulation and apoptosis.

Discussion

In this study, we used an Rs1 knockout mouse model to investigate the association between TRPM1 and Ca2⁺ signaling. Additionally, we conducted preliminary investigations into the relationship between autophagy and apoptosis in ARPE19 cells transfected with a TRPM1 plasmid. In vivo experiments revealed that the TRPM1, phosphorylated CAMKII, autophagy-related proteins, and apoptosis-related proteins showed concordant expression changes in the Rs1-KO model. Notably, in vitro experiments recapitulated the trends observed in vivo. High TRPM1 expression promoted CAMKII phosphorylation, likely through increased intracellular Ca²⁺ accumulation. Increased CAMKII phosphorylation was associated with impaired autophagic flux, as reflected by the accumulation of P62, a selective substrate for autophagy. P62 accumulation was accompanied by increased apoptotic signaling. These findings provide new insight into a potential mechanism underlying retinal apoptosis in Rs1-KO mice.

Previous studies have shown that retinal cells, particularly bipolar cells and photoreceptors, undergo age-dependent apoptosis in patients with X-linked retinoschisis (XLRS) and in the Rs1-KO mouse model7–9. However, the precise mechanisms underlying this type of apoptosis remain poorly understood. Loss of RS1 triggers the activation of Src, a key mediator in the Na+/K+-ATPase signaling pathway, which subsequently induces the activation of CAMKII, a critical effector of Ca²⁺ signaling cascades32,33. A reduced TRPM1 immunofluorescence signal at synaptic sites in the retina of rd10 mice is accompanied by a concomitant increase in the number of somatic cells34. This finding is partly consistent with our findings regarding altered TRPM1 expression in the Rs1-KO mouse model. Although the genetic defects in these mouse models differ, both are associated with retinal diseases.

TRPM1 exhibits constitutive channel activity with high permeability to calcium ions (Ca²⁺) and other divalent cations35. TRPM-mediated Ca²⁺ influx promotes CAMKII phosphorylation, which subsequently phosphorylates BECN1 at Ser295 and inhibits autophagy31. We therefore asked whether upregulation of TRPM1 in the retina of Rs1-KO mice similarly enhances Ca2⁺ influx, promotes CAMKII activation, and thereby inhibits autophagy. Our results revealed that increased CAMKII phosphorylation was present in the retinas of Rs1-KO mice and was accompanied by the accumulation of the autophagy-related protein P62. P62 protein is also involved in apoptosis, and its excessive accumulation can activate caspase-8, thereby inducing apoptosis30. Consistent with these findings, overexpression of the TRPM1 led to increased P62 expression and upregulation of the pro-apoptotic protein BAX in ARPE19 cells.

Through integrative analysis of the mouse retinal proteome and the transcriptome of ARPE19 cells transfected with a TRPM1 plasmid, we constructed a Venn diagram to reveal the overlapping and unique features of the two datasets. There were 276 common genes/proteins between the two datasets (Fig. S3). Moderate TRPM1 upregulation in Rs1-KO retinas (Figs. 2 and 3) and its robust overexpression in transfected ARPE19 cells (Fig. 4) not only support the reliability of the in vitro model, but also suggest that Rs1 deficiency may indirectly modulate endogenous TRPM1 expression, potentially disrupting ON bipolar cell phototransduction. This finding is consistent with the delayed photoreceptor maturation and visual signal transduction defects reported in Rs1-KO mice10,11. As shown in Table S1, shared upregulation of MAP3K5, a core MAPK pathway mediator, suggests that TRPM1 overexpression and Rs1 deficiency converge to activate retinal cell stress and apoptotic signaling cascades, which are well-recognized pathological drivers of retinal neurodegeneration. Elevated DDX58 (encoding the innate immunity regulator RIG-I) in both models indicates that TRPM1 upregulation and Rs1 deficiency may trigger retinal immune and endoplasmic reticulum stress responses, thereby exacerbating inflammatory damage and synaptic dysfunction36. Additionally, the coordinated downregulation of RGS9 (a key modulator of visual signal transduction) and SLC12A6 (a regulator of retinal ionic homeostasis) is consistent with the abnormal transducin translocation and ion imbalance observed in Rs1-KO retinas6, and mirrors the visual signal transmission impairment caused by TRPM1 dysfunction in ON bipolar cells.

In native ON bipolar cells, the gating of TRPM1 channels is tightly regulated by the mGluR6 signaling pathway: in darkness, mGluR6 activation keeps TRPM1 closed, whereas light suppresses mGluR6 activity and permits channel opening21,37. However, previous studies have shown that TRPM1 channels exhibit constitutive activity in heterologous expression systems (e.g., CHO cells) that lack the endogenous retinal regulatory complex (including mGluR6, Gαo, and nyctalopin), resulting in sustained cation influx38. In our study, ARPE19 cells similarly lack this complete retina-specific inhibitory machinery. Therefore, when TRPM1 is overexpressed at high levels in these cells, the ion channels localized to the plasma membrane likely reside in a comparable disinhibited state, thereby contributing to the observed sustained calcium influx.

Notably, this study has several limitations. We did not perform in vivo intervention experiments to establish a direct causal relationship between retinal TRPM1 modulation and retinal damage in Rs1-KO mice. In addition, although the ARPE19 cell model partially recapitulated the molecular alterations observed in Rs1-KO retinas, these retinal pigment epithelial cells cannot fully reproduce the physiological and functional properties of ON bipolar cells, the primary site of TRPM1-mediated visual signaling. Future studies using ON bipolar cell-specific Trpm1 conditional knockout mice will therefore be necessary to define the causal role of TRPM1 in the pathological progression of Rs1-KO retina and to evaluate its therapeutic potential in X-linked retinoschisis. The molecular mechanisms by which Rs1 deficiency regulates TRPM1 expression, together with the downstream pathways through which TRPM1 affects retinal cell survival and function, also warrant further investigation.

Materials and methods

Antibodies and reagents

Antibodies against RS1 (SAB1100315 for western blotting, HPA059546 for immunofluorescence) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The rabbit anti-TRPM1 antibody was purchased from Novus Biologicals (NB110-74925, CO, USA). Antibodies against PKC alpha (ET1608-15), GNAO1 (HA500136), PSD95 (ET1602-20), CAMKII (ET1608-47), BCL2 (ET1603-11), BAX (ET1603-34) and β-Actin (EM21002) were purchased from HUABIO (Hangzhou, China), while antibodies against CAMKIIα/δ (phospho Thr286) were obtained from Signalway Antibody (Nanjing, China). The rabbit anti-NYX antibody was purchased from ImmunoWay (YT7486, Jiangsu, China). Anti-mGluR6 was obtained from Abcepta (AP59571, CA, USA). Anti-SQSTM1/P62 was obtained from Servicebio (GB11531, Wuhan, China). The rabbit anti-LC3B antibody was purchased from Proteintech (18725-1-AP, Wuhan, China). A one-step TUNEL apoptosis assay kit was obtained from Beyotime (C1088, Shanghai, China). Rhod-2 AM was supplied by MedChemExpress (145037-81-6, Shanghai, China).

Generation of Rs1 knockout mice

We created the Rs1 knockout mouse model in our laboratory via CRISPR/Cas9 in C57BL/6J mice, with assistance from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technologies Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). Construction of the XLRS mouse model was described in our previous work9. Animals were housed under a 12 h light/dark cycle and given a standard chow diet. For tissue collection, mice were placed in a dedicated euthanasia chamber that was not prefilled with CO₂, and medical-grade CO₂ was then introduced gradually at a flow rate of 3.0 L/min. Unconsciousness was confirmed by loss of corneal reflex and respiratory arrest. To ensure irreversible death, CO₂ exposure was continued for an additional 2 min, followed by cervical dislocation as a secondary physical method. Animal care and use followed the guidelines formulated by the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO). The animal experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Henan Eye Hospital/Henan Eye Institute (HNEECA-2021-09), and were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and relevant regulations.

Electroretinography (ERG)

To assess retinal function by electroretinography (ERG), the mice were subjected to overnight dark adaptation. The mice were anesthetized with 1.25% tribromoethanol (Aibei Biotechnology, Nanjing, China) by intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 0.2 mL/10 g body weight. Compound tropicamide eye drops (Santen, Chongqing, China) were administered to induce pupil dilation, and ofloxacin eye ointment (Xingqi, Shenyang, China) was applied to prevent corneal drying. A gold reference electrode and a ground electrode were carefully positioned on the conjunctival surface and the tail of the mouse, respectively. Additionally, a custom-made ring-shaped gold wire electrode was gently placed at the center of each cornea. Following these preparations, dark-adapted ERG recordings were performed using full-field flash stimulation at intensities of 0.01, 0.1, 1.0 and 10.0 cd·s/m2. Subsequently, after 5 min of light adaptation, recordings were made at light intensities of 1.0 and 10.0 cd·s/m2. These recordings were used to evaluate the functionality of retinal photoreceptor cells and bipolar cells.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT)

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging was conducted using a Micron IV system (Phoenix Research Labs, Pleasanton, CA, USA). Prior to imaging, the mice were anesthetized with 1.25% tribromoethanol (Aibei Biotechnology, Nanjing, China) by intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 0.2 mL/10 g body weight and then securely positioned on a dedicated mount specifically designed for OCT examinations. Tropicamide eye drops were administered to achieve pupil dilation. Bright-field fundus images were obtained to visualize the retina, and OCT image quality was improved by averaging 20 consecutive scans. By adjusting the angle between the camera and the eye, the peripheral retina could be effectively observed.

Immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded retinal sections were prepared following the established protocol17. Eyecups were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 4 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 6-µm-thick slices using a microtome. The sections were subjected to immunofluorescence and H&E staining. For immunofluorescence, paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and then incubated in 10 mmol/L citrate buffer for antigen retrieval. The primary antibody (anti-RS1, 1:500; Sigma-Aldrich) was applied to the tissue sections overnight at 4 °C, and the sections were subsequently incubated with a fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology) at room temperature for 1 h. The nuclei were stained with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Photographs were taken under standardized conditions to ensure consistency.

TUNEL

Retinal slices were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS at 4 °C for 60 min, and then washed twice with PBS for 10 min each. The samples were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 5 min, washed with PBS, and then incubated with 50 µL of TUNEL working solution at 37 °C for 60 min. DAPI staining was used to visualize the nuclei. Image analysis and fluorescent signal quantification were performed using ImageJ software.

Real-time quantitative PCR

TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to extract total RNA from the retinas of mice. cDNA was synthesized using a PrimeScript RT Reverse Kit (Takara). A SYBR Green Master Mix Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for real-time fluorescence quantification. Gapdh was used as the internal control for normalization of target gene expression. The RT-qPCR primer sequences were as follows: Trpm1-F, 5’-AAAGGAGGATGAGCAGCAGTCGTC-3’; Trpm1-R, 5’-ATTCTCCAGAGCGCTCACCATCCT-3’. Gapdh-F, 5’-TGGATTTGGACGCATTGGTC-3’; Gapdh-R, 5’-TTTGCACTGGTACGTGTTGAT-3’. Grm6-F, 5’-CAGCCATGGAGCCTACTGATGG-3’; Grm6-R, 5’-CACTGCTGGCACTTCCATTGG-3’. Nyx-F, 5’-CCAGGAGTTTCCGTGCGAG-3’; Nyx-R, 5’-TGATGAAGGACAGGTTATTGTGG-3’. Gnao1-F, 5’-TGCACGAGTCTCTCATGCTCT-3’; Gnao1-R, 5’-AGATGGTCAAGGGTGACTTCT-3’.

Immunoblot analysis

Retinal tissues and ARPE19 cells were lysed with SDS lysis buffer and centrifuged to collect total protein. The protein concentrations in the lysates were determined using a BCA assay (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Next, 10-30 µg of protein per sample was separated by 7.5%-10% SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis (Yamei, Shanghai, China). The separated proteins were subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes and detected using primary antibodies. Prior to antibody incubation, the membranes were cut according to the molecular weights of the target proteins to optimize antibody usage efficiency. The original data for the western blotting are presented in Figs. S4 and S5.

Cell culture and plasmid transfection

ARPE19 cells were purchased from QuiCell Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (QuiCell-A125, Shanghai) and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 4.5 g/L glucose, L-glutamine, 10% fetal bovine serum, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The plasmid construction and cell transfection were performed as described previously39. Briefly, the human TRPM1 (NM_001252020.2) plasmid was constructed by synthesis, subcloned and inserted into pCDNA3.1 and validated by sequencing. The plasmids were transfected into ARPE19 cells via Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Immunofluorescence staining of ARPE19 cells

After fixation and blocking with 3% BSA, a primary antibody against TRPM1 (1:100 dilution, Novus, CO, USA) was applied to the cells overnight at 4 ℃. After washing three times with PBS, the cells were incubated with a goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:300 dilution, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). After extensive washing with PBS, the cells were mounted with DAPI-containing anti-fade medium and imaged using a high-resolution microscope and TissueFAXS software (TissueGnostics, Vienna, Austria).

Calcium imaging

ARPE19 cells were incubated with 1 × Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS, 137.93 mM NaCl, 5.33 mM KCl, 4.17 mM NaHCO3, 1.26 mM CaCl₂, 0.493 mM MgCl₂, 0.407 mM MgSO₄, 0.441 mM KH₂PO₄, 0.338 mM Na₂HPO₄, and 5.56 mM D-glucose) for 24 h beginning at 48 h post-transfection. The TRPM1-overexpressing cells were treated with the broad-spectrum TRP channel modulator 2-APB (100 µM, Sigma, D9754) during the final 1 h of HBSS incubation. The Rhod-2 AM calcium indicator was subsequently added at a working concentration of 5 µM, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Images were captured using an Olympus fluorescence microscope, and the fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Proteomic sample preparation and data analysis

Eyeballs were collected from Rs1 knockout and wild-type mice at approximately 12 weeks of age, after which the retinas were isolated. The retinal samples were then subjected to lysis and protein digestion for subsequent protein analysis via mass spectrometry. The protein samples were processed using a previously established procedure8. The data were analyzed using the NovoMagic Cloud Platform (https://magic-plus.novogene.com/).

Transcriptome sample preparation and data analysis.

ARPE19 cells were incubated with 1 × HBSS for 24 h beginning at 48 h post-transfection. Following this incubation period, the cells were collected from each group and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen. The prepared cell samples were sent to a professional sequencing facility for transcriptome sequencing. Upon completion of sequencing, the data were analyzed using the NovoMagic Cloud Platform (https://magic-plus.novogene.com/).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The data are shown as the means ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s t test was utilized to compare differences between two groups. ImageJ software was used to analyze fluorescence intensity. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
