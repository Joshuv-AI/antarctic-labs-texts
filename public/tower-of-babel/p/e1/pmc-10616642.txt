Holistic view of heat acclimation alleviated intestinal lesion in mice with heat stroke based on microbiome‐metabolomics analysis
Authors: Xueyan Huang, Zhen Luo, Tingting Shen, Genlin He, Xueting Yu, Yishan Liu, Yulong Tan, Zeze Wang, Ping Li, Boyi Zhang, Huan Zhou, Xiangyu Chen, Ying Wang, Shasha Yu, Xue Luo, Xuesen Yang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe severity of heat stroke (HS) is associated with intestinal injury, which is generally considered an essential issue for HS. Heat acclimation (HA) is considered the best strategy to protect against HS. In addition, HA has a protective effect on intestinal injuries caused by HS. Considering the essential role of gut microbes in intestinal structure and function, we decided to investigate the potential protective mechanism of HA in reducing intestinal injury caused by HS. HA model was established by male C57BL/6J mice (5–6 weeks old, 17–19 g) were exposed at (34 ± 0.7)°C for 4 weeks to establish an animal HA model. The protective effect of HA on intestinal barrier injury in HS was investigated by 16S rRNA gene sequencing and nontargeted liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (LC‐MS) metabolomics. According to the experimental results, HA can change the composition of the gut microbiota, which increases the proportion of lactobacilli, faecal bacteria, and urinobacteria but decreases the proportion of deoxycholic acid. Moreover, HA can reduce liver and kidney injury and systemic inflammation caused by HS and reduce intestinal injury by enhancing the integrity of the intestinal barrier. In addition, HA regulates inflammation by inhibiting NF‐κB signalling and increasing tight junction protein expression in HS mice. HA induces changes in the gut microbiota, which may enhance tight junction protein expression, thereby reducing intestinal inflammation, promoting bile acid metabolism, and ultimately maintaining the integrity of the intestinal barrier. In conclusion, HA induced changes in the gut microbiota. Among the gut microbiota, lactobacilli may play a key role in the potential protective mechanism of HA.

INTRODUCTION

Heat stroke (HS) is a life‐threatening disease with unclear pathogenesis involving several important organs. It is often associated with systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) and multiple‐organ injury dysfunction syndrome (MODS; Peiris et al., 2017). When people suffer from HS, the mortality is still as high as 63% even if people receive adequate treatment (Jilma & Derhaschnig, 2012; Knapik & Epstein, 2019). At present, there is a lack of effective medicine for treating HS, so the prevention of HS is particularly important.

The gut seemed to be more sensitive to heat stress than other organs (for example, the liver and kidney). Intestinal injuries could occur in the early stages of HS. The intestines are the largest ‘reservoir’ of bacteria and endotoxins. Bacteria and their metabolites, endotoxins in the intestine, stimulate the ‘efficient’ release of inflammatory cytokines. Intestinal inflammation leads to a series of amplified systemic inflammation and causes changes in intestinal morphology and intestinal barrier function. Clinicopathological examination showed that HS patients had persistent intestinal inflammation and injury, the degree of which was highly correlated with prognosis. Therefore, intestinal damage caused by HS plays a key role in the pathogenesis and pathophysiology of HS (Lim, 2018; Ogden et al., 2020). Focusing on protecting the intestinal barrier could help prevent the development of HS.

Heat acclimation (HA) refers to the changes in the body that are conducive to adapting to the environment and improving heat tolerance under the action of repeated heat stimulation. It manifests as decreased core body temperature and skin temperature, improved cardiovascular adaptability, increased sweat volume, and improved body fluid balance. Currently, HA is the most effective measure to prevent HS (Cao et al., 2021; Ihsan et al., 2021). The physiological changes induced by HA can improve the host's adaptation to high temperatures. This change can not only improve the thermoregulation function, thus improving heat tolerance, but also reduce the hazards caused by high temperatures. At present, many studies have explored the mechanism of HA in heat tolerance (Daanen et al., 2018). However, this protective effect of HA may not be due to complex physiological processes alone. Potential mechanisms of HA may include improved sweating, improved blood flow to the skin, decreased body temperature, decreased cardiovascular stress, improved fluid balance, altered metabolism, and enhanced cellular protection (Sekiguchi et al., 2020; Travers et al., 2020). In our study, considering the importance of the intestinal barrier in HS, we hypothesized that intestinal physiological adaptation is one of the essential factors for HA.

The latest research has demonstrated that variation in the gut microbiota could positively support vertebrate adaptation to changes in the external environment, such as high temperature (Cao et al., 2021), high altitude (Karl et al., 2018; Khanna et al., 2018), or low temperature (Khakisahneh et al., 2020; Krisko et al., 2020). The gut microbiota has long coevolved with host health and disease and has become an important functional organ that should not be ignored. What is the relationship between the gut microbiome and thermal tolerance? The interaction between the host and gut microbiota might be systematically described through the microbiome combined with metabolomic analysis. In this study, we applied 16S rRNA gene sequencing and untargeted liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (LC‐MS) to investigate the gut microbiome and faecal metabolic profiles. The potential mechanism by which HA enhances the intestinal barrier is explained based on the gut microbiota.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

Experimental design

Experiment 1: Confirmation of heat acclimation prevention of heat stroke

The aim of this study was to confirm the preventive effect of HA in HS events. Therefore, an HA mouse model was established and exposed to high temperature to assess the protective effect of HA on mortality, multiple organ damage, intestinal permeability, and systemic inflammatory factors. Figure S1 is the flow chart of the experimental design.

Male C57BL/6J mice (5–6 weeks old, 17–19 g) were purchased from Laibite Biotech Co., Ltd. Mice in the control group were housed at an ambient temperature of 23 ± 1°C, with a humidity of 50 ± 5% and a 12:12 h light/dark cycle. Standard chow and sterile water were offered ad libitum. Mice in the heat acclimation group were maintained at an ambient temperature of 34 ± 0.7°C and a 12:12 h light/dark cycle. Standard chow and sterile water were offered ad libitum. The entire HA process was completed in 30 days (Yi et al., 2017). We established a stable acclimation status based on a low basal metabolic rate and improved thermotolerance (Arieli et al., 2003). All the experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of the Army Medical University in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health.

All C57BL/6J mice used in the experiment were randomly assigned to different groups. There were six groups. Control group mice (n = 10) were not subjected to HS. Heat acclimation (HA) group mice (n = 10) were subjected to heat acclimation but not to heatstroke. Heatstroke (HS) group mice (n = 10) were only subjected to HS. HA + HS group mice (n = 10) were subjected to HA and HS. Control + lactobacilli (Con + L) group mice (n = 10) were subjected to lactobacilli intervention but not to HS. Heat stroke +lactobacilli (HS + L) group mice (n = 10) were subjected to lactobacilli intervention and HS.

Mice in the HS, HS + L, and HA + HS groups were placed in a prewarmed specific environmental control smart chamber (HOPE‐MED 8150E) maintained at 40 ± 1°C with a relative humidity of 50 ± 5% in the absence of food and water to induce heat stroke. Core temperature (Tc) was directly monitored by an animal electronic thermometer (ALC‐ET06; Alcbio). Immediately when the Tc increased to 42.7°C (HS onset), mice were removed to recover at ambient temperature (23 ± 1°C) in a new cage with food and water ad libitum. The mice in the control, Con+L, and HA groups underwent the same experimental procedure as the HS, HS + L, and HA + HS mice, with the chamber temperature maintained at 23 ± 1°C and a relative humidity of 50 ± 5% throughout the experiment (Yi et al., 2017).

Experiment 2: Alteration of gut microbiota in heat acclimation

The objective of Experiment 2 was to elucidate the effect of HA on the gut microbiota and speculate on the potential mechanism by which HA enhances heat tolerance. The gut microbiota composition of HA mice was analysed. We hypothesized that the diversity of gut microbiota is important for the physiological adaptation of intestinal tissues and attempted to verify this through experiments (Figure S2).

The preparation of the HA and HS animal models was the same as that in Experiment 1. Two groups of mice (the control group and the HA group) were used to clarify the gut microbiota alteration in HA. Four groups of mice (control group, HA group, HS group, and HA + HS group) were used to verify the potential mechanism of the gut microbiota. All animals involved in this study complied with the ARRIVE Guideline and EU Directive 2010/63/EU for animal experiments.

General techniques

Serum collection

One of the causes of heat stroke (HS) is thermoregulation dysfunction. Animals were allowed to recover at room temperature after HS onset. For their thermoregulation dysfunction, they should endure hypothermia (3–6 h after HS onset). During hypothermia, organ pathological injury is usually more serious (Belval et al., 2018; Shimazaki et al., 2020). The time point of 4 h was chosen as hypothermia based on our previous research (Leon et al., 2005). Therefore, the point of damage detection time related to HS in our study was 4 h after HS onset, including serum biochemical marker measurement, cytokine measurement, and histological examination.

Animals were anaesthetised by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg body weight). Blood samples were harvested, and serum was separated by centrifugation at 1360 g for 30 min at 4°C (Eppendorf 5801R centrifuge) and then stored at −80°C for biochemical and enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analysis.

Serum biochemical marker and cytokine measurements

The levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea (BU), and creatinine (CREA) were determined by a fully automated biochemical analyser (Pointcare M4, MNCHIP). Commercial ELISA kits were used to measure the levels of tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α), interleukin 6 (IL‐6), and interleukin 1 beta (IL‐1β) (Cloud‐Clone Corp).

Histological examination

Samples of the liver, kidney, and ileum were harvested immediately following blood withdrawal and preserved in 10% neutral buffered formalin. All the preserved tissues were paraffin embedded, sectioned, stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and examined microscopically (Leica DM 2000) in a blinded manner.

Plasma endotoxin analysis

The Endotoxin Quant Kit uses amebocyte lysates derived from the blood of horseshoe crabs to quantitate endotoxin in plasma samples (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. All the micropipette tips, tubs, and other instruments used were nonpyrogenic. Plasma samples were placed in a nonpyrogenic plate and gently mixed with Amebocyte lysate Reagent for 20 min at 37°C. Next, chromogenic substrate solution was added and incubated for 6 min at 37°C, and then 25% acetic acid was added to stop the reaction. An endpoint measurement was performed at 405 nm in a microplate spectrophotometer. The results were expressed as EU/mL.

FD4 detection

Gastrointestinal permeability was measured by 4 kDa fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)‐dextran (FD4, Sigma Chemical Co.) solution. FD4 (25 mg/mL) was prepared with sterile distilled water, and the Eppendorf tube was wrapped in silver paper in the dark. Gastric lavage FD4 solution was administered to mice weighing 400 μL/30 g. Plasma was collected from the mice without HS after gavage with FD4 for 4 h. After the HS experiment was finished, the FD4 solution was orally administered to each group of mice for 4 h to collect plasma. The concentration of FD4 was measured using fluorescence spectroscopy (Varioskan LUK, Thermo Scientific). The instrument uses a microplate spectrophotometer with an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission wavelength of 535 nm. In addition, a series of dilutions for FITC were prepared, and a standard curve was plotted. Control group plasma was used as a negative control.

d‐lactate detection

Plasma d‐lactate was also used for gastrointestinal permeability measurement. Plasma d‐lactate levels were measured by a d‐lactate Colorimetric Assay Kit (BioVision) according to the manufacturer's instructions with a wavelength of 485 nm.

Western blot analysis

Tissues were washed in 0.9% saline and then lysed with ice‐cold radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Beyotime Company) supplemented with a protease inhibitor ‘cocktail’ (Thermo Scientific Pierce™ Protease and Phosphatase in EDTA‐free inhibitor mini tablets). The protein concentrations were measured using the BCA (Bicinchoninic acid) Protein Assay Kit (Aidlab Biotechnologies Co., Ltd.). Subsequently, an equal amount of each sample was separated by sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS)‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Bio‐Rad). The samples were incubated with anti‐phospho‐IkBa (1:1000; Affinity Biosciences), anti‐phospho‐p65 (1:1000; Cell Signalling Technology), anti‐p65 (1:1000; Cell Signalling Technology), anti‐occludin (1:500; Abcam), anti‐ZO‐1 (1:500; Abcam), and β‐actin (1:1000, A5316; Sigma Chemical Co.) antibodies for 24 h at 4°C. After three washes in Tris‐buffered saline Tween‐20 (TBST), the membranes were incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1000; Bio‐Rad). Protein bands were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (Bio‐Rad) and analysed with a ChemiDoc MP gel imaging system (Bio‐Rad).

16S rRNA gene sequencing & analysis

DNA was isolated from faecal pellets using an EZNA stool DNA kit according to the instructions of the manufacturer (Omega Biotek, Inc.). DNA was amplified using universal primers (iTRU‐A 515 F and iTRU‐1806 R) to target the V4 regions of microbiota 16S rRNA. Individual samples were barcoded, pooled to construct the sequencing library, and then sequenced (Illumina MiSeq) to generate 250 × 250 paired‐end reads. Analysis of 16S rRNA sequencing data was dependent on Shanghai Majorbio Biopharm Technology Co., Ltd.

Faecal metabolomics profiling

Faecal samples were taken for liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (LC‐MS) after heat acclimation (HA). LC‐MS analyses were performed using a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher) coupled with an Orbitrap Q Exactive Series mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher). Samples were injected onto a Hyperil Gold column (100 9 2.1 mm, 1.9 lm) using a 16‐min linear gradient at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. The eluents for the positive polarity mode were (A) 0.1% formic acid in water and (B) methanol. The eluents for the negative polarity mode were (A) 5 mm ammonium acetate, pH = 9.0, and (B) methanol. The solvent gradient was set as follows: 2% B, 1.5 min; 2–100% B, 12.0 min; 100% B, 14.0 min; 100–2% B, 14.1 min; 2% B, 17 min. The Q‐Exactive series mass spectrometer was operated in positive/negative polarity mode with a spray voltage of 3.2 kV, a capillary temperature of 320°C, a sheath gas flow rate of 35 arb, and an aux gas flow rate of 10 arb.

Data analysis

All experimental data are expressed as the means±SDs. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 8.3.0, GraphPad Software), and microbiome data were analysed utilizing the web platform provided by Shanghai Majorbio Biopharm Technology Co., Ltd. Multiple groups of results were analysed using ANOVA. A double‐tailed p value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. We are grateful to Shanghai Majorbio Biopharm Technology Co., Ltd. for their technical assistance.

RESULTS

Confirmation of heat acclimation prevention of heat stroke

We sought to determine whether HA can protect against HS. HA‐preconditioned mice were subjected to hyperthermic conditions, mortality, multiple organ damage, intestinal permeability, and systemic inflammatory cytokines to evaluate the level of HA protection.

Heat acclimation alleviated multiple organ damage and decreased serum cytokine levels associated with heatstroke

We continuously observed the food intake, body weight, and resting basal body temperature during HA (Figure 1A–C). Mice in the HA group exhibited lower food intake than those in the control group (p < 0.01, two‐way ANOVA test, Figure 1A), while the body mass of the HA group was significantly lower than that of the control group after the HA experiment had finished (p < 0.05, two‐way ANOVA test, Figure 1B). The Tc curve of the HA group significantly decreased in response to high ambient temperature (two‐way ANOVA test, Mauchly's test for sphericity, Figure 1C). The mice in the HA group challenged with high temperature showed better heat tolerance. The Tc in the HA group increased more slowly (p < 0.05, pairwise comparisons of the estimated marginal means, Figure 1D), and the mortality of the HA group after HS was much lower than that of the control group (p < 0.01, Figure 1E).

To better evaluate the protective effect of HA, serum and organs were collected during the hypothermia period (the period of severe pathological injury 4 h after the onset of HS) for analysis. First, serum biomarkers of liver function (ALT, AST) and kidney function (BU, CREA) were determined. There was no significant difference in serum levels between the HA group and the control group, which means that HA does not injure organs. The ALT level in the HS group was significantly higher than that in the control group (F = 20.827, p < 0.001, one‐way ANOVA test, Figure 2A), AST (p = 0.005, Kruskal‐Wallis test, Figure 2B), BU (F = 5.52, p = 0.007, Welch one‐way ANOVA test, Figure 2C), and CREA (F = 14.426, p < 0.001, one‐way ANOVA, Figure 2D). Compared with the HS group, ALT, AST, BU, and CREA levels in the HA + HS group were significantly decreased (Figure 2A–D). Consistent with the levels of these serum biomarkers, HS mice showed marked oedema‐related degeneration of liver cells (Figure 2E, liver). HA significantly attenuated the histological changes in the liver (Figure 2E, liver). The pathological damage to typical renal tissue in HS mice was significant. Proximal tubule autolysis was evident, including loss of cellular adhesions and loss of nuclei (Figure 2E, kidney). HA significantly attenuated renal histological changes (Figure 2E, kidney), TNF‐α (F = 11.131, p < 0.001, one‐way ANOVA, Figure 2F), IL‐6 (F = 33.771, p < 0.001, one‐way ANOVA, Figure 2G), and IL‐1β (p < 0.001, Kruskal‐Wallis test, Figure 2H). The levels of inflammatory factors in the serum of HA + HS mice were significantly lower than those of HS mice (Figure 2F–H). In conclusion, these results suggest that HA could prevent multiple organ damage and reduce systemic levels of inflammatory cytokines in HS.

Heat acclimation reduces the increase in intestinal permeability caused by intestinal injury induced by heatstroke

Intestinal barrier dysfunction is a very common complication in HS (Xia et al., 2017). Moreover, endotoxin released from the damaged intestinal barrier is a key point for activating the systemic inflammatory response (Lian et al., 2020). In HS mice, increased plasma endotoxin levels were detected at 4 h after the onset of HS. In addition, the level of endotoxin was significantly decreased in the HA + HS mice compared with that in the HS mice (F = 7.32, p = 0.002, Welch one‐way ANOVA test, Figure 3A). To further assess intestinal permeability, mice were gavaged with a 4‐kD fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)‐dextran solution (FD4) before heat exposure. When intestinal permeability increases, FD4 passes through the ‘leaky’ gastrointestinal barrier into the circulation. We detected high levels of plasma FD4 in the HS group compared with that in the control group, but the HA + HS mice showed a decreased plasma FD4 level compared with that of the HS mice (F = 10.766, p < 0.001, one‐way ANOVA test, Figure 3B). In addition, we checked another specific marker of intestinal barrier function, d‐lactate, and found the result to be consistent with the results of the endotoxin and FD4 experiments, which indicated similarity among the groups (F = 24.713, p < 0.001, one‐way ANOVA, Figure 3C). There were no significant differences in serum endotoxin, FD4, and d‐lactate levels between the HA and control groups. To explore the bioeffects of HA on the intestinal barrier, a histological examination was performed. No significant difference between the control and HA groups was observed under a light microscope (Figure 3D). In addition, intestinal lesions in the HS group were characterized by markedly dilated central villus lacteals and disordered lamina propria structure (Figure 3D). The histological changes in the intestine were obviously attenuated in the HA group. In summary, HA enabled mice to resist the high intestinal permeability caused by HS.

Adaptation of gut microbiota through heat acclimation

We explored the potential mechanism by which HA can enhance heat tolerance through gut microbiota. The changes in gut microbiota composition after HA were analysed. We hypothesized the potential physiological adaptation of intestinal tissues after HA and verified this experimentally.

Alpha diversity involves the analysis of species diversity in a single sample, which includes the sequencing depth (observed species (sobs), Good's coverage (coverage)), richness (Chao, ACE) and uniformity (Shannon, Simpson) of species composition. Effects of heat acclimation on the regulation of faecal metabolites

Metabolite analysis

We further examined whether the faecal metabolome was perturbed using untargeted LC‐MS in both positive ion (ES+) and negative ion (ES−) modes. The partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS‐DA) score plots showed a clear separation among each group. Tenfold cross‐validation was employed to evaluate the quality of these two PLS‐DA components, giving an R2 of (0, 0.117) and Q2 of (0, −0.2148) (Figure 6A,B). We performed pathway enrichment analysis using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes. The pathways were significantly changed, including Aminoacyl‐tRNA biosynthesis (map00970); mineral absorption (map04978); protein digestion and absorption (map04974); O‐Antigen nucleotide sugar biosynthesis (map00541); amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism (map00520; Figure 6C).

Among the different gut microbiota, the ratio of Lactobacillus increased after HA. The evidence suggests that Lactobacillus can increase the expression of tight junction proteins (Yi et al., 2018). In addition, it can modulate intestinal inflammation (Wu et al., 2020). Therefore, under our conditions, we wondered whether the expression of tight junction proteins was increased and whether inflammation was alleviated in the intestine.

Heat acclimation enhances tight junction protein expression

Tight junction proteins are indispensable in maintaining the integrity of the intestinal barrier. To clarify the potential mechanism by which HA strengthens the intestinal barrier, we analysed the expression of tight junction proteins. The western blot results suggested that the expression of occludin (F = 29.953, p < 0.001, one‐way ANOVA, Figure 8A,B) and zonula occludin‐1 (ZO‐1, F = 24.59, p < 0.001, Welch one‐way ANOVA, Figure 8A,C) was markedly increased in the intestine of the HA group compared with that in the control group. We speculated that HA could enhance the expression of the tight junction proteins occludin and ZO‐1. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to check the ultrastructure of the intestine. In the control and HA groups, enterocytes showed normal cell structure, natural mitochondria, regularly aligned microvilli, and intact tight junctions between the adjoining cells. However, microvilli and intact tight junctions in the HA group were more complete and more stable (Figure 8D). The intestinal ultrastructure in the HS mice showed dehisced or loosened tight junctions and irregularly widened gaps between adjoining cells; destroyed or shorter microvilli, and damaged mitochondria characterized by swelling, vacuolar, and vague cristae with slightly dilated outer membranes. However, the ultrastructural changes in the intestine in the HA + HS group were distinctly attenuated and showed basically normal tight junctions and regularly aligned microvilli, in addition to slight mitochondrial swelling and occasional vacuoles (Figure 8D). In conclusion, these results clearly showed the protective effect of HA on intestinal injury during HS.

Heat acclimation modulates inflammation by suppressing NF‐κB signalling in mice with heat stroke

To explore the mechanism of HA on intestinal inflammation, the mRNA levels of several inflammatory cytokines (IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNFα) in the ileum were detected by Q‐PCR. TNF‐α (F = 78.063, p < 0.001, one‐way ANOVA, Figure 8A), IL‐6 (F = 42.3, p < 0.001, Welch one‐way ANOVA, Figure 9B), and IL‐1β (F = 121.223, p < 0.001, one‐way ANOVA, Figure 9C) were increased in the HS mice compared with the control group. The mRNA expression levels of inflammatory factors were significantly decreased in the HA + HS group compared with the HS group (p < 0.001, Bonferroni, Figure 9A–C). Intestinal NF‐κB signalling pathway activation was assessed. When activated, an inhibitor of κB (IκB)‐α is phosphorylated and then ubiquitinated. Therefore, the combined NF‐κB subunits are released, phosphorylated, and translocated to the nucleus to induce gene expression (e.g., TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β gene transcription). HS increased the phosphorylation of intestinal IkB‐α (F = 163.933, p < 0.001, one‐way ANOVA, Figure 9D,E) and NF‐κB p65 (F = 15.941, p < 0.001, one‐way ANOVA, Figure 9D,F). In addition, the levels of phosphorylated IkB‐α and phosphorylated NF‐κB p65 were attenuated significantly in the HA + HS group (p < 0.001, Bonferroni, Figure 9E,F). These results indicated the anti‐inflammatory effects of HA on HS.

DISCUSSION

Due to the lack of specific and effective drugs for HS, prevention is critical and of greater benefit than treatment after onset. HA is the most effective means to prevent HS, but the possible potential mechanism of HA is still unclear. In our study, HA significantly reduced liver and kidney damage caused by HS and systemic inflammatory cytokine expression, reducing damage to the intestinal barrier. We speculated that HA plays a protective role against HS by strengthening the integrity of the intestinal barrier. Alleviating intestinal injury and sustaining the intestinal barrier are conducive to the prevention of HS onset and its subsequent pathological cycle (Figure S3).

Heat exposure can induce an increase in skin blood flow to promote heat dissipation, followed by decreased visceral perfusion to maintain blood pressure. Persistently decreased visceral perfusion could contribute to multiple organ dysfunction, combined with heat cytotoxicity, coagulation, and SIRS under heatstroke. In this study, we detected increased serum levels of liver and kidney biomarkers and visible pathologic injuries in the liver and kidney in HS mice. Liver function and kidney function were alleviated by HA, as indicated by reduced serum levels of ALT, AST, ALP, BU, and CREA. Moreover, the pathological lesions in the liver and kidney induced by HS were also markedly attenuated by HA. These results indicate that HA could protect against multiorgan dysfunction in mice under HS.

One of the initial consequences of HS is injury to intestinal barrier integrity due to hyperthermia and hypoxia following blood repartition. This exposure can result in a ‘leaky’ gut, followed by the penetration and transfer of luminal antigens, endotoxins, and pathogenic bacteria. Injury to the intestinal epithelium caused by HS is due to cellular oxidative stress, disruption of intestinal integrity, and increased production of proinflammatory cytokines (Lian et al., 2020). Even when treated in intensive care units, some patients with HS may die from sepsis. Therefore, HS is called a sepsis‐like disease (Knapik & Epstein, 2019; Peiris et al., 2017). Bacteria and endotoxins originating in the gut are key exacerbating factors for poor prognosis. We explored the potential mechanism by which HA enhances the intestinal barrier.

The gut microbiota is an important organ acquired by the host because it has some physiological functions that the host does not have (Ross et al., 2019). Focusing on community composition analysis of the gut microbiota, we explored the potential mechanism by which HA enhances the intestinal barrier. No significant differences were observed in alpha diversity indices in HA under our experimental conditions. NMDS based on Bray‐Curtis showed that the HA group was clearly separated from the control group. HA remodels the gut microbiota.

There was a study that reported that, in summer (23 to 34°C, relative humidity 65% to 89%), supplementation with avopacin, flavomycin, bacitricin, zinc, or probiotics is beneficial for physiological states, including thermoregulation (Abdel‐Samee, 1995). Furthermore, it has been found that supplementation with 1 g/kg lactobacillus culture in the diet improves growth performance more than supplementation with 50 mg/kg oxytetracycline in chicks exposed to heat stress (36 ± 1°C, 3 h per day; Zulkifli et al., 2000). A mixture of two lactobacillus strains improved the gut microbiota count in the caecal contents of broilers by increasing the beneficial gut microbiota and decreasing the E. coli population at high temperatures (Mohammad et al., 2016). These studies suggest that some gut microbiota may be potential candidates for alleviating heat stress. Under normal conditions, intestinal microecology is in a state of balance, and beneficial gut microbiota, such as bifidobacterium and lactobacillus, will adhere to the surface of intestinal epithelial cells or distribute in the mucous layer, forming a membrane barrier and inhibiting the adhesion and colonization of pathogenic bacteria (Wu et al., 2020b). Lactobacillus in all parts of the intestinal tract can inhibit the growth and reproduction of pathogenic microorganisms through biological antagonism. Studies have shown that lactobacillus can enhance the intestinal mucosal barrier and can be used in the treatment and prevention of enteritis (Huang et al., 2022). Therefore, HA enhances the intestinal barrier by reshaping gut microbiota and increasing the proportion of lactobacillus, thus stabilizing the intestinal barrier in high temperature. In order to verify the effectiveness of lactobacillus, we gavaged lactobacilli to control group for 7 days (Figure S3A). Subsequently, in the HS, it was found that the heat tolerance of mice in the lactobacilli intervention group increased (Figure S3B). At the same time, the damage to the intestinal tract caused by heat is reduced, and the release of inflammatory factors is also decreased (Figure S3C–F). Therefore, it indicated that HA could improve heat tolerance and reduce intestinal damage caused by heat temperature by increasing the lactobacilli proportion in the gut microbiota. Moreover, microvilli and intact tight junctions in the HA group were complete and more stable, as observed by TEM. We speculated that HA plays a protective role against HS by increasing probiotic levels. Probiotics can increase tight junction protein expression. In addition, Lactobacilli can also modulate intestinal inflammation (Wu et al., 2020). Translocation of xenobiotics and bacterial products evokes an inflammatory response following intestinal barrier damage with HS, which results in exacerbated intestinal barrier dysfunction (Zhang et al., 2020). The anti‐inflammatory properties of Lactobacilli have been studied in chronic inflammatory disorders, such as inflammatory bowel diseases (Ganji‐Arjenaki & Rafieian‐Kopaei, 2018). In our study, HA blocked the production of proinflammatory cytokines by reducing the levels of phosphorylated IkB‐α and NF‐κB p65. This might be another potential mechanism by which HA protects against HS‐induced intestinal barrier injury mediated by the gut microbiota.

Gut microbiota metabolites can directly or indirectly affect the physiological functions of the host (Makki et al., 2018). An increasing number of studies have revealed that most of the physiological effects of the gut microbiota in the host are related to microbial metabolites because the metabolites produced by these microbes directly affect physiological processes (Lavelle & Sokol, 2020; Schoeler & Caesar, 2019). In our study, deoxycholic acid was negatively correlated with Lactobacilli. The metabolism of bile acids is closely related to the gut microbiota. The key enzyme that catalysed the conversion of bound bile acids to free bile acids was bile salt hydrolase. Bile salt lyase can be produced by a variety of probiotics (such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacilli). When probiotic levels are decreased, the metabolism of primary bile acids may be reduced, which could lead to the accumulation of bile acids in the intestine, injuring the intestinal barrier (Liu et al., 2020; van Best et al., 2020). We speculated that HA plays a protective role against HS by increasing probiotic levels to promote the metabolism of bile acids. Protecting the intestinal barrier from the negative effects of bile acids might be the third potential mechanism by which HA protects against HS. Compared with the control group, the HA group increased Firmicutes at the phylum level by 16S rRNA sequencing. Further analysis revealed that the genus‐level gut microbiota was lactobacilli, with significantly increased abundance after heat acclimation. This microbe can promote healthy SCFAs. Lactate and antimicrobials can prevent pathogens from interfering with health (Arieli et al., 2003). Also, it has been proven that reduced levels of lactobacilli can induce the exacerbation of colitis in mice. Lactobacilli can produce small molecular acids, such as lactic acid, acetic acid, and propionic acid, which reduce intestinal pH and create acidic conditions conducive to the growth of beneficial gut microbiota (Abdel‐Samee, 1995). Metabolite analysis of the dominant probiotics in the HA group found that 3‐(3‐Hydroxypheny1) propanoic acid, 1‐Palmitoylglyeerol, Hydroxyisocaproic acid, l‐Glutamate, l‐Phenylalanine, l‐Leucine, and Tyramine were the main up‐regulated metabolites. Glutamic acid is an important metabolite of Lactobacilli (Zulkifli et al., 2000). Glutamate is the precursor of the important neurotransmitter Y‐aminobutyric acid (GABA), which can be synthesized by both gut microbiota and host cells through decarboxylation of glutamate decarboxylase. The gene encoding glutamate decarboxylase is present in the gut microbiome, and luminal GABA from the microbiota is then taken up by the host small intestine for systemic use. Secondly, glutamate and glutamine are important metabolic energy sources in the intestinal mucosa. A study published in Cell has demonstrated that propionic acid, a SCFA, affects gut‐mediated immunity (Jahromi et al., 2016) by increasing the differentiation and function of intestinal regulatory T cells, thereby reducing the expression of inflammatory cytokines. However, changes in palmitoyl glycerol content alter the intestinal absorption function of a variety of nutrients (Wu et al., 2020b). The lactobacilli play an important role in proteolysis. Common metabolites of lactobacilli are phenylalanine and leucine, which are indispensable amino acids to ensure the integrity of the intestinal barrier (Huang et al., 2022).

Limitations

Although 16S rRNA gene sequencing technology is highly useful for microbiome studies, it provides low resolution at the species level and poor discriminatory power for some genera. Because of the limitations of the method itself, we could not determine whether the metabolites came from the gut microbiota or from the host. We obtained many results showing that HA protects the intestinal barrier through gut microbiota, and there was a very strong correlation between them. Although it has been demonstrated that HA can increase heat tolerance by increasing lactobacilli proportion, the changes in the metabolites associated with lactobacilli have only been analysed as correlations between them. Therefore, the focus of this manuscript was to explore the changes in the proportion of gut microbiota caused by HA, which plays an important role in protecting organs and increasing heat tolerance. In future studies, we will also focus on the influence of metabolites in the gut microbiota on host heat tolerance.

Conclusion

Our study indicated that HA could improve the adaptive response to heat. HA also significantly reduces the multiple‐organ injury caused by HS by strengthening the integrity of the intestinal barrier. HA significantly induced changes in the gut microbiota by increasing the ratio of probiotics (Lactobacillus). In addition, Lactobacillus can be considered a candidate biomarker for HA diagnostics. HA increased the expression of tight junction proteins (occludin and ZO‐1) to strengthen the integrity of the intestinal barrier. HA could also alleviate inflammation by suppressing NF‐κB signalling in the gut of mice undergoing HS (Figure S4).
