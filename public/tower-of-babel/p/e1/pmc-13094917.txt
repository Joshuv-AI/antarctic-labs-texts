Nursing students’ views and preferences regarding the Clinical Learning Environment in Morocco: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Khadija Saka, Mohamed-Yassine Amarouch, Nadia Mountaj, Oussama Rharib, Abderazzak Mouiha, Ayoub Amssayef, Jaouad El-Hilaly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:A discrepancy between the clinical environment experienced by student nurses and the environment that mirrors their anticipated preferences is likely to diminish their satisfaction. This study aimed to explore the student nurses’ perceptions in clinical settings and investigate the interplay between the sociodemographic variables and the dimensions of the Clinical Learning Environment Inventory (CLEI).MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional comparative survey design was used. Data collection was carried out using the Moroccan-validated version of the CLEI. Nursing students completed the expected form of the CLEI before their clinical placement and the actual form at its conclusion.RESULTS:The preferred form demonstrated a marked superiority in all domains, revealing significantly higher scores than the actual form. The highest mean score for both actual and preferred clinical learning environments was for the subscale Involvement. The analysis showed no statistically significant differences based on gender, educational level, or clinical placement setting. Personalization emerged as the sole statistically significant (P = 0.02, η2 = 0.03) disparity observed between the training institutes. Nurse branches exhibited significant disparities in satisfaction (P = 0.03, η2 = 0.04). The findings indicate a strong correlation (r = 0.68) between student satisfaction and personalization, involvement, and individualization.CONCLUSION:The results of this study revealed a significant gap between the preferred and actual clinical learning environment, and suggest that students desire a more positive environment, with more personalization, individualization, and involvement, to improve their satisfaction.

Introduction

The clinical learning environment enables nursing students to apply and integrate theoretical knowledge, skills, and professional activities. This process occurs under the guidance of mentors, clinical staff, and nurse educators.[1] In a global context, nursing is an experiential learning discipline based on practical training.[2]

In the nursing curriculum, the clinical learning environment includes hospitals, communal health centers, material resources, and equipment necessary for learning.[34] These clinical placements facilitate the acquisition of the disciplinary competencies required for the nursing profession.[5] In a clinical environment, students are involved in real learning, where they practice the psychomotor, cognitive, and affective skills necessary to become a competent and effective nurse practitioner.[6]

A positive clinical learning environment is characterized by effective communication between students and nurse educators and a variety of learning situations.[7] Studies reported that a supportive learning environment can enhance positive emotions and mitigate negative ones, like anxiety and feelings of hopelessness.[8] These elements may significantly impact the professional development of students.[9]

The efficacy of clinical learning is contingent on other factors, such as the quality of supervision, the accessibility of resources, and the translation of theoretical knowledge into practical application.[1011] Clinical educators play a pivotal role in nursing education through exerting a significant influence on the pedagogical practices and outcomes inside clinical settings.[12]

The social and psychological context is an important factor in the clinical learning environment, governed by communication, interpersonal relationships, and support between students, mentors, and clinical staff.[13] This factor has a significant impact on students’ learning behaviors and the development of clinical nursing skills.[8] The assessment of the quality of the clinical learning environment is of paramount importance in ensuring the clinical expertise of future nursing specialists, which, by extension, contributes to improving patient well-being.[14] Various instruments have been developed to assess students’ overall perceptions of clinical learning environments. These instruments prioritize the quality of staff–student relationships, the reception and integration of students into clinical settings, the educator’s role, and the availability and variety of nursing tasks in clinical placements.[15] In 2001, Chan developed the Clinical Learning Environment Inventory (CLEI).[1617] Accordingly, they postulate that the clinical learning environment is a multidimensional entity with a complex social background, a concept rooted in Moos’s social environment theory. This theory is notable for considering both the objective characteristics of the environment and the alignment between students’ preferences and the environment itself. The CLEI is among the most frequently employed assessment instruments for gauging students’ perceptions of their clinical learning environment.[1516] Numerous authors have widely noted its extensive use and translation into multiple languages.[141819] Indeed, researchers internationally have leveraged the CLEI to investigate nursing students’ perceptions of and satisfaction with their clinical learning environments in diverse countries.[20]

In Morocco, the most recent reform of nursing education took place in 2013 and emphasized public health among the main objectives of nursing education. The three-year undergraduate program is divided into six semesters. The program integrates theoretical and practical coursework, with a minimum requirement of 2310 h of study. Clinical practicums, constituting over half of the program, are conducted in approved settings. Subsequently, the focus has shifted to the assessment and empowerment of nursing competencies.[12] However, our understanding of Moroccan nursing students’ clinical learning experiences remains limited.[1221] This lack of consistent learning opportunities across regions and institutions creates challenges and results in significant variations in student satisfaction. There is a clear need for a contemporary evaluation of nursing students’ perceptions of the learning environment in nursing education in Morocco.[22]

The extant literature reveals a mismatch between the learning environments considered ideal by nursing students and the actual clinical placements. Negative clinical learning environments can impact students’ nursing practice, professional identity, and satisfaction with the nursing profession.[2324]

Therefore, a systematic assessment of the quality of clinical learning environments in nursing education is required, using a validated and reliable instrument.[25] To this end, the CLEI instrument, a widely used tool to assess the perceived quality of healthcare education,[16] was employed to examine the students’ perceptions of their clinical learning environment. The importance of an operative and helpful clinical learning environment for students’ achievement of learning goals and development of the skills necessary to provide safe, quality care has been well-documented.[2627] The CLEI ensures learners’ actual and preferred understanding of clinical learning environments, leading to improved academic success through constructive learning experiences that enhance their skills and self-confidence.[2829]

The objective of this research study was threefold: (1) To assess the perceptions of nursing students of their preferred and actual clinical learning environment placements, using a previously validated CLEI form in the Moroccan context,[30] (2) to investigate gaps between the two CLEI forms, and (3) to explore the relationship between students’ characteristics and the CLEI subscales. Therefore, the following hypotheses were tested: (1) nursing students had positive perceptions of the CLE; (2) there were differences among students’ perceptions based on gender, student year, branch, institute, and clinical placement.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a descriptive and analytical cross-sectional study among nursing students at three public health nursing education institutes in Morocco.

Study participants and sampling

Participants were selected using a convenience sampling method. Eligible second- and third-year students who met the specified criteria and were willing to participate in the 2023–2024 academic year were identified. The study included students enrolled in the nursing program, encompassing a variety of branches.

Inclusion criteria: All students were enrolled in the Nursing program and undertaking clinical placement in the fall period of 2023–2024. The study included second- and third-year nursing students who consented to participate between November and December 2023 and had recently been involved in a clinical practice course in a hospital ward or primary healthcare setting.

Exclusion criteria: First-year nursing students were excluded because they lacked prior clinical experience. Participants who declined to participate were also excluded from the study.

Data collection and measurement

Instrument

The instrument included the sociodemographic variables and the CLEI scale, which consists of six multiitem subscales. The CLEI scale has been rigorously evaluated and validated in numerous settings,[2023242531] including within the Moroccan context.[30] The instrument demonstrated strong reliability and validity across diverse contexts, including ours. In this work, the CLEI scale exhibited good internal consistency for the actual (α = 0.86) and preferred (α = 0.75) forms. The scale consists of 36 items organized into six dimensions: Personalization, Student Involvement, Task Orientation, Innovation, Individualization, and Satisfaction.

Each item on the CLEI is scored from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree” (strongly disagree = 1, disagree = 2, agree = 3, strongly agree = 4). A four-point Likert scale was selected over a five-point scale with a neutral midpoint to minimize central tendency bias and encourage students to articulate a clear orientation. The absence of forced neutrality has been demonstrated to enhance the discernment of responses and mitigate the risk of divergent interpretations associated with an ambiguous neutral option. This choice ensures methodological consistency with existing literature.[161732] This approach may prove frustrating to respondents who lack a definitive stance on the subject, yet it is deemed optimal for the study’s objectives.

Student’s Satisfaction Scale probes the relationships between student results and the clinical learning environment; it is an outcome assessment of the student’s satisfaction level. The remaining five subscales are: (a) Individualization: It measures the degree to which students of varying abilities or interests are treated on an individual basis or permitted to make individual decisions; (b) Innovation: It examines the degree to which the clinical instructor plans new, interesting, and productive experiences; (c) Involvement: It explores the degree to which students actively engage in clinical activities; (d) Personalization: It scrutinizes the importance of opportunities for students to individually interact with their clinical instructor and on tending to student welfare; and (e) Task Orientation: It considers the degree to which clinical tasks are clear and well-organized.

To ascertain the perception of the clinical environment, the actual scale was employed, whereas the preferred scale was devised to gauge perceptions of an ideal or preferred clinical environment, with an emphasis on objectives and values. Despite the identical wording in both scales, the instructions for responding differ.

Data collection tools and technique

Before data collection, the researchers provided the students with an overview of the study, its objectives, benefits, and procedures. Students who consented to participate were invited to complete the preferred version of the CLEI in paper format at their place of training before the commencement of the scheduled community or hospital placement. Toward the end of December, the actual form was completed to collect data about the “actual” CLE. They also responded to demographic and educational questions. Students completed the survey in approximately 10–15 min.

The data collection process was conducted in the fall semester of the 2023–2024 academic year, spanning November and December 2023, across three Moroccan nursing education institutions. The data were collected by doctoral students who are also instructors, with assistance provided by their colleagues working at the respective surveyed nursing institutes.

Data analysis

The statistical analyses of the study results were conducted utilizing JASP (Version 0.18.3, Amsterdam, North Holland, The Netherlands), Psychological Methods Group, University of Amsterdam, Amsterdam, North Holland, The Netherlands. The reliability of the instrument was estimated using Cronbach’s α coefficient. Descriptive statistical methods were employed to summarize the students’ characteristics and the CLEI subscales. Before opting for the parametric tests, normality was checked using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots.[333435] Student’s t-test and Analysis of Variance test were used to compare students’ educational and sociodemographic characteristics and the CLEI subscales. Pearson’s coefficient of correlation (r) and the multiple linear regression model were used to examine the possible relationships between the two forms of CLEI.

The CLEI Satisfaction preferred scale was utilized as a metric to evaluate outcomes.

Ethical considerations

Before collecting the data, the necessary study permission was secured from the heads of the surveyed nursing institutes (Approval No. 2183/2023). Each survey included a cover letter and an information sheet that detailed the study’s rationale. Verbal information about the survey was provided to the students eligible for inclusion in the study before the distribution of the scales. Subsequently, the researchers obtained consent from those students wishing to participate in the study.

Results

Demographic profile

A total of 263 students completed the actual version of the survey, while 258 completed the preferred version. Reliability for each CLEI scale was assessed using Cronbach’s α. The later coefficients ranged from 0.61 to 0.81 for the Actual CLEI and from 0.70 to 0.87 for the preferred one.

The nursing students in this study ranged in age from 19 to 24 years, with a mean age of 20.75 (Actual group: 20.75 ± 1.22 years; Preferred group: 20.72 ± 1.21 years). This young age is consistent with the Moroccan nursing education program’s admission criteria, which require applicants to hold a Bachelor’s degree obtained within the preceding 2 years.

Furthermore, Moroccan participants comprised 97.3% of the sample at both time points, and females were the majority in both samples (68.1% vs. 67.8%). All five nursing specializations responded to the scales, with the polyvalent nurse specialization representing the majority at 51%. This prevalence is likely due to this specialization being offered by all three institutes in the Fez-Meknes region, unlike the other specializations, which have limited enrollment due to their distinct curricula.

Nursing branches were surveyed in hospitals (74.1% vs. 75.2%) and primary healthcare settings (25.9% vs. 24.8%) during the third (56.3% vs. 46.1%) and fifth semesters (43.7% vs. 53.9%). In both samples, the polyvalent nurse specialization (≥50%) was the most prevalent, and most participants were affiliated with hospitals, with very similar percentages (74.1% vs. 75.2%), which showed both groups were not significantly different [Table 1].

A statistically significant discrepancy was identified in the personalization subscale of the CLEI between the training institutes. Specifically, Taza Institute students reported a more favorable perception of this dimension (2.48 ± 0.63, P = 0.019, η2 = 0.03) compared to students from other institutes.

Additionally, Community and Family Health Nurse students reported the highest score with the satisfaction dimension of the CLEI scale (3.00 ± 0.42), while NN students reported the lowest (2.49 ± 0.56, P = 0.032, η2 = 0.04). This finding suggests a substantial discrepancy in student satisfaction across the learning branches [Table 6].

Discussion

This study aimed to describe, analyze, and compare undergraduate nursing students’ perceptions of their clinical learning environment in Morocco’s three higher institutes of nursing and health techniques.

The total CLEI scores for the main scales were low across the study, indicating that the students’ perceptions of their actual CLE were negative across different study branches. The same findings were reported in other studies.[39363738] During clinical placements, students indicated a need for respect, support, and recognition from educators and clinicians. This observation is supported by the high mean score for personalization, which indicates a general need among nursing students for increased support, respect, and recognition from educators and clinicians.

A conducive factor to this negative perception, as reported by Rajeswaran (2017),[39] is the difference between classroom instruction and the supervision student nurses receive in clinical settings. This theory-practice gap creates conflict and negatively impacts their clinical learning experiences.

These findings are in contrast to other studies in Spain and Singapore that demonstrated greater satisfaction among student nurses.[2040] The present results may reflect disparities in resources, infrastructure, and the prevailing context.

Recently, Guejdad et al. indicated student nurses’ concerns regarding insufficient pedagogical support and guidance from professors, nurses, and medical staff within clinical placement organizations.[36] Furthermore, their research demonstrates that inadequate communication between the academic institution and the clinical environment undermines the effectiveness of clinical learning and presents significant challenges for student nurses in Morocco.[36] This study supports the statistically significant gap between the actual and preferred learning environments [Table 3]. Indeed, the CLEI analysis revealed marked differences in mean scores across all six domains when comparing students’ actual experiences with their preferred conditions. The data demonstrate statistically higher scores in all subscales under preferred conditions compared to actual experiences, indicating that students’ expectations for the clinical environment were not fulfilled. This suggests that they sought a more supportive and enriched educational setting. Such results align with prior research on nursing students’ perceptions of differences between actual and preferred clinical learning environments.[39232740414243]

The most substantial differences were observed in individualization, personalization, and student involvement, while the most negligible disparities were identified in task orientation and innovation. This outcome underscores the necessity for nursing students to acknowledge individualization, personalization, and students’ involvement as the most substantial subscales within the clinical learning environment. Consequently, the student’s preference for a more positive clinical learning environment, including heightened levels of individualization, innovation in teaching and learning strategies, student participation, personalization, enhanced teaching quality, and interaction between students, supervisors, and clinicians, is recommended.

Chan recommended the utilization of the student satisfaction scale as an outcome measure of students’ perceptions regarding the social climate of the clinical placement.[17] The relationship between “Student Satisfaction” and all other CLEI scales was estimated using the Pearson correlation coefficient (r). The results indicate significant associations between satisfaction and the five CLEI subscales. These data indicate strong associations between student perceptions of all CLEI scales and their satisfaction with their clinical placement site. Of note, similar results have been reported by several studies.[3172840]

A multiple regression analysis was conducted to investigate the influence of different environmental variables on student satisfaction. The findings indicate that personalization, student involvement, and individualization collectively account for a substantial portion of the variance in student satisfaction, amounting to 46% of the total variance. This finding suggests that students’ perception of innovation during clinical practice is closely tied to their level of satisfaction. This finding is consistent with those of previous studies conducted in Hong Kong and Australia.[294144] Consequently, the study proposes that new educational initiatives focused on personalization, involvement, and individualization should be prioritized by educators and clinical placement supervisors to optimize student learning within the clinical environment.

The present study revealed no statistically significant differences concerning gender, educational level, and current clinical placement setting. In contrast, individualization emerged as the only statistically significant disparity among the training institutes located in Fez, Meknes, and Taza. In addition, the significant influence of the branch of student nurses has been observed to exhibit substantial disparities in satisfaction. The fact that nursing students from the different training branches have different perceptions of satisfaction in the Moroccan context can be explained by the categories of basic and advanced skills and competencies that students are expected to acquire. Indeed, students obtain skills specific to their future professional field.

The findings indicate a strong correlation between student satisfaction and personalization, involvement, and individualization. To our knowledge, no similar results have been previously reported.

This is the first research on the evaluation of clinical placements by student nurses in Morocco, using CLEI, a validated and reliable tool. The results could be useful to health institutions such as hospitals and communal health centers involved in clinical training in the Fez-Meknes region, to improve the clinical experience and skills acquisition of student nurses.

Limitations

The study was conducted in a single region of Morocco with a sample that did not include all students in the neighborhoods. This limits its generalizability to all subpopulations in the country. In addition, participants were enrolled in different clinical settings located in three different cities, which varied considerably in terms of infrastructure and quality of care. Despite these limitations, our findings provide important information about the nursing students’ experiences during their clinical placements in Morocco.

Conclusion

The study demonstrated that students desire a more positive environment, with greater personalization, individualization, and involvement, to improve their satisfaction. The existing conditions are seen as insufficient regarding creativity, activity, and initiative to result in productive clinical experiences. The study suggested that educational leaders and teachers should focus on reorganizing clinical teaching and providing more opportunities for students to innovate in a controlled setting. Reducing the number of trainees at internship sites may facilitate better learning experiences and the acquisition of clinical skills.
