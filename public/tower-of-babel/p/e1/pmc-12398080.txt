Analysing YouTube as a health resource: quality and reliability of videos on pediatric appendicitis
Authors: Nurcan Çoşkun, Emre Demir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to assess the content, quality, and reliability of YouTube videos related to appendicitis in children. It analyzes the extent to which these videos provide accurate information to parents and healthcare professionals and evaluates their potential contribution to improving the quality of medical education.MethodsThe keywords “appendicitis” and “appendectomy”, along with “pediatrics”, “child”, and “children”, were searched both individually and in all possible combinations. Videos were ranked according to the number of views and the first 60 videos with the highest number of views were included in the analysis. The videos were evaluated by two independent pediatric surgeons using the Global Quality Scale (GQS), Modified DISCERN (mDISCERN) and American Medical Association (JAMA) scales. Video Power Index (VPI) values were calculated for each video to assess popularity. Inter-rater agreement was assessed with intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), group comparisons with Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests, and correlations with Spearman’s coefficient.ResultsOf the analyzed videos, 46 (76.7%) were informational, and 14 (23.3%) were surgical. Videos uploaded by hospitals and doctors scored higher on mDISCERN, GQS, and JAMA scales than those by individuals or private channels (P < 0.001). Animation-containing videos had higher views, likes, and VPI scores than non-animated ones (P = 0.015, P = 0.003, P = 0.006). Surgical videos had significantly higher JAMA scores (P = 0.024) and longer durations (P = 0.021). No significant correlation was observed between quality scores and engagement metrics such as views or likes (P > 0.05).ConclusionsThis study demonstrated that YouTube videos on pediatric appendicitis are generally of low-to-moderate quality, with no significant correlation between content quality and popularity metrics such as view count or likes. Videos by hospitals and doctors were more reliable, while visual elements like animations increased engagement but not content quality. Findings highlight the need for scientifically accurate and informative content by healthcare professionals to better educate patients, families, and medical students.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07869-2.Keywords: Childhood appendicitis, YouTube™, Video analysis, Quality, Reliability

Introduction

Appendicitis in children is one of the most common causes of acute abdomen [1–4]. Appendectomy performed under general anesthesia is the standard treatment method [5, 6]. The prevalence of childhood appendicitis is approximately 1–2%, and early diagnosis and treatment are crucial for preventing serious complications [7].

In recent years, social media platforms have become frequently used sources for accessing medical information [8]. Among these platforms, YouTube has emerged as a prominent medium where patients and healthcare professionals seek information regarding surgical procedures, treatments, and complications [8, 9]. Unlike Instagram and Facebook, which emphasize visual content and user interaction, YouTube is particularly preferred in the health domain for offering longer, searchable, and educational video content [9, 10]. However, most medical content shared on YouTube is user-generated and not subject to academic peer review [10, 11]. This raises concerns about the accuracy and reliability of the information presented [12, 13]. Misinformation can hinder caregivers of pediatric patients from making informed decisions, potentially leading to delayed treatment, increased anxiety, or misinterpretation of clinical findings. This study aims to contribute to ongoing efforts to combat online medical misinformation, which is increasingly recognized as a global issue, particularly in the context of pediatric healthcare [9, 10].

Although various studies have investigated YouTube content related to adult diseases, no research has specifically focused on childhood appendicitis. This represents a significant gap, as decision-making for pediatric patients is typically driven by their parents, who may be directly influenced by online content. Based on existing evidence, the hypothesis of this study is that the scientific quality of YouTube videos on childhood appendicitis is inadequate. Therefore, the aim of this study is to evaluate the quality, reliability, and educational value of the most viewed videos using validated assessment tools and independent scorers. The main research question is: Do the most viewed YouTube videos on childhood appendicitis provide scientifically accurate and reliable information for patients and their caregivers?

Methods

Study design and research process

This was a cross-sectional observational study designed to assess the quality, reliability, and popularity of YouTube videos related to pediatric appendicitis. The research process consisted of the following stages: Search and Data Retrieval, Video Screening and Selection (Inclusion/Exclusion), Evaluation by Independent Raters, Calculation of Popularity Metrics, and Statistical Analysis.

Data collection and sampling strategy

The data analyzed in this study were obtained from YouTube on October 1, 2024. Searches were conducted using the keywords “appendicitis” and “appendectomy”, along with “pediatrics”, “child”, and “children”, both individually and in all possible combinations. The search was performed in incognito mode to avoid personalization bias, and results were sorted by view count to reflect audience engagement. A non-probabilistic purposive sampling strategy was employed to capture the most widely viewed and potentially influential content.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The top 60 most-viewed videos were selected based on the following inclusion criteria: videos had to be in English, directly related to pediatric appendicitis or appendectomy, contain audio and/or subtitles, and have a duration between 1 and 20 min. Videos were excluded if they were duplicates, non-medical or promotional in nature, or animations lacking verbal or textual explanation.

Evaluation tools and validity

The methodological evaluation of the videos included in the study was independently conducted by two pediatric surgeons who are experts in the field. To assess the quality, reliability, and transparency of the selected videos, three standardized and validated tools, widely used in health informatics and medical content analysis research, were employed:

Global quality scale-GQS (overall quality assessment)

The GQS, developed by Bernard et al. [14], was used to assess the general educational quality and structural integrity of the videos. This 5-point scale (1 = poor, 5 = excellent) provides a holistic evaluation covering aspects such as the flow of the content, the logical sequence in the presentation of information, the professionalism of audiovisual materials, and the overall effectiveness of the video as a learning tool for viewers.

Modified DISCERN-mDISCERN (reliability of medical information)

To analyze the reliability and evidence-based nature of the medical information in the videos, the mDISCERN tool—an adaptation of the original DISCERN instrument developed by Charnock et al. and later modified for video content by Singh et al. (2012)—was used. This tool focuses on five core reliability criteria, each rated from 0 to 5: (i) whether the objectives of the video are clearly stated, (ii) the degree to which those objectives are achieved, (iii) whether the information presented is relevant and up to date, (iv) whether the sources of the information are clearly cited, and (v) whether the content is balanced and unbiased. mDISCERN is widely used to objectively measure the scientific rigor of medical content [15, 16].

JAMA benchmark criteria (transparency and ethical standards)

To evaluate the transparency of the information source and compliance with publishing ethics, the JAMA benchmark criteria proposed by Silberg et al. (1997) were applied. JAMA uses a binary (yes/no, 1/0) scoring system to assess whether four core standards are met: authorship (clear identification of the responsible author or institution), attribution (provision of sources and references for the information presented), disclosure (transparent declaration of potential conflicts of interest and funding sources), and currency (indication of the publication or last review date of the content). The presence of each criterion is awarded one point, yielding a maximum transparency score of 4 [17].

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 22.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA; licensed to Hitit University). Categorical variables were reported as frequencies (n) and percentages (%), while continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) because the data were not normally distributed. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, along with graphical methods such as Q–Q plots and histograms, to guide the selection of appropriate statistical tests. Inter-rater agreement was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), categorized as poor (< 0.5), moderate (0.5–0.75), good (0.75–0.9), or excellent (> 0.9). Due to non-parametric assumptions, comparisons between two independent groups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test, and comparisons among more than two groups used the Kruskal–Wallis test. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons following significant Kruskal–Wallis tests were carried out using the Dunn–Bonferroni method. Correlations between numerical variables were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 60 videos related to pediatric appendicitis were analyzed. Table 1 presents the descriptive characteristics and evaluation scores. Among these, 46 (76.7%) were informational, while 14 (23.3%) focused on surgical content. Regarding video sources, 12 (20%) were uploaded by hospitals, 23 (38.3%) by physicians, 17 (28.3%) by private YouTube channels, and 8 (13.3%) by individuals. Only 5 videos (8.3%) used animation. Among the analyzed videos, 35 (58.3%) discussed symptoms, 10 (16.7%) mentioned examination findings, and 9 (15%) addressed acute or complicated cases. Diagnosis was covered in 9 videos (15%), surgical procedures in 23 (38.3%), and postoperative processes in 8 (13.3%). Complications were discussed in only 3 videos (5%). Of the 60 videos, only 2 comprehensively covered all evaluated topics: symptoms, examination findings, acute or complicated cases, diagnosis, surgical procedures, postoperative processes, and complications.

The median video duration was 286 s (IQR 189.7–607), and the median time since upload was 2123 days (IQR 1287–3094). The median number of views was 19,849 (IQR 8285–56,297), and the median number of likes was 200 (IQR 56–376). The median Video Power Index (VPI) score was 11.5 (IQR 4.3–34.4).

The median mDISCERN score was 3 (IQR 2–3), the median GQS score was 3 (IQR 2–4), and the median JAMA score was 3 (IQR 1–3). Interrater agreement between the two independent pediatric surgery specialists, assessed via the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), was excellent for all scales: mDISCERN (ICC: 0.901), GQS (ICC: 0.907), and JAMA (ICC: 0.932) (P < 0.001 for all).

According to GQS scores, 23 videos (38.3%) were of low quality, 21 (35%) were of moderate quality, and 16 (26.7%) were of high quality. Four videos received a perfect mDISCERN score of 5, six videos achieved a GQS score of 5, and three videos attained a JAMA score of 4. Only two videos scored the maximum on all three scales, while six videos received the minimum score of 1 on all scales.

The most-viewed video, uploaded in 2022, had 4,771,069 views (VPI: 4671.9) and received mDISCERN, GQS, and JAMA scores of 3, 5, and 3, respectively. The second most-viewed video, uploaded in 2020, had 3,552,146 views (VPI: 2237.5) and scored 2, 2, and 1, respectively. The third most-viewed video, uploaded in 2017, had 1,228,523 views (VPI: 471.3) with scores of 3, 2, and 2. The fourth most-viewed video, from 2018, had 217,058 views (VPI: 85.4) and received scores of 3, 3, and 3. The fifth most-viewed video had 163,453 views (VPI: 97.9) and received the minimum score of 1 on all scales.

Videos focusing on surgery had higher median mDISCERN and GQS scores compared to informational videos, although the differences were not statistically significant (P = 0.117 and P = 0.569, respectively; Fig. 1). However, surgical videos had significantly higher JAMA scores than informational ones (P = 0.024, Fig. 1). There were no statistically significant differences between surgical and informational videos in terms of views, likes, and VPI scores (P = 0.972, P = 0.848, and P = 0.286, respectively). However, surgical videos had significantly longer durations (P = 0.021).

There were no statistically significant differences in mDISCERN, GQS, and JAMA scores between videos with and without animations (P = 0.374, P = 0.434, and P = 0.418, respectively). However, animated videos had significantly higher median view counts (animated: 56,227 [IQR 42,587–1,884,602]; non-animated: 16,220 [IQR 8051–52,046]), likes (animated: 734 [IQR 386–8259]; non-animated: 146 [IQR 50–326]), and VPI scores (animated: 35.8 [IQR 33.5–1161]; non-animated: 9.8 [IQR 4–30.3]) compared to non-animated videos (P = 0.015, P = 0.003, and P = 0.006, respectively).

Significant differences were observed in mDISCERN, GQS, and JAMA scores across video publishers (P < 0.001 for all). Post-hoc analyses revealed that videos published by hospitals and physicians had significantly higher mDISCERN scores than those uploaded by individuals (P < 0.001 for both; Fig. 2). Additionally, physician-uploaded videos had significantly higher mDISCERN scores than those from private YouTube channels (P = 0.045, Fig. 2). Similarly, GQS scores were significantly higher in videos published by hospitals, physicians, and private channels compared to those by individuals (P < 0.001, P < 0.001, and P = 0.033, respectively; Fig. 2). JAMA scores were significantly higher in hospital- and physician-uploaded videos than in those by individuals (P < 0.001 for both) and private channels (P = 0.001 and P = 0.004, respectively; Fig. 2). No significant differences were found among publishers regarding views, likes, or VPI scores (P = 0.996, P = 0.921, and P = 0.438, respectively).

Correlation analyses revealed no significant relationships between mDISCERN, GQS, or JAMA scores and video duration, days since upload, views, likes, or VPI scores (P > 0.05, Table 2). Weak positive correlations were found between video duration and both likes (r = 0.354, P = 0.006) and VPI scores (r = 0.305, P = 0.018, Table 3). Additionally, weak to moderate negative correlations were observed between time since upload and both likes (r = − 0.291, P = 0.024) and VPI scores (r = − 0.511, P < 0.001, Table 3).

Discussion

This study revealed that although YouTube is widely used as an information source on pediatric appendicitis, the overall educational quality and reliability of its content remain insufficient. Notably, only two out of the 60 analyzed videos comprehensively addressed all key aspects of the condition (symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, postoperative care, and complications), and the quality scores based on the mDISCERN, GQS, and JAMA scales were generally low to moderate. The lack of alignment between the most-viewed videos and high-quality content highlights a disconnect between popularity and reliability. Videos uploaded by hospitals and physicians received significantly higher quality scores across all evaluation tools, whereas those uploaded by individuals frequently had the lowest scores. Furthermore, although animated videos significantly increased viewer engagement, they did not improve information quality. These findings underscore a critical deficiency of accurate, comprehensive, and reliable content on pediatric appendicitis within this widely accessed platform.

When evaluating the quality of appendicitis-related YouTube videos in the present study, the overall findings indicated a low to moderate level of quality, with a median mDISCERN score of 3 (2–3), a GQS median of 3 (2–4), and a JAMA median of 3 (1–3). These results are comparable to those of Toksoz and Duran [19], who reported median mDISCERN, GQS, and JAMA scores of 3 (1–5), 3 (1–5), and 3 (1–4), respectively, for videos on vesicoureteral reflux. However, the findings of Keten and Erkan [20] for undescended testes videos showed slightly lower mean scores (mDISCERN: 2.25 ± 1.2, GQS: 2.82 ± 1.1, JAMA: 1.4 ± 0.99), indicating relatively poorer quality. In contrast, Anadolulu et al. [21] reported a higher average JAMA score of 3.20 ± 1.07 (0–4) for bowel management videos, suggesting greater reliability in that specific content. Similarly, Zaliznyak et al. [22] found mDISCERN and GQS scores of 2.9 ± 0.7 and 2.9 ± 1.1, respectively, for newborn male circumcision videos, which closely align with the present study’s values. Collectively, these comparisons indicate that while the quality of medical video content varies across topics, the overall reliability and educational value remain moderate at best.

Overall, scale scores in this study were consistent with the literature. Only two videos received full marks on the mDISCERN, GQS, and JAMA scales, highlighting the scarcity of high-quality, reliable content. Conversely, six videos scored only one point across all scales, reflecting serious deficiencies in accuracy and completeness. Roberts et al. [23] reported similar results: nearly half of appendicitis and cholecystitis videos lacked educational value, and more than one-third contained misinformation. These findings underscore the importance of producing more accurate and comprehensive content to support public health literacy.

The analysis of view counts and the Video Power Index (VPI) revealed notable disparities in public interest across pediatric topics. In our study, appendicitis-related videos had a median view count of 19,849 (8285–56,297) and a median VPI of 11.5 (4.3–34.4), both significantly higher than those observed for other pediatric conditions. For instance, videos on undescended testis and vesicoureteral reflux had markedly lower median view counts (589 and 567, respectively) and negligible VPI values (median VPI: 0 for reflux), indicating limited public engagement [19, 20]. In contrast, newborn male circumcision videos showed a substantially higher median view count (39,247) and VPI (182.6 ± 328.3), even surpassing the popularity of appendicitis-related content [22]. These disparities may reflect differences in parental concern, cultural significance, and the perceived urgency or frequency of these conditions. Circumcision, for example, is a widely practiced and culturally significant procedure in many societies, potentially contributing to its higher visibility. Appendicitis, although less culturally driven, is an acute and common pediatric surgical emergency, which may explain its relatively elevated public interest. Conversely, conditions such as undescended testis or vesicoureteral reflux, often managed through long-term follow-up rather than urgent intervention, may attract less attention from general audiences.

Surgical videos were generally of moderate quality. In a study by Park et al. [24], 25% of laparoscopic appendectomy videos were rated as high quality, 64.3% as moderate, and 10.7% as low. In our study, based on GQS, 28.6% of surgical videos were low quality, 50% moderate, and 21.4% high, aligning with previous findings. Notably, most surgical videos focused on acute appendicitis; videos of perforated appendicitis were nearly absent, indicating a gap in surgical training content. That 76.7% of videos were classified as informational suggests YouTube serves as a valuable resource. However, only 3.3% of videos addressed the entire diagnostic and treatment process, indicating that most content is limited. While videos often focus on symptoms, critical topics such as diagnostic procedures, treatment options, postoperative care, and potential complications were largely overlooked. Thus, there is a need for more comprehensive, high-quality videos covering the full course of pediatric appendicitis. Surgical videos scored higher on the JAMA scale than informational videos, indicating better compliance with standard information criteria. However, the absence of significant differences in mDISCERN and GQS scores suggests comparable overall quality. Furthermore, there were no significant differences in view count, likes, or VPI scores, showing that content type alone does not determine viewer interest.

Animated videos had significantly higher views, likes, and VPI scores than non-animated ones, suggesting a positive impact on popularity. However, there were no significant differences in quality scores (mDISCERN, GQS, JAMA), implying that animation enhances engagement but not content quality. Improving the quality of animated videos may help deliver reliable information more effectively. Significant differences were observed in video quality by publisher type. Videos from hospitals and doctors scored significantly higher on all three quality scales than those from individuals or private channels. Notably, the two videos receiving full scores were published by a doctor and a hospital. However, popularity metrics did not differ significantly across publisher types. Among the six lowest-scoring videos, five were uploaded by individual users. This supports Roberts et al. [23], who found that healthcare professionals produce more reliable content. These results indicate that professionalism is linked to video quality, but not necessarily to popularity.

The most viewed video received full marks on the GQS but only moderate scores on mDISCERN and JAMA, suggesting high watchability but moderate reliability. In contrast, the second, third, and fifth most popular videos had low scores across all scales, indicating that popularity does not reflect quality. This observation is further supported by correlation analyses, which revealed no significant relationships between mDISCERN, GQS, or JAMA scores and popularity metrics such as view count, likes, or VPI. Even low-quality videos attracting large audiences highlights the risk of misinformation driven by misleading popularity indicators. A weak positive correlation between video duration and both likes and VPI was observed, suggesting that shorter videos may limit viewer engagement. Moreover, older videos tended to receive fewer likes and had lower VPI scores, indicating a decline in viewer interest over time. Collectively, these findings emphasize that popularity does not guarantee content quality and underscore the importance of promoting accurate, current, and comprehensive information from reliable sources. These results are consistent with those of Uğurlu and Ugurlu [25], who also reported that professionalism is associated with content quality but not with popularity.

Appendicitis is a common pediatric condition requiring urgent care [26]. YouTube is frequently used by parents and healthcare providers due to its accessibility and reach. However, no prior study has comprehensively evaluated the quality of YouTube videos on pediatric appendicitis. Our study addresses this gap, offering valuable insights for both caregivers and professionals. One limitation is the focus on YouTube alone, excluding other popular platforms like TikTok or Instagram. While YouTube is still a major hub for long-form medical content, future studies should expand to multiple platforms to reflect the broader digital landscape. Nonetheless, our findings highlight YouTube’s central role and the importance of quality control in widely accessed content.

Another limitation is the diverse needs of YouTube audiences, including parents, patients, and professionals. This study assessed content from a general perspective, without tailoring to specific groups. For instance, while professionals may seek detailed technical information, parents often prioritize clarity and reassurance. Future research should explore how different audiences engage with content and whether it meets their specific needs.

Additionally, since the analysis was limited to the top 60 most-viewed videos, the findings may not fully represent the overall quality landscape of all YouTube content. However, these videos are the most frequently watched and therefore likely to have the greatest influence on public understanding and health information access. Moreover, the search strategy relied on commonly used American English terminology. Although unlikely to significantly alter the results, the omission of British English terms such as ‘appendicectomy’ may have introduced a minor linguistic or regional bias in video selection, as YouTube’s search algorithm prioritizes relevance and user engagement over exact keyword matching.

Another limitation is the lack of direct user (patients, carers and non-surgical users) feedback. Prior studies emphasize how non-medical users often prefer visually simple content, which may sometimes be misleading [9]. Swire-Thompson and Lazer [10] highlighted the risks of health misinformation on social media, and Roberts et al. [23] noted that surgical videos can confuse non-professional viewers. These findings underline the need to present accurate content in ways that align with audience comprehension. Future studies should integrate user feedback through methods such as surveys, interviews, or usability tests to better understand how users perceive and interpret YouTube content.

Content quality was assessed using standardized tools such as GQS, mDISCERN, and JAMA, which are widely used in medical literature. These tools evaluate authorship, citations, and reliability, enabling indirect comparison with institutional sources [15, 16, 17]. Still, future studies could benefit from directly comparing YouTube videos with standardized educational materials prepared by medical associations or academic institutions to better identify content gaps and usability challenges.

Finally, two independent pediatric surgery experts evaluated the videos using objective tools. However, the perceptions of patients, parents, and general audiences—who are the primary users—were not assessed. Including their feedback in future studies would help determine whether video content aligns with their informational needs.

Conclusion

This study has revealed that the educational quality of YouTube videos related to pediatric appendicitis is generally inadequate and that there is no significant relationship between video popularity and content reliability. Notably, even the most-viewed videos often had low quality scores, posing a potential risk of misinformation for viewers. Our findings confirm that platform popularity metrics are not reliable indicators of content credibility, whereas videos produced by hospitals and healthcare professionals tend to offer higher quality and more comprehensive information. It was also observed that while animations are effective in increasing viewership, they do not, by themselves, improve content quality. These results highlight the responsibility of healthcare professionals to produce scientifically accurate, patient-centered, and engaging content that addresses all aspects of pediatric appendicitis. At the same time, it is critically important to raise awareness among viewers seeking health information online, encouraging them to question the source of the content and prioritize institutional and expert-based channels over popularity. Future research should aim to clarify existing information gaps by directly comparing YouTube content with standardized educational materials and should focus on user perceptions and the effectiveness of different content formats.
