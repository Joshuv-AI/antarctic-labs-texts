Unraveling the intertwined threads of insomnia and sarcopenia in cognitive and functional impairment in octogenarians
Authors: Elias Rocha de Azevedo Filho, Cristiane Koeche, Flavia Fagundes Bueno, Otávio de Toledo, Vicente Paulo Alves, Henrique Salmazo da Silva, Clayton Franco Moraes, Wladimir M Freitas, Alessandra M Campos-Staffico, Luiz Sergio Fernandes de Carvalho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlthough insomnia and sarcopenia may seem unrelated in the context of older adult health, recent evidence has revealed shared underlying pathophysiological pathways. However, the extent to which these two factors jointly impact crucial clinical outcomes still needs to be determined. This study aimed to elucidate the associations of insomnia and sarcopenia with cognitive dysfunction and functional impairment, shedding light on potential mechanistic connections.MethodsIn this observational study, we scrutinized data collected from 493 individuals aged ≥ 80 years between 2016 and 2018. Sarcopenia was assessed using the EWGSOP2 criteria, insomnia was assessed using the Insomnia Severity Index, functional status was assessed using the Instrumental Activity of Daily Living Scale, and cognition was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). The baseline assessments included blood lipid, IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, and CRP levels and DNA methylation.ResultsInsomnia alone was not associated with cognitive impairment or functional dependence. However, sarcopenia alone increased the odds of cognitive dysfunction by a factor of 2.6 (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.2–5.6, p = 0.011), although it did not impact functional impairment. When examined together, insomnia and sarcopenia were associated with 4.8-fold increased odds of cognitive impairment (95% CI 2.1–10.9, p < 0.001) and 7.1-fold increased odds of functional impairment (95% CI 1.7–29.0, p = 0.007) compared to those with neither condition. Lipid profiles and inflammatory marker levels did not differ, but sarcopenic individuals had significantly greater DNA methylation (p < 0.001). We observed a positive multiplicative interaction between insomnia and sarcopenia on continuous MoCA scores, with a statistically significant synergy index and consistent interaction metrics.ConclusionsInsomnia and sarcopenia synergistically increase the risk of cognitive dysfunction among long-lived older adults. Sarcopenia alone impacted cognition in individuals, which could be related to the increase in inflammation-independent DNA methylation observed among individuals with sarcopenia.Trial registrationClinical Trial Number. NCT02366104.Keywords: Insomnia, Sarcopenia, Long-lived elderly individuals, Cognitive dysfunction, Functional impairment

Introduction

Recent evidence has revealed shared underlying pathophysiological pathways between sleep disorders and loss of muscle mass (sarcopenia) in older adults [1]. Disruptions to the circadian clock accelerate age-related muscle decline in both human and animal studies [2]. Intriguingly, the circadian clock intricately regulates sleep patterns, muscle formation, and differentiation, all of which are influenced by aging [3, 4]. As age advances, both sleep regulation and muscle development tend to deteriorate, and insufficient sleep accelerates the aging process and potentially triggers skeletal muscle catabolism [4, 5]. This interplay sheds light on the association between sarcopenia and sleep disorders [6], with even a one-hour sleep reduction on average doubling the risk of sarcopenia [4].

Sleeping is essential for maintaining cognitive function [7]. Inadequate sleep harms the homeostasis of the whole body, leading to undesirable short- and long-term physiological consequences, including potential cognitive impairment [7, 8]. These changes result in damage to the structure and function of cells, potentially leading to inflammation, dysregulation in glucose metabolism and epigenetic changes [6, 8].

In adults aged > 80 years, both muscle loss and sleep deficiencies accelerate [2]. While sleep disorders and sarcopenia are closely related [9], their joint impact on cognition and functional abilities remains unclear. Likewise, biomarker levels are susceptible to changes due to continuous exposure to various environmental and biological agents, and they could mechanistically explain the impact of muscle loss and insomnia. Hence, this study aimed to elucidate the interplay between recurrent insomnia and sarcopenia in driving cognitive and functional impairment/dysfunction in adults aged > 80 years and to explore the potential underlying mechanisms involved.

Methods

Study design and participants

This multicenter, observational, analytical, cross-sectional, quantitative study analyzed data from the National Academic Cooperation Program (PROCAD), an initiative funded by the Brazilian Coordination for the Improvement of Higher Education Personnel (CAPES).

The study sample population comprised 700 adults aged ≥80 years and was obtained from the PROCAD electronic database. Participants were recruited from various settings, including family homes, long-term care facilities, and outpatient geriatric clinics in three Brazilian cities: Brasília (n = 196), Passo Fundo (n = 272), and Campinas (n = 232).

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Boards (IRBs) of the three participating institutions, ensuring adherence to research norms for human subjects defined by Brazil’s National Research Council. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants prior to participation. Specific study approval was granted by the following university IRBs: Universidade de Passo Fundo 2.097.27/2017, Universidade Católica de Brasília 1.290.368/2015, and Universidade Estadual de Campinas 3.061.534/2018.

Eligibility criteria and clinical outcomes

Patients were included if they had adequate vision, hearing, and cognitive capacity to participate in the study. Patients were excluded if they were unable to respond to questions due to conditions that impaired verbal communication.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was cognitive impairment, defined using prespecified Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) cutoffs [9, 10]. The secondary outcome was functional dependence, defined as the need for total or partial assistance in > 5 IADLs [10, 11].

Sociodemographic and clinical assessment

In-person interviews conducted by trained staff assessed sociodemographic and clinical variables using standardized questionnaires [11, 12]. Anthropometric measurements included weight, height, body mass index (BMI), and waist circumference (WC). Weight was measured using a 300 kg-capacity digital electronic scale with a stadiometer (Welmy® W300 brand). Height was measured after a deep breath, with the patient remaining in a completely erect position. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). Standardized cutoffs were used to categorize BMI based on World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines as underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²), eutrophic (BMI 18.5–25 kg/m²), overweight (BMI > 25.0 to < 30.0 kg/m²), or obese (≥ 30.0 kg/m2) [12]. WC was measured at the midpoint between the iliac crest and the last rib using an inelastic tape. Central adiposity was determined based on WC cutoffs associated with elevated cardiometabolic risk (≥ 88 cm in women and ≥ 102 cm in men) [12].

The presence of clinical conditions was verified through a review of patients’ medical records, including diagnostic codes, physician notes, and laboratory results. Diseases were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10): Ischemic stroke (I63); Type 2 diabetes mellitus (E11); Cancer (C00-C97); Rheumatic diseases (M00-M99); Lung diseases (J00-J99); Depression (F32, F33, F34.1); Osteoporosis (M80-M82); Cardiovascular conditions (I00-I99); Systemic arterial hypertension (I10).

To assess sarcopenia, we used the EWGSOP2 criteria [13] and considered gait speed ≤ 0.8 m/s, grip strength < 27 kg for men and < 16 kg for women and/or appendicular lean mass/ht2 ≤ 7.0 kg/m2. Insomnia severity was assessed using the 7-question Insomnia Severity Index (ISI). Scores of 0–7 indicate no clinically significant insomnia, 8–14 indicate subthreshold insomnia, 15–21 indicate moderate insomnia, and 22–28 indicate severe insomnia [14].

Functional capacity was assessed using the Lawton and Brody scale, which comprises the IADLs. For each question, the elderly person received the following score: 1 - not able to perform the activity, 2 - performs with assistance, 3 - performs alone. Based on the scores of these scales, the participants were classified as follows: Independent, those who performed all activities without assistance; Partially Dependent, those who needed help or did not perform at least one activity; and Totally Dependent, those who needed help in all activities or did not perform any [10, 15]. To analyze the data, we used the proposed standardization adapted to Brazil by Lino et al. [16], which classifies elderly people as independent (six points), partially dependent (four points) and very dependent (2 points).

The investigation of frailty syndrome was based on the model proposed by Fried et al. [17], using five criteria: unintentional weight loss, fatigue, handgrip strength, gait speed and level of physical activity. After evaluating the five criteria, the following were considered: not fragile - no positive items present; pre-fragile - one or two positive items; and fragile - three or more positive items [17].

To assess dementia, we used MoCA [18, 19]. This is a brief screening tool designed to assess cognitive function. It evaluates various cognitive domains, including memory, attention, language, visuospatial skills, and executive functions. The test takes approximately 10 min to administer and is scored out of 30 points. Cognitive status was evaluated using the MoCA version in Brazilian Portuguese [20].

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS), with scores ranging from 0 to 15. A total score of zero was considered as the absence of depressive symptoms, and a score of ≥ 6 was used to determine the presence of depressive symptoms [21].

Biomarker analyses

Biochemical and inflammatory marker levels and DNA methylation were assessed in a subset of 175 participants from the Brasília site. Blood samples collected into EDTA tubes were divided into aliquots of less than 1 mL for DNA methylation assays. Additional blood samples were centrifuged at 2,500 rpm for 15 min at 25 °C to evaluate inflammatory markers. All samples were obtained by trained nursing staff through venipuncture using a vacuum system and stored at − 20 °C until further analysis.

Standard clinical laboratory protocols quantify fasting glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol, and lipid fractions through enzymatic, kinetic, or colorimetric tests (as appropriate) using reagents compatible with HumanStar 600 equipment (InVitro®). LDL-C was estimated using the Martin–Hopkins formula. Ultrasensitive C-reactive protein and glycated hemoglobin were quantified using turbidimetry and ion-exchange high-performance liquid chromatography, respectively.

A cytokine panel was used to evaluate the serum interferon-gamma, interleukin-2, −4, −6, and − 10 concentrations using a high-throughput flow cytometry-based bead assay kit (FACS Verse model; BD Biosciences, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (BD Biosciences). Titration curves generated from kit-provided standards facilitated quantitative analysis. All the scores were estimated through curve interpolation. In cases where a sample yielded outlying readings beyond the expected range, we conducted additional assays using either the original or diluted samples (as necessary) until we obtained a minimum of 300 events for each cytokine bead used. Subsequently, we analyzed all the data using FCAP software, version 3.0 (BD Biosciences).

DNA extraction was carried out using a commercial kit (QIAamp® DNA Blood Mini Kit, QIAGEN, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Quantification by a NanoDrop Lite spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific®, USA) preceded bisulfite conversion, and analysis of methylated cytosines (5-mC) was performed through a colorimetric ELISA-based assay (MethylFlash® Methylated DNA Quantification Kit, Epigentek, USA). The results, expressed as the percentage of methylated DNA (5-mC) in the total DNA sample, were extrapolated from a standard curve generated using serial dilutions of the synthetically methylated positive control DNA provided in the kit. The optical density (OD) of the curve was used to determine the 5-mC concentration.

Data analysis and statistical methods

Multiple imputations were performed using predictive mean matching (for numeric variables), logistic regression (for binary variables with 2 levels), and Bayesian polytomous regression (for factor variables ≥ 2 levels), and missing values (MVs) were addressed for selected covariates in the cohort comprising 493 participants using the mouse package for R. Imputed values, residual distributions and convergence coefficients were checked. Missingness rates reached 35% (mean 14%) for some variables, but multiple imputations were not performed for the main exposures (insomnia and sarcopenia), outcomes (cognitive and functional impairment/dysfunction), or biomarkers. Multiple imputation procedures resulted in five complete data sets, each containing different estimates of missing values for all 493 cohort participants. The complete imputed data sets were pooled and merged into a single data set for downstream statistical analyses.

The assumptions needed for univariate statistical analyses were rigorously assessed, including checking for identifying atypical data points and evaluating the distribution of variables. The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to each continuous variable to assess the null hypothesis that the data followed a normal distribution, as this test is particularly suitable for small samples. Additionally, we used the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test as an extra verification, comparing the data distribution to a theoretical normal distribution. For descriptive analyses, categorical variables are presented as the frequency (f) and percentage (%), while continuous variables are summarized as either the mean±SD or median (Md) and interquartile range (IQR), depending on the distribution of the variables.

Participants were stratified into four study subgroups according to the occurrence of sarcopenia, insomnia, both, or none of the conditions. For continuous variables with normal distribution, ANOVA followed by post-hoc analyses involving multiple comparisons of group means were employed to compare clinical characteristics among the study subgroups. When significant differences emerged, the Bonferroni correction was applied to control for type l errors. Variables with nonnormal distribution were analyzed by Kruskal-Wallis test and were subjected to post hoc analysis based on their rank. The chi-square (ꭕ²) test was used for comparing categorical variables. Whenever the minimum number of cases per cell was not met, Fisher’s test correction factor or the likelihood ratio was applied, as appropriate.

Forward stepwise logistic regression models were used to identify predictors of cognitive impairment (primary outcome) and functional dependence (secondary outcome). The candidate variables included all the sociodemographic, clinical and biochemical characteristics of the participants. Isolated or combined indicators for insomnia, sarcopenia or both conditions were forcibly entered to quantify isolated or combined effects on outcomes. Wald test p values < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance and were subsequently used to select variables for the models. Insomnia, sarcopenia, and both conditions were included in the models, irrespective of their significance according to the Wald test. The final models excluded contributors to outcomes (variables used to diagnose cognitive impairment or functional dependence) and covariates exhibiting high collinearity (variance inflation factor > 5).

To verify the interaction between insomnia and sarcopenia on the risk of cognitive impairment in the elderly, we used the interaction analysis by prevalence of the Relative Risk of Excess due to Interaction (RERI), the proportion attributable to interaction [AP] and the synergy index, which was carried out in STATA 18 [22]. To evaluate the synergistic (multiplicative) interaction, an age- and sex-specific interaction variable (insomnia*sarcopenia) was used in the logistic regression methods. All other statistical analyses were carried out with R Studio v.2023.03 for Mac.

Results

Participant characteristics

From 700 potential participants in the PROCAD database, 493 met the study inclusion criteria and were selected as study participants. These participants were categorized into four distinct subgroups: older adults experiencing sole insomnia (n = 131), sole sarcopenia (n = 69), both insomnia and sarcopenia (n = 58), and devoid of either syndrome (n = 235).

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of participants

As detailed in Table 1, most participants were 80–84 years of age (53.5%), followed by 85–89 years (29.8%) and > 90 years (16.6%). The age distribution exhibited significant variability among the study subgroups. While insomnia was significantly more prevalent among younger participants, sarcopenia was significantly more frequent in participants up to the age of 89 (Table 1). Female sex prevailed across all study subgroups (69.0%), with this sex discrepancy consistently persisting within each study subgroup but being significantly more prominent within the sarcopenia subgroup (89.9%).

Although white patients represented most of the cohort participants (77.8%), the racial proportions did not significantly differ among the study subgroups (Table 1). Regarding family income, only 41.6% had a family income above the top category of 3 minimum wages. No significant differences in educational attainment, family income or marital status were observed between the study subgroups (Table 1).

Nutritional status and anthropometrics

An examination of nutritional status revealed that 26.5% of participants were classified as underweight, 39.4% as eutrophic, 11.4% as overweight, and 22.7% as obese. No statistically significant variations in nutritional status were observed among the subgroups. Regarding anthropometrics and vital signs, no significant differences in waist or abdominal circumference or in either systolic or diastolic blood pressure were observed across the study subgroups (Table 1).

Medical conditions

Systemic arterial hypertension (SAH) emerged as the most prevalent condition in our sample population, affecting 74.1% of participants, followed by rheumatic diseases (30.4%), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) (26.5%), osteoporosis (26.4%), and depression (24%). There were no significant differences in the frequency of these medical conditions among the study subgroups. However, a significantly greater incidence of previous ischemic stroke was observed among participants with sarcopenia than among those in the other study subgroups. Cancer, lung, and cardiovascular diseases were not significantly different across the study subgroups (Table 1).

Medication use

Although there is no standard definition for polypharmacy, polypharmacy is often defined as the routine use of five or more medications [23]. This includes over-the-counter, prescription or complementary medicines used by a patient. In our sample population, polypharmacy was identified in 56.2% of participants, with the significantly highest prevalence observed among participants with sarcopenia (79.1%). Additionally, 27.7% of participants reported medication use for depression, with a significantly greater frequency occurring among those with sarcopenia (49.3%). The use of all the other medications assessed was not significantly different across the study subgroups (Table 1). Polypharmacy and the use of medications for depression were associated with cognitive impairment (OR 1.53 95%CI 1.07–2.21 p = 0.021 and OR 1.87 95%CI 1.28–2.81 p = 0.001, respectively) and functional dependence (OR 2.99 95%CI 2.08–4.32 p < 0.001 and OR 2.22 95%CI 1.53–3.22 p < 0.001, respectively) in multivariate models adjusted for use of medications for SAH and use of insulin. However, as seen ahead, these variables lost significance in stepwise models adjusted for sarcopenia, insomnia, risk for major depressive disorder and other variables in Table 2.

Cognitive and mental health and functional dependency

Findings from cognitive and mental health, functional dependency, and social support assessments are detailed in Table 1. Notably, participants with sarcopenia were significantly more fully dependent. Additionally, participants with sarcopenia were significantly less frequently categorized as at risk for depression according to the GDS but exhibited a significantly greater frequency of cognitive impairment and were more frequently categorized as prefrail (Table 1). In contrast, individuals with both sarcopenia and insomnia were more often classified as fragile (Table 1).

Inflammatory panel and DNA methylation

Table 3 displays the results of biomarker analysis for blood glucose, HbA1c, lipids, creatinine, albumin, C-reactive protein, inflammatory markers, and DNA methylation. No significant differences were found among the study subgroups for blood glucose, HbA1c, total cholesterol, HDL-C, LDL-C, albumin, creatinine, C-reactive protein, interferon-gamma, interleukin-2, −4, −6, or −10. However, Bonferroni post hoc analyses further revealed that individuals with sarcopenia had significantly more RAMDNA (4.71 ± 3.6) than did those with either syndrome (0.64 ± 0.8, p = 0.008) or with sole insomnia (1.52 ± 1.0, p = 0.013). In parallel, individuals with both insomnia and sarcopenia also had significantly more RAMDNA (5.28 ± 3.9) than did those with either syndrome (p = 0.003).

Predicting cognitive impairment and functional dependence

Two stepwise logistic regression models were constructed to identify the risk factors for cognitive impairment as the primary outcome and functional dependence as the secondary outcome. The attributes “only insomnia”, “only sarcopenia” and “both insomnia and sarcopenia” were included as fixed factors within the model. Confirming the observations of other authors (3–5), individuals with insomnia also had an odds ratio (OR) of 1.51 (95% confidence interval [CI] of 1.10–2.27, p = 0.019) for having sarcopenia.

When examining instrumental variables, it became evident that insomnia alone was not a risk factor for either cognitive impairment or functional dependence, as indicated in Table 4. In contrast, sarcopenia alone significantly increased the odds of cognitive impairment by 2.6-fold (95% CI 1.2–5.6, p = 0.011), albeit with no significant impact on functional impairment (p = 0.173). When insomnia and sarcopenia cooccurred, the odds of both cognitive dysfunction and functional impairment were significantly elevated by 4.8-fold (95% CI 2.1–10.9, p < 0.001) and 7.1-fold (95% CI 1.7–29.6, p = 0.007), respectively.

Our model for cognitive impairment achieved an explanatory power of 28% of the likelihood of older adults experiencing cognitive deficits. Age, osteoporosis, and depression emerged as significant risk factors for mortality, each augmenting the odds of cognitive impairment. Conversely, a high self-satisfaction score was strongly associated with a reduced risk of cognitive deficit.

In parallel, our logistic regression models for predicting functional dependence yielded an impressive explanatory capacity, accounting for 50% of the likelihood of older adults experiencing functional dependence. Decreased educational attainment and stage of dementia, as measured by the Morris scale, emerged as significant predictors of increased susceptibility to functional dependence.

As seen in Table 4, a positive interaction with cognitive dysfunction (categorical) was found on an additive scale, as the combined effect of insomnia and sarcopenia was greater than the sum of the individual effects (RSac + Ins = 67.01 > Rsac + Rins = 46.39). On the multiplicative scale, a slightly positive interaction was found (RSac/Ins = 2.97 > Rsac/Rins = 2.40). Calculation of the RERI did not identify a significant interaction (p = 0.386) considering the relative excess risk due to interaction [RERI] = 0.81 [95%CI: −1.02; 2.64]; the proportion attributable to interaction [AP] = 0.44 [95%CI: −0.67, 1.45]; or the synergy index (ratio between combined effect and individual effects) s = 1.50 [−0.11; 2.71].

In addition, to test the robustness of findings we performed the same analyses considering continuous MoCA and functional dependence variable as outcomes. For continuous MoCA as outcome, a positive interaction for insomnia and sarcopenia was seen in both additive (RSac + Ins = 71.81 > Rsac + Rins = 44.95) and multiplicative scale (RSac/Ins = 3.31 > Rsac/Rins = 2.28), with RERI = 0.89 [95%CI: −0.76; 2.41], AP = 0.50 [95%CI: −0.59, 1.0] and s = 1.59 [0.02; 2.30]. Considering functional dependence as the outcome, we observed no significant interaction (neither additive or multiplicative) with RERI = 0.28 [95%CI: −1.12; 2.19], AP = 0.04 [95%CI: −0.56, 0.83] and s = 1.05 [−0.32; 2.90].

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to present findings regarding the interplay between recurrent insomnia and sarcopenia in older adults who have achieved remarkable longevity. Our results revealed significant synergy between these two conditions, as their co-occurrence was associated with substantial 4.8- and 7.1-fold increases in cognitive impairment and functional dependence, respectively. Furthermore, our study also underscores the epidemiological association between insomnia and sarcopenia, demonstrating that the diagnosis of one of the conditions increases the probability of having the other condition diagnosed by 1.5-fold. Intriguingly, we also observed heightened DNA methylation levels in individuals with sarcopenia, particularly those who also suffered from insomnia, although no significant differences were observed in blood glucose, lipids, or inflammatory markers.

These epidemiological findings align with previously published data [20], which have consistently reported a higher prevalence of sarcopenia among long-lived older adults. Notably, our sample population included more women, 89.91% of whom were in the sarcopenia subgroup. This sex distribution was unexpectedly high, as other authors [24] have reported a prevalence of sarcopenia of 31% among older women and 64% among older men. However, our findings could be explained by the sex-specific sleep patterns that have already been documented, in which insomnia is more prevalent among women [25–27].

Furthermore, our investigation revealed that the sarcopenia subgroup exhibited the highest proportion of individuals with cognitive impairment (69.5%), aligning with previous research suggesting an overlap of risk factors between sarcopenia and cognitive decline [28]. In contrast, individuals in the insomnia subgroup (21.9%) reported cognitive impairment characterized by difficulty concentrating, making decisions, and memory problems, consistent with prior observations in insomnia patients [29]. The intricate relationship between sleep loss, as observed in insomnia, and its impact on cognitive function can be attributed to bidirectional effects on neuronal structural plasticity, which subsequently affect cognitive ability [29]. Additionally, insomnia can negatively affect muscle physiology at the cellular level, impeding muscle recovery by stimulating protein degradation, ultimately affecting protein synthesis and leading to muscle atrophy [8]. Individuals with insomnia may also experience changes in hormonal regulation, resulting in a shift toward muscle breakdown due to increased cortisol levels (a catabolic hormone) and decreased levels of IGF-1 (an anabolic hormone). This combination of effects contributed to muscle mass loss [30]. Similarly, Rasmussen et al. [31] associated sarcopenia with increased insulin resistance and proinflammatory marker levels, such as C-reactive protein and interleukin levels, although our study did not corroborate these findings. Proinflammatory cytokines have been implicated in muscle atrophy [32] and are also present at high concentrations during sleep deprivation. In contrast, our study challenges the “inflammatory hypothesis” of sarcopenia and insomnia, highlighting a potential role for inflammation-independent DNA methylation as an underlying mechanism.

Of note, we observed that sarcopenia alone was significantly associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment. However, sarcopenia alone did not show a significant association with functional impairment. This unexpected finding might be due to the presence of dementia in the model, which is a strong predictor of functional dependence. A dominant covariate can mask the association of a weaker covariate, especially if they share a common relationship.

The role of sleep deprivation in disrupting hormonal balance and contributing to age-related muscle loss may explain the greater prevalence of sarcopenia among individuals with inadequate sleep. Notably, a loss of strength and muscle mass is closely associated with reduced functional capacity [33]. Additionally, insomnia has been associated with various other health conditions, including cardiovascular diseases [34], T2DM [35], SAH [34], obesity [36], and colorectal cancer [37]. In this study, the majority of older adult participants exhibited prefrailty, with fatigue being the most prevalent component within the sarcopenia subgroup and among those with both sarcopenia and insomnia. A cross-sectional investigation carried out in community-dwelling older adults revealed that among 243 long-lived older adults, 14.8% were classified as frail, 21.4% as nonfrail, and 63.8% as prefrail [38]. Notably, poor nutritional status was closely related to sarcopenia in older adults [39]. Our study revealed that 26.5% of the participants were underweight. Our observations are consistent with prior research demonstrating that adults who follow a later circadian rhythm characterized by delayed sleep patterns are more prone to adopting unhealthy eating habits [39–41]. Such practices can lead to fatty infiltration of skeletal muscle and heighten the risk of sarcopenia [42].

Our study revealed that the majority of participants (56.2%) were exposed to polypharmacy, which was significantly more prevalent among individuals with sarcopenia (79.1%). Notably, the use of antidepressant drugs was observed in 27.7% of participants, with a higher prevalence among individuals with sarcopenia (49.3%). Notably, individuals with depressive symptoms often exhibit alterations in the production of neurotrophins, including brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF). BDNF, in particular, plays a crucial role in maintaining neuromuscular transmission. Thus, reduced BDNF levels in older adults with depressive symptoms may contribute to diminished muscle strength[46=Shayan Setayesh, Gholam Rasul Mohammad Rahimi, The impact of resistance training on brain-derived neurotrophic factor and depression among older adults aged 60 years or older: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, Geriatric Nursing,n Volume 54, 2023, Pages 23-31, ISSN 0197-4572, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gerinurse.2023.08.022.].

While our study offers valuable insights, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, this was a cross-sectional study with a relatively small sample of individuals with both sarcopenia and insomnia. Nonetheless, the study population from the PROCAD cohort represents a unique cohort in low- and middle-income countries, encompassing the most significant Brazilian sample of ultralong-lived individuals to date. Second, our analysis of the relationship between cytokines, DNA methylation, and the presence of sarcopenia and/or insomnia was conducted in a smaller subset (175 individuals), limiting the performance of the use of mediation analyses that could explore in depth the interplay between biomarkers, phenotypes, and clinically meaningful outcomes. DNA methylation is influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including genetics, environment, lifestyle, and aging itself [6]. This multifaceted context makes it difficult to establish a direct cause-and-effect relationship between DNA methylation and conditions like sarcopenia and insomnia. Third, while our analysis revealed a positive additive interaction between insomnia and sarcopenia on categorical cognitive function, the synergy index and RERI did not achieve statistical significance. This lack of significance may be attributed to the relatively small sample size available for this specific analysis with extreme categorization. In contrast, we observed a positive multiplicative interaction between insomnia and sarcopenia on continuous MoCA scores, with a statistically significant synergy index. While we employed MoCA cutoffs specifically validated for the Brazilian population [9], potentially enhancing its sensitivity here, it’s important to recognize that MoCA is generally considered a first-line screening tool. Fourth, as observed by other researchers[45=Prokopidis K, Giannos P, Reginster JY, Bruyere O, Petrovic M, Cherubini A, Triantafyllidis KK, Kechagias KS, Dionyssiotis Y, Cesari M, Ibrahim K, Scott D, Barbagallo M, Veronese N; Special interest group in Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses and the Task Force on Pharmaceutical Strategy of the European Geriatric Medicine Society (EuGMS). Sarcopenia is associated with a greater risk of polypharmacy and number of medications: a systematic review and meta-analysis. J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle. 2023 Apr;14(2):671-683. doi: 10.1002/jcsm.13190. Epub 2023 Feb 13. PMID: 36781175; PMCID: PMC10067503.], individuals with sarcopenia had a greater incidence of ischemic stroke than did individuals in other groups. This could explain muscle loss and the development of sarcopenia, given that ischemic stroke often leads to movement restrictions. However, it is essential to acknowledge that ischemic stroke may also cause functional and cognitive decline, introducing potential confounding bias. Although the same reasoning can be applied to the higher incidence of depression and polypharmacy among individuals with sarcopenia, the incorporation of these potential confounders in logistic regression models for functional and cognitive decline did not significantly change our main findings (data not shown). Validating these findings in a substantial, real-world cohort is imperative to ensure their robustness and applicability in practical settings. While this study provides valuable insights into the connection between insomnia and sarcopenia among long-lived older adult individuals, a more extensive and diverse cohort would bolster the credibility of the observed relationships and offer a more comprehensive perspective. Additionally, real-world validation would enable researchers to account for a myriad of confounding variables and demographic variances, thereby enhancing the external validity of the results. This validation process is pivotal in establishing the clinical significance of these findings and guiding evidence-based interventions that can effectively improve healthcare and well-being for larger older adult populations.

Conclusion

This study reveals a complex interplay between insomnia, sarcopenia, and cognitive decline in long-lived older adults. Our findings demonstrate a synergistic interaction between insomnia and sarcopenia, amplifying their negative impact on cognitive function, particularly when assessed using continuous MoCA scores. This synergy appears to be mediated by DNA methylation, independent of inflammation.

Importantly, while both insomnia and sarcopenia contribute to cognitive decline, sarcopenia emerged as a stronger predictor of both cognitive and functional impairment. This highlights the critical need for increased attention to sarcopenia in research and clinical practice aimed at promoting healthy aging. Future research should further explore the intricate relationship between sleep, muscle health, and cognitive function, and investigate potential interventions targeting these modifiable factors to mitigate age-related decline.
