Lactate-fueled H3K18 lactylation sustains ΔNp63α-dependent self-renewal in limbal epithelial stem cells
Authors: Longfei Li, Lin Zhu, Liyuan Wang, Jie Kang, Mingyue Zheng, Ziqian Geng, Tao Yu, Bin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The interplay between metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic regulation in controlling stem cell fate remains incompletely understood, particularly in limbal epithelial stem cells (LESCs) that are essential for corneal homeostasis. Here, we identify glycolytic lactate as a critical metabolic regulator that sustains LESC self-renewal through an epigenetic mechanism. We established a progenitor-like LESC model via MYC overexpression (oeMYC-LESCs). Using CUT&Tag, RNA-seq, and a mouse corneal epithelial injury model, we found that oeMYC-LESCs exhibit enhanced glycolysis and lactate efflux, which fueled p300/CBP-mediated histone lactylation, especially histone H3 lysine 18 lactylation (H3K18la). Enrichment of H3K18la at the ΔNp63α promoter drives its transcriptional activation, thereby sustaining LESC self-renewal. Inhibition of lactate generation depleted H3K18la, suppressed ΔNp63α, and consequently impaired LESC function; notably, these phenotypes were partially rescued by exogenous lactate supplementation. In vivo experiments confirmed that lactate accelerates corneal re-epithelialization and upregulates ΔNp63α in an H3K18la-dependent manner, whereas pharmacological blockade of lactate production delayed wound healing. Collectively, our study reveals a lactate-H3K18la-ΔNp63α axis as a key metabolic-epigenetic-transcriptional mechanism sustaining LESC self-renewal, suggesting a novel therapeutic target for treating limbal stem cell deficiency.

Limbal stem cells (LSCs) reside within the palisades of Vogt in the limbal region (1, 2). They maintain their numbers through self-renewal and continuously differentiate into corneal epithelial cells, thereby sustaining corneal epithelial homeostasis (3). Damage to LSCs or their stem cell niche leads to limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD), characterized by persistent corneal epithelial defects, corneal conjunctivalization, and ulceration. Severe cases may result in corneal perforation or even blindness (4, 5).

Currently, limbal stem cell transplantation is considered the most effective treatment for LSCD (6). In 2015, the first limbal stem cell therapy product, Holoclar, received European Union approval for treating moderate-to-severe LSCD caused by physical or chemical burns, offering new hope for patients (7). Follow-up data indicate that maintaining the self-renewal capacity of ex vivo-expanded limbal epithelial stem cells (LESCs) is crucial for the long-term success of transplantation (8). Despite its clinical importance, the molecular mechanisms governing LESC self-renewal remain poorly defined. Therefore, deciphering these regulatory networks is essential for optimizing LESC-based therapies.

Recent research has revealed that stem cells exhibit a distinct metabolic profile characterized by enhanced aerobic glycolysis, known as the Warburg effect (9, 10). Lactate, once considered merely a glycolytic waste product, has emerged as a central metabolic fuel and a pivotal signaling molecule (11, 12). Lactate contributes to cell cycle regulation by inhibiting the SUMO protease SENP1 to remodel the anaphase-promoting complex (APC/C) (13). Furthermore, lactate functions as a precursor for histone lactylation, a novel epigenetic modification (11, 14, 15). Specifically, histone H3 lysine 18 lactylation (H3K18la) has been shown to enhance the expression of stemness-related genes, thereby promoting cellular reprogramming into induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) (16). Dux-mediated H3K18la acts as a switch to regulate metabolic networks, significantly enhancing iPSC reprogramming efficiency (17). These findings collectively posit that H3K18la may constitute a pivotal epigenetic mechanism regulating stem cell self-renewal, including in LESCs.

In the present study, we established an MYC-overexpressing limbal epithelial stem cell line (oeMYC-LESCs) that acquires a molecular signature characteristic of limbal progenitor cells (LPCs). The self-renewal capacity of oeMYC-LESCs was markedly enhanced compared to late-passage LESCs, primarily driven by lactylation-dependent mechanisms. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that H3K18la upregulates ΔNp63α, a master regulator of epithelial stemness and stratification (18). This lactate-H3K18la-ΔNp63α axis was functionally validated in a murine corneal wound healing model. Our work unveils a fundamental metabolite-dependent epitranscriptional circuit that sustains LESC self-renewal, offering novel insights for improving regenerative therapies for LSCD.

Results

A progenitor-like LESC line was established

Our previous study found that LESCs with high self-renewal capacity exhibited higher MYC expression than those with low self-renewal capacity (19) (Fig. 1A). In alignment with the principles of animal welfare and to minimize animal use, we generated an oeMYC-LESC line (Fig. 1B). The oeMYC-LESC line exhibited a cobblestone-like morphology remarkably similar to that of early-passage (P1) LESCs (Fig. 1C and S1A), whereas MYC knockdown resulted in cellular senescence (Fig. S1B). Late-passage LESCs (passage 15) exhibited minimal SA-β-gal activity and show no significant upregulation of the senescence markers p16/p21 or the differentiation markers CK12 compared to oeMYC-LESCs (passage 15). However, an elevated N-cadherin to E-cadherin ratio was observed, suggesting that these cells might undergo a partial epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) rather than entering senescence or terminal differentiation (Fig. S2). The mRNA and protein level of MYC in oeMYC-LESCs was significantly higher than that in late-passage LESCs, but remained lower than that in primary LESCs (Fig. 1, D–F). Robust expression of the stemness markers ABCG2 and ΔNp63α was observed, whereas the corneal epithelial differentiation marker CK3 was almost undetectable in oeMYC-LESCs (Fig. 1G). Cell viability of oeMYC-LESCs was 1.98 ± 0.2-fold that of the late-passage LESCs, reaching levels comparable to primary LESCs (Fig. 1I). EdU-positive rate was approximately 1.4 ± 0.1-fold that of the late-passage LESCs (Fig. 1, J and K). Flow cytometry confirmed that MYC overexpression accelerated cell cycle progression by promoting the G1 to S/G2 phase transition (Fig. 1L). Regarding stemness markers, oeMYC-LESCs showed significant upregulation in ATF3, ABCG2, and ΔNp63 mRNA levels (2.27 ± 0.28, 2.19 ± 0.16, and 16.22 ± 3.26-fold, respectively, of those in late-passage LESCs), although these levels were still lower than those of primary LESCs (Fig. 1M). Similarly, Western blot analysis indicated that protein levels of ATF3, ABCG2, and ΔNp63α were 5.25 ± 1.13, 1.93 ± 0.14, and 1.73 ± 0.07-fold, respectively, of those in late-passage LESCs (Fig. 1, N and O). Collectively, these findings indicate that oeMYC-LESCs exhibit an LPC-like phenotype and maintain self-renewal capacity (Fig. 1P).

Lactate metabolism potentially promoted LESC self-renewal via lactylation

Given that metabolic reprogramming regulates stem cell fate, we investigated the role of glycolysis in LESCs. ECAR assays indicated a significantly enhanced glycolytic capacity in oeMYC-LESCs compared to the late-passage LESCs (Fig. 2, A and B). Consistently, metabolomic profiling revealed an enrichment of both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) pathways in oeMYC-LESCs (Fig. 2C), accompanied by the accumulation of intermediates from glycolysis and the TCA cycle. Quantification of lactate distribution revealed that while intracellular levels remained low, extracellular lactate was significantly elevated in oeMYC-LESCs (Fig. 2E). This pattern was associated with the specific upregulation of the lactate transporter MCT1, but not MCT2 or MCT4 (Fig. 2F). Consistent with this finding, pharmacological inhibition of MCT1 markedly attenuated lactate efflux (Fig. 2G). In addition to altered lactate transport, oeMYC-LESCs displayed enhanced OXPHOS activity (Fig. 2, H and I) and upregulated lactate dehydrogenase subunits LDHA and LDHB (Fig. 2J), suggesting that lactate is rapidly reconverted to pyruvate to fuel mitochondrial oxidative metabolism. We next investigated whether lactate, beyond its metabolic fate, could function as a substrate for protein lactylation. The oeMYC-LESCs exhibited upregulated expression of the lactyltransferases p300 and CBP (Fig. 2J) accompanied by a pronounced elevation in global lysine lactylation (Pan-Kla) (Fig. 2, K and M). Critically, pharmacological or siRNA-mediated inhibition of p300/CBP significantly reduced Pan-Kla levels (Fig. 2, L, N and S4), supporting an essential role for these enzymes in mediating lactylation in oeMYC-LESCs. Taken together, these data demonstrate that lactate in oeMYC-LESCs is allocated to three distinct pathways: extracellular efflux mediated by MCT1; reconversion to pyruvate to support OXPHOS; and utilization as a substrate for protein lactylation (Fig. 2O). In addition, CUT&Tag-qPCR assays revealed significant enrichment of MYC binding at multiple promoter regions of MCT1, LDHA, LDHB, p300, and CBP relative to the IgG control (Fig. S5).

Subsequently, late-passage LESCs were treated with lactate. Notably, treatment with 5 mM lactate increased cell viability to 1.35 ± 0.04-fold of the untreated control (Fig. 3A). Low concentrations (1 and 5 mM) maintained a cellular phenotype similar to controls, whereas higher concentrations (25 and 50 mM) induced cytotoxicity, as evidenced by cell shrinkage, decreased cell density, and a reduced nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio (Figs. 3B and S6A). Treatment with 5 mM lactate also increased the EdU-positive rate to 1.23 ± 0.09-fold of the control, and promoted cell cycle progression (Figs. 3, C–E and S6, B, C). Furthermore, treatment with 5 mM lactate upregulated the stemness markers ΔNp63α, ABCG2, and ATF3, and increased Pan-Kla modification (Fig. 3, F–H). In contrast, 5 mM lactate had only a marginal effect on oeMYC-LESCs compared to controls (Fig. S7), which may be attributed to their intrinsically robust self-renewal capacity. H3K18la emerged as a critical epigenetic modification regulating LESC self-renewal

To validate lysine lactylation serving as a key downstream effector, oeMYC-LESCs were treated with the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG or the LDHA inhibitor oxamate (Fig. 4A). Both treatments markedly reduced intracellular lactate levels (Fig. S8, A and F) and led to a dose-dependent decrease in Pan-Kla level, particularly at the H3K18la site (Figs. 4, B, C and S8, G, H). RNA-seq analysis of 60 mM oxamate-treated cells identified significant enrichment of KEGG pathways related to pluripotency regulation and cell cycle signaling (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, oxamate treatment downregulated the expression of core pluripotency-associated genes, including TP63 and ATF3 (Fig. 4E). Notably, supplementation with 5 mM lactate partially rescued the reduction in lysine lactylation induced by 60 mM oxamate (Fig. 4, F and G) and effectively restored cell number, viability, EdU-positive rate, and colony-forming capacity (Fig. 4, H–M). This partial rescue suggests that lactate-dependent lactylation represents a major, but not exclusive, mechanism underlying LESC self-renewal.

Given that 2-DG and oxamate may exert off-target effects independent of lactate production, we further validated our findings using siRNA-mediated knockdown of LDHA/LDHB. Knockdown of either LDHA or LDHB significantly reduced both Pan-Kla and H3K18la levels, while dual knockdown caused a more pronounced reduction (Fig. S9, A and B). Although no obvious morphological alterations were observed, knockdown of LDHA, LDHB, or both significantly impaired cell viability, EdU-positive rate, and colony formation efficiency (Fig. S9, C–H). Therefore, these results establish H3K18la lactylation as a key mechanism through which lactate maintains LESC self-renewal.

H3K18la directly activated ΔNp63α transcription to maintain LESC self-renewal

The results revealed specific enrichment of H3K18la at gene promoter regions (Figs. 5A and S10), and GO analysis indicated that the target genes were associated with cell proliferation (Fig. 5B). ΔNp63α (encoded by TP63) emerged as a pivotal stemness-related regulator. RT-qPCR confirmed that ΔNp63α was the predominant TP63 isoform expressed in oeMYC-LESCs, whereas the differentiation-associated TAp63 isoform is expressed at low levels (Fig. 5D). Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) showed distinct H3K18la enrichment at the ΔNp63α promoter (Fig. 5E). CUT&Tag-qPCR assays further validated that oxamate treatment reduced this enrichment (Fig. 5, F–H), which corresponded with significant decreases in both ΔNp63α mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5, I and J). These data establish H3K18la as a direct epigenetic activator of ΔNp63 transcription in LESCs (Fig. 5K). Beyond ΔNp63α, ATF3, the other identified target, may also contribute to LESC self-renewal and represents an interesting candidate for future studies (Fig. S11).

Functional validation showed that knockdown of ΔNp63α in oeMYC-LESCs downregulated stemness markers (ATF3, ABCG2) (Fig. 6, A–C), reduced cell numbers and nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratios (Fig. 6D), and impaired cell viability, EdU-positive rate and colony-forming ability (Fig. 6, E–I). Conversely, overexpression of ΔNp63α in late-passage LESCs upregulated ATF3 at both mRNA and protein levels. ABCG2 levels showed no significant change despite a reduction in its mRNA (Fig. 6, J–L). Notably, ΔNp63α overexpression increased cell number, viability, EdU-positive rate and colony-forming ability (Fig. 6, M–R). Discussion

Pluripotent stem cells exhibit a distinct metabolic preference for aerobic glycolysis, a phenomenon that is not exclusive to cancer cells (20). Accumulating evidence identifies lactate, the end-product of glycolysis, as a critical modulator of systemic metabolic coordination rather than mere waste (20, 21). Here, we demonstrate that lactate promotes LESC self-renewal through an epigenetic mechanism involving H3K18la-dependent activation of ΔNp63α.

MYC, a core component of the Yamanaka factors, occupies a central node in transcriptional networks determining stem cell fate (22, 23, 24). Recent scRNA-seq has revealed that MYC is robustly expressed in the limbus and is essential for sustaining LESC stemness and proliferation (25). Consistent with this, we observed significantly elevated MYC expression in LESCs with high self-renewal capacity compared to those with diminished self-renewal potential, directly implicating its role in maintaining limbal homeostasis (19). To recapitulate the native progenitor phenotype, we established an oeMYC-LESC line. This cell line successfully reproduced key characteristics of primary LESCs, including positive expression of established limbal progenitor biomarkers (25) and a self-renewal capacity significantly superior to late-passage LESCs. Notably, late-passage LESCs underwent partial EMT rather than senescence or terminal differentiation, providing a relevant model for studying self-renewal decline (26, 27).

To elucidate the metabolic basis of this enhanced self-renewal, we first compared the glycolytic profiles of oeMYC-LESCs and late-passage LESCs. Consistent with observations in other stem cell systems, such as mouse spermatogonial stem cells (28), oeMYC-LESCs displayed an enhanced glycolysis phenotype characterized by increased extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and an enrichment of glycolytic intermediates. Intriguingly, despite elevated glycolytic flux, intracellular lactate levels remained paradoxically low, prompting the discovery that lactate is partitioned into three coordinated fates. (i) Active extracellular efflux via MCT1, which maintains lactate homeostasis (29). (ii) Mitochondrial fueling for OXPHOS via the lactate shuttle (30, 31), where LDHA/LDHB reconvert lactate to pyruvate to maximize bioenergetic yield (32). (iii) Substrate for epigenetic reprogramming via p300/CBP-mediated histone lactylation (15, 33, 34, 35, 36). oeMYC-LESCs exhibited elevated p300/CBP and global Pan-Kla. Diminishing intracellular lactate reduced Pan-Kla levels and impaired stemness, establishing a direct link between metabolic flux and cell fate. Collectively, these findings delineate a novel metabolic-epigenetic axis in oeMYC-LESCs, linking lactate metabolism to both energy production and epigenetic programming.

Emerging evidence links histone lactylation to stem cell fate determination (17, 37), with H3K18la specifically implicated in promoting stemness-related gene expression (16). By integrating CUT&Tag and RNA-seq data, we identified TP63 as a direct target of H3K18la. Among the TP63 isoforms, ΔNp63α is the predominant regulator of corneal epithelial stemness and is highly enriched in the limbal niche (25, 38, 39, 40, 41). We validated that ΔNp63α is the primary isoform expressed in LESCs, whereas the differentiation-associated TAp63 isoform is expressed at low levels. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that H3K18la is enriched at the ΔNp63α promoter to activate its transcription. Gain/loss of function experiments further confirmed that this H3K18la-mediated upregulation of ΔNp63α is indispensable for maintaining LESC self-renewal.

Corneal injury typically induces epithelial loss and stromal edema, triggering subsequent inflammatory cascades. Thus, rapid re-epithelialization is paramount for restoring ocular surface homeostasis. Histone lactylation has recently emerged as an important epigenetic mechanism regulating diverse biological processes, including metabolic reprogramming, inflammatory responses, and wound repair (42). Although epigenetic regulation is known to participate in corneal wound healing (43, 44), the specific role of histone lactylation in corneal epithelial regeneration remains poorly understood. Our in vivo data demonstrate that lactate promotes re-epithelialization and upregulates H3K18la and ΔNp63α, whereas oxamate impairs healing. Immunofluorescence co-staining confirmed that lactate enhances H3K18la signals specifically within ΔNp63α-positive limbal and basal corneal epithelial cells. Consistent with previous reports, ΔNp63α-positive LESCs are responsible for driving complete corneal surface reconstruction (40, 45). To our knowledge, this is the first study demonstrating that histone lactylation directly regulates limbal epithelial stem cell self-renewal and corneal epithelial regeneration in vivo.

Taken together, our study uncovers a critical lactate-H3K18la-ΔNp63α axis that orchestrates corneal epithelial repair through a coordinated network of metabolic, epigenetic, and transcriptional regulation. These insights suggest that targeting metabolic-epigenetic coupling via lactate supplementation could represent a promising therapeutic strategy for treating limbal stem cell deficiency.

Conclusion

Our study elucidates a critical regulatory network linking metabolic reprogramming to the epigenetic control of LESC fate. We demonstrated that glycolytic lactate fueled histone lactylation, specifically generating H3K18la, which directly binds to and activates the ΔNp63 promoter to sustain self-renewal. Furthermore, our in vivo findings validate that lactate supplementation accelerated corneal epithelial regeneration, an effect that is likely mediated by the H3K18la–ΔNp63α axis. These results highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting this metabolic-epigenetic-transcriptional crosstalk as a novel strategy for the treatment of LSCD.

Experimental procedures

Experimental animals

Ten-week-old male New Zealand White rabbits and eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Pengyue Laboratory Animal Breeding Co., Ltd. Animals were maintained and bred in the Key Laboratory for Corneal Tissue Engineering at Ocean University of China (OUC). All experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (eighth edition, NIH Publication, 2011) and were approved by the Ethics Committee of OUC (Approval No. OUC-AE-2025-243).

Cell culture

Rabbit limbal stem cells (LESCs) were isolated and cultured as previously described (19). Briefly, the limbus region was washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; P1010, Solarbio, China) with 2% Penicillin-Streptomycin (P/S; HY-K1006, MCE, USA), minced into small pieces, and digested with 0.1% dispase II (D6430, Solarbio) for 2 h at 37° C, followed by 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (T1300, Solarbio) for 15 min. The dissociated primary cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured in DMEM/F-12 (21331020) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; A5256701, Gibco), 20 ng/ml EGF (Z00333, GenScript, China), 10 ng/ml IL-6 (Z03034, GenScript), 1% ITS-A (51300044, Gibco) and 1% P/S. Cells were maintained in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. All cell cultures were regularly screened for mycoplasma contamination using a commercial detection kit (MP0035, Sigma–Aldrich), and only mycoplasma-free cultures were used for experiments.

Establishment of oeMYC-LESCs

To establish MYC-overexpressing cell lines, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with the target overexpression plasmid, pHelper and pEnv to generate lentiviral particles. The virus was harvested and verified by DNA sequencing. The late-passage LESCs (P10) were transducted with the lentivirus for 24 h. Stable oeMYC-LESCs were selected by treating with puromycin (4 μg/ml; HY-B1743, MCE) for 7 days. The Late-passage LESCs at passage 10 to 15 were used in the experiments. The stable oeMYC-LESCs and late-passage LESCs were maintained in DMEM/F-12 supplemented with 5% FBS, 1% ITS-A and 1% P/S.

Cell transfection

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting LDHA, LDHB, ΔNp63, p300, and CBP as well as the oe-ΔNp63α plasmid constructed on pcDNA3.1 (+) vector were synthesized by Tsingke (China). Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The siRNA sequences are listed in Table S1.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (RC112, Vazyme, China) and quantified using NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using HiScript IV RT SuperMix (R423, Vazyme). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711, Vazyme) on LightCycler 96 (Roche, Swizerland). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with β-Actin serving as normalizer. Primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (R0010, Solarbio) and separated by 10% SDS-PAGE. Proteins were subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% non-fat milk (9999S, Cell Signaling Technology) in TBST (T1085, Solarbio) for 2 h, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-Pan-Kla (1:1000, PTM-1401, PTM Bio), anti-H3K18la (1:1000, PTM-1427, PTM Bio), anti-ΔNp63 (1:2000, 619001, Biolegend), anti-c-MYC (1:10000, 10828, Proteintechhina), anti-LDHA (1:2000, R24822, Zenbio, China), anti-LDHB (1:2000, R389134, Zenbio), anti-beta-Actin (1:5000, 700068, Zenbio), anti-ABCG2 (1:1000, 222011, Zenbio), anti-ATF3 (1:2000, R26444, Zenbio), anti-Histone H3 (1:2000, R24572, Zenbio), anti-p16 (1:1000, R23895, Zenbio), anti-p21 (1:1000, R381102, Zenbio), anti-CK12 (1:1000, 24789, Proteintech), anti-E-cadherin (1:1000, 340341, Zenbio), anti-N-cadherin (1:1000, 680441, Zenbio). Membranes were washed and incubated with certain HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, SA00001, Proteintech). for 30 min at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL Western Blotting Substrate (6883S, Cell Signaling Technology) and captured using a Tanon 5200 Chemiluminescent Imaging System. Band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.54G).

Immunofluorescent staining

For immunofluorescence staining, adherent cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), washed twice with TBST (T1081, Solarbio), and then permeabilized with a 0.3% Triton X-100 (T8200, Solarbio) for 10 min at room temperature. Non-specific sites were blocked by incubation with 5% BSA in TBST for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were then probed with anti-ABCG2 (1:100, 222011, Zenbio), anti-ΔNp63 (1:100, 619001, Biolegend, USA), anti-cMYC (1:200, 10828, Proteintech), anti-CK3 (1:200, 16850, Proteintech), and anti-CK12 (1:200, 24789, Proteintech) at 4 °C overnight, which were subsequently detected using fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200, SA00013, Proteintech) applied in the dark. Nuclei were labeled with DAPI. All images were captured using Leica DMi8 inverted microscope.

CCK-8 assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; PF00004, Proteintech). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 4000 cells per well and incubated for 12 h. After the indicated treatments, 10 μl of CCK-8 was added to each well, and cells were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance was subsequently measured at 450 nm. Cell viability was calculated using the following formula: Cell viability (%) = [(Asample−Ablank)/(Acontrol−Ablank)] × 100.

EdU staining

Cell proliferation was assessed by using VF 555 Click-iT EdU Universal Cell Proliferation Detection Kit (HY-K1096, MCE). Cells were incubated with EdU working solution for 2 h, fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100, and stained according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst. Images were captured using a Leica DMi8 inverted microscope (Germany), and the percentage of EdU-positive cells was analyzed using ImageJ.

Colony formation assay

LESCs were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1000 cells per well. After 24 h, cells were treated as indicated. The medium was replaced every 3 days. After 10 days, colonies were fixed and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (G1063, Solarbio).

Flow cytometry

Cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and fixed in 70% ethanol overnight at 4 °C. Cells were then stained with propidium iodide (PI) in the presence of RNase A (20 μg/ml) for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. Data were acquired using a BD FACSAria III Cell Sorter (Becton-Dickinson, USA), with a minimum of 30,000 events recorded per sample.

Mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) assay

To assess glycolytic and respiratory function, ECAR and OCR were measured. Cells were equilibrated in XF base medium containing 2 mM glutamate for 1 h in a CO2-free incubator. 10 mM glucose (HY-B0389, MCE), 1 μM oligomycin (HY-N6782, MCE), and 50 mM 2-DG (HY-13966, MCE) were used to perturb glycolysis, with all concentrations denoting final well concentrations. 1 μM oligomycin (HY-N6782, MCE), 1 μM FCCP (HY-100410, MCE), and 1 μM antimycin A (HY-N12257, MCE) were injected sequentially to interrogate distinct aspects of mitochondrial function.

Metabolomics analysis

Metabolites were extracted and profiled using a Shimadzu Nexera X2 UHPLC system coupled to a QTRAP 5500 mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, China). Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column using a gradient of ammonium acetate buffers (pH 8.2–8.8). 13C-nicotinic acid was used as an internal standard. Data were acquired in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode and analyzed using MultiQuant software 3.0.2.

Cell treatments

Cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured in complete medium for 12 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2, then treated for 24 h with lactate (Lac, 5 mM), oxamate (Oxa, 60 mM), Lys-CoA TFA (200 nM), GNE-207 (50 nM), AZD3965 (100 nM) or 2-DG (concentration as indicated).

Measurement of lactate levels

Intracellular and extracellular lactate levels were quantified using a Lactic Acid Content Assay Kit (BC2235, Solarbio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For intracellular measurements, 2.53 × 105 cells were harvested per sample.

Cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag)

CUT&Tag was performed using the Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit (TD903, Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were harvested and bound to Concanavalin A-coated magnetic beads. After resuspension in antibody buffer, the cells were sequentially incubated with either anti-H3K18la antibody (1:100, PTM-1427, PTM Bio) or anti-c-MYC (1:100, 10828, Proteintech), followed by incubation with a goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (B900210, Proteintech), and subsequent treatment with pA-Tn5 transposase. Tagged DNA was subsequently released and used either for qPCR validation or for sequencing library construction. For library preparation, tagged DNA fragments were amplified, purified using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (N411, Vazyme), and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform. Sequencing reads were aligned to the reference genome using Bowtie2, and peak calling was performed with MACS2. Signal tracks and genomic enrichment profiles were visualized using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of peak-associated genes was conducted using the clusterProfiler package in R. Primer sequences are provided in Table S3. Raw data are available under the NCBI GEO accession number GSE315524.

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq)

Sequencing libraries were constructed using the VAHTS Universal DNA Library Prep Kit for MGI (NDM607-01, Vazyme). The workflow included mRNA purification, cDNA synthesis, end-repair, adaptor ligation, and amplification. Libraries were sequenced on the MGI platform. Raw data are available under the NCBI SRA accession number PRJNA1400777.

Establishment of a mouse corneal epithelial injury model

Mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, P3761, Sigma–Aldrich). A 2 mm diameter area of central corneal epithelium was mechanically debrided using a sterile cotton swab soaked in 75% ethanol for 10 s, followed by scraping for 20 s under a stereo microscope (SMZ25, Nikon). The operated eye was extensively rinsed with at least 15 ml of normal saline. Post-procedural analgesia was provided via 0.1 μg/g buprenorphine (Sigma–Aldrich). Based on this procedure, mice with corneal epithelial injury were randomly assigned to three treatment groups (15 mice per group): NS (normal saline-treated), Oxa (100 mM oxamate-treated), and Lac (25 mM lactate-treated). Normal saline, oxamate or lactate was topically administered as eye drops (10 μl per eye, three times daily). All solutions were adjusted to physiological pH before administration.

Evaluation of corneal epithelial defects

To evaluate corneal re-epithelialization, slit-lamp examinations were performed using white light and fluorescein staining. Briefly, 2% fluorescein sodium (HY-D0208, MCE) was instilled into the conjunctival sac. After 20 s, the ocular surface was rinsed with saline, and epithelial defects were visualized and photographed using a slit-lamp microscope (YZ5T, 66 Vision-Tech, China) equipped with a cobalt blue filter.

Measurement of corneal thickness

Central corneal thickness (CCT) was measured using anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT, RTvue-XR 100).

Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses

To assess histopathological structure and protein expression, eyeballs were collected 7 days post-injury and fixed in 4% PFA (P1110, Solarbio) at 4 °C for 24 h. Tissues were dehydrated in 30% sucrose overnight at 4 °C, embedded in OCT compound (4583, Sakura), frozen with liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. Consecutive 10-μm sections were prepared. For histological assessment, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). For immunohistochemistry (IHC), sections were blocked with 5% BSA for 60 min and permeabilized 0.2% Triton X-100 for 20 min. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-ΔNp63 (1:400, 619001, Biolegend), anti-H3K18la (1:300, PTM-1427, PTM Bio), anti-CK12 (1:1000, 24789, Proteintech). Following three washes in TBST, sections were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000, SA00001, Proteintech) for 30 min at 37 °C. Signals were developed using a 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit (HY-K1107, MCE). Images were captured using an upright microscope (E200, Nikon).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Differences between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired Student’s t test. Multiple group comparisons were determined using one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey's test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.
