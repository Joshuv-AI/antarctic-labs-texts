Sustained release of Eugenol from MCM-41 mesoporous silica for preservation of Agaricus bisporus
Authors: Kangqing Yuan, Fangxuan Yi, Jiawei Wang, Xiangyou Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe postharvest quality of Agaricus bisporus declines rapidly during refrigerated storage. A controlled-release system (Eugenol@MCM-41) was prepared by loading eugenol into MCM-41, with a loading capacity of 51%. Physicochemical characterization and all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were combined to clarify the sustained-release mechanism. MD simulations provided supportive evidence for favorable eugenol–silica interfacial interactions and restricted surface-associated mobility in the mesoporous model. In vitro release exhibited clear medium dependence and followed the Weibull model, consistent with diffusion-dominated release under confinement. In storage trials at 4 ± 1 °C, Eugenol@MCM-41 extended acceptable mushroom quality to 15 days. The treatment reduced weight loss, maintained firmness, delayed browning, and lowered malondialdehyde accumulation versus the control and pristine MCM-41. These results connect nanoscale confinement with preservation performance and support Eugenol@MCM-41 as an effective eugenol delivery system for preserving highly perishable foods.Keywords: Agaricus bisporus, Eugenol, MCM-41, Molecular dynamics simulation

Introduction

Agaricus bisporus (A. bisporus) is among the most widely cultivated and consumed edible fungi worldwide, which is attributed to its nutritional value and sensory attributes (Pan et al., 2026). A. bisporus is highly perishable and has a short postharvest life under ambient conditions due to its high moisture content, high respiration rate, and the lack of a protective cuticular barrier in A. bisporus. These characteristics promote water loss, microbial colonization, and physiological senescence, resulting in rapid quality deterioration (Dawadi et al., 2022). Extending storage remains a major focus in the postharvest handling of mushrooms, so as to suppress microbial growth and delay oxidative or browning deterioration.

Essential oils and their active compounds have gained increasing interest as natural preservatives. Eugenol (4-allyl-2-methoxyphenol) is one of the main active components of clove oil, exhibiting broad antimicrobial activity. Eugenol primarily targets the microbial membrane, disrupting its structure and subsequently increasing membrane permeability. These effects impair essential cellular processes and reduce viability (Hyderi et al., 2025). Therefore, eugenol has been tested in food preservation and antimicrobial packaging systems. However, free eugenol cannot be applied directly in packaging. It is volatile and can degrade during storage. Also, its release is difficult to control. These limitations encourage the use of carriers to improve stability and facilitate sustained release (Liu et al., 2024; Weisany et al., 2024). Mesoporous silica is a suitable carrier (Porrang et al., 2025), with MCM-41 being a representative type. It has a high surface area and adjustable pores. It also offers flexible surface chemistry (Alves et al., 2025). MCM-41 has been widely used as a support material because of its uniform pore size, well-defined mesostructure, high density of surface silanol sites, and high specific surface area (Abo El-Yazeed et al., 2022). These properties make MCM-41 useful as a platform for functional composites, because its porous structure provides accessible surface sites and facilitates molecular or ionic transport (Salama et al., 2024). More broadly, advanced porous materials have attracted increasing attention in adsorption, contaminant control, and functional delivery systems because of their tunable porosity, high surface area, and modifiable interfaces (Salahshoori et al., 2025). These features support high loading and controlled release of volatile bioactives (Karczmarska et al., 2025). This finding highlights the effectiveness of silica-based delivery systems in preserving the quality of A. bisporus (Yan et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2022).

However, the mechanisms within the confined mesopores remain unclear, and the host–guest interactions are not well understood. Moreover, predicting the release behavior is challenging. These uncertainties hinder the rational design of delivery systems. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations can provide atomistic insights into MCM-41 systems (Guo et al., 2024). They resolve adsorption configurations, interfacial binding, and confined transport (Chen et al., 2023; Kuppireddy et al., 2024). Recent reviews have further emphasized that molecular simulation methods, including MD, Monte Carlo simulation, and quantum mechanical calculations, can help link molecular interactions with macroscopic adsorption and transport behavior in porous or functional materials (Salahshoori et al., 2024a; Salahshoori et al., 2024b; Salahshoori et al., 2024c). MD trajectories also enable the quantitative analysis of diffusion. The key diffusion parameters thus extracted can describe molecular mobility under specific constraints (Douglas et al., 2024; McCluskey et al., 2024).

This study was conducted to develop a controlled-release system (Eugenol@MCM-41) to improve the postharvest quality of A. bisporus. The loading of eugenol was optimized through single-factor experiments. The encapsulation process and carrier stability were confirmed via various structural, spectroscopic, adsorption, and thermal analyses. MD simulations were performed to explore the adsorption behavior, interaction energies, and diffusion of eugenol within the mesoporous structure. The preservation performance was assessed during cold storage, and the key quality parameters, including weight loss, firmness, surface color, and malondialdehyde (MDA) content, were evaluated. The results linked mesopore interactions between MCM-41 and eugenol to food preservation quality. Eugenol@MCM-41 serves as an effective strategy for extending the shelf life of perishable foods.

Materials and methods

Materials

Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, >99%; Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was used as the structure-directing agent, whereas tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, 98%; Sigma–Aldrich) was used as the silica precursor. Eugenol (C10H12O2, 98%; Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was used as received. Ethanol (anhydrous, 99.5%; Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) served as the solvent for the eugenol loading process, whereas ammonium hydroxide (25%–28%, Sigma–Aldrich) was used for the hydrolysis of TEOS. A. bisporus was obtained from a local cultivation base, with mushrooms selected based on uniform size (cap diameter 3.5–4.5 cm) and absence of mechanical damage or disease. Other chemicals, including acetone (high-performance liquid chromatography grade, >99%; Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), phosphate-buffered saline (Sigma–Aldrich), trichloroacetic acid (TCA, >99%; Sigma–Aldrich), thiobarbituric acid (TBA, ≥99%; Sigma–Aldrich), and hydrochloric acid (AR; Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), were used for various analyses.

Preparation of MCM-41 mesoporous silica

MCM-41 was synthesized via a CTAB-templated sol–gel route followed by hydrothermal treatment. CTAB was used as the structure-directing agent, whereas TEOS was employed as the silica precursor (Candela-Noguera et al., 2024a). Briefly, 4.8 g of CTAB was dissolved in a mixture of deionized water (100 mL) and ethanol (100 mL), followed by the addition of aqueous ammonia (25%–28%, 24 mL). The mixture was stirred for 10 min to obtain a homogeneous micellar solution. Subsequently, 6.8 g of TEOS was added dropwise with continuous stirring, and the reaction was maintained at 25 °C for 24 h to form a white gel.

The resulting gel was transferred into a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and hydrothermally treated at 105 °C for 48 h under static conditions (Khaled et al., 2022). The gel was naturally cooled to room temperature. The precipitate was collected by filtration and washed repeatedly with deionized water to remove residual reagents. The product was dried at 100 °C for 12 h to obtain the as-synthesized powder. Finally, the CTAB template was removed by calcination at 550 °C for 6 h in a muffle furnace with a heating rate of 1 °C/min, yielding the template-free MCM-41 powder (Candela-Noguera et al., 2024b). The obtained MCM-41 was stored in a desiccator for subsequent use.

Preparation and optimization of eugenol@MCM-41

Preparation of eugenol@MCM-41

Eugenol@MCM-41 was prepared using an ultrasound-assisted solvent-evaporation approach (Liu et al., 2024). Briefly, 100 mg of as-synthesized MCM-41 powder was transferred into a 15-mL centrifuge tube. Anhydrous ethanol (10 mL) and a predetermined amount of eugenol were then added and mixed with vigorous vortexing to obtain a uniform suspension. The mixture was subsequently sonicated in an ultrasonic bath at the specified temperature for the designated duration to promote eugenol diffusion into the mesopores. After loading, the suspension was poured into a glass Petri dish, and the solvent was allowed to evaporate in a fume hood under ambient conditions until constant mass was achieved. The resulting solid was collected, gently ground to a uniform powder, and stored at 4 °C in a sealed container prior to further characterization and application.

Single-factor experimental design

Single-factor experiments were conducted to optimize the eugenol loading process. The eugenol-to-MCM-41 mass ratio, ultrasonic time, and loading temperature were evaluated. The eugenol-to-MCM-41 mass ratio was set at five levels (1,1, 1,3, 1,5, 1:7, and 1:9, w/w). Because 100 mg of MCM-41 was used in each loading trial, the corresponding eugenol amounts were 100, 300, 500, 700, and 900 mg, respectively. The optimized feed ratio of 1:5 (w/w) therefore corresponded to 500 mg of eugenol per 100 mg of MCM-41. The ultrasonic time and temperature were fixed at 60 min and 30 °C, respectively. Using the optimized mass ratio, the ultrasonic time was set at 15, 30, 45, 60, and 75 min. The temperature was fixed at 30 °C. Using the optimized mass ratio and ultrasonic time, the loading temperature was set at 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 °C to assess the effect of temperature on encapsulation efficiency.

Determination of loading capacity

The loading capacity (LC) of Eugenol@MCM-41 was quantified via ethanol extraction assisted by ultrasonication, followed by ultraviolet visible (UV–vis) spectrophotometry using an external calibration curve (Liu et al., 2024). Briefly, a known mass of dried Eugenol@MCM-41 (mEG-MCM-41) was dispersed in anhydrous ethanol and ultrasonicated to desorb eugenol into the solvent. Characterization techniques

Structural and morphological analyses

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of MCM-41 and Eugenol@MCM-41 were recorded on an x-ray diffractometer (D8 Advance; Bruker AXS, Oberkochen, Germany) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). The scan range was 2θ = 2°–60°. The data were used to assess the silica framework and check for crystalline phases after eugenol loading.

The hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index of nanoparticles in aqueous dispersion were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) at 25 °C. The structural features of mesoporous silica materials were analyzed with reference to the established framework descriptions (Karczmarska et al., 2025).

Determination of pore structure and surface area

Nitrogen (N₂) adsorption–desorption isotherms were measured at 77 K using a surface area and porosity analyzer (Micromeritics ASAP 2020, Micromeritics Instrument Corp., Norcross, GA, USA). Prior to analysis, the samples (MCM-41 and Eugenol@MCM-41) were degassed under vacuum at 150 °C for 6 h to remove physically adsorbed moisture and impurities. The specific surface area (SBET) was calculated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method (Brunauer et al., 1938). The total pore volume (Vp) and the pore size distribution and BJH pore volume were derived from the adsorption branch using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) model (Barrett et al., 1951).

Analysis of chemical interactions and surface composition

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was used to identify functional groups and verify eugenol incorporation within the silica matrix (Thermo Nicolet iS50).

The surface elemental composition and chemical states were analyzed by x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Thermo ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using monochromatic Al Kα radiation. The binding energies were calibrated by referencing the adventitious carbon C1s peak at 284.8 eV.

Quantification of thermal stability and loading

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed to evaluate the thermal stability of the materials and estimate the eugenol content in Eugenol@MCM-41 (TA Instruments Q500, New Castle, DE, USA). Approximately 5–10 mg of the sample was heated from 30 °C to 800 °C at the rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen flow of 50 mL/min until no further mass loss was observed.

MD simulation

Construction of MCM-41 mesoporous model

All-atom molecular simulations were performed using BIOVIA Materials Studio (MS 2020). An amorphous silica supercell with a target density of 2.2 g·cm−3 was generated using the amorphous cell module, following established protocols for constructing amorphous silicates (Morrissey et al., 2022). The initial unit-cell parameters were set to a = b = c = 64.1846 Å and α = β = γ = 90°, with periodic boundary conditions applied in all three directions. A cylindrical mesopore was created by deleting atoms within a radius of 18 Å from the cell center, corresponding to an effective pore diameter of 3.6 nm (Carta & Scorciapino, 2024; Chen et al., 2023).

Surface under-coordination generated during pore carving was chemically passivated to obtain a silanol-terminated interface. Next, H atoms were added to under-coordinated oxygen atoms, and hydroxyl groups (–OH) were introduced on under-coordinated silicon sites (Carta & Scorciapino, 2024; Chen et al., 2023). The surface silanol density was set to 3.0 OH·nm−2, which falls within the commonly reported range for calcined mesoporous silica surfaces (Karczmarska et al., 2025; Mohanan et al., 2024).

Force field and system preparation

The eugenol molecule was built and geometry-optimized in the visualizer module. Subsequently, 79 eugenol molecules were introduced into the cylindrical pore to construct the initial Eugenol@MCM-41 configuration. This number was selected according to the experimentally optimized loading capacity (∼51 wt%) and the theoretical host–guest mass ratio of the constructed MCM-41 model. All MD simulations were performed using the COMPASS III force field implemented in the Forcite module of BIOVIA Materials Studio 2020. No custom force-field parameters were introduced. The atom types and partial charges of eugenol, silica framework atoms, and surface silanol groups were assigned using the COMPASS III parameter library. COMPASS-type force fields were developed for atomistic simulations of condensed-phase organic and inorganic systems (Sun, 1998). In this study, COMPASS III was used as a consistent force-field framework for the organic eugenol molecules and the silanol-terminated silica surface.

Electrostatic interactions were treated using Ewald summation with an accuracy of 1.0 × 10−4 kcal·mol−1 and a buffer width of 0.5 Å. Van der Waals interactions were calculated using the atom-based method with cubic-spline truncation. The cutoff distance was 15.5 Å, the spline width was 1.0 Å, and long-range corrections were enabled. The silica framework atoms were positionally constrained during MD simulation to maintain the cylindrical pore geometry. Therefore, the simulation focused on eugenol adsorption configuration, eugenol–silica interfacial association, wall-associated localization, and apparent molecular mobility rather than silica framework deformation.

MD and trajectory analysis

Prior to MD, the Eugenol@MCM-41 system was energy-minimized using the Smart algorithm, including steepest descent followed by conjugate gradient, to remove unfavorable contacts (Li et al., 2023). The silica framework atoms were positionally constrained to maintain the cylindrical pore geometry during simulation, whereas the eugenol molecules were allowed to move freely. The constrained atoms corresponded to the silica framework, including Si, framework O, and surface silanol atoms. This setting was used to avoid artificial deformation of the mesoporous framework.

MD simulations were performed in the NVT ensemble at 298 K using a Nosé thermostat with a Q ratio of 0.01. A time step of 1.0 fs was used. The system was first equilibrated for 2 ns in the NVT ensemble. Subsequently, an 8 ns NVT production run was performed for trajectory analysis. Trajectory frames were saved every 2 ps. The NVT ensemble was selected to preserve the designed simulation-cell dimensions and the cylindrical mesopore structure. NPT equilibration was not applied because pressure coupling could artificially alter the pore geometry of the constrained mesoporous silica model.

Radial distribution function (RDF) analysis was further performed using the final 2 ns of the production trajectory to evaluate short-range associations between eugenol and surface silanol groups. Density profile analysis was performed using the final 2 ns of the MD trajectory to evaluate the spatial distribution of eugenol molecules inside the MCM-41 pore. The mass density profiles of eugenol were calculated along the x direction (100), y direction (010), and z direction (001). The x- and y-direction profiles were used to evaluate the in-plane distribution within the pore cross-section, while the z-direction profile was used to evaluate the axial distribution along the pore channel.

Molecule-level wall-contact analysis was performed using sampled frames from the final 2 ns MD trajectory. A eugenol molecule was considered to be in direct contact with the pore wall when the minimum distance between any eugenol heavy atom and pore-wall oxygen atoms was less than 3.5 Å. Molecules with minimum distances between 3.5 and 7.0 Å were classified as secondary-layer molecules. Molecules with minimum distances greater than 7.0 Å were classified as inner-pore molecules. The fractions of direct wall-contact, secondary-layer, and inner-pore molecules were calculated for each sampled frame and then averaged over the sampled trajectory.

In vitro release kinetics of eugenol

In vitro release tests were performed to assess eugenol release from the mesoporous carrier under food-contact conditions. Ethanol-based food simulants were selected according to Commission Regulation (EU) No. 10/2011 (European Commission, 2011). The test design followed general principles for the migration assessment of food-contact materials. The simulants represented relevant food categories.

Release procedure

The release profiles were measured in ethanol-based food simulants (10%, 50%, and 95% ethanol, v/v) and in aqueous media. Phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 3% (w/v) acetic acid were used to represent neutral and acidic conditions, respectively. In vitro antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of the prepared materials was evaluated against Escherichia coli (ATCC 25922) and Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 29213) using an agar well diffusion assay (Chávez-Esquivel et al., 2021). Overnight cultures were adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard and uniformly swabbed onto MHA plates to obtain a confluent bacterial lawn. A sterile cork borer was used to punch a central well (6 mm in diameter). The treatments included the following: (i) blank control (no sample), (ii) MCM-41 powder, and (iii) Eugenol@MCM-41 powder. In the MCM-41 and Eugenol@MCM-41 groups, an identical mass of powder was gently placed into the well.

The plates were left at room temperature for 1 h to facilitate the diffusion of active components into the agar, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. The antibacterial performance was assessed qualitatively based on the presence of a clear inhibition halo (i.e., an area with no visible bacterial growth) surrounding the well. All assays were performed in triplicate, and representative images were captured under standardized photographic conditions (fixed background, lighting, and camera distance).

Application of eugenol@MCM-41 in mushroom preservation

Mushroom preparation and treatment

The mushrooms were randomized into three groups (approximately 150 g per package) and placed in polyethylene (PE) plastic boxes (18 × 12 × 6 cm3) covered with a PE cling film (0.01 mm). The treatments were as follows: (i) CK, mushrooms packaged without any sachet; (ii) MCM-41, mushrooms packaged with a sachet containing 1.0 g of unloaded MCM-41 powder; and (iii) Eugenol@MCM-41, mushrooms packaged with a sachet containing 1.0 g of Eugenol@MCM-41. All samples were stored at 4 ± 1 °C and 90% relative humidity for 15 days. Quality parameters were evaluated at 3-day intervals (days 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15).

Results and discussion

Quantification of eugenol and optimization of loading conditions

A UV–vis method was established to quantify eugenol prior to the loading experiments. As shown in Fig. 1a, the calibration exhibited excellent linearity over 0–120 μg/mL, described by A = 0.02035C + 0.00723 (R2 = 0.99994), confirming reliable quantification for subsequent optimization (Liu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2021).

Quantification of eugenol and optimization of loading conditions for Eugenol@MCM-41. (a) UV–Vis calibration curve of eugenol (0–120 μg·mL−1) used for concentration quantification. Effect of (b) eugenol-to-MCM-41 feed ratio (w/w), (c) ultrasonication time,and (d)loading temperature on LC.

Single-factor tests were then conducted to maximize the loading content (LC) of Eugenol@MCM-41 (Fig. 1b–1d). Increasing the eugenol:MCM-41 feed ratio from 1:1 to 1:5 (w/w) increased LC, reaching a maximum of 51%; further increases in eugenol dosage (1,7–1,9) slightly reduced LC (Fig. 1b). This trend suggested that once accessible mesopores and high-affinity adsorption sites approached saturation, excess eugenol tended to remain as loosely bound surface oil, which was more susceptible to loss during solvent evaporation and post-processing, resulting in no further improvement in effective loading (Khalbas et al., 2024).

The ultrasonication time also influenced LC (Fig. 1c). LC increased with sonication and reached 50% at 45 min. This increase indicated improved eugenol infiltration into the mesoporous channels (Cui et al., 2023). LC decreased when sonication was extended to ≥60 min. Prolonged cavitation promoted eugenol volatilization and enhanced back-diffusion and partial desorption near pore entrances. These effects reduced the effective loading (Hao et al., 2021; Khalid et al., 2024).

Loading temperature showed a clear optimum (Fig. 1d). LC reached 48.9% at 30 °C and decreased at higher temperatures (40 °C–60 °C). Moderate heating accelerated molecular diffusion and promoted eugenol entry into the mesopores. Higher temperatures increased eugenol vapor pressure and intensified evaporation during sonication and solvent removal. This effect lowered the retained loading (Hao et al., 2021; Khalid et al., 2024). Therefore, the optimized loading conditions were as follows: a feed ratio of 1:5 (w/w), ultrasonication time of 45 min, and temperature of 30 °C. This formulation was used for subsequent characterization and evaluation.

It should be noted that the single-factor design was employed as a preliminary strategy to identify a practical preparation condition, and it did not quantitatively evaluate the potential interactions among the eugenol@MCM-41 ratio, ultrasonication time, and loading temperature. Therefore, the selected parameters should be considered an optimized condition within the tested ranges rather than a statistically global optimum. Further experimental designs, such as response surface methodology (RSM), would be valuable in future studies to evaluate factor interactions and refine the loading process.

Physicochemical characterization of eugenol@MCM-41

Transmission electron microscopy

TEM was used to observe the morphology and mesostructural features of MCM-41 before and after eugenol loading. Pristine MCM-41 showed a mesostructured morphology with visible channel-like contrast (Fig. 2a). After eugenol loading, the overall morphology of MCM-41 was still retained (Fig. 2b). The channel contrast became less distinct after loading. This change may be related to the reduced electron-density contrast after eugenol entered or covered the mesoporous region. These TEM observations suggest that the mesoporous framework was not severely damaged during the loading process. However, TEM mainly provides local morphological evidence. Therefore, the structural preservation and pore occupation were further evaluated using XRD and N₂ adsorption–desorption analyses.

XRD and FTIR analyses

XRD patterns were used to evaluate the structural features of MCM-41 and Eugenol@MCM-41 (Fig. 2c). In the wide-angle region (2θ = 10°–60°), both samples displayed a broad halo centered at about 22°. This feature was characteristic of amorphous silica (Fatemipayam et al., 2025). No additional sharp reflections appeared after eugenol loading. This result indicated no detectable crystallization of eugenol and no crystalline secondary phases. Thus, the amorphous silica framework was preserved after the loading process (Candela-Noguera et al., 2024a; Karczmarska et al., 2025).

In the low-angle region, MCM-41 showed a weak diffraction signal at approximately 2θ = 2°–3°, which can be assigned to the characteristic (100) reflection of mesostructured MCM-41. After eugenol loading, this signal became weaker and broader. This change was consistent with partial pore filling or pore-mouth blocking by eugenol. However, XRD alone cannot fully distinguish internal pore occupation from external surface adsorption. Therefore, the XRD results were interpreted together with the N₂ adsorption–desorption, FTIR, XPS, and TGA results to support eugenol incorporation into the MCM-41 mesoporous system.

FTIR spectra further supported the incorporation of eugenol into MCM-41 (Fig. 2d). Pristine MCM-41 showed typical Si–O–Si stretching vibration around 1080 cm−1 and Si—O related vibrations around 800 and 450 cm−1. These bands were retained after eugenol loading, indicating that the silica framework remained chemically stable. Compared with pristine MCM-41, Eugenol@MCM-41 displayed additional organic bands related to eugenol. The aromatic ring vibration around 1513 cm−1 and C—H stretching vibration around 2930 cm−1 were observed after loading. Changes in the broad –OH region were also detected. These changes suggest possible interactions between eugenol and surface silanol groups. However, FTIR alone cannot determine whether eugenol was located inside the mesopores or adsorbed on the external surface. Therefore, FTIR was combined with N₂ adsorption–desorption and TGA results to evaluate eugenol incorporation.

N₂ adsorption–desorption analyses

N₂ adsorption–desorption analysis was used to evaluate the pore-structure changes before and after eugenol loading (Fig. 2e and f; Table 1). Pristine MCM-41 showed a high BET surface area of 1039.26 m2/g and a total pore volume of 0.8435 cm3/g. These values are consistent with the typical porous features of MCM-41. After eugenol loading, the BET surface area decreased sharply to 41.47 m2/g. The total pore volume also decreased to 0.0454 cm3/g. The corresponding reductions were 96.01% and 94.62%, respectively.

The BJH adsorption pore volume decreased from 0.9440 to 0.0353 cm3/g after loading. The BJH pore-size distribution signal was also strongly suppressed after loading (Fig. 2e). These pronounced reductions indicate a marked loss of nitrogen-accessible mesopores and are consistent with substantial pore occupation and partial pore-mouth blocking by eugenol (Bernardotto et al., 2025). However, N₂ adsorption alone cannot fully distinguish internal pore filling from external surface adsorption (Zhu et al., 2022). Therefore, the adsorption results were interpreted together with FTIR, XPS, and TGA data.

The calculated average pore diameter changed from 3.25 nm for MCM-41 to 4.38 nm for Eugenol@MCM-41. This value should be interpreted with caution. After loading, the nitrogen-accessible pore volume was very low. Thus, the calculated pore diameter mainly represents the apparent diameter of the remaining accessible pores. It does not indicate real enlargement of the mesoporous channels. Such pore occupation and interfacial retention are important for controlled release, because guest molecules must overcome interfacial adsorption and confined-diffusion barriers before migrating into the surrounding medium (Sullivan et al., 2021; Weisany et al., 2024).

Thermal behavior and loading quantification

TGA and DTG were used to evaluate the thermal behavior of MCM-41 and Eugenol@MCM-41. They also provided an independent estimate of eugenol content (Fig. 2g and h). Pristine MCM-41 showed only minor weight loss from 30 °C to 800 °C. This loss was mainly related to the removal of physisorbed water and the condensation of surface silanol groups. This behavior was consistent with calcined mesoporous silica (Candela-Noguera et al., 2024b; Karczmarska et al., 2025).

Eugenol@MCM-41 showed an additional and pronounced mass-loss event. A dominant DTG peak appeared at approximately 190 °C–220 °C. This region was associated with the volatilization and thermal decomposition of eugenol-related organic species (Zhang et al., 2021). The extra weight loss relative to pristine MCM-41 indicated an eugenol content of approximately 49 wt%. This value was close to the loading capacity determined by UV–Vis extraction, which was approximately 50%–51%. The small difference between the two methods may be caused by incomplete extraction, residual adsorbed moisture, and overlapping mass loss from eugenol volatilization and silanol condensation during TGA. The agreement between TGA and UV–Vis results supports the reliability of the loading quantification (Liu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2021).

XPS analysis

XPS was used to probe the near-surface chemical states of pristine MCM-41 and Eugenol@MCM-41 (Fig. 3). The Si and O signals confirmed the silica framework for both samples (Isaacs et al., 2021).

High-resolution XPS spectra of pristine MCM-41 and Eugenol@MCM-41.(a–c) Pristine MCM-41: (a) C 1s, (b) O 1s, and (c) Si 2p.(d–f) Eugenol@MCM-41: (d) C 1s, (e) O 1s, and (f) Si 2p.

The C 1s spectrum of pristine MCM-41 exhibited a dominant peak at about 284.8 eV (Fig. 3a). This signal was mainly attributed to adventitious carbon. Minor higher-binding-energy components were also present, consistent with oxidized carbon species. After eugenol loading, the C 1s intensity increased markedly (Fig. 3d). The relative contribution of C—O components also increased. This change supported the presence of eugenol-derived carbon on the silica surface and near-surface region (Zhang et al., 2021).

The O 1s spectra were dominated by the Si–O–Si lattice oxygen component (Fig. 3b,e). A shoulder at a higher binding energy was observed. This feature was attributed to surface hydroxyl groups and adsorbed oxygenated species. The relative contribution of the hydroxyl-related component increased after loading (Isaacs et al., 2021). This trend was consistent with interfacial interactions between eugenol and surface silanols.

The Si 2p spectra remained centered in the characteristic range of Si(IV) in SiO₂ (Fig. 3c and f). No new Si chemical states were detected after loading. This result indicated that the silica framework remained chemically stable during encapsulation (Isaacs et al., 2021).

Eugenol@MCM-41 showed a lower value of 922.99 ± 25.76 nm. Percentile diameters displayed the same trend. These results indicated a smaller aggregate size and a narrower volume-based distribution for Eugenol@MCM-41.

UV–vis quantification in various media and comparative release profiles

The release profiles of Eugenol@MCM-41 exhibited a pronounced dependence on the medium (Fig. 4b and c). The cumulative release rapidly increased in ethanol-based food simulants in the early stage before reaching a plateau, with the total cumulative release F∞ (88 h) increasing with ethanol concentration (Table 3). The release percentages increased with the ethanol concentration, reaching 46.30% for 10% EtOH, 74.90% for 50% EtOH, and 80.69% for 95% EtOH. This trend aligned with the increased solubility and partitioning of hydrophobic eugenol into ethanol-rich media, thus enhancing the thermodynamic driving force for desorption from silica surfaces and diffusion out of confined mesopores (Sullivan et al., 2021; Weisany et al., 2024).

The release plateau was lower in aqueous media than in ethanol simulants (Fig. 4c and Table 3). The lower release in water-dominant media reflected the low aqueous solubility of eugenol, as well as strong adsorption and retention within the silanol-rich mesoporous channels. These factors limited outward transport under diffusion-controlled conditions (Zhu et al., 2022). The plateau reached 39.50% at pH 7.0. The plateau in 3% acetic acid was the lowest at 32.90%. The release process was slower under acidic conditions, indicating that the acidic environment hindered the release.

Importantly, the release behavior in these water-dominant media closely reflects the actual moisture-rich packaging environment of fresh A. bisporus. The relatively slow and restricted release under these conditions is highly advantageous for practical packaging applications. It effectively prevents an initial massive burst of eugenol that could cause local tissue damage or overwhelming odors. Instead, it ensures a sustained, long-term supply of active eugenol vapor within the package headspace to maintain postharvest quality during prolonged cold storage.

Regarding the safety profile for food packaging, recent toxicological assessments indicate that while micro-sized EOC-functionalized MCM-41 particles present a low risk of systemic accumulation, direct exposure to high concentrations or direct cell-particle interactions can induce cellular oxidative stress and cytotoxicity (Fuentes López, 2022). Therefore, the controlled-release mechanism of Eugenol@MCM-41 developed in this study acts as a critical safety-by-design strategy. By preventing a high-concentration burst and avoiding direct particle contact with mushroom tissue (applied as a packaging sachet), it may help maintain eugenol vapor at an effective level for preservation while reducing the risk of excessive local exposure. Nevertheless, further quantitative microbial evaluations, along with rigorous migration and toxicological assessments under practical packaging conditions, are needed to fully ensure its application safety.

Kinetic model fitting and mechanism-relevant interpretation

The release kinetics were compared across media by fitting the normalized release data Fn(t) to multiple classical models. The Weibull model consistently provided the best goodness-of-fit among the tested equations, with R2 ≈ 0.9988–0.9997 across all media (Table 4), whereas the zero-order, first-order, and Higuchi fittings displayed markedly poorer fits (Table 4). The better applicability of the Weibull model can be attributed to the heterogeneous release behavior of Eugenol@MCM-41. Unlike the Higuchi model, which assumes idealized diffusion-controlled release from a homogeneous matrix, the present system involved mesoporous confinement, interfacial adsorption between eugenol and silanol groups, and medium-dependent eugenol solubility. The Korsmeyer–Peppas model is mainly useful for interpreting early-stage release, but it was less suitable for describing the complete release profile because the release rapidly approached a plateau. In contrast, the Weibull model does not require a single idealized release geometry or a constant diffusion regime, and therefore better described the burst-to-plateau release profiles observed in this study (Costa & Lobo, 2001; Langenbucher, 1972).

Weibull parameters quantified the medium dependence (Table 3). The scale parameter α, which represents the characteristic time, was the highest in 3% acetic acid (α = 6.3610 min). This result indicated the slowest approach to equilibrium in 3% acetic acid. Ethanol simulants exhibited much lower α values (0.0413–1.0442 min). This result indicated a faster initial release in organophilic media. The shape parameter β was below 1 in all media (Table 3). This result indicated a steep initial rise followed by a decreasing release rate. The trend matched the observed burst release and subsequent plateau.

Given the rapid rise to the plateau, the Korsmeyer–Peppas model was fitted only to the early-stage data (t≤0.5 h) to obtain an apparent exponent (Table 5). The n values were extremely low (0.032–0.114) in all tested food simulants. These values indicated a burst-dominated response under strong nonequilibrium initial conditions (Noori et al., 2025). They did not represent a canonical diffusion-mechanism exponent. The model outputs supported a two-stage release process. The first stage involved rapid desorption or dissolution of readily accessible eugenol near pore mouths. The second stage proceeded more slowly and was controlled by confined diffusion and interfacial adsorption within mesoporous channels (Sullivan et al., 2021; Weisany et al., 2024).

Molecular simulation as supportive evidence for interfacial retention

The MD simulation was extended to an 8 ns NVT production run to provide more reliable molecular-level information. The simulation was not intended to directly reproduce the long-term release process. Instead, it was used to examine the interfacial association and mobility of eugenol within the MCM-41 mesoporous model. The MSD curve calculated from the final 2 ns showed an increasing trend with time (Fig. 5a), indicating that eugenol molecules retained mobility within the mesoporous environment. However, because the equilibrated configuration showed that eugenol was mainly localized near the pore wall, the MSD-derived diffusion coefficient should be interpreted as apparent surface-associated mobility rather than unrestricted diffusion through the pore volume. The mobility of eugenol molecules, as reflected by MSD analysis, depends on the number of loaded molecules. In this study, the model contained 79 eugenol molecules, which was selected according to the experimentally optimized loading capacity (∼51 wt%). A lower loading would be expected to increase the apparent mobility because of reduced steric hindrance, whereas a higher loading may reduce mobility because of stronger intermolecular interactions and molecular crowding near the pore wall. In addition, because the model consisted of a single pore embedded in a silica matrix and did not include an external release medium, the MSD results should not be interpreted as direct evidence of real release into the surrounding environment.

The interaction energy between eugenol and MCM-41 remained negative during the production run (Fig. 5b). The average interaction energy during the final 2 ns was −1001.41 ± 24.32 kcal/mol, corresponding to −12.68 ± 0.31 kcal/mol per eugenol molecule. This negative value indicates favorable host–guest interactions. These interactions may contribute to the retention of eugenol near the silanol-rich pore wall.

RDF analysis further supported the short-range association between eugenol and surface silanol groups. The RDF of Ophenolic(eugenol)–Hsilanol(MCM-41) showed a distinct peak at approximately 1.95 Å (Fig. 5c). This peak suggests close contact between the phenolic oxygen of eugenol and silanol hydrogen atoms. The RDF of Omethoxy(eugenol)–Hsilanol(MCM-41) showed a peak at approximately 2.05 Å and a broader distribution at longer distances (Fig. 5d). These results indicate preferential interfacial association between the oxygen-containing groups of eugenol and the silanol-rich pore wall. This association may partly explain the restricted mobility and retention of eugenol within the mesoporous system.

To further evaluate the spatial distribution of eugenol molecules in the MCM-41 pore, mass density profiles averaged over the final 2 ns of the MD trajectory were analyzed along the x direction (100), y direction (010), and z direction (001) (Fig. 4f). The z-axis density profile did not show an obvious end-to-end concentration gradient, indicating that no clear axial segregation remained at the end of the simulation. In contrast, the x- and y-direction profiles were clearly non-uniform, with higher density near the pore-wall region and relatively lower density in the pore-center region. These results indicate that the equilibrated configuration was not characterized by homogeneous pore filling. Instead, eugenol molecules preferentially localized near the silanol-rich pore wall. This wall-associated distribution is consistent with the favorable eugenol–silica interfacial interactions revealed by RDF analysis. The persistence of this distribution pattern during the final 2 ns suggests that the system had reached a stable wall-associated adsorbed state within the simulated timescale.

Molecule-level wall-contact analysis was further performed to clarify whether eugenol molecules formed a multilayer stacking structure inside the MCM-41 pore (Table 6). Based on the 3.5 Å distance criterion, 98.54 ± 1.35% of eugenol molecules were in direct contact with the pore wall during the final 2 ns. Only 1.46 ± 1.35% of molecules were located in the secondary layer, and the inner-pore fraction was nearly zero. The mean minimum distance between eugenol molecules and pore-wall oxygen atoms was 2.775 ± 0.032 Å. These results indicate that the confined eugenol phase was dominated by direct wall contact rather than multilayer stacking. Therefore, the equilibrated configuration was better described as a predominantly wall-contacting adsorbed layer with monolayer-like characteristics. This result agrees with the density profiles, which showed wall-associated enrichment and low density in the pore-center region, and with the RDF analysis, which showed short-range associations between eugenol oxygen atoms and silanol hydrogen atoms.

Because no release medium was included in the simulation, the MD results should not be interpreted as direct evidence for solvent-dependent release. The medium-dependent release behavior was mainly supported by the experimental release profiles. This interpretation is consistent with the role of molecular simulation as a supportive tool for identifying adsorption configurations, interfacial interactions, and mobility constraints rather than directly reproducing macroscopic release or migration processes (Salahshoori et al., 2024a; Salahshoori et al., 2024b; Salahshoori et al., 2024c).

Antibacterial performance of eugenol@MCM-41

The antibacterial activity of Eugenol@MCM-41 was preliminarily evaluated against E. coli and S. aureus using the agar well diffusion method (Fig. 6). For both strains, the blank control showed bacterial growth around the central well and no clear inhibition zone. The MCM-41 group also showed no discernible inhibition halo. This result indicated that the silica carrier itself had negligible antibacterial activity under the present assay conditions.

Visual comparison of the antibacterial activity of CK, MCM-41, and Eugenol@MCM-41 against E. coli and S. aureus using the agar well diffusion method. The clear zones around the central wells in the Eugenol@MCM-41 group indicate qualitative bacterial inhibition. The images are representative results.

In contrast, Eugenol@MCM-41 produced visible clear zones around the central wells, especially against S. aureus. This observation indicated that eugenol retained antibacterial activity after incorporation into MCM-41. It also suggested that eugenol released from the carrier could diffuse into the agar matrix and inhibit bacterial proliferation (Kim et al., 2025). However, because the inhibition-zone diameters were not quantitatively measured in the present assay, these results should be interpreted as qualitative evidence of antibacterial activity.

The antibacterial effect may be related to the known action mode of eugenol. Eugenol can perturb bacterial membrane integrity, increase membrane permeability, and cause leakage of intracellular constituents. These effects eventually inhibit bacterial growth (Hyderi et al., 2025). Overall, the agar diffusion results support the potential antibacterial function of Eugenol@MCM-41.

Preservation performance of eugenol@MCM-41 on a. bisporus during cold storage

Weight loss

The weight loss increased progressively in all groups during cold storage (Fig. 7a). This was mainly due to continuous water evaporation from the mushroom surface. Respiration also contributed to moisture loss. This behavior was typical of fresh mushrooms with high water content and a porous structure (Dawadi et al., 2022; Yan et al., 2023). The control (CK) showed the fastest increase. The difference was the largest in the late storage stage. Packaging with MCM-41 produced only a modest reduction relative to CK. Eugenol@MCM-41 produced the lowest weight loss at all sampling times. By day 15, the weight loss reached 8.5%–8.7% in CK, 7.0%–7.2% in MCM-41, and 6.3%–6.5% in Eugenol@MCM-41. These results indicated improved moisture retention in the Eugenol@MCM-41 group and delayed dehydration-related deterioration during cold storage. This finding was consistent with previous reports. Antimicrobial agents reduced postharvest water loss and helped maintain quality (Lakshan et al., 2024).

Firmness

Firmness decreased in all groups during cold storage (Fig. 7b). The decline indicated progressive postharvest softening. Moisture loss and tissue disassembly contributed to texture loss (Dawadi et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024). CK and MCM-41 showed a pronounced drop after day 6. The descent rate of Eugenol@MCM-41 was relatively slow at all sampling times. By day 15, firmness decreased to about 8–9 N in CK and about 7–8 N in MCM-41. Eugenol@MCM-41 maintained a higher firmness of about 13 N. The firmness trend matched the lower weight loss. The result indicated improved tissue integrity and slower structural deterioration under Eugenol@MCM-41 treatment.

Color stability, browning, and visual appearance

Color determines the marketability of A. bisporus (Ni et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022). L⁎ decreased during storage in all groups (Fig. 7c). CK exhibited the greatest decline. MCM-41 showed a small improvement. Eugenol@MCM-41 maintained the highest L⁎ at all time points. On day 15, L⁎ was about 85 for Eugenol@MCM-41, about 70 for CK, and about 73 for MCM-41. BI increased during storage under all treatments (Fig. 7d). This trend confirmed progressive browning (Zhang et al., 2022). The value reached about 31 for CK on day 15, about 30 for MCM-41 on day 15, and about 23 for Eugenol@MCM-41 on day 15. The group separation became clearer after day 6. The result supported a sustained protective effect of Eugenol@MCM-41 (Wang et al., 2024). The images agreed with the instrumental data (Fig. 7f). CK showed obvious browning from day 6, MCM-41 showed moderate browning, and Eugenol@MCM-41 maintained a lighter and more uniform appearance throughout storage. The combined evidence supported improved visual quality retention by Eugenol@MCM-41.

Reduction of MDA content

MDA, a product of lipid peroxidation, is closely linked to off-flavor formation and the development of potentially harmful compounds during mushroom storage (Ni et al., 2021). The MDA content increased steadily over time under all treatments (Fig. 7e), indicating intensified lipid peroxidation during cold storage. Eugenol@MCM-41 showed the lowest MDA accumulation at every sampling point. On day 15, MDA content reached 1.12–1.14 mmol·kg−1 in the CK and MCM-41 groups. In contrast, it remained significantly lower in the Eugenol@MCM-41 group at ∼0.89 mmol·kg−1. These results indicated that Eugenol@MCM-41 mitigated oxidative damage during cold storage and outperformed both the control and pristine MCM-41 treatments (Yan et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022).

Overall assessment and linkage to the active mechanism

Two-way ANOVA was applied to evaluate the effects of treatment group, storage time, and their interaction on the quality parameters of A. bisporus. The analysis revealed that both treatment group and storage time significantly influenced all measured parameters, including weight loss, firmness, L* value, BI, and MDA content (P < 0.05). The interaction between treatment group and storage time was significant for weight loss, firmness, and BI (P < 0.05), indicating that the treatment effects varied over time for these attributes. In contrast, the interaction was not significant for L* value and MDA content, suggesting that these parameters were affected by treatment and storage time independently, but their temporal trends were relatively consistent among treatments.

During the later storage stages, Eugenol@MCM-41 showed the most favorable preservation performance overall. It showed more evident advantages in maintaining firmness, reducing BI, and limiting MDA accumulation compared with CK and MCM-41. Numerical improvements in weight loss and L* value were also observed for Eugenol@MCM-41, although the differences among treatments were less pronounced or not statistically significant at some time points. These results indicate that Eugenol@MCM-41 provided a more stable preservation effect during cold storage.

This improved performance may be associated with two factors. First, the controlled release of eugenol in relevant media may have contributed to moisture retention and delayed dehydration-related deterioration during storage. The sustained release could provide a gradual supply of the active compound, which may help preserve mushroom tissue integrity and delay quality deterioration. This effect may be related to reduced oxidative damage and potential antimicrobial activity. Second, the qualitative agar diffusion results suggested that Eugenol@MCM-41 had antibacterial potential, which may have contributed to the preservation effect. However, since microbial counts during mushroom storage were not measured in this study, the exact contribution of antimicrobial action under practical storage conditions should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

This study successfully developed a novel Eugenol@MCM-41 controlled-release system for food preservation. The optimized eugenol LC reached 51%. Multiple physicochemical characterizations confirmed successful encapsulation of eugenol within the MCM-41 mesoporous framework. MD simulations indicated favorable host–guest interactions and restricted surface-associated mobility in the mesoporous model, supporting a diffusion-limited transport mechanism. In vitro release kinetics were well described by the Weibull model, demonstrating a sustained-release profile. The composite material showed qualitative antibacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus in the agar diffusion assay. During a 15-day cold storage trial, Eugenol@MCM-41 significantly enhanced the postharvest quality of A. bisporus, thus effectively minimizing weight loss and preserving tissue firmness. Furthermore, the system mitigated enzymatic browning and suppressed MDA accumulation. These results support the potential of Eugenol@MCM-41 as an active delivery system for extending the shelf life of perishable agricultural products. Further quantitative microbial and sensory evaluations are needed to support its practical application in food packaging.
