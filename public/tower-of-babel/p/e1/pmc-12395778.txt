AI-powered prediction of critical properties and boiling points: a hybrid ensemble learning and QSPR approach
Authors: Roda Bounaceur, Francisco Paes, Romain Privat, Jean-Noël Jaubert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this paper, we propose a robust deep-learning model based on a Quantitative Structure − Property Relationship (QSPR) approach for estimating the critical temperature (TC), critical pressure (PC), acentric factor (ACEN) and normal boiling point (NBP) of any C, H, O, N, S, P, F, Cl, Br, I molecule. The Mordred calculator was used to determine 247 descriptors to characterize the molecules considered in this work. For each evaluated property, multiple neural networks were trained within a bagging framework. The predictions from the final ensemble were successfully tested against a large set of experimental data comprising more than 1700 molecules and compared with those from different recent learning models found in the literature. Comprehensive comparisons and extensive testing highlight the robustness and predictive power of the newly proposed multimodal learning model. The developed prediction tool is available on a website at https://lrgp-thermoppt.streamlit.app/. Furthermore, a source code for implementing the trained models in Python is available via github https://github.com/bounac80/AI-ThermPpt.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13321-025-01062-9.Keywords: Critical properties, Ensemble learning, Artificial neural network, QSPR, Molecular descriptors

Introduction

Critical properties and acentric factors are fundamental properties in chemical engineering thermodynamics, enabling new products and industrial processes to be designed. Many useful correlations utilize critical constant and acentric factors to predict a range of thermodynamic and transport properties, including vapor pressures [1], surface tensions [2], and binary diffusion coefficients [3, 4]. Moreover, the cubic equations of state [5–9] widely used in process design require critical properties and acentric factors as input information.

For such applications, these parameters are typically obtained from experimental data or estimated using predictive models [10]. Traditionally, group contribution methods have been employed for this purpose; however, they often fall short in accurately describing complex chemical structures, particularly when unexpected atomic arrangements (not foreseen in the available groups) or conformational effects are involved [11]. In response to these limitations, more advanced and innovative approaches have emerged to better address these challenges.

Machine learning, notably via artificial neural networks (ANNs) and multi-task learning (MTL), has demonstrated strong potential in capturing the complex relationships between molecular structure and properties, overcoming the limitations of GCMs [10–14]. Quantitative Structure–Property Relationship (QSPR) methods, combining molecular descriptors and statistical algorithms, offer a flexible and interpretable alternative for property prediction [15]. Finally, the incorporation of advanced molecular descriptors, such as molecular fingerprints, topological indices and quantum descriptors, significantly improves the accuracy and predictive ability of machine learning models [10, 12, 13, 16].

Over the last few decades, several methodologies have been developed to estimate the critical properties of pure compounds using machine learning models. For instance, Carande et al. [16] exploited a database of over 900 pure compounds, employing more than 500 molecular descriptors per compound. These descriptors were derived from information about each molecule's geometry and connectivity to be further used in the development of a support vector regression model (SVR), offering a robust approach to predict critical properties and acentric factors. The study by Que et al. [13] used a dataset of 150 compounds for the learning phase. By combining molecular fingerprints and a topological index, the authors designed a model capable of accurately predicting the critical temperature (TC), demonstrating the effectiveness of structural descriptors in modeling thermodynamic properties.

Moreover, Li et al. [11] used a dataset of 352 organic fluids containing fluorine and/or chlorine, restricting themselves to molecules with less than four carbon atoms. Aromatic compounds were excluded due to the lack of available experimental data for cyclic and long-chain structures. The aim of this study was to develop predictive models for the normal boiling point (NBP), critical temperature (TC) and critical pressure (PC) of these refrigerants, focusing on their industrial applicability. Similarly, Wang and Hu [12] have compiled a data set comprising 742 experimental data points for the normal boiling temperature (NBP) and 166 data points for the critical temperature (TC). This data set mainly includes (halogenated) alkanes, (halogenated) alkenes, ethers and alcohols, as well as a few oxygenated organic compounds such as acids, aldehydes and ketones. The authors explored the use of graph neural networks (GNNs) to predict NBP and TC, highlighting the potential of these methods for modeling the thermodynamic properties of refrigerants.

Fissa et al. [15] exploited a dataset of 223 pure hydrocarbons, representing a variety of chemical families, including olefins, kerosenes (linear, branched and aromatic forms). This data set includes experimental values for three critical properties: critical temperature (TC), critical pressure (PC) and critical volume (VC). The aim of this research was to develop mathematical models based on artificial intelligence methods, capable of predicting these critical properties from the structural information of pure hydrocarbon molecules.

Biswas et al. [10] used a dataset comprising 916 compounds, covering critical properties (TC, PC) and acentric factors (ACEN). The compounds included in this database contain hydrogen (H), carbon (C), nitrogen (N), oxygen (O), sulfur (S), phosphorus (P), fluorine (F), chlorine (Cl), bromine (Br) and iodine (I) atoms. The main objective of this research was to develop multitasking models based on convolutional graph neural networks (GCNN) to simultaneously predict the three critical properties (TC, PC, VC) as well as the acentric factor (ACEN). This approach exploits complex structure–property relationships to improve the predictions accuracy for a wide range of chemical compounds.

In this context, the aim of our study is to propose a new method for estimating the critical properties (TC, PC) and acentric factors (ACEN) for molecules containing hydrogen (H), carbon (C), nitrogen (N), oxygen (O), sulfur (S), phosphorus (P), fluorine (F), chlorine (Cl), bromine (Br) and iodine (I) atoms. Given the importance of this property, the analysis will also be expanded to include normal boiling points (NBP). The learning database used is made up of over 1,700 experimental values for molecules belonging to numerous chemical families. To improve our learning models, we have developed an original method combining ensemble learning techniques with a QSPR approach, resulting in a highly accurate and generalizable model for the thermophysical properties considered in this study.

The wide variety of chemical families and the broad range of property values observed in the dataset reveal the intrinsic relationship between molecular structures and the thermophysical properties considered in this study. QSPR: choice of a software for calculating the molecular descriptors

Quantitative Structure–Property Relationship (QSPR) modeling is a computational approach widely used in chemistry and materials science to predict the quantitative properties of chemical compounds based on their molecular structure [18]. In short, QSPR models establish mathematical correlations between molecular descriptors—quantifiable structural features—and the target properties of interest. The core premise of QSPR modeling is that structural attributes can be systematically linked to its physicochemical and biological properties, such as normal boiling point, melting point, solubility, toxicity, or biological activity. By leveraging data from a set of compounds with known properties, QSPR models can be trained to predict the properties of new, uncharacterized compounds with high reliability.

Molecular descriptors are, thus, the fundamental building blocks of QSPR methodologies, providing a quantitative representation of molecular features that capture the structural, electronic, and topological attributes of chemical compounds. These descriptors act as numerical encodings that translate molecular structure into computable variables, enabling the development of predictive models that can systematically relate molecular architecture to observable properties. Several software programs have been created specifically for calculating these descriptors, e.g. AlvaDesc [19], Dragon [20], PyDescriptor [21], Mold2 [22], T.E.S.T [23], PaDEL [24], RDkit [25], Mordred [26]. These tools provide a wide range of descriptors that capture various aspects of molecular structure, such as size, shape, connectivity, groups of Joback, number of aromatic cycles, chemical bonding patterns, and so forth. These tools support the generation of both 2D and 3D descriptors, allowing for a comprehensive analysis of molecular properties.

Quantitatively speaking, AlvaDesc can generate over 5000 descriptors, providing a detailed set of numerical representations for chemical compounds; Dragon calculates 5270 molecular descriptors, organized in different logical blocks but it seems that the code has been discontinued; PyDescriptor is a good alternative which can estimate more than ten thousands of descriptors mainly in 3D configuration; 900 descriptors are produced with Mold2, over 2000 with T.E.S.T, more than 400 with PaDEL, several hundred with RDkit and over 1800 with Mordred. For our purposes in this work, we selected Mordred due to its accessibility via GitHub at no cost, in addition to its versatility associated with a large variety of 2D and 3D descriptors. Moreover, it can be easily installed and used through a command-line interface, as a web application, or as a highly adaptable Python package, supporting major platforms such as Windows, Linux, and macOS.

Ensemble learning: choice of the machine learning technique

Selecting the appropriate machine learning model is crucial for effectively linking molecular descriptors to the target properties. In this study, we explore ensemble learning [27–29] to achieve this purpose, which is essentially a technique that relies on the combination of multiple machine learning algorithms to enhance the performance of the global learning model, and achieve a higher level of accuracy and generalization than would be possible using any of these algorithms alone. Depending on the situation and the desired result, it is possible to use the same type of algorithm to produce a homogeneous result, or different algorithms to obtain a heterogeneous result. In any event, the concept behind the assembly method is that the performance of learning models can be improved by combining them.

In short, ensemble learning methods can be broadly categorized into two types: sequential and parallel.

Sequential Ensemble Learning: In this approach, models are trained sequentially, with each successive model focusing on correcting the errors made by the previous models. This iterative process allows the ensemble to progressively improve its predictions. Boosting algorithms such as AdaBoost and Gradient Boosting are examples of sequential methods.

Sequential Ensemble Learning: In this approach, models are trained sequentially, with each successive model focusing on correcting the errors made by the previous models. This iterative process allows the ensemble to progressively improve its predictions. Boosting algorithms such as AdaBoost and Gradient Boosting are examples of sequential methods.

Parallel Ensemble Learning: Here, multiple models are trained simultaneously, often using different subsets of the training dataset (e.g., through bootstrapping). The final prediction is determined by combining the outputs of all models—using majority voting for classification tasks or averaging for regression tasks. Bagging techniques, such as Random Forest, are common examples of parallel ensemble learning.

In our study, we used the Bagging (bootstap aggregating) method. When applied to a regression task, Bagging turns to be a powerful technique for improving the accuracy and robustness of predictions. This ensemble method relies on the creation of multiple subsets of training data, obtained by random sampling with replacement from the original dataset. Each subset is used to train an independent regression model, generating a collection of predictors. The improvement in the regression model performance by bagging is mainly due to the reduction in variance achieved by averaging the predictions of multiple models, which attenuate the random fluctuations inherent in each individual model. Basically, this aggregation makes it possible to capture the central tendency of the data more effectively, while minimizing the impact of outliers or peculiarities specific to each training subsets. In addition, Bagging helps to limit overlearning, which is a common problem with complex regression models. By using different subsets to train each model, this technique promotes better generalization, reducing the risk that the final model is over-tuned to the specifics of the training set.

Description of the learning model

This section outlines the development of the learning models and the optimization of their associated hyperparameters for estimating the thermodynamic properties of complex molecules. To predict TC, PC, ACEN, and NBP, artificial intelligence algorithms based on neural networks were employed. These Neural Networks were then combined to generate a bagging model for each property considered in this work. The development framework for these ensemble models is outlined below, beginning with a feature engineering step and followed by the development of the neural networks.

Pre-processing of the data generated by Mordred

In this work, molecular descriptors were computed using the Mordred package (version 1.2.0) from molecular objects generated by RDKit (version 2020.09.1.0) through the parsing of SMILES strings. It is important to note that Mordred can only process molecules containing C, H, O, N, S, P, B and halogens (F, Cl, Br, I) atoms, proposing 1,826 descriptors per molecule.

All these descriptors cannot be directly used as input data for the learning model due to several reasons. First, for some molecules, descriptors are missing because the algorithm was not able to calculate them. Second, from a statistical perspective, it is crucial to avoid interrelated descriptors to prevent numerical bias in the model (interrelated descriptors are descriptors that contain similar structural information).

Therefore, preprocessing or transforming the features before inputting them into the model is necessary. This step may include normalization, scaling, handling missing data, feature extraction, dimensionality reduction, and addressing outliers. To achieve this goal, the following sequential steps were applied to the table generated by Mordred, which contains the numerical values of all the descriptors for each molecule (table rows are molecules and table columns are descriptors).

After completing all the reduction steps on the TC dataset, we retained 247 molecular descriptors. Following extensive testing, we found that the same set of 247 molecular descriptors could be effectively reused across all target properties without compromising predictive accuracy. It is also important to emphasize that no attempt has been made to interpret the list of descriptors from a physical standpoint. The reduction in the number of descriptors was based solely on mathematical criteria.

Applicability domain and dataset chemical diversity

Although no formal applicability domain analysis was performed, care was taken to ensure that all molecules (both in training and test sets) were described using the same set of features and preprocessing steps. All molecular descriptors were generated using the same Mordred tool. Moreover, the chemical diversity of the dataset, supports the assumption that the model operates within a representative domain of applicability. As previously mentioned, the dataset comprises a broad range of chemical families of neutral species, including aliphatic and aromatic hydrocarbons, alcohols, ketones, organic acids, amides and amines, nitriles, halogenated compounds, polyfunctional organics, and various inorganic substances. As previously stated, a key selection criterion was that molecules contain only the elements C, H, O, N, S, P, B, F, Cl, Br, and I.

Ensemble Learning—Strategy used for model fitting

The procedure described above was iterated to generate 5 global models. Therefore, when the bagging model is used for prediction tasks, the final value provided by the ensemble is the average of these 5 global models. The resulting bagging ensemble was then tested on the test dataset generated in step (a).

Hyperparameter optimization and model fitting

The ANN models were trained using the Scikit-Learn library in Python. The training of the learning models was carried out after optimizing their hyperparameters, such as the number of hidden layers (from 1 to 10), the numbers of neurons per layer (from 25 to 27), the maximal number of iterations, the learning rate, the activation function, the optimizer. The Grid Search technique [30] coupled with a 5-folds cross validation [28] was used to determine the best architecture for the ANN models. This approach was chosen due to its exhaustive and systematic exploration of the parameter space, ensuring a thorough evaluation. For both hyperparameter optimization and model training, the mean squared error (MSE) was used as the error criterion in the loss function, due to its sensitivity to large deviations, ensuring more consistent predictions.

It is also important mentioning that the Hyperparameter optimization was performed exclusively on the TC dataset. The resulting configuration was then applied to the remaining properties, as it demonstrated strong generalization and yielded satisfactory performance across them.

Physical meaning of the learning model—focus on the n-alkane family

In this section, we evaluate the robustness and stability of the proposed models, using the n-alkane family once again as a representative example. For this purpose, Fig. 8 shows the evolution of estimated thermodynamic properties as a function of carbon atom number. It can be seen that the experimental data points and the predicted estimates show strong agreement, while the learning model preserves physical consistency, even for n-alkanes with significantly high molecular weights.

Thermodynamic properties as a function of number of carbons for n-alkanes

At first sight, the model predicts a monotonic variation in properties with the chain size, eventually reaching asymptotic trends. In the case of the critical temperature, the predictions increase with the carbon count, asymptotically approaching 1000 K, while critical pressure decreases to a threshold of ≈ 5 bar as the carbon count increases. Similar to critical temperatures, normal boiling points also plateaued after monotonic growth with respect to the chain size, stabilizing around 950 K. The acentric factor grows monotonically as long as the number of carbon atoms remains below 100, then reaches a maximum before decreasing slightly. This decrease is generally interpreted as the result of the molecules wrapping around themselves.

In 2007, Nannoolal et al. [31] developed group contribution methods for estimating the physicochemical properties of organic compounds. They emphasized the importance of selecting appropriate group contribution models for estimating critical properties, particularly when extrapolating for large molecules. Taking critical temperatures of n-alkanes as an example, they showed that their method reaches a plateau at around 1100 K, which agrees with our findings. The authors also highlighted that some models, when applied to high carbon count values, make physically unrealistic predictions, diverging towards abnormal values such as negative infinity [32–35].

The work of Bychinsky et al. [36], published in 2013, described new empirical correlations for calculating critical constants (temperature, pressure, volume, acentric factor and compressibility factor) of linear alkanes based on few universal constants and the carbon count. The method proposed by the authors was developed to deliver consistent and accurate values that are aligned with the experimental data, maintaining physical consistency for extrapolation tasks (up to 100 carbon atoms). In this regard, Fig. 9 compares the results obtained by Bychinsky et al. [36] and our ensemble learning models (referred to as LRGP).

Comparison with the results from Bychinsky et al. [36]

Experimentally, data is available for n-alkanes up to 36 carbon atoms. Within this range, both models show comparable results. However, beyond this limit, they begin to diverge, making it difficult to determine which of the two is more accurate. Notably, Bychynsky’s model predicts a critical pressure approaching zero, whereas our model stabilizes at around 5 bar. Additionally, the critical temperature and normal boiling temperature exhibit a positive slope up to 100 carbon atoms, suggesting that a plateau would only be reached at very high carbon numbers. A similar conclusion can be drawn for the acentric factors.

Comparison with other predictive models

Study of Carande et al. [16]

The paper from Carande et al. [16] presents an improved method for predicting the thermodynamic properties (critical temperature, critical temperature/critical pressure ratio) of pure compounds. Using quantitative structure–property relationship (QSPR) regression and support vector regression (SVR), the researchers exploit a large experimental dataset and over 500 molecular descriptors. A genetic algorithm optimized the model, reducing the number of descriptors while maintaining good accuracy. The final model uses 33 descriptors for the critical temperature (TC) and 11 descriptors for the ratio of critical temperature to critical pressure (TC/PC).

For comparison purposes, Fig. 11 presents the results estimated using Carande's method with those obtained from our models (once again referred to as LRGP). The calculations were conducted using molecules from Carande’s training database, which comprises nearly 900 molecules.

Comparison with the results of Carande et al. [16]

On the basis of simple comparison metrics, the two models appear similar. However, our models have a greater predictive power, as they were trained using a larger dataset that included a greater number of chemical families and molecules.

Study of Que et al. [13]

This study details a new method for predicting the critical temperature of organic refrigerants. By combining molecular fingerprints and a topological index of 155 molecules, the authors improved the accuracy of machine learning algorithms (MLP and SVR) for distinguishing isomers. The accuracy with which the critical temperature values for the molecules considered in the study by Que et al. are predicted is compared in Fig. 12. The comparison is done using our LRGP model, along with the model proposed by Que et al. and various established methods for estimating the critical temperature of working fluids, including three group contribution methods (Lydersen, Joback and Constantinou and Gani) and an empirical correlation proposed by Klincewicz-Reid.

Comparisons between the proposed model and previous models benchmarked by Que et al. [13]

We observe in Fig. 12 that the accuracy provided by our model, in terms of average deviations, is comparable to that of the model proposed by Que and co-authors. We emphasize once again that the strong point of our method is that it is based on a database of more than 1700 molecules grouping together several chemical families, while Que et al. based their study on 150 molecules belonging to the three chemical families—halogenated alkanes, halogenated alkenes and ethers.

Study of Li et al. [11]

Similarly, Li et al. [11] developed accurate predictive models for estimating the thermophysical properties (normal boiling point, critical temperature and pressure) of refrigerants containing fluorine and chlorine. An approach combining the contribution of molecular groups and machine learning algorithms was used by the authors. They based their study on a dataset containing 352 molecules, excluding cyclic compounds and molecules with a carbon count greater than 5. The authors conclude that the models they developed are satisfactorily accurate, with the relative mean square error (RMSE) for the normal boiling temperature, critical temperature, and critical pressure in the dataset being 12.32 K, 21.25 K, and 0.37 MPa, respectively. Study of Wang and Hu, [12]

This scientific paper presents a method using a graph neural network (GNN) and transfer learning to accurately predict the normal boiling points and critical temperatures of refrigerant fluids. GNN links thermodynamic properties to molecular structure, while transfer learning improves the accuracy of critical temperature prediction. It should also be noted that their database mainly comprises alkanes, alkenes, a few ethers and alcohols, making it a less general model than ours.

Study of Fissa et al. [15]

The authors describe the development of QSPR-ANN models to accurately predict three critical properties of pure hydrocarbons: critical temperature (TC), critical pressure (PC) and critical volume (VC). Using a statistical approach, they were able to reduce the number of molecular descriptors to be considered to just 10 for TC values and 17 for PC values. For the 223 molecules in their database, they obtained accuracies relative to TC of 0.9948 for the R2 correlation coefficient, 6.9672 for the RMSE and just 0.7927 for the MAPE (0.9970, 0.048, 1.4261 respectively for PC). Studies of Marrero and Gani [40], and Constantinou and Gani [41]

A final comparison is carried out using Marrero and Gani's third-order group contribution models for calculating critical constants (critical temperature and pressure) [40], along with Constantinou and Gani's second-order group contribution model for determining acentric factors [41]. Note that first-order methods rely solely on decomposing molecules into distinct functional groups, while second- and third-order methods incorporate interactions between adjacent groups. This enables higher order group contribution models to consider conformational and isomerization effects, which is not possible with simplified first-order models.

Decomposing molecules into functional groups becomes a more complex task for such higher order models, which is constrained by the availability of the groups and their ability to represent a wide range of chemical species. This means that if a molecule contains an arrangement of atoms not covered by the available groups, it cannot have its properties calculated through group contribution. On the other hand, group contribution models are easy to implement and interpret, as they are a function of the occurrences of the different functional groups present in the molecules.

Both the Marrero-Gani and Constantinou-Gani models are implemented in the ICAS software (version 4.1), which was used in the following analysis. For this purpose, a large in-house database of over 5000 SMILES was considered to thoroughly explore the different methods studied in this section.

Model interpretation

Our learning model is based on 247 descriptors that best describe a molecule. These descriptors serve as input parameters for our model. The number of descriptors selected in this study is the result of a statistical analysis that enabled us to retain these 247 parameters which, from a mathematical point of view, make sense. As this sorting was done mathematically, it is very difficult to find a physical explanation to their selection and impact. However, it is always interesting to measure the sensitivity of input parameters to the output values of a model, in order to improve or measure the reliability, robustness and interpretability of models.

In this regard, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) [42, 43] analysis is a powerful method used to interpret the predictions of machine learning models. It provides global and local explanations of the impact of input variables on output values, based on concepts from cooperative game theory. The SHAP values and mean absolute SHAP values for the 20 top-performing descriptors, are shown in Fig. 20. Each descriptor is referenced by a number detailed in Table 7. This analysis shows that the 20 descriptors with the greatest impact on estimated values are linked to the molecule’s environment.

Descriptor 16 can be used as an example to illustrate the interpretation of the SHAP plot. As the value of descriptor 16 increases, the corresponding data points transition from blue to red, and their SHAP values shift from negative to positive. This indicates that higher values of descriptor 16 contribute to increasing the predicted value of TC. Note that this descriptor quantifies how atomic properties interact across a molecule depending on how many bonds separate the atoms. This means it captures how structural connectivity influences molecular behavior. Likewise, the analysis of the other top 20 selected descriptors shows that many are closely related to fundamental molecular features (such as topology, molecular size, electronic distribution, polarity, and hydrogen-bonding ability), which are directly reflected in the macroscopic properties like critical constants, acentric factor, and normal boiling points.

Conclusion

Predicting the physical and chemical properties of pure substances is a field where deep learning techniques have shown significant applicability. These advanced methods effectively address the limitations of traditional approaches, offering enhanced generalization and robustness. In this study, we present a robust methodology for predicting key properties essential in the design of chemical products and processes, including critical temperature, critical pressure, acentric factor, and normal boiling temperature. This is achieved through ensemble learning techniques, specifically the Bagging method, which integrates multiple neural networks using molecular descriptors obtained via Mordred. This approach is well-known for enhancing generalization and reducing variance, which was confirmed in our results.

The relative deviations remained below 3% for all properties studied, and the coefficient of determination (R2) consistently exceeded 0.99 in all cases. This is a particularly noteworthy result, especially for acentric factors, which are notoriously challenging to predict with high accuracy. Given that critical constants and acentric factors play a key role in cubic equations of state—enabling the calculation of various thermodynamic properties such as vapor pressures, liquid densities, enthalpies of vaporization, and heat capacities—future work should explore the impact of using machine learning-generated critical temperatures, critical pressures, and acentric factors on the prediction of pure substance properties through a cubic equation of state.
