Endovascular repair of a traumatic hepatic artery dissection and associated intramural hematoma and pseudoaneurysm
Authors: Matthew Robinson, Gina Sisti, Nathan Belkin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hepatic artery pseudoaneurysm is a concerning yet rare complication of blunt abdominal trauma, with treatment recommended upon diagnosis. Endovascular repair of hepatic artery aneurysms has gained popularity in recent years owing to performance benefits when compared with surgical repair. This case report documents the endovascular repair of a post-traumatic hepatic artery pseudoaneurysm with dissection involving the proximal celiac artery and extension of intramural hematoma to the liver.

Hepatic artery pseudoaneurysm (HAP) rupture is a rare yet concerning cause of morbidity and mortality.4,5 Owing to a high risk of rupture, treatment is recommended for HAPs of all sizes as soon as diagnosis is made,1,6 with angiography being the gold standard for diagnosis.5,7 Although there are no clear standards for the management of HAP, current Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines recommend an endovascular-first approach for all hepatic artery aneurysms—including HAP—if anatomically feasible.1,8 The most common endovascular repair strategy is embolization, with stenting being preferred for favorable arterial anatomy.5,9 Endovascular stenting of HAP caused by celiac artery dissection has been described in literature,8 but there are no reported cases of pseudoaneurysm so extensive it requires stenting from the aorta to the distal proper hepatic artery. Here we describe the endovascular repair of a post-traumatic HAP with dissection involving the proximal celiac artery that was further complicated by an intramural hematoma (IMH) extending to the liver.

Case report

Informed consent for the use of medical information explicitly for the purpose of writing this report was obtained from the patient. The patient is a 54-year-old man with a past medical history of hypertension who presented to the emergency department 16 hours after a motor vehicle collision during which he struck his abdomen on the center console of the vehicle as the unrestrained backseat passenger. On arrival, he was hemodynamically stable but hypertensive. He endorsed pain in his left abdomen and left lower back, as well as pain in his left arm and shoulder. Physical examination revealed left upper quadrant and left periumbilical tenderness to palpation without guarding or rebound.

Initial computed tomography scan of the abdomen and pelvis revealed a pseudoaneurysm of the hepatic artery. There was a proximal entry tear of the proximal celiac artery, with a pseudoaneurysm of 3.6 cm involving the common hepatic artery. The associated IMH extended to proximal right and left hepatic arteries in the liver parenchyma (Fig 1). The patient's aneurysm was deemed at high risk for rupture, and consultation with gastroenterology and transplant surgery considered open repair too risky given the extent of the IMH distally. Endovascular repair was scheduled for the following day, with a plan for an initial angiogram for assessment of the aneurysm followed by likely left gastric (and possible splenic or gastroduodenal) coiling with covered stent placement from the distal proper hepatic to the aorta. Preoperative computed tomography angiography confirmed a patent superior mesenteric artery, with adequate collateral networks, thus allowing coverage up to the distal proper hepatic artery if required.

In the operating room, right femoral access was obtained, and an aortobi-iliac arteriogram was performed. The arteriogram confirmed proximal celiac dissection with aneurysmal dilation extending up the common hepatic artery (Fig 2). The proximal celiac artery was then selected, and the left gastric and splenic arteries were identified coming off a dissected portion of the celiac artery. Multiple attempts were made to access these vessels, but were ultimately unsuccessful owing to the challenging angulation of the vessels in a dissected plane. Attempts to access the gastroduodenal artery were similarly unsuccessful. Stenting with GORE VIABAHN VBX Balloon Expandable Endoprostheses was then performed from the distal proper hepatic artery to 5 mm into the aorta. The distal VBX stent was 7 mm × 19 mm, which was postdilated to 7.5 mm. Proximal VBX stents (8 mm × 79 mm, 8 mm × 59 mm, 8 mm × 29 mm) were all dilated to 8 mm, with flaring of the proximal segment in the aorta to 10 mm.Fig 2Aortogram (A) and selective celiac angiography (B) demonstrating intramural hematoma (IMH) (black arrows).

Upon completion of the intervention, angiography revealed a patent hepatic stent with good perfusion to the liver and coverage of the IMH (Fig 3, A). A type II endoleak from the splenic artery was noted, and the operation was concluded with plans to follow-up with serial imaging. The patient was transferred back to the intensive care unit for postoperative care.Fig 3Completion angiography after covered stenting (A), day 2 postoperative computed tomography angiogram showing arterial and delayed phase (B), and maximum intensity projection reconstruction showing the entire course of the stent (C).

The patient recovered well postoperatively. Patient was started on aspirin and clopidogrel, and systolic blood pressure goals were targeted to 120 mm Hg. Repeat computed tomography angiography 2 days postoperatively (Fig 3, B and C) revealed resolution of the endoleak, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 3. He was last seen for his 6-month follow-up visit, at which time he was doing well with no new concerns.

Discussion

HAP occurs after damage to the arterial wall, allowing the creation of a false lumen between the media and adventitia of the hepatic artery.9 HAP can have numerous causes, most commonly blunt trauma, iatrogenic injury, and abdominal inflammatory conditions.5 Dissection is a rarer cause. Treatment of HAP is recommended as soon as a diagnosis is made.1 Treatment consists of excluding the involved portion of the vessel from the larger arterial circulation. This is commonly accomplished through transarterial embolization, in which a number of materials can be used to occlude the blood flow to the involved vessel.4,5 However, covered stenting is also a reasonable choice for HAP management, particularly if the anatomy is favorable.5,9 Favorable anatomy requires an understanding of the particular patient's visceral perfusion and collateral supply from the superior mesenteric artery to avoid persistent type II endoleak or unnecessary ischemic complications. Potential complications of endovascular management include hepatic ischemia; hepatic abscess; rebleeding; stent occlusion or infection; bowel, stomach, or splenic ischemia; and stent or coil migration.3,5 Considerations of collateral network patency is paramount when deciding management of HAP, especially when extensive coverage is required in setting of high rupture risk.

If there is adequate collateral blood flow, such as in an aneurysm involving only the common hepatic artery (CHA), treatment can consist of excluding the CHA proximal and distal to the aneurysm.6 In this circumstance, ample collateral flow is provided by the gastroduodenal and pancreaticoduodenal arteries. In locations that lack sufficient collateral blood flow, particularly in the proper hepatic artery distal to the gastroduodenal artery, HAP can be managed with covered stenting, but may require surgical management.2,5,6 Although unnecessary in this situation, persistent false lumen flow can be managed with retrograde coil embolization via the gastroduodenal artery. In our patient, CHA vessel occlusion was deemed an untenable solution owing to the extensive involvement of the aneurysm, beginning in the proximal celiac and extending up to the liver parenchyma. Additionally, open management options that would preserve flow to the liver parenchyma were limited as distal endpoint was unclear on preoperative imaging.

With this case, endovascular stenting of an extensive HAP with IMH involving the distal proper hepatic artery was demonstrated to be an effective treatment option. To our knowledge, this case is the first to be reported of covered stenting of a pseudoaneurysm extending from the aorta to the liver parenchyma; we could not find any reports with similar aneurysm involvement. We hope this case serves as an example of decision-making in traumatic pseudoaneurysm and also demonstrates the efficacy of a novel application of stent grafting.
