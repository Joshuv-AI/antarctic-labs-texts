Rare child primary intracranial sarcoma associated with DICER1 mutation: a case report and review of the literature

Introduction

In the 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Central Nervous System Tumors, Primary Intracranial Sarcoma Associated with DICER1 Mutation (PIS-DICER1) was defined for the first time. This tumor mainly affects children and adolescents, with no evident gender predominance. Most lesions arise in the supratentorial region of the brain. The clinical manifestations are nonspecific and typically involve central nervous system–related symptoms, including dizziness, seizures, or indications of raised intracranial pressure [1]. The diagnostic criteria for PIS-DICER1 include (1) the presence of a primary intracranial sarcoma and (2) confirmation of a pathogenic DICER1 mutation (germline or somatic). For difficult-to-diagnose cases, further DNA methylation profiling is required to validate whether the DNA methylation patterns are consistent with PIS-DICER1. Hence, molecular genetic testing plays an indispensable role in establishing a definitive diagnosis.

This study reports a case of a 14-year-old male patient who initially presented with tumor-associated intracranial hemorrhage. Postoperative pathology revealed a high-grade spindle cell sarcoma, and further genetic testing confirmed it as a DICER1 mutation-associated sarcoma. Given the rarity of this disease and the complexity of diagnosis, we report the clinical, imaging, pathological, molecular, and therapeutic features of this case and review the relevant literature to provide insights into the diagnostic approach and management of PIS-DICER1.

Case presentation

A 14-year-old male presented with dizziness and intermittent headache triggered by exercise, on April 22, 2022. Initial head MRI (Fig. 1a–c) showed a mixed-signal mass with hyperintensity on T1-weighted imaging. Due to acute deterioration of consciousness, the patient underwent emergency left frontal craniotomy with tumor resection, dural repair, and decompression. Intraoperatively, the mass appeared grayish-white, gelatinous, and soft, with indistinct margins, measuring about 4.0 × 5.0 cm.

Imaging findings in a PIS-DICER1 patient. Preoperative MRI in April 2022 showed a hypointense lesion on axial T1-WI (a), a hyperintense lesion on axial T2-WI (b), and heterogeneous enhancement on contrast-enhanced imaging (c). A second preoperative MRI in May 2022 (d) indicated tumor infarction. Postoperative CT (e) revealed intracavitary air at the surgical site. Follow-up MRI (f) in July 2022 revealed a circular solid mass in the surgical area. Post-chemotherapy MRI (g) on November 5, 2022, showed a reduction in the mass lesion. Post-radiotherapy MRI (i) on January 11, 2023, showed fewer patchy lesions compared to the pre-radiotherapy scan (h) on December 8, 2022

On May 21, 2022, he was readmitted with an acute headache. Emergency MRI (Fig. 1d) indicated hemorrhagic transformation of the residual lesion. A second craniotomy was performed, and the tumor was gray-red, firm, and hypervascular, intermixed with old hematoma and poorly demarcated from adjacent brain tissue. The patient developed a fever following surgery. A lumbar puncture was performed, and a cerebrospinal fluid smear revealed Gram-positive bacteria, suggesting an intracranial infection. Bacterial culture confirmed Staphylococcus epidermidis. The patient’s condition improved following drainage via a lumbar catheter and antibiotic therapy.

Histopathological examination revealed a high-grade spindle cell sarcoma (Fig. 2). Immunohistochemistry demonstrated focal positivity for desmin and weak SMA expression, while myogenin, GFAP, Olig-2, S-100, EMA, Syn, CD34, and STAT6 were negative. Reticulin staining was positive, and INI1 and BRG1 expression were retained. Pathology consultation supported the diagnosis of an intracranial spindle cell sarcoma of mesenchymal origin, with DICER1 mutation-associated sarcoma as a differential consideration.

The tumor cells grow in sheets and display irregular shapes. Giant cells and cells with bizarre nuclei are also observed

Subsequent extended immunohistochemistry showed a high Ki-67 labeling index (~ 80%), focal p53 positivity, and vimentin negativity. Genetic testing revealed both nonsense and missense mutations in DICER1, accompanied by missense mutations in TP53 and PDGFRB, confirming the diagnosis of primary intracranial sarcoma associated with DICER1 mutation.

In July 2022, follow-up MRI after completion of treatment (Fig. 1f) showed a round-shaped mass lesion, suggesting tumor recurrence. From July to November 2022, the patient received six cycles of alternating chemotherapy with EC (etoposide + carboplatin) and VIP (etoposide + ifosfamide) regimens. In December 2022, image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) was administered with a hyperfractionated schedule to a total dose of 60 Gy. Bone marrow suppression during radiotherapy was managed with supportive therapy. During routine post-treatment surveillance, MRI performed in April 2025 (Fig. 3a, b) demonstrated an abnormally enhancing lesion in the gyral region, with a markedly increased extent compared with prior imaging, raising concern for tumor recurrence. The patient subsequently underwent a second surgical resection and stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT), with the specific radiation dose not specified. Thereafter, alternating ICE (ifosfamide, etoposide, and carboplatin) and VIT (vincristine, irinotecan, and temozolomide) chemotherapy regimens were administered. After two cycles of chemotherapy, the patient experienced septic shock as a consequence of treatment-related immunosuppression. Following timely and intensive supportive care, his condition gradually stabilized. Chemotherapy was then discontinued, and he has remained clinically stable throughout subsequent follow-up.

Review of literature

Epidemiology and clinical manifestations

The fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Central Nervous System Tumors has recognized primary intracranial sarcoma with DICER1 mutation as a distinct pathological entity. It represents a mesenchymal tumor that does not originate from meningeal tissue. Clinical manifestations are generally non-disease-specific, typically presenting with symptoms such as headache, epileptic episodes, or localized neurological impairment. Imaging findings often show cystic-solid lesions with bleeding that may adhere to the pia mater. These lesions show low signal intensity on T1-weighted images (T1WI) and high signal intensity on T2-weighted images (T2WI), with variable amplification after contrast injection [2].

Imaging and pathological features

On microscopic examination, the lesions usually appear well delineated and consist predominantly of spindle-shaped or pleomorphic tumor cells. A distinct histologic pattern is the presence of eosinophilic cytoplasmic granularity, accompanied by immunoreactivity suggestive of myogenic lineage differentiation. Desmin and smooth muscle actin (SMA) are commonly expressed, whereas myogenin staining is only focally positive in certain specimens. In addition, foci of cartilaginous or chondroid differentiation may occasionally be observed within the stromal component. Thus, some researchers have suggested that a panel of immunohistochemical markers (SALL4, desmin, SOX2) combined with any of the following specific morphological features (such as spindle-shaped sarcoma cells with eosinophilic globules, striated muscle or striated muscle blast-like components, mucinous matrix, and chondroid differentiation) may serve as a preliminary, simple method for differentiating PIS-DICER1 [3].

Fundamentals of molecular genetics

Function and pathogenic mutation mechanisms of the DICER1 gene

DICER plays a complex role in cancers, operating as both a tumor suppressor and an oncogene that contributes to tumor growth. PIS-DICER1 is characterized by simultaneous biallelic involvement of DICER1: one allele contains a hotspot missense mutation, whereas the other allele frequently contains truncating mutations (frameshift, nonsense, or splice). subsequent “second hit” [4]. The DICER1 protein encoded by the DICER1 gene is an endonuclease that helps regulate the RNA interference pathway by converting precursor RNA into mature microRNA. Mature microRNA binds to mRNA and forms the RNA-Induced silence Complex (RISC), resulting in gene silence (Fig. 2). Mutations in DICER1 occur in the helicase domain, Dicer dimerization domain, Piwi/Argonaut/Zwille domain, RNase III domain, and double-stranded RNA-binding domain, with the RNase III b domain being the most common. The codons implicated are E1705, D1709, G1809, D1810, and E1813 [5]. The RNase III b domain processes 5p miRNAs. When it is mutated, mature 5p miRNAs become deficient, whereas equivalent 3p miRNA levels do not increase compensatorily. As a result, only 3p miRNAs are loaded onto the RISC complex, generating an imbalance in gene silencing and triggering cancer. Furthermore, hotspot mutations in RNase III b may result in the decrease of tumor suppressor proteins such the let-7 family (Fig. 4) [6, 7].

Molecular profile of PIS-DICER1 and associated mutations

DICER1 alterations are often accompanied by secondary mutations in key oncogenic pathways, particularly within the MAPK signaling cascade—such as KRAS, NF1, NRAS, RAF1, PIK3CA, FGFR2, FGFR4, and PDGFRA—as well as in tumor suppressors like TP53 and ATRX [2]. In this report, additional missense variants were identified in TP53 and PDGFRB. The TP53 gene, a central regulator of genomic integrity, mediates cell-cycle control and the cellular response to genotoxic stress by triggering growth arrest or apoptosis under conditions of severe DNA injury, thereby limiting malignant transformation. PDGFRB is a classic proto-oncogene, encoding a tyrosine kinase receptor primarily expressed on the surface of mesenchymal-derived cells. Upon binding to platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), it activates signaling pathways including JAK/STAT, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK/ERK to promote cell proliferation and differentiation. Mutations in this receptor can accelerate tumor growth and progression [8]. This patient’s initial presentation was cerebral hemorrhage, implying that PDGFRB mutations may be linked to aberrant angiogenesis and tumor necrosis hemorrhage. This clinical trait is uncommon in earlier PIS-DICER1 literature, and it sheds fresh light on the disease’s vascular-related pathological processes.

Genetic diagnosis

In patients suspected of carrying DICER1 variants, single-gene analysis can identify point mutations but often misses larger genomic rearrangements. Hence, a negative result from this method does not rule out DICER1 involvement. The advent of next-generation sequencing (NGS) has markedly improved diagnostic precision by allowing parallel examination of multiple genomic regions, facilitating detection of both frequent and uncommon variants as well as concurrent mutations. Currently, no specific diagnostic immunohistochemistry markers are available. A definitive diagnosis necessitates the detection of DICERI mutations using next-generation sequencing. In diagnostically challenging cases, additional methylation sequencing combined with clustering analysis is necessary to confirm the diagnosis. Studies of DNA methylation have demonstrated that PIS-DICER1 exhibits characteristic methylation patterns with distinct clustering profiles. As therefore, PIS-DICER1 has distinctive DICER1 mutations and a distinct DNA methylation pattern. Studies have shown that primary intracranial DICER1 mutation-type sarcomas had higher methylation levels than high-grade gliomas (HGG) [9].

Association of PIS-DICER1 with DICER1 syndrome

DICER1 syndrome is a hereditary autosomal dominant condition caused by germline or early developmental mutations in the DICER1 gene. It is also known as the family tumor susceptibility disorder associated with pleuropulmonary blastomas. Individuals with this mutation are more likely to develop thyroid lesions, pleuropulmonary blastoma (PPB), or ovarian sex cord stromal tumors, although neoplasms involving the pituitary, pineal region, or other regions of the central nervous system are far less prevalent [10]. Approximately one-quarter of PIS-DICER1 patients are reported to have germline or constitutional variations of the DICER1 gene; hence, some researchers argue that this tumor type is part of the larger DICER1 syndrome spectrum. Nevertheless, most published cases show neither inherited mutations nor clinical evidence of other DICER1-related neoplasms. In our pediatric case, next-generation sequencing revealed a somatic DICER1 variant, while germline testing was not undertaken; together with the absence of additional lesions on imaging and clinical assessment, a syndromic association was considered unlikely. Furthermore, PIS-DICER1 has histological characteristics comparable to central nervous system metastases in pleuropulmonary blastoma, including spindle cells, a rhabdomyosarcoma-like appearance, and chondroid differentiation. When a primary intracranial tumor exhibits these characteristics, more examination is required to confirm PIS-DICER1 rather than metastatic PPB or other metastatic DICER1-associated sarcomas. Interestingly, a small proportion of patients who did not have germline involvement later developed secondary extracranial neoplasms [11], showing that even somatic DICER1 mutations may carry a residual risk of carcinogenesis and necessitate extended clinical follow-up.

Treatment strategies and prognosis

Currently, no single therapy strategy for PIS-DICER1 has been established. Previous studies suggest that patients receiving greater radiation doses (≥ 59.9 Gy) have better results, indicating that the most successful treatment is a combination of surgical excision, extensive radiotherapy, and chemotherapy [12]. Fourteen pediatric instances of primary cerebral sarcoma in a Canadian cohort published in 2015 demonstrated better survival following gross complete tumor excision in conjunction with adjuvant radiation therapy and the ICE (ifosfamide, carboplatin, and etoposide) [13]. In a Peruvian study of 70 children with primary CNS sarcomas, those who received ICE-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy before surgery, followed by postoperative radiotherapy and additional ICE cycles, had the longest progression-free survival (PFS) of any treatment subgroup [14]. Taken together, these studies suggest that multimodal therapy—particularly the combination of surgical excision, radiotherapy, and systemic chemotherapy—offers clear benefit in controlling PIS-DICER1. Nevertheless, because of the rarity of the disease, its prognostic profile remains poorly defined. According to reports, around one-third of patients experience local relapse, although the majority survive to the end of follow-up, indicating the possibility of long-term disease stabilization despite the risk of recurrence [15]. The majority of reported individuals with PIS-DICER1 attain long-term remission and survival after multimodal therapy, yet early death is typically associated with quick relapse or poor radiation. High-dose irradiation (≥ 59.9 Gy) combined with multi-agent chemotherapy has been associated with longer progression-free survival, emphasizing the importance of complete resection and sufficient postoperative radiation for disease control. Targeted drug research

Dysregulated microRNA processing caused by DICER1 mutation disrupts normal post-transcriptional regulation, resulting in abnormal oncogenic mRNA expression that drives tumor formation. Whether restoring this balance through modulation of specific non-coding RNAs could provide therapeutic benefit remains to be determined. Experimental findings also show that activating the retinoic acid receptor (RAR) pathway reduces tumor cell viability, indicating that it could be a therapeutic target. Drug screening with patient-derived xenograft models of intracranial DICER1-mutant sarcomas further confirms the pathway’s therapeutic significance [16]. Despite these advances, targeted therapies directly addressing DICER1 alterations have yet to be developed. Preclinical data suggest that metformin may upregulate DICER1 expression by altering AUF1-mRNA binding, partially restoring its enzymatic function, and small compounds such as Ataluren, which attenuate the impact of nonsense mutations, may represent alternative therapy options [17]. Future research should focus on understanding the molecular networks downstream of DICER1 malfunction and identifying actionable targets to advance precision medicine for these rare malignancies.

Conclusions

Primary intracranial DICER1-mutant sarcoma is an exceptionally uncommon mesenchymal tumor of non-meningothelial origin. Its initial presentation usually involves nonspecific neurological symptoms, and the absence of distinct radiologic or histopathologic hallmarks makes diagnosis particularly difficult. This case presented with acute neurological deterioration triggered by tumor hemorrhage as its initial clinical manifestation. It systematically demonstrated the complete diagnostic pathway—from imaging evaluation, surgical exploration, conventional histopathology, and immunohistochemistry to the final confirmation of a DICER1 compound mutation via next-generation sequencing—highlighting the necessity of the “integrated diagnosis” concept in modern neuro-oncology. Molecular and genetic analyses are essential for diagnostic confirmation and therapeutic planning. To date, no unified treatment guideline exists, and the prognosis is generally poor; however, current evidence suggests that gross total resection combined with chemotherapy and high-dose radiotherapy can improve patient survival. Therefore, sustained multidisciplinary efforts are crucial for advancing our understanding of the disease and ultimately improving patient outcomes.
