Impact of emergency medical services volume on outcomes and management in prehospital traumatic cardiac arrest: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Baptiste Morcel, Youri Yordanov, Eric Mercier, Jean-Stéphane David, Tobias Gauss, Karim Tazarourte, Axel Benhamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile volume–outcome relationships for non-traumatic out-of-hospital cardiac arrest have been reported in hospital settings, the impact of prehospital exposure, particularly for the rare and clinically complex entity of traumatic cardiac arrest (TCA), remains uncertain and may influence emergency medical services (EMS) proficiency and survival. The study aimed to assess the relationship between exposure of emergency medical services to traumatic cardiac arrest cases and patients’ survival.MethodsWe conducted a multicentre retrospective cohort study using data from the French nationwide out of hospital cardiac arrest registry (RéAC) from July 2011 to March 2023. EMS volume was defined by the number of traumatic cardiac arrests managed by physician-staffed EMS per year of participation in the registry. The primary outcome was survival upon hospital admission. Secondary outcomes included return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), 30-day survival, and 30-day survival with a favourable neurological outcome. We also investigated key prehospital management variables such as epinephrine administration, intubation, haemorrhage control procedures, fluid resuscitation, blood transfusion, thoracostomy, on-scene time and transport time. A multivariable mixed model was used to account for differences in baseline characteristics.ResultsAmong 151,658 out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, 6,263 were identified as TCA (age 45 years [IQR 29;63]; 77% male) treated across 175 EMS bases. EMS volume ranged from 0.02 to 3.28 TCA per year. No significant relationship was observed between EMS volume and survival to hospital admission (OR 1.00; 95% CI [0.99–1.01]; p = 0.7), as well as with ROSC (OR 1.00; 95% CI [0.99–1.01]; p > 0.9), 30-day survival (OR 1.01; 95% CI [0.98–1.05]; p = 0.4) or 30-day survival with favourable neurological outcome (OR 1.02; 95% CI [0.97–1.07]; p = 0.4). Similarly, no significant associations were observed in pre-hospital interventions or on-scene and transportation times.ConclusionsEMS activity was not associated with differences in outcomes or management for traumatic cardiac arrest. Its rarity and lethality may hinder the acquisition and maintenance of provider expertise, highlighting the importance of targeted training.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13049-026-01622-4.

Introduction

The relationship between case volume and patient outcomes is well established across multiple domains of acute care medicine [1]. In the specific context of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), higher hospital volume has been associated with improved survival for non-traumatic aetiologies [2]. Whether this volume–outcome relationship extends to the prehospital setting remains uncertain. A recent study found no association between emergency medical services (EMS) intervention volume and survival after non-traumatic OHCA, suggesting that the mechanisms linking volume to outcomes in hospitals may not directly translate to prehospital care [3].

Traumatic cardiac arrest (TCA) is a rare event and a distinct clinical entity with unique pathophysiological and therapeutic considerations that differ significantly from medical cardiac arrest [4]. TCA is rarely associated with an initial shockable rhythm [5–7] and is typically unresponsive to conventional cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), including chest compressions [8–10] or epinephrine administration [11]. Current guidelines instead prioritize the rapid identification and correction of reversible causes such as hypovolemia, hypoxia, tension pneumothorax, and cardiac tamponade, through targeted interventions including haemorrhage control, blood transfusion, or thoracostomy [12, 13]. These procedures demand specific technical skills and clinical decision-making that differ substantially from standard advanced life support algorithms. TCA is an uncommon event, and most EMS teams encounter it infrequently, potentially limiting their ability to develop and maintain the expertise required for its management. It is therefore plausible that EMS bases with greater exposure to TCA would demonstrate higher proficiency in trauma-specific resuscitation, translating into improved survival [14]. However, this hypothesis has never been tested.

Using data from a comprehensive national OHCA registry within a physician-staffed prehospital system, we examined the association between annual TCA volume per EMS base and patient survival. We hypothesized that higher-volume EMS bases would achieve greater survival to hospital admission through more frequent use of trauma-specific interventions.

Material and methods

Study design

We conducted a multicentre retrospective cohort study using prospectively collected data from the French nationwide OHCA registry (Registre Électronique des Arrêts Cardiaques, RéAC). The study is reported following the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines [15].

Study setting

In France, prehospital emergency care operates under a two-tiered model [16]. Physician-staffed EMS bases (Centre 15) serve as centralized dispatch systems, responsible for deploying and coordinating all prehospital resources. Upon activation for OHCA, a fire brigade (FB) unit and a mobile medical team (MMT) are dispatched simultaneously. The FB unit provides basic life support (BLS) and may perform trauma-specific procedures such as external haemorrhage control, spinal immobilization, and pelvic stabilization. The MMT comprises an emergency physician, a nurse, and a driver, deployed by ground or helicopter. It delivers advanced life support (ALS) including airway management, vascular access, fluid resuscitation, vasopressor administration, blood transfusion, and thoracostomy. Resuscitative thoracotomy and resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta are not routinely performed in the prehospital setting in France.

Data source and collection

Data were extracted from the RéAC registry whose data collection and management have been previously described [17]. Briefly, participating MMTs complete a standardized case report form based on the Utstein-style resuscitation template, recording a comprehensive set of variables, including sociodemographic characteristics, aetiology of the OHCA, initial presentation (rhythm, signs of life, etc.), bystander interventions, and details of both BLS and ALS procedures, as well as all relevant time points and intervals. Patient outcomes are assessed through a 30-day in-hospital follow-up. All data are entered into the secure RéAC platform (http://www.registreac.org). The registry incorporates several automated in-line and off-line quality control checks, and a clinical research associate ensures the resolution of any data inconsistencies throughout the process.

Population

We screened all OHCA cases recorded between July 2011 and March 2023. We included adults and adolescents (aged ≥ 12 years) with TCA as defined by Utstein criteria [18] who were managed by a MMT. We excluded cases with non-traumatic or indeterminate aetiologies, burns as primary or contributing cause, signs of irreversible death on MMT arrival (rigor mortis, lividity), documented do-not-attempt-resuscitation orders or end-of-life status, and absence of resuscitation by the MMT. EMS bases contributing less than one year of registry data were excluded to ensure reliable volume estimation.

EMS volume

The primary exposure was EMS volume, defined as the number of TCA cases managed per year of active participation in the registry. An EMS base was considered active during a given month if it recorded at least one cardiac arrest case (whether traumatic or non-traumatic), ensuring that volume estimates reflected periods of consistent data reporting rather than intermittent registry contribution.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was survival upon hospital admission, selected as the most proximal EMS-dependent survival endpoint to minimize confounding by in-hospital care. Secondary outcomes were return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) at the scene, 30-day survival, and 30-day survival with a favourable neurological outcome, defined as a Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) score of 1 or 2 [19]. We also assessed prehospital process measures: epinephrine administration, endotracheal intubation, external haemorrhage control, fluid resuscitation, blood transfusion, thoracostomy, as well as on-scene time and transport time among patients transported to hospital.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are reported as counts with percentages and continuous variables as medians with interquartile ranges. The association between EMS volume (continuous, per additional TCA per year) and each outcome was estimated using mixed-effects models with EMS volume treated as a fixed effect and EMS base as a random intercept. Adjusted models incorporated covariates selected on the basis of prior literature and clinical relevance: year of inclusion, age, sex, mechanism of trauma, site(s) of injury, location of arrest, witness status, bystander CPR and AED use, FB and MMT response times, signs of life and initial cardiac rhythm at MMT arrival [5, 20, 21].

Missing covariate data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (10 imputed datasets, 5 iterations), with logistic regression for categorical variables and predictive mean matching for continuous variables [22]. The plausibility of the missing-at-random assumption was assessed by testing the association between missingness indicators and EMS volume using mixed-effects logistic regression.

All tests were 2-sided with α = 0.05. Analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Patient characteristics

The median age was 45 years (IQR, 29–63), and 4,818 patients (77%) were male. Blunt trauma accounted for 5,031 cases (80%), predominantly from motor vehicle crashes (47%) and falls (34%). The most frequently injured body region was the head (77%), followed by thorax (56%) and abdomen (35%); 3,018 patients (48%) had polytrauma. Most arrests occurred in public locations (73%) and were witnessed (78%), predominantly by bystanders (58%); 1,246 (20%) occurred in the presence of EMS personnel. Bystander CPR was performed in 2,006 cases (33%) whereas bystander AED use was rare (<1%). Median response times were 6 minutes (IQR, 1–10) for FB and 15 minutes (IQR, 8–22) for MMT. Prehospital management

Epinephrine was administered to 5,991 patients (96%) and endotracheal intubation performed in 5,639 (91%). Trauma-specific interventions were less commonly performed: fluid resuscitation in 2,328 (37%), thoracostomy in 1,349 (22%), external haemorrhage control in 617 (10%), and blood transfusion in 321 (5%). Median on-scene time was 49 minutes (IQR, 38–64) and median transport time was 20 minutes (IQR, 11–30) among transported patients. In unadjusted analyses, higher EMS volume was associated with a greater rate of thoracostomy (odds ratio [OR], 1.04; 95% CI, 1.01–1.08; p = 0.04) and shorter on-scene time (β, −0.68 min; 95% CI, −1.30 to −0.08; p = 0.03) and transport time (β, −1.20 min; 95% CI, −2.10 to −0.30; p = 0.01) per additional TCA per year. Discussion

In this national cohort study of 6263 patients with prehospital TCA managed across 175 physician-staffed EMS bases over 12 years, we found no association between annual EMS volume and survival to hospital admission. This null finding was consistent across all secondary outcomes, ROSC, 30-day survival, and 30-day survival with favourable neurological outcome, and persisted after multivariable adjustment. EMS volume was also not independently associated with differences in prehospital management practices or time intervals.

These findings contrast with the well-established volume–outcome relationship observed in hospital settings, where higher trauma centre caseload has been consistently linked to improved survival in severely injured patients [23]. Within hospital settings, this relationship is thought to reflect greater institutional experience, optimized team coordination, protocol adherence, and continuous access to subspecialty resources [24]. In the prehospital context, however, EMS bases operate with relatively standardized staffing and protocols regardless of caseload, which may attenuate any volume-related learning effect. Our results are consistent with those of Vincent et al., who reported no association between mobile intensive care unit volume and outcomes following non-traumatic OHCA [3].

Several factors may explain these findings. First, TCA was rare across all participating bases, ranging from 0.2 to 39.4 TCA cases per year, well below the threshold of 40 cardiac arrests per year commonly used to define high-volume centres for non-traumatic OHCA [25]. At such low exposure levels, even substantial relative differences in volume may be insufficient to generate meaningful differences in clinical proficiency. Second, most patients presented with features portending a very poor prognosis regardless of management quality. Asystole was the initial rhythm in 87% of cases, and the median MMT response time was 15 minutes, well above response targets established for non-traumatic OHCA [26]. In the trauma setting, this delay is largely irreducible owing to scene safety requirements, patient access difficulties, and extrication needs. The combination of prolonged arrest duration and non-shockable rhythm suggests that many patients were beyond the point of salvageability on EMS arrival, creating a ceiling effect that would obscure any volume-related benefit. Third, the management patterns observed in this cohort suggest that current practice may not align with contemporary guideline recommendations, irrespective of EMS volume [12, 13]. Epinephrine was administered in 96% of cases despite evidence questioning its benefit, and suggesting potential harm, in TCA [1, 1]. Conversely, trauma-specific interventions targeting reversible causes were markedly underutilized less frequently: thoracostomy was performed in only 22% of patients despite thoracic trauma being present in 56%, and only 5% received packed red blood cells, reflecting persistent logistical and regulatory barriers to prehospital transfusion in France [26]. These patterns were uniform across volume strata, suggesting that underuse of guideline-recommended interventions may represent a system-level rather than experience-level problem. Notably, approximately 20% of patients sustained penetrating trauma, 20% of arrests were EMS-witnessed, and 10% presented with an organised rhythm, a subgroup that might have benefited from resuscitative thoracotomy [27], a procedure not yet implemented in French prehospital practice outside research settings.

If volume alone does not drive proficiency in TCA management, alternative strategies are needed to build and maintain competence. These may include high-fidelity simulation, structured case review, and standardised TCA-specific protocols. Such approaches may be more effective than passive exposure in ensuring readiness for this rare but high-stakes clinical scenario.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first to examine the volume–outcome relationship for TCA in a physician-staffed prehospital system. The use of a large national registry provided substantial statistical power and geographic diversity. Multivariable mixed-effects modelling accounted for clustering within EMS bases and mitigate confounding, while multiple imputation addressed missing covariate data.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the interconnection of French EMS systems may dilute volume effects: low-volume bases can receive helicopter-deployed reinforcement from neighbouring high-volume bases, a resource-sharing mechanism not captured in the registry Second, using TCA volume as a proxy for provider experience is imprecise: individual physician exposure may vary within a given base, and skills acquired from managing other critically ill trauma patients are not captured. Third, several key data were missing from the registry, including the underlying aetiology of cardiac arrest, trauma severity (for example as assessed by the Injury Severity Score [28]), and details of in-hospital management. These omissions may limit risk stratification and confound longer-term outcomes. Finally, our results may not be generalisable to paramedic-led EMS systems, which differ substantially in scope of practice and clinical decision-making authority.

Conclusions

In this national study of prehospital TCA, we found no relationship between EMS volume and management or outcomes. The infrequent encounter and poor prognosis of traumatic cardiac arrest may challenge the development and retention of provider expertise. Its highly specific nature further highlights the need to reinforce training through continuing medical education, high-fidelity simulation, structured case review, and standardised TCA-specific protocols to ensure readiness in this high-stake setting.
