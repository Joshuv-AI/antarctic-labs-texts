Health-related quality of life among adults with hypertension with or without anxiety and depression: estimates from a national US data
Authors: Monira Alwhaibi, Bander Balkhi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension is a prevalent chronic medical condition that can impact health-related quality of life (HRQoL). While a relationship between mental disorders and hypertension is established, the incremental impact of co-occurring mental disorders on HRQoL in hypertensive individuals has not been well-quantified. This study’s primary objective is to examine this relationship using a nationally representative sample.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study design was employed. This research uses data from 2016 to 2021 from the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS). The study included adult patients with hypertension diagnosis who were 18 years of age or older. The Veterans RAND 12 Item Health Survey (VR-12©) was used to determine HRQoL. A linear regression model was used to estimate the predictors of HRQoL.ResultsA total of 11,773 adults with hypertension were identified. Among them, 8.0% had depression, 9.8% had anxiety, and 6.1% had both illnesses. Hypertensive patients with anxiety or depression had a significantly lower mean PCS score and MCS score compared to those without these mental illnesses. After adjusting for other variables, the HRQoL of hypertensive patients with depression (MCS: β = -6.398), anxiety (MCS: β = -4.438), and both illnesses (MCS: β = -9.211) was significantly lower than that of individuals without these conditions.ConclusionsDepression and anxiety were significantly associated with reduced HRQoL among adults with hypertension. These findings emphasize the significant mental burden in this population. Given the cross-sectional natural of this study, causal relationships could not be established; therefore, future longitudinal studies are needed to explore the direction and mechanisms of these associations.Keywords: Anxiety, Hypertension, Depression, Quality of life, VR-12

Introduction

Hypertension is a chronic health condition that requires daily antihypertensive medication as well as dietary and lifestyle changes. It is a major modifiable risk factor for all-cause mortality and morbidity, and it is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease [1, 2]. Globally, the prevalence and total burden of hypertension are growing, particularly in countries with low and middle incomes [2]. It was predicted that around 29.0% of women and 26.0% of men would have hypertension by 2025 [3]. The global burden of hypertension was projected to exceed 1.6 billion by 2025 [4]. Untreated or undertreated hypertension can lead to cerebrovascular diseases [5, 6]. Beyond its clinical consequences, hypertension poses a substantial financial burden and causes productivity loss [7]. For instance, a systematic review conducted in the United States revealed that the national cost of absenteeism attributable to hypertension exceeds $11 billion annually [7]. Hypertension also significantly impacts individuals’ health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [8, 9]. HRQoL has been defined as “how well a person functions in their life and his or her perceived wellbeing in physical, mental, and social domains of health” [10]. HRQoL is a crucial outcome indicator for an individual’s social, emotional, and physical well-being. Patients with hypertension reported lower HRQoL as compared to those without hypertension, as demonstrated by a systematic review and meta-analysis of 20 studies [9].

Mental health conditions, particularly depression and anxiety, frequently co-occur with hypertension and further complicate disease management and outcomes. Evidence suggests a complex and potentially bidirectional relationship between these conditions: individuals with hypertension are at increased risk for developing depression, while depression itself may contribute to the onset or worsening of hypertension [8, 11]. Mechanistic pathways such as hereditary factors and the sympathetic nervous system’s hyperreactivity are believed to underlie this relationship [12]. The complex interplay between physical and mental health is well-supported by the biopsychosocial model, which posits that health outcomes are influenced by a combination of biological, psychological, and social factors [13]. This model is highly relevant to hypertension, within this framework, HRQoL may act not only as an outcome of hypertension and comorbid depression or anxiety but also as a mediator that can influence the severity and course of both conditions, potentially perpetuating a cycle of poorer physical and mental health. For instance, lower HRQoL may exacerbate psychological distress or impair hypertension management, creating a reinforcing cycle.

The interaction between mental health and antihypertensive treatment adds another layer of complexity. For example, depressive symptoms such as fatigue may be exacerbated by antihypertensive medications, such as β-blockers [14]. Conversely, depression might be adversely affect the course of hypertension [12]. For example, the use of some antidepressant medications, such as tricyclic antidepressants, may cause orthostatic hypotension [15]. Additionally, depression is related to poor adherence to antihypertensive medications, a key indicator of hypertension management [16, 17]. Beyond pharmacologic concerns, anxiety and depression are associated with increased risks of morbidity, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality among hypertensive patients [18–21]. Depression and anxiety can also negatively related to HRQoL among hypertensive patients. A study conducted in primary care clinics reported that depression was related to poor HRQoL for individuals with hypertension [22]. Another longitudinal study in Germany, among 4,203 patients with hypertension reported that history of depression predicted lower HRQoL scores at the 5-year follow-up [23]. These findings underscore the importance of mental health assessment in evaluating HRQoL outcomes in hypertensive populations.

While prior research has examined the links between hypertension, mental health, and HRQoL, much of it has concentrated on either anxiety or depression in isolation, with limited attention to their co-occurrence and have been limited to a small sample size [22]. Additionally, a cross sectional nationally representative studies, particularly within the U.S. context, remain scarce [23]. While longitudinal or panel data are ideal to examine causality and temporal direction, cross-sectional analyses remain valuable in quantifying associations and identifying high-risk subgroups that merit clinical attention. Besides, a key limitation in prior research is the omission of important socioeconomic and clinical variables, such as perceived health, employment status, and chronic disease burden, which are known to significantly influence quality of life outcomes. Failing to account for these factors may lead to biased estimates and limit the ability to isolate the specific contribution of mental health conditions. Therefore, by adjusting for these confounders, our study aims to provide more robust estimates of how coexisting depression and anxiety affect HRQoL in individuals with hypertension, using a large, nationally representative dataset. We emphasize that our objective is not to infer causality, but to quantify the extent of HRQoL differences across mental health comorbidity groups while adjusting for a wide range of potential confounders. We hypothesize that individuals with both anxiety and depression will have significantly lower HRQoL scores compared to those with neither or only one of these conditions.

Methods

Study design and data

A retrospective study design was conducted using data from the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS) covering the period from 2016 to 2021. The MEPS survey is carried out annually and tracks respondents for two consecutive calendar years which enables researchers to conduct longitudinal studies. This approach allowed for the examination of changes in health status, healthcare utilization, and associated costs over time. The MEPS dataset offered comprehensive information on various aspects of the study population, including demographics, medical conditions, treatments received, mental and physical health status, HRQoL, and healthcare consumption patterns.

Study population

Adults with hypertension who were at least 18 years old were alive within the study years and had no missing data on HRQoL included. Data on respondents’ self-reported chronic conditions are found in the MEPS medical condition files. These data are coded by qualified coders and then translated into International Classification of Conditions, tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes. The clinical classification code system, which associates ICD-10-CM codes with medical disorders, was used to identify hypertension (ICD10CDX value: I10).

Measures

Outcome: health-related quality of life (HRQoL)

The HRQoL is the dependent variable, to quantify HRQoL, we employed the Veterans RAND 12 Item Health Survey (VR-12©) [24–26]. The VR-12 assesses eight health domains: general health, physical functioning, role physical, bodily pain, vitality, role emotional, mental health, and social functioning. Two summary scores can be obtained for the VR-12: the mental component summary (MCS) and the physical component summary (PCS) to quantify the HRQoL’s mental and physical health components. Higher scores indicate greater self-reported health and, consequently, better HRQoL.

Key independent variable

Anxiety and depression comorbidity were the key independent variables. For this purpose, four mutually exclusive hypertension categories were measured (Hypertension only, Hypertension and anxiety, Hypertension and depression, Hypertension and both diseases).

Other independent variable

Additional independent variables included: gender, race, age, marital status, region of residency, employment status, level of education, poverty, perceived physical health, health insurance, prescription drug coverage, chronic conditions (diabetes, high cholesterol, heart disease, arthritis, asthma, and cancer), and physical activity.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the study sample’s baseline characteristics. Chi-square statistics were used to evaluate the unadjusted associations between the hypertension categories and the baseline characteristics. ANOVA test examined the unadjusted relationship between the HRQoL measures (PCS and MCS) and the hypertension categories. In the ordinary least squares (OLS) regression model, the adjusted association between the hypertension categories and the HRQOL measures (PCS and MCS) was investigated. Prior to regression analysis, the distribution of PCS and MCS scores was assessed using histograms, Q-Q plots, and measures of skewness and kurtosis, which indicated that both variables were approximately normally distributed in the weighted sample. In all of the analyses, the significance thresholds were set at 0.05. Weighted parameter estimates and standard errors were obtained by utilizing the survey procedures (SURVEYMEANS and SURVEYREG) found in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc. 2010) to accommodate the complex survey design of the MEPS.

Results

Characteristics of the study sample

The characteristics of the study sample are shown in Table 1. The study sample was composed of 11,773 adults with hypertension; the majority of the study sample was aged 50 to 64 (69.3%), White (63.2%), and had an education level higher than high school (87.1%). Among adults with hypertension, 8.0% had depression, 9.8% had anxiety, and 6.1% had both illnesses. The prevalence of depression and anxiety was significantly higher among women with hypertension as compared to men (11.4% vs. 5.2%, 11.9% vs. 7.9%, p-value = < 0.0001). Besides, unemployed hypertensive patients had significantly higher rates of depression (12.9% vs. 6.2%) and anxiety (11.9% vs. 8.9%), and both mental illnesses (10.5% vs. 4.2%) than employed hypertensive patients. Moreover, hypertensive adults with diabetes, hyperlipidemia, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), arthritis, and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) had higher rates of depression and anxiety than those without these conditions (p-value < 0.0001).

Comparison of mean Health-Related quality of life score by hypertension groups

There are significant differences in the HRQoL score in PCS and MCS between the hypertensive groups (Table 2), with lower scores in those with coexisting depression and anxiety. For instance, hypertensive patients with depression had a significantly lower mean PCS score (41.11 vs. 47.32, p-value < 0.0001) and MCS score (43.63 vs. 49.59, p-value < 0.0001) compared to those with hypertension only. Similarly, hypertensive patients with both depression and anxiety had significantly lower PCS and MCS scores compared to those without these mental illnesses.

Adjusted linear regression on HRQoL

Table 3 demonstrates the association between hypertension groups and HRQoL after adjustment for other confounders. Hypertensive patients with depression (MCS: β = −6.398), anxiety (MCS: β = −4.438), and both illnesses (MCS: β = −9.211) had a significantly lower HRQoL than those without these illnesses. Factors positively related to HRQoL included employment, positive perception about their general health, and physical activity. For example, adults who perceived their health as excellent or very good have a higher HRQoL in both PCS score (β = 11.379, p-value < 0.0001) and MCS score (β = 6.047, p-value < 0.0001) compared to those who did not exercise. Factors negatively related to HRQoL included poverty status and concurrent chronic conditions (i.e., diabetes, hyperlipidemia, asthma, COPD, arthritis, and GERD).

Discussion

The findings of this study provide insight into how mental comorbidity is related to HRQoL in hypertensive patients and reinforce the negative relationship between comorbid anxiety and depression and HRQoL using a nationally representative sample from the US, fulfilling some gaps concerning this association. The HRQoL was compared between four mutually exclusive hypertensive groups: hypertension only, hypertension and anxiety, hypertension and depression, and hypertension and both diseases. The study found that hypertensive adults with both depression and anxiety had lower HRQoL than those who just had one of these comorbidities or those without any of these comorbidities. The burden of these mental illnesses on hypertensive patients’ HRQoL encompasses both the physical and psychological domains, which is consistent with a previously published study in a primary care clinic and included 240 patients with hypertension (with or without CHF) [22]. This study adds to the existing evidence using a large national representative of the US population, which highlights the need for comprehensive care that addresses both hypertension management and mental health identification and treatment, supporting early screening and management of depression and anxiety among hypertensive patients to enhance their HRQoL.

In this study, physical comorbidities were a crucial issue in the context of HRQoL of hypertensive patients, which predominantly affected the physical domain of HRQoL. Hypertensive adults with concurrent heart disease, diabetes, asthma, COPD, arthritis, and GERD scored significantly lower on the HRQoL PCS scale than people without those comorbidities, consistent with previous research [27–30]. Heart diseases and CV risk factors, such as diabetes mellitus, tend to cluster in patients with hypertension, which has been related to poor physical function domains of HRQoL in individuals with hypertension in the scientific literature [27–29], consistent with our study results.

Regarding other influencing factors, a positive association was found between HRQoL, good socioeconomic status (employment and high income), good perceived general health, and physical activity. Employment and physical activity have been consistently related to good HRQol as described in three studies in China among hypertensive patients, one study using data from the National Health Services Survey among 30,063 hypertensive patients [31], the other using data from the National Health Services Survey among 6145 adults with hypertension [32], and another study was the Henan Rural Cohort study, a representative sample of the rural population, among 8,128 participants with hypertension [28]. Indeed, lifestyle interventions to promote physical activity have been found to improve the quality of life of patients with hypertension [33]. A recent study using the Korea Community Health Survey from 2011 to 2019 among 996 153 adults in the general population suggested that as HRQoL levels were consistently low among the unemployed, comprehensive public health interventions are essential to prevent the decline of HRQoL during unemployment [34]. Given the high prevalence of psychological comorbidities among individuals with hypertension, integrating clinimetric approaches into routine care is especially critical. Clinimetrics is an emerging discipline that emphasizes the clinical utility of measurement tools. It goes beyond traditional psychometrics by prioritizing not only statistical reliability and validity but also clinical relevance, interpretability, and feasibility in real-world settings, and as it has proven valuable in other disease areas [35].

Study strengths and limitations

This study evaluated how depression and anxiety affected the HRQoL of individuals with hypertension using national data. In this study, many confounders related to the HRQoL were considered, such as age, gender, coexisting chronic illnesses, income, health insurance coverage, and physical activity. This study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the study’s findings. First, the cross-sectional design of the MEPS data limits our ability to infer causal relationships between comorbid mental health conditions and HRQoL outcomes. Longitudinal data would be necessary to determine the directionality of these associations. Second, information on the severity of hypertension and hypertension treatment, intensity of mental health symptoms, which can have been reported to have a negative correlation with HRQoL [36]; are not present in MEPS and were not adjusted in the linear regression. Third, this study does not involve older adults; hence, the study findings can only be generalized to adult hypertensive individuals. Lastly, unmeasured confounding variables, such as health literacy, social support, and access to care, may have influenced the associations observed and were not available in the MEPS dataset. Despite these limitations, the study benefits from a large, nationally representative sample and rigorous adjustment for multiple sociodemographic and clinical variables, providing meaningful insights into the relationship between mental comorbidities and HRQoL among adults with hypertension.

Clinical practice and public health implications

This study has significant clinical, policy, and public implications. It highlights the benefits of incorporating HRQoL assessment into patient care and decision-making, which can provide helpful information to healthcare providers as a screening tool and monitor patients for psychosocial problems. It also emphasizes how crucial it is to screen for and treat concurrent mental issues in individuals with hypertension. This will require a far more integrated healthcare (integrated medical clinic) for hypertensive patients, which includes mental health screening and treatment to facilitate early identification and intervention to potentially enhance long-term health outcomes such as HRQoL for hypertensive patients. This study demonstrated public health implications by showing that individuals with hypertension who engage in regular exercise have greater HRQoL. Taking preventative measures and education also helps patients become more aware of how vital exercise is for enhancing HRQoL for individuals with hypertension.

Conclusions

This study highlights that adults with hypertension who also have mental and physical comorbidities experience lower HRQOL. We found that physical illnesses primarily influence the physical function aspects of HRQoL, whereas mental disorders affect both the physical and mental domains of HRQoL. While these findings highlight a significant mental health burden in this population, the cross-sectional design limits any inference of causality between depression, anxiety, hypertension, and HRQoL. Therefore, future research should focus on longitudinal or panel data to better understand the causal pathways linking these conditions.
