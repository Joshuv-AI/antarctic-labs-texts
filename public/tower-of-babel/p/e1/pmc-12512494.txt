Importance of osteoporosis screening in the perimenopausal age
Authors: Kholoud Alzain, Hana Alqahtani, Hatun Alnami, Nourah Bin Klaib, Enas Bedaiwi, Ghaida Alshehri, Faiha Ayoub, Zenat Khired
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe level of awareness of Saudi perimenopausal women regarding the importance of osteoporosis screening should be determined, and obstacles that prevent/delay them from doing so should be identified.MethodsThis was a national-level cross-sectional study conducted during the months of September and October 2021. A total of 1716 respondents were women, after men and any woman younger than 40 years were excluded.ResultsParticipants’ awareness was identified by five questions, dividing the cohort into three groups. Only 22 (1.3%) participants were completely aware, 12 (0.7%) participants had no idea about screening, and the remaining 1682 (98%) participants were partially aware. The aware group was significantly associated with only being older and already diagnosed with osteoporosis (p < 0.001). The obstacles that prevented participants from performing screening were significantly associated with a lack of doctor advice and requests (p < 0.001) and with participants being worried about other diseases (p < 0.001), age (p < 0.001), level of education (p = 0.013) and monthly income (p < 0.001).ConclusionAlthough osteoporosis is a preventable disease, unfortunately, only 22 (1.3%) participants were fully aware of the importance of osteoporosis screening. Therefore, more efforts are needed from all doctors dealing with perimenopausal women to discuss and screen them.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at10.1186/s12891-025-09116-7Keywords: Osteoporosis, Screening, Perimenopausal, DXA scan, Bone mineral density (BMD)

Background

Osteoporosis is a progressive systemic skeletal disorder with low bone mass that gradually deteriorates and, if not treated, causes fragility fractures (pathological fractures) commonly at the spinal column, femur neck, or distal radius, consequently leading to cascading morbidities and even mortality [1–6]. All these risks subsequently add to the burden on an individual’s physical, psychological, and financial health [1–7].

Primary osteoporosis affects millions of people worldwide annually (secondary osteoporosis is consequence to either a medical or surgical conditions), with a prevalence of 19.7%, and in Saudi Arabia, the prevalence is even higher than 28.2% for women above the age of 50 years, because of the religious coverage of adult females in our country, as they are covered by the head’s vail and the full body’s “Abaya” leaving almost no skin exposed to the sun [1, 3, 8, 9].

Unfortunately, most high-risk populations are not aware they could have osteoporosis, which is why it is called the “silent disease” [3, 7, 10, 11]. Osteoporosis screening is simple and involves for diagnosis measuring bone density by the DXA scan (dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry) at the radiology department [5, 9] and doing blood tests to initiate treatment [8, 10, 12].

The term osteoporosis is known in the Saudi community, but the importance of screening for it to prevent its complications is not well understood. This study addresses the importance of osteoporosis screening in the target group and aims to determine the factors that prevent/delay their screening. Additionally, this study aimed to determine the role of physicians in offering and explaining the need for osteoporosis screening.

Methods

After the approval of our Institutional Review Board, the authors conducted a cross-sectional study by creating a questionnaire whose suitability and clarity were confirmed by a pilot study. It contains 23 questions, including sociodemographic information (such as gender, age, educational level, etc.), questions to assess the level of awareness of osteoporosis (will be explained in a paragraph ahead), if the participants know about osteoporosis where did they obtain the information from, the importance of screening, the participants having any other health concerns other than osteoporosis, the obstacles preventing/delaying them from performing screening, and a physician discussing osteoporosis as a disease and the importance of screening.

The questionnaire was distributed at the national level in Saudi Arabia via social media platforms (WhatsApp, Twitter, and Snapchat) from September to October 2021, which was during the COVID-19 pandemic lockdown. A convenience sampling technique was used to collect the data. The target population was Saudi females who were 40 years and older. As the natural age of menopause is defined by the World Health Organization (latest update Oct 2022) as ranging between 45 and 55 years, the authors chose females who were just before menopause, those going through menopause and who are already menopaused, they called them the “perimenopausal” group [13], who are expected to have some degree of primary osteoporosis. As the aim of this study is to address if this target group comprehends the importance of osteoporosis screening, we the authors did not specifically ask about which type of osteoporosis they have or know about. A consent form was attached to the questionnaire, indicating the purpose of the study and the right of the participant to withdraw at any time. The privacy of the participants was assured.

The awareness level of the participants was determined via the following five questions: (1) Do you believe that osteoporosis screening is important? (2) From where did you hear about osteoporosis screening? (3) Which age do you believe is the ideal age for osteoporosis screening? (4) Do you perform general health checkups? (5) Do you do osteoporosis screening? The authors created a score scale; for each correct answer a score of 1 was given, in which a score of “5” was the highest, meaning that the participant was well aware, and a score of “0” was the lowest, indicating that the participant was not aware at all, and any score in between indicated participants was only partially aware. All the statistical analyses were performed for the three subgroups.

All the data were analyzed with the IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences Statistics version 24 (SPSS Inc., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to describe the categorical and quantitative variables. For categorical variables, frequencies and column percentages were calculated. For the continuous variable (level of awareness about osteoporosis screening), we tested for normality via the Shapiro‒Wilk W test. Since the data were not approximately normally distributed, the median and interquartile range (IQR) were calculated instead of the mean and standard deviation. In addition, univariate analysis could not be carried out via Student’s t test for independent samples; one-way analysis of variance followed by a post hoc test for the quantitative outcome variable (level of awareness) was used to compare the mean values in relation to the categorical study variables, which had two or more options because the data were not approximately normally distributed. Therefore, we used nonparametric analyses to test the differences across the categorical variables. Additionally, Pearson’s chi-square test was used to assess the associations between categorical independent and outcome variables. When the chi-square test was not applicable, Fisher’s exact test was used. A p value of ≤ 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals were used to report the statistical significance and precision of the results.

After excluding women younger than 40 years of age and men, the remaining 1716 participants were analyzed. Among the 777 (45.28%) participants who reached menopause and 220 (12.82%) who were in the premenopausal period, only 323 participants (18.82%) confirmed to have osteoporosis. On the other hand, all the participants confirmed that they had some type of deficiency, either in vitamin D (1090, 63.52%), calcium (330, 19.23%) or even both (300, 17.48%). Keeping in mind, as it is a common practice in our society that persons ask any doctor they are following with to do for them general blood checkup, that is why a good number of our participants knew if they had calcium or vitamin D deficiencies, but not necessarily as part of osteoporosis detailed investigations intentionally ordered by doctors. When the participants were asked about other health issues related to osteoporosis, the top three health conditions were musculoskeletal diseases (878, 51.17%), endocrine/autoimmune diseases (709, 41.32%) and hypertension (625, 36.42%).

In this study, only 22 participants (1.3%) were completely aware of the importance of osteoporosis screening, and 12 participants (0.7%) were not aware at all; the remaining 1682 participants (98%) were partially aware of the topic. Almost all participants (1628, 94.87%) believed that osteoporosis screening was important and that the ideal age to do the screening was 40 years old by 1234 participants (71.91%). The main source of knowledge about osteoporosis screening was physicians, family and friends, but 279 (16.26%) of the participants did not even hear about osteoporosis screening.

Discussion

Many studies have focused on the knowledge, attitudes, risk factors, practices, and complications of osteoporosis worldwide, including Brunei, El Salvador, India, Iran, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, the UAE, the USA, and Vietnam, where these studies shed light on how poor the knowledge of most participants with respect to osteoporosis as a disease is [14–25]. To the best of our knowledge, no other study has researched the awareness of the importance of osteoporosis screening among participants.

In our study, the 1716 participants were distributed into three groups based on their answers to the five main questions that indicated their awareness of osteoporosis screening. Unfortunately, most participants (1682, 98%) were partially aware of the importance of screening, and this is a major concern given that the participants in this study were in the perimenopausal period and could already experience fragility fractures, especially in the spine, and they did not even know [5]. Studies have shown that patients do not know that they have osteoporosis until they develop their first fragility fracture, and some patients do not correlate or comprehend that fragility fractures occur due to osteoporosis [7, 9, 14, 15, 18, 25]. A survey performed by Giangregorio et al. of peri- and postmenopausal women revealed that although most women (89%) perceived osteoporosis as a serious condition, only 29% thought they might be susceptible [7].

The importance of screening awareness was significantly associated with only two variables: younger age and being already diagnosed with osteoporosis. Among the participants who were less than 60 years old 1447 (84.32%) and who had osteoporosis 323 (18.82%) they did possess a significantly greater level of awareness about screening with a p value of 0.0001 for both variables. With respect to the significance of age, it is also consistent with several other studies that have demonstrated that the younger the age group is, the more knowledgeable they are about osteoporosis [14, 15, 26]. Additionally, Giangregorio et al. reported that individuals with osteoporosis perceived themselves to be at a greater risk of fragility fractures than did normal individuals [7]. The other variables did not significantly differ from the level of screening awareness: educational level (P = 0.173), family history of fragility bone fractures (P = 0.731), occupation (P = 0.895), doctor advice for screening (P = 0.930) or order screening tests (P = 0.772).

Although all the participants were educated, with 1086 (63.21%) participants actually being highly educated, this did not affect the level of awareness, which is in accordance with studies conducted in Brazil and the USA [6, 24]. In contrast to several other studies, there was a significant relationship between the level of education and knowledge about osteoporosis as a disease [13–15, 18, 28]. Our results are consistent with those of a study conducted by Liza et al., which reported that neither a family history of osteoporosis nor a history of fragility fracture had an impact on the level of participants’ knowledge about osteoporosis [25]. However, studies by Barzanji et al. and Nguyen et al. revealed a significant relationship between the awareness of osteoporosis among participants when they had a family member with osteoporosis [11, 15].

Although almost all the participants (1628, 94.87%) believed that osteoporosis screening is important and most of the participants believed (1234, 71.91%) that the ideal age for screening is above the age of 40 years old, still fewer than half of them (816, 47.55%) underwent screening. A study by Albahrani et al. revealed that 40% of participants were aware of the proper age to start screening [27].

When participants were asked about obstacles preventing them from screening, the top two reported obstacles were a lack of doctor requests (842, 49.07%), followed by a lack of doctor advice (408, 23.78%). A more detailed statistical analysis comparing the obstacles and different variables revealed that the following obstacles significantly prevented/delayed the participants from being screened: being worried about other diseases (P < 0.001), lacking doctor advice (P < 0.001), doctor request (P < 0.001), age (P < 0.001), and level of education (P = 0.013).

The annual expense for the treatment of osteoporosis complications in Saudi Arabia is 1.14 billion U.S. dollars, and internationally, it is variable depending on the country; in Europe, it is 37 billion Euros (40.12 billion U.S. dollars), and in the United States, it is 20.3 billion U.S. dollars [3, 28]. The main goal is to treat a preventable disease, perform screening tests and take prophylactic measures; all these expenses can be greatly reduced [4, 28].

Among the participants who did not perform osteoporosis screening, 243 (27%) were worried about other health-related issues, predominantly musculoskeletal diseases (mainly osteoarthritis), endocrine diseases (mainly diabetes) and hypertension, which indicates that osteoporosis is a less serious condition and does not affect their health or life as much as the other diseases do. This finding is supported by a study showing that elderly women are more concerned about cancer, neurological diseases, and heart disease than about osteoporosis [24]. A survey performed by Giangregorio et al. compared the health consequences of osteoporosis to those of breast cancer and reported that they were almost equal [7].

Physicians were the reliable source of knowledge about screening for 568 (33.10%) of the participants, followed by family and friends (567, 33.04%), social media (428, 24.94%), and medical brochures (389, 22.14%). This is similar to the results of a national study conducted in Saudi Arabia, where the main sources of knowledge were healthcare providers (27%), followed by family members or friends (23.7%), internet (21.5%) and television (19%) [14]. However, these findings are inconsistent with other studies in which the media was the main source of knowledge about osteoporosis [11, 25]. Interestingly, a study performed by Okumus et al. revealed that the main source of information on osteoporosis for postmenopausal women was their doctors, whereas for premenopausal women, the main source of information was the radio and television [18].

Osteoporosis is a major health concern at many health and administrative levels. This is why the goal is to improve doctors’ practices, especially for those in charge of patients with osteoporosis complications; unfortunately, some doctors do not discuss with their patients who their complications are caused by fragility fractures due to primary osteoporosis, nor do they start them on prophylactic treatments; even worse, they do not refer them to medical endocrine physicians to work them up if there was a suspicion of secondary osteoporosis. Therefore, more attention and education should be given to all doctors dealing with adults, especially primary care physicians, family physicians, general practitioners, internal medicine practitioners, obstetricians and orthopedic surgeons, as they should discuss, recommend and perform osteoporosis screening for all of their adult and high-risk patients regardless of why these patients are followed in their clinics, particularly women of perimenopausal age. All these factors, in turn, have a substantial impact on preventing the debilitating outcomes of osteoporosis. Increasing the role of doctors in osteoporosis screening will decrease the number of underdiagnosed females and therefore manage their osteoporosis in its early stages, which will significantly decrease the financial cost to the government and people, and most importantly, individuals will lead healthier lives [28].

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. The questionnaires were not distributed sufficiently in all regions of the kingdom, and the duration was considerably short. If the study was multicenter and conducted in actual patient clinics dealing with adults as well as in patient wards, it would be preferable.

Conclusion

Osteoporosis as a disease (especially primary osteoporosis) which is preventable, is known in the Saudi community; unfortunately, most women of the perimenopausal age do not even comprehend the importance of screening for it, as they do not imagine themselves developing osteoporosis with fragility fractures and its associated comorbidities. In addition, the doctors who care for women are the main obstacle in not advising or requesting osteoporosis screening. Although the Ministry of Health has put forth a national plan to address osteoporosis and is working on it at several levels, it is the recommendation of the authors that each hospital and medical center perform their own assessments to understand the points of weaknesses and actively address them, including making osteoporosis screenings (DXA scan machines to diagnose and do blood tests to start treatment) available and implementing protocols for doctors dealing with adults, females and males, to screen them for osteoporosis. Additionally, the authors recommend individuals in the medical community to personally educate their relatives and friends about osteoporosis and advise them to screen for it, to gradually increase the awareness in the community and be a step closer to leading a healthier life.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

A written informed consent was taken from each participant in the beginning of the on-line questionnaire indicating the purpose of the study, their right to withdraw at any time, as well as the privacy of their information was assured, and if the participant was illiterate or of low education and does not know how to answer a questionnaire on-line then the legally authorized representative (usually the participant’s daughter) is the one to sign the informed consent in the beginning of the questionnaire. The on-line questionnaire was developed for this study by the authors themselves and went through content validation by seven experts in the field, as well as face validation by piloting the questionnaire on twenty individuals of the sample population, corrections were done before the distribution nationwide.
