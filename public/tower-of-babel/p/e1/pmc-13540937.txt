Exploring the value of peritumoral brain zone for classification of two malignant brain tumors based on the MRI interpretable models
Authors: Endong Zhao, Yun-Feng Yang, Hao Zhang, Yuan-Yuan Yang, Yutong Shi, Beibei Li, Xuelin Song, Shiyun Lou, Chao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to develop and validate a novel multimodal interpretable artificial intelligence model capable of fusing radiomics features and imaging features to accurately classify primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL) and glioblastoma (GBM), and to explore the impact of including the tumor and different peritumoral brain zones (PBZs) on the diagnostic performance of the model.Materials and methodsA retrospective cohort of 242 patients with PCNSL or GBM, along with their multi-sequence MRI scans and clinical data, was enrolled from two medical centers. After image preprocessing, tumor and PBZ regions were segmented as volumes of interest (VOIs), and imaging features were evaluated. Based on different combinations of three image sequences and four VOIs, a total of seven models (T1WI-Tumor-Model, T2WI-Tumor-Model, T1CE-Tumor-Model, T1CE-Tumor-Edema-Model, T1CE-Tumor-PBZ10mm-Model, T1CE-Tumor-PBZ20mm-Model, and Multimodal-Radiomics-Model) were established. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, sensitivity, specificity, calibration curves, and Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) were used to assess the models’ performance and clinical application value. Additionally, the best model was analyzed for interpretability using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).ResultsThe T1CE-Tumor-Edema-Model and Multimodal-Radiomics-Model demonstrated superior predictive performance among the seven models, with AUCs of 0.91 and 0.94 in the external validation cohort, respectively. The SHAP interpretation of the results revealed that the Rad-Score and Clinical-Rad-Score features contributed the most to the models’ decision-making process.ConclusionA radiomics framework based on interpretable machine learning is proposed. Based on this framework, models combining different ranges of peritumoral brain regions with tumors were constructed for the first time, and radiomics features were fused with imaging features to improve classification accuracy and interpretability in distinguishing between PCNSL and GBM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12880-026-02434-9.Keywords: Peritumoral brain zone, Radiomics, Interpretable model, Primary central nervous system lymphoma, Glioblastoma

Introduction

PCNSL is a rare extranodal non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma primarily affecting the brain, spinal cord, meninges, or eyes, accounting for approximately 7% of all malignant brain tumors [1]. Its incidence has been increasing in recent years with approximately 0.3–0.6 cases per 100,000 people [2], while GBM is the most common malignant brain tumor in adults [3], representing around 49% of all cases, with an incidence rate of about 3–4 cases per 100,000 people [1]. Surgical resection of PCNSL does not confer a survival benefit, and patients are typically treated with high-dose methotrexate (HD-MTX)-based chemotherapy [4], which is pivotal in achieving long-term disease control [5]. Conversely, the standard of care for GBM involves maximizing the safe resection of the tumor, which significantly impacts patient survival and quality of life [6, 7]. Therefore, accurate non-invasive identification of these two conditions plays a crucial role in guiding clinical treatment decisions and improving prognosis.

MRI is the preferred imaging modality for evaluating brain tumors. However, MRI still has limitations in differentiating between PCNSL and GBM, as they often exhibit atypical characteristics [8–12]. Atypical PCNSL presents as a tumor with interior necrosis, hemorrhage, cystic changes, or heterogeneous contrast enhancement [13–15]. On the other hand, atypical GBM manifests as a solid-enhancing lesion without visible necrosis [16–19], making it challenging to distinguish from PCNSL based on typical imaging features alone. Several advanced MRI techniques [15, 16, 20–22] show promise in differentiating PCNSL from GBM by utilizing quantitative parameters. However, further validation is needed to determine the optimal parameters, thresholds, and their consistency in identifying atypical tumors. Since not all physicians and patients opt for advanced MRI techniques, it becomes crucial to extract and quantify a large number of features that describe tumor heterogeneity between PCNSL and GBM. This enables accurate and robust tumor classification using radiomics models constructed from available conventional MRI sequences.

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a broad conceptual framework encompassing methods that simulate human cognition [23]. Within this domain, machine learning (ML) provides a data-driven approach, automatically extracting patterns from data to perform tasks like classification and prediction. Furthermore, deep learning (DL), an advanced subset of ML, utilizes multi-layered neural networks to autonomously extract high-dimensional features from complex data, such as medical images. Despite their impressive performance, the increasing complexity of these ML and DL models often turns them into “black boxes.” This lack of transparency makes it difficult to explain the clinical reasoning behind their predictions, raising trust concerns. In response to this issue, interpretable machine learning methods have emerged [24]. Interpretable machine learning aims to elucidate model decisions, enabling users to understand how the model arrives at specific decisions. With the increasing demand for model transparency and interpretability, interpretable machine learning methods will play an important role in fields such as radiomics [24, 25]. Additionally, validating model performance using multicenter data can comprehensively evaluate the model’s robustness, generalization capability, and adaptability to different population data distributions [26, 27].

The peritumoral edema in PCNSL and GBM is characterized by different pathological attributes. GBM tends to exhibit invasive growth, with peritumoral edema areas featuring both vasogenic edema [28] and infiltrative edema due to tumor cells infiltrating into the periphery [29]. The concept of peritumoral brain zone (PBZ) was introduced in the current study. It was defined as the tumor’s peripheral brain area without contrast enhancement on MRI images with a gadolinium contrast agent [30]. Peritumoral edema is only the PBZ in a narrow understanding. In contrast, the broader PBZ delineates a region several centimeters wide around the tumor, including the site of tumor invasion, specific molecules, radiological information, and cellular alterations [31]. The usefulness of detailed segmentation of the peritumoral brain region and its impact on the performance of models for discriminating between these two types of tumors remains unexplored. Given the gradual reduction phenomenon of peritumoral infiltration in gliomas, selecting appropriate peritumoral regions that better represent the information of the tumor microenvironment can have far-reaching clinical and biological implications. In this study, by segmenting the tumor with different regions of interest, we extracted multi-sequence radiomics features from conventional MRI scans, combined them with clinical and imaging features, and established an independent cohort for external validation. The focus of this study is on the classification effectiveness of multimodal interpretable models in distinguishing between these two malignant brain tumors and exploring the value of the contribution of different PBZs to the model.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the participating medical centers and adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki throughout the research process.

Patients with PCNSL and GBM who were treated at two medical centers between November 2010 and June 2023 were retrospectively enrolled through the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Complete imaging data and clinical history; (2) No prior interventions such as biopsy, surgery, or other treatments, before the MRI examination; and (3) Histopathologically confirmed primary PCNSL or GBM (IDH-wildtype), diagnosed according to the 2021 WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System [3]. The exclusion criteria were: (1) Poor image quality, significant artifacts hindering the delineation of the region of interest; (2) Tumors without contrast enhancement; and (3) A history of hematological or immune system diseases. A total of 242 patients were ultimately enrolled in the study. Specifically, 138 patients from Medical Center 1 were assigned to the training cohort, while 104 patients from a fully independent institution, Medical Center 2, constituted the external validation cohort. The overall cohort included 104 cases of PCNSL (19 atypical cases) and 138 cases of GBM (26 atypical cases). The flowchart for patient enrollment is shown in Fig. 1.

The flowchart for case admission

Imaging protocol

Scans were performed using GE Signa HDXT 1.5T, Siemens Verio 3.0T, and Philips Ingenia CX 3.0T scanners, along with their corresponding phased-array head coils. Routine scanning sequences included T1WI, T2WI, and T1CE. Detailed image acquisition protocols are provided in Supplementary Material A.

Radiomics model pipeline

The radiomics pipeline of this study is divided into the following six modules: (I) Acquisition and Preprocessing of Multisequence MRI images; (II) Evaluation of Imaging Features; (III) VOI Segmentation; (IV) Extraction and Screening of Radiomics Features; (V) Construction of Different Radiomics Models; and (VI) Model Evaluation and Interpretability. The specific process is shown in Fig. 2.

Radiomics model workflow diagram

Image pre-processing

All patient information was anonymized prior to image processing. The raw DICOM images for each MRI sequence were converted to the Neuroimaging Informatics Technology Initiative (NIfTI) format. To ensure the physical spatial consistency of each voxel across different images and minimize variations introduced by different image acquisition instruments with different parameters, all images were resampled to a voxel size of 1 × 1 × 3 mm³ using a nearest neighbor interpolation method. Artifacts were corrected using the N4 bias field correction algorithm, and image intensities were normalized to the 0-255 interval using the Min-max normalization method. Image registration and adjustments were performed using the Simple Insight Segmentation and Registration Toolkit (SimpleITK, https://simpleitk.org/) and ITK-SNAP software (version: 4.0.1, www.itksnap.org). The parameters for image registration were set as follows: mutual information was used as the image similarity metric, a rigid transformation was applied, and nearest neighbor interpolation was used for the images.

Imaging features analysis

Imaging features of the MRI images were evaluated by two radiologists (both with more than 3 years of experience in craniocerebral MRI diagnosis) without knowledge of the pathological findings of the tumor. When disagreements occurred, a physician with 15 years of diagnostic imaging experience made the final assessment.

Based on previous studies [13, 17, 32–34], the following imaging features were evaluated: (1) Tumor length: the maximum diameter of the tumor was measured on the image of the largest cross-section of the tumor; (2) Tumor volume: the tumor VOI volume parameter was calculated by the 3D Slicer’s calculation function; (3) Tumor and edema volume: the volume parameter for the combined VOI of the tumor and edema was calculated, as above; (4) Localization: supratentorial, infratentorial; (5) Tumor number: single, multiple; (6) Tumor component: defined as solid (80–100% solid component), cystic (80–100% cystic component), or cystic and solid (others); (7) T1WI signal: homogeneous, nonhomogeneous; (8) Enhancement characteristics: mass, nodular, patchy, ring; (9) Edema characteristics: mild, moderate, severe. The edema index (EI) was calculated as (V edema + V tumor)/V tumor. 1 < EI < 1.5 indicates mild edema, 1.5 ≤ EI < 3 indicates moderate edema, and EI ≥ 3 indicates severe edema; (10) Midline shift: yes, no; (11) Basal ganglia involvement: yes, no; (12) Corpus callosum infiltration: yes, no; (13) Dural tail sign: yes, no.

Volume of interest segmentation

The T1WI, T2WI, and T1CE sequences were imported into the 3D Slicer (version 5.0.2) software and manually segmented by a radiologist with three years of craniocerebral MRI diagnostic experience. VOIs of different tumor ranges were saved with varying segmentation labels and registered to the remaining two sequences. A radiologist manually modified the registered VOIs to minimize errors. The schematic diagram of the different range segmentation labels is shown in Fig. 3.

Schematic representation of different VOIs. The first row shows the original images and segmentation labels for an atypical PCNSL patient, and the second row shows those for an atypical GBM patient

(1) Tumor VOI: Tumors on T1WI-enhanced images were outlined along the enhancement edges to create a 3D volume of interest (VOI), which included cystic, necrotic, and hemorrhagic areas; (2) Tumor-Edema VOI: Peritumoral edema with a high signal was outlined by the radiologist on T2WI sequences based on the Tumor VOI; (3) Tumor-PBZ VOI: The peritumoral area was outlined using the “margin” algorithm in the 3D Slicer software, which automatically expanded the peritumoral scope from the tumor boundary in three-dimensional space, with an outward expansion range of 10 mm and 20 mm, respectively. The expanded ROI area was strictly confined to the brain parenchyma, and the radiologists manually erased the cranial bone and ventricles.

Finally, 20 patients were randomly selected for re-segmentation by another radiologist (15 years of experience) to assess inter-observer agreement. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated using a two-way random effects model, and only features with an ICC > 0.85 were retained for subsequent modeling, as this stringent threshold ensures excellent inter-reader reliability [35]. All radiologists were blinded to clinical data and prognostic results. In multiple lesion cases, only the largest histopathologically confirmed lesion was analyzed.

Radiomics feature extraction

In this study, a total of 12 different sets of radiomic features were extracted based on permutations of three MRI sequences (T1WI, T2WI, T1CE) and four VOIs (Tumor VOI, Tumor-Edema VOI, Tumor-PBZ10mm VOI, Tumor-PBZ20mm VOI) using PyRadiomics (https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/en/latest/). Each set comprised 1,762 radiomics features, all of which conformed to the Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI) standards [36]. Detailed feature categories and quantities are provided in Supplementary Material A.

Feature screening and fusion

Each feature was normalized using the Z-score normalization, and the values of each radiomics feature column were tested for normality. Redundant features were then removed using Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis. An ElasticNet regression model was trained on the remaining features, and the Rad-Score was calculated based on the regression coefficients. The mRMR algorithm was then used to select the radiomics features with the highest predictive value.

For clinical and MRI imaging features, univariate analysis was performed to compare PCNSL and GBM in the training set. Variables with P < 0.05 were selected for subsequent model construction.

Radiomics model construction and evaluation

The SMOTE algorithm [37] was used to balance the class distribution within the training set. The Random Forest algorithm, which can adapt to high-dimensional feature data and has strong resistance to overfitting [38], was employed as the classifier. Three stages of experiments were designed to address the main tasks. In the first stage, three single-sequence tumor models (T1WI-Tumor-Model, T2WI-Tumor-Model, and T1CE-Tumor-Model) were built to identify the optimal MRI sequences. In the second stage, based on the optimal sequences, three new models incorporating different PBZ ranges were developed (T1CE-Tumor-Edema-Model, T1CE-Tumor-PBZ10mm-Model, and T1CE-Tumor-PBZ20mm-Model) to explore the impact of different VOIs. In the third stage, the Multimodal-Radiomics-Model was constructed by integrating clinical factors, imaging features, and multi-sequence radiomics features to evaluate the added value of multimodal data.

For the first two stages, the selected radiomics features and Rad-Score were inputted into the RF algorithm for model training. For the multimodal model, a new fusion feature, the Clinical-Rad-Score, was generated using LASSO regression to integrate radiomics features, imaging features, and clinical factors. Finally, the mRMR algorithm selected the top 10 most predictive features to train the multimodal radiomics model, aiming to capture complementary information across modalities and improve classification accuracy.

During RF model training, hyperparameters were optimized via GridSearchCV to minimize both training and generalization errors. Model performance was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, and the area under the curve (AUC). Additionally, calibration and decision curves were plotted to assess the model calibration and clinical utility. To ensure model interpretability, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) [39] values were calculated for each features. These values were then visualized using scatter plots to intuitively demonstrate each feature’s contribution to the final prediction.

Statistics

Clinical information and macroscopic imaging features were statistically analyzed using R version 4.3.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed, or as median (interquartile range, IQR) if non-normally distributed. Group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The “sklearn.metrics” Python library was used to calculate the AUC metrics of the model. The “sklearn.calibration”, “sklearn.metrics,” and “matplotlib.pyplot " libraries were used to calculate and plot calibration curves and decision curves for the model.

Results

Patients characteristics and clinical model

Supplementary Material B details the demographics and imaging features. In the training cohort, significant differences between PCNSL and GBM were found in tumor length, tumor volume, tumor number, tumor component, T1WI signal, enhancement characteristics, edema degree, and basal ganglia involvement (all P < 0.05). Notably, tumor component, T1WI signal, enhancement characteristics, edema degree, and basal ganglia involvement remained statistically significant across all three cohorts (training, external validation, and overall cohorts; all P < 0.05).

Supplementary Material E provides the results of univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses of MRI features in the training set. The three independent predictive factors selected, namely tumor number, enhancement characteristics, and involvement of the basal ganglia, were used to construct a clinical prediction model through logistic regression. For the external validation set, the model achieved an AUC of 0.79 (95% CI: 0.71, 0.88), a sensitivity of 0.69, a specificity of 0.82, and an accuracy of 0.75.

Feature screening

For the multimodal model, 536 features were retained from the initial 5286 features after screening with ICC, Pearson, and Spearman correlation analyses. The ElasticNet training process is shown in Fig. 4a, and the Rad-Score was calculated based on the regression coefficients. The 10 radiomics features initially selected by the mRMR algorithm and the eight imaging features identified via statistical analysis in the training set were combined. The Clinical-Rad-Score was calculated using LASSO regression. Subsequently, 10 multimodal features (including the Rad-Score, two imaging features, and seven radiomics features) were selected by a second round of screening using the mRMR algorithm. Figure 4b demonstrates the training process of the LASSO regression, where the training error was significantly reduced after adding the imaging features. The final obtained multimodal features and fusion features (the Rad-Score and Clinical-Rad-Score) were used to train the model. Detailed information on the screening results for the other model features is provided in Supplementary Material A.

Performance of models

In this study, three stages of experiments were conducted, and a total of seven models were ultimately constructed. In the first stage, three tumor models with different sequences were evaluated on the external validation set, and the T1CE-Tumor-Model showed the best performance with an AUC of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.74, 0.89), a sensitivity of 0.68, a specificity of 0.94, and an F1-value of 0.78. This is shown in Fig. 5a. In the second stage, based on the optimal sequence (i.e., T1CE), three different ranges of PBZs were used for model construction, and the T1CE-Tumor-Edema-Model performed the best on the external validation set, with an AUC of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.84, 0.96), a sensitivity of 0.73, a specificity of 0.88, and an F1-value of 0.80, as shown in Fig. 5b. In the third stage, the Multimodal-Radiomics-Model showed optimal performance with an AUC of 0.94 (95%CI:0.87, 0.98) on the external validation set, a sensitivity of 0.70, a specificity of 0.96, and an F1-value of 0.80, which is shown in Fig. 5c. Supplementary Material C provides the evaluation metrics of all models on the external validation set.

Discussion

For clinical information and imaging features, the findings of this paper are consistent with previous literature [13, 17, 33, 34]. The imaging features of PCNSL differ from GBM, mainly due to their different biological behaviors. GBM usually presents as a giant isolated tumor with a more inhomogeneous signal and more cystic components. However, PCNSL is more prone to multifocal lesions and is more prevalent in the basal ganglia and infratentorial regions than GBM. Typical PCNSL shows homogeneous mass enhancement, which may be attributed to the angiophilic nature of PCNSL cells. This leads to extensive destruction of tumor blood vessels, resulting in significant enhancement due to massive leakage of intravascular contrast agent. In contrast, typical GBM exhibits ring-like enhancement due to its invasiveness and high risk of cystic degeneration, necrosis, and hemorrhage. With increasing incidence and detection rates, these tumors present overlapping imaging features on MRI, the first modality of choice, and increasingly atypical imaging presentations, making accurate identification difficult. This study utilized a clinical prediction model built on three independent predictive factors (tumor number, enhancement characteristics, and basal ganglia involvement), achieving an AUC of 0.79. The majority of cases where the model failed in prediction were concentrated in atypical tumors, indicating limitations in the diagnostic value of MRI macroscopic features. Radiologists may struggle to accurately differentiate them based on past experiences alone, highlighting the importance of significantly improving diagnostic performance through alternative means. In a previous study, Suh HB et al. [19] constructed a model only for patients with typical PCNSL and atypical GBM. However, its diagnostic stability and clinical applicability for typical GBM are questionable. In the study by Kang D et al. [14], where atypical lymphomas were included for training, the diagnostic performance of the model decreased considerably in the external validation set.

In the multimodal radiomics model proposed in this study, the training set and external validation set containing typical and atypical tumors still maintained a high AUC (0.94), demonstrating that the model has strong generalization capability and clinical utility. In addition, we compared the predictive efficacy of models based on separate T1WI, T2WI, and T1CE sequences. We found that the diagnostic effectiveness of the radiomics model based on T1CE was always superior to that of the models based on T1WI and T2WI. Notably, other similar studies have also demonstrated that the best performance was achieved with the highest AUC for the T1CE-based model in both the single-sequence radiomics models [11] and the single-sequence convolutional neural network (CNN) models [12]. Therefore, this study addresses another challenge in modeling T1CE radiomics, namely the selection of the coverage of VOIs, which determines the inclusion range of imaging features related to tumor pathology heterogeneity. We introduced the PBZ in a narrow and broad sense [30, 31], where peritumoral edema and peritumoral brain parenchyma regions both provide potential radiological information. Therefore, we uniformly expanded 10 and 20 mm from the enhancing tumor margins as the region of interest. An autopsy study by Yamahara et al. [40] revealed that tumor cells are densely present within 1 cm from the tumor margin in GBM patients. Hochberg FH et al. [41] described that 90% of GBM recur within 2 cm of the primary site on imaging. Specifically, the 10-mm margin was established as an internal boundary to divide the peritumoral space into proximal and distal zones. The proximal zone (within 10 mm) captures the region with the highest tumor cell density, while the 20-mm margin aligns with the clinically high-risk zone for infiltration and recurrence. Intermediate distances or dynamic margins were not considered to uphold the principles of methodological robustness and model parsimony. Narrow bands (e.g., 5 mm) are highly susceptible to partial volume effects, which compromise feature reproducibility, whereas further subdivision (e.g., 15 mm) would unnecessarily increase feature dimensionality. In our study, we found that the combined tumor and peritumoral edema model achieved the highest AUC (0.91), followed by the combined tumor and PBZ 10 mm model, the combined tumor and PBZ 20 mm model, and finally the tumor model. This suggests that peritumoral edema contains more biologically heterogeneous information, and GBM and PCNSL have different vascular properties. The microenvironment of edema in GBM and PCNSL suggests the emergence, aggregation, and alteration of neuroglia cells, vascular endothelial cells, astrocytes and microglia, and tumor cells to varying degrees. In addition, the role of some inflammatory changes remains to be further explored [42]. The 20 mm mask of the tumor and PBZ was less diagnostic for the model than the 10 mm mask, but better than the tumor alone. This suggests that the PBZ also contains information that distinguishes between PCNSL and GBM [18]. This is likely due to the gradual decrease in tumor infiltration into the surrounding tissues, meaning heterogeneous cells are mainly concentrated near the tumor surface. Consequently, a wider peritumoral range includes too much normal tissue, which over-dilutes the microenvironmental information and introduces more noise. Exactly how many centimeters of PBZ can yield the greatest model performance gain needs to be explored in our subsequent studies.

A recently published study by Yu et al. [43] evaluated radiomics models for differentiating PCNSL and GBM. While they appropriately recognized the diagnostic value of both contrast-enhancing and peritumoral edema regions, their framework evaluated individual modalities independently. Consequently, their highest-performing model remained constrained to a single sequence (CE-T1WI total region), achieving an AUC of 0.917 in the external validation set. The performance of such single-sequence radiomics models is inherently limited because they cannot capture comprehensive information from the overall imaging data. In contrast, the core advantage of our study lies in true multimodal integration. We explicitly fused high-dimensional multimodal radiomics features with clinical semantic features to construct the Clinical-Rad-Score. This comprehensive fusion captures a more holistic tumor profile, allowing our model to achieve a superior external AUC of 0.940. The Clinical-Rad-Score serves as the dominant factor driving accurate individual-level predictions. It should be noted that the initial extraction of 5,286 features relative to the sample size introduces a potential risk of overfitting. Although we mitigated this concern by implementing a rigorous step-by-step dimensionality reduction pipeline to select only 10 key features and validating the model on an independent external cohort, nested cross-validation can be employed to more effectively minimize feature selection bias and further ensure model stability. Furthermore, MRI images with different field strengths were included in both the training and external validation sets, which not only broadens the diversity of the data but also more closely resembles real-world clinical scenarios, thereby providing the trained model with stronger generalization ability.

A recent comprehensive meta-analysis on AI-based brain tumors confirmed the highly robust diagnostic capabilities of various ML and DL algorithms [44]. However, a persistent challenge in the broader field of AI-driven neuro-oncology is that the “black-box” nature of many high-performing models often limits clinical trust and translation. To address this critical trade-off between high performance and transparency, the SHAP approach applied in this paper is a standard interpretable machine learning method. Based on Shapley values from game theory, this method can quantify the impact of each feature on the prediction results and generate SHAP summary plots to show the contribution and importance of different features. The SHAP analysis of the multimodal radiomics model reveals the underlying drivers of the model’s predictions and suggests which features are of great value for the prediction. Moreover, the feature fusion technique proposed in this study introduces new features (Rad-Score and Clinical-Rad-Score) for model training, capturing more complex feature relationships and further helping the model better understand the data. The Random Forest algorithm can further combine these fusion features when integrating multiple decision trees to improve the model’s performance [45]. Additionally, we observed that among the selected radiomics features in the multimodal model, there were four features describing the distribution of voxel intensities (First Order features) and two feature categories quantifying the gray level of the images (Gray Level Run Length Matrix and Gray Level Dependence Matrix). This suggests that subtle differences in voxel intensity distribution and grayscale changes can help to better predict heterogeneity within the tumor and in the peritumoral region.

There are a few limitations to this study that should be noted. Firstly, this study lacked a comparative trial of diagnostic performance by expert radiologists, ideally both with and without AI assistance, to observe the incremental clinical benefits of using the model in a real-world setting. This limits our current ability to directly quantify the model’s added clinical value. Secondly, the model developed in this study is only applicable to tumor binary classification tasks. Further development of a stable multi-class tumor classification model is needed for real-world clinical scenarios. Thirdly, although our multimodal model demonstrated strong overall performance, the sample size of atypical cases in our cohort remains relatively small. This limits the robustness of subgroup analyses specifically targeting these subtypes. Future multi-center studies with larger, dedicated cohorts of atypical cases are necessary to further validate our model’s performance in these complex clinical scenarios. Finally, manual VOI segmentation is labor-intensive, which limits large-scale clinical scalability. Given that automated methods, particularly deep learning-based segmentation, have demonstrated high feasibility for brain tumor delineation, integrating these tools into future clinical workflows is essential to overcome efficiency bottlenecks and ensure rapid, reproducible results.

Conclusion

This study proposes a multimodal interpretable machine learning radiomics research framework, explores the impact of multimodal information on model prediction performance, and investigates the performance of radiomics models based on the tumor and different peritumoral inclusion ranges for discriminating between lymphomas and gliomas, two types of malignant brain tumors. By providing clinicians with a clearer understanding of the model’s decision-making process, this framework can increase its clinical acceptance and usefulness.
