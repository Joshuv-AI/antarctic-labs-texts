Rethinking LC-type in Young-Burgess classification of pelvic fracture: A retrospective study
Authors: Yang Gao, Zhiyuan Lou, Xin Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lateral injuries are the most common type of pelvic fracture, and the Young-Burgess classification system is the most widely used system for classifying pelvic fractures. However, as the complexity of clinical cases increases, the Lateral Compression (LC) subtypes in the Young-Burgess classification system can no longer meet the practical needs. This study aimed to reassess the validity of the LC types by exploring clinical cases that the LC subtypes could not categorize. This study was a retrospective analysis of patients with pelvic fractures admitted to our trauma center between January 2019 and December 2024. Inclusion criteria included age between 18 and 80 years, high-energy injury, closed pelvic fracture, and complete preoperative X-ray and computed tomography images. Preoperative X-ray and computed tomography imaging were used to assess fracture displacement and sacroiliac joint injury. Two senior orthopedic surgeons performed the Young-Burgess classification of all patients, and 3 new LC subtypes were proposed after an in-depth discussion of those cases in which the Young-Burgess system could not be applied for classification. A total of 148 patients were included in the study: 96 (64.9%) males and 52 (35.1%) females, with an average age of 48 years (range 18–78 years). The most common trauma mechanism was a traffic accident. 58.8% of the patients showed LC-type injuries, of which 39.2% were LC1, 18.2% LC2, and 1.4% LC3. Additionally, 6.7% of patients had anterior posterior compression-type injuries, 7.5% vertical shear-type injuries, and 6.7% combined mechanism-type injuries. Thirty patients (20.3%) could not be classified according to the current Young-Burgess classification. All unclassifiable fracture types could be attributed to LC injuries, but no corresponding subtype could be found in the existing classification. Specifically, there were 3 main types: comminuted iliac wing fractures in 12 cases, unilateral LC1 combined with LC2 fractures in 10 cases, and bilateral LC1-type fractures in 8 cases. This study provides increasing evidence that the types of fractures exhibit diversity in the context of pelvic lateral injuries. It is necessary to appropriately increase the subtypes of the LC classification to enrich the Young-Burgess classification system, thus more effectively guiding clinical practice.

1. Introduction

Pelvic fractures are a common form of trauma, and studies have shown that such fractures occur in approximately 3.0% to 8.0% of patients following trauma. Patient presentations range from clinically insignificant fractures to life-threatening pelvic ring fractures with massive hemorrhage.[1] Although only 7% to 13% of patients with pelvic fractures exhibit hemodynamic instability, the mortality rate exceeds 30%.[2–4] Clinicians should pay adequate attention to pelvic fractures, and preoperative diagnosis of pelvic fracture type is essential in patients’ management and prognosis.[5,6] The currently widely used classification of pelvic fractures is the Young-Burgess classification system, which was established in 1986 by Young and Burgess to categorize pelvic fractures into 4 types based on injury mechanism: Lateral Compression (LC), Anterior Posterior Compression (APC), vertical shear, and Combined Mechanism (CM).[7,8] Of these, the LC-type is the most common, accounting for approximately 80% of all pelvic fractures.[8,9] The research found that as high as 21.56% of patients failed to use the Young-Burgess classification for pelvic fractures (such as those involving an intact posterior arch and Type A fractures in the Tile system), and the proportion was even higher in severe pelvic fractures.[10,11] To the best of our knowledge, there are currently no studies detailing fractures that cannot be classified by typical Young-Burgess classification. Therefore, we must reevaluate and rethink the Young-Burgess classification. This article particularly emphasizes the need to revisit the LC classification in light of clinical realities.

2. Methods

The First Affiliated Hospital Ethics Committee of Dalian Medical University approved this retrospective study. Due to the anonymous clinical data analysis, the Ethics Committee waived the requirement for patient approval or informed consent. A total of 148 patients with high-energy pelvic fractures were enrolled in our trauma center between January 2019 and December 2024. Inclusion criteria were as follows: 18 to 80 years of age, high-energy injury, closed pelvic ring fracture, complete preoperative radiographs, and computed tomography (CT) images. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) below 18 years old; (2) a history of surgical treatment for pelvic fractures; (3) pathological and osteoporotic fractures; (4) old fractures or nonunion; (5) low-energy injuries or avulsion fractures; (6) incomplete imaging data.

This study focuses on the classification of pelvic fractures and invites 2 experienced surgeons with senior titles who have accumulated extensive expertise in pelvic treatment to independently view each patient’s radiographs. Each surgeon considered both X-ray and CT and then gave a single classification. The Young-Burgess classification system was used to determine pelvic fracture type. If the 2 surgeons’ opinions differ, the senior surgeon will make the final judgment. Young-Burgess classification focuses on injury mechanisms, and correct classifying requires certain clinical experience, so radiologists were not invited in this study. 4. Discussion

We believe that the incidence of fractures that cannot be classified by typical Young-Burgess classification warrants attention. The reason for this phenomenon is that the Young-Burgess classification system emphasizes LC1 and LC2 as unilateral independent lesions in LC classification, and only LC3 has bilateral characteristics (ipsilateral disruption of LC1 or LC2 injury, contralateral APC-type injury, so-called wind-sweep pelvis).

An efficient fracture classification system enhances communication among surgeons and offers explicit direction for treating patients.[13] Therefore, the classification system should be extremely reliable, cover the vast majority of fracture types, maintain good coordination among interobserver and intraobserver, and be closely linked to surgical guidance.[14–17] The Young-Burgess classification system, as a classical classification of pelvic fractures, has been widely adopted by clinicians. As clinical cases become more complex, some scholars realize that certain categories need to be further refined to provide more precise treatment directions. In 2007, Day noted that LC2 pelvic fractures are complex fractures and proposed 3 different subtypes of this type based on damage to the sacroiliac joint.[18] In 2009, Lefaivre conducted an in-depth study of 100 LC1 fractures and their CT features, summarizing 3 subtypes according to the different regions of sacral fractures.[19] In 2021, Kuršumović explored 2 subtypes of LC1 pelvic fractures: high-energy and low-energy fractures (FFP: fragility fractures of the pelvis) due to differences in functional needs and treatment between these 2 groups of patients.[20] In our study, we did not classify fractures based on these subtypes, as these subtypes are further subdivisions of existing LC subtypes, which requires a prerequisite that fracture types must fit into existing LC classification systems. This study strictly classified pelvic fractures according to the original Young-Burgess classification system.

The results showed that 20.5% of patients had pelvic fractures that could not be classified into Young-Burgess classification and presented 3 main types: comminuted iliac wing fractures (8.1%, 12/148), unilateral LC1 combine LC2 fractures (6.8%, 10/148), and bilateral LC1 fractures (5.4%, 8/148). Essentially, LC3 belongs to the CM-type, a combination of LC and APC injuries. Furthermore, the 142 patients with pelvic fractures included in the preliminary study may have certain biases, as some types of fractures (involving complete posterior arch and type A fractures in the Tile system) cannot be accurately classified using the Young-Burgess system. Iliac wing fractures, classified as Tile A2 fractures, are usually caused by direct violence to the iliac wing. Due to being an extra-articular fracture that does not affect the stability of the pelvic ring, scholars have often favored non-surgical treatment. For patients with significant displacement of fractures or those whose pain does not improve after 2 to 3 weeks of conservative treatment, surgical treatment remains a viable option.[21] The iliac bone has many muscle attachment points, and various deforming forces may lead to postfracture pain and potentially affect the balance of the lumbopelvic region.[22] Therefore, for comminuted fractures of the iliac wing, although the posterior aspect of the pelvic ring is stable, proactive surgical treatment can alleviate patient pain, promote early mobilization, maintain lumbopelvic balance, and minimize the risk of malunion.[23] There has always been controversy regarding the treatment of LC1. Most scholars believe that non-displaced sacral fractures can be treated conservatively, while displaced sacral fractures should be treated surgically.[20,24,25] There is a strong correlation between increased surgical tendency and sacral displacement of ≥ 2 mm or involvement of 3 columns of the sacrum.[26] Studies have confirmed that in clinical practice, displacement of fractures is often underestimated in pelvic lateral injuries, with peak pelvic compressive displacement being 1.3 to 2.2 times the last compression observed on hospital images, and clinicians should adequately estimate displacement and related injuries.[27] However, when crescent fractures involving the sacroiliac joint occur in LC1, combined with LC2, surgical intervention is a more reasonable choice.

Research indicates that the risk of arterial injury is significantly increased in patients with bilateral pelvic injuries compared to those with unilateral injuries.[28] For patients with bilateral LC1 fractures, our study shows that such injuries typically result from high-energy forces, leading to complete sacral fractures. The bilateral pelvic ring’s posterior aspect is unstable, so surgical treatment should be actively considered. Moreover, these fractures are often associated with injuries in other regions, so careful consideration should be given during patient evaluations.

Therefore, we recommend adding these 3 subtypes to the LC category of the Young-Burgess classification system to enhance the richness of the Young-Burgess classification system, thereby facilitating scholars’ learning and application.

This study has several limitations. It is a retrospective single-center study. In addition, since only 2 experienced surgeons were selected, the generalizability of the findings is limited.

5. Conclusions

This study provides increasing evidence that the types of fractures exhibit diversity in the context of pelvic lateral injuries. It is necessary to appropriately increase the subtypes of the LC classification to enrich the Young-Burgess classification system, thus more effectively guiding clinical practice.
