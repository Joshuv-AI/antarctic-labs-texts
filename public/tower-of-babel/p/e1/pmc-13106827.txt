Not all task-unrelated thoughts (TUT) are created equal - TUT characteristics as predictors of affective states and heart-rate variability
Authors: Michał S Skorupski, Izabela Krejtz, Steven Barnes, Celine Baeyens, Thierry Atzeni, Monika Kornacka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTask-Unrelated Thoughts (TUT) are a prevalent transdiagnostic phenomenon, robustly associated with both adaptive and maladaptive outcomes. However, it is still not clear which factors determine the maladaptive outcomes of TUT. In this study, we focused on analysing the role of a wide spectrum of TUT characteristics in everyday functioning, specifically their associations with momentary affective states, depressive symptoms, heart rate variability, and sleep quality. Forty-seven participants took part in a seven-day ecological momentary protocol, completing momentary and daily questionnaires alongside continuous ECG monitoring. Additionally, trait-level measures of mind-wandering, repetitive negative thinking, and depressive and anxiety symptoms were collected using self-report questionnaires. The results show that TUT Emotional Appraisal—especially perceived burden and thought valence—were significant predictors of affective momentary outcomes. In contrast, perceived usefulness of TUT significantly predicted depressive symptoms at the daily level. Part of the associations between TUT characteristics and outcomes was moderated by individual differences, such as tendency to engage in mind-wandering or repetitive negative thinking, as well as trait-level depressive and anxiety symptoms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42261-0.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Everybody knows the feeling of drifting away mid-task, suddenly realizing that their mind has wandered away from their current activity. Stimulus-independent task-unrelated thoughts (TUT) – thoughts directed away from the ongoing focal activity or situation without any direct environmental requirement1,2- have been reported to occupy nearly half of our daily thought content3. Although the term TUT can also be used to describe stimulus-dependent thought, in the present paper we focus on stimulus-independent thinking without any direct relation to the here and now4 following previous research suggesting that these two types of cognition may operate differently5. As a complex transdiagnostic process, TUT play a role in various conditions, including anxiety6, depression7, ADHD8, and dissociative disorders9. Past studies suggest that TUT can be associated with a decline in mood3 and attention10. At the same time, they have been shown to play an important role in facilitating creative processes11, planning future events12, delaying gratification13, and in the development of socio-emotional skills14.

Which factors are responsible for the widely varying cognitive and emotional outcomes associated with TUT? It is challenging to answer this question, in part due to the inconsistent terminology and numerous theoretical frameworks used within the field15. The umbrella term task-unrelated thoughts defined above1,16 encompasses terms such as daydreaming17, mind-wandering18, spontaneous thoughts19, and self-generated thinking20, but also rumination21, repetitive negative thoughts7, and perseverative cognition22. The discussions regarding how to distinguish between different types of TUT, which of the aforementioned terms can be used interchangeably, and which represent overarching categories that include more specific subtypes, seems to be far from over15,20. Additionally, measuring TUT, particularly in the ecological context, when participants perform different sets of tasks (including resting) is a significant challenge2,23.

In the heat of the ongoing debate on the definition and differentiation of TUT types, Seli et al.16 proposed treating all TUT phenomena as one heterogeneous construct. This family resemblance approach enables us to focus on different thought characteristics as variables linked to both the adaptive and maladaptive outcomes, with less attention directed to the categorisation of the thought. Although this perspective risks overlooking some of the debates regarding TUT classification, prioritizing the detection of maladaptive TUT’s features is vital for clinical applications. It may represent the only viable way to push maladaptive TUT research forward, regardless of the continuing definitional controversy24. Although in their original paper Seli et al.14 used the term mind-wandering to describe a higher-order category encompassing all types of TUT, we propose using the more general term task-unrelated thinking to better capture the family resemblance perspective. This nomenclature is particularly important for avoiding confusion in the context of the debate on the distinction between mind-wandering and daydreaming20, both of which constitute specific forms of TUT. Importantly, the heterogeneity in defining TUT and its subtypes is also reflected in empirical research, as definitions of TUT, mind-wandering, daydreaming, and rumination are not applied consistently across studies. Notably, Seli et al.14 subsequently referred to this conceptualization explicitly as “the TUT definition of mind-wandering” (p. 480), further underscoring the close conceptual overlap between mind-wandering and task-unrelated thinking. He and his co-authors also admit that some of the MW definitions are exclusive (and refers only to unguided, freely-moving thinking). Therefore, in the present study we adopt a broad definition of TUT and focus on specific characteristics of TUT to determine which features may be linked to its maladaptive consequences, an issue that appears especially relevant for clinical application.

The effects of certain TUT characteristics have been relatively well explored. First, the literature suggests that thought valence is largely responsible for the affective outcome of TUT, showing that not only are TUT linked generally to poorer mood, but this effect is increased for negatively valenced thoughts25. However the role of TUT valence is nuanced by the studies exploring TUT as an avoidance strategy suggesting that its effect on emotion regulation might be different in short vs. long term perspective26. Second, intentional TUT are associated with lower depressive and anxiety symptoms than their unintentional version27. Third, thoughts considered more freely moving are associated with better mood28. Further, future related thoughts may be linked to more positive affect than past-related thoughts29. Finally, definitional characteristics of repetitive negative thinking (RNT) - repetitiveness, intrusiveness, negative valence, and uncontrollability - are all associated with depressive and anxiety symptoms30. In 2023, Thiemann et al.24 examined both the intentionality and the freely moving nature of thoughts in a single study and found that each of these characteristics mitigates the negative relationship between TUT and negative affect. However, research investigating all these characteristics (or even more than two concurrently in a single study) in the perspective of affect regulation is rare31,32. Thus, the present study aims to fill this gap by considering all the relevant characteristics identified in the literature, in order to test which of them are significant predictors of mood when the others are also included in the model.

Most prior research relies on self-report questionnaires and/or was conducted in laboratory settings20,22. However, recent literature emphasizes the importance of assessing psychological processes in ecological settings33,34 and exploring their link not only with subjective mood evaluation, but also with psychophysiological measures35. This trend is also present in TUT research. A rising number of studies explore the links between task-unrelated thoughts and heart rate variability (HRV) - a measure used as a psychophysiological marker of balance of the autonomic nervous system36 or even general psychological well-being37. Studies by Ottaviani and colleagues22,38 suggest that perseverative cognition, but not mind-wandering, can be associated with lowered HRV. Rumination39, worry40, and repetitive negative thinking41 all appear to have a detrimental effect on HRV. However, to the best of our knowledge, no studies exist linking a wider set of TUT characteristics to changes in HRV in ecological settings.

In the present study, we used ecological momentary assessment (EMA) methodology to investigate the relationship between TUT characteristics (e.g., intentionality, emotional valence, controllability) and (1) affect, (2) heart rate variability, (3) depressive symptoms, and (4) sleep quality. As existing studies often focus on thought characteristics that are particularly salient within one of the TUT subtypes, we aim to integrate characteristics commonly associated with specific TUT types (e.g., repetitiveness and lack of control in RNT42) into a more universal framework, consistent with the family resemblance approach16.

All TUT characteristics were grouped into three factors following the approach previously used by Rosenkranz et al.30 for measuring rumination with the experience sampling method. The Content factor was directly adapted from Rosenkranz et al.30 and includes features describing the thematic focus of thoughts (e.g., problems, feelings, temporal orientation). The second factor, labelled Control, was based on core processual characteristics of RNT as defined by Rosenkranz and colleagues30, but was restricted to variables capturing aspects of perceived mental control - a factor identified as one of the most relevant in TUT (e.g.,26,31,33,43) namely, uncontrollability, repetitiveness, intrusiveness, intentionality, and the freely moving nature of TUT. The variable assessing emotional burden (originally included in the process characteristics in Rosenkranz et al.30 was removed from this cluster and moved to the Emotional Appraisal factor. Distinguishing appraisal factor in the present study was directly linked to the results of recent studies underlying the importance of beliefs and meta-cognitions about TUT in their maladaptive outcomes33,44,45. We hypothesized that TUT characteristics linked to lack of control, negative valence and negative beliefs (i.e., uncontrollability, repetitiveness, intrusiveness, focus on problems, past and future orientation, emotional burden) would be positively related to negative affect; while freely moving feature, intentionality and positive valence would be negatively related to negative affect. The current literature is not clear about the link between focus on feelings and negative affect – while this component is an inherent part of rumination evaluation30, literature also suggests that it might be adaptive (i.e., negatively related to negative affect)42.

Informed by the theoretical framework of TUT46,47 and prior research (e.g.,26), we controlled for ongoing task characteristics, particularly task valence. This builds on prior research indicating that TUT can be maladaptive when used as an avoidance strategy48,49, suggesting that specific TUT characteristics may be especially detrimental when one is trying to escape a negatively valenced task26. Finally, we provide exploratory analyses on whether the trait tendency to ruminate or mind-wander, as well as the level of depressive and anxiety symptoms, can be a moderator of momentary and daily associations.

Methods

Participants

A total of 71 students volunteered to take part in the EMA study. Fifteen participants withdrew before the EMA stage of the study began, and three withdrew during the study. Five participants were excluded for not meeting the minimum compliance criterion (60% compliance for EMA) or for providing inconsistent ID across different parts of the study. As the minimum compliance required from participants varies strongly between studies50, we chose a mid-to-high compliance cut-off of 60% as used in previous studies51, aiming to achieve an average compliance rate around 80%52. One participant was excluded from the study following inspection of the HRV data, which revealed irregularities (frequent sudden stops in measurement, strong declines and increases in ECG signal – beyond the expected thresholds) rendering the data unsuitable for further analysis. A total of 47 participants were included in the final sample (Mage = 24.83, SDage=5.96), of which 33 were female, 11 male and 2 were non-binary. One participant decided to withhold information regarding their gender.

Procedure

The participants were recruited through the university’s internal research recruitment system. After signing up for the study, the participants were asked to provide informed consent and fill out a series of questionnaires in Qualtrics. They were assigned an assistant who guided them throughout the procedure. On the first meeting with the assistant, the participants installed the Movisens XS application and were given the chest ECGMove4 Movisens sensors together with thorough instruction on how to use them.

For 7 consecutive days and nights the participants wore the sensors and filled out momentary and daily questionnaires. The sensors were removed daily for around 20 min for charging. The sensors were also removed during activities that could damage them (bathing, particular forms of physical exercise, using sauna etc.).

At the beginning of the study, participants chose one of 3 daily activity windows (7am-9pm, 9am-11pm or 11am-1am). Each day, participants received: (1) a short questionnaire assessing their sleep quality (30 min after the start of the daily activity windows); (2) a total of 8 questionnaires assessing current thought and task characteristics, as well as changes in mood (7 of which appeared semi-randomly in 2-hour windows covering the daily activity window - additionally, 1 questionnaire was activated by the participants themselves just before sleep); (3) a short questionnaire assessing daily depressive symptoms (30 min before the end of the daily activity window).

After the measurement period, participants returned the devices to the assistants during a previously scheduled appointment, during which they were also given the opportunity to provide feedback on their study experience. Participants who finished the study and met the pre-specified requirements (wearing the sensor for the duration of the study and answering at least 60% of momentary questionnaires) were awarded university credit points. The procedure was approved by the Ethic Committee of Psychology Department in Katowice, SWPS University, Poland (WKEB69/03/2021). All methods described below were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and the study was run in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measures

Momentary measures

Each momentary questionnaire contained a total of 22 short items. All items were available to participants only in Polish (untranslated item content available in supplementary materials, Appendix 1).

Thought characteristics

One item, adapted from Kornacka et al.31., measured the task-relatedness of thought (“To what extent were your thoughts related to your current task?”) with a 7-point Likert scale from 1-”not at all”, to 7-”fully”.

One item measured the level of MW53(“To what extent, just before the signal, were you mind-wandering/your thoughts were divagating/ you were daydreaming” with visual analog scale (VAS) from 0-“not at all”, to 100-“strongly”).

Eight items adapted from Rosenkranz and colleagues30, measured characteristics needed to assess repetitive negative thinking (RNT). These included items assessing the focus of thoughts on (1) feelings, (2) problems, (3) unpleasant memories, (4) unpleasant situations that might occur in the future, as well as items addressing (5) perceived burden caused by them, and the (6) repetitiveness, (7) intrusiveness, and (8) uncontrollability of those thoughts. These items were answered using a 7-point Likert scale, as in the original questionnaire.

Four items were used to measure thought characteristics not included in the RNT measures, but relevant, based on the literature, in evaluating TUT: (1) intentionality (adapted from Banks and colleagues54, (2) emotional valence55, (3) the extent to which thoughts were freely moving56. The last item, measuring thought usefulness (“To what extent are you satisfied that this thought appeared?”) was proposed to address the possibility that the thought might be perceived by the participant as useful, regardless of its specific characteristics57. All items that were not adapted from RNT measures were answered on a VAS scale (0-100).

Based on the theoretical background described in the introduction, thought characteristics were divided into 3 factors: Content (4 items: future or past orientation, focus on feelings, focus on problems), Control (5 items: uncontrollability, repetitiveness, intrusiveness, freely moving, intentionality) and Emotional appraisal (3 items: valence, emotional burden, usefulness).

Task characteristics

To fully assess the context of the occurring thoughts, two items measuring task characteristics were used. The task-focused items were preceded by a message “Think about the task or activity that you have been doing just before the signal…”. One item measured the emotional valence of the task (“What emotions does this task evoke in you?” with VAS from 0-negative, to 100-positive). Another item measured the level of engagement (“To what extent does this task require your focus or engagement”? with VAS from 0-“not at all” to 100-“strongly”).

Mood

Momentary mood was measured using four items, each structured as: “To what extent were you feeling [emotion] just before the signal?”. The specific emotions inserted into the item stem were (1) “happy”, (2) “angry”, and (3) “sad”54, and “nervous, anxious, or on edge”, as a measure of anxiety58. The mood items were answered using a VAS with answers from 0-“not at all” to 100-“strongly”.

Daily measures

Sleep quality

Measured with a single item (“How would you rate your sleep quality” (referring to last night) with VAS from 0-“terrible”, 50-“fair” to 100-“excellent”59.

Daily depressive symptoms

Measured with three items60– “Thinking about today in general: (1) How positive were your thoughts about yourself? (2) How well did things go today? (3) Today, how optimistic are you about how your life (in general) will be tomorrow?” with VAS ranging from 0-“very negative”(for items 1 and 3) or “very badly” for item 2, to 100-(1)“very positive”, (2)”very well”, (3)”very optimistic”. The final daily depressive symptoms score was calculated by reversing and adding the scores of the 3 scales together.

Heart rate variability measure

ECG signal was recorded at 1024 Hz using the Movisens ECG Move 4 sensors. The Root Mean Square of Successive Differences (RMSSD) was calculated using the Movisens DataAnalyzer software (Movisens GmbH, 2024). During the calculation process, the software provided automated artifact correction. The RMSSD values were obtained for 1-minute epochs aligned with the beginning of each of the momentary questionnaires answered by participants.

Trait measures

Daydreaming frequency scale (DDFS)

Frequency of daydreaming/mind-wandering was evaluated using a Polish adaptation of Daydreaming Frequency Scale (DDFS61), - a single-factor, 12-item scale.

Perseverative thinking questionnaire (PTQ)

The PTQ is a 15-item scale measuring the level of perseverative thinking62. It includes one higher-order factor (repetitive negative thinking; RNT) and three lower-order factors: core characteristics of RNT, unproductiveness of RNT, and RNT-related cognitive interference. The Polish version of the PTQ63 was used.

Hospital anxiety and depression scale (HADS)

The HADS is a 14-item scale, with seven items assessing anxiety (HADS A) and seven assessing depression (HADS D)64. Participants indicate how frequently they experience each item on a 4-point Likert-type scale, with anchors varying depending on the item. The Polish version of the HADS65 was used.

Statistical analysis plan

At the beginning of the analyses, the data were converted into z-scores, based on mean and standard deviation for each participant separately. This step allowed us to include the answers to EMA questions, some of which were collected using different answer scales, in the same models. The mean factor scores were then calculated, based on the mean z-scores of all variables included in each factor. Four variables measuring thought characteristics (Freely moving, Intentionality, Usefulness, Thought valence) were included in the factor scores with reversed values to ensure consistent directionality across all variables.

As the data were collected using an EMA method, providing multiple measurement points per day for multiple days, for multiple users - they were therefore analysed using three-level models to appropriately account for the nested data structure and to fully exploit the analytical possibilities provided by intensive repeated measures66. The momentary observations (level 1) were nested within days (level 2), and days nested within participants (level 3). Random intercepts were included for both days and participants to capture unobserved heterogeneity at these levels. An example of the model can be viewed below:

In the model, i - refers to momentary observation, j – day, and k - participant.

Two types of models were analysed. First, we analysed models including the mean factor scores for TUT characteristics. These models were fitted separately for each outcome, with all mean factor scores included simultaneously within a single model. Then, we estimated models based on particular thought characteristics. In models measuring daily outcomes (daily depressive symptoms and sleep quality), a 2-level data structure was used, with daily measurements (level 1) nested within participants (level 2). Random intercepts were included for participants. To analyse models predicting daily outcomes, z-scores of momentary measures were aggregated to compute daily means. This approach was chosen to prevent mismatches in temporal granularity between predictors and outcomes, and to avoid non-independence of observations (multiple momentary predictor entries would otherwise share the same daily outcome, biasing standard errors and possibly inflating significance). All predictors were centered at the participant level to isolate within-person fluctuations from between-person differences and reduce potential multicollinearity, particularly in the presence of random slopes67. To focus the analysis on task-unrelated thoughts, measurement points at which participants’ thoughts were either fully or strongly related to the current activity (answers 7 and 6 in the item measuring task-relatedness) were excluded from the analyses – these points corresponded to 38.6% of the initial dataset. This criterion positions our threshold on the more inclusive end of those commonly employed in previous studies16, allowing for the examination of a broad range of thoughts - from fully to only somewhat task-unrelated. Given that the primary focus of this study lies in examining thought characteristics rather than distinct thought types, adopting a liberal classification criterion minimizes the risk of overlooking data on partially task-unrelated thoughts. For each of the models, R2, the reduction in deviance and the significance of the likelihood ratio test are reported relative to a null model. This allows for the evaluation of whether the inclusion of predictors significantly improved model fit compared to a baseline model68. Moderation analyses for all models were conducted with trait level variables (HADS D, HADS A, DDFS, PTQ) and task valence as moderators of the relationship between mean factor scores and all outcomes. In addition to simple slopes analyses, Johnson-Neyman intervals69 were computed for all significant interaction effects. This was done, firstly, because not all moderators showed a normal distribution, making analyses at ± 1 SD potentially unrepresentative. Moreover, Johnson-Neyman intervals (J-N intervals) offer more detailed information than probing at ± 1 SD. Rather than estimating the effect at two arbitrary points, they indicate the exact range of moderator values outside of which the predictor-outcome association becomes significant. This allows for a more precise interpretation of moderation effects and helps avoid potentially misleading conclusions based on fixed cutoffs. All raw values of J-N intervals were re-calculated and presented as the number of standard deviations from the mean value of the moderator, for an easier interpretation of the results. The analyses were conducted in R (version 4.1.2; R Core Team, 2021), using esmpack (version 0.1–2170) for data management, lme4 (version 1.1-371) for model building, sjPlot (version 2.8.1772) and jtools (version 2.2.0;73) for obtaining results, Interactions (version 1.2.0) for the computation of simple slopes, lavaan (version 0.6–19;74) for confirmatory factor analysis, and reghelper (version 1.1.275) for plotting preliminary figures (the scripts are available in supplementary materials at: https://osf.io/tw9hx/). Although the moderation analyses were conducted for exploratory purposes, and therefore no corrections for multiple comparisons were necessary76, we tested which of the moderation terms were statistically significant after Bonferroni correction.

Results

Descriptive statistics and correlations between measured variables are presented in supplementary materials Tables S1 and S2. The results of CFA show that the factor structure used in this study demonstrates a good model fit (CFI = 0.91, RMSEA = 0.07, SRMR = 0.06). The results of analyses based on models including task-related thoughts are available in supplementary materials Tabls S24–S27. As the factor structure applied in the present study is partially derived from a study by Rosenkranz et al.30, the interpretation of the factor scores follows a comparable logic. Accordingly, higher scores on the Control factor indicate a lower perceived control over one’s thoughts; higher scores on the Content factor reflect more negative thought content; and higher scores on the Emotional Appraisal factor correspond to a more strongly negative emotional appraisal of the thought. Additionally, we tested how momentary task-relatedness of thoughts is associated with momentary self-reported mind-wandering (MW; β = 0.26, SE = 0.03, t-ratio = 10.27, R2 = 0.07, p<0.001). The results might suggest that for participants task-unrelatedness of thoughts does not fully reflect the process of MW. The results for the full data set (including task-related thoughts) are presented in supplementary materials (Appendix 2).

Regarding particular thought characteristics, burden caused by thoughts emerged as the most consistent predictor of momentary outcomes. Burden was positively associated with anxiety, anger, and sadness, and negatively associated with happiness and HRV. Thought valence was a significant negative predictor of anxiety and anger, and a positive predictor of happiness, but showed no statistically significant relationship with sadness or HRV. Freely moving thoughts were negatively associated with anxiety and sadness, and positively associated with happiness, while showing no statistically significant relationship with anger or HRV.

Problem-focused thoughts were positively linked to anxiety and negatively linked to happiness, but were not significantly associated with anger, sadness, or HRV. Intentionality of thoughts was positively associated with anxiety and happiness, but showed no statistically significant relationships with anger, sadness, or HRV. Uncontrollability was positively associated with sadness, but showed no statistically significant relationships with anxiety, anger, happiness, or HRV.

Future-oriented and past-oriented thoughts were both positively associated with sadness, but showed no statistically significant relationships with anxiety, anger, happiness, or HRV. Usefulness of thoughts was positively associated with happiness, but showed no statistically significant relationships with anxiety, anger, sadness, or HRV. Repetitiveness, intrusiveness, and thinking about one’s feelings showed no statistically significant relationships with any affective outcomes or HRV.

Thought intentionality was the strongest (negative) predictor of MW. Thought valence, thoughts focus on problems and feelings, as well as the freely moving characteristic of thought were positively associated with MW.

In the models predicting daily depressive symptoms and sleep quality (Table 4), only one variable emerged as a significant predictor: aggregated daily usefulness of thought was negatively associated with daily depressive symptoms.

Discussion

The primary aim of the present study was to investigate which characteristics of stimulus-independent task-unrelated thoughts (TUT), as identified in existing literature1,4,16, are associated with participants’ momentary affect (assessed through EMA and ambulatory-assessed HRV), daily depressive symptoms, and sleep quality. Rather than focusing on specific categories or definitions of TUT and related phenomena, the present study adopts a characteristic-based approach, allowing for the identification of features relevant for momentary and daily affective outcomes regardless of the theoretical framework or nomenclature used to define TUT. Furthermore, we examined in exploratory analysis whether individual differences in depressive and anxiety symptoms, trait tendencies towards repetitive negative thinking or daydreaming and task valence moderate these associations. To our knowledge, this study is the first to test a comprehensive set of TUT characteristics within an EMA framework, integrated with objective psychophysiological measures.

Several aspects of the results of our study are particularly informative. Firstly, the findings highlight clear differences in the relevance of specific TUT characteristics for momentary affective outcomes while underlining also the differences between particular affective states (e.g., anger, sadness and anxiety). Second, marked discrepancies between models predicting momentary affective states and daily depressive symptoms suggest that the effects of TUT characteristics may depend on the temporal perspective in which the outcomes are assessed. Third, the results indicate that the affective consequences of task-unrelated thoughts may vary as a function of individual differences, rather than representing uniform effects across participants and their mechanisms might differ for participants with higher depressive symptoms.

TUT characteristics linked to affective states

The Emotional Appraisal factor emerged as the strongest predictor of sadness and the only factor consistently associated with daily depressive symptoms. Among its constituent characteristics, Burden of thought proved to be the most robust predictor across all momentary affective outcomes and HRV, while Thought valence represented the second strongest contributor. The third variable associated with this factor - Usefulness, while having a noticeably lower effect on momentary outcomes, was the only TUT characteristic related to daily depressive symptoms. Taken together, these findings suggest that Emotional Appraisal constitutes a central dimension underlying both momentary and daily effects of TUT, capturing its affective impact more comprehensively in comparison to Control and Content factors. The results on the role of Intentionality of TUT nicely complete the role of Emotional appraisal factor. Intentionality, positively associated with the adaptive nature of TUT in literature77,78, in our study showed a positive effect on both happiness and anxiety. As all models predicting momentary outcomes used a cross-sectional design, this result may be explained by the possible use of intentional TUT as an avoidance strategy26- while intentional TUT can be associated with positive affect, it may also be used as a distraction in situations where participants already feel anxious. The positive relationship between Intentionality and anxiety may also be interpreted within the framework of metacognitive theory79 as reflecting deliberate engagement in worry, driven by positive metacognitive beliefs (such as the belief that worrying is a useful strategy that helps to prevent or prepare for potential threat).

These results align with a recent trend in the broad field of TUT research, which involves exploring not only process-based, executive or content characteristics, but also taking into account how our perceptions (i.e., metacognition) regarding TUT may influence their affective outcomes. Although the role of metacognition has been primarily explored in the field of RNT (e.g.33,44,80, the present study provides additional evidence in favor of examining metacognition in other types of TUT as well, particularly while exploring TUT as avoidance strategy45,48.

Finally, our study also brings some clarification to the debate regarding the distinctive features of RNT/ maladaptive MW and adaptive forms of MW - the former being a constrained and “sticky” type of TUT43,81, while the latter is characterized by its freely moving nature. Previous studies have linked momentary negative affect82 and psychopathology symptoms83 to TUT characteristics specific rather for RNT considered as a process (e.g. Repetitiveness, Intrusiveness, Uncontrollability and Burden)80. In the present study, among all RNT process-related characteristics, Burden of thought emerged as the strongest predictor of negative affective outcomes, while Uncontrollability was only related to sadness, and Repetitiveness and Intrusiveness showed no statistically significant effects when controlling for other TUT characteristics. These results seem to be in line with past studies, where thought repetitiveness84 and intrusiveness85–87 were related to maladaptive outcomes depending on other variables. This raises the question of whether Burden represents a particularly central and sufficient dimension, potentially capturing the momentary affective impact of TUT as effectively as the group of characteristics to which it is assigned (RNT Process in past studies).

Freely moving thoughts, which are according to Christoff and colleagues81 a key feature potentially responsible for adaptive TUT outcomes, emerged as a consistent negative predictor of momentary negative affect, and a positive predictor of happiness, even when controlling for a wide list of other TUT characteristics. These findings are in line with the assumption that MW - being an adaptive form of TUT - is characterised by freely moving thoughts, while maladaptive ruminations are more restrained81 corroborating also the results of previous studies indicating that the freely moving characteristic plays an important role in the adaptive function of task-unrelated thoughts24,28.

Implications for future research

The marked difference in results between the models predicting momentary negative affect and daily depressive symptoms may suggest that only Emotional appraisal of TUT, but not its Content or ones perceived Control over it, has an effect that extends into broader patterns of daily functioning. The finding that one of the Emotional Appraisal characteristics – Usefulness – was associated with daily depressive symptoms, while showing no relationship with momentary negative affect, may suggest that the impact of particular TUT characteristics accumulates over time, influencing daily emotional well-being, whereas others might remain limited to momentary outcomes. It is also possible that specific TUT characteristics have differential short-term and long-term consequences, particularly when used as an avoidance strategy. Therefore, it seems crucial to evaluate different temporal dynamics when assessing the link between TUT features and their consequences on affect regulation.

The results also point to substantial differences in how specific TUT characteristics relate to different affective states. Uncontrollability, future- and past- related character of thoughts predicted sadness, but showed no associations with other affective outcomes. Similar differences were also observed in the models based on mean factor scores - with Content being associated with anxiety and sadness, but not with anger. Probably the strongest illustration of differential affective prediction was observed for intentionality: higher intentionality of thoughts was simultaneously associated with higher anxiety (negative affect) and higher happiness (positive affect). In addition, the amount of variance explained by the models varied substantially across affective outcomes, with models predicting anger being consistently the weakest, and models predicting happiness being the strongest. These findings underline the added value of using emotion-specific measurements in the study of task-unrelated thoughts. Such measures can capture differential effects that might be overlooked by relying solely on aggregated negative affect scales or a positive–negative affect continuum, highlighting the necessity of treating specific emotions as distinct points of measurement.

The results of the exploratory moderation models suggest that the relationships between thought characteristics and affective outcomes may differ depending on the level of depressive symptoms exhibited by a participant, as well as by trait-level tendency to engage in TUT. The effects of the Emotional appraisal of TUT on momentary affect seem to be weaker for persons experiencing a higher level of depressive symptoms. Although the exploratory nature of the analyses conducted calls for strong cautiousness in interpretation of the results, this effect might, on the one hand suggest that the lower emotional reactivity to external affective stimuli in patients experiencing depressive disorders, strongly established in past studies88, may also extend to self-generated stimuli in the form of TUT. On the other hand, in patients with higher depressive symptoms we might observe the use of TUT as a habitual response – the effect previously showed in RNT studies89. The link between TUT Content and affective states seem to be stronger for participants who tend to engage in both daydreaming and perseverative cognition more often. A similar effect was hypothesized, but not confirmed, by Weltz and colleagues90 who believed that participants exhibiting higher dispositional ruminative tendencies may experience stronger negative affective outcomes of mind-wandering. The results of our study suggest that the effect assumed by these authors may take place in regard to particular TUT characteristics, focused around thought content, rather than in MW episodes in general.

Although not analysed in the main body of this manuscript, the models based on all momentary observations, without excluding the reports on task-related thoughts (available in supplementary materials Tables S24–S27) show similar result patterns to the models based on TUT only. Most momentary models not excluding task-related thoughts explained more variance and provide more numerous significant results, which may be attributed to the substantially larger number of observations. This may suggest that the same thought characteristics, examined primarily in the context of TUT, are more generally related to well-being and may influence affective functioning beyond off-task thought episodes. The differences between the effect of thought characteristics on well-being in TUT and in task-related thoughts call for an in-depth analysis in future studies. Such comparisons are important yet challenging for three reasons. First, previous research into the characteristics of task-unrelated thinking has rarely compared them systematically across task-related and task-unrelated cognitions and brings the question of the clear cut-off between task-related and task-unrelated thinking10,23,91. Second, it is assumed that when task-focused, cognitions must be flexibly adjusted to meet specific requirements; therefore, their adaptive value may be highly task-dependent. This contributes to the ongoing debate regarding the definition of ‘task’ in the TUT framework2 and highlights the complex interplay between task demands and thought characteristics (e.g.26). Finally, the literature not only offers multiple ways to define TUT, but these definitions are further compounded by a variety of measurement methods92. The relatively weak relationship between task-unrelatedness and MW in the present study may serve as another argument for focusing on task characteristics—rather than just the TUT/MW themselves—when exploring the link between TUT and affect regulation. This result might be explained by findings from a series of earlier studies by Irving and collaborators93 showing that, in participants’ understanding of mind-wandering, the unguided nature of thought (i.e., thoughts shifting frequently from one topic to another) is substantially more central than task-unrelatedness. This interpretation is consistent with the relatively weak association between task-unrelated thoughts and mind-wandering observed in our data; however, two considerations highlight the need for further research . First, future studies should more closely examine the aforementioned role of task characteristics in both TUT and MW. Second, it would be valuable to investigate these effects in non–English-speaking populations, particularly in languages that lack a direct equivalent of the term mind-wandering. In these languages, English terminology is often used in research, whereas everyday language relies on descriptive expressions (e.g., thought divagation) or terms closer to daydreaming (e.g., reverie), and an exact translation of mind-wandering is either rarely used in everyday life or does not exist at all. Studying such populations would help determine whether the guided–unguided dimension of thought is equally central in languages that do not use a direct equivalent of the term mind-wandering, which is particularly important for assessing the cross-linguistic comparability of research on task-unrelated thought and mind-wandering.

Findings in the literature focusing on TUT remain inconsistent, and few studies examine multiple thought characteristics and outcomes simultaneously, yet even fewer use EMA designs with data collected across momentary, daily, and person levels over extended periods. Our study demonstrates that such an approach captures meaningful variability which may otherwise be overlooked. Recent recommendations emphasize aligning sampling frequency with the temporal dynamics of the studied process2,92. Our results support this view, showing that effects vary across timescales with some emerge only under intensive, ecologically valid designs. This is particularly relevant in TUT research, where thought characteristics and the context in which they arise interact in complex, highly time-sensitive ways.

Limitations

The innovative use of HRV ambulatory measures, relatively new to the TUT field, also brings some limitations. Participants’ self-management of sensor placement and removal, limited external control over non-wear episodes, and non-inclusion of control variables such as physical activity or substance intake could significantly affect HRV. Our dataset included some irregular measurement gaps and periods where collected data deviated from expected patterns. Currently there is no clear recommendation on collecting and cleaning HRV data sets collected in ambulatory settings. Moreover, the measurement windows around EMA prompts vary across studies. In future studies, we plan to (1) implement Bluetooth-based alerts to notify participants when data are not being recorded or are being recorded incorrectly; (2) include prompts about physical activity and substance use between measurements; and possibly (3) link a portion of participant compensation to the amount of HRV data collected (even if symbolically, as a motivating factor to react to alerts described in point 1).

As our study procedure included intensive EMA measures for a week, we expect that measurement reactivity might have influenced our results. Based on the findings by Kawashima et al.94 we believe that the frequent measurement of TUT and its characteristics might have reduced the number and length of TUT episodes. The literature on the effects of EMA measurement on affect is mixed, with some studies finding no relationship between sampling frequency and mood95, whereas others indicate high reactivity, with some participants experiencing beneficial and other detrimental effects of EMA protocols on affect96,97. At the end of the study procedure, some participants verbally reported that they felt irritated by the frequent EMA prompts and by the need to wear the HRV monitor for a prolonged period of time.

As our study focuses on momentary and daily effects of TUT, we find it important to highlight, that past research98 suggest, that occurrence of maladaptive TUT (particularly rumination) may affect psychopathological symptoms in the long term irrespectively of its effect on negative affect.

The present study also shares some limitations with previous work that stem from the ongoing debate on how different types of TUT are defined13,16,81, as outlined in the Introduction. In the current study, we adopted a broad definition of TUT as stimulus-independent, task-unrelated thinking. This definition also has limitations. First, as noted by Seli and colleagues16, it may not fully capture task-free forms of mind wandering. The concept of a task inherently implies engagement in an activity, something we are actively doing, even though individuals spend a portion of their daily lives in periods of inactivity. Thus, when discussing task-unrelated thoughts (TUT), it appears that periods of mental inactivity or spontaneous mind-wandering might be potentially excluded16. However, the extent of this limitation remains unclear, as there is an ongoing debate about how a “task” should be defined in the context of task-unrelated thinking2; this issue is particularly salient in ecological assessments of TUT, where participants implicitly determine what they consider to be their primary ongoing task. Relatedly, in studies focusing on RNT, task relatedness is typically not taken into account30, even though rumination and worry are generally conceptualized as recurrent, self-generated thought processes that are not driven by immediate environmental demands99 and that, once triggered (often by goal discrepancies), tend to unfold autonomously, largely independent of current task requirements7. An interesting finding of our study is the relatively low correlation between mind-wandering and task relatedness, suggesting that what participants classify as mind-wandering may not be limited to a lack of focus on the task but may also reflect other characteristics. This pattern further highlights the importance of approaches that assess specific features of TUT, rather than relying solely on broad labels such as mind-wandering or daydreaming, or on task relatedness alone. This approach, although more informative from a clinical perspective, required the use of a single EMA item to measure each characteristic in order to minimize participant fatigue and enhance compliance. However, this choice also entails certain limitations, as our EMA measures were drawn from items used in previous studies rather than from a single, unified study, and they have not been validated as standalone measures.

Finally, an important limitation of the present study is the relatively small sample size (N = 47) in relation to the number of analysed variables, which may have limited the statistical power to detect effects of individual characteristics. This issue may be particularly relevant for the models predicting daily outcomes, which use data collected 7 times less frequently than in the momentary models.

Conclusions

The results of the present study suggest that Emotional appraisal of TUT constitutes a central dimension underlying both momentary and daily affective outcomes. Among the examined characteristics, perceived Burden of thought emerged as the strongest and most consistent predictor of momentary affective states, while Thought valence and Freely moving character of thought seem to have played the second most significant role in shaping momentary emotional experience. Usefulness of thoughts showed an association with daily depressive symptoms, despite not being related to momentary negative affect, suggesting that some TUT characteristics may exert their effects primarily at longer temporal scales. Thus, the present results underline the importance of considering temporal dynamics when examining the consequences of task-unrelated thoughts, as different characteristics appear to be relevant for momentary affect fluctuations and for outcomes related to daily functioning. Finally, an important implication of these findings is that affective outcomes of task-unrelated thoughts should be measured in an emotion-specific manner, as the observed patterns differed strongly across models predicting affective states and may likely be obscured when using aggregated positive–negative affect indices.
