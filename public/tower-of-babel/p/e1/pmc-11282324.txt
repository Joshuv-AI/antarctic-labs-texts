ChatGPT and exercise prescription: Human vs. machine or human plus machine?
Authors: Timothy Gustavo Cavazzotto, Diego Bessa Dantas, Marcos Roberto Queiroga
Published: 2023

The power of artificial intelligence (AI) to create stories, music, art, and other creative projects has been demonstrated and criticized over recent decades in media, mainly because of the idea that AI will replace human jobs. Most recently, in November 2022, reactions to Chat Generative Pre-training Transformer (ChatGPT) release for public use reverberated in news and social media headlines, while its users exceeded 100 million in just a few weeks. ChatGPT is a natural language processing program created by AI non-profit OpenAI Inc. (https://openai.com/) based on the GPT-3.5 architecture. ChatGPT is an AI chatbot created to comprehend and produce human-like answers to questions on various topics based on extensive training data, including books, articles, and web pages. However, unlike AI chatbots such as BARD-Google, it lacks real-time access to external sources.

Researchers worldwide have tested ChatGPT in their respective areas of expertise.1,2 For example, Desaire et al.3 developed a method for discriminating text generated by ChatGPT from that created by (human) academic scientists, and Jeblick et al.4 tested ChatGPT's ability to simplify medical results from radiology records. In exercise prescription, a simple question made to ChatGPT can evoke a structured exercise program, including a frequency, intensity, time, type–volume and progression suggestions, contraindications, and examples for sports rehab or chronic conditions. Thus, we wanted to know: How will ChatGPT (or other AIs) redesign the future of sports and exercise science/profession?

Exercise prescription: Human vs. machine or human plus machine?

We tested ChatGPT's effectiveness at creating exercise prescriptions. Firstly, we conducted the test with a simple demand: “ChatGPT, please create an exercise prescription for an asthmatic”. The response it gave was a recommendation using the frequency, intensity, time, type–volume and progression. In addition, the ChatGPT described contraindications and tips for training. We requested adjustments to the prescription, including tables, examples of training programs, and a 6-month periodization. For each adjustment requested, the prescription became more accurate and coherent. Finally, we requested a supporting scientific reference for the prescription and received a reference from 1993. We requested a more recent reference and received a new frequency, intensity, time, type–volume and progression based on ACSM's guidelines for exercise testing and prescription, 10th edition.5 During this test, ChatGPT conveyed that its responses are based on training data and that it does not recommend exercise practice without a health professional's prior medical examination and monitoring.

What is the role of science in this new scenario?

Science is massive. Millions of papers are produced annually, thus creating a paradox: We make more science than we can read. Filtering content is a demanding mission that requires in-depth knowledge of how to search the scientific literature and extract relevant information supported by a complex analysis of paper quality. Much of this work is accomplished by systematic reviews, which ensure a consistent level of evidence. However, not all questions have been synthesized into reviews, and not all studies are accessible to the non-academic community.

ChatGPT was not built to identify the best evidence or method in any clinical application. The incorrect or outdated response was evocated by AI if, during training, data was incomplete due to closed science (subscription). As more scientific information becomes open and accessible, the training data (and AI's responses) will also become more comprehensive, helping to provide more valid (and faster) responses on a broader range of scientific topics.

The ability of natural language processing to generate compelling human-like text can make it difficult to distinguish between real knowledge and unverified information, leading people to accept false or misleading information as accurate. To reduce this risk, it is essential to teach users how this information can be critically evaluated through exploration, investigation, verification, and corroboration.6 Additionally, AI-specific tasks can be tested and validated by comparing them with accurate models (gold standards) and then published in peer-reviewed journals.

Conclusion

Despite the limitations of the current version, ChatGPT may be helpful as a narrative AI chatbot for filtering scientific content (boosting knowledge translation) and complementing or assisting the development of a personalized exercise prescription (human plus machine). In our view, future specialized natural language processing models, such as ChatGPT, if trained based on science journals, systematic reviews, and other scientific databases and confirmed by the scientific method, will ensure the validity of clinical applications and exercise prescription.
