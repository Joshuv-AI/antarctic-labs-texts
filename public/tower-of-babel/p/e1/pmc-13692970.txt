Developing a cps-genotyping scheme for the emerging zoonotic pathogen Streptococcus parasuis through genetic analysis of capsular polysaccharide synthesis gene loci
Authors: Xiyan Zhang, Xueli Yi, Jianping Wang, Juan Li, Nahuel Fittipaldi, Wenbo Luo, Yan Wang, Chaoyuan Yuan, Wenfei Wei, Xuezhen Li, Jinhui Zhang, Marcelo Gottschalk, Han Zheng, Jianguo Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStreptococcus parasuis is an emerging zoonotic pathogen responsible for pneumonia, meningitis, peritonitis, pleuritis, nephritis, cellulitis, cholecystitis, sepsis, and arthritis in humans. The continuous increase in reported human cases of S. parasuis infection indicates its growing threat to public health and highlights the urgent need to monitor its epidemiology. The lack of high-throughput typing tools has seriously impeded the epidemiological surveillance of S. parasuis. Serotyping based on antigenic differences in capsular polysaccharides (CPSs) synthesized by cps loci is widely applied in epidemiological research and routine surveillance of various pathogens. In this study, the genetic characteristics of cps loci extracted from 259 S. parasuis genomes isolated from multiple sources were investigated.ResultsAmong the 259 S. parasuis genomes, 142 genomes contained an intact cps locus, while the cps loci of 117 genomes were incomplete due to the limited length of the contigs. The intact cps loci were predominantly located between the flanking genes SSU1210 and SSU1209. The presence of wzy genes in all cps loci suggests that CPSs are synthesized via the Wzx/Wzy pathway in S. parasuis population. Based on sequence alignment of the wzy genes, 109 putative serotypes were identified, 10 of which were associated with human infection. The products of the 2047 cps genes from these 109 putative serotypes were grouped into 870 homology groups, of which 79.66% (693/870) were serotype-specific, indicating substantial genetic diversity within the cps loci. Intraspecies and interspecies exchanges of gene fragments and entire cps loci were observed, contributing to the diversity of CPSs in S. parasuis. A cps-genotyping scheme was developed by using whole-genome sequences to align against a curated database of serotype-specific wzy genes.ConclusionsThe study revealed the genetic characteristics of CPSs in S. parasuis population and developed an easy-to-use, expandable, high-throughput cps-genotyping scheme. This study contributed to the evolutionary research, virulence assessment, and epidemiological surveillance of S. parasuis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05398-6.

Introduction

Streptococcus parasuis is an emerging zoonotic pathogen with the growing threat to public health, responsible for pneumonia, meningitis, peritonitis, pleuritis, nephritis, cellulitis, cholecystitis, sepsis, and arthritis in humans [1–4] (from Chinese Pathogen Identification Net). Since 2015, Streptococcus suis serotypes 20, 22, and 26 have been taxonomically reclassified as S. parasuis based on Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI), DNA-DNA hybridization (DDH), and phylogenetic analyses of recN and 16 S rRNA gene sequences [5]. Although S. parasuis was initially considered a potential pig pathogen [6], it has been re-identified as an emerging zoonotic pathogen following two reported human cases of S. parasuis infection in the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region (GX) in 2018 [4]. Furthermore, the earliest known human S. parasuis infection was traced back to 2013 through a retrospective investigation of a case initially identified as S. suis serotype 21 infection in Argentina [1]. To date, 16 human cases have been reported in China, including nine previously reported cases [7] and seven newly identified cases in 2025 (four in Henan Province, one in Hainan Province, one in Anhui Province, and one in Beijing). The incidence of human S. parasuis infections is likely to be substantially underestimated due to the frequent misidentification of S. parasuis as S. suis due to the phylogenetic affinity between two species. In our previous study, the significant diversity in population structure was revealed in S. parasuis, which is composed of 12 minimum core genome (MCG) groups [7]. In addition, considerable diversity in sequence types (STs) has been observed among clinical strains of S. parasuis [7]. The continuous increase in reported human S. parasuis cases, together with the diversity of population structure, highlights the urgent need to monitor S. parasuis epidemiology using high-throughput typing tools with strong discriminatory power.

Capsular polysaccharides (CPSs) are important extracellular components composed of long polysaccharide chains and exhibit high variability both within and between species. Serotyping based on antigenic differences in CPSs is an important epidemiological method and has been widely applied in the surveillance of various pathogens [8–10]. To date, information on the diversity of capsular polysaccharide synthesis (cps) gene loci responsible for CPS production in the S. parasuis population remains limited. In the present study, the genetic characteristics of S. parasuis cps loci were investigated, the distribution of putative serotypes within the S. parasuis population was determined, and a whole-genome sequence based cps-genotyping scheme was established.

Methods and materials

Genomes and strains

A total of 14 complete genomes and 245 assembled genomes from nine countries were downloaded from NCBI in this study, comprising 14 genomes from patients, 189 from pigs, and 51 from environmental samples, as well as one genome each from milk, a calf, and a cow. The sources of the remaining two genomes were unknown (Supplemental Table 1). The multilocus sequence typing (MLST) and MCG group of the genomes were determined by using PubMLST (https://pubmlst.org/organisms/streptococcus-parasuis), and Streptococcus parasuis MCG Typing (https://github.com/1111zxy/Streptococcus_parasuis_MCG_Typing.py) [7], respectively.

Analysis of cps loci and development of a cps-genotyping scheme

In our previous study, S. parasuis CPSs were shown to be synthesized via the Wzx/Wzy pathway, and 10 putative serotypes were identified based on the wzy genes. The intact cps clusters were located between the SSU1210 and SSU1209 genes [4]. Sequences downstream of SSU1210 and upstream of SSU1209 were extracted from259 genomes in the study. Putative wzy and wzx genes were identified using previously described methods [11, 12]. Serotypes with wzy genes exhibiting a global match region of less than 50% of the amino acid sequence and less than 50% identity were classified as distinct serotypes [10, 11, 13]. Novel serotypes were assigned numerical designations beginning with serotype 11. Predicted proteins encoded by the cps genes were clustered into homology groups (HGs), following the methods and standards (both the coverage and identity of amino acid sequence less than 50%) described in previous studies [10, 11, 13].

Multiple sequence alignment of wzy gene sequences was performed using MAFFT (v7.525). Maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenetic trees were inferred using IQ-TREE (v3.1.2) with ModelFinder Plus, which performed automatic model selection. Tree visualization was performed using iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/).

Alignments of the cpsA to cpsD sequences were performed to generate aligned sequences using MEGA (version 5). Phylogenetic trees were then constructed from the aligned sequences using FastTree based on the Generalised Time Reversible (GTR) model.

Sequence comparison of cps loci was performed using the BLASTN program in BLAST with an e-value cutoff of 1e− 10 and was visualized using Easyfig (v2.2.5).

The cps-genotyping scheme was developed by aligning query S. parasuis genomes in FASTA format against a curated database of serotype-specific wzy genes using an in-house Python script to run BLASTN with an e-value cutoff of e− 5. This database comprises nucleotide sequences of 109 wzy genes, each representing a distinct serotype. The program accepts whole-genome sequences in FASTA format as input and supports both complete genomes and draft assemblies, including scaffolds or contigs. For each query genome, the program performs individual BLASTN alignments against all wzy reference sequences. For each significant match, query coverage (qcovs) and percent identity (pident) are calculated, and all candidate hits are ranked based on these two metrics to identify the best-matching wzy gene. The final serotype assignment is automatically determined from the annotation associated with the best-matching reference sequence, and key alignment metrics, including qseqid, qlen, length, qcovs, pident, and E-value, are reported in the output. If no significant match is detected against any of the 109 wzy genes, the result is reported as “nohit”. The program’s output has been deposited at the following link: https://github.com/1111zxy/A-cps-genotyping-Scheme-for-Streptococcus-parasuis.

Results

Identification of cps loci and determination of putative serotypes

In this study, a cps locus was considered intact if it began with the cpsA gene on the 5′ side and included the flanking gene SSU1209 on the 3′ side. Among the 259 S. parasuis genomes, 142 genomes contained an intact cps locus, while the cps loci of 117 genomes were incomplete due to the limited length of the contigs.

Of the 142 genomes with an intact cps locus, 133 carried the locus between flanking genes SSU1210 and SSU1209, eight upstream of flanking gene SSU1209, and one (genome F-15-01-TS-01-SS-C1) between flanking genes SSU1210 and SSU1208. In contrast, the incomplete cps loci of 107 genomes were located downstream of flanking gene SSU1210 and lacked the 3′ flanking gene SSU1209, while the remaining 10 genomes lacked flanking genes on both sides of the cps loci (Supplemental Table 1). The absence of flanking genes in 117 cps loci was due to the assembly fragmentation of corresponding genomes. Complete genome sequences will be required in future studies to determine whether the S. parasuis population exhibits a consistent insertion pattern of cps loci. Although these 117 loci were considered putatively incomplete at the 3′ end, all contained intact wzy genes, and their genetic characteristics were further analyzed in this study.

Among the 259 S. parasuis genomes, 31 genomes were assigned to 10 known serotypes through BLASTN alignment against serotype-specific wzy genes. Based on sequence variation in the wzy genes, 74 novel serotypes with intact cps loci and 25 novel serotypes with deletions on the 3′ side were identified among remaining 230 genomes. Notably, the cps locus of serotype 106 was split into two fragments, containing the SSU1210 and SSU1209 genes on the 5′ and 3′ flanking regions, respectively. The two fragments were located at the end of two contigs, respectively. The central region was absent due to the assembly fragmentation (Supplemental Fig. 1). In total, 109 putative serotypes were identified in 259 S. parasuis genomes, including 10 known serotypes and 99 novel serotypes discovered in this study. Additionally, serotypes 29 and 58 were each divided into two subtypes, A and B, owing to the differences in the 3′ side region containing wzx genes (Fig. 1A and B, Supplemental Fig. 1).

Fifty-six serotypes (51.4%) were identified only once among the 259 S. parasuis genomes, while the remaining 53 serotypes were represented by 2 to 13 genomes each. Serotype 58, with 13 genomes, was the most prevalent in the S. parasuis population, followed by serotype 75 with 11 genomes. Serotype 26 included 8 genomes, while serotypes 2, 12, 14, 15, and 55 each contained 7 genomes, and serotypes 13, 21, and 67 each comprised 6 genomes (Supplemental Table 1). Among the 109 serotypes, serotypes 2, 3, 4, and 6 were distributed across three countries; 16 serotypes (5, 9, 13, 14, 15, 21, 22, 29, 40, 52, 55, 58, 68, 74, 82, and 84) were found in two countries; and the remaining 89 serotypes were restricted to a single country. Additionally, 26 serotypes were isolated from at least two different sources (Supplemental Table 1). The 14 genomes from patients were clustered into 10 serotypes: 6, 10, 15, 32, 40, 58, 74, 96, 108, and 109. Serotype 10 was the most prevalent, containing three genomes.

Genetic characteristics of cps loci

In this study, one representative cps locus was selected (based on the comprehensive consideration of completeness of cps loci, isolation time and MCG groups/STs of host genomes) for each of the 53 serotypes represented by multiple genomes. Together with the 56 cps loci identified only once, the genetic characteristics of all 109 representative cps loci were analyzed, including 83 intact loci and 26 incomplete loci on the 3′ side (Supplemental Table 1, Supplemental Tables 2, and Supplemental Fig. 1).

The length and G + C content of the 83 intact cps loci ranged from 11,876 bp to 31,847 bp and 32.14% to 40.13%, respectively, with each locus containing 12–37 ORFs. For the 26 incomplete cps loci, the length ranged from 11,473 bp to 28,654 bp, and the G + C content ranged from 30.35% to 39.36%, respectively, with each locus containing 12–28 ORFs.

To investigate cps genetic diversity within the S. parasuis population, the products of 2047 genes from 109 representative cps loci were grouped into 870 HGs, including 693 (79.66%) single-member HGs and 177 (20.34%) HGs containing 2 to 109 members.

5′ side of cps loci

The genetic organization of the 5′ cpsA–cpsD region in S. parasuis was conserved and the same as that of S. suis, with the corresponding gene products grouped into HG1 to HG4, respectively. The sequences within the cpsA–cpsD regions were clustered into two lineages based on the similarity: Lineage 1, comprising 98 cps loci, and Lineage 2, comprising 11 cps loci (Supplemental Fig. 2). The cpsA-cpsD region of the two Lineages has only approximately 70% sequence identities.

Among the 109 putative serotypes, the products of the initial glycosyltransferase gene, located on the 5′ side of the region, were classified into nine HGs: HG5 (n = 56), HG9 (n = 33), HG21 (n = 3), HG36 (n = 7), HG46 (n = 4), HG60 (n = 2), HG144 (n = 2), HG472 (n = 1), and HG569 (n = 1). Notably, all initial glycosyltransferases from cps loci in Lineage 2 of the phylogenetic tree based on cpsA–cpsD sequences were assigned to HG5 (Supplemental Fig. 2). Genes encoding filamentation induced by cAMP (Fic) family proteins (HG8) and/or UDP-N-acetylglucosamine 4,6-dehydratase (HG6) were present between cpsD and the initial glycosyltransferase genes in 49 cps loci. Interestingly, both HG6 and HG8 were absent in serotypes harboring HG5 (Supplemental Table 2).

The NAD-dependent epimerase of HG10 was consistently located downstream of the initial glycosyltransferase genes. Additionally, genes encoding modifying enzymes—such as glycosyltransferases (HG11, HG15, and HG16), NAD-dependent epimerase (HG12), and UDP-galactopyranose mutase (HG13)—were frequently present on the 5′ side of the cps loci (Supplemental Table 2).

Serotype-specific HGs

HGs belonging to single-member were considered serotype-specific. Each serotype possessed at least one serotype-specific HG, ranging from one (in serotypes 20 and 72) to 14 (in serotypes 23 and 26), mainly located in the central regions of the cps loci. These serotype-specific HGs mainly were the Wzy polymerase, Wzx flippase, glycosyltransferases, aminotransferases, acetyltransferases, and other polymer-modifying enzymes (Supplemental Tables 2 and Supplemental Fig. 1). In addition, 76 serotype-specific HGs were hypothetical proteins.

Glycosyltransferase

Excluding the initial glycosyltransferases, 466 glycosyltransferases were grouped into 330 HGs, of which 282 were serotype-specific. Each serotype possessed at least one serotype-specific HG of glycosyltransferase, ranging from one to eight, except for serotypes 9, 45, 72, 88, and 106 (Supplemental Table 2).

Wzy and Wzx

The HG of Wzy polymerase was serotype-specific for each serotype. The Wzy protein of serotype 82 shared 85% amino acid identity with its counterpart in serotype 72, covering 57% of the full-length sequence. The 5′ region of the wzy gene was absent in serotype 82, indicating that it may be a pseudogene (Fig. 1C). Considering that serotype 72-specific wzy and wzx genes were absent from the cps locus and genome of serotype 82, we propose that these two cps loci correspond to distinct serotypes. Notably, some wzy genes were not specific to S. parasuis at the species level. Homologues of the Wzy protein from 10 S. parasuis serotypes 7, 9, 31, 32, 46, 66, 68, 69, 75, and 86 were identified in the cps loci of S. suis serotypes 24, 10, 5, 11, 7, NCL5, NCL6, 4, 8, and 12, respectively. The amino acid identities were as follows: 86.55% (serotype 24), 95.73% (serotype 10), 90.86% (serotype 5), 97.37% (serotype 11), 95% (serotype 7), 88% (NCL5), 82.8% (NCL6), 100% (serotype 4), 92% (serotype 8), and 99.3% (serotype 12). All comparisons showed 100% coverage, except for serotype 8 (92% coverage). Homologues of the Wzy protein from six S. parasuis serotypes 5, 10, 22, 58, 80, and 107 were also identified in the eps loci of five S. thermophilus strains (MK483540.1, MK483547.1, AF454501.1, MK483559.1, and AJ289861.1) and the cps loci of S. orisratti strain (AB737836.1), respectively. All homologues showed full-length coverage, with amino acid identities ranging from 98.99% to 99.77% (Fig. 2).

In contrast to the wzy gene, the wzx gene in S. parasuis was not specific for each serotype. Fourteen HGs of Wzx flippase were shared across multiple serotypes. The wzx gene was absent in serotypes 85, 93, and 104 due to incomplete cps loci. Additionally, serotype 102 contained two distinct HGs of Wzx flippase, HG26 and HG826, which were present in six serotypes and specific to serotype 102, respectively. The wzx gene of serotype 94 and the wzy gene of serotype 107 were split into three and two open reading frames, respectively (Supplemental Table 1), likely representing pseudogenes. Further analysis of additional cps loci from these serotypes is needed to determine whether these gene splits are genuine or due to low sequencing quality. Thus, serotypes 85, 93, 94, 106, and 107 were considered provisional serotypes in the present study.

3′ side of cps loci

In contrast to the conserved genetic organization of the 5′ region, the 3′ side of the cps loci exhibited substantial diversity and deletions among the 109 putative serotypes, which could partially attributed to the genes encoding transposases widely present on the 3′ side of cps loci. However, the 3′ regions of several cps loci showed high sequence similarity, including serotypes 15, 31, 36, 46, and 100; serotypes 12, 13, and 39; serotypes 27 and 101; serotypes 2, 84, and 97; serotypes 11 and 22; and serotypes 19 and 42 (Supplemental Table 2).

Horizontal transfer of entire cps loci and gene fragments

Genomes with the same STs were assigned to different serotypes. Among the 31 STs containing multiple genomes, 19 were confined to a single serotype, while 12 included at least two serotypes. Notably, genomes from MCG group 10 exhibited significant serotype diversity. Within this group, ST76 (n = 30) contained 19 serotypes, ST96 (n = 9) contained seven serotypes, ST99 (n = 8) contained five serotypes, and ST101 and ST112 (n = 2 each) contained two serotypes each (Supplemental Table 1). Additionally, MCG group 4 of ST51 (n = 3) included three serotypes, and six STs (ST3, ST26, ST44, ST73, ST84, and ST153) from MCG groups 1, 2, 4, 7, and 12 were each shared by two serotypes (Supplemental Table 1). It indicated that the horizontal transfer entire cps loci may occur between the S. parasuis genomes with same ST.

The intraspecies and cross-species gene fragment exchange were also observed in S. parasuis cps loci. The Wzx flippase HG373 and HG141 was present in the cps loci of serotypes 29 A and 29B, respectively. Notably, HG141 was also found in the cps locus of serotype 76 (Fig. 1A). A similar pattern was observed in S. parasuis serotype 58: the cps locus of serotype 58 A contained serotype specific Wzx flippase HG558, whereas Wzx flippase HG111 in serotype 58B was shared with serotype 18 (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, comparable genetic arrangements in the central regions of the cps loci were observed between serotypes 29B and 76 (Fig. 1A) and between serotypes 58B and 18 (Fig. 1B). High similarity in cps locus organization was also observed between serotypes 72 and 82. In serotype 82, a fragment consisting of HG84, HG85, and HG164 was replaced by a fragment containing HG645, HG84, and HG85, leading to the deletion of the 5′ region of the HG164 in serotype 72 and a serotype switch from 82 to 72 (Fig. 1C).

Furthermore, highly similar sequences in the central regions of cps loci were observed among 15 S. parasuis putative serotypes (5, 7, 9, 10, 22, 31, 32, 46, 58 A, 66, 68, 69, 75, 80, and 86) and the corresponding cps or eps loci with shared wzy genes from S. suis, S. thermophilus, and S. orisratti, despite significant sequence differences in both the 5′ and 3′ regions of these loci.

Development of a cps-genotyping scheme

The wzy genes of different serotypes shared less than 50% of the amino acid sequence in the global match region and exhibited less than 50% identity. This high level of diversity made wzy a suitable target for developing a cps-genotyping scheme.

In this study, we developed a cps-genotyping program that uses whole-genome sequences and performs BLASTN alignment against a curated database of serotype-specific wzy genes. Simulation tests demonstrated that the program accurately assigned the correct serotypes to all 259 S. parasuis genomes, which was consistent with the aforementioned analysis results of cps loci. Thus, the cps-genotyping program can be directly applied to genotype the cps of S. parasuis assembled genomes, regardless of whether the genome contains an intact cps locus.

Discussion

The cps loci of S. parasuis are consistently located in a fixed region of the genome and consist of three main classes of genes: nucleotide sugar biosynthesis pathway genes, glycosyltransferase genes, and sugar unit processing genes (wzx and wzy). The presence of wzy genes in all cps loci indicates that CPS is synthesized via the Wzx/Wzy pathway in the S. parasuis population, which is most frequently utilized in both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria. In the Wzx/Wzy pathway, an oligosaccharide is initially linked to the lipid carrier by an initial glycosyltransferase in the cytoplasm, then sequential transfer of the other oligosaccharides to produce repeat units by glycosyltransferases. The oligosaccharide repeat units are flipped across the cell membrane to the outer surface of the cytoplasmic membrane by Wzx and then polymerized by Wzy to form polysaccharide chains. Some Wzx flippases from different serotypes recognized common oligosaccharide repeat unit in S. parasuis. Similar results have also been reported for the CPSs of S. suis [10]. Consequently, Wzy polymerases were serotype-specific HGs in S. parasuis. Using these serotype-specific wzy genes, extensive serotype diversity was revealed in the S. parasuis population, and a total of 109 putative serotypes were identified. The cpsA-cpsD region of 109 putative serotypes were clustered into two Lineages and shared relatively low sequence identities. Considering the fact that the cpsA-cpsD region was conserved within the same serotype, it indicated the cps loci of two Lineages may originate from different ancestors.

The glycosyltransferases sequentially transfer sugars to growing glycan chains until the oligosaccharide repeat units are fully synthesized. Among the 109 putative serotypes, 85.5% HGs of glycosyltransferases were serotype-specific. The presence of these serotype-specific glycosyltransferases indicates a large number of linkage specificities across the 109 putative serotypes and reflects significant structural variation among serotypes in the S. parasuis population.

Both S. suis serotypes 17 and 19 reference strains are ST76, which suggests that intraspecies horizontal transfer of serotype‑specific cps genes may have occurred. Similarly, strains harboring different cps gene loci but sharing similar genetic backgrounds have also been identified in the S. parasuis population. These findings indicate that horizontal transfer of cps loci likely occurs within the S. parasuis population as well. Significant sequence differences at the two ends of loci harboring similar wzy genes were observed between S. parasuis and other species, suggesting that the sharing of wzy genes is not due to the exchange of entire cps loci. Considering the different cps locus insertion hotspots between S. parasuis and other species, these loci have likely undergone cross-species gene fragment exchange. In addition, gene fragment exchanges observed between S. parasuis serotypes 72 and 82, 29B and 76, and 18 and 58B suggest that large-scale intraspecies reorganization events may also occur within the S. parasuis species. We propose that both interspecies and intraspecies exchanges of gene fragments and entire cps loci have significantly increased CPS diversity in S. parasuis, providing evolutionary advantages that enable the population to better adapt to host or environmental selective pressures. In the present study, we only investigated the genetic characteristics of the representative cps locus for each serotype. The potential replacements, inversions, translocations, and segmental duplications within the same serotype were not examined. To further understand the mosaic structure of the S. parasuis CPSs, the genetic variation of cps loci within same serotype will be investigated in our future studies.

Serotyping is widely used in epidemiological investigations, outbreak tracing, and routine surveillance of various pathogens. Conventional serotyping, which relies on agglutination tests with serotype-specific antisera, is labor-intensive, time-consuming, expensive, and prone to cross-reactivity, making it challenging to meet surveillance needs for pathogens with highly variable CPSs. As a result, cps-genotyping techniques are gradually replacing conventional methods. Several multiplex PCR assays targeting serotype-specific wzy genes have been developed and effectively applied in epidemiological studies of S. suis [12–15]. Since 2016, more than 20 novel cps loci (NCLs) have been reported in S. suis based on NCL-specific wzy genes [11, 13, 16–18], significantly advancing our understanding of capsular genetic diversity in the S. suis population. With the rapid development of next-generation sequencing technologies, it is now possible to obtain pathogen whole-genome sequences quickly and routinely. In this study, a whole genome sequencing (WGS)-based cps-genotyping scheme was developed by aligning S. parasuis genomes against a database of serotype-specific wzy genes. This approach is more cost-effective, rapid, high-throughput, and accurate than multiplex PCR assays, providing a valuable tool for monitoring S. parasuis serotype prevalence. Furthermore, wzy genes from novel serotypes identified in future studies, as well as “signature” sequences for sub-serotyping, can be easily incorporated into the database, significantly enhancing the scheme’s capacity for S. parasuis serotype surveillance. Given the high similarity of some wzy genes between S. parasuis and those of other species in the Streptococcus genus, it is essential to first clarify the species identity [7] before performing cps-genotyping.

CPS is a critical virulence factor that protects pathogens from host immune clearance and facilitates the establishment of infection in vivo [19–21]. Deletion of CPS severely impairs S. suis virulence [22–24]. In the study, no single serotype predominates among the limited number of reported human S. parasuis infections, whereas serotype 2 is clearly the predominant serotype in human S. suis infections worldwide [25, 26]. Additional S. parasuis clinical strains are needed to clarify the relationship between serotype and zoonotic potential.

The genomes with the same zoonotic serotypes were clustered into different MCG groups. However, S. parasuis genomes isolated from patients were clustered into MCG group 2. On the contrary, the serotype of genome 4253 clustered into MCG group 2 was not related to human S. parasuis infection. Notably, genomes from patients with the same STs were sometimes assigned to different serotypes. We propose that combining MLST, MCG, and cps-genotyping is essential for evaluating the public health significance and investigating the epidemiological characteristics of S. parasuis strains. MLST and MCG typing provide valuable insights into the genomic background, evolutionary relationships, and zoonotic potential of S. parasuis serotypes. The cps-genotyping scheme further enhances the ability to monitor human S. parasuis infections.

Conclusion

We identified 109 putative serotypes in the S. parasuis population, characterized the genetic features of the corresponding cps loci, and developed a whole-genome sequence–based cps-genotyping scheme. This study provides valuable insights and an easy-to-use, expandable, high-throughput typing tool for evolutionary studies, virulence assessment, and epidemiological surveillance of S. parasuis.
