A required medical student collaborative case presentation with a pathologist in the surgery clerkship
Authors: Abdallah Flaifel, Kristen M Thomas, Syed T Hoda, Leandra Krowsoski, Isabelle Le Leannec, Colleen Gillespie, Margret S Magid
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn medical education, pathology has traditionally been concentrated in only the preclinical years, often without sufficient emphasis on its practical application in clinical practice. Correspondingly, medical students' interest in pathology as a career has been low. To address this issue and foster a deeper understanding of pathology's clinical relevance and encourage appropriate utilization, we introduced a required exposure to pathology in the surgery clerkship featuring clinicopathological case review in a small group setting. Unlike other approaches, we wanted to create a program that concentrates on pathology cases directly linked to patients whom students cared for during their clerkship rotation, emphasizing the relevance of pathology diagnosis. Feedback has been overwhelmingly positive from participating students, who report an increased awareness of pathology's importance in patient management and of the significance of interdisciplinary collaboration between pathologists and clinicians. A notable feature of this program is its relatively low time and personnel requirements, which facilitate inclusion in the busy clerkship and acceptance in the Department of Pathology. Challenges, such as timely case selection and administrative co-ordination, are being addressed to optimize the program's implementation. In the future, we are considering expanding this model to other clerkships. By rekindling interest in pathology through practical engagement and highlighting its real-world relevance, this approach offers a promising strategy to counteract recruitment challenges in this crucial medical field.Keywords: Clinical clerkships, Medical school curriculum, Pathology education, Pathology pipeline

The inclusion of pathology into the medical school curriculum has traditionally been confined to the preclinical years, without sufficient reference to how pathologists play very specific roles in guiding the care of patients daily. Advantages of required pathology activities in the clinical years include highlighting the critical contributions of pathology to patient management and educating medical students in effective utilization of anatomic and clinical pathology. Direct interaction with pathologists would highlight the role of pathologists as professional colleagues in patient care and help to combat negative stereotypes.1,2 In addition, exposure during the clerkships may have a role in increasing the interest of students in pursuing pathology as a career choice. Interest in pathology as a career has been waning among both United States allopathic and osteopathic medical students.2, 3, 4 This trend, while multifactorial, has been attributed in large part to limited exposure of medical students to the practice of pathology.5,6

In an important development, members of the Undergraduate Medical Educator Section (UMEDS) of the Association of Pathology Chairs developed a compendium of medical school graduation competencies considered to be necessary for the practice of medicine. One of the major categories of competencies is Diagnostic Medicine and Therapeutic Pathology, which, through learning goals, more specific learning objectives, and linked educational cases, focuses on the knowledgeable and efficient use of clinical laboratory testing and anatomic pathology to achieve optimal clinical management.7,8 But what often appears be lacking is the application of these competencies to curricula in the clinical years.

While pathology elective rotations are available at many institutions, with limited elective time, most students do not opt for taking pathology electives. In the United States, schools rarely have designed mandatory programs that expose all medical students to the daily practice of pathology in the management of patient care.9 A dedicated full pathology rotation in the clinical years is beyond the scope of many medical schools’ curriculum.10 To address insufficient exposure to pathology, creative initiatives such as virtual asynchronous pathology electives have been created.11 But while online exposure can provide basic didactics on demand, it fails to provide a hands-on, in-person experience such as a real in-practice rotation can provide. Postsophomore fellowships, mentoring programs, and student interest groups have also been introduced. However, these programs do not provide all students with exposure to the practice of pathology.

Two medical schools have reported creation of a required exposure to pathology within clinical clerkships. In 2013, the Ohio State University Medical Center had mandatory pathology experiences integrated within the surgery and obstetrics and gynecology clinical clerkships, including lecture, gross room, histology and immunohistochemistry laboratory rotation, and 1 h of one-on-one case sign-out with a subspecialty surgical pathologist.12 In 2018, the University of Michigan Health System introduced a mandatory 1-wk rotation through the Pathology Department within the surgery clerkship that incorporates observation in autopsy and surgical pathology and small group sessions on foundational concepts in microbiology, chemistry, and transfusion medicine.13 What both of these commendable programs have in common is a practical review of cases that are selected based on the current workflow.

We describe a required educational experience also integrated into the surgical clerkship, but which focuses on pathology cases directly related to patients students have cared for on the floors or in the operating rooms. We hoped that pathology would seem more relevant to patient management if viewed in conjunction with the students’ own cases. We also felt that regardless of what specialty a medical student chooses to enter later on, this kind of immersive connection with a pathologist and pathology directly related to their own patients would provide an attitudinal reference point for them to carry with them in their future career.

Our pilot was introduced in December 2022, with subsequent sessions expanded into the remainder of the 2022–2023 academic year. During the period that this program was developed, the medical school curriculum at the New York University (NYU) Grossman School of Medicine (NYUGSOM) was transitioning from a 4-y program to a 3-y program. The goal of this curricular change was to permit our medical students to start residency after 3 y or to use an optional 4th year of medical school for research or other professional development activities. The preclerkship curriculum at NYUGSOM follows an integrated format, in which pathology content is incorporated into basic science topics in the introductory modules and with clinical material in the subsequent system-based modules. Compression of the preclerkship curriculum resulted in the students entering their clerkship year having finished either 18 mo (previous schedule) or 1 y (revised schedule) of preclerkship studies. The clerkship year was correspondingly shortened from 48 wk to 40 wk. The surgery clerkship was previously repeated eight times per year but transitioned to four times in the revised curriculum. Correspondingly, the number of students on the clerkship at any given time was doubled to accommodate approximately 30 students in each rotation.

We were fortunate to have the enthusiastic support of the Department of Surgery when we proposed this initiative. Our program for exposure of pathology within the surgery clerkship was developed following close collaboration with the surgery clerkship directors. The mutual goal for development of this activity, as well as to emphasize the role of pathology in clinical management, was to compensate for decreased student exposure to pathology in the compressed preclinical curriculum. There was no student collaboration in the initial design of the pathology sessions, but the sessions were formally reviewed by the students in targeted surveys after sessions at the beginning of the program (see below), and going forward, by general clerkship feedback after every block by participants and student clerkship liaisons. Parenthetically, despite the compression of the clerkship year during the curriculum change, the length of the surgery clerkship was actually extended to 8 wk from 6 wk, facilitating the introduction of supplemental educational activities, including the new pathology conference.

We incorporated small group sessions into the already existing clerkship Friday afternoon didactic modules to accommodate time constraints within the clerkship curriculum, to avoid students being pulled away from their clinical responsibilities, and to prevent excessive time commitments for our pathology instructors.

Two key components were used to introduce students to the Department of Pathology and provide them with an enhanced understanding of pathology's role in clinical settings: an orientation tour and small group working sessions.

The program began with an orientation to the Pathology Department on the 1st day of the students' surgery clerkship rotation. This initial exposure was a 20-min guided tour through surgical pathology led by pathology residents, with each tour accommodating approximately 10 students. We wanted to introduce the students to pathology residents, with whom the students would likely have more in common due to closeness in age and experience. The pathology residents, on their part, were enthusiastic about volunteering their time to interact with the medical students and promote pathology in a session that required a relatively limited time commitment. The tour included essential elements such as the introduction to requisition forms, specimen accessioning, showing the students the Grossing Room with a brief view of an interesting gross specimen, and a short stop in Histology to view the preparation of histology and immunohistochemistry slides. Additionally, the tour visited the Frozen Section area, providing insight into the intra-operative Frozen Section and highlighting both its significance and limitations in surgical pathology. The students also walked through the corridor of pathologists' offices, using it as an opportunity to talk to attending pathologists during the tour and show that a “Doctor's office” also applies to pathology.

The central feature of the program was the clinicopathology small group working session. These sessions were 2 h long. Held twice per clerkship rotation, each session involved half of the students on rotation (14–15). In the interest of time, the students were grouped in pairs, each of which collaborated on presenting one patient. We emphasized that the selected patients could exhibit disorders that could be anything reviewed by pathology, as even the “mundane” disorders, such as acute appendicitis, provided rich possibilities for discussion. The small group working sessions met in the Department of Pathology in a room containing a multiheaded microscope as well as a computer with projector and screen for viewing glass slides and PowerPoint presentations. The sessions were facilitated by a pathology faculty instructor. We selectively recruited pathology attendings to be facilitators who we knew were talented educators and engaging conversationalists.

Organization of the sessions required the participation of the surgery clerkship co-ordinator and a pathology administrative co-ordinator. Before each session, the surgery clerkship co-ordinator assigned students to sessions and designated the working pairs. During the week before the session, each pair of students was asked to select a patient case for presentation. The pathology administrative co-ordinator confirmed the final sign-out status and availability of the case, arranging for slides and pathology reports to be delivered to the participating pathology instructor, who could prepare for the pathology review and discussion in advance.

The format of the sessions for each patient began with assigned presentations by each student pair of a brief PowerPoint review of their patient's clinical history and description of the operative procedure that had been done. The pathology faculty instructor followed with presentation by PowerPoint display of gross images (if available) of the specimen that had been resected and a review of the glass slides on the multiheaded microscope, discussing the pathological findings and their significance for patient management. Returning to their PowerPoint presentation, the responsible students followed with a brief didactic discussion of the patient's disorder. Each case presentation and discussion were limited to 15 min to ensure that the entire session did not exceed 2 h (Fig. 1).

The assessment of students in this program was limited to aspects of professionalism: timely selection of cases, timely submission of the PowerPoint presentation, on-time attendance, and being prepared to discuss the clinical and educational aspects of the case. We had decided not to assign a grade for the students’ presentations for two major reasons: it would be difficult to develop a consistent grading system for the rotating pathology faculty, and we preferred that this pathology experience be (what we hoped would be) a low-key and enjoyable component of the demanding surgery clerkship.

The students were asked to complete an anonymous online survey following each of the 1st five sessions, providing their feedback on the format and educational value of this program. Seven targeted questions solicited responses on a 4-point Likert scale (strongly agree, agree somewhat, disagree somewhat, and strongly disagree), and others were open ended for comments.

We received 48 responses (71% response rate) to our anonymous survey. The use of these anonymous survey data met our institutional review board's criteria for self-certification as Quality Improvement/Program Evaluation (collected for QI/Program Evaluation, anonymous) and therefore did not require human subjects research review.

In the quantitative feedback, all statements received a high proportion (>85%) of agreement (“strongly agree” plus “agree somewhat”; Fig. 2). We were delighted to see that the statement, “Putting together my presentation and reviewing the pathology provided insight into the role of pathology in patient management,” showed 100% agreement. Other responses confirmed overall satisfaction with the session's educational value. In response to an additional yes–no question, only 15% of students stated that pathology had been included in any of their previous clerkships, which they described as reviewing intra-operative biopsies in the Frozen Section room during Surgery or Obstetrics & Gynecology rotations.

The submitted free-form comments highlighted the program's contributions toward achieving three major learning objectives: (1) appreciation of the practical application of pathology toward clinical management, (2) enhanced knowledge of pathological processes in disease, and (3) recognizing the importance of collaborative efforts between different specialties, particularly between pathologists and surgeons.

Detailed feedback attributed the program's success to its format of quick case presentations with a focus on pathology, accompanied by an open table for questions and discussion. “Picking patients we had seen,” with discussion of how pathology guided their treatment decisions, was particularly valued. Students enjoyed the opportunity to view actual patient slides by microscopy in person with a pathologist. The instructors were praised for their approachability and effectiveness in teaching, making the learning environment “relaxed” and “engaging.” Orientation to workflow in pathology and to interpretation of an official pathology report were also appreciated.

When asked for suggestions for improving this activity, time management was identified most frequently, as sessions often ran overtime. Comments included the need for more efficient and concise student presentations. As a result of these comments, more specific instructions provided in advance of subsequent sessions were able to address time management issues more effectively. Some students found timely selection of patient cases challenging because we required that the pathology reports be finalized and the slides available. On rare occasions where selection of an appropriate patient case was not possible, an available “teaching case” from an archive of previously selected cases was provided to students to discuss.

Students also suggested introducing a similar program into other clerkships, particularly obstetrics and gynecology.

Feedback was also informally solicited after each session from the faculty running the small group working conferences and the residents leading the department tours. Their comments contributed to further improvement of the program, including such features as enhancing certain elements of the tours and restricting the departmental multiheaded microscope room to the exclusive use of the student groups during the sessions, thus ensuring that competing noise was eliminated.

In summary, we agree that the integration of pathology into the medical curriculum during the clinical years is highly desirable as a means to illustrate the essential role pathology plays in patient management. Our program at NYUGSOM integrates pathology into the surgery clerkship, but with an emphasis on correlation of pathology findings with the students’ own patients. We believe that this approach not only enriches the educational experience but also enhances the perceived relevance and applicability of pathology to clinical practice.

The preliminary feedback suggests a high degree of student engagement and satisfaction. This hands-on, case-based approach to their patients resonates with students, as it underscores the real-world application of pathology in patient care. Other feedback that was meaningful recognized collaboration of pathologists with clinicians (particularly surgeons) as an important feature of integrative patient care. Student appreciation of the personal interaction with pathologists in the sessions may help to alleviate negative stereotypes. In general, the positive reception of our program aligns with the findings of Taylor et al., where the integration of pathology rotations within surgical clerkships significantly shifted student perspectives about pathology.14 At this time, however, it is too early for us to assess the effect that this program may have on students’ future career choice.

The other advantages of our program include the manageable time and personnel requirements for providing mandatory exposure of all medical students to pathology practice in the clinical years. In addition to the brief orientation tour at the beginning of each rotation of the surgery clerkship, the interactive small group sessions are accommodated within the already designated formal didactic module of the clerkship, thus minimizing competition for curricular time. The length of the two 2-h sessions per clerkship rotation is not tedious for the students and is not an excessive time commitment for the participating pathology instructors. There is a limited number of pathology instructors who are required for this program (approximately 8–10 per year), minimizing the personnel demands on the department and ensuring that the most enthusiastic and effective teachers can be selected.

Challenges of the program included providing the appropriate space with exclusive access for the didactic program. Timely selection of patient cases proved challenging for some students. Meticulous administrative assistance from both Departments of Pathology and Surgery is required for smooth functioning. Detailed instructions to students about length and scope of presentations are critical for time management. As in most important initiatives in health care, communication takes center stage to organize this kind of in-rotation experience.

For the future, we are considering expansion of the program into other clerkships, both predominantly surgical (such as obstetrics and gynecology), and potentially into medical clerkships with an additional focus on Laboratory Medicine. We will discuss other approaches to assessment that evaluate targeted medical knowledge – perhaps via questions introduced into end-of-clerkship tests. Pathology workflow concepts may be addressed in similar fashion, or potentially through an online module about utilization of pathology with follow-up questions.

In conclusion, the NYUGSOM's approach to integrating pathology into a surgery clerkship presents a novel and effective strategy to enhance medical students' exposure to and interest in pathology. By directly tying pathology learning to clinical cases and patient management, the program ensures relevance and applicability and potentially addresses the challenge of declining interest in pathology as a career choice. We hope that this innovative method, which synergizes practical engagement with interactive teaching, may serve as a model for other institutions seeking to revitalize interest in pathology.
