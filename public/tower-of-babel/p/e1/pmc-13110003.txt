Clonal amplification of peripheral CD4+ and CD8+ T cells is associated with clinical response to checkpoint blockade in chronic hepatitis B
Authors: Samuel C Kim, Christopher Richards, Mario Cortese, Julie J Lin, Jeffrey J Wallin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTherapeutic strategies for chronic hepatitis B (CHB) aim to achieve functional cure, specifically defined by sustained off-treatment elimination of hepatitis B virus (HBV) surface antigen. New treatment approaches are being developed to enhance HBV-specific immune responses to reach this goal. To better understand how checkpoint blockade works in CHB, we conducted single-cell sequencing analysis of blood samples from four patients who either responded or did not respond to a single dose of nivolumab (an anti-PD-1 therapy). We examined cells at baseline and at two subsequent timepoints during treatment. Our findings revealed that a patient who achieved functional cure displayed higher baseline levels of stem cell-like memory CD8+ T cells, along with increases in CD8+ T effector cells, CD8+ T effector memory cells, and CD4+ T cells that reexpress CD45RA at 24 weeks after nivolumab administration. Additionally, we observed the emergence of specific T and B cell clones during treatment that correlated with positive response. These clones were identified within CD8 T effector, CD8 T effector memory, CD4 T memory cells re-expressing CD45RA, and memory B cell populations. Notably, some prominent memory B cell clones had not undergone isotype switching, indicating the development of new B cell responses rather than modification of existing immune responses. These results suggest that certain T cell phenotypes in peripheral blood, together with specific clonal expansions of T and B cells, may indicate successful clinical response to checkpoint blockade therapy in CHB. Developing treatments that specifically enhance these immune mechanisms may lead to more effective therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) is a leading cause of chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, and liver cancer, posing a significant global public health concern. The disease is characterized by an ineffective antiviral immune response, allowing persistent hepatitis B virus (HBV) replication that often evades detection by the innate immune system.1 Furthermore, the tolerogenic liver environment and the virus’s ability to produce and secrete large quantities of viral antigens progressively impair the function and number of HBV-specific B and T cells.2,3 Therapeutic strategies for CHB aim to achieve a functional cure, marked by sustained loss of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) without ongoing treatment. Emerging treatments are designed to harness HBV-specific immune responses to reach this goal.

Following antigen exposure, naive CD8+ T cells (Tn) undergo activation, proliferation, and differentiation into diverse T cell subsets, including stem-like memory T cells (Tscm), effector memory T cells (Tem) and effector T cells (Teff). Tscm cells represent an early differentiation stage and exhibit key characteristics, such as self-renewing capacity, longevity and enhanced proliferative and further differentiation potential across the T cell spectrum.4,5 The transcription factors Lef1 and Tcf7 effectively mark Tn and Tscm CD8+ T cells, as their levels decrease as CD8+ T cells differentiate upon stimulation into memory and effector cell populations upon stimulation.6

The generation of diverse virus-specific B and T cell populations is crucial for effective host responses to HBV and ultimately determines infection outcome.7 This diversity is achieved through a complex process of gene recombination that generates unique B cell receptor (BCR) and T cell receptor (TCR) variable regions, allowing each B or T cell to express receptors of a single specificity capable of recognizing and initiating an antigen-specific response.

Sustained control of HBV requires effective coordination of CD8+ T cell and B cell responses by CD4+ T cells. A higher incidence of HBV-specific CD4 memory responses has been associated with functional cure of CHB with significantly stronger responses than those in chronic CHB infection.8 These memory responses were also shown to occur after acute HBV infection after it has been resolved.

A coordinated antibody response requires the recognition of viral proteins by B cells.9 Defects in B cell responses are a hallmark of CHB which are defined by atypical memory B cell subsets and regulatory B cell populations.10 Antibodies targeting the hepatitis B surface antigen (anti-HBsAg) serve as a biomarker for functional cure in chronic hepatitis B (CHB) patients. Additionally, the levels of antibodies against the hepatitis B core antigen (anti-HBcAg) have been shown to predict the efficacy of nucleos(t)ide analog and peginterferon treatments in hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg)-positive CHB.11,12 Furthermore, in CHB, antibodies to HBsAg and HBcAg are predominantly of the immunoglobulin (Ig)M and IgG isotypes, respectively.13

Despite the shortage of immune effector cells and inadequate immune response in CHB patients, evidence suggests that activating the immune system can clear the virus.14 Patients with CHB who demonstrate a high frequency of activated, intrahepatic HBV-specific CD8+ T cells, combined with peripheral cytotoxic T cells that produce cytokines and expand in number, have been shown to achieve HBV control.15

The functional state of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells is important, as the presence of TCF7+CD8+ T cells in melanoma tumors has been associated with improved response to anti-PD-1 therapy.16 Expression of the transcription factor TCF7 is also required to restore CD8+ T cell responses and ameliorate chronic murine LCMV infection.17,18 In addition, PD-1 blockade has been shown to partially restore HBV-specific B-cell responses.19,20

The presence of exhausted CD8+ T cells in CHB patients is a hallmark of CHB.21 Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) is expressed on T cells, dendritic cells, macrophages and hepatocytes.22,23 The binding of PD-L1 to its receptor programmed death-1 (PD-1) plays a central role in inhibiting naïve and effector T-cell responses, thereby promoting T-cell tolerance.24 PD-1 is highly expressed on intrahepatic T cells in chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, particularly on HBV-specific T cells.25,26 Blocking the PD-1:PD-L1 axis has been shown to improve anti-HBV T-cell responses in human liver and in preclinical HBV models.27–29 PD-1 blockade has now been shown in multiple cancer indications to restore the activation of T cells and elevate the host immune system resulting in significant clinical efficacy with durable response.30,31 Recent studies have found that expansion of matching T cell clones within tumors, surrounding normal tissue, and peripheral blood is associated with positive response to PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint immunotherapy for cancer treatment.32

In a phase 1b study, 12 virally suppressed HBeAg negative chronic hepatitis B (CHB) patients received nivolumab (anti-PD-1) with or without the vaccine GS-4774.33 The primary endpoint was the mean change in HBsAg levels 12 weeks after the nivolumab dose. Three subjects exhibited HBsAg declines of over 0.5 log10 by the end of the study. Notably, one patient’s HBsAg levels decreased from greater than 1 × 103 IU/ml HBsAg at baseline to undetectable at week 20, accompanied by a substantial antigen-specific immune response at week 24.

To investigate how PD-1 blockade therapy works in CHB, we performed comprehensive single-cell analysis of peripheral blood samples. This included RNA sequencing (scRNAseq), B-cell receptor sequencing (scBCRseq), and T-cell receptor sequencing (scTCRseq) on blood cells collected from patients who either responded or did not respond to treatment. We analyzed samples at baseline and at 4 and 24 wk of treatment. Our analysis revealed specific baseline characteristics and treatment-induced changes in immune cell populations that correlated with treatment success. Notably, we identified associations between treatment response and changes in CD4+ and CD8+ T cell and memory B cell clone frequencies.

Results

Phase 1b clinical trial of nivolumab in CHB

In an open label phase 1b study, virally suppressed HBeAg negative chronic hepatitis B (CHB) subjects received a single dose of the PD-1 inhibitor nivolumab with or without the vaccine GS-4774.33 The results showed that GS-4774 provided no significant benefit in this small study. However, nivolumab treatment was associated with a decrease in hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) titers in most subjects who received the 0.3 mg nivolumab dose. At this dose, PD-1 receptor occupancy was maintained in the periphery of these patients for at least 6 wk.

To evaluate peripheral immune responses associated with clinical response to checkpoint inhibitor treatment, we extensively profiled longitudinal peripheral blood mononuclear (PBMC) populations (Fig. 1A). Blood samples were collected at baseline, week 4, and 24 wk post single dose nivolumab treatment. We analyzed samples from 2 responding patients (R1 and R2), characterized by an HBsAg decline of ≥0.5 log10 IU/ml and 2 non-responding subjects (NR1 and NR2), to compare response groups (Fig. 1B). All 4 individuals had comparable baseline albumin, bilirubin, and lymphocyte counts and tested negative for HDV, HIV, and HCV (Table S1).

The four participants evaluated in this study (NR1, NR2, R1, and R2) were given a single dose of nivolumab. Two participants (NR2 and R2) also received 2 doses of the yeast-based vaccine GS-4774. R1 achieved HBsAg negative status, indicating functional cure of CHB, developed significant anti-HBs titers and an increase in antigen-specific T cells during treatment (Fig. 1B and33). This subject also experienced a transient grade 3 alanine aminotransferase flare at week 4 and had a significant increase in HBsAg T cells at week 24 (Fig. 1B, Table S1, and33)

Single cell transcriptomic profiling captured shifts in CD8+ T cell makeup with PD-1 blockade

To further characterize the peripheral immune response of the 4 study participants, we performed scRNAseq. We thawed cryopreserved PBMC samples, and prepared and sequenced libraries at 3 timepoints (BL and weeks 4 and 24 post nivolumab dose) in duplicate. The single cell data was integrated to eliminate doublets and low-quality cells damaged during processing. In total, 96,590 high-quality cells with 1,390 median genes per cell were clustered into 22 distinct populations (Fig. 2A). Cells from all clusters were consistently distributed across the different participants. Cell types were annotated using key lineage and phenotypic marker genes (Fig. 2B). The most frequent cell type was CD4+ T cells (32.5% in total; 12.2% Tn, 7.2% Tcm, 6.2% Tem and 2.7% Treg), followed by CD8+ T cells (21.7% in total; 9.4% Tn/Tscm, 7.1% Teff and 5.2% Tem), monocytes (classical 12.5% and CD16+ 3.7%), B cells (naive 11.1% and memory 4.0%), NK cells (CD56dim 6.8% and CD56bright = 0.7%) and γ T cells (3.0%), and mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells (1.2%). The remaining cells, such as dendritic cell populations, represented less than 1% of the total (Fig. 2A and Table S2).

(B) Cell types were assigned to each cluster using selected marker genes. Whenever the dominant cell type of a cluster was unclear, we used differential gene expression testing and analysis of overexpressed signaling pathways in addition to the displayed marker genes.

To identify relationships across CD8+ T cell developmental stages (Tn, Tscm, Tem, and Teff), we subclustered Seurat-defined populations and mapped them onto a pseudotime trajectory generated using the Monocle algorithm34–36 (Fig. 3). Within the PBMC data set, we detected all CD8+ T cell populations based on key lineage and phenotypic marker genes, with the most mature populations reaching pseudotime values up to 34 (Fig. 3A and Fig. S1). We defined 5 distinct Monocle clusters (designated T8.1, T8.2, T8.3, T8.4, and T8.5) and mapped them onto UMAP plots showing defined CD8+ T cell populations (Fig. 3A).

Pseudotime values were plotted on top of the CD8+ T cell monocle clusters (middle) based on pseudotime trajectory values provided on the same UMAP plot (bottom). (B) CD8+ T cell phenotype composition of each monocle cluster. (C) Development trajectories based on pseudotime for the 5 CD8+ T cell monocle clusters.

Our analysis revealed that T8.1 and T8.4 clusters corresponded to CD8+ Tn and Tscm phenotypes, respectively. These early-stage clusters expressed characteristic marker genes (SELL, CCR7, LEF1, and TCF7) and displayed the lowest pseudotime values (T8.1: mean = 5, range 0–10; T8.4: mean = 12, range: 8–15) (Fig. S5, Fig. 3B and C). The T8.2 cluster aligned with Tem CD8+ T cells, characterized by increased gene expression of CXCR3, GZMK, CCL5, and GZMA, and showed an intermediate mean pseudotime values (mean = 23, range 18–28). The most advanced clusters, T8.3 and T8.5 corresponded to distinct Teff populations with high pseudotime values (T8.3: mean = 32, range 28–34; T8.5: mean = 30, range 27–31), respectively. Both Teff clusters exhibited pronounced expression of PRF1, CCL5, GZMA, GZMH, GZMB, LGALS1, and GNLY, though only T8.3 distinctly showed higher expression of KLRB1 and FCGR3A compared to T8.5.

We next identified the longitudinal trajectories of the CD8+ T cell subsets for each participant (Fig. 4). The baseline CD8+ subset frequencies varied substantially between individuals, as evidenced by distinct cluster densities and pseudotime values. NR1 exhibited predominantly naive T cells (T8.1) with a minor Teff component, whereas NR2 showed high proportions of Tem and Teff cells. Importantly, we observed marked differences in the baseline gene expression profiles of the predominant Teff populations between these participants. NR1 contained a significant population of highly differentiated Teff cells (T8.3), while NR2 displayed elevated levels of an alternative Teff cluster (T8.5) with minimal T8.3 representation. In both non-responders to nivolumab, the CD8+ T cell subpopulation distributions remained stable from baseline through weeks 4 and 24 following treatment.

(A) Density of individual cell clusters plotted for each of the four participants profiled by scRNAseq across the three PBMC collection time points. For nivolumab responders, only participant R1 displayed a Tn cell (T8.1) population at baseline (Fig. 4). Both responders, however, exhibited Tscm populations (T8.4) at baseline, with significantly higher levels compared to non-responders. At baseline, R1 and R2 also showed comparable levels of Tem and Teff (T8.3) populations, though the Tn/Tscm populations were predominantly represented at this stage.

Notable differences emerged between R1 and R2 following nivolumab treatment, particularly at week 24. While R2 maintained relatively stable cell populations across all timepoints, R1 demonstrated a dramatic reduction in T8.4 cells at week 24 compared to baseline, accompanied by a substantial increase in T8.3 cells (Fig. 4). Specifically, T8.3 cell counts in R1 increased from fewer than 20 cells at baseline and week 4 to over 600 cells at week 24 (Fig. 4B). Correspondingly, R1’s T8.4 population decreased markedly from more than 400 cells at baseline and week 4 to fewer than 20 cells at week 24. This significant peripheral phenotype shift occurred after R1 achieved functional cure in the study (Fig. 1B) and is consistent with an enhanced HBV antigen-specific T cell response as measured by ELISpot.33

We wanted to find out if signs of CD8+ T cell exhaustion were different in participant R1 compared to the other three participants we studied using scRNAseq. To do this, we utilized a set of 9 genes that are known to indicate exhaustion and have been used before to predict how well patients with liver cancer respond to checkpoint inhibitor treatment.37 We found signs of exhaustion in CD8 Tem and Teff cells but not CD8 Tn/scm cells (Fig. S2). We did not see a significant increase in the exhaustion signature score from baseline at week 4 or 24 for any of the participants.

PD-1 blockade altered the frequency of CD4+ populations that corresponded with response

We also evaluated the developmental trajectory of peripheral CD4+ T cells through pseudotime analysis (Fig. 5). In the PBMC dataset, Seurat-defined clusters revealed distinct CD4+ developmental populations: Tn, Tcm, Tem, Temra and Treg, each identified by key lineage and phenotypic marker genes (Fig. 5A and Fig. S5). These CD4+ T cell subtypes displayed pseudotime values reaching up to 17 in the most developmentally advanced populations. Using the same approach as our CD8+ T cell analysis, we defined Monocle clusters and mapped them onto UMAP plots containing the CD4+ T cell populations. This analysis identified eight major CD4+ monocle clusters, labelled T4.1 through T4.8 (Fig. 5A–C and Fig. S5A). Our results showed that clusters T4.4, T4.6, T4.7, and T4.8 represented CD4+ Tn phenotypes, characterized by expression of SELL, CCR7, LEF1, TCF7 and IL7R genes. These clusters exhibited the earliest developmental stage with the lowest pseudotime values (range: 0 to 9) (Fig. S5A and Fig. 5B and C). Clusters T4.3 and T4.5 primarily contained Tcm and Tem populations expressing TIMP1, KLRB1, and LGALS1 genes, with T4.3 showing a wider pseudotime range (2–13, mean = 8) and T4.5 displaying a more concentrated range (5–11, mean = 9). The T4.2 cluster, characterized by high expression of CCL5, GZMA, and GZMK genes, consisted mainly of Temra cells (pseudotime mean = 12; range: 8–15) but also included substantial numbers of Tem cells. Finally, the T4.1 cluster contained various memory cell populations along with significant numbers of Treg cell phenotypes with expressing FOXP3 and IL2RA genes, representing the most developmentally advanced group with a pseudotime range of 9–17 (mean = 13).

When examining CD4+ T cell subpopulations before and after nivolumab treatment in our study, we identified several distinctive patterns among participants (Fig. 6). All participants exhibited Tn populations at baseline; however, these were represented by unique Tn clusters, indicating participant-specific functional Tn phenotypes. Notably, the 2 participants who responded to nivolumab had detectable cells in the T4.7 cluster, and R1 specifically showed a substantial number of cells with the T4.6 cluster phenotype (Fig. 6A). Regarding memory populations (clusters T4.3 and T4.5), these were identifiable in all participants except for NR2 at baseline. The most differentiated cell populations, characterized by the highest pseudotime scores (clusters T4.1 and T4.2), were present in all participants at baseline, although at varying frequencies (Fig. 6B). Following nivolumab administration, non-responders NR1 and NR2 showed minimal changes from baseline. In contrast, responder R2 demonstrated a shift toward a more differentiated state at week 24 post-treatment within the Tcm/Tem clusters, specifically showing a decrease in cluster T4.5 and an increase in T4.3. Additionally, R2 exhibited a slight increase in Temra and Treg populations at this time point. Paralleling our observations in CD8+ T cells, responder R1 also showed a dramatic shift toward a more differentiated CD4+ state at week 24 post-nivolumab, specifically toward the Temra phenotype. Consequently, the major peak in cell counts shifted from a pseudotime value of 3 at baseline and week 4 to 15 at week 24 (Fig. 6B).

We also performed an unbiased analysis on higher expressed genes to define additional unconventional subsets (Fig. S3A). This analysis identified a few minor CD4+ T cells subsets with distinct sets of enriched genes. One cluster, number 33, which we had previously included in the CD4+ Tcm subset had a strong interferon response and appeared to be distinct from the rest of the CD4+ Tcm cells (Fig. S3A and B). However, longitudinal analysis of cluster 33 across each collection timepoints for each participant did not show any association of prevalence of this cluster with clinical response (Fig. S4). We did not identify any new subsets/clusters beyond those already annotated in Fig. 2 for any other cell types.

CD8+ and CD4+ T cell clones map to differentiated phenotypes at week 24 post nivolumab for the functionally cured study participant

We utilized single cell T cell receptor (TCR) sequencing to determine if clonal populations of T cells could be detected before or after nivolumab treatment (Fig. 7). At baseline, all four participants had clonal T cell populations with a clone frequency above 0.1% (Fig. 7A). By week 4 post nivolumab treatment, some of the clonal populations were no longer detected, but many of the clones were maintained at similar frequencies for each participant. Many of the same clones were detected for NR1, NR2, and R2 at 24 wk post-nivolumab compared to baseline and week 4. However, more than 20 new T cell clones appeared for R1 after 24 wk, including the most prominent clone with a clone frequency near 5%—the highest in the data set.

To determine if the expanded R1 clones were associated with a specific T cell subtype, we mapped those with the highest frequency onto CD8 and CD4 UMAP plots. The plots shown in Fig. 7B and C are the same as in Fig. 2, but with other PBMC populations removed. The top 10 expanded T cell clones mapped exclusively to either CD8 Teff/Tem or CD4 Temra populations (Fig. 7B and C). This aligns with the phenotypes that were prevalent at week 24 in our trajectory analysis for R1, suggesting the emergence of these clonal populations occurred in response to nivolumab treatment and were associated with clinical response.

Memory B cell clones emerged in the periphery on treatment for R1

B cell responses have been demonstrated as crucial for maintaining long-term control of HBV.9 B lymphocyte clusters were consistently identified across all four participants and timepoints in our analysis. We annotated these clusters using essential lineage and phenotypic marker genes (Fig. 8A and Fig. S6). Naive B cell populations expressed established B cell genes including CD79A, CD19, and MS4A1, along with naive population-specific markers IGHM and IGHD (Fig. S6B). While unswitched memory B cell populations expressed similar levels of the standard B cell genes, they showed significantly higher IGHM expression compared to IGHD. As anticipated, switched B cell populations lacked IGHM expression entirely. Plasma cell clusters were characterized by expression of markers typically associated with these antibody-secreting cells, such as CD27 and CD38.38

We utilized single cell B cell receptor (BCR) sequencing to examine clonal B cell populations before and during nivolumab treatment (Fig. 8). Before treatment, all four participants exhibited clonal B cell populations with frequencies above 0.1% (Fig. 8B). Notably, participant R1 had one dominant clone with a frequency greater than 6%, which diminished considerably at subsequent treatment timepoints. Four weeks after nivolumab administration, nearly all previously dominant baseline clones had decreased significantly in frequency. By week 24, we observed another substantial shift in the B cell repertoire. At this timepoint, each participant developed more than 10 new prevalent clones, with responding participants (R1 and R2) showing the most prominent clones at frequencies exceeding 1%.

To determine if the most common B cell clones were associated with specific B cell subtypes, we mapped the highest frequency clones (n = 10 for each timepoint) onto the B cell UMAP plot (Fig. 8A and C). At baseline and week 4, these top clones were distributed across naive, memory, and plasma cell subsets in most participants, with one exception: participant R2 lacked clones mapping to memory B cell subsets. Participants NR1, NR2, and R2 showed similar distribution patterns for their top clones. However, at 24 wk, R2 notably had no top clones associated with the plasma cell phenotype. In contrast, at this same 24-wk timepoint, the functionally cured participant R1 showed top clones predominantly in memory B cell subtypes including both those that had undergone immunoglobulin isotype switching and those that had not (Fig. 8A and C).

Discussion

A robust, coordinated immune response is essential for effectively fighting acute HBV infection. When this immune response is inadequate, the infection can persist lifelong and become difficult to eliminate.39 Nevertheless, a small percentage of chronically infected patients each year achieve functional cure through treatments such as directly acting antivirals, pegylated interferon-alpha, or other drug therapies.40 While scientists have thoroughly investigated the immune mechanisms that resolve acute infections,41 we need a more comprehensive understanding of the processes that can lead to functional cure in chronic cases. In our research, we utilized single cell sequencing techniques to examine peripheral immune responses from a select group of participants in a clinical trial who either responded or did not respond to PD-1 blockade therapy using nivolumab.

Our study yielded two significant findings. First, we identified specific baseline CD4 and CD8 Tn/Tscm phenotypes that correlated with functional cure, and these phenotypes underwent changes at 24 wk following nivolumab treatment. Second, we detected the emergence of new clonal T and B cell populations at this same timepoint. These clonal populations were absent in earlier samples, suggesting they either developed newly or were previously present at levels too low for detection given our study’s cell sampling limitations. These findings are consistent with our prior immunogenicity analysis, which showed that participant R1, who attained functional cure, exhibited enhanced antigen-specific T cell responses at 24 w post-treatment.33

Naive and memory T cell subsets had previously been analyzed by flow cytometry for participants in this study at baseline and during treatment.33,42 In the flow analysis, subject R1 had moderate levels of naive and effector memory CD8+ T cells at baseline compared to other participants. By week 24 of treatment, R1's percentage of naive CD8+ T cells had decreased, while effector memory CD8+ T cells increased. No other subjects exhibited these changes in naive and effector memory CD8+ T cells during treatment. These results were highly consistent with T cell phenotypes at baseline and on treatment as detected by scRNAseq in our study suggesting protein and gene expression were correlated.

The significant shift in peripheral T cell characteristics and corresponding increase in antigen-specific cells identified in an earlier study33—occurring after the patient’s ALT elevation and functional cure—suggests that T cells originally residing or forming in the liver may have migrated into peripheral circulation by the 24-week timepoint. Clones that decreased or remained unchanged following nivolumab treatment may have been exhausted or were targeting antigens that had been eliminated. Since participant R1 achieved functional cure by week 12, the expanded T and B cell clones may be specific to HBV antigens, potentially originating from the liver after infection clearance, though additional research is needed to confirm whether the TCRs can recognize HBV peptides presented on major histocompatibility complexes or the BCRs can recognize soluble HBV antigens. The observation that prominent B cell clones appeared at week 24 in both switched and unswitched memory B cell populations for R1 suggests these clones may produce both IgG and IgM antibodies, potentially providing long-term protection.

This study would have been strengthened by analyzing more samples from both responding and non-responding participants, as well as collecting data at additional timepoints from all subjects. For participant R1, examining a sample at week 12 of treatment would have been valuable, as this was closer to when functional cure occurred; however, such a sample was not available for analysis. These additional data points would have allowed us to determine more precisely when changes began to occur and to assess whether they persisted consistently across different timepoints.

Although rare, it is also important to note that spontaneous HBsAg seroclearance occurs in a small number of CHB individuals.43 Therefore, we cannot conclude that the functional cure for participant R1 was associated with nivolumab treatment. Support for nivolumab’s role in this study would have benefited from the analysis of samples from more responding subjects, especially if more subjects had achieved functional cure. Unfortunately, participant R1 was the only participant in this study to achieve functional cure.

In summary, we have utilized single cell sequencing techniques and analysis to investigate immune-related mechanisms associated with functional cure in CHB. Phenotypic changes in CD4, CD8 and B cell populations suggest that a coordinated response is needed for viral control. The importance of CD8+ T cells in HBV immunity has been well established,44 however, a more coordinated response that involves CD4+ T cells and B cells should not be overlooked. Therapeutic strategies designed to boost a comprehensive antigen specific response are warranted.

Materials and methods

Study design, patients, and sample details

Study design and patient details for clinical study GS-US-330-1938 (Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry ACTRN12615001133527) have been described elsewhere.33 Briefly, CHB subjects were treated with a single dose of the PD-1 inhibitor nivolumab with or without the vaccine GS-4774. All study participants were negative for HBeAg and tested negative for HDV, HIV, or HCV upon enrollment. To compare immune populations in response groups, we analyzed peripheral baseline and 4 and 24 wk post nivolumab by flow cytometry and single cell sequencing from 2 responders (R1 and R2; characterized by an HBsAg decline of ≥0.5 log10 IU/ml) and two non-responders (NR1 and NR2). Subject R1 achieved HBsAg negative status during treatment. These 4 subjects were all male, with an average age of 49 (range 38–63). In addition, these participants included 1 White individual, 1 Pacific Islander and 2 Asian individuals.

Sample collection

PBMC samples were collected at predose and 4 and 24 wk post nivolumab from 4 study participants. All study participants who participated in this study provided informed consent prior to enrollment into the study. Samples were isolated by density gradient centrifugation with Lymphoprep (Axis-shield, Dundee, UK) and Leucosep tubes (Greiner, Kremsmünster, AT).

Immunophenotyping

Cryopreserved peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were thawed and rested for 1 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 20 μg/ml DNase-1 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA). After incubation, the cells were centrifuged at 800× g for 10 min and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) supplemented with Fc Receptor Blocking Solution (5 μL per tube, BioLegend, San Diego, California, USA), Monocyte Blocker (5 μl per tube, BioLegend), Zombie NIR Fixable Viability dye (1:6,400 final dilution, BioLegend, San Diego, California, USA), and 2 mM EDTA (ThermoFisher, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Following a 20-min incubation at room temperature, the cells were stained with antibodies and incubated for an additional 20 min. The stained cells were then washed with D-PBS containing 1% FBS and 2 mM EDTA, and fixed with Fluorofix (BioLegend, San Diego, California, USA) prior to data acquisition on a 4-laser Cytek Aurora flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences, Fremont, California, USA). Data analyses were performed using FCS Express V7 software (De Novo Software, Pasadena, California, USA).

Single cell RNA, TCR, and BCR sequencing

The gene expression (GEX), BCR and TCR libraries for each PBMC sample were prepared according to the 10× Genomics Single Cell 5' Reagent Kit user guide. Briefly, cryopreserved PBMCs were quickly thawed in a 37 °C water bath following the Sample Preparation Demonstrate Protocol (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, California, USA). The cell counts and viability were determined using AO/PI staining on a Cellometer K2 instrument (Nexcelom Bioscience, Lawrence, Massachusetts, USA). The maximum volume was loaded to target capturing of 6,000 cells and two duplicate libraries were created for each sample. After droplet generation, barcoded cDNA was generated within each droplet. The cDNA was recovered using the Recovery Agent and subsequently purified using a Silane DynaBeads (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) mix. The purified cDNA was ligated to adaptors and then amplified before being purified again using SPRIselect beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, California, USA). Samples were analyzed on a TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA) to determine concentrations and size distributions of cDNA and sequencing libraries. V(D)J libraries for TCR and BCR sequences were prepared from the same cDNA molecules as outlined in the manufacturer’s protocol, with modifications to the number of PCR cycles based on the calculated cDNA library input. To obtain TCR and BCR repertoire profiles from the same input samples, V(D)J enrichment was performed using the Chromium Single Cell Human TCR/BCR Amplification Kits (10× Genomics, Pleasanton, California, USA). Finally, sequencing libraries were generated with unique sample indices for each sample and sequenced with an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer at the following sequencing depth: GEX at 300 million read pairs per library, BCR and TCR at 60 million read pairs per library.

Cell type annotation and pseudotime analysis

Sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger (v3.1.0, 10× Genomics) and quantitative analysis of transcriptomic data and TCR/BCR repertoires was performed using R package Seurat (v5.3).45–48 Data were filtered to retain only high-quality cells with less than 10% mitochondrial gene content. Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA) and cells were clustered based on 1,902 variable genes, excluding variable region TCR/BCR genes, and 30 dimensions from PCA using Louvain algorithm with a resolution parameter set to 2. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) coordinates were calculated for visualizing cell clusters.49 Cell types were determined based on the expression pattern of marker genes in each cluster. Cells identified as erythrocytes (expressing hemoglobin genes with low overall transcriptional activity) and multiplets (expressing marker genes from multiple cell types) were excluded from downstream analyses. Cell differentiation trajectory analysis was performed using R package monocle3 (v1.3.7).34–36

Exhaustion signature analysis

The exhaustion status of CD8+ T cell subsets was evaluated using a published 9-gene signature.37 The signature scores were calculated using AddModuleScore() function of Seurat package and visualized with geom boxplot of R package ggplot2 (v4.0.1) using the default configuration for summary statistics.
