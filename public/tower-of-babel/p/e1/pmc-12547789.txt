Large-bore catheter selection in endovascular management of superior vena cava syndrome
Authors: Albert Oh, Omodele Durojaye, Maham Rahimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Acute superior vena cava syndrome (SVCS) is a clinical condition caused by obstruction of blood flow through the SVC, leading to facial swelling, dyspnea, and upper extremity edema. To date, no established guidelines exist regarding optimal device selection for thrombectomy in acute SVCS, and endovascular management with mechanical thrombectomy remains underreported in the literature. We discuss two similarly presenting cases of acute SVCS managed with different large-bore thrombectomy platforms—AngioDynamics AlphaVac and Inari FlowTriever—to highlight the need for case-specific device selection and to compare clinical outcomes.

Acute superior vena cava syndrome (SVCS) is a clinical condition caused by obstruction of blood flow through the SVC, leading to facial swelling, dyspnea, and upper extremity edema. Although historically associated with malignancy, an increasing prevalence of acute SVCS has been linked to central venous catheter (CVC) use.1,2 The increase in acute, catheter-associated thrombosis has emphasized the role of endovascular therapies, such as mechanical thrombectomy.3 To date, no established guidelines exist regarding optimal device selection for thrombectomy in acute SVCS, and comparative data among large-bore aspiration systems are limited. We present two cases of acute SVCS managed with different thrombectomy platforms—AngioDynamics AlphaVac and Inari FlowTriever—chosen based on preoperative imaging and anatomical considerations. These cases highlight the need for case-specific device selection in the endovascular management of SVCS. Written informed consent was obtained from the patients for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Case report

Patient A is a 31-year-old woman with factor V Leiden and end-stage renal disease (ESRD), receiving hemodialysis via a right internal jugular (IJ) vein-tunneled CVC placed 3 months prior. She presented with facial swelling, upper chest edema, and dyspnea. Computed tomography venography demonstrated acute-on-chronic occlusive thrombus within the bilateral brachiocephalic veins (BCVs), left subclavian vein, and SVC (Fig 1). The patient was transitioned to heparin infusion from her home apixaban and taken for mechanical thrombectomy. Right femoral vein access was obtained and serially dilated to accommodate a 26F 33 cm Gore DrySeal sheath (W. L. Gore & Associates). Thrombectomy was performed within the bilateral BCV and SVC using the AngioDynamics AlphaVac system. A post-thrombectomy venogram demonstrated resolution of the thrombus burden and an area of nonflow-limiting stenosis within the SVC, on which angioplasty was not necessary. Owing to continued need for dialysis access, a CVC was placed in the left femoral vein intraoperatively. She experienced symptom resolution by postoperative day 1, and her home apixaban was resumed indefinitely. Patient B is a 57-year-old woman with type 1 Gaucher disease, treated with intravenous enzyme replacement therapy via a left subclavian vein Port-A-Cath, placed 4 years prior. Similar to patient A, she presented with facial swelling, upper extremity swelling, and dyspnea. Imaging revealed nonocclusive acute thrombus in the bilateral IJ vein, left subclavian vein, and bilateral BCVs with SVC occlusion within a stenosed segment (Fig 3). The patient was started on a heparin infusion and taken to the operating room. Right femoral vein access was obtained and serially dilated to accommodate the 24F 33 cm Intri sheath (Inari Medical). Mechanical thrombectomy was performed with the 20F Inari FlowTriever device within the bilateral IJ vein, left subclavian vein, bilateral BCVs, and SVC. A post-thrombectomy venogram demonstrated resolution of the thrombus burden with an area of flow-limiting stenosis within the SVC on which angioplasty was performed with a 12-mm plain balloon (Figs 4 and 5). After multidisciplinary discussion, patient B was transitioned to oral medication for her Gaucher disease and no longer required replacement of her port-a-cath. She experienced symptom resolution by postoperative day 1 and was discharged with a 3-month course of apixaban. Owing to her high bleeding risk secondary to Gaucher disease, the minimum course of anticoagulation was preferred. Discussion

Acute SVCS is a type of thoracic central venous obstruction with rising incidence owing to increasing CVC use.2 Although venous bypass and stenting are effective management options percutaneous mechanical thrombectomy has emerged as a safe, rapid, and minimally invasive treatment option for acute or acute-on-chronic presentations.3, 4, 5, 6 Anticoagulation for a minimum 3-month course with maintenance of the catheter is typically the first-line option.7 CVC removal may be warranted if it is no longer in use, infected, or if symptoms persist despite anticoagulation.7,8 However, no established guidelines exist to date. We add to that discussion by presenting two clinically similar cases that were treated with different devices based on information gathered via preoperative imaging. Device selection should depend on preoperative imaging, anatomical considerations, and surgeon expertise.

For patients with significant symptoms or extensive thrombus burden, endovascular therapy provides faster symptom relief. Azizi et al8 emphasize mechanical thrombectomy and catheter-directed thrombolysis as effective interventions in acute, catheter-associated SVCS, often preferred over medical management alone. López-Rubio et al9 further support this strategy, particularly in acute presentations where rapid venous decompression is required.

Preoperative imaging in patient A demonstrated acute-on-chronic occlusive thrombus, in with to patient B, whose preoperative imaging demonstrated primarily acute thrombus with a significantly stenosed region. The presence or absence of significant central venous stenosis helps to guide the decision between funnel-tipped vs straight-bore aspiration systems. When there is minimal to no central venous stenosis associated with the thrombosed area, a funnel-tipped system such as AlphaVac can fully expand within the lumen, enabling more efficient clot aspiration. In contrast, when there is concern for significant or flow-limiting stenosis, a funnel tip may not expand fully, making a straight-bore system like FlowTriever better suited to navigate the stenosed region and remove thrombus effectively.

It is important to note that thrombus chronicity cannot be determined with complete certainty. Our approach relies on catheter dwell time, symptom onset, and imaging, with the goal of removing acute components via thrombectomy and addressing chronic segments with balloon angioplasty as needed.

For patient A, the AngioDynamics AlphaVac system was preferred owing to its flexible, funnel-shaped cannula that approximates the diameter of the SVC, enabling effective retrieval of acute-on-chronic thrombus while minimizing embolic risk during aspiration. In patients with mixed thrombus morphology, the AlphaVac system may be better suited, particularly when robust aspiration is needed in a high-flow central venous structure.

For patient B, the Inari FlowTriever system, straight-bore aspiration catheter without a funnel component minimized the risk of device-related vessel wall injury owing to its less aggressive profile compared with the AlphaVac. In the presence of significant SVC stenosis, careful attention must be paid to reduce central vein wall trauma. Balloon angioplasty can address regions of stenosis, if flow limiting, after thrombectomy. For patient A, further management with angioplasty was deemed unnecessary owing to lack of flow-limiting stenosis. Furthermore, the nitinol disc component was not deployed to decrease embolization risk. However, disc deployment may be reasonable in other cases.

There is no standardized approach to CVC management after catheter-associated SVC thrombosis. Our practice is to avoid recannulating previously thrombosed central veins whenever possible, prioritizing the creation of arteriovenous fistulas or grafts for patients with ESRD. If a CVC is still required for patients without ESRD, a multidisciplinary approach is helpful in timing of replacement. We ideally wait 3 to 6 months before replacing the CVC and confirm vessel patency with follow-up imaging before proceeding.

These two patients presented with similar symptoms but required different devices. Both achieved rapid symptom resolution without complications. Although multiple large-bore thrombectomy devices exist, this case report emphasizes the need for a case-specific approach to device selection.10 Surgeon familiarity, preoperative imaging, and dynamic intraoperative assessment all contribute to management options and decisions.

Conclusions

These cases demonstrate that individualized selection of mechanical thrombectomy devices—based on thrombus characteristics and venous anatomy—optimizes safety and outcomes in the endovascular management of acute SVCS.
