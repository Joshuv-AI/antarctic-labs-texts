Association between Relative Skeletal Muscle Mass and Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease Development in a Community-Based Population
Authors: Yiting Xu, Tingting Hu, Xiaoya Li, Yun Shen, Yunfeng Xiao, Yufei Wang, Yuqian Bao, Xiaojing Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study explores how relative skeletal muscle mass is associated with the development of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and the remission of baseline MASLD in a community-based population cohort.MethodsThe study included 1,544 participants with an average age of 58 years. All participants underwent baseline and follow-up assessments in 2015 or 2016. Appendicular skeletal muscle mass was measured using an automatic bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), and total skeletal muscle mass was calculated using the BIA equation. Relative skeletal muscle mass was evaluated in two ways: divided by weight and divided by visceral fat area (VFA). Liver fat content was assessed using ultrasonography, and the NAFLD fibrosis score was calculated to quantify the degree of liver fibrosis.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 2.1 years, each one-standard deviation increase in relative total skeletal muscle mass was associated with a decreased risk of MASLD incidence among males (hazard ratio [HR], 0.56; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.43 to 0.74, adjusted for weight; and HR, 0.23; 95% CI, 0.13 to 0.42, adjusted for VFA) and females (HR, 0.62; 95% CI, 0.47 to 0.83, adjusted for weight; and HR, 0.37; 95% CI, 0.19 to 0.70, adjusted for VFA). In both sexes, the increase in relative appendicular skeletal muscle mass was also associated with a reduced MASLD risk. We found statistically significant inverse associations between relative skeletal muscle mass and both liver fat content and liver fibrosis.ConclusionLow relative muscle mass is associated with an increased risk of MASLD incidence and persistence. Therefore, increasing skeletal muscle mass over time might aid in the prevention and management of MASLD.

INTRODUCTION

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), histopathologically characterized by the presence of ≥5% of hepatocytes containing fat in the absence of excess alcohol use and other concomitant liver diseases, is emerging as a leading cause of liver-related morbidity. Additionally, its association with cardiovascular disease is gaining prominence. Recognizing the intricate relationship between dysmetabolism and steatotic liver disease, a recent multi-society Delphi consensus statement proposed a novel nomenclature for fatty liver disease, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), and effectively phased out the term NAFLD.1 The MASLD definition mandates that individuals with hepatic steatosis meet at least one cardiometabolic criterion.2

Similar to the liver, skeletal muscle plays a pivotal role in whole-body insulin-mediated glucose metabolism. Sarcopenia, characterized by a general loss of skeletal muscle mass and function, is associated with increased metabolic dysfunction.3 Sarcopenia and MASLD share pathological and physiological mechanisms, and the co-occurrence of sarcopenia and NAFLD has been observed in certain populations.4,5 Although several studies have reported an increased risk of sarcopenia in individuals with fatty liver disease, little research has explored the influence of sarcopenia on the incidence and progression of NAFLD. Recent studies reported that NAFLD is proportionally affected by muscle mass, and a lower muscle mass index is an important contributor to the development of NAFLD, even when muscle reduction does not reach the threshold for sarcopenia.5,6 Furthermore, increases in relative skeletal muscle mass might lead to lower rates of NAFLD or the resolution of existing NAFLD.7,8 NAFLD includes a significant number of patients without metabolic risk factors (lean NAFLD). However, unlike NAFLD, MASLD includes any patient with relevant metabolic risk factors and steatosis, regardless of additional causes.9 Few recent prospective studies have explored the association between muscle mass and the risk of MASLD. Therefore, in this study, we investigated the association between skeletal muscle mass and the incidence and development of MASLD in a community-based population cohort.

METHODS

Study population

We conducted a prospective cohort study that included participants recruited from four communities in Shanghai between 2013 and 2014.10,11 All participants provided relevant information through standardized questionnaires and underwent comprehensive assessments, including physical examinations, laboratory testing, and body composition measurements. Participants with missing data were excluded, and eligible participants underwent a follow-up assessment in 2015 or 2016. We included 1,544 participants with complete data in these analyses.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (approval number 2023-KY-132[K]). Informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Baseline clinical and laboratory measurements

All participants fasted for a minimum of 10 hours before blood sampling. Fasting blood samples were collected to measure fasting plasma glucose, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting serum insulin, serum total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and C-reactive protein levels. Patients without a valid history of diabetes performed a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, and those with diabetes followed a standard bread meal test protocol. Height, weight, waist circumference, and blood pressure were measured using standardized methods. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height2 (m2).

Evaluations for covariates

Following the 2000 World Health Organization criteria, overweight/obesity was defined as a BMI ≥25.0 kg/m2.12 Diabetes, in accordance with the 2023 American Diabetes Association guidelines, was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, 2-hour plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥6.5%, or a previous diagnosis of diabetes.13 Impaired glucose regulation was diagnosed as 6.1 mmol/L≤ fasting plasma glucose <7.0 mmol/L, 7.8 mmol/L≤ 2-hour plasma glucose <11.1 mmol/L, or 5.7%≤ HbA1c <6.5%.14 Participants with diabetes and those with impaired glucose tolerance were classified as hyperglycemic. Smoking was defined as the consumption of at least one cigarette per day for at least 6 months.15 Drinking was defined as the consumption of at least 20 g of alcohol per day for at least 6 months.16 Physical activity levels were classified as light, moderate, or high according to the 2001 International Physical Activity Questionnaire.17 A dietary quality score was collected using a food frequency questionnaire and defined according to five healthy dietary behaviors, as previously described.18

Body composition measurement

Due to the high cost and time required for accurate body composition measurements through dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, we used prediction equations and an automatic bioelectric analyzer to evaluate muscle mass in a relatively large community-based population. The total fat percentage, total fat mass, and fat-free mass were measured using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA; TBF-418B; Tanita Corp.).19 Fat distribution, including the visceral fat area (VFA) and subcutaneous fat area, was assessed using magnetic resonance imaging (Archiva 3.0 T; Philips Medical Systems). Image quality was controlled by an experienced operator. Average VFA values were calculated using image analysis software (slice-O-matic version 4.2; Tomovision Inc.) according to a previously described protocol.18

Total skeletal muscle mass was calculated using the BIA equation developed by Janssen et al.20: total skeletal muscle mass (kg)=[(height2/BIA-resistance×0.401)+(sex×3.825)+(age×–0.071)]+5.102, where height is in centimeters, BIA-resistance is in ohms, sex is coded as male=1 and female=0, and age is in years. Subsequently, relative total skeletal muscle mass was calculated in two ways: total skeletal muscle mass divided by weight in kilograms, and total skeletal muscle mass divided by VFA. Appendicular skeletal muscle mass was measured using BIA. Relative appendicular skeletal muscle mass was calculated in two ways: appendicular skeletal muscle mass divided by weight in kilograms, and appendicular skeletal muscle mass divided by VFA. Relative skeletal muscle mass (both total skeletal muscle mass and appendicular skeletal muscle mass) was defined as the ratio of skeletal muscle mass to VFA and log-transformed.

Definition of MASLD

A trained ultrasonographer who was blinded to the study design and clinical details of the participants performed abdominal ultrasonographic examinations using a Voluson 730 Expert B-mode ultrasound machine (5.0-MHz transducer; GE Healthcare), as reported previously.21 Participants with hepatic steatosis who had at least one of the following cardiometabolic risk factors and no other underlying causes of stenosis were categorized as having MASLD22: (1) BMI ≥25 kg/m2 or waist circumference ≥90 cm for males and ≥85 cm for females; (2) fasting glucose levels ≥5.6 mmol/L, 2-hour glucose levels ≥7.8 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥5.7%, or type 2 diabetes mellitus; (3) blood pressure ≥130/85 mmHg or undergoing specific antihypertensive drug treatment; (4) triglycerides ≥1.70 mmol/L or undergoing lipid-lowering treatment; and (5) high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≤1.0 mmol/L for males and ≤1.3 mmol/L for females or undergoing lipid-lowering treatment.

All the instrument settings, including ‘gain,’ and ‘depth,’ were calibrated using a tissue-mimicking phantom (model 057; Computerized Imaging Reference Systems). The regions of interest in the ultrasound images were analyzed using image software certified by the National Institutes of Health (ImageJ version 1.41o), as described previously.21 Liver fat content was then calculated based on the following equation: −62.592×liver-kidney echo ratio+168.076×liver attenuation coefficient−27.863.23 The NAFLD fibrosis score (NFS) comprised six variables (age, hyperglycemia, BMI, platelet count, albumin, and aspartate transaminase [AST]/alanine transaminase [ALT] ratio) and was used as an independent indicator of advanced liver fibrosis; the score was calculated as follows: −1.675+0.037×age (year)+0.094×BMI (kg/m2)+1.13×impaired fasting glycemia or diabetes/diabetes (yes=1, no=0)+0.99×AST/ALT ratio−0.013×platelet count (×109/L)−0.66×albumin (g/dL).24

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS for Windows version 20.0 (IBM Corp.). For continuous variables, normally distributed data are expressed as the mean±standard deviation (SD), and skewed data are presented as the median and interquartile range. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Intergroup comparisons for normally distributed variables, skewed variables, and categorical variables were performed using Student’s t-test, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and the chi-square test, respectively. The Cox hazard regression model was used to estimate the hazard ratio and 95% confidence interval of MASLD incidence according to skeletal muscle mass. Relative skeletal muscle was treated as a continuous variable, with results reported per one-SD increase in skeletal muscle. To account for the significant physiological differences in skeletal muscle mass between males and females, we conducted a sex-stratified analysis. Separate multivariate models were run for males and females while adjusting for the covariates. The subgroup analysis was further stratified according to overweight/obesity and hyperglycemic status. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to determine the association between the skeletal muscle mass index and liver fat content and liver fibrosis. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05 (two-tailed).

RESULTS

Characteristics of the study participants

The age of the participants was 24 to 80 years (mean 58), and the proportion of males was 40.3%. At baseline, 686 patients were identified as having MASLD. Table 1 presents the general characteristics of the study population. Participants with MASLD were older than those without it, and metabolic dysfunctions such as hyperglycemia, high systolic/diastolic blood pressure, hypertriglyceridemia, and hyper-low-density lipoprotein cholesterolemia were more frequently observed in the MASLD group (all P<0.01). Similarly, most anthropometric and laboratory variables, including BMI, waist circumference, lipid, glucose, and C-reactive protein levels, were significantly less favorable in patients with MASLD than in those without it (P<0.001).

Relative skeletal muscle mass and incidence of MASLD

The incidence analysis included the 858 participants who did not have MASLD at baseline. During the follow-up period, 150 (17.5%) of those participants developed MASLD. Among the 686 participants who had MASLD at baseline, 237 (34.5%) experienced remission of MASLD. Relative skeletal muscle mass was evaluated in two ways: total skeletal muscle mass index and appendicular skeletal muscle mass index. Both indices were separately adjusted by both weight and VFA. Among the participants who did not have MASLD at baseline, a sex-stratified analysis revealed that relative skeletal muscle mass was inversely associated with MASLD onset in both males and females (Table 2). In males, the appendicular skeletal muscle mass index adjusted by VFA was independently associated with MASLD when the skeletal muscle indices were simultaneously measured in a multivariate model; however, in females, total skeletal muscle mass index adjusted by weight stood out as the independent predictor (Table 2).

In the subgroup analyses, the significant inverse association between relative skeletal muscle mass and the risk of MASLD persisted in males, regardless of overweight/obesity or hyperglycemic status (Table 3). Among females, the association remained statistically significant only in those without overweight/obesity or hyperglycemia (Table 4).

Relative skeletal muscle mass level according to MASLD subgroups

Patients without MASLD at baseline were classified into two groups based on the incidence of MASLD during follow-up: the no MASLD group (n=708) and the MASLD group (n=150). Additionally, patients with MASLD at baseline were categorized into two groups: the remission of MASLD group (n=237) and the sustained MASLD group (n=449). The score on the total skeletal muscle mass index adjusted by weight was significantly lower in the developed MASLD group than the no MASLD group, and the score on the total skeletal muscle mass index was lower in the sustained MASLD group than the MASLD remission group (Fig. 1).

Association between relative skeletal muscle mass and liver fat content and liver fibrosis

Among patients who developed MASLD, a multiple linear regression analysis demonstrated that relative skeletal muscle mass adjusted by weight, but not by VFA, was inversely associated with liver fat content (standardized β=–0.339, P=0.048; standardized β=–0.328, P=0.048). However, none of the skeletal muscle mass indices were associated with NFS in the developed MASLD group. In the remission of MASLD group, only the total skeletal muscle mass index adjusted by weight was associated with NFS (standardized β=–0.239, P=0.023). In the sustained MASLD group, the inverse relationships between relative skeletal muscle mass and liver fat content were statistically significant, whether adjusting by weight or VFA (all P<0.05) (Table 5). The association between relative skeletal muscle mass and liver fibrosis was significant in this group only when it was adjusted for weight (P<0.05) (Table 5).

Sensitivity analysis

To ensure the robustness of our results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using an alcohol consumption threshold of 30 g/day for males and 20 g/day for females to redefine MASLD. After excluding participants who had more alcohol intake than that (n=164), the inverse association between relative skeletal muscle mass and MASLD incidence remained significant, which was consistent with the main results (Supplementary Table 1).

DISCUSSION

This study reports an inverse association between skeletal muscle mass, standardized by weight and VFA, and MASLD in a community-based population. Patients who developed MASLD exhibited lower skeletal muscle mass than those who did not, and those with sustained MASLD had lower skeletal muscle mass than those with remission of MASLD. Additionally, the skeletal muscle mass indices showed inverse associations with liver fat content and fibrosis.

These findings emphasize the importance of the early detection and management of muscle mass loss because muscle mass maintenance might modify the risk of developing fatty liver disease. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between muscle mass and the risk of MASLD incidence and development in a general, community-based Chinese population.

MASLD was previously associated with an 80% elevated risk of low muscle mass weight and a 30% increased risk of low muscle mass BMI in a cohort of 6,414 patients with a mean age of 53.9 years.25 Although previous studies have examined the relationship between NAFLD and sarcopenia or sarcopenic obesity, relatively few studies have explored the link between myosteatosis and fatty liver disease. In a Korean study, individuals in the lowest quartile of a skeletal muscle mass index had odds ratio for NAFLD risk of 5.16 compared with the highest quartile, after adjusting for potential confounding factors.26 Among 309 patients with biopsy-proven NAFLD, those with sarcopenia were more likely to exhibit non-alcoholic steatohepatitis than those without sarcopenia.27 In a large longitudinal cohort study spanning 7 years and involving 10,534 patients from Seoul, the highest sex-specific skeletal muscle mass index tertile at baseline was inversely associated with incident NAFLD, with an adjusted HR of 0.44 compared with the lowest tertile.7 However, that study defined incident NAFLD or the resolution of existing baseline NAFLD using the hepatic steatosis index scoring system rather than abdominal ultrasonography.

The recent adoption of the term MASLD as a result of a Delphi process involving key global stakeholders provides a non-stigmatizing description of this condition, departing from the previous diagnosis of exclusion historically known as NAFLD. This study is the first to examine the relationship between skeletal muscle mass and MASLD in a general population.

Consistent with the results of previous studies, our results suggest that skeletal muscle mass is independently and inversely associated with the incidence of MASLD in both males and females. Given that changes in body weight can influence skeletal muscle mass and MASLD incidence or remission, we used both weight and VFA adjustments to define the relative muscle mass index. As anticipated, the results for VFA adjustments of the indices were consistent with those for weight adjustment, although the association between MASLD and the skeletal muscle mass index adjusted by VFA was more pronounced than that with the index adjusted by weight. Similarly, individuals with sustained MASLD exhibited lower relative skeletal muscle mass levels than those with remission of MASLD, and skeletal muscle mass displayed inverse associations with liver fat content and liver fibrosis. However, only skeletal muscle mass levels adjusted by weight were associated with liver fat in patients who developed MASLD and with liver fibrosis in those with remission of MASLD. Considering the gradual development of liver fibrosis over an extended period, this association might become more pronounced with longer follow-up.

We observed sex-specific differences, with skeletal muscle mass showing a stronger inverse association with MASLD in males, particularly in those with obesity and hyperglycemia. From a physiological standpoint, males generally have greater muscle mass and strength than females, a disparity that might stem from variations in hormone levels such as testosterone. In terms of lifestyle, traditional gender roles and societal norms often result in distinct patterns of physical activity and dietary choices between men and women, which can subsequently influence skeletal muscle health. Given that females typically face greater challenges in building muscle mass than males, they might need to engage in more physical activity and resistance training to achieve the same muscle gains. However, that extra effort might reduce the metabolic and cardiovascular benefits typically associated with increased muscle mass.28

Several possible mechanisms connecting sarcopenia to MASLD have been proposed. Insulin resistance is the most common biochemical pathophenotype associated with obesity that leads to progressive dysfunction of the adipose–muscle–liver axis.29,30 Additionally, increased levels of inflammatory mediators, such as tumor necrosis factor-α, could contribute to the development of fatty liver disease by promoting lipid accumulation and enhancing the catabolism of muscle through a reduction in muscle protein synthesis.31 Moreover, decreased physical activity and vitamin D deficiency have been reported as risk factors for fatty liver disease and sarcopenia.32,33

This study has some limitations. First, this was a single-center, single-nationality study, and as such the findings might not be generalizable to larger populations. Second, hepatic steatosis was diagnosed by ultrasonographic examination. Although liver biopsy is considered the gold standard for the diagnosis of NAFLD, it is not typically used in asymptomatic individuals or epidemiological investigations. Third, the most precise method for measuring skeletal muscle mass is dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry or magnetic resonance imaging; however, those modalities expose patients to harmful radiation, have high costs, or take a long time, reducing their applicability in large-scale epidemiologic studies. Therefore, we used BIA instead of the gold standard for body composition measurement because it is reliable and accurate in muscle assessment, with a correlation coefficient of 0.93 with magnetic resonance imaging.34 Fourth, this study was based only on muscle mass; the effect of muscle function on MASLD was not investigated. Finally, the follow-up time was short. Ideally, future studies should extend the follow-up period to verify these results.

This study is valuable because it is the first to report the sex-stratified relationship between skeletal muscle mass and MASLD, and it confirms that relationship in individuals with obesity or hyperglycemia. Moreover, to examine the robustness of our findings, we used different methods to assess skeletal muscle mass. Absolute skeletal muscle mass included both total muscle mass and appendicular skeletal muscle mass, and relative skeletal muscle mass was adjusted by both weight and VFA.

In conclusion, during a 2-year follow-up period, we observed that relative skeletal muscle mass was significantly and inversely associated with MASLD, even after adjusting for confounding factors. This highlights the importance of the early detection of skeletal muscle mass decline in a community population. The maintenance of skeletal muscle mass might help to reduce the incidence and improve the management of MASLD.
