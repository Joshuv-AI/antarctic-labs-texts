Effect of a nighttime medical order adjustment protocol on objective and subjective sleep quality in hospitalized adults: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Sofía Parra-Avila, Juan Enrique Santos-Macías, Arnulfo Garza-Silva, Dalia Gutierrez-González, Iván Francisco Fernández-Chau, Sara Jaqueline Castruita-Von Chong, Oscar Alberto Quiroga Gómez, Juan Guillermo Rodríguez Barrientos, Maria Elena Romero-Ibarguengoitia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesSleep disruption is common among hospitalized patients and is often exacerbated by non-essential nighttime medical activities. Evidence from general hospital wards in Latin America remains limited. We evaluated the effect of an adjusted nighttime medical order protocol on sleep quality in hospitalized adults.Materials and MethodsIn this randomized controlled trial, patients were allocated to standard care or an intervention protocol in which non-essential nocturnal procedures (routine vital sign checks, medication administration, and laboratory sampling) were rescheduled. Objective sleep parameters were monitored with Fitbit Sense over three consecutive nights. The first night served as a baseline, followed by two intervention nights. Subjective sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) at baseline and study completion.ResultsFifty-one patients were included [mean (SD) age 41.2 (13.0) years; 56.9% female], with 26 (51.0%) assigned to the intervention group. Compared with controls, the intervention group had longer geometric mean [geometric standard deviation (GSD)] total sleep time [398 (1.28) vs. 301 (1.40) min; p = .015] and longer estimated Stage R [rapid eye movement (REM)] sleep duration [79.2 (1.36) vs. 58.0 (1.49) min; p = .010]. In addition, nocturnal awakenings were eliminated by the second intervention night. Subjective sleep quality also improved in the intervention group, with lower PSQI scores at study completion than in the control group [4.6 (2.5) vs. 8.7 (3.2); p = .040].ConclusionsReorganizing nighttime medical routines significantly improved objective and subjective sleep quality in hospitalized patients. This low-cost, pragmatic intervention may represent a scalable strategy for implementing sleep-friendly hospital practices. Statement of SignificanceSleep disruption in hospitalized patients is common and largely driven by modifiable care practices yet remains insufficiently targeted in routine clinical care. In this randomized controlled trial, restructuring nighttime medical orders significantly improved objective sleep parameters (Stage R [rapid eye movement] sleep, non-rapid eye movement sleep, total sleep time) and subjective sleep quality in general ward patients. These findings support workflow modification as a feasible, low-cost strategy to reduce sleep disruption, and improve inpatient experience. Key gaps remain regarding durability of effects, underlying physiological mechanisms, and applicability across different healthcare settings. Future studies should evaluate longer-term outcomes and incorporate objective physiological markers to better define the role of sleep optimization as a component of high-quality hospital care.

Introduction

Sleep is an active and reversible physiological process essential for maintaining homeostasis, metabolic recovery, and memory consolidation. It is characterized by reduced responsiveness to external stimuli and regulated by complex neurophysiological interactions between cortical and subcortical structures. The sleep cycle alternates between non–rapid eye movement sleep (NREM sleep) and Stage R (rapid eye movement [REM]) sleep, both fundamental for cognitive, immune, and metabolic integrity [1].

Sleep quality is a key determinant of recovery and overall well-being in hospitalized patients. Evidence shows that nocturnal interruptions caused by routine medical procedures significantly affect subjective sleep quality and physiological recovery, contributing to prolonged hospital stays and increased morbidity [2]. Numerous environmental and care-related factors, including ambient noise, lighting, temperature, and the timing of nursing interventions have been identified as major disruptors of sleep in hospital settings [3].

Despite growing awareness of this problem, most interventions have relied primarily on subjective evaluations of sleep quality through questionnaires, while objective measurements using physiological or wearable monitoring tools remain limited. Furthermore, few studies have assessed whether reorganizing nighttime medical orders could reduce interruptions and improve sleep architecture. In Mexico, this gap is particularly evident, as studies integrating both subjective and objective sleep assessments among patients in non-critical care wards remain scarce [4].

The primary objective of this randomized controlled trial was to evaluate whether a sleep-promoting nighttime protocol improved objective sleep parameters among hospitalized patients, including total sleep time (TST), Stage R sleep, and NREM sleep duration. Secondary objectives were to assess subjective sleep quality and nocturnal awakenings during hospitalization. Exploratory analyses examined the effects of the intervention on selected overnight physiological variables, including vital signs and glucose measurements. We hypothesized that reducing non-essential nighttime interruptions would improve both objective and subjective sleep outcomes compared with standard hospital care.

Materials and Methods

This single-blind, randomized controlled clinical trial was conducted at Hospital Clinica Nova (HCN) from 2024 to 2025 to evaluate the effect of adjusting nighttime medical orders on sleep–wake quality in hospitalized adults, measured through Stage R sleep duration and subjective sleep quality using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).

The study was conducted in accordance with the CONSORT reporting guidelines and approved by an Institutional Review Board (IRB) (Ref. 06102023-CN-N-CI) [5]. The clinical trial was registered with the Brazilian Clinical Trials Registry (ReBEC; https://ensaiosclinicos.gov.br/) under the identifier RBR-7dd6wcz and with ClinicalTrials.gov (https://clinicaltrials.gov/) under the identifier NCT07288775. The study was also assigned a Universal Trial Number (UTN) U1111-1330-9425. The study complied with the World Medical Association’s Code of Ethics (Declaration of Helsinki), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Adults aged 18–65 years admitted to general wards were eligible. Exclusion criteria were selected to minimize factors that could independently alter sleep architecture or sleep quality, including pre-existing insomnia, diagnosed sleep disorders, and the use of medications known to affect central nervous system activity (e.g. sedative, central nervous system stimulant agents or opioid). Additional exclusion criteria included prior intensive care unit admission, hemodynamic, or neurological instability. Similar exclusion criteria have been employed in previous studies evaluating sleep interventions and inpatient sleep quality [6, 7]. Hospital census lists were reviewed twice daily to identify newly admitted patients who met the eligibility criteria. Eligible patients were approached on the day of hospital admission, invited to participate, and provided written informed consent before the start of nighttime monitoring. Patients who were discharged before study completion, experienced clinical deterioration, or withdrew consent were excluded from the final analysis.

Consenting participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group, which received adjusted nighttime medical orders aimed at minimizing sleep interruptions, or the control group, which followed standard hospital protocols. Randomization was computer-generated. Following enrollment and randomization, all participants began Fitbit Sense (Healthy Metrics Research, Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA) monitoring at 8:00 pm on the day of hospital admission, regardless of the time of admission, and were monitored for three consecutive nights. Throughout the study, all participants were accommodated in single-occupancy private rooms; no patients shared hospital rooms during the study period.

Objective sleep parameters were assessed using Fitbit Sense wearable device. Although polysomnography remains the reference standard for sleep assessment, wearable devices provide a practical alternative for continuous monitoring in clinical environments. Validation studies have demonstrated moderate agreement with polysomnography for estimating TST and sleep efficiency, with an adjusted κ of 0.77 [8, 9], as well as strong concordance with actigraphy for global sleep parameters [9, 10]. However, performance is lower for individual sleep stages, particularly REM sleep, and may vary according to participant characteristics and study settings. Recent reviews have highlighted the generally high sensitivity of Fitbit devices for sleep detection while emphasizing the limited evidence available in hospitalized populations and individuals with disrupted sleep patterns. Therefore, Fitbit-derived sleep-stage measures were interpreted as estimates of sleep architecture rather than direct polysomnographic measurements. Objective sleep outcomes were complemented by subjective assessment using the PSQI.

For the intervention group, the first night served as baseline, while the adjusted medical order protocol was implemented beginning on the second night to ensure an uninterrupted 8-h rest period (10:00 pm to 6:00 am). This duration was selected because it reflects the recommended sleep opportunity for healthy adults (7–9 h per night) [11] and was considered feasible within routine ward workflow. The same nighttime interval was maintained for both study groups; however, only the intervention group underwent modifications designed to minimize sleep interruptions during this period.

The intervention consisted of rescheduling non-urgent laboratory tests and imaging studies to daytime hours, postponing routine medication administration unless time-critical, and extending the interval of vital sign monitoring to every 8 h in clinically stable patients. Prior to study initiation, nursing personnel received training regarding study procedures and implementation of intervention-specific medical orders. In the intervention group, nursing staff were instructed to cluster essential nighttime activities (such as charting, medication delivery, and assessments) before 10:00 pm, minimizing room entries and unnecessary disturbances. All clinical measurements continued to be performed as part of routine patient care. In contrast, the control group continued under the hospital’s standard 12-h nursing shift routine, which included vital sign assessments every 4 h, medication administration, and laboratory sampling according to fixed schedules, often resulting in multiple sleep interruptions.

To monitor protocol adherence, nursing activities were documented according to routine hospital practice in both manual nursing records and the electronic medical record system, including completion times for each medical order. Investigators reviewed these records daily for each participant to verify compliance with the assigned protocol and confirm implementation of intervention-specific modifications. No ward-wide changes to nursing practice were introduced during the study.

Objective sleep metrics included Stage R sleep duration (min), TST (min), non-REM (NREM, min) duration, and light sleep time (min), all measured using Fitbit Sense [10]. Subjective sleep quality was assessed using the validated Spanish version of the PSQI at baseline and study completion. Global PSQI scores range from 0 to 21, with lower scores indicating better sleep quality. Consistent with the validated Spanish version, participants with a global PSQI score ≤ 5 were classified as having good sleep quality, whereas scores >5 indicated poor sleep quality [12, 13]. The Baseline Night corresponded to the first overnight monitoring period beginning at 8:00 pm on the day of hospital admission.

Additional variables included the frequency and causes of nocturnal awakenings (pain, noise, vital sign measurement, medication administration, and blood sampling). Vital signs [heart rate (bpm), blood pressure (mmHg), respiratory rate (rpm), temperature (°C), and oxygen saturation (%) and capillary glucose measurements were obtained from routine nursing assessments conducted according to standardized hospital protocols and physician orders, and extracted from the electronic medical record. The intervention, consisting of the reorganization of nighttime medical orders, was coded as a dichotomous variable (1 = intervention, 0 = control). Other variables evaluated were age (years), sex, body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), admission diagnosis, glucose levels at baseline and study completion, and comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus and hypertension. Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data—including age, weight (kg), height (m), and medical history—were obtained through direct patient interviews and supplemented with electronic medical records.

Statistical analysis

The normality of quantitative variables was assessed using histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. When deviations from normality were detected, data were log10-transformed prior to analysis. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables, geometric mean and geometric standard deviation (GSD) for log-transformed variables, and median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages.

The primary outcomes were objective sleep parameters measured across three consecutive nights, including TST, Stage R sleep duration, NREM sleep duration, wake time, and nocturnal awakenings. Secondary outcomes included subjective sleep quality assessed using the PSQI. Exploratory outcomes included overnight physiological variables (heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, temperature) and glucose levels.

Baseline characteristics were compared between groups using the independent samples t-test, chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. For repeated-measures analyses, between-group differences over time were evaluated using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA when model assumptions were met. When assumptions were violated, an Aligned Rank Transform (ART)-ANOVA mixed-effects approach was used. Significant interactions were explored using Sidak-adjusted post hoc comparisons. Categorical repeated measures were analyzed using Cochran’s Q test or McNemar’s test, as appropriate.

Missing data were handled through imputation methods when the proportion of missing values was below 20%. Statistical significance was defined as p < .05. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the tidyverse, ggplot2, ARTool, afex, and emmeans packages.

Sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1 for repeated-measures ANOVA with two groups and three measurements per participant. Assuming a two-sided α level of 0.05, 80% statistical power, and an effect size of f = 0.35, the required sample size was estimated at 46 participants. The selected effect size represented a value between Cohen’s conventional medium (f = 0.25) and large (f = 0.40) effect sizes, reflecting a conservative yet clinically plausible estimate for a behavioral intervention in the absence of prior randomized studies evaluating comparable nighttime workflow modifications in hospitalized patients [14].

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

A total of 56 patients were initially enrolled in the study. Five patients were excluded from the final analysis due to medical discharge during the study period. Therefore, the final analysis included 51 patients, of whom 26 (51.0%) were assigned to the intervention group and 25 (49.0%) to the control group. The mean (SD) BMI was 29.7 (5.9) kg/m2, also without significant differences between groups. Primary outcomes: Objective sleep parameters

Objective sleep outcomes were assessed across three consecutive study nights (Baseline, Night 1, and Night 2), whereas PSQI was administered at baseline and study completion.

Between-group analyses demonstrated greater improvements in objective sleep outcomes from Baseline Night to Night 2 in the intervention group compared with the control group (Table 2 and Fig. 2). Mean (GSD) TST increased from 239 (1.30) to 398 (1.28) min in the intervention group and from 221 (1.45) to 301 (1.40) min in the control group (group-by-time interaction, p = .015). Similarly, Stage R sleep duration increased from 38.0 (1.44) to 79.2 (1.36) min in the intervention group and from 33.9 (1.48) to 58.0 (1.49) min in the control group (group-by-time interaction p = .010). NREM sleep duration increased from 169 (1.42) to 293 (1.33) min in the intervention group and from 160 (1.53) to 211 (1.45) min in the control group (group-by-time interaction, p = .017).

Secondary outcomes: Sleep consolidation and subjective sleep quality

Sleep consolidation outcomes favored the intervention group. The proportion of participants experiencing nocturnal awakenings decreased from 19 (73.1%) at baseline to 0 (0%) by Night 2 (p < .001). In contrast, reductions in awakenings were less consistent in the control group. Among causes of awakening, interruptions related to vital sign measurements decreased from 5 (19.2%) to 0 (0%) in the intervention group (p = .018). Detailed analyses are presented in Tables S1 and S2.

Subjective sleep quality improved significantly in the intervention group compared with the control group (Table 3 and Fig. 3). Mean (SD) PSQI scores decreased from 8.2 (3.4) at baseline to 4.6 (2.5) at study completion in the intervention group, whereas scores increased from 7.5 (4.1) to 8.7 (3.2) in the control group (between-group comparison, p = .040).

Discussion

This single-blind, randomized controlled trial demonstrates that implementing an adjusted nighttime medical order protocol significantly improved both objective and subjective sleep quality (Fitbit Sense and PSQI, respectively) in hospitalized patients. Participants in the intervention group demonstrated improvements in Fitbit-derived estimates of Stage R and NREM sleep duration, increased TST, fewer awakenings, and better subjective sleep quality compared with controls. These findings highlight the impact of workflow reorganization on hospital sleep and provide novel evidence from a Latin American population, where such interventions have been scarcely evaluated.

Subjective sleep quality, as measured by the PSQI, improved significantly in the intervention group, with mean scores decreasing from 8.2 at hospital admission to 4.6 at study completion. This subjective improvement was accompanied by objective changes in sleep architecture: TST increased by approximately 160 min, Stage R sleep duration nearly doubled, NREM sleep was significantly prolonged, and nocturnal awakenings were eliminated by the second hospital night in the intervention group. These effects exceeded those reported in many prior hospital-based interventions. Hospitalized patients frequently report poor sleep quality, averaging only 3–5 h of TST per night, often fragmented by multiple awakenings and reduced Stage R proportion [15]. In our study, participants in both groups had baseline sleep durations of approximately 4 h. Following the intervention, sleep duration increased to nearly 6 h by the second night, whereas the control group improved to approximately 5 h.

A straightforward explanation for these findings is that participants in the intervention group experienced fewer externally imposed sleep interruptions related to routine hospital care. By reducing nighttime room entries for non-urgent clinical activities, the intervention likely limited awakenings attributable to staff interactions, leaving sleep disruption more closely related to patients’ underlying medical condition rather than hospital processes. This mechanism is consistent with both the objective improvements observed through wearable monitoring and the parallel improvement in subjective sleep quality, as patients experiencing fewer nocturnal interruptions are likely to perceive their sleep as less fragmented and more restorative.

Beyond this direct effect, several physiological mechanisms may underlie these improvements. Sleep fragmentation activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, increasing cortisol secretion, and promoting sympathetic predominance, thereby contributing to cardiovascular stress [16]. By reducing nocturnal awakenings, the intervention may have facilitated parasympathetic predominance, improved autonomic balance, and enhanced baroreflex stabilization, consistent with the observed downward trends in heart rate and blood pressure among intervention participants, although these changes did not reach statistical significance.

Sleep deprivation during hospitalization has been associated with delayed wound healing, impaired glycemic control, heightened pain perception, and an increased risk of delirium [17–19]. Although awakenings attributed to pain numerically decreased in the intervention group, this difference was not statistically significant. Therefore, no conclusions can be drawn regarding the effect of the intervention on pain-related awakenings. Nevertheless, preserving sleep continuity remains an important therapeutic target given the well-established relationship between sleep fragmentation and pain perception.

Our findings are consistent with the SLEEP-ICU trial by Alegria et al. (2023), which implemented a multicomponent environmental protocol including dynamic lighting, sound masking, and rationalized nighttime care activities among mechanically ventilated ICU patients [6]. Although environmental strategies such as noise reduction and lighting optimization have shown inconsistent effects on sleep outcomes, organizational interventions targeting nighttime clinical routines appear to provide more consistent benefits [20–22]. Despite methodological differences, both studies support the concept that hospital routines and staff-related interruptions are major determinants of inpatient sleep quality. While the SLEEP-ICU trial relied on polysomnography and circadian biomarkers to characterize sleep physiology, our workflow-based intervention in general wards was associated with improvements in wearable-derived estimates of Stage R and NREM sleep, longer TST, and better subjective sleep quality. By restructuring routine nighttime care and reducing unnecessary staff-related interruptions, our findings suggest that relatively simple organizational changes may represent a practical and scalable strategy for preserving sleep continuity during hospitalization.

The parallel improvement in subjective and objective sleep measures underscores the value of incorporating both assessment modalities in sleep research. Previous studies have frequently reported discordance between self-reported and physiologically measured sleep outcomes [7, 20]. Prior studies have shown that environmental interventions may improve perceived sleep quality without corresponding gains in objective sleep parameters [7], reflecting biases inherent to self-reported measures [20]. In contrast, our findings suggest that workflow-based interventions, targeting hospital routines, can simultaneously enhance physiological sleep metrics and patients’ perception of sleep quality, strengthening the ecological validity of sleep assessments.

This study has several notable strengths. First, it addresses a gap in the literature by evaluating sleep quality in general wards within a middle-income country, whereas most existing studies have been conducted in high-income settings or focused primarily on ICUs or environmental interventions [23–26]. Second, our trial integrated objective sleep monitoring using Fitbit Sense with subjective assessment via the PSQI, enabling a comprehensive evaluation of inpatient sleep. This dual approach captures both physiological sleep parameters and patient-reported experience, addressing a common limitation of hospital sleep research where reliance on subjective measures alone may introduce recall or perception bias [27]. While polysomnography remains the diagnostic gold standard, its use in general wards is often impractical. Wearable devices, with acceptable accuracy for estimating TST and sleep stage transitions, offer a practical alternative for clinical research. Third, the intervention—a reorganization of nighttime medical workflows—demonstrates the feasibility of a low-cost, scalable strategy that targets modifiable institutional factors. Importantly, improvements were consistently observed across both objective and subjective sleep measures, which represented the primary and secondary outcomes of the study, including longer TST, increased Stage R and NREM duration, elimination of nocturnal awakenings, and better perceived sleep quality. These features support the translational potential of our findings, positioning sleep preservation as an integral component of hospital safety and quality frameworks, with potential downstream benefits for recovery and patient satisfaction [17–19].

An important consideration is the safety of reducing routine nighttime interruptions. In this study, the intervention was restricted to clinically stable patients and involved only the rescheduling of non-urgent activities. Furthermore, no adverse events attributable to the intervention were identified, and no significant between-group differences were observed in vital sign trajectories. These findings support the feasibility of implementing sleep-preserving strategies while maintaining patient safety.

Regarding study limitations, the single-center design may limit generalizability. Environmental factors such as noise intensity, light exposure, and staff workload were not objectively measured and may have contributed to residual confounding. Although Fitbit Sense has demonstrated acceptable performance for estimating sleep duration and broad sleep-stage patterns, it does not provide the same level of accuracy as polysomnography, particularly for Stage R sleep classification. Recent reviews have reported generally acceptable accuracy and high sensitivity of Fitbit devices for sleep assessment, while emphasizing that performance may vary according to sleep characteristics, device type, and study population. Furthermore, most validation studies have been conducted in ambulatory or laboratory settings, and evidence in hospitalized patients remains limited [28, 29]. Because all participants were admitted to single-occupancy private rooms, environmental sleep disruptions associated with shared accommodations were minimized. Consequently, our findings may not be directly generalizable to hospitals with multi-bedrooms or open ward layouts. Nevertheless, Stage R sleep findings should be interpreted as wearable-derived estimates rather than definitive measurements of sleep architecture. The concordance between objective Fitbit measurements and subjective improvements in PSQI scores provides additional support for the observed findings. Future studies incorporating polysomnography are warranted to confirm these results. Although participants were cared for in single-occupancy rooms, both study groups were managed within the same hospital ward by the same nursing staff. Therefore, some degree of contamination between groups cannot be completely excluded. However, the intervention was implemented through individualized medical orders, and adherence was monitored through daily review of nursing documentation and electronic medical records. Nevertheless, a formal treatment fidelity instrument was not employed, and future studies should incorporate standardized fidelity measures and cluster-randomized designs to further minimize contamination.

Future studies could also investigate the isolated contribution of routine hospital interruptions to sleep by evaluating standardized nighttime care protocols in low-acuity or healthy volunteer settings, helping to disentangle the effects of the hospital environment from those of acute illness. Studies should also replicate these findings in multicenter and public hospital settings, incorporating longer hospital stays (e.g. beyond three nights) to determine whether improvements in objective sleep parameters persist, plateau, or diverge between groups over time. The inclusion of environmental measurements and physiological biomarkers such as cortisol, melatonin, or heart rate variability would further clarify mechanistic pathways. Exploring intervention effects across different patient subgroups may also help identify populations that derive greater benefit. Beyond improving rest, such interventions may promote physiological recovery and patient-centered care. Overall, this trial supports positioning sleep as a core component of hospital quality of care and provides a reproducible, low-cost framework adaptable across diverse healthcare systems.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that simple procedural modifications in nighttime medical care can lead to meaningful improvements in sleep quantity, architecture, and perceived sleep quality among hospitalized adults. Unlike previous research emphasizing environmental factors alone, our findings suggest that workflow organization and nighttime medical practices are important, modifiable determinants of inpatient sleep quality. Promoting sleep-friendly hospital routines may improve patient comfort, satisfaction, and sleep continuity during hospitalization.
