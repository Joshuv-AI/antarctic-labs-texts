SARS-CoV-2 and climate factors: a study between linear regression and quantile regression model using longitudinal count data
Authors: Nazmin Akter, Md. Mobarak Hossain Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRegression is the most popular statistical tool used to assess the relationship between predictors and response variables. SARS-CoV-2 infections counts, however are typically right-skewed, heteroskedastic, and show episodic surges. In longitudinal epidemic data, mean-based linear regression-which focusses on the conditional mean under homoscedasticity and normality assumptions- may provide an incomplete representation of transmission dynamics, particularly when extreme outbreak periods disproportionately influence estimates. Quantile regression offers a flexible alternative by allowing covariate effects to vary across the conditional distribution of the response, thereby capturing heterogeneous effects across different transmission regimes. This feature is especially useful for understanding epidemic extremes. The aim of this study is to determine the predictors of SARS-CoV-2 infected cases, using quantile regression and comparing the models at several quantiles.MethodsThe data utilized in this study were sourced from the website of the Institute of Epidemiology Disease Control and Research (IEDCR), Dhaka Bangladesh. The daily temperature and humidity with a number of daily infected case are taken as predictors and to describe the spread of SAR-CoV-2, we consider the daily number of SAR-CoV-2 infected cases as a response variable, during the period of March 08, 2020 to May 31, 2023. Classical mean regression and Quantile regression was performed to understand the association among climate variables and the results were compared across quantiles.ResultsUnder the mean and quantile regression framework we found that high temperature and high humidity have a substantial impact on spread of SARS-CoV-2 (in log scale) at a different levels of quantiles (0 < τ < 1). Temperature’s effect steadily decreases as we move from low to high infection quantiles. Temperature effect flips from positive to slightly negative (-1.8%) at the highest quantile 95th quantile and 10.2% at 25th quantile. Humidity remains positively associated across all quantiles but with decreasing strength. For τ = 0.25,0.50,0.75,0.90 and 0.95, Humidity’s effect drops significantly as move to higher quantile: 8.2%, 3.9%, 1.5%, 2.1%, 2.0%, respectively. We also compared conditional quantile models using HQC, AIC, BIC values and found quantile regression model fit at τ = 0.75 is relatively stable across all the conditional distribution.ConclusionsThe findings of this study shows that temperature and humidity have significant impact on SARS-CoV-2 transmission in Bangladesh, with heterogeneous effects across different levels of infection intensity. By modeling multiple points of the conditional distribution, quantile regression provides a more comprehensive understanding of epidemic behavior than mean-based approaches. The regression models also suggest that, even in lower infection scenarios, warmer and more humid conditions are linked to higher infection levels. However, Quantile regression is distribution-agnostic, which is both a strength and a limitation in this work. Nevertheless, as the data are longitudinal in nature and the models do not explicitly incorporate dynamic temporal structures, the results should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Future research incorporating time-series–based quantile or autoregressive frameworks may further elucidate the role of climate in epidemic progression.

Introduction

Regression analysis is one of the most popular and helpful methods used in statistical data analysis to analyze the relationship between response and predictors by fitting a line to the observed data [1]. Three major uses for regression analysis include (1) determining the strength of predictors, (2) forecasting an effect and (3) forecasting the trend. For instance, regression models are widely used to assess data on climate change [2, 3], pollution, natural resources, and ecosystem dynamics; to model air quality [4], assess the impact of rising CO2 concentrations on global temperature, and analyze climate change scenarios [5]. In regression model, we often summarize the behavior of response variables for fixed value of predictors using the measures of central tendency (mean, median and mode) [6]. Classical regression modeling is focused on the mean, where it summarizes the association between response and predictors using conditional mean functions for response variables. Based on this idea, the core of a board family of regression models such as simple linear regression, multiple regression, and nonlinear regression model are developed. Although conditional mean models are easy to calculate and interpret, having certain attractive assumptions including normality, homoscedasticity, and independence [7]. However, these traditional conditional mean models are sensitive to skewed distributions and outliers [8]. For instance, environmental data frequently shows complex patterns; the data of health inequality in height, weight, incidence of disease, treatments, life expectancy; Demographic factors on infant’s birth weights; educational inequality in academic achievement [6, 9], such variables effect under different situations or heterogeneous variability. An alternative approach- Quantile Regression model, introduced by Koenker and Bassett (1978) [10]- is gradually becoming a popular standard econometric procedure in situations where estimation of the conditional mean function is insufficient to capture the full pattern of associations between the dependent variable and the covariates over the distribution of the former. Although, the goal of both regression models is same but quantile model predicts the conditional quantiles for sufficient data instead of conditional mean for small sample size. Applications of Quantile regression spread to many fields notably, ecology, environmental science [11, 12]; medicine and public health [13–17]; environmental health [18, 19], health service research (e.g. health care expenditure analysis) [20–23]; wage distribution and modeling [24–27]; Health economics [28, 29], Business analytics (risk management [9]) etc.

A recent pandemic caused by novel coronavirus was identified in Wuhan, Hubei Province, China [30], in December 2019 which caused a highly transferable viral disease brought about by severe acute respiratory condition coronavirus 2 (official name: SARS-CoV2). It caused over 700 million cases with 7.01 million deaths worldwide to date [31] and became as a Public Health Emergency of International concern by World Health Organization (WHO) [32]. Bangladesh also faced this crisis moment due to pandemic SARS-CoV-2 and almost 2.04 million people were infected by SARS-CoV-2 in Bangladesh [31]. The transmission of viruses can be influenced by many meteorological factors, such as temperature and humidity, wind speed, precipitation, radiation; however, the effect of meteorological parameters on SARS-CoV-2 remains controversial. Irfan et al. (2022), Sarwar et al. (2021) have shown that the meteorological factors, for instance, humidity and temperature, may drive the pace of the SARS-CoV-2 infections using quantile regression analysis [33, 34]. Another studies such as Karim and Nazmin (2022) illustrated that climate factors (temperature and humidity) have significant impact on mortality due to SARS-CoV-2 transmission using a parametric (GAM) and semiparametric (GAMLSS) regression model [19, 34]. Guo et. al. (2021) and H Ai et al. (2022) showed that higher temperatures were linked to a reduction in the effective reproductive number of COVID-19 in both China and the U.S using distributed lag non-linear model [35, 36]. Many existing QR applications to COVID-19 focus on asymmetric impacts of weather or financial variables on outcomes [33, 37], There’s less work on purely epidemiological quantile regression models infection rates with climate covariates. Most QR based study treated data as cross-sectional (e.g., climate vs. cases) or basic time components [38] and most research focuses on a few quantile levels (e.g., 25th, 50th, 75th). While previous studies have primarily relied on mean regression approaches to identify determinants of SARS-CoV-2 transmission, such methods implicitly assume homogeneous effects across the distribution of transmission intensity. This study addresses this limitation by employing quantile regression to uncover heterogeneous determinants across low, median, and higher transmission regimes, and by systematically comparing these results across quantiles.

In this study, we discussed quantile regression from an applied perspective. In particular, we illustrate the concept, advantages and disadvantages of both linear and quantile regression using SARS-CoV-2 datasets as an example and uncover whether the climatic determinants of SARS-CoV-2 transmission differ across the conditional distribution (e.g. lower vs. upper tails dynamics) to understand how do predictors affect mild vs. severe infection scenarios. Although Poisson and Negative Binomial models are standard approaches for count data, they focus on modeling the conditional mean of infections [19]. Our objective was to examine whether the effects of temperature and humidity differ across low and high transmission regimes. Given the strong right-skewness, heteroscedasticity, and episodic surges in SARS-CoV-2 cases, quantile regression allows covariate effects to vary across the conditional distribution, providing insights into heterogeneous transmission dynamics that cannot be captured by mean-based count models. This study included 2,023,058 SARS-CoV-2-infected cases in Bangladesh during the observation period March 08, 2020, to May 31, 2023. In the following section, we will go over the data sources and procedures used for developing statistical models in detail. Sect. "Data and Methods" explained how the Linear Regression Model and Quantile Regression will be used to study the impact of predictor variables on the response variable. Sect. "Results and Discussions" provides a summary of the data analysis and findings.

Quantile regression model

Koenker and Bassett (1978) proposed a general linear model by introducing “regression quantiles” of linear combination of sample quantiles and trimmed mean along with robustness properties [10, 41]. Basically, Quantiles are cut points in a data distribution that divide the data set into equal size groups with equal probabilities. Koenker (2005) and Hao and Naiman (2007) added more excellent introduction to the theory and applications of quantile regression model estimation techniques and properties in literature [6, 8]. Quantile regression (QR) model uses least-absolute-distance estimation method instead of least square estimation and has greater computing power capabilities than OLS method [6].

The minimization problem for obtaining the estimate of quantile parameter is done by using Linear Programming Problem (LPP) which are well implemented in many software (R, SAS, Python). Koenker–Machado specification test [42] is also used in this study to assess whether regression coefficients vary across quantiles.

While the linear regression specifies the changes in conditional mean of response variable, the quantile model specifies changes in the conditional median. This approach is also refereed as conditional median model which is a natural extension of linear regression model. Although, the goal of both regression model is same but quantile model predicts the conditional median for sufficient data instead of conditional mean for small sample size. Unlike OLS, which focuses on a single summary measure, quantile regression reveals how predictors influence low, median, and high response counts differently, thus offering insight into whether relationships vary across epidemic intensity [43]. This conceptual distinction means that while OLS captures average epidemic behavior, quantile regression uncovers distribution-dependent effects that a mean model might overlook, making it particularly valuable in skewed longitudinal epidemic contexts [44].

Comparison standards

The Information Criterion measures the deviation of a model specified by the probability distribution from the true distribution [45]. There are several information criteria for model selection. Out of them HQC, AIC, BIC are commonly used in regression analysis. Useful reviews of these model selection criteria are found in [46, 47].

Hannan–Quinn information criterion (HQC)

The Hannan-Quinn information criterion (HQC) measures the goodness of fit of a statistical model. It is often used as a criterion for model selection among a finite set of models [48]. It is essential to remember that the HQC can be used to compare estimated models only when the numerical values of the dependent variable are identical for all estimates being compared. It is computed by following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$HQC=-2L_{max}+2Kln\mathit{\left({ln\left(n\right)}\right)}$$\end{document}

Akaike Information Criteria (AIC)

Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) measure used to evaluate models by balancing fit and complexity, which help to select the best model without overfitting. It is calculated according to the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$AIC=2L_{max}+2K$$\end{document}

Schwartz Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC)

The Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) is comparable to the AIC but has a stricter approach to model complexity. It is based on Bayesian statistics, which impose a higher penalty for models with more parameters, especially when the dataset is large. It is calculated according to the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$BIC=-2L_{max}+K\ln\left(n\right)$$\end{document}

The skewness statistic, which measures the degree of asymmetry in infected new cases, is more than one and positive. It means that the response variable, COVID-19 infected new cases, has a skew distribution. Positive skewness means that the tail on the right side is larger than the one on the left. The kurtosis statistic, which measures the relative peakness or fatness of a distribution in comparison to the normal distribution, for infected new cases is larger than 3 (See the detailed results in Table 1). It shows that the data set has larger tails than a normal distribution (see Fig. 1a). In this scenario, if we applied a classical regression model of the outcome variable log (number of infected new cases) caused by COVID-19 to determine the effect of covariates, the results may be deceptive because the normality condition is not met. Quantile regression, which does not require a normal distribution, could be an ideal option for dealing with this situation.

Test of heteroscedasticy

The Breusch-Pagan (BP) test is a statistical test used to detect heteroscedasticity in a regression model to check if the variance of errors in a regression model changes based on the predictors. The test value of BP test is found 126.30 with p-value < 0.05, which indicates strong statistical evidence of heteroscedasticity in model residuals. Since the quantile regression model does not assume homoscedasticity (mentioned in Sect. "Data and Methods"), Heteroscedasticity is another reasons to use quantile regression later.

Quantile regression model

The quantile regression requires a real-valued response variable (see Koenker and Bassett (1978) [41]). As a result, the daily number of infected cases is not directly usable in the quantile regression analysis described in Sect. "Data and Methods". Many researchers use the logarithmic transformation of the "number of infected new cases" in mean regression analysis (see for example [49, 50]). We also use the logarithmic transformation of "number of infected cases" caused by SARS-CoV-2 to change a real-valued response variable for quantile regression analysis. Because the quantile regression estimators have the equivariance properties, we add 1.1 to the response variable before the logarithmic transformation to avoid a log-scale transformation of zero. This change of the response variables with 1.1 extra value has no effect on the final results (see Koenker 2005).

Conclusions

Quantile regression provides an alternative method for accounting for the asymmetries that the classical linear regression model ignores [8]. In this study, we used the quantile regression model for highly skewed data using the temperature and humidity effect on spread of SARS-CoV-2 Virus among the people in Bangladesh. To investigate the effect, a sample of size 1134 days are provided from the government website of IEDCR, Bangladesh and per day’s temperature, daily humidity and daily infected number of people are taken as variables. Therefore, applied mean and quantile regression model for investigating the significant effects among the variables. The both regression model findings revealed the significant effect among the variables with non-linear relationships that indicates the positive impact on Transmission on the SARS-Cov-2 virus. The quantile regression model provides more accurate results at different level of quantile (0 < τ < 1) than traditional linear regression model, however some temperature effects are insignificant at 75% and 90% location of percentiles with daily temperature in Bangladesh. Humidity’s effect decreases sharply from 25th to 75th percentile but slightly rises again at the 90th percentile. At extremely high infection levels, higher temperatures might slightly reduce infection counts, while humidity still tends to increase them. This study indicates that climatic factors like temperature and humidity have stronger influence on lower to median infection levels, but at very high infection counts, other factors might dominate. Our study indicates high temperature and humidity are more favorable for spread of disease due to SARS-CoV-2 (crowding indoors, air retention etc.) and this finding could imply that temperature might help limit the highest infection counts, while humidity consistently supports infection spread. However, for better explaining of actual behavior of spread of SARS-CoV-2, the potential impact of temperature, humidity on the number of daily infected people may not be strong enough to reduce the spread of disease, only controlled laboratory experiments can decisively prove it. However, other factors such as people’s lifestyle and their social activities during these such pandemic have to be considered for analyzing the statistical model intensively. The research limitations suggest the improved generalizability, integration of more variables, comparison analyses, and intervention studies. Future research should address these limitations (including temporal autocorrelation) to enhance understanding of the associated factors influencing the disease transmission.

Limitation of the study and future work

Quantile regression model is primarily applicable for continuous (at-least quasi continuous) predictors and over dispersion (variance > mean) in dataset. In this study our response variable is discrete (non-negative integers) often skewed and over dispersed. However, to suit quantile regression, we applied log transformation on predictor which smooths the distribution and reduces the skewness as well. As results, quantities become more stable and meaningful. Since quantile regression does not model count generation process directly, (like Poisson and Negative Binomial regression can do) it’s distribution-agnostic, which is both a strength and a limitation for this study. Additionally, this study analyzes daily SARS-CoV-2 case counts over time; therefore, the data are inherently longitudinal and may exhibit serial dependence due to epidemic persistence and temporal clustering. The regression models applied in this study did not explicitly incorporate autoregressive or temporal correlation structures. Ignoring longitudinal dependence may influence standard error estimation and the statistical significance of covariate effects. Consequently, results should be interpreted as associative rather than dynamic causal relationships. Future research may benefit from employing time-series quantile regression, autoregressive distributed lag models, mixed-effects quantile approaches, or other longitudinal frameworks that explicitly account for serial correlation in epidemic data. Our findings also do not report causal association between climatic factors and SARS-CoV-2. Controlled Laboratory based experiments will be required to address these. For better understanding and interpretation of discrete data, quantile regression with Bayesian methods can be combined in future research work. Nevertheless, Distribution Regression can be also employed for handling discreteness with excess zeros having strong theoretical foundation.
