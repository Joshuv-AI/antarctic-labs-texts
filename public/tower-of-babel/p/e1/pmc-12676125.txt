Optical Coherence Tomography Measurements of Retina and Choroid in Autism Spectrum Disorder: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Nasim Hosseinzadeh, Mehrdad Mahalleh, Sara KamaliZonouzi, Sogol Alikarami, Mahdi Gouravani, J. Fernando Arevalo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe purpose of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to compare optical coherence tomography (OCT) retinal measurements between patients with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and the neurotypical controls, exploring the potential of OCT as a noninvasive biomarker for ASD-related neurodevelopmental alterations.MethodsPubMed, Embase, and Scopus databases were explored to determine eligible articles reporting OCT measurements of the retina and choroid in patients with ASD compared to healthy controls. Statistical analysis of OCT metrics was performed if reported in at least three discrete studies. In the process, fixed- and random-effects models were utilized, depending on the heterogeneity level between studies. Subgroup analysis based on the age group of cases, the method of eye selection, age and sex matching of cases and controls, and the OCT device used was also conducted.ResultsTen studies with 373 ASD cases (with a total of 640 eyes) and 443 controls (with a total of 760 eyes) were included in this study. No significant alteration was observed in the average total macular layer, macular inner nuclear layer (INL), macular inner plexiform layer (IPL), macular ganglion cell layer (GCL), and macular retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness. There was also no significant difference in the peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (pRNFL) thickness of the eyes of cases with ASD compared to healthy controls, except for the inferonasal portion of the pRNFL, which was significantly thicker in ASD subjects when compared to controls (p = 0.02).ConclusionThe findings of the present meta-analysis indicate a localized thickening of the inferonasal pRNFL with no alteration of other portions of pRNFL and macular layers (IPL, INL, GCL, RNFL). Although OCT may reflect subtle neurodevelopmental differences in ASD, current evidence is limited by small sample sizes, methodological heterogeneity, and potential confounders.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder with different manifestations centered on three fundamental diagnostic features: a deficit in social skills, repetitive behaviors, and restrictive interests [1]. According to an investigation of 30,502 children and adolescents in the USA in 2015, the prevalence of this disorder was approximately 2.5% [2]. During the 29 years from 1990, the incidence of ASD is estimated to have increased by 1.5% worldwide [3], and it accounted for 4.31 million disability-adjusted life years globally [4]. Although several studies have proposed an association between this condition and genetic, neurobiological, and environmental factors, many aspects of its etiology remain unclear [5].

Although no specific findings in brain imaging have been definitively associated with this disorder, various studies have proposed significant correlations between the disorder and alterations observed in anatomical structures, functional activity, and neurophysiological patterns in neuroimaging [5]. A study using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated greater global white and gray matter volumes, mostly in the right superior temporal gyrus [6]. Furthermore, a study on functional MRI findings to investigate connectivity patterns found reduced connectivity in certain regions of interest and increased connectivity in sensory-motor and supramodal areas [7].

During embryological development, the neuroectoderm differentiates into several components, e.g., the brain and the retina, forming the central nervous system (CNS) [8]; thus, brain and retinal changes can be correlated [9]. Furthermore, exploring retinal structure can be a means to finding biological explanations for the atypical visual processing (e.g., contrast sensitivity, static vs. dynamic visual tasks, visual fields) that reportedly underlies many of the behavioral and sensory deficits in ASD [10–13].

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) mediates light waves to provide high-resolution cross-sectional images of the retina and choroid [14]. This modality is extensively employed to assess the progression of retinal changes and monitor treatment in neurological conditions, e.g., multiple sclerosis [15], Alzheimer’s disease [16], Parkinson’s disease [17], and Huntington’s disease [18].

In several studies, OCT has been utilized to identify distinctive retinal and choroidal characteristics correlated to ASD [19–23]. Evaluation of OCT findings in patients with ASD has manifested contradictory findings in retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness. Even though a study by Bozkurt et al. [19] reported thicker RNFL and another found reduced thickness of this layer, Little et al. [23] discovered no changes in the RNFL thickness in autistic adults compared to healthy controls. In addition, multiple investigations of macular thickness (MT) in patients with ASD demonstrated inconsistent results. e.g., Bozkurt et al. [19] observed no significant differences in MT between patients with ASD and healthy controls, while Del-Rio-Vellosillo et al. [20] found higher MT in adults with ASD.

This study is motivated by the need to clarify these inconsistencies and to validate the retina as a potential source of biomarkers for ASD. We hypothesize that, given the shared neurodevelopmental pathways of the brain and retina, individuals with ASD will exhibit consistent and quantifiable structural differences in their retinal layers compared to neurotypical controls. The clinical implication of these findings could be the development of an adjunct diagnostic or screening tool for ASD. Therefore, these findings could be of significant value for clinicians and also researchers seeking to better understand, diagnose, and stratify this complex disorder.

The main objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to evaluate the alterations in retinal and choroidal measurements using OCT among patients with ASD and compare them to healthy neurotypical controls with the goal of identifying a consistent retinal phenotype that could serve as a reliable clinical biomarker.

Methods

We conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines to provide a transparent and standard report of the results, and a completed PRISMA checklist is provided as online supplementary material (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000549417) [24]. The study protocol was registered at the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with the registration number: CRD42024543214.

Data Collection and Extraction

We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, and Scopus databases on September 30, 2025. The detailed search strategy is mentioned in online supplementary Table 1. In this step, duplicate articles were eliminated automatically and manually. Then three independent reviewers (N.H., M.G., and M.M.) assessed the titles and abstracts of every article obtained from the electronic search. Afterward, they screened the full texts of the remaining articles precisely and selected the studies with these two conditions: (1) fulfilling inclusion criteria and (2) lacking any exclusion criteria. Any disagreement was resolved by discussion. Additionally, we investigated the references of related articles to reduce the possibility of missing any eligible findings.

We included all original studies containing OCT measurements of the retina and choroid in patients with ASD. No limitation was applied to the publication date, age of participants, or stage of the condition. Nonhuman and non-original articles, reviews, conference letters, book chapters, letters to editors, study protocols, case reports, studies lacking a healthy control (HC) group, and non-English articles were excluded.

Three independent authors (N.H., M.G., and M.M.) collected the following data from articles: the first author’s name, year of publication, country, study design, number, age, and sex of participants; medications; the utilized diagnostic criteria for ASD; and the employed model of OCT. Reported OCT metrics of the optic nerve, including average and sectoral peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (pRNFL) thickness, including temporal, superotemporal (ST) and inferotemporal (IT); nasal, superonasal (SN) [25], and inferonasal (IN) areas; and OCT measurements of macula, i.e., average MT; and particular layers, i.e., RNFL, ganglion cell layer (GCL), inner plexiform layer (IPL), outer plexiform layer [26], inner nuclear layer (INL), outer nuclear layer (ONL), and photoreceptor thickness and volume, as well as foveal and choroidal thickness, are also provided. Any conflicts during this process were resolved by discussion.

Quality Assessment

We have applied the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS) to evaluate the quality of the included studies and the risk of bias. In this strategy, a score ranging from 0 to 9 is assigned to each study based on the quality of selection, comparability, and exposure or outcome for case-control and cohort articles, respectively [27]. Three independent authors (N.H., M.G., and S.A.) reviewed the articles and determined the score of each study. Studies are categorized into low, good, and high quality according to their obtained scores. High-quality studies with a lower risk of bias earn grades 7–9, and studies with grades 0–4 are recognized as low-quality. At this stage, any discrepancy is resolved by discussion.

Statistical Analysis

Weighted and standardized mean differences in OCT quantitative metrics, including average total MT, macular GCL, INL, IPL, and RNFL thickness, as well as average total and regional (temporal, IT, ST, nasal, SN, and IN) pRNFL thicknesses, were calculated between patients with ASD and healthy controls if assessed in at least three distinct studies. The acquired data were analyzed via Stata 17 software (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and a significant p value of less than 0.05, measured by standardized mean difference (SMD) as Hedges’ g and weighted mean difference. The heterogeneity among studies was approximated via Higgins’ I2 test, ranging from 0 to 100%. Heterogeneity amounts of less than 40% are classified as unimportant. Fixed- and random-effects models are applied in cases of I2 less and more than 40%, respectively, and the leave-one-out test was conducted to ensure the robustness of the findings. In addition, subgroup analysis was performed based on the age group of cases, the method of eye selection, matching cases and controls by age and sex, and the OCT device used to confirm the relevance of the findings. Publication bias was estimated through a funnel plot and Egger’s test. An asymmetric plot and a p value of <0.05 suggest a potential risk of publication bias. The trim-and-fill method was applied when Egger’s test indicated potential publication bias, to adjust the pooled effect estimate accordingly. A meta-regression analysis was conducted to examine the potential influence of study-level variables, including the number of cases, mean age, and percentage of male participants, on the pooled effect estimates.

Results

Study Selection

As shown in Figure 1, the primary literature search identified 205 studies (28 articles from PubMed, 103 articles from Embase, and 74 articles from Scopus). Automatic and manual deletion of duplicates removed 91 articles. In the initial stage of the study selection, 113 studies underwent title and abstract revision, resulting in the exclusion of 96 studies. Afterward, the full texts of the remaining studies were screened, which led to the exclusion of 7 studies. As demonstrated in Figure 1, these records were excluded for being conference abstracts, study populations other than ASD, and lack of healthy controls. Eventually, we included 10 studies with 219 cases (375 eyes) and 257 controls (413 eyes) in this study [5, 9, 19, 21, 22, 28–32]. Moreover, out of the 10 included studies that were able to fulfill the inclusion criteria, the studies by Garcia-Medina et al. [22] and Li et al. [29] did not enter meta-analysis due to a lack of reporting variables with a desirable format of mean and standard deviation. Overall, 8 studies were included in the meta-analysis.

Table 1 visualizes the primary characteristics of all of the included studies. All of the studies had case-control designs. Three studies had a study population with a mean age of more than 18 years old and were classified as adult [21, 31, 32], while other studies had study populations with a mean age of under 18 years old and were classified as non-adult [5, 9, 19, 22, 28–30]. Average Macular RNFL Thickness. A total of 4 studies reported macular RNFL thickness [9, 21, 30, 31]. There was a nonsignificant increase in the RNFL layer thickness in the macula of cases with ASD compared to healthy controls (SMD, 0.16; 95% CI: −0.25 to 0.58; p = 0.44; I2 = 79.10%; Table 2).

Average GCL Thickness. Four studies reported macular GCL thickness [9, 21, 30, 31]. There was no significant difference in the analysis between the two groups (SMD, 0.11; 95% CI: −0.33 to 0.55; p = 0.63; I2 = 81.31; Table 2).

Average IPL Thickness. Average IPL thickness was measured in four studies [9, 21, 30, 31]. There was no statistically significant difference between patients with ASD and healthy cases (SMD, 0.17; 95% CI: −0.33 to 0.66; p = 0.50; I2 = 85.07; Table 2).

Average INL Thickness. Four studies reported average INL thickness [9, 21, 30, 31]. There was a nonsignificant increase in average INL thickness in patients with ASD compared to the healthy control group (SMD, 0.31; 95% CI: 0.00 to 0.63; p = 0.05; I2 = 63.48; Table 2).

Average pRNFL Thickness. Average pRNFL thickness was measured in 6 studies [5, 19, 21, 28, 30, 32]. Analysis results demonstrated that average pRNFL thickness had a statistically insignificant decrease in patients with ASD when compared to the HC group (SMD, −0.10; 95% CI: −0.44 to 0.24; p = 0.55; I2 = 79.13%; Table 2; Fig. 3).

Sectoral pRNFL Thickness. Four studies measured temporal and nasal pRNFL thickness [5, 19, 30, 32]. Results indicated that temporal pRNFL thickness was not significantly different between ASD eyes compared and healthy controls (SMD, −0.07; 95% CI: −0.45 to 0.30; p = 0.70; I2 = 70.40%; Table 2). In the same manner, nasal pRNFL thickness had a nonsignificant lower value in patients with ASD than in the HC group (SMD, −0.27; 95% CI: −0.64 to 0.10; p = 0.15; I2 = 73.68%; Table 2).

Results of analysis on three studies [5, 19, 30], reporting ST and SN pRNFL thickness indicated that ASD cases had insignificantly reduced ST pRNFL thickness (SMD, −0.16; 95% CI: −0.60 to 0.28; p = 0.48; I2 = 75.90%) and SN pRNFL thickness (SMD, −0.30; 95% CI: −0.82 to 0.21; p = 0.25; I2 = 82.52%; Table 2) compared to the HC group.

The same three studies [5, 19, 30] also reported IT and IN pRNFL thickness, analysis of which revealed significantly thicker IN pRNFL thickness in ASD cases compared to the HC group (SMD, 0.24; 95% CI: 0.03 to 0.45; p = 0.02; I2 = 27.95%; Table 2) with an absolute increase of 4.45 µm (0.40–8.50). However, results demonstrated that SN pRNFL thickness had a statistically insignificant decrease in ASD patients when compared to healthy controls (SMD, −0.30; 95% CI: −0.82 to 0.21; p = 0.25; I2 = 82.52%; Table 2).

Publication Bias

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test (Table 2). Among all evaluated parameters, only the average macular INL thickness showed a significant Egger’s test (p = 0.04; Table 2). The trim-and-fill method was subsequently applied to adjust for possible publication bias; however, no additional studies were imputed, and the pooled effect size remained unchanged.

Subgroup Analysis

In instances where there was a notable presence of heterogeneity and a sufficient quantity of studies available, subgroup analyses were conducted based on variables that may introduce potential confounding factors to the identified disparities. The comparison between ASD cases and the HC group involved conducting subgroup analysis by age group (adult vs. non-adult), age and sex matching, eye selection method (single, both, or mixed), and the OCT device used.

Age Group

We studied the possible effect of the age group of the study population by the subgroup analysis (online suppl. Table 2). Age groups were classified based on the mean age: studies with a mean age below 18 years were categorized as non-adult and those with a mean age of 18 years or older as adult.

Similar to the overall result, the analysis outputs indicated a nonsignificant decrease in average pRNFL thickness in both adults (SMD, −0.23; 95% CI: −0.50 to 0.03; p = 0.09) and non-adult cases (SMD, −0.10; 95% CI: −0.44 to 0.24; p = 0.55). Additionally, temporal pRNFL had a similar result as the unmatched cases and showed a nonsignificant decrease in both adults (SMD, −0.33; 95% CI: −0.73 to 0.07; p = 0.10) and non-adult cases (SMD, 0.01; 95% CI: −0.46 to 0.49; p = 0.96).

Unlike average pRNFL and temporal pRNFL, nasal pRNFL thickness had a significant decrease in adult cases (SMD, −0.83; 95% CI: −1.24 to −0.42; p < 0.01); however, it did not demonstrate a significant decrease in non-adult cases (SMD, −0.13; 95% CI: −0.33 to 0.08; p = 0.24).

Average total MT demonstrated a significant decrease in non-adult cases (SMD, 0.64; 95% CI: 0.14 to 1.14; p = 0.01). Yet there was a nonsignificant increase in adult cases (SMD, −0.30; 95% CI: −1.0 to 0.50; p = 0.46). Average macular GCL thickness demonstrated a significant increase in non-adult cases (SMD, 0.37; 95% CI: 0.08 to 0.65; p = 0.01), while adult cases showed no significant difference (SMD, −0.15; 95% CI: −0.90 to 0.60; p = 0.69). Similarly, average macular INL thickness increased significantly in non-adult cases (SMD, 0.53; 95% CI: 0.10 to 0.96; p = 0.02), but not in adults (SMD, 0.12; 95% CI: −0.21 to 0.45; p = 0.48). Average macular IPL thickness also showed a significant increase in non-adults (SMD, 0.44; 95% CI: 0.03 to 0.85; p = 0.04), while remaining nonsignificant in adults (SMD, −0.10; 95% CI: −0.95 to 0.74; p = 0.81). Lastly, average macular RNFL thickness did not show a significant difference in either adults (SMD, 0.04; 95% CI: −0.60 to 0.68; p = 0.90) or non-adults (SMD, 0.29; 95% CI: −0.44 to 1.03; p = 0.43).

Comparability of Cases and Controls

Similar to the overall results, none of the average pRNFL, temporal pRNFL, and nasal pRNFL thicknesses demonstrated a significant alteration when matched for age and sex (online suppl. Table 3). Average pRNFL thickness had a nonsignificant decrease in studies that matched for age and sex (SMD, −0.15; 95% CI: −0.54 to 0.25; p = 0.47) and a nonsignificant increase in studies that did not match (SMD, 0.14; 95% CI: −0.30 to 0.57; p = 0.54). Temporal pRNFL thickness also had a nonsignificant decrease in studies matched for age and sex (SMD, −0.21; 95% CI: −0.56 to 0.14; p = 0.24) and a nonsignificant increase in studies that did not match (SMD, 0.37; 95% CI: −0.07 to 0.81; p = 0.10). Likewise, nasal pRNFL thickness had a nonsignificant decrease in studies matched for age and sex (SMD, −0.38; 95% CI: −0.80 to 0.04; p = 0.07) while showing a nonsignificant increase in studies that did not match (SMD, 0.09; 95% CI: −0.35 to 0.52; p = 0.70).

Among the quadrant analyses, only SN pRNFL thickness demonstrated a significant decrease in studies that did not match for age and sex (SMD, −0.64; 95% CI: −1.09 to −0.20; p < 0.01), whereas studies that matched showed no significant change (SMD, −0.15; 95% CI: −0.85 to 0.56; p = 0.68). Both ST pRNFL thickness (SMD, −0.23; 95% CI: −0.91 to 0.44; p = 0.49) and IT pRNFL thickness (SMD, 0.32; 95% CI: −1.79 to 1.15; p = 0.67) revealed no significant alterations in matched studies and remained nonsignificant in unmatched studies (SMD, 0.02; 95% CI: −0.41 to 0.46; p = 0.92 and SMD, −0.02; 95% CI: −0.46 to 0.41; p = 0.92, respectively). Similarly, IN pRNFL thickness showed a borderline increase in studies matched for age and sex (SMD, 0.31; 95% CI: −0.04 to 0.66; p = 0.08), while unmatched studies showed no significant difference (SMD, 0.07; 95% CI: −0.36 to 0.51; p = 0.75).

Eye Selection Method

The studies incorporated in the analysis utilized various techniques for eye selection, leading to potential heterogeneity. Subgroup analysis based on the method of eye selection revealed that the method of eye selection did not substantially alter overall results (online suppl. Table 4). There was a nonsignificant decrease in average pRNFL thickness (SMD, −0.27; 95% CI: −0.90 to 0.36; p = 0.40), temporal pRNFL thickness (SMD, −0.21; 95% CI: −0.56 to 0.14; p = 0.24), and nasal pRNFL thickness (SMD, −0.38; 95% CI: −0.80 to 0.04; p = 0.07) when analyzing studies that measured both eyes. In studies that measured a single eye, average pRNFL thickness (SMD, 0.14; 95% CI: −0.30 to 0.57; p = 0.54), temporal pRNFL thickness (SMD, 0.37; 95% CI: −0.07 to 0.81; p = 0.10), and nasal pRNFL thickness (SMD, 0.09; 95% CI: −0.35 to 0.52; p = 0.70) all showed nonsignificant increases. For studies with a mixed method, average pRNFL thickness demonstrated a nonsignificant change (SMD, 0.04; 95% CI: −0.32 to 0.39; p = 0.84).

SN pRNFL thickness showed a significant decrease in single-eye studies (SMD, −0.64; 95% CI: −1.09 to −0.20; p < 0.01) and remained nonsignificant in the both-eye method (SMD, −0.15; 95% CI: −0.85 to 0.56; p = 0.68). ST pRNFL thickness showed nonsignificant differences across both-eye (SMD, −0.23; 95% CI: −0.91 to 0.44; p = 0.49) and single-eye studies (SMD, 0.02; 95% CI: −0.41 to 0.46; p = 0.92). IN pRNFL thickness had a borderline increase in both-eye studies (SMD, 0.31; 95% CI: −0.04 to 0.66; p = 0.08) and nonsignificant changes in single-eye studies (SMD, 0.07; 95% CI: −0.36 to 0.51; p = 0.75). IT pRNFL thickness demonstrated a significant decrease in both-eye studies (SMD, −0.32; 95% CI: −1.79 to 1.15; p < 0.01), while remaining nonsignificant in single-eye studies (SMD, −0.02; 95% CI: −0.46 to 0.41; p = 0.92).

Regarding macular layers, studies that measured both eyes showed a significant increase in average total MT (SMD, 0.40; 95% CI: 0.02 to 0.78; p = 0.04), while average macular RNFL (SMD, −0.07; 95% CI: −0.44 to 0.30; p = 0.72), GCL (SMD, 0.27; 95% CI: −0.11 to 0.65; p = 0.16), INL (SMD, 0.32; 95% CI: −0.05 to 0.70; p = 0.09), and IPL (SMD, 0.24; 95% CI: −0.13 to 0.62; p = 0.21) had nonsignificant increases. Single-eye studies demonstrated significant increases in average total MT (SMD, 0.91; 95% CI: 0.46 to 1.35; p < 0.01), GCL (SMD, 0.49; 95% CI: 0.06 to 0.92; p = 0.02), INL (SMD, 0.77; 95% CI: 0.33 to 1.21; p < 0.01), IPL (SMD, 0.66; 95% CI: 0.23 to 1.10; p < 0.01), and RNFL (SMD, 0.68; 95% CI: 0.24 to 1.11; p < 0.01). Studies with a mixed eye selection method had nonsignificant changes across all macular layers, including average total MT (SMD, −0.30; 95% CI: −1.10 to 0.50; p = 0.46), GCL (SMD, −0.15; 95% CI: −0.90 to 0.60; p = 0.69), INL (SMD, 0.12; 95% CI: −0.21 to 0.45; p = 0.48), IPL (SMD, −0.10; 95% CI: −0.95 to 0.74; p = 0.81), and RNFL (SMD, 0.04; 95% CI: −0.60 to 0.68; p = 0.90).

OCT Device

Subgroup analysis based on the OCT device used revealed that the choice of device had some influence on the reported layer thicknesses, although many results remained nonsignificant (online suppl. Table 5). For average pRNFL thickness, no significant differences were observed in studies using Heidelberg Spectralis (SMD, −0.17; 95% CI: −0.55 to 0.22; p = 0.39) or Optovue RTVue (SMD, 0.23; 95% CI: −0.18 to 0.63; p = 0.27). Average total MT showed a significant increase in studies using Topcon 3D-OCT (SMD, 0.64; 95% CI: 0.14 to 1.14; p = 0.01), whereas studies with Heidelberg Spectralis had a nonsignificant decrease (SMD, −0.30; 95% CI: −1.0 to 0.50; p = 0.46).

For macular layers, average macular GCL thickness was nonsignificant across the two devices used (Heidelberg Spectralis: SMD, 0.07; 95% CI: −0.55 to 0.68; p = 0.83; Topcon 3D-OCT: SMD, 0.23; 95% CI: −0.12 to 0.58; p = 0.20). Average macular RNFL thickness showed a significant increase in Heidelberg Spectralis studies (SMD, 0.36; 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.71; p = 0.04) but was nonsignificant in Topcon 3D-OCT studies (SMD, 0.10; 95% CI: −0.47 to 0.66; p = 0.74). Average macular INL thickness demonstrated nonsignificant increases across Heidelberg Spectralis (SMD, 0.33; 95% CI: −0.13 to 0.79; p = 0.16) and Topcon 3D-OCT studies (SMD, 0.28; 95% CI: −0.07 to 0.63; p = 0.11). Average macular IPL thickness also showed nonsignificant changes (Heidelberg Spectralis: SMD, 0.12; 95% CI: −0.57 to 0.80; p = 0.74; Topcon 3D-OCT: SMD, 0.33; 95% CI: −0.02 to 0.68; p = 0.07).

Sensitivity Analysis

As there was significant heterogeneity in the performed meta-analyses, we performed sensitivity analysis on all 12 OCT parameters, i.e., average pRNFL thickness, temporal pRNFL thickness, nasal pRNFL thickness, ST pRNFL thickness, SN pRNFL thickness, IT pRNFL thickness, IN pRNFL thickness, average total MT, and average macular RNFL, IPL, INL, and GCL thicknesses. We omitted the articles one by one sequentially to potentially identify an article as a source of the heterogeneity. Results of sensitivity analysis revealed that the omission of the study by Garcia-Medina et al. [30] in the analysis of SN and IN pRNFL thickness and the study by Friedel et al. [21] in the analysis of average total, IPL, and INL MTs altered the statistical significance of the findings. Exclusion of other studies did not change the significance or direction of the pooled effects (online suppl. Table 6).

Meta-Regression

To further explore potential sources of heterogeneity, meta-regression analyses were performed using sample size, mean age, and percentage of male participants in case and control groups as covariates. Results are shown in the online supplementary Table 7.

A significant negative association was observed in average total MT with the number of cases (β = −0.0771, p = 0.014). Also, the percentage of male participants in both cases (β = 0.0451, p < 0.001) and controls (β = 0.0514, p < 0.001) showed significant positive effects on the overall effect size. Average macular RNFL thickness was significantly influenced by the number of cases (β = −0.0564, p < 0.001). Macular GCL and IPL thicknesses also showed significant negative associations with the number of cases (β = −0.0506, p = 0.025; β = −0.0615, p = 0.002, respectively) and positive associations with the percentage of male participants in both cases (β = 0.0269, p = 0.031; β = 0.0294, p = 0.047, respectively) and controls (β = 0.0335, p < 0.001; β = 0.0363, p = 0.001, respectively). Similarly, macular INL thickness demonstrated significant effects for the number of cases (β = −0.0410, p = 0.008) and the proportion of males in both cases (β = 0.0206, p = 0.012) and controls (β = 0.0230, p = 0.009).

The average pRNFL thickness was significantly associated with the number of cases (β = −0.0224, p = 0.019) and controls (β = −0.0227, p = 0.018), both negatively. Nasal pRNFL thickness showed significant negative associations with mean age in both cases (β = −0.0508, p = 0.019) and controls (β = −0.0492, p = 0.018). The ST pRNFL sector was significantly influenced by the number of cases (β = −0.0172, p = 0.032), whereas the SN pRNFL was positively associated with the mean age of both cases (β = 0.1800, p = 0.001) and controls (β = 0.1801, p = 0.001). No other significant associations were observed for the remaining sectors.

Additional Findings

Several studies reported a correlation between OCT metrics and cognitive or behavioral scores (Table 2). For instance, there was a report of a positive correlation between IQ level and SN pRNFL thickness [19], average pRNFL thickness [32], and IT pRNFL thickness [30]. In the study conducted by Wang et al. [28], there was a significant positive correlation between the thickness of peripapillary INL in the IN quadrant and the total IQ score. Also, negative correlation was observed between the social awareness domain in SRS and the thickness of peripapillary INL in the IN quadrant in the same study. Moreover, there was a positive correlation between composite IQ level and inferior pRNFL thickness [30]. Some studies also reported data on other scores as well. A negative correlation between The Social Responsiveness Scale-Second Edition (SRS-2) scores with average total MT and ONL thickness and average ONL volume was found in the study by Friedel et al. [21]. Autism behavior checklist (ABC) score had a positive correlation with average and nasal parafoveal MT, average macular RNFL thickness, IPL, nasal parafoveal, and average INL thickness while CGI severity scores were associated with average and nasal parafoveal MT, average macular RNFL, IPL, and nasal parafoveal and temporal perifoveal INL thicknesses [9]. On the other hand, the CARS score had a negative correlation with average macular IPL and GCL thicknesses of the left eye [5].

Additional findings include other structural alterations in various macular and peripapillary layers, which were sparsely reported across studies and were not included in the analysis. The details of these findings for each study are summarized in Table 1.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis of the OCT findings of patients with ASD. The results of the meta-analysis were not statistically significant except for IN pRNFL which was significantly increased in patients suffering from ASD compared to the HC group. There was no evidence of publication bias in the meta-analyses carried out. The meta-analysis of most variables demonstrated a substantial heterogeneity except for the meta-analysis of IN pRNFL which showed a low heterogeneity.

ASD is a group of neurodevelopmental disorders [37]. Most perinatal disorders resulting in the development of ASD are mostly developed inside the CNS [26]. Although there is an agreement regarding the early excessive growth of the brain in infancy, which is then followed by a halted growth and subsequent normalization in brain volume from adolescence to late middle age, the modifications in cerebral structures in individuals with ASD during adulthood are currently a topic of contention [38, 39]. While certain studies have indicated a decrease in brain volume among autistic adults [38], others have documented normal brain and gray matter volumes, along with unchanged cortical thickness in adults with ASD [40, 41]. Since the retina and the brain are both developed from the ectoderm, the alterations in cerebral structure or function may also be reflected in the retina [42].

There are some neuro-anatomical changes in patients with ASD which encompass both gray (including amygdala, hippocampus, and precuneus) and white matter (including uncinate fasciculus) [25, 43]. Due to brain morphology studies, RNFL, GCL, and IPL are related to the brain’s gray and white matter alterations more than other retinal layers [44]. Mutlu et al. [44] observed that the thinning of RNFL, GCL, and IPL layers might be the indicators of the smaller gray and white matter volumes. In addition, the thinner RNFL and GCL might demonstrate worse white matter microstructure [44]. In another study carried out by Mauschitz et al. [45], RNFL was correlated with the white matter volume of the occipital lobe. On the other hand, GCL and IPL were both associated with the total gray and white matter volume in the brain and occipital lobe gray matter volume [45]. In particular, it is hypothesized that RNFL alterations might be a good indicator of these neuronal alterations [5]. The unmyelinated axons of retinal ganglion cells make the RNFL particularly sensitive to neuronal damage, and its thinning may serve as a potential marker of neuronal loss, reflecting defects in neuronal development or chronic axonal degeneration processes [37, 46]. It is reported that the RNFL layer is considerably thinner in neurologic diseases like multiple sclerosis, and it is associated with clinical and paraclinical variables, including disability, visual function, and MRI changes [47]. Additionally, Carriello et al. [48] demonstrated that RNFL thickness can be a marker for the severity of schizophrenia symptoms.

There are two possible mechanisms suggested as the underlying causes of the CNS changes that may lead to retinal alterations in patients with ASD: the neuro-inflammatory process [49] and an impairment of programmed cell death (apoptosis) in early development [50]. Postmortem studies have demonstrated a neuro-inflammatory process in the brains of patients with ASD [51]. During a pathologic change in the brain, microglial cells are the first cells that respond to these alterations by releasing cytokines, including interleukin (IL)-1, IL-2, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-a [52]. These cells also distribute across multiple retinal sublayers, potentially contributing to changes in the retina [53]. Moreover, postmortem studies have shown that CNS apoptosis regulation is impaired during the first years of life, leading to early brain overgrowth. Further in childhood and adolescence, apoptosis then becomes excessive, further disrupting brain maturation and preventing the formation of proper neural connections in the CNS [50, 54]. These changes in the CNS can hypothetically lead to alterations in the retinal layer in patients with ASD [21, 55].

These structural differences in individuals with ASD can contribute to the atypical visual processing that is seen in ASD [10, 30]. For instance, individuals with ASD often show enhanced performance on static visual tasks that rely on local detail processing but show reduced performance on dynamic visual tasks that require motion perception [10, 11]. Additionally, recent studies have shown that ASD patients have a more restricted visual field compared to neurotypical controls [13] and display abnormal electroretinograms indicating functional alterations in the retina and visual processing pathways [56].

Most of the included studies in this systematic review (except for Bagci et al. [9] and Montazeri et al. [31]) investigated the alterations of pRNFL in patients with ASD, suggesting changes in this layer. The overall result of our current meta-analysis indicated that only the IN section of the pRNFL is likely to be significantly thickened in patients with ASD.

Garcia-Medina et al. [30] suggested that there is a significant increase in the pRNFL layer’s thickness, which is a paradigm of white matter in the retina, particularly in the inferior sections in the ASD group. Similarly, Friedel et al. [21] demonstrated that although overall pRNFL thickness was not significantly altered in patients with ASD compared to neurotypical controls, a significant increase in pRNFL thickness was observed among female subjects.

Conversely, Emberti Gialloreti et al. [32] reported a significant decrease in the nasal quadrant of the pRNFL layer in patients with ASD. They also observed different patterns of pRNFL alterations among ASD subtypes; a significant thinning was reported in high-functioning autism, while no alterations were found in Asperger Syndrome when compared to neurotypical subjects. However, the very small cohort size limits the reliability of this finding [32]. Nevertheless, the last study suggests that the interstudy inconsistencies may be a result of the heterogeneity of ASD itself, as well as other methodological confounders.

Our meta-regression on the mean age of ASD and control groups revealed no significant effect of age on most retinal layers, except for the average and ST pRNFL thickness, where higher mean age was associated with greater thinning. This suggests that pRNFL thinning in ASD can become more evident with aging. Consistently, the subgroup analysis comparing the adult and non-adult groups showed thinner average, temporal, and nasal pRNFL in adults, with only the nasal pRNFL thinning being statistically significant. Together, these findings may suggest a potential age-related enhancement of existing age-related pRNFL thinning in ASD [57]. The significant thickening found in the IN pRNFL appears to be independent of sample size, age, and sex distribution, indicating a potentially meaningful finding across different study designs.

Previous studies have reported inconsistent alterations across retinal layers in ASD. Several investigations found increased INL, IPL, RNFL, and total MT [9, 29, 30], whereas others observed reductions in total macular, IPL, and GCL measurements, particularly in adults [5, 21]. Our meta-analysis did not demonstrate significant alterations in these layers. Considering that GCL and IPL are regarded as retinal counterparts of cerebral gray matter and relate to its volume [5], it is plausible that the parenchymal overgrowth observed in early neurodevelopment among individuals with ASD could similarly manifest as increased retinal parenchymal thickness, particularly in younger participants. Our subgroup analysis showed a consistent pattern of significantly increased thickness in the non-adult group. Whereas the adult subgroup demonstrated either smaller or even negative (nonsignificant) effect sizes. Although meta-regression did not identify significant associations for these layers, the negative beta coefficients suggest a trend toward reduced macular thickening with increasing age. This pattern may in part be explained by the physiological age-related thinning of macular layers, such as the GCL, INL, and IPL [58].

Regarding possible confounding methodological factors, our meta-regression revealed significantly larger SMDs for populations with a higher percentage of male participants in all macular layers except for RNFL. Larger study sample sizes are consistently associated with a shift toward smaller effect sizes in the macular layers, and studies that used a single-eye selection method reported significantly larger effect sizes for macular layer thicknesses. Subgroup analysis on the OCT device used showed that the Optovue RTVue appeared sensitive to total MT differences, while the Heidelberg Spectralis was sensitive to macular RNFL differences. All these findings suggest that small sample size, male sex, single-eye selection method, and certain OCT devices could cause a positive bias in reported differences in macular layers.

Some of the included studies reported a positive correlation between pRNFL thickness and cognitive test scoring. For instance, Emberti Gialloreti et al. [32] reported a thinner global pRNFL in subjects with a more selective verbal IQ impairment in ASD cases. Additionally, Garcia-Medina et al. [30] reported a positive correlation between inferior pRNFL thickness and IQ. Wang et al. [28] reported a significant positive correlation between the thickness of the INL in the IN quadrant and the total IQ score. Symptom severity is another parameter linked to the IPL and GCL thinning in patients with ASD. Kara et al. [5] found a significant negative correlation between disease severity and IPL and GCL. Friedel et al. [21] also found associations between reduced MT and volume and ASD symptom severity but found no significant association regarding RNFL. Although it is suggested that the correlation of disease severity and neuroinflammation could be the cause of these associations [21, 59, 60], these results and their application in clinical practice need to be investigated by more studies in order to be interpreted vividly.

Only one of the included studies, Kara et al. [5], investigated choroidal thickness and reported significantly decreased values in ASD cases compared with the HC group, indicating significant choroidal thinning. They hypothesized that the reduction in regional cerebral blood flow and oxygenation associated with ASD [61] could be related to this finding. Furthermore, they also suggested that the involvement of dopamine receptors in choroidal tissue development [62, 63] and the altered dopamine levels observed in ASD patients [64, 65] may imply a possible link [5]. There is a need for more studies to further investigate these possible correlations.

The variances observed in the included studies may indicate that the standard database of SD-OCT for individuals without neurological conditions might not be directly relevant to individuals with ASD [30]. Consequently, it is advisable to exercise caution in analyzing SD-OCT findings in individuals with ASD when investigating or suspecting the presence of other eye-related or neuro-ophthalmic disorders, such as glaucoma, to prevent making inaccurate positive or negative interpretations.

This study had several limitations that should be taken into consideration. First of all, the included OCT studies were all case-controls with relatively small sample sizes, which may affect their results. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were different, including different criteria for ASD. Moreover, the structures of the retina and the choroid are dynamic, making them susceptible to various situations like aging [58] and medications used by patients, including antidepressants, neuroleptics, and anti-seizure medications. There is evidence in the literature that the prolonged use of antipsychotics can lead to dopamine receptor blockade, which eventually results in less input to retinal ganglion cells, leading to their death [55]. However, due to the small number of studies and reported medication data, subgroup analysis on this important factor was not possible. Therefore, more longitudinal studies controlling these conditions are needed to draw a more accurate conclusion. In addition, the amount of heterogeneity across most of the studies was considerable, most probably arising from factors such as employment of different age groups, sex distributions, OCT devices, and segmentation accuracy in OCT, which is another factor causing bias in the interpretation of the results. The overall small number of studies reporting the alterations of OCT findings in patients with ASD is another limitation of our study. As a result, our meta-analysis may not be reliable. More studies with larger populations with standardized methodologies should be carried out to draw a firm conclusion and to assess the effects of different confounders, such as parameters like intraocular pressure, visual acuity, antidepressant usage, symptom severity, and different ASD variations like Asperger’s in OCT findings.

Conclusion

The findings of the present systematic review and meta-analysis have confirmed the observations made in included studies regarding the significant increase of IN pRNFL in individuals with ASD when compared to the HC group. Although statistical methods were applied to account for some of the confounding factors, the overall evidence remains limited due to small sample sizes and interstudy heterogeneity, and the results do not provide conclusive support for a true association among other retinal layers. While OCT may hold potential as a noninvasive tool for detecting retinal alterations in ASD in the future, current evidence remains insufficient, and larger longitudinal studies are needed to clarify its value, explore underlying mechanisms, and assess its role in early detection or monitoring of neurodevelopmental conditions like ASD.
