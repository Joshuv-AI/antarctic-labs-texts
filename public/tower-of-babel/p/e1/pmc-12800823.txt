Safety of bioelectrical impedance analysis in patients with cardiac implantable electronic devices: A systematic review
Authors: Leonardo J. Uribe-Cavero, Fabian A. Chavez-Ecos, Kiara Camacho-Caballero, José Carlos Grados-Pintos, Patricia Vera-Maccha, Anthony Siguas-Huasasquiche, Dinesh Sharma, Carlos J. Toro-Huamanchumo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) is widely used to assess body composition. However, its safety in patients with cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) remains uncertain owing to potential electromagnetic interference (EMI).ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate potential complications associated with clinical-grade BIA use in patients with CIEDs, based on currently available evidence.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review of studies published up to December 26, 2024, identified through PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. The primary outcome was the occurrence of adverse events, including EMI, device malfunction, or arrhythmias.ResultsOf 3668 records, 6 cohort studies (n = 531 patients) met the inclusion criteria. These included patients with pacemakers (n = 175), implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (n = 268), and cardiac resynchronization therapy defibrillators (n = 154). BIA protocols varied in frequency (5–500 kHz) and current intensity (typically ≤0.8 mA). No study reported clinically significant EMI, device malfunctions, or BIA-induced arrhythmias. Device parameters (eg, lead impedance, pacing thresholds) remained stable before and after BIA exposure. Risk of bias was rated as good in 1 study and fair in 5.ConclusionCurrent evidence suggests that clinical-grade BIA is safe in patients with CIEDs, with no reported adverse effects or device interferences. These findings contribute to informing and potentially updating previous recommendations that discouraged BIA in this population. However, further high-quality studies are needed to confirm safety across different BIA protocols and device types. Importantly, these findings apply to clinical-grade BIA and should not be extrapolated to consumer-grade wearables, which may present a theoretical risk of CIED interference.

Key Findings ▪Clinical-grade bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) showed no electromagnetic interference, malfunctions, or induced arrhythmias in cardiac implantable electronic device recipients; device parameters remained stable.▪Continuous telemetry/electrogram revealed no oversensing or pacing inhibition during BIA.▪A small subset had same-day arrhythmias without telemetry changes, suggesting non-BIA causes.▪Heterogeneity limits certainty, protocol standardization is required, and results do not generalize to consumer wearables.

Introduction

Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) is a widely used, noninvasive method to assess body composition, including fat mass, fat-free mass, and total body water.1,2 It also provides estimates of phase angle and visceral fat, which serve as markers of nutritional and metabolic status.3 In clinical practice, BIA is increasingly applied to evaluate body composition in individuals with obesity,4 monitor fluid balance in patients with heart failure, detect early signs of decompensation before hospitalization, and assess overall cardiovascular health.5 Its practicality, affordability, and usefulness in patient monitoring and disease management make it a valuable tool in routine care. However, its use may be contraindicated in specific populations.

The number of pacemaker (PM) and defibrillator implantations continues to grow, with approximately 1 million devices placed annually worldwide.6 These devices are a cornerstone therapy for managing atrioventricular block, malignant ventricular arrhythmias, and advanced heart failure.7 Although BIA offers clinical benefits in assessing patients with cardiac conditions,8 concerns about potential electromagnetic interference (EMI) have led to longstanding caution in individuals with cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs), such as PMs and defibrillators. In 2004, the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism raised concerns regarding EMI risks and recommended caution despite the absence of documented adverse events.9,10 Subsequently, the American Heart Association issued a statement in 2011 advising against BIA use in patients with PMs, although the recommendation lacked detailed justification or supporting evidence.10 This uncertainty has contributed to inconsistent clinical practices and reluctance to use BIA in patients with CIEDs.

More recently, observational studies have begun to challenge these early recommendations, suggesting that contemporary BIA devices may be safe for individuals with CIEDs.11 However, the overall safety profile remains insufficiently defined. Clarifying this issue is clinically relevant, given that it could support broader and safer integration of BIA into cardiology and obesity care. This systematic review aimed to evaluate potential complications associated with BIA use in patients with CIEDs, based on currently available evidence.

Methods

This systematic review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and was conducted in accordance with a registered protocol (PROSPERO: CRD42024587548). The clinical question guiding this review was as follows: what are the complications associated with the use of BIA in patients with CIEDs?

Data sources

We conducted a comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library, covering all literature up to December 26, 2024. Search algorithms were adapted to the syntax and indexing of each database. No restrictions on language or publication status were applied. The full search strategy is presented in Supplemental Table 1.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) nonrandomized designs, including cohort studies, case-control studies, and prospective case series, and (2) participants with CIEDs—including PMs, implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs), or cardiac resynchronization therapy defibrillators (CRT-Ds)—who underwent clinical-grade BIA assessment. Narrative reviews, case reports, and letters to the editor were excluded. No minimum sample size threshold was applied.

Study selection

Search results were first screened by title and abstract. Two independent reviewers (A.S. and J.G.) screened records at both the title/abstract and full-text levels. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or by consulting a third reviewer (C.J.T.H.). A list of excluded full-text articles is presented in Supplemental Table 2.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (L.U. and P.V.) independently extracted data using a standardized Excel form. Any discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer (C.J.T.H.).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the safety of clinical-grade BIA in patients with CIEDs, evaluated through the occurrence of EMI, device malfunction, arrhythmias, or clinically significant adverse events. EMI was defined as any disruption in CIED function caused by extracorporeal voltage induction from clinical-grade BIA-related electric, magnetic, or electromagnetic fields.12 Device malfunctions included inappropriate sensing, misdetection of arrhythmias, unintended therapy delivery, or pacing inhibition.13 Arrhythmias were defined as new-onset or worsened arrhythmias temporally associated with BIA exposure, as documented by device interrogation or external monitoring.14 Clinically relevant adverse events included symptoms such as dizziness, syncope, or palpitations associated with suspected device dysfunction.15

Synthesis of results

A narrative synthesis of the included studies was performed. We first described the study populations, detailing their sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. Subsequently, we examined key aspects of BIA protocols, including frequency (kHz), current intensity (A), signal type (steady sinusoid or modulated), modulation characteristics (>1 kHz when reported), pulse features, duration of measurement, electrode configuration, and proximity to the CIED. We recorded “NR” when information was not reported. Safety outcomes were stratified by device type (PMs, ICDs, and CRT-Ds) and the type of adverse event reported. When available, we also summarized quantitative changes in device function parameters, such as lead impedance, pacing thresholds, and battery voltage, comparing pre- and post-BIA measurements. A meta-analysis was not conducted owing to significant heterogeneity in study designs, BIA protocols, and outcome definitions. Categorical variables were summarized using absolute and relative frequencies, whereas continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations

Risk-of-bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies.16 This tool includes 14 binary (yes/no) criteria evaluating key methodological aspects, including clarity of the research question, population definition, sample size justification, and control of confounding. Each study received 1 point per criterion met, for a maximum score of 14. Studies scoring ≥70% were classified as good quality, 40%–69% as fair quality, and <40% as poor quality. Two reviewers (A.S. and P.V.) conducted assessments independently. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or, when necessary, by a third reviewer (L.U. or F.C.).

Ethics

This work is a systematic review of previously published data. It was conducted in accordance with PRISMA and did not involve direct interaction with human participants or access to identifiable individual-level data. Accordingly, institutional review board/ethics committee review and the requirement for informed consent were not applicable and were waived.

Results

Selection of studies

A total of 3668 records were identified through the database search. After removing 1307 duplicates, 2361 studies were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 2329 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Thirty-two full-text articles were assessed in detail, and 6 studies were ultimately included in this systematic review. Study population and characteristics

Six cohort studies comprising 531 participants with CIEDs were included.17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22 These studies were conducted in the United States, Switzerland, Brazil, France, and Germany. Recruitment periods ranged from 3 to 18 months. Only 1 study included formal follow-up visits at 6 and 12 months; the remaining studies assessed safety during single sessions or over short intervals. The study populations varied in comorbidities, left ventricular ejection fraction, and New York Heart Association functional class, with 163 participants classified as New York Heart Association class III–IV. The included studies evaluated a range of CIED types, encompassing 175 patients with PMs, 268 with ICDs, and 154 with CRT-Ds. Across studies, clinical-grade BIA used frequencies between 5 and 500 kHz with low currents (usually ≤ 0.8 mA). Details of waveform, modulation, and pulse characteristics were rarely reported, whereas electrode configuration, patient posture, proximity to the CIED, and measurement duration were more consistently described. Protocol-level technical details, including underreported fields (NR), are presented in Supplemental Table 3.

Risk of bias

Of the 6 included studies, only 1 was rated as having good quality (12 of 14 points on the NIH scale), whereas the remaining 5 were classified as having fair quality according to the NIH quality assessment tool for before-after studies with no control group. The most frequent methodological limitations across studies included the lack of sample size justification, the absence of blinding of outcome assessors, and the failure to control for potential confounders. In all 6 studies, outcome assessors were not blinded to the exposure status of participants, which could have introduced detection bias.

In addition, in several studies, some domains could not be assessed owing to insufficient reporting, particularly regarding the participation rate of eligible individuals and loss to follow-up after baseline, which were marked as “Cannot Determine (CD).” These reporting gaps may limit the internal validity and suggest that findings should be interpreted with caution. Full scoring details for each study are presented in Supplemental Table 4.

Safety outcomes

EMIs

None of the included studies reported clinically significant EMI associated with clinical-grade BIA use in patients with CIEDs. Continuous telemetry and intracardiac electrogram (EGM) monitoring consistently showed that BIA-induced electrical currents did not interfere with device function, affect signal interpretation, or generate oversensing artifacts.17, 18, 19, 20

In a cohort of 63 patients with ICDs undergoing BIA at 0.8 mA and 5–100 kHz, continuous electrogram monitoring and postexposure device interrogation showed no evidence of EMI, including electrical noise, oversensing, or pacing disruption.19 Similarly, a study involving 200 patients with PMs and ICDs reported no changes in device parameters such as lead impedance or pacing thresholds, with follow-up assessments at 6 and 12 months confirming the absence of delayed effects.20

Additional studies using continuous telemetry during BIA exposure further supported these findings. One study involving 43 patients undergoing CIED implantation found no signal distortion or inappropriate arrhythmia detection during or after BIA.18 Another analysis of 20 patients with heart failure and ICDs or CRT-Ds reported no oversensing, no loss of communication, and no unintended therapy delivery.17

Devices malfunctions

No study reported device malfunctions attributable to clinical-grade BIA exposure. Across all CIED types and variable follow-up durations, sensing and pacing functions remained stable, with no evidence of unintended therapies, inhibition, or the need for device reprogramming. Device parameters remained within normal operational ranges before and after BIA in all reported cases. In a study with extended follow-up to 12 months, no significant changes in pacing thresholds, lead impedance, or battery voltage were observed.20 In patients with heart failure and ICDs, telemetry monitoring during BIA confirmed no inappropriate detection of tachyarrhythmias or shocks, further supporting device stability.17

Arrhythmic events

No study identified new-onset arrhythmias directly attributable to clinical-grade BIA. Continuous electrocardiogram monitoring before, during, and after BIA confirmed that the procedure did not provoke ventricular or supraventricular arrhythmias, nor did it interfere with arrhythmia detection algorithms. Although 1 study reported arrhythmic events in 6 patients (4%) on the same day as BIA assessment, all episodes occurred at least 30 minutes after the procedure, showed no temporal association with telemetry anomalies, and none required emergent device reprogramming or therapy delivery.21 This suggests an underlying cardiac etiology rather than a BIA-related effect.

Clinical adverse events

No clinically significant adverse events were reported in any of the included studies. Patients undergoing clinical-grade BIA did not experience dizziness, syncope, palpitations, or other symptoms suggestive of hemodynamic instability or device dysfunction. Continuous clinical monitoring confirmed the absence of acute complications, whereas extended follow-up evaluations ruled out delayed adverse effects. One study using 24-hour electrocardiogram monitoring post-BIA found no evidence of cardiovascular decompensation, and no symptoms were reported in studies with long-term follow-up.21

Discussion

Summary of main findings

This systematic review evaluated the safety of clinical-grade BIA in patients with CIEDs, synthesizing evidence from 6 cohort studies involving 531 participants. Overall, clinical-grade BIA did not induce clinically significant EMI, device malfunction, or immediate arrhythmias during continuous telemetry monitoring across various CIED types, including PMs, ICDs, and CRT-Ds. Although 1 study reported 6 arrhythmic events (4%) occurring on the same day as BIA assessment, these events occurred at least 30 minutes after exposure, without telemetry anomalies, and involved patients with a history of recurrent arrhythmias. Therefore, the aggregated evidence supports that clinical-grade BIA, when performed under reported protocols, seems to be safe for individuals with CIEDs, although the certainty of evidence is limited by methodological constraints.

Interpretation and clinical relevance

This systematic review synthesizes evidence supporting the safety of clinical-grade BIA in patients with CIEDs and heart failure when applied under controlled clinical conditions. Most studies were conducted in inpatient or outpatient cardiology settings with standardized protocols and continuous telemetry monitoring, ensuring detection of any real-time EMI or device abnormalities.

These findings have direct clinical relevance. Clinical-grade BIA offers a noninvasive and accessible method for assessing body composition and fluid distribution, parameters essential to optimizing heart failure management. Phase angle, which reflects cellular membrane integrity and fluid balance, has been shown to correlate with N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide levels and lung fluid content.23 Similarly, the edema index and the extracellular water–to–total body water ratio have been associated with adverse outcomes, including mortality and hospital readmission.24,25 These markers have growing utility in both clinical monitoring and research, particularly in longitudinal studies aiming to monitor changes in heart failure congestion status and prognosis.26

From a mechanical standpoint, CIEDs sense low-frequency intracardiac signals, whereas the kHz-range carriers of clinical-grade BIA are attenuated. However, low-frequency envelopes (<1 kHz) are more likely to be detected, consistent with ISO 14117:2019.27 The absence of EMI across studies exposed to ≥5 kHz supports the safety of clinical-grade BIA within these ranges. Nevertheless, detailed reporting of BIA protocols is essential to strengthen reproducibility and appraisal of safety.

Although this review focused on clinical-grade BIA, it is essential to distinguish them from consumer-grade bioimpedance wearables, which may present a theoretical risk of CIED interference reported in recent studies.28 Our findings support the cautious but feasible integration of BIA as a complementary tool in the care of patients with heart failure and CIEDs, especially when used in monitored, clinical settings.

Interpretation in the context of existing evidence

Studies reporting a safety profile

Multiple studies have reported the safety of BIA among patients with CIEDs. Chabin et al20 included 200 patients with PMs, ICDs, and CRTs who underwent BIA using frequencies between 5 and 100 kHz and an amperage of 0.8 mA; they reported no EMI, device malfunction, or arrhythmias. Similarly, Roehrich et al21 analyzed 217 BIA assessments in patients with advanced heart failure and ICDs or CRT-D devices, without evidence of device malfunctions or arrhythmias under continuous telemetry monitoring. Meyer et al19 evaluated patients with single-chamber, dual-chamber, and biventricular ICDs from 5 manufacturers and found no detectable EMI or arrhythmic complications. Garlini et al18 also confirmed this safety profile by assessing patients before and after CIED implantation, with no telemetry abnormalities observed during BIA. Perioperative data from Pinto et al22 also showed no EMI during single-frequency BIA performed minutes after implantation under real-time telemetry with therapies disabled, including a conservative ipsilateral near-generator pathway. These findings collectively support the safety of kHz, low-current clinical-grade BIA in both routine and periprocedural contexts.

Studies reporting isolated safety concerns

Although the overall safety profile of BIA remains favorable, a few observations were reported. In the study by Roehrich et al,21 6 arrhythmic events (4%) occurred within 24 hours of BIA measurement; however, all events occurred at least 30 minutes after exposure and involved patients with a known history of arrhythmias, including those receiving inotropic support or awaiting heart transplantation, suggesting that these events were caused by underlying cardiac conditions rather than BIA-related effects. Separately, Smeets et al29 reported that 28% of all alerts in patients undergoing remote monitoring with CIEDs were generated by the device’s built-in bioimpedance algorithm. However, most of these alerts led to patient education rather than therapeutic interventions, highlighting their limited clinical impact in routine practice.

BIA in specific patient populations

Many studies also highlight the clinical utility of BIA across diverse heart failure populations. Lyons et al30 reported that an elevated edema index was associated with worse outcomes, including death, urgent transplantation, or ventricular assist device implantation in chronic heart failure patients. Similarly, Lee et al24 used segmental BIA techniques to assess lung fluid status and demonstrated strong correlations with established clinical markers of pulmonary congestion and heart failure exacerbation, without reporting adverse effects or device interference.

Strengths and limitations

This systematic review has several strengths. First, it adhered to PRISMA guidelines and included a comprehensive literature search across 4 major databases. Second, the use of the NIH risk-of-bias tool provided a structured assessment of the quality of the included studies. Third, the included studies used real-time telemetry and postprocedural device interrogation, providing robust safety data across a range of CIED types. Limitations include heterogeneity in BIA methodologies, relatively small study sizes, and the absence of randomized controlled trials. In addition, most studies involved specific device manufacturers, which may limit extrapolation to other CIED models. Methodological reporting gaps, particularly concerning device parameters and patient follow-up details, also limit the generalizability of our conclusions. These limitations emphasize the need for standardized research frameworks and prospective studies with larger and more diverse populations.

Recommendations for future research

Future research should prioritize large prospective studies, ideally randomized controlled trials evaluating the safety and clinical utility of BIA in patients with CIEDs. Standardization of protocol-level parameters, including signal characteristics, frequency and current settings, electrode configuration, posture, exposure duration, and number of measurements, is essential to enhance reproducibility and inform guideline development. Evaluation of nontransvenous systems such as leadless PMs, as well as subcutaneous and extravascular ICDs, is also needed to address generalizability. Furthermore, evaluating the use of BIA in different clinical scenarios, including acute care, long-term acute care, inpatient, nursing homes, and home-based monitoring programs, could broaden its utility in heart failure management.

Conclusion

Our systematic review reinforces the safety profile of BIA in patients with CIEDs. The included studies did not report clinically meaningful interference, arrhythmogenic effects, or device malfunction during and after BIA assessments. However, methodological heterogeneity across existing studies highlights the need for standardized assessment protocols and larger prospective trials to confirm these findings before widespread adoption in routine practice. Importantly, clinical-grade BIA should be distinguished from consumer-grade wearables, which may present a theoretical risk of CIED interference.
