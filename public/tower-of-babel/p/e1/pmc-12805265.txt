Introduction

Inferior vena cava (IVC) anomalies, including left-sided IVC, duplicated IVC, and IVC interruption with azygos continuation, are often incidentally detected during electrophysiological studies and can complicate catheter manipulation during ablation. The internal jugular vein approach, which reverses catheter manipulation compared with the standard approach, is often required in cases of IVC interruption.

We report a case of successful catheter ablation of atrial tachycardia (AT) using a vertically flipped (upside-down) 3-dimensional (3D) mapping technique to overcome the challenges posed by IVC interruption.

Case report

A 49-year-old male patient with a history of pulmonary artery stenosis surgery at the age of 7 years was referred for catheter ablation of AT. Detailed records of the previous surgery were unavailable. The patient had been followed at a local clinic but was transferred to our hospital after the detection of new-onset atrial tachyarrhythmia and elevated B-type natriuretic peptide levels. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed right ventricular outflow tract stenosis, with anterior wall hypertrophy (20 mm), narrowing (6.3 mm), and a mosaic flow pattern with a peak gradient of 18 mm Hg. The IVC was not connected to the inferior portion of the right atrium; instead, only the hepatic veins drained into the right atrium. The IVC joined the azygos vein and returned to the right atrium via the superior vena cava (SVC) (Figure 1A). Given that the inferior aspect of the right atrium was not connected to the IVC, the conventional femoral vein approach was impossible. Catheter ablation was performed after obtaining an informed consent from the patient.

A steerable sheath was advanced into the right atrium via the internal jugular vein and used to introduce both the ablation and mapping catheters. A 20-pole electrode catheter was introduced from the right femoral vein and advanced through the azygos vein into the right atrium (Figure 1B). Sequential high-density contact mapping was performed with a multispline mapping catheter (OCTARAY, Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA) and a 3D mapping system (CARTO, Biosense Webster). The surface body patches were placed in the standard configuration.

The right atrium voltage mapping revealed diffuse areas of low voltage and free wall scarring consistent with the previous surgery (Figure 2A). The activation map showed 2 AT circuits: one consistent with typical counterclockwise flutter around the tricuspid annulus (AT1) (Figure 2B) and the other representing a localized reentrant AT in the posterior wall of the right atrium (AT2) (Figure 2C).

Using the jugular vein approach, the direction of catheter rotation was reversed compared with the IVC approach, making manipulation counterintuitive for the operator. To overcome this challenge, the operator stood on the patient’s head side (Figure 3A), and the 3D map was flipped vertically, making catheter movements on the map match those in the IVC approach (Figure 3B). During ablation, the target contact force was approximately 10 g, and the ablation index was set at 500. Linear ablation at the cavotricuspid isthmus successfully terminated AT1, whereas linear ablation at the narrow isthmus in the posterior wall of the right atrium rendered AT2 noninducible. Bidirectional conduction block across the linear ablation lesion was confirmed using a multipolar mapping catheter positioned across the line. Pacing was performed from a spline on 1 side of the lesion, and conduction block was verified by recording split potentials from splines on the contralateral side. This assessment was conducted in both directions.

The patient was followed up for 9 months, and no AT recurrence was observed.

Discussion

IVC interruption is a rare anomaly with a prevalence of 0.3% in the general population and 0.6% in patients with congenital heart disease.1,2 In patients with IVC interruption, the IVC is typically connected to the SVC via the azygos vein. Although several case reports have described cases of catheter ablation performed through the azygos vein, this approach is challenging because of the difficulty in achieving adequate catheter contact. When conventional transfemoral access for catheter ablation is not feasible, it is necessary to have alternative vascular access routes. In cases where transjugular access is limited or contraindicated, transhepatic venous access has been successfully used, particularly in patients with pediatric or complex congenital heart disease. This method involves percutaneous puncture of the hepatic vein under imaging guidance, providing direct access to the right atrium, although with an increased risk of complications such as bleeding and liver injury.3 A less frequently reported alternative is the transaxillary approach, in which access through the axillary vein and subclavian vein allows entry into the SVC4; however, the catheter handling is similar to that of the internal jugular vein approach, and there is a concern that achieving hemostasis by compression may be difficult when inserting a large sheath. Retrograde transaortic approach may be considered.5 However, that procedure requires advanced catheter manipulation skills.

With internal jugular vein access, catheter movements appear inverted on standard 3D maps if the operator stands on the patient’s body side, similar to the usual femoral vein approach. This reversed manipulation may be confusing, particularly for less experienced operators. Conversely, by standing on the patient’s head side and vertically inverting the 3D mapping display, catheter movements appear identical to those with the femoral approach, thereby simplifying the procedure. Furthermore, this approach facilitated better torque transmission and catheter maneuverability by maintaining a straighter alignment of the sheath and catheter. Furthermore, a shielding panel can be placed between the tube and the operator to reduce the operator’s increased exposure to radiation owing to being closer to the fluoroscopic radiograph tube.

In conclusion, the upside-down 3D mapping technique provides a practical method that enables intuitive catheter manipulation via the internal jugular vein in patients with IVC interruption.
