Ag2Se quantum dots neurotoxicity: mitochondrial stress drives ferroptosis-dependent microglial inflammation
Authors: Yongshuai Yao, Zhihui Wang, Chunhui Zhang, Xiaoquan Huang, Tingting Wei, Na Liu, Lingyue Zou, Changcun Bai, Yuanyuan Hu, Qing Fang, Min Chen, Shujing Guan, Yuying Xue, Tianshu Wu, Ting Zhang, Meng Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSilver Selenide quantum dots (Ag2Se QDs) have great potential for biomedical applications due to their excellent optical properties, which has raised concerns about their health hazards. Previous studies have shown that exposure to Ag2Se QDs can induce neurotoxicity, but the underlying mechanisms have not been fully elucidated. In this study, Ag2Se QDs activated Nod-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasomes in microglia. Interestingly, we found that Ag2Se QDs-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation was ferroptosis-dependent. Mechanistically, the overproduction of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) led to the leakage of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cytoplasm via the open mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), thereby activating stimulator of interferon genes (STING)-dependent ferritinophagy. Nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4)-mediated ferritinophagy resulted in the degradation of ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) and the release of iron ions, ultimately triggering ferroptosis. In order to assist in the risk assessment of Ag2Se QDs and the development of risk management strategies. Based on the above experimental data, we constructed an Adverse Outcome Pathway (AOP) framework for Ag2Se QDs neurotoxicity and emphasized the significance of alleviating mitochondrial damage in preventing neurotoxicity. Overall, the present study reveals new mechanisms of Ag2Se QDs-induced neurotoxicity. Introduction

Quantum dots (QDs) have been developed as fluorescent probes for bioimaging because of their unique optical properties. However, the heavy metal content (e.g. Cd, Pb and Hg) contained in conventional QDs is unacceptable for clinical use [1]. Silver selenide quantum dots (Ag2Se QDs) are considered to be a new type of low-toxicity QDs, and the low solubility product constant of Ag2X ensures that the smallest amount of silver ions is released into the organism [2]. Currently, Ag2Se QDs show promise in tumor, cerebrovascular structural and gastrointestinal imaging [3, 4]. However, the ultra-small particle size of Ag2Se QDs enables them to cross the blood-brain barrier into the brain and induce neuroinflammation [5]. Microglia are the resident macrophages of the central nervous system and are important effector cells in neuroinflammation [6]. Microglial M1-like phenotype secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β(IL-1β), interleukin-6(IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), which can exacerbate immune responses and brain damage [7]. Meanwhile, inflammation is involved in the pathogenesis of affective and cognitive disorders in the central nervous system [8]. Therefore, it is crucial to deeply explore the potential mechanisms of neuroinflammation induced by Ag2Se QDs to find coping strategy to control their neurotoxicity.

Ferroptosis is a novel form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxide accumulation. Unfortunately, the brain is abundant in polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) and is characterized by high oxygen consumption and low antioxidant defenses, rendering it susceptible to ferroptosis [9]. Oxidative by-products, e.g., prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), leukotriene B4 (LTB4) and 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), not only inflict direct damage on cellular components, but also initiate and exacerbate inflammatory responses via multiple molecular pathways, which in turn elicits secondary damage to brain tissue [10]. Several studies have demonstrated that ferroptosis inhibitors, e.g., liproxstatin-1(Lip-1) [11] and ferrostatin (Fer-1) [12], can effectively antagonize inflammatory processes in certain neurological disease models. Nanoparticles were recently found to induce ferroptosis in macrophages. Silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs), Graphene quantum dots (GQDs), Fe3O4 nanoparticles and zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) led to cellular iron overload, glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and reduced glutathione (GSH) depletion, redox imbalance and lipid peroxidation (LPO) in RAW 264.7 cells, BV2 cells or Ana-1 cells [13–16]. However, whether Ag2Se QDs trigger ferroptosis in microglia, and if ferroptosis contributes to Ag2Se QD-induced neuroinflammation, remains unclear.

Ferroptosis is actually an autophagy-dependent form of cell death. Nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4)-mediated ferritinophagy has been proposed as a potential upstream regulator of ferroptosis. Iron not involved in biological processes can be stored in ferritin heavy chain 1 (FHT1) of ferritin [17]. NCOA4 acts as a selective autophagy receptor and binds to FTH1, which mediates intracellular ferritin translocation to autophagic lysosomes, ultimately releasing free iron [18]. Overactivation of ferritinophagy leads to iron overload and the Fenton reaction, which results in LPO and ultimately ferroptosis [19]. Previously, it was found that CdTe QDs exposure induced NCOA4-regulated ferritinophagy mediated ferroptosis in RAW264.7 cells [20], but the role of NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy in Ag2Se QD-induced ferroptosis and neuroinflammation has not been explored.

The cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling axis has been identified as a classical key mediator of innate immunity. Mechanistically, cGAS senses cytosolic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) and produces the second messenger cGAMP, thereby triggering the activation of STING. Activated STING exits the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane and translocates to Golgi via the endoplasmic reticulum-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC), leading to the transcription of genes encoding type I interferons and proinflammatory cytokines [21]. In addition to inflammatory responses, emerging research indicates that STING activation is also implicated in ferritinophagy-mediated diseases, e.g., acute kidney injury [22] and cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury [23]. The cGAS-STING pathway can be activated not only by DNA derived from microbial pathogens and nucleus, but also by mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) in the cytoplasm [24]. Mitochondria serve as the primary target of toxic effects by nanoparticles. Nanoparticles can be directly distributed in mitochondria and cause structural damage and dysfunction of mitochondria [25]. Dysregulation of mitochondrial homeostasis triggers the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) and subsequent release of mtDNA into the cytosol [7]. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that Ag2Se QDs activate STING via mtDNA cytosolic leakage, thereby regulating NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy and ferroptosis, and ultimately driving microglial inflammation.

The Adverse Outcome Pathway (AOP) framework is a conceptual framework that initiates from a molecular initiating event (MIE), is followed by triggering multiple key events (KE) at the cellular or organ level, and ultimately leads to an organism or population level adverse outcome (AO). The AOP framework is increasingly being applied to the safety assessment of chemical or environmental pollution exposure. Meanwhile, the AOP framework provides a causal mechanistic diagram depicting the toxicity-inducing processes across different levels, thereby facilitating the formulation of risk management strategies grounded in toxicological mechanisms. However, the AOP framework for Ag2Se QDs-mediated neurotoxicity has not yet been established.

Given the gaps in current knowledge, this study aims to: (1) investigate whether ferroptosis is involved in Ag2Se QD-induced microglial inflammation; (2) elucidate the upstream mechanism, focusing on mtDNA-STING-NCOA4 regulatory axis; (3) construct an AOP framework for Ag2Se QD-induced neurotoxicity to support risk assessment and the development of mitigation strategies. By addressing these questions, this work seeks to provide a novel mechanistic understanding of Ag2Se QDs neurotoxicity, which may provide information for the safer application of Ag2Se QDs in the field of biomedicine.

Materials and methods

Preparation and characterization of Ag2Se QDs

Ag2Se QDs were synthesized according to the previous method [26]. The prepared Ag2Se QDs were homogeneous in particle size and well dispersed, with an average particle size of about 2.5 ± 0.3 nm under transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Ag2Se QDs had a distinct absorption peak around 560 nm. The maximum emission wavelength was 680 nm at an excitation wavelength of 490 nm. In ultrapure water, the hydrated particle size of Ag2Se QDs was 4.0 ± 0.2 nm. Zeta potential was − 23.2 ± 0.6 mV. After incubation in culture medium for 24 h, the hydrodynamic size and zeta potential of Ag2Se QDs dispersed in culture medium were measured by using a Malvern laser particle sizer (Malvern Instruments, Nano-ZS90, UK). In culture medium, the hydrated particle size of Ag2Se QDs was 5.9 ± 0.4 nm (Figure S1a, b). Zeta potential was − 26.4 ± 0.2 mV (Figure S1c, d).

Animal treatment

Specific pathogen free (SPF) grade C57BL/6 J male mice (6–8 weeks, 18–22 g) were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Centre of Southeast University. The mice were bred by Dingjiaqiao Experimental Animal Centre of Southeast University. All mice were housed in stainless steel cages in a well-ventilated animal facility, maintained at 22 ± 2℃ and 65 ± 10% relative humidity, with a 12-h light/dark cycle, and fed with sterilized food and distilled water. The animal experiments were in accordance with the Regulations of the People’s Republic of China on the Administration of Laboratory Animals (2017). The animal experiment protocols were approved by the Experimental Animal Control Committee of Southeast University.

After 2 weeks of adaptive feeding, the mice were randomly divided into 3 groups (10 mice per group), namely control, 15 mg/kg, 30 mg/kg. 15 mg/kg is the application dosage of Ag2Se QDs for tumor tissue imaging [2], which directly corresponds to the biomedical application exposure scenario. 30 mg/kg represents the double imaging dose, intended to explore the toxicological profile at an overloaded level and evaluate dose-response relationship. Given the biomedical applications of Ag2Se QDs, we chose tail vein injection as the exposure route. This study was concerned with neurotoxicity of Ag2Se QDs under acute toxicity experimental conditions, so mice were executed 24 h after treatment. Brain tissues were fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and then sectioned for immunohistochemistry (IHC) and Nissl staining. Some dissected hippocampi were stored in liquid nitrogen for quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis.

IHC

Paraffin sections were processed for dewaxing, antigen repair, endogenous enzyme blocking, serum blocking, primary antibody incubation (IL-1β, 1:500, Servicebio, China), HRP-labeled secondary antibody incubation, DAB staining, hematoxylin staining, dehydration, and sealing. Images were obtained through scanning device (3DHISTECH, PANNORAMIC SCAN, Hungary) and analyzed through Image J.

Nissl staining

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized, stained with toluidine blue, and sealed with neutral resin. Images were obtained through scanning device. Nissl bodies within neurons appear as dark blue granules. The number of Nissl bodies in the hippocampus was analyzed using Image J.

Cell lines and treatment

The BV2 microglial cells were obtained from the Shanghai Cell Research Center (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in specific medium for BV2 cells (Procell, CM-0493 A, China). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

In the preliminary studies of our research group, after 24 h exposure, Ag2Se QDs at concentrations of 2, 4, and 8 µg/mL were found to induce dose-dependent alterations in inflammation-related indicators in BV2 cells [26]. This simulates the inflammatory response triggered by Ag2Se QDs in in vivo experiments. To further investigate the mechanisms underlying the inflammatory response, combined with the results of the cell viability assays in the present study, 2, 4, and 8 µg/mL were selected as the exposure concentrations of Ag2Se QDs for in vitro experiments. For inhibitor intervention groups, a specific ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, 500 nM, MedChemExpress, HY-100579, USA), an iron chelator Deferoxamine mesylate (DFOM, 5 µM, MedChemExpress, HY-B0988, USA), a specific STING inhibitor C-176 (1 µM, MedChemExpress, HY-112906, USA), an inhibitor of mPTP opening Cyclosporine A (CsA, 1 µM, MedChemExpress, HY-B0579, USA), a mitochondrial ROS scavenger Mito-tempo (50 µM, MedChemExpress, HY-112879, USA) were added to the BV2 cells medium along with 8 µg/mL Ag2Se QDs, respectively and then maintained in the medium for 24 h. The selection of inhibitors was based on cell viability assays (Figure S2) and literature reports [5].

MTT assay

BV2 cells were inoculated into 96-well plate culture. Cells were subjected to different Ag2Se QDs treatments (0–32 µg/mL Ag2Se QDs) for 24 h, then incubated with 10% 3-(4,5)-dimethylthiahiazo (-z-y1)-3,5-di-phenytetrazoliumromide (MTT) for 4 h. The supernatant was discarded and 150 µL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added. The absorbance at 490 nm was detected by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay reader (ELISA reader, Tecan, INFINITE 200 PRO, Switzerland).

Cell uptake of Ag2Se QDs

After exposure to 2, 4 and 8 µg/mL Ag2Se QDs for 24 h, the cellular uptakes of Ag2Se QDs were measured by using a flow cytometry (BD, FACSCabilur, USA). The FL3 channel (excitation wavelength: 488 nm, emission wavelength: 670 nm) can detect the fluorescence of Ag2Se QDs. Side scatter (SSC) parameter represents cellular complexity/granularity, which is closely associated with an increase in cellular uptake of NPs [27]. Therefore, the mean fluorescent intensity (MFI) quantification of FL3 channel and SSC were recorded.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay

LDH levels in the supernatant were determined using the LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Bryotime, C0016, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance at 490 nm was measured using an ELISA reader.

PI staining

The treated cells were incubated with PI working solution (2 µg/mL) in an incubator for 20 min, and then observed by fluorescence microscopy (ZEISS, Vert.A1, Germany).

Detection of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP)

MMP changes in BV2 cells were detected using the MMP assay kit with JC-1 (Beyotime, C2006, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence changes were detected by fluorescence microscopy.

Detection of mtROS

MitoSOX Red (YEASEN, 40778ES50, China) was used to detect mtROS levels in BV2 cells according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Intracellular fluorescence intensity changes were detected using flow cytometry at the FL2 channel and fluorescence microscope.

Detection of mPTP opening

mPTP was detected using an mPTP assay kit (Beyotime, C2009S, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence intensity changes were detected using flow cytometry at the FL1 channel.

Detection of malondialdehyde (MDA)

Intracellular MDA levels were detected using the Lipid Peroxidation MDA Assay Kit (Solarbio, BC0025, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance at 532 nm and 600 nm were measured using ELISA reader.

Detection of Reduced glutathione (GSH)/Oxidized glutathione disulfide (GSSG)

GSH and GSSG was measured using the GSH and GSSG Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0053, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance at 412 nm was measured using an ELISA reader.

Detection of lipid ROS

The content of lipid ROS was measured using C11-BODIPY581/591 (Thermo Fisher, D3861, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence changes were detected by a flow cytometer at the FL1 channel.

Detection of ferrous ion (Fe2+)

Fe2+ content in the cytoplasm was measured using FerroOrange (Dojindo, F374, Japen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence changes were detected by fluorescence microscopy and a flow cytometer at the FL2 channel.

TEM analysis of BV2 cells

After exposure, BV2 cells were immediately fixed in glutaraldehyde. Samples were embedded, sectioned, stained. Then, using a TEM (Hitachi, HT7800, Japan) to observe the samples.

Caspase-1 activity assay

Caspase-1 activity was measured using the Caspase-1 Activity Assay Kit (Beyotime, C1102, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance at 405 nm was measured using ELISA reader.

ELISA

After exposure, the supernatant of BV2 cells was centrifuged at 4 °C, 3000 rpm for 20 min to remove cells. The levels of IL-1β in the supernatants were subsequently measured using an ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Yi Fei Xue Biotechnology, Nanjing, China).

Immunofluorescence

At the end of the exposure, cells were fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized by 0.25% Triton X-100 and closed by 5% goat serum. Diluted primary antibody was added and incubated in a wet box overnight at 4 °C, including anti-LC3B (1:200, Cell signaling Technology, USA), anti-NCOA4 (1:100, immunoway, China), anti-STING (1:200, ABclonal, China), anti-dsDNA (1:100, Santa cruz, USA), anti-TOM20 (1:200, ABclonal, China). After rinsing with PBS, fluorescent secondary antibody corresponding to the primary antibody was added and incubated for 2 h at room temperature, then rinsed again with PBS. Then, cell nuclei were stained with hoechst 33342. Finally, the slides were sealed on slides containing anti-fluorescent quenching agent. Images were captured by a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Imager M2, Germany) or a confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Olympus, FV3000, Japen).

Western blot

Protein expression levels in BV2 cells were detected using immunoblotting. The total protein of BV2 cells was extracted with RIPA (BOSTER, AR0102, China) and PMSF (100:1, BOSTER, AR1179, China). Protein samples were separated by 10% or 12.5% sodium polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) electrophoresis. Subsequently, the proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (PVDF, Millipore, ISEQ00010, USA). Next, non-specific binding sites on the membrane were closed with a sealing solution (5% skimmed milk powder). Then, specific primary antibody was added for incubation, including anti-NLRP3 (1:1000, ABclonal, China), anti-pro-IL-1β (1:1000, ABclonal, China), anti-IL-1β (1:1000, Immunoway, China), anti-LC3B (1:1000, ABclonal, China), anti-P62 (1:1000, ABclonal, China), anti-NCOA4 (1:1000, immunoway, China), anti-FTH1 (1:1000, ABclonal, China), anti-FPN1 (1:1000, Proteintech, China), anti-TFRC (1:1000, ABclonal, China), anti-GPX4 (1:1000, ABclonal, China), anti-SLC7A11 (1:1000, ABclonal, China), anti-ACSL4 (1:1000, ABclonal, China), anti-STING (1:1000, ABclonal, China), anti-COX2 (1:1000, Proteintech, China), anti-GAPDH (1:5000, ABclonal, China). After that, incubation was performed using HRP-conjugated IgG secondary antibody (1:5000, Yi Fei Xue Biotechnology, China). Finally, the expression of the target protein was detected by chemiluminescent reagents (ABclonal, RM00021, China) and chemiluminescent imaging system (Tanon, 5200, China).

qRT-PCR

RNA was extracted from hippocampus and BV2 cells using RNA-easy Isolation Reagent (Vazyme, R701, China). cDNA was synthesized by a Reverse transcription kit (VAZYME, R223-01, China) using a mastercycler gradient PCR system (Eppendorf, USA). Then, amplification of cDNA was conducted using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (VAZYME, Q711-02, China) on the StepOnePlus system (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The qRT-PCR primers are showed in Table S1. The gene relative expression levels were calculated though the 2−ΔΔCt method and were normalized against the internal reference gene GAPDH.

Isolation and detection of cytosolic mtDNA

After treatment, BV2 cells were harvested and split into two equal aliquots. One aliquot was subjected to total cellular DNA extraction with a DNA extraction kit (TIANGEN, DP304, China). The other was used to isolate the cytoplasm via a mitochondrial isolation kit (Beyotime, C3601, China). Briefly, the cells were first homogenized using a homogenizer, and the homogenate was centrifuged at 600 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove nuclei and unbroken cells. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged again at 12,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove mitochondria; the collected supernatant corresponds to the cytoplasm. Cytosolic DNA was then extracted using a DNA extraction kit. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was used to quantify mtDNA in total cellular extracts and cytosolic fractions. mtDNA levels in total extracts served as the normalization control for cytosolic mtDNA, with relative quantification via the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

RNA interference

For NCOA4 knockdown, we used Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11668019, USA) to transfect BV2 cells with 50 nM NCOA4 small-interfering RNA (si-NCOA4, KeyGEN BioTECH). The siRNA sequences were as follows: Sense (5′-3′): CCUUCCAGAAAUGAGCUAATT, antisense (5′-3′): UUAGCUCAUUUCUGGAAGGGC. The knockdown effect of NCOA4 was verified by Western Blot analysis.

Qualitative AOP framework construction

We developed a putative AOP for Ag2Se QDs neurotoxicity based on our experimental data and the AOP Wiki database (https://aopwiki.org).

Data analysis

Data are shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) collected from at least three independent experiments. GraphPad Prism software version 9.5 was used for creating graph and data analysis. Significant differences were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Probability levels of P < 0.05, P < 0.01 and P < 0.001 were considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and #P < 0.05, ##P < 0.01, ###P < 0.001).

Results

Ag2Se QDs triggered NLRP3 inflammasome activation in vivo and in vitro

24 h after mice received a single tail vein injection of Ag2Se QDs, Nissl staining showed that the number of Nissl bodies in the CA1, CA3, and DG regions of the hippocampus slightly decreased in Ag2Se QDs treated groups, indicating a lack of neurons (Fig. 1a, b). Meanwhile, IHC showed a significant increase of IL-1β expression in the CA1, CA3, and DG regions of the hippocampus (Fig. 1c, d). Nod-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome promotes the maturation and release of the inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 in response to cellular perturbations [28]. The qRT-PCR results showed that IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, CD68, CD11b, NLRP3, caspase-1, ASC mRNA expression levels were significantly elevated in hippocampus (Fig. 1e). In addition, in our previous research, the expression levels of NLRP3, ASC, pro-IL-1β, and IL-1β proteins, as well as the activity of caspase-1 were all increased in the hippocampus [26]. These results indicated that Ag2Se QDs promoted microglia inflammatory activation and NLRP3 inflammasome activation in mouse hippocampus.

In vitro, BV2 cells were used as study subjects. MTT assay results showed that 0–32 µg/mL Ag2Se QDs caused a dose-dependent decrease in BV2 cell viability (Fig. 1f). As mentioned before, 2, 4, and 8 µg/mL were used as concentrations for subsequent experiments. The rate of PI-positive cells (Figure S3) and LDH in culture supernatant released by BV2 cells (Fig. 1g) showed a dose-dependent elevation. This suggested that Ag2Se QDs caused cell membrane disruption in BV2 cells. The results from qRT-PCR showed a significant dose-dependent increase in mRNA expressions of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, CD68, CD11b, NLRP3 and caspase-1 (Fig. 1h). The expression levels of NLRP3, pro-IL-1β and IL-1β proteins, caspase-1 activity and IL-1β level in cell supernatant significantly elevated in a dose-dependent manner in our previous study [26]. This suggested that Ag2Se QDs caused microglia inflammatory activation as well as NLRP3 inflammasome activation in vitro. To increase the credibility of the dose-dependent pro-inflammatory effects caused by internalized Ag2Se QDs. We detected the uptake of Ag2Se QDs by BV2 cells using flow cytometry. The MFI (Fig. 1i, j) and SSC (Fig. 1k, l) of BV2 cells showed a dose-dependent increase after Ag2Se QDs treatment for 24 h. This indicated the internalization of Ag2Se QDs in BV2 cells was dose-dependent, which enhances the credibility of the pro-inflammatory effect induced by internalized Ag2Se QDs.

Ag2Se QDs-induced ferroptosis promoted NLRP3 inflammasome activation in BV2 cells

LPO products and iron ions produced during ferroptosis can directly stimulate cells to produce proinflammatory factors [10]. To investigate whether ferroptosis is involved in the inflammatory activation of microglia induced by Ag2Se QDs, we first explored whether ferroptosis occurred in BV2 cells. we exanimated the four widely-reported biomarkers of ferroptosis, including ferroptosis inhibition protein solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) and GPX4, ferroptosis promotion protein cyclooxygenase2 (COX2) and Acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4). The western blot results showed that SLC7A11, ACSL4 and COX2 expression were significantly elevated at 4 and 8 µg/mL and GPX4 expression was significantly decreased in a dose dependent manner (Fig. 2a and b). Then, the levels of intracellular redox state, LPO and iron were measured to verify the occurrence of ferroptosis. Results showed that Ag2Se QDs treatment led to decreased GSH/GSSG (Fig. 2c), increased LPO marker MDA (Fig. 2d) and lipid ROS (Fig. 2e, f), and Fe2+ accumulation (Fig. 2h, i and j). Abnormal alterations in MMP are recognized as markers of mitochondrial dysfunction and early warnings of ferroptosis [29]. Our results showed that MMP significantly decreased as well (Fig. 2g, k).

After BV2 cells were exposed to 2, 4, 8 µg/mL Ag2Se QDs for 24 h, (a, b) ferroptosis-related protein expression and quantitative results; (c) GSH/GSSG ratio; (d) MDA levels; (e, f) representative flow cytometry images for the detection of lipid ROS and quantitative results; (g, k) representative fluorescence images of MMP and quantitative results; (h) representative fluorescent images of intracellular Fe2+ level; (i, j) representative flow cytometry images for the detection of intracellular Fe2+ level and quantitative results. * indicates control group vs. Ag2Se QDs group. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001

We further employed ferroptosis inhibitors to verify the occurrence of ferroptosis. Fer-1, a specific ferroptosis inhibitor, can inhibit LPO. Results showed that Fer-1 inhibited MDA upregulation (Fig. 3a) and cell viability decline (Fig. 3b) in Ag2Se QDs treated group. DFOM, an iron chelator, is another representative ferroptosis inhibitor. DFOM effectively blocked Fe2+ accumulation (Fig. 3c, d and e), cell death (Fig. 3f), MDA level (Fig. 3g) and lipid ROS increase (Fig. 3h, i). Consistently, the decreased protein expression of GPX4 and increased protein expression of ACSL4 and COX2 were significantly alleviated by co-treatment of DFOM (Fig. 3j, k). This indicated that the LPO increase and cell viability decline in BV2 cells induced by Ag2Se QDs were iron-dependent. All the above results further proved that Ag2Se QDs exposure triggered ferroptosis in BV2 cells. It is worth noting that, following treatment with ferroptosis-related inhibitors, Fer-1 restored the viability of BV2 cells from 80.2% to 94.4%, while DFOM increased the viability of BV2 cells from 77.9% to 93.5%. Both of them markedly reversed the decline in cell viability caused by Ag2Se QDs. Consequently, we propose that ferroptosis represents the major mode of BV2 cell death triggered by Ag2Se QDs.

To confirm whether Ag2Se QDs-induced ferroptosis are related to the inflammation response, we investigated alterations in the NLRP3 inflammatory pathway in BV2 cells in the presence of Fer-1 or DFOM. Results showed that Fer-1 and DFOM attenuated Ag2Se QDs-induced elevated NLRP3, pro-IL-1β, and IL-1β protein expression (Figure S4a, b and e, f), caspase-1 activity (Figure S4c, g), and IL-1β secretion (Figure S4d, h). These data indicated that ferroptosis promoted NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy was responsible for Ag2Se QDs-induced ferroptosis

Intracellular iron homeostasis depends on the balance between iron uptake, export and storage [30]. Therefore, we analyzed changes in the expression levels of iron-regulated proteins, including transferrin receptor protein 1 (TFRC, iron input), FTH1 (iron storage) and ferroportin-1 (FPN1, iron output). As shown in Fig. 4a, b, compared with the control group, the expressions of TFRC and FPN1 remain unchanged. The expression of FTH1 significantly decreased. This indicated that the intracellular Fe2+ overload may be the result of reduced iron storage. Recent study identified NCOA4 mediated excessive autophagic degradation of FTH1, leading to increased intracellular iron content. Therefore, we investigated whether ferritinophagy is activated during Ag2Se QDs induced ferroptosis of BV2 cells. The western blot results showed that Ag2Se QDs treatment upregulated NCOA4, LC3B-II expression and downregulated P62 expression (Fig. 4c, d). The immunofluorescence images showed increased co-localization of NCOA4 and LC3B (Fig. 4e), demonstrating increased colocalization of NCOA4 with autophagosomes. The TEM results showed that 8 µg/mL Ag2Se QDs treatment increased the number of autolysosomes in BV2 cells (Fig. 4f). These results suggested that NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy was activated accompanying normal activated autophagic flux. Autophagy is dynamic and develops over time. It is worth exploring the trend of FTH1 degradation. We examined the protein levels of FTH1, NCOA4, LC3B and p62 in BV2 cells treated with 8 µg/mL Ag2Se QDs for 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h (Fig. 4g, h). The results showed that the expression of LC3B-Ⅱ and P62 first increased and then decreased. The expression of LC3B-Ⅱ reached the highest level at 24 h and P62 at 6 h. The FTH1 expression decreased in a time-dependent manner, accompanied by the up-regulation of NCOA4 from 0 to 24 h. From 24 to 48 h, the trends of NCOA4 and FTH1 were consistent. We speculated that there may be other NCOA4-independent FTH1 degradation pathways in BV2 cells after 24 h. Contrary to the tendency of FTH1, the level of intracellular Fe2+ significantly increased over time (Fig. 4i). The above results preliminarily suggested that Ag2Se QDs activated NCOA4-mediated FTH1 degradation in a dose- and time- dependent manner in BV2 cells.

mtDNA leakage-triggered STING activation was essential for ferritinophagy mediated ferroptosis

Emerging evidence has revealed that STING is a key regulator of autophagy and cell death, such as apoptosis, necroptosis and ferroptosis [31]. Interestingly, some studies have shown STING promoted ferroptosis through NCOA4-dependent ferritinophagy [22, 32]. In our study, the results of immunofluorescence and western blot show that Ag2Se QDs notably enhanced the STING protein level in BV2 cells (Fig. 6a, b and c). To evaluate the effect of STING on ferritinophagy mediated ferroptosis, we used the specific inhibitor C-176 to suppress STING expression (Fig. 6d, e and S6). We found the C-176 reversed 8 µg/mL Ag2Se QDs-induced NCOA4-dependent FHT1 degradation (Fig. 6d, e), increased co-localization of NCOA4 with LC3B (Fig. 6f) and excessive Fe2+ (Fig. 6g, h and i). Simultaneously, C-176 alleviated the 8 µg/mL Ag2Se QDs-induced elevation of lipid ROS (Fig. 6j, k), MDA (Fig. 6l) and cell death (Fig. 6m). Furthermore, decreased GPX4 protein expression and increased COX2 and ACSL4 protein expression were all mitigated by C-176 (Fig. 6n, o). Therefore, the STING activation caused the occurrence of NCOA4-dependent ferritinophagy and subsequent ferroptosis in BV2 cells. In fact, STING signaling pathway is a cytosolic double stranded DNA (dsDNA) sensing system. Considering previously, we detected mitochondrial stress (decreased MMP). We hypothesized that dsDNA leakage from damaged mitochondria activated the STING signaling. We first examined the presence of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) in the cytosol. Immunofluorescence images showed that a large amount of dsDNA was released into the microglial cytoplasm after Ag2Se QDs treatment (Figure S7a). To further verify the leakage of mtDNA, we performed qPCR to detect mtDNA (Dloop1, Dloop2 and Dloop3) in the cytosolic fractions. The results showed that Ag2Se QDs exposure induced mtDNA increase in the cytosol (Figure S7b). These results suggested that STING signaling was activated by cytosolic mtDNA in Ag2Se QDs treated BV2 cells.

mPTP opening induced mtDNA cytosolic leakage and subsequent STING-NCOA4 axis-dependent ferroptosis

mPTP opening leads to a disruption of the electrochemical gradient between the inner and outer mitochondrial membranes and a decrease in the MMP [33]. mtDNA can be released from mitochondria into the cytoplasm via open mPTP. In this study, Ag2Se QDs promoted mPTP opening in BV2 cells (Fig. 7a, b). To investigate the role of the mPTP opening in the mtDNA leakage and STING signaling activation, we used the mPTP inhibitor CSA (Fig. 7c, d). The experimental results showed that CSA treatment reduced Ag2Se QDs-triggered mtDNA cytosolic escape (Fig. 7e, f) and STING activation (Fig. 7g, h and i). This indicated that mtDNA leaked into the cytoplasm through the opened mPTP. In addition, CSA inhibited NCOA4-dependent FHT1 degradation (Fig. 7h, i), ameliorated increased intracellular Fe2+ (Figure S8a, b and c), lipid ROS (Figure S8d, e), MDA (Figure S8f), and cell death (Figure S8g) in Ag2Se QDs treated BV2 cells. Meanwhile, CSA increased GPX4 protein expression and decreased COX2 and ACSL4 protein expression in Ag2Se QDs treated BV2 cells (Figure S8h, i). These data suggested that mPTP opening induced mtDNA cytosolic leakage and subsequent STING-NCOA4 axis-dependent ferroptosis.

Excessive mtROS induced mPTP opening and subsequent mtDNA-STING-NCOA4 axis-dependent ferroptosis

Previous studies have reported mtROS induced mPTP opening and excessive accumulation of mtDNA in the cytoplasm [34]. We found that Ag2Se QDs significantly increased the level of mtROS in BV2 cells (Fig. 8a, b and c). To know whether mtROS triggered the mPTP opening, we examined the effects of mtROS inhibitors Mito-tempo (Fig. 8d, e). The results showed that Mito-tempo treatment reduced mPTP opening in Ag2Se QDs treated BV2 cells (Fig. 8f, g), suggesting that mtROS induced mPTP opening. Mito-tempo attenuated Ag2Se QDs induced mtDNA release (Fig. 8h, i) and STING activation (Fig. 8j, k and l). Moreover, Mito-tempo inhibited the Ag2Se QDs treatment induced NCOA4-mediated FTH1 degradation (Fig. 8k, l), intracellular Fe2+ accumulation (Figure S9a, b and c), increased lipid ROS (Figure S9d, e), MDA content (Figure S9f) and cell death (Figure S9g). Additionally, decreased GPX4 and increased COX2 and ACSL4 were attenuated by Mito-tempo (Figure S9h, i). These results suggested Ag2Se QDs-caused excess mtROS triggered the opening of mPTP, which induced mtDNA-STING-NCOA4 axis-dependent ferroptosis.

Construction of an AOP framework for Ag2Se QDs-induced neurotoxicity

Based on the above results and the AOP Wiki database, we proposed an AOP hypothesis to elucidate the neurotoxic effects of Ag2Se QDs (Fig. 9): altered signaling pathways (mtROS-mtPTP-mtDNA-STING axis) was considered as the MIE, followed by dysfunctional autophagy (NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy), ferroptosis and secretion of proinflammatory mediators in microglia, CNS neural cell death and neurodegeneration (KE), ultimately leading to altered hippocampal physiology (AO). It provides a comprehensive model for understanding the process from molecular disruption to observable adverse outcomes. At the same time, it emphasizes the importance of identifying the event that cause mitochondrial damage in the future. This will help to fundamentally avoid the health risks of Ag2Se QDs.

Discussion

Ag2Se QDs, with their excellent optical properties and low biotoxicity, hold great promise for bioimaging applications. Conducting toxicity risk assessments and developing response strategies of Ag2Se QDs can help promote their biological applications. In this study, we have delved into the neurotoxicity mechanisms of Ag2Se QDs. The present study demonstrated that Ag2Se QDs induced NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy dependent ferroptosis via mtROS-mPTP-mtDNA-STING axis, which subsequently promoted the NLPR3 inflammasome activation in microglia. Based on the research findings, we constructed a potential AOP framework to elucidate the neurotoxic effects of Ag2Se QDs. This facilitates the identification of knowledge gaps, thereby guiding the optimization of subsequent toxicity testing strategies, while providing potential therapeutic targets for mitigating neural injury.

Microglia are the primary immune cells in the central nervous system. It is well documented that the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in microglia and the subsequent release of pro-inflammatory cytokines significantly contribute to the progression of neurodegenerative diseases [35]. The activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome requires two steps: Signal 1 (priming step): Inflammatory stimuli (e.g., TLR4 agonists) induce NF-κB -mediated expression of NLRP3 and pro-IL-1β; signal 2 (activation step): pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) promote the assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome and caspase-1-mediated secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 and pyroptosis [36]. Interestingly, recent studies have shown that nanoparticles are activators of the NLRP3 inflammasome [37, 38]. In the present study, Ag2Se QDs were internalized into the BV2 cells, potentially via macropinocytosis, clathrin and caveolin mediated endocytosis [39]. These Ag2Se QDs subsequently triggered NLPR3 inflammasome activation, thereby potentially exacerbating hippocampal neuronal damage. This demonstrates that Ag2Se QDs may contribute to the onset and progression of neurodegenerative diseases, and these findings collectively represent potential adverse outcomes at the organismal level.

Ferroptosis is a non-apoptotic cell death mechanism characterized by iron-dependent membrane LPO and is widely involved in the neuroinflammation [40]. Iron is one of the essential trace elements in the human body, involved in oxygen transport, DNA biosynthesis and ATP production. However, excess iron can generate highly reactive hydroxyl radicals via the Fenton reaction and increase the activity of iron-containing enzymes like lipoxygenases (LOXs), leading to the accumulation of LPO products such as MDA and 4-HNE [41]. ACSL4 plays a key role in promoting ferroptosis by doping long-chain PUFAs (main substrates of LPO) into cellular phospholipids [42]. COX2 can oxidize lysophospholipids and is thus recognized as a biomarker of ferroptosis [43]. The system xc−-GSH-GPX4 axis is canonical ferroptosis-controlling axis. Cystine is translocated into cells via the system xc− cystine-glutamate antiporter and is reduced to cysteine for GSH synthesis. using GSH as a cofactor, GPX4 reduce toxic phospholipid hydroperoxides (PLOOHs, a form of lipid-based ROS) to corresponding non-toxic alcohols (PLOHs) [44]. Therefore, SLC7A11 (a catalytic subunit of system xc−), GSH and GPX4 are key regulators that protect cells from LPO. In this study, Ag2Se QDs exposure caused iron overload, LPO and xc−-GSH-GPX4 axis breakdown. Both Fer-1 (LPO inhibitor) and DFOM (iron chelator) obviously alleviated Ag2Se QDs-induced LPO and cell death. Based on the above results, we conclude that ferroptosis is an important contributor to Ag2Se QDs-induced BV2 cell death. Consistent with our study, nanomaterials including Ag NPs [14], Fe3O4 NPs [15] and GQDs [45] have been successively reported to induce ferroptosis in macrophages. This indicates that ferroptosis may be a general mechanism underlying nanomaterial-induced cytotoxicity. In our previous study, Ag2Se QDs did not alter caspase-3 expression (a key mediator of intrinsic and extrinsic apoptosis) in BV2 cells [5]. Similarly, TUNEL-positive cells were not detected in the mouse hippocampus in our pre-experiment. Despite this, silver-based nanomaterials often cause apoptosis-mediated cell death. Thus, following exposure to silver-based nanomaterials, analyzing apoptosis-related indicators and pathways remains critical to strengthen the mechanistic understanding of cell fate decisions. In addition, NLRP3 inflammasome activation is also associated with pyroptosis. Determining whether pyroptosis is involved in Ag2Se QD-induced BV2 cell death will be pivotal for achieving a comprehensive understanding of the cell death modalities involved.

We found that Fer-1 and DFOM remarkably inhibited NLRP3 inflammasome activation, suggesting that Ag2Se QDs-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation is an ferroptosis-dependent process. Similarly, it has been reported that deferoxamine attenuates ROS/NF-κB pathway-mediated NLRP3 inflammasome activation by reducing iron accumulation in microglial after traumatic brain injury [46]. Ferroptosis regulators are tightly linked to the inflammatory response of microglia. For instance, GPX4 knockout mice exhibit higher microglia M1 polarization and pro-inflammatory cytokines production [47]. The upregulation of divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1, involvement in iron absorption) on microglia membranes leads to iron overload and M1 polarization [48]. ACSL4 has been shown to promote LPS-induced inflammation in microglia by regulating vestigial-like family member 4 (VGLL4) expression and lipid metabolism [49]. This study, together with previous findings, collectively indicates that ferroptosis-related iron homeostasis, redox state and LPO play a crucial role in regulating phenotypic changes of microglia [50]. The NLRP3 inflammasome may in turn be a key step in ferroptosis [41]. Therefore, the inter-regulatory mechanism at the molecular level between Ag2Se QDs-induced ferroptosis and NLRP3 inflammasome activation need to be further investigated in the future.

Cellular iron can affect several biological processes through mitochondrial respiration, gene regulation and DNA synthesis or repair. Iron homeostasis is controlled by highly orchestrated regulatory systems [17]. Fe3+ is imported into cells through the membrane protein TFRC, excess iron can be stored in ferritin or released into the extracellular space by FPN1 [51]. In our study, TFRC and FPN1 expression was not altered and FTH1 (subunit of ferritin) expression was decreased. This suggested that Ag2Se QDs-induced iron overload in the BV2 cells is likely due to decreased iron storage. Ferritinophagy maintains intracellular iron homeostasis by promoting ferritin degradation and iron recycling. In this process, NCOA4 functions as a selective autophagy receptor that specifically recognizes FTH1 and mediates the translocation of ferritin to lysosomes for degradation, ultimately releasing iron [52]. However, excessive ferritinophagy leads to iron overload. Recent studies have demonstrated the promoting effect of ferritinophagy on ferroptosis [53, 54]. In this study, Ag2Se QDs induced overactivated NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy in a dose-and time-dependent manner, which resulted in cellular iron overload and ferroptosis. Consistent with our results, ferritinophagy-dependent ferroptosis participates in silica NPs and CdTe QDs induced liver injury [20, 55], and ZnO NPs induced vascular endothelial cells damage [56]. Our findings extend the role of NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy in nanoparticle-induced neuroinflammation.

In addition to being an innate immune pathway, recent studies have established STING as a double-edged sword in autophagy regulation [57]: it directly activates autophagosome biogenesis by using the ERGIC as a membrane source for LC3 lipidation [58], while also inhibiting energy stress-induced autophagy via physical interaction with Syntaxin-17 (STX17) [59]. In the context of Ag2Se QDs exposure, we observed elevated STING expression, which specifically drove iron accumulation and subsequent ferroptosis via NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy. We speculate that the following potential interaction modes exist between STING and NCOA4 within our model. First, direct protein-protein interaction. Interaction between the CDN-binding domain (CBD) of STING and the coiled-coil (CC) domain of NCOA4 enhances the binding of NCOA4 and ferritin and following ferritinophagy-mediated ferroptosis [22]. Wu et al.. identified critical residues (E316, S322 and Q237) within STING’s CBD domain that are essential for this interaction with NCOA4’s CC domain [32]. Second, the transcriptional regulation. STING upregulates gene transcription and enhances protein expression of NCOA4 [60]. While NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy in turn modulates STING activation [61]. These two molecules form a positive feedback loop, thereby further amplifying iron ion release. Collectively, these multi-dimensional molecular interactions position STING as a key upstream regulator of iron metabolism disorders and ferroptosis. Conventionally, STING is primarily recognized as a mediator of type I interferon responses. This study underscores that in nanomaterial models, STING mediates inflammatory responses through regulating iron metabolism and ferroptosis.

STING signaling could be activated by pathogens- and host-derived dsDNA in the cytoplasm, which need cGAS sense cytosolic dsDNA to produce a secondary messenger cGAMP [62]. Mitochondrion is an organelle with its own genome. Due to the lack of histone protection and damage repair mechanisms, mtDNA is highly sensitive to exogenous compound [25]. mtDNA in cytoplasm is one of the triggers for the STING signaling activation. The opened mPTP permits the transport of fragmented mtDNA that is under 700 bp in length and exhibits a single-stranded, double-stranded, or linear configuration [63]. Cytosolic mtDNA released through opened mPTP has been shown to trigger STING activation. e.g., in tert-butylhydroperoxide treated nucleus pulposus cells [33], sevoflurane treated microglia [6] and conduritol-β-epoxide and glucosylceramides treated microglia [34]. Consistent with the above results, our current study observed leakage of mtDNA into the cytoplasm via mPTP, thereby activating the STING signaling pathway. The molecular nature of the mPTP has not been clarified. A group of proteins have been proposed to responsible for the formation of the mPTP, for instance clophilin D (Cyp-D), adenine nucleotide transporter and mitochondrial inorganic phosphate carrier [64]. CSA used in this study inhibited CypD. It is not clear whether other components of mPTP are involved in mtDNA leakage. Futhermore, mDNA leakage may not be completely dependent on mPTP on mitochondrial inner membrane, BAX/BAK and VDAC oligomers formed pores on mitochondrial outer membrane also mediated mtDNA leakage [65]. It has been reported that oxidative stress triggers mPTP opening [66]. Mitochondria are a significant source of ROS within the cell. We observed that mPTP opening was inhibited by mtROS inhibitor Mito-tempo. This may be due to the fact that mtROS potentiate calcium-induced mPTP opening [67], which need further validation. It should be noted that mtDNA encodes 13 essential subunits of the electron transport chain (ETC) complexes, which are associated with the production of ROS and energy generation. Therefore, it remains unclear whether mtDNA cytosolic leakage induces ETC defects, thereby further amplifying Ag2Se QDs-induced mitochondrial damage and subsequent ferroptosis.

In our previous studies, we found that Ag2Se QDs induce time- and dose-dependent mitophagy in microglia, which exerts a suppressive effect on inflammatory responses [26]. According to the western blot results, the peak level of mitophagy occurs at 12 h post-exposure, while that of ferritinophagy is observed at 24 h post-exposure. Regarding the inflammatory response investigated in this study, the pro-inflammatory effect of ferritinophagy outweighs the anti-inflammatory effect of mitophagy after treatment with 8 µg/mL Ag₂Se QDs for 24 h, thereby yielding an inflammatory phenotype. The dynamic balance between ferritinophagy and mitophagy in microglia determines the ultimate intensity of the inflammatory response. This strongly confirms that the effect of autophagy in nanomaterial toxicity depends on its subtypes and time context. Meanwhile, in this study, it is not ruled out that other types of autophagy may also play a role in the inflammatory response.

In the Ag2Se QDs-induced neurotoxicity AOP framework constructed in this study, the core hub role of mitochondria is highly consistent with conclusions from previous studies on nanomaterial toxicity. The literature clearly demonstrates that nanomaterials including ZnO NPs, Ag NPs and CdTe QDs selectively target mitochondria as their primary site of action, inducing dysfunction through mechanisms including mtROS overproduction, disruption of MMP, and impairment of mitochondrial cristae structure [25]. This is consistent with “Ag2Se QDs-induced mtROS burst, mPTP opening and mtDNA leakage” in our AOP. From the perspective of practical value, the development of toxicity mitigation strategies can be precisely implemented based on key nodes in the AOP (e.g., mitochondria damage). This not only validates the causal rationality of the AOP but also provides a targeted direction for reducing nanomaterial hazards.

The AOP framework established in this study directly identifies multiple actionable pharmacological targets. Notably, the core application value of Ag2Se QDs lies in their excellent bioimaging performance, and thus, toxicity mitigation strategies must be premised on not compromising their imaging properties. Although direct verification of the toxicity mitigation and imaging performance was not conducted in vivo in this study. Our data provide an optimistic theoretical basis. The bioimaging potential of Ag2Se QDs derives from their unique physicochemical properties, which are intrinsic attributes of Ag2Se QDs. These AOP node-based targeted regulatory strategies are only directed at the downstream toxic signaling pathways induced by Ag2Se QDs, rather than altering the physicochemical properties of Ag2Se QDs. Therefore, theoretically, the intervention at these nodes will not affect the bioimaging efficacy of Ag2Se QDs.

To achieve the ultimate goal of maximizing imaging efficacy and minimizing neurotoxicity, we have proposed administration strategies tailored to different application scenarios. (1) The application of Ag2Se QDs in tumor imaging typically involves short-term imaging requirements (e.g., intraoperative imaging, single in vivo imaging), whereas neuroinflammation represents a delayed toxicity. Given this application scenario, there is no need for Ag2Se QDs to remain in the body for an extended period after imaging, with the key focus being on blocking subsequent neuroinflammation. We propose a strategy termed “post-imaging drug administration”, in which no inhibitors interfere with Ag2Se QDs during the imaging phase, and post-imaging inhibitors can block the transduction of the AOP. (2) For long-term tracing scenarios, where Ag2Se QDs remain in the body for an extended duration, it is necessary to balance the sustainability of imaging and the accumulation of toxicity. We propose a strategy termed “co-administration of low-dose Ag2Se QDs and drug”. Low-dose Ag2Se QDs can meet the basic requirements for imaging, while the combined use of inhibitors can counteract the cumulative toxicity induced by low-dose Ag2Se QDs. The ideal approach is to directly conjugate or load inhibitors onto the surface of Ag2Se QDs, thereby constructing a theragnostic nanoplatform that enables the synchronous coordination of imaging and detoxification. This strategy can reduce the off-target effects associated with systemic administration and enhance the local mitigation efficacy. Subsequent in vivo experiments are still required to verify the effectiveness of the proposed strategies, and thus advance the clinical translation of Ag2Se QDs.

Conclusion

In summary, at the mouse imaging dose and double imaging dose, Ag2Se QDs induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation in microglia and neuronal damage in the hippocampus. Our study showed the crucial involvement of the ferroptosis in NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Mechanistically, we found that mtROS generation-induced mPTP opening led mtDNA leakage into the cytosol, which triggered the activation of STING. STING-regulated NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy contributed ferroptosis in microglia. Based on the experimental data, we constructed the AOP framework of Ag2Se QDs neurotoxicity. The AOP framework supports that mitochondrial damage plays a key role in the neurotoxicity of Ag2Se QDs. This study provides new insights into the neurotoxicity of Ag2Se QDs and offers guidance for developing toxicity mitigation strategies.

This study has the following limitations: (1) The present study focuses on acute neurotoxicity, while the long-term toxic effects of Ag2Se QDs (e.g., chronic neuroinflammation and cognitive impairment) remain unclear. (2) Although we proposed potential toxicity mitigation strategies based on key nodes of the AOP framework, the lack of direct in vivo validation of these strategies limits their direct translational value. (3) This study has not yet been able to distinguish definitively whether the observed neurotoxicity stems primarily from the intrinsic effects of the Ag2Se QDs themselves, or from the silver ions and selenium ions that they release. This is a key issue when it comes to assessing the biosafety of such nanomaterials.

In light of the aforementioned findings and limitations, future research can be expanded in the following aspects. (1) Conduct long-term experiments (e.g., 1–3 months) to evaluate the chronic neurotoxic effects of Ag2Se QDs, and clarify the toxicity accumulation pattern under long-term condition. (2) Conduct in vivo verification of the proposed AOP node-based intervention strategies; optimize the administration dosage, timing and route of drug and Ag2Se QDs; balance the imaging efficacy and the toxicity mitigation effect. (3) Design specific control group to distinguish the sources of toxicity, including simulating silver ions effects using AgNO3; employing specific metal ion chelators; and fabricating surface-coated or functionally modified Ag2Se QDs to inhibit ion release.
