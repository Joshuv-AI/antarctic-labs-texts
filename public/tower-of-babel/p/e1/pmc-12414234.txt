Pragmatic trial evaluating a randomly allocated Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program outreach and enrolment assistance programme in Michigan: study protocol
Authors: Laura Samuel, Elizabeth A Stuart, Yifan Liu, Sarah L Szanton, Alexis L Clark, Marta Miguel, Matthew Schneider, Alymamah Mashrah, Mitch Laretz, Bonnielin Swenor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLow-income adults with disabilities experience disproportionately high rates of food insecurity and preventable healthcare utilisation. The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) can reduce food insecurity and improve health, but there are accessibility gaps in the SNAP enrolment process. Existing outreach and enrolment assistance programmes have been shown to boost SNAP enrolment, but their health effects are understudied. This study estimates the effects of a SNAP outreach and enrolment assistance programme on health outcomes among low-income adults with disabilities.Methods and analysisThe study pragmatically evaluates a programme that provided outreach and SNAP enrolment assistance for low-income households. The study leverages a random process that selected households for one of two types of outreach, including an information-only arm and an information plus enrolment assistance arm, which formed a control group by default. The study will estimate the effect of this programme among low-income adults with disabilities using Medicaid and SNAP administrative data. Study outcomes include emergency department, hospital and long-term nursing home utilisation. SNAP enrolment and benefit amounts are secondary study outcomes and will be tested as a mediating mechanism of action. The study will test effect heterogeneity based on race, ethnicity, age and chronic conditions.Ethics and disseminationThe study, which relies on deidentified data, was determined to be exempt as human subjects research by the Institutional Review Boards at Johns Hopkins Medicine and the Michigan Department of Health and Human Services. The study is being conducted in ongoing consultation with an Advisory Group of experts in food advocacy and disability advocacy. In addition to disseminating findings in peer-reviewed publications, findings will be disseminated to state decision-makers and the community in partnership with an advisory group.

Introduction

Over 70 million Americans have a disability,1 of whom 27% experience poverty,2 contributing to food insecurity rates that are triple that of the general population (30% vs 10%).3 4 Although enrolment in the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) can reduce food insecurity by as much as 30%,5 the enrolment process is cumbersome and has barriers to accessibility for disabled people.6 Many states understand the importance of SNAP access and are implementing cross-enrolment interventions, which are interventions that identify people who are likely eligible for SNAP based on their income documented by other programmes (eg, Medicaid) and then provide SNAP outreach and enrolment assistance. Although this type of programme has been shown to boost SNAP enrolment in a randomised study,7 the health effects of those efforts are understudied.

Disparities in food insecurity are notable because food-insecure adults have worse outcomes than their peers, including a higher risk of hypertension, high cholesterol,8 diabetes,9 and functional limitations,10 11 greater healthcare expenditures12 and earlier mortality.10 There is also evidence of spikes in rates of hypoglycaemia hospitalisations13 and emergency department visits among older adults14 or those with hypertension15 at the end of the month when funds such as benefits tend to run low, which is likely due to monthly fluctuations in food insecurity.1622 Conversely, there is evidence that better access to SNAP is associated with less healthcare utilisation. As examples, SNAP participants have less hospital and nursing home utilisation2325 and evidence of better chronic condition management26 27 than their peers, and enrolling in SNAP may reduce hospital utilisation.28 29 Further, higher SNAP benefit amounts are associated with less healthcare utilisation30 31 and attenuation of monthly health fluctuations.15 18 32 Therefore, interventions that improve SNAP access, such as cross-enrolment assistance programmes, may improve health outcomes for low-income adults with disabilities, but further rigorous evaluation is needed.

In addition, intersecting structural inequities are understudied; both disability and food insecurity are relatively more common among disabled people who are Black or Hispanic than those who are White, and higher among older than younger adults.10 33 34 However, there are gaps within these intersectional groups with disproportionately low SNAP utilisation, who are also groups that may benefit the most from SNAP enrolment assistance. Notably, people with incomes near the poverty limit (who are eligible for SNAP) as well as people with disabilities and those racialised as Black or Hispanic are all identified as health disparity groups by the National Institute of Health, highlighting the importance and urgency of addressing intersectional disparities across multiple dimensions of social adversity.35

This paper outlines the methods for a study that seeks to (1) evaluate the impact of a SNAP cross-enrolment intervention, which provides outreach and enrolment assistance, on health and healthcare utilisation and SNAP utilisation among low-income adults with disabilities, (2) examine SNAP enrolment as a mediator of the programme effects on health and healthcare utilisation and (3) determine if the impacts of a cross-benefit enrolment intervention differ based on race/ethnicity, age and presence of ambulatory care-sensitive conditions among adults with disabilities.

Methods and analysis

Setting

The proposed study will be conducted among Medicaid beneficiaries in Michigan. The Michigan Medicaid programme was expanded after the Affordable Care Act to allow individuals with incomes ≤133% of the federal poverty limit to enrol. Therefore, the majority of Michigan Medicaid beneficiaries should be eligible for the SNAP programme. Notably, individuals with incomes ≤130% of poverty have food insecurity rates that are triple those of the general US population (33% vs 10.5%),36 suggesting that many Medicaid beneficiaries who are not yet participating in SNAP would likely benefit from SNAP enrolment. Benefits Data Trust (BDT), a non-profit organisation, partnered with the Michigan Department of Health and Human Services (MDHHS) to start providing SNAP outreach and enrolment assistance in 2021 to community-dwelling adult Medicaid beneficiaries who were not yet enrolled in SNAP. The study has been conducted in partnership with MDHHS and BDT and with advisory committee guidance from the disability and food assistance communities.

Pragmatic approach leveraging naturally occurring random allocation

The current study leverages existing administrative data to evaluate an assistance programme. The study also takes advantage of the programme’s random selection of households for one of two types of outreach, while a third equivalent group will receive outreach at a later date, forming a control group. Outreach arms include an information-only arm and an information plus SNAP application assistance arm. The study is pragmatic across multiple domains based on the PRagmatic Explanatory Continuum Indicator Summary-237 because it is embedded within a programme that was provided by BDT. Therefore, the study did not change the setting, organisation or any aspect of the programme, including the recruitment, delivery or adherence practices. Additionally, outcomes will be assessed retrospectively using existing administrative data so that follow-up practices are not changed.

Sample

The effectiveness of the SNAP cross-enrolment intervention will be estimated among Medicaid beneficiary adults with disabilities, defined for this study as individuals who meet any of the following four criteria: (a) receive disability benefits (eg, Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) or, if younger than age 65, Supplemental Security Income (SSI)); (b) are Medicaid eligible due to disability or blindness; (c) are eligible for home and community-based services or (d) are Adult Home Help recipients who need assistance with Activities of Daily Living.

SNAP cross-enrolment programme

A four-step approach was used to conduct the newly launched SNAP cross-enrolment intervention (figure 1).

Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) cross-enrolment intervention programme process map.

Step 1: linking SNAP and Medicaid data

MDHHS linked SNAP enrolment data with Medicaid enrolment data in 2021. This linkage was used to generate an outreach list containing the names and contact information of 1 204 244 Medicaid beneficiaries who were not enrolled in SNAP between 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2021. The outreach list was then sent to BDT for SNAP outreach and enrolment assistance in January 2022.

Step 2: identifying SNAP-eligible households for BDT outreach

BDT identified individuals on the outreach list who were likely eligible for SNAP (ie, community-dwelling adults aged 18 and older), which formed the group eligible for the intervention (n=1 005 916) and grouped individuals who shared the same last name and address together into 884 617 households. The oldest person in the household group was designated as the contact person for outreach activities (figure 1).

Step 3: naturally random outreach process

BDT began conducting outreach in February 2022 (figure 2). During each week of outreach activities, among households not yet selected for outreach, BDT randomly selected households to receive one of two outreach conditions (figure 1). The first outreach condition was an information-only arm; during each week of outreach, BDT randomly selected, on average, 6144 households to receive accessible information mailed to their address about how to apply for SNAP on their own. The second outreach condition was the information plus application assistance arm; during each week of outreach, BDT randomly selected, on average, 2858 households to receive a mailed offer of SNAP application assistance which included a phone number for BDT’s Michigan Benefits call centre number in addition to the information about how to enrol in SNAP on their own. To each of these outreach groups, BDT sent text message reminders at 2 weeks, 4 weeks and 6 weeks after outreach. The information-only arm was stopped in February 2023. All outreach activities for this list were paused between September 2023 and May 2024 because the outreach approach temporarily shifted to focus on newly eligible individuals. Although outreach for this list resumed briefly during May and June of 2024, the outreach was no longer randomised and was minimal, reaching only 1.5% of the households that were included in the list of intended outreach recipients. Thus, the study will not examine outreach occurring after September 2023. Most importantly, BDT ceased operations in August 2024, ending all outreach. Overall, this process formed a control arm by default, with essentially a set of repeated randomised trials each week. By the time outreach paused on 22 September 2023, 22% of all people on the outreach list were provided with information plus assistance (n=220 810), 26% received information only (n=260 540), and 28% remained on the list without receiving outreach (ie, control, (n=280 490) (figures2 3). Notably, 24% of the people on the original outreach list (n=244 076) were removed from the outreach list before receiving any outreach due to two reasons. First, some people were removed from the outreach list because they enrolled in SNAP, and those people will be included in our study. Second, some people were removed from the outreach list because they were no longer enrolled in Michigan’s Medicaid programme, and these people will remain in our control group until their Medicaid end date.

Step 4: SNAP enrolment assistance

Individuals who were offered SNAP enrolment assistance could call or have a personal representative/assistant call the BDT hotline to assess their eligibility for public benefit programmes including SNAP. For those who were likely eligible, a public benefits specialist would screen the caller for eligibility, complete the application, submit the application on their behalf and advise the caller on the supporting documentation required for SNAP applications. All households could enrol in SNAP on their own at any time.

Evaluation plan based on a target trial emulation framework

Based on this four-step process, the SNAP cross-enrolment intervention will be evaluated by leveraging the random selection for treatment that was naturally embedded in the programme and by applying principles of the target trial emulation framework to estimate treatment effects.38 Therefore, components of the study protocol will be designed and articulated in a way to emulate an ideal trial that could theoretically have been conducted among low-income adults with disabilities (table 1). Specifically, based on study eligibility criteria, the study will not include everyone on the outreach list but will only include those whom BDT determined to be eligible for SNAP and with disabilities. Additionally, the study will leverage the weekly random selection to treatment by treating the study as a repeated series of trials wherein eligible participants are randomised each week, with baseline data collected for 2 years before the randomisation and outcomes assessed for 2 years after randomisation. In this way, time is centred at the time of random selection to treatment (T0) for each trial. To address the risk of immortal time bias, which could occur when defining treatment at T0 based on future treatment,39 all participants who have not yet received outreach each week will be included in the control group, and any who later receive outreach will be administratively censored at the time of their outreach. This trial emulation approach ensures a clear definition of the eligible population, the treatment and control conditions, ensures temporal ordering of covariates, treatment assignment and outcome and avoids inadvertent bias by defining the cohorts based only on baseline (pretreatment) factors and not outcomes.

Outcome measures

Outcomes selected for this study are important predictors of adverse outcomes in acute care settings, total cost of care and mortality.40 41 Importantly, hospitalisations and emergency department visits for ambulatory care-sensitive conditions are typically considered to be preventable because they could have been treated with less costly and timely care in ambulatory care settings rather than the hospital setting.40 42 43 Therefore, hospital and emergency department visits for ambulatory care-sensitive conditions are considered indicators of the quality of care and services accessible to individuals in the community. Outcomes are captured monthly to address privacy concerns.

Because outcomes are tracked monthly, treatment status information will be pooled across months for monthly stacked trials to compare those treated within a month to those not yet treated. Primary study outcomes will be tracked for 2 years after T0 for each monthly trial and include counts of emergency department visits and inpatient hospitalisations. Corresponding baseline versions of the outcomes (covariates) will be defined using 2 years of data before T0. Monthly counts will be generated by first rolling up claims showing overlapping or contiguous dates of service into one healthcare encounter, as recommended.44 Then, monthly counts of emergency department visits and hospitalisations will be generated, and length of stay for each hospitalisation will be calculated based on the first date of the encounter.7 44 The count of emergency department visits will include those that result in inpatient hospital admissions and those that end in discharge to home.45

Secondary study outcomes include monthly counts of nursing home admissions, nursing home days per person-months, inpatient hospitalisation days per person-months, annual counts of the end-of-the-month emergency department visits and hospitalisations, counts of emergency department visits and hospitalisations for ambulatory care-sensitive conditions, SNAP enrolment and, among enrollees, benefit amounts. As in prior work among Medicaid beneficiaries42 and based on common reasons for hospitalisations,46 ambulatory care-sensitive conditions that will be examined in the current study include diabetes, chronic obstructive lung disease/asthma, heart failure and pneumonia. Nursing home admission counts and days per person-month will be calculated using the same approach as emergency department and hospital outcomes. The nursing home admission count may be dichotomised based on any admission in the year because it may be a rare event. Because prior studies have identified end-of-the-month spikes in emergency department visits and hospitalisations in low-income populations131532 47 and because this correlates with the depletion of household financial resources for low-income people with disabilities who often rely on income sources that are paid at the beginning of the month (eg, SSI, SSDI, Veterans disability benefits),1622 we will also generate a variable indicating whether the emergency department visit or hospital admission occurred in the last week of the month. Also, total counts for all emergency department visits and hospitalisations for all ambulatory-sensitive conditions will also be generated. To minimise the risk of reidentification, counts for hospitalisations/emergency department visits for specific ambulatory care-sensitivity conditions will be given per year rather than per month. Because some households can receive SNAP benefits temporarily while the state verifies eligibility but may lose benefits after the eligibility verification process, which typically occurs within 1 or 2 months after submitting an application, we will classify SNAP enrolment based on whether the household has remained on SNAP benefits for at least 3 months.

Sample size and minimum detectable difference

We expect that at least 23% of the people on the outreach list have disabilities and would be included in this study because about 282 000 of the 1 204 244 individuals on the outreach list were eligible for Medicaid due to being either blind or disabled. Therefore, we estimate that at least 50 786 people with disabilities received information plus assistance, at least 59 924 people with disabilities received information only, at least 64 513 people with disabilities should be included in the control group, and at least 56 137 people with disabilities were removed from the outreach list before receiving any outreach. Based on an estimated annual risk of hospitalisation of 0.61 among low-income adults with disabilities,48 power=0.80 and alpha=0.05, these sample sizes should be sufficient to detect incident rate ratios as small as 1.01 in Poisson regression when comparing either the information plus assistance arm or the information-only arm to the control group.

Data analysis plan

Data management

Throughout the outreach process, BDT collected data about their process, including indicators for SNAP eligibility, the household grouping variable, treatment status information and outreach response information. Outreach process data will be sent along with individual identifiers to MDHHS. MDHHS will then identify Medicaid beneficiaries eligible for the proposed study (ie, adults with disabilities on the BDT outreach list who were eligible for SNAP) (figure 1). Among those eligible for the proposed study, MDHHS will provide BDT data and annual Medicaid and SNAP data files capturing the time period between 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2025 to the Hopkins team for analyses. All data files will have identifiers removed prior to being shared with Hopkins; the Hopkins team will use a study-specific ID to merge annual data files across years. Data will be examined for implausible values and missingness. Implausible values may be excluded from analyses, and sensitivity analyses will evaluate their influence on study findings. The data will also be examined for duplications or inconsistencies across data elements, and these will be addressed as appropriate in consultation with the MDHHS team.

Since healthcare utilisation and SNAP utilisation data are used for payment, there will likely be minimal missing outcome data. However, if needed for independent variables, such as baseline characteristics, multiple imputation with chained equations will be performed to address missingness as much as possible. As is considered best practice, the imputations will be generated using covariates, intervention status and outcomes altogether.49 Analyses will also account for potential differential attrition across groups. The study is strengthened by the Medicaid continuous enrolment requirement that began in 2020 in response to the COVID-19 pandemic and remained in place until March 2023. However, there may be loss to follow-up after the requirement ended. Since this study relies on Medicaid data for health outcomes, we will identify individual characteristics that predict loss of Medicaid coverage to generate attrition weights as needed to account for differential attrition.

Data analyses

The intervention effectiveness will be estimated by comparing outcomes across outreach arms. Since we are primarily interested in the Average Treatment Effect (ATE),50 we will leverage the fact that the programme randomly selects households for outreach. Analyses will be strengthened by the use of longitudinal data on individuals; the randomisation should ensure that treatment and comparison arms are similar on baseline trajectories and we will be able to compare changes in outcomes after randomisation.51 The average treatment effect will be estimated based on interactions between outreach status (measured at baseline for each stacked trial) and time in difference-in-differences models. Because of the repeated randomisation each month, with underlying longitudinal data on individuals, we will treat the study as a series of stacked monthly randomised trials. Our primary estimand will be the overall average effect pooled across trials. A pooled (joint) model will be fit, with the variance estimation accounting for the clustering of data within individuals, given that each individual can be in multiple of the stacked trials. For each monthly trial, treated participants will be compared with control participants (ie, those not yet treated before or in that month). In this design, some control participants for early months of the trial may be offered treatment during later months. Control participants who are later selected for outreach (ie, randomised to the treatment group in a subsequent month) will be administratively censored at the time of treatment. In this way, the study follows a per-protocol approach by censoring control participants from early trials who are treated in later trials. We expect that this censoring will not bias results because treatment allocation is random and <1% of participants were selected for treatment each week. Analyses will estimate treatment effects by comparing changes in outcomes since T0 for both treatment arms (eg, information-only and information plus assistance) to changes among the control group. An additional (calendar) time variable capturing time since the study started in 2021 will account for historical and secular trends. Outcomes will be examined prior to analyses to evaluate potential seasonal trends, and analyses will account for seasonality if it improves model fit. All models will account for age, sex and race/ethnicity. Additionally, baseline differences across groups will be examined prior to analyses to ensure covariate balance was achieved for household characteristics (eg, income, size, marital status and receipt of other supports like SSI and Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF)) and individual characteristics (eg, dual eligible status, scope of Medicaid benefits, duration of Medicaid enrolment, veteran indicator, language, citizenship, immigration status and disability types). Although we expect good balance on these variables due to the randomisation, if balance is not achieved through the random assignment process for prerandomisation values of these variables (ie, standardised mean difference values exceeding 0.2), those variables will be added as additional covariates in the models.52 Differential attrition across treatment and control groups will be evaluated. If there is more than a five percentage point difference in the rate of attrition across groups,53 we will address attrition using inverse probability of attrition weights that will be constructed using time-varying values for the same set of covariates as balance will be assessed for (listed above).

Since the ATE may be small due to relatively low SNAP enrolment rates (eg, 5–17% in prior studies),7 28 additional analyses will also estimate the Complier Average Causal Effect (CACE) using an instrumental variable analysis.54 The instrumental variable analyses will estimate the effect of SNAP enrolment itself rather than outreach. The effect of SNAP enrolment among people with disabilities is informative because individuals who enrol with assistance are likely qualitatively different from their peers who were successful in enrolling without assistance. As a result, the instrumental variable estimate will be useful to examine the intervention effect among this important subgroup of people whose SNAP enrolment is changed as a result of the outreach. Additionally, among those who enrol in SNAP, analyses will also estimate the CACE on SNAP benefit amounts. SNAP benefit amount models will account for household size and income, which are used in the benefit amount determination process by the state SNAP programme.

Finally, effect heterogeneity will be examined. Analyses will examine whether the effect of the intervention differs based on being Black, Hispanic, aged 65 years and older or with at least one ambulatory care-sensitive condition. Hypothesised effect modification will be tested by adding three-way interaction terms with the potential moderator, treatment arm and time to the longitudinal models described above separately for each potential moderator.

Importance of the knowledge to be gained

Despite strong evidence of disparities based on both income and disability status, there is scant evidence of effective strategies to advance health equity. The proposed study is innovative in leveraging existing data and in estimating the health effects of a promising and scalable social service programme addressing structural barriers to SNAP for low-income individuals with disabilities within a pragmatic evaluation of a naturally randomly allocated intervention. Importantly, this study evaluates the role of intersecting structural barriers to SNAP based on disability, race/ethnicity and age among a low-income population. The programme uses an innovative data-driven approach to identify individuals who are likely eligible for SNAP based on income but not yet enrolled in the programme. Therefore, the programme is efficient in targeting outreach and SNAP application assistance to households who are most likely to benefit from assistance. Although this programme has been implemented in 12 states and there is some evidence of reduced healthcare utilisation among dually eligible older adults,28 the health impact among adults with disability has not yet been studied. The study is also innovative in leveraging the random selection for treatment that naturally occurs as part of implementing this programme, nesting a random experiment in an existing programme. Therefore, although the study team are not directly manipulating exposure to the programme, the study is naturally randomised in a way that mimics a randomised trial and allows direct comparison of equivalent groups. Additionally, a key strength of this study is that our approach takes advantage of large-scale data from an entire population, not just a sample, which increases the efficiency and generalisability of study findings.

Patient and public involvement

This project uses principles of integrated knowledge translation55—a community engagement approach. An Advisory Committee of national and Michigan-based experts, advocates and organisations was assembled to inform this research. Committee members were selected based on their experience working with the Medicaid home and community-based services waiver programme and the SNAP programme. This committee meets with the study team virtually quarterly to: (1) ensure relevance of the research for the disability community and policy makers; (2) provide insight into the approach to evaluate the programme; (3) provide insight into subgroups of interest and (4) broaden the reach and impact of the research results.

Ethics and dissemination

The study was determined to be exempt from Human Subjects Research Review by the IRBs of Johns Hopkins Medicine and the Michigan Department of Health and Human Services. Informed consent was determined to be not required and was not feasible because of the retrospective use of deidentified administrative data. In addition to being disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, study findings will be disseminated via policy briefs to a broad audience of policy decision-makers and state programme administrators. Interim findings will be disseminated to research-, policy- and practice-oriented conferences to obtain timely feedback on the study design, analyses and interpretation of study findings. To ensure these results are also accessible to the public, especially the disability community, we will develop a community communication campaign that includes posts on social media, information released via established networks and commentaries in mainstream media outlets to put the results into a social context.
