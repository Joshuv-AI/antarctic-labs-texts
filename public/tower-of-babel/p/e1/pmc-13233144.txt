Mirikizumab four-year sustained and durable efficacy and safety in ulcerative colitis: Final findings from the LUCENT-3 open-label extension study
Authors: Bruce E Sands, David B Clemow, Geert D’Haens, Peter M Irving, Taku Kobayashi, Laurent Peyrin-Biroulet, Karen Samaan, Anil Gaur, Ravneet Arora, Kris Todd, Richard Moses, Axel Dignass
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMirikizumab, an anti-interleukin-23 monoclonal antibody targeting the p19 subunit, has shown efficacy in inducing and maintaining clinical remission in patients with moderately-to-severely active ulcerative colitis (UC). This report summarizes the final long-term outcomes from the LUCENT-3 open-label extension (OLE) through week (W)212 of continuous treatment.MethodsAmong 868 participants who received mirikizumab induction therapy in the LUCENT clinical trial program, 544 achieved clinical response. Of these, 365 were rerandomized to mirikizumab maintenance, with 324 completing W52, 316 entering the OLE, and 182 completing the OLE (4 years of continuous treatment), resulting in 835.3 total patient years of exposure. These analyses include efficacy and safety data from the LUCENT-3 cohort, stratified by LUCENT-1 baseline biologic experience, W52 remitter, and W52 responder status on entering the OLE study. Outcomes were analyzed using modified nonresponder imputation, traditional nonresponder imputation, and observed cases (OC) to handle missing data.ResultsAt W212, W52 Maintenance Remitters showed 77.7% clinical, 77.7% corticosteroid-free (CSF), 81.3% endoscopic, 66.0% histologic-endoscopic mucosal (HEMR), 94.3% symptomatic, and 73.6% bowel urgency (BU) remission rates (OC); 67.9% achieved histologic-endoscopic mucosal improvement (HEMI), 97.1% sustained clinical response, and 92.9% achieved BU clinically meaningful improvement (CMI). At W212, W52 Maintenance Responders showed 68.7% clinical, 68.7% CSF, 70.2% endoscopic, 55.1% HEMR, 92.7% symptomatic, and 74.8% BU remission rates (OC). For W52 Maintenance Completers, reductions in stool frequency, rectal bleeding, bowel urgency, and abdominal pain from baseline were sustained to W212. W52 Maintenance Remitters and Responders achieved Inflammatory Bowel Disease Questionnaire (IBDQ; quality of life measure) response (70%) and remission (>66%) at W212. Across biologic failed and bionaive subgroups, efficacy results were generally similar. For over 160 weeks of OLE treatment in the safety population, no new safety signals emerged from long-term exposure compared with induction and maintenance treatment.ConclusionsMirikizumab provided sustained symptomatic, clinical, endoscopic, and histologic benefits over 4 years in responding patients with moderately-to-severely active UC, including those with prior biologic failure. These treatment goals, which are integral to comprehensive disease management, support the long-term use of mirikizumab.

What is already known?

Mirikizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the p19 subunit of interleukin-23, has demonstrated efficacy in inducing remission at 12 weeks and maintaining response through 1, 2, and 3 years in patients with moderate-to-severe ulcerative colitis (UC).

What is new here?

Four-year data show that continued mirikizumab treatment durably maintains clinical, symptomatic, endoscopic, and histologic benefits, including corticosteroid-free remission and improved quality of life, in patients with or without prior biologic failure.

How can this study help patient care?

These results support the use of mirikizumab as maintenance treatment to provide durable long-term efficacy, aiding clinicians in making informed benefit-risk decisions to support comprehensive disease management.

Introduction

For ulcerative colitis (UC), key therapeutic goals involve rapid symptom relief, achievement of clinical response, and sustained remission—ideally including endoscopic healing to limit disease burden and functional impairment, with improvement in the quality of life.1,2 Although a wide array of treatments exists—including 5-aminosalicylates, corticosteroids (CSs), immunosuppressants, biologics, Janus kinase inhibitors, and sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor modulators—many patients with moderate-to-severe UC fail to achieve remission.3

Initial nonresponse is common, with failure to achieve response in up to one-third of patients during induction and loss of effectiveness over time in approximately 40% of initial ­responders.4 This underscores a continued unmet need in the management of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), where durable, effective therapies remain lacking for a substantial subset of patients.4 Key unmet goals include achieving CS-free (CSF) remission, reducing bowel urgency (BU), and enhancing overall patient quality of life. In addition, it has been proposed that comprehensive disease management should, therefore, aim not only for symptomatic relief but also for histologic-endoscopic mucosal remission (HEMR),5,6 which has been associated with more favorable long-term outcomes.

Systemic CSs are commonly used early in clinical practice for rapid induction of remission in patients with moderately-to-severely active UC.7 However, because of their ineffectiveness in maintaining remission and the complications associated with their long-term use, CS use to maintain remission is not recommended,8 and the duration of CS therapy should be limited because of the availability of steroid-sparing therapies.8 CSF clinical remission, defined as clinical remission with no CS exposure during a prespecified period, is an achievable and sustainable treatment target and an important clinical outcome in clinical trials.8,9

Interleukin (IL)-23, a key cytokine in UC pathophysiology, drives a proinflammatory immune cascade via the differentiation and expansion of Th17 cells.2 Mirikizumab, a humanized IgG4 ­monoclonal antibody that selectively targets the p19 subunit of IL-23, offers a novel mechanism of action. It is the first agent of its class to gain regulatory approval for the treatment of UC. Clinical trials have previously demonstrated the ability of mirikizumab to induce clinical response and maintain remission for 3 years with continuous treatment in patients with moderate-to-severe UC, including those with prior biologic treatment failure.6,10-12

We present the final outcomes from the LUCENT-3 open-label extension, evaluating the long-term safety and efficacy of mirikizumab over a four-year period (W212) in adults with moderately-to-severely active UC.

Methods

Study oversight

All participants gave written informed consent prior to enrollment. Study protocols, including all amendments and consent forms, were approved by local ethical review boards. The study was registered with the European Network of Centres for Pharmacoepidemiology and Pharmacovigilance. The trial was conducted in alignment with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and followed applicable regulatory guidance, including the International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice guidelines and Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practices.5,6,13 An independent data monitoring committee oversaw patient safety and study conduct across LUCENT-1, LUCENT-2, and LUCENT-3, which were registered at ClinicalTrials.gov under the identifiers: NCT03518086, NCT03524092, and NCT03519945, respectively.

Study design

Details of the study design and dosing regimens for the LUCENT-1 (12-week) induction, LUCENT-2 (40-week) maintenance, and LUCENT-3 long-term extension trials have been reported previously.5,6,10 LUCENT-3 is an ongoing, single-arm, open-label, multicenter, phase 3 extension trial designed to assess the long-term efficacy and safety of mirikizumab in patients with moderately-to-severely active UC who completed both the LUCENT-1 induction and LUCENT-2 maintenance studies (Figure 1A). Outcomes from the first 52 and 100 weeks of LUCENT-3 (corresponding to 104 and 152 weeks of total continuous mirikizumab treatment) have been previously published.5,6 During the 12-week LUCENT-1 induction phase, patients were treated with intravenous (IV) mirikizumab 300 mg at weeks 0, 4, and 8. Responders at week (W) 12 entered LUCENT-2 (W0 of maintenance phase), and were rerandomized to receive subcutaneous (SC) mirikizumab 200 mg or placebo every 4 weeks through week 52 (W40 of the maintenance phase). Induction nonresponders at W12 underwent extended induction, receiving mirikizumab 300 mg IV at W12, W16, and W20. Those who subsequently responded were transitioned to mirikizumab 200 mg SC every 4 weeks starting at W24 (W12 of maintenance). Patients who experienced a loss of response were eligible for reinduction with three IV doses of mirikizumab 300 mg administered every 4 weeks. Those with evidence of benefit, based on the investigator’s clinical judgment, resumed 200 mg SC dosing every 4 weeks and transitioned into LUCENT-3 as reinduction responders. In LUCENT-3, all enrolled patients continued on mirikizumab 200 mg SC every 4 weeks. Additional subpopulations within the LUCENT program not analyzed here have been previously reported.10-12 Separate analyses of the extended induction and loss-of-response cohorts for four-year data are reported here. Two- and three-year outcomes for these groups have been published previously.5,6

Key inclusion criteria for LUCENT-3 were completion of the LUCENT-2 maintenance study receiving 52 weeks of continuous blinded mirikizumab treatment, and, in the investigator’s opinion, having the potential to benefit from continued mirikizumab therapy. Week numbering is reported cumulatively across studies. For example, W12 of LUCENT-2 equals W24 overall; W40 of LUCENT-2 corresponds to W52; and W52, W100, and W160 in LUCENT-3 align with W104, W152, and W212 of continuous treatment, respectively.

Patients who required rescue therapy due to loss of response were eligible only after completing at least 12 weeks of blinded maintenance therapy. These patients then transitioned into LUCENT-3 following reinduction. This subset of patients received at least 12 weeks of treatment during each of the LUCENT-1 ­(induction), LUCENT-2 (maintenance), and reinduction phases (totaling a minimum of 36 weeks of prior mirikizumab exposure) before enrolling in LUCENT-3. Therefore, these rescue patients had undergone a minimum of 88, 136, or 196 weeks of uninterrupted mirikizumab treatment corresponding to W52, W100, and W160 of LUCENT-3, respectively.

Patient groups

Within the modified intent-to-treat (mITT) population (N = 1162) of the LUCENT program, 868 patients were randomized to receive induction therapy with mirikizumab in the LUCENT-1 study. Among them, 816 progressed into the LUCENT-2 maintenance phase: 544 were classified as induction responders and 272 as nonresponders who initially received extended induction therapy. Of the 544 responders, 365 were rerandomized to mirikizumab maintenance treatment, with 324 completing the full 52-week maintenance period. Of the 272 initial nonresponders, 146 achieved clinical response at W24 of LUCENT-2 and 144 of 146 continued treatment beyond W24, with 134 completed 52-weeks of therapy.

Among patients who completed W52 of LUCENT-2 and entered LUCENT-3, 266 induction responders were eligible for the LUCENT-3 database lock (W104 of continuous treatment) and are included in the corresponding analysis.5,6 Fifty additional mirikizumab responders were excluded from the W104 analysis due to delays in protocol addendum approval at their respective sites, which precluded their inclusion in the interim database lock.6 These patients were subsequently included in the W152 and W212 analyses. Therefore, 316 patients (Table S1) who completed W52 of LUCENT-2, enrolled in LUCENT-3 and were included in the current database lock for evaluation at W160 (W212 of continuous mirikizumab treatment; Figure 1A).

Not all patients classified as LUCENT-1 induction responders achieved clinical response or remission at their final assessment in LUCENT-2. As a result, the W52 Maintenance Responder and W52 Maintenance Remitter subgroups represent subsets of the broader induction responder population that transitioned into LUCENT-3. Of the 316 induction responders included in the W160 LUCENT-3 analysis (W212 of continuous mirikizumab treatment), only 30 did not meet responder criteria at W40 of LUCENT-2 (W52 of continuous treatment). Patients randomized to placebo at any point either as induction responders who were rerandomized to placebo in LUCENT-2 or directly assigned to placebo, were excluded from these analyses.

For LUCENT-1 responders who were on CS (≤20 mg/day prednisone equivalent, ≤9 mg/day budesonide MMX, or beclomethasone dipropionate 5 mg/day), CS tapering was commenced on entering LUCENT-2 with a goal to complete tapering within 12 weeks (W24). The recommended prednisone-equivalent tapering reduced the daily dose by 5 mg every week until it was <10 mg/day, then the daily dose was tapered by 2.5 mg each week until it was discontinued. For patients with starting daily doses of ≤10 mg, the dose was tapered by 2.5 mg/week until it was discontinued. Among patients receiving daily budesonide MMX, the dose was tapered to once every other day for 2 weeks, followed by once every third day for 2 weeks, and it was then discontinued. The recommended tapering for patients receiving beclomethasone dipropionate was every other day for 4 weeks, and then, discontinue. For patients who could not tolerate the CS tapering without recurring clinical symptoms, CS tapering could be paused and/or the CS dose increased up to no more than the original dose at LUCENT-1 baseline. In such cases, attempts to reinitiate CS tapering were made within 2 weeks of taper interruption.14 Over 98% of W52 Maintenance Completers were not taking steroids to begin LUCENT-3. During LUCENT-3, CS initiation was allowed for UC flares as described for LUCENT-2. CS doses were adjusted up or down at the investigator’s discretion and were tapered as soon as clinically appropriate. Topical administration of corticosteroids (eg, enemas or suppositories) was allowed during the study with the goal of tapering as soon as clinically feasible or discontinuing within 3 months and was considered new corticosteroid use.

The baseline for the current analyses was defined as the induction baseline from LUCENT-1. Endoscopy was a required protocol assessment in LUCENT-3.

SF and RB were evaluated as individual subscores from the MMS. Consistent with the MMS-based clinical response and remission definitions established for the LUCENT program, an improvement of ≥1 on the 0-3 subscore scale for both SF and RB was considered the minimum threshold indicative of a clinically meaningful change from baseline.10 For abdominal pain, CMI was defined as either a reduction of ≥3 points on the 0-10 Abdominal Pain NRS or a ≥30% decrease from baseline—criteria supported by established thresholds in irritable bowel syndrome and previously applied in UC studies.10 For the evaluation of maintenance of treatment benefit, only those participants who had achieved the endpoint at W52 (W40 of LUCENT-2) were assessed for durable maintenance at later timepoints from W52 to W212 (W0 to W160 of LUCENT-3).

Discontinuation of CS use was defined as no CS use without study treatment discontinuation or CS reinitiation for the remainder of the study period. For on versus off CS data, on or off CSs was determined for the individual timepoints of interest without other qualifiers.

Statistical analyses

All endpoints were analyzed using descriptive statistical methods. For categorical efficacy outcomes, proportions and associated confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. When CIs were calculated, the Wilson score method was applied unless otherwise noted.17 Continuous efficacy endpoints were analyzed as mean changes from the LUCENT-1 induction baseline, with standard deviations. Efficacy analyses were conducted using the mITT population at each specified time point. Safety analyses were based on the Safety Population and included all adverse events (AEs) reported during W52 to W212 of continuous mirikizumab treatment (W0 to W160 of the LUCENT-3 extension).

For continuous efficacy outcomes measured over time, a mixed-effects model for repeated measures (MMRM) was used to estimate the average change from induction baseline, with visit included as a covariate. Proportions of patients at 2, 3, and 4 years of continuous treatment achieving CSF remission, symptomatic remission, clinical remission, and endoscopic remission and no CS use at W52 were analyzed by logistic regression analysis with treatment, subgroup, and treatment-by-subgroup interaction and prior biologic or tofacitinib failure (yes/no), corticosteroid use (yes/no), region (North America/Europe/Other), and clinical remission status (yes/no) at LUCENT-1 W12.

Exposure-adjusted incidence rates (EAIRs) were calculated as the number of patients experiencing a treatment-emergent adverse event (TEAE) divided by the total person-years (PY) at risk (PYR) and expressed per 100 PY. EAIR per 100 PY represents the number of events expected for every 100 cumulative years of patient exposure; for example, this could reflect 100 patients treated for one year each, 50 patients treated for 2 years, or a similar combination. This estimate assumes a constant hazard of the event over the period of observation. PYR was defined as the time from the start of treatment to either the first occurrence of an event or the end of treatment, summed across all patients in the analysis population. Person-years of exposure, in contrast, is a global value that reflects the total duration of study treatment exposure from first to last dose across all ­participants.

Missing data handling

Approaches for managing missing data in the mITT population, including patients with early discontinuation or sporadically missing data (missing at random), have been previously described.5,6 For patients with sporadically missing data before discontinuation, modified baseline observation carried forward (mBOCF) was used, with the last available nonmissing value carried forward to the corresponding visit to preserve longitudinal consistency. For categorical and binary efficacy endpoints, nonresponder imputation (NRI) was prespecified as the primary method to address missing data. Under this approach, patients who either discontinued treatment or had missing endpoint data were classified as nonresponders.

For continuous measures at a single time point, mBOCF was applied. If a patient discontinued due to an adverse event (AE), their baseline observation for the endpoint was carried forward to the corresponding visit for all missing observations after the discontinuation. For discontinuations due to reasons other than AEs, the last post-baseline nonmissing value before discontinuation was carried forward for all missing observations. Patients without at least one post-baseline data point were excluded from the analysis. Patients discontinuing investigational product due to a geopolitical conflict or commercial approval with missing data will be excluded from this approach for time points at or following their discontinuation.

Observed case (OC) analyses were also conducted as secondary analyses for categorical endpoints, where missing values were neither imputed nor included in the calculation of proportions. OC analyses can be biased to show high remission/response rates. As efficacy endpoints are assessed further into long-term extension studies, the OC data become particularly valuable because they minimize bias introduced by patient discontinuations due, not to lack of efficacy or AEs, but to reasons such as “approved drug available for indication.” This reason was the main explanation for patient discontinuation in LUCENT-3; 40 (12.7%) W52 Maintenance Completers discontinued the study to go on marketed drug.

Additionally, a modified NRI (mNRI) approach was used to complement NRI and OC analyses. mNRI integrates elements of both strategies: it imputes discontinuations as nonresponse while addressing sporadic missing data. Discontinuations due to commercial availability and geopolitical restrictions are exceptions to this rule. For patients with sporadic missing data or those who discontinued mirikizumab due to commercial availability or geopolitical reasons, missing data were addressed using multiple imputation. For all other treatment discontinuations, standard NRI was applied.

The multiple imputation process used logistic regression models to generate multiple predicted values and estimations for each missing data point, resulting in an imputation value. Final estimates of response percentages and associated confidence intervals were calculated using Rubin’s rules.18 Because multiple imputation generates multiple estimates, final sample sizes for imputed analyses do not yield a fixed number of patients but rather, they yield estimated proportions of responder rates.

The LUCENT-2 W52 maintenance completer safety population includes a subset of 23 patients affected by an electronic clinical outcome assessment transcription error in Poland and Turkey.10

Results

Patient disposition and treatment discontinuation

Of 1162 patients included in the modified intent-to-treat population, 868 received mirikizumab during LUCENT-1 induction and 294 received placebo. At W12, 835 (96.2%) mirikizumab-treated patients completed induction, and 816 (94.0%) entered LUCENT-2 maintenance. Among 544 mirikizumab induction responders, 365 were rerandomized to blinded mirikizumab maintenance, and 324 (88.8%) completed LUCENT-2 W40 (W52 of continuous treatment). A total of 316 mirikizumab-treated maintenance completers entered LUCENT-3, of whom 182 (57.6%) completed W212 (4 years of continuous treatment).

The most frequent reasons for discontinuation during LUCENT-3 were availability of an approved drug for the indication (n = 40, 12.7%), geopolitical or logistical restrictions (n = 23, 7.3%), adverse events (n = 21, 6.6%), and withdrawal by subject (n = 18, 5.7%; Figure 1). Fewer discontinuations were attributed to lack of efficacy (n = 14, 4.4%).

Baseline demographics and disease characteristics

Baseline demographics were comparable across the W52 Maintenance Completer, W52 Maintenance Responder, and W52 Maintenance Remitter groups as well as the Extended Induction W52 Maintenance Remitters. However, about a 10% greater proportion of Extended Induction W52 Maintenance Remitters than the other groups had left-side colitis and prior biologic or tofacitinib failure (Table S1).

In LUCENT-1, of the 868 patients randomized to mirikizumab induction, 351 (40.4%) received CSs at baseline. Among W12 induction responders, 135 patients rerandomized to mirikizumab and 68 rerandomized to placebo in LUCENT-2 received CSs at LUCENT-1 baseline. A total of 316 induction responders in the LUCENT-2 blinded mirikizumab group were W52 Maintenance Completers and transitioned into the LUCENT-3 open-label extension. Of these, 78 were using CSs at LUCENT-1 baseline, while 161 were not (Table S2). Demographics and patient characteristics were similar between the LUCENT-3 patients on or off CSs at LUCENT-1 baseline, with about 10% more patients on CSs having pancolitis and somewhat higher inflammatory biomarkers (eg, median fecal calprotectin, 1842.5 µg/g vs 1429.0 µg/g).

LUCENT-2 W52 continuous treatment

Among the patients receiving CSs at LUCENT-1 baseline, a significantly greater proportion of patients treated with mirikizumab (for maintenance in LUCENT-2) versus placebo achieved clinical remission while off CSs at W52 (44.4% vs 17.6%), clinical remission while off CSs for ≥12 weeks (43.7% vs 17.6%), and CS-free remission (41.5% vs 14.7%; using NRI). Discussion

As treatment goals for UC continue to evolve, there is growing emphasis not only on endoscopic improvement and control of SF or RB but also on symptoms more meaningful to patients, such as BU,20 and on histologic-endoscopic endpoints (mucosal healing).8,21,22 Despite therapeutic advances, many patients with UC experience diminishing treatment efficacy over time, with up to 40% experiencing loss of treatment response after initial success.4 The LUCENT-3 study is the first long-term extension trial in UC to report comprehensive outcomes—clinical response, remission, HEMI, HEMR, and endoscopic remission that together align with the evolving treatment concept of disease clearance. This ­endpoint, increasingly recognized in the literature, is defined by concurrent achievement of clinical and histologic-endoscopic remission and represents a higher therapeutic benchmark associated with favorable long-term outcomes.23

Whereas prior long-term studies of other therapies have largely focused on symptom-based outcomes and safety, the LUCENT-3 study reflects a broader shift toward comprehensive disease management with treatment goals that emphasize not only symptom relief but also sustained remission across multiple domains of disease activity.24,25 An international Delphi consensus recommends that the following aspects of UC be considered in defining comprehensive disease control: RB, SF, BU, abdominal pain, ­extraintestinal manifestations, disease-related quality of life, endoscopy, histological inflammatory activity, inflammatory biomarkers, CS use, fatigue, and sleep disturbance. Comprehensive disease control is a target goal for the broader strategy of comprehensive disease management, which incorporates patient-centered outcomes, mucosal and histologic healing, biomarker normalization, and symptom control, including BU, into a unified, long-term approach to achieving and maintaining deep, durable remission in UC to achieve optimal outcomes.22,26,27

Building on findings at W104 and W152,5,6 the current four-year results (212 weeks) further demonstrate that mirikizumab offers durable long-term benefits for patients who initially respond to therapy. Improvements were seen consistently across multiple clinical, endoscopic, histologic, and patient-reported outcomes, supporting sustained efficacy with mirikizumab treatment and indicating benefit in both biologic-naïve and previously biologic-failed populations.

Although the biologic-failed subgroup demonstrated numerically lower response and remission rates over time than the overall population, >50% of patients in the biologic-failed subgroup had maintained clinical response at 4 years of treatment (Figure S2A). Declines over time appeared more pronounced in this group, likely reflecting greater disease refractoriness.10 The small decreases are influenced by the smaller number of patients in the biologic failed subgroup and the imputation method rather than the overall change in maintenance of efficacy. For instance, using the maintenance responders' clinical response at 4 years, 61.3% (141/230) of overall blinded maintenance responders had maintained clinical response compared to 51.4% (37/72) in the biologic-failed subgroup (Figure S2A). At W152 these rates were 71.6% and 60.4%, respectively, while at W104 these rates were 74.5% and 68.5%, respectively.5,6 The difference in denominators between the total population and the biologic-failed subgroup is 158 patients, where a change in the number of patients by 2 equates to 0.9% for the overall population and 2.8% for the biologic-failed subgroup. Of the several discontinuations contributing to reduced maintenance response, 21(15.9%) were due to AEs, 14(10.6%) were due to efficacy loss, and 18(13.6%) withdrew for other reasons. Therefore, the small decreases in percent changes were likely highly influenced by nonefficacy related ­discontinuations.

Although long-term safety and efficacy data have been ­published for vedolizumab (an α4β7-integrin inhibitor)28 and ustekinumab (an IL-12 and IL-23p40 antagonist),29 no long-term endoscopic outcomes data have been reported over the full extension period. In contrast, mirikizumab’s LUCENT-3 provides histologic and endoscopic endpoints, including HEMI and HEMR, with sustained long-term response over 4 years. This is clinically relevant given the strong association between mucosal and histological healing and better long-term UC outcomes.30

Patients often rank BU as more disruptive than SF or RB.31,32 The Urgency NRS used here for BU is validated to assess symptom severity over time rather than reporting binary (yes/no) symptoms,15,33,34 and the scores demonstrated long-term improvement with mirikizumab. Sustained BU resolution is associated with improved quality of life and UC control.30

Anti-TNF-α biologics such as infliximab, adalimumab, or golimumab and vedolizumab (α4β7-integrin inhibitor), remain first-line therapies for moderate-to-severe UC,35 though secondary loss of response is common in some primary responders.36 Mirikizumab’s long-term efficacy in anti-TNF nonresponders may stem in part from its ability to suppress the molecular pathways linked to anti-TNF resistance.4,37

The European Crohn’s and Colitis Organisation (ECCO) quality standards caution against prolonged CS use (>20 mg/day prednisolone or equivalent for >16 weeks or >9 months of any systemic CS annually). Patients requiring >2 steroid cycles annually should transition to CS-sparing therapy.38 These four-year results demonstrate mirikizumab’s significant and sustained CS-sparing benefit. This is a clinically important observation, since clinical remission without CS use is an important target in long-term UC care.9 The third ECCO consensus identifies CSF clinical and endoscopic remission as a goal for maintenance therapy in UC.7 Most evidence supports continuation of the same therapy that induced remission to maintain CSF remission.8,39 In line with U.S. Food and Drug Administration UC clinical trial guidance, CSF remission defined as ≥12 weeks without CS use40 was robustly sustained.

LUCENT-3 W12-W212 OLE continuous treatment safety findings remained consistent with LUCENT-1 induction and LUCENT-2 maintenance results.10 Mirikizumab continued to demonstrate a favorable safety profile through year 4, aligning with the results of earlier 2- and 3-year analyses.5,6 Hepatic AEs were rare and not clinically significant. Infection and malignancy rates did not indicate significant systemic immunosuppression. COVID-19 was the most frequently reported AE, since the study was conducted during the pandemic. Anti-drug antibody (ADA) data was not available in LUCENT-3 for year 4 but has been previously reported for baseline through year 3 of continuous treatment.6 Patients who were ADA + generally had titers reach a maximum during their initial 52 weeks of treatment followed by a decrease through the remainder of the clinical program such that they had low titers or undetected ADA during their third year of treatment. During the third year, only 3.4% of patients were ADA + and only 1.0% were ADA + with a ≥1:160 titer, with symptomatic remission, clinical response, or endoscopic remission proportions similar for ADA + patients and ADA − patients.

Limitations

Limitations of the LUCENT-3 study have been previously noted, including its open-label design and the inclusion of only LUCENT-1 induction responders due to the responder randomization methodology used in the LUCENT program.5,6 As a result, patients who did not respond to induction therapy were not included in long-term maintenance evaluations, which likely led to overestimation of long-term efficacy proportions compared to analyses that include the full baseline population. Additionally, the small number of Black or African American participants (4/316, 1.3% of W52 Maintenance Completers) limits the generalizability of the findings across diverse populations. Although NRI becomes less informative in the context of a four-year long-term extension study due to increasing rates of missing data, it was retained in our analyses for consistency with prior LUCENT-3 reports at years 1-3 and because it was prespecified in the study protocol. However, mNRI provides a more meaningful estimate for maintenance, and OC becomes increasingly relevant over longer timeframes, given that most discontinuations were not due to lack of efficacy but to other reasons such as transition to marketed therapy or withdrawal from the study for personal reasons.

Conclusion

These results support the sustained benefit of mirikizumab through 4 years of continuous treatment with durable maintenance of improvement across multiple treatment goals, including symptomatic, clinical, histologic-endoscopic, CS-sparing, and quality-of-life outcomes. Clinically meaningful improvements in stool frequency, rectal bleeding, and bowel urgency were maintained over time, and high rates of clinical remission and HEMR at 4 years were observed among patients who had achieved these outcomes at W52. These benefits were consistently seen in both biologic failed and bionaive patients, as well as in patients with extended induction who had a delayed response to induction treatment. With a low rate of treatment discontinuation due to adverse events and no new safety signals identified, mirikizumab demonstrated a favorable long-term safety profile. Mirikizumab treatment demonstrated maintenance of several treatment goals included as part of comprehensive disease management of patients with moderate to severe UC. These findings highlight mirikizumab as an important treatment option for patients with UC.
