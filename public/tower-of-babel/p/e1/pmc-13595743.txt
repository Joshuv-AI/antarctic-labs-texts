Comparative transcriptomics of biopesticide-treated olives subjected to Xylella infection
Authors: Annalisa Giampetruzzi, Eva M Molin, Ana Falcón-Piñeiro, José Manuel García-Madero, Raied Abou Kubaa, Tina Kogej, Liesbeth Zwarts, Carmine Del Grosso, Nicoletta Contaldo, Crescenza Dongiovanni, Stéphane Compant, Pasquale Saldarelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe management of Xylella fastidiosa infections relies on integrated strategies of controlling vector populations and removing inoculum sources. The impact of a subspecies pauca strain of this bacterium (XfDD) was particularly severe in olive in Apulia, Italy, where an entire economy was affected, and a territory was devastated. There is an urgent request for strategies to combat the pathogen in planta, as well as for bioinsecticides targeting its vector insect species, and that these should conform to criteria of environmental acceptance. Therefore, this study analyses the Mechanisms of Action (MoA) of three novel biopesticides for pest management of XfDD in olive.ResultsIn vitro assays showed that the Onion extract (Onion) inhibits the growth of XfDD while the bacterium Paraburkholderia phytofirmans strain PsJN (PsJN) did not. Using a time course transcriptomics approach, the molecular response of olive to the biopesticides PsJN and Onion, aiming to control XfDD, and to the natural insecticide Sankari© were observed. As early as 6 to 12 h after the application of PsJN, Onion or Sankari©, olives showed altered gene expression patterns, which decayed after 15 days. All olives, successively inoculated with XfDD, were analysed 10 days later. All three biopesticides induced an early immune response, including genes related to the control of hormone levels or membrane-associated kinase. In addition, PsJN also modulated ethylene levels, thus showing a potential promotion of plant growth. The expression of a set of early induced genes, monitored by quantitative RT-PCR, confirmed both, the PsJN and Sankari© transcriptomic response. Moreover, XfDD inoculation stimulated a more intense transcriptomic response in treated compared to untreated and inoculated olives, indicating that the biopesticides induced a priming response.ConclusionsDissecting the MoA of a potential biopesticide complements the evaluation of its efficacy in controlling the pathogen. In the case of XfDD, this assessment requires extensive field trials due to the prolonged period before symptoms appear in infected olives and the occurrence of repeated inoculations under natural conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-08671-y.Keywords: Xylella, Biopesticide, Comparative transcriptomics, Olea europaea, Onion extract, Paraburkholderia phytofirmans PsJN

Background

The xylem-limited bacterium Xylella fastidiosa (Xf) [1] is arguably one of the greatest threats to plant health worldwide, causing enormous socioeconomic and environmental losses [2]. It can infect 712 plant species across 312 genera and 89 families, and has been identified as a major transboundary plant pathogen [3]. In the Americas, Xf is associated with diseases affecting grapevine, almond, coffee and citrus, causing significant economic losses [4]. Its recent invasion into European countries has devastated olive [5] and almond [6] groves, with severe economic and environmental consequences. In Europe, three out of five described Xylella subspecies—pauca, multiplex and fastidiosa—have already been detected in the field in Italy, Spain, France, and Portugal [7]. In the broader Mediterranean region, Xylella has established itself in Israel and Lebanon (https://gd.eppo.int/taxon/XYLEFA/distribution). In Southern Italy since 2013, the pauca subspecies strain De Donno (XfDD) ST53 caused the Olive Quick Decline Syndrome (OQDS), a devastating disease characterized by severe desiccations of susceptible olive cultivars, which can ultimately lead to plant death [5]. In the epidemic area, both local cultivars ‘Ogliarola salentina’ and ‘Cellina di Nardò’, have proven susceptible to the bacterium, which is efficiently spread by the xylem-feeding insect Philaenus spumarius. Over 10 million trees, many of them centuries old, have been destroyed in the epidemic area. Projections of its massive spread throughout Europe forecast losses of agricultural production of up to €5.2 billion per year in the olive sector alone [8]. X. fastidiosa is an endophytic commensal that moves from the site of inoculation to colonize the xylem network in infected plants. Within the xylem, it induces vessel occlusion and general hydraulic failure, mimicking drought stress conditions. The progressive onset of desiccation symptoms ultimately leads to plant die-off [9].

According to the most recent EFSA Scientific Opinion, there are currently no available therapies to reduce the population of, or eliminate X. fastidiosa from infected plants [10]. Containment of the bacterium’s spread therefore relies on the correct and timely implementation of phytosanitary measures, which include the removal of infected plants and control of the vector populations. A more promising management strategy involves the identification and breeding of plants resistant to XfDD. At present, only four resistant cultivars are authorized for use in Apulia, Italy: ‘Leccino’, ‘FS17®’, ‘Lecciana’ and ‘Leccio del Corno’. Furthermore, a recent study reported that resistance-related traits identified in the olive cultivar ‘Leccino’ can be introgressed into naturally occurring genotypes [11], opening new perspectives for the development of Xf-resistant varieties through conventional breeding or gene-editing strategies.

The “Farm to Fork Strategy” of the European Green Deal aims to reduce the use of chemical pesticides by 50% by 2030, strongly encouraging the identification and adoption of environmentally sustainable solutions in the agricultural practices to combat plant pathogen infections. Biopesticides, which are natural products primarily derived from plants and microorganisms, offer effective protection against pests and pathogens while preserving ecosystems [12, 13]. However, designing and producing effective biopesticides requires an understanding of their mode of action (MoA), which can be gained through the study of plant gene expression following biopesticide application. To this end, we analyzed the transcriptome profiles of olive trees treated with two potential biopesticides targeting X. fastidiosa subspecies pauca ST53, the strain devastating olive orchards in Apulia, and one bioinsecticide targeting Philaenus spumarius, its main vector [14].

Among the biopesticides, the gammaproteobacterium Paraburkholderia phytofirmans strain PsJN was chosen, which was isolated from surface-sterilized onion roots [15]. This strain is able to epi- and endophytically colonize either monocotyledonous (wheat, [16], rice [17],) or dicotyledonous plant species [18], such as Arabidopsis thaliana [19], potato [15], and grapevine [20]. Mainly studied for its plant growth-promoting activity, PsJN was also shown to protect plants from either abiotic stress, such as drought [21], extreme temperatures [20, 22], and salinity [23], or biotic stress, such as infections by Verticillium daliae [24], Botrytis cinerea [25], and Pseudomonas syringae [19]. PsJN has also shown beneficial effects in the control of X. fastidiosa subsp. fastidiosa in Pierce’s Disease (PD) of grapevine [26]. In a recent report by the same authors [27], the prevention of PD symptoms was achieved in field trials through spray or “pin prick” inoculation of PsJN. Surprisingly, PsJN titers declined over time, suggesting that immune priming induced by the bacterium may create host conditions that are less permissive for sustained bacterial persistence. Evidence supporting this mechanism includes the induction of Pathogenesis-related protein 1 (PR1) and ethylene-responsive genes (ETR1) [26], as also demonstrated by Miotto-Vilanova et al.. [25]. These authors showed that PsJN induced the expression of similar genes, increased calls deposition and H2O2 production conferring resistance to Botrytis cinerea in grapevine [25].

The second selected biopesticide falls into the class of organosulfur compounds (OSCs), which are known to confer antimicrobial properties of certain naturally occurring products from the plant genus Allium, being known and used for centuries both for the characteristic flavor and health benefits. In intact onion tissues, OSCs exist as non-volatile, odorless precursors known as S-alk(en)yl-L-cysteine sulfoxides (ACSOs), mainly methiin, isoalliin, and propiin. Upon tissue disruption, propiin is enzymatically converted by alliinase, leading to the formation of propyl-propane thiosulfinate (PTS) [28]. PTS is a labile compound that undergoes dismutation or disproportionation reactions, resulting in dipropyl disulphide and the more stable propyl-propane thiosulfonate (PTSO) [29]. OSCs derived from onion and other Allium species exhibit strong antimicrobial activity against a broad spectrum of plant pathogens. This effect is primarily attributed to their ability to oxidize thiol groups in microbial enzymes, specifically through the modification of protein cysteine and glutathione residues. This oxidative mechanism leads to the inhibition of key enzymes involved in essential cellular processes, including energy metabolism and transcription, such as acetyl-CoA-related enzymes and RNA polymerase [30, 31]. OSCs such as PTS and PTSO have exhibited notable antifungal activity against Verticillium dahliae, a highly destructive, soil-borne pathogen of olive trees that colonizes the xylem tissue. In controlled chamber experiments, application of these organosulfur compounds to infested soils significantly reduced inoculum density, vascular colonization, and disease symptoms in olive plantlets [30]. Field trials further demonstrated that, while infection incidence remained largely unchanged, treated trees exhibited lower pathogen loads in foliar tissue and milder symptom expression [32].

In addition to their antimicrobial effects, OSCs from onion have also demonstrated biostimulant properties. In olive trees, the application of an OSC-rich onion extract has been associated with enhanced physiological responses, including reduced lipid peroxidation, as indicated by lower malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in treated plants. These responses were observed both under controlled conditions and in field trials, without signs of phytotoxicity. Notably, the extract also promoted root development and increased budbreak rates, particularly when applied via irrigation [32].

Furthermore, complementary to the above-described biopesticides, the product Sankari© was included as a preventive treatment. Sankari© is formulated based on pelargonic (nonanoic) acid, a naturally occurring fatty acid widely used in agriculture as a bioherbicide and blossom thinning agent due to its rapid contact phytotoxic activity and relatively low environmental persistence compared to conventional synthetic pesticides [33, 34]. The compound acts primarily through membrane disruption and rapid desiccation of plant tissues following contact. In the present study, Sankari© was not evaluated for vector selectivity or direct effects on insect physiology. Rather, it was investigated for its potential indirect influence on plant responses and pathogen colonization following preventive application prior to infection.

To support the potential use of P. phytofirmans PsJN and onion extract in the control of XfDD infections in olives, and of Sankari© as insecticide targeting P. spumarius, we analyzed the transcriptome profiles of olive plants grown under controlled greenhouse conditions and treated with these biopesticides. The study focused on early plant responses to XfDD infection following treatment. In addition, the in vitro antimicrobial activity of PsJN and of onion extract against XfDD were tested. The present work complements ongoing large-scale field trials currently underway in the epidemic area of Apulia and aims to evaluate the efficacy of these three biopesticides in controlling XfDD infections under field conditions.

Methods

Antagonistic activity of Paraburkholderia phytofirmans PsJN and onion extract against XfDD in vitro

The antagonistic activity of Paraburkholderia phytofirmans PsJN against XfDD was studied by the dual culture method on solid nutrient media [35]. Preliminary experiments were carried out to set up and define the best conditions for the co-cultivation of XfDD and PsJN, using the media PWG [36], BCYE and PD3 [37]. In the case of PsJN, selected conditions consisted of using PD3 agar plates at 25° C. While, for the onion extract, normal XfDD growth conditions of 28° C were used in the same medium. PsJN dual-culture assays were performed in triplicates. Three drops of 20 μL each of a suspension of XfDD (107 CFU/mL) were pipetted at the top of the Petri dish and slowly let to flow down to the opposite side of the plate, generating three parallel rows of XfDD cultures. After 24 h of incubation at 25 °C, an aliquot of 3 μL of a suspension in NaCl 0.85% of the PsJN (109 CFU/mL) was placed on the top of the middle row of the XfDD cultures. After 7–10 days of further incubation at 25 °C, XfDD inhibition was measured as the distance between the edges of the XfDD culture and the PsJN colony.

The onion extract was prepared at the pilot processing facility of DMC Research Center (Granada, Spain) using maceration and vacuum distillation techniques. Discarded second-grade onions (provided by local agricultural suppliers in Fuente Vaqueros, Granada) were initially washed with a 2% (v/v) acetic acid solution, thoroughly rinsed with potable water, and mechanically chopped using stainless steel equipment. A total of 50 kg of chopped onion was macerated in a 30:70 (v/v) ethanol–water solution for 24 h at room temperature under continuous stirring (60 rpm). After filtration, the liquid phase was distilled to obtain a hydroalcoholic extract enriched in organosulfur compounds (OSCs). Ethanol was subsequently removed under reduced pressure and the resulting concentrate was blended with food-grade glycerine (used as a dispersing agent) in a 200 L mixing reactor to obtain the final onion extract formulation (TROFIC EVO, DOMCA, Granada, Spain). Quantification of major OSCs, including thiosulfinates and thiosulfonates, was performed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) following the method described by Abad et al. (2015) [38]. The final formulation exhibited a pH of 6.1, a viscosity of 730 cP and a total OSCs content of 1.52% (w/w). The antimicrobial activity of onion extract was evaluated by the agar-well diffusion method. PD3 agar plates were seeded with a XfDD to obtain three rows as described above. Then, a well (8 mm diameter) was made at the end of the central row and filled with 150 μL of 0.05%—2% onion extract. Plates were incubated at 28 °C for 6 days and growth inhibition was measured as above.

The relationship between onion extract concentration (%) and inhibition zone diameter (mm) was evaluated using simple linear regression (ordinary least squares, OLS). The coefficient of determination (R2) was calculated based on the mean inhibition values obtained at each tested concentration (0.05–2.0%). The untreated water control (0 mm inhibition) was excluded from the regression analysis to avoid bias in the dose–response assessment.

Greenhouse treatments of olives with biopesticides

Two-year-old potted olive trees (Olea europaea L.) of the cultivar ‘Ogliarola salentina’, commercially available, were grown in the greenhouse at 25 ± 2 °C and relative humidity (RH) of 65 ± 10%. The onion extract (OO) (16%), the bacterium Paraburkholderia phytofirmans strain PsJN formulation (PsJN) at 108 CFU/mL PsJN with 0.05% Silwet™ L-77 (Momentive) and the Sankari© formulation (GIP) (0.5%) were applied by foliar spray on the plants’ canopy. The experimental design of the trial is depicted in Fig. 1.

Experimental design. Experimental setup of the greenhouse trial of olives untreated control (AA), treated with Onion (OO), PsJN (PP) and Sankari© (GIP) inoculated or not with XfDD. Plants were sampled at five time points: T0 (before treatment), T1 (6 h post-treatment, hpt), T2 (12 hpt), T5 (15 days post-treatment, dpt), and T6 (10 days post-infection, dpi). For each time point, six biological replicates were included (Rep1–Rep6). Plant codes indicate the treatment and replicate at each sampling point. Differential gene expression analyses were performed on xylem tissues collected at all time points. At the final time point (T6), plants were either inoculated with XfDD (dark green background) or maintained as non-infected controls; within this latter group, plant samples AA28, AA29, and AA30, which were neither treated nor inoculated (light background), were referred to as mock control

In detail, the trial encompassed groups of six plants per treatment: i) treated with the onion extract (OO); ii) treated with P. phytofirmans PsJN (PP); iii) treated with Sankari© (GIP) and iv) sprayed with water as control treatment (AA). The first tissue sampling was done at time 0 (T0), before starting the biopesticide applications.

Then, samplings at 6 h (T1) and 12 h (T2) were carried out on olives subjected to single treatments, namely Sankari© (T1), PsJN (T2), or onion extract (T2), to assess the early response.

The anticipated 6 h sampling of Sankari©−treated olives was suggested by available information (confidential information from Globachem) indicating a prompt response of different plant species to its application. Additional samplings for PsJN, onion extract and Sankari©-treated olives, as well as for the untreated control (AA), were done 15 days after the treatments (T5). Immediately after T5, three out of the six plants per treatment group were artificially inoculated by needle inoculation with XfDD. Inoculations were performed with 8-day-old XfDD suspensions in 1X PBS at 1 × 109 CFU/mL. Five twigs per plant were needle punctured after placing a 10 µL drop of bacterial culture suspension below the petioles [39]. The remaining three plants were inoculated only with 1X PBS (AA untreated and not infected, mock control). A final tissue sampling was done 10 days after XfDD inoculation (T6). At each sampling date, twigs of 50–60 cm length, containing the inoculation point, were sampled and immediately processed for total nucleic acid extraction.

RNA extraction & sequencing

Twigs were debarked with a scalpel to expose woody xylem tissues. Total RNA was then extracted from 2 g of wood tissue that was powdered in liquid nitrogen as described by [40]. RNA concentrations were quantified using NanoDrop measurements (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) and their integrity was assessed by visualization on a 1% agarose gel. Ninety-six (96) cDNA libraries were constructed using the NEB’s polyA procedure (method i7) and sequenced in paired-end mode (2 × 150 bp) distributed over three S4 lanes with the Illumina NovaSeq XP technology at the Next Generation Sequencing Facility of the Vienna BioCenter Core Facilities (VBCF, https://www.viennabiocenter.org/vbcf/).

Differential gene expression analysis

Raw reads were quality trimmed with BBDuk, part of BBTools v38.90 (Joint Genome Institute, 2022), using the suggested parameters for paired-end reads as described in the respective BBDuk Guide, including a k-mer length of 23, trimming of reads to the right (ktrim = r), trimming of read regions below an average quality threshold of Q30 (trimq = 30), and discarding reads shorter than 50 bp. After a preliminary mapping test with randomly selected samples using Olea europaea cv. ‘Picual’ [41] https://genomaolivar.dipujaen.es/db/index.php) and O. europaea cv. ‘Farga’ as the reference genomes [42, 43], the final mapping was performed using the latter resource, due to its overall higher mapping quality (i.e., higher ratio of uniquely mapped reads, and a lower lesser proportion of multimapped reads; see Additional file 2: Fig. S2). For indexing and mapping, the splice-aware aligner HISAT2 v2.2.1 [44] was used with default parameters. File conversion (sam to bam) was performed using, SAMtools v1.14 [45]. Mapping quality was further assessed using Qualimap v2.2.2a [46] and MmultiQC v1.11 [47]. A read count matrix was generated using the function featureCounts of the R package Rsubread v2.10.5 [48] with default parameters. The data were then normalized using variance stabilizing transformation (VST) with DESeq2 v1.36.0 [49]. The dataset was first visualized by principal component analysis (PCA) using the R package ggplot2 v3.3.6 [50]. A differential gene expression analysis was done with DESeq2 to identify significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the treatments for each time point, applying thresholds of a Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) adjusted p-value (padj) of less than 0.1 and a log2 fold change (LFC) of greater than |2|. To estimate the treatment effect, pairwise comparisons within each time point between non-treated (AA) and biopesticide treated (OO, PP, GIP) plants were performed. For the infection effect, only samples from T6 were considered, where within each treatment group, the XfDD infected samples were compared to the non-infected ones.

To estimate bacterial colonization from RNA-seq data, raw reads were first mapped to the Olea europaea reference genome using a splice-aware aligner (HISAT2). Unmapped reads were subsequently extracted and remapped to the Xylella fastidiosa reference genome [51]. Alignment statistics were used to retrieve uniquely mapped bacterial reads (paired_aligned_one), while multi-mapped reads were recorded separately. To account for differences in sequencing depth among libraries, Xylella-mapped reads were normalized to the total number of input reads for each sample. Normalized uniquely mapped reads were used as a conservative proxy for bacterial load, as they provide higher specificity and reduce potential bias due to repetitive or conserved bacterial regions. Bacterial abundance values were log10-transformed (log10[RPM + 1]) prior to visualization and statistical analyses. Statistical comparisons among treatments at T6 were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with BH correction for multiple testing.

Gene ontology analysis

Gene ontology (GO) terms were preliminarily added to the DEG lists (padj < 0.1 and LFC >|2|) based on the genome annotation file (GTF/GFF) from the genomic resource used above [43] with the help of the R package topGO v. 2.48.0 [52]. Then a specific organism package based on NCBI information (O. europaea var. sylvestris, NCBI: txid158386) was created using the R package AnnotationForge v. 1.38.1 [53]. A similarity matrix of GO terms was then created with the R package rrvgo v. 1.8.0 [54]. Reduced terms were further calculated (threshold = 0.7), and a treemap showing the GO terms belonging to the category “biological process” was finally plotted, without separating up- and downregulated genes, to provide a holistic overview of the activated processes.

In addition, GO term enrichment analysis was separately performed on up- and downregulated genes, using the ShinyGO platform (http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/goc/) where the cv. ‘Farga’ genome’s annotations and GO terms description were previously uploaded.

Olive treatments with PsJN and Sankari© for quantitative RT-PCR gene expression analysis

Three two-year-old olives of the cultivar ‘Ogliarola salentina’ were sprayed with PsJN bacterial suspension at ≈ 108 CFU/mL amended with 0.05% (v/v) of Silwet™ L-77 (Momentive) and maintained in greenhouse conditions. Three additional plants sprayed only with water served as untreated control. Twelve hours after the treatment (T2), leaves were detached to isolate total RNA using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, USA).

A further group of four olives of the same cultivar and age were sprayed with 0.05% (v/v) of Sankari© and leaves were sampled at 6, 24 and 72 h after the treatment to validate the expression of selected genes by quantitative RT-PCR. Two additional water-sprayed plants were sampled at the same time points and included in the molecular analysis as untreated control.

Validation of gene expression by quantitative RT-PCR

For each sample, total RNA was digested with RQ1 RNase-Free DNase (Promega Corporation, USA), reverse transcribed with M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and the cDNA was amplified in three technical replicates by qPCR. Based on the results obtained by RNAseq analysis, four genes were selected for qRT-PCR validation. Primers for ACC oxidase (ACCOXI), 12-oxophytodienoate reductase (OPR3), chloroplastic lipoxygenase 3 (LOX), and Pathogenesis-related protein 5 (PR5) were thus designed using Primer-Blast tool, available at NCBI website, and checked in silico for their specificity for the target transcript. Results

Antagonistic activity of Paraburkholderia phytofirmans PsJN and onion extract against XfDDin vitro

The in vitro antagonistic activities of Paraburkholderia phytofirmans strain PsJN and onion extract against XfDD were evaluated by dual culture and well-diffusion agar assays, respectively. As shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S1a, PsJN was not able to inhibit the growth of XfDD, whereas the onion extract inhibited the growth of XfDD already at low concentrations such as 0.05% (Additional file 1: Fig. S1b and Table 2).. A clear inhibitory effect of the onion extract on XfDD growth was observed at all tested concentrations. The inhibition zone increased progressively with increasing concentration, reaching the highest values at 2%, the maximum concentration evaluated. Linear regression analysis confirmed a positive dose–response relationship between onion extract concentration and bacterial growth inhibition (R2 = 0.764), indicating a concentration-dependent inhibitory effect. In contrast, the untreated control showed unrestricted bacterial proliferation across the agar surface (Table 2).

Transcriptome analysis

This study aimed to investigate the olive gene expression in two contexts: i) in response to biopesticides at both early and late time points after application, and ii) during the early response to Xylella infection in treated and untreated olive trees. To address these objectives, tissue samples were collected 6 or 12 h and 15 days following treatments, as well as 10 days after inoculation with XfDD (Fig. 1).

Read statistics

A total of 4,146.4 million raw sequences were generated as fastq files for the 96 samples, including fastqc and multiQC data. Quality-filtered paired reads achieved a unique mapping rate to the olive genome cv. ‘Farga’ of at least 90.9% across all samples, except for AA01. On average, an overall read mapping rate of 92.2% was observed. The number of successfully aligned reads ranged from 18.4 to 47.3 million (Additional file 5: Table S1).

Principal component analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) showed that major factors influencing gene expression were the time of sampling (Fig. 2a) and the XfDD inoculation (Fig. 2b). The same analysis considering the different treatments was not significant when all time points were included.

Because of this, additional PCAs were carried out focusing on single time points. T1/T2 sampling points showed that the PsJN-treated olives form a distinct cluster, similarly to plants treated with onion extract and Sankari© (Fig. 3a). Further elimination of the AA10 and OO7 outlier samples more clearly illustrates the separation of the untreated plants from all treated plants (Fig. 3b). Nevertheless, the grouping of biopesticide treated olives clearly indicates early (6 h/12 h) effects of all biopesticides on plant gene expression.

PCA of samples from T1 and T2, with (a) and without (b) the defined outlier, and from T5 (c) and T6 (d)

At the later time point, 15 days after the treatment (T5), olives treated with all biopesticides no longer distinctively clustered as they grouped with untreated control plants, indicating that late treatments effects ceased (Fig. 3c).

At time point 6 (T6, 10 days after XfDD inoculation), the clustering of the healthy treated olives (dots in Fig. 3d) was compact for PsJN, onion extract and Sankari©, whereas the untreated control plants were more dispersed. Sankari© clearly separated from all other treatments. A careful observation of the PCA points towards an effect of the XfDD infection since a clear separation between XfDD-infected versus non inoculated untreated control olives was observed in the onion- and PsJN-treated plants, and more consistently for Sankari©-treated samples (Fig. 3d).

Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis

To describe the early olive response for each biopesticide, a differential gene expression analysis was carried out among Sankari©-, onion- and PsJN-treated plants in comparison with the corresponding untreated controls at the different times of sampling.

DEGs at T1/T2

Although plants after Sankari© treatment were taken six hours (T1) after treatment at the earliest, and the samples of plants treated with the other two biopesticides (onion extract and PsJN) after 12 h (T2) at the earliest, they were analyzed together.

Six (T1) hours after application, 614 and 611 up- and downregulated genes, respectively, had altered expression in Sankari©-treated (GIP7-12) vs untreated (GIP1-6) olives (Fig. 4, Additional file 6: Table S2). Genes involved in plant stress response belonging to the Ethylene Responsive Factor (OE9A100232) and DMR6-LIKE OXYGENASE (OE9A047267, OE9A092818, OE9A002628) were upregulated (Additional file 6: Table S2, sheet UP FC > 2), while several dehydration-responsive element-binding factors (OE9A009930, OE9A005875, OE9A000299) were downregulated upon Sankari© treatment (Additional file 6: Table S2, sheet DOWN FC < −2). In line with this stress response, ShinyGO analysis points to the enrichment of the “Oxidoreductase activity” (GO:0016491) and “Transcription factor activity, sequence specific DNA binding” (GO:00037000) terms, for up- and downregulated genes, respectively (Additional file 6: Table S2, sheets ShinyGO UP FC > 2 and DOWN FC < −2).

Twelve (T2) hours after the treatment, 334 and 448 genes were up- and downregulated, respectively, in olives treated with the onion extract (OO7-12) compared with the same plants at T0 (OO1-6) (Fig. 4). Although many upregulated DEGs were not annotated (Additional file 7: Table S3, sheet UP FC > 2), a stimulated expression of wall-associated receptor kinase-like 8 (OE9A100467, OE9A069039) and pathogenesis-related STH-2-like genes (OE9A096921) were observed in onion extract-treated olives. Of note is the upregulation of cytokinin dehydrogenase 5 (OE9A094198), involved in the metabolic process of cytokinin, which is an important phytohormone involved in cell division as well as in plant immunity [56, 57]. Two dehydration-responsive element-binding 1E-like (OE9A005875) and 1 A (OE9A000299) [58] and two ethylene-responsive transcription factors (OE9A076184, OE9A051495) [59] were strongly downregulated in these plants (Additional file 7: Table S3, sheet DOWN FC < −2). The corresponding GO analysis showed the enrichment of the terms “Nitric oxide biosynthetic process” (GO:0006809) [60] and “Transcription factor activity, sequence-specific DNA binding” (GO:0003700) for up- and downregulated genes, respectively (Additional file 7: Table S3, sheets “ShinyGO UP FC > 2 and DOWN FC < −2”).

In PsJN-treated plants at T2 (PP7-12), 2542 up- and 889 downregulated genes (Fig. 4 and Table S4, sheets UP FC > 2 and DOWN FC < −2) were differentially expressed when compared with T0 (PP1-6). One of the main induced genes (ca. tenfold change stimulation) was a “probable glucan 1,3-beta-glucosidase A” (OE9A051307). Conversely, “polygalacturonase At1g48100-like” (OE9A018184) involved in plant cell wall remodeling [61] was strongly downregulated. A striking upregulation of several ethylene-responsive transcription factors (OE9A100232, OE9A062084, OE9A083187) was observed in PsJN-treated plants (Additional file 8: Table S4, sheet UP > 2), prompting further investigation into the ethylene biosynthetic pathway. Indeed, a 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate oxidase-like (ACCOXI, OE9A066037), a key enzyme in the biosynthesis of this hormone, was found to be upregulated in the PsJN-treated olives. The Gene Ontology analysis of PsJN-upregulated genes indicates the enrichment of two main terms, “Protein phosphorylation (GO:0006468) and “Protein kinase activity” (GO:0004672), both pointing to a stress effect induced by this bacterium. The most enriched term for downregulated genes was “Hydrolase activity, hydrolyzing O-glycosil compounds” (GO:0004553) (Additional file 8: Table S4, sheets “ShinyGOUP FC > 2 and DOWN FC < −2”).

As already observed on different plant species (Globachem, unpublished data), the application of Sankari© induced the expression of LOX, OPR and PR5 in a time-course analysis (Fig. 5). Due to the lack of a clear separation of the different treated olives at T5, we decided to focus our attention on the last time point, T6, since a clear grouping was observed after XfDD inoculation (Fig. 3d). This differential gene expression analysis had the objective of disentangling the main effect of the XfDD infection (infection effect, Fig. 6a) on plants preventively treated with biopesticides, by comparing XfDD infected samples in the context of mock or each treated group of plants. Infection effect

The lowest number of DEGs (24) was seen in within the untreated group by comparing the XfDD infected (AA25–AA27) with the non-infected olives (mock controls, AA28–AA30) (Fig. 6a). Interestingly, the infection was perceived differently in each treatment group, with little overlap between them. The most overlap was seen between Sankari© and onion (57 DEGs), followed by PsJN and control (10 DEGs). Sankari© and PsJN shared five DEGs, while PsJN and onion shared two. No DEGs were shared between three or more treatment groups.

An additive effect was observed in XfDD-infected olives preventively treated with PsJN (PP19-PP21) (95 DEGs), onion extract (217 DEGs), and Sankari© (574 DEGs), where the combined activities of XfDD and each single biopesticide likely occurred (Fig. 6a). The very limited response to XfDD infection in the untreated control samples (Mock group) is very likely due to the slow progress of infections. Within the 21 upregulated genes in the XfDD vs Mock comparison (Additional file 9: Table S5), genes coding for a pathogenesis-related STH-2-like (OE9A096921) and a “probable LRR receptor-like serine threonine- kinase At3g47570” were found (OE9A095595). Consistent with these data, which suggest a plant response to XfDD, the ShinyGO analysis showed the enrichment of “response to biotic stimulus” (GO:0009607), supported by a high FDR (1.30E-6) (Additional file 9: Table S5, sheet ShinyGO). Moreover, the enrichment of the term “abscisic acid binding” (GO:0010427) indicates an activated response to abiotic stresses [64] and stomata opening regulations. Confirming this plant response to the XfDD infection, the GO analysis indicates the enrichment of the terms “response to stress” (Additional file 3: Fig. S3, GO enrichment, biological process, of DEGs of XfDD-infected vs. non-infected at T6).

The infection effect is more evident in onion extract-treated plants (infected OO19-OO21 vs not infected OO22-OO24) at T6 (Fig. 6a), where a balanced ratio of up- (111 DEGs) and downregulated (106 DEGs) genes was observed. The most upregulated DEG in the onion-extract treated and XfDD-inoculated plants was a transcription factor CYCLOIDEA-like (OE9A012996), which belongs to the TCP family [65] (Additional file 10: Table S6). Moreover, a further dehydration-responsive element-binding 3-like transcription factor (OE9A102799), already differentially regulated at T2 by the same biopesticide, was present. By ShinyGO analysis, the 111 upregulated DEGs were enriched with the GO terms “Seed coat development” (GO: 0010214) and “Mitochondrial transport” (GO:0006839) (Additional file 10: Table S6, sheet ShinyGO UP DEGs). The rrvgo-based GO analysis supported the enrichment of the “heterocycle biosynthetic process” (GO:0018130), a generic term indicating an active biosynthesis of compounds having a cyclic molecular structure (Additional file 3: Fig. S3 GO enrichment, biological process, of DEGs of XfDD-infected vs. non-infected under onion-extract treatment at T6). Even though 106 DEGs were found to be downregulated in the XfDD-infected and onion-extract treated plants, their GO analysis resulted in only two enriched and scarcely (low FDR) supported pathways: “Ubiquitin-protein transferase activator activity” (GO:0097027) and “Anaphase-promoting complex binding” (GO:0010997) (Additional file 10: Table S6, sheet ShinyGO DOWN DEGs), both involved in the modulation of plant immunity.

Genes with diverse functions were differentially regulated in PsJN-treated and XfDD-infected olives (PP19-PP21) compared to PsJN-treated healthy (PP22-PP24) olives (Additional file 11: Table S7). The upregulation of an exocyst complex component EXO70A1-like (OE9A074401) and a delta(24)-sterol reductase (OE9A033397) point to the involvement of genes related to vesicle trafficking and membrane-associated proteins [66]; while a kirola-like (OE9A015659) indicates that a plant immune response is occurring in these plants. A moderate (FDR 5.4E-3) enrichment of genes belonging to the Ribulose-biphosphate carboxylase activity (GO: 0016984) was found with the ShinyGO analysis (Additional file 11: Table S7, sheet ShinyGO UP DEGs), confirmed by rrvgo-base enriched term “photorespiration” (Additional file 3: Fig. S3, GO enrichment, biological process, of DEGs of XfDD-infected vs. non-infected under PsJN treatment at T6). The involvement of these two latter pathways points to an altered functioning of the photosynthetic machinery in PsJN treated plants upon XfDD infection.

Five hundred fifty-four functionally annotated DEGs were found to be upregulated in XfDD-infected plants (GIP31-GIP33) compared to non-infected plants (GIP34-GIP36) treated with Sankari© (Fig. 6a), which mainly refer to transcription factors (probable WRKY transcription factor 53, OE9A042006; myb-related Myb4-like, OE9A041554) and plant defense-associated genes (Defense-related containing SCP domain, OE9A019272; wall-associated receptor kinase 2-like, OE9A078886; disease resistance RPM1-like, OE9A109149) (Additional file 12: Table S8). The corresponding ShinyGO showed enrichment of the biological processes “Mitochondrial transport” (GO:0006839) and “defense response, incompatible interaction” (GO:0009814), likely indicating the induction of the plant response strongly requesting energy in its support. The rrvgo-based GO analysis is in line with these results as an intense transcriptional activity (transcription, DNA-templated, GO: 0006351) occurred in these Sankari©-treated olives infected with XfDD (Additional file 3: Fig. S3, GO enrichment, biological process, of DEGs of XfDD-infected vs. non infected under Sankari© treatment at T6).

Gene enrichment analysis on the 20 downregulated DEGs in the XfDD-infected plants of Sankari©-treated plants resulted in poorly supported (small FDR) pathways. Because of this limited statistical support, the corresponding chart was not shown.

Treatment effect

The treatment-based changes of differential gene expression at 25 days after the treatment (T6) were studied by comparing treated and non-infected olives with the Mock (untreated and non-infected olives) (Fig. 6b). The lowest difference of gene expression between the treatment and the Mock plants was observed in the onion extract-treated olives (OO22-24) (146 DEGs), followed by the PsJN-treated group (PP22-24) (187 DEGs) (Fig. 6b). While a higher number of DEGs (526) were present in the Sankari©-treated olives (GIP34-36) compared to the Mock (AA28-30), most of them (486) were downregulated. The reaction to the treatments seemed to be very treatment-specific, as not many genes were shared among the treatments. PsJN and Sankari© had 49 DEGs in common, whilst Sankari© shared only four with onion, and onion shared only one with PsJN. Two DEGs were in common for all three treatments: OE9A114387, annotated as C-repeat dehydration-responsive element-binding factor 4, and OE9A116990, a pentatricopeptide repeat-containing At3g29230-like. Regarding the first, dehydration responsive element binding proteins (DBPs) are known to play an important role in enhancing plant tolerance to environmental stresses such as drought, cold, and high salinity [67, 68]. Only one DEG is shared between PsJN and onion, OE9A033588, a multicopper oxidase. Onion and Sankari© had 4 DEGs in common, whereas Sankari© shared 49 DEGs with PsJN.

At T6, the treatment with onion extract in comparison to mock (untreated and not inoculated) olives upregulated the transcription of several heat shock protein genes (class I heat shock -like, OE9A089849; heat stress transcription factor A-7a-like, OE9A086519; heat shock 83, OE9A071045), while the most downregulated gene was a different kirola-like gene (OE9A044979) (Additional file 13: Table S9). Confirming the excess of heat shock protein genes, the upregulated genes were enriched in the “Chaperone binding” (GO:0051087) term in ShinyGO analysis, while the rrvgo-based analysis evidenced the transcription of these genes, having the “transcription, DNA templated” as major term (Additional file 4: Fig. S4, GO enrichment, biological process of DEGs of onion-treated vs untreated (Mock) at T6).

A member of the NPF/NRT1/PTR family of plant transporters (NRT1 PTR FAMILY-like, OE9A010126) [69] was a major upregulated gene family in Sankari©-treated olives (Additional file 14: Table S10). Conversely several WRKY (OE9A042006; OE9A105450), ethylene-responsive (OE9A012128) and bHLH35 (OE9A057817) transcription factors were all downregulated upon Sankari© treatment. Consistently, both GO enrichment analyses converged to the enrichment of “Transcription factor activity, sequence specific DNA binding” (GO:0003700) and “transcription, DNA-templated” terms (Additional file 4: Fig. S4, GO enrichment, biological process, of DEGs of Sankari©-treated vs untreated, Mock, at T6).

Comparison of the PsJN-treated with the untreated (Mock) olives at T6, showed that the sole bacterium was able to induce a plant response. A Multicopper oxidase (OE9A033588), an alcohol dehydrogenase class-3-like (OE9A059588) and a DOWNY MILDEW RESISTANCE 6-like enzyme (OE9A079697) (Zeilmaker et al., 2015) were upregulated (Additional file 15: Table S11). The Shiny GO analysis of upregulated DEGs underlines this oxido reductase activity since it showed the enrichment of the “Nitrate reductase (NADH)” (GO:0009703) and oxidoreductase activity, acting on the CH-CH group of donors, NAD or NADP as acceptor” (GO:0016628), while rrvgo-based analyses indicated the enriched generic term “cellular lipid metabolic process” (GO:0044255). A strong (FDR 10–10) enrichment of genes related to cell wall remodeling (biological process “xyloglucan:xyloglucosyl transferase”, GO:0016762) was found among the 102 downregulated DEGs by ShinyGO (Additional file 15: Table S11, sheet ShinyGO DOWN DEGs) of plants treated with PsJN.

Effect of preventive treatments on Xylella fastidiosa colonization assessed by pathogen-mapped RNAseq reads

To assess whether treatments influenced bacterial colonization, host-unmapped reads were remapped to the Xylella fastidiosa genome and normalized uniquely mapped reads were used as an indicator of pathogen abundance. As expected, Xf reads were nearly absent at T5 across all treatments, consistent with the pre-infection stage. In contrast, a clear increase in bacterial reads was observed at T6 in infected plants, confirming successful colonization. Among treatments, onion- and PsJN-treated plants showed a lower median abundance of Xf reads compared to untreated controls at T6, suggesting a potential reduction in bacterial colonization (Fig. 7). However, these differences were not statistically significant after multiple testing correction (adjusted p-value = 0.12 for onion vs untreated), likely due to the high biological variability among independent plants (n = 3).

Discussion

The objective of reducing the agricultural use of chemical pesticides is increasingly urgent, in order to mitigate emergencies related to climate change and safeguard the environment [70]. A significant contribution to the realization of this goal may be provided by the incorporation of biopesticides into current crop management practices, either for plant growth or for controlling abiotic and biotic stresses. The promotion of biopesticide development, registration, and adoption is predicated on a foundation of in-depth knowledge concerning their mechanism(s) of action (MOA).

In the present work we aimed at elucidating the MOAs of selected biopesticides (PsJN, onion extract and Sankari©) on XfDD-infected and non-infected olives (O. europaea) by means of comparative transcriptomics (RNAseq), as a knowledge base for their further product development and registration. For this purpose, the trial was carried out in controlled greenhouse conditions, wherein olive plants of the XfDD susceptible cultivar, ‘Ogliarola salentina’, were cultivated. The same biopesticides are being assessed for their efficacy in controlling XfDD on olives in open field trials in the epidemic area of Salento, Italy. A description of these trials will be the object of a parallel manuscript as evaluation of the efficacy of biopesticides in natural conditions of infections needs several years of observation.

A preliminary in vitro test revealed that PsJN did not demonstrate antagonistic activity against XfDD, as previously reported [26], while the onion extract was able to inhibit the growth of the bacterium. A time course transcriptome analysis was performed at early (T1/T2) and late (T5 and T6) time points after treatments, with the objective to disentangle the biopesticide effects on healthy olive tissues. Subsequent late effects of the biopesticide treatments were studied at T6, 25 days after their application in treated olives, with half of the olives successively subjected to artificial XfDD inoculation.

The overall picture showed that the gene expression pattern changed with the progress of the time, homogeneously grouping plants collected at the same sampling time as indicated by PCA. Olives perceived the three biopesticides early 6–12 h after their application by spraying, modulating their gene expression. Plants treated with Sankari©, PsJN and onion extract had their gene expression profiles clearly different from those of untreated plants at the same sampling points (T1/T2). In the course of the time, 15 days after the application (T5), the biopesticides effects were no more observed although these became evident after XfDD inoculation. At T6, plants inoculated with XfDD and previously treated with the biopesticides had a stronger response to the bacterium in comparison with the untreated olives.

Transcriptomics analysis showed that six hours (T1) after its application Sankari© stimulates a plant immune response, as exemplified by the overexpression of a DMR6-LIKE OXYGENASE and an Ethylene Responsive Factor. An immune response which was also demonstrated in a parallel expression analysis of specific genes related to the Jasmonic and Salycilic acid pathways (Fig. 5), and that confers to this biopesticide the double property of insecticide and plant immune stimulator. DMR6-LIKE OXYGENASE is known to be induced during pathogen infection in A. thaliana, in which it is considered a susceptibility gene due to its ability to suppress plant immunity [71], by controlling the salicylic acid levels. Whereas Ethylene Responsive Factors belongs to one of the largest families of proteins whose functions span from plant growth to the response to environmental stimuli [72].

A similar effect of plant response was also observed by onion treatment at T2, where the upregulation of a wall-associated receptor kinase-like 8 (OE9A100467, OE9A069039) and pathogenesis-related STH-2-like genes (OE9A096921) was observed. In fact, a wall-associated receptor kinase-like 8 was induced during Xanthomonas citri subsp. citri infection in sweet orange [73], while the pathogenesis-related STH-2-like expression was elicited in potato by Phytophtora infestans [74]. In support of this early stimulation of the plant immune systems also stands the overexpression of a cytokinin deidrogenase 5, a cytokinin degrading enzyme deputed to the control of the hormone levels, whose expression is modulated during Botrytis cinerea infections in A. thaliana [57].

The differential expression of 1.187 genes was observed twelve hours (T2) after PsJN + Sylwet application (Fig. 6). In line with the known PsJN “double mechanisms” of action [18], either the plant immune [24, 25] and the plant growth pathways were stimulated by this microorganism, as shown respectively by the overexpression of a probable glucan 1,3-beta-glucosidase A, a known pathogenesis-related protein active in the degradation of fungi cell wall [75] and the upregulation of several ethylene-responsive transcription factors. These latter factors indicate that mechanisms aiming at the control of ethylene are ongoing in PsJN treated olives. Indeed, a key enzyme mediating the ethylene biosynthesis, a 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate oxidase-like (ACCOXI) was upregulated in PsJN + Sylwet treated olives at T2, by RNAseq analysis, likely reflecting a plant stress condition leading to the production of ethylene and the consequential induced expression of ethylene-responsive transcription factors. An ACCOXI upregulation which was also independently demonstrated by a parallel quantitative RT-PCR expression analysis, where a PsJN/Sylwet combined effect of stress response was observed (Fig. 5). It is known that PsJN encodes for a 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxilic acid (ACC) deaminase, an enzyme that degrades the ACC precursor of ethylene [18, 76]. Likely, to compensate the ACC deaminase depletion operated by the bacterial enzyme, plants over express ACCOXI (OE9A066037), that we found upregulated in PsJN-treated olives. Decreasing the ethylene levels is a common trait of plant growth promoting bacteria that express ACC deaminase, thus stimulating plant growth and making plants more resistant to biotic and environmental stresses [76].

Besides changes of gene expression observed among the different times of tissue sampling, XfDD inoculation was the additional perturbating factor at T6 (Fig. 2). Ten days after the inoculation, XfDD induced a very low number of DEGs in untreated olives, likely because of the slow rate of replication of this bacterium. Despite this low DEGs number XfDD alone upregulated the expression of a “probable LRR receptor-like serine threonine- kinase At3g47570”, a cell-wall associated receptor known to be involved in Pathogen Triggered Immunity (PTI) [77] and belonging to the LRR-XII subfamily, of which EF-Tu and Flagellin sensing 2 receptors are members. It was reported to be upregulated in Ponkan mandarin infected by a strain of Xylella fastidiosa subspecies pauca where it was supposed to trigger the PTI response in the early stages of infection in citrus [78]. Moreover LRR-LRK genes of the same subfamily were reported upregulated in the XfDD/olive pathosystems in our previous transcriptome study [40]. The GO analysis using two different pipelines converged on the enrichment of genes belonging to the GO term “response to stress” and the homologous pathway “response to biotic stimulus” in olives infected with XfDD. Moreover, the enrichment of the “abscisic acid binding” (GO:0010427) term points to the involvement of abscisic acid (ABA), a plant hormone known to play key roles in plant immunity, abiotic stress responses [64] and the regulation of stomatal opening, as previously observed in XfDD-infected olives [79]. DEG numbers were higher in XfDD inoculated and treated plants in comparison with the untreated, independently of the treatment, which likely indicates a sort of priming effect of the biopesticides to the presence of the bacterium that lasts up to 25 days after their application (Fig. 6). This plant response suggests a possible priming exerted by the biopesticides which induces a stronger response to XfDD in treated olives. Evidence of a priming ability of PsJN was already described in Arabidopsis thaliana where plants subjected to high salinity had a better tolerance to this stress [80]. These authors demonstrated that this stronger response was due to the capability of PsJN-bacterized plants to maintain over-time the expression of genes related to salt stress.

Onion-treated olives, successively inoculated with XfDD, display a general transcriptional reprogramming at T6, also indicated by the GO analysis of upregulated genes, which possibly indicates the need to molecularly sustain biosynthetic processes occurring during plant defense. An active reprogramming which is also shown by the analysis of the Shiny GO terms “Ubiquitin-protein transferase activator activity” (GO:0097027) enriched with downregulated genes, that indicate a control of the protein degradation mediated by Ubiquitin.

The two-faced behavior of PsJN was also evident at T6, in olives inoculated with XfDD. A DMR6-LIKE enzyme (OE9A079797), which was different from that induced by Sankari©, had increased expression during this interaction. As already discussed, DMR6 and DMR6-like enzymes modulate the salicylic acid levels during plant immunity processes. However, both GO analysis points to the stimulation of plant growth promoting activity as biological processes related to an increased photosynthesis and carbon fixation have higher numbers of upregulated DEGs.

Sankari©-treatment resulted in a clear gene upregulation even 25 days after its application in XfDD-inoculated olives.

The analysis of the biological processes enriched with upregulated DEGs indicates that this biopesticide increases the plant response to XfDD also providing the necessary energy, as exemplified by the enriched term “Mitochondrial transport and increasing DNA transcription”.

Comparison of the total DEGs in T2 and T6 showed a strong reduction in healthy olives treated with PsJN (T2: 1.187; T6: 187), that was limited in Sankari© (T2: 639; T6:526) and onion (T2: 360; T6: 146). Between the two times of sampling there was, however, opposite distribution of DEGs for Sankari© treated olives, which were mainly downregulated. An overview of the biological processes indicates that late effects of the onion and Sankari© are evident as shown by the enrichment of terms related to transcriptional activity, regulation of metabolic processes and ATP synthesis while processes sustained by PsJN are very limited and related to the remodelling of cell-wall. This suggests a different mode of action between bacteria-based and non-bacteria-based treatments.

In addition to the plant transcriptional response, the RNA-seq data also allowed to estimate the relative abundance of Xylella fastidiosa transcripts by mapping reads against the bacterial genome. Although RNA-seq was primarily designed to investigate host gene expression, the number of bacterial reads provided an indirect indication of pathogen presence within the plant tissues. Comparisons among treatments suggested a reduction in the relative abundance of Xylella reads in Onion treated plants, supporting the hypothesis that the biopesticide treatments may contribute not only to the activation of plant defense responses but also to a partial limitation of bacterial colonization.

The present work aims to complement information about the efficacy of these biopesticides in the control of XfDD infections in olives. The long time of symptoms to appear in infected olives and the specific epidemiology of this vector-transmitted pathogen, which is repeatedly inoculated during the season, makes field trials indispensable to evaluate the effectiveness of solution in natural conditions. Such evaluation is ongoing in Apulia. Results of these three-year-long field trials will be published in a parallel manuscript.

Conclusions

This study provides a comparative transcriptomic framework to elucidate the mechanisms of action of three biopesticides, Paraburkholderia phytofirmans strain PsJN, an onion-derived extract, and the natural insecticide Sankari©, in olive plants challenged with Xylella fastidiosa subsp. pauca. All treatments were rapidly perceived by olive plants and induced early transcriptional reprogramming, followed by an enhanced molecular response to XfDD inoculation several weeks after application.

Despite this shared outcome, the three biopesticides displayed distinct modes of action, involving different combinations of immune activation, hormone signaling, and metabolic reprogramming. These differences highlight the diversity of molecular strategies through which biopesticides can modulate plant responses, ranging from immune stimulation to growth-related processes.

The stronger transcriptional response observed in XfDD-inoculated plants previously treated with biopesticides supports the induction of a primed physiological state that persists over time. Such priming may represent a key component of sustainable disease management strategies, particularly for pathosystems such as XfDD–olive, characterized by slow pathogen replication and delayed symptom development.

Overall, this work demonstrates the value of transcriptomics-based approaches for dissecting biopesticide mechanisms of action and for providing a mechanistic basis to interpret efficacy data from long-term field trials. These insights contribute to the rational development, registration, and deployment of biopesticides as environmentally sustainable tools for managing Xylella fastidiosa infections in olive.
