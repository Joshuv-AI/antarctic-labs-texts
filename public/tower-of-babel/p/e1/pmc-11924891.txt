Stroke as the first manifestation of an atrial myxoma. Review of the literature
Authors: Mihai-Marius Botezat, Anca-Maria Istrate-Ofiţeru, Larisa Iovan, Elena Cristina Andrei, Cristian Militaru, Emanuel Botezat, Marian Valentin Zorilă, Sebastian Militaru, Ilona Mihaela Liliac
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Cardiac myxoma (CM) is the most common benign tumor of the heart and an important etiology of stroke in young adults. The study aimed to provide the latest literature data on CMs and, in particular, on the association between CM and cardioembolic ischemic stroke. It also discussed a specific instance of atrial myxoma (AM) in a young patient who suffered an ischemic stroke linked with familial cavernomatosis. The case presentation focuses on a 43-year-old woman showed up at the hospital’s Emergency Room for transient speech disorders lasting about 15 minutes. The physical examination revealed preserved mental and cognitive status. Cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed three areas of microvascular ischemia (frontal, parietal, and left occipital), in addition to multiple cavernomatous lesions. Because these microvascular ischemias were located in different vascular territories, we considered the cause to be embolic and completed the investigation with a transesophageal cardiac ultrasound that revealed a mobile and heterogeneous tumor mass in the left atrium. Histopathological investigation with immunohistochemistry proved the tumor to be a CM. The patient therapy included a multidisciplinary approach, which led to surgical excision of the tumor. The co-existence of cerebral cavernous malformations (CCMs) and AM might thus be coincidental or induced by a common genetic component. More cases are needed to verify or disprove the concept of a shared genetic basis for CCMs and myxoma.

Introduction

Cardiac myxoma (CM) is the most frequent “benign” tumor of the heart and poses a significant diagnostic difficulty [1]. The Latin word “myxoma” is derived from the Greek word “muxa”, which signifies mucus [2]. A majority of myxomas, over 75%, typically originate in the left atrium (LA), specifically at the mitral annulus or fossa ovalis border of the interatrial septum; 20% are thought to originate in the right atrium (RA), and 5% are thought to appear in both the atria and the ventricle [3, 4].

Although recent studies [5] suggest that CM’s neoplastic cell arises from multipotent mesenchymal cell, the pathogenesis remains poorly understood and their clinical manifestations might vary. There have been some previously recorded incidences of malignancy. In 20% of cases, CMs have no symptoms, and in 15% of cases, they cause sudden death [6].

According to some research, multipotent mesenchymal cells – which can differentiate into both neural and endothelial cells – are the source of myxoma cells [7]. Compared to men, women are more likely to develop these heart tumors. According to certain research, the ratio between the sexes is 3:1 [8], other research indicates that 53–77.4% of myxoma patients are female [9]. The tumor can be found in all age groups. The peak age among individuals with myxomas is approximately 56 years [10]. There are extremely few documented cases of atrial myxoma (AM) in pediatric patients [2, 11].

Aim

The aim of this study was to provide the latest literature data on CMs and, in particular, on the association between CM and cardioembolic ischemic stroke. We also discussed a specific instance of AM in a young patient who suffered an ischemic stroke linked with familial cavernomatosis, emphasizing the significance of multidisciplinary approaches to patient care.

Epidemiology

Females are more likely to develop AMs, which typically manifest in the fourth and sixth decades of life [11, 12]. For left and right AMs, the proportions of women to men are 2.05:1 and 0.75:1, respectively, based on specific research [4]. There may be between 0.5 and 1 instance of CM per million people per year [13, 14]. There are two epidemiological types of CM: familial and sporadic. The first type accounts for over 95% of instances, making it significantly more prevalent than the second [15].

Clinical manifestation

Intracardiac blockage, embolic signs, and constitutional symptoms are the three hallmarks of CMs. They can, however, be completely asymptomatic, exhibit traditional symptoms, or result in a potentially fatal systemic embolization or even sudden cardiac death because to variations in location, size, morphology, and histology [16]. Constitutional symptoms include fatigue, fever, and weight loss. Nonetheless, the majority of patients have symptoms from left AMs through three main mechanisms such as mitral valve blockage, peripheral or cerebral systemic embolization, and constitutional symptoms. Dizziness, heart palpitations, breathing difficulties and congestive heart failure (CHF) are among the most frequent early symptoms that prompt a diagnosis of mitral valve blockage. About 50% of patients experience obstructive cardiac symptoms as their triggering symptoms, however up to 70% may experience them at any time [1]. Some individuals experience systemic symptoms such as fever, and arthralgias as part of a clinical condition that might be mistaken for bacterial endocarditis or collagen vascular disease [17]. Left AMs were strongly linked to an increased risk of systemic embolization because of the elevated systolic pressure and the location, especially in the retinal arteries, spleen, adrenal glands, kidneys, abdominal aorta, femoropopliteal, and iliac, as well as the central nervous system [18]. Patients may therefore present with a range of symptoms, including hemiplegia, visual loss, chest discomfort, dyspnea, and transient ischemic stroke. The most dangerous side effects of embolization linked to left-sided AMs are probably neurological abnormalities [19]. When CM symptoms are left-sided and larger than 5 cm in circumference, they are more noticeable. In a recent study, 46.2% of patients had embolism, 16% breathing difficulties, 8% constitutional symptoms, 8% syncope, and 23.1% unintentionally detected myxoma [20].

Positive diagnosis

Most of the patients have an abnormal electrocardiogram (ECG) and LA enlargement is quite often encountered, while atrial arrhythmias and conduction disturbances are significantly fewer common findings associated with CMs [21].

Chest radiography reveals nonspecific changes, showing signs of elevated LA pressure like cardiomegaly–LA enlargement, redistribution of pulmonary blood flow or pulmonary edema. Sometimes intracardiac tumoral calcification may be seen, especially in right AMs [22].

Echocardiography is usually the first diagnostic tool used, mostly because of its wide availability [23]. In addition, it is cost-effective, portable and it is also non-ionizing. Two-dimensional transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is particularly valuable in the assessment of the location, size, attachment, morphological aspects and mobility of the cardiac mass. Furthermore, the hemodynamic effects of the tumor may be accurately evaluated using TTE. Based on their morphological features, myxomas are divided into polypoid and papillary. Polypoid myxomas typically present as a solitary, hyperechogenic, mobile mass, that is attached through a narrow stalk or pedicle to the subendothelial layer. They have a smooth, compact surface or a “cluster of grapes” aspect and they are usually associated with obstructive symptoms. By contrast, papillary tumors are smaller, friable, they have a villous surface and are more prone to embolize. In three thirds of the cases, they are located in the LA, usually adjacent to the fossa ovalis, but they may also be found in the RA (20%) and exceptionally in the ventricles. Rarely, they may originate from the cardiac valves [24].

Atypical, as well as multifocal, localization of the tumor suggests a genetic disorder like Carney complex. Approximately 7% of the myxomas occur within the Carney syndrome, which is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, and it is related with lentiginosis and endocrine overactivity [25]. These tumors tend to present at younger ages (i.e., second decade) and have a higher recurrence rate compared to the sporadic myxomas.

However, American Association of Cardiology (AAC) recommends advanced cardiac imaging modalities, such as transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) as first approach for the diagnosis and assessment of a suspected cardiac mass [26]. It is particularly helpful in detecting small lesions (<1 cm). In addition, it provides more accurate data regarding mass heterogeneity, stalk analysis and anatomical relationships, especially if it is combined with real-time three-dimensional echocardiography (RT3DE). The use of ultrasound-enhancing agents (UEAs) facilitates the evaluation of the CM’s perfusion and aids the early identification of thrombi.

For further assessment of the tumor, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides a better tissue characterization and may even aid the pathological diagnosis. Usually, myxomas present a heterogenous signal due to the mixed composition, which may display a gelatinous appearance with areas of hemorrhage, ossification or necrosis. They are hyperintense on T1- and T2-weighted images, hypointense on first pass perfusion (FPP) and show heterogenous enhancement on myocardial delayed enhancement (MDE) imaging [27].

On cardiac computed tomography (CT), myxomas are revealed as intracavitary masses with an attenuation lower or equivalent to the myocardium. Given the limited soft tissue resolution, compared to cardiac MRI, cardiac CT is recommended when cardiac MRI is not available or contraindicated. Additionally, cardiac CT is used when obstructive coronary artery disease is suspected, for the calcified masses assessment and global chest examination.

Histopathological and immunohistochemical examinations

Gross pathology

CMs are typically single pedunculated tumors, with variable, soft to firm consistency. They may also have a broad base and are attached to the endocardial surface, usually in the LA. Although most of them are 4 cm to 8 cm in diameter, their dimensions may vary from a few millimeters to 16 cm. Their weight ranges between 15 g and 180 g, with a mean of 37 g [28]. About 50% of them have a smooth and lobulated surface, whereas the other half presents a gelatinous frondlike surface. On cross section, they may exhibit cystic changes, foci of calcification or hemorrhage [29].

Histopathology

Microscopically, myxomas are composed of sparse cellular components and a myxomatous matrix. The cells are usually arranged in single or multiple layers around thin-walled blood vessels [30]. The myxoma cell is a fusiform, stellate or polyhedral (lepidic) cell, with a slightly acidophilic cytoplasm and a round or oval nucleus, that has a variable staining’s intensity and inconspicuous nucleoli. Mitotic activity is exceptional. Multinucleated cells can also be seen. Other cells that may be encountered are fibroblasts, macrophages and smooth muscle cells [31]. They are embedded in an abundant ground substance that is rich in acid mucopolysaccharides, which are hyaluronidase sensitive. The myxoid stroma stains positive with Alcian Blue and it shows a patchy pattern to Periodic Acid–Schiff (PAS) and mucicarmine stains. Prominent connective tissue fibers are seen mostly in the tumor’s stalk and the presence of cyst-like areas, fibrous areas and foci of dystrophic calcification within the same mass demonstrates the extensive variability among the tumors. Hemorrhage is usually seen directly, but indirect signs like Gamna–Gandy bodies or hemosiderin-laden macrophages are also frequently found. Rarely, glandular structures, extramedullary hematopoiesis and thymic rest may be present within the mass [32].

Immunohistochemistry

On immunohistochemical (IHC) study, most of the myxoma cells stain positive to calretinin [33] and vimentin, and they are negative to the keratin stain. The reaction to the anti-S100 and anti-neuron-specific enolase (NSE) antibodies is variable. The endothelial cells from the vascular channels demonstrate a positive reaction to the anti-von Willebrand factor (vWF), anti-cluster of differentiation (CD)31 and anti-Ulex europaeus agglutinin I (UEA I) antibodies. Protein kinase cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)-dependent type 1 regulatory subunit alpha (PRKAR1A) immunostaining is quite a sensitive screen method for Carney complex [34].

Differential diagnosis

Cardiac echocardiography is the investigation that can guide the diagnosis of intracardiac formations. In the case of AMs, it is important to make a differential diagnosis with intracardiac thrombi, the rest of the primitive cardiac tumors and malignant cardiac tumors. Another important investigation for the differential diagnosis is cardiac MRI. In the case of intracardiac thrombus, the specific localization is in the left ventricle (close to the akinetic segments) or in the LA (which is enlarged in volume). On ultrasonographic investigation, a homogeneous echogenicity is observed, not emphasized by contrast medium. At cardiac MRI, the thrombus is homogeneous, with hyperintensity/hypointensity pattern with short time intensity and hypo-intensity with long time intensity scout; no FPP [after gadolinium (Gd) injection] or late Gd enhancement. In the case of benign tumors, the specific localization is in the LA or in the aortic and mitral valves. At cardiac ultrasonography, a single intracardiac mass is observed, with a mobile, narrow implantation base, with low enhancement after contrast-medium injection (CMI). On cardiac MRI in rhabdomyomas and fibroids, possible late Gd enhancement and FPP (after Gd injection) in myxomas and hemangiomas. In the case of malignant cardiac tumors, the specific localizations include multiple cardiac cavities (including pericardium), LA, RA, right cavities and pulmonary artery trunk. Ultrasound examination may reveal multiple intracardiac masses with wide base of implantation, that infiltrate neighboring structures and show moderate-to-high enhancement after CMI. In cardiac MRI, FPP (after Gd injection) and late Gd enhancement are most frequently seen. Histological and IHC diagnosis is the diagnostic of certainty [35, 36].

Treatment

In the case of AMs, the curative treatment is surgical excision. As a rule, surgical removal of AMs is a successful operation, with a low operative mortality rate of between 0 and 3%. The most commonly used surgical approach is median sternotomy, but video-assisted cardioscopy and minimally invasive partial sternotomy have also been used successfully [35, 37]. In most situations, surgery is curative, as recurrence rates are between 0–3%, except in hereditary syndromes where recurrence rates are between 12–22%. Postoperative complications include supraventricular arrhythmias, the most common of which is atrial fibrillation [38].

Case presentation

We describe the instance of a 43-year-old woman who showed up at the Emergency Department of the Emergency County Clinical Hospital, Craiova, Romania for transient speech disorders lasting about 15 minutes. She had no known history of heart disease and was not taking any medication at that time. The physical examination revealed a preserved mental and cognitive status, no signs of pulmonary or systemic venous congestion, normal peripheral oxygen saturation, rhythmic heart sounds, a heart rate of 66 beats/minute, and blood pressure of 130/70 mmHg.

Neurological examination: no neck wrinkling, no cranial nerve signs, no comparative motor deficits, no coordination disorders, no deep or superficial sensibility disorders, no speech disorders, osteotendinous reflexes present, symmetrical, plantar cutaneous reflex in bilateral flexion, conscious, cooperative, temporo-spatially oriented. Usual blood tests, ECG, and CT skull were done in the Emergency Department; the diagnosis of transient ischemic stroke was established, and the patient was admitted to the Neurology Department for more testing and specialized care.

At 48 hours after hospitalization, cranial CT was repeated, which showed no changes in the brain substance, reason for which the investigation was completed by cranial MRI, which showed three areas of microvascular ischemia, with a maximum diameter of 7 mm (frontal, parietal, and left occipital), in addition to multiple cavernomatous lesions (Figures 1, 2, 3). Because these microvascular ischemias were located in different vascular territories, we considered the cause to be embolic and completed the investigation with a transesophageal cardiac ultrasound that revealed a mobile and inconsistent tumor mass located in the LA with normal-sized heart chambers that protrudes toward the left ventricle during diastole and is connected to the interatrial septum by a long, narrow stalk, a normal ejection fraction (most likely a myxoma), and a patent foramen ovale, both of which could be the cause of ischemic stroke (Figure 4A, 4B). In the following days, further investigations were performed in order to rule out other possible causes for a stroke in a young person, namely: tests for thrombophilia, vasculitis, or systemic diseases. All collected blood tests were within normal limits, and the cause of the embolism was established as AM.

This patient’s case was discussed in a multidisciplinary team including neurologists, radiologists, cardiologists, and cardiovascular surgeons, and in agreement with the patient, it was decided to perform surgery to remove the tumor formation (Figure 5). The surgical objective was complete resection of the tumor formation, removal of the implantation base, and closure of the patent foramen ovale.

The macroscopic appearance of the tumor with large size, irregular margins, yellowish color and areas of hemorrhage was suggestive for cardiac myxoma

Immediately after resection, the tumor formation was sent to the Pathological Anatomy Department of Emergency County Clinical Hospital, Craiova, where, following the traditional histopathological procedure, it was embedded in a paraffin block after being fixed for 48 hours in a 10% neutral buffered formalin solution. Subsequently, sections with a thickness of 4 μm were made using microtomes, which were initially stained in classical histological techniques with Hematoxylin–Eosin (HE), Goldner–Szekely (GS) trichrome, and PAS–Hematoxylin. Microscopically, a diffuse tumor proliferation consisting of fusiform cells and uni- or multinucleated stellate cells with hyperchromatic nuclei has been described. The tumor cells are separated by an abundant myxoid stroma and a rich vascular network with dilated blood vessels. Hemorrhagic areas, reduced hemosiderin pigment, rare lymphocytes, and plasmacytes were also noted. The described appearance is suggestive of a benign mesenchymal tumor lesion, most likely myxoma (Figures 6, 7, 8, 9).

The diagnosis of certainty was established by histopathological examination, which showed that the tumor was a CM. The postoperative course of the patient was favorable; there were no ineffective complications and no other cerebral emboli.

Discussions

Because CM is an uncommon condition, its precise prevalence is unclear. In the general population, the stated prevalence is 0.03% [39]. There may be 0.5 to one case of CM per million individuals each year [13, 14].

Compared to left AMs, right AMs typically have a larger connection to the atrial wall or septum and are more robust and sessile [6]. The majority of cases are sporadic, with 10% or so being familial and transmitted by autosomal dominant type. About 50% of familial cases had multiple tumors, which are more often found in the ventricle. They typically have a flat or lobulated surface. They are also gelatinous. The tumor’s motility is determined by the length of the stalk and its degree of adherence to the interatrial septum [2].

It has been demonstrated that up to 30–50% of patients experience embolic events [40], one of the clinical symptoms of CM, which can be caused by detachable tumor tissue, released thrombotic material covering the tumor, or a combination of both [41, 42]. Over half of embolic events impact unfortunately the retinal arteries and the central nervous system [43] due to the common left-sided location of CM, with 75–90% occurring in the LA of the heart [15].

It has been demonstrated that CM-related cerebrovascular episodes (CVEs) are recurring [1, 44], usually manifesting as embolic ischemic events and infrequently as hemorrhagic events [19]. Despite the fact that CVE is prevalent among CM patients, CM is a relatively uncommon cause of CVEs, making up just 0.5% of all these instances [45, 46].

Additionally, the myxoma may provide a risk of valve obstruction and, if the tumor continues to develop, a blockage of the left ventricular outflow pathway. These people may be more susceptible to sudden cardiac death or acute cardiogenic shock [47].

For the diagnosis and description of cardiac mass lesions, TEE has surpassed TTE [48]. A polypoid or papillary mass connected to the interatrial septum via a stalk and migrating back and forth into the cavity, occasionally expanding beyond the atrioventricular valve into the corresponding ventricular cavity, are the hallmark characteristics of an AM in echocardiography. Areas of calcification or liquefaction can occasionally appear inside the tumor mass. The hemodynamic effects of CM are demonstrated by Doppler echocardiography. Distinguishing between myxoma and thrombus during echocardiography is vital. Usually, myxomas display a stalk, like to adhere to the atrial septum’s limbus fossa ovalis, and exhibit distinctive motility. Conversely, the thrombus is often located in the atrium’s posterior region, prefers the atrial appendage, appears layered, and is most frequently observed when valvular mitral stenosis and atrial fibrillation are present [49, 50, 51].

Prior to the discovery of angiocardiography in 1951, CM was diagnosed exclusively by autopsy [52]. Additional imaging modalities such as MRI and CT scanning play a helpful role. Electrocardiography and chest X-rays are not particularly valuable. Hematological tests are also performed frequently. Histopathology validates the diagnoses. Genetic testing is critical in family instances of myxomas.

Once diagnosed, the tumor must be removed as soon as possible [47, 53, 54]. Following surgical excision, individuals with single myxomas have had favorable prognoses. Compared to other cardiac procedures, postoperative consequences were similar. After an AM is removed, the hospital mortality rate is typically around 4% [13]. Recurrence rates appear to be minimal for all current surgical methods. Up to 22 years following surgery, 0.4–5% of individuals have been documented to experience late recurrences [55].

Embolic consequences or unexpected death are always conceivable, even in asymptomatic people: 8% of patients in various studies [47, 53, 54]. Only one-third of individuals have abnormal serological and hematological tests [15].

Up to 10% of AM patients show up symptom-free. Nonetheless, the majority of patients have symptoms from left AMs through three main mechanisms: mitral valve blockage, peripheral or cerebral systemic embolization, and constitutional symptoms. Dizziness, dyspnea, palpitations, and CHF are among the most frequent early symptoms that prompt a diagnosis of mitral valve blockage [1].

CM was divided into two categories based on its gross appearance: type 1, which is associated with an uneven or villous surface and a fine and soft consistency, and the second type, with a smooth surface and a firm consistency [56]. Histopathological examination showed a benign, soft tumor, consisting of polygonal/stellate cells, without nuclear atypia, isolated or clustered, surrounded by a myxoid stroma. Embolic potential is generally determined by mobility rather than the size of the myxoma [19]. The most common neurological consequences of AM are cerebral infarctions produced by detached thrombus from the myxoma, with tumor fragments occurring rarely [19].

Differential diagnoses comprise thrombotic lesions, various cardiac tumors, and, in rare cases, neoplastic invasion of the inferior caval vein, such as a pheochromocytoma. MRI can also determine tissue density and identify myxomas from calcified and fatty tumors [14].

CMs are occasionally identified for the first time during pregnancy, most commonly with echocardiography. A recent review of 44 studies with 51 patients found that surgical therapy of CM in pregnant women resulted in favorable mother and feto-neonatal outcomes [57].

CMs typically develop sporadically; however, recurring or familial occurrences have been reported. The estimated recurrence rate ranges between 5% and 14% [1]. In the presented case, we did not find a family history of myxoma.

Comprehensive genetic and immunological screening may identify family members susceptible to myxoma or for a potential return of the illness in myxoma families [14].

Cerebral cavernous malformations (CCMs) are hamartomatous dysplasias defined by improperly dilated vascular channels.

CCM happens seldom, and several cases clearly imply that the illness is inherited. In a 2007 case study, numerous CCMs, hepatic cavernoma, and cardiac AM – all of which are thought to be the consequence of abnormal mesenchymal cell differentiation processes – coexisted with CCMs. As a result, the co-occurrence of CCM and AM may be accidental or the result of a shared genetic component. To confirm or infirm the idea that CCM and myxoma have a genetic foundation, more instances are required [34].

Conclusions

The particularity of the present case is the diagnosis of AM in a young patient with no neurological or cardiac history who was presented to the Emergency Department for neurological symptoms and was diagnosed with ischemic stroke. Although cardiac tumors require a multidisciplinary approach to be diagnosed, surgical resection remains the mainstay treatment for these patients.
