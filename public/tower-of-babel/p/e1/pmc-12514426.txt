Prognosis and Follow‐Up Recommendations for Subcutaneous and Dermal Leiomyosarcoma: Local Recurrence, Metastasis, and Overall Survival in a Danish Nationwide Cohort of 661 Patients
Authors: Kiya Abebe, Mathias Ørholt, Erik E. F. Bak, Andreas Larsen, Anne Lene Wagenblast, Grethe Schmidt, David Hebbelstrup Jensen, Michael M. Petersen, Anand C. Loya, Søren Daugaard, Mikkel Herly, Jason L. Hornick, Thomas Mentzel, Peter Vester‐Glowinski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectivesThe prognostic differences between subcutaneous leiomyosarcoma and dermal leiomyosarcoma are not well defined due to limited cohort sizes and insufficient follow‐up in prior studies. This study aimed to investigate the clinicopathological features of subcutaneous and dermal leiomyosarcoma and estimate their 10‐year rates of metastasis, local recurrence, and overall survival. Additionally, we propose standardized follow‐up recommendations.MethodsAll patients diagnosed with cutaneous leiomyosarcoma in Denmark from 1980 to 2022 were included. The prognosis was estimated using age‐ and sex‐standardized stratified cause‐specific Cox‐regression with all‐cause mortality as competing risk.ResultsWe included 196 patients with subcutaneous leiomyosarcoma and 465 with dermal leiomyosarcoma. The 10‐year local recurrence rate was similar in patients with subcutaneous leiomyosarcoma (15%) and dermal leiomyosarcoma (11%, p = 0.13). However, patients with subcutaneous leiomyosarcoma had a significantly higher 10‐year risk of metastasis (25%), primarily observed in grade 2 and 3 tumors, compared with dermal leiomyosarcoma (2.7%), p < 0.001, and a lower 10‐year‐overall survival (56% vs. 64%), p = 0.02.ConclusionsGrade 2 and 3 subcutaneous leiomyosarcoma should be classified as a high‐risk sarcoma with a substantial risk of metastasis and poor overall survival, necessitating follow‐up that includes both clinical examinations and PET/CT or CT of the thorax for 5 years to detect both locoregional and distant metastases. Dermal leiomyosarcoma should be considered a low‐risk sarcoma due to its low risk of metastasis and moderate risk of local recurrence, suggesting that the follow‐up can focus on clinical examinations for 4 years as the 10‐year risk of local recurrence is < 1% after this time point.

Introduction

Cutaneous leiomyosarcoma (cLMS) is a rare mesenchymal neoplasm originating from smooth muscle cells in the dermis and subcutis [1]. It has been hypothesized that the prognosis depends on the depth of the tumor [2], where subcutaneous LMS (scLMS) is regarded as highly aggressive, exhibiting a significant risk of local recurrence and metastasis [3, 4, 5, 6]. In contrast, dermal LMS (dLMS) originating in the dermis is considered locally aggressive but rarely metastasizing [7, 8, 9, 10]. However, the current literature on the prognosis of patients with scLMS and dLMS presents conflicting evidence concerning the risks of local recurrence and metastasis. For scLMS, the risk estimates range from 0% to 61% for local recurrence and 27%–75% for metastasis [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. For dLMS, the risk of local recurrence range from 0% to 66.7%, and metastasis between 0% and 14% [7, 13, 15, 16, 17, 18]. These risk estimates are however highly uncertain due to small patient cohorts [13, 17, 18], possible misclassifications, lack of depth‐based tumor stratification [3, 4, 12, 14, 17, 19, 20] and insufficient follow‐up [3, 7, 16]. Furthermore, evidence‐based guidelines for the treatment and follow‐up are lacking as only one expert‐based guideline offer recommendations for the management of cutaneous LMS [21].

Additional evidence is required to assess whether the prognostic differences between patients with scLMS and dLMS warrant distinct therapeutic and follow‐up protocols. This study aimed to investigate the clinicopathological features and estimate the 5‐ and 10‐year rates of local recurrence, metastasis, and overall survival in patients with scLMS and dLMS in a large, nationwide cohort of patients with long‐term follow‐up.

Materials and Methods

The study has been conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines and the Helsinki Declaration [22, 23].

The Danish National Health Registers

All Danish residents are given a unique and personal identification number (CPR‐number), which enables accurate individual‐level linkage of data across all the National Danish Health Registers and provides unique possibilities for nationwide epidemiological studies [24]. The registers have been described in detail previously [25].

Study Design and Participants

All patients in Denmark with a diagnosis of LMS between 1980 and 2022 were included. Patients with dermal and subcutaneous LMS were identified using the SNOMED [26] codes for LMS (M88903/4/9/X) and topography codes for the skin (T01‐T02), subcutaneous tissue (T03), and unspecified soft tissue (TY) from the Danish National Pathology Register. Based on this, a total of 1314 unique patients were identified, and all corresponding pathology reports were reviewed manually by the first author (KA). To ensure correct classification, all equivocal cases were discussed with the other first author (MØ). Additionally, all equivocal cases were manually reviewed in our electronic medical record system when applicable, and relevant surgical‐ or cross‐sectional imaging reports, and other clinical documentation were reviewed to clarify the anatomical location. If the anatomical location could not be determined with sufficient certainty, the case was classified as deep‐seated and subsequently excluded.

Patient and tumor characteristics included age at presentation, sex, anatomical location, pathologic margin status, tumor size, invasion depth, FNCLCC‐grade [27] and perineural/intravascular infiltration. Necrosis and mitotic grade were extracted for dLMS as these are not routinely FNCLCC‐graded in Denmark. Treatment characteristics were available in a subset of the patients from 2014 to 2022 and included excision margins and depths, and oncologic treatment with radiotherapy and chemotherapy.

Tumor Diagnosis and Classification

All tumors were diagnosed according to the WHO guidelines for sarcomas and managed at specialized referral sarcoma centers by sarcoma pathologists and surgeons according to national guidelines [28]. ScLMS was defined as LMS arising within the subcutaneous tissue above the deep fascia [29], whereas dLMS was defined as an LMS arising within the dermis and included both tumors with and without subcutaneous extension, such as atypical intradermal smooth muscle neoplasms (AISMN) [30, 31, 32]. In all cases of metastatic LMS, pathology reports were manually reassessed to ensure that an accurate diagnosis was made and to prevent misclassification. A wide panel of immunohistochemical markers such as SMA, caldesmon, vimentin, desmin, cytokeratins (pancytokeratin, AE1/AE3, CK5/6, EMA), melanocytic markers (S100, SOX10, HMB‐45), and vascular markers (CD31, CD34, ERG) was routinely applied to distinguish LMS from other spindle‐cell neoplasms [33].

We only included patients who underwent wide local excision (WLE) according to national guidelines. Patients who had a punch, shave, or excisional biopsy were only included if they were subsequently treated with WLE. Non‐cutaneous LMS and patients with metastases at presentation were excluded.

Definitions

Local recurrence was coded as a repeated LMS diagnosis at the anatomic site of the primary LMS or accompanied by a local recurrence code, whereas metastasis was coded as an LMS diagnosis accompanied by a supplementary metastasis code. Clinically and radiologically suspected metastases (i.e., non‐biopsied metastases) were identified using the ICD‐8/10 metastasis codes obtained from the National Patient Register (DC78‐9) in cases of no other competing cancers. Excision margins refer to the intended clinical surgical margins as documented in the surgical reports, and margin status refers to the microscopic assessment of margins described in the pathology reports.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were to investigate the clinicopathological features and to provide 5‐ and 10‐year estimates of local recurrence, metastasis, and overall survival in patients with scLMS and dLMS. Additionally, we propose a follow‐up program for patients with scLMS stratified on FNCLCC grade and for dLMS.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline demographics were described as simple proportions or medians with interquartile range (IQR). Baseline differences were tested with chi‐squared tests if categorical or Kruskal‐Wallis tests if continuous. The distributions of continuous variables were assessed visually with histograms, QQ‐plots, and tested with Shapiro‐Wilks tests. The risks of local recurrence, metastasis, and overall survival were estimated with stratified cause‐specific Cox regressions with all‐cause mortality as a competing risk. All models were age‐ and sex‐standardized using G‐computation to account for potential confounding [34]. Differences between the prognosis of scLMS and dLMS were compared with pointwise risk differences at five and ten years and tested with one‐sample t‐tests [35]. The assumptions of proportional hazards were visually tested with Schoenfield residuals for each cause‐specific Cox regression submodel and were not violated [36]. Conditional time‐to‐event analysis was used to calculate the 10‐year estimates of local recurrence, metastasis, and overall survival conditioned by passed time without an event. All results are presented with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) or interquartile ranges (IQR) and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All plots and analyses were performed in R, version 4.3.2. with the packages riskRegression, prodlim, rms, and tidyverse [37, 38, 39, 40].

Results

Patient Demographics

Between 1980 and 2022, a total of 17,561 soft‐tissue and visceral sarcomas were identified. LMS accounted for 2317/17,561 (13%) of the cases, of which 1324/2317(56%) were soft‐tissue LMS. Among the soft‐tissue LMS, 661/1324 (50%) were cutaneous with 465 (70%) being dermal and 196 (30%) being subcutaneous. The overall distribution of sarcomas is shown in Figure 1. The mean incidence rate was 1.21 (95% CI 0.492–2.52) per million person‐years (PMPY) for scLMS and 2.74 (95% CI 1.56–4.49) PMPY for dLMS. The median age at diagnosis was similar between the two groups. Patients with scLMS had a more balanced gender distribution, whereas most of the patients with dLMS were males (71%). For scLMS, most tumors developed in the extremities (66%) and trunk (29%), whereas for dLMS, most tumors occurred in the lower extremity (35%) and trunk (30%). The median follow‐up time was 17 years (IQR 8–24 years) for patients with scLMS and 14 years (IQR 7–23) for patients with dLMS. Tumor Characteristics

The median tumor size at the time of diagnosis was 35 mm for scLMS compared to 16 mm for dLMS, p < 0.001. Invasion through the deep fascia was seen in 27 patients with scLMS (14%) and in 13 patients with dLMS (6%). Perineural and intravascular invasion was significantly more frequent in patients with subcutaneous LMS (6%) compared with dLMS (1.1%), p < 0.001. The frequency of necrosis and the mitotic grade were both associated with increasing tumor depth, with 49% of the scLMS having necrosis present in the tumor compared to 11% in dLMS, and 40% of the scLMS having a mitotic grade 3 compared to 21% in dLMS, p < 0.001. Patients with scLMS frequently presented with high malignancy grades with FNCLCC grade 2 in 41% of the cases and FNCLCC grade 3 in 40% of the cases, p < 0.001.

Surgical Characteristics

Detailed information on treatment regimens was available in > 95% of the patients in the period of 2014–2022, including 32 patients with scLMS (16%) and 105 patients with dLMS (23%). The median clinical surgical excision margins were 20 mm (IQR 20–30) for scLMS and 10 mm (IQR 10–20) for dLMS. Most patients with scLMS were treated with WLE with 82% involving fascia or muscle, whereas most patients with dLMS were treated to the fascia (35%) or including the fascia (51%), p < 0.001. A total of 16 patients with scLMS (8%) and 42 patients with dLMS (9%) had positive pathologic surgical margins after primary and subsequent re‐excisions but did not undergo further surgery. Among these patients with positive margins, 7 out of 16 (44%) with scLMS and 25 out of 42 (60%) with dLMS most likely abstained from additional surgery due to the presence of synchronous or metachronous cancers of different origins (p = 0.34). Risk of Local Recurrence

The 10‐year local recurrence rate was 15% (95% CI 10–20) for scLMS and 11% (95% CI 8–14) for dLMS, yielding a risk difference of 4% (95% CI −2–10), p = 0.13. The median time to local recurrence was 10 months (IQR 6‐32) for scLMS and 94% of the local recurrences occurred within 5 years post‐surgery, resulting in a 10‐year conditional risk of local recurrence of < 1% after 5 years. The median time to local recurrence was 8 months (IQR 6–25) for dLMS and 90% of the local recurrences in dLMS occurred within 4 years post‐surgery, resulting in a 10‐year conditional risk of local recurrence of < 1% after 4 years.

Two of 31 patients (6%) with scLMS and ten of 48 patients (21%) with dLMS had positive surgical margins after the primary surgery without further re‐excision and subsequently developed a local recurrence. Plots of the cumulative incidence and conditional 10‐year risk of local recurrence in patients with scLMS and dLMS are illustrated in Supplementary Figure 2.

Risk of Metastasis

Patients with scLMS had a significantly higher 10‐year risk of metastasis (25%, 95% CI 18–31) compared with dLMS (3%, 95% CI 1.2–4.3) resulting in a risk difference of 22% (95% CI 15–29), p < 0.001. The median time to metastasis was 17 months (IQR 12–41) for scLMS and 13 months (IQR 2–133) for dLMS. When stratifying patients with scLMS on FNCLCC grade, the 10‐year risks of metastasis were 3% (95% CI 0–9.2) for grade 1, 20% (95% CI 10–28) for grade 2% and 40% (95% CI 26–49) for grade 3. The conditional 10‐year risk of metastasis gradually decreased to 2.5% after 10 years post‐surgery for patients with scLMS, whereas for dLMS, the 10‐year conditional risk of metastasis decreased to < 1% within 2 years after the primary surgery. Three patients with scLMS (6.3%) and three patients with dLMS (23%) had positive margins despite undergoing re‐excision and later developed metastasis. Location of Metastases

Metastases in patients with scLMS were both locoregional (n = 18) and distant (n = 30) of which 18 of the patients had metastases in multiple locations. The most common sites of metastases were the lungs (n = 24), bones (n = 11), internal organs (n = 3), and brain (n = 2). Locoregional metastases involved the skin or deep tissue (n = 17) and regional lymph nodes (n = 5).

In patients with dLMS, eight patients developed locoregional metastases and five developed distant metastases. Two of the patients with dLMS developed metastases in multiple locations, resulting in a total of nine locoregional and six distant metastases. Metastases were observed in local skin or deep tissue (n = 5), regional lymph nodes (n = 3), parotid gland (n = 1), lungs (n = 3), distant soft tissue (n = 1), liver (n = 1), and small intestine (n = 1).

Overall Survival

Patients with scLMS had a significantly lower 10‐year overall survival (56%, 95% CI 49–62), compared with dLMS (64%, 95% CI 60–69), p = 0.02. Stratified on FNCLCC‐grade for scLMS, the 10‐year rates of OS were 76% (95% CI 61–91) for grade 1, 59% (95% CI 47–71) for grade 2% and 37% (95% CI 26–49) for grade 3. The median overall survival was 12 years (IQR 9–16) for patients with scLMS and 16 years (IQR 14–20) for patients with dLMS. Discussion

Key Results

To our knowledge, this is the largest study of patients with cutaneous leiomyosarcoma subclassified as subcutaneous and dermal LMS. We found that patients with scLMS had a significantly higher 10‐year risk of metastasis than dLMS (25% vs. 2.7%) and a lower overall survival at 10 years (56% vs. 64%). Furthermore, we found a slightly higher risk of local recurrence in the patients with scLMS (15%) compared with dLMS (11%). The differences between scLMS and dLMS in the risk of metastasis were highly significant (p < 0.001), whereas the difference in overall survival were less substantial (p = 0.02), suggesting that the patients could be dying of unrelated causes.

Our results align with the current consensus in the literature that scLMS should be classified as a high‐risk sarcoma due to its significant risk of local recurrence and metastasis with poor overall survival. Specifically, our findings demonstrate a significant 10‐year risk of local recurrence of 15% and a 25% risk of metastasis, supporting this classification. Our results are, however, at the lower end of previously reported risk estimates, which may be attributable to the completeness of our cohort, long‐term follow‐up, and differences in the distribution of FNCLCC grades between cohorts reported in the literature.

Furthermore, the results from our study suggest that dLMS has a moderate 10‐year risk of local recurrence (11%) and a low 10‐year risk of metastasis ( < 3%) and therefore should be classified as a low‐risk neoplasm. This contrasts with the current literature, which reports metastasis rates up to 14% [3, 10, 11, 15, 19, 30, 41]. This discrepancy could be explained by the nationwide cohort design, which limits the risk of selection bias combined with a large sample size and long follow‐up period. Notably, some studies have proposed that dermal tumors strictly confined to the dermis or with minimal subcutaneous extension should be termed atypical intradermal smooth muscle neoplasms (AISMN) to emphasize their nonmetastatic nature [30, 31, 32]. The World Health Organization recently introduced AISMN as an ICD diagnosis for tumors of uncertain malignant potential [42]. However, our previous study demonstrated no significant prognostic difference between AISMN and dLMS with subcutaneous invasion [43], suggesting that AISMN and dLMS may likely represent different stages of the same tumor with no difference in prognosis. Further studies are needed to explore key risk factors in patients with LMS.

Treatment Recommendations of Cutaneous LMS

There are currently few evidence‐based recommendations for the surgical treatment and follow‐up of cutaneous LMS. Previous reports have suggested WLE with 2–5 cm margins for scLMS [19, 21, 44, 45, 46] and 1–2 cm margins for dermal LMS [7, 15, 16, 45]. In our study, the median excision margin for scLMS was 20 mm, with 89% achieving negative lateral and deep surgical margins, compared to a median excision margin of 10 mm for dLMS, where 90% achieved negative lateral and deep surgical margins. With regard to the depth of excision, surgical excision, including the fascia, was the most common for both scLMS (50%) and dLMS (51%). In our cohort, 2/31 scLMS and 10/48 dLMS cases with local recurrence and 3/48 scLMS and 3/13 dLMS cases with metastasis had positive surgical margins before the occurrence of these events. This stresses the importance of achieving negative surgical margins, as the presence of positive surgical margins may significantly increase the risk of developing local or distant relapse.

Clinical and Radiologic Follow‐Up Recommendations of Subcutaneous and Dermal Leiomyosarcoma

A recent guideline [47] stated that there is no clear evidence for a systematic follow‐up of patients with cutaneous LMS and proposed that all patients should be followed with clinical examinations for 5 years and in cases of conspicuous findings also sonography should be applied in the region of the primary tumor. However, the use of cross‐sectional imaging appeared to be indicated only in cases of conspicuous ultrasonographic findings, or for scLMS with risk factors, recurrences, or metastases. These recommendations are contrasted by our study, in which 63% of patients with scLMS who developed metastases had distant spread, most commonly to the lungs, suggesting that routine imaging of the lungs would be more appropriate than ultrasonography in the standardized follow‐up of patients with scLMS. We suggest that patients with grade 2 and 3 scLMS should be followed reflecting the ESMO guidelines [48] with clinical examinations and local MRI to detect local relapses, and PET/CT or CT of the thorax every 4 months for the first 3 years to detect distant metastases, as this period carries the highest risk of relapse, and every 6 months for the following 2 years, as the 5‐year timeframe will capture > 95% of the local recurrences and > 85% of the metastases. Routine imaging may be omitted in FNCLCC 1 grade scLMS as the 5‐year risk of metastasis is low (3%). For tumors with high‐risk features, such as soft tissue invasion, recurrent or metastatic disease, extended follow‐up beyond 5 years may be warranted to ensure ongoing surveillance and early detection of late‐occurring metastases.

The follow‐up of patients with dLMS should mainly focus on clinical examinations due to a moderate risk of local recurrence (11%). The follow‐up length could be limited to 4 years, as the risk of local recurrence after this time point is < 1%. The role of routine imaging in patients with dLMS is more unclear given the low risk of metastasis ( < 3%). However, as 6/15 (40%) of the metastases in patients with dLMS were distant, imaging with PET‐CT or CT of the thorax could have a limited but important role in the follow‐up of potential high‐risk patients with dLMS. Consequently, utilizing annual scans for the first 2 years may have a role in the follow‐up of the patients, as the risk of metastasis after this timepoint was < 1%.

Limitations

Despite the strengths of our study in terms of a large sample size with comprehensive data and long follow‐up, there are limitations. The extensive study period could have implications for both diagnostic misclassification and the estimated risks of event. However, the diagnostic criteria for LMS have not been revised in the study period and we assume that the surgical treatment with wide local excision of these tumors have not undergone substantial changes within this time frame. Furthermore, all pathologists in Denmark have the option to refer cases to certified sarcoma pathologists at a tertiary center. Therefore, we assume that the large sample size combined with the routinely used panel of immunohistochemical markers by soft‐tissue pathologist will limit the risk of bias due to misclassification. Another limitation is that we only assessed the excision margins and excision depths in a subset of patients with available surgical details. However, in this subset all excision margins were more than 2 cm in patients with scLMS and more than 1 cm in patients with dLMS as previously reported in the literature [7, 15, 16, 19, 21, 44, 45, 46].

Conclusions

ScLMS is associated with a significantly higher 10‐year risk of metastasis and lower 10‐year overall survival compared to dLMS. Consequently, grade 2 and 3 scLMS should be classified as a high‐risk sarcoma due to its substantial risk of metastasis and poor overall survival, indicating that the follow‐up of these patients should include PET/CT or CT of the thorax for the detection of distant metastases for 5 years. In contrast, dLMS should be classified as a low‐risk sarcoma, suggesting that the follow‐up could be limited to clinical examinations for 4 years.

Synopsis

FNCLCC grade 2 and 3 subcutaneous leiomyosarcoma should be classified as a high‐risk sarcoma due to its significant metastatic potential and poor overall survival. In contrast, dermal leiomyosarcoma should be considered a low‐risk sarcoma, given its low metastatic rate but substantial risk of local recurrence.
