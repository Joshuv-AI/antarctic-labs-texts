Promoting cervical cancer screening participation among Chinese women from resource-limited settings: a multicenter qualitative study
Authors: Huike Wang, Yitong Zhu, Bo Zhang, Hanyue Ding, Yao Yang, Youlin Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis qualitative study aimed to explore the factors influencing participation in HPV-based cervical cancer screening among women from resource-limited settings areas in mainland China.MethodsWe conducted semi-structured interviews based on the health belief model among women from resource-limited settings areas in China. The sample population was stratified by age and screening status, and the interviewees were continually included until the data were saturated for every subgroup. Thematic content analysis was adopted in the inductive approach.ResultsOf the 23 participants, we summarized three themes: (1) organized screening has a significant promotion, (2) lack of knowledge leads to lower perceived susceptibility and benefits, and (3) life burden keeps women away from accessing cervical cancer screening services.ConclusionTo improve the coverage rate of cervical cancer screening, authority and attractive information, as well as a supportive community, are essential to bridge the knowledge gap and increase screening participation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04546-4.

Background

Cervical cancer has caused more than 150,000 new cases and 55,700 new deaths in China in 2022 [1]. According to the IARC, cervical cancer is a disease that affects health inequity, and low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have a much higher disease burden than high-income countries (HICs) [2, 3]. Low health resource areas are typically characterized by a lack of funds to cover healthcare costs on an individual or societal basis, which may lead to suboptimal healthcare service delivery, underdeveloped infrastructure, and lack of knowledge [4]. Through effective primary screening, colposcopy, and prompt treatment to prevent cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), cervical cancer mortality has been significantly reduced [5]. To achieve cervical cancer elimination in China, a national free screening program has been implemented nationwide since 2009, with a screening coverage rate of only 36.8% among all Chinese women aged 35–64 in 2019 [6]. The barriers of women taking part in the national screening program needed to be identified, and focused intervention was called upon to boost screening coverage in resource-limited settings.

As the main body participating in cervical cancer screening, the views of women aged 35–64 on cervical cancer screening are of great significance in promoting its implementation. A previous online questionnaire survey of 4,518 Chinese women showed the obstacles for women to participate in screening regularly included “Fear of pain,” “Don’t want to visit the hospital,” and “No idea about the benefit of screening” [7], but quantitative research couldn’t explore the reason out of the preset options as a qualitative study. In the past decades, many qualitative studies conducted in Uganda, Malaysia, and other LMICs have identified several barriers to cervical cancer screening, including embarrassment, lack of knowledge, health system challenges, fear of test outcomes, and financial constraints [8]. However, methods of information dissemination have changed significantly. With the rise of TikTok, there has been a shift from the traditional media to the widespread use of short videos [9, 10]. Therefore, in this era of information explosion, it is crucial to determine whether women’s perceptions of cervical cancer screening as well as the factors that facilitate or hinder it have undergone new changes.

In Chinese resource-limited settings, benefiting from the effectiveness of China’s poverty reduction efforts in recent years, there are almost no completely illiterate women, while only a small scale has access to university [11]. In the recent decade, due to the popularization of TikTok and other media platforms, women may have more access to health information, but the health related information is not always credible [10–12, 13]. Primary healthcare institutions not only provide regular examinations, but also take responsibility for health education14. Thus, low health-resources don’t refer to absolute scarcity, but emphasize the unbalanced economic status and health service conditions. Therefore, identifying the knowledge gap, misconceptions, and barriers to cervical cancer screening in women may help develop focused solutions, contributing to a higher screening participation rate [8]. This qualitative study aimed to gain insights into the barriers to national screening program uptake in resource-limited settings and inform future interventions.

Methods

Settings and population

This qualitative study was conducted across four purposively selected low-resource health settings in China: Shimian County (Sichuan Province), Xinping County (Yunnan Province), Xiangyuan County (Shanxi Province), and Ordos City (Inner Mongolia). To ensure maximum variation and a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing screening decisions, participants were recruited through purposive sampling and stratified by age group and screening status (screened versus unscreened), with a target of 1–2 participants per sub-group to maintain a balanced representation across categories in each site. Recruitment was facilitated by physicians at local maternal and child health hospitals. Eligible women were approached face-to-face during clinic visits.

Initially, a total of 32 women were approached across the four sites, of whom 6 declined participation primarily due to time constraints or disinterest. A total of 26 women consented and participated in the interviews. For quality control and to ensure sufficient depth of the qualitative inquiry, 3 interviews were subsequently excluded from the analysis because they lasted less than 30 min, resulting in a final sample of 23 participants. The final sample size was driven by data saturation. To ensure rigorous methodological transparency, saturation was assessed iteratively and calculated across subgroups. A subgroup matrix was utilized to track emerging themes, and data saturation was deemed to be reached when three consecutive interviews within a specific stratified subgroup yielded no new codes or conceptual insights [15].

Study design and procedures

The interview guide was developed using the Health Belief Model (HBM) as a foundational theoretical framework. The HBM posits that the adoption of preventive health behaviors depends on an individual’s perceived susceptibility to and severity of an illness, weighed against the perceived benefits of and barriers to the action, alongside self-efficacy and cues to action [16, 17]. Previous studies have demonstrated that incorporating HBM constructs is highly effective in predicting cancer screening behaviors [18]. By structuring our guide around these socio-behavioral domains, we were able to systematically isolate and explore the nuanced decision-making processes of the participants, helping to identify the specific challenges they face in implementing positive health changes [19]. The full interview framework is detailed in Supplementary Table 1. This theoretical structure provided the foundation for the subsequent hybrid thematic analysis, as detailed in the Data Analysis section.

Individual, face-to-face, semi-structured interviews were conducted between January and March 2024 led by qualitative research experts from Peking Union Medical College. Because most participants spoke strong regional dialects, each session was facilitated by two professionally trained local interviewers (a public health professor and their postgraduate student, both of whom are native speakers of the local dialects). To ensure high methodological rigor, both interviewers completed comprehensive interviewer training led by qualitative research experts from Peking Union Medical College. This training included both theoretical sessions and practical exercises focusing on probing techniques, the theoretical nuances of the HBM, and ethical considerations. Furthermore, interviewers were required to submit pilot interview transcripts and recordings for evaluation by the expert team to ensure their competency before formal data collection commenced.

Demographic characteristics—including age, education level, occupation, annual household income, and health insurance status—were collected at the start of the interview. To minimize social desirability bias and establish rapport, several strict measures were implemented. Interviews were held in private settings, and participants were explicitly assured of their anonymity and the confidentiality of their responses. Furthermore, to encourage authentic narratives, each session began with a broad, opening question: “Please share your overall thoughts on cervical cancer screening.” This was followed by targeted questions exploring their previous experiences, knowledge, and HBM-related perceptions. To elicit deeper insights, interviewers actively utilized probing techniques (e.g., “Could you elaborate on that detail?” or “What were the in-depth reasons for that decision?”). Finally, to enhance the trustworthiness of the data, the interviewers maintained a reflexive stance throughout the process, carefully documenting their observations and mitigating personal preconceptions.

Data analysis

With participants’ permission, all interviews were audio-recorded, automatically transcribed, and subsequently verified by the interviewers against the original audio. Data collection and analysis occurred concurrently. All data processing was conducted in Chinese. The quotations were translated into English by HW and back-translated into Chinese by YZ to ensure consistency of meaning.

To address the complexity of participants’ experiences while utilizing our theoretical framework, we adopted a hybrid approach of inductive and deductive thematic analysis. Colaizzi’s method of data analysis was employed for the inductive phase [20], involving seven steps: (1) multiple transcript reading, (2) significant statement extraction, (3) code rephrasing, (4) (sub) theme creation, (5) transcript re-examination, (6) redundant description removal, and (7) code validation. Following the inductive generation of these emergent themes, a deductive mapping process was conducted. Two co-authors independently categorized the inductively derived statements and (sub)themes into the predefined constructs of the HBM framework: perceived severity, perceived susceptibility, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, cues to action, and self-efficacy. Any discrepancies during this hybrid coding process were resolved through iterative discussions among YZ, HW, and HD until a consensus was reached, ensuring a consistent interpretation.

Methodological rigor and trustworthiness were maintained through investigator triangulation and member checking; specifically, two participants reviewed the synthesized results to confirm they accurately reflected their lived experiences. An audit trail was meticulously documented to ensure all findings were traceable to the raw data. All qualitative data management and analyses were performed using NVivo 14.0 (Lumivero, Denver, CO, USA).

Ethical considerations and quality assurance

The study objectives were explained to all participants and oral informed consent was obtained before each interview. To ensure confidentiality, letters and numbers were used instead of names (e.g., unscreened women U1, U2, etc., and screened women S1, S2, etc.), and identifying information was also removed from the transcripts. Throughout this study, we followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) guidelines [21], detailed in Supplementary Table 2. We conducted several training sessions for interviewers and assessed the pilot interviews to ensure interviewers’ qualifications.

The significant facilitative role of organized screening

Free screening mobilizes the reluctant

The Chinese government implemented the National Cervical Cancer Screening Program (NCCSP) in 2009 and integrated it into basic public health services in 2019, which has been a pivotal driver of participation. By directly eliminating financial barriers, this free screening policy effectively mobilized women who were previously hesitant due to out-of-pocket costs or the misconception that screening was unnecessary. Ultimately, the removal of economic constraints transformed initial reluctance into active participation.

“I wouldn’t usually think about going for a screening, but since it’s free, I’ll go and get it done.” [S04].

“I wouldn’t usually think about going for a screening, but since it’s free, I’ll go and get it done.” [S04].

“Our nation gives us the opportunity to get screened for free, which gives us lots of benefits.” [S11].

“Our nation gives us the opportunity to get screened for free, which gives us lots of benefits.” [S11].

Collective participation acts as a key motivator

Because local screenings require healthcare workers to transport diagnostic equipment to remote sites, these organized events are typically condensed into a concentrated timeframe, with notifications efficiently disseminated via village group chats. For women in resource-limited settings, the subjective norms shaped by collective participation serve as powerful motivators for organized screening.Women are significantly more likely to attend if their friends, relatives, and neighbors are also participating, leveraging peer influence to overcome individual hesitation.

“I could be influenced by others. If you go alone, you might feel like you’re standing out. It’s much better when many people go together in groups.” [S12].

“I could be influenced by others. If you go alone, you might feel like you’re standing out. It’s much better when many people go together in groups.” [S12].

“If others are going for screening, then I will go too.” [S06].

Annual Screening Efficiently Reduces Embarrassment

Furthermore, the annual integration of screening with community educational sessions establishes a predictable routine. This regularity, coupled with the formal and professional conduct of healthcare workers, significantly diminishes the perceived embarrassment and stigma historically associated with gynecological examinations. This structured, community-wide approach normalizes the experience, transforming what was once an intimidating procedure into a familiar and widely accepted health practice.

“I don’t think there is anything embarrassing about this kind of routine screening.” [S01].

“I don’t think there is anything embarrassing about this kind of routine screening.” [S01].

“There might be some [embarrassment], but from a medical perspective, the examination is necessary. While there may be some discomfort, it is just a process [you have to go through].” [S07].

“Initially, people might be afraid, but the notifications became more frequent, and it turned into a routine procedure. There is nothing to worry about now. People’s health awareness has also improved.” [S08].

Knowledge deficits and misinformation reduce perceived susceptibility and benefits

Symptom-driven health beliefs diminish perceived benefits

Many women rely on a symptom-driven approach to health, remaining unaware of the need for routine screening. They mistakenly assume that a lack of visible symptoms, or having a regular sexual partner, means they are completely free from risk. Because early-stage cervical cancer is largely asymptomatic, this misunderstanding severely delays diagnosis. As a result, witnessing late-stage, terminal cases in their community creates a sense of hopelessness. This reinforces the negative belief that screening is useless, reducing the perceived benefits even among those who have never been examined.

“In recent years, there are many diseases that occur frequently. We have heard about many cases that were diagnosed at terminal stages. I feel there’s no point in screening; it might not make a difference.” [U05].

“In recent years, there are many diseases that occur frequently. We have heard about many cases that were diagnosed at terminal stages. I feel there’s no point in screening; it might not make a difference.” [U05].

“When I feel unwell, I will go to the hospital. I only do the examinations when I feel sick.” [S10].

“When I feel unwell, I will go to the hospital. I only do the examinations when I feel sick.” [S10].

Limited education hinders traditional knowledge dissemination

Compounding this knowledge gap is the challenge of limited formal education. As indicated by our demographic data, the lower educational attainment prevalent in these low-resource settings makes it difficult for some participants to comprehend traditional, text-heavy, or medically complex educational materials. This poses a significant structural barrier to the mass popularization and dissemination of health knowledge.

“I only have a junior high school education, and I often can’t understand the complicated contents in the lectures.” [U08].

“I only have a junior high school education, and I often can’t understand the complicated contents in the lectures.” [U08].

“I can’t understand textual materials. I cannot even write my name.” [S02].

Unreliable information and cultural myths distort perceived susceptibility

In recent years, women’s perceived susceptibility has been further eroded by widespread misinformation propagated through unreliable social media sources (e.g., independent content creators). This often leads to significant misconceptions about the disease and its risk factors. Nearly half of the participants attributed HPV infection to daily vulva-vaginal cleansing or mere bad luck, leading to an underestimation of their susceptibility. Additionally, some women mistakenly believe that they will not develop cervical cancer after menopause.

“I can’t really pinpoint it [the risk factor], but I have good lifestyle habits, and I’ve never had much contact with men or engaged in anything risky [behavior]. I just feel like I do not have any illnesses, so I do not see the need for screening.” [U06].

Conflicting information leads to question the necessity of screening

The overwhelming availability of unverified information, especially through informal channels and word-of-mouth, exacerbates public confusion, leading women to question the necessity of screening. Many women, including those with bachelor’s or master’s degrees, do not necessarily possess better understanding of cervical cancer, indicating that general education does not guarantee health literacy or critical evaluation of online content.

“At my age, I have already gone through menopause, so I think there is no need [to get cervical cancer screening].” [U07, Bachelor’s degree].

Structural burdens and low self-efficacy prevent access to screening

Economic constraints and mobility marginalize migrant workers

Many women aged 35–64 from low-resource settings migrate to urban centers for employment. This high mobility structurally excludes them from local, campaign-style screening events. By the time they return to their hometowns, the organized free screening periods have often concluded. Consequently, they are forced to rely on opportunistic screening in the city, where the out-of-pocket costs, combined with the loss of wages from taking time off work, present insurmountable economic barriers.

“It’s usually just inconvenient and the timing doesn’t work out. I cannot come back [to the village] and do it [cervical cancer screening] …if you do the screening in the city, it may cost hundreds.” [U04].

“It’s usually just inconvenient and the timing doesn’t work out. I cannot come back [to the village] and do it [cervical cancer screening] …if you do the screening in the city, it may cost hundreds.” [U04].

“If I were to take a vacation to go home, I wouldn’t get paid and I would have to pay extra for transportation. The cost is too high, [getting cervical cancer screening] not worth it.” [U06].

“Nobody informed me about the screening. Nobody cares about me. I have been laid off and my medical registration is not in the village.” [U07].

The “sandwich generation” burden diminishes self-efficacy and agency

Beyond financial and structural barriers, intensive domestic caregiving responsibilities profoundly limit women’s engagement with preventive health. Middle-aged rural women often serve as the “sandwich generation,” bearing the dual burden of caring for both elderly parents and young grandchildren. This overwhelming responsibility is not merely a physical barrier that consumes their free time; it systematically erodes their perceived self-efficacy. This constant prioritization of family needs over their own fosters a sense of internalized unworthiness, drastically diminishing their agency to schedule appointments and actively manage their own health.

“Nowadays, with my granddaughter around, I hardly have any free time. I need to take care of the elderly and children in my family, so I do not have time to watch TV. I do not usually watch or use my phone much either, I do not have time.” [U08].

“Nowadays, with my granddaughter around, I hardly have any free time. I need to take care of the elderly and children in my family, so I do not have time to watch TV. I do not usually watch or use my phone much either, I do not have time.” [U08].

Anticipated financial toxicity and the psychological avoidance of screening

Many women conceptualized the severity of cervical cancer not only in terms of the disease itself, but more acutely in terms of the anticipated financial burden resulting from treatment. The fear encompasses both direct medical costs and indirect consequences, such as becoming a burden to their children and losing their ability to work. For these women, engaging with cancer-related information induces immediate anxiety.

“I don’t want to think about cancer [restless]… (If I get cervical cancer, ) I won’t be able to work or earn an income. Moreover, I experience mental pressure. I would become a burden to my kids… To be honest, I don’t even want to get screened.” [S09, covered by MCI].

“I don’t want to think about cancer [restless]… (If I get cervical cancer, ) I won’t be able to work or earn an income. Moreover, I experience mental pressure. I would become a burden to my kids… To be honest, I don’t even want to get screened.” [S09, covered by MCI].

“I don’t want to hear about this kind of thing (cervical cancer). If I hear too much, I feel like I have cervical cancer myself… If I get cervical cancer [deep breath], I would rather give up treatment.” [U09, annual income 50,000].

“I don’t want to hear about this kind of thing (cervical cancer). If I hear too much, I feel like I have cervical cancer myself… If I get cervical cancer [deep breath], I would rather give up treatment.” [U09, annual income 50,000].

“Anyway, you can do whatever you want before you get sick, but if you get sick, your family will have to give you nursing care, and you will not be able to work, have less payment, and you will drain money treating the illness.” [S11, annual income 30,000].

Consequently, this profound fear of economic ruin triggers an active avoidance coping mechanism. In a vulnerable household economy, “not knowing” is psychologically preferable to facing a diagnosis that could devastate the family’s finances. This perceived severity of treatment costs inadvertently functions as a critical barrier, driving a cycle of medical avoidance.

“Why bother screening? If the cancer is there, we can’t afford the treatment anyway. It’s like setting a debt trap for the family. I’d rather live in uncertainty than drag my children into poverty because of me.” [U03].

“Why bother screening? If the cancer is there, we can’t afford the treatment anyway. It’s like setting a debt trap for the family. I’d rather live in uncertainty than drag my children into poverty because of me.” [U03].

“What’s the use of getting screening? For a family like ours, where would the money for treatment come from? Better not to know, just take each day as it comes.” [U10].

Ultimately, these accounts describe how the anticipated financial burden of treatment acts as a formidable barrier, reinforcing a cycle of medical avoidance among the interviewed women.

Discussion

This multi-center qualitative study provides a comprehensive exploration of the facilitators and barriers to cervical cancer screening among women in China’s low-resource health settings. While many of our findings align with established evidence from other LMICs, such as knowledge deficits, perceived financial burdens, and psychological barriers. This research uncovers several unique socio-cultural and systemic challenges embedded in the Chinese context. The sites where we implemented our research are multi-ethnic, with low health resources and low health literacy, but have a higher risk of diseases [22]. Addressing these localized barriers is paramount to achieving the targets of the global strategy, which include “70% of women screened with a high-performance test by 35 and again by 45 years of age” by 2030 [23, 24]. It is imperative to take targeted measures to ensure equitable access to healthcare services, thereby alleviating hardships and advancing their health outcomes [25]. Highlighting these critical pain points not only enriches the understanding of barriers in low-resource regions but also provides targeted insights for tailoring public health interventions. The following sections interpret these barriers through the HBM framework, focusing on the roles of organized screening, digital health literacy, and gendered domestic burdens.

Organized screening and collective norms as double-edged drivers

Consistent with previous studies, our findings confirm that the NCCSP plays a decisive role in mobilizing rural women through free, organized screening [26]. Collective participation fosters powerful subjective norms, which not only reduce embarrassment but also normalize preventive care recognition within the community. From early psychological theories to contemporary behavioral models, individuals are not regarded as isolated decision-makers but as actors embedded within a broader social context, where the perceptions and behaviors of others critically shape their own actions [27]. This may explain why, unlike in Ethiopia or Cameroon, where embarrassment and stigma remain strong deterrents, our participants did not cite these factors as major barriers [28, 29]. In countries where screening is opportunistic and individually scheduled, women may feel attending screening highlights them as “at risk,” reinforcing stigma; by contrast, China’s campaign-style model frames screening as a universal routine, thereby embedding it in community norms [30]. Nevertheless, in resource-limited settings, government advocacy plays a vital role in shaping people’s perceptions of appropriate behavior, as public policy often serves as the primary guidance in the absence of strong market influences [31].

Additionally, economic constraints have been emphasized in other low-resource contexts, such as Nepal and Iran [32]. In contrast, the provision of free services within the NCCSP substantially alleviates financial concerns in China, lowering the threshold for participation. However, the campaign-style delivery model also introduces structural vulnerabilities: rural-urban migrant women often miss fixed-schedule campaigns in their hometowns and are unable to access free services at their work destinations, resulting in systematic exclusion. Unlike high-income settings, where screening is individually scheduled within primary care, China’s current model risks inadvertently amplifying disparities for mobile populations, particularly younger working-age women. To overcome these challenges, future strategies should prioritize the normalization of free screening services as a routine healthcare offering, moving beyond the temporal and geographic constraints of periodic campaigns. Furthermore, promoting inter-regional screening initiatives is essential to ensure that eligibility is no longer strictly tethered to household registration locations. By establishing a cross-regional service framework and portable health records, the system can ensure that migrant women are able to access free preventive care at their work destinations, thereby preventing mobility from resulting in systematic medical exclusion.

Information overload and digital health literacy gaps

Another emerging challenge lies in the information environment of the digital era. While earlier studies consistently identified absolute knowledge scarcity as a primary barrier to cervical cancer screening [33, 34], our findings indicate a paradigm shift: the primary obstacle in low-resource settings is no longer a lack of information, but rather the overwhelming difficulty of distinguishing credible medical knowledge from digital misinformation. Most participants reported frequent exposure to health content via short-video platforms and social media, yet misconceptions remained widespread. Some women believed that HPV infection was caused by poor hygiene or “bad luck,” while others assumed that screening was unnecessary after menopause, or feared that repeated screening could itself be harmful. These narratives illustrate the critical challenge of navigating a saturated but unverified digital landscape. In this context, abundant but unverified information fosters anxiety, distorts women’s perceived susceptibility, and ultimately undermines their screening intentions.

Importantly, the challenge of discerning misinformation appeared independent of formal education. In our study, even participants who had completed undergraduate education acknowledged difficulties in critically evaluating online claims, particularly regarding HPV transmission routes and the necessity of screening after sexual inactivity. This highlights a vital distinction: general academic education does not equate to digital health literacy, nor does it automatically protect against the algorithmic amplification of deep-seated cultural myths. Similar misconceptions have also been documented in other settings, including Malaysia and the United Kingdom [35]. Given evidence that improving awareness of susceptibility and benefits can facilitate screening uptake [36, 37], future interventions should not only emphasize individual digital health literacy, but also necessitate national-level partnerships with professional institutions to establish authoritative health communication platforms. By actively providing reliable, accessible, and scientifically accurate information on these popular digital channels, authorities can effectively counteract misinformation and strengthen public trust in preventive services.

Gendered life burden and inter-generational care-giving

Life burden emerged as another central theme, yet our findings highlight that the challenge is not simply a matter of “being too busy,” but rather the result of structural gender disparities in the distribution of care-giving responsibilities. In Chinese rural households, middle-aged women disproportionately bear a “sandwich” role, simultaneously caring for elderly parents and grandchildren while also contributing to household income. This inter-generational care-giving leaves them with limited time, energy, and autonomy to prioritize their own health, which resulting systematically erodes a woman’s self-efficacy. By internalizing the societal expectation that family needs must always supersede their own well-being, many women exhibited a sense of low self-esteem and gendered “unworthiness”. Consequently, they subconsciously perceive themselves as undeserving of the time and resources required for preventive self-care. While domestic duties have been noted as barriers in international studies, the intensity of these gendered and multi-generational expectations appears particularly pronounced in rural China, where men are rarely expected to share equivalent household or care-giving duties.

Indeed, for some participants, non-attendance was a deliberate choice made “after consideration”; they rationally concluded that the perceived barriers far outweighed their limited understanding of the benefits, driven by the combined burden of domestic responsibilities and job strain. Such patterns align with prior evidence showing that the dual burden of paid and unpaid labor disproportionately restricts women’s agency and ability to engage in health-promoting behaviors [38]. Because unequal domestic workloads directly contribute to worse health outcomes for women [39], addressing these systemic constraints requires a shift from individual-focused solutions to broader structural transformations. Accordingly, future interventions should focus on the “socialization of care,” developing community-based support infrastructures for elderly and childcare to alleviate the private burdens that currently tether women to the domestic sphere. Crucially, health promotion must transition from individual-centered education to a transformative social discourse that challenges the culturally entrenched “virtue of self-sacrifice.” By reframing preventive care not as a personal luxury but as a fundamental right of the autonomous subject, we can begin to decouple a woman’s self-worth from her domestic utility. Furthermore, implementing HPV self-sampling offers a practical solution for women burdened by caregiving. By allowing home-based screening, it overcomes temporal and physical barriers, further restoring women’s agency and ensuring that domestic duties do not preclude life-saving preventive care.

In addition, population mobility compounds these challenges: migrant women often are ineligible for free screening in urban areas and remain highly price-sensitive to out-of-pocket options, especially when compared to populations in higher-income settings like London [40]. Anxiety about the potential financial burden of cervical cancer, both direct medical costs and indirect costs related to care-giving disruptions, was a common theme, further underscoring the importance of equitable household responsibility sharing to protect women’s health. To effectively overcome these systemic barriers, future strategies should prioritize the digital transformation of health services and promote the integration of regional health information systems with personal identity verification. This approach would accommodate population mobility and streamline identity verification, thereby reducing missed screenings and preventing unnecessary repeated screenings.

Study strengths

To our knowledge, this is the first multi-centered qualitative study focusing on the facilitators and barriers to cervical cancer screening participation among women in China’s low-resource health settings. By recruiting eligible women nationwide across diverse geographic regions, this study captures a wide spectrum of socio-cultural contexts. Furthermore, the rigorous data collection process was facilitated by highly trained public health researchers from leading local medical institutions who are native speakers of the regional dialects. This professional involvement ensured the cultural sensitivity, linguistic accuracy, and robust credibility of the qualitative findings.

Study limitations

The inherently interpretive nature of qualitative inquiry introduces the possibility of researcher bias during both data collection and analysis. Although rigorous procedures including independent double-coding, iterative consensus discussions, and reflexive documentation were employed to enhance analytic credibility, the potential for researcher subjectivity cannot be entirely excluded. Furthermore, the transferability of our findings may be constrained by several factors. First, recruitment was conducted through hospitals, which might have introduced a selection bias; participants recruited in a clinical setting may possess higher health literacy or greater awareness of cervical cancer screening compared to those who do not access formal healthcare services. Second, although we included four geographically and culturally diverse sites, the hospital-based recruitment strategy means these findings are primarily intended to reflect the specific socio-cultural contexts within these four study settings rather than serve as a representative snapshot of all low-resource areas in China. Nevertheless, the “thick description” provided in this study offers valuable insights that may be transferable to other regions with similar socio-economic profiles. Finally, as the data were collected at a single time point, the findings reflect participants’ perceptions within a specific temporal and situational context, rather than the dynamic evolution of their experiences over time.

Conclusion

This study identified critical barriers and facilitators of cervical cancer screening among women in low-resource areas in China. Free, annual organized cervical cancer screening effectively diminishes stigma and promotes participation. However, widespread digital misinformation and heavy caregiving burdens, exacerbated by population mobility, remain critical barriers that diminish women’s perceived susceptibility and self-efficacy. To improve equitable uptake, future strategies must provide authoritative digital health communication, offer flexible screening schedules, and promote family-inclusive responsibility sharing.
