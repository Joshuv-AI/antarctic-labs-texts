Response to “Comment on ‘Evidence Synthesis of Observational Studies in Environmental Health: Lessons Learned from a Systematic Review on Traffic-Related Air Pollution’”
Authors: Hanna Boogaard, Richard W Atkinson, Jeffrey R Brook, Howard H Chang, Gerard Hoek, Barbara Hoffmann, Sharon K Sagiv, Evangelia Samoli, Audrey Smargiassi, Adam A Szpiro, Danielle Vienneau, Jennifer Weuve, Frederick W Lurmann, Francesco Forastiere
Published: 2024

We appreciate the opportunity to continue the open dialog with Taylor et al.1 in response to our Commentary.2 We aimed to discuss several challenges with the US Office of Health Assessment and Translation (OHAT) approach3 and its implementation and suggested improvements for synthesizing evidence from observational studies in environmental health more broadly. We deemed the OHAT approach better suited than the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach for synthesizing evidence from observational studies in environmental health, and we took care to apply the method as properly as possible.

We appreciate the emphasis on the flexibility of the OHAT approach, including a narrative assessment. We respectfully disagree that we incorrectly portrayed the OHAT approach, and we clarify below with several examples some of the nuances of our suggestions that could have been missed.

One of our proposals was to routinely include a narrative assessment—not only to up- or downgrade confidence or as an alternative when a meta-analysis is not appropriate. In fact, we considered the determination of confidence using a formal rating scheme of up- and downgrading of certain factors, the treatment of every factor as equally important, and the lower initial confidence rating of observational studies to be fundamental issues in the OHAT approach. Hence, we deemed it necessary to accompany the OHAT assessment with a broader narrative approach to fully capture some of the important and complex nuances that would have otherwise been missed by conducting either approach alone. As stated in our Commentary, there are respected alternative confidence assessment schemes that do not use a formal rating scheme that are just as transparent.4,5

The OHAT Handbook3 is furthermore not entirely consistent about addressing unexplained heterogeneity, specifically whether to downgrade only when there is heterogeneity in direction of associations across studies or when there is heterogeneity in magnitude. We endorse the former as the way forward. Offering both options is indeed flexible but offers less evaluative capacity.

Although the OHAT approach does not explicitly declare that randomized controlled trials or other experimental studies are the gold standard, the requirement of a controlled exposure as one of the four key study design features in the initial confidence scheme implies that observational studies will never start with a high confidence rating.

We agree that the factors considered in the narrative approach broadly overlap with those included in the OHAT approach, but we note that they differ in how they are considered and weighted. As stated in the Commentary, what we considered problematic in the OHAT approach was not the factors to evaluate, but rather the application and the “formulaic” rating scheme of up- and downgrades and treating every factor as equally important. We do not see the issue of double counting when using these complementary approaches because the narrative assessment does not involve such a rating scheme.

We indeed did not apply two OHAT grading factors—indirectness and large magnitude of effect—in the process of up- and downgrading confidence in the body of evidence. Indirectness was not applicable because we included only studies of human exposure to traffic-related air pollution in direct association with the health outcomes. Furthermore, large magnitude of effect (i.e., large relative risk >2 or very large relative risk >5 as defined in OHAT)3 never occurred.

Taylor and colleagues added some responses to Table 1 of the Commentary. However, our table was designed to capture GRADE-type assessments more widely—thus not confined to the OHAT approach per se. Hence, that might explain some of the “inaccuracies and misinterpretations” Taylor et al. noted. We also found that several of the critiques did not directly inform the challenging task of synthesizing environmental health research. Our Commentary and the response illustrate the need for continued open discussion about how to maximize what can be learned from observational studies in environmental health for evidence synthesis.
