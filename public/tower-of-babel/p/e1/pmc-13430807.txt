Hematologic toxicity of immune checkpoint inhibitors: real-world burden and risk profiles from a five-year cohort from the Middle East
Authors: Ali Awada, Ali Tarhini, Abbas Hammoud, Mohammad Kassem, Ali Ghais, Nicole Charbel, Amal El Masri, Ali ElHadi Zreik, Firas Kreidieh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized the treatment and prognosis of a variety of solid tumors. However, exaggerated immune activation can lead to immune-related adverse events (irAEs), among which hematologic irAEs remain poorly characterized. This study evaluated the frequency, spectrum, and risk factors of treatment-emergent cytopenia in patients with melanoma, renal cell carcinoma (RCC), and urothelial carcinoma (UCC) treated with ICIs.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed patients at the American University of Beirut Medical Center who received at least one dose of ICIs between January 2019 and January 2024. Clinical, treatment, and laboratory data were collected from electronic medical records. Binary logistic regressions assessed the relationships between potential predictors and development of treatment-emergent cytopenia. The primary outcome of interest was the incidence of at least one treatment-emergent cytopenia.ResultsWe included 153 patients (median age 68.21 years, 64.7% male, mean BMI 27.6 ± 4.32 kg/m²) receiving ICIs for melanoma (27.45%), RCC (27.45%), or UCC (45.09%). Pembrolizumab (52.94%) and nivolumab (23.52%) were the most used ICIs, with 28.1% receiving combination therapy. Initially, 47.05% of patients had pre-treatment anemia (mostly grade 1), while thrombocytopenia was rare (5.9%, all grade 1), and no patients had neutropenia. Following initiation with ICIs, 61.43% (94/153) experienced at least one new-onset or worsening treatment-emergent cytopenia. Most common were anemia (46.4%), followed by thrombocytopenia (27.45%) and neutropenia (26.14%). Pancytopenia occurred in 15.6% and bicytopenia in 18.3%. Median time to first hematologic disturbance was 1.39 months (mean 4.71 months, IQR (Long et al., Ann Oncol, 35:1191-1199, 2024); (Motzer et al., N Engl J Med, 378:1277-1290, 2018); (Gupta et al., ESMO Open, 10:104506, 2025), ~ 5.8 cycles). Most treatment-emergent cytopenia were grade 1 (71.24%), fewer grade 2 (20.26%) and 3 (8.49%). There was no grade 4 or 5 events or hematology-related fatalities. Supportive treatment included transfusions in 22.22% and (granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) administration in 12.41%; no patient required steroids, IVIG, or treatment discontinuation due to hematologic irAEs. Univariate regression analysis evaluating the predictors of developing at least one treatment-emergent cytopenia showed that lower baseline hemoglobin and a history of anemia were significant factors (p < 0.001; OR = 0.56 and p < 0.002; OR = 6.50, respectively). Additionally, regression analysis showed that baseline hemoglobin (OR 0.42, p < 0.001) and eosinophil count (OR 1.42, p = 0.004) were significant predictors of anemia and thrombocytopenia, respectively. On multivariable analysis, hemoglobin emerged as a significant predictor, with HGB showing an OR of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.869–0.963, p = 0.001).ConclusionAlthough usually rare in literature, we found treatment-emergent cytopenia to be common but mild and manageable with ICIs. Introduction

Over the past decade, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have become an essential component of modern oncology by improving survival across several solid tumors, including melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), renal cell carcinoma (RCC), and urothelial carcinoma (UCC) [1–3]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) exert their therapeutic effect by blocking key inhibitory signaling pathways, notably PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4, which reactivates cytotoxic T-cell activity against tumor cells [4]. Several agents in this class, including pembrolizumab, nivolumab, atezolizumab, durvalumab, and ipilimumab, have received regulatory approval for use across multiple malignancies [5, 6].

The same immune activation that drives tumor control can trigger immune-related adverse events (irAEs), which can affect any organ system [4]. The type and severity of these irAEs vary by drug class and combination. Dermatologic irAEs (rash, pruritus) are among the most common [7]. Endocrine irAEs (thyroiditis/hypothyroidism, hypophysitis), gastrointestinal irAEs (colitis), hepatic irAEs, and pulmonary irAEs (pneumonitis) vary according to the treatment regimen [8, 9]. For instance, pneumonitis occurs in a few percent of patients treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and at higher rates with dual blockade [10]. Although fatal irAEs are uncommon overall, myocarditis and fulminant pneumonitis/hepatitis carry disproportionately high fatality among reported cases [11].

Hematologic irAEs are relatively rare but clinically significant. Individual events are generally reported at < 5% in clinical-trial datasets, yet pharmacovigilance and observational series suggest under recognition and heterogeneity in case definitions. Presentations include cytopenias, most commonly anemia, thrombocytopenia, and neutropenia, as well as less frequent but severe immune syndromes such as autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA), immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), aplastic anemia, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), and marrow failure [12]. Early onset within weeks has been reported, and some cases can be life-threatening [13].

The pathogenesis of hematologic irAEs is not yet fully understood [14]. Among the proposed mechanisms are autoreactive T-cell expansion, cytokine-mediated marrow suppression, and autoantibody formation against hematopoietic precursors or peripheral elements [4]. Marrow biopsies may show hypocellularity or immune-mediated aplasia in select cases [15]. Alternative causes of cytopenia, such as prior chemotherapy, marrow infiltration/progression, infection, nutritional deficiency, or chronic illness, can also explain the laboratory findings, complicate the diagnosis, and necessitate a systematic work-up [16].

From a clinical-outcomes perspective, multiple meta-analyses and cohort studies suggest that the occurrence of (typically low-grade) irAEs correlates with improved response and survival, whereas severe irAEs increase morbidity and can force treatment interruption (16). Accordingly, early diagnosis and graded management are essential both to reduce harm and to avoid unnecessary permanent ICIs discontinuation when safe to resume—principles emphasized in the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines [5, 6].

Despite the common use of ICIs in practice, real-world data on the incidence, timing, predictors, and outcomes of heme-irAEs remain limited compared with other organ systems, with limited studies and representation from Middle Eastern populations. Identifying baseline rates and risk factors would improve differential diagnosis, guide marrow and laboratory evaluation, inform steroid-sparing approaches in refractory cases (e.g., intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), rituximab), and help clinicians make decisions regarding holding, discontinuation, or safely rechallenging with ICIs [12–15]. We performed a five-year retrospective observational study at the American University of Beirut Medical Center (AUBMC) to investigate the incidence, timing, clinical features, treatment approaches, outcomes, and predictors of heme-irAEs in a Middle Eastern cohort of patients who received ICIs for melanoma, RCC, and urothelial carcinoma.

Methods

This is a single-centered retrospective observational study that aims to evaluate the adverse hematological side effects associated with ICIs therapy in patients treated for three major cancer types: renal cell carcinoma (RCC), bladder cancer, and melanoma. The study included 153 adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) diagnosed with one of the three malignancies who subsequently received ICIs as part of their treatment regimen at AUBMC between January 2018 and January 2024. The ICIs used included Ipilimumab, Nivolumab, Pembrolizumab, Atezolizumab, Avelumab or any combination of these agents.

Data was extracted from electronic health records and included demographic parameters, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group status (ECOG) performance status, cancer characteristics (staging and grade), prior chemotherapy/radiotherapy, immunotherapy characteristics, and hematologic parameters. Baseline hematologic values were defined as the closest available measurement taken prior to the first ICI cycle. Hematologic data were collected before, during, and after ICI therapy. For each patient, the onset, grade, and course of cytopenia (anemia, thrombocytopenia, and neutropenia) were documented, as well as cases of pancytopenia, multilineage involvement, or immune-mediated hematologic syndromes (autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA), HLH, and myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS).We excluded patients with concurrent cytotoxic chemotherapy or chemotherapy administered in the four weeks leading up to ICI initiation to minimize confounding hematologic toxicity. Bone marrow biopsies were not routinely performed, but patients with known or documented bone marrow infiltration prior to ICI initiation were excluded. Cytopenias were considered related to ICIs when an alternative explanation, including infection, overt bleeding or trauma, and radiologic disease progression, was absent. Specifically, patients were evaluated for disease progression using radiologic findings and clinical assessment. Relevant laboratory investigations were reviewed, including iron studies, vitamin B12 and folate levels, and hemolysis parameters (lactate dehydrogenase, bilirubin, haptoglobin, and reticulocyte count), as well as infection markers.

The primary outcome was the incidence of any treatment-emergent cytopenia, defined as new or worsening cytopenia following ICI initiation not explained by disease progression, infection, or chemotherapy. These events were then classified as “Hematological irAEs” only if there was a clear clinical or laboratory evidence supporting immune-mediated mechanisms. Grading of adverse events followed the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. Worsening cytopenia was defined as any increase in grade following ICI initiation. Secondary outcomes included the timing and grade distribution of all events, supportive treatment used (granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), transfusion, corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and resolution status.

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Logistic regression was used to identify significant predictors of the primary outcome (incidence of at least one treatment-emergent cytopenia) at the univariate level. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Candidate predictors were then selected based on their established possible clinical association and included in the multivariable model.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.00 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), with a two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

This study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) at the American University of Beirut, with a waiver of the requirement to obtain patient informed consent.

Results

A total of 153 patients were included in the study. Their baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age at treatment initiation was 68.2 years, and 64.7% were male. The mean BMI was 27.6 ± 4.32 kg/m². Patients were treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) for urothelial carcinoma (45.01%), melanoma (27.45%), and renal cell carcinoma (27.45%). Nearly all patients (96%) demonstrated good functional status (ECOG performance status of 0–1). A history of smoking was present in 65.35%, and 9.15% had a preexisting autoimmune condition. Pembrolizumab (52.94%) and nivolumab (23.52%) were the most frequently administered agents, while 28.1% received combination ICI therapy. The mean duration of ICI treatment was 14.7 ± 19.1 months. 30% of patients had received prior chemotherapy, and 74.5% had undergone previous surgical intervention for primary cancer. No patients had a baseline hematologic malignancy.

All baseline hematologic parameters are summarized in Table 2. 47.05% of patients had documented anemia, predominantly grade 1. Baseline thrombocytopenia was less common (5.9%, all grade 1), and no patients had baseline neutropenia. Following initiation of ICIs, 61.43% of all patients experienced at least one new-onset or worsening treatment-emergent cytopenia; most of them were mild. Grade 1 events accounted for 71.24% of all toxicities, while grade 2 and grade 3 events accounted for 20.26% and 8.49%, respectively. No grade 4 or grade 5 hematologic toxicities occurred. Additionally, no cases of isolated autoimmune hematologic syndromes were identified, including AIHA, HLH, or new-onset MDS. Most hematologic abnormalities were limited to laboratory changes and did not result in significant clinical decline.

Anemia was the most frequently observed treatment-emergent cytopenia (46.4%), followed by thrombocytopenia (27.45%) and neutropenia (26.14%). Most events were grade 1 or 2. Grade 1 and 2 anemia occurred in 87.32% and 8.45% of patients who experienced it, respectively. Grade 1 and 2 thrombocytopenia occurred in 64.28% and 23.8%, respectively. Grade 1 and 2 neutropenia occurred in 50% and 37.5%, respectively. Grade III events were less common, observed in 4.2% of anemia cases, 11.9% of thrombocytopenia cases, and 12.5% of neutropenia cases. Multilineage cytopenias were also noted: 15.6% developed pancytopenia and 18.3% experienced bi-cytopenia. Hematologic disturbances appeared relatively early, with a median onset of 1.39 months, corresponding to approximately six treatment cycles.

Management strategies were primarily supportive. Red blood cell transfusions were administered to 22.22% of patients, while 12.41% required G-CSF administration for neutropenia. Notably, no patients required systemic immunosuppression, including corticosteroids or IVIG, and no one discontinued ICI therapy due to these events. Hematologic parameters typically returned to baseline or near-baseline levels following supportive measures only, and no hematology-related mortality was recorded.

We performed logistic regression analyses to explore potential predictors of treatment-emergent cytopenia (Tables 3, 4, 5 and 6). Univariate regression assessing the likelihood of developing at least one treatment-emergent cytopenia demonstrated that lower baseline hemoglobin levels and a history of anemia were significant predictors (p < 0.001; OR = 0.56, p = 0.002; OR = 6.50), respectively. Additionally, hemoglobin was identified as a significant predictor of anemia development (p < 0.001, 95% CI 0.28–0.61, OR 0.42), indicating that higher baseline hemoglobin levels were associated with substantially lower odds of developing new or worsening anemia following ICI therapy. Additionally, eosinophil count was a significant predictor of thrombocytopenia (p = 0.004, 95% CI 1.12–1.79, OR 1.42), suggesting that higher eosinophil levels were linked to an increased likelihood of experiencing worsening thrombocytopenia. No other demographic, clinical, or treatment-related variables demonstrated statistically significant associations. Similarly, in the multivariable analysis 9 predictors were included in the final model based on clinical relevance. Hemoglobin emerged as a significant predictor, with HGB showing an OR of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.869–0.963, p = 0.001) (Table 7). Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, which indicated adequate calibration (p = 0.12). Model discrimination was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), demonstrating good discriminatory ability (AUC = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.74–0.96, p < 0.001). Discussion

The incidence of any grade treatment-emergent cytopenia in our cohort (61.43%) is notably higher than that reported in clinical trials, where such events are generally considered rare (< 5%) [17, 18]. The distinction in the results may be due to the limited recognition and underreporting in controlled trials and may showcase this study’s inclusion of all cytopenia grades, such as mild and transient laboratory changes. Additionally, our study reflects a real-world population, including patients with prior chemotherapy exposure, older age, and comorbidities, which may contribute to higher rates of mild hematologic abnormalities. The results align with a recent meta-analysis of phase III randomized controlled trials, which highlighted that mild cytopenias are more likely to be common than previously reported, especially among patients who are taking PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors or a combination regimen [19]. The meta-analysis reported all grades were anemia in 36.5% of patients, neutropenia in 29.7%. and thrombocytopenia in 18.8%. Our study found comparable event frequencies, anemia was the most frequently observed in 46.4%, followed by thrombocytopenia (27.45%) and neutropenia (26.14%). In this research, serious consequences, including hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, and myelodysplastic syndromes, were absent. This is similar to previous research that demonstrated the rarity of life-threatening hematologic irAEs [5].

Baseline hemoglobin emerged as a key predictor of at least one of treatment-emergent cytopenia. Lower hemoglobin independently predicted anemia and higher eosinophil count independently predicted thrombocytopenia. A potential explanation for the first finding is that higher hemoglobin may reflect greater erythroid reserve. A potential explanation for the second finding is that elevated eosinophils may be a feature of a “primed” immune environment that promotes platelet suppression. These findings align with prior studies showing linking eosinophil proportion ≥ 3.0% to the incidence of irAEs across cancers (OR 2.57) [20]. Moreover, in RCC patients on ipilimumab + nivolumab, eosinophils ≥ 3.0% after two weeks predicted grade ≥ 2 irAEs (OR 4.18) [21, 22]. The absence of other strong predictors underscores the unpredictability of hematologic irAEs, emphasizing careful blood count monitoring during ICI therapy.

Although the pathophysiology of ICI-related hematologic toxicity is not fully understood, some studies have shown that it may include an immune-mediated reaction that disrupts hematopoiesis [23, 24]. Possible mechanisms for this disruption include T cell mediated destruction of hematopoietic cells and cytokine-driven suppression of bone marrow function, along with a severe decrease in self-tolerance to erythroid or myeloid antigens [25]. Autoimmune hemolytic anemia and other events that were reported in previous studies may be due to autoantibody formation, showing the necessity of conducting mechanistic studies that investigate immune cell subsets, bone marrow microenvironment, and interactions related to the checkpoint pathway [18, 26].

Our findings highlight several important clinical considerations. First, treatment-emergent cytopenia often arise early in the treatment course, underscoring the need for routine laboratory monitoring, particularly during the initial 2–3 months of therapy. Second, treatment-emergent cytopenia are generally mild and amenable to supportive treatment, which supports the safety of continuing ICI-treatment in spite of complete blood count abnormalities. Third, significant predictors of treatment-emergent cytopenia were absent in regression analyses, necessitating the significance of vigilance in all cancer patients, irrespective of their baseline or cancer type. When cytopenias are detected, healthcare providers should be ready to begin supportive therapy, such as transfusions and G-CSF. Fourth, the study’s findings signify the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration among oncologists, hematologists, and pharmacists to ensure that ICI therapy and patient outcomes are optimized.

The main strength of our study is its comprehensive characterization of the types, grades, and temporal distributions of treatment-emergent cytopenia. Other strengths include the inclusion of multiple cancer types and ICI regimens in a real-world cohort. Limitations include the retrospective, single-center design and relatively small sample size, which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. Moreover, this study lacked immune profiling and mechanistic data, which prevented the investigation of possible underlying biological pathways contributing to events. Although early events were comprehensively detected, treatment-emergent cytopenia with a late onset (after date of lost follow up) may have been missed. Additionally, the absence of systematic diagnostic workup, including hemolysis markers and bone marrow examination, limits our ability to confidently attribute those cytopenias to immune-mediated mechanisms.

Future prospective research should aim to (1) increase sample sizes to better power similar investigations into the predictors of treatment-emergent cytopenia and (2) clarify the pathophysiologic mechanisms underlying hematologic irAEs. Mechanistic investigations involving cytokine analysis and bone marrow examination, in addition to immune profiling and genetic or HLA-related predictors, could enable the identification of patients who are at higher risks than others [27, 28]. This can also help in guiding early interventions and enable personalized monitoring strategies. In addition, investigating whether treatment-emergent cytopenia correlate with overall survival or progression-free survival could clarify their role as potential predictors of treatment response, as shown with other immune-related toxicities [29].

Conclusions

Treatment-emergent cytopenia are frequently observed among patients with RCC, melanoma, urothelial carcinoma receiving ICIs. However, these events are predominantly mild laboratory abnormalities, rarely necessitating intervention or change in the treatment regimen. These findings underscore the importance of routine monitoring and early recognition, particularly during the initial phases of immunotherapy. Collectively, this study provides practical evidence that can be beneficial for healthcare professionals involved in the monitoring, detection, and management of treatment-emergent cytopenia in oncology practice.
