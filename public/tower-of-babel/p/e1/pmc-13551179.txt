Accuracy of Factor VIII Assays for Measuring Damoctocog Alfa Pegol: A Comparative Analysis
Authors: Judith C A Cluitmans, Piet Meijer, Sanna R Rijpma, An K Stroobants
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionAccurate monitoring of treatment with modified factor VIII products remains challenging, due to known variability in assay performance across different laboratory methods and reagents. For the extended half‐life product Damoctocog alfa pegol discrepancies have been reported.AimsThis multicentre field study was designed to evaluate the analytical accuracy of Damoctocog alfa pegol using various methods for one‐stage clotting and chromogenic assays.MethodsThe study was conducted under conditions closely mimicking routine laboratory settings. Five plasma samples were spiked with varying concentrations of Damoctocog alfa pegol (0.05 to 1.3 IU/ml) and distributed to 118 participating laboratories. FVIII activity of the samples was analysed using 12 one‐stage and 7 chromogenic methods. Accuracy results were evaluated per specific reagent with a maximal acceptable mean bias of 20% from the target. Interlaboratory variation was established for reagents with ≥3 participants.ResultsThe reliability of factor VIII measurements differ between the tested reagents. Usage of SynthasIL and Pathromtin (one stage) and Biophen (chromogenic) is in most cases reliable, although inter‐lab variation contributes to inaccuracy. The reagents STA PTT A and Triniclot (one stage), as well as Siemens (chromogenic) (strongly) underestimate FVIII activity and are therefore unsuitable for accurate measurement of Damoctocog alfa pegol.ConclusionWe have evaluated accuracy results for 19 Factor VIII assays in order to provide evidence‐based guidance for clinical laboratories in selecting appropriate assay methodologies for reliable quantification of the activity of Damoctocog alfa pegol. Assay performance should be locally validated as large interlaboratory variation is observed.Keywords: damoctocog alfa pegol, drug monitoring, factor VIII, haemophilia A, medical laboratory science

Introduction

Haemophilia A is an inherited bleeding disorder characterized by a qualitative or quantitative deficiency of coagulation factor VIII (FVIII). FVIII replacement therapy has been the main treatment option during the last decennia. Recently, many novel therapeutic possibilities have emerged [1, 2]. The original replacement concentrates were derived from human blood, but advances in biotechnology have led to the development of recombinant FVIII products, with a significantly reduced risk of blood‐transmitted diseases. Over the past decade, these recombinant FVIII products have been further developed into extended half‐life (EHL) concentrates through molecular modifications, such as PEGylation or albumin fusion. These innovations have substantially improved patients’ quality of life by reducing the injection frequency to only once or twice weekly [3].

Damoctocog alfa pegol (Jivi, Bayer, Berkeley, United States) is an EHL product based on a PEGylated B‐domain deleted form of recombinant FVIII. Damoctocog alfa pegol received regulatory approval from both the European Medicines Agency and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 2018. Target FVIII levels vary depending on the clinical context, ranging from >1% (prophylactic use) to 20%–100% in case of active (small to life‐threatening) bleedings [4, 5].

Monitoring Damoctocog alfa pegol‐FVIII activity during treatment is essential for optimizing clinical outcomes. FVIII activity can be measured using one‐stage clotting or chromogenic assays. While the original one‐stage clotting assay is still the most widely used, haemophilia treatment centre laboratories have access to both assay types. International guidelines recommend the use of both the one‐stage FVIII and the chromogenic FVIII assay in the initial diagnostic workup [1].

According to the international guidelines, laboratories intending to measure Damoctocog alfa pegol activity should validate their procedures to ensure assay accuracy [1]. Previous studies have indicated that the FVIII activity of Damoctocog alfa pegol can be accurately measured in plasma provided that appropriate reagents are used for either one‐stage or chromogenic assays [5, 6, 7, 8]. Therefore, the World Federation of Hemophilia (WFH) guidelines offer specific recommendations regarding reagent selection: ‘the WFH recommends use of a chromogenic FVIII assay or APTT‐based one stage FVIII assay with validated reagents, including the ellagic acid activator reagent Actin FSL and some silica activator reagents (Pathromtin SL, SynthASil), calibrated with a plasma standard traceable to a WHO international standard.’ It also states which specific reagents are not suitable for this purpose: ‘One‐stage FVIII assays with the ellagic acid activator reagent Actin FS or the kaolin activator reagent CK Prest significantly overestimate true FVIII activity and should not be used. One‐stage FVIII assays with APTT‐SP and STA‐PTT Automate reagents significantly underestimate true FVIII activity and should not be used’ [1].

Given the documented variability in FVIII assay performance for modified products [9, 10], a more extensive evaluation of both one‐stage and chromogenic assay reliability following Damoctocog alfa pegol administration is warranted. To address this need, we conducted a field study designed to reflect the daily practice of haemostasis laboratories. The objective was to assess the accuracy of FVIII measurements obtained using various one‐stage and chromogenic methods in a large cohort of laboratories, in order to support laboratories in making evidence‐based decisions regarding the use of their analytical equipment and reagents.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study was designed as a multicentre field evaluation to investigate the recovery of Damoctocog alfa pegol by one‐stage clotting and chromogenic assays using laboratory methods under the same conditions clinical samples are routinely measured.

A set of five lyophilised plasma samples containing different concentrations of Damoctocog alfa pegol (for details see below) was distributed to 118 clinical laboratories using the global network of the external quality assessment provider ECAT Foundation (external quality control for assays and tests) in the period 2018–2022. The samples were coded and the concentration of Damoctocog alfa pegol was unknown to the participants in the study. Sample sets were distributed by either postal or courier service. Instructions for the reconstitution of the samples were included. Laboratories were asked to measure FVIII activity using their regular measurement procedure(s) for FVIII. No specific instructions were given on the order in which samples should be measured. Both one‐stage clotting assays and chromogenic assays could be used. Results were reported to ECAT for evaluation using a standardised report form which included the request for information on assay type, reagents, calibrators and instrumentation.

Plasma Samples

A set of 5 plasma samples was prepared in which congenital deficient FVIII plasma (FVIII level < 0.01 IU/mL) was spiked with different concentrations of Damoctocog alfa pegol (kindly provided by Bayer, Berkeley, United States). Damoctocog alfa pegol was dissolved according to the manufacturer's instructions resulting in a stock solution of approximately 1500 IU/mL. The actual concentration according to the certificate of analysis provided by Bayer was used for sample preparation. Plasmas with the following intended Damoctocog alfa pegol concentrations were prepared: 0.05 – 0.10 – 0.25 – 0.50 – 1.50 IU/mL (see Table 1). A first batch of 50 sets was prepared followed by an additional batch of 200 sets. Samples were lyophilised by Technoclone (Vienna, Austria) according to a standard procedure for coagulation plasma samples. Target values were established using the TECHNOCHROM FVIII chromogenic assay on a Ceveron alpha instrument (Technoclone) and an identical calibrator used by Bayer for value assignment (calibrator provided by Bayer). Each level was measured in triplicate in three independent runs, with three dilutions per run. A summary of the value assignment for both batches of samples is given in Table 1. The expanded uncertainty of the assigned value ranged from 2.8% to 7.7%.

Factor VIII Measurement

Participating laboratories measured FVIII activity with their routinely used one‐stage clotting and/or chromogenic assay, including their standard FVIII calibrator. Samples were measured on one day to minimize the effect of the between‐run variability. Laboratories were requested to report the specific reagents, calibrator, and instrument used (see Table S1 and S2). All samples were measured in three different dilutions. For each dilution the FVIII level is established, corrected for the dilution factor used. This results in 3 FVIII values per sample. The mean of the three corrected values was used to calculate the recovery of each sample.

Recovery

Due to the use of two separate production batches, recovery was selected as the primary evaluation parameter to normalize inter‐batch variability. For each laboratory and sample, relative recovery was calculated as: Recovery (%) = (mean FVIII level / the assigned value) X 100.

A recovery range of 80%–120% was predefined as acceptable, in line with established analytical performance criteria [11].

Data Analysis and Statistics

All laboratories that provided data for at least one sample were included in the full analysis set. In the case of missing information on the used reagent, the data were excluded from the analysis (n = 2). The results from the different laboratories were subsequently grouped by reagent. Detailed information on the used analysers, reagents, and calibrator is provided in Tables S1 and S2.

Sample statistics (mean, standard deviation, maximum, and minimum) were calculated for numeric data. As the lower limits of quantification (LLOQs) used by the individual participants were unknown, the analysis was restricted to results that were reported numerically. GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1) was used for the visualization of the mean recovery, as well as the standard deviation.

Results

Participating Laboratories

Laboratories from 17 different countries participated in this study, mainly from Europe (62%), North America (29%) and South America (8%).

The samples were sent to 118 different clinical laboratories who performed only an one‐stage assay (42%), only a chromogenic assay (2%) or an one‐stage assay and a chromogenic assay (56%) for FVIII activity measurement. No distinction was made between laboratories that use their factor VIII assay for haemophilia A therapeutic monitoring and those that use it in other diagnostic settings. Of the 204 performed assays, 63% were one‐stage (n = 128) and 37% were chromogenic assays (n = 76). For the one‐stage assay, 12 different commercial reagents were used by the participating laboratories. For the chromogenic assay seven different commercial kits were applied.

The assay results were divided into 12 and 7 method groups for one‐stage and chromogenic assays, respectively. The number of participants per reagent group varied considerably, ranging from 54 participants in the largest group, namely SynthasIL (Instrumentation Laboratory, Bedford, MA, USA) to a single participant for several rarely used one‐stage and chromogenic assay methods.

One‐Stage Assay Results

The one‐stage assay results demonstrate substantial variation in the number of users per reagent, reflecting the inherent diversity of real‐world laboratory practice. Five reagents were used by only one or two participants. The other seven were used by at least four participants and the three most commonly used reagents were used by 18, 20 or even 54 participants, respectively CK Prest (Stago, Asnières sur Seine, France), Pathromtin SL (Siemens, Marburg, Germany) and SynthasIL.

The less commonly used reagents were largely formulated with ellagic acid as the activator; that is, Actin (Siemens), Cephascreen (Stago), SynthaFax (Instrumentation Laboratory) and Coagpia APTT FS (Sekisui Medical, Tokyo, Japan). More widely used reagents with ellagic acid as activator were Actin FS (Siemens) (n = 11) and Actin FSL (Siemens) (n = 7). Almost all these assays tend to overestimate FVIII activity, however to a varying extent. The only ellagic acid‐containing reagent which performed accurately was Actin FSL (Figure 1).

The results seem to be more heterogeneous for the methods that use silica activation (see Figure 1). Starting with the reagent used by only one participant, Triniclot S (Stago), which resulted in an underestimation at all concentrations. Similar results of complete underestimation were found for STA PTT A (Stago) (n = 4). SynthasIL and Pathromtin SL, on the other hand, seem to lead to the most accurate results in this study when assessing mean results; they are also used by the largest groups of participants (respectively n = 54 and n = 20). The fifth silica activated reagent, APTT SP (Instrumentation Laboratory), performs in our study within or very close to range for three of the four participants. Just one participant strongly underestimates FVIII activity, which decreases mean results. The three participants who perform consistently all use the same equipment, calibrator and deficient plasma for their assay, whereas this information was not provided by the outlier.

The only Kaolin‐activated reagent, CK Prest, tends to slightly overestimate the FVIII concentrations, but performs most stable over the whole concentration range. Most other reagents demonstrate a, sometimes subtle, trend toward lower recovery at lower concentrations and progressively higher recovery with increasing Damoctocog alfa pegol concentrations (see Figure 1).

Notable for the one‐stage assays is the large interlaboratory variability. While certain assays seem to perform well on average, substantial variability persists across laboratories (Figure 1 and Table S3). For assays such as Pathromtin SL, this suggests that their reliability for Damoctocog alfa pegol measurements depends on the specific laboratory conditions more than the reagent use alone, as individual laboratories may still substantially underestimate or overestimate results. This greater inaccuracy was observed not only at low concentrations but also across all samples.

Chromogenic Assay Results

For the chromogenic assay there is a variation in the number of participants per reagent as well. One reagent, Precision Biologic (Precision BioLogic Inc, Dartmouth, Canada), was used by only one participant. For the other reagents, there were at least 4 up to 29 participants for the most widely used reagent Chromogenix (Chromogenix, Milan, Italy).

A consistent trend was observed across nearly all reagents for the chromogenic assays, with lower recovery at lower concentrations (mostly underestimation) and progressively higher recovery with increasing Damoctocog alfa pegol concentrations. Among the reagents tested, Trinichrom (Stago,) was the only one to exhibit stable recovery across the full concentration range, though it consistently underestimated FVIII activity with recoveries around 60% (Figure 2 and Table S3). Biophen (Hyphen Biomed, Neuville‐sur‐Oise, Frankrijk), the only assay with human coagulation factors in its reagent, produced the most reliable results; all clinically relevant concentrations, starting from 0.12 IU/ml, fall within the acceptance limits. Chromogenix was the most used reagent by the participating laboratories. This reagent was more affected by underestimation at lower concentrations and overestimation at higher concentrations. In the mid‐range, however, it performed within the acceptance criteria. A similar, but even more pronounced trend was seen for Elektrachrome (HemosIL, Instrumentation Laboratory). Siemens, as well as Trinichrom, consistently underestimated Damoctocog alfa pegol concentrations and did not meet the acceptance criteria. Since Precision Biologic was used by only one participant, the interpretability and generalizability of the results are limited. The reagent seems to be suboptimal in measuring Damoctocog alfa pegol‐activity reliable.

Discussion

Since the introduction of a wide range of FVIII replacement products, it has been well established that monitoring treatment with these agents has become increasingly complex. Multiple studies have demonstrated that assay results may vary depending on the laboratory test and reagents used [9, 10, 12]. New therapeutic agents are typically evaluated under optimized conditions in initial studies, often with only a few reagents. However, the results of these studies may not fully reflect the realities of routine practice in a coagulation laboratory. Therefore, this study was designed as a field investigation to provide supplementary data on the reliability of Damoctocog alfa pegol measurements across various platform, calibrator and reagent combinations in a real‐world clinical setting. This large‐scale field study again highlights the challenges faced by clinical laboratories in reliably measuring a commonly used recombinant FVIII product, Damoctocog alfa pegol, in routine practice when no product‐specific validation has been performed.

Our data indicates that considerable variability exists among different assays with respect to the reliability of the measurement of the Damoctocog alfa pegol concentration (Factor VIII). A finding recently also described by Williams et al. [8]. For the one‐stage assays, this variability appears to be predominantly reagent‐dependent rather than activator‐dependent. Nevertheless, almost all reagents that are ellagic acid activated (i.e., Actin, Cephascreen, SynthaFax, Coagpia APTT FS and Actin FS) tend to overestimate FVIII activity to some extent. This effect has been described before for Actin FS [6]. The only ellagic acid‐containing reagent which performed close to range was Actin FSL; an effect which is reported in a previous study [6].

For the silica activated assays we found more heterogeneous results. Triniclot S and STA PTT A both were unable to measure FVIII activity in any of our samples. These outcomes were consistent with previous findings for STA PTT A [6]. On the other hand, using SynthasIL or Pathromtin SL as a reagent seem to lead to accurate values when taking the mean results of all participants in consideration. Most of our participants use one of these one stage assay reagents in daily practice (respectively 43% and 16% of the participants). This performance of both assays is in line with the results of Church et al. [6]. Nevertheless, within a given reagent type, interlaboratory differences are pronounced. While certain one stage assays appear to perform well on average, consistent with previous reports, substantial variability persists across laboratories, as is depicted in Figure 1. For some assays (e.g., Pathromtin SL), this suggests that despite their presumed reliability for Damoctocog alfa pegol measurements, individual laboratories may still substantially underestimate or overestimate results. This greater inaccuracy was observed not only at low concentrations, as described by previous studies on factor VIII analytical variation [13], but across all samples. Conversely, assays often described in literature and guidelines as unsuitable may yield poor outcomes in isolated laboratories, yet produce more than acceptable results in others. In our study APTT SP, which is described to underestimate Damoctocog alfa pegol concentrations, performs within or very close to range for most participants in our study [6, 7].

These discrepancies may be attributable to variations in instrumentation, calibrators, and other methodological factors. A study by Suzuki et al. showed, for example, the effect of different used congenital FVIII depleted plasmas (FVIIIDP) [10]. Information regarding the type of deficient plasma used in the participating laboratories was not collected in this study. But differences in the deficient plasmas used (congenital vs. immunodepleted) may have contributed to differences in results. The summary of the used reagents, equipment, calibrator in our study (supplemental data) shows the large diversity of combinations used in clinical practice. Due to the considerable variability in combinations of reagents, calibrators, and deficient plasma, it was not possible to perform a reliable sub‐analysis on these data. Nevertheless, our data underlines again that the different combinations can have a large effect on the reliability of the assay.

At lower concentrations, in routine settings measured in case of prophylactic purposes, a slightly less accurate measurement would often be adequate for clinical decision making. In the WFH guidelines, there are no recommendation for target factor levels for physical activity, nevertheless they suggest prophylaxis treatment could be individualized. For active individuals more frequent infusions for greater protection could be considered. In these cases, reliable activity measurements of prophylaxis would be important. Also at higher concentrations, in the context of planned surgeries or acute bleeding episodes, it would be important to reliably quantify the FVIII concentration [1, 14, 15]. Unfortunately, also at the higher concentrations many reagents performed suboptimal. Of the one‐stage assays, only SynthasIL seemed to perform accurately for all participants at the three higher concentrations. For the chromogenic assays Biophen performed well at all concentrations and Chromogenix performed adequately mainly in the middle region (0.12‐0.48 IU/ml).

As described by Bowyer and Kitchen [16], the use of product‐specific calibrators (PSC) may overcome the reagent variability in FVIII activity, as shown for several haemostasis molecules. Since haemostasis laboratories are often constrained by the local reagent portfolio available, this could enable laboratories to use their routine reagents for accurate Damoctocog alfa pegol monitoring. Ideally such a PSC would be developed during the regulatory approval process of new FVIII therapies. Commercially available PSC can help in the reliable monitoring of the product once used in the clinical setting.

Conclusion

In this study, samples were analysed using the standard procedures routinely employed by laboratories for FVIII assays. However, given the interlaboratory variability, the use of a Damoctocog alfa pegol‐specific calibrator instead of a generic FVIII calibrator may enhance analytical accuracy, as well as the reliability of clinical decision‐making. However, this makes it more challenging in daily practice to implement the test for routine diagnostics and therefore to offer it on a 24/7 basis. On top of that, concomitant use of non‐replacement factor therapy with add‐on FVIII, for example, to resolve breakthrough bleeds or manage surgery, provides further challenges to accurately measure FVIII activity in vivo. Nevertheless, the use of these current underperforming assays in clinical laboratories can lead to wrong decisions and under‐ or over‐dosing of the patient, with the potential to result in significant clinical consequences.

Further standardization and harmonization across assays are required to ensure accurate and reliable measurement of FVIII concentrations, especially with the wide use of recombinant FVIII substrates. Until such harmonization is achieved, it is of critical importance that each individual laboratory validates whether its chosen method is capable of reliably quantifying the various add‐on factor VIII concentrates.
