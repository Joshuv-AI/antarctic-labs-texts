Taxonomic descriptions of Nocardia anocheti sp. nov. and Streptomyces odontomachicola sp. nov. isolated from ants
Authors: Achiraya Somphong, Tuangrat Tunvongvinis, Chanwit Suriyachadkun, Weeyawat Jaitrong, Enjuro Harunari, Somboon Tanasupawat, Wongsakorn Phongsopitanun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Two novel actinomycete strains, AG03ᵀ and ODS28ᵀ, were isolated from worker ants Anochetus graeffei and Odontomachus simillimus, respectively, collected in Thailand. Strain AG03ᵀ exhibited the highest 16S rRNA gene sequence similarity (98.28%) to Nocardia grenadensis NBRC 108939ᵀ, whereas strain ODS28ᵀ showed the highest similarity (97.73%) to Actinacidiphila bryophytorum NEAU-HZ10ᵀ. Chemotaxonomic analysis of AG03ᵀ revealed the presence of meso-diaminopimelic acid in the cell wall, glucose and mannose as whole-cell sugars, and MK-8(H₄ω-cycl) as the predominant menaquinone. In contrast, ODS28ᵀ contained LL-diaminopimelic acid, glucose, ribose, and xylose as whole-cell sugars, with MK-9(H₈) and MK-9(H₆) as the predominant menaquinones. Whole genome sequence-based relatedness values, including average nucleotide identity (ANI) and digital DNA-DNA hybridization (dDDH), supported the classification of both strains as representing novel species. Based on polyphasic taxonomic evidence, strains AG03ᵀ and ODS28ᵀ are proposed as the type strains of Nocardia anocheti sp. nov. (AG03ᵀ = TBRC 16206ᵀ = NBRC 115864ᵀ) and Streptomyces odontomachicola sp. nov. (ODS28ᵀ = TBRC 18345ᵀ = NBRC 116641ᵀ), respectively. The genomes of both strains contained biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) potentially responsible for the production of bioactive secondary metabolites. These findings suggest that social insects, such as ants, represent a promising source of novel actinomycetes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43878-x.

Introduction

Actinomycetes, as used here in a classical, descriptive sense, refer to a heterogeneous assemblage of Gram-positive bacteria within the phylum Actinomycetota (formerly Actinobacteria) that typically exhibit filamentous growth and/or mycelium-like morphology and possess relatively high genomic G + C content. Many, but not all, members of this bacterial group display complex morphological differentiation and are recognized for their capacity to synthesize diverse secondary metabolites, including antibiotics, antifungal agents, antitumor compounds, and immunosuppressants¹,². Among these bacteria, species of the genus Streptomyces account most of the clinically important antibiotics in current use, while other rare actinomycete genera such as Nocardia, Amycolatopsis, and Actinoplanesare also recognized as notable producers of pharmaceutically relevant natural products3,4.. The ongoing global threat of antimicrobial resistance has intensified the urgency to explore underexploited ecological niches for the discovery of new bioactive actinomycetes5. In this context, insects, particularly ants, have gained increasing attention as promising reservoirs of novel actinomycetes taxa6,7. Symbiotic relationships between ants and actinomycetes have been widely documented. These bacteria are often localized on the ant cuticle or housed in specialized crypts, where they produce antimicrobial compounds that suppress entomopathogens and protect the ant colony8. This mutualism has been particularly well studied in attine (fungus-farming) ants, where Pseudonocardia and Streptomyces symbionts produce antibiotics to inhibit pathogenic fungi such as Escovopsis, which threaten the ants cultivated fungal food source9,10. Beyond attine ants, other ant genera have also been found to harbor diverse actinomycetes. Notably, several ant-associated strains have exhibited antimicrobial activity against human pathogens such as Candida albicans and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). For example, Streptomyces amphotericinicus and Streptomyces lasiicapitis, isolated from Camponotus japonicus and Lasius fuliginosus respectively, produce amphotericin and kanchanamycin both with potent antifungal and antibacterial properties11. A study conducted in Thailand further emphasized the potential of this ecological niche by isolating actinomycetes strains from ants, among which a considerable number exhibited bioactivity against clinical bacterial strains, including MRSA12. Ant-associated actinomycetes thus constitute a promising but largely uninvestigated reservoir of microbial and chemical diversity. Despite Thailand’s rich biodiversity and ecological complexity, the actinomycetes diversity associated with ants in this region remains poorly characterized. Only a few studies have reported the isolation of actinomycetes from ants in Thailand, and formal taxonomic descriptions are notably lacking. This limited knowledge constrains our understanding of their ecological functions and biomedical potential. Continued investigation of ant-associated actinomycetes in this region therefore holds substantial promise for the discovery of novel taxa.

As part of such an investigation, strain AG03ᵀ (Nocardia sp.) and strain ODS28ᵀ (Streptomyces sp.) were isolated from Anochetus graeffei and Odontomachus simillimus, respectively. This study aimed to characterize both strains using a polyphasic approach to determine their taxonomic positions and assess their novelty at the species level, as well as their potential relevance for future of potential pharmaceutical applications.

Results

16S rRNA gene analysis and phylogeny

Strain AG03ᵀ exhibited the highest 16S rRNA gene sequence similarity (98.28%) to Nocardia grenadensis NBRC 108939ᵀ. Phylogenetic analysis based on 16S rRNA gene sequences consistently placed strain AG03ᵀ within the genus Nocardia, where it formed a distinct node closely associated with Nocardia camponoti 1 H-HV4ᵀ across all constructed phylogenetic trees (Fig. 1; Figs. S1 and S2). On the other hand, strain ODS28ᵀ exhibited the highest 16S rRNA gene sequence similarity (97.73%) to Actinacidiphila bryophytorum NEAU-HZ10ᵀ. However, phylogenetic analysis based on 16S rRNA gene sequences placed ODS28ᵀ within the Streptomyces clade, where it formed a distinct, independent branch separate from A. bryophytorum NEAU-HZ10ᵀ the closest taxon identified by sequence similarity but grouped with Streptomyces nanshensis SCSIO 01066T, Streptomyces abyssalis YIM M 10400T and Streptomyces xishensis YIM M 10378T (Fig. 2; Figs S3-S4).

Genome Features

The draft genome sequences of strains AG03T and ODS28T were deposited in the DDBJ/ENA/GenBank databases under the accession numbers JAQQFV000000000 and JBCFOK000000000, respectively. The draft genome assembly of strain AG03ᵀ comprises 42 contigs totaling 6,424,294 base pairs, with a G + C content of 69.61%. Genome quality was evaluated using CheckM (v1.2.2), which indicated a completeness of 98.66% and contamination of 3.76%. The genome of strain AG03ᵀ was predicted to contain multiple biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) related to secondary metabolite production. Notably, six BGCs were identified with high or medium confidence. These included a non-alpha poly-amino acid (NAPAA) cluster for ε-poly-L-lysine, an aminopolycarboxylic acid cluster, and a hybrid NRPS-T1PKS cluster showing high similarity to the collismycin biosynthetic pathway. Additionally, NRP-metallophore/NRPS cluster showed moderate similarity to heterobactin-type siderophores. Furthermore, a high confidence ectoine biosynthetic gene cluster was also detected, indicating the potential for osmoprotectant production, The predicted secondary metabolite clusters in strain AG03ᵀ are listed in Table S1.

To determine the taxonomic position within the genus Nocardia, a genome-based phylogenetic analysis was performed. A MLSA tree, constructed using high-quality genome sequences of strain AG03ᵀ and closely related Nocardia type strains, revealed that AG03ᵀ formed a distinct monophyletic clade within the genus. Although AG03ᵀ shared the highest 16S rRNA gene sequence similarity (98.28%) with N. grenadensis NBRC 108939ᵀ, it did not cluster directly with this strain in the MLSA tree. Instead, strain AG03ᵀ branched independently, forming a stable and well-supported lineage with high bootstrap values across all nodes (Fig. 3). Based on 16S rRNA gene phylogeny and MLSA phylogeny, N. camponoti was selected for comparison with strain AG03T. The digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) value between strain AG03ᵀ and N. camponoti was 21.5%, while the average nucleotide identity based on MUMmer (ANIm) and average nucleotide identity based on BLAST (ANIb) were 76.8% and 84.7%, respectively. Similarly, the dDDH, ANIm, and ANIb values between strain AG03ᵀ and N. grenadensis were 20.7%, 84.2%, and 74.6%, respectively (Table S2). All these values are substantially below the established species delineation thresholds of 70% for dDDH and 95–96% for ANI13,14, supporting the conclusion that strain AG03ᵀ does not belong to any currently described species. Therefore, strain AG03ᵀ represents a novel species within the genus Nocardia, for which the name Nocardia anocheti sp. nov. is proposed.

Meanwhile, the draft genome assembly of strain ODS28ᵀ comprises 51 contigs, totaling 8,137,818 base pairs, with a calculated G + C content of 72.3%. Quality assessment using CheckM (v1.2.2) estimated the genome to be 91.78% complete with 1.26% contamination. The genome of strain ODS28ᵀ harbors a diverse array of biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) associated with secondary metabolite production. These included terpene pathways, such as geosmin and hopene, as well as a hybrid polyketide synthase cluster similar to A-94964. The genome also carried genes associated with the production of pristinol and a SapB-like lanthipeptide. In addition, NRPS-like clusters potentially responsible for the synthesis of minimycin or indigoidine were identified. A siderophore cluster related to the legonoxamine and desferrioxamine families was present, along with genes for ectoine biosynthesis. Notably, the genome also harbored a large multi-functional cluster containing NRPS, RiPP-like, and metallophore components, with similarity to pathways for thiazostatin, watasemycin, and pyochelin analogues. A summary of the predicted clusters is provided in Table S3.

In contrast to strain AG03ᵀ, the phylogenetic position of strain ODS28ᵀ could not be determined using genome-based analysis due to the unavailability of whole-genome sequences for the type strains S. nanshensis SCSIO 01066ᵀ, S. abyssalis YIM M 10400ᵀ, and S. xishensis YIM M 10378ᵀ in public databases. To address this limitation, we followed the recommendation of Kim et al.15, which suggests that a 16S rRNA gene sequence similarity of 98.65 % serves as a threshold for species delineation. The 16S rRNA gene sequence similarity between strain ODS28ᵀ and the aforementioned three type strains was 97.57%, which is significantly below the proposed cutoff. This clearly indicates that strain ODS28ᵀ represents a species distinct from S. nanshensis, S. abyssalis, and S. xishensis. Furthermore, comparative genome analysis was performed with A. bryophytorum CGMCC 4.7151ᵀ, the closest type strain with available genome data. The digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH), average nucleotide identity based on BLAST (ANIb), and MUMmer-based ANI (ANIm) values between strain ODS28ᵀ and A. bryophytorum CGMCC 4.7151ᵀ were 21.5%, 74.5%, and 84.3%, respectively (Table S4)—well below the thresholds of 70% for dDDH and 95% for ANI used to delineate bacterial species. Based on these results, strain ODS28ᵀ is proposed to represent a novel species of the genus Streptomyces, for which the name Streptomyces odontomachicola sp. nov. is proposed.

Phenotypic properties and Chemotaxonomy

Strain AG03ᵀ developed colony colors ranging from moderate yellowish pink to pinkish gray, depending on the medium. The substrate mycelia appeared pale to brilliant orange-yellow. Aerial mycelium formation was observed on ISP 2, ISP 4, and ISP 5, where purplish white to pale pink aerial masses were produced. No soluble pigments were detected on any of the media tested. Detailed on cultural characteristics are presented in Table 1. Scanning electron micrographs revealed that strain AG03ᵀ produced long chains of spores, which fragmented into rod-shaped spores with smooth surfaces (Fig. 4). The chemotaxonomic characteristics of strain AG03ᵀ were consistent with those of members of the genus Nocardia. The cell wall contained meso-diaminopimelic acid as the diagnostic diamino acid. Whole-cell sugar analysis revealed the presence of glucose and mannose. The predominant menaquinone was MK-8(H₄ω-cycl). The polar lipid profile included phosphatidylethanolamine, diphosphatidylglycerol, phosphatidylinositol, and phosphatidylinositol mannosides, along with several unidentified lipids and one unidentified glycolipid (Fig. S5). The major cellular fatty acids were C18:0 (33.1%), C16:0 (28.2%), and 10-methyl C18:0 (12.8%).

Based on biochemical analyses, strain AG03ᵀ shares several characteristics with N. camponoti 1 H-HV4ᵀ and N. grenadensis NBRC 108939ᵀ. All three strains are capable of utilizing glucose, sucrose, and D-galactose as sole carbon sources. However, several phenotypic traits distinguish strain AG03ᵀ from its closely related Nocardia species. Notably, strain AG03ᵀ does not utilize myo-inositol or D-mannose, whereas both N. camponoti 1 H-HV4ᵀ and N. grenadensis NBRC 108939ᵀ can metabolize these substrates. Additionally, strain AG03ᵀ is unable to utilize L-rhamnose, D-raffinose, and L-arabinose, in contrast to N. camponoti 1 H-HV4ᵀ, which can utilize all three. Growth conditions further differentiate strain AG03ᵀ from N. camponoti 1 H-HV4ᵀ. Strain AG03ᵀ grows within a temperature range of 25–37 °C, pH 7.0–10.0, and tolerates up to 3% (w/v) NaCl. In comparison, N. camponoti 1 H-HV4ᵀ grows at 15–30 °C, pH 5.0–10.0, and tolerates NaCl concentrations up to 2% (w/v). These differences in carbon source utilization and physiological characteristics support the distinct taxonomic status of strain AG03ᵀ within the genus Nocardia (Table 2).

Strain ODS28ᵀ exhibited good growth on ISP 2, ISP 3, and ISP 4, moderate growth on ISP 6 and nutrient agar, and poor growth on ISP 5 and ISP 7. The strain developed extensively branched substrate mycelia but did not produce aerial mycelia on most media, with the exception of ISP 3, where white aerial masses were observed (Fig. 5). In the absence of aerial mycelia, the colony coloration varied by medium: grayish white on ISP 2, yellowish white on ISP 3, pale yellow on ISP 4, pale yellowish green on ISP 5, dark orange-yellow on ISP 6 and ISP 7, and greenish white on nutrient agar (Table 1). No soluble pigments were detected on any of the media used. The spore chains were not observed on any of the agar media used in this study.

Strain ODS28ᵀ and its closely related Streptomyces type strains exhibited growth at temperatures up to 37 °C. Several phenotypic features distinguish strain ODS28ᵀ from its phylogenetically close relatives, including A. bryophytorum NEAU-HZ10ᵀ, S. xishensis YIM M 10378ᵀ, S. abyssalis YIM M 10400ᵀ, and S. nanshensis SCSIO 01066ᵀ. Notably, strain ODS28ᵀ tolerated NaCl concentrations up to 9% (w/v), whereas the maximum tolerances of the reference strains were 2%, 7%, 6%, and 7%, respectively. Strain ODS28ᵀ is neutrophilic, with optimal growth at pH 7 and a pH growth range of 6–10. This is comparable to A. bryophytorum NEAU-HZ10ᵀ but differs from S. xishensis, S. abyssalis, and S. nanshensis, which grow within a narrower pH range of 6–8. Additionally, strain ODS28ᵀ demonstrated the ability to hydrolyze starch and gelatin traits shared with A. bryophytorum NEAU-HZ10ᵀ but absent in the other three Streptomyces species. Carbon source utilization further differentiates strain ODS28ᵀ. It utilized D-mannitol, D-mannose, L-rhamnose, sucrose, D-raffinose, D-xylose, D-cellobiose, L-arabinose, and D-trehalose. A comparative summary of these phenotypic characteristics with related type strains is provided in Table 3.

Description of Nocardia anocheti sp. nov.

Nocardia anocheti (a.no.che′.ti. N.L. gen. n. anocheti of Anochetus, referring to the ant genus Anochetus graeffei from which the type strain was isolated)

Nocardia anocheti sp. nov. comprises Gram-positive, aerobic, and filamentous actinomycetes that form an extensively branched substrate mycelium and fragment into irregular elements. Colonies are moderate to good on ISP 2-ISP 6 and nutrient agar, but poor on ISP 7. Colony colour ranges from moderate yellowish pink to pinkish gray depending on the medium. Aerial mycelia are produced on ISP 2, ISP 4, and ISP 5, and appear purplish white to pale pink. No diffusible pigment is observed. Grows at 25–37 °C, tolerates up to 3% (w/v), NaCl, and grows between pH 7.0–10.0. Positive for nitrate reduction. Hydrolyses neither starch nor gelatin. Utilizes glucose, sucrose, glycerol, D-cellobiose, and D-galactose as sole carbon sources, but not utilized L-arabinose, D-mannitol, creatine, myo-inositol, D-mannose, D-melezitose, L-rhamnose, D-raffinose, D-xylose, D-melibiose, trehalose and cellulose. L-asparagine was utilized, whereas L-threonine, L-creatine, L-valine, L-proline, and were not utilized as nitrogen sources. Enzyme activity includes strong activity for leucine arylamidase, β-glucosidase, esterase (C4), and β-galactosidase; moderate for esterase lipase (C8) and acid phosphatase; weak activity for valine arylamidase, cystine arylamidase, α-chymotrypsin, and naphthol-AS-BI-phosphohydrolase. No activity is observed for alkaline phosphatase, lipase (C14), trypsin, α-galactosidase, β-glucuronidase, α-glucosidase, N-acetyl-β-D-glucosaminidase, α-mannosidase, or α-fucosidase. Chemotaxonomic features include the presence of meso-diaminopimelic acid in the cell wall. Whole-cell sugars include glucose and mannose. The major menaquinones are MK-8(H4ω-cycl). Polar lipids consist of phosphatidylethanolamine, diphosphatidylglycerol, phosphatidylinositol, phosphatidylinositol mannosides, and unidentified lipids, and unidentified glycolipids. The majority of fatty acids were C18:0, C16:0, and 10-methyl C18:0. DNA G + C content is 69.6%.

The type strain is AG03T (= TBRC 16206T= NBRC 115864T), isolated from a worker ant Anochetus graeffei collected in Trang, Thailand. The accession numbers for the 16S rRNA gene sequence and genome of the type strain are OQ561212 and JAQQFV000000000, respectively.

Description of Streptomyces odontomachicola sp. nov.

Streptomyces odontomachicola (o.don.to.ma.chi′co.la. N.L. masc. n. Odontomachus, and ant genus; L. suff. -cola (from L. masc./gen. n. incola), inhabitant; N.L. masc. n. odontomachicola, inhabitant of Odontomachus simillimus)

Streptomyces odontomachicola sp. nov. is a Gram-positive, aerobic, and filamentous bacterium. It forms well-developed substrate mycelia but does not produce stable aerial mycelia on most media, except for ISP 3, where white aerial masses are visible. Colonies grow well on ISP 2-ISP 4, moderately on ISP 6 and nutrient agar, and poorly on ISP 5 and ISP 7. Colony colours range from grayish yellow to dark orange yellow or greenish white, depending on the medium. No diffusible pigment is produced. The strain grows at 25–37 °C, tolerates up to 9% NaCl, and grows within a pH range of 6.0–10.0. It shows positive results for nitrate reduction and hydrolyzes starch and gelatin. It utilizes carbon sources including glucose, sucrose, glycerol, myo-inositol, D-mannitol, D-mannose, D-melezitose, L-rhamnose, D-raffinose, D-xylose, D-galactose, and trehalose, but does not utilize D-cellobiose, D-melibiose, cellulose, L-arabinose, or creatine. L-asparagine, L-threonine, L-valine, and L-proline are not utilized as sole nitrogen sources. Enzymatic activity includes strong reactions for alkaline phosphatase, leucine arylamidase, trypsin, acid phosphatase, α-glucosidase, β-glucosidase, and N-acetyl-β-D-glucosaminidase; moderate for valine arylamidase, cystine arylamidase, β-galactosidase, and α-mannosidase; and weak for esterase (C4), esterase lipase (C8), α-chymotrypsin, and naphthol-AS-BI-phosphohydrolase. No activity is observed for lipase (C14), α-galactosidase, β-glucuronidase, or α-fucosidase. Chemotaxonomic analysis reveals the presence of LL-diaminopimelic acid in the cell wall. Whole-cell sugars include glucose, ribose, and xylose. The predominant menaquinones are MK-9(H8) and MK-9(H6). The polar lipids include phosphatidylethanolamine, diphosphatidylglycerol, phosphatidylglycerol, phosphatidylinositol, phosphatidylinositol mannosides, seven unidentified phospholipids and one unidentified aminophospholipid. The major fatty acids are anteiso-C15:0, iso-C16:0, iso-C15:0, and anteiso-C17:0.

The type strain is ODS28T (= TBRC 18345T= NBRC 116641T), isolated from Odontomachus simillimus collected at the National Science Museum, Pathum Thani, Thailand. The genome comprises 8,137,818 bp and has a DNA G + C content of 72.3%. The accession numbers for the 16S rRNA gene sequence and genome of the type strain are OQ559042 and JBCFOK000000000, respectively.

Discussion and Conclusion

Ants have increasingly been recognized as a promising source of novel actinomycetes with antimicrobial potential. Over the past decade, several new actinomycete species have been isolated from various ant species. For example, Streptomyces camponoticapitis21 and Nocardia camponoti16 were isolated from the head of Camponotus japonicus Mayr; Amycolatopsis camponoti from carpenter ant Camponotus vagus22; “Streptomyces lasii”23 from the head of Lasius flavus; Streptomyces polyrhachii from the Chinese black ant Polyrhachis vicina24; Streptomyces odontomachi from Odontomachus simillimus25; and both Streptomyces lasiicapitis and Nocardia lasii from the head and cuticle of Lasius fuliginosus26,27, respectively. A notable example of an antibiotic-producing, ant-associated actinomycete is Streptomyces formicae, which was first isolated from the head of Camponotus japonicus28 and later from Tetraponera penzigi, an African ant species29. This Streptomyces species was shown to produce a series of novel pentacyclic polyketides, including fasamycins C–E and formicamycins A–M, which exhibit potent activity against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE)29. Another notable example is the discovery of two novel 20-membered macrolides, formicolides A and B, produced by Streptomyces sp. BA01, which was isolated from the gut of the wood ant Formica yessensis. These compounds exhibited significant antioxidative and antiangiogenic activities30. In this context, the isolation of strains AG03ᵀ and ODS28ᵀ from Thai ant species further supports ants as an important ecological niche for actinomycetes with significant biosynthetic and pharmaceutical potential.

The findings of this study contribute to the understanding of ant-associated actinomycetes in Thailand and emphasize their potential ecological and pharmaceutical importance. Strains AG03ᵀ and ODS28ᵀ, isolated from Anochetus graeffei and Odontomachus simillimus, respectively, exhibited distinctive phenotypic, chemotaxonomic, and genomic characteristics clearly differentiating them from their closely related type strains. Genome-based ANI and dDDH analyses confirmed the novelty of these two strains at the species level, with values significantly below established thresholds for species delineation. These genomic findings were further corroborated by differences in phenotypic traits, including distinct profiles of carbon and nitrogen source utilization and enzymatic activities. Notably, both strains harbor diverse biosynthetic gene clusters associated with siderophore, polyketide, lanthipeptide, and ectoine biosynthesis. The limitation of this study is that, although the genomes of strains AG03ᵀ and ODS28ᵀ contain several predicted BGCs related to secondary metabolite biosynthesis, both assemblies are draft genomes derived from short-read Illumina sequencing data; therefore, some clusters may be fragmented or incomplete. Consequently, these BGCs represent preliminary predictions rather than experimentally validated biosynthetic pathways. Further work, such as complete genome sequencing or experimental activation of these clusters, will be required to assess their biosynthetic potential.

Most Nocardia species have been isolated from clinical specimens and terrestrial soil environments. However, several Nocardia species have also been recovered from insect sources. For example, as mentioned, N. camponoti and N. lasii were isolated from ants, with their type strains originating from Camponotus and Lasius species, respectively. Additionally, not only ant, Nocardia macrotermitis sp. nov. and Nocardia aurantia sp. nov. were isolated from the gut of the fungus-growing termite Macrotermes natalensis31. In the present study, strain AG03ᵀ exhibited close phylogenetic relatedness to N. camponoti, which was previously isolated from the ant C. japonicus. These findings suggest a potential ecological association between these Nocardia species and insect hosts. Thus, insects may serve as a natural reservoir for Nocardia; however, further investigations are required to clarify the nature of this association.

In conclusion, this study broadens the knowledge of the diversity of actinomycetes associated with ants in Thailand, resulting in the description of two novel species within the genera Nocardia and Streptomyces, for which the names Nocardia anocheti sp. nov. and Streptomyces odontomachicola sp. nov. are proposed. These findings underscore the significance of ants as reservoirs of actinomycetes with biotechnological potential, emphasizing the importance of ongoing research into insect-microbe symbioses, especially within underexplored ecosystems.

Methodology

Sample collection and isolation

In our previous study, the worker ants of Anochetus graeffei and Odontomachus simillimus were collected from Trang and National Science Museum, Pathum Thani Thailand, respectively12. Ant specimens were rinsed with sterile water to remove adhering debris. The ants were then aseptically crushed in mortars, and the homogenate was serially diluted in sterile distilled water. Aliquots (0.1 mL) of the dilutions were spread on starch casein nitrate agar (SCNA) for strain AG03T and humic acid-salts vitamin agar (HV) for strain ODS28T, adapted from that developed by Hayakawa and Nonomura32 and supplemented with cycloheximide (50 µg mL− 1) and nalidixic acid (25 µg mL− 1) to inhibit fungal and Gram-negative bacterial growth. The plates were incubated at 30 °C for 14 days. After incubation, colonies of strains AG03ᵀ and ODS28ᵀ were isolated from ant samples A. graeffei and O. simillimus, respectively. The strains were maintained on ISP 2 agar slants as working cultures and preserved in lyophilized form for long-term storage.

16S rRNA gene analysis and phylogeny

Genomic DNA was extracted from fresh cell cultures (3–5 days) in ISP 2 broth using the PureLink Genomic DNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The 16S rRNA gene was amplified using universal primers 27F and 1492R33 and sequenced by Macrogen (Seoul, South Korea). Sequence similarity and phylogenetic analyses were conducted using the EzBioCloud database and MEGA X software, respectively34,35. Neighbor-joining (NJ), maximum-parsimony (MP), and maximum-likelihood (ML) trees were reconstructed. Bootstrap analyses were performed with 1000 replications. The evolutionary distances among the strains in the neighbor-joining (NJ) and maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenetic trees were calculated using Kimura two-parameter model, while the maximum-parsimony (MP) tree was optimized using the subtree-pruning-and-regrafting (SPR) algorithm to refine tree topology.

Genome Features

The whole genome sequencing was carried out using the Illumina NovaSeq platform. The raw sequence reads were quality-checked using fastQC and trimmed to remove adapters and low-quality sequences using fastp36,37. Cleaned reads were assembled de novo using Unicycler38. The draft genome assembly was deposited in GenBank. The multi-locus sequence analysis-based phylogenetic tree was constructed using AutoMLST web server (https://automlst.ziemertlab.com/)39. Digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) values were obtained using the TYGS platform (http://tygs.dsmz.de)40. The dDDH (d4, %) values, corresponding to the recommended formula for incomplete draft genomes, were used for species delineation. Average nucleotide identity (ANI) values were calculated using the JSpeciesWS web service (http://jspecies.ribohost.com/jspeciesws)41. Secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) were predicted using antiSMASH version 8.0.1 (https://antismash.secondarymetabolites.org/) with default parameters42.

Phenotypic properties

The cultural characteristics of the novel strains were observed after incubation on various International Streptomyces Project (ISP) media, including ISP 2 (yeast extract-malt extract agar), ISP 3 (oatmeal agar), ISP 4 (inorganic salts starch agar), ISP 5 (glycerol asparagine agar), ISP 6 (peptone yeast extract iron agar), ISP 7 (tyrosine agar), and NA (nutrient agar), at 30 °C for 14 days. The observed characteristics included colony colour, substrate, and aerial mycelia, and the production of soluble pigments was determined using the National Bureau of Standards/Inter-Society Color Council colour chart43. Observation of spore shape and surface was performed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM); (JSM-IT500HR, JEOL) on cultures grown on appropriate media. Samples for SEM observation were prepared as described previously44. Physiological and biochemical characteristics were observed using standard classical methods as described by Shirling & Gottlieb, Arai, and Williams45–47. Growth at different temperatures was assessed on ISP 2 agar at 4, 15, 25, 30, 37, 40, and 50 °C. The pH range for growth was determined in ISP 2 broth adjusted to pH values from 4.0 to 10.0 at 1.0-unit intervals. Tolerance to NaCl was tested by incorporating various concentrations of NaCl (0–10% (w/v) at 1% intervals) into ISP 2 broth. Enzymatic activities were investigated using the API ZYM system (bioMérieux, France), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Utilization of different carbon and nitrogen sources was examined using basal medium (ISP 9) supplemented with individual substrates (1%, w/v)44.

Chemotaxonomy

Cell biomass used for chemotaxonomic analyses was obtained from cultures grown in ISP 2 broth at 28 °C for 3–5 days and subsequently freeze-dried. The isomeric form of diaminopimelic acid (DAP) in the peptidoglycan, as well as whole-cell sugars, was determined by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) following the method described by Staneck and Roberts48. Quinones were extracted from freeze-dried cells according to the procedure of Collins et al.49, and analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a Cosmosil 5C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm; Nacalai Tesque). The HPLC separations were carried out under isocratic conditions using a 1:4 (v/v) isopropanol/methanol mobile phase at a flow rate of 1.2 ml min⁻¹, with an injection volume of 20 µl and UV detection at 270 nm. Polar lipids were extracted following the protocols of Minnikin et al. and Lechevalier et al.5051, separated by two-dimensional TLC, and visualized using appropriate spray reagents. The cellular fatty acid profile was determined by gas chromatography following the method of Sasser52, employing the MIDI Sherlock Microbial Identification System (version 6.1; database: TSBA6). Fatty acids constituting more than 10% of the total were designated as predominant components.
