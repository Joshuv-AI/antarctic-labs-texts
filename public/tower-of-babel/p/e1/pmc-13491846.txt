Human parvovirus B19-associated Guillain-Barré syndrome diagnosed by cerebrospinal fluid targeted next-generation sequencing: a case report and literature review
Authors: Yujun Wu, Ye Liu, Liyan Gu, Yongqiu Zhang, Jie Wei, Xiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGuillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is a post-infectious autoimmune disorder of the peripheral nerves. While human parvovirus B19 (B19V) is a recognized rare trigger, its diagnosis can be challenging without advanced molecular techniques.Case presentationA 24-year-old male presented with acute symmetrical limb weakness and paresthesia following a prodrome of diarrhea, fever, and joint pain. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed albuminocytological dissociation. Electromyography indicated acute motor and sensory axonal neuropathy (AMSAN). Conventional serology for common pathogens was negative. Targeted Next-generation Sequencing (tNGS) of the CSF was performed and revealed a high read count of B19V DNA, suggesting a probable B19V-triggered GBS.The patient was treated with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), resulting in significant clinical improvement at discharge. At the 6-month follow-up, the patient was confirmed to have developed lower limb joint pain 3 weeks after discharge, accompanied by persistent B19V-IgM/IgG positivity, which resolved gradually after retreatment with IVIG. He ultimately achieved a G-DS score of 0 with no residual neurological sequelae.ConclusionsThis case suggests that human parvovirus B19 (B19V) may be a rare cause of Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), and confirms that cerebrospinal fluid targeted next-generation sequencing (CSF tNGS) has important diagnostic value in identifying atypical pathogens such as B19V. B19V-associated GBS tends to respond well to immunomodulatory therapy such as intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), with a generally favorable prognosis. tNGS may be considered for etiological detection in GBS cases with unknown causes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is an acute inflammatory polyradiculoneuropathy, often triggered by preceding infections. Common pathogens include Campylobacter jejuni, cytomegalovirus (CMV), and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) [1]. Human parvovirus B19 (B19V), primarily known for causing erythema infectiosum, arthropathy, and transient aplastic crisis, is a rarely reported trigger of GBS [2, 3]. Neurological manifestations of B19V infection, though uncommon, can include encephalitis, meningitis, and various peripheral neuropathies [4, 5].Diagnosing B19V as a trigger of GBS is difficult. Serology may be false-negative early or in immunocompromised patients [6]. PCR targets limited pathogens. Targeted Next-generation Sequencing (tNGS) is a hypothesis-free molecular assay that enriches and sequences predefined pathogen nucleic acids in clinical specimens. Unlike conventional PCR that covers only a limited pathogen panel, tNGS simultaneously detects a broad range of viruses, bacteria, fungi, and parasites. Its hypothesis-free design avoids reliance on a pre-specified pathogen list, enabling identification of rare or atypical agents missed by routine tests. Thus, tNGS is highly useful for etiologic diagnosis in unexplained cases like atypical GBS [7, 8]. Therefore, it may be challenging to confirm B19V as the definite etiology of GBS, and its clinical profile remains incompletely characterized.We report a case of GBS associated with primary B19V infection, confirmed by CSF tNGS, and review the clinical characteristics, treatment, and outcomes of similar cases in the literature, emphasizing the diagnostic power of unbiased sequencing technologies.

Case report

A previously healthy 24-year-old male was admitted to the neurology department with a 3-day history of rapidly progressive numbness and weakness in his lower limbs, which later involved the upper limbs. His symptoms began one week after an episode of diarrhea and fever, accompanied by arthralgia, following the consumption of raw seafood. On examination, he was afebrile and alert. Neurological examination revealed symmetric flaccid paralysis: muscle strength was grade II in the lower limbs and grade III in the upper limbs (Medical Research Council scale). Deep tendon reflexes were absent in all four limbs. There was a glove-and-stocking distribution of sensory impairment to light touch and pinprick. During the treatment course, the patient developed new-onset left peripheral facial palsy.

The patient’s initial laboratory workup revealed a normal complete blood count, and routine serological screenings for common pathogens associated with GBS, including Campylobacter jejuni, CMV, EBV, and Mycoplasma pneumoniae, were negative. A key immunological finding was positivity for anti-ganglioside antibodies, with elevated levels of both IgG and IgM anti-GM1 antibodies (38 AU/mL and 18 AU/mL, respectively) and IgG anti-GD1b antibodies (57 AU/mL) supporting an autoimmune axonal pathology. CSF analysis at admission demonstrated albuminocytological dissociation, characterized by elevated protein levels (0.75 g/L; normal range 0.15–0.45 g/L) in the absence of pleocytosis.

Electrophysiological studies confirmed acute motor and sensory axonal neuropathy (AMSAN). Consistent with these findings, contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MRI of the lumbosacral plexus revealed prominent enhancement of the nerve roots (Fig. 1), providing radiological evidence of inflammatory radiculopathy. Given the atypical presentation and negative standard infectious workup, tNGS was performed on the CSF using an AMPLICON strategy (Library: LIB001; Layout: SINGLE) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The sequencing generated 803,572 spots and 40.2 M bases of data (SRA accession: PRJNA1394716; Release date: 2025-12-29), and detected a high viral load of B19V with an estimated concentration of 2.72 × 10³ copies/mL. Subsequent serum analysis confirmed B19V viremia via qualitative PCR (positive) and demonstrated seroconversion with significantly elevated levels of both B19V-IgM (28.11 AU/mL; positive ≥ 10.00) and B19V-IgG (38.37 IU/mL; positive ≥ 3.00) antibodies. This comprehensive virological profiling supports B19V as a potential triggering agent for the AMSAN variant of GBS.The patient received IVIG 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days, plus empiric ganciclovir (5 mg/kg intravenously every 12 h for 14 days) and dexamethasone (10 mg/day intravenously for 5 days, followed by tapering over 2 weeks). Motor function improved toward the end of treatment. At admission, G-DS score was 4 (bedridden). The patient completed the 5-day IVIG course and was discharged approximately 1 week later, with approximately 80% of symptoms resolved and G-DS score improved to 2 (able to walk independently).

During the 6-month follow-up period, the patient developed isolated lower limb arthralgia 3 weeks after discharge, without motor or sensory relapse of GBS. Laboratory testing confirmed persistent positivity of B19V IgM and IgG. Given his sustained immune activation and arthralgia, IVIG was readministered at a dosage of 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 consecutive days. His joint pain was alleviated and resolved completely by week 7. In the subsequent 2 months of follow-up, the patient’s B19V-IgM antibody gradually turned negative, and no recurrence of joint pain was observed. Eventually, he achieved a GBS Disability Scale (G-DS) score of 1 (able to walk with minor symptoms), with only mild residual distal paresthesia and no notable functional limitations. At the 6-month follow-up, the patient’s neurological function further improved, allowing him to achieve complete self-care in daily life. His GBS Disability Scale (G-DS) score was 0. No recurrence of arthralgia or peripheral neuropathy was observed.

Clinical features and diagnostic value of tNGS

This patient demonstrated a characteristic “clinical-laboratory dissociation”: although presenting with prodromal arthralgia, typical manifestations of human parvovirus B19 (B19V) infection such as rash or severe arthritis were absent. Despite severe neurological symptoms (limb numbness and muscle weakness), routine serological screening for common pathogens including Campylobacter jejuni, cytomegalovirus, and EBV yielded negative results. This discrepancy between “significant neurological deficits” and “negative conventional infectious markers” represents a hallmark feature distinguishing B19V-associated GBS from more common subtypes [10].

This clinical paradox underscores the demand for more precise pathogen detection approaches. Although tNGS provides substantial diagnostic value, it is associated with several limitations, including high cost, limited availability, risk of contamination, and potential false-positive results. Nevertheless, its hypothesis-free and unbiased nature allows for the identification of B19V, which is prone to be missed by conventional detection methods. As evidenced by our patient and the case series by Guo et al. (Table 1), the application of NGS has significantly improved the detection rate of B19V in neurological diseases [7, 8]. The concurrent detection of acute B19V infection markers in both cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and serum aligns with the diagnostic principle emphasized in a systematic review [12], which stipulates that the diagnosis of B19V-associated neurological complications must be supported by direct laboratory evidence of the pathogen (e.g., B19V DNA or specific antibodies). Notably, the mere detection of B19V does not confirm a definitive causal relationship; incidental positivity or an immune-mediated pathogenesis should be rigorously considered [8]. This necessitates careful distinction between para-infectious and post-infectious processes, particularly given that B19V DNA and IgM antibodies may persist for several months in affected individuals [3, 12].

Regarding prognosis, both the literature and our patient confirm that B19V-associated GBS generally responds well to IVIG therapy [4, 6, 10, 11]. The patient showed significant neurological improvement after IVIG treatment (with only mild residual symptoms at follow-up), further supporting the favorable overall prognosis of B19V-associated GBS. However, this clinical course—marked by delayed joint symptoms, persistent viral markers, and need for repeated immunomodulatory therapy—highlights the disease’s potentially more protracted pathogenic process than previously thought: unlike the typically self-limited B19V infections in immunocompetent individuals [3, 12], B19V-triggered autoimmune neurological complications (e.g., AMSAN subtype of GBS) may see viral clearance and inflammatory sequelae resolution extend beyond the initial acute phase. The patient’s favorable response to repeated IVIG confirms immunomodulatory therapy works for both acute neurological deficits and late-onset inflammatory manifestations of B19V-associated GBS, and underscores the need for prolonged follow-up to monitor delayed viral or autoimmune recurrence.

Treatment considerations

There is currently no established consensus on specific antiviral therapy for B19 virus-associated GBS. Standard immunomodulatory therapies—IVIG or plasma exchange—remain the primary interventions, effectively mitigating the virus-triggered autoimmune response [1, 6]. In this case, based on the IVIG regimen (0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days), dexamethasone (initially 10 mg/day, tapered after the acute phase) was added to enhance control of neuroinflammation, and ganciclovir was empirically administered for antiviral coverage.

Given the limited evidence supporting the efficacy of glucocorticoids as monotherapy for GBS, their use in GBS treatment remains controversial [13]. However, the short-term dexamethasone regimen administered in this case was primarily based on its potential to suppress the excessive inflammatory response triggered by B19V. Current guidelines generally do not recommend the routine use of glucocorticoids [1]; instead, individualized short-term combination therapy with glucocorticoids may be considered only in specific pathophysiological scenarios, such as concurrent severe radiculitis or cytokine storm. Specifically, the combination of short-term glucocorticoids and IVIG can exert synergistic effects through multi-targeted interventions, including antibody neutralization, immunomodulation, and anti-inflammatory actions. It is important to note that when adopting this combination regimen, the therapeutic benefits must be carefully balanced against potential adverse effects (e.g., increased risk of infection, blood glucose fluctuations). Additionally, this regimen should be restricted to strictly selected cases, with close monitoring of patients’ clinical responses and adverse reactions.

The clinical efficacy of ganciclovir against B19 virus has not been fully established, although clinical studies have suggested its potential value. Guo et al. [8] reported 16 patients with B19V, 12 of whom were treated with ganciclovir or penciclovir; 14 patients showed improvement, indicating that ganciclovir combined with immunoglobulin may have therapeutic effects on neurological manifestations caused by B19V. The use of ganciclovir in this case reflects a theoretical risk management strategy for ongoing viral replication in an immunocompetent patient, rather than adherence to a standard protocol. While large-scale clinical evidence is lacking, theoretical rationale and case reports suggest that antiviral agents may be more reasonably considered for immunosuppressed GBS patients with evidence of active viral replication.

Study limitation

This study has several limitations to acknowledge to avoid overinterpreting findings: it’s a single - case design with a 24 - year - old immunocompetent male, so results can’t easily apply to other groups like the elderly or immunocompromised, or other GBS subtypes; the NGS panel only targeted predefined pathogens, possibly missing rare ones that cause GBS; ganciclovir was used empirically for B19V, but without large - scale proof it works for B19V - associated GBS, and IVIG was given too, so we can’t say ganciclovir alone helped; in addition, the 6-month follow-up duration remains insufficient; at least 12 months of observation is required to fully evaluate long-term recovery, late relapse, and recurrence of B19V-related symptoms such as arthralgia.

Conclusion

B19V is a potential etiological agent in this case of GBS. This case further validates the association between B19V infection and GBS in this context, while highlighting that targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) of CSF serves as a useful tool for the precise etiological diagnosis of atypical GBS—particularly enabling the identification of triggers such as B19V in this case. The clinical course of B19V-associated GBS in this patient is typically monophasic, and this patient responded favorably to standard immunomodulatory therapy, resulting in good clinical outcomes in this case. tNGS may be considered when conventional diagnostic approaches fail to identify an etiological trigger in patients with GBS. Additionally, further research is warranted to clarify the therapeutic value of combining targeted antiviral therapy with immunomodulatory therapy in specific GBS subgroups, with larger-scale studies needed to generalize findings.
