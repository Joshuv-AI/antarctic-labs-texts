Methylation-Driven downregulation of GABRB2 in oral carcinoma’s subtype: implications for early diagnosis and differentiation
Authors: Sena Sen, Can Doruk, Murat Ulusan, Vakur Olgaç, Gülsüm Ak, Semra Demokan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) remains a major global health burden, often diagnosed at advanced stages, with limited survival improvement. Epigenetic dysregulation, particularly promoter hypermethylation, plays a significant role in OSCC pathogenesis. Previous study by Demokan et al. identified Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid Receptor Beta-2 (GABRB2) as a candidate gene subject to methylation-dependent transcriptional silencing. This study aimed to evaluate the methylation and expression profiles of GABRB2 in OSCC and to explore its potential as a diagnostic biomarker.MethodsTissue and serum samples from 48 OSCC patients and 15 healthy controls were analyzed for GABRB2 promoter methylation via quantitative methylation-specific PCR and for gene expression levels via quantitative real-time PCR. Relationships between methylation, expression levels, clinicopathological parameters, and patient outcomes were statistically assessed.ResultsGABRB2 promoter hypermethylation was detected in 14.6% of tumor samples, predominantly in floor of the mouth and buccal tumors. Decreased GABRB2 expression was observed in 52.1% of tumor tissues compared to matched-normal tissues, while 22.9% of tumors exhibited increased expression. Methylation-dependent expression loss was confirmed in 71.4% of methylated tumors. Notably, decreased expression and hypermethylation of GABRB2 were correlated with poor prognosis parameters (p < 0.05). ROC analysis showed moderate discrimination for GABRB2 expression in distinguishing tumor from normal tissues (AUC = 0.635, p = 0.022), while serum-based analysis demonstrated poor diagnostic performance. Survival analysis revealed no statistically significant prognostic impact of GABRB2 expression levels (p > 0.05). However, alcohol consumption (p = 0.006) and recurrence developed (p = 0.030) as independent predictors of poor prognosis in multivariate analysis.ConclusionOur study suggests that there was association between methylation-based expression loss of GABRB2 with OSCC’ subgroups. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate GABRB2 gene methylation and expression profiling in both invasive/non-invasive samples from OSCC patients. Hypermethylation in the newly identified candidate GABRB2 gene may play a role in the development of tumors originating from the mouth floor and buccal anatomical regions of the oral cavity. However, since the loss of expression seen in the majority of our patients, we thought that only methylation may not the inhibition mechanism for GABRB2 gene decreased expression. The new identified candidate gene may be specific for the OSCC’s subgroups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06899-y.Keywords: Biomarker, Expression, Methylation, New target gene, Oral cancer

Introduction

Oral cavity cancer is one of the most common neoplastic lesions in the head and neck region, and it constitutes up to 50% of the annual head and neck cancer (HNC) cases worldwide [1–3]. Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is the 6th most common type of cancer [4] and the histological subtypes of more than 90% of oral cancers are SCC [5, 6]. The OSCC is usually diagnosed at an advanced stage, which causes high morbidity and mortality. The overall 5-years survival rate of OSCC is 56%, and there has been no progress in recent years that could significantly contribute to extending life expectancy [3]. Etiological factors including smoking and alcohol use, usage of snuff, tobacco chewing, ethnicity, age, nutrition, geographic region, viral factors (Human Papilloma Virus), chronic irritations, immunodeficiency, poor oral hygiene, and high dose radiation play a role in the development of OSCC [7]. The incidence of oral cancers varies according to anatomical regions. In Western countries, the tongue is the most common localization of OSCC with 20–40% of cases, followed by the floor of mouth (FoM), which in sum accounts for approximately 50% of all OSCC cases [5]. The buccal mucosa, gingiva, palate, and retromolar trigone are among the less frequently affected oral regions [8].

Cancer is a complex disease caused by the accumulation of genetic and epigenetic changes that occur as a result of defects in mechanisms that regulate the functions of the cell, such as survival, differentiation, migration, apoptosis, and transformation [9, 10]. Moreover, in many types of cancer, it has been shown that tumor suppressor genes lose function by methylation of CpG islands in the promoter regions [11]. Gene-environment interaction is best shown in HNC, and epigenetic changes are frequently observed in this cancer type [12]. The discovery of new genetic/epigenetic biomarkers for early diagnosis in HNC is very important for better prognosis and prolong survival of the disease.

In the previous project conducted by Dr. Demokan (project no. TÜBİTAK-SBAG-114S497), they aimed to investigate the potential epigenetic biomarker candidate genes observed methylation-dependent expression loss in matched-normal and tumor tissues of OSCC patients by using methylation/expression array methods. Their results indicated Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid Receptor Beta-2 (GABRB2) gene hypermethylation and decreased expression (unpublished data) according to the expression/methylation array performed in tumor/matched-normal tissues of OSCC patients. GABA receptors have been shown to be expressed not only in the central nervous system but also in peripheral tissues, where they may exert dual roles in tumor progression [13]. While GABAA receptors are generally associated with tumor-suppressive functions in central nervous system tumors, they have been implicated in promoting proliferation, metastasis, and immunosuppression in various peripheral solid tumors, including those of the breast, colon, lung, and pancreas [14]. These pro-tumorigenic effects are thought to be mediated by specific subunits through the activation of oncogenic pathways, including EGFR, AKT, ERK1/2, and NF-κB. Similarly, GABAB receptors have been reported to function as either tumor suppressors or promoters, depending on the cancer type [15]. In this context, the overexpression of certain GABA receptor subunits—particularly GABRB2—in OSCC has raised the possibility that GABAergic signaling may contribute to tumor progression via oncogenic mechanisms. The potential involvement of GABAergic pathways in regulating cell proliferation, invasion, and immune suppression within the OSCC microenvironment suggests that GABA receptors may serve as valuable diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets in this malignancy. In the literature, GABRB2 expression was found to significantly differ between tumor and normal tissues in colon adenocarcinoma, showing high diagnostic value [13]. In the other study, GABRB2 was found to be significantly overexpressed in papillary thyroid carcinoma tissues and associated with lymph node metastasis, and than functional assays demonstrated that GABRB2 promotes tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, suggesting its role as a novel oncogene in papillary thyroid carcinoma [16]. Xiu et al. showed that GABRB2 is highly expressed in thyroid cancer and promotes tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while miR-144-3p suppresses GABRB2 expression and inhibits cancer progression by downregulating the PI3K/AKT pathway [17]. Monteiro et al., GABRB2 expression was highest in Group 3 medulloblastoma and significantly associated with better overall survival, suggesting its potential prognostic role and relevance in subgroup-specific tumor progression [18].

To the best of our knowledge, there are no studies present in the literature investigating methylation, expression, and methylation-based expression changes of the GABRB2 gene in OSCC patients. The aim of the present study was to investigate the methylation and expression dynamics of the GABRB2 gene in OSCC by analyzing both invasive and non-invasive samples. Building on preliminary results that identified GABRB2 as a gene that is methylation-dependently silenced, this study aimed to validate its role in a larger, well-characterized OSCC patient cohort. Specifically, we aimed to determine whether GABRB2 promoter hypermethylation and/or transcriptional downregulation are associated with clinicopathological parameters. Furthermore, we evaluated the diagnostic potential of GABRB2 expression/methylation levels and investigated their prognostic significance. By integrating molecular and clinical data, this study aims to elucidate the biological significance of GABRB2 in OSCC pathogenesis and evaluate its utility as a potential biomarker for early diagnosis, risk stratification and personalized patient treatment.

Materials and methods

This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Istanbul Faculty of Medicine. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in line with the principles outlined in the World Medical Association (WMA) Declaration of Helsinki. According to these guidelines, participation in medical research must be voluntary for individuals who are capable of providing informed consent. Although consultation with family members or community leaders may be appropriate in some cases, no individual was enrolled without their voluntary agreement. The privacy and confidentiality of all patient data were rigorously protected throughout the study.

Patient selection

OSCC patients treated with primary surgical intervention for OSCC from the Department of Otorhinolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery of Istanbul University Hospital were included in the study. The control group of the study included healthy individuals who were operated on for sleep apnea with uvulopalatopharyngoplasty (UPPP) with no history of benign or malignant tumor and premalignant lesion history were included. The patient group who had not received chemotherapy or radiotherapy treatment before and whose primary treatment was surgery was included in the study. The patient group was further classified according to the anatomical origin of the primary tumor.

An a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1 software (Düsseldorf, Germany). The significance level (type I error, α) was set at 0.05 and the power of the test (1-β) was set at 0.80. Based on an expected difference of 30 units between the group means, the required minimum sample size was calculated to be 16 subjects per group. Accordingly, a total of 48 patients diagnosed with OSCC and 15 healthy individuals with no personal or family history of cancer were included in the study after giving their informed consent.

Sample collecting

Peripheral blood samples were collected in a serum separation tube (dry tube) before the operation from both patients and healthy individuals. Specimens of blood were centrifuged at 1950 rpm for 30 min and the top yellow phase (serum) was divided into 1 ml of cryotubes. Tumor tissue samples from the patient group and healthy mucosa samples from the control group were taken during the surgery and rapidly frozen until it was microdissected ≥ 75% purity on the cryostat. All the specimens were preserved at −80 °C upto DNA/RNA isolation.

DNA isolation and bisulfite modification

DNA obtained from tissues (tumor and matched normal) and serum samples of OSCC patients and healthy individuals were extracted by using the Quick-DNA Miniprep Plus Kit(Zymo Research, CA, USA). After the isolation of DNA samples, bisulfite conversion was performed. DNA (200 ng) was converted to bisulfite by using the ZymoMethylation Gold Kit (Zymo Research, CA, USA) according to the producer’s instructions. The bisulfite-converted DNA (BC-DNA) samples were stored at −20 °C until quantitative methylation-specific PCR experiments.

Quantitative methylation-specific PCR (QMSP)

Primer and probe sequences (sence, 5’ AGTATTTTACGTCGTTCGTGG ‘3; antisence, 5’ AACACTATATTCTCCCGCGA ‘3; probe, 5’ FAM-GTTTTTTTCGGGTTTCGGCGGTTA-TAMRA ‘3) specific to the methylated promoter region of GABRB2 gene were designed by us utilizing from MethPrimer (https://www.urogene.org/methprimer/) [19] and Primer-BLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/) [20] online free programs. The methylation-specific primers and probes of the ACTB (βeta Actin) gene were referred to from the study of Carvalho [21]. Promoter methylation status of GABRB2 gene was investigated by QMSP method (Mono Color Hydrolysis Probe/UPL Probe) using BC-DNA samples obtained from tumor and matched normal tissues of 48 OSCC patients, normal mucosa samples of 15 healthy individuals and from serum samples of patients and healthy cohort. The PCR reaction started with denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 50 cycles at 95 °C for 15 s, 64 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 15 s. Analysis of the results was performed by the Absolute Calculator/Fit Points program of LightCycler 480 software (all from Roche Diagnostics GmbH). Each reaction was performed in duplicates or triplicates. Lymphocytes obtained from a healthy individual were “in vitro” methylated using excess SssI Methyltransferase and S-adenosylmethionine (Zymo Research, CA, USA) to generate completely methylated DNA that was used as a positive control standard. A standard curve plot for the gene was generated by the program based on the 100% methylated standard used separately for each experiment and the Ct values. The ratio between the QMSP values of the GABRB2 of interest relative to a control gene were calculated to quantitate the relative percent of methylation (target gene/reference gene ×100) [22].

Total RNA isolation and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA isolation from tissues and serum samples were performed in accordance with the procedure using the “Quick-RNA MiniprepKit” (Zymo Research, CA, USA). “SensiFAST cDNA Synthesis Kit” (Bioline Meridian Bioscience, London, UK) was used for cDNA synthesis from total RNA samples. The resulting cDNA samples were spectrophotometrically measured with a Nanodrop 2000c (Thermofisher, USA) and then stored at −80 °C until the expression experiments were run.

Quantitative real-time PCR (QRT-PCR)

Primers of the GABRB2 gene (sence, 5’ TTTTACTGGCGTGGCGATGA ‘3; antisence, 5’ AACGAAGACAAAGCACCCCA ‘3) were designed by us and checked using the Primer-BLAST website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/) [20]. Specific primers to ACTB gene expression were also obtained from our previous study [23]. The expression levels of the GABRB2 gene were investigated by the QRT-PCR method (SYBR Green I/HRM Dye) using cDNA samples obtained from tumor and matched normal tissues of 48 OSCC patients, normal mucosa samples of 15 healthy individuals, and serum samples of patients and healthy cohort. Each reaction was performed in duplicates or triplicates. The PCR reaction began with denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles amplification step at 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, melting step at 95 °C for 10 s, 55 °C for 20 s, 95 °C for continues. Analysis of the results was performed by the Basic Relative Quantification program on LightCycler 480 software (all from Roche Diagnostics GmbH). In fact that, melting curve analysis was used to verify that the amplification of the target product. The ACTB gene was used as an internal control for normalization of the gene expression levels. GABRB2 gene and the reference gene expression status were analyzed by calculating the threshold cycle threshold (Ct) as fold changes using the 2−ΔΔCt method [24]. After evaluation of the expression levels, we selected the ratio of > = 2 as the threshold for differentially expressed GABRB2. In addition, to determine biologically relevant changes in gene expression, a threshold for a ≥ 1.5-fold change was applied, as has been standard practice in previous study [25]. The FoldChange (FC) value was calculated by comparing the averages of the Ct values for all samples of the patients and the average of the Ct values of the samples of healthy individuals.

Statistical analysis

The analysis and graphics were performed using the SPSS statistical software package version 30.0 (SPSS Inc., USA) and GraphPad Prism 10. Chi-square tests (Yates and Fisher) were used to compare clinical information with methylation and expression levels. Values with p ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Expression and methylation levels of GABRB2 between healthy group and OSCC patients were compared using the Wilcoxon-Rank test. SPSS statistical software was used to install receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curves to investigate the diagnostic value of the GABRB2 gene in patients. The value of p < 0.05 was accepted as a statistically significant difference. According to the median cut-off value of GABRB2, the patients were categorized according to decreased and increased expression groups, smoking, and treatment options. p-value and overall survival (OS) of the GABRB2 gene and clinicopathological parameters were calculated by using Kaplan-Meier analysis and a log-rank test. Cox’s proportional hazards model was used to investigate the association of clinical parameters with overall survival. Values of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinicopathological characteristics of patients with OSCC and demographic parameters of control subjects

The mean ± SD age of the patients was 59.5 ± 10.7 Years. The patient group consists of 11(23%) females and 37 (77%) males. While 64.6% of the OSCC patients were known to be smokers, 18.8% used alcohol. The mean ± SD age of healthy individuals was 50.8 ± 12.3 Years. The healthy group consists of 4 (26.7%) females and 11 (73.3%) males. Furthermore, 40% of the healthy population was smokers, and 20% consumes alcohol. All OSCC patients were diagnosed with squamous cell carcinoma; 14.6% of the patients had stage I, 14.6% stage II, 20.8% stage III, and 50.0% stage IV. Lymph node involvement was available in 50% of the population and recurrence was observed in 31.3% during the follow-up. The most common side of origin was tongue (29.2%) followed by retromolar trigone (20.8%), floor of mouth (18.8%), buccal (18.8%), and inferior alveolar arch (12.5%).

Expression levels of GABRB2

The expression profile of the GABRB2 gene (2−ΔCt) was examined in tumor tissue, matched normal tissue and serum samples from 48 OSCC patients. Heatmap analysis revealed a remarkable variability between patients and generally higher GABRB2 expression levels in tumor tissue compared to the corresponding normal samples. Notably, in several cases, serum samples also showed elevated GABRB2 expression levels similar to those in tumor tissue. These differences suggest that GABRB2 may serve as a potential biomarker in both tissue and circulating samples from OSCC patients (Fig. 1).

Each row corresponds to an individual patient (n = 48). The expression intensity is represented by a color scale, with red indicating high expression levels and green indicating low expression levels

The expression levels of the GABRB2 gene were analyzed in tumor tissues, matched-normal tissues from OSCC patients, and healthy mucosa samples. Expression values were normalized and calculated using the 2−ΔCt method. As shown in Fig. 2, GABRB2 expression varied across individual samples; however, a general trend of decreased expression was observed in tumor tissues compared to their matched-normal counterparts and healthy mucosa samples. The data revealed that healthy mucosa (H1–H15) exhibited relatively higher expression levels than both tumor and matched-normal tissues. To statistically evaluate these differences, a comparative analysis was conducted between the tumor and matched-normal tissues from 48 OSCC patients. As presented in Fig. 3, GABRB2 expression was significantly downregulated in tumor tissues compared to matched-normal tissues (p < 0.0001). These results suggest that GABRB2 may be transcriptionally suppressed in OSCC tumor tissues, potentially indicating its involvement in oral squamous cell carcinoma pathogenesis.

Correlation between methylation and expression of the GABRB2

The methylation and expression states were compared with each other (Fig. 8) and with the clinical parameters of the patients (Suppl. Table 1). While expression levels decreased in 5 of 7 patients with methylation, an increase in expression level was observed in 1 of 7 patients. Also, methylation was absent in the patient’s normal tissue, which had relatively higher GABRB2 expression compared to the tumor sample, suggesting that the gene silencing observed in the tumor may be tumor-specific and methylation-related. In addition, 21 (48.8%) of the 43 patients without methylation had decreased expression levels and 11 (25.6%) patients had increased expression levels.

The methylation-based expression levels of the GABRB2 gene were found to be significantly altered between tumor tissues of methylated patients and healthy mucosa samples, as demonstrated in Fig. 9. Methylation-based expression loss was observed in OSCC patients numbered P1, P24, P27, P38, and P41. While an increased expression level was observed in P39-patient, no change was observed in the expression level in P16-patient. In methylated-patients, a 4.6-fold decreased expression levels were observed (Fig. 9). Statistical significance was observed between the two groups by using the Wilcoxon Rank test.

Relationship between Methylation and Expression Levels with Clinical Parameters

Clinical parameters of patients with decreased and increased expression levels, patients with methylation in the tumor, and changes in expression level due to tumor were showed in Supplementary Tables 1–3 and Supplementary Fig. 1.

There was a statistically significant difference between the decreased expression levels with the age (p = 0.05) according to chi-square test. No statistical significance was found with other clinical parameters.

According to the ANOVA (F-Test), a statistically significant difference between tumor methylation with differentiation degree (low and medium) (p = 0.036) and increased expression levels with medium and good differentiation degree (p = 0.005) was observed. Also, a statistically significant difference between the decreased expression levels of retromolar trigone and tongue (p = 0.038), and the decreased expression levels of retromolar trigone and floor of the mouth (p = 0.003) was found. No statistical significance was found between TNM stages with methylation and expression changes.

Among the 7 patients with present tumor methylation, 5 patients were male, 4 patients smoked, one patient used alcohol, 6 patients had advanced stage (TNM III + IV) disease, 2 patients had medium differentiation degree, 4 patients had lymph node involvement, 6 patients had a recurrence. It was observed that all of the patients (n = 5) who had methylation-based expression loss were in the late stage (TNM III-IV). It was determined that these patients had tumors localized at the floor of the mouth and buccal region. Methylation was also observed in matched-normal tissue of one person (P38) of the patients in whom methylation was observed in tumor tissue. Dysplasia formation was observed in a part of the matched-normal tissue after histopathological examination. In only one patient who had methylation in tumor tissue, an increased expression level was observed in tumor tissue compared to matched normal tissue.

To assess the diagnostic potential of GABRB2 gene expression levels, ROC curve analyses were conducted across various sample comparisons (Fig. 10). In the comparison between OSCC tumor tissues and matched-normal tissues, GABRB2 expression demonstrated moderate diagnostic accuracy, with an area under the curve (AUC) value of 0.635 (95% CI: 0.523–0.748, p = 0.022), indicating a statistically significant ability to distinguish tumor tissue from adjacent normal tissue (Fig. 10a). Conversely, the analysis of GABRB2 expression in OSCC serum versus healthy serum samples yielded a lower AUC value of 0.439 (95% CI: 0.253–0.625, p = 0.478), suggesting poor discriminatory performance (Fig. 10b). Similarly, the comparison between OSCC tumor tissues and healthy normal mucosa resulted in an AUC of 0.568 (95% CI: 0.385–0.751, p = 0.429), indicating limited diagnostic utility in this context (Fig. 10c). These findings suggest that GABRB2 expression may serve as a potential biomarker in tissue-based analyses, but its performance in serum-based applications appears to be limited.

To evaluate the prognostic relevance of GABRB2 expression and treatment modalities in OSCC patients, Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were performed (Fig. 11). In patients stratified by GABRB2 expression levels, no statistically significant difference in overall survival was observed, although a trend toward reduced survival was noted in patients with higher expression levels (Log-rank p = 0.095; HR = 1.27, 95% CI: 0.58–2.77) (Fig. 11a). Additionally, survival outcomes were analyzed based on treatment approaches, including surgery alone, surgery plus radiotherapy, and surgery plus chemoradiotherapy. Again, no significant difference was found among the treatment groups (Log-rank p = 0.095; HR = 1.27, 95% CI: 0.58–2.77) (Fig. 11b). These findings suggest that while GABRB2 expression and treatment type may influence survival trends, neither reached statistical significance in this cohort.

(HR Hazard Ratio, CI Confidence Interval.)

To evaluate the prognostic value of clinical and molecular variables in OSCC patients, both univariate and multivariate Cox regression hazard analyses were performed, as shown in Table 1. In univariate analysis, significant associations with overall survival were observed for alcohol consumption (HR: 0.51, 95% CI: 0.34–0.69, p = 0.046) and tumor recurrence (HR: 0.47, 95% CI: 0.37–0.57, p < 0.001). However, in multivariate analysis, only alcohol consumption (HR: 0.03, 95% CI: 0.004–0.39, p = 0.006) and recurrence (HR: 0.03, 95% CI: 0.01–0.79, p = 0.03) remained statistically significant predictors of poor prognosis. Other variables, including gender, smoking status, TNM stage, anatomical involvement, lymph node involvement, histological differentiation, tumor methylation status, and GABRB2 expression levels (both increased and decreased), did not reach statistical significance in the multivariate model. These findings suggest that recurrence and alcohol use are independent prognostic factors influencing survival outcomes in OSCC patients.

Discussion

Previous investigations identified GABRB2 as a gene exhibiting methylation-dependent transcriptional silencing, based on integrated methylome and transcriptome analyses. Building upon these findings, the present study sought to validate the methylation and expression dynamics of GABRB2 in a larger, well-characterized cohort of OSCC patients by analyzing paired normal-tumor tissues and assessing methylation status in circulating DNA from serum samples.

The GABRB2 gene is effective in ion-channel transplantation and associated signal pathways on chemical synapses. Firstly, it was reported that dysfunction in GABRB2 is an important factor in both schizophrenia and bipolar disorder [26–28]. Studies suggested that single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in some regions of the GABRB2 gene which cause decreased expression levels of the gene, may play a role in neurological diseases and developmental disorders such as epilepsy [29–32].

The GABRB2 gene has been found associated effective in thyroid cancer pathophysiology and has been reported to act as an oncogene in carcinogenesis [16, 33, 34]. In the literature, a few studies were examining only the expression levels of the GABRB2 gene in diseases [13, 34–36]. In one of the studies, whole-transcriptome sequencing was performed to distinguish benign and malignant thyroid nodules, and it was found that the GABRB2 gene was expressed more in malignant nodules than benign nodules [34]. In another study, the level of expression was examined in the cell line study conducted to investigate the role of the GABRB2 gene in lymph node metastasis in papillary thyroid cancer, and it was reported that the gene was highly expressed in patients with lymph node involvement [16]. In addition, expression analysis of the tumor and matched normal tissue samples from patients with papillary thyroid cancer was performed, and according to the results of the array, which was further validated by QRT-PCR, it was stated that the GABRB2 gene was significantly over-expressed in the tumor tissues compared to the matched normal [35]. In another study, the expression levels of GABA-A receptor family genes in colon cancer patients were examined according to data obtained from TCGA dataset [35]. It has been reported that low GABRB2 gene expression levels are not associated with the prognosis of colon cancer patients. Furthermore, in another study, expression levels of GABA receptor family genes in different glioma subtypes were investigated by using database of Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery, and the Biological Networks Gene Ontology (BiNGO) software [13], and it has been reported that over-expression of GABRB2 is higher in diffuse astrocytomas and anaplastic astrocytomas.

In contrast to findings from other malignancies, our data demonstrate a predominant downregulation of GABRB2 expression in OSCC tumor tissues (52%), with upregulation detected in a smaller subset (24%). Notably, decreased expression was most frequent in tumors of the floor of the mouth and buccal mucosa. Specifically, 66.7% of buccal, 42.8% of tongue, 20% of retromolar trigone, and 50% of inferior alveolar arch tumors exhibited reduced GABRB2 expression. Decreased expression correlated with adverse clinical features, including advanced stage (76%) and lymph node metastasis (56%), alongside established risk factors such as tobacco (64%) and alcohol use (20%). This variability likely reflects the underlying molecular heterogeneity of individual tumors, as each of the 48 patients included in the study has a different molecular and genetic background. Such differences between patients are increasingly recognized as key factors in cancer behavior and response to treatment, highlighting the limitations of a one-size-fits-all approach in oncology. Instead, these findings underscore the importance of personalized medicine, where molecular profiling of individual tumors can inform risk stratification, prognosis and therapeutic decisions. In the context of GABRB2, its variable expression patterns may suggest different roles depending on tumor microenvironment, anatomical location or concomitant genomic alterations. Therefore, rather than serving as a universal biomarker, GABRB2 may have value within patient-specific molecular signatures that provide tailored therapeutic approaches. Future studies combining transcriptomic, epigenomic and clinical data at the individual level will be critical to unlock the potential of GABRB2 and other biomarkers in precision oncology for OSCC.

DNA methylation plays an important role in tumor initiation and progression. The suppression of gene expression by the methylation of tumor suppressor genes in the promoter regions is an important promising mechanism for early diagnosis and screening for the search of new epigenetic biomarkers [22]. Promoter hypermethylation of GABRB2 was observed in 71.8% of tumor samples, particularly in the floor of the mouth and buccal regions, aligning with the observed downregulation patterns. However, discordant cases, including one tumor sample (P39) exhibiting promoter methylation alongside increased gene expression, suggest the involvement of additional regulatory mechanisms beyond DNA methylation, such as chromatin remodeling or transcription factor dysregulation. Although methylation is observed at a rate of 71.8% in tumor tissues, it is thought that other mechanisms also have roles. This outlier case illustrates the complexity of epigenetic regulation and indicates that other levels of regulation are involved in addition to DNA methylation. One possible explanation could be active chromatin remodeling in this particular tumor, which overrides the repressive effects of promoter methylation. Alternatively, the upregulation of specific transcription factors or the presence of mutations in methylation-recognizing proteins (e.g. MeCP2, MBDs) could lead to transcriptional activation despite promoter hypermethylation. Notably, this patient was diagnosed at an early stage (TNM-II) and the tumor was located in the inferior alveolar arch, suggesting that anatomical site-specific or stage-dependent epigenetic dynamics may also play a role. Further functional investigations would be required to clarify the molecular basis of this atypical regulatory pattern. While decreased expression levels were observed in about half of our patients with un-methylation, increased expression levels were observed in 25% of the patients. The decreased expression levels are thought to be induced by genetic or epigenetic mechanisms other than methylation. The observation that methylation was absent in some comparable normal tissues in which relatively higher GABRB2 expression was maintained supports the tumor-specific nature of epigenetic regulation. This tissue-specific difference in methylation and expression levels underscores the importance of using matched normal/tumor pairs in epigenetic studies. This finding does not suggest that methylation leads to increased gene expression — which would contradict established epigenetic principles— but reflects the loss of methylation and maintenance of expression in normal mucosa, whereas the repression induced by hypermethylation is restricted to tumor tissue. Such distinct patterns are particularly relevant in heterogeneous cancers such as OSCC, where site-specific and patient-specific molecular mechanisms are often at play.

Interestingly, a substantial proportion of unmethylated tumors also exhibited decreased GABRB2 expression, reinforcing the notion that multiple, potentially overlapping mechanisms—such as microRNA-mediated repression, histone modifications, and somatic mutations—may contribute to GABRB2 dysregulation [37]. In another study investigating the effect of BRAFv600E mutation on epigenetic changes in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), methylation and expression profiling was performed in papillary thyroid tissue-normal tissue and tumor-normal tissue belonging to BRAFWildType and BRAFv600E patient group. As a result of integrative data analysis, it was determined that 83 genes (including GABRB2) were regulated by DNA methylation in BRAF mutant PTC patients. Candidate genes were examined by pyrosequencing and RT-QPCR, and it was found that the GABRB2 gene was over-expressed due to hypomethylation (p < 0.0001). As a result of the study, although GABRB2 was not associated with clinical features or somatic changes, it was reported that GABRB2 hypomethylation and overexpression were significantly altered in BRAFV600E tumors. This hypothesis is supported by integrative analyses in papillary thyroid carcinoma, wherein hypomethylation-driven overexpression of GABRB2 was observed in BRAFV600E-mutated tumors, independent of classical promoter hypermethylation [33]. In our study, it is thought that the increased (24.4%) and decreased (46.1%) expression levels observed in un-methylated primary tumor samples are caused by other genetic/epigenetic mechanisms other than the hypermethylation mechanism.

The distinctive downregulation of GABRB2 in OSCC, as opposed to its upregulation in thyroid and brain malignancies, suggests tumor type-specific functional roles. These findings position GABRB2 as a potential tumor suppressor candidate in oral cavity cancers, contrasting its putative oncogenic role in other malignancies. Further functional studies are warranted to elucidate the mechanistic underpinnings of this context-dependent gene behavior. When patients were classified according to their anatomical involvement regions and compared with their expression and methylation values, expression reduction was detected in all of the patients with a diagnosis of the floor of the mouth tumor. Since the number of patients was insufficient, no comment was made on statistical significance. Particularly notable was the consistent downregulation of GABRB2 in all cases of floor of the mouth tumors, accompanied by a 30% promoter methylation rate. Given the known aggressive nature and poor prognosis associated with floor of the mouth carcinomas [38], the consistent reduction in GABRB2 expression within this anatomical subgroup may reflect an early event in tumor progression. Although statistical power was limited by the small sample size, the high proportion of advanced-stage cases (88.9%) further supports this association. Similar studies with the GABRB2 gene in a larger tumor sub-group can give more detailed results about the role of the gene in this tumor type.

In this study, the diagnostic potential of GABRB2 gene expression in OSCC was preliminarily evaluated. While GABRB2 expression showed moderate diagnostic accuracy in distinguishing tumor tissue from adjacent normal tissue (AUC = 0.635), its performance remained limited, especially in serum-based comparisons and when differentiating tumor tissues from healthy mucosa samples. These findings indicate that GABRB2 alone may not serve as a robust diagnostic biomarker, particularly in non-invasive settings. The observed limitations may be attributed to tumor heterogeneity, biological variability among individuals, and the complexity of circulating biomolecules. Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory. Larger-scale, multi-center validation studies using more sensitive detection technologies and multi-marker panels are warranted to more accurately assess the diagnostic relevance of GABRB2.

Similarly, while patients with higher GABRB2 expression showed a trend toward worse overall survival, this association did not reach statistical significance. Moreover, treatment modality (surgery alone, surgery with radiotherapy, or surgery with chemoradiotherapy) did not significantly affect survival outcomes. These findings suggest that GABRB2 expression may not function as an independent prognostic biomarker in OSCC. However, the small cohort size and clinical heterogeneity likely limited statistical power, underscoring the need for future studies incorporating integrated clinical and molecular profiling. Mechanistically, this study did not include functional validation experiments (e.g., GABRB2 knockdown or overexpression) to confirm its biological role in OSCC progression. The discordance observed between promoter methylation and gene expression in certain samples (e.g., P39) further suggests the involvement of alternative regulatory mechanisms, such as chromatin remodeling, transcription factor activity, or post-transcriptional regulation (e.g., microRNAs). Future functional studies are needed to elucidate whether GABRB2 acts as a tumor suppressor by regulating cell proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, or migration in OSCC. For instance, in vitro assays evaluating the effects of GABRB2 silencing or overexpression on OSCC cell behavior could provide critical mechanistic insights.

Furthermore, GABRB2 appears to exhibit context-dependent roles across cancer types. In thyroid cancer, it has been implicated as an oncogene, whereas in OSCC, our findings suggest a potential tumor suppressor function. This dichotomy may be explained by tissue-specific signaling networks or differences in methylation dynamics—with promoter hypermethylation frequently associated with gene silencing in OSCC, while hypomethylation may drive overexpression in thyroid and other cancers. These context-specific effects warrant further investigation through comparative studies across tissue types. In this study, both univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to identify clinical and molecular factors associated with overall survival of OSCC patients. Among the variables examined, alcohol consumption and tumor recurrence were found to be independent predictors of poor prognosis. While several clinical characteristics such as TNM stage, anatomic location, lymph node involvement and histologic differentiation showed an association with survival in the univariate analysis, they did not retain statistical significance in the multivariate model. This suggests that although these factors initially appear to have an impact on the results, their effects are not independent when adjusted for other variables. Interestingly, despite their biological relevance, decreased or increased GABRB2 expression and tumor methylation status showed no significant association with survival outcomes in our cohort. Interestingly, although downregulation of GABRB2 was the predominant pattern, a subset of patients (approximately 24%) exhibited increased GABRB2 expression associated with poor prognosis. This bidirectional change emphasizes the biological complexity and inter-patient variability commonly seen in head and neck cancers. Such findings may be due to differential epigenetic regulation, differences in the tumor microenvironment, or coexisting molecular alterations. The presence of both upregulated and downregulated expression patterns in the same cohort underscores the importance of stratifying patients not only by histologic subtype but also by molecular and anatomic features. Future studies with larger cohorts and integrated omics data are warranted to decipher the context-dependent role of GABRB2 in oral cavity cancer and evaluate its utility in the context of precision medicine. These results emphasise the crucial influence of lifestyle factors, especially alcohol consumption, and disease progression characteristics, such as recurrence, on the survival curve of OSCC patients. Closer monitoring of patients with a history of alcohol use and of patients who relapse could therefore be crucial for improving clinical management and outcomes. Future studies with larger sample sizes and functional analyses are needed to further validate these observations and investigate the underlying biological mechanisms.

Despite the limitations of the present study—including a relatively small sample size, lack of functional validation experiments, and lack of longitudinal data-—our results provide preliminary insights into the potential role of GABRB2 dysregulation in the pathobiology of OSCC. The observed downregulation of GABRB2 in tumor tissues, together with the frequent hypermethylation of the promoter, suggests a possible tumor suppressive function in OSCC. However, these results should be interpreted with caution as they do not establish a causal relationship. To strengthen and extend these findings, future research should focus on several directions. First, large-scale validation studies in independent, preferably multicenter cohorts are needed to confirm the observed methylation and expression patterns and assess their reproducibility in different populations. Second, functional studies — including in vitro assays in which GABRB2 is knocked down or overexpressed, and in vivo models examining tumor growth and invasion — are essential to elucidate the biological role of GABRB2 in OSCC progression. Such studies may help to clarify whether GABRB2 modulates important cellular processes such as proliferation, apoptosis or epithelial-mesenchymal transition. Thirdly, the integration of GABRB2 with other molecular biomarkers could improve diagnostic and prognostic accuracy, as single-gene approaches often do not adequately reflect the complexity of cancer biology. Multi-omics strategies combining epigenetic, transcriptomic and proteomic data may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular landscape of OSCC and the context-specific function of GABRB2. In summary, although GABRB2 has the potential to be part of a broader biomarker panel, its clinical applicability is not yet proven. Therefore, further mechanistic and translational studies are required before GABRB2 is considered as a diagnostic or therapeutic target in the clinical setting.

Conclusion

In summary, this study is the first to investigate both methylation and expression status of the GABRB2 gene in oral malignancies within the same patient cohort. The results suggest that GABRB2 may be subject to epigenetic and transcriptional dysregulation in OSCC, with promoter hypermethylation possibly contributing to its downregulation in tumor tissue. However, the observed heterogeneity of expression and methylation patterns in the different anatomical subgroups and the presence of discordance cases suggest that additional regulatory mechanisms may be involved. Although associations with unfavorable clinicopathological features were noted, GABRB2 expression alone was not identified as an independent prognostic marker, and its moderate diagnostic potential requires further investigation.

Given the limited sample size and lack of functional validation, the present results should be interpreted as exploratory. Future studies are needed to validate these results in larger, multicenter cohorts and to integrate GABRB2 with other biomarker candidates for improved diagnostic and prognostic stratification. In addition, functional studies are needed to clarify the mechanistic role of GABRB2 in oral tumorigenesis. The potential utility of GABRB2 methylation status (e.g. >5%) and expression changes as biomarker candidates should be further investigated in prospective, well-controlled studies using multi-omics approaches.
