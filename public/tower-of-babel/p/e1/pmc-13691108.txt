East Asian herbal medicine as an adjunctive treatment for schizophrenia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yujin Choi, Pyung-Wha Kim, Chan-Young Kwon, Min-Jae Kim, Kyoung-Eun Lee, Suha Lee, Suyeon Hwang, Ji-Won Kim, Ji-Won Oh, Hye-Li Jeon, Yunna Kim, Boram Lee, Seung-Hun Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHerbal medicine is widely used as an adjunctive treatment for schizophrenia, but evidence remains limited.AimsTo evaluate the effectiveness and safety of East Asian herbal medicine combined with antipsychotics in schizophrenia spectrum disorders.MethodsTen sources including MEDLINE, EMBASE and CENTRAL were searched up to January 2025 for randomised controlled trials comparing East Asian herbal medicine combined with antipsychotics versus antipsychotics alone. Primary outcome was the overall symptom scores (PANSS/BPRS). Secondary outcomes included adverse events, negative and positive symptom scores, response rates, social function, quality of life, recurrence, and adherence. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2, and evidence certainty using GRADE.Results270 studies comprising 26,053 participants were included. East Asian herbal medicine combined with standard-dose antipsychotics improved overall symptom (SMD − 1.38, 95% CI − 1.60 to −1.17; 181 studies, 18,569 participants; low-certainty evidence) and reduced adverse events (RR 0.56, 95% CI 0.51–0.62; 92 studies, 8983 participants; low-certainty evidence) versus antipsychotics alone. East Asian herbal medicine combined with low-dose antipsychotics versus standard-dose antipsychotics showed symptom improvement (SMD − 0.92, 95% CI − 1.54– − 0.29; 25 studies, 1907 participants; low-certainty evidence) and fewer adverse events (RR 0.44, 95% CI 0.34–0.58; 6 studies, 450 participants; low-certainty evidence).ConclusionLow-certainty evidence suggests that East Asian herbal medicine as an adjunct to antipsychotics may improve symptoms and reduce adverse events in schizophrenia. However, most studies raised concerns regarding the risk of bias, and potential publication bias was suspected. Results require cautious interpretation and further high-quality studies are needed.

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a severe mental illness characterised by positive symptoms, such as delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech and grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour, as well as negative symptoms, including diminished emotional expression1–3. Schizophrenia affects approximately 23.6 million people worldwide4, with a lifetime prevalence of 0.48%5, and represents a substantial global health burden, accounting for 13.4 million years lived with disability6.

Long-term antipsychotic medication is essential for the treatment of schizophrenia7. However, significant unmet medical needs remain despite standard antipsychotic medications. Negative symptoms show limited response to conventional antipsychotic treatment8, and one systematic review reported that more than two-thirds of patients who received adequate doses and durations of antipsychotic treatment failed to achieve satisfactory therapeutic response9. Even with continuous antipsychotic treatment, relapse occurs in a substantial proportion of patients10. In addition, antipsychotic medications are associated with numerous adverse effects, including extrapyramidal symptoms, sedation, weight gain and metabolic syndrome11.

Given these limitations, there is increasing interest in complementary treatment approaches. One study conducted in China showed that over one-third of schizophrenia patients used herbal medicine in combination with conventional antipsychotic therapy12. Herbal medicine represents one of the most common traditional approaches used globally for the treatment of schizophrenia13. East Asian herbal medicine is characterised by its multi-target therapeutic mechanisms14, which may provide better therapeutic efficiency and fewer adverse effects15 when combined with conventional antipsychotic medications. For instance, a network pharmacology study suggested that Ondam-tang (Wendan-tang in Chinese), one of the most frequently used herbal formulas for schizophrenia, may act as an enhancer of antipsychotics in schizophrenia treatment through the modulation of multiple therapeutic targets, including dopaminergic, serotonergic, and inflammatory signalling pathways16.

The most comprehensive evaluation to date was a Cochrane review published in 2005, which included only seven trials based on database searches conducted between 2003 and 200417,18. Subsequent systematic reviews have been limited in scope, focusing on specific herbal formulas (e.g., Ondam-tang19,20 and Jeongwangmongseong-tang21,) refractory schizophrenia22 or chronic schizophrenia23. Given the substantial growth in clinical research on East Asian herbal medicines for schizophrenia over the past two decades, there is a need for a comprehensive, up-to-date systematic review and meta-analysis.

The primary objective of this systematic review is to evaluate the effects of East Asian herbal medicine as an adjunctive therapy based on overall symptom scores. Secondary objectives include assessing the effects based on the overall adverse event rate, positive and negative symptom scores, response rates, social function, quality of life, recurrence rates and adherence to antipsychotic treatment.

Results

Study selection

Our comprehensive search identified 4578 records from 10 sources. After removing 432 duplicates, 4146 records underwent title and abstract screening, resulting in the exclusion of 3440 records that clearly did not meet the inclusion criteria. A citation search of the included studies and relevant reviews identified an additional 20 potentially eligible records. A total of 726 full-text reports were retrieved for detailed eligibility assessment, with one report unable to be retrieved.

Of the 725 assessed reports, 425 were excluded for the following reasons: not RCTs (n = 76), participants without schizophrenia spectrum disorders (n = 9), absence or unclear diagnostic criteria (n = 79), focus on co-existing symptoms rather than core schizophrenia symptoms (n = 86), intervention and comparison criteria not met (n = 74), no relevant outcomes reported (n = 60), trial registrations or conference abstracts without results (n = 38) and duplicate publications (n = 3).

The final analysis included 270 studies reported across 300 publications. A complete list of included and excluded studies is provided in Supplementary Material 3. Study characteristics

A total of 270 RCTs comprising 26,053 participants were included. Detailed study characteristics are presented in PICOS tables (Supplementary Material 4). The majority of the studies were conducted in China (n = 266), with the remaining studies being conducted in Japan (n = 3)24–26 and Turkey (n = 1)27. Of the included studies, 239 (89%) compared East Asian herbal medicine combined with standard-dose antipsychotics with standard-dose antipsychotics alone, while 31 (11%) compared East Asian herbal medicine combined with low-dose antipsychotics with standard-dose antipsychotics alone.

Regarding participant characteristics, 189 studies included patients with general schizophrenia, 17 studies focused on treatment-resistant schizophrenia, 25 studies included patients with chronic schizophrenia and 25 studies targeted patients with predominantly negative symptoms. The most commonly studied herbal formulations included modified Ondam-tang (24 studies), modified Jeongwangmongseong-tang (10 studies), modified Yangsim-tang (6 studies) and modified Dodam-tang (6 studies). Detailed information on East Asian herbal medicine interventions is provided in Supplementary Material 5. For the primary outcome measurement, 161 studies reported PANSS total scores and 57 studies reported BPRS total scores, with 11 studies reporting both measures.

Risk of bias of the included studies

Risk of bias assessment using RoB 2 showed consistent results across outcomes for all studies except for two (Horiguchi 202326 and Chen 2009a28,) which used different analysis sets for different outcomes.

For the randomisation process, only six studies (2%) were rated as low risk, with the remaining studies raising some concerns due to insufficient reporting of allocation concealment methods. For deviations from intended interventions, 237 studies (88%) were low risk, 23 (9%) raised some concerns and eight (3%) were high risk. The two exceptions, Horiguchi 202326 and Chen 2009a28, received domain ratings of either low risk or some concerns depending on the specific outcome assessed. Twenty-nine studies used placebo controls for blinding. Studies that did not conduct intention-to-treat analyses were rated as raising some concerns or high risk when combined with high dropout rates. In terms of outcome data, 262 studies (97%) were rated as low risk, with outcome data available for nearly all participants. In terms of outcome measurements, only 33 studies (12%) were low risk, due to the use of placebo controls or assessor blinding, while the remaining studies raised some concerns due to a lack of blinding information. In terms of selective reporting, only three studies (1%) were rated as low risk, due to prospective trial registration, with the remaining studies raising some concerns due to the absence of prespecified analytical plans.

In terms of overall risk of bias, only two studies were rated as low risk (Miyaoka 201524 for all outcomes and Horiguchi 202326 for adverse events), 261 studies (97%) raised some concerns (including Horiguchi 202326 for other outcomes) and eight studies (3%) had a high risk of bias. Primary outcome

A meta-analysis of 181 studies comprising 18,569 participants showed that East Asian herbal medicine as an adjunctive therapy significantly improved overall symptom scores compared with standard-dose antipsychotics alone (SMD − 1.38, 95% CI − 1.60– − 1.17; low-certainty evidence; Supplementary Material 7, Fig. S1). One study (Li 2021a) was excluded from this analysis owing to an implausible standard deviation in the original report that produced an erroneous effect size (SMD − 23.76). Statistical heterogeneity was high (I² = 97%), with effects consistently favouring the intervention. The 95% prediction interval was −4.12 to 1.36, which includes zero, indicating that the true effect may vary considerably across settings. Visual inspection of the funnel plot and Egger’s test suggested potential publication bias (p < 0.0001); given this funnel plot asymmetry, the prediction interval should also be interpreted with caution29. A sensitivity analysis excluding five additional studies with extreme effect sizes (studentized residual > 3 in absolute value) yielded a consistent result (SMD − 1.22, 95% CI − 1.38– − 1.06; 176 studies; I² = 96%; Supplementary Material 7, Fig. S17).

Although subgroup analyses were conducted, they did not adequately explain the observed heterogeneity (Supplementary Material 7, Fig. S18–S23). Placebo-controlled studies showed smaller treatment effects (SMD − 0.92, 95% CI − 1.36– − 0.48) compared with non-placebo-controlled studies (SMD − 1.44, 95% CI − 1.67– − 1.21).

Secondary outcomes

East Asian herbal medicine significantly reduced overall adverse event incidence (RR 0.56, 95% CI 0.51–0.62; 92 studies, 8983 participants; I² = 25%; low-certainty evidence) and improved negative symptoms (SMD − 1.20, 95% CI − 1.38– − 1.03; 154 studies, 14,573 participants; I² = 95%; low-certainty evidence), positive symptoms (SMD − 0.87, 95% CI − 1.10– − 0.64; 138 studies, 12,802 participants; I² = 94%; low-certainty evidence) and response rates (RR 1.32, 95% CI 1.28–1.36; 137 studies, 13,391 participants; I² = 12%; low-certainty evidence). Social function (SMD 1.28, 95% CI 0.84–1.71; 19 studies, 1980 participants; I² = 94%; low-certainty evidence) and quality of life (SMD − 1.13, 95% CI − 1.65– − 0.60; 18 studies, 1725 participants; I² = 95%; low-certainty evidence) also improved significantly. Relapse rates were lower (RR 0.49, 95% CI 0.29–0.83; 6 studies, 583 participants; I² = 29%; low-certainty evidence), while medication adherence showed no significant differences (RR 1.48, 95% CI 0.82–2.66; 3 studies, 286 participants; I² = 72%; low-certainty evidence). Potential publication bias was detected for most outcomes.

Primary outcome

A meta-analysis of 25 studies comprising 1907 participants showed that East Asian herbal medicine with low-dose antipsychotics significantly improved overall symptom scores compared with standard-dose antipsychotics alone (SMD − 0.92, 95% CI − 1.54– − 0.29; low-certainty evidence; Fig. 3). Statistical heterogeneity was high (I² = 97%), with the effects consistently favouring the intervention across studies. The 95% prediction interval was −3.72 to 1.88, which includes zero, indicating that the true effect may vary considerably across settings. Visual inspection of the funnel plot and Egger’s test suggested potential publication bias (p = 0.0046); given this funnel plot asymmetry, the prediction interval should also be interpreted with caution29. A sensitivity analysis excluding one study with an extreme effect size (Miao 2019; studentized residual > 3 in absolute value) yielded a consistent result (SMD − 0.70, 95% CI − 1.07– − 0.32; 24 studies; I² = 92%; Supplementary Material 7, Fig. S33). Secondary outcomes

Significant reduction in adverse events (RR 0.44, 95% CI 0.34–0.58; 6 studies, 450 participants; I² = 0%; low-certainty evidence) and improvement in negative symptoms (SMD − 1.23, 95% CI − 2.30– − 0.15; 12 studies, 990 participants; I² = 98%; low-certainty evidence), positive symptoms (SMD − 0.46, 95% CI − 0.73– − 0.20; 11 studies, 949 participants; I² = 66%; low-certainty evidence) and response rates (RR 1.16, 95% CI 1.01–1.33; 12 studies, 952 participants; I² = 0%; low-certainty evidence) were observed. Publication bias assessment using Egger’s test showed potential bias for the response rates (p = 0.0349) but not for negative symptoms (p = 0.1802) or positive symptoms (p = 0.3483).

Discussion

Summary of the main results

This systematic review and meta-analysis of 270 RCTs comprising 26,053 participants provides the most comprehensive evaluation to date of East Asian herbal medicine as an adjunctive therapy for schizophrenia spectrum disorders. The evidence suggests that East Asian herbal medicine combined with standard-dose antipsychotics may improve overall symptoms and reduce adverse events compared with antipsychotics alone, with consistent benefits observed for positive and negative symptoms, response rates, social functioning and quality of life. Similarly, East Asian herbal medicine combined with low-dose antipsychotics showed symptom improvement and fewer adverse events compared with standard-dose antipsychotics alone, suggesting the potential for antipsychotic dose reduction while maintaining efficacy. However, the certainty of the evidence was rated as low across most outcomes due to methodological limitations and suspected publication bias. While these findings suggest the potential clinical benefits of East Asian herbal medicine as an adjunctive therapy, the results require cautious interpretation.

Agreements and disagreements with other reviews

Our findings are consistent with previous systematic reviews, while substantially expanding the evidence base and improving methodological rigour. The previous Cochrane review, published in 2005, included only six trials evaluating East Asian herbal medicine as an adjunctive therapy for schizophrenia17,18, compared with our comprehensive analysis of 270 trials. Despite this substantial quantitative increase in scope, the fundamental conclusion remains consistent. However, our review employed more standardised outcome measures, utilising continuous variables from PANSS and BPRS total scores and standardised response rates defined as a 50% or greater reduction from baseline. This methodological advancement to the integrated PANSS/BPRS evaluation provides more generalisable effect estimates than previous single-scale analyses. Our findings align with earlier reviews across various clinical contexts, including treatment-resistant schizophrenia22, chronic schizophrenia23 and specific formulations, such as Ondam-tang19,20 and Jeongwangmongseong-tang 21.

Notably, East Asian herbal medicine showed larger effect sizes for negative symptoms than for positive symptoms. This finding is particularly meaningful given the limited response of negative symptoms to conventional antipsychotic treatment8. The substantial proportion of included studies targeting patients with predominantly negative symptoms reflects the clinical need and application patterns of East Asian herbal medicine in schizophrenia.

Pharmacological studies suggest that the most extensively studied formulations, including Ondam-tang16,30 and Ukgan-san31,32, may exert their effects through modulation of multiple neurotransmitter systems, including dopaminergic, serotonergic, glutamatergic, and inflammatory pathways.

However, the smaller treatment effects observed in placebo-controlled studies than in non-placebo-controlled studies warrant careful interpretation. This pattern is consistent with evidence that non-blinded psychiatric trials overestimate treatment effects33, suggesting that the large pooled effect sizes reported in this review may be overestimated.

Limitations of the review

Several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, the certainty of evidence was substantially affected by methodological limitations, with the majority of included studies rated as raising ‘some concerns’, primarily due to insufficient reporting of allocation concealment, absence of prospective trial registration, and lack of assessor blinding, increasing the likelihood that the observed treatment effects are overestimated. Second, geographic concentration raises concerns about generalisability, as most studies were conducted in China. The extent to which the findings can be applied to non-Asian patient populations remains uncertain. Furthermore, the predominance of Chinese-language studies, together with the methodological limitations and suspected publication bias identified in this review, suggests that the pooled effect estimates may be inflated. Third, potential publication bias was detected across multiple outcomes, suggesting a potential overestimation of the treatment effects. Fourth, substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed across most outcomes and was not adequately explained by our prespecified or post-hoc subgroup analyses. While the direction of effects was consistent across studies, the pooled effect estimates should be interpreted with caution. The prediction intervals for the primary outcome included zero in both comparisons, suggesting that the true treatment effect may be negligible in some clinical settings. In addition, a small number of studies exhibited extreme effect sizes. The diversity of herbal formulations and study duration included in this review further limits the interpretability of the pooled analyses. Fifth, our analysis was limited by its reliance on published data, precluding individual patient data meta-analysis. Related to this, baseline symptom severity could not be examined as a subgroup variable due to insufficient reporting across the included studies.

Clinical and research implications

Our findings suggest that East Asian herbal medicine may be considered an adjunctive therapy to standard antipsychotic medication. The evidence indicates the potential for antipsychotic dose reduction when combined with East Asian herbal medicine, although this strategy should be attempted only in multidisciplinary care settings with established collaborations between psychiatrists and traditional medicine practitioners. Given the current low certainty of evidence, cautious clinical application with careful monitoring is essential. Among the various herbal formulations, Ondam-tang16,19,20 emerged as the most extensively studied intervention and may be considered a primary option for schizophrenia spectrum disorders.

Future research should prioritise high-quality RCTs with improved methodological rigour, including adequate allocation concealment, appropriate blinding, intention-to-treat analysis and prospective protocol registration. Multinational studies are essential to enhance generalisability, while long-term follow-up is needed to establish sustained efficacy and safety profiles. While pattern identification–based individualised treatment approaches warrant investigation34–36, recent evidence supporting the clinical value of standardised treatment protocols in schizophrenia spectrum disorders37 highlights the practical need for robust large-scale studies of representative standardised herbal formulations. In addition, detailed analyses of specific adverse event types are needed, particularly extrapyramidal symptoms, metabolic abnormalities and weight gain.

Conclusion

This comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis substantially expands the evidence base from previous reviews and provides low-certainty evidence suggesting the potential benefits of East Asian herbal medicine as an adjunctive therapy for schizophrenia spectrum disorders. While the findings suggest possible improvements in symptom severity and reductions in adverse events, the methodological limitations and geographic concentration of the included studies preclude definitive clinical recommendations. The substantial heterogeneity and suspected publication bias further underscore the need for methodologically rigorous, multiregional RCTs.

Methods

The review protocol was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO: CRD42025637636) and is available at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42025637636. This systematic review and meta-analysis is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement (Supplementary Material 1) 38.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Study design

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) with parallel-group designs comparing East Asian herbal medicine combined with antipsychotic medication versus antipsychotic medication alone in patients with schizophrenia spectrum disorders were included. Quasi-randomised trials, crossover trials, cluster-randomised trials and conference abstracts without sufficient methodological details or outcome data were excluded.

Participants

Studies involving participants of any age diagnosed with schizophrenia spectrum disorders according to established diagnostic criteria were included. Accepted diagnostic criteria included the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV, DSM-5), the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10, ICD-11), the Chinese Classification of Mental Disorders (CCMD-2, CCMD-3), and other internationally recognised systems. Eligible conditions encompassed schizophrenia, delusional disorder, brief psychotic disorder, schizophreniform disorder and schizoaffective disorder. Studies conducted in any clinical setting were included.

Studies involving participants with substance- or medication-induced psychotic disorders, enrolling patients specifically selected for antipsychotic-induced adverse effects (e.g., weight gain, metabolic abnormalities) and targeting comorbid symptoms beyond core positive and negative symptoms (e.g., agitation, depression, insomnia) were excluded.

Intervention types and controls

Eligible interventions comprised oral East Asian herbal medicine preparations based on East Asian traditional medicine, regardless of dosage form, administered as an adjunctive therapy to antipsychotic medication. Control interventions included antipsychotic medication alone or placebo plus antipsychotic medication. Studies were excluded if the herbal composition could not be verified, particularly for customised decoctions. Proprietary or standardised formulas were included if the official composition was established, even if not explicitly detailed in the paper. Studies employing additional therapies (e.g., acupuncture, moxibustion, cupping) were excluded. Standard supportive care (e.g., routine nursing, rehabilitation) was permitted and not considered exclusionary if applied identically to each group.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was overall symptom scores measured using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS)39 or the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS)40 total scores at treatment completion, with PANSS prioritised when both were available.

Secondary outcomes included overall adverse event incidence (defined as the number of patients experiencing any adverse event), negative and positive symptom subscale scores (PANSS preferred over the Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms [SANS]/Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms [SAPS]), response rates ( ≥ 50% reduction in PANSS or BPRS total scores)40,41, social functioning, quality of life, relapse rates at study completion and antipsychotic adherence. Studies without at least one relevant outcome measure were excluded.

Literature search

Comprehensive electronic searches were conducted to identify relevant RCTs as follows: MEDLINE (PubMed), EMBASE (Ovid), the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Citation Information by NII, the Korean Studies Information Service System, ScienceON, the Oriental Medicine Advanced Searching Integrated System, the World Health Organisation International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP) and Evidence Reports of Kampo Treatment on the Japan Society for Oriental Medicine website.

All searches were conducted on 15 January 2025 without language or publication date restrictions. Search strategies combined terms related to schizophrenia spectrum disorders, East Asian herbal medicine interventions and RCT study designs, developed in consultation with systematic review experts. Complete search strategies are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

The reference lists of all included studies and relevant systematic reviews were manually searched to identify additional eligible trials. Full-text availability was verified for trials identified through the ICTRP and conference abstracts.

Data selection

Two reviewers (Y.C. and P.W.K.) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records against the inclusion criteria. Full-text articles were then independently assessed for eligibility by the same reviewers. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (B.L.), consulted when necessary. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented and are presented in a PRISMA flow diagram.

Data extraction and risk-of-bias assessment

Data extraction was conducted between 15 February and 10 April 2025 by pairs of reviewers (Y.C., M.J.K., K.E.L., S.L., S.H., J.W.K., J.W.O., H.L.J.) using a standardised data extraction form. Extracted data included study characteristics (design, setting, country), participant details (diagnostic criteria, demographics, illness duration), intervention details (names of herbal formulations, doses, durations), antipsychotic medication details (names of antipsychotics, doses, durations) and outcome data. For continuous outcomes, means and standard deviations were extracted; for dichotomous outcomes, event numbers and sample sizes were recorded. Funding and conflicts of interest were also documented. Data were extracted as reported without contacting the study authors.

Risk of bias was independently assessed by the same reviewer pairs using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2)42. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with third reviewers (B.L., P.W.K.), consulted when necessary.

Data analysis

Random-effects meta-analyses were conducted separately for two main comparisons: (1) East Asian herbal medicine combined with standard-dose antipsychotics versus standard-dose antipsychotics alone and (2) East Asian herbal medicine combined with low-dose antipsychotics versus standard-dose antipsychotics alone. For the purpose of this review, studies were classified as comparing East Asian herbal medicine combined with low-dose antipsychotics versus standard-dose antipsychotics when the planned antipsychotic dose in the intervention arm was lower than that in the control arm.

Effect sizes were calculated as standardised mean differences (SMDs) for outcomes measured by multiple instruments and mean differences (MDs) for single instruments for continuous outcomes and risk ratios (RRs) for dichotomous outcomes, all with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Meta-analyses used the restricted maximum likelihood method with Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman confidence intervals. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic43. Prediction intervals were calculated for the primary outcome to further characterise the extent of heterogeneity44. To assess the robustness of the primary outcome to extreme outliers, sensitivity analyses were additionally conducted by excluding studies with extreme effect sizes, defined as a studentized residual greater than 3 in absolute value45. Meta-analyses were performed using RevMan Web version 8.20, and extreme outlier identification and the linear regression test for funnel plot asymmetry were conducted using the metafor46 and meta47 packages in R, respectively.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted to investigate the sources of heterogeneity. Four prespecified subgroup analyses were planned for the primary outcome based on (1) risk of bias assessment (low risk, some concerns, high risk), (2) comparison type (antipsychotics alone versus placebo plus antipsychotics), (3) participant characteristics (refractory schizophrenia, chronic schizophrenia, other subtypes) and (4) traditional medicine pattern identification. Traditional medicine pattern identification refers to the classification of patients into distinct syndrome types based on signs and symptoms, with herbal prescriptions tailored to the identified pattern35,36. Additional post hoc subgroup analyses by specific herbal formulations and treatment duration ( ≤ 4 weeks, 5–11 weeks, ≥12 weeks) were conducted.

Evidence certainty assessment

Two reviewers (Y.C. and P.W.K.) independently assessed evidence certainty using the GRADE approach for outcomes included in the summary of findings tables48. Evidence was evaluated across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. Evidence was downgraded by one level for risk of bias when more than half the studies had overall bias ratings of ‘some concerns’ or ‘high risk’; for inconsistency when confidence intervals showed minimal overlap and opposite effect directions across studies; for imprecision when effect estimates crossed pre-defined thresholds for trivial effects (RR 0.75–1.25 for dichotomous outcomes, SMD − 0.2–0.2 for continuous outcomes) or optimal information size was not met ( ≥ 300 events for dichotomous outcomes, ≥400 participants for continuous outcomes); and for publication bias based on funnel plot asymmetry and Egger’s test49 results when 10 or more studies were available.
