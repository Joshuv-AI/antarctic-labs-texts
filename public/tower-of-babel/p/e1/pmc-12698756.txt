Elimination of docetaxel-induced senescence attenuates malignant progression in RB1-deficient CRPC
Authors: Huilan Su, Liqun Huang, Wenwen Xia, Dean G Tang, Jianjun Zhou, Guosheng Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose retinoblastoma l(RB1) mutations frequently emerge as late subclonal events in advanced prostate cancer (PCa), driving inevitable recurrence and therapy resistance. Therapy-induced senescence (TIS) could promote metastasis at a late stage. However, the underlying mechanisms and therapeutic approaches for decetaxel-induced senescence (DIS) in RB1-deficient castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) remain poorly understood.MethodsWe systematically evaluated the association between RB1 expression and tumor malignancy using TCGA-PRAD data and clinical prostate cancer samples. Multiple CRPC models were established, including RM-1 C57BL/6 and PC-3 BALB/c-nu mouse models, as well as human PC-3 and 22RV1 cells to uncover the double-edged nature of DIS. Subsequently, RNA sequencing of shRB1-DIS identified tumorigenic SASP factors. Furthermore, we investigated the molecular mechanisms of the combined treatment using techniques such as immunofluorescence, flow cytometry, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP), dual luciferase reporter assay, and molecular docking.ResultsThe clinical significance and negative correlation between RB1 expression and malignancy were verified in human PCa samples. Using murine and human CRPC models, we demonstrated that DIS response was retained in both RB1-knockdown and control groups. Strikingly, DIS promoted metastasis and accelerated the transition to neuroendocrine prostate cancer (NEPC) in RB1-deficient models. shRB1-DIS was marked by elevated senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity and upregulation of p27Kip. RNA-seq analysis revealed a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) profile of shRB1-DIS, with upregulated IL-1α, CCL5, CCL20, MMP3, and IL-20. Mechanistically, we identified a novel FOXA1-IL20-IL20Rβ signaling axis which promoted macrophage polarization to M2-like phenotype. Notably, our data revealed that administration of ABT-263, eliminated shRB1 DIS-associated markers and SASPs, particularly, IL-20, both in vitro and in vivo experiments. Furthermore, molecular docking confirmed ABT-263 could directly bond to the IL-20 pocket with high affinity, and oeIL-20 advanced CRPC cells exhibited increased sensitivity to ABT-263 treatment. Therefore, the suppression of M2-like macrophages by ABT-263 was associated with reduced aggressiveness and decreased resistance to docetaxel in RB1-deficient CRPC.ConclusionDIS accelerates the malignant progression of shRB1 CRPC, mediated by tumorigenic SASP, especially IL-20 enrichment. Notably, we identifies a novel FOXA1-IL-20-IL20Rβ axis that drives M2-like macrophage polarization and contributes to tumor aggressiveness and docetaxel resistance. Importantly, senolytic agent ABT-263 not only selectively eliminated shRB1-DIS cells but also restricted expression of tumorigenic SASPs, thereby restoring sensitivity to docetaxel. Wherein, IL-20 is inhibited through its interaction with ABT-263. These results provide a novel mechanistic rationale for using senolytic therapies to mitigate SASP-driven malignancy and improve treatment response in RB1-deficient CRPC.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13402-025-01126-w.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) represents the most commonly diagnosed tumors in the male population and accounts for 29% of all incident cases in men (2024) [1]. Due to its high prevalence worldwide, approximately 700,000 of PCa patients would develop to metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), a life-limiting stage of disease [2, 3]. It is worth mentioning that PCa is believed to be strongly associated with the accumulation of somatic mutations during disease progression [4]. Of note, retinoblastoma l (RB1), an essential tumor suppressor and cell cycle regulator, is frequently mutated in advanced PCa, with alterations found in 70% of neuroendocrine prostate cancer (NEPC) and 21% of mCRPC cases [5–7]. Besides, the genomic and expression heterogeneity harvested from patients suggests that RB1 aberrations often emerge as late subclonal events, driving metastatic progression and therapeutic resistance [8–10]. Considering an incomplete understanding of the mechanisms driving lineage plasticity, especially RB1, developing novel targeted therapeutic strategies remains an active area of research specifically geared toward this population.

As a response to cancer therapies, senescence traditionally has been viewed as a beneficial outcome due to its association with stable growth arrest [11]. However, accumulating evidence supports the premise that senescence is instead a durable state from which cells can escape, contributing to the emergence of drug-resistant and more aggressive phenotypes [12–14]. Furthermore, therapy-induced senescence (TIS), an usual response to anticancer therapies, has been implicated in promoting metastasis and disease recurrence [15, 16]. Given that docetaxel is a commonly used chemotherapeutic drug in CRPC patients, it sparked our interest in understanding how docetaxel-induced senescence (DIS) contributes to tumor outgrowth [17]. In general, senescence in cancerous tissues is prevailingly mediated by the p53/p21WAF1/CIP1 and p16INK4a/pRb pathways [11, 18, 19]. It is noteworthy that the precise molecular mechanisms governing DIS in the progression of mCRPC are not well defined. Considering the non-negligible effect of RB1 deficiency in mCRPC and the involvement of RB1 in cell cycle arrest during cellular senescence, whether shRB1-DIS can be activated by alternative pathways warrants further investigation.

On the other hand, a variety of senescent cells are present in the tumor microenvironment (TME) throughout the lifespan of a tumor [20]. Wherein, senescent cells communicate with and affect their neighbors via senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), a secretome includes chemokines, growth factors, cytokines, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [15]. Strikingly, SASP can exhibit a “double-edged sword” in tumor progression by remodeling the TME [20]. This dual role is accomplished by a two-pronged approach: SASPs could impede tumor development by enhancing immune system’s self-sustained surveillance [21, 22]. SASP can also reshape the TME to facilitate immune destruction [23–25]. These opposing roles underscore the complexity and heterogeneity of senescence cells and SASPs in diverse cancers. Moreover, Carpenter’s group demonstrated that androgen deprivation therapy (ADT)-induced senescence, involving SASP factors such as IL-6 and IL-1β in PCa, is permissive for the development of castration-resistance but susceptible to senolytic therapy [26]. While extensive preclinical studies have highlighted that targeting senescence and SASP can benefit cancer patients, the exact mechanisms revealing tumorigenic SASP in RB1-deficient CRPC remain unclear.

Collectively, we propose the hypothesis that DIS in the shRB1 CRPC may contribute to tumor progression. This study elucidates the mechanism by which DIS mediates a reprogramming of the SASP in shRB1 CRPC models, both in vivo and in vitro, shifting it towards a tumor-promoting and metastatic response. Furthermore, we explore the potential of senolytic therapy to exclusively clear senescent cells and mitigate the tumorigenic SASP. Our findings suggest a therapeutic strategy to effectively prevent metastatic spread in RB1-deficient senescent tumors and sensitize prostate cancers to conventional therapies.

DIS accelerated metastasis and NEPC transition in RB1 deficient CRPC models

Analysis from TCGA prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD) cohort which included 52 normal and 479 tumor samples, revealed a significant reduction in RB1 expression in PCa patients (Supplementary Fig. S1A, B). Furthermore, RB1 expression was strongly negatively correlated with Gleason score (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. S1B) and significantly lower in samples with lymph node metastasis (Supplementary Fig. S1C), highlighting the high-risk nature of RB1 deficiency. Based on these findings, immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on PCa tumor and matched non-malignant tissues, revealing markedly reduced RB1 level in tumors (Fig. 1B).

To establish an experimental CRPC model, RM-1 cells with stable Rb1 knockdown (shRb1) or non-targeting control (shNC) were generated by lentiviral transduction of Rb1-specific shRNA, followed by puromycin selection. Western blot analysis confirmed a significant reduction in RB1 protein levels in shRb1 cells, validating the efficiency of knockdown. These cells were then injected into C57BL/6 mice, which were subsequently subjected to castration and treated with docetaxel (10 mg/kg) or vehicle (Fig. 1C) [15]. When the treatment was terminated for four administrations, most of the docetaxel-treated mice displayed overt tumor shrinkage compared with shNC samples (Fig. 1D, E). In contrast, tumors derived from the shRb1 group were approximately 1.67-fold larger in volume and 2.27-fold heavier in weight than those from the shNC group, suggesting that RB1 deficiency promoted tumor progression in CRPC (Fig. 1D, E). Of note, no noteworthy differences in tumor volume or weight were observed between the docetaxel-treated and vehicle-treated shRb1 groups, indicating that RB1 deficiency conferred resistance to docetaxel treatment (Fig. 1D, E). Strikingly, immunofluorescence (IF) assay demonstrated that docetaxel treatment enhanced the capability of shRb1 RM-1 cells to metastasize and further differentiate into the NEPC subtype, as determined by increased CD44, neuron specific enolase (NSE), and synaptophysin (SYP) fluorescences when relative to shRb1 control (Fig. 1F). RB1 regulates the cell cycle through binding to E2F transcription factors, implicating its role in the senescence response [27]. Meanwhile, results in Fig. 1G confirmed that docetaxel treatment upregulated senescence-related factors including Cdkn1a (p21Cip1), Cdkn2a (p16INK4A), Cdkn1b (p27Kip1), and cathepsin D (Ctsd). The induction of cyclin-dependent kinases (CDK) inhibitors (p21, p16, and p27) were also responsible for reduced expressions of CDKs and Cyclins when compared with shNC groups, causing DIS in CRPC (Fig. 1G). Comparatively, in shRb1 samples, enhanced Cdkn1b level not Cdkn1a or Cdkn2a was detected, accompanied by decreased CDK and Cyclin mRNA expressions upon docetaxel treatment, indicating that docetaxel-treated shRb1 tumors retained the senescence response in an Rb1-independent manner. Above results were consistent with the findings in IF and western blot assays (Fig. 1F, H). Correspondingly, docetaxel also increased the senescence-associated SA-β-gal level in shRb1 group as determined SA-β-gal staining and CellEvent™ Senescence Green Flow Cytometry Assay Kit (Fig. 1I). Notably, docetaxel promoted the metastasis of Rb1-deficient RM-1 cells to the liver, as evidenced by pronounced local tumor characteristics via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and elevated proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) expression in IHC staining (Fig. 1J).

To further validate these data in a human-relevant setting, BALB/c-nu mice and in vitro CRPC models (PC-3 and 22RV1 cells) were established (Supplementary Fig. S2A, S3A). Correspondingly, shRB1-DIS induced docetaxel resistance and greater potential for differentiation into NEPC evidenced by elevated neuro-basal characteristics (KRT14 and KRT5) and reduced luminal features (KRT8 and KRT18) than DIS group (Supplementary Fig. S2B-H, J and K) [28, 29]. The CCK-8 assays determined the IC50 of docetaxel to be 9.47 nM and 1.5 nM, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3B). The results of wound healing, transwell, and qPCR assays exhibited increased aggressive capacities of shRB1 DIS group compared to shRB1 control (Supplementary Fig. S3C, D, F, and G). Wherein the senescence-related marker p27 was presented in both treated groups (Supplementary Fig. S2I, S3E-G). In conclusion, docetaxel treatment induced senescence-related response in both shNC and shRB1 groups but selectively exacerbated CRPC progression in the shRB1 group.

Docetaxel reprogrammed the SASP in shRB1 CRPC models

To dissect the mechanism by which shRB1-DIS contributed to the aggressive tumor phenotype, a cohort of both shNC and shRB1 PC-3 cells under docetaxel treatment was analyzed using RNA-seq assay. The top 20 enriched Reactome pathways were shown in Fig. 2A. Comparatively, shRB1-DIS group not only markedly stimulated SASP and cellular senescence but also signaling pathways including interleukins and some cytokines compared with shRB1 control group. Meanwhile, interleukins and cytokines such as not limited to IL-10, IL-4, and IL-13 pathways, were also activated towards docetaxel treatment in shRB1 genetic backgrounds (shRB1-DIS vs. DIS). Given the most enrichment of IL-10 signaling among the pathways, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was also performed and showed the similar trend (Fig. 2B). Although the enrichment did not reach statistical significance, senescence-related pathways such as mitochondrial signaling and DNA damage response exhibited an enrichment trend in the shRB1-DIS group (Supplementary Fig. S7), consistent with our experimental observations of senescence induction [30, 31]. Figure 2C detailedly listed the factors involved in IL-10 signaling, where the expressions of IL-6, CCL5, CCL20, CXCL2, IL-24, INFL1, and IL-20 in shRB1-DIS group were apparently higher than that in DIS as well as shRB1 control groups. Besides, the KEGG pathway classification presented the percent of up-regulated genes wherein nervous system was indeed stimulated upon docetaxel treatment in shRB1 PC-3 cells. Subsequently, we assessed whether the enhanced migration and invasion potentials of shRB1-DIS cells were mediated by the secretome of SASPs. Notably, factors related to the IL-10 pathway (IL-6, CCL5, CCL20, CXCL2, IL-24, and IL-20), as well as FOS, IL-1α, CXCL3, EDN1, IL-2RG, MMP19, and MMP3, were up-regulated in the shRB1-DIS group compared to both the shRB1 and DIS groups (Fig. 2D). We thus speculated that shRB1 DIS cells differentially regulated the level of secreted factors facilitating metastases while sustaining senescence response. Intracellular and murine experiments validated above results (Fig. 2E). Besides, the results also demonstrated that IL-20 was the most prominent and stable factor among those up-regulated in the shRB1-DIS group, making IL-20 a compelling topic that warrants further investigation.

Elimination of shRB1 DIS impaired tumor progression in CRPC models

Motivated by the observation that shRB1 DIS could be account for malignant process in CRPC models, we screened several senolytic therapies, including ABT-263 (5 µM) [15], Naringenin (10 µM) [32], SR9009 (20 µM) [33], Azithromycin (50 µM) [34], and Quercetin (20 µM) [35]. As shown in Fig. 3A, ABT-263 and SR9009 significantly decreased docetaxel-induced up-regulation of CDKN1B in shRB1 PC-3 cells. Comparatively, only ABT-263 supplementation displayed the best inhibitory effect on other senescence-related markers (galactosidase beta 1 (GLB1), CTSD, and SERPINE). Meanwhile, the SA-β-gal positive cells were reduced under ABT-263 treatment in shRB1-DIS group compared with control (Fig. 3B). Notably, no visible changes of metastasis-related factors were exhibited in the tested group, indicating ABT-263 treatment did not affect the progression of shRB1 PC-3 cells (Supplementary Fig. S4A). To validate this in vivo, castrated shRb1 RM-1 mice were subjected to ABT-263 therapy (50 mg/kg) [15], which was started at the onset of senescence (docetaxel administration for half a month) and ended for another half a month (Fig. 3C). Figure 3D showed that the average weight of shRb1 RM-1 tumors in the control group was 0.95 g, and those in ABT-263 or/and docetaxel treatments were 0.96 g, 1.04 g, and 0.48 g respectively. The altered indicators showed that shRb1 RM-1 tumors were resistant to docetaxel but their sensitivity was restored upon ABT-263 supplementation. In addition, ABT-263 monotherapy had minimal impact on tumor growth compared to shRb1 control group, consistent with its senolytic activity being specific to shRb1 DIS cells. The IF (Fig. 3E) and western blot assays (Fig. 3F) showed that ABT-263 markedly attenuated shRb1 DIS-induced upregulation of metastasis-associated markers (CD44, SYP, NSE) and the senescence marker p27. As expected, similar trends were also observed in shRB1 PC-3 BALB/c-nu mouse experiments (Supplementary Fig. S4B-D).

For intracellular assessment, a migration assay using a conditioned co-culture system revealed that the supplementation of ABT-263 exhibited lower wound healing potential than that in the shRB1 DIS group, likely by blocking the release of relevant secretory factors which supported CRPC progression (Fig. 3G). Above results were consistent with the findings in transwell and western blot assays (Supplementary Fig. S4E-G). Consistent with its senolytic function, SA-β-gal staining and quantitative analysis revealed that ABT-263 specifically reduced the proportion of senescent cells in the shRb1-DIS group under both in vitro and in vivo conditions. (Fig. 3H, Supplementary S4H). Besides, ABT-263 treatment reinstated the cytotoxic effects of docetaxel, as demonstrated by both colony formation and CCK-8 assays (Supplementary S4I). For SASP, co-treatment of ABT-263 and docetaxel significantly restricted expressions of SASP factors, as exemplified by the cases of Ccl5, Mmp3, Ccl20, Il-20, and Il-1α with the efficacy evident throughout the course of senescence induction (Fig. 3I). Taken together, these results supported that ABT-263 exclusively abrogated shRB1-DIS as well as progression in RB1-deficient CRPC.

Senolytic ABT-263 suppressed M2 macrophage polarization driven by shRb1-DIS

These findings suggested that ABT-263 suppressed specific components of the SASP, thereby modulating the tumor microenvironment with broader implications. To further investigate this, we interrogated the phenotypic and quantitative changes of macrophages upon ABT-263 treatment, and whether they were causal for specific consequences during shRb1-DIS. Docetaxel treatment led to a marked increase in M2-type markers (CD206 and CD163) co-localized with F4/80-positive macrophages, alongside a notable reduction in the M1 marker CD86, as shown by IF staining in shRb1 C57BL/6 tumor tissues. These effects were reversed upon the addition of ABT-263 (Fig. 4A). For quantitative analysis using flow cytometry (Fig. 4B), the results confirmed that 70.1% of the macrophages were CD163-APC positive in docetaxel-treated mice under Rb1 deficiency, representing a 2.12-fold increase over control. Meanwhile, shRb1-DIS significantly increased CD206+ but decreased CD86+ cell counts. Conversely, the effect of shRb1-DIS on the M2 polarization of macrophages was impaired by ABT-263 supplementation. Subsequently, in vitro experiment confirmed the possibility (Supplementary Fig. S5D-F). Above results showed that docetaxel-induced senescence in RB1-deficient tumors promoted M2-type macrophage polarization, which was reversed by ABT-263 treatment, indicating that this immunosuppressive shift was primarily driven by the senescent state rather than RB1 deficiency.

Based on RNA-seq evidence and qPCR results shown in Figs. 2E and 3I, Supplementary S5A, we next examined the expression of IL-20 in shRB1-DIS. Fluorescence intensity, western blot and ELISA analyses confirmed elevated IL-20 level following docetaxel stimulation, which was effectively reversed by the addition of ABT-263 (Fig. 4C, D, Supplementary S5B and C). Given the prominent role of IL-20 in development of SASP, we sought to investigate whether IL-20 enrichment served as a driver of macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype. Strikingly, IF staining showed that the IL-20 receptor (IL-20Rβ) co-localized with CD163+ macrophages, and ABT-263 supplementation weaken the docetaxel-induced increase of fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4E). Using AutoDock software, molecule-protein docking simulation revealed a high binding affinity between IL-20 and ABT-263, with the lowest binding energy calculated at -6.2 kcal/mol (Fig. 4F). The docking model displayed four close interactions between ABT-263 and IL-20 within a distance of 3.1 Å. Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)-western blotting analysis of the soluble fraction showed an enhanced thermal stability of IL-20 in the ABT-263-treated group (Fig. 4G). The CCK-8 assay further demonstrated that ABT-263 significantly inhibited the viability of shRB1 PC-3 cells in the oeIL-20 group, indicating that IL-20 overexpression enhanced the sensitivity of these cells to ABT-263 (Fig. 4G). In summary, ABT-263 inhibited docetaxel-induced secretion of SASP, especially IL-20 in shRB1-DIS group.

IL-20 regulation was responsible for shRB1 DIS-mediated M2-like macrophage polarization

Given the prominent role of IL-20 in promoting macrophage polarization toward M2-like, we next reasoned the potential of selectively harnessing this target to improve therapeutic efficacy in vivo. To this end, we performed gene-specific shRNA-mediated knockdown of Rb1 and Il-20 from stromal cells with shRNA, after which cells were subject to DIS (Fig. 5A). As shown in Fig. 5B, the tumor size at endpoint exhibited a slight reduction in the shRb1-DIS + shIl-20 group compared to the shRb1-DIS group. Western blot and ELISA assays confirmed a marked decrease in IL-20 protein level in the shRb1 + shIl-20 groups relative to the shRb1 groups, validating the successful establishment of the Il-20 knockdown model (Fig. 5C). Subsequently, we assessed macrophage polarization within the tumor tissues. IF staining revealed a marked reduction in CD163+ F4/80+ and CD206+ F4/80+ M2-type macrophages in the shRb1-DIS + shIl-20 group, in sharp contrast to the shRb1-DIS group (Fig. 5D). This tendency was phenocopied straightly by quantitative flow cytometry analysis, which confirmed the decrease in M2 macrophage populations (Fig. 5E). Additionally, Il-20 knockdown led to an approximate 1.76-fold increase in CD86 + M1-type macrophages (CD86-PB450), highlighting the pivotal role of IL-20 in skewing macrophage polarization (Fig. 5E).

Through treatment of THP-1 cells with human recombinant IL-20 (RIL-20, 200 ng/mL) [36] and IL-20 neutralizing antibody (Anti-IL-20, 2 µg/mL) [36], flow cytometry assay from in vitro experiment further supported above hypothesis (Fig. 5F). In addition, Anti-IL-20 treatment inhibited RIL-20–induced polarization toward the M2c subtype, as characterized by downregulated expressions of CD206, CD163, CCL13, CCL16, and its receptor IL-20Rβ, without affecting other subtypes (Fig. 5G). We next defined IL-20-generated consequence in a virtual microenvironment, utilizing an embedded co-culture system that involved shRB1-DIS cells with RIL-20/Anti-IL-20 treated THP-1 cell-derived conditioned media. The wound healing assay exhibited a positive response in RIL-20 treatment while a negative response upon Anti-IL-20 addition in scratch closure (Fig. 5H). This evidence underscored the possibility that IL-20 might be an undesirable outcome of anticancer treatments. Collectively, these results identified IL-20 as a key mediator of docetaxel-induced M2 macrophage polarization.

FOXA1 regulated IL-20 expression in shRB1-DIS cells

To investigate the regulatory mechanisms governing IL-20 expression in shRB1-DIS cells, we examined the physical association between its target gene regulatory regions and transcription factors (TFs). Using publicly available CHIP-Seq databases (KnockTF, GTRD, ChIP-Atlas, and ENCODE), factors like forkhead box A1 (FOXA1), transcription factor AP-2 gamma (TFAP2C), and MYC were identified as potential TFs shown in Fig. 6A. The enrichment of FOXA1, TFAP2C, and MYC at IL-20 regulatory regions was assessed using ENCODE TFBS ChIP-seq database (Fig. 6B, Supplementary S6A). Specifically, for FOXA1, we interrogated two ChIP-seq datasets involving LNCaP (GSM1164142) and DU145 (GSM2219865) cells and identified a consensus transcription factor binding site (TFBS) within the IL-20 regulatory region (Fig. 6B). Further analysis using JARSPER database identified putative IL-20 binding site(s) for FOXA1, TFAP2C, and MYC, respectively. These binding sites were then functionally validated using ChIP assay and a dual-luciferase reporter system. ChIP-qPCR and ChIP-PCR results found that FOXA1 and TFAP2C, but not MYC, significantly enhanced binding to the IL-20 enhancer element, as indicated by increased IL-20 expression compared to the IgG control group, with KLK3 and ROCK serving as positive controls (Fig. 6C, Supplementary S6B). Wherein, the positive regulatory effect of FOXA1 was the most pronounced. In the dual-luciferase reporter system, a significant increase in luciferase activity was observed after a 6-hour co-treatment with IL-20 WT and FOXA1-overexpressing vectors in shRB1 DIS cells. In contrast, deletion of the binding sites (GCAAACAC) in IL-20 MUT blocked FOXA1-induced activation of IL-20 enhancer element (Fig. 6D). More importantly, analysis of the TCGA database revealed that FOXA1 exhibited the highest expression level in PRAD among TCGA tumor types (Fig. 5E). A significant positive correlation was observed between FOXA1 expression and primary tumor presence as well as lymphatic metastasis in the treated cohort, indicating the enrichment of FOXA1 in PCa (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, TCGA database analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between FOXA1 and IL-20 expression in breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA) and Uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS) tumor tissues (Fig. 6F). Besides, western blot analysis, confirmed elevated FOXA1 expression in the RB1-deficient DIS group (Fig. 6G). Collectively, results suggested that the IL-20 enhancer element was positively regulated by FOXA1 in shRB1-DIS cells.

Discussion

Given that whole-genome sequencing has identified RB1 loss as a key prognostic factor, we are particularly interested in RB1-deficient CRPC, where RB1 deletion is linked to cell cycle dysregulation, plasticity, AR independence, and may act as an early driver of NEPC progression [4, 10, 37]. Our work also proved a significantly positive correlation between RB1 expression and PCa prognosis as well as downregulation of RB1 in the tumor compared with matched non-malignant tissues (Fig. 1A, B, Supplementary S1). To address this, several therapies are currently available for patients with CRPC, including chemotherapy, a standard first-line therapy for mCRPC [38]. The results from C57BL/6 mice demonstrated that docetaxel treatment inhibited tumor growth in the shNC group but had no effect on the shRb1 group (Fig. 1C-E). More strikingly, both mouse models (RM−1 C57BL/6 and PC−3 BALB/c-nu mice) and relevant human cell settings (PC−3 and 22RV1 cells) revealed that docetaxel treatment enhanced the metastatic potential of shRb1/shRB1 samples and promoted their differentiation into the NEPC subtype (Fig. 1F, H, Supplementary S2 and S3). This paradoxical outcome promotes us to investigate the underlying mechanisms driving metastatic transition in shRB1 CRPC upon docetaxel treatment. It is well known that most cancer cells undergo growth arrest with phenotypic characteristics of cell senescence in response to chemotherapeutic drugs, which is referred as TIS [11, 39, 40]. Notably, accumulating evidence revealed an alternative mechanism of tumor immune evasion in TIS [41]. For instance, Adams’s group reported that while p53 activity declined following cell cycle exit during senescence, SASP expression continued to increase, driven by cytoplasmic chromatin fragments (CCFs) that activated a mitochondria-to-nucleus retrograde signaling cascade [31]. Similarly, Gorospe et al.., showed that the YAP-TEAD complex facilitated senescent cell survival by mitigating endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress through sustained mTOR activity, which in turn enhanced SASP production and prevented apoptosis [42]. These findings underscore that metabolic adaptations, including mitochondrial and ER reprogramming, could sustain senescent phenotypes independently of strict proliferative arrest. In light of these insights, Our results suggested that docetaxel caused TIS in both shNC and shRB1 groups but uniquely accelerated shRB1 CRPC progression, supporting the notion that docetaxel reprogrammed the secretome of RB1-deficient cells. Mechanistically, we found that the canonical p53/p21WAF1/CIP1 and p16INK4a/pRb pathways were not substantially activated in shRB1-DIS cells [11]. Interestingly, our findings suggested that docetaxel-treated shRB1 samples retained the senescence response in an Rb1/RB1-independent manner, driven instead by the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p27Kip1 (Fig. 1G, I, Supplementary S2I, S3E-G). Our findings indicated that DIS engaged p21 in shNC samples but fails to induce p21 in RB1-deficient DIS cells, suggesting a shift in CDKI dependency according to RB1 status. The stability of p21 is tightly regulated by multiple ubiquitin ligases, including SCFSkp2, CRL4Cdt2, and APC/CCdc20 [43, 44]. Notably, CRL4Cdt2 mediates PCNA-dependent degradation of p21 during the G1/S transition at a much higher rate than SCFSkp2, thereby maintaining low p21 levels throughout S-phase [43]. Given that RB1 deficiency enhances E2F-driven replication programs and PCNA loading, it is plausible that CRL4Cdt2-mediated turnover of p21 is favored in RB1-deficient cells, preventing its accumulation [45, 46]. By contrast, p27 degradation requires CDK2-dependent phosphorylation at Thr187 and subsequent recognition by SCFSkp2 [47]. In RB1-deficient DIS cells, where CDK2 activity is markedly suppressed, p27 may become more resistant to degradation. In addition, docetaxel has been reported to upregulate p27 expression, which may further contribute to its preferential stabilization [48]. Collectively, these findings suggest that RB1-deficient DIS is characterized by impaired p21 accumulation coupled with selective stabilization of p27. Future studies are warranted to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which p27 regulates shRB1 DIS. Additionally, the unchanged expressions of p21Cip1 and p16INK4A upon shRB1-DIS, highlighting the heterogeneity of TIS across different genetic backgrounds.

Determining the underlying mechanisms of metastatic transition in CRPC tumors is crucial for developing strategies to prevent the emergence of docetaxel resistance [49]. Moreover, the genetic background of tumor cells can influence SASP, which in turn determines how these cells engage in tumor-promoting processes. However, most evidence supporting the role of SASP in tumor deterioration has been generated from other cancer models, with limited research in mCRPC or RB1-dificient CRPC. This study identified key SASP factors that were exclusively upregulated in shRB1-DIS models, including IL1-α, CCL20, CCL5, MMP3, and IL−20 (Fig. 2) [15, 21, 50–52]. For chemokines, CCL20 and CCL5 have been extendedly studied. Particularly, CCL20 promotes tumor progression via immune suppression and CCL5 is elevated in TIS of aged male mice [53, 54]. Moreover, Punicalin (PUN) has been shown to inhibit the expression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), such as MMP−3 and MMP−9, thereby suppressing SASP secretion in aging mouse brains [55]. Cytokines also play a crucial role in senescence reprogramming. Wherein, the production of the IL−1α could promote the recruitment of an immunosuppressive myeloid population which is associated with enhanced tumor growth in older mice [56]. Additionally, IL−6 and IL−8 have the capacity to directly interact with AR and are a part of the pRB1-E2F dependent senescence program [14, 57, 58]. This also explains why IL−6 did not show significant changes in RB1-deficient DIS both in vivo and in vitro (Fig. 2E). Notably, the upregulation of IL-20 during ageing has been linked to several cancer cells including breast cancer, oral cancer, and prostate cancer [59, 60]. Macrophages, as key SASP-spreading cells, contribute to maintaining SASP response within the microenvironment [61]. Depending on the microenvironmental signals, macrophages can polarize into two distinct phenotypes: classically activated (M1) and alternative activated (M2), with tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) predominantly adopting the M2 phenotype [62, 63]. Considering that the mechanism behind SASP remains an object of intense investigation, we showed that IL−20 produced by shRB1-DIS CRPC could bind to IL−20Rβ on M2 macrophages (Fig. 4E). Correspondingly, Rao et al.., and Wang et al.., demonstrated a strong association between IL−20 and M2 macrophages, with IL−20Rβ expressed on the surface of macrophages [60, 64]. Further in vivo and in vitro co-culture studies demonstrated that targeting IL−20 not only attenuated the cell counts of CD163 + CD206 + M2 macrophages but also inhibited the migration of shRB1 DIS cells (Fig. 5). These findings suggest that IL−20 could serve as a potential therapeutic target for shRB1 DIS-related diseases. This work further demonstrated that FOXA1, as the most frequently enriched TF motif in PCa-specific enhancer-promoter loops [65], could directly activated the IL−20 gene transcription (Fig. 6C, D). Moreover, findings from Ludwig’s group suggested that elevated FOXA1 activity was a hallmark of NEPC subtype particularly in cases with concurrent RB1 deletion [66]. As anticipated, a high expression level of FOXA1 was observed in the shRB1-DIS group, highlighting its pivotal role in transcriptional regulation (Fig. 6G). Collectively, this study enhances the current understanding of the regulatory mechanisms governing IL−20 expression in cancer and highlights FOXA1 as a key player in this process.

Given the limited efficacy of docetaxel, there is a pressing clinical need to develop alternative therapeutic strategies for mCRPC, especially RB1-deficient CRPC. Notably, both senescent and cancer cells share a characteristic resistance to apoptosis [67, 68]. Consequently, targeting shRB1-DIS with senotherapeutics could be a promising strategy for mCRPC. Senotherapeutics aim to reduce the proportion of senescent cells relative to non-senescent cells either by selectively eliminating senescent cells (senolytics) or by modulating their characteristics (senomorphics) [11]. Our study confirmed and extended both in vitro and in vivo that the clearance of shRB1-DIS using ABT263 (navitoclax) mitigated metastasis and malignant progression, compared to other senolytics (Fig. 3, Supplementary S4). Furthermore, a growing body of literature supported the potency of the BCL-XL/BCL−2 inhibitor ABT−263 when administered following chemotherapy [69]. Besides, existing studies have shown that ABT−263 could selectively eliminate certain SASP factors in response to TIS, such as IL−1α, CCL20, CCL5, MMP3 [54, 70–74], which is further expanded in our work. The key finding of our work is that ABT−263 inhibited tumor progression by significantly reducing docetaxel-regulated IL−20 level, with the most pronounced efficacy observed in RB1-deficient models. Specifically, this work firstly suggested that ABT−263 directly bond to IL−20, and IL−20 overexpression shRB1 cells were more sensitive to ABT−263 treatment, providing insights into its potential mechanism of action and therapeutic implications (Fig. 4F, G). Collectively, our study vouches for senolytic medicine in RB1-dificient CRPC treatment, especially in combating metastatic transition in tumors resistant to docetaxel. An integrated assessment of non-clinical toxicology and clinical data further confirmed the tolerability of ABT−263 in both cancer patients and healthy volunteers, with no unforeseen safety concerns [75]. Despite encouraging preclinical efficacy, the clinical development of BCL-XL inhibitors such as ABT−263 has been significantly constrained by on-target toxicities, most notably thrombocytopenia [76]. In addition, BCL-XL is also expressed in normal epithelial and neuronal cells, raising concerns over off-tumor toxicity [77]. A further limitation lies in the lack of isoform selectivity where most BH3 mimetics fail to discriminate effectively among BCL−2 family members, increasing the risk of unintended target inhibition [78]. Moreover, resistance mechanisms or altered apoptotic priming may diminish the long-term therapeutic benefit of BCL-XL targeting agents [79]. These challenges underscore the need for combination strategies to enhance therapeutic selectivity and minimize toxicity. Specifically, proteolysis targeting chimeras (PROTACs) and specific non-genetic IAP-based protein erasers (SNIPERs) have been developed to selectively degrade BCL-XL while sparing platelets [80, 81]. Alternative dosing regimens, particularly low-dose administration in combination with chemotherapy, have also shown promise in reducing toxicity [82]. In a phase I study (NCT03181126), venetoclax combined with low-dose navitoclax and chemotherapy was demonstrated favorable efficacy without thrombocytopenia [83]. Additionally, AMPK inhibition has been shown to sensitize acute leukemia cells to multiple BH3 mimetics, potentially allowing for effective tumor suppression at lower doses [84]. Together, these advances support the feasibility of refining BCL-XL-targeted strategies through next-generation degraders and rational combination regimens to expand their therapeutic window in selected patient populations. In addition, we acknowledge that the 5 µM concentration of ABT−263 in vitro used in our study exceeds the commonly adopted upper limit of 2 µM in many senolytic assays. Phase I clinical studies have reported total plasma Cmax for ABT−263 in the low-micromolar range (3–7 µM), indicating that 5 µM still lies within the broader spectrum of reported systemic exposures [75, 85, 86]. Given the high degree of protein binding, however, clinically achievable free concentrations are likely lower. Accordingly, we considered 5 µM primarily as a mechanistic probe rather than a clinically translatable dose. Future work will focus on evaluating alternative senolytic agents and dosing strategies that are more closely aligned with clinically relevant exposures, with the aim of developing therapeutic approaches with improved translational potential for RB1-deficient CRPC.

In summary, our study elucidated that DIS could accelerate the progression of shRB1 CRPC to mCRPC, particularly facilitating NEPC transition, suggesting a remodeling of tumor-associated regulatory factors driven by RB1 deficiency. Through RNA-seq analysis and comprehensive assays, we identified a novel role for the FOXA1-IL20-IL20Rβ axis in promoting M2 macrophage polarization and advancing shRB1 CRPC progression during docetaxel treatment. What’s more, the addition of ABT-263 not only selectively eliminated shRB1-DIS cells but also restricted expression of tumorigenic SASPs, thereby restoring sensitivity to docetaxel. Wherein, IL-20 is inhibited through its interaction with ABT-263. This study explaining the exact mechanisms and effects of DIS in shRB1 CRPC may help to prevent therapy resistances and prolong the survival of patients.
