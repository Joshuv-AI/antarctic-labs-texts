Postoperative supplementation with high-titer pancreatic enzymes and zinc acetate dihydrate could reduce the risk of liver fat deposition, especially in patients with severe remnant pancreatic atrophy after pancreaticoduodenectomy: a retrospective study
Authors: Satoshi Matsukuma, Naoki Kinjo, Atomu Suzuki, Yusuke Nishio, Naohiro Yamamoto, Yohei Taura, Kazuhisa Tokuno, Hisashi Sakano, Toru Kawaoka, Norio Akiyama, Shigeru Yamamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPancreatic exocrine insufficiency due to pancreatic atrophy is considered a major factor in the occurrence of liver fat deposition after pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD). This study aimed to investigate whether liver fat deposition was less frequent in patients who received combination therapy with high-titer pancreatic enzyme and zinc supplementation, considering the severity of remnant pancreatic atrophy between reconstruction methods (pancreaticogastrostomy (PG) vs. pancreaticojejunostomy (PJ)).MethodsPatients who underwent PD and periodic postoperative computed tomography (CT) were retrospectively reviewed. Remnant pancreatic volume and atrophy, estimated by 3D reconstruction CT, were compared between the PG and PJ groups. Liver fat deposition was defined as a ≥ 0.10 decrease in the liver-to-spleen attenuation ratio from postoperative day 7 to 3, 6, or 12 months. The risk of liver fat deposition was assessed after adjusting for confounders using traditional and Bayesian logistic regression analyses.ResultsSixty-two patients (48 PJ, 14 PG) were analyzed; 13 patients received combination therapy. PG resulted in more severe atrophy than PJ (median maximum atrophy rate 0.37 [interquartile range: 0.24–0.54] vs. 0.23 [0.13–0.34], p = 0.037). Compared with the non-therapy group (n = 49), the combination therapy group (n = 13) had a higher frequency of PG reconstruction (54% vs. 14%, p = 0.006) and greater maximum pancreatic atrophy rate (0.35 [0.25–0.55] vs. 0.23 [0.13–0.34], p = 0.009), yet similar fat deposition rates (31% vs. 47%, p = 0.30). Multivariate analyses suggested that combination therapy modified the effect of pancreatic atrophy on fat deposition (p for interaction = 0.066). Predicted probabilities showed that without therapy, fat deposition increased from 79.0% (95% credible interval: 38.8–96.4%) at an atrophy rate of 0.15 to 92.7% (64.8–99.3%) at 0.45, whereas with therapy, probabilities decreased from 69.7% (20.0–96.5%) to 35.1% (6.0–75.6%). The corresponding odds ratios declined from 0.62 to 0.04 at these levels of atrophy.ConclusionsCombination therapy with high-titer pancreatic enzyme and zinc supplementation could reduce the risk of liver fat deposition after PD in patients with severe remnant pancreatic atrophy, often associated with PG.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03268-y.

Introduction

Innovations in surgical procedures and management have improved the short- and long-term outcomes of patients after pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD). Nonetheless, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and liver fat deposition after PD are emerging problems.

Pancreatic exocrine insufficiency (PEI) due to pancreatic atrophy after PD is believed to play a major role in the occurrence of these diseases [1–4]; however, the detailed pathogenic mechanism remains unknown. Furthermore, pancreaticogastrostomy (PG) has been reported to be more associated with PEI than pancreaticojejunostomy (PJ) after PD [5, 6] following more severe remnant pancreatic atrophy in PG compared with PJ [4, 7].

If PEI is indeed the major cause of MASLD, oral pancreatic enzyme supplementation as a preventive strategy against it appears to be a natural concept. Consequently, several trials have been conducted on pancreatic enzyme supplementation. The first randomized controlled trial (RCT) on high-titer pancreatic enzyme supplementation did not show a significant effect on MASLD prevention [8]. In contrast, subsequent trials demonstrated that the addition of branched-chain amino acids [9] or multiple digestive enzymes [10] to pancreatic enzymes was effective in preventing MASLD. The reasons for the failure of pancreatic enzyme supplementation to prevent MASLD may be that 1.8 g/day pancreatic enzyme supplementation alone was insufficient to prevent MASLD and/or that the severity of remnant pancreatic atrophy and subsequent PEI may be effect modifiers of pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) on MASLD.

Zinc is an essential trace element that exerts several physiological effects on the body, including the maintenance of urea circuit function, and zinc deficiency occurs in patients with MASLD. Zinc supplementation has been shown to improve insulin resistance and oxidative stress in patients with MASLD [11]. Zinc deficiency occurs at a high rate in post-PD patients, similar to patients with MASLD, and is reportedly associated with PEI [12]. Some studies have examined the short-term effects of zinc supplementation before and after PD [13, 14]; however, the effects were limited. Moreover, few studies have evaluated the long-term effects of zinc supplementation after PD, including the prevention of MASLD or fat deposition.

The present study aimed to investigate whether liver fat deposition after PD was less frequently observed in patients who received combination therapy with high-titer pancreatic enzyme and zinc supplementation, and more frequently observed in patients who underwent pancreaticogastrostomy (PG) compared with pancreaticojejunostomy (PJ), via severe remnant pancreatic atrophy.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective study included patients who underwent PD for periampullary tumors or for any other reason at Japan Community Healthcare Organization Tokuyama Central Hospital. Patients were excluded if they (i) did not undergo computed tomography (CT) within a week and/or within 3–6 months after surgery, (ii) died within 6 months after surgery, or (iii) developed MASLD or fat deposition before PD. Only patients who received both high-titer pancreatic enzyme supplementation (1800 mg/day; LipaCreon, Eisai Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and zinc acetate dihydrate supplementation (100 mg/day; Nobelzin, Nobelpharma Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) within 2 weeks after surgery were classified as the combination therapy group.

Surgical procedure

The surgical method used in almost all patients was subtotal stomach-preserving PD. Conventional PD was performed in five patients with gastroduodenal cancer. Although the choice of reconstruction method (PG or PJ) was determined intraoperatively by the attending surgeon, primarily based on the pancreatic texture and duct diameter, PG reconstruction was performed by a limited number of surgeons in our department. The prescription of high-titer pancreatic enzymes and zinc supplementation was not guided by predefined institutional criteria. In the latter quarter of the study period (the final 2 of 8 years), these agents were administered consistently to most patients. Accordingly, the use of combination therapy in this cohort reflected temporal changes in practice patterns.

Definition of MASLD and fat deposition

MASLD was diagnosed at 3, 6, and 12 months after PD using two different methods with unenhanced CT attenuation of the liver—namely, (i) the absolute mean CT attenuation of five different liver sections < 40 Hounsfield units (HU) [15] and (ii) the liver-to-spleen attenuation ratio (L/S) < 0.9 [16]. In this study, the steatosis defined using the first method was named “steatosis CT40”. In the second method, the mean CT attenuation obtained and the spleen attenuation in a single region of interest were used (named “steatosis ratio” in this study). Fat deposition was defined as the preliminary stage of MASLD. Patients whose L/S ratio decreased by 0.10 or more from postoperative day7 to 3, 6, or 12 months after surgery were classified to have fat deposition [17].

Evaluation of the remnant pancreatic volume and atrophic rate of the remnant pancreas

The remnant pancreatic volume (RPV) was calculated using the SYNAPSE VINCENT system (Fujifilm Medical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The pancreatic atrophy rate was calculated as the rate of decrease in the RPV at approximately 3 or 6 months after PD from the reference, which was the RPV at approximately 7 days after PD. The largest decrease in either was considered the maximum atrophy rate. Postoperative complications were recorded and classified according to the Clavien–Dindo classification [18]. Clinical factors and short- and long-term surgical outcomes were compared between the two groups. Changes in the RPV were also compared between the two groups.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was whether hepatic steatosis or fat deposition after PD was less frequently observed in patients who received combination therapy including sufficient pancreatic enzyme and zinc supplementation. The secondary outcome was whether hepatic steatosis or fat deposition was more frequently observed in patients who underwent PG compared with those who underwent PJ, via severe atrophic changes in the remnant pancreas.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges and were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical data are expressed as numbers and percentages and were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Potential confounders for the outcomes were prespecified based on clinical knowledge and a comprehensive literature review, ensuring the inclusion of variables with established relevance. The confounders included sex, body mass index (BMI), pancreatic reconstruction method (PG or PJ), maximum atrophy rate of the remnant pancreas, and combination therapy. There were no missing values for any of the variables. Variance inflation factor (VIF) values were calculated to assess the multicollinearity among confounders and ensure model stability and interpretability.

Given the limited number of events for the outcomes, the traditional frequentist approach was complemented with a Bayesian logistic regression analysis to provide additional robustness and quantify the posterior probabilities. The priors for the regression coefficients were specified as normal distributions with a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 10 (weakly informative prior) to stabilize the parameter estimation in the presence of small event counts. A Bayesian analysis was designed based on a causal model hypothesizing that the effect of the reconstruction method on fat deposition might vary according to the severity of the maximum atrophy rate of the remnant pancreas. The mediator model for the maximum atrophy rate was adjusted for the reconstruction method, sex, and combination therapy. The outcome model for fat deposition quantified the posterior probability of an interaction between the maximum atrophy rate and combination therapy while adjusting for reconstruction method and sex. The analysis involved four chains of 2,000 iterations each, including a burn-in phase of 1,000 iterations without thinning. Model convergence was assessed by inspection of trace plots and R-hat statistics, with values ≤ 1.01 indicating adequate convergence. Posterior parameter estimates were reported with 95% credible intervals (CrIs) to reflect the uncertainty of parameter estimates.

To further address potential confounding, we estimated a propensity score (PS) for receiving combination therapy using a logistic regression model that included sex, BMI, pancreatic reconstruction method (PG or PJ), maximum atrophy rate of the remnant pancreas and pancreatic cancer or not as covariates. The treatment effect of combination therapy on fat deposition was evaluated using logistic regression, adjusting for the estimated PS. In addition, we applied inverse probability of treatment weighting with stabilized average treatment effect (ATE) weights to estimate the effect of combination therapy on fat deposition. Covariate balance was assessed by standardized mean differences (SMDs).

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and its packages, rms version 6.7-1.7, rstan version 2.32.6, and brms version 2.22.0.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles embodied in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Japan Community Healthcare Organization Tokuyama Central Hospital (approval number: K519-20250205). Informed consent was obtained from each patient using the opt-out method. This study adhered to the STROBE statement [19].

Results

Patients’ characteristics

A total of 117 patients who underwent PD from January 2017 to March 2024 were retrospectively reviewed. Thirty-one and sixteen patients who did not undergo CT at 7 days and 3–6 months after surgery, respectively, were excluded. Three patients who underwent unplanned total pancreatectomy, three patients who died within 3 months after surgery and two patients who developed steatosis before surgery were also excluded. A flow diagram of the study is shown in Fig. 1 and detailed comparison of patients’ backgrounds between included and excluded patients is shown in Additional file1.

Ultimately, 62 patients were included in this study. Comparison of RPV changes after PD between the PG and PJ groups

No significant difference in RPV was observed at 7 days after PD (24.3 [11.8–30.4] cm3 for PG vs. 23.4 [15.1–30.8] cm3 for PJ, p = 0.840; Fig. 2A). Atrophic changes in the remnant pancreas were detected after PD in most cases; however, the two groups exhibited no significant difference in RPV (15.6 [11.9–21.1] cm3 vs. 18.1 [10.9–25.5] cm3 at 3 months after PD, p = 0.521 and 13.6 [10.3–21.3] cm3 vs. 16.3 [12.9–23.8] cm3 at 6 months after PD, p = 0.176; Fig. 2B and C). The maximum atrophy rate was higher in the PG group than in the PJ group (0.37 [0.24–0.54] vs. 0.23 [0.13–0.34], p = 0.037; Fig. 3).

Risk factors for fat deposition revealed in the traditional frequentist approach

Owing to the small number of MASLD events in our cohort, subsequent risk factor analyses were conducted with fat deposition as an outcome, which is considered a preliminary stage of MASLD. The multivariate models, including several covariates, are presented in Table 2. The VIF value for each variable, including each model, was less than 10. Female sex was significantly associated with fat deposition in these models; however, BMI (Model 1) and differences in pancreatic reconstruction (Model 2) did not affect fat deposition. When the maximum atrophy rate was added to Model 2, the odds ratio (OR) for the difference in pancreatic reconstruction remained virtually unchanged (Model 3). In Model 4, the effect of modification of combination therapy by the maximum atrophy rate was significant (p for interaction = 0.066). Although logistic regression analysis with fat deposition as the outcome should have been performed separately with and without combination therapy, the number of fat deposition events was too small, and overfitting was deemed unavoidable; therefore, univariate analysis with and without combination therapy was performed. The background difference of patients who underwent PG reconstruction was higher, the maximum atrophy rate was higher in the combination therapy group, and the incidences of MASLD and fat deposition were similar between the groups (Table 3).

Risk factors for fat deposition revealed in the bayesian approach

The multivariate models used in the Bayesian approach are listed in Table 4. All Rhat values were below 1.01, indicating chain convergence. Multiple regression analysis with the maximum atrophy rate of the remnant pancreas as the outcome revealed that sex, pancreatic reconstruction methods, and combination therapy were not associated with the maximum atrophy rate (mediator model). Female sex and maximum atrophy rate were risk factors for fat deposition, and an effect modification of combination therapy by the maximum atrophy rate was observed in this model (outcome model). The trace plots further confirmed adequate mixing and stationarity of the chains (Additional file 2). The effect of the maximum atrophy rate on fat deposition, with or without combination therapy, is shown in Fig. 4. To further illustrate these findings, predicted probabilities of fat deposition were calculated at representative levels of maximum remnant pancreatic atrophy (0.15, 0.25, 0.35, and 0.45). Without combination therapy, the probability of fat deposition gradually increased from 79.0% (95% CrI: 38.8–96.4%) at 0.15 to 92.7% (95% CrI: 64.8–99.3%) at 0.45. In contrast, with combination therapy, the probability decreased from 69.7% (95% CrI: 20.0–96.5%) at 0.15 to 35.1% (95% CrI: 6.0–75.6%) at 0.45 (Table 5). The corresponding odds ratios (therapy vs. no therapy) were 0.62 (95% CrI: 0.05–6.53), 0.25 (95% CrI: 0.03–1.74), 0.10 (95% CrI: 0.01–0.68) and 0.04 (95% CrI: 0.003–0.39) (Table 5), respectively, indicating that the protective effect of combination therapy became more pronounced as pancreatic atrophy progressed.

Estimation of the treatment effect of combination therapy on fat deposition adjusting for propensity score

In the PS-adjusted logistic regression model, combination therapy was associated with a reduced odds of fat deposition (OR, 0.189; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.025–0.983, p = 0.068). The C-static for the goodness of fit was 0.889 (95%CI, 0.806–0.971). Although the 95% CI was below 1.0, the p-value did not reach the conventional threshold for statistical significance, likely reflecting the limited sample size and imprecision of the estimate.

In the inverse probability of treatment weighting with stabilized ATE weights, the odds ratio similarly suggested a reduced risk (OR, 0.376; 95%CI, 0.036–3.864, p = 0.410), but the estimate was highly unstable, SMDs remained above 0.1 for all covariates, indicating that covariate balance was not achieved (Additional file3).

Discussion

In this study, we suggested that three things about fat deposition after PD. First, the maximum atrophy rate of the remnant pancreas after PD was a risk factor for liver fat deposition. Second, PG reconstruction could lead to remnant pancreatic atrophy; however, the results of the mediation analysis did not indicate whether PG could cause liver fat deposition with pancreatic atrophy as a mediator. Third, postoperative supplementation with high-titer pancreatic enzymes and zinc acetate dihydrate could have a context-dependent preventive effect on fat deposition according to the severity of remnant pancreatic atrophy. In other words, combination therapy could reduce the risk of fat deposition in situations where severe atrophic changes occur in the remnant pancreas.

Several studies have reported female sex [4, 20–23], high BMI [20, 21, 23–26], pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) [15, 17, 27], pre- or postoperative chemotherapy [20, 28], malnutrition [17, 29], PG reconstruction [4–7], complicated procedure (e.g., PVR, extensive lymph node dissection) and subsequent prolonged operative time or higher blood loss [22, 23, 26, 30, 31], postoperative complication (e.g., pancreatic fistula, diarrhea) [15, 17, 23, 25, 32], RPV or atrophy [4, 15, 22, 28, 33, 34], and possible subsequent PEI [1, 3] as risk factors for MASLD or liver fat deposition after PD. Perioperative chemotherapy, complex procedures, prolonged operative time, and increased blood loss could be confounding factors for PDAC and post-PD MASLD, suggesting that patients undergoing PD for PDAC are at risk for MASLD. In patients with PDAC, chronic pancreatitis is associated with pancreatic duct obstruction, and PEI occurs before pancreatic resection. Based on the above literature review, we limited the confounding factors to sex, BMI, maximum atrophy rate of the pancreas, pancreatic reconstruction method, and combination therapy in the current study. Female sex was a strong risk factor for fat deposition in our cohort; however, high BMI was not. In a recent report from Japan, MASLD was detected in 25.8% of cases during a medical health check-up and was more common in male than female [35]. In contrast, some reports have pointed out that the incidence of MASLD increases after menopause in female, and the mechanism of MASLD development is different from that in male. Most female patients undergoing PD are in their 50 s to 60 s or older, and postoperative changes combined with the unique pathophysiology of this patient population can be inferred [36]. In addition, the incidence of clinically relevant pancreatic fistula in our cohort was higher than in previous reports [3, 4, 17], which may be attributable to the lack of standardized techniques for pancreatic reconstruction and postoperative management within our institution. Although pancreatic fistula has been reported as a risk factor for liver fat deposition and MASLD, the incidence of these outcomes in our study was comparable to prior reports [17, 32], indicating that the influence of the high pancreatic fistula rate on the result of this study was likely limited.

Several studies have shown that PG reconstruction after PD induces PEI, leading to MASLD [4–7] and more severe atrophy of the remnant pancreas occurred in the PG group than in the PJ group in our cohort. However, we could not reveal whether PG could induce MASLD or fat deposition via severe atrophic changes in the remnant pancreas as a mediator, and the combination therapy with pancreatic enzyme and zinc supplementation could be a confounder of PG reconstruction and fat deposition in this situation. In addition, we revealed that combination therapy could be an effective modifier of pancreatic atrophic changes on fat deposition.

It is unknown whether PERT with 1,800 mg/day pancreatic enzyme supplementation alone is sufficient and why the first RCT conducted in Japan did not demonstrate the preventive effect of PERT on MASLD [8]. It is possible that this trial was statistically underpowered owing to the small number of participants, and it is possible that PERT exerts only a limited preventive effect in cases of severe remnant pancreatic atrophy, as suggested in this study. It is also unknown whether high-dose and several digestive enzyme supplementations used in a trial [10] (Excelase 3.0 g/day, Pancreatin 3.0 g/day, Berizym 3.0 g/day and Toughmac-E 3.0 g/day) are required for the prevention of MASLD. A position paper by the International Study Group of Pancreatic Surgery stated that PERT should be routinely initiated in post-PD patients and continued for at least 6 months [37].

Several reports have shown that zinc deficiency occurs in many patients before [14, 38] and after PD [12, 39, 40] and is associated with postoperative infectious complications [38]. A RCT demonstrated that zinc supplementation in overweight or obese patients with MASLD improved insulin resistance, as indicated by reduced serum insulin and HOMA-IR, and ameliorated oxidative stress, as evidenced by increased serum superoxide dismutase and decreased malondialdhyde [11]. Another study demonstrated that zinc deficiency after PD is associated with PEI, manifesting as steatorrhea, diarrhea, and weight loss [12]. These phenomena may partly explain our findings that combination therapy could reduce the risk of liver fat deposition, especially in patients with severe remnant pancreatic atrophy. Some studies have reported the short-term limited efficacy of oral zinc supplementation before and after PD [13, 14], and Takishita et al. concluded that zinc supplementation at 50 mg/day may be insufficient [14]. The zinc acetate dihydrate (Nobelzin) used in our study targets hypozincemia, could elevate the serum zinc concentration, and maintain it more efficiently than other digestive enzymes (e.g., Toughmac-E). If combination therapy with high-concentration zinc and high-titer pancreatic enzyme supplementation can inhibit fat deposition after PD, it may eliminate the need for high doses of pancreatic and digestive enzymes. The recently developed zinc histidine hydrate is noninferior to zinc acetate dihydrate in maintaining blood zinc levels and is expected to improve adherence owing to fewer digestive symptoms and once-daily dosing [41]. Thus, it may be a promising drug for preventing MASLD and fat deposition in patients with PEI after PD.

This study has several important limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, and the incidence of MASLD was low, which inevitably limited the statistical power and resulted in wide confidence and credible interval. Although we applied Bayesian methods to partially address this issue, the precision of the estimates remains limited, and thus the findings should be interpreted with caution. Second, nearly half of the initially reviewed patients were excluded because of insufficient follow-up CT data, raising the possibility of selection bias. We therefore compared baseline characteristics between included and excluded patients and acknowledge that systemic differences may still exist. Third, the retrospective and non-randomized design of this single-center study introduces confounding by indication, particularly because the choice of reconstruction method depended on the surgeon’s discretion. While we attempted to mitigate this limitation using multivariate adjustments and propensity score methods, the possibility of residual confounding remains, and thus causal inferences cannot by drawn. Moreover, the result of this study, derived from a single institution in Japan, may have limited generalizability to other centers, regions, countries, or ethnic populations. Fourth, the follow-up period was limited to 12 months. Although this timeframe allowed us to capture early-onset liver fat deposition, longer-term follow-up will be necessary to fully assess the impact of postoperative supplementation and the progression of MASLD. Fifth, adherence to supplementation therapy was not systematically assessed in this retrospective cohort, and poor compliance may have influenced the observed effects. Future studies should incorporate rigorous methods to monitor and ensure patients adherence to supplementation therapy in order to more accurately evaluate its effectiveness. Finally, fat deposition was defined as a decrease in the L/S ratio of 0.10 or more, which serve as a surrogate marker for the development of MASLD and not a histological confirmation. Because histological confirmation was not available in the present study, caution is required when interpreting our results. Future investigations should incorporate pathological confirmation of liver fat deposition and clarify whether the observed changes represent a preliminary stage of MASLD.

In conclusion, combination therapy with high-titer pancreatic enzyme and zinc supplementation could reduce the risk of liver fat deposition after PD in patients with severe remnant pancreatic atrophy, often caused by PG.
