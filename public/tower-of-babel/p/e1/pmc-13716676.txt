Aldosterone synthase inhibition for treatment of people with chronic kidney disease
Authors: Katherine R Tuttle, Jasna B Trbojevic-Stankovic, Radica Z Alicic, Maria Luiza Caramori, David Cherney, Ricardo Correa Rotter, William G Herrington, Parminder K Judge, Adeera Levin, Peter Rossing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAldosterone contributes to the progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD) not only through its effects on blood pressure (BP) but also by promoting inflammation and fibrosis in the kidney and cardiovascular system. Despite contemporary treatment with renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, residual risk persists for adverse kidney and cardiovascular outcomes. Aldosterone synthase inhibition (ASI) has emerged as a potent strategy to directly suppress aldosterone production. Several selective inhibitors, including baxdrostat, lorundrostat, and vicadrostat, have been shown to lower aldosterone levels without affecting cortisol synthesis in a clinically meaningful manner. Clinical studies in hypertension consistently show a decrease in systolic BP with these agents. In a CKD population, vicadrostat dose-dependently reduced the urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio with additional BP lowering, and possibly greater albuminuria reduction when combined with empagliflozin. Similar findings have been observed with lorundrostat and baxdrostat in patients with CKD and uncontrolled hypertension. Median increases in serum potassium of ∼0.3 mmol/l that have been observed with ASI were generally manageable in clinical trial populations. Emerging evidence from clinical trials and meta-analyses suggests that concomitant SGLT2 inhibitor therapy may mitigate the risk of clinically significant hyperkalaemia, also supporting the use of combination therapy. Overall, ASI provide a potentially beneficial approach to reduce residual risks of CKD. Several phase 3 trials testing vicadrostat or baxdrostat across different populations with CKD, hypertension, or cardiovascular disease will determine whether favourable effects on albuminuria translate into improved kidney and cardiovascular outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

In addition to kidney failure, chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, with hypertension acting as both a driver and a consequence of disease progression. Despite the use of therapies including renin–angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitors, sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, and a non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA) for CKD in general, and a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) agonist in type 2 diabetes with CKD, residual risk persists for adverse kidney and cardiovascular outcomes [1–3].

Persistently elevated aldosterone may contribute to these residual risks. Beyond raising blood pressure (BP) through sodium and fluid retention, aldosterone contributes to tissue injury in the heart, kidney, and vasculature through mechanisms that extend beyond hemodynamics, promoting pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic signals through activation of MRs and non-receptor-mediated actions [4, 5]. Furthermore, the phenomenon of ‘aldosterone breakthrough’ may occur in which aldosterone levels increase despite ongoing RAS inhibition and MR blockade, reflecting incomplete suppression of the pathway and compensatory increase in circulating aldosterone [6, 7].

In CKD, conventional MRAs have primarily demonstrated efficacy in reducing albuminuria, with hyperkalaemia as a major side effect limiting clinical use. Selective non-steroidal agents, such as finerenone and balcinerenone, also reduce albuminuria, and finerenone in particular has demonstrated reduced risks of major kidney and cardiovascular outcomes in broad CKD populations, although hyperkalaemia remains an important side effect [2, 8, 9]. Since these strategies may not fully suppress aldosterone actions, and aldosterone levels may increase despite treatment, interest has grown in approaches that reduce aldosterone synthesis [10, 11]. Clinical studies suggest that this approach may yield additional reductions in BP and albuminuria, particularly when used alongside established therapies [12, 13]. This review summarizes the rationale for targeting aldosterone activity, outlines emerging clinical evidence with aldosterone synthase inhibitors (ASIs), and discusses their potential role within the current therapeutic framework for CKD.

Aldosterone-mediated cardiovascular and kidney actions

Aldosterone is synthesized in the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex from cholesterol through a series of enzymatic steps, culminating in the action of aldosterone synthase (CYP11B2) [14]. Its production is tightly regulated, predominantly by the RAS, extracellular potassium concentration, and, to a lesser extent, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) [15–17]. Physiologically, aldosterone release is stimulated by angiotensin II following kidney hypoperfusion, volume depletion, or sympathetic activation. In parallel, hyperkalaemia directly depolarizes the cells of the zona glomerulosa, providing a potent RAS-independent stimulus for aldosterone secretion [16, 18]. Conversely, atrial natriuretic peptide and volume expansion suppress aldosterone release, as do emerging regulators, such as Klotho and epigenetic mechanisms through CYP11B2 transcription (Fig. 1) [19–21].

Chronic aldosterone excess promotes inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrosis in the kidney, myocardium, and vasculature. These effects contribute to endothelial dysfunction, vascular remodelling, myocardial fibrosis, and progressive CKD, independently of blood pressure.

At the cellular level, aldosterone manifests its classical effects through binding to cytosolic MRs, a ligand-activated nuclear transcription factor expressed most abundantly in the aldosterone-sensitive distal nephron, but also widely distributed in parenchymal cells of the heart and the kidney, and immune cells [18, 22, 23]. Upon ligand binding, the MRs translocate to the nucleus and modulate transcription of early response genes, such as serum- and glucocorticoid-regulated kinase 1 (SGK1), which enhances the expression and stability of epithelial sodium channels (ENaC), and late-response genes encoding ENaC subunits and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase [14, 24, 25] (Fig. 1). Slower genomic mechanisms promote sodium retention, potassium excretion, and extracellular volume expansion. In addition, aldosterone also mediates rapid non-genomic actions via cytosolic or membrane-associated MRs, and MR-independent pathways, including activation of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), protein kinase C, and NADPH oxidase signalling [14, 15, 26]. The integration of these pathways enables context-dependent responses whereby sodium conservation and potassium excretion may be differentially regulated depending on angiotensin II and potassium signals [14].

Beyond its essential role in electrolyte and volume homeostasis, chronic aldosterone excess exerts pleiotropic effects mediated through genomic MR-dependent, non-genomic MR-dependent, and MR-independent mechanisms that converge on inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrosis (Fig. 1). In the vasculature, aldosterone promotes endothelial dysfunction through increasing reactive oxygen species generation via NADPH oxidase activation and reducing nitric oxide bioavailability, thus impairing vasodilatation [18, 27–29]. It upregulates adhesion molecules, such as intercellular adhesion molecule (ICAM)-1 and vascular cell adhesion molecule (VCAM)-1, and pro-inflammatory cytokines, facilitating leukocyte recruitment and vascular inflammation [30, 31]. Concurrently, these processes contribute to arterial stiffness, atherogenesis, and vascular remodelling [32]. Crosstalk between aldosterone and endothelin-1 further enhances vasoconstriction and tissue injury, with experimental models demonstrating reciprocal regulation between endothelin signalling and MR activation [18, 33]. In the myocardium, aldosterone induces profibrotic mediators, including transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) and connective tissue growth factor, promoting myocardial fibrosis and stiffening [34, 35]. These structural alterations lead to diastolic dysfunction, heart failure, and increased susceptibility to arrhythmias [32]. Experimental and clinical studies have demonstrated that MRAs reduce myocardial fibrosis and improve outcomes in heart failure independently of haemodynamic effects, underscoring the pathogenic role of aldosterone excess [36–38].

Effects of aldosterone on the kidney also extend beyond sodium retention and potassium homeostasis. Kidney injury provoked by aldosterone affects both glomerular and tubulointerstitial compartments. Inappropriate or sustained MR activation triggers mesangial cell proliferation, extracellular matrix accumulation, podocyte injury, and glomerulosclerosis. Podocyte cytoskeletal disruption and slit diaphragm protein loss contribute to albuminuria and progressive nephron loss [30, 39]. In the tubulointerstitium, aldosterone promotes oxidative stress and inflammation by upregulating the production of inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)-6 and monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP)-1, and profibrotic pathways mediated by TGF-β and MAPK signalling, ultimately leading to interstitial fibrosis and CKD progression [30, 32, 40–42]. These deleterious effects are further amplified by high dietary sodium intake and hypertension, emphasizing interactions between environmental and hormonal factors. Notably, in remote indigenous tribal populations of South America, who consume a very low sodium diet, BP is typically normal into old age [43]. These observations suggest that even if aldosterone may increase in response to low sodium intake, a dietary effect modification could protect against hypertension and possibly target organ damage.

Overall, aldosterone is a key regulator of cardiovascular and kidney physiology, with contributions that extend beyond BP control. Maladaptive activation of aldosterone signalling contributes to inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrosis, positioning this hormone as a pivotal pathogenic mediator and therapeutic target in the management of cardiovascular and kidney diseases.

Rationale for ASI and clinical evidence in resistant or uncontrolled hypertension

Aldosterone is a fundamental regulator of BP through effects on sodium and fluid retention by the kidney. Furthermore, adverse effects on vascular and cardiac inflammation and remodelling contribute to the resistant hypertensive state. Conventional treatments for resistant hypertension have focused on blocking the RAS and MRs (Fig. 2). However, this approach has limitations, including the phenomenon of ‘aldosterone breakthrough,’ in which aldosterone levels increase with these treatments [6, 7]. For this reason, approaches to resistant hypertension have evolved to strategies that reduce aldosterone production through direct inhibition of aldosterone synthase [44]. Although this concept is not new, its clinical evolution has been limited by challenges in drug selectivity [45–47]. Aldosterone and cortisol are produced through closely related enzymatic pathways in the adrenal cortex, with aldosterone synthase (CYP11B2) responsible for the final steps of aldosterone production and 11β-hydroxylase (CYP11B1) mediating cortisol synthesis. Aldosterone synthase shares ∼90% homology with cortisol synthase CYP11B1 responsible for the production of cortisol from 17-deoxycortisol, and earlier compounds were associated with off-target inhibition of cortisol production, leading to hypocortisolism. Advances in medicinal chemistry have improved the selectivity of ASIs, allowing the development of agents with a high degree of specificity for aldosterone synthase over cortisol synthase (250–300-fold selectivity) and renewed clinical interest in the ASI class [48–52].

Aldosterone excess is a central driver of resistant hypertension, promoting sodium retention, volume expansion, vascular dysfunction, and remodelling. Conventional therapies incompletely suppress aldosterone due to compensatory mechanisms and aldosterone breakthrough. ASIs target CYP11B2 in the adrenal zona glomerulosa, directly reducing aldosterone production. This upstream approach leads to natriuresis, reduced vascular resistance, and attenuation of inflammation and fibrosis. Emerging clinical data demonstrate significant BP reduction, although long-term outcomes, safety, and optimal positioning within therapeutic algorithms remain to be established.

Early clinical evidence came from a multicentre, placebo-controlled trial of baxdrostat in patients with treatment-resistant hypertension, defined as BP ≥130/80 mmHg despite treatment with at least three antihypertensive agents, including a diuretic. The study population had largely preserved kidney function, with a mean estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of ∼85 ml/min/1.73m². Baxdrostat produced rapid, dose-dependent reductions in systolic BP over 12 weeks, with the greatest effect observed at the 2 mg dose, and a placebo-adjusted reduction of ∼11 mm Hg. Treatment was generally well tolerated, with no cases of adrenal insufficiency and only mild, reversible increases in potassium levels [53]. Similar findings were observed with lorundrostat in a subsequent randomized trial in patients with uncontrolled hypertension. As in the baxdrostat study, this population did not include patients with advanced CKD, with a mean eGFR of ∼80 ml/min/1.73m². Lorundrostat resulted in clinically meaningful reductions in systolic BP, with placebo-adjusted effects of ∼8–10 mm Hg. These reductions were consistent across levels of plasma renin activity, suggesting efficacy across different pathophysiological subtypes of hypertension. Treatment was generally well tolerated, with no evidence of cortisol insufficiency and, in fact, a subtle increase in serum cortisol levels. Only occasional, reversible increases in potassium levels were observed [52].

More recently, these findings have been supported by a longer-term study of lorundrostat, which confirmed sustained BP-lowering effects over 12 weeks in patients with uncontrolled or treatment-resistant hypertension. Unlike earlier trials, this study included a period of background therapy optimization before randomization, strengthening the overall design. Lorundrostat led to consistent reductions in 24-h ambulatory systolic BP, with substantial decreases of around 6–8 mm Hg compared with placebo. These reductions appeared early and were maintained throughout the study [54]. No excess safety concerns were observed in these studies, particularly concerning adrenal insufficiency or hyperkalaemia. Nevertheless, although the primary target of achieving BP control was consistently met, the largely preserved kidney function of these populations limits applicability to patients with CKD.

Rationale and clinical evidence for ASI in resistant or uncontrolled hypertension associated with CKD

MRAs, particularly the nonsteroidal agent finerenone, have demonstrated significant improvements in kidney and cardiovascular outcomes that have been attributed to anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects [55]. However, residual risks for CKD remain despite non-steroidal MR antagonism, possibly related to MR-independent signalling and ‘aldosterone breakthrough’ with compensatory increases in circulating aldosterone [18, 48–52, 56]. In this context, ASIs have emerged as a mechanistically distinct and promising therapeutic option for the CKD population that targets the aldosterone pathway at its source, directly reducing circulating and tissue aldosterone levels and attenuating its genomic and non-genomic effects, including oxidative stress, inflammation, and fibrosis [48–52, 56]. Furthermore, since they do not directly affect the MR, physiological receptor activation by glucocorticoids may be preserved, which could potentially blunt increases in serum potassium [44, 56]. Additionally, ASIs also lower BP and albuminuria, further supporting a potential nephroprotective role [45, 56].

In parallel, recent clinical studies indicate that, when added to standard therapy, ASI may provide an additional option for managing resistant hypertension in the CKD population. This is illustrated by the phase 2b Explore-CKD crossover trial, which evaluated lorundrostat in patients with uncontrolled hypertension and CKD receiving background SGLT2 inhibitors and RAS blockade. In addition to a reduction in systolic BP of –7.5 mmHg compared with placebo, lorundrostat was also associated with a reduction in albuminuria of ∼26% and a modest decline in eGFR, consistent with a haemodynamic effect by week 4. Notably, these improvements were observed in patients receiving optimized background therapy, supporting the clinical relevance of the effect [57].

These findings were further supported by randomized data with baxdrostat in patients with uncontrolled hypertension and CKD. In a phase 2, multicentre, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, baxdrostat reduced systolic BP by –8.1 mmHg compared with placebo at 26 weeks, with consistent effects across high and low dose groups [58]. The study population had an average baseline eGFR of 44 ml/min/1.73m2, the median urinary albumin to creatinine ratio (UACR) 714 mg/g and were receiving background RAS blockade with or without SGLT2i. Baxdrostat treatment (pooled dose groups) produced a placebo-corrected reduction in UACR of 55% and an early, transient eGFR dip. Hyperkalaemia was the most frequently reported adverse event and occurred more often with baxdrostat than with placebo, especially in the higher dose group at 51% (titrated to 4 mg daily), in contrast to the lower dose group at 32% (titrated to 1 mg daily) and 5% with placebo. However, most adverse events were considered mild to moderate and non-serious by the investigators [58]. These data extend the findings observed with lorundrostat, showing that ASI provides sustained BP reduction in CKD with an expected safety profile.

Building on these results, ongoing clinical trials are further defining the role of ASI in combination with SGLT2 inhibition in CKD. A phase 2b study (NCT07222917) is evaluating baxdrostat in combination with dapagliflozin compared with baxdrostat alone, focusing on changes in albuminuria and thereby assessing the incremental effect of SGLT2 inhibition [59]. In parallel, large phase 3 outcome trials (NCT06268873, NCT06742723) are investigating the addition of baxdrostat to background dapagliflozin therapy, with event-driven designs assessing kidney disease progression and cardiovascular outcomes, including sustained decline in eGFR, kidney failure, heart failure events, and cardiovascular death (Table 1) [60, 61].

ASI as a therapeutic target for CKD

While baxdrostat and lorundrostat are highly effective in lowering BP in resistant or uncontrolled hypertension, vicadrostat is a highly selective ASI that was specifically studied for CKD treatment (Table 1). Vicadrostat shows ∼250-fold selectivity for CYP11B2 over CYP11B1, allowing targeted suppression of aldosterone while preserving cortisol production. Although inhibition of aldosterone synthesis could theoretically affect cortisol levels through shared precursors, clinical data do not show meaningful changes in serum cortisol [62]. In a recent trial evaluating the effect of vicadrostat in the CKD population, vicadrostat was associated with dose-dependent reductions in plasma aldosterone, reaching up to a 60%–65% decrease by week 14 [68]. In contrast, no changes in morning serum cortisol were observed, supporting preserved glucocorticoid synthesis. Together, these data suggest that direct inhibition of aldosterone synthesis may provide a complementary strategy to reduce residual risk in CKD (Table 2).

In a recent prespecified analysis from the phase 2 vicadrostat trial in CKD, detailed steroid profiling further clarifies these effects. Treatment was associated with increases in upstream steroid intermediates, including corticosterone, 11-deoxycorticosterone, and 11-deoxycortisol [49]. At the highest dose, 11-deoxycorticosterone increased by more than 200%, and 11-deoxycortisol by ∼110%–120%, with similar findings regardless of background empagliflozin use. This pattern supports selective inhibition of aldosterone synthesis, with accumulation of upstream precursors while cortisol production is preserved. Notably, aldosterone suppression persisted for up to 4 weeks after treatment discontinuation, whereas precursor levels returned to baseline within ∼1 week, thus supporting a targeted and sustained effect on aldosterone synthesis [68]. The increase in active metabolites that can bind the MR, such as corticosterone and 11-deoxycorticosterone, raise the possibility of mineralocorticoid activation via alternative ligands. However, based on studies of receptor–ligand interactions, these precursors may act as antagonists rather than agonists for the human MR, which in theory could provide protection from aldosterone mediated actions [69].

The role of highly selective ASI vicadrostat in mitigating albuminuria was evaluated in a phase 2 trial in a CKD population with or without type 2 diabetes or hypertension [68]. A total of 714 adults with CKD were included, ∼70% with diabetes, all receiving stable background therapy with an ACE inhibitor or ARB for at least 4 weeks. Mean eGFR rate was 50–55 ml/min/1.73 m², and median UACR ranged from 350 to 460 mg/g across treatment groups. The study used a sequential randomization design. Participants were first assigned to empagliflozin 10 mg or a matching placebo for 8 weeks and were then randomized to vicadrostat doses of 3, 10, or 20 mg, or placebo. This allowed evaluation of ASI both with and without background SGLT2 inhibition. Combination therapy continued for 14 weeks. The primary outcome was the change in UACR from baseline at the second randomization to week 14. Secondary outcomes included responder rates (≥15% and ≥30% UACR reduction), as well as changes in eGFR, BP, serum potassium, plasma aldosterone, and overall safety.

Vicadrostat led to dose-dependent reductions in albuminuria of 22%, 39%, and 37% at the 3, 10, and 20 mg doses, respectively, compared to placebo [68]. Albuminuria lowering effects were enhanced in the presence of empagliflozin, with reductions exceeding 60% at the 10 mg dose. This supports the idea of the benefits of combined use of SGLT2 inhibition and ASI [70]. Responder analyses were consistent with these findings, with up to ∼70% of patients achieving reductions of at least 30% in UACR with combination therapy. This degree of reduction is clinically relevant, as data from large clinical trials have shown that a ≥30% decrease in UACR is associated with a corresponding reduction in risk of clinical CKD outcomes, including sustained declines in eGFR and kidney failure [70]. The rationale for combination therapy is further supported by the CONFIDENCE trial, which found additive reductions in albuminuria when finerenone and empagliflozin were given together to a population with type 2 diabetes and CKD [71].

In addition to effects on albuminuria, ASI with vicadrostat was associated with reductions in BP [68]. Although the study population was selected for CKD rather than hypertension, ∼90% of participants had coexisting hypertension. BP was therefore assessed as an important secondary outcome, including analyses according to background SGLT2 inhibition. Vicadrostat alone produced notable dose-dependent reductions in BP, consistent with findings from earlier studies of ASI in resistant or uncontrolled hypertension. However, even greater reductions were observed when vicadrostat was combined with empagliflozin, suggesting an additive effect.

Together, these findings point to a dual benefit of ASI in CKD, with simultaneous reductions in albuminuria and BP, both of which are key drivers of cardiovascular and kidney risk. Consistent with its mechanism of action, vicadrostat was associated with a modest early decline in eGFR, with mean reductions of ∼3–5 ml/min/1.73 m² across dose groups. This was observed both with and without empagliflozin, with slightly greater reductions in the combination groups, in line with the known hemodynamic effects of both therapies. The pattern of an initial decline followed by stabilization is consistent with a reduction in intraglomerular pressure, as observed with other therapies targeting glomerular hyperfiltration, such as RAS inhibitors and SGLT2 inhibition. Nevertheless, the ongoing large-scale trial: EASi-KIDNEY (The Studies of Heart and Kidney Protection With BI 690517 in Combination With Empagliflozin; NCT06531824), evaluating ASI across a broad spectrum of patients with CKD at risk of progression, is assessing the efficacy and safety of vicadrostat in combination with empagliflozin for preserving kidney function and reducing cardiovascular risks [63]. This event-driven trial includes a large and diverse CKD population, with and without diabetes or hypertension, with a planned sample size of ∼11 000 participants receiving background therapy with empagliflozin, along with ACE inhibitors or ARBs, where indicated and tolerated, in line with the contemporary standard of care. The trial is designed with a primary outcome, encompassing both cardiovascular events—such as heart failure and cardiovascular death—and kidney disease, including sustained >40% eGFR decline or kidney failure. As such, EASi-KIDNEY represents a key step in determining whether this strategy can further reduce residual risks of CKD [62].

Currently available safety data suggest that vicadrostat is generally well tolerated, with similar rates of adverse and serious adverse events compared with placebo. As expected, hyperkalaemia occurred more frequently than with placebo, reflecting the mechanism of ASI action. The increase was dose-dependent, with rates reaching ∼20%–25% at higher doses, regardless of background empagliflozin therapy. Most events were mild, with ∼86% consisting of small, asymptomatic increases in serum potassium (typically around 5.5–5.6 mmol/l) not requiring specific intervention. Potassium at baseline was required to be 4.8 mmol/l or lower, so moderate (serum potassium 6.0–6.4 mmol/l) or severe elevations (serum potassium >6.5 mmol/l) were uncommon and occurred in ∼1% of participants in both active treatment and placebo groups [68]. Discontinuation due to hyperkalaemia was infrequent, occurring in ∼4% of patients receiving vicadrostat and not observed in the placebo group. Although this was a closely monitored clinical trial population, these findings suggest that hyperkalaemia is a predictable and generally manageable safety signal in the CKD population. Other adverse events, including hypotension and acute kidney injury, were infrequent. Potential effects on cortisol metabolism were specifically evaluated, given the shared pathways involved in aldosterone and cortisol synthesis. Inhibiting aldosterone synthase may lead to the accumulation of upstream intermediates, which could theoretically increase or decrease cortisol production. Thus, both cortisol excess and deficiency were considered as potential safety concerns. In practice, however, no signal for cortisol excess was observed, and no cases of Cushing’s syndrome were reported. There was also no evidence of clinically meaningful cortisol deficiency. Cases of investigator reported adrenal insufficiency were rare (1%–3%), occurred in both treatment and placebo groups, and did not show a consistent relationship with treatment. Biochemical findings were consistent with these observations, with most participants maintaining normal morning cortisol levels and only a small proportion developing low cortisol values during follow-up (3%–4% in the vicadrostat group vs. 2%–3% in the placebo group), all of which were asymptomatic and not associated with clinical adrenal insufficiency [68].

Potassium dynamics with ASI and the role of SGLT2 inhibition

In patients with CKD receiving vicadrostat alone at the 10 and 20 mg doses, the median increases in serum potassium at 14 weeks were 0.3 mmol/l compared to placebo. However, with concurrent empagliflozin use, the corresponding median increases in serum potassium were 0.2 mmol/l [68]. Importantly, modelling analyses found that increases in serum potassium were projected to be no larger in those with higher baseline potassium levels [62]. These findings suggest that empagliflozin may help buffer potassium-related risk when used alongside aldosterone-targeting therapies [62, 71].

Besides the well-known efficacy of potassium binders, SGLT2 inhibitors also lower the incidence of clinically significant hyperkalaemia [72–74]. In a post hoc analysis of pooled empagliflozin trials, including studies in populations relevant to CKD, empagliflozin was associated with lower rates of investigator-reported hyperkalaemia compared with placebo. At the same time, reductions in oedema were observed, consistent with decreased volume overload [75]. These observations are supported by broader evidence showing that SGLT2 inhibition may reduce the risk of hyperkalaemia. In a meta-analysis of major cardiovascular outcome trials and CKD studies, SGLT2 inhibitors were associated with an ∼16% lower risk of hyperkalaemia compared with placebo (hazard ratio ∼0.84; 95% CI 0.76–0.93) [76]. This effect was consistent across different populations, including both CKD and cardiovascular cohorts [76]. Considering the greater reduction in albuminuria by combined SGLT2 inhibition and ASI, and potentially improved tolerability owing to mitigating the risk of hyperkalaemia, this combination therapy offers prospective benefits of improving both efficacy and safety (Fig. 3). Thus, SGLT2 inhibitors may support the safer use of potassium-raising agents, including ACE inhibitors, ARBs, finerenone, balcinrenone, and the ASIs.

Additive benefits of combined aldosterone synthesis inhibition and SLGT2 inhibition

Dual inhibition of aldosterone synthesis and SGLT2-mediated solute reabsorption offers potent additive cardio-renal benefits. ASIs and SGLT2 inhibitors target complementary pathophysiological pathways implicated in cardiovascular and kidney disease. ASIs attenuate mineralocorticoid receptor-mediated sodium retention, inflammation, oxidative stress, fibrosis, and adverse heart and kidney remodelling. SGLT2 inhibitors promote natriuresis and glucose-induced osmotic diuresis, reduce intraglomerular pressure, improve metabolic efficiency, and exert anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects. The combination of these agents may provide additive or synergistic protection across the heart, kidneys and vasculature. The major effects include reducing myocardial hypertrophy, fibrosis, ventricular remodelling, and heart failure progression, mitigating glomerular hyperfiltration, albuminuria, inflammation, and fibrotic kidney remodelling, as well as reducing endothelial dysfunction, vascular stiffness, oxidative stress, and inflammation. Importantly, the natriuretic and diuretic properties of SGLT2i help mitigate the risk of hyperkalaemia often induced by aldosterone blockade.

CONCLUSION

Excess aldosterone plays a central role in CKD, contributing not only to elevated BP but also to direct inflammatory and fibrotic injury in the kidney, heart, and vasculature. Given that these effects are only partially addressed by current therapies, strategies that more effectively suppress aldosterone activity are needed. ASI offers a targeted approach to this pathway, with clinical studies demonstrating consistent reductions in BP and dose-dependent reductions in albuminuria in CKD. The combination with SGLT2 inhibition appears particularly promising, by further reducing albuminuria and BP, and possibly mitigating the risk of hyperkalaemia associated with aldosterone-targeting therapies, supporting a more balanced efficacy–safety profile. ASI is being evaluated in large phase 3 clinical trials to determine whether these effects translate into improved kidney and cardiovascular outcomes (Table 1) for CKD and heart failure. Targeting aldosterone synthesis may represent an important next step in the management of these conditions, with combination strategies aimed at reducing residual risks with current therapeutic approaches.
