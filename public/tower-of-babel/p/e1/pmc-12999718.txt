Toxicogenomic Characterization of Perfluorooctanoic Acid–Associated Bladder Carcinogenesis
Authors: Yang Liu, Aifa Tang, Han Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to bridge toxicological target prediction and bladder cancer transcriptomics by systematically identifying molecular signatures and pathways that converge between PFOA-associated toxicological effects and bladder cancer biology, using an integrative multi-cohort computational framework.MethodsBioinformatics strategies were applied to assemble 9,591 putative molecular targets associated with perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) from five independent databases. Differential expression analysis and weighted gene co-expression network analysis were then conducted across five bladder cancer cohorts to delineate tumor-related genes. Functional enrichment analyses, machine learning–based modeling with SHAP interpretation, and molecular docking were subsequently employed to explore affected biological pathways and evaluate predictive performance.ResultsA total of 69 candidate genes, predominantly associated with cell cycle control and DNA damage–related processes, were delineated. Introduction

As a malignancy arising from the urinary tract, bladder cancer is highly prevalent and ranks among the leading cancers diagnosed in men, occupying the fourth position. Epidemiological data indicate that bladder cancer results in over 80,000 new cases and approximately 18,000 deaths annually worldwide(Siegel et al. 2024). From a clinical-pathological perspective, bladder cancer comprises two major forms, with non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) representing one subtype, which is generally less aggressive and associated with more favorable clinical prognosis, whereas muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC), which is highly aggressive and poses a significantly higher risk of metastasis, leading to poorer prognosis and diminished patient survival(Ghervan et al. 2017). In the clinical classification of bladder cancer, superficial lesions predominantly fall under the category of NMIBC, which represents approximately 70%−80% of newly diagnosed cases. Transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) remains the standard therapeutic approach for this subtype, with most patients achieving clinical remission(Wang et al. 2024; Gontero et al. 2024). However, this subgroup presents notable clinical challenges: firstly, the recurrence rate post-surgery remains high, approaching 70%; secondly, among those with recurrent disease, approximately one-third will progress to more aggressive MIBC(Jacobs et al. 2010). In light of these clinical features, it is strongly recommended that patients with NMIBC undergo lifelong, standardized surveillance and monitoring(Karakiewicz et al. 2006). From a clinical-pathological perspective, bladder cancers exhibiting highly malignant features are predominantly categorized as MIBC. For patients with this subtype, the current standard treatment modalities include either partial cystectomy or radical cystectomy(Nuijens et al. 2025). However, clinical evidence indicates that MIBC tumors demonstrate a pronounced propensity for early distant metastasis, resulting in a substantial proportion of patients missing the optimal surgical intervention. Consequently, the five-year survival rate for this cohort is significantly lower than that observed in other bladder cancer subtypes(Subiela et al. 2025).

While the clinical management of bladder cancer remains challenging, emerging environmental pollutants such as perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) have raised significant concerns regarding their potential contribution to bladder cancer pathogenesis. As a widely studied PFAS compound, perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) displays pronounced resistance to environmental degradation and possesses distinctive chemical features, such as its hydrophobic/hydrophilic nature due to the fully fluorinated carbon chain.(Cotruvo et al. 2023). The unusually high bond energy of the carbon–fluorine linkages in PFOA (486 kJ/mol) underlies its chemical stability and accounts for its prolonged persistence in the environment. It accumulates in organs such as the liver, kidneys, and bladder, where it exerts toxic effects over prolonged exposure (Cotruvo et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2015). Widespread environmental contamination with PFOA results from industrial emissions and the degradation of PFOA-containing products. It is particularly prevalent in water bodies near manufacturing and military sites, as well as in soil and airborne particles. In non-occupational settings, contaminated drinking water constitutes the major source of PFOA exposure.(Consonni and Fustinoni 2024; Kanetani et al. 2025; Steenland et al. 2015, 2013). Under current U.S. regulations, the Environmental Protection Agency mandates that PFOA concentrations in drinking water must not exceed 4.0 ng/L (ppt), as part of the first U.S. drinking water standards formally targeting PFAS compounds (Zhang et al. 2021);In Veneto, Italy, due to fire-fighting foam contamination, the median serum PFOA concentration in residents reached 80 ng/mL, substantially higher than the general population (< 5 ng/mL)(Pitter et al. 2020). Following internal exposure, the majority of PFOA remains albumin-bound in circulation (> 98%), while long-term retention is observed mainly in metabolically active organs, including the liver, kidneys, and testes. Upon entering the human body, PFOA binds predominantly to serum albumin, with a binding rate exceeding 98%. The removal of PFOA from the body occurs mainly through renal excretory pathways, mediated by glomerular filtration and transporter-dependent tubular secretion. Consequently, the bladder, serving as the reservoir for urine, is subjected to prolonged exposure to PFOA. Studies have shown that the concentration of PFOA in urine can reach up to one-tenth of its serum concentration, with a local half-life of approximately 1.5 years. This sustained accumulation within the bladder microenvironment is believed to enhance its carcinogenic potential by continuously exposing urothelial cells to the compound(Worley et al. 2017; Jian et al. 2017). In male rat models receiving 50 ppm PFOA, hepatocellular adenomas were observed at a markedly higher frequency compared with controls (62% versus 8%)(Filgo et al. 2015). A clear dose–response relationship was observed for Leydig cell tumor development, while 5 ppm was identified as the highest exposure level without detectable adverse effects (NOAEL)(Tian et al. 2019; Sun et al. 2018). Previous research has explored whether exposure to PFOA might contribute to bladder cancer development, this relationship between PFOA and bladder cancer remains controversial and lacks sufficient direct evidence. The C8 Health Project (n = 32,254) reported that participants with serum PFOA levels above the 75th percentile had a hazard ratio of 1.3 (95% CI: 1.0–1.7) for developing bladder cancer, although confounding factors such as smoking were not fully adjusted(Steenland et al. 2010). Researchers examined workers at a 3 M production plant, a significant association was observed, with an SMR of 1.34 for bladder cancer (p = 0.04) and a corresponding exposure–response trend reaching statistical significance (p-trend = 0.02) (Steenland and Woskie 2012). However, these findings are based on indirect evidence, such as single serum measurements and inconsistent exposure assessments, and further investigation is required to establish a more definitive link. Although animal studies and some epidemiological evidence suggest a potential link between PFOA and bladder cancer, the precise biological mechanisms underlying PFOA-associated bladder cancer have yet to be clarified, and existing studies have several limitations: Traditional toxicology often relies on high-dose animal experiments, an approach that compromises the predictive validity for real-world, prolonged low-dose exposure scenario.; Epidemiological findings are inconsistent and may be influenced by exposure assessment errors (e.g., single serum measurements) and confounding factors (e.g., smoking or occupational exposure to aromatic amines); Current studies have not systematically explored the toxicological mechanisms of PFOA in bladder cancer, particularly through high-throughput, computational approaches. This research aims to bridge this gap by integrating 113 machine learning methods to explore the molecular interactions and identify key pathways involved in PFOA-induced bladder cancer. Additionally, Molecular docking simulations (AutoDock Vina) are utilized to predict the binding modes and affinities of PFOA toward potential target proteins, including MCM7 and IGFBP2. The principal objectives of this study are threefold: (1) to explore transcriptional pathways that may overlap between predicted PFOA-associated targets and bladder cancer–related molecular programs; (2) to prioritize candidate molecular targets for subsequent experimental validation; and (3) to develop a cost-effective computational framework for the systematic toxicological assessment of PFAS compounds.

Methods

workflow of this study

Chemical Structure Retrieval and Target Prediction of Perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA)

The PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) served as the source for acquiring the initial molecular data of PFOA, including its defined chemical structure (SMILES: C(= O)(C(C(C(C(C(C(C(F)(F)F)(F)F)(F)F)(F)F)(F)F)(F)F)(F)F)O) and a representative three-dimensional conformer.. To systematically delineate its potential molecular targets, an integrative in silico prediction strategy was applied, leveraging five independent target identification platforms: bSDTNBI, ChEMBL, CTD, STITCH and PharmMapper. This comprehensive computational screen yielded a total of 9591 putative protein targets, establishing a robust target landscape for subsequent functional network reconstruction and mechanistic investigation.

Transcriptomic Data Acquisition and Preprocessing

Transcriptomic data from five publicly available bladder cancer cohorts in the NCBI GEO database were integrated using a systems bioinformatics framework to ensure comparability across datasets and robustness of downstream analyses, including GSE3167, GSE13507, GSE166716, GSE19915, and GSE52519, detailed information on each GEO dataset, including accession number, platform, sample size (tumor/normal), and analytical usage (training or validation), is summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Two datasets (GSE52519 and GSE13507) were designated as the discovery set for model training, while the remaining three served as external validation cohorts to assess generalizability across diverse patient populations. Gene-level harmonization was achieved by intersecting common features across platforms to maintain consistency. Probe redundancy was resolved by computing average expression values using the avereps function. Subsequently, all expression matrices were merged and adjusted for batch effects using the ComBat algorithm within the sva package, which applies an empirical Bayes approach to reduce non-biological variation while preserving underlying biological signals. To evaluate the effectiveness of batch correction and explore inter-sample heterogeneity, principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on the transposed expression matrices using the prcomp function in R. PCA plots incorporating 95% confidence ellipses were generated for each cohort, allowing visual inspection of sample dispersion and batch alignment pre- and post-normalization.

Discovery of Bladder Cancer–Related Gene Modules Through Integrated Expression Profiling and Network-Based Analysis

To capture bladder cancer–associated transcriptional features, we applied an integrative analytical strategy that jointly leveraged differential gene expression profiling and co-expression network modeling based on WGCNA. An initial differential expression analysis was conducted using the limma framework to screen genes exhibiting significant expression differences between bladder tumors and non-tumor tissues, applying stringent thresholds of |log₂ fold change|> 0.585 and adjusted p < 0.05. Two complementary visual analytics were employed: volcano plots for a statistical overview of the DEGs, and heatmaps to depict the architecture of their global expression trends across samples. To further explore co-regulatory patterns, WGCNA was performed on the DEG set to establish a scale-free, co-expressive gene network topology and uncover functionally enriched modules. Preprocessing steps included the exclusion of low-variance genes and aberrant samples, hierarchical clustering to assess sample integrity, and determination of an optimal soft-thresholding power to ensure adherence to scale-free network properties. The topological overlap matrix (TOM) was calculated to capture pairwise gene connectivity, followed by hierarchical clustering and dynamic tree cutting to define discrete gene modules. Highly correlated modules were subsequently merged to enhance the biological coherence of the network. To determine clinically relevant co-expression patterns, module eigengenes were correlated with phenotypic traits, allowing the prioritization of modules associated with disease status. To highlight key regulatory genes within each module, both module membership (MM) and gene significance (GS) were integrated to define hub genes. The final analysis yielded detailed module structures, trait-association heatmaps, and candidate hub genes, offering mechanistic insights into bladder cancer pathophysiology and potential molecular targets for further investigation. All module–trait correlation p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.

Identify DEGs, Construct Co‑expression Networks, and Annotate Functional Modules

To elucidate potential molecular mediators of PFOA-associated bladder carcinogenesis, DEGs were first identified through transcriptomic analysis. WGCNA was then applied to pinpoint central (hub) genes within co-regulated gene modules. The consensus gene set from DEG and hub analyses was cross-referenced with predicted PFOA targets curated from established toxicogenomic databases, yielding a subset of candidate interaction genes. Overlapping genes were visualized using set-based analytical tools to highlight convergence across data sources. Gene Ontology enrichment analysis covering biological process, cellular component, and molecular function terms, as well as KEGG pathway analysis, was conducted to interpret the functional roles of the candidate genes using the clusterProfiler R package. Enrichment results were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) approach, and only pathways with FDR-adjusted values below 0.05 were retained.

Machine Learning Model Construction and Performance Evaluation

A robust machine learning pipeline was implemented to construct and evaluate predictive models for bladder cancer risk stratification. The R platform (v4.4.2) served as an integrated environment for analysis, incorporating a range of algorithms from tree-based, regression-based, ensemble, and kernel methods. The models included gradient boosting machine (GBM), naive Bayes, extreme gradient boosting (xgboost)random forest (randomForestSRC), LASSO and ridge regression (glmnet), elastic net (glmnet), CoxBoost (mboost), Bayesian additive regression trees (BART), logistic regression, linear discriminant analysis (LDA), partial least squares regression (plsRglm), and support vector machine (SVM). Training and validation datasets were normalized independently using cohort-specific z-score standardization to prevent information leakage, with cohort-specific scaling applied to account for potential batch effects. Only genes or features shared between datasets were retained for subsequent analysis, ensuring consistency across cohorts. Feature selection was performed according to the intrinsic procedures embedded in each algorithm. Composite models were also developed by integrating feature selection techniques with downstream classifiers (e.g., LASSO + SVM, CoxBoost + GBM), based on predefined combinations. A threshold of at least five features was applied to eliminate models with insufficient predictive variables. Model training was performed using the training dataset, with class labels defined as dependent variables. Hyperparameter optimization for LASSO, Ridge, and Elastic Net was conducted within the glmnet framework through built-in cross-validation, ensuring an optimal bias-variance tradeoff. For tree-based and ensemble models, stratified repeated k-fold cross-validation was applied during model construction. Trained models were evaluated on both the training set and an independent validation cohort to generate risk scores and class probabilities. Predictive performance of the models was quantitatively evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), computed across multiple external validation cohorts. Model performance was quantified and compared using the cross-cohort average AUC as the primary metric. To assess the consistency and robustness of the 113 machine learning models, we computed the mean AUC, standard deviation (SD) of AUC, and coefficient of variation (CV_AUC) across all validation cohorts for each model. CV was defined as the ratio of SD to mean AUC, providing a normalized metric of inter-cohort variability in model performance. Lower CV values indicate higher robustness and predictive stability across datasets. The features selected by each algorithm were further analyzed for biological relevance. All prediction results, classification outcomes, feature sets, and performance metrics were systematically recorded. To visualize model performance across algorithms and cohorts, heatmaps were generated using the ComplexHeatmap package, with color schemes defined by the RColorBrewer package.

SHAP-Based Model Interpretation and Feature Contribution Analysis

Model interpretation was performed using SHAP to compute Shapley values for individual prediction decomposition. Following initial preprocessing, which included feature standardization and selection of candidate genes, to ensure representative splits, stratified sampling guided the division of the data into training (70%) and hold-out test (30%) cohorts, executed using the caret package. Class labels were binarized to facilitate downstream modeling. Multiple classification algorithms were assessed, including Random Forest (randomForest), Support Vector Machines (kernlab), Naive Bayes (klaR), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (xgboost), along with other models from a predefined algorithm list. To improve model generalizability and mitigate overfitting, training was performed using repeated five-fold cross-validation. The AUC for each model, derived from independent test set predictions, was calculated with the pROC package, and the algorithm yielding the highest AUC was selected for subsequent analysis.The best-performing model was then used to calculate SHAP values to evaluate feature importance. Permutation SHAP values (via permshap) provided per-feature, per-sample attribution of model predictions. The shapviz package was utilized to generate a range of visualizations, including summary bar charts, SHAP bee swarm plots, and dependence plots, individual prediction waterfall plots, and force plots. These visualizations provided both global and local insights into the impact of features, thereby enhancing the interpretability of the model's biological relevance and clinical applicability.

Integrated Differential Expression, Immune Correlation, and Genomic Localization Analysis

The selection of DEGs adhered to stringent thresholds: a minimum absolute log₂ fold change of 0.585 and a false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted p-value below 0.05. These DEGs were cross-validated against feature genes selected by a combined Stepwise Generalized Linear Model (backward) and Random Forest algorithm. Volcano plots were used to visualize the differential expression, with key model genes annotated for clarity. For each model gene, normalized expression data were extracted and stratified by experimental groups (tumor versus normal samples). Statistical assessment of expression differences between groups was conducted using the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test. To investigate gene relationships, Pearson correlation matrices and distribution histograms were used to characterize co-expression patterns. Immune cell composition was estimated applying the CIBERSORT analytical tool based on the integrated and batch-corrected bladder cancer transcriptomic datasets. The LM22 leukocyte gene signature matrix, comprising 22 human immune cell subtypes, served as the reference standard for comparative analysis. Quantile normalization was enabled, and permutation testing was conducted with 1,000 permutations to assess deconvolution confidence. Downstream immune analyses were restricted to samples passing the CIBERSORT significance filter (P < 0.05), and Spearman correlation analyses were performed to quantify the associative links between specific gene expression signatures and quantitative measures of immune cell abundance. These correlations were classified based on statistical significance and effect size, and a weighted gene-immune network was visualized using the linkET framework. Raw genomic positional information was processed and localized against the GRCh38 (hg38) reference assembly, and a circos plot was constructed to spatially localize candidate genes on chromosomal ideograms, providing context for their distribution in further functional analyses.

Molecular Docking and Interaction Analysis

To investigate potential interactions between PFOA and the nine key proteins highlighted by machine learning, molecular docking simulations were carried out. Protein coordinates for docking were retrieved from the PDB or AlphaFold databases (Table 1). For complex-derived or domain-level structures, including ADRM1 (PDB ID: 9E7K) and MAD1L1 (PDB ID: 1GO4), only the target protein chains were retained, with all interacting partners removed. The solution NMR structure of the IGFBP2 C-terminal domain (PDB ID: 2H7T) was used with a representative conformer selected, while an AlphaFold-predicted model was employed for UPK3A. Protein structures were processed with AutoDockTools, where water molecules were deleted, polar hydrogens were added, and Gasteiger charges were applied, followed by conversion to PDBQT format. The PubChem-derived PFOA structure was energy-minimized using OpenBabel. Docking was performed with AutoDock Vina using grid boxes covering ligand-accessible regions and an exhaustiveness value of 8. Interaction analysis was performed on the lowest-energy docking pose, and molecular interactions were visualized using PyMOL.

Identification and Functional Characterization of PFOA-Associated Core Genes in Bladder Carcinogenesis

To further delineate the molecular targets of PFOA in bladder cancer, an integrative analysis was performed by intersecting differentially expressed genes, the WGCNA-derived black module, and predicted PFOA target genes, yielding 69 core candidate genes (Fig. 4A). Protein–protein interaction network analysis (Fig. 4B) revealed extensive interconnectivity among these genes, suggesting coordinated functional roles.

(B) Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of the 69 core genes constructed using STRING, illustrating the extensive interconnectivity and potential functional cooperation among these targets. Gene Ontology enrichment analysis of the 69 overlapping genes between PFOA targets and bladder cancer-associated genes revealed a predominant involvement in cell cycle regulation. Key biological processes included mitotic cell cycle phase transition, regulation of mitotic nuclear division, and G2/M transition, underscoring potential perturbations in mitotic fidelity. Cellular component annotations were enriched in chromosomal domains, including the centromeric region, telomeric region, and replication fork, suggesting genomic instability as a plausible mechanistic axis. Kinase-related molecular functions were prominent, encompassing regulation of cyclin-dependent protein serine/threonine kinases, enzymatic activity toward histones, and affinity for damaged DNA, implicating dysregulation of phosphorylation cascades and DNA repair pathways. Collectively, these results suggest that PFOA may promote bladder carcinogenesis through interference with genome maintenance and cell cycle progression (Fig. 4C). Concurrently, Pathway enrichment analysis (KEGG) of the 69 genes derived from dual-omics integration confirmed their significant convergence on central axes of tumorigenesis and cell cycle governance. Key enriched pathways included cell cycle, apoptosis, cellular senescence, and the p53 and FoxO signaling pathways. Multiple cancer types were implicated, including bladder cancer, glioma, melanoma, and leukemia, alongside pathways related to drug resistance and microRNA regulation. Notably, several virus-associated carcinogenic pathways-such as HPV, EBV, HIV-1, and hepatitis B/C-were also enriched, suggesting that PFOA may engage host oncogenic programs via viral mimicry or immune evasion. These findings indicate that PFOA-associated genes converge on conserved oncogenic and cell regulatory networks implicated in bladder cancer progression (Fig. 4D).

Machine Learning-Based Core Gene Selection and SHAP Interpretation of Predictive Contributions

Following comprehensive model construction and performance evaluation using 113 distinct machine learning algorithms. Among the 113 machine learning models evaluated, glmBoost exhibited the highest mean AUC (0.980) with a low CV_AUC (0.0251)(Table S3, Fig S2), indicating excellent predictive accuracy and stability across validation cohorts. Other top-performing models, such as Stepglm[both] + GBM (Mean_AUC = 0.966, CV_AUC = 0.0246) and Lasso + glmBoost (Mean_AUC = 0.975, CV_AUC = 0.0275), also demonstrated robust performance with minimal variation. In contrast, models like Lasso + Stepglm[both] showed lower predictive accuracy (Mean_AUC = 0.611) and higher variability (CV_AUC = 0.4261), reflecting poorer generalizability. Based on these findings, the Gradient Boosting with Component-wise Linear Models (glmBoost) approach was selected as the final predictive model. This choice was driven by glmBoost’s ability to balance high predictive accuracy, feature interpretability, and robustness across both internal and external validation cohorts. Its component-wise linear modeling framework enhances interpretability by quantifying the contribution of individual features, while the iterative boosting process effectively captures complex, non-linear relationships inherent in biological data. To ensure model generalizability and minimize overfitting, a repeated five-fold cross-validation strategy was embedded within the iterative training routine of all candidate algorithms. Across all external validation cohorts, the AUC served as the core metric for assessing the predictive performance of each model. Ultimately, the glmBoost model was prioritized for downstream analysis due to its optimal combination of gene feature count, predictive accuracy, and performance stability. This final model identified nine core predictive genes: ADRM1, BCL2L1, CDC25C, IGFBP2, MAD1L1, MCM7, SKP2, TOP2A and UPK3A. Performance comparisons of multiple machine learning models across datasets are presented (Fig. 5A), while the predictive accuracy and validity of the selected model genes are demonstrated (Fig. 5B). The predictive performance of the constructed nine-gene signature was further assessed using an internal–external validation strategy. In the training set, the model achieved near-perfect classification performance with an AUC of 0.986 (Figure S3A). Robust generalizability was demonstrated across independent validation cohorts, yielding AUC values of 0.991 in GSE19915 (Figure S3B), 1.000 in GSE3167 (Figure S3C), and 0.944 in GSE166716 (Figure S3D), thereby confirming the stability and high discriminatory power of the proposed biomarker panel across diverse clinical cohorts. Analysis of expression distribution showed that seven out of nine signature genes resided within the regions of elevated expression on the volcano plot, while two genes were found in the low-expression regions (Fig. 5C), further supporting their functional relevance in tumor biology. The impact of individual genes on prediction performance was assessed through SHAP analysis. The SHAP summary plot (Fig. 5E) highlighted MCM7 as the most influential gene, underscoring its pivotal role in DNA replication and cell cycle regulation, both of which are critical processes in bladder carcinogenesis. The SHAP bar plot (Fig. 5D) ranked all nine genes by their mean absolute SHAP values, providing a quantitative assessment of their relative impact on the model’s output. Analysis of the SHAP dependence plot (Fig. 5F) indicated that malignancy risk varied non-linearly with MCM7 expression levels, with a sharp increase in risk when expression exceeded 9 FPKM, suggesting a threshold effect with potential diagnostic utility. ROC curve analysis was used to evaluate the discriminative ability of ten machine learning models constructed using the selected gene signature. Among them, the random forest (RF) model showed the best performance, achieving an AUC of 0.990, and notably, even the least performing model, decision tree (DTS), yielded an AUC of 0.867 (Fig. 5G). These results collectively underscore the robust and consistent discriminative capacity of the identified biomarkers across diverse machine learning algorithms. The SHAP force plot (Fig. 5H) provides an individualized visualization of gene-specific contributions to prediction outcomes, further enhancing model interpretability for potential clinical application.

Subgroup Analysis of Signature Performance

To evaluate the clinical applicability of the nine-gene signature, subgroup analyses were performed across clinically relevant variables, including age, pathological stage, tumor grade, muscle invasion, and gender, in the training cohort and three independent validation datasets (GSE166716, GSE19915, and GSE3167) (Figure S4). In the training cohort (Figure S4A-E), the nine-gene signature achieved near-perfect predictive accuracy in age-based subgroups (AUC = 1.00), while demonstrating moderate discrimination for tumor grade, muscle invasion, and pathological stage (AUC range: 0.594–0.714).Across external validation cohorts, the model consistently showed strong performance in age and tumor grade subgroups (AUCs up to 1.00 and 0.944, respectively), whereas predictive accuracy in gender-, stage-, and muscle invasion-based subgroups remained modest and variable across datasets (Figure S4F-O). Overall, these results indicate that the nine-gene signature is robust across age and tumor grade strata, while reduced performance in other subgroups likely reflects biological heterogeneity and limited subgroup sample sizes. Accordingly, these subgroup analyses were intended as sensitivity analyses to assess model robustness rather than to derive subgroup-specific clinical conclusions.

Integrative Analysis of Core PFOA-Associated Genes and Their Functional Implications

Spatial genomic mapping revealed the chromosomal distribution of the nine PFOA-associated core genes (Fig. 6A), providing a framework for subsequent functional interrogation. Integration of normalized gene expression profiles with CIBERSORT-estimated immune cell fractions demonstrated significant associations between core gene expression and tumor immune microenvironment composition (Fig. 6B). Correlation analysis revealed that several core genes exhibited distinct associations with specific immune cell subsets. ADRM1 expression was positively correlated with resting NK cells, while CDC25C showed positive correlations with plasma cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and M1 macrophages. IGFBP2 demonstrated a notable positive correlation with Tregs, suggesting a potential role in modulating immune suppression. MAD1L1 expression showed significant positive correlations with neutrophils, activated mast cells, and activated dendritic cells, indicating involvement in innate immune responses. Furthermore, MCM7 and TOP2A were both positively correlated with M1 macrophages, with TOP2A also showing a significant association with gamma delta T cells. UPK3A expression exhibited a positive correlation with Tregs, implicating it in immune evasion mechanisms. These findings highlight the immune-modulatory roles of the identified core genes and suggest potential crosstalk between PFOA-associated molecular pathways and the tumor immune microenvironment in bladder cancer. Protein–protein interaction network analysis delineated direct and indirect interactions among these core genes (Fig. 6C). Differential expression analysis revealed distinct expression patterns among the nine model genes. ADRM1, BCL2L1, MAD1L1, MCM7, SKP2, CDC25C, and TOP2A expression were substantially elevated in bladder tumors when contrasted with normal bladder tissue, suggesting their potential oncogenic involvement. In contrast, IGFBP2 and UPK3A exhibited higher expression levels in normal bladder tissues, implying possible tumor-suppressive roles. These findings underscore the heterogeneous transcriptional behavior of the model genes and highlight the functional relevance of the majority in bladder carcinogenesis (Fig. 6D).

Binding Affinity Profiling of PFOA with Core Bladder Cancer Targets

Molecular docking analysis was conducted to explore the potential binding interactions between PFOA and each of the nine model proteins (Fig. 7A–I). The predicted binding energies ranged from − 6.4 to − 13.0 kcal/mol, indicating variable affinity across targets (Table 1). Among them, IGFBP2 exhibited the most favorable predicted binding energy (− 13.0 kcal/mol), whereas UPK3A showed comparatively weaker interaction (− 6.4 kcal/mol). Intermediate binding affinities were observed for TOP2A (− 9.0 kcal/mol), MCM7 (− 8.0 kcal/mol), MAD1L1 (− 7.9 kcal/mol), and SKP2 (− 7.7 kcal/mol). These results suggest that PFOA may have the physicochemical potential to associate with multiple proteins implicated in cell cycle regulation, DNA replication, and chromatin-related processes. However, the docking analysis represents an in silico prediction of binding feasibility rather than experimental evidence of physical interaction or functional modulation. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and provide a rationale for prioritizing selected targets-such as IGFBP2 and MCM7-for further experimental validation.

Discussion

Bladder cancer (BC) is a common and frequently recurring tumor of the urinary system and continues to impose a substantial burden on global public health(Sung et al. 2021). The majority of cases are classified as urothelial carcinomas, with known etiological factors including tobacco exposure, aromatic amines, and arsenic-contaminated water (Cumberbatch et al. 2016; Burger et al. 2013; Letasiova et al. 2012). However, emerging evidence suggests that industrial and environmental chemicals, especially persistent organic pollutants (POPs), may contribute substantially to urothelial carcinogenesis through mechanisms that remain incompletely defined(Richters et al. 2020; Takacs and Abbott 2007).Among these environmental agents, PFOA, a widely used per- and polyfluoroalkyl substance(PFAS), has garnered increasing concern due to its persistence, bioaccumulation, and systemic toxicity (Lau et al. 2007; Post et al. 2012; Sunderland et al. 2019). Accumulating evidence indicates that PFOA exposure is associated with multiple detrimental health effects, such as reproductive toxicity, immune dysfunction, endocrine interference, and carcinogenic potential(Steenland et al. 2010; Barry et al. 2013). Population-based studies suggest that exposure to PFAS is associated with a higher likelihood of developing certain cancers, such as kidney, testicular, and potentially bladder malignancies (Lopez-Espinosa et al. 2012; Vieira et al. 2013). Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms linking PFOA exposure to bladder carcinogenesis remain poorly characterized.

In this study, an integrative computational and transcriptomic framework was employed to systematically explore potential molecular mechanisms through which PFOA may be associated with bladder carcinogenesis. Importantly, the transcriptomic datasets analyzed in this study do not contain explicit information on PFOA exposure; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of direct causality. The 3D structure of PFOA was first retrieved and investigated for carcinogenicity potential, revealing a high in silico carcinogenicity score (~ 80%), supporting its prioritization for hypothesis-driven toxicogenomic investigation, consistent with its classification as a possible human carcinogen by regulatory agencies (Grandjean and Clapp 2015). we employed an integrative computational and transcriptomic framework to elucidate potential molecular mechanisms through which PFOA may promote bladder carcinogenesis. Our target prediction strategy identified 9,591 potential protein interactors of PFOA, underscoring its broad molecular footprint. Importantly, by intersecting these predicted targets with bladder cancer-specific transcriptomic alterations derived from five GEO datasets, a subset of 69 core genes was identified that may represent shared molecular nodes between PFOA exposure and bladder cancer-related transcriptional dysregulation. The functional profile identified these genes as core participants in critical oncogenic processes, notably cell cycle regulation, G2/M checkpoint control, DNA repair pathways, and chromatin remodeling-hallmarks of genomic instability and malignant transformation (Malumbres and Barbacid 2009; Musgrove et al. 2011; Hanahan and Weinberg 2011). Notably, pathways associated with p53 signaling and cellular senescence were significantly enriched, suggesting that PFOA exposure may potentially perturb tumor suppressor networks implicated in urothelial carcinogenesis. Previous studies have hinted at PFAS-induced DNA damage responses and oxidative stress-mediated p53 inactivation(Vousden and Lane 2007), which align with our findings. Furthermore, our enrichment of virus-associated carcinogenic pathways (e.g., HPV, EBV, HIV-1, HBV/HCV) raises a compelling hypothesis: PFOA may interfere with host antiviral defense mechanisms or mimic viral oncoproteins, thereby modulating immune evasion and oncogenic reprogramming. While direct molecular mimicry by PFOA remains speculative, PFAS-induced immunosuppression and cytokine dysregulation have been documented(Dewitt et al. 2008; Grandjean et al. 2012; Wang et al. 2015), warranting further investigation into PFOA’s role in perturbing virus-host interactions within the tumor microenvironment.

To refine the molecular landscape and identify candidate biomarkers potentially associated with PFOA-related bladder cancer, we applied 113 machine learning models and ultimately selected a glmboost-based approach, yielding a robust nine-gene signature: ADRM1, BCL2L1, CDC25C, IGFBP2, MAD1L1, MCM7, SKP2, TOP2A, and UPK3A. The model demonstrated exceptional performance across both training and independent validation datasets, with AUC values consistently > 0.94. Despite this strong predictive performance, these results should be interpreted cautiously. The GEO datasets utilized may not fully capture the demographic and genetic diversity of global populations, nor account for individual variability in PFAS metabolism and exposure levels. Inter-individual differences in PFOA biotransformation, influenced by genetic polymorphisms in metabolic enzymes and differential pharmacokinetics, could modulate susceptibility to bladder carcinogenesis. Future studies incorporating exposure biomonitoring and dose–response assessments in diverse populations are essential to validate these findings in real-world contexts. SHAP interpretability analysis ranked MCM7 as the most influential predictor, suggesting its central role in PFOA-associated transcriptional reprogramming in BC. Notably, MCM7 is a well-established replication licensing factor and is frequently overexpressed in multiple cancers(Wang et al. 2017; Dudderidge et al. 2005; Qu et al. 2017). Its prominence in the predictive model suggests that dysregulation of DNA replication machinery may represent a key transcriptional feature potentially associated with PFOA-related carcinogenic processes, although direct causation cannot be inferred from the current analysis. Interestingly, IGFBP2 exhibited the highest binding affinity with PFOA in molecular docking simulations, suggesting that PFOA may interfere with IGFBP2-mediated growth factor signaling, potentially contributing to the observed tumor-suppressive downregulation of IGFBP2 in bladder cancer tissues. It should be noted that several docking analyses were performed using domain-level or complex-derived protein structures, as well as predicted models in the absence of experimental structures. Therefore, the docking results should be interpreted as exploratory predictions that require further validation. Spatial expression profiling revealed upregulation of ADRM1, BCL2L1, CDC25C, MCM7, MAD1L1, SKP2, and TOP2A in bladder tumors, consistent with their roles in cell cycle progression, chromatin dynamics, and apoptosis resistance(Yu et al. 2004; Dai et al. 2019; Schepers et al. 2005; Tamamori-Adachi et al. 2004). While these observations support their relevance as candidate biomarkers, direct molecular interactions between PFOA and these proteins remain to be experimentally validated. Conversely, IGFBP2 and UPK3A were downregulated, suggesting potential tumor-suppressive functions. While direct interactions between PFOA and these genes remain to be experimentally validated, their consistent dysregulation across multiple datasets supports their candidacy as biomarkers for PFOA-related carcinogenesis. Molecular docking simulations revealed direct physical interactions between PFOA and multiple core proteins, with IGFBP2 showing the highest binding affinity (−13.0 kcal/mol). TOP2A, MCM7, and SKP2 also displayed favorable interactions, suggesting potential physicochemical interactions between PFOA and key regulatory proteins that warrant further experimental investigation involved in DNA replication, repair, and chromatin remodeling (Classen et al. 2003; Nitiss 2009). However, confirming these interactions requires applying molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, which can elucidate the atomic-scale stability and conformational evolution of the complex in a physiologically relevant milieu. Additionally, Confirmation of these computational predictions necessitates validation through established biophysical and biochemical approaches, including SPR and ITC for affinity measurement, alongside co-immunoprecipitation for interaction mapping in complex biological matrices. We acknowledge that certain target proteins may present challenges in expression, purification, or assay optimization, which could limit immediate translational applications. Nonetheless, the prioritization of genes such as MCM7 and IGFBP2, based on SHAP importance and docking results, provides a rational framework for targeted validation efforts. Importantly, it should be noted that the nine-gene signature identified in this study was constructed as a bladder cancer classification model derived from tumor-normal transcriptomic differences, rather than a biomarker reflecting direct PFOA exposure. In addition, all transcriptomic analyses in this study were based on microarray datasets, which provide lower resolution than bulk RNA-seq or single-cell transcriptomic technologies. While multi-cohort microarray integration enabled robust external validation and reproducibility assessment, future studies incorporating population-level (bulk) and individual-cell (scRNA-seq) transcriptional data will be essential to further refine cell-type–specific mechanisms and strengthen causal inference. Although the integration of multiple independent transcriptomic cohorts enhances analytical robustness, this strategy does not capture inter-individual variability in PFAS exposure levels, metabolic processing, or cumulative environmental co-exposures. Consequently, while several identified genes overlap with pathways previously implicated in PFOA-related toxicological effects, the present study does not establish a PFOA-specific exposure signature, and the observed associations should be interpreted as biologically plausible consistencies rather than direct evidence of causality. The absence of transcription factor binding site (TFBS) analyses further limits our understanding of upstream regulatory mechanisms. Incorporating TFBS motif scanning, ChIP-seq, or ATAC-seq in future studies will be instrumental in elucidating the transcriptional regulatory circuits perturbed by PFOA exposure. Moreover, proteomic and metabolomic profiling will offer a systems-level perspective, enabling the validation of post-transcriptional modifications and metabolic alterations associated with PFOA-mediated carcinogenesis. Functional assays, including CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene perturbations and phenotypic rescue experiments, will provide mechanistic insights into the roles of the identified core genes in tumorigenic processes.

Finally, the translational implications of our findings warrant emphasis. The identified nine-gene signature may hold potential, pending further validation, as a tool for risk assessment in individuals susceptible to PFOA-related bladder cancer. particularly in occupational or environmentally exposed populations. Moreover, the integration of machine learning-driven feature selection with toxicogenomic data provides a scalable framework for evaluating other environmental carcinogens. In clinical practice, such predictive models could enhance risk stratification, inform surveillance protocols, and guide early intervention strategies for populations vulnerable to environmental carcinogens.
