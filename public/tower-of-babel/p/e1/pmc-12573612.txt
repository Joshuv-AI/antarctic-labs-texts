Randomized controlled trials investigating proximal humerus fractures lack consensus in inclusion criteria
Authors: Cailan L. Feingold, Justin Castonguay, Eric H. Lin, Andrew B. Barcenas, Jacob L. Kotlier, Meng-Yung Ong, Joseph Mixa, Ioanna K. Bolia, Joseph N. Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate inclusion and exclusion criteria for subjects of randomized control trials (RCTs) studying operative management of proximal humerus fractures (PHFs) and to define the presence or absence of objectivity and consensus among these inclusion and exclusion criteria of these studies.MethodsFollowing Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus were queried from inception to September 1, 2023, for RCTs studying PHFs. Studies were included if they investigated operative management of PHFs, had defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, were published in the English language, and were in peer-reviewed journals. RCTs were excluded if they were investigating only nonoperative management of PHFs. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were extracted from these studies and compared using descriptive statistics following study screening.ResultsA total of 42 RCTs met the inclusion criteria, of which 9 investigated surgical vs. conservative management and 33 compared surgical techniques. The Neer fracture classification system was utilized most frequently in 30 (71.4%) studies in fracture pattern description. Only 8 studies defined the direction of angulation and 3 defined the ratio of displacement of the shaft with relation to the head. Injury chronicity and mechanism were rarely included (21.4% and 19.0%, respectively). Most (73.9%) included age within their inclusion and exclusion criteria. Other patient-specific factors including sex and baseline function/independence were scarce.ConclusionIn RCTs studying PHFs, most studies rely on fracture pattern classification systems to describe inclusion and exclusion criteria, but these fail to objectively delineate which fractures should be included. Few studies include additional elements to improve objectivity of fracture pattern characterization. Besides age, most RCTs on surgical management of PHFs do not include patient-specific variables within their inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Proximal humerus fractures (PHFs) make up around 5% of all fractures and are more common in older adults.57 Furthermore, they are the third most common osteoporotic fracture in the elderly, following wrist and hip fractures.39 A study in New York from 1990 to 2010 showed that the incidence of PHFs increased by 28% in patients aged 65 and older, likely secondary to an increasing median population age.36 Importantly, PHFs are a source of considerable morbidity and mortality.16,72

PHFs can be broken down into several subtypes based on displacement, fracture lines, surgical neck status, and the presence or absence of glenohumeral dislocation.31,65 For example, the Neer classification breaks these fractures into 4 categories based on the anatomic relationship of 4 segments: the greater tuberosity, lesser tuberosity, articular surface, and shaft.50 Another common classification is the Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen (AO)/Orthopedic Trauma Association classification which categorizes into 3 main groups with subgroups based on fracture location, status of surgical neck, and dislocation status.45 To classify these fractures appropriately, imaging in the form of X-rays and computed tomography scans are usually required.56,63 Further classification of these injuries is useful because it helps direct management; in a survey distributed to fellowship-trained trauma surgeons and shoulder and elbow surgeons, factors like dislocation status, displacement, and fracture type all contributed to their treatment decisions.30

Outside of characteristics of the injury itself, other variables like patient age, osteoporotic status, and injury mechanism contribute to these decisions as well.30,40,51 For example, younger patients, who are more likely to be involved in higher-energy injuries, are more likely to undergo open reduction and internal fixation with intramedullary nails or fixed-angle plates if undergoing surgery.7 On the other hand, a patient sustaining a PHF from a lower energy mechanism like a fall, likely someone who is older with comorbidities like osteoporosis, is more likely to undergo reverse total shoulder arthroplasty if surgery is necessary or nonoperative management.25

Given the role that fracture classification and patient variables play in PHF management, these factors must be well-defined for the subject groups in studies on PHFs. There is a need within the literature to investigate and define the presence or absence of uniformity of criteria in randomized control trials (RCTs) studying PHFs. This is critical because, without well-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, clinicians are not able to easily apply the findings of these RCTs to their patients with unique injuries and variables. A lack of uniformity in criteria has been identified within the orthopedic field.1,24,60 This study aims to investigate inclusion and exclusion criteria for subjects of RCTs studying PHFs and to define the presence or absence of consensus among these studies.

Methods

This systematic review utilized a priori protocol and was conducted following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines.48,70

Study selection inclusion/exclusion criteria

Duplicates were removed and articles were screened using titles and abstracts by 2 authors (J.C. and M.Y.). Studies were included if they were (1) RCTs investigating operative vs. nonoperative management of or a comparison of surgical techniques of PHFs, (2) published in English, and (3) had defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. RCTs investigating only nonoperative management of PHFs were excluded. Publications of RCT protocols without results were also excluded. Full-text screening was performed by 2 authors (J.C. and M.Y.). If conflicts arose, they were resolved by a more senior, third author (J.K.). Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Jadad scale which evaluates randomization, blinding, and accounting for all patients.34 The maximum score is 5 and the minimum score is 0.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed by 2 authors (J.C. and M.Y.), one was responsible for extracting the data and the other verified for accuracy and completeness. If disagreements arose, a third, more senior author (J.K.) was available to help reach consensus. The information extracted and recorded included year of publication, total subject number, number of sites participating, mean age overall, mean age of control/intervention groups, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and mechanism of injury.

Data analysis

Extracted data were organized descriptively. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were analyzed for fracture pattern descriptions and patient-specific factors. Patient-specific factors included age, sex, and independence or autonomy. Other patient-specific factors included chronicity of injury and energy level of injury mechanism.

Results

A total of 42 studies met the inclusion criteria. Nine (21.4%) of these studies were investigating surgical vs. nonsurgical intervention, and 33 (78.6%) were investigating different types of surgical techniques. The mean Jadad quality score was 3.26 ± 0.94. These studies were published between 1988 and 2023, with the majority (22, 52.4%) published in the last decade since 2015.

Surgical vs. nonsurgical management

The vast majority of studies comparing surgical techniques had elements of subjectivity within their inclusion criteria. Of the 33 studies comparing surgical techniques, 13 (39.4%) explained within their inclusion criteria that patients either met surgical indications or were undergoing surgical management of acute proximal humerus fractures without additional context to the surgical indications. Five (15.2%) studies did not define displacement and 5 (15.2%) studies who did define displacement in distance and/or angulation did not indicate varus displacement, valgus displacement, or both. Unstable fractures without definition of instability were mentioned in the inclusion criteria of 2 (6.1%) studies.

Patient sociodemographics were evaluated in addition to fracture characteristics. Ten (30.3%) studies did not include age specifications within their inclusion or exclusion criteria and 7 (21.2%) studies only excluded pediatric or skeletally immature patients. Seven (21.2%) studies included mechanism of injury (high vs. low energy) and 8 (24.2%) included chronicity within their criteria. Discussion

Based on the findings of this systematic review, there is little consensus on inclusion and exclusion criteria among RCTs investigating PHFs. Most studies included age specifications as an element of their inclusion or exclusion criteria, but patient sex and independence or autonomy level were incorporated much less frequently. For fracture patterns, Neer classification was the most commonly utilized descriptor. However, most studies failed to include additional qualifiers, such as direction of displacement, that would more objectively describe fracture patterns. Most RCTs on PHFs include subjective elements in their inclusion and exclusion criteria, making it difficult to apply study findings to unique patient populations and PHFs.

While the use of fracture pattern classification systems for PHF is helpful, they can fail to provide an objective description that can be utilized homogenously in surgical decision-making. The Neer and AO classification systems are valuable because they are well-known and have been in use since the 1970s and 1980s, respectively.2,50 However, multiple studies have demonstrated that the reliability and intraobserver/interobserver agreement on the Neer classification is low.26,44 Chelli et al. found that in more than one-third of PHF cases, shoulder surgeons disagreed with article authors on the interpretation of the Neer classification of deidentified images.13 Heterogenous results in the use and interpretation of Neer's system have been found across geographic regions and subspecialty training as well.3,4 The AO system, which was also commonly utilized among these RCTs, was found to have the lowest interobserver reliability among proximal humerus fracture classification systems.44 Given that these 2 classifications were employed in 86% of inclusion and exclusion criteria, the low reliability means that surgeons will have a difficult time applying the results of these RCTs if we cannot agree on the surgical indications.

Fracture pattern classifications are often used as the sole criterion in the description of the injury. However, they fail to capture the range of important details and factors in clinical decision-making. For example, Gracitelli et al. found that the most cited variable used to decide on surgical treatment was head angulation in the coronal plane, which included varus or valgus direction in angulations greater than 20°.26 Additionally, head angulation in the coronal plane was chosen as the most important variable in 45% of PHF cases.26 Despite its value in surgical decision-making, only 4 studies analyzed here included a description of the direction of displacement within their inclusion and exclusion criteria. The other most influential factors for surgical indication were displacement of the greater tuberosity and displacement of the humeral shaft in the coronal and sagittal planes.26 Displacement of the humeral shaft in the coronal and sagittal planes was defined by head-diaphysis contact ≥50%, <50%, or no contact.26 Only 3 RCTs included displacement of the humeral shaft as a measure of both translation or angulation and ratio of head-diaphysis contact. All 3 of these studies did indicate in which plane this ratio had to be deduced, though, or if this measurement could be taken from either plane. There are multiple elements not captured by PHF classification systems that influence surgical decision-making, and these are rarely included in RCT patient selection criteria.

Outside of fracture pattern descriptions, patient-specific factors are also important in management decisions. Age, for example, is an essential variable in the decision about conservative vs. operative management. In patients over 65, surgeons may be more inclined to opt for nonoperative management due to increased concerns for patient health and safety in surgery.46 For those who do undergo operative treatment, shoulder arthroplasty may be employed for this age group, whereas younger patients may be more likely to undergo open reduction and internal fixation due to differences in bone quality and functional demands.18,28,47 Physiologic vs. chronologic age is another important question, as a patient who is chronologically older but still suffers from poor bone quality or has a high comorbidity burden may be managed as though they were a chronologically older patient.16,49 Patient sex is another important consideration, given that postmenopausal women may be more likely to have poor bone quality due to osteoporosis.69 Additionally, a patient's level of autonomy and independence, especially in an older cohort of patients, may affect their ability to participate in postoperative rehabilitation, which may affect outcomes. Likewise, the patient's goals and requirements in terms of functional status may differ based on age, lifestyle, and living situation. Despite the role that patient demographics play; 11 studies did not include age within inclusion or exclusion criteria. Of the majority that did include age, many solely excluded minors or skeletally immature individuals. Patient autonomy and sex were rarely incorporated. Patient-specific variables are influential in the clinical decision-making process for PHFs and should be included in RCT inclusion and exclusion criteria to improve their application to unique patients.

RCTs on PHFs must aim to increase the objectivity of their inclusion and exclusion criteria to facilitate the application of their findings. Age specifications should be a patient variable that is always incorporated into the inclusion and exclusion given its role in surgical vs. nonsurgical management, as well as the type of surgical management. In terms of fracture pattern, the use of classification systems like Neer is valuable, but authors should incorporate additional details to improve interpretation for readers. For example, including the direction of angulation or specifying that while the degree of angulation was a criterion, the direction of angulation was not. Furthermore, reporting what type of imaging was used to determine eligibility: radiographs vs. 2D or 3D computed tomography scans. Alternatively, the pathomorphologic classification described more recently by Resch et al. was found to have higher reliability than Neer's system.26,62 However, even this fracture classification system may not capture all elements involved in decision-making. Authors could improve the applicability of their studies by being more descriptive regarding fracture patterns, which could even include mentioning if a certain element was not considered a criterion.

This study has multiple limitations. There was inherent subjectivity in deciding whether inclusion and exclusion criteria were objective as this decision was made by the senior author. To minimize this effect, we instead sought to identify elements utilized within these criteria rather than assigning a single label of objective or subjective to each study. Furthermore, this study is cross-sectional and as such, the results of this study can only be applied to RCTs investigating surgical vs. nonsurgical or comparison of surgical techniques for RCTs.

Conclusion

The majority of RCTs on surgical management of PHFs include subjectivity, which limits the applicability of their findings. Neer's fracture classification system was the most common fracture pattern descriptor but is weakened by its low reliability. RCTs on PHFs should prioritize improving the objectivity of their inclusion and exclusion criteria by incorporating patient-specific factors and additional fracture elements not covered by fracture classification systems.
