Encounter based energy sharing in wildlife communication systems
Authors: Saeid Iranmanesh, Raad Raad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In this work, the concept of peer-to-peer energy sharing in wildlife communication systems is explored. In this context, wild animals can share energy wirelessly besides their data communications as they opportunistically come into range of each other. Our goal is to find a way to balance the energy among the nodes and minimize this energy loss. We propose a novel encounter-based energy-sharing scheme, called EBES, that utilizes single and multi-hop transmission to achieve energy balance, minimize energy losses, and maximize the lifetime of the wildlife communication system. EBES is based on a variety of parameters, including the amount of energy left in the system and the nodes’ encounter rate, and buffer sizes. In the simulation studies, we considered a wildlife communication network that is involved in data communication and applied EBES over the opportunistic routing protocols such as EBR, Spray&Wait, and Epidemic resulting in a network lifetime increase of 35% and improving the routing protocols performance. Additionally, we compared EBES with the other well-known energy balancing techniques that also contribute to data communication such as EA-Epidemic, EERPFAnt, and OE-OLSR and the results show the remaining energy was improved by 31%, 26%, and 15%, respectively.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73838-2.

Introduction

Energy scarcity poses a significant challenge in the lifespan of mobile wireless networks, primarily due to the prevalent use of battery-powered devices. The lifespan of a network is the duration from the initial deployment of the network until the point when the network can no longer fulfill its intended functions due to energy depletion in a significant number of nodes. To address this issue, substantial research endeavours1 have been dedicated to optimizing energy consumption among mobile agents, thereby extending the overall network longevity. However, these efforts merely defer the inevitable requirement for energy replenishment. To ensure seamless node operation, the concept of energy harvesting2–4 has gained attention, drawing from diverse sources such as vibrations, radio waves, and solar power. Nonetheless, practical implementation remains restricted due to the unreliability of predictions and the typically modest energy yield from harvesting. In a similar vein, while immediate relief can be sought through battery replacement5, its viability is undermined by its high cost and often impractical nature.

Recently, an alternative approach has emerged in the form of mobile chargers, specialized devices boasting significantly greater energy reserves compared to the mobile agents within the network. These innovative chargers autonomously accumulate energy from external sources, subsequently navigating to the locations of mobile agents to periodically transfer energy. While this strategy effectively addresses the perpetual energy requirements of mobile devices, it introduces a host of challenges, including the need for efficient scheduling, and is contingent upon the availability of these specialised devices.

Motivation

To optimize energy management in mobile networks, various studies have been conducted using different approaches such as sleep schedules, harvesting, and cross-layer design5–7. Recently, advancements in wireless power transfer (WPT) technology have made it possible to use wireless charging as a means of supplying energy to mobile nodes to ensure the continuous operation of the networks. Previous research, particularly in the field of wireless sensor networks8–10, has explored the use of mobile chargers (such as robots or unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs)) with large energy reserves to periodically charge sensors in the field. There have also been studies on the wireless charging of various mobile devices, such as smartphones11–13, EVs13–16, and Internet-of-Things (IoT) devices17–20. Most of these studies focus on charging mobile nodes wirelessly or with direct energy from a mobile charger or source. However, there may be situations where it is not possible to use an external charger due to environmental constraints or operational costs. In these cases, peer-to-peer energy sharing, using the resources available between network nodes, may be a useful solution, particularly in wildlife networks, disasters and emergency situations when electricity is scarce. For instance, in wildlife communication networks, animals (mobile nodes) come within each other’s communication range and can exchange data they have collected, such as environmental conditions, wildlife monitoring, and animal behavior analysis in their natural habitats. Hence, per-to-peer energy sharing can ensure continuous operation and data collection in wildlife networks, despite the energy limitations of batteries attached to the individual animals. Such applications are crucial for wildlife monitoring, conservation efforts, and studying animal behavior in their natural habitats. The network formed by animals exhibits the characteristics of an opportunistic network where there is no fixed infrastructure and node may be disconnected at times. This implies that the animals as mobile nodes are not connected together all the time as they may have different mobility patterns. A Note here for clarification; any two nodes are wirelessly connected if they are within radio range of each other but we note that this range for data communication and the range for wireless charging are different (Refer to sections “System description” and “Encounter-based energy sharing (EBES)”).

Related work

To extend the lifespan of such opportunistic network networks, it can be helpful to utilize the resources available among the nodes. One example of this is in the context of mobile social networks, where individuals can use the bidirectional energy-sharing capability of their smartphones (such as the Samsung Galaxy S10 or Huawei Mate 20 Pro) to help charge each other’s devices, a practice known as crowd charging13,21,22. This can be motivated by reciprocal altruism among friends23 or through incentives. Current wireless charging methods, such as the Qi standard22, allow compatible devices to charge their batteries when placed on a Qi charging pad. Although they are effective for short charging distances i.e., up to 4 cm, they are still more convenient than sharing energy through cables or charging devices through outlets. Peer-to-peer energy sharing can also be vital in disaster or emergency situations when energy is scarce. For instance, a group of friends or family members can equally share the available energy in their phone batteries to stay connected longer while searching for a missing member. Additionally, the charging of Internet of Things (IoT) devices can be accomplished through the crowdsourcing of energy from people’s smartphones19,24.

There are several technologies for WPT, including inductive charging, magnetic resonant coupling, and RF charging, each with its own set of benefits and drawbacks25. Many studies have evaluated the potential of WPT for sensor networks by considering the charging schedules of mobile charger nodes that recharge the sensors on a periodic basis. These studies typically involve one-way energy transfer from chargers to sensors. Mobile opportunistic networks have also been examined for bidirectional wireless energy transfer, such as peer-to-peer energy sharing systems for various purposes21. For example, mobile chargers have been used to create energy-efficient and collaborative charging schedules for devices to be crowd charged by other devices. There have also been studies on peer-to-peer wireless energy exchange as a means of balancing energy in mobile opportunistic networks, proposing various energy-sharing protocols26. This process has also been modelled in some studies taking into account the structure and formation of the network and the relationships within online social networks among users23. However, these approaches may result in significant energy loss due to nodes constantly changing their energy levels relative to the average energy in the network, leading to unnecessary energy loss. In13, authors have attempted to address this issue, but they often assume the availability of a central network or path between the source and destination, which may not be applicable in opportunistic networks.

There are also routing protocols that contributes to reduce the energy consumption of the network by choosing the right nodes to relay the traffic such that network lifetime increases and the probability of data delivery also increases. For example, Energy-Aware Epidemic (EA-Epidemic) routing protocol27 was introduced in opportunistic networks. The goal is to increase the probability of message delivery while extending the network lifetime as energy consumption reduces. In this approach, only a node with a higher remaining energy than the sending node’s energy will receive a copy of the message and store it for later transmission to other nodes or the destination node. In another work28, the authors proposed Optimized Energy Optimized Link-State Routing (OE-OLSR), aiming to improve energy management in mobile ad hoc networks. In this approach, nodes with most residual energy will be selected as next hop. The results show that the number of dead nodes can noticeably decrease.

The authors in29 also proposed a new method called Energy Efficient Routing Protocol for opportunistic networks based on Fuzzy Logic and Ant Colony (EERPFAnt), which draws inspiration from ant colony intelligence and is enhanced by the fuzzy logic technique. This method combines the nodes’ energy levels with knowledge about relays that have already received a copy of the message in order to estimate the nodes’ energy levels when they come into contact with the desired destination. However, EERPFAnt uses the Ant and Fuzzy algorithm in each choice for the node, which increases network overhead.

Contribution

As discussed in the previous section, most of the work13,21–24 focuses on the direct sharing of energy with the demanding node. The other work25,26 also considers a centralised network to make an efficient decision on choosing the right node for relaying energy based on the collected information from nodes. This implies that the network is not really opportunistic as the paths can be discovered and the relay nodes can be determined. Therefore, we propose a novel encounter-based energy sharing scheme, called EBES, for peer-to-peer wireless power transfer in wildlife networks30 that aims to prolong the lifespan of the network and minimize power transfer loss. In this network the contacts are opportunistic and the network topology is highly dynamic. Our approach takes advantage of the observation that if there is a demand for power, nodes across the network can contribute to the transfer of power directly or through multi-hops to that node. For multi-hops, we suggest selecting the best hop based on encounter rate, as a node with a high encounter rate is more likely to meet the destination and balance energy in a populated area with minimal energy loss due to the smaller number of relays. In addition, we consider the minimum efficiency required to be met before transferring power in each contact. Indeed, the duration of contact will play a very important role in the amount of energy that can be transferred, and our solution will consider different contact durations in the opportunistic networks.

The main contributions of our work are:

Unlike existing multi-hop approaches, our solution does not rely on predetermined paths that may not be applicable in opportunistic networks. Problem formulation

The objective is to simultaneously maximise the network lifetime and minimise the total energy loss. The network lifetime can be increased by balancing the energy amongst m nodes and minimising energy loss when the number of hops is minimised. Subject to the following constraints:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{ll} \left[ {C1} \right]&\quad {\upbeta _{i,j}}\left( t \right) \geq \gamma ;\quad 0 \leq {\upbeta _{i,j}}\left( t \right) \leq 1 \\ \left[ {C2} \right] &\quad {R_{{u_i},{u_j}}}\left( t \right) \leq D \\ \left[ {C3} \right] &\quad \varepsilon \times T<{E_{budget}} \end{array}$$\end{document}

Constraint [C1] ensures that the energy efficiency meets a minimum threshold, while constraint [C2] determines the maximum distance between nodes for efficient charging. Lastly, constraint [C3] ensures that the maximum energy transmitted does not exceed the energy budget of the node.

Encounter-based energy sharing (EBES)

An energy-sharing scheme aims to extend the network’s lifespan while minimizing energy loss during distribution. However, existing schemes in the literature primarily focus on maximizing network lifespan without considering energy loss. Furthermore, there is no literature that addresses a fully opportunistic network without a connected graph at a given time. Previous schemes assume the presence of a centralized network for exchanging information.

In response, we propose Encounter-based Energy Sharing (EBES) to maximize network lifespan while minimizing energy loss, taking advantage of the mobility patterns of animals in a network. EBES considers the predicted future rate of animal encounters, which can be approximated using past data. In other words, many animals exhibit consistent behavioral patterns based on factors such as habitat, seasonal migrations, and daily routines. By analyzing historical movement data, these patterns can be identified and used to predict future movements. This property is useful because animals that encounter others frequently are more likely to directly connect with more animals than those that do so infrequently. Many networks exhibit this phenomenon, such as disaster recovery networks where ambulances and police may be more mobile and connect clusters more than civilians, and wildlife networks where certain animals take popular routes.

EBES limits power transfer to a select group of encountered animals based on their encounter rate, resulting in less energy loss due to fewer hops in power transfer. This means that transferring power to animals with high encounter rates leads to a higher probability of encountering many animals, resulting in the charging of more nodes with fewer hops.

In EBES, information about animal’s encounter rate is a purely local metric and can be tracked using a small number of variables, resulting in low state overhead complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O\left(n\right)$$\end{document} where n represents the total number of animals participating in the energy sharing network. In other words, each animal tracks its encounter rate with other animals that involves simple updates and comparisons, which are linear with respect to the number of animals. Also, when two nodes come within range, the decision on how much energy to transfer is based on their respective encounter rates. This decision process is also linear with the number of animals involved in each encounter. EBES also has the advantage of simple, easy-to-understand and implement energy-sharing rules, unlike many other schemes in the literature. This minimizes the risk of bugs and reduces computational complexity, such as the resources required in terms of CPU cycles to operate the scheme.

Algorithm

Every node running EBES for energy sharing is responsible for maintaining their average past encounter rate, which is used to predict future encounter rates. When two animals, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}_{i}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}_{j}$$\end{document}, come within the range i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{{u}_{i},{u}_{j}}\le D$$\end{document}, the relative ratio of their respective encounter rates determines the appropriate fraction of energy to be transmitted. In fact, the relative ratio refers to the proportionate comparison of the encounter rates of two nodes (or animals) when they come into contact with each other to make a decision on the amount of energy to be transmitted. The primary purpose of tracking the encounter rate is to intelligently decide how much power a node should transfer during a contact opportunity. The contact duration of the nodes also plays a role in this decision, but it does not necessarily mean that the maximum amount of energy should be transferred if the contact is long enough.

To track a node’s encounter rate, every node maintains two pieces of local information: an encounter rate (ER) and a current encounter counter (CEC). ER represents the animal’s past encounter rate as an exponentially weighted moving average, while CEC provides information about the number of encounters in the current time interval. The update interval defines the frequency at which ER is updated with the most recent CEC values. Hence, ER is periodically updated to reflect the most recent CEC, and updates are calculated as follows:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ER=\alpha\times CEC+\left(1-\alpha\right)\times ER$$\end{document}

The exponentially weighted moving average places an emphasis proportional to alpha \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left(\alpha\right)$$\end{document} on the most recent complete CEC. In other words, a higher value gives more weight to recent encounters that makes the encounter rate more responsive to recent changes in animal behavior. On the other hand, a lower value gives more weight to the historical encounter rate that makes the encounter rate more stable and less sensitive to short-term fluctuations. To update CEC, it is simply incremented for each encounter. When the current encounter update interval has expired, the encounter value is updated and CEC is reset to zero. In our experiments, we found that an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} of 0.85 and an update interval of around 30 s resulted in reasonable results in a variety of networks.

The animal with the high ER is more likely to encounter other animals in a given time interval. When two animals meet, they compare their ERs and the power transferred during the interaction is proportional to the ratio of their ERs. In other words, the animal with the higher ER will transfer more energy to the animal node with the lower ER. In the following, we consider the impact of EBES when different opportunistic routing protocols are in use. The reason for such comparison is that each of these routing protocols has different dissemination rate of data messages across the network that causes the battery consumption of nodes to be different. For example, Spray&Wait (S&W)32 forwards n copies of a message to the first n encountered nodes. This implies that if the source nodes are located in high density areas, the replicas are quickly disseminated. This causes nodes in high density areas to experience quicker battery drain due to more data communication compared to those in remote areas. Encounter-Based Routing (EBR)33, as another routing protocol, instructs nodes to disseminate a limited number of replicas to other nodes that have a higher encounter rate compared to the sender. Each node calculates and maintains its encounter rate by recording the frequency of its contacts with other nodes. When a source node encounters another node with a higher encounter rate, it follows the EBR protocol by forwarding a replica of the message to that node, thereby increasing the chances of successful delivery. Hence, similar to Spray&Wait, nodes in high density areas will experience quicker battery drain as they are engaged in data communication more compared to those in remote/low density areas. Epidemic34 is another routing protocol whereby every encountered node receives a copy of message. This implies that after a while, most of the nodes in the network will have a copy of message. This imposes a massive overhead to the networks and most of the nodes will run out of the battery power quickly. Figure 2 illustrates that the energy consumption of the original versions of S&W, EBR, and Epidemic protocols, as well as when EBES is used (S&W-EBES, EBR-EBES, and Epidemic-EBES), with 300 nodes, has decreased. For example, when EBES was applied for energy sharing, EBR and S&W had up to 25% better average energy for the relayed nodes. This was more noticeable for Epidemic where applying EBES gives much more energy to the relayed nodes in high density areas. It should be noted logically, nodes in high density areas have more likelihood to come within communication ranges of the destination nodes. That is why by sharing the energy with nodes in high density areas, EBES improved the network performance such as delivery ration, and delay.

Figures 4 and 5 depict the average remaining energy and number of dead nodes when the data messages have varying Time-To-Live (TTL) i.e., from 6 h to 72 h. TTL is a mechanism that limits the lifespan of a message in the network before being discarded. It is used to prevent messages from circulating indefinitely, which would otherwise lead to network congestion and excessive resource consumption. A short TTL limits the number of nodes that will receive and forward the message. This reduces network traffic and conserves energy but might result in lower coverage or missed messages. On the other hand, a longer TTL enables the message to live in the network for an extended period, increasing the chances of delivery. However, this can lead to higher energy consumption and potential network congestion. The results in Fig. 4 support the above analysis. The buffer size of a node in a network impacts its energy consumption. Larger buffers can lead to higher power consumption simply to maintain the data in memory. On the other hand, Smaller buffers might need to flush (send or discard) data more frequently to avoid overflow, leading to increased transmission and processing activities, which can also consume more energy. Figure 7 shows that with smaller buffer sizes, all protocols maintain relatively high average remaining energy that is due to the small volume of transmissions. As the buffer size increases, the remaining energy decreases for all protocols, but the rate of decline is significantly steeper for the Epidemic protocol. This indicates that larger buffers lead to higher energy consumption, particularly for protocols with less efficient message management. In Epidemic, the steep decline in remaining energy with increasing buffer size can be attributed to the high frequency of message forwarding and the overhead associated with maintaining larger buffers. EBR and S&W have more efficient message management strategies, leading to more controlled and reduced energy consumption even with larger buffers. The introduction of EBES provides a slight improvement in energy efficiency across all protocols. This suggests that EBES fairly balances the energy in high density areas and in such cases, nodes in high density areas with large buffer space can be more efficient.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this work explores the concept of peer-to-peer energy sharing in an opportunistic wildlife communication network, where nodes can share energy wirelessly as they come into range. The inefficiency of wireless charging technologies results in energy loss during this process. The goal of this study is to find a way to balance the energy among the nodes and minimize energy loss. To achieve this, we propose a novel encounter-based energy sharing scheme, called EBES, that utilizes single and multi-hop transmission to achieve energy balance, minimize energy losses, and maximize the lifetime of opportunistic wildlife communication networks. EBES is applied to various routing protocols to redistribute energy from nodes that are less likely to serve as relay nodes to those located in high-density areas. These high-density nodes have a greater chance of encountering other nodes, particularly the destination node, because their frequent interactions within the network increase their likelihood of crossing paths with a wider variety of nodes. As they regularly connect with many different nodes, they are more likely to eventually encounter the destination node, thereby enhancing the chances of successful message delivery. Simulation studies demonstrate that the EBES method significantly reduces the number of dead nodes and increases the average remaining energy compared to other energy consumption methods. While EBES is not a routing protocol, it outperforms energy-efficient routing protocols in terms of preserving node energy and extending network lifespan. Moreover, EBES is used in conjunction with other routing protocols which may not be energy efficient in order to maximize the performance of those protocols.
