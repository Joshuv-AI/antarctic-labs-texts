The utilization of N-acetylgalactosamine and its effect on the metabolism of amino acids in Erysipelotrichaceae strain
Authors: Mengqing Zhou, Jinyuan Wu, Lin Wu, Xiao Sun, Congying Chen, Lusheng Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe metabolism of gut microbiota produces bioactive metabolites that modulate host physiology and promote self-growth. Erysipelotrichaceae is one of the most common anaerobic microorganism families in the gut, which has been discovered to play a vital role in host metabolic disorders and inflammatory diseases. Our previous study found that N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) in caecal content of pigs significantly affected the abundance of Erysipelotrichaceae strains. However, it remains unknown how GalNAc feeding in vitro culture affects the expression levels of genes in the GalNAc metabolic pathway and the concentrations of intermediate metabolites in the Erysipelotrichaceae strain. Whether GalNAc feeding should influence the metabolism of other nutrients, such as amino acids, remains unrevealed.ResultsIn this study, whole-genome sequence, transcriptome, and metabolome data were analyzed to assess the utilization of a Erysipelotrichaceae strain on GalNAc. The results showed the presence of a complete GalNAc catabolism pathway in the genome of this Erysipelotrichaceae strain. GalNAc feeding to this Erysipelotrichaceae strain significantly changed the expression levels of genes involved in glycolysis and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Meanwhile, the concentrations of lactate, pyruvate, citrate, succinate and malate from the glycolysis and TCA cycle were significantly increased. In addition, transcriptome analysis indicated that the genes involved in the metabolism of amino acids were affected by GalNAc, including lysA (a gene involved in lysine biosynthesis) that was significantly down-regulated. The intracellular concentrations of 14 amino acids in the Erysipelotrichaceae strain were significantly increased after feeding GalNAc.ConclusionsOur findings comfirmed and extended our previous works that demonstrated the utilization of GalNAc by Erysipelotrichaceae strain, and explained the possible mechanism of GalNAc affecting the abundance of Erysipelotrichaceae strain in vitro.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-024-03505-z.

Background

Erysipelotrichaceae belongs to Firmicutes and is a kind of Gram-positive, slender or filamentous rods [1]. This family was originally considered as facultatively anaerobic and microaerophilic organisms, but later classified into obligate (strictly) anaerobic microbes [2–4]. Many studies have found that the bacteria from Erysipelotrichaceae are involved in host metabolic disorders and inflammatory diseases. For example, higher abundances of Erysipelotrichaceae in the gut are associated with obesity, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and colorectal cancer in humans [5–7]. More recently, Miyauchi et al. observed that, similar to the effect of an adjuvant, Erysipelotrichaceae bacteria play their roles in enhancing the pathogenicity of myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG)-specific T-helper 17 (Th17) cells via inducing pro-inflammatory factors serum amyloid A (SAA) and interleukin (IL)-23 in the small intestine. Activated Th17 cells migrated into the spinal cord and induced demyelination [8]. In addition, our previous studies have reported that the abundance of Erysipelotrichaceae strains in the gut was regulated by the ABO blood type which determined the concentration of N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) in the intestinal contents [9]. Whole-genome sequence analysis of five Erysipelotrichaceae strains revealed their capacities for metabolizing carbohydrate substrates and utilizing plant polysaccharides [10]. Moreover, the medium containing sheep serum and liver extracts which provided a large amount of protein, lipid, and certain growth factors was more suitable for the isolation and culture of Erysipelotrichaceae strains in vitro [10]. However, most of these studies have more focused on their potential functional capacities based on its genome. Whether and how Erysipelotrichaceae strains catabolize carbohydrate substrates are largely unknown and need to be investigated by experiments.

GalNAc is an amino sugar derivative of galactose and the terminal carbohydrate forming the antigen of blood group A in humans [11, 12]. It is also the first monosaccharide linking to serine or threonine in a specialized form of protein O-glycosylation that is necessary for intercellular communication [13]. In bacteria, GalNAc is a major component of the peptidoglycan, capsular polysaccharides and outer membrane lipopolysaccharides [14]. It supports the growth of Escherichia coli as carbon source via GalNAc metabolic pathway [15]. In recent years, the pathway catabolizing GalNAc has also been found in a small number of bacteria, such as Bacillus subtilis, Streptomycetes coelicolor and Erysipelotrichaceae [10, 16, 17]. In particular, our previous studies reported that the GalNAc metabolism pathway was enriched in the genomes of all five novel isolates of Erysipelotrichaceae from commercial pigs and 16 Erysipelotrichaceae strains downloaded from GenBank [10]. Erysipelotrichaceae strains could catabolise GalNAc, and GalNAc concentration in the intestinal content could significantly affect the abundance of gut Erysipelotrichaceae strains [9]. However, how GalNAc feeding changes the expression levels of genes involved in the GalNAc metabolism and shifts the concentrations of intermediate metabolites of GalNAc during the culture, and whether GalNAc feeding influences the metabolism of amino acids have previously been unknown.

In this study, we selected an Erysipelotrichaceae strain (named 4-8-110) showing 100% of sequence similarity of the V3-V4 region of 16S rRNA gene with the OTU476 which was affected by the GalNAc concentration in the gut content [9] as the experimental microbe. We combined the data of whole-genome sequencing, transcriptome, and metabolome from the GalNAc feeding experiment to address the issues described above. We found that GalNAc was catabolized by the Erysipelotrichaceae strain. GalNAc feeding significantly changed the gene expression levels and the concentrations of intermediate metabolites in the GalNAc metabolic pathway. Moreover, GalNAc feeding also affected the concentration of amino acids in Erysipelotrichaceae strain cells.

Methods

Bacterial strains and culture

The Erysipelotrichaceae strain (named 4-8-110) used in this study was isolated from fresh feces of pigs and anaerobically stored with 20% (v/v) glycerol in an -80 °C freezer. The detailed procedures for isolation and culture of the Erysipelotrichaceae strain (4-8-110) were described in our previous study [10]. In this study, the GalNAc solution (1.0 g/L) was filtered with 0.22-µm filter membrane. The Erysipelotrichaceae strain was cultured in Gifu anaerobic medium (GAM) broth containing 22.9% digested serum and 2.0% liver extract [18] (Nissui Pharmaceutical, Japan) either with a final concentration of 1.0 g/L GalNAc (GalNAc group) [9] or without adding GalNAc (control group) at 37 °C in an anaerobic chamber (Electrotek, UK) under an atmosphere of 80% nitrogen, 10% hydrogen, and 10% carbon dioxide. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation (1,500 ×g, 5 min) at 13 and 24 h of culture. The cultures for each of GalNAc and control groups were repeated in triplicate for each time point. All manipulations with Erysipelotrichaceae strain were performed in the anaerobic chamber which was treated by UV for at least 30 min before the experiment, and the mediums and reagents had been submitted to consume oxygen for at least 48 h before use.

Targeted measurements of GalNAc metabolites and amino acids in Erysipelotrichaceae strain cells by gas chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (GC–MS)

A total of 200 µL of liquid inoculum (4.84 × 106 CFU) of Erysipelotrichaceae strain was inoculated into 6 mL of GAM medium adding 1.0 g/L or 0 g/L GalNAc in an anaerobic chamber (Electrotek, UK). Three repeats of cultures in each group were used for each time point (a total of 3 × 2 × 2 cultures). After 13 h and 24 h of culture, bacterial cells were harvested via centrifugation at 1,500 ×g for 5 minutes. We used GC-MS to measure the concentrations of intermediate metabolites of GalNAc and amino acids in Erysipelotrichaceae strain cells. In brief, bacterial cells were resuspended in 0.6 mL of 50% cold (-40°C) aqueous methanol containing 100 µM n-valine as an internal standard, and bathed in dry ice for 30 min. After that, the samples were thawed on ice. A total of 0.4-mL chloroform was added to the mixture. After vortexed for 30 sec, the mixture was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm (4°C) for 10 minutes. The supernatant was transferred to a new 1.5-mL tube for evaporation and stored at -80°C until analysis. Derivatized products were analyzed by a GC-MS following the procesure: (i) 70 µL of pyridine was added to the dried pellet and incubated at 80°C for 20 min. (ii) After cooling, 30 µL of N-tert-butyldimethylsilyl-N-methyltrifluoroacetamide (Sigma, USA) was added. (iii) the mixture was re-incubated at 80°C for 60 min, and then centrifugated at 14,000 rpm (4°C) for 10 min. Finally, the supernatant was transferred to autosampler vials for GC/MS analysis on a QP-2010 Ultra GC-MS (Shimadzu, Japan) with GC column (20 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 mm Rxi’-5ms). The injection temperature was set at 250℃ and the injection volume of supernatant was 1µL. The parameters were set as follows: GC oven temperature started at 110 °C for 4 min, raised to 230 °C at 3 °C/min, to 280 °C at 20 °C/min, and held at 280 °C for 2 min. The GC flow rate of helium carrier gas was constant at 50 cm/s. The temperatures of GC-MS interface and (electron impact) ion source were set at 330 °C and 200 °C, respectively. The ion source was set at ionization voltage of 70 V. The scan range was 50–800 m/z with 1 kV detector. Quantities of metabolites were determined by GC-MS analysis. Metabolites with baseline separated peaks were quantified based on total ion count peak area using a standard curve, which was obtained from internal standards running with the same batch of samples [19, 20].

RNA extraction, sequencing and data analysis

Bacterial cells were cultured in GAM medium with (1.0 g/L) or without (0 g/L) GalNAc for 13 h. Three biological replicates were prepared for each of two experimental groups. Bacterial cells were then harvested by centrifugation at 7,000 rpm for 3 min at 4 °C. After rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, the pellets were stored at -80 °C until RNA extraction. Total RNA of bacterial cells was extracted with TRIzol® reagent (Thermofisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Genomic DNA contamination was removed by DNase I treatment (TaKara, China). Total RNA was eluted in nuclease-free water (Thermofisher Scientific, USA). The quantification and quality of total RNA were measured by an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA) and NanoDrop-2000 (Thermofisher Scientific, USA). The RNA samples with high-quality (OD260/280 = 1.8 ~ 2.0, OD260/230 ≥ 2.0, RIN ≥ 6.5, and 28 S:18 S ≥ 1.0) were used for RNA sequencing. The procedures of RNA sequencing were described in our previous study [9]. In brief, RNA sequencing libraries was constructed with 2 µg of total RNA using TruSeq RNA sample preparation Kit (Illumina, USA) according to manufacturer’s protocols. The constructed libraries were then sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 2 × 150 bp paired-end strategy. Illumina GA Pipeline (v.1.6) (Illumina USA) was used for base calling and quality value calculations. The raw data were first cleaned by clipping the adapter sequences, trimming low-quality reads (base quality score less than Q20), removing non-A, G, C, T bases of the 5′ end, and filtering fragments (more than 10% of “N” bases or less than 25 bp in length). Bowtie2 (v2.4.2) [21] was then used to align clean reads to the whole-genome of Erysipelotrichaceae strain (4-8-110), and FeatureCounts (v1.6.4) was used to calculate the sequence read counts that were mapped to the whole-genome. Finally, the FPKM (Fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads) method was used to calculate the expression level of each gene, and DESeq2 [22] was used to detect the differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Identifying the genes involved in the GalNAc metabolism in the genome of experimental Erysipelotrichaceae strain

The whole-genome sequence of the Erysipelotrichaceae strain 4-8-110 was obtained from our previous studies [9, 10]. We used prodigal (v2.6.3) [23] and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) to annotate the genome. As described in our previous study [9], the genes involved in the catabolism of GalNAc were selected according to the previous references [24–30]. To identify all genes involved in the utilization/metabolism of GalNAc as carbon source, we also referred to genes related to Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis (Map00010) and Citrate cycle (TCA cycle) (Map00020) in the KEGG (https://www.genome.jp/kegg).

Statistical analysis

We used the Student’s t-test to compare the concentrations of 10 intermediate metabolites of GalNAc metabolism, including metabolites in glycolysis and TCA cycle, and 16 amino acids including essential and non-essential amino acids in Erysipelotrichaceae strain cells between 1.0 g/L GalNAc and control groups.

Results

Genes involved in the catabolism of GalNAc to produce lactate were detected in the genome of experimental Erysipelotrichaceae strain

Carbohydrate metabolism, the basis of many essential metabolism pathways including glycolysis, citric acid cycle and pentose phosphate pathway, is a biochemical process including metabolic formation, breakdown, and interconversion of carbohydrates [31, 32]. The catabolism pathway of GalNAc in the experimental Erysipelotrichaceae strain was reconstructed based on the whole-genome sequence obtained from our previous studies [9, 10]. The result showed that a total of 15 genes are involved in the catabolism of GalNAc to produce lactate in Erysipelotrichaceae strain, including four genes (AgaV, AgaW, AgaE, and AgaF) for agaPTS system (Fig. 1), one gene for each of five steps from the import of GalNAc into bacterial cells to the production of metabolic intermediates Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (GAP) and Dihydroxyacetone-phosphate (DHAP), and one gene for the interconversion between GAP and DHAP (Triosephosphate isomerase (TPI)). The genome of this Erysipelotrichaceae strain was also comprised of genes encoding glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK), 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate-independent phosphoglycerate mutase (gpmI), enolase (ENO), pyruvate kinase (PK), and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), which further catabolize GAP/DHAP to 1,3-phosphoglycerate (1,3-PG), 3-phosphoglycerate (3PG), 2-phosphoglycerate (2PG), phosphoenol-pyruvate (PEP), pyruvate (PYR), and lactate. This indicated a potential capacity of this Erysipelotrichaceae strain for lactate production via the catabolism pathway of GalNAc, which represented the capacity to metabolize GalNAc to provide energy for its growth.

Changes in the expression levels of genes related to glycolysis, TCA cycle, and amino acid metabolism in the Erysipelotrichaceae strain fed with 1.0 g/L GalNAc

Our previous study observed that GalNAc feeding significantly increased the expression levels of genes involved in GalNAc catabolism [9]. To further investigate whether GalNAc feeding altered the expression levels of genes involved in glycolysis, TCA cycle, and amino acid metabolism in Erysipelotrichaceae strain, a transcriptome analysis was performed for Erysipelotrichaceae strain cultured in the medium added 0 g/L or 1.0 g/L GalNAc. The results revealed the significant changes in the expression levels of 446 genes in Erysipelotrichaceae strain by feeding 1.0 g/L GalNAc. Among them, 234 genes were upregulated and 212 genes were downregulated (|Fold change| ≥ 1 and P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). More details, the genes involved in glycolysis were upregulated by GalNAc. A total of eight DEGs involved in the TCA cycle were identified between GalNAc feeding and control groups (ranging from 1.03-fold to 1.84-fold), including CS, pckA, ACO, por, IDH3, fumA, fumB, and frdA (Fig. 2B). Among them, four genes were upregulated, and the other four gene were downregulated. We identified 21 genes related to amino acid biosynthesis and degradation in the Erysipelotrichaceae strain showing differential expression levels (Table S1). Among them, 12 genes were upregulated (1.1 ~ 2.5-fold) and nine genes were downregulated (1.1 ~ 1.8-fold) (Fig. 2C). These results indicated that the feeding of 1.0 g/L GalNAc influenced multiple metabolism pathways and altered the expression levels of genes involved in glycolysis, TCA cycle, and amino acid metabolism.

The concentrations of intermediate metabolites of GalNAc metabolism in Erysipelotrichaceae strain

We further determined and compared the concentrations of intermediate metabolites in the GalNAc catabolism pathway by designing a in vitro feeding experiment. Erysipelotrichaceae strain cells were collected from GAM medium supplemented with 1.0 g/L or 0 g/L GalNAc at 13 and 24 h of culture. The concentrations of 10 intermediate metabolites involved in GalNAc metabolism were measured by targeted GC-MS, including GAP, DHAP, 3PG, pyruvate, lactate, citrate, alpha-ketoglutarate (α-KG), succinate, fumarate, and malate. These metabolites included the products of GalNAc catabolism and TCA cycle. Compared with the 0 g/L GalNAc group, the supplementation of 1.0 g/L GalNAc significantly increased the concentration of DHAP, pyruvate, lactate, citrate, succinate, and malate (P < 0.05) at 13 h (the logarithmic growth phase). The same tendencies were also observed on GAP (P = 0.063), 3PG (P = 0.066), and α-KG (P = 0.094) although their increased level did not achieve significance level (Fig. 3, Table S3). At 24 h of feeding culture, there was no significant difference in the concentrations of all 10 intermediate metabolites between 1.0 g/L GalNAc and 0 g/L GalNAc group. But we observed a significant decrease in the concentrations of pyruvate, 3PG, lactate, citrate, α-KG, succinate, and malate (P < 0.05, except for 3PG (P = 0.057)) in the 1.0 g/L GalNAc group from 13 h to 24 h. In the 0 g/L GalNAc group, the significant decreases were only found in the concentrations of citrate, α-KG, succinate, and malate that were involved in TCA cycle (P < 0.05). Particularly, the average concentration of citrate was significantly decreased from 90.20 to 65.07 nmol/mg in the control group (P = 0.007), and from 157.64 to 69.84 nmol/mg in the 1.0 g/L GalNAc group (P = 0.019). A decreased concentration of α-KG was also observed from 13 h to 24 h in the control group, it decreased from 5.60 nmol/mg to 3.71 nmol/mg (P = 0.026), and from 9.83 nmol/mg to 3.54 nmol/mg (P = 0.039) in the 1.0 g/L GalNAc group. Significantly decreased concentrations of most intermediate metabolites from 13 h to 24 h of the feeding culture indicated that GalNAc in the medium had been depleted. As we expected, the growth of the experimental Erysipelotrichaceae strain started to enter the decline phase at 24 h (Fig. S1).

GalNAc feeding significantly affected the concentration of amino acids in Erysipelotrichaceae strain cells

Supplementation of 1.0 g/L GalNAc in culture medium significantly increased the concentrations of metabolites related to glycolysis and TCA cycle. Whether it should also influence the metabolism of other nutrients in this Erysipelotrichaceae strain has been yet unknown. Here, we focused on the effect of GalNAc feeding on the concentrations of intracellular essential amino acids (EAAs) and non-essential amino acids (non-EAAs), the two important components of basic nutrients. We first analyzed the intracellular levels of EAAs including lysine, phenylalanine, methionine, and isoleucine. Compared to the control group (0 g/L GalNAc), the supplementation of 1.0 g/L GalNAc significantly increased the intracellular concentrations of phenylalanine, methionine, and isoleucine at 13 h of feeding culture (an average of 1.8 folds). However, the concentration of lysine was slightly decreased (P = 0.084) (Fig. 4, Table S2). The concentrations of all eight EAAs were significantly decreased from 13 h to 24 h of feeding culture in both control and GalNAc supplementation groups. It was no strange because amino acids were expended following the time of culture.

The similar results were also observed in non-EAAs including glycine, alanine, proline, tyrosine, serine, glutamate, cysteine, and aspartate. The intracellular concentrations of alanine, proline, tyrosine, serine, glutamate, cysteine, and aspartate were increased about 1.7-fold at 13 h of feeding culture, and about 1.2-fold at 24 h of culture time in the group adding 1.0 g/L GalNAc. However, the intracellular concentration of glycine was slightly decreased in the GalNAc feeding group. The concentrations of all non-EAAs were decreased following the culture time (from 13 h to 24 h). All these results suggested that GalNAc feeding influenced the concentrations of amino acids in Erysipelotrichaceae strain cells.

Discussion

Erysipelotrichaceae is considered as key commensal bacteria inhabiting in the gut. Specific taxa within Erysipelotrichaceae are correlated with host inflammatory gastrointestinal diseases and metabolism [7, 10, 33, 34]. Therefore, it is important to determine their metabolic characteristics of these organisms. Carbon source is essential nutrients for the growth and metabolism of Erysipelotrichaceae strains. Previous studies have confirmed that GalNAc is a carbon source promoting the growth of Erysipelotrichaceae strains [9, 35]. In this study, we also found a complete GalNAc catabolism pathway in the genome of Erysipelotrichaceae strain, including glycolysis and TCA cycle. In addition, feeding experiment confirmed the metabolic activity of Erysipelotrichaceae strain on GalNAc, and identified the altered amino acid levels of Erysipelotrichaceae strain cells by combining transcriptome and targeted metabolome analyses.

Feeding experiment revealed that 1.0 g/L GalNAc significantly increased the concentrations of several metabolites involved in the glycolysis and TCA cycle in this Erysipelotrichaceae strain at 13 h of culture. In particular, high levels of lactate, pyruvate, citrate, succinate and malate were identified, of which lactate had the highest concentration. In cellular hexoses metabolism, such as the metabolism of fructose, mannose, and glucose, glycolysis is a common pathway for energy generation that provided energy for host physiological activities. Therefore, we hypothesized that the high concentrations of pyruvate and lactate could be caused by GalNAc feeding. It is generally believed that glycolysis and TCA cycle are regarded as two major metabolic pathways for energy production [36]. Lactate and pyruvate are known to be the glycolytic end products and a substrate for the TCA cycle. Citrate, succinate and malate are important metabolites of TCA cycle [37, 38]. We reasoned that GalNAc could efficiently promote energy production, therefore improved the growth of the Erysipelotrichaceae strain. As a proof, the expression levels of genes involved in glycolysis was significantly upregulated by GalNAc, which was also consistent with our previous findings [9]. Furthermore, we found that the expressions of several key genes involved in TCA cycle were significantly changed by 1.0 g/L of GalNAc. In our previous study, Erysipelotrichaceae strains exhibited high biomass accumulation when the concentration of GalNAc was increased in caecal content of pigs [9].

GalNAc feeding experiment first identified the effect of GalNAc on the intracellular concentrations of amino acids in Erysipelotrichaceae strain cells. The intracellular concentrations of 14 amino acids were significantly increased after feeding GalNAc. Amino acids play an important role in the metabolism of bacterial cells [39]. Previous studies suggested that amino acids should support bacterial survival and growth in the gastrointestinal tract, and regulated protein homeostasis and energy balance [40–42]. It has been reported that amino acids can directly enter cells as protein building blocks, or be catabolized and utilized after being absorbed by bacterial cells [43, 44]. These amino acids can be converted to intermediate metabolites, such as pyruvate and alpha-ketoglutarate that participate in the TCA cycle to provide energy through specific metabolic pathways [45]. Amino acids can also be metabolized to produce short chain fatty acids and branched chain fatty acids [46]. It has been reported that bacterial metabolic networks can respond to the changes in the external environments [47], and the changes in node metabolites might activate or inhibit other metabolic pathways, resulting in fluctuations in related metabolites [48, 49]. By transcriptome analysis, we observed that the genes involved in the metabolism of amino acids were affected by GalNAc, including lysA (a gene involved in lysine biosynthesis) that was significantly down-regulated. Because of the interconversion between amino acids, for example, phenylalanine can be converted to tyrosine and tryptophan, it is thus difficult to describe the role of those genes regulated by GalNAc in amino acids metabolism. However, from the results obtained in this study, the increased concentration of intracellular amino acids should be caused by inhabiting the conversion or catabolism of amino acids by GalNAc feeding.

GalNAc is an important component of mammalian gastrointestinal mucins [50], and the ability to utilize GalNAc should facilitate the Erysipelotrichaceae strain to colonize in the mammalian gut. ABO, as an acetylgalactosaminyltransferase, influence the concentration of GalNAc in intestinal content, which further affects the abundance of Erysipelotrichaceae strains [9]. Interestingly, GalNAc metabolic pathway has also been reported in Escherichia coli [25], but we did not observe the significant effect of ABO genotypes on the abundance of Escherichia coli in our previous study [9]. By combining the results obtained in this study, we speculated that GalNAc not only provides energy for the growth of Erysipelotrichaceae strain, but also affects the concentrations of amino acids in the Erysipelotrichaceae strains affected by ABO genotypes. Whether GalNAc could also affect the intracellular levels of amino acids in E. coli or not would need to be investigated in the future. Furthermore, Erysipelotrichaceae has been reported to be related to human lipid metabolism, atherosclerosis, and intestinal inflammation [7, 51, 52]. The results from this study may not only provide basic knowledge of the molecular mechanism and functional roles of GalNAc in host phenotypes (diseases) through regulating Erysipelotrichaceae, but also give a potential target for the therapy of human health and diseases.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that GalNAc feeding changed the expression levels of genes involved in glycolysis, TCA cycle, and the metabolism of amino acids. The concentrations of intermediate metabolites of GalNAc metabolic pathway were also significantly increased by GalNAc feeding, suggesting the utilization of GalNAc by the Erysipelotrichaceae strain. In addition, we first found the influence of GalNAc on the intracellular concentrations of amino acids. The results from this study provide another evidence about the ability of utilization of GalNAc by Erysipelotrichaceae strain, and explained the possible mechanism of GalNAc affecting the abundance of Erysipelotrichaceae strains.
