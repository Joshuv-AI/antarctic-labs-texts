Loss of energy homeostasis contributes to hepatic damage development in sickle cell disease
Authors: Lucía Beltrán-Camacho, Mercedes Vallejo-Mudarra, Isabel Pozuelo-Sánchez, Cristina García-Caballero, Marta Rojas-Torres, Iolanda Lazaro, Aleix Sala-Vila, Leo Valentín-Aragón, Javier Caballero-Villarraso, Lucas Opazo-Rios, Fernando Leiva-Cepas, Sebastián Mas-Fontao, Jesús Egido, José Manuel Villalba, Ma Carmen Durán-Ruiz, Juan Antonio Moreno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSickle cell disease (SCD) is characterized by the expression of an abnormal hemoglobin variant (HbS) that promotes distortion and early destruction of red blood cells, resulting in hemolytic anemia, vaso-occlusive crisis, ischemia and, ultimately, tissue damage. Hepatic function is specially compromised in SCD patients; however, the underlying pathological mechanisms remain largely unknown. In the current study, we confirmed the presence of hepatic damage in a murine model of SCD and, through a label free quantitative proteomic approach, we identified significant alterations in protein expression compared to healthy controls. These changes unveiled distinct proteome expression profiles between groups, with molecular alterations linked to impaired hepatic function, anemia, mitochondrial dysfunction, and alteration in lipid metabolism. We also confirmed these novel alterations through molecular and functional analyses, revealing a previously undescribed liver energy homeostasis imbalance, accompanied by accumulation of foam cells. Our findings provide new insights into the complex mechanisms underlying liver disease and potential therapeutic targets in SCD.Keywords: Sickle cell disease, Liver, Hepatic damage, Lipid metabolism, Mitochondrial disorder

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is an inherited blood disorder characterized by an abnormal hemoglobin (Hb) expression. Patients with SCD have a mutation in the β-globin gene, resulting in misshape and early destruction of red blood cells (RBCs). The abnormal form of Hb, so-called HbS and expressed instead of healthy HbA, suffers polymerization when exposed to prolonged deoxygenation, producing the sickle form of RBCs [[1], [2], [3]]. According to Global Burden of Disease, SCD affected almost 8 million people worldwide in 2021, being among the top 20 causes of death in children under 5 years [4]. Clinically, patients suffer hemolytic anemia, vaso-occlusive crises and, ultimately, tissue damage. The most common complication is intermittent acute pain, related to ischemia-reperfusion injury and tissue infarction, although chronic pain, caused by nervous system sensitization, is also common [1]. So far, the only curative therapy for these patients is allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, though its use is limited due to lack of suitable donors and significant morbidity [5]. Other therapeutic strategies have been applied for SCD patients, but unfortunately these approaches are only partially effective, so further investigation is still needed [6,7].

SCD can cause injury in multiple organs, with the hepatobiliary system being one of the most affected, which leads to both acute and chronic liver disorders. Complications occurring in this organ fall under the term sickle cell hepatopathy (SCH) and usually start with vascular occlusion and/or hyperbilirubinemia [[8], [9], [10]]. In addition, patients need recurrent transfusions that worsens the situation by iron overload and viral hepatitis [8]. SCH has not been well characterized in part because of the large variety of etiologic factors, leading to overlapping manifestations [11,12]. Moreover, liver biopsy is mostly contraindicated due to bleeding complications or even death [12,13], causing difficulties in diagnosis and limiting SCD clinical investigation.

A better understanding of liver damage mechanisms is crucial to predict, prevent, or even treat complications. For this reason, we have performed, for the first time to our knowledge, a label free-quantitative (LFQ) proteomics approach comparing hepatic tissue from SCD and healthy mice. The differential proteomic expression pattern detected has highlighted the potential involvement of mitochondrial and lipid metabolism disorders in SCD mice, therefore postulating these functions as key pathological mechanisms involved in liver damage.

Methods

Animal model of SCD

HbAA Townes (healthy; n = 12) and HbSS Townes (SCD; n = 11) male mice were used in this study. Mice genotyping was performed using KAPA2G Fast HotStart Genotyping Mix (Merck; KK5621) with specific primers. At 6-months of age, blood was collected, and the mice were sacrificed and perfused with PBS. Then, livers were harvested, dissected and stored according to their further processing: formalin fixed, ethanol dehydrated, and paraffin embedded for histological analysis; formalin fixed, sucrose dehydrated, and then frozen in OCT for lipid droplets (LDs) detection and immunohistochemistry; or snap-frozen and stored at −80 °C for further molecular analysis. Serum samples were obtained after centrifugation in blood collection tubes with coagulation activator (Vacutest; 1522A102) for determination of transaminases and lipid profile using an Atellica IM 1600 Solution analyzer (Siemens Healthineers Diagnostics). The study was performed according to Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament and was approved by the bioethics committee of the University of Córdoba and authorized by the Consejería de Agricultura, Pesca y Desarrollo Rural, Junta de Andalucía, Spain (authorization code: 07/08/2024/120).

Histological analysis

Paraffin-embedded 3 μm tissue sections were examined with hematoxylin & eosin staining for tissue injury evaluation; Sirius Red and Masson's trichrome staining for tissue fibrosis; and Prussian Blue staining for iron deposits detection. OCT-frozen 10 μm tissue sections were stained using Oil Red O (ORO) (0.5% in 60% isopropanol; Sigma; O0625) during 40 min before counterstaining with hematoxylin for LDs detection; and macrophages were detected with anti-F4/80 by immunohistochemistry.

Quantitative-PCR

Gene expression analysis was performed by quantitative-PCR (qPCR) using RNA extracted from whole liver tissue. Total RNA from livers (healthy n = 12 and SCD n = 11) was extracted with Total RNA Purification kit (Norgen Biotek; 17250). Then, RNA was quantified with Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher; ND-1000) and reverse transcribed using a high-capacity cDNA kit (Thermo Fisher; 4368813). Target gene expression was quantified by qPCR on a CFX Connect Real-Time System (Biorad). Cq values were analyzed with Bio-Rad CFX manager software (BioRad). For normalized relative ratio, 18S and GAPDH levels were used as housekeeping genes in TaqMan probe and SYBR assay respectively (Table S1).

Hepatic tissue parameters

The content of specific liver metabolites (healthy n = 12 and SCD n = 11) was measured using commercial kits and following the manufacturer's instructions, namely, heme (Sigma; MAK316), NAD+/NADH (Promega; G9071), ATP (Cayman; 700410), triglycerides (Cayman; 10010303) and cholesterol (Millipore; 428901) levels. All analyses were performed using liver tissue snap-frozen immediately after extraction. Tissue levels of NAD+ and NADH were determined separately. Samples were mechanically homogenized in the extraction buffer (PBS:bicarbonate + 0.5 % DTAB in 1:1 ratio) and maintained on ice throughout the procedure. Homogenate samples (equivalent to 10 μg of protein) were transferred to well plates and mixed with either acidic (for NAD+ measurement) or basic (for NADH measurement) media. After adding the luciferase reagent, plates were incubated at room temperature for 30 min and luminescence was measured using a plate luminometer. The NAD+/NADH ratio was calculated by dividing the levels of each molecule.

Label-free quantitative proteomic analysis

Liver tissue samples (healthy n = 6 and SCD n = 4) were homogenized in liquid nitrogen prior to being denatured, reduced and alkylated by boiling for 10 min in 100 mM Tris buffer (pH 8,5) containing 5% SDS, 5 mM Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phospine, 10 mM chloroacetamide and supplemented with protease inhibitors (Roche; 1697498). Samples were then centrifuged at 10000×g for 5 min, and the protein contained in supernatants was quantified through BCA method (Pierce), following the manufacturer's instructions. For the LFQ assay, proteins were digested overnight following the protein aggregation capture (PAC) protocol [14], with some modifications. Briefly, samples (50 μg) were incubated in 70% acetonitrile with magnetic Amine beads (ReSyn Biosciences) in a protein/bead ratio of 1:2. Samples were sequentially washed and proteins bound to magnetic beads were incubated overnight at 37 °C in agitation using 1:50 Trypsin/LysC Mix (Promega; V5073) in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate solution. Digestion was quenched by acidification with 5% trifluoroacetic acid.

Peptide samples were analyzed on an EvosepOne nanoLC (Evosep) coupled to a TIMSTOF-Flex (Bruker Daltonics). Prior to analysis, the system was calibrated both in mass and ion mobility dimension by using a spiked filter with three reference calibrants (lock masses). A total amount of 200 ng of peptides per sample was loaded in each evotip. The acquisition method used was data independent analysis (DIA) acquisition in PASEF and PaSER mode. For DIA-PASEF method, the mass range was adjusted using PyDIAID package from Mann's Lab [15] from 445.26 m/z to 1247.6 m/z and ion mobility range was set up in two mobility ranges from 0.85 1/K0 to 1.35 1/K0. The collision energy was decreased linearly from 59 eV at 1/K0 = 1.60 Vs cm−2 to 20 eV at 1/K0 = 0.60 Vs cm−2. Spectronaut 16 (version 16.3.221108.53000) proprietary software was used to perform the identification and quantification analysis. A directDIA approach was done using a fasta reference proteome for mouse (Uniprot Mouse February 2022), the enzyme was set to trypsin/LysC and the remaining variables were set using default settings.

Data processing was carried out with Perseus (v2.0.3.1). Briefly, data were log2 transformed, 70% valid values were filtered and missing values were imputed from normal distribution. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were selected when q-value <0.05. Functional analysis was done using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA®, QIAGEN), while potential interactions between altered proteins was analyzed using STRING (https://string-db.org/).

Western blot

Tissue proteins (healthy n = 12 and SCD n = 11) were extracted by mechanical procedure using lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7, containing 1% NP40, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM EDTA, and supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma; PPC1010)). Proteins were quantified with BCA method and 30 μg of lysate were resuspended in Laemmli buffer (50 mM Tris pH 6.8, 10% v/v glycerol, 2% w/v SDS, 0.1% w/v bromophenol blue). Proteins were separated on 8–12% polyacrylamide-SDS gels under reducing conditions and transferred to PVDF membranes, which were then blocked with 5% milk and immunoblotted with primary antibodies (Table S2). After several washes, membranes were incubated with corresponding species-specific secondary antibodies. Images were acquired using ChemiDoc Touch System. Ponceau S staining or GAPDH levels were used as loading controls.

Mitochondrial isolation and respirometry assays

Liver tissue pieces (healthy n = 9 and SCD n = 9) were disrupted with an electric tissue homogenizer (Retsch) in homogenization buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.6, 40 mM KCl, 0.2 M sucrose, 1 mM PMSF, 10 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT and 20 μg/μL CLAP (chymostatin, leupeptin, antipain, and pepstatin A), supplemented with phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma; P5726 and P0044)). Homogenates were centrifuged at 420×g for 10 min and supernatants were collected and centrifuged again at 6700×g for 10 min to obtain the pellets containing a heavy membranes fraction (mitochondria-enriched). Next, mitochondrial oxygen consumption was analyzed as previously described [16]. Maximal complexes II + III + IV and complex IV-linked respiration were measured in a SeaHorse XFe24 flux analyzer (Agilent). For these respirometry assays, isolated mitochondria (equivalent to 10 μg of protein) were resuspended in MAS buffer (2 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, containing 70 mM sucrose, 220 mM mannitol, 5 mM KH2PO4, 5 mM MgCl2 and 1 mM EGTA) supplemented with cytochrome c (10 μg/mL; Sigma; C2506), placed in 24-well SeaHorse plates, and then centrifuged for 20 min at 4 °C and 3200×g in a plate centrifuge (Eppendorf; 5810R) to sediment the mitochondria. Maximal complex II + III + IV-linked respiration was calculated from the oxygen consumption rate that was stimulated by addition of succinate (0.65 mM) in the presence of rotenone (0.26 μM) and then inhibited by antimycin A (0.46 μM). Maximal complex IV respiration was calculated from the oxygen consumption rate that was stimulated by addition of N,N,N′, N′-tetramethyl-p-phenylenediamine (TMPD) (50 μM) plus ascorbic acid (0.1 mM) and then inhibited by sodium azide (4.6 mM).

Fatty acids profile determination

Fatty acids from liver (healthy n = 12 and SCD n = 11) were quantified as previously described [17]. A weighted piece of liver (around 20 mg) was homogenized with an OMNI TH Tissue Homogenizer (Omni International) in sterile 0.9% NaCl. The homogenate was spiked with 10 μg of trinonadecanoin (Nu-Chek Prep, Elysian, MN) as internal standard (ISTDs). The lipids were extracted with chloroform/methanol (2:1 v/v) and dried under nitrogen at 30 °C. Fatty acids were hydrolyzed and methylated as previously described [18]. Then, they were analyzed by gas chromatography/electron ionization mass spectrometry using an Agilent 6890N GC equipped with Agilent 7683 autosampler, Agilent 5973N mass spectrometry detector and J&W DB-Fast FAME capillary column (30 m × 0.2 mm × 0.25 μm) (Agilent Technologies). Gas chromatography was runned as follows: started at 50 °C, held for 0.5 min, increased to 194 °C (rate of 25 °C/min), held for 1 min, increased to 245 °C (rate of 5 °C/min), and held for 3 min. Selected ion monitoring mode was used. Several m/z ions common to saturated, monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids were monitored as previously described [19].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 9 software. Data were verified for normal distribution using Shapiro–Wilk normality test and difference between groups was assessed with unpaired t-test or Mann–Whitney test as appropriate. Means per group were presented with individual values or ±SEM. Differences were considered statistically significant when p-value <0.05. LFQ statistics were performed with Perseus by two-sample student's t-test with a permutation-based FDR (5%) and DEPs were selected when q-value <0.05. Relevant functions were determined with IPA® software based on p-value and z-score, calculated by overlapping with integrated databases.

Results

SCD promotes liver injury in mice

SCD mice presented hepatomegaly (Figure 1A–B), as well as a significant increase in serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), a liver injury marker (Figure 1C), and a decrease of serum lipidic molecules such as HDL cholesterol (HDLc), total cholesterol and triglycerides compared to healthy mice (Figure 1D). Moreover, we observed a significant increase in the expression of genes encoding inflammatory mediators (Ccl2, Ccl5 and Tnf) and endothelial injury markers (Vcam and Icam) were detected in SCD livers as compared with healthy mice (Figure 1E–F). Regarding histological changes, the hepatic tissue from SCD mice showed structural alterations such as inflammation, sickled RBCs accumulation in sinusoids, ischemic areas and hemosiderin deposits (Figure 1G–H). In terms of fibrosis, histological analysis with Sirius Red and Masson's trichrome staining showed localized fibrotic areas in the hepatic tissue of SCD mice (Figure 1I–J). Iron deposits were also detected in the liver of SCD (Figure 1K), as well as higher levels of heme compared to healthy mice (Figure 1L).

Hepatic proteome clearly differentiates SCD from healthy mice

The LFQ proteomic approach allowed the identification of two differentiated protein profiles in SCD and healthy mice, as indicated in the principal component analysis (PCA) classification (Figure 2A). From the total of 1389 proteins identified in the liver samples, statistical analysis (q-value <0.05) selected several up-regulated proteins (113 proteins) and down-regulated (29 proteins) in SCD vs healthy mice (Figure 2B). Hierarchical clustering, focusing on DEPs, also revealed a totally different profile between the two groups (Figure 2C-D). Next, functional classification of the altered proteins highlighted major pathways and functions affected in SCD vs healthy liver mice. Among others, DEPs correlated with hepatic diseases such as cholestasis, steatohepatitis or nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (Figure 2E), as well as with relevant canonical pathways such as NRF2-mediated oxidative stress response, oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial dysfunction and EIF2 signaling, among others (Table 1). Proteomic results are in complete agreement with the previous hepatic characterization carried out, confirming structural alterations and inflammation, but also revealing new possible mechanisms involved in SCD liver injury. Similarly, DEPs were also associated with relevant functions and diseases, highlighting, among others, hematological and metabolic diseases (Table 2), but also revealed clear alterations in important processes such as iron and lipid metabolism, cellular respiration, immunity and iron binding (Figure 2F). Complete results about LFQ proteomic approach and functional classification of DEPs are shown in supplementary information (Tables S3-6).

SCD livers present reduced mitochondrial respiration and energetic disorder

Proteomic analysis revealed an alteration of proteins associated with energy metabolism-related pathways such as β-oxidation (ACADM, HCDH, HSD17B4, IVD and SCP2), tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (ACO2, DLST, FH and SUCLG1) and sirtuin signaling (ACADL, ATP5F1C, ATP5F1D, GOT2, NDUFB4, NDUFB8, NDUFV2, SOD1 and TUBA4A), among others (Figure 3A). Also, DEPs were associated with mitochondrial disorders and respiratory chain deficiency (Figure 3B). In accordance with the proteomic results, the levels of OXPHOS proteins were altered in the liver comparing SCD and healthy mice. In this regard, complex I and III were down-regulated, while complex IV and V were up-regulated (Figure 3C–D). Moreover, the results from a respirometry assay with isolated mitochondria (Figure 3E), revealed a significant decrease in maximal complex IV-linked oxygen consumption in SCD vs healthy mice, although no significant changes were observed in maximal respiration rate linked to complexes II + III + IV (Figure 3F). Voltage-dependent anion channel 1 (VDAC1) did not show expression changes, indicating that mitochondrial mass was constant between groups (Figure 3G). In addition, levels of NAD+, NADH and ATP were significantly decreased in SCD liver mice (Figure 3H–J), without alteration in the NAD+/NADH ratio (Figure 3I). Expression analysis of genes involved in the salvage pathway of NAD+ synthesis did not show clear alterations (Fig. S1).

Hepatic lipid metabolism is altered in SCD mice

Functional enrichment analyses of DEPs also highlighted several functions related to lipid metabolism that were altered in SCD vs healthy mice (Figure 4A). In line with this, genes and proteins driving de novo fatty acids synthesis were upregulated in SCD, reaching statistical significance for sterol regulatory element binding protein-1 (SREBP1) and stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1) (Figure 4B–F). Moreover, levels of phosphorylated acetyl-CoA carboxylase 1 (pACC1), the inactive form, were significantly reduced in SCD mice, suggesting enhanced ACC1 activity and increased fatty acid synthesis. Individual DEPs analysis further confirmed the changes detected in the functional enrichment, highlighting proteins involved in fatty acids oxidation and uptake (Figure 4G). Histological analysis revealed lipid accumulation in hepatic tissue of SCD mice (Figure 5A), although total triglycerides content did not differ between groups (Figure 5B). Interestingly, within the triglyceride fraction, palmitic acid (C16:0), a major substrate for mitochondrial β-oxidation and ATP production, was significantly reduced in SCD livers (Figure 5C–D). Complete details about fatty acids quantification are shown in Table S7. In addition, protein levels of diacylglycerol acyltransferase 2 (DGAT2), the enzyme catalyzing the final step of triglyceride synthesis, was diminished in SCD livers, despite unchanged mRNA expression (Figure 5E–G). Furthermore, genes involved in cholesterol biosynthesis (Srebp2 and Soat1) were upregulated, together with a significant increase in hepatic cholesterol levels in SCD livers (Figure 5H–I). Collectively, these findings indicate a complex dysregulation of lipid metabolism in SCD livers, characterized by altered fatty acid synthesis and cholesterol accumulation.

Lipids are accumulated in necrotic areas of SCD livers

We next investigated whether the lipid imbalance observed in SCD livers may be also influenced by altered cellular uptake and storage mechanisms. Notably, CD36 and fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4), key scavenger receptors that mediate lipid internalization in macrophages, were significantly upregulated in the liver of SCD mice (Figure 6A–D). In the same way, Perilipins, structural proteins that coat LDs and regulate their stability, were also dysregulated. Perilipin-1, an isoform only present in LDs of adipocytes and foam cells [20], was upregulated in SCD mice, whereas Perilipin-2, an isoform broadly expressed in several cell types including hepatocytes, was down-regulated compared with healthy controls (Figure 6E–F). Histological analysis of livers revealed that lipids detected by ORO staining co-localized with the macrophage marker F4/80, particularly in ischemic/necrotic areas (Figure 6G). Interestingly, we also detected reduced expression of the Hb scavenger receptor CD163 (Figure 6H), suggesting an impaired capacity of hepatic macrophages to clear Hb. On the other hand, the expression of low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLr) and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) remained unchanged (Figure 6I–J), suggesting lipid disbalance in SCD livers arises from local cellular dysfunction instead of a global dysregulation of cholesterol homeostasis. Altogether, our results indicate that, while healthy livers have a normal lipid distribution among hepatocytes, SCD mice exhibit lipid accumulation not only in hepatocytes but also in macrophage-derived foam cells that are mainly localized in ischemic regions.

Discussion

SCD is a genetic hematological disease characterized by an abnormal Hb expression and subsequent early destruction of RBCs. RBCs acquire a sickle shape, reducing their elasticity and intravascular deformation capacity [21]. Hepatic function is specially compromised in these patients, with variable degrees of liver injury and heterogeneous clinical manifestation [22]. In this pathological context, liver damage has been attributed to recurrent vaso-occlusion and chronic hemolysis [23,24]. In terms of circulation, patients suffer vaso-occlusive crisis, chronic sinusoidal obstruction and tissue ischemia, also including ischemia-reperfusion events, which participate in progressive liver injury [24,25]. These events promote vascular and hepatocellular dysfunction and foster a pro-inflammatory milieu [[26], [27], [28]]. On the other hand, hemolysis and ineffective erythropoiesis releases large quantities of free Hb and heme into circulation that can promote hepatic iron overload damage [9,12].

To date, there is no specific treatment to SCD-associated liver injury. Therefore, a more comprehensive characterization of hepatic damage in this pathological setting is necessary to identify novel therapeutic targets. To this end, we used the well validated Townes mouse model of SCD, which recapitulates key features of human sickle cell hepatopathy. These mice developed hepatomegaly, hepatic injury and liver inflammation, along with notable alterations of the organ structure, presence of focal necrotic areas and localized fibrosis, in line with previous observations [24]. Additionally, we detected increased iron and heme content in the liver of the SCD mice, reflecting pathological heme deposition secondary to chronic hemolysis [29]. Therefore, this model provides a suitable platform to investigate the underlying mechanisms of liver injury in this disease. In this context, we conducted, for the first time to our knowledge, a comprehensive proteomic analysis of the liver in SCD mice. This approach revealed widespread dysregulation in fundamental metabolic processes, including β-oxidation and TCA cycle activation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and lipid metabolism alteration.

Indeed, functional classification of DEPs highlighted mitochondrial disorders and respiratory chain deficiency. These proteomic findings were confirmed in functional assays, where mitochondria isolated from the liver of SCD mice showed reduced respiratory capacity, with a decrease in maximal oxygen consumption associated to complex IV, as well as decreased hepatic levels of NAD+, NADH and ATP, confirming mitochondrial energy dysfunction. Our data also revealed a decreased abundance of mitochondrial complexes I and III as well as higher levels of complexes IV and V in the liver of SCD mice. This altered expression pattern may represent a compensatory response aimed at reducing oxidative stress and preserving ATP production; however, such adaptations may become maladaptive in pathological contexts [30]. In a healthy state, mitochondrial complex V is an ATP synthase; even so, under ischemic conditions, this enzyme can paradoxically reverse its activity and hydrolyze ATP [31]. This fact is consistent with the reduced hepatic ATP levels found in our SCD model, indicating a chronic energy deficit in this setting. This pathological effect may be exacerbated by hypoxia associated with SCD-sinusoidal vaso-occlusion, forcing hepatocytes to shift toward an anaerobic metabolism and glycolysis for ATP production. Upon resolution of ischemia, mitochondria suddenly receive oxygen and produce burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS) as consequence of a compromised mitochondrial respiratory chain. This ischemia-reperfusion cycle induces mitochondrial injury and lipid oxidation in the organelle membranes, which may be augmented by the abundance of pro-oxidant free heme in SCD [32]. Excess of free heme can directly impair mitochondrial function [33], and induce ROS production via Fenton reaction, leading to oxidative damage of lipids, protein and DNA [34]. In support of this, our proteomic data demonstrated activation of the NRF2-antioxidant pathway in SCD livers. However, in the context of iron overload, these antioxidant defenses can be overwhelmed, allowing free ferrous iron to promote lipid peroxidation and ultimately hepatocellular ferroptosis [35]. Similarly, hyperbilirubinemia is a well-established consequence of chronic hemolysis in SCD [9]. Elevated bilirubin levels have been linked to mitochondrial impairment in the liver [36]; therefore, the energetic dysfunction observed in our SCD model could be further exacerbated by this pathological environment, contributing to the loss of hepatic metabolic efficiency.

Our study confirmed increased expression of pro-inflammatory chemokines, cytokines and adhesion molecules, as well as abundance of macrophages in the liver of SCD mice. Liver macrophages constitute a full spectrum of different populations (resident Kupffer cells and infiltrating monocytes/macrophages) involved in either progression of liver disease or repairing hepatic damage [37]. Impaired recruitment of monocytes/macrophages in the liver has been associated with increased ischemic injury in SCD mice, an effect that was associated to decreased iron clearance, increased cellular senescence and reduced epithelial cell proliferation [23]. During hemolysis, macrophages phagocytose damaged erythrocytes and scavenge free Hb and heme, thereby limiting their pro-oxidant and proinflammatory effects [38]. Hemolysis differentiates macrophages into an anti-inflammatory and antioxidant phenotype, contributing to the resolution of sterile liver inflammation [39] and tissue repair [[40], [41], [42]]. These macrophages express CD163 receptor to mediate Hb endocytosis for heme degradation [43]. However, we observed reduced hepatic CD163 expression in SCD mice, suggesting an impaired capacity of hepatic macrophages to clear Hb in this pathological context. In this line, loss of CD163 was associated with worse prognosis in SCD patients due to impaired Hb clearance in the liver [44]. However, this reparative phenotype contrasts with the macrophage-derived foam cells, which are characterized by enhanced lipid uptake and storage rather than iron handling [43]. The accumulation of macrophage-derived foam cells has been associated with the progression of liver disease in other contexts of damage such as hepatocellular carcinoma [45]. Furthermore, in murine systems, CD163 mediated uptake accounts for only a portion of total Hb scavenging [46]. Therefore, the reduced expression of Cd163 observed in our model likely reflects a shift in macrophage activation state rather than definitive evidence of impaired Hb uptake. This altered polarization, potentially towards a more pro-inflammatory or exhausted phenotype due to chronic heme exposure, could further exacerbate the loss of hepatic energy homeostasis.

In our SCD model, ORO and F4/80 staining revealed a marked accumulation of lipid-laden macrophages in the liver, consistent with foam cell formation. This was further supported by elevated expression of Perilipin-1, a LDs coat protein characteristic of macrophages, along with upregulation of CD36 and FABP4, key regulators of lipid uptake and metabolism by macrophages [47,48]. The up-regulation of FABP4 has been previously observed in response to ischemia-reperfusion injury [49], suggesting a maladaptive response. Although FABP4 facilitates fatty acid trafficking, its ectopic expression in the liver has been previously associated with the induction of oxidative stress and inflammation [50]. In the SCD context, this may exacerbate the metabolic dysfunction, shifting the hepatic environment toward a state of chronic lipotoxicity. Moreover, we observed increased hepatic cholesterol content and abnormal LDs accumulation, along with alterations of lipogenic metabolism in SCD livers. These findings are in line with previous work demonstrating that acute intravascular hemolysis promoted hepatic lipids accumulation primarily through enhanced lipid uptake, blockade of lipophagy and elevated synthesis of cholesterol [17]. Likewise, Vats et al. demonstrated hepatobiliary injury in SCD mice, accompanied by hepatic accumulation of bile acid precursors and augmented cholesterol levels [24]. They also described that these pathological effects were caused by sustained NF-κB activation and impaired activity of farnesoid X receptor (FXR), a key regulator of bile acid and cholesterol homeostasis [24]. Despite these findings, hepatic triglycerides levels did not differ between SCD and control mice in our study. Instead, we observed different distribution of these molecules in specific areas of the liver. Under physiological conditions, hepatocytes generate LDs to store triglycerides and cholesteryl esters as a response to increased fatty acid overload, thus maintaining energy homeostasis and protecting against lipotoxicity [45,51]. However, in SCD livers, LDs were not only present in hepatocytes but also prominently localized within macrophage-derived foam cells surrounding necrotic areas, a phenomenon described in other organs undergoing ischemic injury [52]. This observation aligns with the concept of hepatic metabolic zonation. Hepatic function is determined by a complex spatial organization displaying three distinct metabolic zones (periportal, midzonal and pericentral), depending on oxygen availability [53,54]. The existence of this oxygen gradient determines opposite metabolic functions across the hepatic zones: oxidative metabolism, including β-oxidation, are predominant in the oxygen-rich periportal region; while glycolysis and lipogenesis are favored in the hypoxic pericentral zone [54]. Spatial metabolic imaging has recently confirmed that lipid signatures vary significantly across these hepatic zones [55]. In the context of SCD, sinusoidal vasoconstriction and subsequent hypoxia may exacerbate zonal metabolic differences, favoring lipid accumulation in hepatocytes localized in ischemic regions [56]. In our study, macrophage-derived foam cells appeared to be the primary lipid-storing cells in SCD livers. This may be consequence of enhanced uptake of lipids derived from dead hepatocytes and residual RBC membranes within ischemic areas [57]. A recent characterization of another SCD model, Berkeley mice, demonstrates a predominant extravascular hemolysis [58]. In this context, hepatic macrophages would be tasked with a source of cellular debris from necrosis and continuous erythrophagocytosis of sickle RBCs. Given that RBC membranes are highly enriched in cholesterol and phospholipids, the sustained engulfment and processing of these cells likely represent a primary driver for macrophage lipid loading. Collectively, these findings may explain why lipid laden hepatocytes and foam cells were restricted to necrotic regions in livers from SCD mice, resulting in focal steatosis. Although hepatic steatosis is a well-recognized feature of many chronic liver diseases, evidence of lipid accumulation in patients with SCD remains limited. In addition, mechanisms observed in this study distinguish hepatic steatosis in SCD from conventional non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. Therefore, new studies are needed to validate whether focal steatosis may represent an underrecognized consequence of this pathological condition. In the same way, although our results provide a comprehensive overview view of the energetic collapse in the SCD liver, the use of whole tissue homogenates does not allow for the discrimination of cell-type specific metabolic alterations. Future studies will be essential to further dissect the cell-type specific metabolic reprogramming and to define its contribution to the progression of hepatic damage in SCD.

In conclusion, our data provides a comprehensive characterization of hepatic damage in SCD based on liver proteomics, highlighting novel molecular and cellular alterations not described until now. We report, for the first time, energy homeostasis impairment, mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid metabolism imbalance, and foam cells accumulation in SCD hepatic tissue. This detailed characterization of the liver injury could help us to develop new therapeutic strategies to prevent hepatic complications in SCD patients.
