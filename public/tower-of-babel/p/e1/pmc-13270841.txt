Polydeoxyribonucleotide improves scar healing following limited scar resection for chronic Achilles tendon rupture in a rat model
Authors: Haochun Zhang, Wenguang Wu, Yun Su, Chi Xiao, Hongxun Wang, Fengcheng Cui, Weixuan Duan, Xiangjun Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic Achilles tendon rupture (CATR) results from delayed diagnosis or treatment, leading to retracted tendon ends filled with biomechanically inferior scar tissue. Limited scar resection (LSR) offers a less invasive surgical option by preserving and repairing scar tissue between tendon stumps, but the quality of healing remains suboptimal. Polydeoxyribonucleotide (PDRN) has demonstrated pro-healing properties in tendon disorders, yet its effects on scar remodeling after LSR are unknown.MethodsChronic Achilles tendon rupture was induced in male Sprague-Dawley rats by full-thickness transection without repair and 4 weeks of unrestricted activity. Animals were randomized into four groups: normal, control (conservative treatment), LSR, and LSR+PDRN. Outcomes were assessed at 3 and 6 weeks post-intervention via macroscopical scoring, biomechanical testing (maximum load, stiffness, stress), histology (modified Movin score), and immunohistochemistry (COL I/III expression).ResultsCompared to conservative treatment, LSR technique demonstrated a faster recovery of maximum load to baseline levels, regardless of whether it was combined with PDRN adjuvant therapy. With PDRN supplementation, stiffness was also restored to baseline. Biomechanical outcomes improved throughout the healing process in all experimental groups, with the LSR+PDRN group exhibiting higher maximum load, stiffness, and stress than both the control and LSR groups. At week 3, the LSR+PDRN group showed enhanced fibroblast proliferation and COL III secretion. By week 6, COL I expression was significantly better in the LSR+PDRN group compared to the control and LSR groups, although it still differed from that of normal Achilles tendon.ConclusionPDRN treatment enhances the biomechanical strength, histological structure, and COL I composition of the scar tissue following LSR in a rat model of CATR.Keywords: Achilles tendon, Polydeoxyribonucleotide, Biologic healing enhancement, Biomechanics of tendon, Biology of tendon

Introduction

The Achilles tendon (AT), which is formed by the distal extensions of the gastrocnemius and soleus muscles, attaches to the calcaneal tuberosity and ranks among the strongest tendons in the human body [1, 2]. Achilles tendon rupture (ATR) is a common motor system disorder that can significantly hinder daily activities and can contribute to disability, with an annual incidence of 5 to 50 events per 100,000 persons [3, 4]. Most ATRs are diagnosed promptly and treated immediately, either surgically or non-surgically, with the objective of preserving optimal tendon length and function [5, 6]. Even though complete Achilles tendon ruptures are common and are usually not difficult to diagnose for experienced physicians, more than 20% of acute injuries are misdiagnosed, leading to a chronic Achilles tendon rupture (CATR) (defined as a complete rupture with a delay in diagnosis or treatment for more than 4–6 weeks) [5, 7]. At this point, the tendon ends retract, and the gap fills with scar, making treatment more difficult.

Current treatment for CATR involves debridement of fibrotic tissue back to normal tendon stumps, as scar tissue is thought to have poorer healing and mechanical properties [8]. However, the resultant defect between normal tendon stumps poses a major challenge in the management of CATR. According to the Myerson’s classification and Kuwada’s classification, different surgical methods can be selected, mainly including primary repair [9, 10], gastrocnemius fascial advancement flap [9, 11], local tendon transfer [12], tendon allo- and autografts [13–15], and reconstruction using synthetic materials [16, 17]. Although these treatment options can effectively restore the length of ATs and exhibit a low incidence of re-ruptures following repair, they are invasive, may present technical challenges, and can lead to donor-site morbidities when harvesting grafts from otherwise healthy anatomical sites.

As clinical studies have progressed, limited scar resection (LSR) offers a less invasive, simpler treatment option for CATR [18, 19]. This procedure debrides a limited amount of scar tissue and, after primary repair of the scar stumps, restores a healthy AT length. This technique reduces surgical complexity, avoids donor-site morbidity, and eliminates additional procedural costs. In the previous clinical studies, the scar tissue after the LSR technique seemed to provide adequate repair strength as no re-rupture occurred and functional scores improved post-operatively [18, 19]. Scar tissue between the AT stumps contained dense collagen fibers running parallel along the tendon axis. However, the bundles of collagen fibers were thinner, and more fibroblasts were seen, compared with the intact AT [19]. This may be due to the fact that the AT scar tissue contains more type III collagen (COL III), while the normal AT tissue is over 90% type I collagen (COL I) [20]. The cross-sectional area (CSA) of scar tissue formed by CATR was larger than that of healthy AT [21, 22]. Due to the angiogenesis and the migration and proliferation of fibroblasts, the CSA of the scar tissue was further increased in the recovery process after the LSR technique. This makes the scar tissue a fusiform-shaped structure, and the increase in the number of fibroblasts and collagen fibers increases the tensile strength. The increased fibroblast and collagen density enhances tensile strength, but this gain appears to reflect improved structural rather than histological properties (i.e., COL I content).

Polydeoxyribonucleotide (PDRN) is a compound formed by deoxyribonucleotide polymers of different lengths ranging from 50 to 2000 base pairs and nucleosides [23]. It is mainly extracted and purified from the sperm cells of Salmon trout or Chum Salmon [24]. PDRN is an agonist against of the adenosine A2A receptor (A2AR), and stimulates nucleic acid synthesis by serving as a source of pyrimidines and purines [24, 25]. PDRN can induce angiogenesis, promote fibroblast maturation and collagen synthesis, and also exhibits anti-inflammatory activity [26]. Recent studies have highlighted the positive impact of PDRN in treating tendon disorders [25, 27–29]. PDRN has shown the potential to promote postoperative healing of CATR.

Therefore, we hypothesize that PDRN can enhance both the biomechanical and histological healing of AT scar tissue after the LSR technique in rats.

Methods

Study design

Approvals for all the study protocols were obtained from the Animal Review Committee of Zhongshan Hospital Affiliated to Dalian University (DW2024-017-01). The experimental animals were 80 male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (6 months old, 400–450 g; Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). They were housed five per cage at room temperature (24 °C ± 1 °C), relative humidity, and 12-hour light/dark cycles and were able to obtain food and water freely. They can effectively represents young-to-middle-aged human males in which ATR occurs most frequently [5, 30]. Rats were observed for 7 days in the animal care laboratory to exclude any underlying diseases, then they were used for the experiments. Twenty rats were randomly selected as the normal group, the remaining rats were randomly divided into the control group (n = 20), the LSR group (n = 20) and the LSR+PDRN group (n = 20) after CATR inducted (Fig. 1). To minimize confounding, treatment order was randomized with a blocked random-number sequence, the surgeon and outcome assessor were blinded, and cage location was rotated daily within the rack.

Study design. Chronic Achilles tendon rupture was induced in male SD rats allocated to the control, LSR and LSR+PDRN groups by full-thickness transection without repair and 4 weeks of unrestricted cage activity. At week 0, animals were randomly assigned to one of three interventions: no repair (the control group), limited scar resection (the LSR group), or LSR plus intraperitoneal PDRN (8 mg/kg every alternate day for 6 weeks; the LSR+PDRN group). All rats underwent 1 week of immobilization (IM) in 160° plantar-flexion immediately after surgery, resumed normal cage activity thereafter, and were sacrificed at 3 or 6 weeks post-intervention

Induction of CATR

The rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 2.5% Avertin at a dose of 13 µL/kg (MA1613, MeilunBio). Next, the posterior part of the right leg was shaved. Rats were placed in a prone position on the surgery table and covered with a sterile surgical drape, leaving only the prepared limbs exposed (Fig. 2A). A 1 cm longitudinal incision was made centered 5 mm proximal to the calcaneal insertion followed by longitudinal splitting of the crural fascia and paratenon which surrounds the AT (Fig. 2B). The tendon was transected perpendicular to the collagen fibers (Fig. 2C). The ruptured ends were left untreated; after thorough irrigation and disinfection, the subcutaneous tissue and skin were closed with 5 − 0 Prolene suture (8556 H, Ethicon) after rinsing and disinfecting (Fig. 2D). The rats were given intramuscular injections of cefazolin (PHR1291, Merck), with a postoperative regimen of 30 mg/kg daily for 3 days, a sterile dressing was applied, and the operated limb was left without splint immobilization. All animals were allowed unrestricted cage activity for 4 weeks to permit scar maturation, resulting in a CATR model.

Induction of a chronic Achilles tendon rupture model in rats. A Surgical-site preparation. B Achilles tendon exposure. C Complete transection 5 mm proximal to the calcaneal tuberosity. D Skin closure

LSR

Preoperative preparation was the same as the corresponding steps described in “Induction of CATR”. The original surgical incision was extended proximal to the level of the musculotendinous junction and distal to the level of the calcaneal tuberosity; then the scar tissue was isolated (Fig. 3A). The triceps surae was released by blunt dissection of the adhesion. The scar tissue was transected 5 mm proximal to the calcaneal tuberosity (Fig. 3B). In plantarflexion 160°, the scar tissue was moderately debrided to restore proper length of the AT (Fig. 3C), followed by end-to-end primary repair of the remaining scar tissue ends using 5−0 PGA absorbable suture (G51132Q, Shandong Weiqiao Pioneering Group Co., Ltd.) with a modified Kessler and figure-of-8 technique (Fig. 3D). The subcutaneous tissue and skin were sutured with a 5−0 Prolene suture (8556 H, Ethicon) after wound irrigation and disinfection (Fig. 3E). The rats were given intramuscular injections of cefazolin (PHR1291, Merck), with a postoperative regimen of 30 mg/kg daily for 3 days, a sterile dressing was applied, and the limb was immobilized for 1 week in 160° plantarflexion (Fig. 3F).

PDRN administration

The PDRN solution (1 g/100 mL) was prepared by dissolving PDRN (RN04, ReaLi Tide Biological Technology Co., Ltd.) in saline. Beginning at week 0, the rats in the LSR+PDRN group received intraperitoneal administration of the PDRN solution every alternate day for 3 weeks. The dosage was 8 mg/kg/day according to a previous experiment by Kang et al. [28]

Tissue sampling

The rats were sacrificed at 3 and 6 weeks with CO2 gas (n = 10 per group at both time points). Extensive dissection of the adjacent tissue was performed and repair site tissues were harvested from calcaneus to gastrocnemius muscle. At both time points, clinical evaluations were performed with all 10 samples per group using the improved macroscopical scoring system, five were then subjected to biomechanical testing, and others were submitted for histological and immunohistochemical analyses.

The relative AT length (L1, mm) was defined as the distance from the lower margin of the medial head of gastrocnemius muscle to the calcaneal tuberosity of rats. A digital caliper (SY-2001, Syntek) was used to measure the width (W, mm) and thickness (T, mm) of AT at 5 mm proximal to the calcaneal tuberosity. The AT sectional area (CSA) (S, mm2) was estimated according to the oval area formula (S = π*W*T/4).

Biomechanical evaluation

The tissue samples of the ATs from each group were installed in the universal testing machine (C43, MTS). The tension test was performed at the speed of 10 mm/min, starting at 0 N, and the start length (L₀, mm) of the Achilles tendon between the clamping jaws was recorded. The samples were tested to mechanical failure; the maximum load (Fmax, N) and the stretch distance (ΔL, mm) applied to each specimen were recorded by a computer for subsequent data analysis. The ultimate stress (σ, MPa) and stiffness (k, N/mm) were calculated using the load-displacement data.

Histological evaluation

The tissue samples were fixed at 4% paraformaldehyde (Solibao Biotechnology Co.) overnight, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin. Longitudinal sections of the Achilles tendon were cut and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (HE). The AT scar was examined under a upright microscope (Ni-E, Nikon) at 200× magnification, and the degree of adhesion was evaluated independently by three blinded observers using the modified Movin grading scale [32] (Table 2).

Immunohistochemical staining

Sections were blocked for 10 min in 3% H2O2 (v/v, in 95% ethanol) and then for 30 min in 10% normal goat serum at room temperature. After rinsing in PBS, sections were incubated with primary polyclonal rabbit antibodies against rat COL I or COL III (mQ3Z and GD50, Nature Biosciences Co., 1:100, respectively) for 30 min at 4 ℃ and then with secondary antibodies (1:100) for 3 min at room temperature. Then labeling was visualized with diaminobenzidine. The sections were examined by a upright microscope (Ni-E, Nikon), and imaged at 200× magnification, then ImageJ was used to assess the positive area ratio (PAR) of COL I and COL III, and average optical density (AOD) of COL I and COL III of the images.

Statistical analyses

The normality (assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (assessed by Bartlett’s test) of all continuous outcome data were confirmed using IBM SPSS Statistics v26.0 (SPSS Inc). All data met the assumptions for parametric analysis; therefore, subsequent statistical tests were performed using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). For comparisons among the four groups (Normal, Control, LSR, LSR+PDRN) at each individual time point, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. To evaluate the main effects of intervention and time (3 vs. 6 weeks) as well as their interaction, data from the four groups were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. When ANOVA indicated a significant effect, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted with Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test to control the family-wise error rate. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Discussion

Tendon healing occurs in 3 distinct but overlapping phases [33]. First, during the inflammatory stage, erythrocytes, platelets, neutrophils, monocytes and macrophages migrate to the lesion and release angiogenic factors. Second, in the proliferative stage, fibroblasts begin to synthesis collagen, proteoglycans and randomly oriented extracellular matrix that is composed mainly of COL III. Third, during the remodeling and maturation phase, COL III fibers are replaced by COL I fibers, thereby restoring the mechanical strength of the tendon [34, 35]. Despite these healing efforts, the process often fails to achieve complete regeneration of the injured tissue, leading to the formation of biomechanically inferior scar tissue. Despite reparative efforts, injured tendon rarely regenerates completely, yielding a biomechanically inferior scar that predisposes to recurrent injury and functional decline—a sequela especially pronounced in CATR, which, if left untreated, markedly impair gait and overall ankle function [16]. Unless otherwise contraindicated, the effect of conservative treatment is very limited, this condition is typically rectified through surgical reconstruction [9]. Preclinical studies related to CATR remain scarce.

In previous studies, chronic rupture was created by surgical blunt transection of the AT, followed by 1 week of immobilization of the hindlimb in a maximally dorsiflexed position (IM, 20°), and then 5 weeks of unrestricted cage activity for scar tissue maturation [5, 36]. Fixation in maximal dorsiflexion causes greater retraction of the AT ends, resulting in an elongated scar during the initial phase. Although this approach promotes scar tissue formation, it does not accurately reflect the true pathogenesis of chronic rupture. In our study, the transected ATs were not immobilized, and the rats self-regulated their limb activity according to the degree of pain. This approach more closely reflects the real-world clinical situation.

Based on the control group findings, conservative management of CATR in rats still resulted in tendon elongation and proximal muscle contracture. Although the scar tissue in the control group was significantly thicker than that in normal tendons at both time points, it consisted predominantly of COL III, whereas COL I is essential for tensile-strength recovery [37]. Consequently, the control group exhibited inferior biomechanical and histological outcomes, highlighting the limited efficacy of conservative treatment for CATR. Current clinical intervention strategies for CATR are complex surgical procedures that often involve donor-site morbidity when harvesting healthy tissue or present considerable technical challenges. Although the previous rat experiment [5] has demonstrated that LSR is a viable and simple intervention for CATR—performing similarly to, if not better than, the clinically established the gastrocnemius fascial turndown procedure—doubts about the quality of scar-tissue healing persist. Previous studies have reported that axial mechanical stimulation promotes the synthesis of COL I by both tenocytes and fibroblasts [38, 39]. In this study, LSR restored physiological tension across the rat AT complex. Immunohistochemistry revealed superior COL I expression in the LSR group relative to controls at both time points, and maximum load reached normal values. However, stiffness, ultimate stress and COL I content remained markedly below those of intact tendon, indicating that the favorable peak load was achieved predominantly by increased structural bulk rather than improved histological property. This conclusion is supported by the persistently enlarged CSA and high COL III fraction in the scar. Thus, LSR alone confers only modest histological benefit to the AT scar.

Biological adjuvant therapies for acute ATR and Achilles tendinopathy, such as growth-factor cocktails [40, 41], platelet-rich plasma [42], adipose-derived stem cells [43] and stromal vascular fraction [44, 45], have been extensively evaluated in both pre-clinical and clinical studies. In contrast, investigations concerning CATR remain confined mainly to surgical technique. PDRN is a DNA-derived drug. Published studies have confirmed that PDRN exhibits low immunogenicity and no detectable toxicity to major organs [46]. This ensures its safety for use in treating CATR. PDRN is degraded into bio-active oligo- and mononucleotides, purines and pyrimidines, which exert their respective biological activities through two distinct mechanisms. nucleotides, nucleosides, and purine/pyrimidine bases stimulate cellular proliferation, they enhance nucleic acid synthesis through the salvage pathways and/or binding to the purinergic receptors. Purine receptors are divided into two subclasses: P1, which responds to nucleosides, and P2, which responds to nucleotides. P1 receptors are subdivided into A1, A2A/2B, and A3 subtypes, while P2 receptors include a number of subtypes designated P2X, Y, U, Z [28]. A2AR, associated with Gs proteins, stimulates adenylate cyclase and increases intracellular cAMP. cAMP activates then PKA. Adenosine acts through its receptors and has been shown to accelerate the resolution of acute inflammation, stimulate not only the secretion of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) but also the proliferation of fibroblasts and collagen synthesis [28, 47]. Previous studies have shown that PDRN promotes tendon healing after acute Achilles tendon rupture [28, 29, 48]. Moreover, published studies have shown that PDRN reduces scar formation of skin [49–51]. These observations support the hypothesis that PDRN can improve scar healing after LSR for CATR in rats.

At week 3, the significantly larger CSA in the PDRN group meant that, despite a smoother and more intact scar surface than the LSR group, the improved macroscopical score did not differ between the two groups. In normal healing, the inflammatory phase typically lasts until approximately 1 week after injury. Abnormal inflammatory prolongation results in excessive scarring [47]. PDRN, acting as an adenosine A2AR agonist, has been shown to suppress the pro-inflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and Interleukin-6 (IL-6) via A2AR activation. Consequently, the PDRN group exhibited markedly fewer infiltrating inflammatory cells, thereby reducing the risk of pathological scar formation. In a previous rat skin-wound study, administration of PDRN during the inflammatory phase significantly attenuated the inflammatory response, compared with the sham group, PDRN treatment increased COL I and COL III content in the skin scar [51]. At 3 weeks, the LSR+PDRN group exhibited a superior modified Movin score, reflecting better histo-architectural recovery, while maximum load, stress and stiffness were all higher than in the LSR and control groups, indicating favorable biomechanical restoration. Nevertheless, immunohistochemistry revealed that after three weeks of PDRN administration the fibrillar matrix was dominated by COL III, whose relative abundance exceeded that in the LSR group. Previous studies on acute ATR reported enhanced COL I synthesis during the early phase of PDRN treatment [28, 48], a finding that contrasts with our observations in chronic rupture. In this study, the scar tissue between the AT stumps was preserved and remodeled by LSR, thereby re-initiating the healing cascade. Studies have shown that during the proliferative phase PDRN activates the ERK pathway in fibroblasts, markedly up-regulating collagen synthesis and producing a “fibroplastic” effect [46, 51]. Because scar tissue is composed predominantly of fibroblasts, continuous PDRN administration drives increased secretion of COL III throughout this phase. The morphological features of the AT in each group corroborate these findings (Fig. 4A). At week 3, the ATs in the LSR and LSR+PDRN groups showed notable fusiform thickening. Although the control group also exhibited an increased CSA compared to normal tendons, its morphology was more reminiscent of healthy AT tissue. Having undergone only the initial CATR injury, and considering the 4-week modeling period, the scar tissue in the control group was already in the remodeling phase by the 3- and 6-week post-surgery time points (equivalent to 7 and 10 weeks post-injury). In contrast, the LSR procedure in the other two groups re-initiated the healing cascade, leading to the formation of fusiform scar tissue at the repair site.

We selected week 6 as the second observation time-point because the remodeling and maturation phase begins 3–4 weeks after the proliferative stage, and by 6 weeks the rat model has entered the remodeling and maturation phase. During this stage, COL III is gradually replaced by COL I, and the mechanical strength of the tissue is further enhanced. Transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) is a crucial cytokine that acts in both the inflammatory and healing stages of tissue repair [52]. It promotes fibroblast expression of COL I while suppressing the sustained synthesis of COL III [53]. Fibroblast growth factor (FGF) can synergize with TGF-β to promote COL I formation [54]. PDRN has been demonstrated to promote the secretion of TGF-β and FGF by activating the A2AR [55, 56]. In the previous study, TGF-β and FGF expression increased after four weeks of PDRN administration, whereas no significant elevation was observed at the one- or two-week time points [28]. Kim et al.‘s most recent study has likewise confirmed this observation [48]. This appears to indicate that, during the remodeling and maturation phase, PDRN accelerates the conversion of type III to type I collagen by promoting the expression of relevant cytokines. At week 6, the LSR+PDRN group no longer exhibited fusiform-shaped structure; instead, the tendons gradually transitioned toward a normal appearance, a change that was more pronounced than in the LSR group (Fig. 4A). The LSR+PDRN group exhibited significant improvements in the improved macroscopical score and CSA compared with week 3, accompanied by markedly enhanced biomechanical outcomes. Histological examination revealed more regularly aligned collagen fibers in the LSR+PDRN group, with no evident neovascularization or inflammatory cell infiltration, and the modified Movin score was significantly better than that of both the LSR and control groups. Immunohistochemistry revealed that the PAR and AOD of COL I in the LSR+PDRN group were significantly higher than those in the LSR and control groups and had markedly recovered relative to the week 3 time point. Concurrently, COL III expression in the LSR+PDRN group was significantly reduced. At both the biomechanical and histological levels, PDRN-assisted LSR yielded superior outcomes compared with surgical treatment alone in a rat model of CATR.

An interesting observation in this study was that the maximum load in the LSR+PDRN group was significantly higher than that in the normal group at both time points. At week 3 although the PAR of COL I in the normal group was 4.66 times that in the LSR+PDRN group, the CSA of the LSR+PDRN group was 3.22 times larger. This larger CSA helped offset the difference in total COL I content within the AT tissue that would have been expected based on PAR alone. Additionally, the presence of COL III contributed to some degree of initial mechanical support. Nevertheless, the inferior stress result indicated persistent structural difference between the LSR+PDRN group and normal tendons at the histological level. As a result, the combination of a substantially larger CSA and partially restored histological properties allowed the LSR+PDRN group to bear 1.5 times the load of the normal group at week 3. At week 6, the CSA of the LSR+PDRN group had slightly decreased compared to week 3, suggesting ongoing tendon remodeling, yet it remained significantly larger than that of the normal group. At this stage, COL I expression had increased relative to week 3, and the improved histological property contributed to the excellent load-bearing capacity observed in the LSR+PDRN group (1.61 times that of the normal group). Furthermore, stress level was also significantly improved compared to that at week 3.

Although the application of PDRN significantly improved the biomechanical strength of CATR, ankle function and range of motion were not assessed in this study. AT thickness is moderately negatively correlated with the Achilles Tendon Rupture Score (ATRS) and the Foot and Ankle Outcome Score (FAOS) [57], indicating that a larger CSA may still lead to poor ankle function. Previous studies have shown a strong correlation between high TGF-β expression and tendon adhesion [58, 59], which can also result in joint stiffness and impaired function [60, 61]. Given the relatively small sample size used for biomechanical testing at each time point in this study (n = 5), further validation with larger sample sizes and supplemental assessment of ankle function are needed.This study also has several limitations. First, only male SD rats were used in this study, and no sex-matched controls were included. Second, as the observation endpoint was limited to week 6, the long-term equivalence of this therapeutic strategy to normal tendon tissue remains undetermined; the prolonged effects of PDRN on CATR therefore warrant further investigation. Additionally, the PDRN dose employed herein was extrapolated from previous rat studies of acute Achilles tendon rupture. Given the short half-life of PDRN and its administration in the present study as an intraperitoneal injection every alternate day, the optimal dose and delivery regimen for CATR remain to be defined. Nevertheless, our findings support the hypothesis that intraperitoneal PDRN administration enhances biomechanical and histological recovery after the LSR technique of CATR in rats. Further investigations are required to delineate the stage-specific mechanisms of PDRN within the complex healing cascade of CATR, establish its safety profile in humans, and define both the optimal therapeutic dose and the most effective route of administration.

Conclusion

For chronic Achilles tendon rupture, PDRN combined with LSR technique offers a promising novel modality that improves scar tissue healing. However, the translation of these findings into clinical practice requires further preclinica and clinical trials to confirm their efficacy and safety.
