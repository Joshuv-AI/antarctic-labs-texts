Successful Surgical Management of Ruptured Hepatic Artery Pseudoaneurysm Post-Liver Retransplant: A Case Report
Authors: Seyed Mohammad Reza Nejatollahi, Arman Hasanzade, Fariba Ghorbani
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Liver transplantation stands as the primary treatment for end-stage liver diseases [1,2]. Despite numerous efforts to reduce transplantation-related complications, the risk of various complications still exists, which could lead to the necessity of additional operation or even re-transplantation [3]. Postoperative vascular complications are a subset of these complications that pose a threat to recipients' survival. The reported incidence of vascular complications in the literature ranges from 0.8 % to 20 %. Mortality rates associated with these complications vary, ranging from 11 % to 60 %. [2,4].

Hepatic Artery Pseudoaneurysm (HAP) is one of these vascular complications, occurring in approximately 0.27 % to 2 % of cases [1,[4], [5], [6]]. Despite its rarity, HAP is considered a severe and potentially fatal complication with a high mortality rate ranging from 69 % to 75 % [4,6]. HAP arises when there is a partial disruption in the arterial wall, causing blood to leakage from the arterial lumen and form a blood-filled sac that resembles an aneurysm [7].

Here we are reporting a case of hepatic artery pseudoaneurysm in an adult male patient following his second orthotopic liver transplantation. We would like to note that this case report has been prepared in accordance with the SCARE guidelines [8].

Presentation of case

A twenty-year-old male patient underwent second orthotopic liver transplantation (OLT) ten months after the initial transplantation. The patient's medical history revealed autoimmune hepatitis, which had led to liver cirrhosis necessitating the first liver transplantation. There were no reported prior medical conditions, surgeries, medications. Furthermore, there was no history of risk factors associated with vascular diseases, such as diabetes or smoking.

During first transplantation anastomosis of hepatic artery was end to end and biliary anastomosis was performed duct to duct. Following the first transplantation, hepatic artery thrombosis was diagnosed on the fifth day after transplantation, necessitating surgical intervention. During the procedure, thrombosis was observed in both the recipient's common hepatic artery and branches of the donor hepatic artery. The arterial anatomy of the recipient displayed an early bifurcation of the hepatic artery, a recognized anomaly that may be linked to a heightened risk of thrombosis formation [9]. Additionally, a cadaveric iliac artery conduit from donor's iliac artery which was kept in the tissue bank was employed and previous end to end hepatic artery anastomosis changed to the infra-renal aorto-hepatic jump graft anastomosis. Following the salvage therapy surgery, a follow-up Doppler ultrasound showed normal hepatic artery flow without any signs of further complications. Consequently, the patient was discharged with normal laboratory test results and remained symptom-free, experiencing no further complications.

Seven months after the first transplantation, follow-up laboratory tests indicated an elevation in liver enzymes. Upon further investigation, a biliary stricture was identified during Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) and subsequently treated with a biliary stenting. Five months later, at the twelve-month mark following the first transplantation, the patient began experiencing symptoms such as jaundice, fever, and abdominal pain. Ultrasonography showed evidence of multiple hepatic collections, suggesting the presence of liver abscesses. An abdominal CT scan confirmed this diagnosis and demonstrated low blood flow of the hepatic artery, accompanied with evidences of chronic ischemic liver disease and diffuse cholangiopathy.

Consequently, the patient became a candidate for re-transplantation. After the primary management, the patient underwent a liver re-transplantation when a liver from a brain-dead donor became available. The surgery was conducted using the orthotopic liver transplantation technique. The donor was a 19-year-old male who had been in a car accident. During this procedure, hepatic artery anastomosis was established using a new infra-renal aorto-hepatic jump graft anastomosis with an iliac artery conduit from the same donor. For the biliary anastomosis, the Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy method was employed.

On the fifth day following the second OLT, the patient presented with melena, which progressively led to tachycardia with a pulse rate of 105 beats per minute and mild hypotension, indicated by a systolic blood pressure of 95 mmHg. Immediate fluid resuscitation was initiated, and a blood transfusion was administered. Consequently, the patient was promptly transferred for an abdominal CT scan with intravenous (IV) contrast. The abdominal CT scan with IV contrast detected contrast leakage from the hepatic artery into the Roux limb (refer to Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Following this imaging, the patient was promptly transferred to the operating room due to a diagnosis of a ruptured HAP. During this transfer, the patient experienced active rectorrhagia. Within minutes of the patient's arrival in the operating room, while fluid resuscitation and blood transfusion were underway, the systolic blood pressure dropped to 70 mmHg. Furthermore, an emergency laparotomy was performed through the previous incision.

In the surgical field, active arterial bleeding was identified originating from the previously ligated gastro-duodenal branch of the donor's hepatic artery at the hilum, which had become necrotic. Additionally, a leakage was detected at the hepaticojejunostomy anastomosis site, along with a visible open area adjacent to the necrotic arterial branch.

Following the control of bleeding through the temporary clamping of the hepatic artery, the hepatic artery orifice was repaired using PROLENE 7.0, and a revision of the hepaticojejunostomy anastomosis was performed. The transplanted liver exhibited a healthy color and consistency, with the remaining portions of the hepatic artery remained intact, providing good flow and pulsation into the transplanted liver following the arterial repair. The patient's care during operation included blood and FFP transfusions administered by an anesthesiologist. After the operation, the patient was transferred to the liver transplant intensive care unit (ITU) in a stable hemodynamic condition. After operation broad-spectrum antibiotic and antifungal treatment were administrated for patient. While the pathological evaluation of specimen and specimen culture demonstrated no sign of fungal or bacterial infection, the broad-spectrum antibiotic and antifungal therapy continued.

During the hospitalization period, the patient was closely monitored and daily Doppler US was utilized for monitoring liver vascular condition. Fortunately, there were no signs of further complications, and laboratory tests, along with normal follow-up Doppler US. The patient was discharged two weeks after the HAP complication surgery.

Discussion

This report presents a case of a ruptured extrahepatic artery pseudoaneurysm following liver re-transplantation. Hepatic artery pseudoaneurysm is a rare complication of liver transplantation, and its precise etiology remains unclear [1,7]. Several studies have suggested factors associated with pseudoaneurysm formation. While many studies have identified predisposing factors, some evidence differentiates factors related to two distinct types of HAP; intrahepatic and extrahepatic [10].

Extrahepatic pseudoaneurysms are more common, accounting for more than 69 % of HAP cases in different studies [10,11]. Local infection and biliary-enteric anastomosis are two predisposing factors, which, while related to both intra and extrahepatic pseudoaneurysms, exhibit a stronger association with the extrahepatic form [1,[4], [5], [6], [7],10,12]. The presumed mechanism behind HAP formation due to infection is that bacteria caused the erosion of the adventitia layer of the artery, leading to pseudoaneurysm development. A previous systematic review suggested that local infection is the primary predisposing factor for HAP, with around 80 % of HAP cases being associated with infection [4].

The role of biliary-enteric anastomosis, particularly Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy, is also significant. One study reported an association rate of 41.9 % between Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy and HAP [4], while another study demonstrated an association ranging from 63 % to 78 % in the studied population [10]. Some studies suggest that this association is due to the potential for microorganisms to colonize the subhepatic space following Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy [7]. In intrahepatic pseudoaneurysms, iatrogenic injuries appear to play a major role, including percutaneous transhepatic interventions such as cholangiography and biopsy, biliary stents via endoscopy, and hepatic artery balloon angioplasty [10,12].

In addition to the mentioned predisposing factors, some studies suggest an association between HAP and surgical difficulties, vascular interventions, arterial manipulation during surgery, reoperation, primary sclerosing cholangitis, pancreatitis and biliary leakage [4,6,7,10]. While the primary cause of HAP in the presented case remains uncertain, many of the mentioned predisposing factors were observed, including biliary stent, diffuse cholangiopathy with local infection and Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy. Consequently, our case provides evidence of an association between these factors and HAP, although the precise role of each factor remains unclear.

While Hepatic Artery Pseudoaneurysms (HAPs) tend to occur in the first few months following transplantation, they are typically asymptomatic until they rupture. When HAP rupture occurs, they most commonly result in bleeding into the gastrointestinal tract. However, it can also lead to bleeding within the peritoneal cavity, causing abdominal pain and peritoneal sign, or into adjacent liver tissues, forming hematomas. In some cases, HAPs can affect the biliary tract, leading to haemobilia [4,5,10,12,13].

Ruptured HAPs often lead to severe hypotension and may even result in shock. Hemodynamic instability is the most common presentation, observed in approximately 50–67 % of cases [10], mirroring the scenario in the presented case. Consequently, ruptured HAPs are considered life-threatening events, associated with a high mortality rate, emphasizing the need for strong clinical suspicion, early diagnosis, and emergent intervention [4,5].

While most HAPs are diagnosed after rupture, early diagnosis before rupture is the main key factor for a favorable prognosis [4,12]. Occasionally, HAPs may be incidentally discovered during radiologic evaluations for other reasons or during routine follow-up investigations. Although rare, HAPs can also present symptoms without rupture. In some instances, HAPs can reduce hepatic artery blood flow, leading to hepatic and biliary ischemia, which may manifest as elevated liver function tests and graft dysfunction [10].

Various imaging modalities are valuable in diagnosing HAP. Doppler US, abdominal CT scans, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can provide evidence of HAP presence. While these modalities can suggest the presence of a pseudoaneurysm, the definitive diagnosis is typically established through angiography or during surgery. However, angiography is invasive and not always feasible. In suspected cases, the use of Doppler US and CT scans can expedite the diagnosis process, enabling prompt and appropriate intervention [2,6,12]. While all these modalities can be helpful, in most cases of ruptured HAP there is no time for radiologic evaluation. Therefore, the high clinical suspicion of surgeon becomes crucial and can be life-saving in such instances.

Numerous surgical and non-surgical approaches are available for the management of Hepatic Artery Pseudoaneurysm (HAP). While the optimal strategy remains a matter of debate and should be tailored to each patient's specific condition, most studies agree on the necessity of intervention due to the elevated risk of mortality associated with HAP rupture [7,10].

Traditional surgical methods for HAP encompass hepatic artery ligation or excision of the affected arterial segment often accompanied by the use of extra-anatomical autologous bypass grafts, or re-transplantation [1,10]. Some studies suggest non-surgical interventions in non-ruptured HAP due to risk of reoperations such as transcatheter embolization, direct percutaneous coil placement and endovascular stent grafts. It is essential to note that while these techniques are less invasive, they may not commonly provide definitive solutions, potentially necessitating eventual surgical intervention [4,6,7,10]. Numerous surgical techniques were recommended for managing HAP until re-transplantation became available. However, early diagnosis and intervention could preserve the graft and enhance the patient's chances of survival.

Conclusion

Hepatic Artery Pseudoaneurysm (HAP) represents a rare yet critical complication in patients who have undergone liver transplantation, as its rupture can result in a fatal outcome. Therefore, it is essential for transplant surgeons to maintain awareness of this uncommon complication, its predisposing factors, and associated signs and symptoms such as gastrointestinal bleeding, unstable hemodynamics and unusual abdominal pain. Furthermore, maintaining a high level of clinical suspicion and promptly initiating intervention is crucial, as timely action can make the difference in saving a patient's life in critical conditions.
