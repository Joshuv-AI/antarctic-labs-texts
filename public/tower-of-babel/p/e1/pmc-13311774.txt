Sequential involvement of hypoxia and anti-PD-1 treatment in shaping tumor-regional CD39+CD8+ T cells highlights immunotherapy-resistant features in NSCLC
Authors: Jiajuan Wu, Jiawei Zhai, Leilei Lv, Yaoxin Zhang, Yu Shen, Qiuxia Qu, Cheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding resistance to anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) therapy is critical for developing reversal strategies. Although ectonucleotidase CD39 (ENTPD1) (CD39)+ cluster of differentiation 8 (CD8)+ T cells have been associated with antitumor responses, the naïve regional distribution of these cells and their dysregulated dynamics as determinants of response or resistance to anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (mAb) therapy remain poorly characterized.MethodsWe employed targeted biopsy techniques to characterize the spatial infiltration of CD39+CD8+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME) of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). It was further performed independent NSCLC cohort, flow cytometry, multiplex immunohistochemistry, single-cell RNA sequencing and functional studies to investigate these cells in vitro and in vivo.ResultsRegion-specific CD39+CD8+ T cells located outside hypoxic zones initially served as potential biomarkers to predict response to anti-PD-1 therapy. However, hypoxia gradients within the TME were associated with regional depletion of these cells in NSCLC. Subsequently, anti-PD-1 mAb treatment further reduced intratumoral CD39+CD8+ T-cell levels, even in the context of improved oxygenation following tumor regression, suggesting a feedback mechanism modulating antitumor immunity. Multiple assays revealed that the absence of CD39 impaired functional restoration of CD8+ T cells on PD-1 blockade. This finding was corroborated by distinct transcriptional profiles, enhanced cytotoxic/effector signatures, and a more oligoclonal and expanded T-cell receptor (TCR) repertoire in CD39+CD8+ T cells across the CD8+ T-cell differentiation continuum.ConclusionsThese findings identify CD39+CD8+ T cells as an important regulator of anti-PD-1 therapy, whereas initial hypoxia followed by anti-PD-1 treatment sequentially shapes tumor-regional localization of these cells, ultimately linking to suboptimal immunotherapeutic responses.

Background

Tumors use diverse mechanisms to establish a tolerogenic microenvironment, including the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines, competition for essential nutrients, and expression of inhibitory ligands.1 2 Therapeutic strategies targeting the tumor microenvironment (TME) have emerged as promising approaches in cancer treatment.3 4 Notably, although immunotherapy with anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) or anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) monoclonal antibody (mAb) has demonstrated significant clinical responses, efficacy remains limited to a subset of patients, underscoring the need to understand mechanisms underlying TME-mediated resistance.

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), particularly cluster of differentiation 8 (CD8)+ T cells, exhibit an exhausted phenotype in various animal models and in human cancers.5 6 Evidence from both human and murine studies supports the existence of a heterogeneous CD8+ T-cell population, differing in transcriptomic, epigenomic, proteomic, and functional profiles.7 8 Spatial interactions between immune and tumor cells generate complex ecological dynamics that influence tumor progression and therapeutic outcomes.9 10 Heterogeneous spatial distribution of immune infiltrates within the TME has been linked to clinical implications in multiple malignancies, including melanoma and biliary tract cancer.11

Recent research has focused on the expression and function of ectonucleotidase CD39 (ENTPD1) (CD39) and ecto-5'-nucleotidase (CD73) in human solid tumors.12 CD39, an ectonucleotidase expressed on B cells, innate immune cells, regulatory T cells, and activated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, acts in concert with CD73 to promote local adenosine production, thereby fostering an immunosuppressive milieu.13 Furthermore, CD39 expression on CD8+ T cells has been proposed as a marker of T-cell exhaustion.14 However, CD39 is highly expressed on neoantigen-associated and tumor-associated antigen-reactive T cells identified in lung cancer and melanoma, suggesting its utility as a marker of tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells.15 These findings position CD39 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for tumor-specific CD8+ T cells.

We hypothesize that distinct tumor biopsy strategies enable effective sampling of region-specific immune infiltrates within the non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) TME, particularly CD39-defined CD8+ T cells distributed across heterogeneous regions. We further investigate whether regional CD39+CD8+ T-cell dynamics are governed by a sequential program initiated by intrinsic hypoxia and shaped by selection pressure during anti-PD-1 therapy, ultimately contributing to suboptimal immunotherapeutic responses. To characterize this cell population, we employ flow cytometry, multiplex immunohistochemistry, co-culture assays, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), and single-cell T-cell receptor sequencing (scTCR-seq), aiming to establish its role as an important regulator of anti-PD-1 therapy.

Methods

Patients

53 patients with lung cancer were enrolled from the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between 2019 and 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histologically confirmed NSCLC or small cell lung cancer (SCLC); (2) availability of sufficient freshly biopsied specimens for immune cell analysis prior to initiation of antitumor treatment; and (3) presence of measurable lesions according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) V.1.1. Among them, 19 patients with NSCLC who received first-line anti-PD-1-based therapy for at least four cycles were included in the immunotherapy efficacy analysis. Clinical data collected included age, sex, histological subtype, pathological stage, primary tumor size, tumor proportion score (TPS) for PD-L1 expression, and oncogenic driver mutations. All clinical tissue samples were handled in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Acquisition of tumor samples

Tumor tissues were obtained via bronchoscopy. On visualization of the neoplasm under bronchoscopic guidance, superficial biopsies were performed. For lesions not visible under conventional bronchoscopy, endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) was conducted using a 22-gage needle (Olympus).

Isolation of lymphocytes from lung cancer tissues

Fresh bronchoscopic biopsy specimens were dissected and placed in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium, then mechanically dissociated using scissors. Tissues were enzymatically digested with a solution containing DNase I (0.3 mg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) and Liberase TL (0.2 mg/mL, Roche) in serum-free RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at 37°C, followed by filtration through a 70 µm cell strainer (Beyotime Biotechnology). For fresh specimens obtained by EBUS-TBNA, red blood cells were lysed using 10 mL of ACK lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology) at 37°C for 10 min. Isolated cells were resuspended in 1 mL of flow cytometry staining buffer and prepared for subsequent staining.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions were adjusted to a concentration of 2×10⁶ cells/mL prior to staining. Cells were incubated with a panel of fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. The antibody panel included APC-Cyanine7-conjugated anti-CD45 (clone HI30, BioLegend, Cat# 304014, RRID:AB_314402), FITC-conjugated anti-CD8α (clone RPA-T8, BioLegend, Cat# 301006, RRID:AB_314124), PE-Cyanine7-conjugated anti-CD39 (clone A1, BioLegend, Cat# 328211, RRID:AB_2293623), and APC-conjugated anti-PD-1 (clone EH12.2H7, BioLegend, Cat# 329907, RRID:AB_940473). Fluorescence minus one controls were used for accurate identification and gating of cell populations.16 Lymphocytes were initially gated based on forward scatter area and side scatter area. CD39+ and PD-1+ TILs were identified within the CD45+CD8+ T-cell subset. Multicolor flow cytometry was performed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, RRID:SCR_025068), and data were analyzed with FlowJo software (Tree Star, RRID:SCR_008520).

Multiplex immunohistochemistry

Tumor tissues embedded in paraffin were sectioned into 5 µm slices. Following deparaffinization, rehydration, and heat-induced antigen retrieval in citrate buffer, sections were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin at 37°C for 1 hour. Sequential immunofluorescent staining for CD39, CD8, and hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) was then performed in successive rounds. Primary antibodies used were rat anti-human CD39 mAb (1:150, OriGene, Cat# TA804559; RRID: AB_2627349), rabbit anti-human CD8 mAb (1:300, ZSGB-Bio, Cat# ZA-0508; RRID: AB_2890107), and rat anti-human HIF-1α mAb (1:400, Proteintech, Cat# 20960–1 AP; RRID: AB_10732601). After final washes, all sections were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Immunofluorescence images were acquired using matched spectral filters on the Akoya Mantra 2 Quantitative Pathology Workstation (RRID:SCR_023772). To quantify target cell numbers, three representative high-power fields (×200 magnification) were selected from each of six cases. Target cells were counted within each randomly selected field, and the average count per case was calculated. Mean fluorescence intensity of HIF-1α was analyzed using ImageJ (RRID:SCR_003070).

Preparation of cobalt chloride stock solution and hypoxia treatment

A 100 mM cobalt chloride (CoCl2) stock solution (MCE, Cat# HY-W019980D) was prepared in sterile distilled water and diluted in culture medium to achieve the desired concentrations. Single-cell suspensions derived from fresh specimens were treated with 200 µM CoCl₂ and incubated for 24 hours at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂ (n=10). After incubation, cells were stained for flow cytometric analysis using anti-CD45 mAb, anti-CD8α mAb, and anti-CD39 mAb. The antibody panel was identical to that described in Flow cytometry. In parallel experiments, single-cell suspensions were treated with 50 ng/mL recombinant human HIF-1α protein (MCE, Cat# HY-P74888). Flow cytometry was subsequently employed to assess the effect of HIF-1α on CD39 expression in CD8+ T cells.

For western blot analysis (n=3), total protein was extracted from CoCl₂-treated cells using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, P0013B, China), and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, P0012S, China). A total of 30 µg protein was loaded per lane, separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and transferred electrophoretically onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Merck Millipore, USA). Primary antibodies, including anti-α-tubulin (1:10,000, Proteintech, Cat# 66031–1 Ig, RRID: AB_11042766) and anti-HIF-1α (1:5,000, Proteintech, Cat# 20960–1 AP, RRID: AB_10732601), were diluted in blocking buffer to their working concentrations. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight (16 hours), washed three times with Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween (TBST), and then incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies for 2 hours. Following additional washes with TBST, membranes were developed using NBT/BCIP substrate (AMRESCO). Protein signals were visualized and captured using an E-Gel Imager (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (RRID:SCR_003070).

In vitro CD39 inhibition assay

Single cells derived from fresh patient specimens (n=13) were incubated with 100 µM CD39 inhibitor POM-1 (Sigma-Aldrich) or vehicle control for 2 hours at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator.13 17 Following incubation, cells were stained with antibodies against cell surface markers, including anti-CD45, anti-CD8α, and anti-CD39 mAb. The antibody panel was identical to that described in Flow cytometry, and staining was performed at 4°C for 30 min in the dark. After surface staining, cells were fixed using the eBioscience Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher) for 30 min. Subsequently, cells were incubated with PE-conjugated anti-granzyme B mAb (clone QA18A28, BioLegend, Cat# 396405; RRID: AB_2801074) in permeabilization buffer (Thermo Fisher) for an additional 30 min.18 Following permeabilization and intracellular staining, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

In vitro PD-1 blockade culture

Single cells isolated from fresh specimens were resuspended in culture medium and randomly assigned to two treatment groups: (1) RPMI-1640 medium alone; and (2) RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with human anti-PD-1 mAb (10 µg/mL, Selleck, Cat# A2005, RRID: AB_3076193). Cells were incubated at 37°C for 72 hours, then harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry. Surface and intracellular staining for CD45, CD8, CD39, and granzyme B was performed as detailed in In vitro CD39 inhibition assay. An additional experimental group was included in which cells were first treated with anti-PD-1 mAb in vitro for 72 hours, followed by exposure to POM-1 to evaluate potential synergistic effects.

Assessment of immunotherapy

Antitumor efficacy was evaluated by reviewing CT scans obtained after four cycles of therapy. The same imaging protocol was applied across all patients. The change from baseline to the sum of the target lesion diameters was measured according to RECIST V.1.1 (RRID:SCR_012298).

ScRNA-seq and scTCR-seq data generation and preprocessing

ScRNA-seq was conducted on tumor samples from seven patients, yielding a total of 53,760 cells after quality control. Single-cell suspensions were processed using the 10x Genomics Chromium platform to generate 5′ gene-expression libraries and matched V(D)J (TCR) libraries according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Raw FASTQ files were processed using Cell Ranger (human reference) to generate filtered gene-by-cell unique molecular identifier (UMI) count matrices and per-cell V(D)J contig annotations, which served as inputs for downstream transcriptomic and repertoire analyses.

ScRNA-seq integration, clustering, and cell-type annotation

UMI count matrices were imported using Read10X and converted into Seurat objects. Mitochondrial gene content was calculated using PercentageFeatureSet, and cells with mitochondrial gene percentage ≥5% were excluded. Data were log-normalized and integrated across samples. Highly variable genes were identified using DUBStepR, followed by data scaling and principal component analysis for dimensionality reduction. Batch labels extracted from cell barcodes were used for batch correction with Harmony. Nearest-neighbor graphs were constructed in the corrected low-dimensional space, followed by Louvain clustering and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualization. Major cell lineages were annotated based on canonical marker gene expression.

Analysis of CD8+ T cells in relation to CD39 expression

T cells were subset and re-clustered in Harmony space, with putative outlier clusters removed prior to final re-clustering. The CD4+, CD8+, and Treg subsets were identified using a focused marker panel (eg, CD3D/E, CD4, FOXP3, TCF7/LEF1, and cytotoxic and inhibitory receptors). Within the CD8+ compartment, CD39-high and CD39-low populations were defined based on median imputed ENTPD1/CD39 expression following MAGIC imputation. Differential gene expression analysis was performed in Seurat using log-normalized, non-imputed RNA expression data, with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing (false discovery rate <0.05). Significant genes were mapped to Entrez IDs (org.Hs.eg.db) and tested for enrichment in Gene Ontology Biological Processes, using the set of genes detected in the CD8+ subset as background. To explore the relationship between CD39 expression and CD8+ T-cell differentiation, pseudotime trajectories were inferred using Monocle2 based on highly variable ordered genes, with the trajectory root anchored to progenitor-like or naïve CD8+ states when identifiable. CD39-associated functional programs were assessed using curated gene modules (including cytotoxic granules, effector cytokines, naive/central memory T cells/memory, terminal/effector, exhaustion receptors, exhaustion transcription factors, exhausted T (Tex) progenitor-like, tissue-resident memory (TRM), early activation, and proliferation), and both module activity and gene-level associations with ENTPD1 were evaluated through correlation analyses.

TCR repertoire analyses

Filtered contig annotations were imported per sample, cell barcodes were harmonized to match the scRNA-seq object, and analyses were restricted to CD8+ T cells. Only productive, in-frame α/β TCR rearrangements were retained. Clonotypes were assembled using scRepertoire and integrated with transcriptomic profiles. Clonal architecture was summarized by clone size categories (rare, small, medium, large, and hyperexpanded) and by the proportion of repertoire space occupied by ranked clonotype bins (top 1–10, 11–100, 101–1,000, etc). Repertoire diversity and inequality were quantified using complementary indices (Shannon, inverse Simpson, Gini, and ACE), with metrics calculated within each sample and compared between CD39-low and CD39-high groups.

Statistical analysis

Differences in means±SEM were assessed using Student’s t-test, paired t-test, and Welch’s t-test as appropriate. Correlation analyses were conducted using Pearson’s correlation coefficient and linear regression. The association between response status and TIL frequency was evaluated using Fisher’s exact test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to assess the prognostic performance of TIL frequencies with respect to response status. The optimal cut-off value was determined using the Youden Index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a significance level of 5% (p<0.05) was applied. Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V.8.0 (RRID:SCR_002798).

Results

Patient characteristics

Baseline characteristics of the 53 patients with lung cancer are summarized in online supplemental Table S1. The median age was 66 years (range, 37–87 years), and 83.01% of the patients were male. Histological subtypes included 30 squamous cell carcinomas, 14 adenocarcinomas, and 9 SCLC. A total of 50.95% of patients were diagnosed with pathological stage IIIB-IV disease. Among the cohort, 30 cases (56.6%) harbored a primary lesion larger than 3 cm.

Region-specific infiltration of CD39+CD8+ T cells in human lung cancer

Since total intratumoral CD8+ T cells could not serve as a biomarker for differentiating clinical phenotypes in our experimental model (online supplemental figure S1), we further analyzed CD39-expressing CD8+ T cells in treatment-naïve lung cancer tissues to investigate the role of this cell subset in the TME (figure 1A). No significant differences in the infiltration of CD39+CD8+ T cells were observed among subgroups stratified by age, pathological type, or lesion size. Similarly, the levels of tumorous CD39+CD8+ T cells were comparable between stage II-IIIA and IIIB-IV lung cancer groups (36.12±4.53% vs 28.46±4.80%, p>0.05). However, the proportion of CD39+CD8+ T cells was higher in male patients than in female patients (35.54±3.71 vs 15.98±4.39%, p<0.05) (figure 1B).

To further characterize tumorous CD39+CD8+ T cells, we assessed their heterogeneous distribution within the TME. Peripheral and central tumor specimens were obtained via superficial tumor biopsy and intratumoral EBUS-TBNA, respectively (figure 1C). As shown in figure 1D, CD39+CD8+ T cells were detected in both peripheral and central tumor regions. Notably, this T-cell subset was more enriched in the peripheral TME compared with the central TME (36.84±3.58% vs 16.45±6.34%, p<0.01). Clinically, the frequency of tumorous CD39+CD8+ T cells in peripheral and central regions showed no correlation with other clinical parameters (figure 1E,F).

Spatial abundance of CD39+CD8+T cells and anti-PD-1 efficacy

19 patients diagnosed with NSCLC received first-line anti-PD-1 therapy (online supplemental Table S1). Given that PD-L1 TPS is a validated biomarker, we investigated whether CD39+CD8+ T cells could serve as an additional biomarker for anti-PD-1 therapy. Preliminary analysis revealed that tumorous CD39+CD8+ T cells were more abundant in regions with PD-L1 TPS ≥50% (54.54±6.53% vs 34.41±4.72%, p<0.05, figure 2A). When comparing to randomly sampled tumorous CD39+CD8+ T cells (n=19, 47.69±20.19% vs 36.28±17.02%, p>0.05), CD39+CD8+ T cells in peripheral tumor regions (n=14, 54.53±13.32% vs 24.72±4.433%, p≤0.001) exhibited significant differences in abundance between responders and non-responders to anti-PD-1 therapy (figure 2B).

We next evaluated whether CD39+CD8+ T cells could predict response to PD-1 blockade in NSCLC (figure 2C). ROC analysis was performed to determine the optimal cut-off of CD39+CD8+ T cells for predicting response to PD-1 blockade. All responders exhibited a CD39+ cell proportion exceeding 37.05% among CD8+ T cells in peripheral tumor regions, whereas this threshold was not reached in any non-responder. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) for this marker was 1.00, indicating better predictive performance relative to randomly sampled CD39+CD8+ T cells (AUC=0.6705) and PD-L1 TPS (AUC=0.7143).

Furthermore, we analyzed the association between the baseline of tumorous CD39+CD8+ T cells and the depth of response to anti-PD-1 therapy. As shown in figure 2D,E, in contrast to randomly sampled CD39+CD8+ T cells (r=−0.2241, p=0.3563), higher infiltration of tumor-peripheral CD39+CD8+ T cells was correlated with deeper clinical remission (r=−0.7345, p<0.01). These findings suggest that patients with NSCLC with elevated region-specific CD39+CD8+ T cells may achieve better treatment outcomes, and that these cells might represent an important component of the antitumor immune response.

Gradient hypoxia acts as a primary contributor to reduced region-specific CD39+CD8+ T cells

Previous findings suggest that heterogeneous hypoxia within tumors is more closely associated with tumor growth than intrinsic genetic differences. We next evaluated whether the heterogeneous distribution of CD39+CD8+ T cells correlated with hypoxic gradients and tumor size. As shown in figure 3A, the central regions of tumors with a diameter ≥3 cm exhibited higher HIF-1α expression compared with the peripheral regions, whereas similar HIF-1α levels were observed in both peripheral and central TME of tumors with a diameter <3 cm. Immunofluorescence staining again confirmed the spatial distribution pattern of tumorous CD39+CD8+ T cells within the TME, which aligned with regional HIF-1α expression (figure 3B,C). Furthermore, flow cytometric analysis revealed that the differential infiltration of CD39+CD8+ T cells between peripheral and central regions was only evident in tumors larger than 3 cm in diameter (39.98±4.68% vs 3.59±1.42%, p<0.0001), but not in smaller tumors (31.94±5.51% vs 25.64±9.56%, p>0.05; figure 3D). These data indicate that a hypoxic gradient is closely linked to region-specific infiltration of CD39+CD8+ T cells.

We then hypothesized that hypoxia has distinct effects on the continuous experience of CD8+T cells within the TME. In vivo, multiplex immunofluorescence analysis further demonstrated a mutually exclusive localization of CD39+CD8+ T cells and HIF-1α expression in tumor regions (figure 4A,B). In vitro, HIF-1α accumulation, indicative of hypoxic conditions, was validated using a CoCl₂-induced hypoxia model (figure 4C). Under these conditions, both CoCl₂ and HIF-1α overexpression significantly downregulated CD39 expression on CD8+ T cells, leading to a marked reduction in the frequency of CD39+CD8+ T cells (figure 4D,E). Collectively, these results suggest that regional hypoxia plays a regulatory role in shaping the heterogeneous infiltration of CD39+CD8+ T cells within the NSCLC TME. Secondary loss of CD39+CD8+ T cells following PD-1 blockade

Given that most responders exhibited higher baseline levels of CD39+CD8+ T cells in the TME, we investigated whether these cells persisted after anti-PD-1 therapy. We analyzed a publicly available paired pre-immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) and post-ICI scRNA-seq dataset from patients with NSCLC (GSE179994).19 No significant changes were detected in either mean ENTPD1 (CD39) expression or the proportion of CD39+ cells among total CD8+ T cells following treatment (p=0.46 and p=0.38, respectively; online supplemental figure S2A). Pseudobulk paired analysis corroborated these findings, showing no significant change in ENTPD1 expression (p=0.31; online supplemental figure S2B). Thus, paired pre-ICI and post-ICI data do not support a global reduction in CD39/ENTPD1 expression across the entire CD8+ T-cell compartment.

We therefore hypothesized that ICI-induced dynamics of CD39+CD8+ T cells may reflect state-dependent or compositional remodeling rather than a uniform decline. To model clinical conditions, we stimulated single-cell suspensions from NSCLC tissues with an anti-PD-1 mAb in vitro and assessed CD39+CD8+ T-cell frequencies. As shown in figure 5, anti-PD-1 treatment reduced CD39 expression on CD8+ T cells, resulting in a significant decrease in the proportion of CD39+CD8+ T cells (28.30±8.87% vs 19.29±6.98%, p<0.05). Notably, this reduction was restricted to the tumor periphery and was not observed in CD8+ T cells derived from the tumor core. Intriguingly, samples with higher baseline levels of CD39+CD8+ T cells exhibited a more pronounced decline in this subset after anti-PD-1 treatment. These findings suggest pharmacodynamic changes in the CD39+CD8+ T-cell fraction that may reflect differences between responding and non-responding TMEs, specifically within the tumor-peripheral region rather than across the entire TME.

CD39 identifies a functionally distinct subset of CD8+ T cells in NSCLC tumors

UMAP analysis identified six major cell populations among the 53,760 cells: T cells, myeloid cells, natural killer cells, B cells, epithelial cells, and neutrophils. ENTPD1 (CD39) was expressed in 3,763 cells (7%) and was predominantly detected in myeloid cells, B cells, and a subset of T cells (online supplemental figure S3A and B). Given the established role of CD39+CD8+ T cells in immunotherapy, we further characterized their molecular features using scRNA-seq. In NSCLC specimens, single-cell transcriptomic profiling of tumor-infiltrating T cells revealed a distinct cellular architecture on UMAP, clearly segregating CD4+ T cells from CD8+ T cells (figure 6A). Projection of CD39 (ENTPD1) expression onto this embedding revealed a highly heterogeneous distribution, with ENTPD1 expression restricted to a specific subset of T cells (figure 6B). To investigate the relationship between CD39 expression and CD8+ T-cell differentiation, pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed within the CD8+ T-cell compartment (figure 6C). The inferred trajectory ordered CD8+ T cells along a continuous differentiation axis, with ENTPD1 expression exhibiting dynamic, state-dependent changes across pseudotime. We further evaluated CD39-associated gene programs in CD8+ T cells through correlation analysis (figure 6D). CD39 expression correlated with cytotoxic/effector genes and, more robustly, with an exhaustion signature defined by inhibitory receptors (eg, PDCD1, LAG3, HAVCR2, TIGIT, and CTLA-4) and exhaustion-related transcription factors (TOX/TOX2 and NR4A1/2/3), as well as LAYN and CXCL13. Positive associations were also observed with progenitor-like, TRM, early activation, and proliferative gene signatures (figure 6D).

We subsequently compared CD39-high (ENTPD1high) cells with the remaining CD8+ T cells and conducted Gene Ontology enrichment analysis on genes upregulated in the ENTPD1high subset (figure 6E–H). Enriched biological processes were primarily related to immune cell interactions and effector functions, including leukocyte cell–cell adhesion, regulation of T-cell activation, T-cell receptor signaling, cell killing and leukocyte-mediated cytotoxicity, and leukocyte migration, along with antigen processing and presentation and interferon-related responses (figure 6E–H). Taken together, these findings indicate that CD39+CD8+ T cells represent a transcriptionally distinct state within the CD8+ T-cell differentiation continuum, co-expressing programs associated with both effector activation and functional exhaustion in NSCLC tumors.

CD39+ fraction was identified as a responsive CD8+T cell subset to PD-1 blockade

We hypothesized that CD39+CD8+T cells represent a fundamental CD8+T cell subset responsive to PD-1 blockade. First, as shown in figure 7A, CD39+CD8+T cells exhibited enhanced cytotoxic potential following treatment with the CD39 inhibitor POM-1 compared with CD39−CD8+ T cells, as indicated by a higher frequency of granzyme B+ cells (36.32±2.69% vs 29.47±3.41%, p<0.01). Specifically, anti-PD-1 treatment selectively increased granzyme B production in the CD39+ subset within the total CD8+T cell population (42.29±6.69% vs 35.98±8.15%, p<0.05, figure 7B), supporting its role as a predominant CD8+ T-cell subpopulation responsive to PD-1 blockade. However, combination treatment with anti-PD-1 and POM-1 did not enhance granzyme B production synergistically, likely due to a reduction in the proportion of CD39+ cells among CD8+ T cells induced by anti-PD-1 mAb, thereby attenuating the effect of CD39 inhibition (39.43±6.17% vs 43.25±4.89%, p>0.05, figure 7C). Furthermore, as shown in figure 7D, CD39+CD8+ T cells expressed significantly higher levels of PD-1 than CD39− counterparts (54.21±4.00% vs 16.18±2.77%, p<0.0001). Notably, CD39+CD8+T cells from the tumor-peripheral region exhibited higher PD-1 expression than those from the central TME (59.83±3.40% vs 44.83±7.45%, p<0.05). This pattern suggests that CD39+CD8+ T cells located in non-hypoxic regions may serve as a more favorable target for anti-PD-1 mAb. Collectively, these data indicate that CD39 is not merely a marker defining a CD8+ T-cell subset, but identifies a functionally responsive population through which anti-PD-1 therapy exerts its effects.

TCR repertoire analysis revealed clonal skewing in CD39+CD8+ T cells

To assess whether CD39 marks clonally expanded T cells, we compared the TCR repertoires of CD39low and CD39high subsets. Clonal composition analysis revealed a shift in CD39high cells toward higher-frequency clone categories, along with a greater proportion of the repertoire occupied by the top-ranked clonotypes (figure 7E,F). Consistently, CD39high cells display reduced repertoire diversity (lower Shannon, inverse Simpson, and ACE indices) and increased clonal inequality (higher Gini index) compared with CD39low cells (figure 7G), indicating a more oligoclonal/expanded TCR repertoire in the CD39high state.

Discussion

In this study, we identify a novel Pandora-like pathway associated with resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy. Initial gradient hypoxia contributes to the spatially heterogeneous distribution of CD39+CD8+ T cells within TME; a decline in these cells may subsequently contribute to diminished response to immunotherapy. Moreover, the reduction of CD39+CD8+T cells following PD-1 blockade may represent a potential mechanism of acquired resistance to immunotherapy, even when oxygen deprivation is alleviated during tumor regression (online supplemental figure 4).

Prior studies on tumor heterogeneity have provided critical insights into cancer management. Heterogeneous immune infiltration across intratumoral, peritumoral, and stromal compartments has been linked to distinct clinical outcomes in melanoma and breast cancer.2022 Similarly, our study characterizes regional heterogeneity in the distribution of CD39+CD8+ T cells within NSCLC, showing significant enrichment in tumor-peripheral regions compared with the tumor core. Consistently, previous reports indicate that PD-1+Tim-3+CD8+ tissue-resident memory T cells preferentially localize at the invasive margin, and reduced spatial interactions of these cells are independently associated with lung cancer recurrence.23 These findings corroborate our results, collectively suggesting that the spatial heterogeneity of CD39+CD8+T cell distribution within the NSCLC TME expands the conceptual framework of spatial tumor immunology.

As reported, Tian et al demonstrated that CD8+ T-cell infiltration is significantly reduced in larger tumors.24 Tumors containing tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) exhibit higher proportions of precursor exhausted T cells compared with those without TLS, suggesting a critical role of cellular microenvironment in antitumor immune responses.25 Furthermore, hypoxic conditions not only promote exhaustion-like phenotypes through the generation of reactive oxygen species, but also rapidly induce T-cell exhaustion when combined with persistent TCR stimulation.26 27 We therefore hypothesized that regional hypoxia correlates with the extent of CD39+CD8+ T-cell infiltration. In this study, regional hypoxia was identified as a condition shaping CD39+CD8+ T cells within the TME of NSCLC, and HIF-1α was shown to directly downregulate CD39 expression on CD8+ T cells in vitro. More pronounced hypoxia in the cores of larger tumors may partly explain why spatial heterogeneity in CD39+CD8+ T-cell distribution is observed exclusively in tumors exceeding 3 cm in diameter. Supporting this, Giatromanolaki et al confirmed that CD73 and CD39 expression correlates with HIF-1α, contributing to an immunosuppressive feedback loop.28 Collectively, these findings extend current understanding of how regional hypoxia influences CD8+ T-cell differentiation, potentially playing a central role in impairing immunotherapy efficacy via modulation of CD39+CD8+ T cells.

Theoretically, as tumors regress in response to immunotherapy, local hypoxic conditions should improve, thereby preventing further reduction in CD39+CD8+ T cells. However, resistance to anti-PD-1 mAb therapy inevitably develops in clinical practice, indicating that additional acquired mechanisms underlie therapeutic resistance. We thus investigated whether tumorous CD39+CD8+ T cells would decline following anti-PD-1 mAb treatment. Due to ethical limitations in acquiring post-treatment tumor biopsies, we first sought publicly available human paired pre-ICI and post-ICI datasets with region-specific annotations as an alternative. To date, only one such publicly available paired pre-ICI/post-ICI scRNA-seq dataset from human NSCLC (GSE179994) has been identified, in which no significant changes in ENTPD1 expression or the frequency of CD39+ cells were observed after treatment. Based on our observations regarding spatial distribution of CD39+CD8+ T cells, we propose that ICI-associated CD39 dynamics are more likely reflected in compositional remodeling rather than a uniform reduction across the entire CD8+ T-cell compartment. Notably, the reduction in CD39+CD8+ T cells following anti-PD-1 treatment occurred specifically in the tumor-peripheral region, but not in the tumor core, indicating that these changes are context-dependent (eg, regionally or compositionally restricted) and not universally applicable.

Despite existing biomarker-based stratification approaches, identifying reliable biomarkers to predict immunotherapy response remains a major unmet clinical need.29 Previous studies have shown that CD39 is highly expressed in neoantigen-reactive CD8+ T cells and may serve as a predictive biomarker for response to PD-1 or PD-L1 blockade.16 30 31 Duhen et al reported that CD103+CD39+CD8+ T cells are enriched for tumor-specific T-cell receptor clones across multiple solid tumors and retain cytotoxic functionality through granzyme B and perforin expression.14 In this study, we further suggest that region-specific CD39+CD8+ T cells may serve as a potentially more informative indicator of favorable post-treatment outcomes compared with total CD39+CD8+ T cells. Assessing the proportion of regionally defined CD39+CD8+ T cells within the TME may enhance patient stratification when used alongside PD-L1 TPS prior to immunotherapy initiation. Nevertheless, the limited sample size of patients receiving anti-PD-1 therapy and potential confounding factors should be acknowledged, as they may affect the generalizability of these findings .32 33 Hence, large-scale prospective cohort studies are warranted to validate region-specific CD39+CD8+ T cells as a reliable biomarker for immunotherapy response.

Collectively, these findings suggest that CD39+CD8+ T cells are likely to be one of the potential elements involved in the antitumor immune response reinvigorated by anti-PD-1 mAb. The co-expression pattern of CD39 and PD-1 appears to have significant implications, with CD39+CD8+ T cells potentially representing the predominant population targeted by PD-1 blockade. Consistent with the above observations, CD39+CD8+ T cells were selectively depleted by anti-PD-1 mAb in vitro, further supporting the hypothesis that the CD39+ subset constitutes a primary target among CD8+ T cells modulated by anti-PD-1 therapy. Additionally, CD39+CD8+ T cells were characterized as a transcriptionally distinct state within the CD8+ T-cell differentiation continuum, marked by coordinated expression of activation/effector programs, dysfunction-associated signatures, and a more oligoclonal or expanded TCR repertoire, further underscoring their identity as a unique subpopulation of CD8+ T cells playing a critical role in ICI-mediated antitumor immunity. However, it should be noted that in vitro lung cancer tissue cultures may not fully recapitulate the long-term effects of in vivo treatment.

Taken together, our results indicate that region-specific CD39+CD8+ T cells are not only candidate indicators and regulators of anti-PD-1 therapeutic efficacy but also integral components of the complex spatial immune architecture and resistance pathway within TME. Although anti-PD-1 therapy promotes tumor regression and alleviates hypoxia along with a condition facilitating CD39+CD8+ T-cell infiltration, it concurrently leads to the downregulation or depletion of this specific cell population. This dual effect may partially explain why some initial responders eventually develop resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy, suggesting that strategies aimed at preserving or sustaining CD39+CD8+ T cells would represent a viable approach to overcoming ICI resistance in NSCLC.
