Subtle Genetic Shifts of African Swine Fever Virus Among Vietnamese Domestic Swine Following Live‐Attenuated Vaccine Commercialization
Authors: Thanh Che Nguyen, Phakawat Tantitaveewattana, Nakarin Pamornchainavakul, Duy Tien Do, Roongtham Kedkovid, Roongroje Thanawongnuwech
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Since its first incursion in 2019, African swine fever (ASF) has remained a constant threat to the Vietnamese swine industry. The nationwide commercialization and implementation of live‐attenuated vaccines (LAVs), alongside depopulation‐repopulation efforts, necessitate a critical assessment of whether these strategies have driven inadvertent changes in circulating African swine fever virus (ASFV) strains. In this study, molecular surveillance of ASFV core genes was conducted in pigs from non‐vaccinated farms across Central and Southern Vietnam, comparing strains before and after LAVs’ introduction. The results confirmed spillover and ongoing field circulation of LAV‐related strains and revealed a marked increase in genetic diversity, encompassing both vaccine‐like and novel wild‐type microvariants in the post‐vaccine commercialization phase. Notably, these microvariants harbored non‐synonymous single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and large segmental deletions within the EP153R-EP402R gene, suggesting potential alterations in viral function. Most compellingly, a unique 15‐AGACCAGCAACAAAC‐nucleotide repeat in the E183L gene resulted in a five‐amino‐acid insertion (Arg‐Pro‐Ala‐Thr‐Asn) in the p54 protein, serving as a key marker for Vietnamese strains. Initial in silico analysis indicated that this novel p54 protein contained a longer α‐helix structure, which could influence viral movement and interactions with host cells. Overall, these subtle yet significant genetic shifts imply that ASFV might be rapidly evolving intra‐host under selective pressure from widespread immunization and/or diverse introduction sources during repopulation, potentially enabling it to evade the host immune system. This highlights the essential need for robust biosecurity protocols, stringent regulatory oversight of LAV deployment, and the required genetic surveillance. For diagnosis and management, the urgent development of reliable DIVA (Differentiating Infected and Vaccinated Animals) tools and real‐time, adaptable molecular identification methods, coupled with a vaccination‐tracking system, is necessary for accurate and sustained ASF control.

1. Introduction

Pork is a fundamental protein source in most Asian cuisines, with China and the Southeast Asian region alone accounting for over half of global pork consumption [1]. However, emerging and re‐emerging infectious diseases have profoundly impacted the swine industry, threatening both regional food security and international commerce [2]. Indeed, the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH) has reported widespread outbreaks of African swine fever (ASF), a ruinous disease in domestic and wild pigs, across Asia since 2018, resulting in massive livestock losses and market disruptions [3].

African swine fever virus (ASFV), the sole etiological agent of ASF, is a sizable double‐stranded DNA (dsDNA) virus enclosed within a sophisticated architectural envelope. Its virion comprises a nucleoprotein core, a core–shell structure, an inner lipid layer, an icosahedral capsid, and an outer envelope membrane. The viral genome exceeds 194 kilobases and is divided into a central conserved region (CCR), flanked by the left variable region (LVR) and the right variable region (RVR). These variable regions harbor multi‐gene families (MGFs) with a high mutation rate, a key contributor to ASFV’s significant genetic diversity [4]. Its estimated substitution rate was approximately 10−4 substitutions/site/year, slightly higher than that of other dsDNA viruses [5].

Genetic characterization approaches for ASFV continue to evolve. Traditionally, ASFV has been classified into 24 genotypes (I‐XXIV) based on analysis of a 478‐base‐pair (bp) fragment at the 3′‐end of the B646L gene, which encodes the p72 structural protein [6]. The reliability of this method is limited because it relies on only a short fragment relative to the large, complex ASFV genome. Alternatively, using multiple marker genes simultaneously, such as E183L, EP402R, B602L, and I73R/I329L, may provide improved resolution for molecular epidemiology studies of ASFV [7]. The EP402R gene, which encodes the CD2v‐like protein responsible for viral hemadsorption, has also been used to serotype ASFV into eight serogroups (1–8). An adjacent gene to EP402R, the EP153R gene, features similarities to the C‐type lectin‐like protein, which is responsible for viral persistence. Mutations in these genes were associated with a partial reduction in viral virulence and were consequently applied in vaccine development [8]. The central variable region (CVR) of the B602L gene remains unclear with respect to its function; however, its sequence length ranges from 228 to 686 bp and contains tandem repeat sequences (TRSs) of tetrameric amino acids, which are valuable for sub‐genotyping [9, 10]. A p54 structural transmembrane protein, encoded by the E183L gene, plays a role in virion movement and host interaction and serves as a supplementary marker that distinguishes p54 sub‐lineages within a p72‐classifying genotype [11]. Tracking the origin of the viral strain and identifying entry routes into new areas by analyzing a TRS spanning many nucleotides in the intergenic region (IGR) or coding genes is the most effective approach. For instance, the TRS of 10‐GGAATATATA‐nucleotide in the non‐coding IGR between the I37R and I329L genes categorized ASFV genotype II into four clusters: IGR‐I to IGR‐IV [12].

Since its emergence in 2019, ASF in Vietnam has transitioned from a severe epidemic to an endemic situation, likely influenced by strengthened nationwide biosecurity measures and, possibly, by vaccine implementation. The initial epidemic in 2019 occurred in the northern part of the country, resulting in the loss of nearly 6 million pigs, representing a 25.5% reduction in the national herd and an estimated economic loss of approximately US$573 million [13]. In mid‐2020, the Vietnamese government issued a national plan for controlling the disease, emphasizing both on‐farm and regional biosecurity measures, coupled with depopulation‐repopulation strategies [14], which steered the country’s ASF situation toward an endemic stage. In July 2022, two live‐attenuated vaccines (LAVs), NAVET ASFVAC and AVAC ASF LIVE, were introduced for prophylaxis in the field. Both vaccines are based on the Georgia 2007/1 backbone, with NAVET ASFVAC carrying a deletion of the I177L gene (ASFV‐G‐∆I177L) and AVAC ASF LIVE carrying a deletion of six MGF360/505 genes (ASFV‐G‐∆MGF) [15]. The introduction of these LAVs represents a significant milestone in combating endemic ASF in Vietnam. During 2022–2023, despite ongoing disease control strategies, ASF epicenters shifted toward the Central and Southern regions [16], while concerns about the vaccines’ potential drawbacks, including spillover [17] and virulent reversion [18], began to rise. As a result, ASF in Vietnam continues to pose a substantial challenge, with the transition to endemic status not yet complete.

These considerations underscore the current lack of information on the ASF situation in Vietnam’s Central and Southern regions, particularly following changes in the domestic swine population and the introduction of commercial LAVs. This study aimed to (1) evaluate the potential circulation of vaccine‐related strains in non‐vaccinated animals and (2) investigate the molecular epidemiology and ASFV genetic diversity using a multi‐marker gene approach. To our knowledge, this represents the first analysis of ASFV genome characterization in core genes across multiple field‐driven samples from the Central and Southern regions following the commercialization of LAVs in Vietnam.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Sample Sources and Collection

Vietnam comprises three major geographic regions: the Northern (Northeast, Northwest, and Red River Delta), the Central (North Central Coast, South Central Coast, and Central Highlands), and the Southern (Southeast and Mekong Delta). As of July 1, 2025, Vietnam’s provincial‐level administrative subdivisions were reorganized into 34 provinces/municipalities. However, this study was rolled out before 2025; the provincial designations used here follow the former structure, which included 63 provinces/municipalities.

A total of 298 swine samples collected before and after the introduction of LAVs (mid‐2022) were included in this study. Pre‐vaccine introduction samples (n = 10), collected between 2019 and 2020, were kindly provided by the Vietnamese Swine Research Team, Nong Lam University, Ho Chi Minh City (NLU‐HCMC), Vietnam. Post‐vaccine introduction samples (n = 288) were obtained from repositories of ASF‐positive cases maintained by various diagnostic service laboratories. These samples were originally collected between late 2022 and 2023 from ASFV‐experienced farms across 11 provinces in Vietnam’s Central and Southern regions. All sampled farms had no history of ASF vaccination. The geographic distribution and sample counts for each province are presented in Figure 1A and Supporting Information 1, respectively.

2.2. Sample Processing and Quality Control

Viral DNA from pig samples and vaccines was extracted using the IndiSpin Pathogen Kit (Indical Bioscience GmbH, Leipzig, Germany) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The quantity and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed by measuring the A260/280 ratio using an Epoch2NS Microplate Spectrophotometer Reader and Gen5 Microplate Software (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). DNA extraction was performed at the Department of Infectious Diseases and Veterinary Public Health laboratory, Faculty of Animal Science and Veterinary Medicine, NLU‐HCMC, Vietnam. The extracted DNA was stored at −80°C and then transported to the Chulalongkorn University Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory in Bangkok, Thailand, for downstream analyses.

To confirm the presence of ASFV DNA, a quantitative PCR (qPCR) assay targeting the ASFV p72‐coding gene was performed [19]. Only samples with a cycle threshold (Ct) value below 35 were included in subsequent analyses.

2.3. ASFV Genotyping and Identification of Vaccine‐Like Strains

Conventional PCR (cPCR) assays were employed for ASFV genotyping and identification of vaccine‐like strains. Genotyping was done using a standard protocol targeting a 478‐bp C‐terminal fragment of the B646L gene [6]. Vaccine‐like strains were identified when the virulence genes (MGF505/360 or I177L) were replaced with marker genes (mCherry or β-GUS). Vaccine‐like strain detection was conducted using two cPCR assays designed to amplify the unique insertion/deletion regions of the ASFV‐G‐∆MGF (GenBank accession no. PP529961) and ASFV‐G‐∆I177L (GenBank accession no. PV067679) vaccine viruses. A previously described protocol was used for the identification of ASFV‐G‐∆MGF‐related strains [17], whereas a new primer pair (designed in this study) targeting a 730‐bp fragment spanning the junction between the partially deleted I177L gene and the inserted mCherry gene was used for the identification of ASFV‐G‐∆I177L‐related strains (Figure 2A). Optimization of the cPCR assays for detecting ASFV‐G‐∆I177L‐related strains primarily focused on adjusting the annealing temperature.

2.4. Determining ASFV Genetic Diversification Using Molecular Markers

To evaluate ASFV genetic diversification, five viral genomics regions were analyzed in a subset of samples randomly selected to represent all 11 provinces and both pre‐ and post‐vaccine introduction periods. The five regions included (1) the full‐length E183L gene [11]; (2) the 10‐bp TRS in the IGR between the I73R and I329L genes [20]; (3) the full‐length EP153R gene; (4) the full‐length EP402R gene; and (5) the TRS of tetrameric amino acids in the CVR of the B602L gene.

The cPCR assays were performed to amplify the target DNA with primer details provided in Table 1. All cPCR reactions were carried out using a 96‐well Mastercycler Nexus (Eppendorf SE, Hamburg, Germany). Reactions were prepared using OneTaq Quick‐Load 2X Master Mix with Standard Buffer (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Each 25 μL reaction contained 12.5 μL of ready‐to‐use master mix (1.8 mM MgCl2 and 0.2 mM dNTPs), 0.2 μM of each primer, 500 ng of DNA template, and nuclease‐free water.

The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 94°C for 30 sec, 40 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 20 sec, annealing at a temperature specific to each primer pair for 15 sec (details in Table 1), and extension at 68°C for 30–60 sec, followed by a final extension at 68°C for 10 min. Amplicons were resolved on a 1.5% agarose gel stained with RedSafe Nucleic Acid stain (iNtRON Biotechnology Inc., Gyeonggi‐do, South Korea) and visualized under UV illumination. Positive and specific bands were excised and purified using the Nucleospin Gel and PCR Clean‐up Kit (Macherey‐Nagel GmbH & Co. KG, Duren, Germany).

2.5. Sequencing and Phylogenetic Trees Analysis

Amplicons were submitted to a third‐party service laboratory (Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) for sequencing using the FastNGS platform. Briefly, the PCR products were fragmented using S‐TN5 transposase and incubated at 55°C for 10 min. The fragmented DNA was then used to construct PCR‐based sequencing libraries with the S‐1.2x‐TN5 library preparation system, followed by purification via gel extraction. The purified libraries underwent quality control assessment and were subsequently sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq short‐read platform using the MiSeq Reagent Micro Kit V2 (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Retrieved raw sequencing reads were preprocessed using various tools available in Geneious Prime v2025.2.2 [21]. Default parameters were applied for all tools unless otherwise specified. Raw reads were quality‐controlled with fastp v0.20.1 [22] and subsequently mapped to the ASF reference genome (Georgia 2007/1, GenBank accession no. FR682468) with Minimap2 v2.24 [23]. Consensus sequences were automatically calculated and generated by Geneious Prime after the mapping process.

Genetic variations, or single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), within the five targeted genomic regions were analyzed. The consensus sequences obtained from the mapping process were compared with ASFV reference sequences and related strains available in GenBank using the “Find Variants/SNPs” function in Geneious Prime. Detected mutations within the target regions were subsequently verified by Sanger sequencing (Bionic Co., Ltd., Seoul, South Korea).

Further insights into the genetic relationships among ASFV strains were obtained through phylogenetic analysis. Nucleotide sequences from the consensus and reference strains were aligned using Multiple Alignment using Fast Fourier Transform (MAFFT, v7.490) [24]. Maximum‐likelihood (ML) phylogenetic trees for the partial B646L, EP402R, and full‐length E183L genes were constructed employing IQ‐TREE v1.6.12 [25] with the ultrafast bootstrap statistical resampling method (UFBoot2) [26], based on 1000 replicates to ascertain the robustness of each branch or node. ModelFinder was used to search for and select the best‐fit model for each ML tree, based on the Bayesian information criterion [27]. The Kimura 2‐parameter substitution model with gamma‐distributed rates (K2P + G4) was applied for the B646L and E183L genes. For the EP402R gene, a Tamura 3‐parameter model combined a gamma distribution (TNe + G4) to model rate heterogeneity among sites prior to phylogeny. All phylogenetic trees were visualized and annotated using FigTree v1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/, accessed on July 28, 2025).

2.6. B Lymphocyte Epitopes and Protein Structure Prediction of the Novel p54 Protein

The amino acid sequence of a novel p54 protein was translated from the obtained E183L gene nucleotide sequence. Subsequently, an in silico approach to antibody epitope prediction analysis using the B‐Cell Epitope Prediction Tools, incorporated in Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) and Tools Analyze Resources v2.28 (https://www.iedb.org/, accessed on August 9, 2025). The linear B lymphocyte antigenic propensity regions were predicted from the primary protein sequence using the BepiPred‐2.0 Sequential B‐Cell Epitope Predictor [28]. The validated and best protein model was selected to predict discontinuous B‐cell epitopes using the ElliPro tool, with default settings of 0.5 and 6 for the minimum score and maximum distance (Å), respectively [29].

To further investigate the biological features of a novel p54 protein, several computer‐based tools were utilized for analysis. Hydrophilic amino acid residues, which are exposed on the protein’s surface, were measured with Relative Solvent Accessibility (RSA) scores using SABLE v2.0 (https://sable.cchmc.org, accessed on August 21, 2025). Domain, secondary, and three‐dissemination (3D) protein structures were predicted using STRIDE [30], AlphaFold v3.0 [31], and I‐TASSER [32], respectively. The ERRAT method [33], integrated into the SAVES v6.1 web‐based tool (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu, accessed on September 12, 2025) and ProSA (https://prosa.services.came.sbg.ac.at/prosa.php, accessed on September 15, 2025), was used to validate the predicted protein structures. Subsequently, the tertiary structure with the acceptable prediction was visualized using the Mol ∗3D Viewer (RSCB PDB, https://www.rcsb.org/3d-view, accessed on September 15, 2025). A protein structure encoded by the E183L gene of the wild‐type ASFV strain Georgia 2007 (UniProtKB accession no. B2MUM6) served as a standard [34].

2.7. Mapping and Data Analysis

Geographical distribution maps of the respective ASFV variants for each targeted gene were generated utilizing the MAPOG online service (https://www.mapog.com, accessed on September 25, 2025), which operates under an OpenStreetMap License and was polished with Microsoft PowerPoint. The data for the analyzed samples were aggregated and illustrated at the commune level. Descriptive statistics were analyzed using Prism v9.5.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

3. Results

3.1. Verification of ASFV‐Positive Samples

Of 298 DNA samples from service laboratories, the OIE‐recommended qPCR assays confirmed the presence of ASFV DNA in 257 samples after reexamination. Ct values among positive samples ranged from 14.9 to 38.0, with a median of 26.3. The interquartile range (IQR), which encompasses the middle 50% of the data, spanned from the 25th percentile (Q1) of 23.3 to the 75th percentile (Q3) of 29.8 (IQR = 6.5) (Figure 1B). The quality control process qualified 245 of 257 (95.3%) samples with Ct values below 35; these samples were subsequently used for downstream analyses.

3.2. ASFV Genotyping by Phylogenetic Analysis of the Partial B646L Gene

All 245 samples and two LAVs were classified as genotype II ASFV based on phylogenetic analysis of the C‐terminal region of the B646L gene. In the ML tree containing representative sequences from 24 ASFV genotypes, these analyzed samples clustered with the genotype II Georgia 2007/1 isolate, along with Chinese and other Southeast Asian isolates, sharing 100% nucleotide identity (Supporting Information 2). In the current study, neither genotype I nor an emerging recombinant genotype I/II was detected by B646L gene analysis, as the existing recombinant contains a p72‐based genotype I.

3.3. Vaccine‐Like Strains Identification

Six of 245 samples were identified as vaccine‐like strains from non‐ASF‐vaccinated farms in three of 11 provinces. To determine vaccine‐like variants, two primer pairs targeting the unique insertion/deletion regions of the two ASF vaccines were used, one of which was newly designed in this study for detecting ASFV‐G‐∆I177L‐related strains. cPCR assay and subsequent Sanger sequencing of the NAVET ASFVAC vaccine confirmed the specificity of this new primer pair (Figure 2). Among the 245 screened samples, six (2.4%) were detected as vaccine‐like strains (based on the absence of virulence genes and the presence of marker genes), including four (1.6%) related to ASFV‐G‐∆MGF and two (0.8%) related to ASFV‐G‐∆I177L (Table 2). Metadata analysis revealed that five of these vaccine‐like strains were submitted to service laboratories in late 2023, originating from regions with high pig density, including Dong Nai and Binh Duong provinces, except for one sample (23/DNG_62) from the Central Highlands (Figure 3A).

3.4. ASFV Genetic Diversity Analysis

From the 245 ASFV‐positive samples, 64 (26.1%) were selected for genetic diversity analysis across five genomic regions. The selected set comprised (1) 58 non‐vaccine‐like strains randomly chosen to represent all 11 provinces and both pre‐ and post‐vaccine introduction periods, and (2) all six vaccine‐like strains. Two commercial LAVs were also included for comparison with the genetic profiles of the vaccine seed references. Overall, low genetic diversity was observed in samples from 2019 to 2020 (before LAVs’ introduction), whereas multiple variants were detected in those collected during 2022–2023 (after LAVs’ introduction). Additionally, the genetic features of commercial LAVs were consistent with those of vaccine seeds (Tables 2 and 3). The GenBank accession numbers of these sequences are provided in the Data Availability section and Supporting Informations 3 and 4.

3.4.1. Phylogeny of the E183L Gene

The E183L gene, a supportive intragenotype gene, was amplified by cPCR for subsequent sequencing. Accounted for 95.3% (61/64) of the samples shared 100% nucleotide similarity with the predominant Eurasian strain, classified as genotype IIa (Table 2 and Figure 4A). Remarkably, in the other three samples, a 15‐AGACCAGCAACAAAC‐nucleotide sequence was inserted at position 343 of the gene, leading to a threefold increase of the 15‐bp TRS, whereas other genotype II references showed only a twofold increase. As a result of translation, the p54 protein length was up to 189 amino acids, caused by a five‐amino acid RPATN insertion (Figure 4B). It was identified as a unique sequence of the E183L gene among published ASFV isolates in the NCBI database. Conclusively, this novel variant was a characteristic genetic feature of the genotype IIa lineage, but, based solely on the E183L gene tree, it does not cause a major, deep‐branching separation within genotype IIa (Figure 4A). There was no variability in the E183L gene in commercial LAVs and vaccine‐like variants.

The epidemiology of the novel E183L gene was retrospectively investigated. The results pointed out that the first detection (22/DLK_12) occurred in Dak Lak Province, with sampling conducted in December 2022. 3.4.2. Analysis of the EP153R Gene and Phylogeny of the EP402R Gene

The combination of the EP153R and EP402R genes is responsible for ASFV hemadsorption and plays a crucial role in viral virulence, persistence, and immune evasion. Therefore, these two genes of the Central and Southern ASFV strains were comprehensively studied. A 90‐nucleotide sequence in the central region of the EP402R gene was analyzed for serotyping, as previously described [35]. An ML phylogenetic tree revealed that all 64 samples and two commercial LAVs clustered into serogroup 8, consistent with the circulating strains in Vietnam and neighboring countries (Figure 5A).

Sequencing analysis of samples collected in 2023 exposed multiple microvariants in either the EP153R or EP402R gene, or in both genes simultaneously, unlike the Georgia 2007/1 strain (Tables 2 and 3). Geographical analysis revealed a broad distribution of these variants, seven of eleven (63.6%) investigated provinces exhibited at least one variant (Figure 3C). For the single EP153R gene, there was an A‐deletion (eight samples) or an A‐insertion (two samples) in the poly A tract, leading to a truncation of the C‐type lectin‐like protein (Supporting Information 5). Nine of these mutated EP153R variants conjoined additional EP402R mutations. The most common mutation observed across the four samples was a 56G >A substitution that shifted a premature stop codon (W19X). Other SNPs, such as a C/T‐deletion and an A‐insertion, were recorded in a single sample per polymorphism (Supporting Information 5). Furthermore, the EP402R gene exhibited minor deletions of fragment lengths 11‐GAATATAACTG‐nucleotide (unidentified in public databases) and 18‐TATCCCGCTACTACCCA‐nucleotide (parallel with a previously reported 2021 isolate) in 23/BP_6232 and 23/TG_446, respectively (Figures 5B and C). Sequences of the samples submitted from the same farms declared homologous mutations (data not shown). Interestingly, a 23/DN_196 variant was uniquely found to lack a large 537‐bp fragment that includes the C‐terminal EP153R and the N‐terminal EP402R genes (Figure 5D), thereby precluding classification by the applied method.

In genetic terms of vaccines, two ASFV‐G‐∆MGF vaccine‐like variants, namely 23/DN_G1, presented a TC deletion and were replaced by CT (28_29delinsCT) in the EP402R gene, resulting in a transition from Ser to Leu at the 10th residue (S10L); and 23/DNG_62 deleted an A nucleotide in the EP153R gene. Meanwhile, the 23/DN_S3 sample, an ASFV‐G‐∆I177L vaccine‐like variant, carried an A‐insertion and a T‐deletion in the EP153R and EP402R genes, respectively (Supporting Information 5). These mutations resulted in early termination of translation, and the common virulence trait could be lost.

3.4.3. CVR Analysis of the B602L Gene

This specific gene region, alongside assessments of the E183L, CP204L, and EP402R genes, which have been used as pivotal genetic markers for sub‐genotyping or finer‐resolution differentiation, was subjected to conventional sequencing employing the same sample cohort. The new primer‐based amplification indicated that all strains contained 479 nucleotides, which were later translated into 160‐amino acid sequences and analyzed. The consensus data confirmed the TRS of the 10‐tetrameric amino acid pattern BNDABNDBNAA, known as the CVR‐1 or Tet‐10a group. This outcome was congruent with the fact that only one CVR‐1 group has been circulating in Vietnam and other Southeast Asian countries (Table 4 and Supporting Information 6).

3.4.4. IGR Analysis Between the I73R and I329L Genes

Several TRSs of a specific nucleotide sequence were found in multiple IGRs across the ASFV genome, especially between the I73R and I329L genes, which was an optional evaluation to trace the source and spread of closely related circulating viruses. In this study, the findings revealed that multiple TRS‐based groups of GGAATATATA (I, II, and III) co‐circulated in the Central and Southern regions following vaccine introduction. Conversely, solely the IGR‐II group circulated during the pre‐vaccine introduction period (Tables 2 and 3). The IGR‐II group, containing three TRSs — more than an extra TRS compared to the Georgia 2007/1 strain (IGR‐I) — was the most dominant variant (53/64, 82.8%). There were seven variants (10.9%) and two LAVs belonging to the IGR‐I group, of which five were vaccine‐like, consistent with vaccine seeds; the remaining two were non‐vaccine‐like (23/BRVT_47 and 23/LD_070). With a minor prevalence of 6.3%, four IGR‐III variants characterized by four TRSs were detected. Most strikingly, an ASFV‐G‐∆I177L vaccine‐like variant, 23/DN_S3, belonged to this group, with two additional TRSs inserted compared to the vaccine seed (Figure 6). The distribution of IGR‐I and ‐III variants is illustrated in Figure 3D. In this study, no IGR‐IV variant with five TRS was found, as previously announced in the Northern pig population [36].

3.5. B‐Cell Epitope and Protein Structure Prediction of the Novel p54 Protein

Since the novel E183L gene has not been reported elsewhere, several computational methodologies were applied to analyze the function of this novel protein, including predictions of immunogenic epitopes and protein structure. The p54 protein sequence was translated, and subsequently, both linear and discontinuous B‐cell epitopes were predicted. A threshold of 0.5 (red line) identified four potential linear epitopes located in high‐antigenic propensity regions (yellow areas), based on the average epitope probability score. These predicted epitope lengths varied from 7 to 35 residues, comprising amino acids 1–7, 72−106, 119–149, and 183–189 (Figure 7A and Supporting Information 7).

Discontinuous B‐cell epitopes are generated by the juxtaposition of distant amino acid residues through protein folding. From the inner membrane p54 protein structure, the ElliPro module identified nine stretches of amino acids as conformational B‐cell epitopes, ranging in score from 0.522 to 0.981. The identified epitopes spanned a length of 3–22 amino acids, demonstrating either complete or partial overlap with linear B‐cell epitopes (Supporting Information 8).

Key structural biology properties of the novel protein were estimated in silico, encompassing the surface exposure, secondary structure, and tertiary structure. RSA is a crucial parameter in determining hydrophilicity, as it indicates whether a residue is exposed (on a hydrophilic surface, with RSA approaching 0) or buried (in a hydrophobic core, with RSA approaching 9). The RSA plot displays a portion of 19 amino acids from the N‐terminus that approaches zero, declaring that the novel p54 is a remaining transmembrane protein (Figure 7B). Meanwhile, the stretch from residues 1–23 was predicted as the extracellular domain, followed by residues 24–54 and 143–152 as transmembrane domains, and the final stretch as the cytoplasmic domain.

After validating the predicted protein structures, with an overall quality factor of 88.333 and a Z‐score of −4.23 (ideally within the range of −10 to 10), the most acceptable model was the novel p54 protein, generated using STRIDE and AlphaFold v3.0. In view of the secondary and tertiary structures, it was observed that 39.2% (74/189) of the amino acid sequence formed an alpha‐helix structure, out of which 15.3% (29/74) lie in the transmembrane domain (Figure 7C and Supporting Information 9). This finding is slightly higher than the alpha‐helix content in the p54 protein of the Georgia 2007/1 isolate, which accounts for only 28.8% (53/184). However, the amino acid residue sequence located in the transmembrane domain was identical between a novel and original protein [34] (Figures 7C and D).

4. Discussion

Since its first incursion into Vietnam, ASF has severely hindered the domestic swine industry and food security, triggering substantial economic losses and a high demand for pork supply. In response, the government and relevant authorities have continually improved and issued plans to prevent and manage ASF effectively in each outbreak phase. These efforts primarily focus on strengthening biosecurity, controlling the movement of animal and pork products, and depopulation and/or repopulation to preserve national porcine herds. Importantly, the subsequent commercialization and widespread use of Vietnamese LAVs against ASF marked a critical turning point [16]. Albeit these vaccines have proven safe and effective, various adverse clinical events have been observed in clinical practice. It was hypothesized that these field manifestations resulted from genetic shifting, as observed in other LAVs [37]. Additionally, the rapid repopulation likely affected the ASFV genetic pool [38]. Literally, a diverse range of microvariants, including low‐virulence genotypes II and I/II recombinants, has been circulating in the Northern Vietnamese swine population [39]. Therefore, an updated molecular analysis of ASFV isolates from Central and Southern Vietnam is necessary to understand the impact of LAVs’ introduction and repopulation efforts on viral diversification and to determine the risk of LAVs spillover.

A phylogenetic tree of the p72‐coding gene, constructed from 245 ASFV‐positive samples, revealed genotype relationships and showed that all samples clustered into genotype II, identical to the predominant circulating strain reported in Vietnam [40, 41]. Recombinant genotype I/II first emerged in September 2023 near the Vietnam‐China border [39]; however, this study was unable to detect the recombinant strains. The most probable reasons for this non‐detection were the sampling time point and the study area. Sample collection was completed at the end of 2023, alongside the implementation of new policies on animal movement control, which likely prevented the recombinant strain from disseminating to the sampled pig population. A national report stated that the recombinant strains were detected in the Central and Southern regions as early as September 2024 [42]. Therefore, only the ASFV p72‐genotyping class II circulated in the two examined phases and partially consolidated an early introduction of the genotype I/II recombinant to Vietnam in late 2023.

In this study, results from using specific primer amplification determined that both existing commercial LAVs could be shed into immunologically naive pig populations in 2023. This outcome confirmed the potential for and repeatability of spillover from commercial LAVs‐related strains, consistent with prior observations [17, 43]. Additionally, the prevalence of ASFV‐G‐∆I177L vaccine‐related cases was lower, likely due to the USDA’s withdrawal of its announcement following a suspiciously virulent reversion in post‐vaccinated animals [44]. This dissemination has implications for vaccine strategy: the introduction and nationwide rollout of LAVs, launched in December 2022, significantly affected the fight against ASF in Vietnam. However, following the emergence of recombinant ASFV in Vietnam, genotype II‐designed LAVs failed to protect challenged pigs and developed chronic clinical signs [45].

The primary cause for the repeatable spillover in this study, perhaps, lies in the design of LAVs and the local pork production context. Existing LAVs are generated by deleting virulent genes, thereby forming a weakened version of the natural virus. This design enables vaccine viruses to replicate in the host and shed into the environment through secretions and excretion, subsequently contacting susceptible animals [15]. This inherent risk is amplified by the Vietnamese swine production structure, which is dominated by small‐scale household farms operating in an open system with inadequate biosecurity measures, creating an ideal environment for LAV spillover. Unfortunately, the clinical details of the farms involved were not obtained in a comprehensive manner. Moreover, a reliable DIVA tool is crucial for controlling widespread vaccine viruses, yet it remains under development. Finally, limited regulatory oversight and inconsistent monitoring of vaccine administration, dosage, and post‐vaccination shedding have made enforcement challenging across thousands of small farms [46]. These factors indicate that the detected spillover may be directly attributable to the interaction between the vaccine’s nature and the vulnerable environment. Hence, vaccine spillover scenarios reiterate that robust biosecurity measures and active surveillance are essential for controlling and managing disease‐related issues after vaccination.

Since the vaccine virus can replicate, shed, and circulate among the swine population, there is a risk of genetic instability that could lead to reversion to increased virulence over time, effectively creating a new, circulating pathogenic strain [47]. Indeed, all six vaccine‐related variants were confirmed to be similar to genotype II based on analysis of the B646L, E183L, and B602L CVR genes. However, three variants (two ASFV‐G‐∆MGF and one ASFV‐G‐∆I177L) exhibited minor variations in the EP153R-EP402R gene. These changes could potentially lead to loss of hemadsorption by truncating the gene or altering its amino acid sequence, thereby interrupting protein translation and causing less severe clinical signs [8]. Interestingly, the 23/DN_S3 sample had two 10‐bp TRS inserted in the IGR of the I73R/I329L gene (assigned to IGR‐III), which is twice the number found in a vaccine virus (IGR‐I). This compensatory mutation and TRS insertion likely result from inherent biological properties of ASFV (genomic instability and replication slippage) and from intense selective pressure for a fitness advantage acting on the virus population in vivo [48]. Conclusively, the genetic features of vaccine‐related strains have undergone active adaptation in the field since implementation, representing a crucial first step toward the eventual emergence and selection of more severe mutations [17, 18, 43].

A multi‐gene approach was conducted in 58 non‐vaccine‐like samples to identify subtle differences in ASFV characterization. There was a precise disparity in the viral genetic profile between the two studied phases. In particular, in the first phase (before 2023), ASFV genetic stability was observed across all five genes (apart from the E183L gene in 22/DLK_12). These samples were 100% similar to Georgia 2007/1 and were classified as E183L genotype IIa, serotype 8, CVR‐1/Tet‐10a, and IGR‐II. During this epidemic, the single strain circulating was a highly lethal genotype II, causing infections that ranged from per‐acute to acute hemorrhagic forms with up to 100% mortality [40, 41]. Furthermore, the government’s policies aimed at eradicating susceptible/infected animals limited the virus’s circulation time within the host, preventing it from replicating, accumulating mutations, and undergoing selection pressure. This short “dead‐end” host cycle could thus restrict viral evolutionary opportunities [49]. However, the study noted that it focused on the core genes of ASFV, which are highly conserved relative to other, more variable genes, such as MGFs located in the LVR and RVR [4]. These gene sequences are recommended for further analysis within the same sample sets, if possible.

In the phase after LAVs’ introduction (2023), while B646L and B602L CVR genes remained conserved, the findings identified several microvariants in the Central and Southern swine populations, particularly in the EP153R-EP402R gene, affecting 31.3% (10/32 samples collected in 2023). Multiple non‐synonymous SNPs in these genes were identified, thereby disrupting viral function during host‐pathogen interactions. An A‐deletion in the poly A region of the EP153R gene was consistent with previous identification in a Singaporean wild boar (SG/NParks/A‐MAM‐2023, GenBank accession no. OR135685) [50] and a Chinese domestic pig (CN/GD/2022, GenBank accession no. OR290104, unpublished), leading to the absence of the EP153R open reading frame in the genome. Meanwhile, an A‐insertion within this genomic region was first identified in sample 23/BP_6232.

Other segmental mutations, ranging from 11 to 18 bp, and a larger fragment between the EP153R and EP402R genes were retrieved. Notably, this study first identified an 11‐bp deletion in the EP402R gene and a deletion at the EP153-EP402R gene junction; consistent results from other same‐farm‐derived samples further solidified this finding (data not shown). Our prior publication reported the absence of a 5′‐head CD2v‐like gene in a vaccine‐like variant, VN/sows_32005/23, which shared 99.3% nucleotide sequence similarity with the current finding [17]. However, the sample 23/DN_196 in this study did not show a vaccine relationship. It is speculated that the vaccine and the field virus may interact to rearrange or recombine their genomes, thereby allowing them to evade the host immune system — a phenomenon warranting further study. An 18‐bp (six amino acids) deletion in the EP402R gene, as identified in sample 23/TG_446, which aligned with the CanTho‐OM/2021, was isolated in 2021 from Can Tho province, a neighboring province [51]. Interestingly, compared with this strain, 23/TG_446 was classified as IGR‐II, lacking one 10‐bp TRS in the IGR I73R/I329L gene. This suggests that the existing ASFV has been disseminated to other areas and undergone genetic shifting.

Sequence alignment analysis of the IGR I73R/I329L gene showed greater diversity in the ASFV genetic pool. At least three IGR groups (I, II, and III) circulated during the covered period. This scenario may have occurred due to either viral intra‐host evolution or the diverse introduction pathways of these variants, possibly originating from population selection pressure or from various sources under repopulation strategies. Presumably, an interaction between LAVs (which are similar to IGR‐I in the vaccine backbone) and the predominant field strain is plausible. No IGR‐IV group strain has been reported among the Vietnamese swine population since its initial detection in 2021, including in this study. This observation may stem from the lowest prevalence rate, a trend reported not only in Vietnam but also in other countries [20, 36].

A significant finding in this study was the insertion of a 15‐bp TRS into the E183L gene, encoding the p54 enveloped protein — a critical component of the viral replication process. NCBI Nucleotide BLAST revealed that it shared the highest identity with the Indonesia/2022/Pig/PS.J (99%, GenBank accession no. PQ145178, unpublished) and 97.4% with the predominant Eurasian strain. The novel p54 protein contained a five‐amino‐acid repeat, Arg‐Pro‐Ala‐Thr‐Asn, increasing its length to 189 residues. To further support epidemiological studies, this 15‐bp insertion can serve as a marker for the origin of ASFV in Vietnam. The repeated amino acids could arise after multiple in vitro virus replication cycles [52], allowing rapid evolution, particularly for immune evasion or host adaptation, once the population had experienced widespread immune pressure from vaccination [53]. However, the exact mechanism by which a TRS array is duplicated within a protein‐coding region and its consequences remain to be investigated.

The p54 protein, an intramembrane constituent, facilitates viral translocation within host cells. Its manifestation during the incipient infection stage elicits a robust antigenic response, triggering a humoral immune response that can impede viral propagation [54]. Thus, an initial in silico study was conducted to investigate the novel p54 protein’s impact on antigenicity and biological protein structure. The predicted B‐cell epitopes, spanning residues 119–149, partially matched the stretch that exhibited vigorous neutralization activity, as identified in previous studies [55, 56]. Additionally, the protein’s hydrophobicity remained unchanged compared to the original protein due to the position of the TRS insertion. However, it incentivized the protein to adopt a more extensive helical structure, exceeding the protein’s tolerated range. This change could lead to functional consequences, such as alterations in virulence or in the ability to evade the host immune system. The virus could exploit this mild structural instability to trigger rapid evolution [57]. This variability in the novel p54 protein structure may influence the current immunological‐based diagnostic tools. Nevertheless, in the current study, this hypothesis was not tested due to sampling limitations, suggesting the need for a subsequent experiment.

Overall, all microvariants were observed in 2023 (post‐vaccine introduction period), speculating that their emergence was driven by repopulation from multiple sources, coupled with the nationwide LAVs rollout for prophylaxis (which imposed selective pressure on the viral genome). These endeavors resulted in a prolonged lifespan for the widely immunized pig population and the co‐circulation of multiple ASFV strains, likely creating selective pressure on the viral genome within the host environment. Consequently, ASFV is rapidly evolving to evade the host immune system and survive to virion production. It underscores that the ASFV genome is evolving, creating a complex situation that substantially challenges recent disease control efforts, diagnostic tools, and vaccine utilization. To achieve a comprehensive understanding of ASFV genome variation, these microvariants must be sequenced in greater depth to achieve whole‐genome coverage. A robust DIVA strategy, based on either molecular or immunological features, must be established as a priority to enable early detection of vaccine‐related strains in the field. Swine herd restoration must be handled carefully (with clear information about sources and epidemiological aspects), and vaccination must comply with the manufacturer’s instructions, avoiding off‐label use or overdosing. Following biosecurity control and robust active genetic surveillance to manage the circulating virus strains, thereby providing an accurate and timely disease control plan. At the national level, establishing a digitized system for tracking animal movement and vaccination records is paramount. Specifically, the system must stringently ensure that vaccinated animals and their associated products are confined to designated, high‐biosecurity quarantine areas, thereby precluding their integration into the general domestic swine trade network.

5. Conclusions

This investigation represents the initial comprehensive comparison of ASFV molecular characteristics in Central and Southern Vietnam during a pivotal shift in disease control strategies. The findings revealed considerable genetic adaptation and diversification in 2023, coinciding with the introduction of commercial LAVs. Consistent observations across various contexts, including this study, repeatedly confirm the spillover and field circulation of LAV strains, alongside the emergence of several wild‐type microvariants. Noteworthy genetic alterations include novel deletions within the EP153R-EP402R region and a unique TRS insertion in the E183L gene. These subtle genetic shifts were identified subsequent to periods of intensive herd repopulation and the commencement of widespread vaccination, suggesting that host immune pressure and co‐circulating strains are accelerating ASFV evolution in Vietnam. The persistent genetic instability of both field and vaccine strains poses a threat to the sustained efficacy of current control measures, underscoring the urgent need for enhanced surveillance, focusing on the recording, zoning, and tracking of vaccinated animals and related products.
