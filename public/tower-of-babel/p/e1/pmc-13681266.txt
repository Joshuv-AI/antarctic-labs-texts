Live births following universal single-step warming across multiple vitrification systems
Authors: Lodovico Parmegiani, Gabor Vajta, Colleen Lynch, Alessandra Arnone, Caterina Pantoli, Julia Ruggieri, Silvia Bernardi, Azzurra Rastellini, Walter Ciampaglia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo report live birth and neonatal outcomes across multiple commercial kit brands, including cross-brand combinations, thereby providing clinical validation of a universal warming method on the basis of single-step elution using 1 M nonpermeating solutes (NPS). Human blastocysts are routinely vitrified using commercial kits with minor compositional differences. Previous studies have shown that simplified single-step warming with 1 M NPS can reduce handling time and improve laboratory efficiency. The use of different vitrification kits for the cryopreservation of human gametes and embryos requires the corresponding brand-specific warming kits approved by national and supranational regulatory authorities, creating persistent logistical and economic challenges for assisted reproductive technology (ART) centers. To address these constraints, a universal warming (UW) protocol was introduced, enabling the warming of any cell or tissue regardless of the original cryopreservation system using a standardized solution containing 1 M nonpermeating solutes (NPS), such as sucrose or trehalose (1). This solution, commonly referred to as thawing solution (TS), is included in most commercially available warming kits. The UW concept has subsequently evolved to incorporate procedural refinements, including reduced volumes and simplified workflows (1). Building on the streamlined single-step rehydration approach described by Liebermann et al (2) using a specific vitrification–warming kit combination, Parmegiani et al (1) recently demonstrated that short single-step rehydration can be performed using the TS solution independently of the warming kit brand. These findings suggest that the “short protocol”, on the basis of a single exposure to 1 M NPS—defined as universal single-step warming (USSW)—represents a promising evolution of the UW strategy, enabling rapid and efficient warming of vitrified specimens regardless of kit brand, cryoprotectant composition or basal medium.

The present study aimed to complete the validation of the USSW strategy for human blastocysts by evaluating the effectiveness and safety of single-step elution across multiple vitrification and warming kit combinations, including cross-brand applications, and by reporting live birth and neonatal outcomes.

Materials and methods

This retrospective longitudinal cohort study was conducted in a private ART center and included infertile patients undergoing intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) procedure. A total of 469 vitrified–warmed blastocysts were analyzed from frozen embryo transfers (FRET) performed between January 1, 2024, and December 31, 2024. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the clinic (approval number and date: 22.02.2017). All patients participating in the cryopreservation program provided written informed consent for the use of their clinical data.

Inclusion criteria included embryos derived from fresh autologous oocytes (freeze-all cycles or supernumerary embryos after fresh transfer), transfer within one year of cryopreservation, female age ≤43 years at cryopreservation, and embryos not biopsied for preimplantation genetic testing. Cleavage-stage embryos, oocyte-donation embryos, and subsequent frozen transfers from the same patient were excluded; only the first FRET was analyzed. Blastocysts were vitrified using four commercially available kits and warmed by single-step elution in 1 M nonpermeating solutes (NPS) from the corresponding thawing solutions, generating 12 vitrification–warming combinations. Kits were obtained from Irvine Scientific (Vit Kit Freeze 90133-SO; Vit Kit Thaw 90137-SO, USA), Kitazato (vitrification media VT601; thawing media VT602, Japan), SAGE CooperSurgical (Sage vitrification kit ART-8026; Sage warming kit ART-8031, USA), and VitaVitro (Warming Kit V003004, China).

Vitrification was performed at room temperature (12–15 min in Equilibration Solution [ES] followed by 60 s in Vitrification Solution [VS] before plunging). Warming consisted of a single-step exposure in TS (1 M NPS) at 37 °C followed by a rapid rinsing in basic medium. Embryo transfer was performed after 1–2 h of incubation. Groups were defined according to vitrification and warming media (II, IK, IS, IV, KI, KK, KS, KV, SI, SK, SS, and SV). Cryosurvival, clinical pregnancy, implantation, live birth and neonatal outcomes were recorded and analyzed.

Kitazato kits contain trehalose as NPS and hydroxypropyl cellulose, whereas Sage, Irvine and VitaVitro contain sucrose as NPS. Sage and VitaVitro kits include human serum albumin, whereas Irvine kits contain dextran sulphate sodium. The basal medium is TCM-199 for Kitazato, Irvine Fujifilm, and VitaVitro kits, and modified HTF with MOPS for Sage kits. In all vitrification systems, the cryoprotectant combination consists of 7.5% dimethylsulphoxide (DMSO) plus 7.5% ethylene glycol (EG) in the equilibration solution and 15% DMSO plus 15% EG in the vitrification solution (Parmegiani et al, 2025).

Continuous variables were analyzed using the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s χ2 test (with Bonferroni qcorrection) or pairwise Fisher’s exact test when needed. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparability of study groups

Females mean age at vitrification and warming was statistically comparable between all the groups (Table 1). FRET was performed using day 5 or day 6 blastocysts. The mean number of transferred embryos was 1.1 ± 0.2 (varying from 1.0 ± 0.0 to 1.5 ± 0.7 between groups). Single-embryo transfer was the prevailing strategy, with the percentage of transfers involving two embryos being 8.7% (varying from 0.0% to 33.3%). Pairwise comparisons using Fisher’s exact test showed a statistically significant difference only between the KI (6.4%) and SI (14.0%) groups (P=.029), whereas all other 65 comparisons were not significant.

Results of outcome measures

Overall, 469 blastocysts were warmed and transferred in 428 cycles, resulting in 100% survival, clinical pregnancy rate of 45.8%, implantation of 43.1%, and live birth rate of 36.0%; 159 babies were born. There was no significant difference in pregnancy (P=.522), implantation (P=.298), or live birth rates (P=.150) between the groups. More specifically: embryo survival after warming was 65/65 (II), 3/3 (IK), 28/28 (IS), 3/3 (IV), 156/156 (KI), 15/15 (KK), 49/49 (KS), 3/3 (KV), 107/107 (SI), 4/4 (SK), 31/31 (SS), and 5/5 (SV). The clinical pregnancy rate was 40.4% (23/57) in II, 33.3% (1/3) in IK, 41.7% (10/24) in IS, 50.0% (1/2) in IV, 45.9% (67/146) in KI, 53.3% (8/15) in KK, 48.9% (23/47) in KS, 100% (3/3) in KV, 40.2% (37/92) in SI, 50.0% (2/4) in SK, 63.3% (19/30) in SS, and 40.0% (2/5) in SV. The implantation rate was 36.9% (24/65) in II, 33.3% (1/3) in IK, 39.3% (11/28) in IS, 33.3% (1/3) in IV, 43.6% (68/156) in KI, 53.3% (8/15) in KK, 49.0% (24/49) in KS, 100% (3/3) in KV, 36.4% (39/107) in SI, 50.0% (2/4) in SK, 61.3% (19/31) in SS, and 40.0% (2/5) in SV. The live birth rate was 29.8% (17/57) in II, 33.3% (1/3) in IK, 37.5% (9/24) in IS, 50.0% (1/2) in IV, 37.0% (54/146) in KI, 46.7% (7/15) in KK, 29.8% (14/47) in KS, 100% (3/3) in KV, 29.3% (27/92) in SI, 50.0% (2/4) in SK, 56.7% (17/30) in SS, and 40.0% (2/5) in SV. Mean neonatal weight was 3.2 ± 0.4 kg —it was statistically comparable between groups (P=.436) varying from 3.0 ± 0.7 to 3.6 ± 0.5 kg (3.1 ± 0.5 II,3.0 ± 0.0 IK, 3.2 ± 0.5 IS, 3.3 ± 0.0 IV, 3.1 ± 0.6 KI, 3.4 ± 0.3 KK, 3.2 ± 0.7 KS, 3.5 ± 0.1 KV, 3.2 ± 0.6 SI, 3.6 ± 0.5 SK, 3.6 ± 0.5 SS, and 3.0 ± 0.7 SV). Weight was also comparable with newborns from fresh embryo transfers in the same period in our center (3.1 ± 0.5 kg). Preterm live birth rate (birth before 37 gestational weeks) was 6.3%, varying from 0.0% to 7.4% and comparable between the groups (P=.729), with fresh embryo transfers (9.0%) and with data from the national registry for babies born from ART (12.1%): 5.6% (1/18) II, 0% (0/1) IK, 0% (0/10) IS, 0% (0/1) IV, 7.4% (4/54) KI, 0% (0/7) KK, 20% (3/15) KS, 0% (0/3) KV, 6.9% (2/29) SI, 0% (0/2) SK, 0% (0/17) SS, and 0% (0/2) SS. Male and female babies varied within groups from 0.0 % to 100.0 %; sex distribution (males 48.4 %/females 51.6%) was comparable between groups (P=.325). With fresh transfers (males 46.9%/females 53.1%) and with national registry data, no babies were born with major diseases or malformations.

Discussion

The present study supports the clinical applicability of the universal single-step warming, demonstrating high survival and consistent clinical outcomes independently of the commercial vitrification–warming system used. The 100% postwarming survival rate meets the key performance indicators defined by the 2017 Vienna Consensus developed by European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) and Alpha Scientists in Reproductive Medicine. Pregnancy, implantation and live birth rates were comparable between groups and with our previous results using conventional Universal Warming (1).

These findings are consistent with growing evidence supporting simplified warming protocols. In a large retrospective cohort of 3,167 transfers, Yelke et al (3) reported higher clinical pregnancy, ongoing pregnancy, and live birth rates with one-step warming compared with multistep protocols, whereas neonatal outcomes were comparable. Similarly, Karagianni et al (4), in a prospective cohort of 802 frozen embryo transfers, reported comparable pregnancy, implantation, miscarriage, and live birth rates between fast one-step warming and conventional protocols, supporting noninferiority in routine practice. Kato et al (5) also reported improved blastocyst re-expansion and higher clinical pregnancy rates with one-step warming and demonstrated feasibility across different thawing solutions without significant brand-related differences. In addition, Liebermann et al (6), analyzing 1,266 live births, reported no differences in gestational age, birth weight, or miscarriage rates compared with multistep warming.

Although most previous studies were conducted within single centers and limited commercial kit combinations, the present study systematically evaluated twelve vitrification–warming combinations derived from four commercially available systems, providing broader evidence of kit interchangeability under standardized single-step conditions. The concept of universal warming was originally proposed to address laboratory challenges related to kit dependency, logistical constraints and embryo exchange between centers (1). In this context, “universal” refers to the feasibility of applying a consistent warming strategy across different commercial systems.

The present findings reinforce this concept by demonstrating stable survival and comparable outcomes across heterogeneous kit combinations, suggesting that postwarming success is mainly determined by shared cryobiological principles rather than proprietary compositional differences. This has practical implications for contemporary IVF practice, where embryo transport, cross-center collaboration and transitions between cryopreservation systems are increasingly common.

Neonatal outcomes were reassuring—birth weight and preterm delivery rates were comparable across vitrification–warming combinations, and no major congenital anomalies or severe neonatal diseases were observed. Together with large datasets supporting the safety of one-step warming (6), these findings support the reliability of the simplified approach for both clinical and perinatal outcomes.

Limitations include the retrospective design, single-center setting, and smaller sample sizes in some vitrification–warming combinations. Because of small sample sizes in several vitrification-warming groups, statistical power is limited. These results offer initial evidence of feasibility and safety but do not support broad conclusions about their clinical use. In addition, cumulative live birth rate per patient was not assessed. Prospective randomized studies would further strengthen the evidence for a broadly clinically applicable universal single-step warming strategy.

Conclusions

Overall, the clinical and neonatal outcomes observed across different vitrification–warming combinations are reassuring and appear consistent with the biological rationale underlying the USSW protocol. However, given the limited sample size of some subgroup combinations, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and further adequately powered studies are required to confirm the safety, clinical effectiveness, and broader applicability of this approach across different commercial systems. If validated, USSW may offer potential advantages in terms of laboratory efficiency, cost reduction, and wider access to IVF and fertility preservation.
