Missed opportunities and associated factors for institutional delivery services among women in Hararghe eastern Ethiopia a mixed methods study
Authors: Ibsa Mussa, On Makhubela-Nkondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Maternal mortality remains high globally, with 94% of deaths in low-resource settings. In Ethiopia, only 48% of births take place in health facilities, and just 25–35% in Hararghe. Despite contact with health services, many women still miss institutional deliveries. This study aimed to quantitatively identify factors associated with non-institutional delivery and qualitatively explore contextual, behavioral, and health system factors influencing maternal health service utilization, integrating findings to provide a comprehensive understanding of barriers to institutional delivery. A convergent parallel mixed-methods study with equal weighting of quantitative and qualitative strands (QUAN + QUAL) was conducted in Grawa, Meta, and Haramaya woredas from September 5 to December 5, 2017. For the quantitative component, 357 women who had attended at least one ANC visit were included to identify factors associated with non-institutional delivery. Data were entered into EpiData version 3.1 and analyzed using SPSS version 25, applying multivariable logistic regression at p < 0.05. For the qualitative component, purposive sampling was used to select participants for key informant interviews and eight focus group discussions. Thematic analysis was conducted using Atlas.ti version 8.2. Findings from both strands were integrated during interpretation through triangulation to generate complementary and corroborative insights. Among the 357 ANC-attending women, 41.2% (95% CI: 36.2–46.5) delivered outside health facilities, reflecting a missed opportunity for skilled birth attendance. Notably, women residing in urban areas were significantly more likely to have non-institutional deliveries (AOR = 4.82, 95% CI: 1.97–11.79). Maternal age between 26 and 34 years (AOR = 3.87, 95% CI: 1.18–12.72) and attending fewer than four ANC visits (AOR = 3.26, 95% CI: 1.02–10.39) were also associated with higher odds of non-institutional delivery. Conversely, spontaneous vaginal delivery was linked to lower odds of non-institutional delivery (AOR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.07–0.44). Meanwhile, respectful treatment and ambulance access showed no statistically significant associations with delivery location. ANC attendance, more than one-quarter of women experienced missed opportunities for institutional delivery, indicating that service contact alone is insufficient to ensure facility-based birth. This underscores persistent gaps in Ethiopia’s efforts to achieve universal facility-based delivery, particularly in rural and underserved areas. Key factors influencing institutional delivery included urban residence, previous delivery experience, antenatal care (ANC) attendance, and maternal age, suggesting that mere contact with health services does not guarantee facility-based births.

Introduction

Maternal mortality remains high globally, with 94% of deaths occurring in low-resource settings, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa1. Institutional delivery, attended by skilled health personnel is a critical intervention proven to reduce maternal and neonatal mortality. The World Health Organization (WHO) emphasizes that timely access to skilled care during childbirth can prevent the majority of maternal deaths2. Despite this, institutional delivery coverage remains insufficient across many low- and middle-income countries due to structural, cultural, and systemic barriers3.

In Ethiopia, maternal health outcomes have gradually improved, yet substantial challenges persist. According to the 2019 Ethiopian Mini Demographic and Health Survey, only 48% of births were delivered in health facilities nationwide4. This figure is markedly lower in certain regions, such as Hararghe in eastern Ethiopia, where institutional delivery rates range between 25% and 35%5. These persistently low rates are concerning given the government’s commitment to achieving Sustainable Development Goal 3, which aims to reduce maternal mortality to fewer than 70 per 100,000 live births by 20306.

Even among women who engage with the health system through antenatal care (ANC), many still deliver at home or in non-institutional settings. This phenomenon, referred to as “missed opportunities” for institutional delivery, represents a critical gap in the continuum of maternal care7. Such missed opportunities indicate systemic weaknesses that hinder women from completing the recommended maternal health pathway, despite initial contact with health services.

Although several studies have examined factors influencing institutional delivery, such as maternal education, residence, age, ANC attendance, and the perceived quality of care, few have investigated these dynamics specifically within the Hararghe context8. To the best of the investigators’ knowledge, there is limited documented evidence on missed opportunities and their associated factors in this area. Furthermore, no prior study has employed a mixed-methods approach to explore this issue in depth. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of missed opportunities and identify factors associated with institutional delivery in eastern Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study design

A convergent parallel mixed-methods study design was employed, integrating both quantitative and qualitative data collected concurrently, analyzed separately, and then triangulated during interpretation using methodological triangulation through convergence and complementarity of findings to enhance validity and contextual understanding. The quantitative component used a community-based cross-sectional design, while the qualitative component followed an exploratory approach to provide in-depth insight into contextual barriers and missed opportunities for institutional delivery.

Study setting and period

The study was conducted in three woredas of eastern Ethiopia: Grawa, Meta, and Haramaya, from September 5 to December 5, 2017. These woredas are located 552 km, 423 km, and 526 km, respectively, from Addis Ababa. As of 2015, the districts had populations of 313,000 (Grawa), 226,000 (Meta), and 291,000 (Haramaya), with 124,470 women of reproductive age. Agriculture and petty trade are the predominant livelihoods.

Quantitative study

Population and eligibility criteria

All woreda-dwelling women of reproductive age who had given birth during the previous five years were considered as the source population. The study population comprised women who were permanent residents of the study area (living for more than one year), aged 15–49 years, and who had attended at least one antenatal care (ANC) visit during their most recent pregnancy. Women were randomly selected from this eligible group and included if they were available during the data collection period. Women with hearing impairments, severe illness, mental disturbances, or who had lived in the study area for less than one year were excluded from the study.

Sample size and sampling procedure

The sample size was determined using a single population proportion formula, assuming a prevalence (P) of 31%, a 95% confidence level, and a 4% margin of error9. Given the three-stage sampling procedure (kebeles, villages, and households), a design effect of 1.5 was applied to account for intra-cluster correlation commonly observed in multistage community-based surveys. An additional 10% was added to compensate for potential non-response. Based on these parameters, the corrected final sample size was 422 women, which was consistently applied during data collection and analysis9.

A three-stage sampling technique was employed. In the first stage, Kebeles were selected using simple random sampling (lottery method) from the total list of kebeles in the woreda. In the second stage, villages within the selected kebeles were selected using simple random sampling. In the third stage, households were selected using systematic random sampling, based on an updated household sampling frame obtained from health extension workers. The sampling interval (k) was determined by dividing the total number of households by the required sample size for each village. When more than one eligible woman was identified in a household, one participant was selected using a lottery method.

Data collection methods

For quantitative data collection, a pretested, structured questionnaire was administered by trained female data collectors. These data collectors had a minimum of secondary-level education and were fluent in Afaan Oromo, the local language. The questionnaire consisted of closed-ended questions to capture socio-demographic, economic, and health-related variables. Behavioral checklists and relevant records were also used to gather additional data.

Data processing and analysis

The quantitative data were first checked for completeness and internal consistency. Any missing values or inconsistencies were reviewed and corrected to ensure data quality. The cleaned data were then coded and entered into EpiData version 3.1, after which they were exported to SPSS version 25 for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, tables, and figures, were generated to summarize participant characteristics and key study variables.

To examine associations between independent variables and the outcome, binary logistic regression analysis was performed. Model adequacy was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and the Omnibus test of model coefficients. Multivariable logistic regression was subsequently applied to identify independent determinants of the outcome. The outcome variable was defined as missed opportunity for institutional delivery (coded as 1 = home or non-institutional delivery among ANC attendees and 0 = institutional delivery). Accordingly, adjusted odds ratios greater than one indicate increased odds of a missed opportunity.

Before conducting the statistical tests, a multicollinearity test was performed to assess potential correlations among the independent variables using standard error and collinearity statistics. The Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values were examined to detect possible multicollinearity. A VIF cut-off point of 5 was used as the acceptable threshold (some scholars suggest up to 10). In this study, all variables had VIF values well below this limit, with a mean VIF of 0.951, indicating that there was no significant multicollinearity among the independent variables and that they were suitable for inclusion in the regression model.

Subsequently, a binary logistic regression model was constructed to explore relationships between the independent variables and the outcome variable. The model’s fitness was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and the Omnibus test. Multivariable analysis was then conducted to identify the independent predictors of the outcome variables. Statistical associations were reported using odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and a p-value of < 0.05 was set as the threshold for statistical significance in both bivariate and multivariable analyses.

Qualitative study

Sample size and sampling procedure

Purposive sampling was employed for the qualitative component to capture in-depth perspectives from participants directly involved in childbirth experiences or decision-making related to place of delivery. The qualitative study population therefore comprised women of reproductive age who had given birth within the last five years Women of reproductive age without prior childbirth experience were not included, as they were less able to meaningfully reflect on barriers to institutional delivery, as well as key influencers and service providers involved in maternal health decisions. These included husbands, mothers-in-law and other close relatives, community and religious leaders, health extension workers, and facility-based health professionals. The participants’ direct involvement in or impact on the decision-making process surrounding childbirth was guaranteed by the selection process.

These groups were selected because of their direct experience with, or influence over, decisions regarding institutional delivery, making them well positioned to explore barriers and missed opportunities for facility-based childbirth (Fig. 1).

Data collection methods

Qualitative data were gathered through focus group discussions (FGDs) conducted in Afaan Oromo. The FGDs took place in local health facilities and community centers within the study woredas to provide convenient and familiar settings for participants. A total of 8 FGDs were conducted, each lasting approximately 60–90 min, to allow sufficient time for participants to share their experiences and perspectives in depth.

The number of FGDs was justified based on the principle of data saturation, ensuring that no new themes emerged by the final discussions. Participants included women of reproductive age, community and religious leaders, husbands, and in-laws, selected to capture diverse perspectives on antenatal care utilization and decision-making regarding childbirth. All discussions were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and translated into English, and open-ended discussion guides were used to explore participants’ perceptions, experiences, and insights.

Data processing and analysis

For the qualitative data, all interviews and focus group discussions were audio-recorded, transcribed, and translated into English. The transcribed data were then analyzed using thematic analysis in Atlas.ti version 8.2. The data were systematically coded, and each code was grouped into categories. These categories were subsequently organized into broader themes that represented key patterns and insights identified in the data. This process allowed for the identification of recurring ideas and perspectives across different participants. The analysis aimed to capture the depth and context of the participants’ experiences and perceptions, providing a rich qualitative understanding of the study’s objectives. The thematic analysis was carried out iteratively, ensuring that all relevant themes were adequately represented and explored in detail.

Independent and dependent variables

The dependent variable in this study was the missed opportunity for institutional delivery, which refers to cases where women who had attended at least one antenatal care (ANC) visit did not deliver in a healthcare facility, opting instead for home delivery or other non-institutional settings. Several independent variables are considered to influence this decision. These include demographic and reproductive factors such as age at first marriage, age at first pregnancy, gravidity, and parity. Pregnancy-related factors, including pregnancy intention, pregnancy complications, and the level of autonomy and decision-making a woman has regarding her healthcare choices, are also important.

Furthermore, intimate violence, husband support, and advice from others in the community are considered influential in shaping delivery choices. Additionally, knowledge about delivery services and access-related factors, such as transport cost, healthcare provider behavior, communication advice from healthcare professionals, and the distance to the healthcare facility, all contribute to the decision-making process surrounding institutional delivery services. Together, these independent variables provide a comprehensive framework for understanding the factors influencing the utilization of institutional delivery services.

Data quality assurance

A multilingual expert, fluent in Afaan Oromo, initially developed the questionnaire in English, which was then translated into Afaan Oromo. To ensure consistency, the questionnaire was subsequently back-translated into English. Data collectors underwent a three-day training, and supervisors reviewed the completed tools daily to ensure accuracy and completeness. A pretest was carried out with 10% of the sample in Combolcha Woreda, using conditions that closely mirrored those of the actual data collection.

A pretest (pilot study) was conducted with 10% of the sample in Combolcha Woreda, under conditions closely mirroring those of the actual data collection. The pilot study was used to assess the reliability and validity of the questionnaire. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding a value of 0.82, indicating good reliability. Face and content validity were established through expert review and pretesting, ensuring that the questions were clear, culturally appropriate, and capable of capturing the intended information.

Regular supervision was provided by the investigators and senior research managers throughout the data collection process to maintain quality and adherence to the study protocol.

Variable definitions

Institutional delivery: Childbirth conducted in public or private health facilities.

Missed opportunity: Women who had contact with the health system during pregnancy but did not deliver in a health facility despite indications or intentions to do so10.

Traditional birth attendant (TBA): A non-formally trained community birth assistant.

Trustworthiness of the qualitative study

To ensure the trustworthiness of the qualitative findings, the study applied the four criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba: credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability.

Credibility was achieved by employing purposive sampling to include participants with diverse perspectives, including women of reproductive age, community and religious leaders, husbands, and in-laws. Data were collected through FGDs conducted in familiar local settings and facilitated in the participants’ native language (Afaan Oromo), enhancing authenticity of responses. Prolonged engagement during each FGD (60–90 min) and member checking with selected participants helped ensure that the interpretations accurately reflected participants’ views.

Transferability was addressed by providing a detailed description of the study context, participants, and data collection procedures, allowing readers to assess applicability to similar settings.

Dependability was ensured through audit trails, including detailed documentation of data collection, coding, and thematic analysis processes. Peer review of the coding framework by the research team further strengthened the dependability of the findings.

Confirmability was enhanced by maintaining reflexive notes and using verbatim quotes from participants to support themes, demonstrating that findings were grounded in the data rather than researcher bias.

By addressing these criteria, the study aimed to produce qualitative findings that are credible, transferable, dependable, and confirmable, thereby ensuring the overall trustworthiness of the research.

Results

This study employed a mixed-methods approach to comprehensively examine the factors contributing to missed opportunities for institutional delivery among women of reproductive age in Eastern Ethiopia. The findings are presented through an integrated analysis of quantitative survey data from 422 participants and qualitative insights from eight focus group discussions were conducted, involving a total of 32 discussants, with four participants per discussion on average. While methodological guidelines commonly recommend 6–10 discussants per focus group, smaller and more homogeneous groups were deliberately used in this study to encourage active participation and in-depth discussion of sensitive issues related to childbirth experiences and institutional delivery decision-making.

The results are organized into key themes that highlight socio-demographic characteristics, cultural and behavioral factors, health service utilization patterns, and systemic barriers influencing women’s delivery choices.

Socio demographic and economic characteristics

The study included 422 women had a mean age of 26 years (± 6.3), with nearly half (46.4%) aged 15–24. A majority (75.1%) resided in rural areas, and over half (53.3%) had no formal education, while only 29.1% had completed primary school. Economically, 57.1% were self-employed, primarily in small-scale trade or farming, and 34.1% were housewives. Marital status data showed that 76.8% were married, while 12.3% were divorced or widowed. Ethnically, 93.6% identified as Oromo, and religiously, 92.2% were Muslim, reflecting the region’s demographic profile. Household sizes varied, with 47.9% living in families of 5–8 members (Table 1).

Qualitative findings reinforced these statistics, revealing that economic constraints significantly influenced delivery choices. Rural women, in particular, face the prohibitive costs of transportation and lost income during hospital stays. One participant explained, “We must pay for transport, food, and lodging at the hospital. At home, we only need a local midwife” (Rural Woman, Grawa). These financial barriers were compounded by low education levels, which limited awareness of maternal health services.

Delivery, cultural, and behavioral factors

Reproductive history data indicated that the mean age at first marriage was 25.7 years (± 5.7), with 9.5% married before age 18, a known risk factor for adverse maternal outcomes. The majority of women (74.6%) had given birth three or more times, and 78.0% reported three or more pregnancies. Female genital mutilation (FGM) was prevalent, affecting 48.8% of respondents, and intimate partner violence (IPV) was alarmingly high at 88.2%. Decision-making autonomy was shared with husbands in 81% of cases, though this did not always translate into facility-based deliveries (Table 2).

Cultural norms emerged as a dominant theme in qualitative discussions. Many women expressed a strong preference for traditional birth attendants (TBAs), citing familiarity and distrust of formal healthcare providers. A participant noted, “A male doctor examined me, and I felt ashamed. Next time, I will deliver at home” (IDI, Married Woman, Haramaya). Religious modesty also played a role, with Muslim women avoiding male providers. These cultural and social dynamics were deeply entrenched, often overriding the potential health benefits of institutional delivery.

Obstetric and reproductive health characteristics

Antenatal care utilization was relatively high, with 84.6% of women attending at least one visit. However, only 48.3% completed the recommended three or more visits, and 69.7% reported privacy violations during ANC sessions. Despite ANC engagement, institutional delivery rates were suboptimal: 70.1% delivered in health facilities, while 28.7% gave birth at home (with five cases unaccounted for). Spontaneous vaginal deliveries (80.6%) far outnumbered cesarean section (19.4%) (Table 3).

Health system barriers emerged as a recurring challenge affecting women’s access to institutional delivery. Nearly all women (97.2%) traveled 31–60 min to reach the nearest facility, and 49.1% walked due to lack of transportation. Ambulance services were unreliable, with delays forcing some women to deliver en route. “They carried me on a wooden stretcher because no ambulance came. I delivered on the way” (Rural Woman, Chelenko).

Dissatisfaction with care also contributed to missed opportunities for facility-based delivery. About 33.2% of women feeling disrespected by healthcare providers, and only 29.9% reported being “completely satisfied” with their delivery experience, while 4.3% expressed outright dissatisfaction.

Of the total 422 women surveyed, analyses related to missed opportunities for institutional delivery were restricted to the 357 women who had attended at least one ANC visit, in line with the study’s inclusion criteria. Among the 357 ANC-attending women, 202 (56.6%) initiated ANC in the first trimester (95% CI: 51.4–61.9), while 31 (8.7%) began ANC in the third trimester (95% CI: 5.9–11.8) (Fig. 2).

Obstetrics and health service-related variables

The study revealed that 96 (26.9%) of the 357 women who had attended at least one ANC visit missed the opportunity to deliver in a health facility. Among these women, 261 (73.1%) delivered in health institutions, while 96 (26.9%) gave birth at home or in other non-institutional settings. Regarding mode of delivery, 340 respondents (80.6%) had spontaneous vaginal delivery (SVD), and 82 respondents (19.4%) delivered by caesarean section (CS), while 86 (20.4%) of the delivered women had complications during giving birth. Almost 37% of the respondents reported that they delivered their babies in that particular place because there was little or no expense for delivery services.

The majority of respondents, 410 (97.2%), stated that it takes 31 to 60 min to walk the distance to the closest health facility. In addition, the respondents (45.3%) stated that they went to a health facility by foot; the survey also shows that almost 412 (97.6%) of the respondents reported that it took them 30–60 min to see a medical staff for assistance. The study revealed that most of the respondents were happy with the treatment they received from the experienced birth attendant, and it also revealed that 217 (51.4%) of them were absolutely pleased with such care. However, the study found that 18 (9.0%) of the respondents were unhappy with the care they received at a medical facility (Table 4).

Missed opportunity of institutional birth

Out of 357 women who attended antenatal care, 96 (26.9%) experienced a missed opportunity for institutional delivery, resulting in an overall prevalence of 26.9% (95% CI: 22.4–31.8). The observed difference between the reported facility delivery rate (73.1%) and regression-adjusted estimates is explained by excluding women who did not attend ANC, thereby ensuring consistency with the heading on institutional delivery among ANC attendees. The difference between the observed facility delivery rate (73.1%) and the regression-predicted probability of institutional delivery arises because the model excludes women who did not attend antenatal care, thereby adjusting for maternal and obstetric characteristics (Fig. 3).

In multivariate logistic regression analysis, several factors were significantly associated with institutional delivery. Women residing in rural areas were 5.73 times more likely to deliver in a health facility compared to urban residents (AOR: 5.73, 95% CI: 2.26–14.51). Among multiparous women (parity ≥ 2, n = 315), those whose previous delivery was spontaneous vaginal delivery (SVD) were 88% less likely to miss institutional delivery compared to women with previous cesarean section (AOR: 0.12, 95% CI: 0.06–0.28). Women who had attended antenatal care follow-up were significantly 4.25 times less likely to experience a missed opportunity for institutional delivery compared to those who had not attended ANC (AOR: 4.25, 95% CI: 1.09–12.73).

Women whose age at first pregnancy was 26–34 years had significantly lower odds of experiencing a missed opportunity for institutional delivery compared with women aged 35–49 years at first pregnancy (AOR: 4.51, 95% CI: 1.24–16.36). Use of ambulance for transportation to health facilities was not statistically significantly associated with missed opportunity for institutional delivery compared to public transport (AOR: 2.64, 95% CI: 0.73–9.59). Similarly, reporting respectful treatment from health workers showed no statistically significant associated with missed opportunity for institutional delivery (AOR: 0.73, 95% CI: 0.35–1.42).

Other variables, including gestational age at ANC visit, complications during the last pregnancy, and danger signs during pregnancy, mode of transportation, delivery complications, and history of abortion, were not significantly associated with institutional delivery in the multivariable model (Table 5).

Discussions

This study assessed the missed opportunity of institutional delivery and associated factors among reproductive-age women in the Haramaya, Chelenko, and Grawa districts, Eastern Ethiopia. Among women who had attended at least one antenatal care (ANC) visit, 26.9% (95% CI: 22.4–31.8) experienced a missed opportunity for institutional delivery. This indicates that approximately one out of four women miss to deliver in a health facility despite ANC contact. Key factors associated with missed opportunities included residential area, mode of previous delivery, ANC follow-up, respectful treatment by health workers, means of transportation to health facilities, and age at first pregnancy.

According to this study, 26.9% of women who had attended at least one ANC visit experienced a missed opportunity for institutional delivery. The prevalence of institutional delivery in this study is in harmony with studies conducted in different settings, such as Southwest Ethiopia 28.8%11, South Tigray Zone 28.8%12, SNNPR, Ethiopia 26%13, and Jimma Town, Southwest Ethiopia 25.6%14. The fast construction of healthcare facilities and the training of health personnel, particularly health extension workers, as part of the health sector development program’s multifaceted methods may be to blame for this drop15,16. These discrepancies may have an explanation based on variations in sample sizes, evaluation techniques, and research period time gaps.

However, this study’s finding of missed opportunity in institutional delivery was lower than previous studies conducted in different settings, like the Northwest of Ethiopia, 87.9%17; the Northwest of Ethiopia, 86.5%18; the Southwest of Ethiopia, 78.3%19; and the Amhara region, Ethiopia, 78.8%20. In contrast to this, the findings from this study are relatively higher than those from studies conducted in different settings, such as Southeast Ethiopia 12.3%21, Southwest Ethiopia 24%22, North Gondar Zone 13.5%23, Ethiopia 71%24, Enugu, Nigeria 47.1%25, Kenya 45.7%26, southern Tanzania 46.7%27, and EDHS 28%28. The apparent disparity in the results may be explained by seasonal variations at the time the study was done. Different sample sizes, socio-demographic variations, research settings, and service accessibility might all be contributing factors to the variance.

This study revealed that women’s residence was another missed opportunity that has had a significant impact on the use of skilled delivery care. Urban resident women were 5.73% more likely than rural resident mothers to use institutional delivery services. Qualitative insights explained this disparity: rural women faced greater transportation barriers, with many reporting long travel times and unreliable ambulance services. As one participant noted, “They carried me on a wooden stretcher because no ambulance came. I delivered on the way” ( Rural Woman). This result is consistent with other research from Bahirdar, Ethiopia; the northwest of Ethiopia; the South Tigray Zone; West Ethiopia; and Nigeria that revealed a statistically significant relationship between domicile and the need for specialized care12,17,24,25,29–34.

These findings are consistent with one another, which suggests that urban mothers are more likely to be educated, to be able to access services more easily and without having to travel far, to be more informed about pregnancy and delivery complications, and to have better access to information than mothers in rural areas. Similarly, the DHS report (2016) shows significant differences between residency and some critical health metrics, indicating that residing in a rural region makes it more difficult to access contemporary health care28.

The current study’s findings show that prenatal clinic attendance is a significant indicator of institutional delivery. Mothers who accessed ANC during the index pregnancy were four times more likely to give birth in a medical facility than mothers who did not attend ANC. ANC served not only to monitor health but also as an educational tool and trust-building mechanism between women and the health system. “After my ANC visits, I knew the hospital was safer, but getting there was still hard” (IDI, Urban Woman, Haramaya). These findings emphasize ANC as a strategic intervention point for improving institutional delivery rates, particularly if supported by logistical planning and family engagement. This underscores the need to strengthen referral linkages between ANC and delivery services.

This outcome is comparable with research from the Amhara region’s Bahir Dar Town, Northwest Ethiopia, Southwest Ethiopia, and Tanzania35, where women who had low ANC attendance were more likely to give birth at home11,12,20,33,36. Research in the Sekela region, which revealed that women who attended ANC were four times more likely to give birth in health facilities than those who did not make ANC visits, lends credence to the current conclusion30. This might be explained by the fact that they may have chosen to give birth at home due to a lack of knowledge as well as possible experiences they experienced during antenatal clinic appointments. Another possibility is that institutional delivery service use may have been similarly impacted by the same causes that affected ANC use, since ANC follow-up is one type of maternity care usage.

In addition, study participants who received respectful treatment from medical staff were 27% less likely to miss a skilled birth than their peers. Women who felt mistreated during previous facility encounters expressed reluctance to return, a sentiment echoed in focus groups. “She said the nurse scolded her. That’s why she stayed home this time, “(Community Representative, Meta). These findings are consistent with studies done in Central Ethiopia37, Eastern Ethiopia38, Northwest Ethiopia39, Southern Ethiopia40, Northeast Ethiopia41, and a systematic review and meta-analysis in Ethiopia42. This discrepancy might be caused by the research period’s variability, the participants’ degree of service comprehension, their educational and socioeconomic background, the quality of the service, or their capacity for reporting. Moreover, it could be brought on by client volume, social diversity, and hospitals’ devotion to women-friendly facilities.

Moreover, study participants who had access to institutional ambulance transportation had a 2.64 lower risk of missing skilled delivery services than their pregnant female counterparts who took public transportation. However, qualitative data revealed frequent ambulance shortages, forcing many women to walk or use makeshift stretchers. As one participant explained, “The ambulance didn’t arrive… we were forced to deliver at home” (Rural Woman), and the other discussant said that “main barriers to institutional delivery service utilization are long distance, lack of transport…… to health facilities, and our husbands do not give us money even for transport, and finance has its impact on service utilization of maternal health care” (Grawa, health worker-23).

These findings are consistent with the studies done in Ethiopia43, East Ethiopia44, Western Ethiopia45, India46, Mali47, and Zambia48. This highlights an urgent need to strengthen emergency transport systems, particularly in rural areas. This suggests that accessibility to transportation and proximity to the health institution are deciding factors for choosing trained birth attendants during delivery.

Furthermore, the usage of institutional delivery services was similarly influenced by the age at first pregnancy and early ANC visits. According to our research, mothers who become pregnant between the ages of 26 and 34 have a 4.5-fold higher likelihood of giving birth in a health institution than mothers who become pregnant between the ages of 35 and 49. Qualitative narratives revealed that younger mothers were more exposed to health education and often more influenced by community outreach programs, unlike older women, who were more inclined toward traditional birth practices. “Older women tell us to deliver like they did… But now we learn from health talks at the clinic” (Young Woman, Meta).

This finding is consistent with a study done in the Amhara region, Ethiopia20; Southwest Ethiopia49; Ethiopia24; East Ethiopia44; East Nepal50; and Tanzania35. This may be the women receiving advice on complication readiness and delivery preparation during their ANC follow-up, which will motivate to give birth in a health facility.

Limitation

This study has several methodological limitations. First, selection bias may have occurred, although we applied multistage and systematic sampling to ensure representative selection of participants. Inclusion criteria were clearly defined, and non-respondents were minimized through repeat visits and follow-ups. Second, information bias is possible, despite using trained and supervised data collectors, structured and pretested questionnaires, and maintaining confidentiality to encourage truthful responses. Third, recall bias may have affected responses, as participants were asked to report on recent deliveries and ANC visits; however, focusing on deliveries within the past year helped reduce memory errors. Fourth, analytical bias was addressed by checking for multicollinearity and including only relevant independent variables in the regression analysis.

Additionally, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causality. While qualitative data were collected to explore contextual factors, they may not have fully captured the depth of these factors. Future studies could consider longitudinal designs and more comprehensive qualitative approaches to strengthen causal inference and contextual understanding.

Conclusions

Despite ANC attendance, more than one-quarter of women experienced missed opportunities for institutional delivery, indicating that service contact alone is insufficient to ensure facility-based birth. This underscores persistent gaps in Ethiopia’s efforts to achieve universal facility-based delivery, particularly in rural and underserved areas. Key factors influencing institutional delivery included urban residence, previous delivery experience, antenatal care (ANC) attendance, and maternal age, suggesting that mere contact with health services does not guarantee facility-based births.

Interventions should focus on strengthening the quality and continuity of ANC, promoting birth preparedness, addressing socio-cultural and perception-related barriers, and ensuring equitable access to transport and respectful care. Further research is warranted to understand why respectful care and transport availability did not significantly influence delivery decisions among ANC users in these districts.

Implications

The findings highlight persistent gaps in the continuum of maternal care in Ethiopia, even among women who had established contact with health services through ANC. The presence of missed opportunities suggests that ANC attendance alone is insufficient to ensure institutional delivery without addressing quality, trust, and continuity of care.
