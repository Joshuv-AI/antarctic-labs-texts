Sorting nexin 3 promotes ischemic retinopathy through RIP1- and RIP3-mediated myeloid cell necroptosis and mitochondrial fission
Authors: Jiaojiao Wang, Chunhong Zhou, Kai Zhuang, Jiami Zou, Wanlu Qiu, Mei Jin, Weile Ye, Pinglian Yang, Zhihua Zheng, Qing Zhou, Zunnan Huang, Yuanxiang Wang, Peiqing Liu, Jing Lu, Yuqing Huo, Zhiping Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceRetinopathy is a leading cause of irreversible blindness, with unmet medical requirements for innovative prevention and treatment strategies. Here, we showed that sorting nexin 3 (SNX3) was significantly upregulated in retinal myeloid cells from mice with oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR). Myeloid-specific deficiency of SNX3 decreased retinal neovascularization (NV) and improved visual function in the OIR model. Mechanistic studies demonstrated deficiency of SNX3 suppressed the production of proinflammatory and proangiogenic factors by inhibiting hypoxia-triggered myeloid cell necroptosis and mitochondrial fission through promoting receptor-interacting protein 1/3 (RIP1/3) degradation. Importantly, W1122 as an SNX3 inhibitor, effectively ameliorated retinal NV in OIR mice. Our findings revealed an interaction between SNX3 and RIP1/3 signaling in myeloid cells that may dictate pathological angiogenesis in ischemic retinopathy.

Proliferative retinopathy (PR) is one of the leading causes of blindness across all age groups, including neonates (retinopathy of prematurity, ROP), middle-aged adults (proliferative diabetic retinopathy, PDR), and the elderly (age-related macular degeneration). Although anti-VEGF medications have shown promise for treating PR in the clinic, many patients are resistant to anti-VEGF therapy or still have recurrent retinal neovascularization (NV) (1–3). Thus, there is an imperative need for alternative therapeutic modalities.

Immune cells, particularly macrophages and microglia, function as immune surveillance guards and key effectors and regulators of the retinal microenvironment. They undergo a number of specific activation processes in response to the disruption of retinal homeostasis induced by a variety of stimulations or stresses, such as ischemia/hypoxia, neuroinflammation, injury stress, and degeneration (4, 5). Activated immune cells frequently undergo apoptosis or death within the central nervous system to eliminate overactive inflammatory responses and maintain immune tolerance (6). Emerging evidence demonstrates that receptor-interacting protein 1/3 (RIP1/3)-mediated microglial necroptosis in the retina is essential for regulating pathological retinal angiogenesis (7). However, whether macrophages, along with microglia, also undergo necroptosis during retinal angiogenesis and how RIP1/3-mediated signal transduction in myeloid cells drives retinal angiogenesis are still largely unknown. Over the past two decades, numerous small molecules targeting the RIP1/3-mediated necroptosis pathway for potential therapeutics have been identified (8). Excitingly, a RIP1 inhibitor, GSK2982772, is currently in clinical trials as a therapeutic prospect (9). Nonetheless, despite recent achievements, several of the identified compounds exhibit minimal in vivo potential. Consequently, it will be essential to uncover additional small molecules or other pivotal regulators of the RIP1/3 signaling to discover new targets for drug screens.

The physiological and pathological functions of signaling proteins are associated with their subcellular localization, intracellular trafficking, and degradation, which are governed by the retromer complex and associated sorting nexins (SNXs). Mammalian SNXs consist of 33 members and are characterized by a highly conserved Phox homology-domain (10). SNXs target the endosomal membrane through the Phox homology-domain and bind to the vacuolar protein sorting (VPS) family to form a retromer complex (11, 12). The abnormal expression or dysfunction of SNXs has been linked to multiple diseases, including neurodegenerative diseases, cardiovascular diseases, and inflammatory and infectious diseases (13–16), but little is known about the roles of the SNXs in pathological retinal angiogenesis.

In this study, we used both genetic and pharmacological approaches to investigate the role of SNX3 and the underlying mechanism in the oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) model, a clinically relevant mouse model of PR (17). Our results reveal that SNX3 mediates microglia/macrophage necroptosis and mitochondrial fission to facilitate the explosive release of both proinflammatory and proangiogenic cytokines through promoting RIP1/3 degradation, ultimately leading to retinal NV in OIR mice. Additionally, we identified a novel small-molecule inhibitor of SNX3, W1122, and assessed its therapeutic efficacy in the OIR model.

Results

SNX3 Expression in the Retinal Myeloid Cells Is Associated with PR in Humans and Rodents.

To delineate the complete repertoire of the transcription profile of SNX-related genes in myeloid cells during retinopathy, we conducted single-cell RNA analysis in OIR mice, a mouse model of hypoxia-induced retinopathy that mimics ROP (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A) and some aspects of PDR in humans (18). Both CD11b- and F4/80-positive myeloid cells were enriched by FACS from pooled retinas of OIR or control mice maintained in room air (RA), respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). We first screened CD45+ myeloid cells (n = 1,545 cells) from the total cell population before performing unsupervised clustering with the “Seurat” package for downstream analysis. Myeloid cells were clustered into four distinct clusters: resting microglia, active microglia, macrophage, and monocyte (Fig. 1 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Specifically, as shown in Fig. 1 A and B and SI Appendix, Figs. S1C and S2, cells in resting microglia cluster, predominantly from control RA retinas, express high levels of genes such as P2ry12, Siglech, and Tmem119, which are associated with microglial homeostasis, while cells in active microglia cluster, which are significantly enriched in the OIR retinas, express high levels of activated microglial markers, such as Cst7, Lpl, and Spp1 (19, 20). Interestingly, pathway enrichment analysis uncovered that SNX-related pathways (e.g., early endosome, endosome membrane, and endocytic recycling) and the necroptosis pathway were enriched in microglia/macrophages from OIR retinas (Fig. 1C). Among the 20 detected SNX gene family members, Snx3 was the most significantly upregulated gene in retinal myeloid cells of the OIR mice (adjusted P-value = 5.96E-50, effect size = 0.81, OIR vs. RA) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). Notably, the violin plots showed that gene expression of Snx3 and key components of the retromer complex, including Vps29 and Vps35, were significantly upregulated in both macrophages and activated microglia from OIR retinas (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). We further analyzed SNX3 expression in retinal fibrovascular membranes (FVMs) excised from patients with PDR in a microarray dataset (GSE60436) and an RNA sequencing dataset (GSE102485). In both datasets, the expression of SNX3, numerous necroptosis-related genes, and myeloid cell markers were higher in retinal neovascular proliferative membranes compared to normal retinas and non-PDR retinas (Fig. 1 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S4), implying that SNX3 expression might be associated with myeloid cell necroptosis and the development of PDR. Using immunofluorescence staining, we observed that CD11b+ myeloid cells accumulated within the areas of neovascular tufts in OIR retinas, as indicated by three-dimensional (3D) images of the flat-mounted retinas (Fig. 1G). To further verify the Snx3 gene expression in activated myeloid cells from the pathological retinal NV, we isolated myeloid cell-rich neovascular tufts from OIR retinas using laser capture microdissection (Fig. 1H). As shown in Fig. 1I, Snx3 expression was elevated in myeloid cell-rich neovascular tufts from OIR animals in comparison to the normal retinal vasculature of control mice. Moreover, by immunostaining of the flat-mounted retinas, we observed a markedly increased level of SNX3 expression in the retina with OIR, predominantly in F4/80- and CD11b-positive macrophages/microglia (Fig. 1 J–M). Notably, SNX3-positive macrophages/microglia are accumulated within and around the neovascular tufts in OIR retinas (Fig. 1 J–M and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Consistently, by RT-PCR and western blot analyses, we observed that SNX3 expression was elevated in myeloid cells from OIR retinas isolated using anti-CD11b microbeads (Fig. 1 N–Q). Collectively, these findings indicate an association of elevated SNX3 expression in retinal myeloid cells with PR in both humans and rodents.

Global and Myeloid-Specific Deficiency of SNX3 Decreases Retinal NV and Vascular Leakage in OIR Mice.

To study the role of SNX3 in retinopathy, we generated global inducible Snx3-deficient mice (Snx3iKO) by intercrossing Snx3flox/flox mice (13) with Rosa26Cre/ERT2 mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B). Inducing Snx3 gene deletion in neonatal mice was conducted by tamoxifen administration according to a previous protocol (21). The Rosa26Cre/ERT2 littermate mice were used as wild-type (Snx3WT) controls. SNX3 expression was markedly reduced in retinas from Snx3iKO mice after tamoxifen treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C), confirming effective deletion of Snx3. Global SNX3 deficiency did not affect the normal retinal vascular development under RA (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). In contrast, the area of neovascular tufts and vaso-obliterative regions were much smaller in the retinas of Snx3iKO OIR mice at P17, compared to Snx3WT OIR mice (Fig. 2 A–C). We further investigated whether SNX3 deficiency improves vascular hyperpermeability, a characteristic feature of pathological angiogenesis. As illustrated in SI Appendix, Fig. S6D, retinas of Snx3iKO OIR mice exhibited less severe hemorrhages compared to Snx3WT OIR mice. Moreover, retinas of Snx3iKO OIR mice displayed reduced retinal vascular permeability, as evidenced by fluorescein angiography with fluorescein isothiocyanate–dextran and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated isolectin B4 (IB4) (SI Appendix, Fig. S6E). Consistently, western blot analysis showed that the levels of albumin, a sign of capillary leakage, in retinas from Snx3iKO OIR were lower than that in the control group (SI Appendix, Fig. S6F).

To further establish the myeloid cell–specific role of SNX3 in retinal NV, we generated myeloid-specific Snx3-deficient mice (Snx3ΔMΦ) and littermate control mice (Snx3WT) by breeding Snx3flox/flox mice with LysM-Cre mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and B). Compared to Snx3WT mice, SNX3 expression was markedly reduced in bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs) and retinal microglia cultured from Snx3ΔMΦ mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 C–E), thereby validating the successful deletion of SNX3 in the myeloid cells of Snx3ΔMΦ mice. The physiological development of retinal vasculature under RA was unaffected by SNX3 deletion in myeloid cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). However, similar to Snx3iKO, retinas of Snx3ΔMΦ OIR mice exhibited a decrease in the neovascular cluster area, avascular area, vascular permeability, and hemorrhage area compared to controls at P17 (Fig. 2 D–F and SI Appendix, Fig. S8 F–H). Taken together, these observations suggest that both lobal and myeloid-specific deficiency of SNX3 alleviate retinal NV and vascular leakage in OIR.

Myeloid-Specific Deficiency of SNX3 Improves Retinal Function and Protects Against Long-Term Retinal Degeneration in OIR Mice.

To assess the impact of SNX3 deletion on retinal function in OIR, we first performed dark-adapted electroretinogram (ERG) studies. The Snx3ΔMΦ OIR mice exhibited a substantial enhancement in a-wave and b-wave amplitudes compared to Snx3WT OIR mice at P26 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A–C), indicating that SNX3 deficiency in myeloid cells improves retinal function. Next, the visual acuity was assessed using an optomotor response test following an established protocol (22). Compared to age-matched control mice housed in RA, visual acuity in OIR mice was decreased (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 D–F). However, myeloid-specific deficiency of SNX3 improved visual impairments in OIR mice.

To further assess the long-term effects of SNX3 deletion in OIR, we first performed H&E staining of retinal cross sections. Reduced retinal thickness, as well as disorganization in the structure and morphology of the retinal layers, were observed in the Snx3WT OIR mice compared to the RA controls at P30 and P42 (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 A–C). In contrast, the retinas of Snx3ΔMΦ OIR mice exhibited less retinal degeneration and structural abnormalities than Snx3WT OIR retinas. Furthermore, to explore the potential effect of SNX3 deficiency in myeloid cells on retinal vascular recovery, we did a fluorescein fundus angiography (FFA) analysis of the mouse OIR retinas at P30 as we described previously (23). No significant difference was observed in the retinal vein width among the groups, and retinas of Snx3WT RA mice displayed no vascular tortuosity (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 D and E). In contrast, the retinas of Snx3WT OIR mice had abnormalities such as venous dilation and pronounced retinal arterial tortuosity, which were mitigated by myeloid SNX3 deficiency. Collectively, these findings suggest that deletion of SNX3 in myeloid cells improves retinal function and protects against long-term retinal degeneration and structural abnormalities in the OIR model.

SNX3 Deletion Inhibits Hypoxia-Induced Myeloid Cell Necroptosis and Subsequent Production of Proinflammatory and Proangiogenic Cytokines.

Our observations that myeloid SNX3 is essential for retinal angiogenesis in mice with OIR and that the SNX3 upregulation was accompanied by an increased expression of necroptotic genes in retinal myeloid cells from both mice with OIR or patients with PDR prompted us to explore the potential role of SNX3 in modulating myeloid cell necroptosis during retinopathy. Pathway enrichment analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from retinal myeloid cells of RA/OIR mice uncovered that necroptosis pathway was significantly enriched in both microglia, particularly in active microglia and macrophages from OIR retinas (Fig. 3A). Immunostaining uncovered a marked increase in the fluorescence intensities of RIP1 and RIP3, two key components of the necroptosis pathway (24, 25), in Iba1+ microglia/macrophages located within neovascular tufts in OIR retinas (Fig. 3 B–E). Additionally, the expressions of SNX3, RIP1/3, and albumin in OIR retinas were significantly upregulated as early as P14, peaking at P17, followed by a gradual decline (SI Appendix, Fig. S12), indicating a close link between SNX3, RIP1/3, and the progression of retinal NV and permeability during OIR. Strikingly, SNX3 deficiency largely attenuated OIR-induced elevation of RIP1/3 staining (Fig. 3 B–E). Consistent with these findings, western blot analysis showed that protein levels of RIP1/3 and phosphorylated (p)-MLKL were upregulated in CD11b+ cells isolated from OIR retinas, while SNX3 deletion substantially reduced OIR-induced expression of RIP1/3 and p-MLKL (Fig. 3F). Similar results were obtained in cultured mouse microglia (Fig. 3G) and macrophages (SI Appendix, Fig. S13A) exposed to hypoxia in vitro. In contrast, SNX3 overexpression induced a robust increase in RIP1/3 expression in microglia (SI Appendix, Fig. S13B). Collectively, both the loss-of-function and gain-of-function studies demonstrate the crucial role of SNX3 in regulating RIP1/3 protein expression. Since the kinases RIP1/3 are key triggers of necroptosis (24), it was logical to determine whether SNX3 regulates myeloid cell necroptosis. Indeed, SNX3 deletion significantly counteracted the necroptosis in retinal microglia exposed to hypoxia, as indicated by a decreased population of necroptotic (Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL)+caspase-3−) cells isolated from Snx3ΔMΦ mice (Fig. 3H). Because RIP1/3 were reported to trigger inflammation in a necroptosis-dependent and -independent manner (26, 27) and recent evidence has also demonstrated that RIP1/3–mediated necroptosis promoted the expression and release of FGF2 from microglia (7), we speculated that SNX3 could influence the expression and release of proinflammatory and proangiogenic cytokines, which mediate retinal NV. To authenticate this presupposition, we first analyzed gene expression profiles in CD11b+ retinal cells isolated from Snx3ΔMΦ OIR mice. The results showed that SNX3 deficiency markedly decreased messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) expression of key proinflammatory and proangiogenic cytokines, including Il1b, Tnfα, and Fgf2, in CD11b+ myeloid cells isolated from OIR mice (Fig. 3I). Intriguingly, although the protein levels of RIP1/3 was considerably decreased in SNX3-deleted myeloid cells (Fig. 3 B–G), their mRNA levels remained unchanged (Fig. 3J and SI Appendix, Fig. S13C). In addition, we assessed the production of proinflammatory cytokines and FGF2, from cultured retinal microglia in vitro using ELISA. As shown in Fig. 3 K–N, the protein levels of IL-1β, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), IL-6, and FGF2 were elevated in the cultured medium of hypoxia-exposed retinal microglia, which were largely abrogated when SNX3 was deleted. Consistently, western blot analysis demonstrated the decreased expression of FGF2 protein in CD11b+ cells isolated from Snx3ΔMΦ OIR retinas (Fig. 3F). Similar results were obtained in hypoxia-exposed retinal microglia cultured from Snx3ΔMΦ mice (Fig. 3O). Overall, these data indicate that SNX3 facilitates RIP1/3-mediated necroptosis and the production of proinflammatory cytokines and FGF2 in retinal myeloid cells.

Deficiency of SNX3 Decreases the Protein Levels of RIP1/3 by Accelerating Their Degradation.

Since SNX3 deficiency downregulated RIP1/3 protein expression without influencing their transcriptional induction, we posited that SNX3 deficiency could promote the degradation of RIP1/3 protein. To test this hypothesis, we used cycloheximide to inhibit RIP1/3 protein de novo synthesis. As shown in Fig. 4A, the protein levels of RIP1/3, but not MLKL, declined at a significantly faster rate in SNX3-deleted BMDMs than in WT cells. Given that SNX3 has been shown to interact with VPS to regulate retromer-dependent endosome trafficking (12), we questioned whether SNX3 affects RIP1/3 degradation by directly interacting with RIP1/3 and VPS to form a retromer complex, thereby mediating their intracellular recycling and protecting them from endosomal or lysosomal degradation. To verify this hypothesis, we first performed immunofluorescent staining, coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP), and glutathioneS-transferase (GST) pull-down experiments to evaluate the protein colocalization and interaction. Indeed, immunofluorescent staining indicated that RIP1/3 and retromer proteins (specifically, SNX3 and VPS35) were localized in the same cellular compartments in both macrophages (Fig. 4B) and microglia (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). Co-IP assays revealed that SNX3 associated physically with RIP1/3 and VPS35 in macrophages (Fig. 4C) and microglia (Fig. 4D) transfected with FLAG-labeled SNX3. In addition, the GST pull-down assay showed that purified GST-SNX3, but not GST alone, specifically pulled down His-RIP1/3 in vitro, confirming a direct interaction between SNX3 and RIP1/3 (Fig. 4E). To further determine how SNX3 interacts with RIP1/3 and regulates their intracellular recycling and degradation, we performed immunofluorescence analysis using lysosomal marker antibody LAMP1. As illustrated in Fig. 4 F and G, deletion of SNX3 enhanced the RIP1/3 colocalization with LAMP1 and decreased the RIP1/3 protein levels. Next, chloroquine (CQ) and MG132 were used to inhibit autophagy/lysosomal- and proteasome-mediated protein degradation, respectively. As expected, CQ, but not MG132, increased the levels of RIP1/3 protein in WT BMDMs and restored the decreased levels of RIP1/3 in SNX3-deleted macrophages (Fig. 4H). To further determine that SNX3 deletion accelerates RIP1/3 degradation through the lysosomal pathway, we isolated the lysosomal fraction to detect RIP1/3 protein expression. The results showed that RIP1/3 protein was increased in the lysosome fraction in SNX3-deleted BMDMs (Fig. 4I). Together, correlative evidence implies that the absence of SNX3 might facilitate the delivery of RIP1/3 to the lysosome for degradation.

Deletion of SNX3 Downregulates MatrixMetalloproteinases (MMP)12 via Inhibition of a RIP1/3–Dynamin-Related Protein 1 (DRP1)–Mitochondrial Fission–Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Axis in Hypoxic Myeloid Cells.

Since the RIP1–RIP3 complex has been reported to regulate mitochondrial damage (25), we postulated that SNX3 could influence mitochondrial machinery via RIP1/3. To test this hypothesis, we used a Seahorse extracellular flux analyzer to evaluate mitochondrial function in Snx3ΔMΦ BMDMs compared to Snx3WT macrophages. We observed that hypoxia exposure induced substantial impairments in oxygen consumption rate, including mitochondrial respiration and ATP production in WT BMDMs. However, these were reversed when SNX3 was depleted from the macrophages (SI Appendix, Fig. S15 A–C). Likewise, the effect of hypoxia on WT macrophages was mitigated by the administration of RIP1 or RIP3 inhibitors, Nec-1 or GSK872. Moreover, as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S15 D and E, WT macrophages exposed to hypoxia exhibited decreased fluorescence of a mitochondrial-specific detection probe. Conversely, confocal imaging of Snx3ΔMΦ BMDMs demonstrated that the cells exhibited an extended mitochondrial network and elevated mitochondrial signal, indicating an increase in mitochondrial volume or number in the absence of SNX3. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of hypoxia-stimulated Snx3ΔMΦ BMDMs showed notably enlarged mitochondrial structures, in contrast to the smaller morphology observed in Snx3WT macrophages (SI Appendix, Fig. S15F). The number of mitochondria per cell was only increased in Snx3WT macrophages that was exposed to hypoxia; however, it was not observed in Snx3ΔMΦ cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S15G). The data suggest that SNX3 may influence mitochondrial fission. Consistent with this notion, the activation of mitochondrial fission protein, DRP1, was augmented in BMDMs exposed to hypoxia, as evidenced by its enhanced phosphorylation (p-DRP1) level, whereas this was diminished in the absence of SNX3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S15 H and I) or in the presence of RIP1 or RIP3 inhibitors (SI Appendix, Fig. S15 J and K). SNX3 overexpression substantially increased p-DRP1 levels, which were largely ablated by RIP1/3 inhibition (SI Appendix, Fig. S15 L and M), indicating that SNX3 mediates DRP1 phosphorylation via RIP1/3. In accordance with these in vitro findings, the p-DRP1 levels were elevated in CD11b+ cells isolated from the retinas of OIR mice as compared to RA mice, whereas SNX3 deletion significantly downregulated OIR-induced DRP1 phosphorylation SI Appendix, Fig. S15 N and O). Together, these results suggest that SNX3 mediates mitochondrial fission and dysfunction via the RIP1/3-DRP1 axis.

To elucidate the functional significance of SNX3-mediated mitochondrial fission in myeloid cells during retinopathy, we detected ROS, as mitochondrial fission is reported to induce the production of mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) (28). MitoSOX labeling revealed a significant increase in mtROS following hypoxic stimulation of WT BMDMs (SI Appendix, Fig. S15P). However, this was blunted in the presence of the mitochondrial fission inhibitor Mdivi-1 or when SNX3 was depleted from the macrophages. Consistent with the in vitro findings, the OIR retinas exhibited a significant increase in mtROS production in comparison to RA retinas, whereas SNX3 deletion in myeloid cells significantly attenuated OIR-induced production of mitochondrial superoxide (SI Appendix, Fig. S15Q).

We and others have previously demonstrated that ROS is capable of upregulating MMPs (29, 30), and retinal myeloid cell-derived MMPs facilitate the retinal NV (31–33). Based on our data indicating a significant reduction in retinal NV in the absence of myeloid cell SNX3 and its capacity to govern mtROS production, we hypothesized that SNX3 may regulate MMP expression through mitochondrial oxidative stress. Interestingly, we observed that Mmp12 was the most upregulated MMP gene in retinal myeloid cells of WT OIR mice, whereas its expression was repressed in myeloid cells from Snx3ΔMΦ OIR retinas (SI Appendix, Fig. S16A). In vitro, mRNA level of Mmp12, but not Mmp2/3/8/9, was substantially increased in hypoxia-exposed WT BMDMs but reduced when SNX3 was depleted from the macrophages (SI Appendix, Fig. S16B), or when macrophages were pretreated with Nec-1, GSK872, Mdivi-1, or the mitochondrial superoxide scavenger Mito-TEMPO (SI Appendix, Fig. S16C). Additionally, the changes in protein expression of MMP12 paralleled with the alterations in mRNA expression, as demonstrated by western blot (SI Appendix, Fig. S16D).

Next, we assessed the angiogenic effect of SNX3-mediated activation of macrophage RIP1/3-MMP12 signaling on endothelial cells. As illustrated in SI Appendix, Fig. S16 E and F, conditional medium (CM) derived from hypoxia-exposed Snx3WT BMDMs enhanced the sprouting of human retinal endothelial cells (HRECs), as revealed by a 3D spheroid sprouting assay. Nevertheless, the ability of CM from hypoxia-exposed Snx3WT macrophages to promote the sprouting of HRECs was largely weakened when using CM from Snx3-deficient BMDMs or from BMDMs treated with small interfering RNA of Rip1, Rip3, or Mmp12. The angiogenic effect of SNX3-induced upregulation of RIP1/3-MMP12 signaling was further evaluated using an ex vivo angiogenesis model. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S16 G and H, CM from hypoxia-exposed WT BMDMs increased the sprouting of choroidal explants, while the proangiogenic effect was reduced when SNX3 was deleted or when RIP1/3 and MMP12 were knocked down, supporting the notion that SNX3-induced expression of MMP12 in myeloid cells is a key regulator of ocular angiogenesis.

RIP1/3 Inhibition Diminishes SNX3 Overexpression-Induced Retinal NV in OIR Mice.

To reinforce a critical role for myeloid cell SNX3 in regulating retinal NV, we further used a gain-of-function approach to assess the effect of SNX3 overexpression in myeloid cells on retinal angiogenesis. To this end, we generated myeloid-specific Snx3 transgenic (Snx3MΦ-TG) mice by crossing Snx3 transgenic mice with LysM-Cre mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S17 A and B). This transgenic strain employs a Cre/loxP system, wherein transgenic Cre expression is driven by the mouse-specific gene (Lyz2) promoter, as we previously described (13, 34). In comparison to littermate negative controls (Snx3WT), retinal myeloid cells of Snx3MΦ-TG mice exhibited robust SNX3 protein expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S17C). Notably, retinal neovascular areas and avascular areas were substantially increased in OIR Snx3MΦ-TG mice relative to control mice at P17 (Fig. 5). However, intravitreal administration of RIP1 inhibitor (Nec-1) or RIP3 inhibitor (GSK872) significantly ablated SNX3 overexpression-induced retinal NV, indicating that RIP1/3 signaling functions downstream of SNX3 in regulating pathological angiogenesis in OIR.

Inhibition of SNX3 by W1122 Effectively Ameliorates Retinal NV in OIR Mice.

Finally, we explored the feasibility of targeting SNX3 with a small-molecule drug to inhibit retinal NV in OIR. To achieve this goal, we conducted virtual screening of more than 10,000 compounds from the small molecule library of Sun Yat-sen University based on the SNX3 structure as published (12) and subsequently synthesized 42 compounds for further evaluation. Ultimately, we identified a small-molecule, W1122, as a promising candidate (Fig. 6 A and B). In addition to being able to interact with the N terminus of SNX3, W1122’s oxygen-sulfur double bond forms a hydrogen bond with the Arg206 site of VPS35, while its benzothiophene structure forms a π stacking with the Phe28 site (Fig. 6C). Accordingly, W1122 exhibited a moderate binding affinity to the full-length SNX3 protein, as indicated by surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis, with an estimated KD constant of approximately 2.6 μM (Fig. 6 D and E). Furthermore, the cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) revealed a significant increase in the stabilization of the SNX3 protein at temperatures ranging from 43 °C to 49 °C in the presence of W1122 (Fig. 6F). Concentrations of 0.25 to 5 μM W1122 showed no significant toxicity to the macrophages and microglia used in this study (SI Appendix, Fig. S18 A and B). W1122 treatment downregulated SNX3 protein expression and disrupted the interaction of SNX3 with RIP1/3 and VPS35 in BMDMs exposed to hypoxia (Fig. 6G). Additionally, immunostaining demonstrated that W1122 diminished the colocalization of RIP1/3 and SNX3 in response to hypoxia stimulus (Fig. 6 H and I). The findings indicate that W1122 can bind to the SNX3 protein, functioning as a negative regulator and protein–protein interaction (PPI) inhibitor to obstruct the scaffolding function of SNX3.

Next, we examined whether W1122 could mimic the effects of SNX3 deletion on inhibition of RIP1/3 signaling and retinal NV. As shown in Fig. 6 J–L and SI Appendix, Fig. S18 C and D, W1122 exhibited a potent inhibitory effect on the expression of RIP1/3 and p-DRP1, mtROS production, as well as necroptosis, in BMDMs exposed to hypoxia. Consistent with SNX3 deficiency, retinal neovascular areas and avascular areas in OIR mice were markedly reduced by W1122 (Fig. 6 M–P). Furthermore, W1122 treatment had no significant effect on the retinal morphology or vascular density at P17, P30, and P42 compared with the vehicle-treated control (SI Appendix, Fig. S19), demonstrating that W1122 does not exhibit long-term retinal toxicity.

Given that anti-VEGF drugs are routinely used in the treatment of PR, we further investigated the combination therapy by simultaneously targeting the SNX3-RIP1/3-FGF2 and VEGF-vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) signaling pathways. Notably, the combined therapies of SNX3 inhibition by W1122 and VEGF blockade markedly reduced retinal NV compared to monotherapy (Fig. 6 M–P).

Discussion

Although the SNX family of protein-mediated recycling signals are implicated in a variety of human diseases, their roles in PR remain elusive. The present study demonstrated that, among detected SNXs, SNX3 was prominently upregulated in the myeloid cells of OIR retinas. Both global and myeloid-specific Snx3 deficiency ameliorated pathologic angiogenesis in OIR (SI Appendix, Table S1), indicating that myeloid SNX3 played a pathogenic role in retinopathy. We identified RIP1/3 as novel cargo proteins of SNX3-retromer. SNX3-retromer interacted with RIP1/3 to mediate their intracellular recycling, protecting them from lysosomal degradation, which caused macrophage/microglia necroptosis and mitochondrial fission, thereby facilitating the release of proinflammatory and proangiogenic factors. Based on the structure of SNX3, we found a small-molecule inhibitor W1122 and verified its therapeutic efficacy in OIR mice. These findings suggest that SNX3 overexpression is a pathogenic mechanism for retinal NV in ischemic retinopathy.

Programmed cell death plays a crucial role in the maintenance of homeostasis, host defense against pathogens, and a variety of other pathologies (35); yet, the resultant inflammatory response and cytokine release may facilitate the onset and progression of disease. Here, we demonstrated that SNX3-mediated upregulation of RIP1/3 triggers microglial necroptosis and retinal NV in OIR mice, consistent with a previous study that revealed that a disease-associated microglial subpopulation mainly experienced necroptosis, but not apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, in OIR retinas (7). Furthermore, our findings uncovered that macrophages also undergo RIP1/3-mediated necroptosis during OIR in addition to microglia and promote retinal NV, further corroborating previous evidence that RIP1 was highly expressed in infiltrating macrophages during angiogenesis and facilitates pathological angiogenesis by modulating macrophage activation in experimental NV (36). These RIP1/3+ microglia/macrophages migrate and reside around retinal neovascular tufts, undergo necroptosis and mitochondrial fission, thereby releasing proinflammatory and proangiogenic factors to induce retinal NV, which advances the understanding of the role of myeloid cell RIP kinases in retinal angiogenesis.

Mechanistically, deficiency of SNX3 reduces mtROS production and the expression of proinflammatory and proangiogenic factors, particularly FGF2 and MMP12, through promoting RIP1/3 lysosomal degradation, which in turn inhibits myeloid cell necroptosis and mitochondrial fission (SI Appendix, Fig. S20). While our data demonstrate that SNX3 deletion accelerates RIP1/3 lysosomal degradation and disrupts SNX3-RIP1/3-VPS35 interactions, we acknowledge that definitive proof of retromer-mediated lysosomal avoidance requires future retromer disruption experiments with VPS35 knockout or dominant-negative mutants. The current evidence supports a correlative rather than strictly causal relationship between SNX3-retromer function and RIP1/3 stabilization. Furthermore, since ROS and inflammatory factors can activate the redox-sensitive transcription factor NF-κB to upregulate its target genes, including FGF2 (37) and MMP12 (38), it is likely that SNX3-dependent stabilization of RIP1/3 promotes transcriptional upregulation of FGF2 and MMP12 by activating NF-κB through RIP1/3-mediated inflammatory signaling, though the exact mechanisms require further study. Of note, FGF2 is less prone to being actively released by its biosynthetic cells due to the absence of a secretory signal peptide (39). He et al. revealed that hypoxia triggered a substantial release of FGF2 in microglia via RIP-mediated necroptosis (7), reinforcing the hypothesis that FGF2 may be passively released via DAMPs during necroptosis. We and others previously demonstrated that proinflammatory cytokines and proangiogenic factors, particularly FGF2 and MMPs, derived from retinal microglia/macrophages, contribute to abnormal ocular angiogenesis (7, 31–33, 40–43). While proinflammatory cytokines (44) and FGF2 (45) are able to induce VEGF expression in the inflammatory microenvironment of tissue and in the endothelial cells, respectively, it is possible that myeloid-specific deficiency of SNX3 indirectly affects VEGF production by attenuating the release of inflammatory cytokines and FGF2 by microglia/macrophages. Therefore, combined targeting of SNX3 and VEGF may offer enhanced antiangiogenic effects, though crosstalk between necroptotic and VEGF-VEGFR pathways necessitates further investigation.

To the best of our knowledge, no available SNX3 inhibitor has been reported so far. The present study identified W1122 as a small-molecule inhibitor of SNX3, providing a tool to conduct research on pharmacological SNX3 inhibition. Co-IP and immunostaining revealed that W1122 was able to effectively block the interaction between SNX3 and RIP1/3, implying that W1122 might function as a PPI inhibitor to hinder SNX3’s scaffolding role. Importantly, our preclinical study verified the potential therapeutic efficacy of W1122 in OIR mice. Given SNX3’s essential functions in endosomal sorting across tissues, systemic inhibition may pose off-target risks. Future studies should explore localized delivery methods (e.g., intravitreal injection of W1122) or tissue-specific targeting (e.g., myeloid-restricted or retina-specific promoters) to minimize systemic effects.

Notwithstanding the encouraging results presented in this study, there are several limitations that require further research. First, we analyzed two different GEO datasets, both of which indicated that the levels of SNX3 gene expression were significantly higher in FVMs from PDR patients than in normal human retinas. However, considering the modest sample size in these datasets is relatively small (n = 2 to 4 per group), further studies are required to clarify and validate the correlation between SNX3 expression and disease severity or clinical outcomes through larger-scale patient samples. Second, we focused on the role of SNX3 on microglia/macrophages in retinopathy, since we observed that SNX3 is mainly expressed in these cells; however, the effects of SNX3 deletion or inhibition by W1122 in other retinal cell types, such as endothelial cells and neurons, in regulating the retinal phenotype necessitate additional exploration. Third, although LysM-Cre efficiently targets macrophages and microglia, we recognize that it may also affect neutrophils and other myeloid lineage cells. This represents an important caveat in interpreting our myeloid-specific knockout results. Future studies using Cx3cr1-CreERT2 (for microglia) or Csf1r-Cre (for macrophages) models could provide more precise cellular resolution. Fourth, W1122 may exhibit off-target effects on other SNX or unrelated signaling pathways; thus, systematic profiling of its specificity is imperative. Fifth, while no overt toxicity was observed in our preclinical models, thorough safety assessments, including pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and long-term toxicity studies, are essential to advance W1122 toward clinical translation. Finally, considering the potent antiangiogenic effects of W1122 and SNX3 deletion demonstrated in the OIR model, further exploration of their therapeutic potential in other NV models, such as laser-induced CNV, is critically warranted. Additionally, while our OIR model primarily reflects ischemic retinopathy, potential glucose-mediated effects on SNX3 expression in diabetic retinopathy warrant separate evaluation.

In summary, our study establishes a pivotal role of the SNX3-retromer-RIP1/3 axis as a mediator of myeloid cell necroptosis and mitochondrial fission in regulating pathological retinal angiogenesis in OIR mice. Based on our data, modulating RIP1/3 signaling via SNX3 in myeloid cells merits further investigation as a potential therapeutic approach for ischemic retinopathy.

Materials and Methods

Animal.

All animal experiments followed the protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of Jinan University. Myeloid-specific Snx3 knockout and overexpressed mice were achieved by cross-breeding Snx3flox/flox and Snx3Tg mice with LysM-Cre transgenic mice, respectively. OIR model was established following a previously described method (18). All mice were on a C57BL/6 J background.
