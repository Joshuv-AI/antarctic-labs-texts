Preoperative 3D slicer reconstruction and the prognostic value of dynamic intraoperative lateral spread response assessment in vertebrobasilar dolichoectasia-associated hemifacial spasm
Authors: Yang Li, Hailiang Shi, Yinzhan Wang, Tao Qian, Xiaolong Wen, Zhenghao Zhang, Yihui Du, Haowei Shi, Zijiang Yang, Jianzhong Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the value of 3D Slicer-based reconstruction for preoperative identification of offending vessels in patients with vertebrobasilar dolichoectasia (VBD)-associated hemifacial spasm (HFS) undergoing microvascular decompression (MVD), and to investigate the prognostic value of dynamic intraoperative lateral spread response (LSR) assessment.MethodsThis retrospective study included 176 patients with HFS who underwent MVD between June 2021 and February 2023; among them, 84 patients had VBD. All patients underwent preoperative 3D time-of-flight magnetic resonance angiography and 3D fast imaging employing steady-state acquisition magnetic resonance imaging, followed by 3D Slicer-based reconstruction. Intraoperative findings were used as the reference standard to compare conventional image assessment with 3D Slicer-based reconstruction for offending-vessel identification in patients with VBD. Postoperative outcomes, complications, and the associations of conventional LSR findings, dynamic intraoperative LSR findings, and immediate postoperative symptom relief with 1-year outcome were also analyzed.ResultsIn patients with VBD, the concordance rate with intraoperative findings for identification of a single offending vessel was significantly higher with 3D Slicer-based reconstruction than with conventional image assessment (88.37% vs. 60.47%, P = 0.006). For multiple offending vessels, the concordance rates were 81.08% and 62.16%, respectively (P = 0.121). Overall concordance rates were 82.14% and 58.33%, respectively (P = 0.001). The difficulty score for neurovascular relationship assessment was lower with 3D Slicer-based reconstruction than with conventional image assessment (0.369 ± 0.757 vs. 1.13 ± 0.915, P < 0.001). At 1 year, 78 of 84 patients in the VBD group achieved complete symptom resolution. Symptom relief did not differ significantly between the VBD and non-VBD groups at any follow-up time point (all P > 0.05). Postoperative hearing loss was more frequent in the VBD group (22.62% vs. 11.24%, P = 0.036), whereas facial palsy rates were comparable (P = 0.494). Within the VBD group, dynamic intraoperative LSR findings and immediate postoperative symptom relief were significantly associated with 1-year outcome (both P < 0.001), whereas conventional LSR findings were not (P = 0.683).ConclusionsIn VBD-associated HFS, 3D Slicer-based reconstruction improved offending-vessel identification and reduced the difficulty of preoperative neurovascular assessment. Dynamic intraoperative LSR findings, rather than conventional LSR findings, were associated with 1-year outcome and may represent a potentially useful prognostic indicator.Keywords: Hemifacial spasm, Vertebrobasilar dolichoectasia, Microvascular decompression, 3D Slicer, Lateral spread response

Introduction

Hemifacial spasm (HFS) is a cranial nerve hyperactivity disorder characterized by involuntary, intermittent contractions of the muscles on one side of the face [1]. The most widely accepted pathophysiological mechanism is vascular compression of the facial nerve at the root exit zone (REZ) [2], and microvascular decompression (MVD) is therefore regarded as the standard etiological treatment for HFS [3]. In most patients, the offending vessels arise from the vertebrobasilar system or its branches [4], whereas anatomical abnormalities of the vertebrobasilar artery may further complicate the neurovascular relationship [5].

Vertebrobasilar dolichoectasia (VBD) is a vascular abnormality characterized by dilatation, elongation, and tortuosity of the vertebral and basilar arteries [6]. Previous studies have suggested that a relatively crowded posterior fossa may increase the likelihood of facial nerve compression and contribute to the development of HFS [7]. In patients with VBD-associated HFS, the enlarged and tortuous vertebrobasilar artery may further compromise the already limited space within the cerebellopontine angle (CPA), making MVD more technically demanding. Compared with patients without VBD, these patients often present with a narrower operative corridor, more complex neurovascular relationships, greater difficulty in exposing the offending vessel, and increased challenges in achieving adequate vascular transposition and decompression [8].

Accurate preoperative evaluation of the neurovascular anatomy is therefore particularly important in this subset of patients. Previous studies have shown that three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction based on high-resolution magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can facilitate preoperative planning for MVD by improving visualization of the spatial relationships between cranial nerves and surrounding vessels, potentially reducing surgical exploration time and procedure-related complications [9]. Ruiz-Juretschke et al. reported that high-resolution MRI with 3D reconstruction was helpful for identifying offending vessels and assessing neurovascular conflict in cranial nerve compression syndromes [10]. Nevertheless, evidence regarding the value of preoperative 3D reconstruction specifically in VBD-associated HFS remains limited, particularly in studies validating imaging findings against intraoperative observations.

In addition, although VBD-associated HFS is generally considered a more complex surgical entity, data on postoperative complications and long-term outcomes in these patients remain scarce. Intraoperative lateral spread response (LSR), an electrophysiological monitoring marker widely used during MVD for HFS, has been suggested to correlate with surgical efficacy; however, its prognostic significance in patients with VBD-associated HFS has not been fully clarified.

Therefore, in this retrospective study, we focused on patients with HFS who underwent MVD at our center, with particular emphasis on those with VBD-associated HFS. First, using 3D Slicer-based reconstruction of preoperative 3D-TOF MRA and 3D-FIESTA MRI, we evaluated the value of this technique for identifying offending vessels and assessing neurovascular relationships by comparing imaging findings with intraoperative observations. In addition to preoperative anatomical evaluation, we analyzed postoperative complications and long-term outcomes and further explored the prognostic value of dynamic intraoperative LSR assessment in patients with VBD-associated HFS.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study included 176 consecutive patients with hemifacial spasm (HFS) who underwent microvascular decompression (MVD) in the First Department of Neurosurgery, Hebei General Hospital, between June 2021 and February 2023. Of these, 84 patients met the diagnostic criteria for vertebrobasilar dolichoectasia (VBD). In the VBD group, 35 patients were male and 49 were female; the affected side was left in 61 patients and right in 23 patients. The age ranged from 17 to 76 years, with a mean age of 53.7 years. The duration of symptoms ranged from 0.5 to 31 years, with a mean duration of 4.5 years.

All procedures performed in this study were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The study flow and follow-up status are shown in Fig. 1.

Study flow diagram. Of 176 patients with HFS treated with MVD, 84 were assigned to the VBD group and 92 to the non-VBD group. All patients in the VBD group and 89 patients in the non-VBD group completed the 1-year follow-up; 3 patients in the non-VBD group were lost to follow-up. A total of 173 patients were included in the 1-year outcome analysis

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with HFS treated with MVD; (2) completion of preoperative 3D time-of-flight magnetic resonance angiography (3D-TOF MRA) and 3D fast imaging employing steady-state acquisition magnetic resonance imaging (3D-FIESTA MRI); and (3) availability of complete clinical, imaging, surgical, and follow-up data.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) secondary intracranial lesions potentially causing HFS, including tumors, cerebrovascular malformations, and skull base malformations; and (2) insufficient image quality for assessment of offending vessels and neurovascular relationships.

MRI acquisition

All patients underwent preoperative MRI using a 1.5-T HDx scanner (GE Healthcare, USA) with an 8-channel head coil. The scanning parameters for 3D-TOF MRA were as follows: repetition time (TR), 21 ms; echo time (TE), 2.8 ms; flip angle, 15°; field of view (FOV), 240 × 240 mm; matrix, 512 × 512; number of acquisitions, 1; and slice thickness, 1.0 mm. The scanning parameters for 3D-FIESTA MRI were as follows: TR, 4.8 ms; TE, 1.5 ms; flip angle, 55°; FOV, 240 × 240 mm; matrix, 512 × 512; number of acquisitions, 2; and slice thickness, 1.0 mm.

Radiological diagnosis and measurement of VBD

The diagnosis of VBD was based on the semiquantitative MRA criteria proposed by Ubogu et al. [11]. Dilatation was defined as an arterial diameter > 4.5 mm at any location within the vertebrobasilar system. Elongation was defined by any of the following: (1) bifurcation of the basilar artery above the suprasellar cistern or lateral deviation to the margin of the clivus or dorsum sellae; (2) basilar artery length > 29.5 mm or lateral deviation > 10 mm from the vertical line drawn from the origin to the bifurcation of the basilar artery; (3) the origin of the vertebral artery or basilar artery located above the pontomedullary sulcus; or (4) intracranial vertebral artery length > 23.5 mm or lateral deviation > 10 mm from the vertical line between the vertebral artery and the origin of the basilar artery. Patients meeting the criteria for either dilatation or elongation were diagnosed with VBD (Fig. 2).

The DICOM data from 3D-TOF MRA were imported into 3D Slicer for measurements in a three-dimensional scene, including the visible length of the basilar artery, lateral deviation of the basilar artery from the vertical line to its origin and bifurcation, intracranial vertebral artery length, the vertical distance from the vertebral artery to the origin of the basilar artery, and the diameters of the vertebral and basilar arteries. All measurements were independently performed by two neurosurgeons. Disagreements were resolved by joint re-review of the original MRI sequences and reconstructed 3D models, followed by consensus discussion between the two observers.

Three-dimensional reconstruction

The DICOM data from 3D-TOF MRA and 3D-FIESTA MRI were imported into 3D Slicer by the first author, who was familiar with 3D Slicer-based neurovascular reconstruction. Each reconstruction required approximately 20–30 min and was subsequently reviewed by the operating neurosurgeons as part of preoperative planning. Vessels were segmented and reconstructed based on 3D-TOF MRA data, whereas the brainstem and the facial–vestibulocochlear nerve complex were segmented and reconstructed based on 3D-FIESTA MRI data. Because both datasets were acquired during the same imaging session with consistent anatomical positioning, no additional registration was performed, and alignment was visually verified during reconstruction. A three-dimensional visualization model of the CPA, including the brainstem, facial–vestibulocochlear nerve complex, and adjacent vessels, was constructed for subsequent assessment of offending vessels and neurovascular relationships. Reconstructed images were considered suitable for analysis only when the offending vessel, facial–vestibulocochlear nerve complex, and root exit zone (REZ) were clearly visualized. Image usability was independently assessed by two observers. Disagreements were resolved by joint review and consensus discussion between the two observers.

Assessment of offending vessels and neurovascular relationships

In patients with VBD, offending vessels and neurovascular relationships were evaluated preoperatively using two methods: (1) conventional image assessment based on 3D-TOF MRA combined with 3D-FIESTA MRI; and (2) 3D reconstruction-based assessment using 3D Slicer. Both assessments were independently performed by two neurosurgeons experienced in MVD. When disagreement occurred, the two observers jointly reviewed the original MRI sequences and reconstructed 3D models and reached a final decision by consensus. In conventional image assessment, offending vessels and neurovascular relationships were evaluated on coronal, sagittal, and axial images. In the 3D reconstruction-based assessment, judgments were made on the basis of the three-dimensional visualization model of the CPA. The two assessment methods were performed independently.

Intraoperative findings were used as the reference standard. Because preoperative imaging and 3D reconstructed images were used for surgical planning, the operating surgeons were not blinded to the preoperative imaging assessments when documenting intraoperative findings. The intraoperative findings were retrieved from operative records before the formal postoperative concordance analysis. Agreement between each preoperative assessment method and intraoperative findings in identifying single offending vessels, multiple offending vessels, and overall offending vessels was compared, and concordance rates were calculated. Direct visual interpretation of the 3D reconstructed images by the operating surgeons was also compared with intraoperative findings.

Difficulty scoring for neurovascular relationship assessment

After surgery, the two operating surgeons independently scored the difficulty of conventional image assessment and 3D reconstruction-based assessment, with reference to intraoperative findings, in terms of three aspects: (1) source of the offending vessel; (2) number of offending vessels; and (3) site of compression. One point was assigned for each item considered difficult to determine, and 0 points were assigned when no difficulty was encountered, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 3. Higher scores indicated greater difficulty in neurovascular relationship assessment. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Surgical procedure and intraoperative electrophysiological monitoring

All patients underwent MVD through a retrosigmoid approach. Teflon felt was used for vascular decompression, and the dura was closed tightly [12]. The operating surgeons recorded the offending vessels and neurovascular relationships. Neurovascular relationships were classified into three categories: (1) compression, defined as contact between the offending vessel and the REZ or facial nerve with an identifiable indentation; (2) contact, defined as contact without an obvious indentation; and (3) irrelevant, defined as no contact between the offending vessel and the REZ or facial nerve.

Intraoperative electrophysiological monitoring was performed using the Xltek Protektor 32 system (Natus, USA). Brainstem auditory evoked potentials were monitored, and lateral spread response (LSR) was continuously recorded to assist in evaluating decompression effectiveness [13]. Z-L response (ZLR) was also recorded to facilitate identification of the vertebrobasilar artery and other suspected offending vessels [14–16]. For conventional LSR assessment, complete disappearance of LSR at 30 mA stimulation was defined as a favorable finding, whereas persistence of LSR was defined as an unfavorable finding. For dynamic intraoperative LSR assessment, intermittent disappearance of the LSR, reduction in amplitude, simplification of waveform morphology, or elevation of the stimulation threshold was defined as favorable, whereas no obvious change in the LSR, a decreased threshold, or increased amplitude despite an unchanged stimulation threshold was defined as unfavorable [17]. Findings from dynamic intraoperative LSR assessment were interpreted by a designated examiner.

Follow-up and outcome assessment

Symptom relief at different postoperative time points and postoperative complications, including facial palsy and hearing loss, were recorded during follow-up. Symptom relief and postoperative complications were compared between the VBD and non-VBD groups at different follow-up time points, with the 1-year follow-up result defined as the primary outcome measure. Patients lost to follow-up were handled by complete-case analysis. They were included in baseline and perioperative analyses when relevant data were available, but were excluded from analyses requiring 1-year outcome data.

Within the VBD group, complete symptom resolution at 1 year was defined as a favorable outcome, whereas persistent symptoms or recurrence at 1 year was defined as an unfavorable outcome. Associations of conventional LSR findings, findings from dynamic intraoperative LSR assessment, and immediate postoperative symptom relief with 1-year outcome were further analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the distribution of continuous variables. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the independent-samples t test. Non-normally distributed variables are presented as median (range) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as number (percentage) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Agreement in offending-vessel identification was expressed as the concordance rate between preoperative assessment and intraoperative findings. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For analyses involving 1-year outcomes, patients lost to follow-up were excluded using a complete-case approach, and no imputation was performed.

Agreement with intraoperative findings in offending-vessel identification

In patients with VBD, the concordance rate with intraoperative findings for identification of a single offending vessel was 60.47% (26/43) with conventional image assessment based on 3D-TOF MRA combined with 3D-FIESTA MRI, compared with 88.37% (38/43) with 3D Slicer-based reconstruction, and the difference was statistically significant (P = 0.006). For multiple offending vessels, the corresponding concordance rates were 62.16% (23/37) and 81.08% (30/37), respectively, without a significant difference (P = 0.121). In branch-vessel cases, concordance rates were low with both methods, and no statistical analysis was performed because of the small sample size. Overall concordance rates were 58.33% (49/84) for conventional image assessment and 82.14% (69/84) for 3D Slicer-based reconstruction, with a statistically significant difference (P = 0.001).

Direct visual interpretation of 3D reconstructed images by the operating surgeons yielded concordance rates of 95.35% (41/43) for single offending vessels and 89.19% (33/37) for multiple offending vessels, with no significant differences compared with formal 3D reconstruction-based assessment (P = 0.443 and P = 0.515, respectively). The overall concordance rate of direct visual interpretation was 89.29% (75/84), which was also not significantly different from that of formal 3D reconstruction-based assessment (P = 0.272). Difficulty of neurovascular relationship assessment

The difficulty score for evaluating neurovascular relationships was significantly higher with conventional image assessment than with 3D Slicer-based reconstruction (1.13 ± 0.915 vs. 0.369 ± 0.757, P < 0.001).

Interobserver agreement

Interobserver agreement was excellent for radiological assessment and measurement of VBD (ICC = 0.972) and good for evaluation of reconstructed image usability (ICC = 0.865). For offending-vessel identification, interobserver agreement was 0.732 for conventional image assessment and 0.787 for 3D Slicer-based reconstruction. For difficulty scoring of neurovascular relationship assessment, the corresponding values were 0.933 and 0.943, respectively.

At 1 year, 78 patients in the VBD group had complete symptom resolution, 4 had persistent symptoms, and 2 experienced recurrence. In the non-VBD group, 83 patients had complete symptom resolution, 5 had persistent symptoms, and 1 experienced recurrence.

Regarding complications, postoperative facial palsy occurred in 12 patients (14.29%) in the VBD group and 10 patients (11.24%) in the non-VBD group, with no significant difference (P = 0.494). Postoperative hearing loss occurred in 19 patients (22.62%) in the VBD group and 10 patients (11.24%) in the non-VBD group, and the difference was statistically significant (P = 0.036). Combined facial palsy and hearing loss occurred in 4 patients in the VBD group and 2 patients in the non-VBD group.

Immediate postoperative symptom relief was also significantly associated with 1-year outcome (P < 0.001). Discussion

The present study has several key findings. First, in patients with VBD-associated HFS, 3D Slicer-based reconstruction showed higher agreement with intraoperative findings than conventional assessment based on 3D-TOF MRA combined with 3D-FIESTA MRI, particularly in the identification of a single offending vessel. Second, 3D reconstruction significantly reduced the difficulty of evaluating neurovascular relationships. Third, direct visual interpretation of reconstructed images by the operating surgeons showed similarly high agreement with intraoperative findings, suggesting that intuitive three-dimensional visualization may provide additional spatial anatomical information. Fourth, although symptom relief rates at different postoperative time points did not differ significantly between the VBD and non-VBD groups, postoperative hearing loss occurred more frequently in the VBD group. Finally, within the VBD group, findings from dynamic intraoperative lateral spread response (LSR) assessment and immediate postoperative symptom relief were significantly associated with 1-year outcome, whereas conventional LSR findings were not.

In our previous work, preoperative use of 3D Slicer was considered helpful for accurately depicting the details of neurovascular relationships and facilitating surgical planning [18]. In patients with HFS, the offending vessel may occasionally be only a small branch, and its identification requires meticulous exploration of the root exit zone (REZ) of the facial nerve. Visualization of the exact compression site, the direction of vascular conflict, and the extent of nerve deformation is considered crucial for successful surgery [19]. Even among patients with similar symptoms and the same diagnosis, microsurgical anatomy may vary substantially. When MVD is performed within the relatively restricted cerebellopontine angle (CPA), the neurosurgeon must understand the spatial relationships between the cranial nerves and surrounding vessels as precisely as possible [20]. Preoperative three-dimensional reconstruction can provide stereoscopic information on CPA anatomy and simulated MVD anatomy, and may also serve as a useful training tool for neurosurgeons [21].

At present, preoperative MRI-based evaluation has been widely adopted before MVD, and related imaging studies have also been reported by multiple centers [22–25]. Combined assessment with 3D-TOF MRA and 3D-FIESTA MRI is valuable for evaluating intracranial neurovascular relationships; however, interpretation of these two-dimensional images often requires a considerable learning curve, and spatial constraints may limit accurate understanding of vessel origin, course, and positional relationships. With recent advances in three-dimensional post-processing, visualization of neurovascular relationships from 3D-TOF MRA and 3D-FIESTA MRI data has become more feasible, allowing clearer depiction of vessel origin, trajectory, and compression status. Building on our previous observations [18], the present study specifically focused on the more complex subgroup of patients with VBD-associated HFS. Because VBD is characterized by dilated, elongated, and tortuous vertebrobasilar arteries, the neurovascular anatomy is generally more complex, rendering identification of offending vessels more difficult. In this setting, 3D Slicer-based reconstruction showed higher concordance with intraoperative findings and lower difficulty scores than conventional image assessment, supporting its value in preoperative evaluation of these complex cases.

From a surgical perspective, 3D Slicer-based reconstruction may provide several practical advantages in VBD-associated HFS. First, it allows surgeons to evaluate the course, size, and direction of the vertebrobasilar artery and its branches before entering the operative field. Second, it may help anticipate whether the offending vessel is a large ectatic vertebrobasilar artery, a smaller branch vessel, or a combination of multiple vessels, which is important for planning the decompression strategy. Third, visualization of the spatial relationship among the CPA vessels, facial–vestibulocochlear nerve complex, and brainstem may help surgeons select the safest dissection corridor and prepare for vascular transposition in advance. These advantages may be particularly relevant in VBD-associated HFS, where the CPA is often crowded and the operative corridor is relatively narrow.

It is noteworthy that, in our earlier work, some reconstructed images appeared highly similar to the intraoperative anatomy, although their preoperative interpretation of neurovascular relationships was not always completely consistent with intraoperative findings. Therefore, in the present study, we further asked the surgeons to review the reconstructed images postoperatively and judge whether the images corresponded to the operative findings. Direct visual interpretation of the reconstructed images showed even higher agreement with intraoperative findings, although the difference compared with formal 3D reconstruction-based assessment did not reach statistical significance. One possible explanation is that intuitive 3D visualization provides additional spatial and positional information, allowing surgeons to mentally match the reconstructed anatomy with the operative field rather than relying solely on predefined judgments regarding vessel origin or mode of compression. The lack of statistical significance may be attributable to the relatively limited sample size, particularly for cases with multiple offending vessels or branch-vessel involvement.

The pathogenesis of VBD remains controversial, but it is likely related to marked thinning or loss of the internal elastic lamina, smooth muscle atrophy, thinning of the media, and hyaline degeneration of connective tissue [26]. Because the elastic lamina plays an important role in resisting the distending effect of systolic pressure, prolonged systemic hypertension may contribute to vascular dilatation and elongation [27], and atherosclerotic changes may further aggravate this process [23, 28]. In the present cohort, 84 of 176 patients with HFS met the imaging criteria for VBD, corresponding to a relatively high proportion within our surgical series, which appears higher than that reported in some previous HFS cohorts [8]. This finding may be partly explained by the pathophysiology of HFS itself, since mechanical vascular compression of the facial nerve is regarded as the major cause of the disorder [29]. In the presence of VBD, the enlarged and tortuous vertebrobasilar artery and its branches occupy more space within the posterior fossa and thereby increase the likelihood of neurovascular conflict [30]. At the same time, diagnostic criteria for VBD have not been completely uniform across studies, particularly regarding how dilatation, elongation, and tortuosity are combined in the final diagnosis [11, 27, 31, 32]. Therefore, our result may be better interpreted as indicating a relatively high proportion of VBD among patients with HFS undergoing MVD at our center, rather than a directly comparable estimate of prevalence. In addition, previous studies have suggested that HFS in Asian populations may be associated with a relatively smaller posterior fossa, which may further increase the likelihood of neurovascular conflict when tortuous vessels are present [30, 33].

From the perspective of postoperative outcome, our study did not demonstrate poorer long-term symptom relief in the VBD group than in the non-VBD group. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in symptom resolution rates immediately after surgery or at 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, or 1 year. These findings suggest that although VBD is associated with more complex neurovascular anatomy and greater surgical difficulty [34], it does not necessarily translate into worse long-term symptom control when MVD is properly performed. However, postoperative hearing loss occurred significantly more often in the VBD group, whereas the rate of facial palsy did not differ significantly between groups. This indicates that, although long-term symptom relief may be comparable, the perioperative risk profile differs in patients with VBD, with hearing preservation requiring particular attention. One possible explanation is that the CPA is more crowded in patients with VBD, the offending vessels are often larger, and vascular transposition may require more extensive manipulation, thereby increasing the risk of cochlear nerve traction or injury.

Another important finding of this study is that findings from dynamic intraoperative LSR assessment were significantly associated with 1-year outcome in the VBD group, whereas conventional LSR findings were not. Previous studies on the relationship between LSR and postoperative outcome in HFS have reported inconsistent results [13–16]. Our findings suggest that, in the specific setting of VBD-associated HFS, the traditional binary criterion of whether LSR disappears completely may be insufficient to reflect the actual functional improvement achieved after decompression of complex vascular conflicts. In contrast, dynamic intraoperative assessment that captures changes in LSR amplitude, threshold, and waveform pattern may provide more nuanced information and better reflect recovery of abnormal facial nerve conduction. In addition, immediate postoperative symptom relief was also significantly associated with 1-year outcome, indicating that early clinical response remains an important predictor of long-term prognosis. Taken together, these findings suggest that dynamic intraoperative LSR assessment may be more informative than conventional LSR assessment in this subgroup. However, given the retrospective design and limited sample size of this study, these findings should be interpreted as an association rather than proof of a causal predictive mechanism.

The main clinical value of imaging assessment in VBD-associated HFS lies in facilitating preoperative planning and surgical strategy selection. Although our study does not prove that 3D reconstruction itself reduces operative difficulty, it allows neurovascular relationships to be analyzed from multiple perspectives before surgery and may help surgeons optimize operative strategy and preparation. Because we did not include a control workflow without 3D reconstruction, we were unable to determine whether this technique independently shortens operative time or reduces complications.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study and is therefore subject to selection bias. The sample size was limited, especially in the subgroup with branch-vessel involvement, which restricted the statistical power of subgroup analyses. Second, although intraoperative findings were used as the reference standard, the operating surgeons were not blinded to preoperative imaging assessments because these images were part of routine surgical planning. This lack of blinding may have introduced verification bias and inflated the imaging-to-intraoperative concordance rates. In addition, intraoperative interpretation itself may still be influenced by the extent of exposure and surgeon experience. Third, a small number of patients in the non-VBD group were lost to follow-up, which may have affected the final outcome analysis. Finally, 3D Slicer-based reconstruction still requires time-consuming manual segmentation and post-processing, which may limit its broader clinical applicability. In addition, most image assessments and surgical interpretations were performed by a limited number of experienced neurosurgeons, which may introduce operator-dependent bias and may limit the generalizability of the findings to centers with different levels of experience in MVD and 3D reconstruction. Larger, multicenter, prospective studies are warranted to further validate the role of 3D reconstruction and dynamic intraoperative LSR assessment in patients with VBD-associated HFS.

Conclusion

In patients with VBD-associated HFS, 3D Slicer-based reconstruction showed higher agreement with intraoperative findings than conventional image assessment for identification of offending vessels and reduced the difficulty of neurovascular assessment. Although long-term symptom relief was comparable between patients with and without VBD, postoperative hearing loss occurred more frequently in the VBD group. Findings from dynamic intraoperative LSR assessment, rather than conventional LSR findings, were associated with 1-year outcome and may serve as a useful prognostic indicator in this subgroup. These findings warrant further validation in larger prospective multicenter studies.
