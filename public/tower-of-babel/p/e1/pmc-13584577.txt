Factors associated with fall prevention–related health literacy practices among community-dwelling older adults in Northern Thailand: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Thitima Thasuwanain, Aksara Thongprachum, Sirinun Boripuntakul, Waraporn Boonchieng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFalls are a major public health concern among older adults and contribute substantially to disability, reduced quality of life, and increased healthcare burden. Fall prevention–related health literacy practices, reflecting the application of health literacy skills in daily life, may influence an individual’s ability to recognize risk factors and adopt appropriate preventive behaviors. This study aimed to examine factors associated with fall prevention–related health literacy practices among community-dwelling older adults in Northern Thailand.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 415 older adults residing in Chiang Rai Province, Thailand, between February to May 2025. Participants were selected using a multi-stage stratified sampling approach. Data were collected through structured questionnaires assessing sociodemographic characteristics, fear of falling, and fall prevention-related health literacy. Functional mobility was evaluated using the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors independently associated with adequate fall prevention-related health literacy and its subdomains. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.ResultsMost participants were female (58.3%), and 61.9% were aged 60–69 years. Overall, 88.70% demonstrated inadequate fall prevention-related health literacy. Housing characteristics (AOR = 3.166, 95% CI: 1.505–6.660) and TUG performance (AOR = 0.352, 95% CI: 0.163–0.761) were significantly associated with adequate fall prevention-related health literacy. Housing characteristics were consistently associated with access (AOR = 1.959, 95% CI: 1.168–3.284), cognitive (AOR = 2.055, 95% CI: 1.203–3.511), communication (AOR = 2.125, 95% CI: 1.233–3.665), and self-management skills (AOR = 1.830, 95% CI: 1.095–3.059). TUG performance was significantly associated with access and cognitive literacy. Additionally, cognitive literacy was significantly associated with marital status and fear of falling, while education level was significantly associated with self-management (AOR = 0.504, 95% CI: 0.296–0.861) and media literacy (AOR = 0.342, 95% CI: 0.211–0.556).ConclusionsA substantial proportion of community-dwelling older adults had inadequate fall prevention-related health literacy. Environmental and functional factors were consistently associated with health literacy across multiple domains, with additional associations for marital status, educational attainment, and fear of falling. These findings highlight the need for community-based fall prevention strategies incorporating adapted health communication, environmental modification, mobility assessment, and targeted interventions to strengthen preventive capacity among older adults.Trial registrationNot applicable.

Introduction

The global population is experiencing a continuous rise in the proportion of older adults. In 2022, individuals aged 60 years and over accounted for 9.82% of the world’s population [1], and this proportion is projected to increase to 16% by 2050 [2], indicating a transition toward a fully aged society. In Thailand, older adults accounted for 20% of the total population in 2024 [3], marking the country’s entry into a complete aged society and its progression toward a super-aged society. Ageing is associated with progressive physiological decline, including reduced vision, decreased muscle mass and strength, diminished proprioception, and impaired cognitive function [4–6]. Functional limitations increase substantially with age, with prevalence rising from approximately 20% among those aged 65 years and over to more than 80% among women aged 90 years. Women are more likely than men to experience mobility-related disability [7]. These changes may contribute to anxiety, reduced self-esteem, and subsequent depressive symptoms [8].

Falls among older adults are multifactorial events resulting from impairments in neuromuscular coordination, vision, hearing, proprioception, and movement control [9]. Falls are also associated with adverse drug effects, acute illness, and environmental hazards [10]. Globally, approximately 26% of older adults experience falls annually, while in Asia the pooled annual incidence is 25.8% (95% CI 22.1–29.9) [11]. In Thailand, 17.7% of community-dwelling older adults reported at least one fall in the previous year, and 4.1% reported recurrent falls [12]. In Chiang Rai province, fall-related mortality among older adults increased from 15.80 per 100,000 population in 2017 to 21.98 per 100,000 in 2023 [13]. In 2024, outpatient visits due to falls among older adults in Chiang Rai reached 4,548.12 per 100,000 population, with 796.00 per 100,000 requiring hospitalization, comparable to national rates (4,691.93 and 892.00 per 100,000, respectively). Beyond physical injury, falls can lead to psychological consequences, particularly fear of falling (FOF). In Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Vietnam, the prevalence of FOF ranges from 21.6% to 88.2% [14]. Fear of falling may contribute to activity restriction and premature dependency. As the ageing population increases, the burden associated with falls is expected to rise correspondingly. Risk factors for falls include intrinsic factors—such as female sex, advanced age, and balance impairment—as well as extrinsic factors, including environmental hazards and medications affecting balance [15–18]. Evidence suggests that health literacy plays a protective role by improving knowledge, understanding, and preventive behaviors related to falls [19].

Health literacy refers to individuals’ abilities to access, understand, appraise, and apply health information to make appropriate decisions regarding disease prevention, health promotion, and self-management [20, 21]. Nutbeam conceptualized health literacy as comprising three levels: functional, interactive, and critical literacy [20]. In addition, integrated models, such as that proposed by Sørensen et al. [21], describe health literacy as a set of competencies involving accessing, understanding, appraising, and applying health information. In the context of fall prevention, these competencies are translated into practical behaviors that enable older adults to identify environmental hazards, adhere to mobility and safety recommendations, manage medications appropriately, and make informed decisions to reduce fall risk in daily life. Therefore, in the present study, these conceptual foundations were adapted and operationalized to develop a context-specific, practice-oriented framework for fall prevention. The framework comprised six domains: access to information, cognitive understanding, communication skills, self-management skills, decision-making skills, and media literacy. Rather than replicating a single existing model, this framework was developed through synthesis of established health literacy concepts and adapted to reflect everyday fall prevention practices among older adults. Health literacy has been positively associated with health behaviors and knowledge, which form an important foundation for behavioral change [22].

Previous studies have identified significant associations between health literacy and falls among older adults [22]. Lower levels of health literacy have been associated with male sex, lower education, and lower household income, while chronic disease status and receipt of health education from village health volunteers are also related to fall-prevention health literacy [23]. Li et al. (2024) reported that adequate health literacy was associated with lower fall prevalence among older adults. Notably, no significant association was observed among women (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.59–1.44) [22], whereas a significant association was found among men (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.37–0.90). Adequate health literacy supports engagement in fall-prevention behaviors, including participation in exercise programs [24]. Furthermore, components of health literacy decision-making (β = 0.303), information appraisal (β = 0.770), and reading skills (β = 0.241) have been shown to predict quality of life among older adults [25].

Chiang Rai Province, located in northern Thailand, is experiencing rapid socioeconomic development alongside demographic ageing. In 2024, older adults accounted for 26.1% of the provincial population, with an age dependency ratio of 44.3 [3]. This demographic transition increases caregiving demands and may influence family structures and community resources. In addition, Chiang Rai Province is characterized by mountainous terrain and dispersed rural communities, which may create barriers to accessing hospitals and healthcare services among older adults. Transportation limitations and geographic distance may reduce opportunities to receive continuous health education and preventive care. In addition, sociocultural characteristics such as strong family-based caregiving systems and reliance on community health volunteers in rural northern Thailand may influence older adults’ access to and utilization of health information, thereby shaping health literacy across different domains. This gap may constrain older adults’ ability to access and apply fall-prevention information. Therefore, strengthening fall prevention–related health literacy may be particularly important in enabling older adults to recognize fall risks, engage in preventive practices, and maintain functional independence within community settings. As Chiang Rai moves toward a super-aged society, age-related syndromes, particularly falls, pose significant health and social challenges affecting older adults, caregivers, and communities. Despite growing recognition of the role of health literacy in fall prevention, limited research has examined fall prevention-related health literacy practices within the socio-demographic and environmental context of rapidly ageing provinces in Thailand. Older adults with inadequate health literacy may lack sufficient understanding of fall prevention strategies, potentially increasing exposure to modifiable risks [26]. Health literacy facilitates informed decision-making, appropriate use of services, and adherence to preventive recommendations, thereby contributing to effective fall prevention. Most studies have assessed fall prevention-related health literacy practices at an overall level, with limited domain-specific evaluation among older adults. This gap may constrain understanding of key literacy components needed to inform targeted health promotion strategies for fall prevention in older populations. A domain-specific approach was therefore necessary, as different determinants may differentially influence distinct health literacy competencies, which is essential for designing targeted public health interventions rather than relying solely on an overall health literacy score. Therefore, this study aimed to examine factors associated with fall prevention-related health literacy practices among community-dwelling older adults in Chiang Rai Province. The findings are expected to inform context-specific strategies to enhance literacy capacity, promote sustainable preventive behaviors, reduce fall incidence, and improve quality of life in a super-aged society.

This study aligns with Sustainable Development Goal 3: Good Health and Well-being, which aims to ensure healthy lives across the life course. In rapidly ageing settings such as Chiang Rai, strengthening fall prevention-related health literacy may support healthy ageing by preserving functional ability and independence among older adults. Accordingly, the present study focused on fall prevention-related health literacy practices, reflecting the behavioral application of health literacy competencies in everyday life. Study design and participants

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Chiang Rai Province, Thailand, between February and May 2025. The sample size was calculated using the single population proportion formula: n = Z²P(1 − P) / d², where n represents the required sample size, Z = 1.96 (95% confidence level), P = 0.57 (estimated prevalence of fall risk based on previous regional data [27]), and d = 0.05 (margin of error). After adding a 10% non-response rate, the final required sample size was 415 participants.

Sampling procedure

A multistage stratified sampling technique was used. In the first stage, the 18 districts in Chiang Rai Province were stratified into three categories based on population size: large (4 districts), medium (5 districts), and small (9 districts). One district was randomly selected from each stratum using a computer-generated randomization procedure, resulting in the selection of Mueang Chiang Rai District (large stratum), Khun Tan District (medium stratum), and Chiang Khong District (small stratum). In the second stage, villages within each selected district were identified from complete administrative records and randomly selected using simple random sampling, with proportional allocation based on district population size. A total of 16 villages were selected from Mueang Chiang Rai District, 14 villages from Khun Tan District, and 11 villages from Chiang Khong District. In the third stage, households were selected from updated community household registries maintained by local administrative authorities. Systematic sampling was applied using a fixed sampling interval, calculated by dividing the total number of households by the required sample size per village, with a random starting point. In the final stage, all eligible older adults within selected households were identified. If more than one eligible participant was present, one individual was selected using simple random sampling (lottery method). Only one participant per household was included to minimize clustering effects. Recruitment continued until the required sample size was achieved.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were included if they met the following criteria: (1) aged 60 years or older; (2) residing in the selected community for at least six months; (3) able to communicate in Thai; and (4) willing to provide written informed consent. Participants were excluded if they had: (1) severe cognitive impairment that prevented reliable questionnaire response; (2) severe physical or medical conditions that precluded participation in interviews or functional assessment; or (3) refusal to participate or withdrawal at any stage of the study.

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained research assistants using a structured questionnaire. Participants were approached in their community settings according to the sampling list and invited to participate in the study. Written informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. Interviews were conducted in private and quiet settings at participants’ homes or community locations to ensure comfort and confidentiality. The questionnaire was administered face-to-face, with trained research assistants reading questions aloud when necessary to accommodate older adults with visual or literacy limitations. Each interview lasted approximately 30–45 min. The instrument included sociodemographic characteristics, fear of falling, and fall prevention–related health literacy practices. Functional mobility was assessed using the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, following standardized procedures. Participants were instructed to stand from a chair, walk three meters, turn, return, and sit down. The total time required to complete the task was recorded in seconds, with a completion time of ≥ 12 s considered indicative of impaired mobility and increased fall risk [28]. All research assistants received standardized training prior to data collection to ensure consistency in questionnaire administration and physical performance assessment.

Bias control and quality assurance

To minimize potential sources of bias, several strategies were implemented. Selection bias was reduced through multistage probability sampling with proportional allocation at each stage. Information bias was minimized using a standardized, pre-tested questionnaire and standardized procedures for the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test. All research assistants received standardized training prior to data collection, and a pilot test was conducted in a non-study area to improve instrument clarity and feasibility. Interviewer bias was minimized through the use of a standardized interview protocol to ensure consistency across interviewers. Data quality was ensured through daily supervision by field supervisors, including completeness and consistency checks. Missing or inconsistent data were addressed immediately through participant follow-up when necessary. These procedures enhanced data quality, reliability, and internal validity.

Measures

Fall prevention–related health literacy practices

Fall prevention–related health literacy practices were assessed using a researcher-developed questionnaire designed to capture the application of health literacy skills in daily fall prevention behaviors among older adults. The instrument was informed by established health literacy frameworks, including Nutbeam’s conceptual model of functional, interactive, and critical health literacy and the integrated health literacy model proposed by Sørensen et al. (2012) [29].,but was adapted and operationalized to reflect a context-specific, practice-oriented construct. Unlike standardized instruments such as the HLS-EU-Q47, which primarily assess perceived difficulty in accessing, understanding, appraising, and applying health information, the present instrument was designed to measure the behavioral application of these competencies in fall prevention contexts. A frequency-based Likert scale was used to capture the extent to which individuals engage in fall prevention health literacy practices in daily life. The instrument comprised 31 items rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = never practice to 5 = regularly practice), with total scores ranging from 31 to 155. Higher scores indicate greater engagement in these practices. Overall scores were categorized as inadequate (31–98) and adequate (99–155). Consistent with the domain-specific classifications, overall fall prevention-related health literacy scores representing less than 60% of the maximum possible score were classified as inadequate, whereas scores ≥ 60% were classified as adequate.

The instrument covered six domains: (1) access to information (5 items; score range 5–25; inadequate 5–14, adequate 15–25); (2) cognitive understanding (5 items; 5–25; inadequate 5–14, adequate 15–25); (3) communication skills (6 items; 6–30; inadequate 6–17, adequate 18–30); (4) self-management skills (4 items; 4–20; inadequate 4–11, adequate 12–20); (5) decision-making skills (5 items; 5–25; inadequate 5–14, adequate 15–25); and (6) media literacy (6 items; 6–30; inadequate 6–17, adequate 18–30). Content validity was evaluated by five experts in geriatrics, health literacy, health promotion, and community health, yielding item–objective congruence values ranging from 0.88 to 1.00. Internal consistency was assessed during the pilot study in Phan District, Chiang Rai Province and demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.896).

Methods

Measures and conceptual considerations

The present study was informed by multidimensional health literacy theory and fall prevention models. Environmental factors (housing characteristics), functional factors (mobility performance and fall risk), psychosocial factors (fear of falling), and individual characteristics (education and marital status) were considered potential determinants of fall prevention-related health literacy among older adults. These factors were selected based on previous literature and their theoretical relevance to multiple health literacy domains, including access, cognitive understanding, communication, self-management, decision-making, and media literacy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Bivariate analyses were conducted to examine associations between independent variables and adequate fall prevention-related health literacy across all domains. Chi-square tests were used to assess associations between categorical variables and health literacy outcomes, while univariable logistic regression analyses were performed to estimate crude odds ratios (CORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The assumptions for Chi-square tests were assessed based on expected cell frequencies prior to analysis.

Variables with p-values < 0.20 in univariable analyses were considered for inclusion in multivariable logistic regression models to avoid prematurely excluding potentially relevant predictors and confounding variables, consistent with recommendations for purposeful variable selection [30, 31]. In addition, theoretically relevant variables were retained across domains of fall prevention–related health literacy regardless of statistical significance. Because the six domains of fall prevention–related health literacy represent distinct but related constructs, variable selection for each multivariable model was guided by both theoretical relevance and empirical associations identified in univariable analyses. Multicollinearity diagnostics and model fit assessments were conducted prior to the final interpretation of the model.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with adequate fall prevention-related health literacy. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for all variables retained in the final models. In some domains, certain variables were not retained in multivariable models due to the limited numbers of outcome events per variable, resulting in reduced model stability and decreased precision of estimates.

Given the relatively low prevalence of adequate fall prevention-related health literacy (11.3%), regression estimates were interpreted with caution, as outcome imbalance may have reduced statistical power and precision.

Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with values < 5 indicating no evidence of multicollinearity. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. A complete-case analysis was performed because the proportion of missing data was < 5%. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Exact p-values were reported as p < 0.001 when applicable.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 415 community-dwelling older adults participated in the study. The majority were female (58.3%) and aged below 70 years (61.9%). Most participants were married (65.6%) and had completed primary education (66.6%). Approximately 55.4% reported no chronic disease, while 18.6% had experienced at least one fall in the previous year. More than half (53.0%) were classified as being at risk of falls according to the Thai-FRAT. Overall, 88.7% of participants were categorized as having inadequate fall prevention-related health literacy. The practice score was 74.87 ± 19.15 (Median = 71.0, IQR = 64.0–81.0; range: 42–155), indicating that most participants scored below the adequacy threshold of 60% of the maximum possible score, are presented in Table 1.

Bivariate associations

Chi-square showed that overall fall prevention-related health literacy was significantly associated with housing characteristics, Thai-FRAT classification, and TUG performance (p < 0.05). When examined by domain, distinct patterns emerged. Access and cognitive literacy were associated with marital status and housing characteristic. Cognitive and communication literacy were additionally associated with fear of falling. Self-management literacy was associated with educational attainment and housing characteristic. Media literacy was associated with educational level and presence of chronic disease.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

After adjustment for potential confounders, housing characteristics and TUG performance were independently associated with overall fall prevention-related health literacy. Participants living in two-story houses had higher odds of adequate literacy (AOR = 3.166; 95% CI: 1.505–6.660), whereas those with impaired mobility (TUG ≥ 12 s) had lower odds (AOR = 0.352; 95% CI: 0.163–0.761). suggesting that both environmental context and functional mobility play important roles in shaping older adults’ ability to apply fall prevention-related health literacy in daily life. Thai-FRAT was not significantly associated after adjustment, indicating that perceived fall risk alone may not directly translate into differences in fall prevention-related health literacy when other functional and environmental factors are considered.

In domain-specific analyses, housing characteristics were associated with access, cognitive, communication, and self-management literacy. TUG performance was associated with all domains except communication and decision-making, indicating that functional mobility is particularly relevant to cognitive and behavioral aspects of fall prevention-related health literacy. Fear of falling was associated with cognitive and communication literacy, suggesting that psychological perception of fall risk may influence how older adults process and communicate fall prevention information. Educational attainment was associated with self-management and media literacy, indicating that higher education may enhance older adults’ ability to interpret and apply health information, particularly in self-care and digital information contexts. No significant predictors were identified for decision-making literacy, suggesting that decision-making ability in fall prevention may be influenced more by experiential or contextual factors rather than the measured sociodemographic variables.

In the self-management domain, educational attainment, housing characteristics, and TUG performance were significant predictors. In the decision-making domain, only TUG performance showed a significant association, while in the media literacy domain, educational attainment and chronic disease status were significant. Discussion

This study examined factors associated with fall prevention-related health literacy among community-dwelling older adults in northern Thailand.

Overall fall prevention-related health literacy

The findings indicate that a high proportion of participants (88.7%) had inadequate fall prevention health literacy, indicating a pronounced floor effect. This limited variability in the outcome may have reduced the statistical sensitivity to detect stronger or more differentiated associations between predictors and health literacy domains, potentially leading to attenuation of effect estimates. Although most participants were young-old adults (< 70 years) who remained functionally independent, more than half of participants were classified as being at risk of falls according to the Thai-FRAT. These findings suggest a mismatch between functional independence and the ability to access, understand, and apply fall prevention-related health information. Age-related physical decline, including deterioration of musculoskeletal and neurological systems, may indirectly influence fall prevention literacy by limiting mobility, cognitive processing speed, and engagement in health information–seeking behaviors [32–34]. Our findings are consistent with previous evidence showing that fall prevention-related health literacy mediates the relationship between physical function and health-related quality of life [35]. Cognitive function may play a central role in linking physical capability with the ability to process health information. Most participants were female. In the Thai sociocultural context, women often assume caregiving responsibilities, which may reduce time and opportunities for seeking health information for themselves. Previous studies have identified gender and age as important determinants of health literacy [36]. Differences in health information-seeking behavior, comprehension, and processing skills may partly explain observed disparities [37]. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as gender differences in literacy may also reflect broader socioeconomic and educational inequalities.

Housing characteristics were significantly associated with adequate fall prevention-related literacy. Older adults with higher literacy may be more capable of identifying environmental hazards and implementing home modifications [38]. Conversely, limited literacy may reduce risk awareness and increase vulnerability to environmental fall hazards [39]. These findings reinforce the importance of multifactorial fall prevention strategies, including environmental assessment and modification [23]. Fall prevention-related Health literacy may function as an enabling factor that supports preventive decision-making and adaptive behaviors in aging populations [40]. Mobility performance, measured by the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, was also significantly associated with literacy. Reduced mobility and balance, common consequences of musculoskeletal decline, increase fall risk [41–43]. Women, who constituted the majority of the sample, have been reported to experience higher rates of fall-related injuries and fractures [44, 45], as well as higher prevalence of musculoskeletal pain [46]. The association between TUG performance and literacy suggests a bidirectional relationship: impaired mobility may limit engagement with health information, while limited literacy may reduce adherence to preventive behaviors.

Unlike previous studies that primarily examined general fall prevention-related health literacy in relation to falls, this study conceptualized fall prevention literacy as comprising six distinct functional domains. This domain-based approach enabled a more nuanced understanding of how environmental, functional, psychological, and socioeconomic factors differentially shape specific literacy capacities. The identification of domain-specific associations indicates that fall prevention literacy is not a unidimensional construct but a multidimensional competency situated within broader contextual conditions. This distinction carries important implications for intervention development, as strengthening fall prevention capacity may require targeted strategies addressing specific literacy domains rather than uniform educational approaches.

Domain-specific findings

The six health literacy domains showed differential associations.

Access skill was associated with housing characteristics and TUG performance. The ability to seek and obtain relevant health information may enhance recognition of environmental hazards such as uneven surfaces or inadequate lighting. While some studies found no association between housing floor level and fear of falling [47], our findings suggest that Fear of falling and perceived fall risk may increase motivation to seek fall prevention information and behaviors. Lower educational attainment has also been linked to reduced fall awareness and increased fall risk [48]. As individuals age, the risk of experiencing both initial and recurrent falls increases substantially [49, 50].

Cognitive skill was associated with marital status, housing characteristics, TUG performance, and fear of falling. Married older adults may benefit from greater social interaction and emotional support, which are associated with improved psychological well-being and engagement in health-promoting behaviors [51, 52]. Cognitive processes are essential for hazard recognition, movement planning, and balance control [53, 54]. The observed association between cognitive skill and fear of falling highlights the interrelationship between psychological and physical components of fall risk. These findings are consistent with a recent study by Alkhamis et al. (2025), which reported that cognitive function and fear of falling were significant predictors of balance performance among older adults. Impaired balance performance is a well-established risk factor for falls and recurrent falls. Together, these findings suggest that cognitive and psychological factors play an integral role in fall risk pathways, potentially influencing both functional performance and subsequent fall outcomes [55].

Communication skill was associated with housing characteristics and fear of falling. Living in single-story houses may facilitate safer mobility and social participation [56–58], whereas multi-level housing has been linked to increased fall risk [59]. Effective communication may enhance confidence in discussing fall concerns with family members or health professionals, thereby reducing fear and promoting active participation [60]. Since fear of falling independently predicts future falls [61], strengthening communication skills may have indirect protective effects.

Self-management skill was associated with education level and housing characteristics. Higher education may improve comprehension and implementation of fall prevention strategies. Prior research has demonstrated positive associations between health literacy, self-efficacy, and chronic disease self-management [62, 63]. Environmental constraints may also limit opportunities to practice self-management behaviors [64, 65]. In particular, housing environments with limited accessibility, physical barriers such as stairs, or inadequate safety features may restrict older adults’ ability to independently perform daily preventive activities, including home modifications, regular exercise, and environmental risk reduction. In the context of Northern Thailand, such housing conditions may also reflect differences in household structure and available family support, which can further influence opportunities for self-management practices.

Decision skill showed no significant association with demographic factors, fall risk, or housing characteristics. Decision-making capacity may be shaped by accumulated life experience and adaptive strategies developed over time [66, 67]. The absence of significant differences suggests that experiential knowledge may compensate for age-related physical decline. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies [22, 65–68]. Fall risk is multifactorial and influenced by complex interactions between biological, psychological, and environmental factors [69].

Media literacy skill was significantly associated with education level. Higher education enhances the ability to critically evaluate and apply digital health information [70]. Digital literacy disparities are influenced by socioeconomic status and access to technology [71], which may contribute to unequal access to fall prevention resources.

Notably, housing characteristics showed consistent associations across multiple domains, including access, cognitive, communication, and self-management literacy, suggesting that the environmental context may function as a cross-domain determinant of fall prevention-related health literacy. In contrast, educational attainment was primarily associated with higher-order domains, particularly self-management and media literacy, whereas fear of falling was more strongly related to cognitive and communication domains. These findings suggest that different dimensions of fall prevention-related health literacy are influenced by distinct but interrelated contextual, functional, and psychosocial factors, rather than a single uniform mechanism. This underscores the importance of considering domain-specific pathways when designing interventions to improve fall prevention-related health literacy in older adults.

Conceptual implications

Overall, the findings support a multidimensional conceptual model in which environmental (housing), functional (mobility), psychological (fear of falling), and socioeconomic (education) factors interact dynamically to shape fall prevention-related health literacy. Housing may represent an upstream structural determinant, mobility reflects functional capacity, fear of falling operates as a psychological mediator influencing communication and cognition, and education contributes to higher-order evaluative competencies such as media literacy and self-management.

This study further suggests a potential mismatch between objective fall risk and fall prevention-related health literacy among older adults. Although a substantial proportion of participants were classified as being at risk of falls based on mobility performance and fall risk assessment, their ability to access, understand, and apply fall prevention information remained limited. This discrepancy indicates that fall prevention-related health literacy may function as an intermediate construct linking objective health status with behavioral adaptation, rather than merely reflecting cognitive knowledge. In this sense, physically vulnerable individuals may not necessarily possess adequate awareness or capacity to engage in preventive behaviors, highlighting the importance of aligning risk status with health literacy enhancement to improve effective fall prevention strategies.

Importantly, fall prevention-related health literacy should not be conceptualized solely as an individual cognitive attribute. Rather, it is embedded within broader environmental and social contexts that shape both risk exposure and adaptive capacity. This ecological framing has significant implications for intervention design, suggesting that effective fall prevention strategies must integrate environmental modification, functional assessment, psychological support, and domain-specific literacy enhancement.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides novel evidence on multidimensional fall prevention-related health literacy practices among community-dwelling older adults in northern Thailand. The inclusion of domain-specific literacy components allowed for a nuanced analysis of associated factors and improved understanding of fall prevention-related health literacy practices as a multidimensional construct. However, several limitations should be acknowledged that the study did not directly assess specific home environmental hazards. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causal relationships, as data were collected at a single time point and temporal relationships cannot be established. In addition, the relatively low prevalence of adequate fall prevention–related health literacy practices (11.3%) and the pronounced floor effect observed in the outcome variable may have reduced statistical variability, limited statistical power, and attenuated some effect estimates, thereby limiting the ability to detect stronger associations between predictors and literacy domains. Social desirability bias may also have influenced responses in interviewer-administered questionnaires, potentially leading to overreporting of positive behaviors or knowledge. Furthermore, important physiological confounders, including frailty status, comorbidities, and functional impairment, were not fully controlled, which may have resulted in residual confounding. Finally, the findings may not be fully generalizable beyond similar community-based older adult populations in northern Thailand.

Implications for practice and research

Interventions should both overall and domain-specific fall prevention literacy. Integrating approaches combining environmental modification, tailored education, cognitive support environmental modification, tailored education, cognitive support, communication enhancement, and self-management training are likely to be most effective. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify causal pathways and evaluate intervention effectiveness. In addition, culturally tailored, literacy-based fall prevention programs may contribute to safer aging and improved quality of life among community-dwelling older adults.

Conclusion

This study revealed a high prevalence of inadequate fall prevention-related health literacy among community-dwelling older adults in Northern Thailand. Environmental and functional factors, particularly housing characteristics and mobility performance, were consistently associated with multiple literacy domains. Domain-specific associations with marital status, education level, and fear of falling further suggest that fall prevention-related health literacy is multidimensional and context-dependent. These findings highlight the importance of integrated community-based strategies combining environmental modification, functional assessment, and literacy-enhancing interventions. Longitudinal and intervention studies are warranted to clarify causal pathways and evaluate the effectiveness of literacy-focused fall prevention programs.
