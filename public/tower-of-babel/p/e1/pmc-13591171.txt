Application of Ultrasound Shear Wave Elastography as an Objective Outcome Measure in Nonspecific Low Back Pain Associated with Myofascial Trigger Points
Authors: Wiraphong Sucharit, Piangdaw Adchaithor, Pham Thi Thuy Linh, Wichai Eungpinichpong, Punthip Thammaroj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess the reliability, clinical association, and discrimination of an ultrasound shear wave elastography (SWE) protocol in individuals with nonspecific low back pain (NSLBP) associated with myofascial trigger points.DesignCross-sectional observational study.SettingA university laboratory.ParticipantsFifteen individuals (N=15) with NSLBP had a mean age of 37.60±6.50 years, with a sex distribution of 4 men and 11 women and a symptom duration of >3 months. An additional 15 asymptomatic controls (ACs) (mean age 36.73±6.65y) were individually matched to the NSLBP group based on age (±5y), sex, physical activity level, and side of image acquisition.InterventionsNot applicable.Main Outcome MeasuresThe primary outcome was muscle stiffness, assessed using SWE and quantified by shear wave speed. Secondary outcomes included pain intensity and disability, assessed using the Visual Analog Scale and the Oswestry Disability Index, respectively.ResultsBoth physiotherapist and radiologist demonstrated good-to-excellent reliability for SWE and shear wave speed measurements. Quadratus lumborum (QL) stiffness was strongly and negatively correlated with pain intensity (r=−0.64, P=.03) and age (r=−0.64, P=.02). Psoas major (PM) stiffness was also negatively correlated with age (r=−0.58, P<.05). The protocol discriminated QL stiffness between individuals with NSLBP and AC, with a median between-group difference of 5.98 kPa (95% CI, 0.42-7.30 kPa; P=.02).ConclusionsA well-trained physiotherapist appears to be able to quantify deep lumbar muscle stiffness. QL and PM stiffness values were preliminarily associated with clinical characteristics and showed potential to discriminate between individuals with NSLBP and ACs. Further validation with larger samples and multiple raters is required to support its potential as an objective outcome measure for clinical assessment and treatment monitoring in NSLBP.KEYWORDS: Muscle stiffness, Myofascial trigger points, Nonspecific low back pain, Rehabilitation, Ultrasound shear wave elastography

Approximately 90% of all low back pain cases are classified as nonspecific low back pain (NSLBP),1 which defined as pain located between the costal margin and the gluteal fold that cannot be attributed to a specific pathologic cause.2, 3, 4 Prolonged sitting in desk workers may lead to psoas major (PM) muscle tightness, potentially increasing mechanical stress on the lumbar spine and the risk of NSLBP.5 Most evidence suggests that pain symptoms in NSLBP primarily originate from active myofascial trigger points (MTrPs) in the back and hip muscles,3,6 with the quadratus lumborum (QL) muscle being one of the most commonly affected sites.2 Clinically, MTrPs are diagnosed by the presence of palpable tenderness within a taut band, reproduction of the subject’s typical pain, and/or referred pain. Although manual palpation to identify contraction knots, combined with the observation of referred pain patterns, can help localize specific muscles during physical examination, there remains a need for objective and quantitative assessment methods.7 Such methods may enable physiotherapists to gain a deeper understanding of the deep lower back muscles.8

Ultrasound shear wave elastography (SWE) is a noninvasive, cost-effective imaging technique that is widely applied in research settings.9,10 The method is based on the generation of shear waves by acoustic radiation force impulses from the ultrasound transducer. The shear wave speed (SWS) is calculated as the propagation distance (m) divided by the travel time (s) and is reported in meters per second (m/s). The shear modulus (μ) can be estimated from SWS and tissue density, and Young’s modulus (E) can be approximated as E=3μ under standard assumptions, with values expressed in kilopascals (kPa).11

The applications of SWE to deep muscles have reported that individuals with NSLBP exhibit significantly lower QL muscle stiffness compared with asymptomatic controls (ACs).3,12 These findings contradict the traditional concept that greater stiffness is typically observed in MTrPs and is associated with worse clinical characteristics, which may affect the interpretation and prediction of stiffness values.13, 14, 15 However, prior studies did not clearly document (1) specific diagnostic criteria for NSLBP3,12,16; (2) control of known factors influencing stiffness, such as age17; physical activity level8; and sex8; or (3) the use of standardized offline analysis protocols. By adequately controlling for these factors, clinical associations and differences in muscle stiffness between NSLBP and AC may be observed, which may support clinical decision-making for prognosis and monitoring of treatment outcomes in clinical and research settings.

We hypothesize that the proposed measurement protocol will demonstrate improve reliability, correlate with clinical outcome measures, and discriminate between NSLBP and AC. Accordingly, the study aimed to (1) assess intra- and interrater reliability; (2) explore correlations with clinical outcome measures; and (3) evaluate discriminative validity compared with the AC group. If this protocol demonstrates reliability, clinical association, and discriminative ability, it may be used as an objective outcome measure to monitor prognosis and treatment response in individuals with NSLBP associated with MTrPs, with potential clinical and research implications.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional observational study design was used (fig 1). All participants provided written informed consent prior to study participation, and all procedures were reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Khon Kaen University (KKU) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (Reference: HE672245) and registered in the Thai Clinical Trails Registry (TCTR20250102006).

Study flow diagram showing the recruitment process for participants with NSLBP and ACs.

Participants

Eligibility criteria included men or women aged 30-50 years; individuals in the NSLBP group reported lower back pain for >3 months and were diagnosed with NSLBP associated with active MTrPs in the QL muscle on at least 1 side based on standard criteria of Simon7 including palpable tender points within taut bands and reproduction of the subject’s typical pain upon manual pressure, with or without referred pain. In contrast, AC group reported no current lower back pain and had no diagnosis of active MTrPs in the lower back. Exclusion criteria for both groups included a body mass index≥30 kg/m², uncontrolled vital signs, complete disability, lumbar radiculopathy, recent lower back surgery, or specific pathologic causes of low back pain (eg, tumors, infections, or inflammatory disorders).

All participants were recruited through advertisements at a government clinic. Screening was conducted by a physiotherapist with 5 years of experience using questionnaires and physical examinations. Participants with NSLBP were recruited first until the target sample size (n=15) was reached. Subsequently, AC participants were recruited using the same method and individually matched to NSLBP participants based on age (±5y), sex, physical activity level, and side of image acquisition.

The sample size was determined based on the reliability analysis. The minimum acceptable intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was set at 0.7, with an expected ICC of 0.9. Assuming an ICC (3,1) model with 2 raters and 3 repeated measurements per participant, a sample of 15 participants with NSLBP was required to achieve 80% statistical power at a 5% significance level for reliability testing.18 An additional 15 ACs were recruited for preliminary assessment of discriminative ability, resulting in a total sample size of 30 participants.

Data acquisition protocol for ultrasound shear wave elastography

An EPIQ Elite ultrasound system (Philips19) equipped with a low-frequency curvilinear probe (C5-1, 1-5 MHz) was used to collect the data. The standard console of presettings was applied for all acquisitions (gain: optimize 2D gain, dynamic range: 55 dB, confidence map threshold: 60%).

The present study followed the protocol established by Zhou et al.8 Participants were positioned in a side-lying posture, with the torso, vertebral column, and lower limbs aligned in a neutral position. A towel was used as needed to maintain a neutral lumbar spine. The ipsilateral lower limb was fully supported by pillows to ensure muscle relaxation. For participants with hip flexor tightness, slight hip flexion of no more than 20° was allowed.

Anatomical structure was identified using brightness-mode (B-mode) ultrasonography, which the curvilinear probe was placed above the iliac crest along the midaxillary line and then rotated posteriorly by approximately 20° until the L4 vertebra appeared in the center of the field of view. In each B-mode image, the iliac crest was visualized laterally and the L4 vertebra centrally. The QL muscle, appearing as a relatively hypoechoic structure overlying the PM muscle, was identified, with the PM muscle located directly over the vertebrae (fig 2A). Participants were then instructed to relax their muscles to prevent contractions. The SWE mode was activated, and a confidence map was used to verify an acceptable measurement region (fig 2B). A region of interest (ROI) was placed over the identified QL and PM muscle, and the focus depth was manually adjusted to center the ROI beneath the probe. A 10-second real-time cine loop was recorded at the end of tidal expiration while participants held their breath to minimize motion artifacts.8

Offline analysis

All analyses were performed offline after data collection was completed. Five images with homogeneous and well-filled ROIs were selected from each 10-second video for analysis. A 1.0-cm² circular quantification box (Q-box) was drawn for the QL muscle (fig 2C) and a 1.8-cm² box for the PM muscle (fig 2D) at the center of each ROI. The median values of SWE and SWS were extracted from each Q-box, and the mean of the 5 measurements was used to represent the muscle stiffness (kPa) and SWS (m/s) for each participant.

Clinical outcome measures

Pain intensity was assessed using a 10-cm visual analog scale, where 0 cm indicated no pain and 10 cm represented the worst pain imaginable.20 Thai version of the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI),21 which contains 10 items assessing limitations in daily activities related to low back pain.

Procedure

This observational study followed the Guidelines for Reporting Reliability and Agreement Studies.22 Reliability was evaluated for both intra- and interrater measurements using 2 examiners: a radiologist (Rad) with >10 years of experience in musculoskeletal ultrasonography (US), and a musculoskeletal PT with 5 years of clinical experience but <1 year of US experience, who had completed 30 hours of structured training in US. Each NSLBP participant selected the side of the body that was most painful, after which the PT rater confirmed the presence of MTrPs through manual palpation. Participants then rested for 5 minutes before the measurements. The sequence of data acquisition was randomized for each of the 15 NSLBP participants using a simple randomization. The Rad rater and PT rater were blinded to each other’s results to minimize observer bias, and all measurements were performed and recorded independently. However, raters were not blinded to participant group during data acquisition; stiffness measurements were subsequently analyzed independently using offline analysis, with each rater blinded to the other’s results. SWE assessments were performed 3 times per rater, with a 2-minute interval between measurements and a 5-minute interval between raters.23 The first and second measurements were used to assess intrarater reliability22, and the third measurement from each rater was used to assess interrater reliability. In addition, correlation analyses were explored using the third stiffness measurement obtained by the PT rater and clinical outcome measures in NSLBP group. Discriminative validity was further evaluated by comparing the third measurement from the NSLBP group with single measurements obtained from 15 ACs, all assessed once by the same PT rater. All data acquisition was performed during the morning session.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and visualization were performed using R softwarea (Version 4.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing 2025). The Shapiro–Wilk test and skewness statistics were used to assess the normality of the data. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD.

Reliability was assessed from both relative and absolute perspectives. Relative reliability was evaluated through intrarater and interrater reliability using the ICC 3,1.18 Absolute reliability was examined using Bland–Altman analysis,8 including assessment of fixed and proportional bias. Limits of agreement were calculated as the mean difference ± 1.96 × SD of the differences, and 95% CIs were determined for the upper and lower bounds.18 Measurement precision was quantified by the standard error of measurement (SEM), calculated as SEM = SD × √(1 − ICC). The minimal detectable change at the 95% CI (MDC95) was then derived as MDC95 = SEM × 1.96 × √2.

Correlations between the stiffness and clinical outcomes were explored using partial correlation analysis (Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficient, as appropriate based on data distribution), with adjustment for adipose tissue thickness (ATT), because overlying adipose tissue may influence SWE measurements by attenuating shear wave propagation.11

Discriminative validity was assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and the median difference with a 95% CI was estimated using a bootstrap resampling procedure.

Results

Demographic data and clinical characteristics

Sixty-four participants were screened, including 34 individuals with NSLBP and 30 ACs; of these, 15 NSLBP participants met the inclusion criteria, and 15 ACs were individually matched. In the NSLBP group, pain intensity was moderate (4.70±2.33 cm), and the ODI indicated minimal disability (16±10%). An additional 15 AC had a mean age of 36.73±6.65 years. Differences between groups were observed in ATT and lumbar range of motion in the sagittal plane (flexion and extension) (P<.05) (table 1).

The QL was negatively correlation with both pain intensity (r=−0.64, P=.03) and age (r=−0.64, P=.02), whereas the PM was negatively correlated with age (ρ=−0.76, P<.01) (fig 5). Discriminative validity

The Mann–Whitney U test comparing the NSLBP and AC groups showed median difference for the QL of 5.98 kPa (95% CI, [0.42-7.30 kPa]; P=.02) and 0.43 m/s (95% CI, [0.02-0.78 m/s]; P=.02), whereas the PM showed median differences of 4.97 kPa (95% CI, [2.23-6.57 kPa]; P=.08) and 0.51 m/s (95% CI, [−0.14 to 0.71 m/s]; P=.15) (fig 6).

Discussion

This preliminary study suggests that the SWE protocol may have potential as an objective outcome measure for assessing QL and PM stiffness in individuals with NSLBP in clinical practice. This is supported by the good-to-excellent reliability of the protocol and the observed associations between QL and PM stiffness and clinical outcome measures. In addition, the protocol showed potential to discriminate QL stiffness between individuals with NSLBP and AC.

Individuals with NSLBP presented with moderate pain intensity (4.70±2.33 cm), minimal ODI (16±10%), and significantly reduced active lumbar range of motion compared with AC in the sagittal plane. This restricted range of motion may be related to the presence of active MTrPs in the QL, as this muscle assists lumbar extension, and may be further compounded by PM tightness, which can increase mechanical demand on the lumbar extensor muscles.5 Further investigation of this finding is recommended.

It appears that a well-trained physiotherapist has sufficient skill to perform the measurements. Our findings showed good-to-excellent intrarater reliability and excellent interrater reliability for the QL (ICC3,1=0.96), as well as good interrater reliability for the PM (ICC3,1=0.81) when compared with the radiologist. Similar findings have been reported in individuals without chronic low back pain8 and in those with chronic low back pain.16 Our results also confirmed with previous studies that averaging multiple measurements substantially improves reliability compared with a single measurement obtained from a Q-box.24

Bland–Altman analysis revealed no evidence of fixed or proportional bias, which may be attributable to the use of shear wave-based stiffness measurements in this study. In contrast, previous studies have reported that mechanical pressure–based techniques, such as tissue hardness meters, demonstrate both fixed and proportional bias.23,25 Fixed bias occurs when significant differences are observed between repeated measurements or between raters, likely reflecting variability in measurement technique. Proportional bias indicates that measurement accuracy decreases as the target structure becomes deeper. This may be related to the fact that mechanical pressure–based measurement techniques require raters to maintain a consistent rate of applied pressure throughout the measurement,25 whereas shear wave-based stiffness measurements require only minimal probe pressure and allow offline analysis to isolate specific muscles. The SWE technique may have potential for application in both research and clinical practice.

Although no systematic bias was detected, interrater measurements of the PM showed wider 95% limits of agreement, as well as larger SEM, and MDC95, than those of the QL. A recent study also reported that reliability tended to be lower in deeper muscles, such as the transversus abdominis, with ICC values ranging from 0.45 to 0.97.26 Moreover, our findings also found that positive correlations between ultrasound parameters, with a stronger relationship observed in the QL muscle compared with the PM muscle. This may partly reflect (1) the greater depth of the PM, which can reduce shear wave amplitude and signal-to-noise ratio because of attenuation by ATT and other superficial tissues27; and (2) the anisotropic nature of muscle, which requires raters to carefully maintain perpendicular insonation relative to the muscle fibers, potentially increasing interrater measurement variability.28 We recommend using a single rater for deep muscle measurements and reporting SEM and MDC95 to distinguish true physiological changes from measurement error. It may also be useful to report both ultrasound parameters.3

Our findings support the traditional concept that greater stiffness is typically observed in patients with MTrPs.13, 14, 15 Specifically, QL stiffness in individuals with NSLBP showed a median difference of 5.98 kPa (95% CI, 0.42-7.30 kPa), whereas the PM did not reach statistical significance compared with AC. This may be related to the inclusion criteria, which were based on the presence of active MTrPs in the QL muscle. Previous studies have also reported that stiffness of the thoracolumbar fascia (TLF), erector spinae (ES), and multifidus (MF) is significantly higher in patients with chronic NSLBP than in individuals without low back pain.4

Despite higher stiffness being observed in individuals with NSLBP compared with AC, its correlations with clinical outcomes remain inconclusive when compared with previous studies. Liu et al4 reported positive correlations between increased stiffness of the TLF, ES, and MF and both pain intensity and disability. However, our results showed that QL stiffness was strongly and negatively correlated with pain intensity and age, whereas PM stiffness was also negatively correlated with age. Similar findings have been reported in a previous QL study.12 These contrasting findings may be explained by different biomechanical roles and pain-adaptation movement strategies of the involved structures.29 First, the TLF, ES, and MF are posterior lower back structures that primarily function as sagittal plane stabilizers and frequently undergo eccentric loading during prolonged sitting.4 Repetitive poor postural mechanics may contribute to cumulative tissue stress, resulting in increased passive and active stiffness as an adaptive load-sharing response.30,31 Moreover, these muscles are often over-recruited during pain as part of a protective guarding mechanism, resulting in increased muscle activity.32 Consistent with this, lumbar loading increases from cranial to caudal segments, with stiffness at L4-5 being significantly higher, whereas stiffness at L1-2 shows no significant difference.4 The L4-5 segment serves as a key junction for weight bearing and force transmission,30 which may contribute to elevated resting stiffness. In contrast, the QL functions as a posterolateral stabilizer and frontal-plane controller.12 With increasing pain, individuals may adopt pain-avoidance strategies characterized by reduced lateral flexion, pelvic hiking, and asymmetric loading. These adaptations may reduce QL activation and mechanical loading. Over time, this may result in muscle inhibition and increased intramuscular fat infiltration in individuals with chronic pain.33 Moreover, age-related muscle weakness may contribute to reductions in muscle stiffness.34 We may conclude that PTs applying this technique should consider that stiffness adaptations may vary across lumbar structures depending on anatomical location, functional role, and adaptive responses to chronic pain and aging.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was calculated to assess reliability and may therefore have been insufficient for evaluating clinical associations and discriminative validity. These findings should be validated in larger samples. The small number of men also limits subgroup analyses related to sex differences, and most participants had moderate pain intensity and minimal disability. Although the age range of 30-50 years was selected to reduce potential confounding from age-related changes and to focus on peak prevalence in middle-aged adults,34,35 future studies should include individuals with more severe symptoms, a more balanced sex distribution, and a wider age range to enhance generalizability. Second, imaging was not performed directly over individual MTrPs and assessed reliability only for resting stiffness, which may limit sensitivity to localized pathologic changes and to muscle behavior during contraction. Future studies should develop protocols for imaging directly over MTrPs and during contraction to better characterize mechanical alterations in NSLBP. This study included only 1 radiologist and 1 physiotherapist; future studies should include a larger number of raters to enhance generalizability in clinical practice. Third, the penetration depth of SWE is limited to approximately 7 cm, which may restrict its application in obesity. Alternative imaging techniques, such as magnetic resonance elastography,36 may therefore be more suitable in these populations. Finally, responsiveness should be investigated to determine whether SWE can capture treatment effects. A self-reported measure of muscle stiffness was not included, which may limit the clinical interpretation of the SWE findings. Future studies should incorporate both subjective and objective measures to better understand their relationship.

Conclusions

This preliminary study demonstrated the good-to-excellent reliability for measuring QL and PM stiffness. Stiffness in NSLBP appeared to be associated with clinical outcome measures and showed potential to discriminate between NSLBP and AC. Further studies with larger sample sizes, multiple raters, and multicenter designs are needed to confirm these findings and to support their potential application in clinical and research settings.
