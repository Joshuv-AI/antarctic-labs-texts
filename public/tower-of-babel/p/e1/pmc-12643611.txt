Application effect of individualized nursing in rehabilitation nursing of patients with severe acute pancreatitis
Authors: Jie Chen, Yun Ma, Mei-Rong Shi, Juan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The recovery outcomes of patients with severe acute pancreatitis (SAP) are closely related to the nursing approach. This study aims to explore the differences in therapeutic effects between standard care and personalized care in SAP patients and analyze factors influencing patient recovery. The baseline characteristics of SAP patients receiving standard care (n = 568) and personalized care (n = 292) (including medical history, disease severity, and treatment interventions) were compared. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were then performed to evaluate the effects of C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), Ranson score, intensive care unit (ICU) stay duration, mechanical ventilation, diabetes history, binge eating history, and nursing approach on patient recovery. Propensity score matching was applied to balance baseline differences, followed by stratified multivariate logistic regression analyses based on mechanical ventilation status. Finally, chi-square tests were used to assess the long-term impact of nursing approaches on recurrence and readmission rates. Baseline analysis revealed that patients in the personalized care group had higher rates of diabetes and binge eating histories, as well as more severe clinical conditions. Univariate logistic regression showed that CRP, PCT, Ranson score, ICU treatment duration, mechanical ventilation, diabetes history, binge eating history, and nursing method were all significantly associated with recovery outcomes. Multivariate logistic regression further confirmed that CRP (P = .021), ICU treatment duration (P = .002), mechanical ventilation (P < .001), diabetes history (P < .001), binge eating history (P < .001), and nursing method (P < .001) were independent predictors of recovery, with personalized care significantly improving recovery rates (odds ratios [OR] = 7.212). Stratified regression analysis showed that among patients not requiring mechanical ventilation, diabetes history (P = .030, OR = 0.314), and ICU treatment duration (P = .021, OR = 0.889) were significantly associated with recovery. In patients who required mechanical ventilation, binge eating (P = .002, OR = 0.148) and diabetes history (P = .001, OR = 0.159) had negative impacts on recovery, whereas personalized nursing care had a positive effect (P = .048, OR = 1.152). Chi-square tests further indicated that personalized care significantly reduced recurrence and readmission rates. Personalized nursing care improves recovery outcomes in patients with SAP compared to standard care and reduces recurrence and readmission rates. Personalized nursing care should be widely implemented in the recovery management of SAP patients.

1. Introduction

Severe acute pancreatitis (SAP) is an acute gastrointestinal disease with high morbidity and mortality, commonly caused by gallstones, overeating, and excessive alcohol consumption.[1,2] Its pathogenesis is complex, involving multiple pathological processes such as inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and multi-organ failure.[3,4] Despite considerable advancements in medical technology in recent years that have improved treatment outcomes for SAP, challenges such as low recovery rates, high recurrence, and frequent readmissions continue to pose significant obstacles in clinical practice.[5] This is especially true for patients with more severe conditions, where recovery time is longer and complications are more likely, increasing the complexity of treatment and the suffering of the patients.[6,7] Consequently, optimizing nursing care to enhance recovery outcomes while minimizing recurrence and hospital readmissions has become a critical priority in contemporary clinical practice.

Nursing care plays a critical role in the recovery of SAP patients. Traditional standard nursing methods usually rely on routine care procedures, focusing on basic care and managing common complications.[8] However, with the development of personalized medicine, individualized nursing care has gradually emerged as a more effective model. Personalized nursing care involves the development of a customized care plan grounded in a thorough assessment of the patient’s unique clinical presentation, disease severity, physical functionality, psychological condition, and lifestyle factors, integrated with laboratory and imaging findings. This approach emphasizes patient-centered care, fully respecting individual differences and needs, and dynamically adjusting the nursing content and methods accordingly.[9] Studies have shown that personalized nursing care significantly improves patient outcomes, shortens hospital stays, and enhances quality of life.[10]

In patients with SAP, factors including disease severity, medical history (such as diabetes and hypertension), and lifestyle habits (e.g., binge eating and high-fat diets) play a critical role in influencing recovery outcomes. Clinical indicators of disease severity, like the Ranson score, are frequently employed to evaluate prognosis risk.[11,12] Additionally, inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT) reflect the intensity of the inflammatory response in the body, and changes in these markers during the acute phase are closely related to disease progression.[13–15] Previous studies have indicated that factors such as the length of intensive care unit (ICU) stay, use of mechanical ventilation, and a history of diabetes substantially influence patient recovery. Therefore, when examining the effects of nursing care on recovery outcomes in SAP patients, it is crucial to account for these important confounding variables.

The aim of this study is to compare the efficacy of standard care versus personalized care in patients with SAP, specifically assessing how different nursing approaches influence recovery outcomes, recurrence rates, and hospital readmissions. We will 1st analyze the baseline characteristic differences between patients under both types of care. Then, we will use univariate and multivariate logistic regression models to explore the key factors affecting recovery. To reduce potential confounding bias, we will conduct propensity score matching (PSM) and perform stratified analyses for patients with and without mechanical ventilation. Finally, we will use chi-square tests to analyze the long-term effects of nursing methods on patient outcomes, including recurrence and readmission rates.

Previous studies on the rehabilitation of SAP patients mainly focused on univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis, without conducting more detailed stratified analyses. This study innovatively employed stratified multivariate logistic regression analysis according to mechanical ventilation usage, thereby addressing a critical gap and improving the precision and clinical relevance of the findings.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Patient selection

The study subjects were patients with SAP from the Department of Gastroenterology at Sichuan University West China Hospital, admitted between January 2019 and January 2022. All included patients met the diagnostic criteria for SAP established by the International Association of Pancreatology and the Revised Atlanta Classification, confirmed by clinical presentation and laboratory tests, and received corresponding nursing care and treatment. We included a total of 860 eligible patients with SAP. Based on the type of nursing care they received, patients were divided into 2 groups: the standard care group (n = 568) and the personalized care group (n = 292) (Figure S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q660). Inclusion criteria were: diagnosis of SAP based on clinical manifestations, serum enzyme indicators, and imaging findings, with SAP defined as persistent organ failure (including respiratory, circulatory, or renal dysfunction) lasting ≥ 48 hours; received at least 5 days of nursing intervention during hospitalization; and complete medical history, treatment, and follow-up data. Exclusion criteria were: patients in a terminal condition upon admission; patients who did not complete nursing interventions during hospitalization; and patients with significant data missing; patients with severe psychiatric disorders or poor compliance; patients with other severe systemic diseases such as malignancies. All patients signed informed consent forms upon admission, and the study was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee. The study is retrospective, which avoids the challenges of patient recruitment, as well as time and resource constraints that may be encountered in prospective studies, thus enhancing the feasibility of the research. We performed the calculations in R (University of Auckland, Auckland, New Zealand) using the “pwr” package. A statistical power of at least 80% is required, with the effect size set at a medium level for meaningful results. For continuous data, the required sample size for each group is 175 or more; for categorical data, the total sample size needs to be 88 or more; for the multivariate logistic regression analysis, a sample size of at least 123 is required. The sample size in this study meets these criteria.

2.2. Data collection

We collected clinical data from the electronic medical records system, including baseline characteristics: age, gender, body mass index, smoking history, drinking history, diabetes history, hypertension history, cardiovascular disease history, binge eating history, and high-fat diet history. Disease severity was assessed using the Ranson score to evaluate the severity of the patient’s condition, and we recorded hospital stay duration, ICU treatment duration, and the use of mechanical ventilation. Biochemical markers such as CRP, PCT, serum amylase, and lipase were collected to reflect the patient’s inflammation level and pancreatic function. By recording the patients’ recovery status at discharge (recovered/not recovered), recurrence (whether recurrence occurred), and readmission rate within 1 year, the effectiveness of the nursing interventions was evaluated. In addition, the SF-36 questionnaire was used to assess patients’ quality of life during hospitalization, with scores ranging from 0 to 100. The primary outcome is the patient’s recovery status at discharge (recovered/not recovered), and the secondary outcomes are the recurrence rate (whether recurrence occurred) and readmission rate within 1 year. The recurrence rate and readmission rate are important indicators for evaluating the long-term health status of SAP patients and are closely related to their recovery status. Patients with better recovery typically exhibit better physical function and disease control, resulting in lower recurrence and readmission rates. Recovered: the patient’s main symptoms, such as abdominal pain, vomiting, and nausea, significantly decrease or disappear. Vital signs, including temperature, blood pressure, pulse, and respiratory rate, return to normal levels. The patient gradually resumes normal eating, and their weight remains stable or increases. Physiological and biochemical indicators trend toward normal. Not recovered: the patient’s symptoms, such as abdominal pain and vomiting, show no significant improvement or reoccur during recovery. Multi-organ failure occurs, and severe complications, such as pancreatic necrosis, bleeding, and infection, arise. Physiological and biochemical indicators remain abnormal.

The nursing interventions mainly included standard care and personalized care. Patients in the standard care group received routine hospital nursing interventions, which included daily monitoring of vital signs (temperature, pulse, respiratory rate, blood pressure) and clinical symptoms to promptly detect and manage any changes in condition; medication management according to medical orders, supervision of medication adherence, and monitoring of drug efficacy and adverse reactions to ensure medication safety; preventive and routine care for common complications of SAP, such as maintaining airway patency, monitoring urine output, and preventing pressure ulcers; additionally, patients were provided with appropriate guidance on rest and activity, basic dietary advice to help restore nutritional balance, and lifestyle adjustments to promote physical and mental health. Patients in the individualized nursing group underwent a comprehensive assessment upon admission, including physiological evaluation of vital signs, pain scores, gastrointestinal symptoms, nutritional status, and organ function. Psychological status was assessed using the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale and Self-Rating Depression Scale. Laboratory and imaging examinations, including CRP, PCT, liver and kidney function tests, and relevant imaging studies, were conducted to evaluate disease severity and complications. Based on these assessment results, individualized nursing plans were developed through communication with patients and their families. During hospitalization, reassessments were performed at least every 48 hours. In cases of acute changes in vital signs, laboratory indicators, or clinical symptoms, reassessments were conducted every 12 to 24 hours. The nursing plan was adjusted if pain scores increased by more than 2 points compared to previous assessments, inflammatory markers (CRP or PCT) rose by over 20%, new organ dysfunction occurred, or psychological scores (Self-Rating Anxiety Scale or Self-Rating Depression Scale) increased by 5 points or more. Patients in the personalized care group received more individualized nursing interventions tailored to their specific conditions. Based on each patient’s medical history, constitution, and lifestyle, a personalized nursing plan was developed, which included dietary management, psychological counseling, rehabilitation training, and prevention and management of complications. For patients with diabetes, a history of binge eating, or a high-fat diet, specific individualized dietary and lifestyle guidance was provided.

Our data collection followed a standardized process, and all patient data were extracted by experienced medical staff from the electronic medical record system. For the assessment of recovery status, the judgments were reviewed by 2 experts with over 5 years of clinical experience to ensure objectivity and consistency in the evaluation.

2.3. Univariate logistic regression analysis of recovery status in SAP patients

To initially assess the influence of various clinical and nursing-related factors on the recovery status of SAP patients, we 1st conducted univariate logistic regression analysis. The goal of this analysis was to evaluate the independent effect of each variable on patient recovery (recovered vs not recovered) without accounting for other potential confounding factors. Recovery status was used as the dependent variable (0 = not recovered, 1 = recovered), and each independent variable was included in the logistic regression model separately for analysis.

2.4. Multivariate logistic regression analysis of recovery status in SAP patients

To further control for confounding factors, multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the independent impact of nursing methods (standard care vs personalized care) on patient recovery rates. Variables included in the model were those found to be significant in the univariate analysis, as well as variables with large baseline differences.

2.5. PSM

PSM in this study is primarily used to reduce potential confounding factors between the groups. Given that there is some imbalance in our baseline data, the study subjects may have selection bias due to differences in baseline characteristics. Therefore, PSM helps to balance the distribution of key clinical variables between the standard nursing and personalized nursing groups, allowing us to accurately assess the impact of personalized nursing on the recovery of SAP patients.[16,17] First, a logistic regression model was used to calculate the propensity score for each patient to receive personalized care, reflecting the probability of each patient receiving personalized care based on their baseline characteristics. In the matching process, we used a 1:1 nearest neighbor matching method, where for each patient in the personalized care group, a patient from the standard care group with the closest propensity score was selected, ensuring comparability of baseline characteristics between the 2 groups. After matching, chi-square tests (for categorical variables) and standardized mean differences (SMD) (for continuous variables) were used to assess the balance of baseline characteristics before and after matching. A smaller difference in baseline characteristics, an SMD of <0.1, and non-significant chi-square test results (P > .05) indicated that the 2 groups achieved good balance in baseline characteristics after matching, eliminating the potential influence of baseline differences on the results. This process ensures that the subsequent analysis of the effect of nursing methods on recovery is more reliable and scientific. When selecting variables for PSM, we chose variables that showed significant imbalances between the 2 groups’ baseline characteristics (e.g., history of diabetes, initial Ranson score, etc). This helps reduce confounding bias and ensures that the standard care group and the personalized care group are balanced in key baseline characteristics, thereby enhancing the credibility and accuracy of the research results.

2.6. Stratified multivariate logistic regression analysis

After adjusting for baseline characteristics using PSM, stratified multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to further explore the effects of nursing methods on patients with different levels of disease severity. We performed separate analyses for patients who did and did not use mechanical ventilation. This stratified analysis aimed to distinguish between patients based on the severity of their illness, as mechanical ventilation is typically required for more critical SAP patients. By dividing patients into those with and without mechanical ventilation, we were able to better assess the specific effects of nursing methods (standard care vs personalized care) in different clinical contexts.

2.7. Statistical methods

In this study, all statistical tests were 2-sided, and normality tests were performed before conducting parametric analyses. For continuous variables (e.g., age, body mass index, Ranson score), independent sample t tests or Mann–Whitney U tests were used to assess the differences between the 2 groups, with results presented as medians (minimum–maximum), depending on whether the data followed a normal distribution. For categorical variables (e.g., gender, diabetes history, binge eating history), chi-square tests or Fisher exact tests (when the sample size was small) were used for analysis, with results presented as n (%). After PSM, the balance of baseline characteristics was assessed using SMD, with SMD values <0.1 indicating good baseline balance. For the evaluation of recovery outcomes, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used, and odds ratios (OR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. When the CI did not include 1, it indicated that the factor had a significant impact on recovery outcomes. We used cross-validation to perform sensitivity analysis on the multivariate logistic regression models after PSM.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics and disease data of SAP patients

The results showed significant differences between the standard care group and the personalized care group in terms of diabetes history (P = 2.66E-39), binge eating history (P = 1.37E-11), Ranson score (P = .00425), length of hospital stay (P = .0289), ICU treatment duration (P = .044), use of mechanical ventilation (P = 7.63E-30), PCT (P = .0247), CRP (P = .0172), recovery status (P = 1.27E-19), and SF-36 quality of life score (P = .0152). Patients in the personalized care group had more severe conditions (e.g., higher Ranson scores and ICU treatment duration), but their recovery rate and quality of life scores were significantly better than those in the standard care group (Table 1). After PSM, there were no significant differences in the baseline characteristics (such as history of diabetes, Ranson score, etc) between the 2 groups (Table 2).

3.4. Stratified multivariate logistic regression analysis results

As shown in the SMD line graph (Fig. 1), before matching (green line), the SMD values of multiple variables, such as the Ranson Score, were large. After matching (red line), the SMD values of most variables were significantly reduced and approached 0, indicating that the baseline characteristics of the 2 groups were well balanced after matching.

Standardized mean differences (SMD) line plot before and after PSM. PSM = propensity score matching.

After PSM, stratified multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed based on whether mechanical ventilation was used in patients with SAP. The results show that in the group of patients not receiving mechanical ventilation (Model 1), nursing methods (P = .014, OR = 3.346, 95% CI = 1.278–8.763) had a significant positive effect on recovery. In addition, ICU treatment duration (P = .005, OR = 0.882, 95% CI = 0.808–0.963) and history of diabetes (P = .001, OR = 0.213, 95% CI = 0.083–0.547) had significant negative effects on recovery. Also, a history of binge eating (P = .003, OR = 0.219, 95% CI = 0.081–0.593) was associated with poor recovery. In the group of patients receiving mechanical ventilation (Model 2), nursing methods (P < .001, OR = 6.452, 95% CI = 2.743–15.178) had a significant positive effect on recovery. Additionally, elevated CRP levels (P = .030, OR = 0.983, 95% CI = 0.967–0.998), history of binge eating (P < .001, OR = 0.092, 95% CI = 0.028–0.303), and history of diabetes (P < .001, OR = 0.185, 95% CI = 0.075–0.458) all significantly reduced the likelihood of recovery (Table 5). Our nursing methods showed a higher OR value in patients receiving mechanical ventilation compared to those not receiving mechanical ventilation, suggesting that personalized care has a better effect in patients with mechanical ventilation.

3.5. The impact of nursing methods on recurrence and readmission rates in SAP patients

An analysis was conducted on the impact of different nursing approaches on recovery in mechanically ventilated and non-mechanically ventilated patients. The results showed that, among mechanically ventilated patients, 75% of those without recurrence received standard care, and 25% received personalized care; among those with recurrence, 89% received standard care, and 11% received personalized care, indicating that personalized care is associated with a lower recurrence rate (P = .04) (Fig. 2A). Among patients who did not require readmission, 75% received standard care, and 25% received personalized care; whereas, among those who were readmitted, 93% received standard care, and 7% received personalized care, suggesting that personalized care is associated with a lower readmission rate (P = .008) (Fig. 2B). In non-mechanically ventilated patients, a similar trend was observed, with a higher proportion of personalized care in patients who did not experience recurrence or readmission compared to those who did, though the difference was not as significant as in the mechanically ventilated group (Fig. 2C and D).

4. Discussion

This study aimed to compare the efficacy of standard care versus personalized care in patients with SAP and to analyze various factors influencing recovery. The results demonstrated that personalized care significantly enhanced recovery rates, lowered recurrence rates, and reduced readmission rates: effects that were particularly pronounced in patients with more severe conditions requiring mechanical ventilation. This study demonstrates that personalized nursing care significantly improves the prognosis of SAP patients, which is consistent with previously reported studies. Personalized nursing care plays an important role in improving recovery, reducing complications, and enhancing clinical outcomes in critically ill patients.[18,19]

First, the study found that personalized care significantly improved the recovery rate of SAP patients, consistent with previous reports in the literature.[20] Personalized care, by tailoring nursing plans according to the patient’s specific condition, lifestyle, and psychological needs, enhanced the recovery process in multiple ways.[21] This approach not only focuses on disease treatment but also integrates measures such as nutritional management, psychological support, and complication prevention, thereby improving the patient’s overall prognosis. Personalized care was especially vital for patients requiring mechanical ventilation, significantly contributing to respiratory support, complication prevention, and long-term recovery. These findings highlight the imperative to expand the implementation of personalized care models in clinical practice.

Second, both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses confirmed the significant influence of various factors on patient recovery. Clinical indicators including CRP, PCT, Ranson score, and ICU treatment duration proved to be particularly critical in predicting recovery, highlighting that the intensity of inflammatory responses and the nature of interventions during treatment exert a profound influence on patient outcomes.[22,23] Additionally, factors such as mechanical ventilation and a history of diabetes had negative effects on recovery. Specifically, in patients requiring mechanical ventilation, binge eating and a history of diabetes significantly reduced the likelihood of recovery. This aligns with the established role of binge eating and diabetes in the pathogenesis of acute pancreatitis, as these factors can intensify pancreatic stress, thereby contributing to more severe complications and poorer clinical outcomes.

The results of the stratified multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed different effects of nursing methods in subgroups of patients with varying disease severity. For patients who did not require mechanical ventilation, personalized care had a relatively insignificant impact on recovery, possibly because these patients were less severe and generally did not require complex therapeutic interventions. Since their condition was more stable, standard care was sufficient to meet their basic treatment needs, resulting in minimal differences in the effects of nursing interventions. The potential for recovery improvement in these patients was relatively limited, and the influence of nursing methods on recovery outcomes was less pronounced. Nevertheless, personalized care may still provide benefits for specific individuals, such as better psychological support or lifestyle guidance, though it did not show statistically significant effects on overall recovery rates. In contrast, for patients who required mechanical ventilation, personalized care had a significant positive impact on recovery. Mechanical ventilation often serves as an indicator of disease progression in SAP patients, signifying the need for increased support and intervention to sustain vital functions. In these circumstances, personalized care assumes an even more critical role. First, personalized care provided more precise nutritional support to help critically ill patients obtain adequate nutrition to support recovery and accelerate physical rehabilitation. Second, personalized care allowed for the development of individualized respiratory management plans based on the patient’s specific condition, ensuring that the use of mechanical ventilation minimized complications and helping patients recover independent breathing function as soon as possible. Furthermore, personalized care played an essential role in psychological support. Critically ill patients often face significant psychological stress and anxiety, particularly during long-term mechanical ventilation, which may cause anxiety, fear, and even depression.[24] Personalized care, through specialized psychological interventions, helped alleviate mental stress, build confidence in recovery, and promote comprehensive physical and mental rehabilitation.[25] These findings indicate that personalized care is particularly well-suited for patients with severe conditions and should be actively promoted in the management of SAP to improve recovery outcomes and enhance quality of life for this high-risk population.

Additionally, the effectiveness of personalized care in reducing recurrence and readmission rates was also confirmed. These outcomes are often closely associated with post-discharge factors such as lifestyle habits, medication adherence, and complication management. Through structured follow-up and targeted health education, personalized care supported patients in maintaining their health after discharge and enabled early identification and management of potential risk factors. Compared to standard care, personalized care demonstrated greater advantages in promoting long-term health, thereby lowering the risk of recurrence and readmission.

Our results demonstrate that personalized nursing care significantly enhances recovery rates in SAP patients, effectively reduces recurrence and readmission rates, and yields particularly favorable outcomes in those requiring mechanical ventilation. These findings can be extended to a broader population. In different healthcare systems, although it may not be possible to implement all the personalized nursing measures exactly as we did, the adoption of key interventions and the enhancement of nursing staff’s professional skills can still achieve similar effects as in our study. In mild SAP patients, personalized nursing can focus more on early intervention, health education, and disease prevention. In moderate-to-severe patients who require mechanical ventilation, personalized nursing would involve more meticulous monitoring and treatment adjustments. This demonstrates the broad potential for applying personalized nursing care to SAP patients with varying degrees of disease severity.

In our study, we emphasized the impact of personalized nursing care on the recovery of SAP patients. Similarly, international guidelines emphasize the importance of individualized management in SAP treatment. Personalized nursing care encompasses more precise airway management, vigilant infection monitoring, and stringent sterile procedures, all of which align closely with the guidelines’ focus on early detection of infection risks and the implementation of optimized nursing interventions. This study also enhances nursing education by reminding healthcare professionals to pay greater attention to patients’ individualized needs, ensuring that the care for SAP patients is more meticulous and precise.

Personalized nursing care is not only applicable to SAP but is also widely used in the treatment and rehabilitation of other diseases. For instance, the multidimensional intervention strategy inherent in personalized nursing has been demonstrated to significantly facilitate the recovery of musculoskeletal symptoms and improve overall quality of life in patients suffering from long COVID.[26,27] Additionally, studies have indicated that environmental factors can affect chronic pain,[28] suggesting that personalized nursing should take into account the patient’s long-term recovery environment.

The actual sample size included in this study meets the minimum requirements for various analyses, ensuring sufficient statistical power to reduce the likelihood of Type I and Type II errors, thereby enhancing the reliability and robustness of the conclusions. In addition, a reasonable sample size contributes to the stability of model parameter estimates and the reproducibility of results, providing a solid foundation for the scientific validity of our findings. This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective study, although PSM was used to reduce baseline differences, selection bias in data collection may not be entirely eliminated, and randomized controlled trials remain the gold standard for assessing causal relationships in nursing interventions.[29] Second, other potential confounding factors, such as patients’ social support and psychological status, were not included in the analysis, which may lead to incomplete results. Future studies could incorporate more factors to further refine the evaluation of the effectiveness of personalized nursing interventions. The hospital in this study is an urban hospital, equipped with more advanced medical equipment and technology. Additionally, urban hospitals typically receive patients with more severe or complex conditions. In contrast, hospitals in rural and less-developed areas may have relatively limited resources, and patients in these settings may not receive optimal treatment due to limitations in medical facilities, which may lead to conclusions that differ from our study. Therefore, the generalizability of the findings is somewhat limited. This study is retrospective in nature, and the data collection and assessment were not blinded. Assessors may have been aware of patient group assignments, which introduces a potential risk of detection bias, especially in the evaluation of subjective measures. This limitation may affect the objectivity of the results and should be considered cautiously when interpreting the findings.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated the significant therapeutic efficacy of personalized care in SAP patients. Personalized care provided notable recovery benefits, particularly for patients with severe conditions, and effectively reduced recurrence and readmission rates. In clinical practice, personalized care should be an integral part of SAP recovery management and should be further promoted. However, to better understand its underlying mechanisms and long-term effects, more prospective randomized controlled studies are needed.

5. Conclusion

This study provides preliminary retrospective evidence suggesting that personalized care may be associated with improved recovery outcomes in patients with SAP, particularly among those requiring mechanical ventilation. Additionally, it may help reduce recurrence and readmission rates. Further prospective studies are needed to validate these findings.
