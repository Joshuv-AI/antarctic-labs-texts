Real-time risk prediction model for sepsis in patients with acute gastrointestinal bleeding: development and multicenter validation of a dynamic monitoring tool
Authors: Guijun Jiang, Shengnan Sun, Qingyuan Wang, Zhou Liu, Chencui Huang, Fei Quan, Xiaoshu Zuo, Tianfang Peng, Jingxu Xu, Hongyi Duan, Hector Barajas-Martínez, Di Zhang, Dan Hu, Liying Zhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundPatients with acute gastrointestinal bleeding (AGIB) carries a significant risk of sepsis, particularly in intensive care units. We aimed to develop a validated predictive model for sepsis risk stratification to guide clinical management.MethodsThis multicenter study (Jan, 2020–Jul, 2024) in China included 1449 patients with AGIB. Participants were enrolled into a retrospective training (n = 878) cohort, and prospective internal validation (n = 187; prospectively enrolled from lead center) and external validation (n = 384; from three independent tertiary hospitals) cohorts. We excluded patients with hospitalization <24 h or pre-existing infection or sepsis. A multivariable logistic regression-based nomogram to predict risk of sepsis (defined per Sepsis-3 criteria) was developed and evaluated using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). Predictive performance was compared against established scoring systems–Glasgow Blatchford Score (GBS), Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation Ⅱ (APACHE Ⅱ), and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA)–using discrimination metrics to differentiate high- and low-risk patients. A real-time clinical warning system was implemented.FindingsAmong 1449 patients (68.7% male; median age 65 [IQR 54–73.5]), 223 (15.4%) developed sepsis, linked to higher mortality (23.7% vs. 6.8%, p < 0.001). Key predictors included chronic kidney disease (CKD), elevated respiratory rate (RR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), C-reactive protein (CRP), creatinine (Cr), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and fibrinogen (FIB). This model outperformed some traditional scores, with AUCs of 0.827 (95% CI: 0.759–0.888), 0.836 (95% CI: 0.776–0.896) and 0.884 (95% CI: 0.816–0.952) in training, internal, and external validation sets, respectively. Calibration curves and DCA confirmed accuracy (showed excellent agreement between actual sepsis occurrences and nomogram-estimated probabilities, with mean square error [MSE] of 0.00094, 0.00791, and 0.00045 for the training, internal validation, and external validation sets, respectively) and clinical utility. SHAP analysis highlighted key predictors. An online platform enabled real-time risk monitoring.InterpretationThis validated model effectively identifies patients with AGIB at high risk for sepsis, addressing a critical unmet need in emergency care. Its integration with dynamic warning systems will optimize risk stratification, facilitate preemptive management, and improve clinical outcomes.FundingThe National Key R&D Program of China, the National Natural Science Foundation Project of China, the Knowledge Innovation Program of Wuhan, and the Cross Innovation Talent Project of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University.Keywords: Acute gastrointestinal bleeding (AGIB), Sepsis, Nomogram, Predictive model, Shapley additive explanations (SHAP)

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We systematically searched PubMed and Web of Science from inception to December 2024 using terms ("acute gastrointestinal bleeding" OR "AGIB") AND ("sepsis prediction" OR "sepsis risk"), identifying critical evidence gaps through English-language literature. Although extensive research exists on sepsis-induced acute gastrointestinal bleeding (AGIB), studies addressing the reverse causality—specifically AGIB-triggered sepsis—remain limited. More notably, no studies have developed and externally validated prediction tools for this complication, and multicenter analyses of sepsis risk factors in AGIB populations are scarce. Existing evidence confirms that patients with AGIB experience elevated sepsis incidence and mortality, yet conventional risk scores (e.g., Glasgow Blatchford Score [GBS], Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation Ⅱ [APACHE Ⅱ], and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment [SOFA]) lack specificity for sepsis stratification in this cohort.

Added value of this study

This is the first multicenter study to develop and validate a sepsis-specific prediction model for patients with AGIB. We identified 7 key predictors—chronic kidney disease (CKD), respiratory rate (RR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), C-reactive protein (CRP), creatinine (Cr), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and fibrinogen (FIB)—and integrated them into a nomogram model. Crucially, we engineered this into a deployable online platform providing real-time risk alerts, achieving area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.884 in external validation. Our model significantly outperformed conventional scores (GBS, APACHE II, SOFA) and is the first tool to enable early risk stratification for AGIB-related sepsis and optimize clinical outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Implications of all the available evidence

This model offers clinicians an evidence-based tool for early risk stratification and personalized antibiotic stewardship in patients with AGIB. High-risk patients should receive preemptive broad-spectrum antibiotics, while low-risk patients can avoid unnecessary anti-infection treatments. Implementing this predictive model could help reduce sepsis-related morbidity and mortality, particularly in intensive care units (ICUs) and emergency departments. Further studies are needed to validate its impact on clinical outcomes and antimicrobial resistance rates.

Introduction

Acute gastrointestinal bleeding (AGIB) represents a critical medical emergency, with an annual incidence of 100–200 per 100,000 for acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding (AUGIB) and 20–35 per 100,000 for acute lower gastrointestinal bleeding (ALGIB).1,2 As a life-threatening emergency, AGIB carries substantial morbidity and mortality burdens in clinical practice, frequently necessitating hospitalizations particularly in comorbid patients.1,2 Concurrently, sepsis remains a global health burden, affecting approximately 30 million individuals annually and contributing to 6 million deaths.3,4 In addition, emerging evidence highlights a bidirectional relationship between AGIB and sepsis.5,6 Septic shock patients exhibit a 5.4% incidence of AGIB with poor outcomes.7 Conversely, patients with AGIB are also highly susceptible to severe infections, and AGIB itself is considered an independent risk factor for bloodstream infections.8

In clinical practice, patients with AGIB frequently develop severe infections, with sepsis posing significant therapeutic challenges and escalating healthcare costs. At our tertiary referral center specializing in severe gastrointestinal bleeding, we have consistently observed this high-risk association, underscoring the need for improved risk stratification tools.

Current clinical management lacks validated instruments for sepsis risk prediction in patients with AGIB, often resulting in empirical antibiotic usage without evidence-based guidance. While scoring systems such as the Glasgow Blatchford Score (GBS)9 and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation Ⅱ (APACHE II)10 assess bleeding risk and disease severity, respectively, they fail to predict infection-related complications. Similarly, the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, though useful for diagnosing sepsis severity, offers limited predictive value for sepsis risk in AGIB populations.

To address this critical gap, we conducted a multicenter cohort study to develop and prospectively validate a predictive model for sepsis risk in patients with AGIB. By integrating multiple predictors through scientific statistical modeling, this tool aims to facilitate early risk stratification for AGIB-related sepsis and optimize clinical outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This multicenter cohort study involved four tertiary hospitals in China. Due to the retrospective nature of the cohort and the anonymization of data, individual informed consent was waived in training cohort. Nevertheless, informed consent was obtained from all participants in prospective validation cohorts. The protocol for the retrospective training cohort was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University (IRB No. WDRY2024-K103), with prospective validation cohorts authorized by Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (KYLL-2024032), First Affiliated Hospital of Xi'an Jiaotong University (XJTU1AF2024LSYY-464), and Xiangyang No.1 People's Hospital (XYYYE20240144). This study was reported according to the Transparent Reporting of a multivariate predictive model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines.

Cases of training set were from the lead center (Wuhan; Jan, 2020–Sep, 2023), internal validation set from the same center (Wuhan; Jan–Jul, 2024), and external validation set from three collaborating centers (Jan–Jul, 2024). Baseline variables, such as demographics, comorbidities, previous surgeries, pre-study medication history, vital signs, symptoms, and severity scores, along with laboratory parameters within 24 h of admission, including complete blood count, biochemistry, coagulation, biomarkers, and microbiology, were systematically recorded.

Eligible patients met the following criteria: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) presenting with hematemesis, melena, coffee-ground vomitus, or signs of hemodynamic instability (e.g., syncope, shock); (3) confirmed AGIB diagnosis via clinical evaluation or endoscopy. Exclusion criteria included: (1) hospitalization <24 h; (2) incomplete medical records; (3) pre-existing infection or sepsis at admission.

Clinical management protocol

All patients received standardized management including: (1) Nil Per Os (NPO) and oxygen therapy; (2) continuous ECG monitoring; (3) proton pump inhibitors for acid suppression; (4) hemodynamic stabilization with crystalloids and colloids; (5) blood product transfusion thresholds: Hb < 70 g/L, Hct < 22%, PLT < 30 × 109/L. Endoscopic hemostasis was prioritized within 24 h. Refractory bleeding cases received transcatheter arterial embolization or surgery per guidelines.11, 12, 13, 14

Outcome measures

Primary endpoint: AGIB-induced sepsis (defined by: (1) all patients presented with AGIB as their primary admission diagnosis; (2) sepsis diagnosis strictly adhered to Sepsis-3 criteria released by the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine and the Society of Critical Care Medicine3; (3) sepsis occurred more than 24 h after patients with AGIB were admitted, with pre-existing sepsis excluded via systematic screening (including clinical symptom assessment, microbial culture, imaging examinations, biomarker testing, and SOFA scoring). Secondary endpoints: in-hospital mortality, hospitalization days of intensive care unit (ICU), and total hospitalization days.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was derived using the events-per-variable (EPV) criterion, requiring a minimum of 50–100 sepsis events for the anticipated 5–10 predictor variables. Based on retrospective institutional data showing about 15% sepsis incidence in patients with AGIB, this translated to 333–667 patients. Accounting for 20% potential data loss, the target of training cohort was adjusted to 400–800 patients. Thus, the prospective validation cohorts aimed to include at least 267–533 patients for prospective validation based on the 6:4 ratio. Ultimately, 1449 patients with AGIB were enrolled (878 in the training cohort, 571 in the prospective validation cohorts), which exceeded the calculated requirement.

Model development and validation

The predictive model was developed using data from the training set. First, clinical characteristics were compared between the sepsis and non-sepsis groups to identify variables significantly associated with sepsis risk. Variables showing a significant association with sepsis (p < 0.05) in univariate logistic regression and a variance inflation factor (VIF) less than 10 were subjected to multivariable analysis using backward stepwise selection (retention threshold: p < 0.05). Final predictors from multivariable logistic regression were incorporated into a nomogram, with regression coefficients converted to weighted points for visual risk quantification.

The predictive performance of the nomogram was systematically evaluated against established scoring tools–GBS, APACHE II, and SOFA–using discrimination metrics to differentiate high- and low-risk sepsis patients. Validation was conducted through a two-stage process: (1) internal validation: 187 prospectively enrolled patients from the lead center; (2) external validation: 384 patients from three independent tertiary hospitals. To rigorously evaluate the model's generalizability across clinically distinct populations, we also conducted subgroup analyses for four key cohorts: AUGIB, ALGIB, esophagogastric variceal bleeding (EGVB), and acute non-variceal gastrointestinal bleeding (ANVGIB). Subgroups were defined using standardized diagnostic criteria (e.g., AUGIB: bleeding proximal to ligament of Treitz; ALGIB: distal bleeding) and stratified within training (n = 878), internal validation (n = 187), and external validation (n = 384) cohorts. Performance was quantified via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis using R (Version: 4.1.1) with the “pROC” (Version: 1.18.0) package, reporting the area under the ROC curve (AUC) and 95% confidence interval (CI), sensitivity (SEN), specificity (SPE), accuracy (ACC), negative predictive value (NPV), and positive predictive value (PPV). All results were visualized through stratified ROC curves and detailed performance parameters were presented to enable direct comparison of discriminative ability across etiologies and datasets.

To enhance the interpretability of the model, the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method was utilized to assess the importance of each selected variable using Python (Version: 3.8) and SHAP (Version: 0.44.1), including SHAP summary plots that illustrated each variable's importance and SHAP dependence plots that explored interactions between variables. These plots help demonstrate how each variable's level and SHAP value interact with other predictors, reflecting their joint impact on the model's predictive ability.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 and R version 4.1.1. Missing data were addressed through a two-stage protocol: (1) cases with missing ≥1 critical predictors, such as lactate, ammonia, N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-ProBNP), cardiac troponin I (cTnI), or C-reactive protein (CRP), were excluded to establish a final training set with 0% missingness; (2) Little's MCAR test was used to assess whether the missing data were missing completely at random (MCAR). For non-modeled variables with less than 5% missingness that met the MCAR criteria, median imputation was applied in both the internal and external validation sets. Otherwise, multiple imputation was utilized. Differences in categorical variables between the training and validation sets were assessed using the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test. For continuous variables, normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of Q–Q plots, variables with a p-value >0.05 in the Shapiro–Wilk test and symmetric Q–Q plots were considered normally distributed, justifying the use of Student's t-test. Otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Variables demonstrating statistical significance in univariate analyses (two-tailed p < 0.05) and low VIF (<10) were entered into multivariable logistic regression to identify independent risk factors for AGIB-induced sepsis, with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs calculated as summary statistics.

To visualize the aggregated distribution patterns of the independent risk factors across sepsis and non-sepsis cohorts, we performed unsupervised hierarchical clustering using Euclidean distance and Ward's linkage method. Continuous variables were Z-score normalized to ensure equal scaling. The resulting heatmaps were generated with the “ComplexHeatmap” package (Version: 2.13.1) in R, with dendrograms illustrating cluster structures and color gradients reflecting normalized risk factor intensities (red: high, blue: low). Additionally, nonlinear relationships between continuous predictors and sepsis risk were evaluated using restricted cubic splines (RCS) with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of each variable's distribution. The RCS models were fitted via the packages of “rms” (Version: 6.4.0), “ggplot2” (Version: 3.4.4), and “plotRCS” (Version: 0.1.3) in R, with 3 degrees of freedom to balance flexibility and overfitting avoidance. Statistical significance of nonlinearity was assessed using likelihood ratio tests comparing linear vs. spline models (p < 0.05 indicating significant deviation from linearity). The resultant curves visualize adjusted ORs with 95% CIs relative to reference values (median levels).

For model validation, the AUC was used to assess the discriminative ability of the nomogram, while calibration curves and mean square error (MSE) evaluated its accuracy. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of the nomogram against traditional scoring tools (SOFA, APACHE II, GBS) across a range of risk thresholds (0.0–1.0). For each cohort, we calculated the net benefit (NB) using the formula: NB = TP/n–FP/n ∗ (Pt/(1–Pt)), where TP and FP represent true positives and false positives at each threshold, n is the total number of patients and Pt is the threshold probability. Analyses were implemented in R with the packages of “rmda” (Version: 1.6) and “ggplot2”. The performance of the nomogram was further validated externally on a prospective multicenter cohort of patients with AGIB. The sensitivity analysis comprised two key comparisons: (1) the total study cohort versus the cohort excluding the internal validation set; (2) the imputed-case datasets versus the complete-case datasets within the validation cohorts. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

Baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes

Of 1750 initially eligible patients with AGIB (Jan, 2020–Jul, 2024), 301 were excluded: 182 with missing data, 65 with short stays, 29 with pre-existing sepsis, and 25 declining participation (Fig. 1). The final cohort comprised 1449 patients stratified into: (1) training set: 878 patients from the lead center (Jan, 2020–Sep, 2023); (2) internal validation set: 187 patients from same center (Jan–Jul, 2024); (3) external validation set: 384 patients from three collaborating centers (Jan–Jul, 2024). The baseline characteristics and outcomes of the 1449 eligible patients with AGIB are shown in Table 1, and the characteristics were well balanced between the training set and validation sets. After comprehensive management of missing data, final training set (n = 878) achieved 0% missingness for all features, and non-modeled variables in validation sets were imputed by median because of the low missingness (<5%) with MCAR mechanism (Little's MCAR test: χ2(10) = 14.23, p = 0.12 in pre-training set; χ2(8) = 6.83, p = 0.33 in internal set; and χ2(6) = 9.17, p = 0.16 in external set, Tables S1–S2). Among 1449 enrolled patients (median age 65 years, IQR 54–73.5; 68.7% male), 223 (15.4%) developed sepsis during hospitalization. In the patients with AGIB-induced sepsis, pathogen detection and positive rates varied across cohorts (Figure S1A): total cohort (77.1% detected, 58.7% positive), training set (81.7%, 57.0%), internal (75.5%, 70.0%), and external (64.1%, 48.0%). Figure S1B illustrates the top 3 pathogens by positive rates across diverse clinical samples. Enterobacteriaceae emerges as the dominant pathogen in most samples, achieving the highest rates in sputum (52.7%), blood (42.1%), urine (42.9%), and bile (50.0%), and ranking highly in others. Candida follows closely, with notable prevalence in blood (42.9%), urine (33.3%), feces (100%), ascites (30.0%), and sputum (25.5%). Enterococcaceae, Pseudomonadaceae, and Staphylococcaceae also show significant but more sample-specific prominence (e.g., Enterococcaceae at 40.0% in ascites and 33.3% in both blood and urine). Overall, Enterobacteriaceae had the highest positive rate (54.5%), followed by Candida (30.7%) and Enterococcaceae (20.8%), with others decreasing gradually (Figure S1C and Table S3). Compared with non-sepsis group, the in-hospital mortality was significantly higher (23.7% vs. 6.8%, p < 0.05), values of risk scores such as the GBS, APACHEⅡ, and SOFA were higher, and the ICU hospitalization days (5 vs. 4, p < 0.05) as well as total hospitalization days (10 vs. 8, p < 0.05) were prolonged in the sepsis group (Table S4).

Independent risk factors associated with AGIB-induced sepsis

In the training set, clinical characteristics were compared between the sepsis and non-sepsis groups (Table S4). Variables with statistically significant differences were included in univariate logistic regression analysis, followed by multivariate logistic regression to identify independent risk factors for AGIB-induced sepsis. Univariate analysis revealed significant associations with sepsis risk for a range of factors (p < 0.05, Table S5), including a history of anemia, hypertension, chronic kidney disease (CKD), cardiac dysfunction (CD), rapid heart rate (HR) and respiratory rate (RR), EGVB, endoscopic and vasoactive agents treatments, elevated neutrophil percentage (Neut%), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), CRP, alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cr), lactate (Lac), NT-ProBNP, prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), international normalized ratio (INR), fibrinogen (FIB), and D-dimer, as well as decreased albumin (ALB).

In multivariate analysis, a history of CKD (OR = 1.797, 95% CI: 1.068–3.025), elevated RR (OR = 1.078, 95% CI: 1.020–1.139), increased NLR (OR = 1.042, 95% CI: 1.014–1.071), CRP (OR = 1.010, 95% CI: 1.005–1.014), Cr (OR = 1.002, 95% CI: 1.000–1.004), APTT (OR = 1.051, 95% CI: 1.019–1.084), and FIB (OR = 1.513, 95% CI: 1.238–1.850) were identified as independent risk factors for AGIB-induced sepsis (Table S5).

Variables verification and model development

Cluster heatmaps were employed to illustrate the distribution patterns of seven independent risk factors—CKD, RR, NLR, CRP, Cr, APTT, and FIB—across sepsis and non-sepsis groups (Figure S2). These visualizations revealed denser clustering of risk factor values in the sepsis cohort. RCS with knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles were applied to evaluate variables-sepsis associations (Figure S3). Results showed that the RR, NLR, CRP, APTT, and FIB levels were linearly correlated with AGIB-induced sepsis (p for overall <0.05 and p for non-linear >0.05), while the Cr level was non-linearly correlated with that (p for non-linear <0.05). Cr exhibited a threshold effect, with sepsis risk remaining stable below 80 μmol/L, escalating sharply between 80 and 380 μmol/L, and plateauing thereafter. Overall, all variables were positively correlated with sepsis risk.

The multivariable logistic regression model was translated into a clinically accessible nomogram (Fig. 2), where each predictor was assigned weighted points based on its regression coefficient. Total points corresponded to individualized sepsis probabilities. Comparative analyses demonstrated superior predictive performance of the nomogram model versus individual predictors (Figure S4).

An online warning system was developed to facilitate real-time risk stratification (https://keyan.deepwise.com/predict/?taskId=423398&taskName=sepsis&language=en). This tool enables dynamic monitoring and supports evidence-based clinical decision-making for patients with AGIB (Figure S5). In addition, it supports running both on mobile and computer terminals.

Internal and external validation of the predictive model

The nomogram model demonstrated robust discriminative performance across all datasets, with AUC values of 0.827 (95% CI: 0.759–0.888) in the training set, 0.836 (95% CI: 0.776–0.896) in the prospective internal validation set, and 0.884 (95% CI: 0.816–0.952) in the prospective external validation set (Fig. 3A–C and Table S6). And sensitivity analyses confirmed robust performance of imputed datasets in the internal set (AUC = 0.920, 95% CI: 0.795–1.000) and the external set (AUC = 0.930, 95% CI: 0.838–1.000; Table S7 and Figure S6). Moreover, the performance of this model significantly outperformed conventional scoring tools: the SOFA score showed an AUC of 0.789 (95% CI: 0.703–0.875), the APACHE Ⅱ yielded an AUC of 0.715 (95% CI: 0.627–0.802), and the GBS displayed the lowest predictive accuracy with an AUC of 0.656 (95% CI: 0.570–0.742) in the external validation set (p < 0.05, Table S8). The nomogram demonstrated consistently strong predictive performance across three geographically distinct external hospitals (The First Affiliated Hospital of Xi'an Jiaotong University: AUC = 0.945, n = 179; Xiangyang No.1 People's Hospital: AUC = 0.940, n = 107; Qilu Hospital of Shandong University: AUC = 0.803, n = 98), outperforming traditional tools (GBS: 0.681–0.733, SOFA: 0.729–0.892, APACHE II: 0.692–0.744). And site-specific strengths were observed: Xi'an exhibited the highest positive predictive value (PPV = 0.987) for high-risk sepsis identification, Xiangyang showed superior specificity (SPE = 0.900) to exclude non-sepsis cases, and Qilu prioritized sensitivity (SEN = 0.953) to minimize missed diagnoses, collectively validating the nomogram's robust generalizability and clinical utility (Table S9 and Figures S7).

Additionally, the nomogram demonstrated consistently high predictive accuracy across all subgroups (Table S10). EGVB achieved exceptional discrimination externally (AUC = 0.884, 95% CI: 0.816–0.952), with PPV = 0.982 underscoring its utility for ruling in high-risk sepsis cases. ANVGIB maintained acceptable discrimination externally (AUC = 0.795), with SEN = 0.799 and SPE = 0.958. Visual analysis of ROC curves (Figures S8-9) further validated these findings. Critically, PPVs consistently exceeded 0.930 across most subgroups and cohorts, confirming the model's reliability for clinical risk stratification in diverse AGIB presentations.

Finally, the calibration curves also showed excellent agreement between the observed and predicted sepsis rates (Fig. 3D–F), with MSEs of 0.00094, 0.00791, and 0.00045 in the training set, prospective internal validation set, and prospective external validation set, respectively. As shown in Fig. 3G–I, the DCA demonstrated that the nomogram model provided a higher net benefit compared to the SOFA, APACHE II, and GBS scores across a wide range of threshold probabilities (approximately 10%–70% in the training and internal validation set, and 10%–90% in the external validation set). Within this range, the nomogram model also outperformed both the 'treat all' and 'treat none' strategies, indicating its superior clinical utility for guiding decision-making.

SHAP dependence plots (Fig. 4B–G) further demonstrate how interactions between variables shape the predictive outcomes of the model. In these plots, positive SHAP values indicate a higher sepsis risk, with higher values signaling an increased risk. Key interactions identified in the model include: APTT and FIB (Fig. 4B), where APTT values exceeding 30 s combined with higher FIB levels correlate with an increased SHAP value, thus raising sepsis risk, while APTT values below 30 s result in negative SHAP values. FIB and APTT (Fig. 4C) demonstrate that FIB levels above 2.5 g/L lead to increased SHAP values with APTT, whereas levels below 2.5 g/L yield negative SHAP values. CRP and CKD (Fig. 4D) show that with CRP levels over 10 mg/L, presence of CKD raises SHAP values, but levels below 10 mg/L result in negative SHAP values. Similarly, for RR and Cr (Fig. 4E), RR values above 18 bpm combined with higher Cr levels result in increased SHAP values, whereas values below 18 bpm show negative SHAP values. Cr and CRP (Fig. 4F) indicate that Cr levels above 80 μmol/L display positive SHAP values that increase with CRP levels, while Cr levels below 80 μmol/L show negative SHAP values. Finally, NLR and CKD (Fig. 4G) reveal that for NLR levels above 10, presence of CKD results in higher SHAP values, signaling an increased sepsis risk, while NLR levels below 10 have negative SHAP values.

In summary, a SHAP value above zero for the main feature suggests a synergistic effect between it and its interacting feature, jointly increasing sepsis risk. Overall, the SHAP values for APTT, FIB, CRP, RR, Cr, and NLR are directly proportional to their levels, underscoring their combined impact on risk prediction. This systematic SHAP analysis not only identifies critical predictors but also reveals complex interaction networks that enhance our understanding of sepsis pathogenesis and inform precision medicine strategies.

Discussion

The intricate bidirectional relationship between AGIB and sepsis presents a complex clinical interplay that demands systematic investigation. While substantial research has elucidated sepsis-induced AGIB mechanisms,5, 6, 7 the reverse causality, AGIB triggering sepsis, remains understudied despite its clinical relevance. In intensive care units, both AGIB and sepsis are associated with high morbidity and mortality. Patients presenting with concomitant AGIB and sepsis face a significantly elevated mortality risk. Our multicenter cohort analysis revealed that 15.4% (223/1449) of patients with AGIB developed sepsis, with 14.9% (131/878) in the training set, 28.3% (53/187) in the internal validation set, and 10.2% (39/384) in the external validation set. The study ultimately included 1449 patients, stratified into three distinct cohorts: a training set comprising 878 cases from Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University (Jan, 2020–Sep, 2023), an internal validation set of 187 cases from the same institution (Jan–Jul, 2024), and an external validation set consisting of 384 cases from The First Affiliated Hospital of Xi'an Jiaotong University, Qilu Hospital of Shandong University, and Xiangyang No.1 People's Hospital during the same period in 2024. While all participating centers are university-affiliated hospitals, their geographical distribution across China introduces potential heterogeneity in disease severity. The hospitals represent diverse regions: Qingdao in Shandong Province (East China), Xi'an in Shaanxi Province (Northwest China), as well as Wuhan and Xiangyang in Hubei Province (Central China), collectively reflecting patients' characteristics across most of China and validating the broad applicability of our prediction model. Notably, disease severity varied significantly among centers, with patients from Wuhan center demonstrating more critical conditions and higher risk of sepsis compared to external hospitals.

Our analysis revealed that the elevated incidence of sepsis in the internal validation set (28.3% in 2024) versus the training set (14.0%–19.2% in 2020–2023) and external hospitals (9.3%–12.2% in 2024) primarily stems from both greater severity of baseline patients and center-specific factors. This conclusion is supported by multiple observations: firstly, Wuhan center patients showed higher APACHE Ⅱ scores (median 15, upper quartile 21) compared to historical data (median 13–15, IQRs typically 12–16) and external validation centers (median 13–14, IQRs 10–16); secondly, the mortality rate in the internal validation set (15.5%) substantially exceeded the rates observed in both the training set (8.0%–9.5%) and external cohort (8.3%–12.1%); finally, Renmin Hospital serves as a crucial provincial referral center for severe patients with AGIB in Hubei, with consistently increasing admissions of critical patients, further underscores the observed regional and institutional variations in patients (Table S11). The results of additional sensitivity analyses (AUC of 0.853, 95% CI: 0.825–0.880 before exclusion and AUC of 0.849, 95% CI: 0.816–0.881 after exclusion) also confirmed the robustness of the model after excluding the internal validation cohort (Figure S10 and Table S12). In addition, patients with AGIB-induced sepsis were the population exhibiting significantly poorer outcomes: 3.5-fold higher in-hospital mortality (23.7% vs. 6.8%, p < 0.001), prolonged ICU stays (median 5 vs. 4 days, p = 0.012), and extended total hospitalization (median 10 vs. 8 days, p = 0.008) compared to non-septic counterparts. These findings underscore the critical need to reevaluate the management of AGIB through a sepsis-prevention perspective.

The pathophysiology underlying AGIB-associated sepsis likely involves a triad of mechanisms: (1) stress responses, anemia, enteral nutrition deficiency, and immune dysregulation can lead to enterocyte dysfunction, gut barrier disruption, and bacterial translocation, ultimately causing systemic inflammation and sepsis15, 16, 17, 18; (2) AGIB-induced ischemic insults may disrupt intestinal microcirculation and endothelial integrity, predisposing to gut-derived infections due to impaired perfusion autoregulation in the small intestine19; (3) the high prevalence of liver cirrhosis in patients with AGIB establishes a gut-liver pathogenic cycle: intestinal barrier dysfunction induced by portal hypertension facilitates bacterial translocation, which exacerbates hepatic inflammation and perpetuates circulatory compromise through the gut-liver axis.20,21 Additionally, the gut-lung axis is considered an important mechanism for the transport of gut microbiota and mediators to the lungs, leading to or exacerbating pulmonary infections.22 In summary, although the exact pathogenesis of AGIB-induced sepsis is not fully understood, we are exploring the link between intestinal microecological disruption and sepsis susceptibility in patients with AGIB to better explain the possible pathogenesis. Further clinical and mechanistic studies are urgently required to clarify this connection.

To our knowledge, this represents the first predictive model for sepsis designed for AGIB populations, addressing a critical gap in risk stratification for this high-mortality cohort. Differences in etiology, underlying conditions, comorbidities, and inflammation levels necessitate tailored treatment approaches for patients with AGIB. Currently, there is considerable debate over the early use of antibiotics in AGIB. It is unclear which patients with AGIB do not require antibiotics and which require higher-dosage antibiotics, often resulting in over-treatment with increased risk of multidrug-resistant bacterial infections or under-treatment leading to severe sepsis. The nomogram model and early warning system developed in this study aim to address this issue, helping reduce the incidence of sepsis in high-risk patients while minimizing the rate of drug-resistant bacterial infections in low-risk patients.

The resulting nomogram demonstrated more excellent discrimination than that of the GBS, APACHE Ⅱ and SOFA, with AUCs of 0.827 (95% CI: 0.789–0.865) for the training set, 0.836 (95% CI: 0.776–0.896) for the internal validation set, and 0.884 (95% CI: 0.816–0.952) for the external validation set. While the EPV of 18.7 in the training set exceeds the typical 10 EPV threshold, it falls to 7.6 in the internal validation set and 5.6 in the external validation set. However, machine learning models have been demonstrated reliable through regularization, even with EPVs < 10.23 In addition, the nomogram model is primarily based on data of training set, adhering to the EPV > 10 principle, while the validation sets focus more on the model's generalization and practicality. Certainly, further extensive study is necessary to confirm this in the future. The calibration curves also showed excellent agreement between actual sepsis occurrences and nomogram-estimated probabilities, with MSE of 0.00094, 0.00791, and 0.00045 for the training, internal validation, and external validation sets, respectively. The DCA further demonstrated that the nomogram model may provide better clinical benefit than the GBS, APACHE Ⅱ and SOFA across a wide range of threshold probabilities. Our model demonstrated robust clinical applicability across diverse populations, maintaining high discrimination despite significant heterogeneity in sepsis incidence (9.3%–28.3%) and disease severity (median APACHE Ⅱ score ranged 13–15). As shown in Table S9 and Figure S7, nomogram model achieved excellent AUC values at external hospitals (Xiangyang 0.940, Xi'an 0.945, Qingdao 0.803). In addition, as demonstrated in Table S10, the model maintains good discrimination for common presentations (AUGIB: external AUC 0.874), high-risk subgroups (EGVB: external AUC 0.884), and challenging cohorts (ALGIB: external AUC 0.964).

The warning platform integrated into the Electronic Health Record (EHR) system enables clinicians to assess sepsis risk immediately upon admission by entering patients' features. This facilitates the formulation of appropriate anti-infective strategies and allows for dynamic risk assessment and timely treatment adjustments during subsequent management. For example, the predictive model may facilitate risk-adaptive clinical decision-making as follows: for low-risk patients, prioritize primary disease management without immediate antibiotics, complemented by ongoing risk reassessment via the nomogram or online system; moderate-risk cases may warrant prophylactic, narrow-spectrum antibiotics after evaluation, guided by prevalent pathogens (e.g., Enterobacteriaceae, the most frequently detected across samples); for high-risk scenarios, a broader or combined regimen of antibiotics should be tailored to the infection site and pathogen distribution. Emphasis should be placed on coverage for Enterobacteriaceae, Candida, and Enterococcaceae (notably dominant in infection of bloodstream, lung, urinary system, or intra-abdominal) for that they have been demonstrated to induce systemic infection following microbiota dysbiosis and impairment of the intestinal barrier.24, 25, 26 However, specific treatment should balance local antimicrobial resistance, patients' comorbidities, and treatment availability. This integration of risk stratification with pathogen-specific data enhances targeted intervention, aiming to improve outcomes and promote antimicrobial stewardship. In addition, the online platform can be seamlessly integrated with hospital EHR systems (e.g., Epic/Cerner) through HL7/FHIR standard interfaces, enabling real-time retrieval of patient data and calculation of sepsis risk. In order to reduce alarm fatigue, we can implement the following measures: (1) three-tiered alert hierarchy: >90% risk (pop-up + sound), 70–90% (taskbar blinking), <70% (silent flag); (2) intelligent suppression: user-configurable snooze (a maximum of 2 alerts per hour) with role-based filtering (e.g., residents receive only alerts >80%); (3) mobile optimization: Progressive Web Apps (PWA) support offline operation via scanning quick response (QR) code at the bedside.

Our study identified CKD, elevated RR, and increased levels of NLR, CRP, Cr, APTT, and FIB as independent risk factors for AGIB-induced sepsis. Furthermore, SHAP summary and dependence plots indicated that the levels of APTT, FIB, CRP, RR, Cr, and NLR are proportional to their contribution values in predicting the risk of sepsis. Notably, interactions between features (e.g., APTT and FIB, CRP and CKD, RR and Cr, Cr and CRP, NLR and CKD) revealed a synergistic effect on sepsis risk. For example, when APTT values exceeded 30 s, high FIB levels significantly amplified the contribution of APTT to sepsis prediction. FIB, a key inflammatory biomarker in the coagulation cascade, plays a crucial role in sepsis pathogenesis.27 AGIB-induced coagulopathy and sepsis-associated inflammatory pathways jointly exacerbate microvascular thrombosis and organ dysfunction.28,29 Thus, prolonged APTT is considered an important prognostic factor for sepsis and has been validated as a risk predictor for sepsis.30 Therefore, the synergy between FIB and APTT underscores their combined role in coagulation dysfunction during sepsis development.

In addition, this study showed that the presence of CKD elevated SHAP values when CRP levels over 10 mg/L, and Cr levels above 80 μmol/L yielded positive SHAP values that increased with CRP levels. This aligns with established mechanisms that CKD increases sepsis susceptibility through gut permeability disruption, endotoxemia, and innate immune dysfunction.31 Patients with CKD face higher incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI), compounding poor outcomes associated with sepsis.32 And elevated CRP are related with decreased citrulline, indicating a failure of gut barrier and driving nosocomial infections and increased mortality in critically ill patients.33,34 Moreover, the connection between gut microbiota, CRP, and sepsis has been confirmed, highlighting the potential role of CRP as a mediator in facilitating the impact of gut microbiota on sepsis.35 These findings support the synergistic interplay between CKD and CRP, as well as Cr and CRP. Further evidence shows that RR is the most significant component of quick sequential organ failure assessment (qSOFA), indicating the risk of hypoxia-mediated sepsis.36 NLR, CRP, and FIB effectively reflect inflammation levels in patients with AGIB, consistent with growing evidence that inflammation and immune responses are central to the development and progression of sepsis.37 Among them, NLR levels were considered to be a good indicator for predicting bloodstream infection and sepsis. CRP and NLR were also confirmed to be effective predictors of bacteremia.38 Furthermore, AGIB-induced organ ischemia, hypoperfusion, and subsequent hemorrhagic shock can lead to intestinal microcirculation disorders, damaging the gut barrier and increasing sepsis risk.19 These findings also support the synergistic relationship between RR and Cr, as well as NLR and CKD, reinforcing the importance of considering these interdependencies in clinical risk assessments. In summary, integrating these variables into our nomogram enables dynamic assessment for sepsis risk, facilitating more personalized treatment.

While external validation across multiple centers enhances generalizability, the study focused on variables relevant to clinical utility of the nomogram (7 predictors). Consequently, median imputation was applied to address minor gaps (less than 5%) in non-modeled baseline data within the validation sets. Although this reflects real-world data limitations and the rationality of median imputation is indirectly confirmed, residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be completely excluded. Further validation in prospectively harmonized cohorts is planned. Additionally, our study exclusively included Han Chinese patients, reflecting the demographic composition of over 90% Chinese. Although racial or ethnic differences were not formally assessed, the identified biological predictors (e.g., NLR, APTT, FIB, CRP) are physiologically universal, suggesting broader applicability. Multinational studies with diverse cohorts are warranted to investigate racial or ethnic effects.

As the first prediction model for AGIB-related sepsis, this tool enables risk-stratified antimicrobial stewardship but lacks correlative analysis between intervention protocols (e.g., antibiotic selection, dosing) and outcomes. Furthermore, our findings demonstrate that early sepsis prevention in patients with AGIB yields significant reductions in both ICU and overall hospital length of stay, with potential benefits for hospitalization costs. However, the current study was not designed to collect detailed cost data required for formal cost-effectiveness analysis. To address these limitations, we have designed our subsequent prospective study to incorporate two critical evaluations in the future: first, the assessment of correlations between risk thresholds and antibiotic regimens, and second, a comprehensive economic analysis. Finally, the pathophysiology linking AGIB to sepsis susceptibility remains multifactorial and underexplored, potentially involving gut microbiota dysbiosis, intestinal barrier compromise, and maladaptive immune activation. Future research will prioritize targeted mechanistic studies to unravel these pathways.

In conclusion, this study specifically developed and validated the first predictive model and online warning system for sepsis risk in patients with AGIB, facilitating real-time risk stratification to prompt timely clinical interventions and personalized strategies. Its implementation holds significant promise for ICU and emergency department, where it prospectively improves antimicrobial stewardship, reducing incidence and mortality of AGIB-related sepsis.
