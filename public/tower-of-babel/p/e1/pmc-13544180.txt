Integrating pharmacovigilance and machine learning: bleeding signal detection and risk prediction for oral anticoagulants in cancer patients
Authors: Meina Lv, Xiaochun Zheng, Hang Xie, Bijuan Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral anticoagulants (OACs) have been widely used for the prevention and treatment of thrombosis in cancer patients, but the bleeding signals associated with different OACs in this population have not been fully studied.ObjectivesThis study aimed to compare bleeding signals, identify risk factors, and establish a prediction model for OACs in cancer patients.MethodsThis study was based on the Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System database and used the reporting odds ratio to evaluate the bleeding event signals associated with OACs in cancer patients from Q1 2014 to Q1 2024. Additionally, this study used a retrospective cohort design to assess the impact of OACs on bleeding risk in cancer patients.ResultsThis study analyzed 29,897 adverse event reports of OACs in cancer patients, which included 4084 bleeding events. We found that bleeding events were mainly concentrated within the first 2 months of medication use. Compared with apixaban, the other 4 OACs (warfarin, edoxaban, rivaroxaban, and dabigatran) showed increased risks of bleeding, especially in the gastrointestinal system. Male, cardiovascular diseases, cerebral infarction, renal insufficiency, gastrointestinal cancer, and elevated international normalized ratio are linked to an increased risk of bleeding from OACs in cancer patients. The XGBoost model was the top-performing model among the 4 bleeding prediction models (training set area under the curve = 0.788; test set area under the curve = 0.783).ConclusionBleeding adverse events related to OACs primarily occur early in the treatment. Apixaban shows weaker bleeding signals. Male sex, cardiovascular diseases, cerebral infarction, renal insufficiency, gastrointestinal cancer, and an elevated international normalized ratio may be potential risk factors for bleeding events. The prediction model for OACs-related bleeding developed in this study demonstrated acceptable performance in internal validation.

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) refers to the abnormal clotting of blood within veins, causing either complete or partial obstruction of venous return, and encompasses deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. It is a relatively common complication in cancer patients [[1], [2], [3]], and studies have shown that VTE is the second leading cause of death among cancer patients [4,5]. Therefore, in clinical practice, effective anticoagulation constitutes an essential component for cancer patients, particularly those at high risk of thrombosis [1]. Furthermore, anticoagulants play a critical role in cancer patients with comorbidities associated with high thrombotic risk, such as atrial fibrillation (AF) and valvular heart disease [6,7].

For patients receiving anticoagulant therapy (eg, those being treated for cancer-associated thrombosis or AF), the risk of bleeding is further increased. Relevant studies have shown that patients with cancer have a 2- to 3-fold higher risk of bleeding events than noncancer patients receiving similar anticoagulant therapy [[8], [9], [10]]. According to a recent study from Japan, among patients receiving VTE treatment, those with active cancer (solid tumors) had cumulative major bleeding (MB) incidences of 6.8% and 11.5% at 90 days and 1 year, respectively, compared with 3.6% and 5.3%, respectively, for those without active cancer [11].

Oral anticoagulants (OACs) include vitamin K antagonists and non-vitamin K antagonists. For many years, warfarin, as a vitamin K antagonist, has been widely used in clinical practice [12,13]. However, in cancer patients, relevant clinical trials have shown that low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) offers advantages over vitamin K antagonists in preventing recurrent VTE or reducing bleeding risks. The CLOT (Comparison of Low-Molecular-Weight Heparin versus Oral Anticoagulant Therapy for the Prevention of Recurrent Venous Thromboembolism in Patients with Cancer) trial demonstrated that in patients with cancer and acute VTE, dalteparin was more effective than an OAC in reducing the risk of recurrent thromboembolism without increasing the risk of bleeding [14]. The CATCH (Comparison of Acute Treatments in Cancer Haemostasis) trial indicated that in patients with active cancer and acute symptomatic VTE, full-dose tinzaparin (175 IU/kg) was associated with a lower incidence of clinically relevant non-MB compared with warfarin [15]. Although LMWH has long been the standard of care in cancer patients, studies have shown that direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), as non-vitamin K antagonist OACs, have demonstrated noninferior or superior efficacy compared with LMWH in cancer patients, with an acceptable safety profile. In 2018, the trial (Comparison of an Oral Factor Xa Inhibitor with Low‑Molecular‑Weight Heparin in Patients with Cancer with Venous Thromboembolism, SELECT‑D) demonstrated that in cancer patients with VTE, rivaroxaban was associated with a relatively lower recurrence of VTE than dalteparin [16]. The Caravaggio trial suggested that oral apixaban was noninferior to subcutaneous dalteparin in treating cancer-associated VTE without increasing the risk of MB [17]. However, the abovementioned findings are all derived from comparisons between OACs and LMWH. Head-to-head studies of OACs in cancer patients regarding their efficacy and safety still warrant further validation, particularly regarding bleeding—a critical clinical outcome.

This study, using data from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database and cohort studies, aimed to compare bleeding signals of various OACs, identify potential risk factors, and establish a prediction model. It is hoped that this study will provide a robust reference for the clinical monitoring and management of bleeding adverse events (AEs) associated with OAC use in cancer patients.

Methods

Data source and study design

The data for this study came from the FAERS database (https://fis.fda.gov/extensions/FPD-QDE-FAERS/FPD-QDE-FAERS.html) and a clinical cohort study. A detailed introduction to the FAERS database is provided in the Supplementary Materials. Pharmacovigilance study data selection ranged from Q1 2014 to Q1 2024. Each quarterly file contains 7 subfiles, including demographic information, drug information (eg, medication, dosage, and start time), indications for use, AEs and their impact on patients, sources of reports, and more. The study population consisted of cancer patients taking OACs (warfarin, apixaban, edoxaban, rivaroxaban, and dabigatran). The clinical cohort study was a retrospective case/noncase study in cancer patients on oral anticoagulation, with the outcome being bleeding and exposure of interest being the OAC used. This study included patients who presented to Fujian Medical University Union Hospital between October 2017 and September 2021. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital (Ethical Number: 2022QH007). As the study was observational in nature, using the global open database FAERS, which contains anonymized information, and did not involve any treatment interventions or the collection of human samples, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Data extraction and processing

We used the generic and brand names of warfarin, apixaban, edoxaban, rivaroxaban, and dabigatran as search keywords. Reports were selected that identified OACs as the primary suspected drugs in bleeding AEs among cancer patients. The key variables extracted from the pharmacovigilance study included report date, age, sex, reporter source, drug name, indication, reaction description, and outcome. For the clinical cohort study, the extracted key variables were sex, age, drug name, a medical history, comorbidities, cancer site, cancer stage, laboratory test parameters, concomitant medications, and outcome. To ensure data consistency and accuracy, AE coding used the system organ class and preferred terms from the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA; version 26.0). During data processing, the first step for FAERS data was to identify and remove duplicate report entries. Reports lacking critical information (eg, drug name or AE description) were also excluded. In addition, irregular or anomalous data were examined and corrected. For the cohort data, when key variables (eg, drug name and adverse reaction type) had missing values, we applied a complete case analysis (ie, cases with missing values for these variables were excluded). For missing data in other variables, multiple imputation was used.

Outcome of interest

The outcome of interest was hemorrhagic event. The primary outcome was the overall hemorrhagic event. Secondary outcomes included bleeding events affecting the top 5 physiological systems: gastrointestinal system, nervous system (including intracranial hemorrhage), renal and urinary systems, skin and subcutaneous tissue, and ocular system. To assess OACs-associated hemorrhagic events in cancer patients, we used standardized MedDRA queries for hemorrhagic events (standardized MedDRA query code: 20000039) and the system organ class to identify bleeding events from case reports listing the interventions of interest. Cases were defined as cancer patients reported with hemorrhagic reactions, while noncases included cancer patients associated with all other reports. Subgroup analyses of the primary outcomes were conducted based on the severity of AEs and the clinical indications of OACs. The serious AEs were defined as death, life threatening, disability, hospitalization, and other serious illness.

Statistical analysis

In the descriptive analysis, normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD; nonnormally distributed continuous variables as median (IQR); and categorical variables as percentages. Time-to-onset was defined as the period from the initiation of medication to the onset of a bleeding event. The Weibull shape parameter (WSP) test was used for time-to-onset analysis (Supplementary Table 1) [[18], [19], [20]]. In this study, the reporting odds ratio (ROR) was used to quantify the association between OACs and bleeding events in cancer patients [21]. The ROR method was based on a 4-grid table, as shown in Supplementary Table 2. The lower limit of the 95% CI of ROR > 1 indicates that patients receiving a specific drug are more likely to report this adverse reaction than those receiving a control drug, and vice versa (Supplementary Table 3). Regarding the rationale for selecting the reference drug in the ROR analysis among DOACs, we first assessed the strength of bleeding risk signals by calculating the ROR for each drug. Then, based on preliminary analysis, the DOAC with the smallest ROR value, indicating the weakest bleeding risk signals relative to warfarin, was selected as the reference drug for subsequent comparisons among DOACs.

The clinical cohort data were split into a training set and a test set in an 8:2 ratio, and variable selection was performed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. LASSO regression is an effective method for handling high-dimensional data. Its core principle lies in introducing an L1 norm as a penalty term for the regression coefficients on the basis of traditional linear regression. This penalty mechanism drives the estimates of some regression coefficients to exactly zero, thereby enabling automatic feature selection [22]. Following variable selection, prediction models were constructed using 4 methods: logistic regression, random forest, support vector machine, and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) [23]. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUC), specificity, sensitivity, and accuracy. Among these, the AUC served as the primary performance metric, and the model with the best predictive performance was selected as the main model for this study. A 2-tailed P value of <.05 was considered significant. In this study, data analysis and graphing were performed using R software (version 4.1.3; R Studio) and Python software (version 3.8.13; Python Software Foundation).

Results

Descriptive analysis

This study analyzed a total of 1,954,497 case reports, filtering out 29,897 reports of AEs related to OACs in cancer patients, which included 4048 reports of bleeding events (Figure 1). The data originated from 77 countries (Supplementary Table 4), with the majority of reports coming from North America, Europe, and Asia. The study reported demographic information such as age and sex for each drug individually (Table). Except for edoxaban, reports of bleeding events involving older patients (age > 65 years) accounted for >50% of the cases for warfarin, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, and apixaban, with dabigatran having the highest proportion (76.07%). Additionally, males slightly outnumbered females, and the hospitalization rates in the bleeding group were higher than those in the nonbleeding group (Table). Time-to-onset analysis

To assess the overview of bleeding events in cancer patients on OACs, an analysis of time-to-onset was conducted. The results of the WSP test are provided in Supplementary Table 5, and the histogram of bleeding event numbers among cancer patients using OACs is shown in Figure 2. The median times to overall bleeding events for warfarin, apixaban, edoxaban, rivaroxaban, and dabigatran were 34.50 (range, 10.00-125.75) days, 43.00 (range, 11.00-145.00) days, 28.00 (range, 8.00-85.50) days, 61.00 (range, 16.00-149.00) days, and 56.50 (range, 16.00-201.50) days, respectively. Additionally, the median times for gastrointestinal bleeding events ranged from the shortest for warfarin at 25.00 (range, 7.00-87.50) days to the longest for rivaroxaban at 61.00 (range, 18.00-147.50) days. In the WSP tests, the upper 95% CIs of the shape parameter (β) for all OACs were <1, indicating a type of early failure, that is, the risk of bleeding decreases over time (Supplementary Table 6).Figure 2Time-to-onset analysis of total bleeding events and gastrointestinal bleeding (GIB) events associated with 5 oral anticoagulants in cancer patients.

Disproportionality analysis

Comparing the bleeding risk signals between DOACs and warfarin in cancer patients, the results showed that apixaban (ROR, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.57-0.68) and rivaroxaban (ROR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.82-0.98) showed signals of reduced bleeding risk, whereas edoxaban (ROR, 1.02; 95% CI, 0.88-1.18) and dabigatran (ROR, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.80-1.14) did not show reduced bleeding risk signals (Figure 3). Comparing edoxaban, rivaroxaban, and dabigatran with apixaban, elevated signals of bleeding risk were found for edoxaban (ROR, 1.62; 95% CI, 1.41-1.88), rivaroxaban (ROR, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.31-1.56), and dabigatran (ROR, 1.53; 95% CI, 1.28-1.82) (Figure 4). Among the 4 DOACs, apixaban showed the weakest bleeding risk signals in the gastrointestinal, nervous, renal and urinary, and ocular systems (Figure 4). Subgroup analysis

In the subgroup analysis by severity of AEs, compared with warfarin, the results showed that apixaban (ROR, 0.48; 95% CI, 0.45-0.52), edoxaban (ROR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.64-0.80), rivaroxaban (ROR, 0.68; 95% CI, 0.63-0.73), and dabigatran (ROR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.63-0.84) signaled a reduced risk of serious bleeding, with apixaban demonstrating the lowest serious bleeding risk signals (Supplementary Table 7).

Subgroup analysis by indication showed that in the primary prevention of VTE, apixaban demonstrated a significantly reduced signal of bleeding risk compared with warfarin (ROR, 0.65, 95% CI 0.59-0.71), followed by dabigatran (ROR, 0.74, 95% CI 0.58-0.94) and rivaroxaban (ROR, 0.86, 95% CI 0.78-0.96). In the secondary prevention of VTE, only apixaban suggested a reduction in bleeding risk signals (ROR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.60-0.97), while the other DOACs showed no significant associations. Among patients with AF, all 3 other DOACs (apixaban, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban) demonstrated signals of reduced bleeding risk, with the exception of dabigatran (Supplementary Table 8). Apixaban showed weaker bleeding risk signals across all 3 indication subgroups.

Discussion

This study compared the bleeding profiles of different OACs in cancer patients based on the FAERS database and cohort study data. Our main findings indicated that bleeding events associated with OACs were most frequently observed during the early stages of medication use. Additionally, significant differences were observed in the occurrence of bleeding events among different OACs. Compared with apixaban, the other 4 anticoagulants demonstrated higher reporting frequency of bleeding, particularly in the gastrointestinal system. When analyzing factors influencing bleeding risk, in addition to male, cardiovascular diseases, cerebral infarction, renal insufficiency, and elevated INR, gastrointestinal cancers were identified as significant predictive factors. Among the 4 predictive models, the XGBoost model performed the best. These findings emphasize the importance of considering individual differences when prescribing OACs to cancer patients and provided significant data support for optimizing anticoagulation treatment strategies.

In the analysis of time-to-onset, we found that bleeding events associated with OACs mainly occurred in the early stages of treatment within the first 2 months, consistent with the findings by Ferrazzini et al. [24]. Their study showed that the cumulative incidence of bleeding reached 16% over 36 months, with nearly half of the MB events occurring in the initial stages of anticoagulation therapy. However, there were differences in the timing of bleeding events among different OACs. Edoxaban tended to cause bleeding earlier, while dabigatran had the latest onset of bleeding, which may be related to the pharmacokinetics of the drugs or individual differences among patients [25]. This is particularly important in clinical management, as it may influence the choice of monitoring strategies for patients.

In comparisons among different OACs, there were also significant differences in bleeding risk signals. The Hokusai-VTE Cancer trial reported that edoxaban, compared with dalteparin, was associated with a higher rate of MB (6.9% vs 4.0%), despite a lower recurrent VTE rate [26]. Conversely, the trial (Rivaroxaban versus Dalteparin in Cancer‑Associated Thromboembolism: A Randomized Trial, CASTA-DIVA), although limited by sample size, showed numerically lower MB with rivaroxaban than with dalteparin (1.4% vs 3.7%), consistent with the overall bleeding profile of rivaroxaban in our analysis [27]. In the comparison between DOACs and warfarin, apixaban and rivaroxaban showed lower bleeding risk signals, while edoxaban and dabigatran had bleeding risk signals similar to warfarin. Additionally, compared with apixaban, the other 3 DOACs (edoxaban, rivaroxaban, and dabigatran) exhibited increased bleeding risk signals. This finding is similar to the results of the studies by Wu et al. [28] and Samaranayake et al. [29]. The COBRRA (Comparison of Bleeding Risk between Rivaroxaban and Apixaban) study showed that during the 3-month treatment period, the risk of clinically relevant bleeding was significantly lower in the apixaban group than in the rivaroxaban group among patients with acute VTE, a finding consistent with the results of our signal detection [30]. The possible reason is that the pharmacodynamic effects of new OACs closely reflected their pharmacokinetic characteristics, and higher bleeding risks may have been associated with maximum drug concentration [25,[31], [32], [33]]. However, unlike previous studies that suggested dabigatran was relatively safe [33,34], our data showed that dabigatran had a higher bleeding risk signals in cancer patients than apixaban or rivaroxaban, which could be due to population heterogeneity. Therefore, to more accurately assess the safety of OACs in different patient groups, further research is needed, particularly trials targeting specific subgroups.

Our subgroup analysis revealed key differences in the bleeding risk signals of DOACs across various clinical indications. It is also important to assess bleeding risk signals based on clinical indications, such as AF and primary or secondary prevention of VTE. In the primary prevention of VTE, apixaban (ROR, 0.65) showed the lowest risk signal for bleeding, followed by dabigatran (ROR, 0.74) and rivaroxaban (ROR, 0.86). The American Society of Clinical Oncology guidelines also recommend rivaroxaban and apixaban for primary prevention of VTE in high risk ambulatory cancer patients [35]. In the secondary prevention of VTE, only apixaban showed a significantly reduced risk signal for bleeding (ROR, 0.77), consistent with the findings by Cohen et al. [36]. Their study indicated that, in high risk subgroups of cancer patients with VTE, apixaban generally had positive outcomes compared with LMWH and warfarin. Patients with recurrent VTE have an increased risk of bleeding due to endothelial damage caused by previous thrombotic events. These patients often require extended anticoagulation treatment, and apixaban may be a better choice. For the AF population, a reduction in bleeding signals was observed with apixaban/edoxaban/rivaroxaban (but not with dabigatran), possibly due to the reliance of dabigatran on renal excretion (80% vs 27%-50%) [25]. It is important to note that cancer complicates the clinical history of patients with AF; those with concurrent cancer vary in vascular outcomes depending on the cancer site, and cancer increases the risk of bleeding in patients with AF [[37], [38], [39]]. Future analyses should be tailored according to the type and stage of cancer to further refine these findings.

Regarding the sites of bleeding, the 5 OACs primarily caused gastrointestinal bleeding events, with other common bleeding sites including the neurologic system, urinary system, and others. The study by Nathan et al. [40] also showed that rivaroxaban, dabigatran, and apixaban frequently caused bleeding in the gastrointestinal system, neurologic system, and urinary system, among others. Additionally, the study by Ingason et al. [41] indicated that, compared with apixaban and dabigatran, rivaroxaban led to a higher rate of gastrointestinal bleeding. In our study, the number of adverse drug reaction reports for gastrointestinal bleeding with rivaroxaban was significantly higher than with other drugs, but due to the lack of total medication usage data in the FAERS, the incidence rate of AEs could not be determined. Further research is anticipated to confirm these findings.

We analyzed 19 predictive factors potentially associated with anticoagulation in cancer patients, such as sex, the combined use of antiplatelet drugs, and specific types of cancer. The results indicate that factors including male sex, cardiovascular disease, cerebral infarction, renal insufficiency, elevated INR, and gastrointestinal cancer are significantly associated with an increased risk of anticoagulation-related bleeding in cancer patients. Managing these risk factors may be a strategy to help reduce bleeding in cancer patients undergoing anticoagulation. Furthermore, based on the aforementioned risk factors, we developed a bleeding risk prediction model using a machine learning algorithm to quantify individual bleeding risk, providing an important decision support tool for assessing and managing the safety of anticoagulant therapy in cancer patients in clinical practice.

To further contextualize the methodological quality of our prediction model, we refer to the PROBAST (Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool) framework [42] and the requirements proposed by Sanfilippo et al. [43] for risk score development in cancer populations. According to PROBAST, a robust prediction model should demonstrate low risk of bias across 4 key domains: participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis. In our study, while the selection of participants and predictors was clearly defined, the discrepancy between training and test performance in some algorithms suggests potential overfitting. Furthermore, as highlighted by Sanfilippo et al. [43], external validation is essential for establishing generalizability. Our model currently lacks external validation. Therefore, although our model shows preliminary promise, it should be considered exploratory. Future studies should undertake external validation with independent datasets and include calibration measures before any clinical application.

The strength of our study is that the findings have significant implications for clinical practice. According to our study, cancer patients in the initial stages of medication use (first 2 months) exhibited a higher risk of bleeding, suggesting that this group should receive special attention during the early stages of anticoagulation treatment in clinical practice. Additionally, in bleeding events associated with OAC use among cancer patients, gastrointestinal cancers are regarded as a significant risk factor. Therefore, particular caution should be exercised when administering anticoagulation therapy to this population. Among the 5 OACs analyzed, apixaban demonstrated the lowest risk of bleeding in cancer patients, making it the preferred choice for patients at high risk of bleeding. These findings provide critical evidence in support of optimizing anticoagulation strategies.

Although our study provided valuable insights, it also had some limitations. First, inherent FAERS limitations (no denominator data, reporting bias, and unmeasured confounders) mean observed estimates (including ROR) can only generate safety signals but not infer causal risk or incidence; a single-center retrospective design (small sample, few bleeding events, and missing key variables such as platelet counts and treatment details) may cause residual confounding and limit external validity. Furthermore, for drugs with small sample sizes (eg, edoxaban), the estimated ROR may be unstable and the CIs wider. Future validation in larger sample sizes is warranted. Second, nonrandomized treatment allocation inevitably introduces indication bias: patients receiving different OACs may differ in underlying indications (eg, AF vs cancer-associated thrombosis), cancer type/stage, comorbidities, and baseline bleeding risk, and despite multivariable adjustment, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Third, the generalizability of our findings is limited to the category of warfarin (the most widely used vitamin K inhibitor in FAERS) and may not fully reflect the safety profile of other vitamin K antagonists. Fourth, due to the limited sample size and the lack of detailed cancer staging data, further subgroup analyses could not be conducted. Finally, bleeding events were identified using MedDRA coding rather than the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) standardized classification, which may limit comparability with future prospective studies. Future large-scale, multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate the abovementioned limitations.

Conclusion

Bleeding events associated with OACs primarily occur in the early stages of medication use, with gastrointestinal bleeding being the most frequent. Although all 5 OACs studied could increase the risk of bleeding, there were significant differences in the timing of occurrences and the main types of events. Among the 5 OACs, apixaban showed the weakest bleeding signal. Additionally, our study found that factors such as male sex, cardiovascular disease, cerebral infarction, renal insufficiency, elevated INR, and gastrointestinal cancers were significantly associated with an increased risk of anticoagulation-related bleeding in cancer patients. The prediction model for OACs-related bleeding developed in this study demonstrated acceptable performance in internal validation; however, its generalizability and clinical utility remain to be established through external validation and calibration.
