AI-guided integration of aging-related functional assessment to predict nonresponse to cardiac rehabilitation in heart failure
Authors: Martin Chemineau, Ryeonshi Kang, Mathieu Cinato, Lesia Savchenko, Solomiia Kramar, Audrey Swiader, Anne Negre-Salvayre, Alexandre Heurteau, Raoul Raffel, Vanessa Nader, Charlotte Laborde, Nathalie Pizzinat, Jerome Roncalli, Yoan Sallami, Oxana Kunduzova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiac rehabilitation (CR) improves functional capacity and outcomes in patients with heart failure (HF). However, a clinically significant subgroup of patients exhibits limited improvements in aerobic capacity, indicating substantial heterogeneity in response and limiting the effectiveness of standardized rehabilitation programs.ObjectiveTo develop an interpretable artificial intelligence (AI)–guided framework to identify predictors of nonresponse to CR and support personalized rehabilitation strategies in HF, with age-related functional decline as a key determinant of heterogeneity.MethodsData on patient characteristics, exercise interventions, and response rates were extracted from Randomized controlled trials evaluating exercise-based CR in patients with HF that reported changes in peak oxygen uptake (VO₂peak) or functional capacity. Nonresponse was defined as <10% improvement in VO₂peak after CR. Coupling of eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) was introduced to characterize age-related determinants of nonresponse to CR in HF.ResultsAcross 38 randomized trials including 5,198 patients, 30–45% failed to achieve clinically meaningful improvements in VO₂peak after CR. Advanced age was consistently associated with attenuated benefit, with older patients showing reduced gains. Non-response was linked to higher age, lower baseline aerobic capacity, metabolic comorbidities, impaired skeletal muscle function, and adverse psychosocial status. An interpretable AI-guided approach integrating aging-associated functional and psychosocial parameters was introduced to CR in patients with HF. Principal predictors of nonresponse included impaired baseline aerobic capacity, metabolic comorbidities, diminished skeletal muscle function and adverse psychosocial factors, delineating an age-modulated vulnerability to impaired rehabilitation adaptation.ConclusionAge is associated with cardiac rehabilitation response in heart failure, with heterogeneous effects across outcomes. An AI-driven framework can be introduced to identify age-related non-response phenotypes, supporting precision rehabilitation strategies.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) represents a major global health challenge associated with high healthcare costs and frequent hospitalizations [1]. Despite advances in cardiovascular disease management improving survival, HF prevalence continues to rise, underscoring a major unmet clinical need [1]. The prevalence, severity, and mortality of HF increase with age, driven by biological aging processes, contributing substantially to the healthcare and socioeconomic burden [2]. Biological aging is increasingly recognized as a fundamental mechanistic driver of HF progression, characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction, chronic low-grade inflammation, cellular senescence, and progressive loss of regenerative capacity, which synergistically promote maladaptive cardiac remodeling, impaired myocardial resilience, and progressive functional decline [2]. Current therapies primarily reduce cardiac workload and modulate neurohormonal pathways, providing symptomatic benefit but limited disease modification [3]. The interplay between HF pathophysiology and biological aging may therefore be a critical determinant of rehabilitation outcomes.

Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) is a structured program for patients recovering from cardiovascular events, including HF [4]. It combines supervised exercise, risk-factor modification, pharmacological optimization, and psychosocial support. CR reduces mortality, rehospitalization, and improves functional capacity and quality of life [5]. It is delivered in three phases: Phase I (inpatient), Phase II (supervised outpatient training and education), and Phase III (long-term maintenance) [4,5]. In Phase II, peak oxygen uptake (VO₂peak) is the gold-standard measure of cardiorespiratory fitness and a strong prognostic marker in HF [6]. However, responses to CR are highly variable. Approximately 30% of patients are classified as nonresponders, showing <10% improvement in VO₂peak, with poorer clinical outcomes [7,8]. In older patients, this variability may be linked to aging-related mechanisms interacting with HF progression, including skeletal muscle dysfunction, endothelial impairment, autonomic dysregulation and reduced mitochondrial capacity [9].

Traditional outcome metrics such as VO₂peak incompletely capture the multidimensional adaptations induced by CR, underscoring the need for precision approaches to improve patient stratification and intervention efficacy. In this context, machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) are increasingly applied in cardiovascular medicine for risk prediction, phenotyping and treatment response modelling [[10], [11], [12]]. However, their translation to CR remains limited by data heterogeneity, limited interpretability, and lack of robust personalized frameworks. Interpretable ML approaches including tree-based ensemble models and explainable AI techniques, represent a promising strategy to address these barriers while preserving clinical transparency.

This study introduces an interpretable AI strategy combining eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to identify key predictors of VO₂peak response to CR in HF patients. By integrating age-related functional and psychosocial variables, this strategy supports precision rehabilitation and enables early identification of nonresponders.

Methods and materials

The systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis) guidelines. A comprehensive literature search was performed in MEDLINE (PubMed), EMBASE, and CENTRAL to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating exercise-based CR in HF patients. The search strategy covered studies published between January 1, 2021 and August 10, 2025. Search terms included combinations of “HF,” “HFrEF,” “HFpEF,” “CR,” “exercise training,” “exercise therapy,” “aerobic training,” “high-intensity interval training,” “peak oxygen uptake,” “VO₂peak,” “cardiopulmonary exercise testing,” “exercise capacity,” “functional capacity,” and “randomized controlled trial.” Boolean operators (AND, OR) were applied, and search strategies were adapted for each database. No restrictions were applied regarding geographic region. Ethnic origin was not included because it was not consistently reported across the included randomized controlled trials, precluding reliable extraction and harmonized analysis across studies. Only peer-reviewed publications in the English language were considered.

Eligible studies enrolled adult patients (≥18 years) with HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), or mixed phenotypes. Interventions predominantly involved aerobic exercise training, often combined with resistance training or patient education, with program durations spanning 8–24 weeks and frequencies of two to five sessions per week. Baseline data encompassed demographic, anthropometric, clinical, biological, functional, and psychosocial characteristics. Demographic and anthropometric variables included age, sex, weight, height, BMI. Clinical status is described using NYHA class, HF phenotype (HFrEF or HFpEF), comorbidities such as diabetes, obesity, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, duration of the underlying cardiac condition, prior cardiovascular events including acute coronary syndromes, and the complete pharmacological treatment regimen.

Biological and physiological measurements incorporated N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), creatinine, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), the lipid profile, arterial blood pressure, and a detailed assessment of cardiac remodeling, including left ventricular ejection fraction, ventricular volumes, and myocardial strain. Additional physiological measures included resting heart rate, maximal heart rate, VO₂peak at baseline, peak power output, ventilatory thresholds, 6MWT, maximal muscle strength assessments, and markers of body composition.

Data extraction

Data were systematically extracted from each eligible study, including study characteristics (publication year, country, sample size), patient demographics (age, HF phenotype, left ventricular ejection fraction), exercise intervention details (type: aerobic, high-intensity interval, or combined training; duration and frequency of CR programs), primary outcomes (VO₂peak, functional capacity), and the number or proportion of responders versus non-responders. Responder classification is based on the relative percentage change in VO₂peak. Outcome measures

The primary outcome was cardiac rehabilitation response status, defined as a binary variable (responder vs non-responder) based on changes in VO₂peak following completion of CR. Non-response was defined as a <10% relative improvement in VO₂peak, whereas a >10% increase was classified as a response. This threshold was selected a priori to reflect a clinically meaningful improvement in aerobic capacity and to ensure harmonization across heterogeneous trial definitions.

Secondary outcomes included changes in VO₂peak, 6-minute walk distance, left ventricular ejection fraction, and health-related quality of life.

Quality assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2) for randomized trials. Each study was evaluated across domains including the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Assessments were performed at the outcome level and independently applied according to the RoB 2 signalling questions and algorithm-based judgement framework. Each domain was rated as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk” of bias, with an overall risk-of-bias judgement derived from domain-level assessments. Risk-of-bias judgements were incorporated into the interpretation of pooled estimates and considered in sensitivity analysis to evaluate the robustness of the findings to study quality.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative syntheses were performed using a random-effects model to account for between-study heterogeneity. Continuous outcomes were expressed as mean differences with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI), while categorical outcomes, including responder status, were pooled using inverse-variance weighting. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with values >50% indicating moderate-to-substantial heterogeneity. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted where data permitted, according to heart failure phenotype, exercise training modality, and intervention duration, to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. All analyses were performed using R software (meta package).

Given the aggregate-level nature of the available data, individual participant data were not accessible. Consequently, patient-level modeling, causal inference, and ML–based predictive analyses were not feasible within the present dataset. Analyses were therefore restricted to study-level pooled estimates. To support future translational and precision rehabilitation research, we introduce a methodological framework for individual participant data-based predictive modeling. In contrast to aggregate-level synthesis, such an approach would enable patient-level inference and the development of clinically applicable prediction tools for CR response. The proposed framework integrates a supervised ML pipeline based on XGBoost, selected for its ability to model nonlinear relationships and high-order feature interactions in complex clinical datasets. Model interpretability integrates SHAP, enabling both global and individual-level attribution of feature importance and mechanistic interpretation of predictors of VO₂peak response. Planned methodological components include: (a) integration of multidomain predictors (clinical, functional, biological and psychosocial variables), (b) standardized preprocessing, including normalization, missing data handling, and feature encoding, (c) feature selection based on clinical relevance and data-driven importance ranking, (d) stratified k-fold cross-validation for internal model validation, (e) performance evaluation using discrimination and calibration metrics, (f) SHAP-based interpretability for identification of key determinants and phenotype stratification.

Exercise-based CR, delivered as aerobic training or high-intensity interval training (2–5 sessions per week over 8–24 weeks), consistently improved VO₂peak, with pooled increases ranging from 2.4 to 3.9 mL kg−1 min−1 compared with control groups. However, the magnitude of improvement appeared to decline with increasing age, with studies including older populations generally reporting smaller gains in aerobic capacity. This attenuation is consistent with established age-related physiological changes, including reduced cardiovascular reserve, impaired skeletal muscle function and altered metabolic flexibility, which may limit adaptive responses to exercise. Although high-intensity interval training was associated with greater improvements overall, its relative efficacy in older cohorts was less consistent. Moderate heterogeneity across studies (I² ≈ 52%) likely reflects variability in age distribution, comorbidity burden and intervention characteristics.

Moderate heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² ≈ 52%), reflecting differences in exercise protocols, patient populations, and response definitions. Subgroup analyses based on mean age revealed that studies with higher mean age demonstrated a lower pooled response rate and smaller improvements in VO₂peak compared with studies including younger populations, supporting an age-related gradient in rehabilitation responsiveness.

Similarly, subgroup analyses based on sex distribution suggested that studies with a higher proportion of female participants showed slightly lower improvements in VO₂peak, although these differences did not consistently reach statistical significance, indicating potential but inconclusive sex-related differences in CR response.

Between-subgroup differences were modest and should be interpreted cautiously given the aggregate nature of the data and potential confounding factors. Additional factors associated with lower response rates included: metabolic comorbidities (diabetes, obesity) and shorter rehabilitation duration.

Three-step structured model

To identify HF patients at risk of non-response to CR, we introduce a structured three-step approach integrating feature selection, model development, and interpretability analysis to enable clinically meaningful prediction of response status (Fig. 2). First, nine clinically informed features – cardiac function, exercise capacity, motivation for physical activity, anxiety and depression, physical activity and sedentary behavior, muscle strength, quality of life, body composition, and functional capacity – are integrated into a XGBoost model. Second, the model is optimized, evaluated and benchmarked against performance metrics to ensure robustness and predictive accuracy. Third, XGBoost-based interpretability techniques are applied to explain and contextualize predictions, providing mechanistic insights to guide clinical decision-making.Fig. 2Study design to identify HF patients at risk of non-response to CR. Psychosocial and motivational factors included baseline motivation for physical activity assessed with the EMAPS questionnaire [13], health-related quality of life measured using EQ-5D5L Questionnaire [14], depressive or anxiety symptoms, and documented indicators of adherence to rehabilitation as well as aging-related characteristics. Predictive modeling strategy and baseline evaluation

The proposed model development strategy incorporates clinical, functional, and psychosocial features and employs XGBoost to predict changes in exercise capacity. Feature selection, preprocessing, and model optimization are key steps, with evaluation of discrimination, calibration, and interpretability. Traditional statistical approaches often fail to capture the emergent and multiscale dynamics driving complex biological responses, thereby constraining mechanistic understanding and translational application to precision CR strategies. To introduce a multidimensional AI-driven strategy for identifying age-related phenotypes of nonresponse to CR in HF patients, we suggest combining XGBoost, a robust and scalable method for modelling heterogeneous and nonlinear outcome patterns [15], with SHAP, a model-agnostic explainability approach [16]. This approach enables delineation of responder and non-responder phenotypes, incorporating age-related biological and functional variability. The combination of predictive performance and interpretability supports individualized risk stratification and clinical decision-making. Multidimensional assessment integrates physiological, behavioral, psychological, cardiac, and patient-reported domains, including exercise capacity, muscle strength, functional capacity, body composition, physical activity, mood, cardiac function, and quality of life [13,14,[17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. Outcomes includes exercise capacity (CPET), motivation (EMAPS), anxiety and depression (HADS), cardiac function (echocardiography), quality of life (EQ-5D-5L), physical activity (Onaps-PAQ), muscle strength, functional capacity (6MWT, NYHA class), and body composition (bioimpedance analysis). The program was designed to promote functional recovery, enhance adherence to physical activity, and address be-havioral and psychological determinants of health within a comprehensive, multidimensional framework, while accounting for aging-related functional and physiological heterogeneity.

Supervision and hyperparameter optimization

An XGBoost classifier with a binary logistic objective addresses imbalance through weighting of the minority group. Comparative benchmarking allows quantification of algorithm-specific contributions to prediction of non-response, identification of potential complementary insights across modeling approaches, and mechanistic interpretation of key predictors, including age-related determinants, driving patient-level outcomes.

The primary measure of model performance is the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) assessed on the independent test set. Secondary metrics include the area under the precision-recall curve, sensitivity, specificity, the confusion matrix, the Brier score, and model calibration assessed through calibration curves and reliability diagrams. Explainability and profiling

Global interpretability can be quantified via SHAP summary plots, which rank predictors according to their contribution to model output and allow identification of variables with the largest impact on nonresponse, including age-specific determinants. SHAP dependence plots further characterize the functional relationship between individual predictors and the predicted probability of nonresponse, including both directionality and effect magnitude. Individual-level interpretability can be supported by SHAP force plots, providing patient-specific insights into predictor contributions and aging-related risk profiles.

To delineate potential nonresponder phenotypes, unsupervised clustering algorithms, such as k-means, could be applied to the SHAP value matrix. This approach facilitates detection of subgroups with distinct mechanistic profiles, including aging-dependent phenotypes, for instance, patients primarily limited by structural cardiac deficits versus those whose outcomes are predominantly influenced by psychosocial, behavioral, or age-related functional decline. Such stratification enables a mechanistically informed, precision-guided interpretation of model predictions and supports hypothesis generation for tailored therapeutic strategies.

Discussion

Although CR confers CR confers well-documented population-level benefits, substantial inter-individual variability in response remains a critical barrier to optimizing functional outcomes, particularly in the context of aging-related physiological heterogeneity in HF populations. Meta-analysis evidence indicates that approximately 40% of HF patients fail to achieve meaningful improvements in VO₂peak following exercise-based CR, underscoring the prevalence of non-response in this population and the potential contribution of age-associated decline in functional reserve.

Across studies, participants were predominantly older adults, with several cohorts reporting mean ages ≥70 years, indicating that the evidence base is largely derived from elderly patients representative of routine care populations. This profile reflects the epidemiology of HF, in which risk and disease burden increase with advancing age, supporting external validity. Exercise-based interventions consistently improved peak VO₂ in HF. However, age emerged as a key modifier of response, with attenuated gains observed in older patients. Despite inter-study heterogeneity, the direction of effect remained consistent. Overall, structured exercise training improves cardiorespiratory fitness in HF, although benefits appear progressively reduced with increasing age.

In the present study, we introduce a novel, interpretable AI-guided framework designed to predict individual VO₂peak responses and characterize non-responder phenotypes in Phase II CR. By integrating multidimensional age-dependent clinical, functional and behavioral variables with SHAP–interpreted predictions, this approach can dissect key determinants of nonresponse in HF. Clustering of feature contributions can identify subgroups influenced by central hemodynamics, peripheral oxygen extraction, skeletal muscle oxidative capacity, aging-related functional decline, and psychosocial constraints, highlighting actionable targets for interventions.

In clinical practice, VO₂peak is a robust prognostic marker in HF and a primary endpoint of CR; however, responses are highly heterogeneous, reflecting inter-individual specificity in central hemodynamic reserve, ventricular-arterial coupling, pulmonary pressures, skeletal muscle oxidative capacity, microvascular density, and mitochondrial function, leaving a substantial subset unable to achieve clinically meaningful gains [[22], [23], [24]]. In HF, maladaptive hemodynamic alterations comprising elevated left and right ventricular filling pressures and reduced stroke volume, direct to progressive organ dysfunction [25,26]. Advanced age and comorbidities including diabetes, obesity, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and depression, can impair exercise tolerance and adherence, blunting physiological adaptation to training [27]. Consequently, VO₂peak responsiveness to CR reflects an integrated cardiopulmonary-skeletal muscle phenotype, constrained by central hemodynamic limitations and peripheral maladaptations, including skeletal myopathy, microvascular rarefaction, and mitochondrial dysfunction. Behavioral and environmental factors further modulate adaptation, resulting in marked inter-individual heterogeneity in functional improvement.

The predictive modeling approach explored in this study suggests that routinely collected clinical, functional and behavioral variables in an age appropriate manner can delineate patient-specific determinants of VO₂peak responsiveness, integrating central hemodynamic reserve, ventricular–arterial coupling, peripheral oxygen extraction, skeletal muscle oxidative capacity, and systemic modulators. Nonresponse is therefore not stochastic but reflects identifiable baseline age-specific physiological and behavioral phenotypes, detectable prior to the initiation of CR program. The model’s performance metrics support this interpretation, demonstrating discrimination and calibration consistent with clinically meaningful prediction. Importantly, the inclusion of age-dependent psychosocial and behavioral predictors underscores the relevance of key determinants, aligning with the concept that CR efficacy depends on both biological aging and patient engagement. These results therefore support the feasibility of early risk stratification in routine practice and open the possibility of adapting rehabilitation strategies a priori, such as intensifying training, optimizing medical therapy, or providing targeted behavioral interventions. This approach also enables consideration of age-associated functional reserve and recovery capacity, thereby shifting CR toward a more personalized, precision-based model of care.

Leveraging SHAP for model interpretability represents a key methodological advance of this study. Predictive modeling systems trained on high-dimensional clinical datasets can achieve strong outcome-oriented performance but often function as ‘black boxes,’ limiting interpretability and clinical translation. This framework can provide not only global interpretability through ranking of age-specific determinants, but also individualized explanation, which is essential in HF where therapeutic decisions require nuanced balancing of potential benefit, risk, and patient burden, particularly in older individuals with heterogeneous physiological reserve. In this context, SHAP provides a clinically meaningful bridge between predictive performance and actionable insight, allowing clinicians to understand which baseline characteristics, including aging-related determinants, drive the predicted likelihood of non-response.

Importantly, the SHAP-derived hierarchy pinpoints that determinants of non-esponse extend beyond conventional physiological markers. While measures of cardiac function and exercise capacity remain prominent, motivational, psychosocial, and aging-associated dimensions emerge as significant contributors to predicted outcomes [28]. This finding is consistent with a growing body of evidence that behavioral factors and adherence are not merely modifiers but core determinants of rehabilitation efficacy. Indeed, the physiological potential for improvement may be unrealized if patients are unable or unwilling to engage with the program, or if symptom burden, frailty during aging and psychological distress limit participation. Thus, the model’s interpretability underscores the need to integrate biopsychosocial assessment into CR planning, rather than relying solely on cardiac physiology.

Beyond prediction, our clustering of SHAP value matrices directs to a novel strategy to define mechanistically coherent nonresponder phenotypes. Clustering the contributions of individual features, rather than the raw clinical variables themselves, enables identification of patient subgroups characterized by key physiological, behavioral, and aging-dependent drivers of predicted non-response [29]. Preliminary clusters, such as cardiac-limited, peripheral-limited and psychosocially constrained profiles, provide a functional taxonomy that is directly interpretable and potentially actionable. For example, individuals in the cardiac-limited cluster may have limited cardiac output reserve, high filling pressures, or severe remodeling [30], suggesting that optimization of pharmacological therapy or consideration of advanced HF interventions may be necessary before exercise adaptation can occur. Conversely, patients within the psychosocially constrained or aging-related vulnerability clusters may exhibit low intrinsic motivation, frailty, depressive symptoms, or poor adherence, suggesting that targeted behavioral, geriatric-informed, and psychological interventions may be required to enable subsequent physiological gains. Phenotype-based stratification thus has the potential to shift CR from a standardized protocol toward a precision approach, optimizing training modalities, intensity, and supportive interventions according to dominant limiting mechanisms, and ultimately improving both efficiency and clinical outcomes.

Several limitations should be considered. The retrospective design and single-center setting may limit applicability, particularly in aging HF populations with diverse clinical trajectories, and missing or unreliable data could introduce bias despite stringent inclusion criteria. The definition of non-response using a relative VO₂peak change threshold (<10%) is supported by existing literature [31,32] but may not capture the full clinical significance of functional improvement, particularly in older adults where modest gains may still be clinically meaningful. Moreover, while SHAP provides interpretability, it does not establish causality; prospective studies are required to confirm whether modifying identified physiological, behavioral, and aging-related determinants alters response trajectories. This approach is proposed for application in future individual participant datasets, as reliable training, validation, and generalization of machine learning models require individual-level observations. External validation in independent cohorts would be essential to confirm model robustness, transportability, and clinical utility in diverse HF populations.

Conclusions

In this study, we introduce an interpretable AI framework integrating XGBoost and SHAP to identify predictors of CR nonresponse in HF patients, with a specific focus on age-related determinants. This approach addresses pronounced inter-individual heterogeneity in rehabilitation response, driven in part by aging-related functional alterations. By enabling the identification of distinct phenotypes of nonresponse, this approach can provide mechanistic and clinically interpretable insights that may support precision CR program. Collectively, these results underscore the potential of explainable AI to refine patient stratification and guide individualized exercise-based interventions in aging patients with HF.
