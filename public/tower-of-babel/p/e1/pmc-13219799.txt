An exploratory study of rural landscape health assessment based on multi-functionality and the integrated weighting method in nine villages of Guangzhou
Authors: Siqi Sun, Hao Luo, Yu Li, Kun Wang, Qing Zhao, Yuanzhi Yang, Qiuju Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRural landscape health assessment, which integrates ecology and culture, is crucial for China’s rural revitalization strategy. However, existing studies primarily focus on large-scale regions, neglecting small-scale areas. This study established a rural landscape health assessment system encompassing ecological conservation, recreational utilization, and socioeconomic production taking nine typical villages in Guangzhou as case studies for exploration. Principal component analysis and the entropy weight method determined indicator weights, and a comprehensive spatial assessment was conducted using a grid-based method. Overall rural landscape health in Guangzhou was relatively low, with the combined area proportion of the “very poor” and “poor” grades > 23.96%; the average area proportion of the “very good” and “good” grades was < 8%, mostly distributed at junctions between patches and corridors with different landscape functions. Significant differences among village types were observed only for the ecological conservation function, with the highest disparity in the area proportion of “very poor” and “poor” grades within villages reaching 48.55%. No significant correlation was found between landscape health and the forest-to-non-forest area ratio; however, a positive correlation with the numerical ratio was identified under ecological conservation and comprehensive functions. The area and numerical ratio maintained a significant positive correlation across all functions. This study reveals the multidimensional characteristics and spatial differentiation of rural landscape health in Guangzhou, providing scientific evidence for targeted optimization. Due to restrictions imposed by urban no-fly zones on drone data collection, this study’s sample only covered nine villages across four districts in Guangzhou. Introduction

Rural landscapes arise from the interaction between human activities and the natural environment, forming mosaics of diverse land units. They embody dual ecological and cultural attributes and function as complex regional ecosystems1. With the advancement of China’s rural revitalization strategy, rural landscape health assessment has become a frontier research field bridging landscape architecture, ecology, and related disciplines. Recent studies have scientifically evaluated the current status of rural landscapes, identified challenges, and promoted healthy and sustainable development through precise enhancement and optimization2.

The concept of “land health” was first proposed by the American scholar, Leopold, in the mid-20th century. Subsequently, Rapport et al. defined “ecosystem health” as the ability of ecosystems to maintain complexity and functionality in a disease-free state3,4. Costanza further expanded this concept, constructing a theoretical framework encompassing three dimensions—vigor, organization, and resilience—providing a foundation for systematic assessment5. With theoretical development, landscape health assessment has gradually evolved from single ecological attributes to multi-dimensional approaches encompassing ecosystem succession, spatiotemporal changes, and landscape structure, thereby forming a more comprehensive assessment perspective6–9.

Methodologically, early landscape health assessments relied on the pressure–state–response (PSR) model, vigor–organization–resilience (VOR) model, and integration of remote sensing with geographic information system (GIS) technologies to quantify ecosystem states through landscape pattern indices10,11. With the advancement of research, methods such as the minimum cumulative resistance model and the remotely-sensed ecosystem health assessment model have been introduced, further supporting multi-scale and multi-functional evaluations of landscape health. However, the commonly used VOR evaluation model in existing studies aims to assess the sustainability of ecosystem structure and function, emphasizing the natural attributes and intrinsic integrity of ecosystems12. In contrast, the PSR model centers on systematically analyzing interaction mechanisms between human activities and the ecological environment, incorporating social and economic factors into a comprehensive evaluation framework13. However, certain indicators analyzed by these two GIS-based models still exhibit subjectivity and uncertainty14. Moreover, they fail to characterize spatial distribution differences in landscape health from a functional perspective and lack quantitative analysis of the relationship between landscape health and forest-to-non-forest landscape elements. In terms of weight determination, methods such as the analytic hierarchy process (AHP), entropy weight method (EWM), and principal component analysis (PCA) are widely applied. Existing studies predominantly employ comprehensive weighting methods combining AHP and entropy weighting, which enhances the scientific rigor and objectivity of evaluations15–17. However, when applied to systems with numerous indicators, AHP is prone to inconsistencies arising from expert judgment. In contrast, PCA extracts the primary directions of data variation to reflect the structural information of the indicators18,19. Adopting a combined “PCA–EWM” weighting approach can better balance both the structural commonalities and discrete information across multiple indicators, thereby strengthening the reliability and practical utility of the results.

Regarding research subjects, landscape health assessment has been widely applied across various ecosystem types, including wetlands, urban systems, terrestrial habitats, rivers, coastal zones, and forest parks20–27. However, existing studies on landscape health assessment predominantly focus on large-scale areas such as mountainous regions and wetlands24–28, with limited research conducted in small-scale rural areas29,30.

Therefore, this study aimed to develop a multi-functional landscape health evaluation system tailored to small-scale rural areas, and reveal differentiated associations between forest structural characteristics and rural landscape functions. The “landscape health” assessment in this study primarily relies on landscape indicators describing spatial structure and configuration, focusing on structural health rather than a comprehensive evaluation of ecosystem health. This structure-oriented approach reveals existing conditions and issues in rural landscape spatial configuration and functional organization, thereby providing a basis for optimizing landscape patterns. Further, this study focused on three key functions: ecological conservation, recreational utilization, and socioeconomic production and offers new insights for the targeted optimization of rural landscapes.

Materials and methods

Study area

Guangzhou (22°26′–23°56′N, 112°57′–114°03′E) is located in south-central Guangdong Province, adjacent to the South China Sea. It experiences a subtropical monsoon climate, with annual precipitation of approximately 1,720 mm. The overall terrain exhibits a higher elevation in the northeast and a lower elevation in the southwest, characterized by hills and basins in the central region, with the Pearl River and its numerous tributaries traversing the city31,32. The “Guangdong Province Village Classification Method” serves as the official classification framework under Guangdong’s rural revitalization plan, directly corresponding to different rural development paths and spatial control requirements, with clear policy guidance and practical significance. To scientifically reflect the diversity and typical characteristics of rural landscapes in Guangzhou, this study systematically selected three village types based on the “Guangdong Province Village Classification Method"33: consolidation, suburban integration, and characteristic conservation villages. Three villages were selected for each type, resulting in a total of nine study villages (Fig. 1). The selection criteria were as follows: (1) Type coverage: ensured inclusion of major policy categories in Guangzhou’s rural development, thereby reflecting landscape characteristics under different functional orientations. (2) Geographical distribution balance: villages were selected from northern, central, southern, suburban, and remote suburban areas of Guangzhou to account for variations in topography and location. (3) Socioeconomic heterogeneity: variations in population size, industrial structure, and economic development levels among villages were considered. For example, consolidation villages remained predominantly agricultural, suburban integration villages exhibited higher degree of non-agriculturalization, while characteristic conservation villages demonstrated rapid development in rural tourism industries. (4) Landscape health relevance: different village types exhibited systematic differences in land-use structure, landscape pattern, and the intensity of human interference, which directly affected the spatial differentiation and functional performance of landscape health. This classification revealed health disparities among village types and their underlying mechanisms. The villages in the study area had certain statistical representativeness in terms of population size and leading industries, which revealed the overall characteristics and typological differentiation of rural landscape health in Guangzhou and provided a reliable sample basis for subsequent evaluation of landscape health in a larger area.

Data sources and preprocessing

This study used a DJI Mavic 3E quadcopter unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) to obtain remote sensing imagery of nine villages between April and July 2025. During this period, vegetation exhibited distinct morphological characteristics associated with both the growing season of major crops in Guangzhou and the dense canopy phase of forests, facilitating visual interpretation and classification. Notably, the classification was based on stable landscape types rather than specific crop categories or seasonal phases, thus minimizing the impact of seasonal variations on the identification of landscape types and functions. The UAV operated at a relative altitude of 150 m, with a flight speed of 15 m/s. The forward overlap was 80%, while the side overlap was 70%. All flights were conducted under clear, windless or light-wind conditions between 10:00 and 16:00.

The Mavic 3E drone camera underwent factory radiometric calibration. After data acquisition, the built-in module in DJI Terra software performed radiometric correction on the imagery to eliminate discrepancies caused by lighting variations and sensor response, ensuring consistent image tonal quality. For geometric correction, the built-in GNSS provided positioning information. By setting an 80% frontal overlap and 70% side overlap, and based on feature matching, aerial triangulation was executed in DJI Terra; thereby effectively enhancing the relative geometric accuracy among images. Subsequently, using the aerial triangulation results and the Digital Surface Model (DSM), seamless orthophotos with a spatial resolution of 2 cm were generated through digital differential rectification, color balancing, and seamline optimization.

This study utilized UAV remote sensing imagery databased on the following considerations: (1) The study area consists of small-scale rural units, requiring a spatial resolution higher than that of conventional satellite data to accurately identify landscape element boundaries; (2) UAV data offers high timeliness and acquisition flexibility, accommodating the rapidly changing characteristics of rural landscapes; (3) Provision of the static assessment of landscape structure and function, where high-resolution imagery supports detailed visual interpretation, thereby generating high-precision landscape classification maps for calculating landscape pattern indices. Although optical satellite data are widely used in macro-scale monitoring, their spatial resolution is relatively low (typically ≥ 10 m)34, making it difficult to meet the precision requirements for landscape classification and health assessment in small-scale rural areas, and thus they were not integrated into this study.

Based on the Chinese land classification standard of the “Third National Land Survey Technical Regulation” (TD/T 1055–2019)35 and landscape ecology theory, we classified the study area into five primary landscape types: cropland, grassland, water bodies, forestland, and construction land36. To ensure the accuracy of classification, the on-screen display resolution was controlled within a scale of 1:1,000, and all visual interpretation was conducted by the same individual to maintain consistency in judgment criteria. The accuracy assessment of landscape type classification was conducted between April and July 2025. To simultaneously highlight the functional attributes of rural landscapes and avoid the difficulty in interpreting landscape functions solely through structural indices37, this study therefore approaches landscape classification from three distinct functional perspectives—ecological conservation, recreational use, and production–living. By coupling structure with function through landscape classification38, the patches and corridors within the study area were delineated, establishing a three-tiered landscape functional classification system39(Table 2).

To address potential multicollinearity issues in the selection of indicators and to avoid introducing redundancy while improving interpretability, this study conducted Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) tests on the 42 indicators55. The VIF test results showed that most VIF values were significantly < 10.00. Key indicators for all three functions—including patch area ratio, patch edge density, patch diversity, corridor width ratio, corridor connectivity, and corridor fractal dimension—had significantly low VIF values (< 5.00) and played a leading role, indicating no multicollinearity among these variables. However, some indicators (such as X4, X5, Y2, Y5, and Z5) exhibited VIF values > 10, suggesting the presence of multicollinearity. Despite this, these indicators with VIF values < 10 are distinctly different in their conceptual meanings. For example, X4 reflects the degree of structural disruption in ecological conservation patches, while X5 measures the dispersion between such patches; Y11 describes the complexity of the recreational corridor network, whereas Y12 indicates the average number of connections per node within the recreational corridor network. Therefore, the indicators with potential multicollinearity were retained in this study.

This study employs the grid method as the fundamental spatial sampling unit, aiming to discretize continuous landscape spaces into computable evaluation units. The side length of the basic sampling unit for each study area was determined by calculating the square root of the median patch and corridor areas and rounding to the nearest integer. Grids were established for the study areas, and areas smaller than half the sampling unit were excluded. Since an overly small spatial sampling unit size can lead to a large number of outliers and poor spatial continuity, it does not necessarily mean that a smaller sampling unit is more conducive to analyzing landscape health56. Utilizing previously summarized calculation methods for each indicator54 and the ArcGIS 10.6.1 spatial analysis module, indicator values for ecological conservation, recreational utilization, and socioeconomic production functions of patches and corridors were calculated for each sampling unit (Table 4).

Indicator weight calculation method

The EWM determines weights by measuring the amount of information contained in the data57; however, it is relatively sensitive to extreme values in the dataset, which may lead to deviations in the calculated weights. Through sensitivity analysis, this study found58 that the weights of some indicators fluctuated significantly under the influence of extreme values. To reduce the interference of such sensitivity on the objectivity of the weighting, this study adopted a comprehensive weighting strategy that combined PCA and the EWM. PCA reflects the structural information of indicators by extracting the principal directions of data variation18,19, thereby complementing the entropy method, which focuses on information quantity. Combining the two methods can preserve the structural characteristics of the data while mitigating weight bias caused by extreme values, thereby providing a more comprehensive reflection of indicator importance59. Given the complexity of the rural landscape health evaluation system and the strong interrelationships among indicators, a comprehensive weighting approach was employed to overcome the limitations of a single weighting method, enhancing the robustness and practicality of the evaluation results. The specific steps were as follows: first, the data were normalized using Min–Max scaling; indicator weights were then calculated separately using PCA and the EWM; finally, the final comprehensive weights were derived using the multiplicative synthesis method60.

In the PCA conducted in this study, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin value exceeded 0.6, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity yielded a value less than 0.05, indicating that PCA was appropriate. By computing the correlation coefficient matrix among the indicators, principal components (PCs) with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, and their variance contribution rates were used as the basis for weighting. PCs with a cumulative variance contribution rate of ≥ 85% were retained to ensure that the selected components adequately represented the information structure of the original indicators. Coefficients with absolute values greater than 0.6 in the loading matrix were selected, where a larger absolute loading indicated that the corresponding indicator was more representative of that PC. The variance contribution rate of each PC was then used to represent the PCA-based weight.

The multiplicative synthesis method is suitable for complex data analysis involving numerous and highly correlated indicators. It effectively eliminates redundant information among indicators while precisely capturing the discrete characteristics of the data, thereby constructing a statistically robust weighting system that aligns with the objective patterns in the data. This approach effectively avoids biases associated with single-method weighting and significantly enhances the scientific rigor and robustness of weight determination61. Health assessment and spatial analysis

Landscape health scores for each functional dimension within the sampling units were determined by multiplying standardized indicator values with their respective comprehensive weights and summing the resulting products. Finally, the ecological conservation, recreational utilization, socioeconomic production, and comprehensive functional landscape health values in each sampling unit were normalized. Combined with the existing research results and the basic situation of the study area, the equal spacing method62,63 was used to divide the landscape health into five grades: very poor (0–0.2], poor (0.2–0.4], fair (0.4–0.6], good (0.6–0.8], and very good (0.8–1].

Kriging is a geostatistical interpolation method based on spatial autocorrelation. It assumes that the landscape health indicator has spatial stationarity and a certain spatial dependence structure. It can use the spatial structure information of known points to make an unbiased estimation of the unsampled area and generate the prediction error variance map to reflect the uncertainty of the interpolation results64. We applied Kriging interpolation within ArcGIS to spatially visualize the assessment results. We further calculated forest-to-non-forest ratios (both area-based and numerical) within each sampling unit to analyze relationships between forest composition and landscape health across various functional dimensions and health levels.

Method framework of this study

This study takes nine typical villages in Guangzhou as the research area. Based on high-resolution remote sensing imagery acquired by the DJI Mavic 3E drone, orthophoto maps were generated after radiometric and geometric corrections. Through visual interpretation, the landscape was classified into five primary types and eighteen secondary types. Combined with a functional perspective, the landscape was further categorized into three functional systems—ecological conservation, recreational utilization, and socioeconomic production—and patch and corridor landscape units were identified accordingly. On this basis, a landscape health evaluation system comprising 42 indicators was constructed. Weights were comprehensively determined using PCA and EWM. Sampling units were generated using a grid-based approach to calculate landscape health values and grades for each function. Finally, spatial visualization of landscape health was achieved through Kriging interpolation. The research systematically analyzes three aspects: the spatial distribution characteristics of landscape health, health disparities among different village types, and the relationships between landscape health and forest-to-non-forest structural factors, aiming to reveal the multidimensional features of rural landscape health and their influencing factors (Fig. 2).

Results

Spatial patterns of landscape health

As illustrated in Fig. 3, all villages exhibited poor performance in the ecological conservation function. In all nine villages, the areas rated as “very poor” and “poor” exceeded 50.83%, with Cangshuyuan Village (79.42%), Xihe Village (90.37%), and Nanping Village (90.32%). These low-performing areas were predominantly distributed across paddy fields, irrigated land, facility agriculture, rural residential areas, and portions of water bodies. Areas rated as “good” and “very good” averaged less than 10% and were sparsely distributed, primarily concentrated in village core areas. Kengbei Village was the only village to exhibit “good” and “very good” ratings (48.52%), primarily situated along ecological corridors, within smaller ecological patches, and at the boundaries of complex-shaped ecological patches. This area includes various landscape types, such as grassland, forestland, roads, and portions of water bodies.

Spatial distribution map of landscape health for ecological conservation function.

As observed in Fig. 4, the recreation function exhibited similarly poor performance. Areas rated as “very poor” or “poor” accounted for over 68.62% across all villages, with Cangshuyuan (95.70%), Liantang (93.77%), and Kengbei (93.17%). These areas were distributed across most cropland, rural residential areas, industrial land, and portions of water bodies and forestland. Areas rated as “good” and “very good” averaged below 12%, with only Kengbei showing “good” and “very good” ratings at 46.77% (this village is primarily located in core areas and at landscape nodes). The predominant landscape types included grassland, water bodies, sports facilities, parks, special-use land, service areas, roads, and limited forestland. Notably, sampling units at the interfaces between recreational corridors and recreational patches consistently demonstrated higher health levels than those of interfaces between other corridor types and recreational patches.

Spatial distribution map of landscape health for recreational utilization function.

The socioeconomic production function exhibited a relatively balanced but still suboptimal performance (Fig. 5). Areas rated as “very poor” and “poor” exceeded 23.96% across villages, with 72.51% in Kengbei and 67.21% in Langtou. These areas were distributed across extensive irrigated land, orchards, facility agriculture, urban residential areas, and portions of grassland, water bodies, forestland, and service areas. Areas rated as “good” and “very good” averaged below 20%, with only Caotang Village displaying “good” and “very good” ratings at 36.88%. The predominant landscape types included edges of paddy fields, irrigated land, dry land, orchards, facility agriculture, and portions of water bodies, industrial land, rural residential areas, and roads.

spatial distribution map of landscape health for socioeconomic production function.

As illustrated in Fig. 6, comprehensive functional landscape health demonstrated consistently poor performance. Areas rated as “very poor” or “poor” surpassed 54.09% across all villages, with 76.43% in Xihe, 75.21% in Liantang, and 74.70% in Langtou. These areas were distributed across extensive irrigated land, orchards, facility agriculture, rural residential areas, forestland, and water bodies. Areas rated as “good” and “very good” averaged below 8%, primarily located at village peripheries. Only Caotang achieved a “good” and “very good” rating of 20.10%, which is concentrated in limited areas of paddy fields, irrigated land, grassland, water bodies, forestland, rural residential areas, parks, and roads. Notably, sampling units exhibiting higher comprehensive functional ratings consistently overlapped with interfaces between various functional patch and corridor types, with landscape health levels positively correlating with internal functional diversity.

Variations across village types

The study employed one-way ANOVA to compare differences in the area proportions of landscape health among consolidation, suburban integration, and characteristic conservation, and comprehensive functions among the three village types. The results of the homogeneity of variance test met the prerequisite assumptions for ANOVA.

The average area ratio of the “very poor” and “poor” grades across the three village types ranged from 59.30% to 86.70%, with a difference of 27.40% between the highest and lowest values (Fig. 7(a)). The average area ratio for the “very good” and “good” grades showed a difference of 18.70% between the highest and lowest values, with suburban integration villages having the highest average area ratio at 20.81%. This village type also showed significant variation in the area proportion of the “fair” grade among villages, with a difference of 35.38% between the highest (Caotang Village) and the lowest (Kengbei Village).

Discussion

From the perspective of the spatial distribution characteristics of landscape health, the landscape health levels of the nine villages in the study area, across ecological conservation, recreational utilization, socioeconomic production, and comprehensive functions are all at relatively low levels. This reflects the reality of rural ecological space being compressed and functionally imbalanced during rapid urbanization, which is consistent with existing research and observations. This finding aligns with conclusions from previous studies on cities or protected areas26, jointly revealing the issue of continuous compression of ecological space under rapid urbanization. However, the present study demonstrated that the degradation of landscape functions in rural settings was more complex, manifested not only in area reduction but also in structural fragmentation and functional segregation. This phenomenon may be related to the more complex functions and more diverse human disturbances inherent to rural areas. Meanwhile, the consistently low levels of landscape health across all nine villages may also be influenced by the equal interval method, which could amplify minor differences into distinct categories in densely distributed data intervals, leading to excessive segmentation, while creating empty classes with no practical significance in sparsely distributed intervals, thereby wasting classification resources. Ultimately, by failing to capture the inherent data structure and natural clustering points, this method may result in classification outcomes that lack practical interpretability and application efficiency. Nonetheless, since we need to perform parallel classification of assessment results across multiple dimensions, the equal interval method helps maintain consistency in classification thresholds, thereby facilitating subsequent quantitative analysis65. We found that landscape units with higher health grades were often distributed at junctions between multifunctional patches and corridors. This indicated that small-sized, complex-shaped ecological patches and transitional zones between different functional types contributed positively to landscape health. This result may be related to the diversity and edge density of patches and corridors. The greater the variety of landscape function types within a sampling unit, the higher its health grade, suggesting that multifunctional composite utilization may be an important approach to improving landscape health. This finding verified the positive role of edge effects in rural landscapes66 and further revealed that edge areas in rural landscapes were not only ecologically sensitive zones but also potential spaces for multifunctional synergy. This is likely because patch edges in rural landscapes are often located in transitional zones between multiple functional types, facilitating coordination and balance among them—an aspect that has not been fully captured in previous research focused primarily on natural ecosystems. Furthermore, similar to findings in the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River67 and in southern Italy68, this study also identified ecological function degradation during rapid urbanization, which may be related to the important role of the structural complexity of patch edges in maintaining ecological processes in rural landscapes, further revealing the role of landscape structural complexity in sustaining landscape health. Concurrently, long-term studies in the Yangtze River Delta suggest that future research could consider incorporating multi-year remote-sensing imagery for temporal analysis, while parameter selections such as naturalness and visual quality in the southern Italy study provide new ideas for further research on rural landscape health.

This study also found that significant differences in the area proportion of the “very poor” and “poor” grades among different village types existed only for the ecological conservation function. No significant differences were observed among the three village types for recreational utilization, socioeconomic production, or the comprehensive function. This finding echoes the landscape “structure–function” adaptability theory69, making it difficult to identify regular patterns from a macroscopic classification perspective. One possible explanation is that ecological conservation function is more directly constrained by rigid policies such as land-use control and ecological protection redlines. In contrast, the development of recreational utilization and socioeconomic production functions relies more on the specific resource endowments, infrastructure, locational conditions, and market environment of each village—factors that are unevenly distributed across villages of different policy types. Although landscape health grades for most functions did not show significant differences between village types, significant variations in landscape health grades existed within the same village type, particularly among suburban integration villages, across all functions. This finding is consistent with existing research on the spillover effects of suburban ecological services and simultaneously reveals the spatial complexity of the protection–development contradiction in suburban integration villages70. However, unlike studies focusing on macro-scale ecological network construction, this research emphasizes identifying the details of spatial conflicts among different functions at individual village level. This phenomenon may stem from dynamic landscape patterns and spatial competition among different functional demands within suburban integration villages. Driven by urban expansion, infrastructure construction, and agricultural transformation, landscape types within suburban integration villages vary significantly, ultimately manifesting as substantial internal variations in landscape health grades. Therefore, in future practices aimed at enhancing rural landscape health, beyond village typology, greater emphasis should be placed on diagnosing the specific resource conditions, spatial patterns, and management models of each village to implement precise landscape function improvement measures.

The present study revealed that under ecological conservation and comprehensive functions, landscape health grade showed a positive correlation trend with the forest-to-non-forest landscape numerical ratio, whereas no significant correlation was observed with the area ratio. This finding aligns with those from studies on suburban forest parks, which suggest that simply increasing forest coverage does not necessarily improve landscape health grade. Planning layouts dominated by large-area, single landscape types can easily lead to landscape structure homogenization, which is detrimental to achieving landscape health in parks51–53. The present study further demonstrated that when the health grade reached “very good,” the forest-to-non-forest landscape numerical ratio exhibited a relatively wide range. This suggested that forest distribution combined moderate coverage with high integrity, which was conducive to the maintaining stable landscape health. A possible explanation is that, within a certain range, a higher numerical ratio indicates forest patches and non-forest patches are interwoven more frequently in space, enhancing edge effects. This may facilitate species migration and energy flow, thereby improving the overall ecological integrity and landscape resilience. However, unlike protected areas or forest parks, ecological functions in rural landscapes are often mosaic with production and living spaces. Therefore, the specific threshold for forest coverage may differ.

This study employed multifunctional landscape classification and grid-based evaluation to reveal the spatial heterogeneity of internal rural landscape structures and their relationship with internal factors. It offers finer spatial analytical resolution compared with previous studies that emphasized “PSR” relationships. However, this study still has certain limitations. First, urban no-fly zone restrictions limited the selection of study cases to nine villages across four districts and counties in Guangzhou. This limited sample size restricted the generalizability of the conclusions to some extent and was insufficient to fully represent the diversity of rural landscape patterns in Guangzhou. Further verification is necessary to establish the broader applicability of the findings to rural construction practice. Future research should broaden its scope by selecting a more representative sample of villages across Guangzhou. Second, this study was based on 2025 cross-sectional data. Data from a single year failed to capture the spatiotemporal dynamics of landscape health and weakened temporal reliability, making it difficult to explore its evolutionary patterns in depth. Subsequent studies could introduce multi‑year remote sensing imagery data for tracking and comparative analysis, thereby establishing a scientific basis for intervening in and managing rural landscape health evolution and promoting sustained landscape health improvement71. Additionally, retaining certain indicators with excessively high VIF values (exceeding 200) may affect model stability and weighting sensitivity, thereby limiting the robustness of evaluation results. Future research could further refine the indicator system by employing variable screening, dimensionality reduction, or structural equation modeling, thereby enhancing the reliability and explanatory power of the evaluation framework. Finally, this study employed a single grid scale as the basic sampling unit and did not investigate the potential impacts of different grid scales on rural landscape health assessment results and spatial variations. Future research could conduct multi-scale analysis of rural landscape health to address the effects of modifiable areal unit problem and the explanatory power of driving factors, thereby yielding more robust and refined findings.

Through the establishment of an assessment indicator system for rural landscape health, this study conducts an exploratory investigation using nine villages in Guangzhou City as case studies, revealed the spatial differentiation characteristics, variations in landscape health, and the relationships between landscape health grades and internal forest characteristics within rural areas of Guangzhou. The findings provide a scientific basis for targeted rural landscape enhancement and promote their sustainable development. The specific findings are as follows:

A certain degree of variation existed in the spatial distribution of landscape health. For the villages in the study area, under ecological conservation, recreational utilization, socioeconomic production, and comprehensive functions, areas classified as “very poor” and “poor” accounted for a relatively large proportion of the landscape health regions, exceeding 50% in seven villages. The proportion of areas with “very good” and “good” landscape health grades was relatively low, and their spatial distribution was relatively concentrated. These areas were associated with village core zones, patch and corridor boundaries, and specific landscape types.

Comprehensive optimization strategies centered on “differentiated zoning, structural optimization, and functional coordination” should be implemented to achieve the targeted enhancement of Guangzhou’s rural landscape health. In terms of classified management and control, strict adherence to the village classification system of Guangdong Province should be followed to implement differentiated controls based on village types: for consolidation villages, priority should be given to rehabilitating areas with weak ecological conservation functions, supplementing recreational utilization function, promoting the intensification of production and living spaces, and enhancing landscape connectivity—such as constructing native tree-shrub-grass composite vegetation buffer belts or ecological ditches to improve edge structural complexity, while strengthening soil and water conservation and habitat functions, thereby achieving coordinated development between ecological conservation and socioeconomic production functions, and incorporating small recreational sites within villages or at main road nodes to address gaps in recreational utilization functions; for suburban integration villages, a balanced approach to protection and development should be adopted, with differentiated zoning controls to reduce disparities in the health of functional landscapes among villages and promote synergistic improvement in village quality—for instance, advancing the “multifunctional mosaic” model for villages72, arranging composite spaces, and enhancing landscape diversity through coordinated spatial utilization to achieve organic integration of ecological conservation, recreational utilization use, and socioeconomic production functions; for characteristic conservation villages, emphasis should be placed on the holistic protection of natural and cultural landscapes, moderate development of leisure and recreation, infrastructure upgrades, and maintaining landscape resilience to foster synergistic functional development—such as leveraging historical, cultural, and ecological elements including stream systems and Fengshui forests, exploring the cultural DNA of villages, and constructing “cultural-ecological” composite corridors to link key nodes such as ancestral halls, ancient wells, and plazas, thereby enhancing landscape connectivity and narrative structure.In terms of structural optimization, guided by policies such as the “Beautiful Guangdong” Ecological Construction Initiative and the “Hundred Counties, Thousand Towns, Ten Thousand Villages” High-Quality Development Project, rural landscape pattern should be optimized by integrating Guangzhou’s Lingnan water-town characteristics with its rapid urbanization context. Given Guangzhou’s rural features of “dense water networks and scattered construction land,” priority should be given to constructing ecological networks by organically linking Blueways, Greenways, and natural river channels to improve rural ecological connectivity. Composite vegetation buffer zones should be established at the boundaries between socioeconomic production/living land and ecological conservation land to enhance patch interactions and landscape resilience. Attention should also be paid to shaping forest landscape diversity, avoiding simplification and homogenization, and precisely regulating the area ratio and numerical ratio between forest and non-forest landscapes according to different functional needs. For functional coordination, the integration of production–living–ecological spaces should be strengthened. This involves strict protection of ecological land from excessive encroachment by production spaces while ensuring the maintenance of basic production functions. In addition, ecological conservation and rural recreation functions should be actively expanded, alongside the promotion of composite land use.
