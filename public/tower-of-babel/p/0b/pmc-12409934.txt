Association between dietary inflammatory index, empirical dietary inflammatory patterns, dietary and lifestyle inflammation scores, and polycystic ovary syndrome: a case-control study
Authors: Fatemeh Vaziri Esfarjani, Parvin Dorfeshan, Anahita Mansouri, Farnush Bakhshimoghaddam, Roshan Nikbakht, Shahla Vaziri Esfarjani, Hadis Varaee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common metabolic and endocrine disorder affecting women of reproductive age. Chronic low-grade inflammation is implicated in its pathogenesis, with diet and lifestyle playing pivotal roles. The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII), Empirical Dietary Inflammatory Pattern (EDIP), and Dietary and Lifestyle Inflammation Scores (DLIS) are distinct indices developed to assess systemic inflammation. The DII focuses on nutrient composition, EDIP derives from data-driven food patterns, and DLIS integrates both dietary and lifestyle factors. While these inflammatory indices have been linked to metabolic diseases, their associations with PCOS remain unexplored. This study aimed to evaluate these associations.MethodsThis case-control study included 100 women with newly diagnosed PCOS (confirmed by Rotterdam criteria, within 3 months) and 100 age-matched (± 2 years) controls, recruited from the Imam Khomeini Hospital Infertility Centre in Ahvaz, Iran. Controls were selected from women visiting the same center for routine fertility check-ups, excluding those with PCOS symptoms or hormonal disorders. Dietary intake was assessed using a validated 147-item Food Frequency Questionnaire. Inflammatory scores were calculated, and logistic regression was used to analyze their associations with PCOS.ResultsThe results showed that the odds of PCOS were higher in the multivariable model across tertiles of DLIS (OR: 3.70; 95%CI: 1.05–5.70, P-trend = 0.002) and DII (OR: 2.82; 95%CI: 1.10–5.60, P-trend = 0.03) after controlling for age, BMI, energy intake (except for DII), physical activity, educational level, marital status, parity, employment status, household income, history of diabetes mellitus, and hypothyroidism. However, no statistical association was found between EDIP and the likelihood of PCOS.ConclusionPro-inflammatory diets (higher DII) and combined diet-lifestyle patterns (higher DLIS) were associated with odds of PCOS, highlighting the importance of anti-inflammatory interventions. However, the case-control design limits causal interpretation, and future cohort studies are needed to confirm temporality.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is among the most common endocrine disorders affecting women of reproductive age, with a global prevalence of 8–13% [1, 2]. According to the Rotterdam criteria, PCOS diagnosis requires at least two of the following: oligo- or anovulation, clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, or polycystic ovarian morphology [3]. In Iran, the estimated prevalence of PCOS reaches 19.5% [4]. Beyond its reproductive manifestations, PCOS is associated with metabolic complications, including insulin resistance, obesity, and increased risk of type 2 diabetes, along with significant psychological impacts [2]. Risk factors that predispose women to PCOS include an unhealthy lifestyle, genetic predisposition, neuroendocrine changes, environmental influences, poor dietary habits, and obesity [5].

The pathophysiology of PCOS involves chronic low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress. Elevated circulating androgens and insulin resistance create a pro-inflammatory state characterized by increased reactive oxygen species and reduced antioxidant capacity [6, 7]. This imbalance contributes to cellular damage and exacerbates PCOS symptoms through multiple pathways, including nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) activation and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines [8].

A recent study revealed that plant-based dietary patterns are associated with lower levels of oxidative stress and inflammation, suggesting they may serve as an effective strategy for preventing chronic diseases [9]. Foods high in anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, which help decrease fat tissue and improve gut bacteria balance, have been shown to enhance anti-inflammatory effects in individuals with metabolic disorders [10]. Anti-inflammatory dietary patterns, such as the Mediterranean diet, which is rich in ω−3 polyunsaturated fatty acids, antioxidants, and fiber, can help alleviate PCOS symptoms by reducing oxidative stress and inflammatory markers [11]. In contrast, Western-style dietary patterns with inflammatory properties, characterized by high intakes of refined grains, red and processed meats, sweets, high-sugar drinks, high-fat dairy products, and fried foods, are associated with higher levels of oxidative stress and inflammation [9, 12].

Among various dietary assessment tools, the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) and Empirical Dietary Inflammatory Pattern (EDIP) have been widely studied for their ability to quantify the inflammatory potential of diets [13, 14]. The DII evaluates the inflammatory effects of individual nutrients and food components, while EDIP derives inflammatory patterns from food groups [13, 14]. More recently, the Dietary and Lifestyle Inflammation Score (DLIS) has emerged as a comprehensive tool that combines both dietary inflammation score (DIS) and lifestyle inflammatory score (LIS) factors [15]. The DIS evaluates the inflammatory potential of nutritional components, whereas the LIS incorporates lifestyle factors, including physical activity, alcohol intake, body mass index (BMI), and smoking status [15]. The DLIS integrates both DIS and LIS to provide a holistic measure of inflammatory exposure [16].

Existing studies have primarily focused on isolated nutrients or food groups in PCOS [17, 18], with limited attention to integrated indices that quantify systemic inflammatory potential. For instance, Zirak Sharkesh et al. [17] demonstrated an association between DII and PCOS risk in Iran, while Wang et al. [18] investigated the link between pro-inflammatory dietary patterns and PCOS in a Chinese cohort. However, these studies did not evaluate EDIP or DLIS, nor did they compare the discriminatory power of multiple indices in the same population. In addition, the DLIS, which combines dietary and lifestyle factors, has shown promise in predicting diabetes and cardiovascular risk [19–21] but has never been investigated in PCOS.

To our knowledge, no prior studies have explored the association between inflammatory indices, including DII, EDIP, and DLIS, and the odds of developing PCOS in non-Western populations. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the associations between the DII, EDIP, and DLIS and the likelihood of PCOS among women participants referred to the Imam Khomeini Hospital Infertility Centre in Ahvaz, Iran.

Methods

Study design and participants

This age-matched case-control study investigated the relationship between dietary inflammatory indices (DII, EDIP, DLIS) and PCOS among women referred to the Imam Khomeini Hospital Infertility Centre in Ahvaz, Iran. Participant recruitment took place from October 2023 to December 2023. We used a consecutive sampling approach to recruit participants. Exclusion criteria for both groups included use of hormonal medications within 3 months (e.g., contraceptives, androgen-affecting drugs), history of endocrine disorders (type 1 diabetes, Cushing’s syndrome, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, hyperprolactinemia), food allergies, special diets, malignancies (self-reported history of any cancer diagnosis), autoimmune diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, lupus), chronic inflammatory conditions (e.g., IBD, chronic infections), acute infections within the past 3 months, or misreporting of energy intake (< 500 or > 3500 kcal/day). Of the 220 women initially invited, 12 women (5.5%) refused to participate due to personal reasons (e.g., time constraints, lack of interest), and eight women (3.6%) were excluded for implausible energy intake (< 500 or > 3500 kcal/day) (three women) and use of hormonal medications (five women). Therefore, the final sample consisted of 200 participants (100 cases/100 controls). Cases were aged 18–45 years newly diagnosed with PCOS within 3 months according to the Rotterdam criteria, requiring at least two of the following oligo-/anovulation, clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, or polycystic ovaries on ultrasound (≥ 12 follicles per ovary [2–9 mm diameter] or ovarian volume > 10 cm³) [22]. Controls were age-matched (± 2 years) women without PCOS, selected from the same center during routine fertility check-ups. The parity was recorded for all participants and included as a covariate. Controls were rigorously screened to exclude not only PCOS but also hypertension and diabetes (self-reported or diagnosed via medical records), and other ovulatory disorders or subfertility conditions (e.g., luteal phase deficiency, tubal factors, or male-factor infertility) via medical record review. This ensured the control group represented women with confirmed normal fertility status. All diagnoses were made under the supervision of a gynecologist-obstetrician, in accordance with the Rotterdam criteria.

Trained research staff, blinded to participants’ case/control status, conducted face-to-face interviews and filled all questionnaires, including anthropometric measurements, physical activity, and dietary intake, to minimize bias.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined a priori using G*Power 3.1, estimating that 100 women per group would provide 80% power (α = 0.05, two-tailed) to detect standardized differences of ≥ 0.4 in inflammatory indices, based on effect sizes from prior PCOS-diet studies [17, 18]. This accounts for a potential 10% attrition while maintaining adequate power for multivariable analyses.

Assessment of demographic characteristics

A self-administered questionnaire collected information such as age, educational level, marital status, parity, employment status, and household income. The history of diabetes mellitus and hypothyroidism was self-reported by participants during baseline interviews and verified through medical records when available.

Anthropometric measurements

Standardized methods and a trained investigator were used to measure all anthropometric characteristics. Height (cm) was measured with the women standing upright and barefoot using an unstretched, wall-mounted tape measure (accuracy to within 0.1 cm). Body weight (kg) to the nearest 0.1 kg on a digital scale, standing barefoot and wearing minimal clothing. The weight (kg) was then divided by the height (m²) to calculate the body mass index (BMI) (kg/m²).

Physical activity assessment

Physical activity levels were assessed using the validated International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ-SF) [23]. The reliability and validity of the IPAQ-SF have been confirmed in Iranian adults, supporting its use in this population [24]. Trained research staff administered the questionnaire through face-to-face interviews to ensure accurate data collection and minimize reporting bias. Interviewers underwent standardized training to ensure consistency in administering the questionnaire, including instructions on how to probe for clarification and record responses uniformly. The IPAQ-SF assesses the frequency (in days per week) and duration (in minutes per day) of three activity types: walking, moderate-intensity activities, and vigorous-intensity activities. Metabolic equivalent of task (MET) values were assigned to each activity type (3.3 METs for walking, 4.0 METs for moderate-intensity, and 8.0 METs for vigorous-intensity activities). Total physical activity was calculated as MET-hours/week (MET-h/week) by summing the products of duration, frequency, and MET values for each activity, then dividing by 60 to convert MET-hours/week for interpretability. This approach aligns with established protocols for quantifying physical activity in epidemiological studies [23].

Dietary intake assessment

Dietary intake was assessed using a validated 147-item semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) [25]. Trained interviewers administered the FFQ in face-to-face sessions, asking participants to report their usual dietary intake over the past 12 months to capture long-term dietary patterns. To improve accuracy in portion size estimation, interviewers utilized standard household measures (e.g., cups, spoons) and a validated photographic atlas of common Iranian foods [25]. Participants reported consumption frequencies for each food item using a 9-point scale ranging from “never or less than once a month” to “6 or more times per day.” Frequency responses were converted to daily intake (grams per day) using standardized portion sizes, and macro- and micro-nutrient intakes were calculated using Nutritionist IV software. The FFQ’s validity and reliability were previously confirmed in the Iranian population through comparison with 24-hour dietary recalls and biochemical markers [25].

Dietary inflammatory index (DII) calculation

The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) was calculated using the method developed by Shivappa et al. [13]. This index is based on 45 specific foods and nutrients linked to either pro-inflammatory biomarkers (such as interleukin-1β, interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha, or C-reactive protein) or anti-inflammatory biomarkers (such as IL-4 and IL-10). Each food item was assigned an inflammatory potential score: +1 for increasing pro-inflammatory or decreasing anti-inflammatory biomarkers, −1 for decreasing pro-inflammatory or increasing anti-inflammatory biomarkers, or 0 for not affecting these biomarkers. Shivappa et al. established global means and standard deviations for each of the 45 food parameters using data from 11 countries. In this study, the DII was calculated based on 30 food parameters instead of 45, as some foods are not commonly consumed in Iran, and specific nutrient data (e.g., polyphenols) were unavailable in the local nutrient database. The dietary parameters included in this study were as follows: pro-inflammatory parameters including energy, protein, carbohydrates, fat, cholesterol, trans-fat, saturated fat, iron, and vitamin B12, and anti-inflammatory parameters including dietary fiber, monounsaturated fatty acids, vitamin A, vitamin B6, vitamin C, polyunsaturated fatty acids, thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, folic acid, vitamin E, vitamin D, beta-carotene, onion, garlic, caffeine, tea, zinc, magnesium, and selenium.

To calculate the DII score for each participant, the amount of each food parameter consumed was subtracted from the global mean and divided by the international standard deviation to derive a z-score. These global means and standard deviations were obtained from the study by Shivappa et al. The z-scores were then converted to centered percentile scores and multiplied by the corresponding food parameter’s effect score from Shivappa et al.‘s research. Finally, the overall DII score for each participant was determined by summing the scores of all individual food parameters. A higher (more positive) DII score indicates a more pro-inflammatory diet, while a lower (more negative) score suggests a less inflammatory diet.

Empirical dietary inflammatory pattern (EDIP) calculation

The Empirical Dietary Inflammatory Pattern (EDIP) was calculated using 18 food parameters as outlined by Tabung et al. [14]. However, due to religious and cultural practices in Iran, wine and beer are not commonly consumed or are likely under-reported. Additionally, high-energy and low-energy drinks were combined into a single category in the FFQ. As a result, 15 food parameters were used to calculate the EDIP score, divided into two groups based on their inflammatory potential:

Anti-inflammatory group: green leafy vegetables, dark yellow vegetables, fruit juice, snacks, pizza, coffee, and tea.Pro-inflammatory group: vegetables, tomatoes, other refined grains, red meats, processed meats, organ meats, other fish, low- and high-energy drinks.

The mean daily intake of each food group was weighted using regression coefficients provided by Tabung et al. To simplify interpretation and reduce the magnitude of the score, the weighted food group intakes were summed and then divided by 1,000 to derive the final EDIP score. Higher (more positive) EDIP scores indicate a more pro-inflammatory diet, while lower (more negative) scores reflect a more anti-inflammatory diet.

Dietary and lifestyle inflammatory scores (DLIS) calculation

The DIS and LIS have recently been defined in the study by Byrd et al. [15]. The DIS was originally calculated using 19 components; however, due to a lack of data on supplement intakes, we calculated the overall score using 18 food groups. These included legumes, leafy greens and cruciferous vegetables, refined grains and starchy vegetables, other vegetables, tomatoes, deep yellow or orange vegetables and fruits, apples and berries, different fruits and real fruit juices, red and organ meats, processed meats, poultry, fish, high-fat dairy products, nuts, low-fat dairy products, other fats, tea, and added sugars. Each of the food groups was standardized, and then the values were added up. The LIS score was calculated using physical activity, BMI, smoking status, and alcohol intake. We have not included alcohol consumption in the calculation of the LIS, as the Iranian population does not report it due to religious and legal restrictions. The LIS was calculated by first creating a dummy variable for each component, multiplying the components by the proposed regression coefficients, and then summing all the weighted values. Finally, DLIS was created by combining LIS and DIS. The DLIS’s inclusion of lifestyle factors (e.g., BMI, physical activity) captures interactions not reflected in purely dietary indices, offering a more holistic measure of inflammatory exposure.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS version 15.0; SPSS, Chicago, IL). The normality of the variables was assessed using histogram plots and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables were compared between PCOS and control groups using independent samples t-tests (for normally distributed data) or Mann-Whitney U tests (for non-normal distributions, assessed via Shapiro-Wilk tests). Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests for expected cell counts ≥ 5 and Fisher’s exact tests for expected cell counts < 5. For analysis of inflammatory indices (DII, EDIP, DLIS), participants were categorized into tertiles (T1-T3), with T1 representing the lowest (most anti-inflammatory) and T3 the highest (most pro-inflammatory) tertile of each index. Baseline characteristics of the study population, including mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies (%) for categorical variables, were compared across tertiles of the three inflammation scores (DII, EDIP, and DLIS). Logistic regression analysis was employed to estimate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for PCOS across tertiles of the DII, EDIP, and DLIS scores. Separate logistic regression models were run for each inflammatory index (DII, EDIP, DLIS) to avoid multicollinearity. Two models were constructed: an unadjusted model and a multivariable-adjusted model, which included covariates for age, BMI (except for DLIS, because BMI is a component of DLIS), energy intake (except for DII, because energy is a component of DII), physical activity (except for DLIS, because physical activity is a component of DLIS), history of diabetes mellitus and hypothyroidism, education level, marital status, parity, employment status, and household income. Model fit was assessed using Hosmer-Lemeshow tests (all P > 0.05), and multicollinearity between covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with all VIFs < 2.0 indicating no significant collinearity. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Table 1 presents the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants. Women with PCOS were significantly younger (30.36 ± 5.53 vs. 32.24 ± 5.06 years; P = 0.01), had higher body weight (77.60 ± 14.70 vs. 67.99 ± 13.44 kg; P < 0.001), and higher BMI (29.57 ± 5.23 vs. 25.65 ± 4.84 kg/m²; P < 0.001) compared to controls. Additionally, PCOS patients exhibited a higher prevalence of diabetes (8.0% vs. 0.0%; P = 0.003) and lower physical activity levels (33.90 ± 5.30 vs. 38.90 ± 6.34 MET-hours/week; P < 0.001). Notably, as shown in Fig. 1, the mean DII (− 3.20 ± 1.06 vs. −3.78 ± 1.06; P < 0.001) and DLIS (2.07 ± 2.30 vs. 0.12 ± 2.86; P < 0.001) were significantly higher in the PCOS group, suggesting a stronger pro-inflammatory dietary and lifestyle profile among these women. No significant differences were observed for other variables, including educational level, marital status, employment status, or household income.

Characteristics across inflammatory score tertiles

Table 2 presents participant characteristics stratified by extreme tertiles (T1 vs. T3) of each inflammatory index to highlight differences between the most anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory dietary patterns. Women in the highest tertile of DII were younger (P < 0.05) and less educated (P < 0.05) compared to those in the lowest tertile. Similarly, participants in the highest tertile of EDIP were more likely to be single (P < 0.05) and self-employed (P < 0.05), while those in the highest DLIS tertile had higher body weight (P < 0.05) and BMI (P < 0.05). These findings underscore the association between pro-inflammatory diets and adverse metabolic profiles, which may contribute to the pathogenesis of PCOS.

Association between dietary inflammatory scores and odds of PCOS

Logistic regression models were used to evaluate the relationship between inflammatory indices and PCOS (Table 3 and Fig. 2). In the crude model, women in the highest tertile (T3) of the DII had 2.87-fold higher odds of PCOS (95% CI: 1.44–5.80; P-trend = 0.003) compared to the lowest tertile (T1). After multivariable adjustment for age, BMI, physical activity, educational level, marriage status, parity, employment status, household income, history of diabetes mellitus, and hypothyroidism, this association remained significant (OR: 2.82; 95% CI: 1.10–5.60; P-trend = 0.03).

Discussion

Prior studies have investigated the relationship between diet and inflammation in PCOS [17, 18, 26]. However, to our knowledge, this is the first study to directly compare three distinct inflammatory indices (DII, EDIP, DLIS) in PCOS, revealing that DLIS, integrating both diet and lifestyle, exhibits the strongest association. This underscores the limitations of prior single-index approaches and highlights the synergistic role of pro-inflammatory lifestyles (e.g., physical inactivity, high BMI) in amplifying dietary risks. For instance, Scarfò et al. [27] demonstrated that sedentary behavior exacerbates metabolic inflammation in PCOS, supporting our finding that DLIS (OR: 3.70) outperformed DII (OR: 2.82) in risk prediction. This synergy suggests that clinical strategies targeting PCOS should concurrently address diet and modifiable lifestyle factors. However, the wide confidence intervals for some associations may reflect limited sample size or heterogeneity in dietary/lifestyle exposures. While our study was powered to detect moderate effects (OR ≥ 2.0), smaller effect sizes or unmeasured confounders (e.g., genetic factors) may have contributed to variability. Future larger cohorts should validate these estimates.

Our findings reveal essential distinctions between inflammatory indices in their association with PCOS. While the crude model for EDIP showed a borderline significant association (P = 0.04), this association attenuated after adjustment for confounders (P = 0.13). This null-adjusted finding does not imply that dietary patterns are unimportant in the pathogenesis of PCOS. It suggests that its initial signal may reflect shared variance with age, BMI (a key metabolic factor), lifestyle (including physical activity), socioeconomic factors (such as educational level and household income), and comorbidities (diabetes and hypothyroidism), rather than independent effects. In addition, post-hoc power analysis confirmed 82% power to detect EDIP’s observed effect (data not shown), suggesting that the null association was not due to insufficient power but instead to the index’s attenuated discriminative capacity in our adapted form (e.g., alcohol exclusion). This contrasts with DII and DLIS, which retained significance after adjustment, likely because their nutrient-based (DII) and combined diet-lifestyle (DLIS) approaches better capture PCOS-relevant inflammation in our population.

While our results align with those of studies conducted by Sharkesh et al. [26] and Zirak Sharkesh et al. [17] in demonstrating associations between DII/EDIP and PCOS, the magnitude of these associations differs notably. For instance, Sharkesh et al. [26] reported a stronger association between EDIP-PCOS in their Iranian study compared to our findings. Additionally, the null association between EDIP and PCOS in our study contrasts with that observed in Western cohorts, where EDIP performs robustly [14]. This discrepancy may stem from methodological variations; their EDIP calculation included all 18 original food groups, whereas ours excluded alcohol and merged beverage categories due to cultural dietary practices in Iran. Such adaptations might dilute the inflammatory signal captured by EDIP, particularly for pro-inflammatory components, such as alcohol. Additionally, the dietary composition of Iranian diets, which typically emphasizes legumes and herbs with anti-inflammatory properties, is not fully captured by EDIP’s Western-centric food groupings. Similarly, Wang et al. [18] observed a stronger association between DII and the odds of PCOS in a Chinese population than our study. This could reflect differences in dietary patterns, such as traditional Chinese diets, which are higher in refined carbohydrates and lower in anti-inflammatory foods (e.g., olive oil, nuts) compared to Iranian diets. Taken together, these observations highlight that the performance of dietary inflammation indices such as DII and EDIP is deeply influenced by cultural and dietary contexts, underscoring the importance of population-specific calibration. Our DLIS findings, however, surpass prior reports, likely due to the inclusion of lifestyle factors (e.g., physical activity) that are uniquely influential in PCOS pathophysiology within sedentary populations. The stronger DLIS association suggests that PCOS risk is modulated by combined dietary-lifestyle inflammation rather than diet al.one. Our findings thus underscore the need for culturally adapted indices that account for local nutritional habits (e.g., high legume intake) and lifestyle norms (e.g., lower alcohol use).

Several studies have linked pro-inflammatory dietary and lifestyle patterns to adverse metabolic outcomes, including insulin resistance and systemic inflammation, both of which are central to PCOS pathophysiology [28, 29]. However, findings from populations with metabolic syndrome or liver disease must be cautiously interpreted, as their relevance to PCOS is limited. We, therefore, focused our discussion on studies directly evaluating inflammatory indices in PCOS.

In this study, we adapted the DII, EDIP, and DLIS to the Iranian context; however, these modifications may impact their performance. For the DII, only 30 out of the original 45 food parameters were included due to the unavailability of specific anti-inflammatory nutrients (e.g., polyphenols, flavonoids) in local databases. Since these compounds are known to reduce inflammatory markers like IL-6 and CRP [30], their exclusion likely underestimates the diet’s anti-inflammatory potential. Nevertheless, the included parameters, such as fiber, monounsaturated fats, vitamins A, C, and E, and minerals like magnesium and selenium, still capture a broad spectrum of inflammatory and anti-inflammatory dietary components [13].

Similarly, the EDIP was modified by excluding alcohol (wine and beer), in alignment with religious and cultural practices in Iran. Additionally, high- and low-energy drinks were combined into a single category due to insufficient granularity in FFQ reporting. These adjustments resulted in 15 out of the original 18 food groups being retained, with original weighting coefficients preserved to minimize bias. While the exclusion of alcohol, a strong pro-inflammatory component in Western diets, may attenuate the EDIP’s inflammatory signal, the remaining food groups (e.g., processed meats, refined grains, and anti-inflammatory vegetables) still provide a meaningful measure of dietary inflammation [14]. This adaptation may partially explain the weaker association between EDIP and PCOS in our study compared to Western countries, where alcohol plays a more prominent dietary role [31].

For the DLIS, 18 out of 19 food groups were included, and the LIS was calculated without alcohol due to cultural restrictions. Instead, we incorporated physical activity, BMI, and smoking status. Since alcohol consumption is negligible in this population, exclusion was deemed more appropriate than imputation, though this may underestimate the pro-inflammatory impact of lifestyle factors. The retained components, particularly physical inactivity, and high BMI, remain firmly linked to inflammation and are highly relevant in the context of PCOS.

While these adaptations reflect real-world dietary and lifestyle exposures in Iran, they underscore the need for population-specific index calibration. Future studies should explore the quantitative impact of these modifications by comparing adapted versus original indices in populations with negligible alcohol consumption. Additionally, efforts to include missing nutrients (e.g., polyphenols) and investigate alternative lifestyle factors more prevalent in Iranian cohorts could enhance the precision and applicability of these indices.

The mechanism linking pro-inflammatory diets and lifestyle factors to PCOS remains an area of active investigation. Studies have consistently shown that women with PCOS exhibit elevated circulating levels of inflammatory biomarkers, such as C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), compared to healthy women [32]. These biomarkers are associated with both insulin resistance and hyperandrogenism, two central features of PCOS [7]. Furthermore, dietary patterns with high DII or DLIS scores have been associated with increased systemic inflammation, as indicated by higher levels of CRP and IL-6 [13, 15]. Therefore, it is plausible that pro-inflammatory diets and lifestyles contribute to the chronic low-grade inflammation observed in PCOS, thereby exacerbating its clinical manifestations [7]. Women with PCOS in our cohort exhibited significantly higher DII and DLIS scores alongside elevated BMI and lower physical activity, key drivers of low-grade inflammation. The DII’s association with PCOS likely reflects the cumulative pro-inflammatory effects of nutrients captured by this index, such as saturated fats and refined carbohydrates, which are known to activate NF-κB and increase circulating IL-6 [7]. Our DLIS findings extend this by implicating synergistic interactions between diet and lifestyle; for example, the combination of high DIS and LIS components may amplify oxidative stress, a pathway supported by the higher prevalence of insulin resistance and diabetes in our PCOS group [33–35]. While prior studies broadly implicate inflammation in PCOS pathogenesis [6], our data specifically link empirically derived inflammatory scores to clinical phenotypes. For instance, the lack of association with EDIP may stem from its emphasis on food groups (e.g., alcohol and processed meats) that are less central to Iranian diets. In contrast, DII/DLIS better captures local pro-inflammatory exposures. This aligns with our participants’ lower consumption of anti-inflammatory nutrients in higher DII tertiles. Future research should validate these indices using direct inflammatory biomarkers, such as interleukin-6 and C-reactive protein, in PCOS cohorts to clarify the mechanistic pathways.

The strengths of this study include the use of validated and reliable measures administered under standardized conditions. Notably, our use of newly diagnosed PCOS cases minimizes the likelihood of dietary/lifestyle changes secondary to PCOS awareness, and blinding of interviewers to case/control status reduces information bias. The FFQ and IPAQ-SF were implemented by trained interviewers using face-to-face interviews, photographic portion guides, and standardized protocols to minimize reporting bias [23, 25]. These methods enhance accuracy by reducing recall errors and improving portion-size estimation, as demonstrated in prior validation studies [25]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between the DII, EDIP, and DLIS with the development of PCOS.

While our conclusions highlight significant associations between pro-inflammatory diets/lifestyle (higher DII/DLIS) and PCOS, several limitations must be considered. The case-control design precludes causal inference, and reverse causation remains a plausible explanation. Although we enrolled newly diagnosed PCOS cases, some participants may have had subclinical symptoms (e.g., weight gain, irregular menstruation) for years, potentially prompting dietary/lifestyle changes that could bias associations. For instance, women with undiagnosed PCOS might adopt healthier diets to manage symptoms, attenuating observed effects. Prospective cohorts tracking dietary changes before and after diagnosis are needed to disentangle this relationship. Despite adjusting for key confounders (e.g., age, BMI, physical activity, and metabolic comorbidities), residual confounding from unmeasured or imprecisely measured factors may persist. For instance, genetic predisposition to PCOS or insulin resistance, factors strongly implicated in PCOS pathogenesis, was not accounted for in our analysis. Observational designs cannot establish causality, and our findings should be interpreted in light of these inherent limitations. Similarly, dietary assessments, though validated, may not capture nuanced nutritional components (e.g., cooking methods, food additives, or regional variations in food composition) that could influence inflammatory potential. Additionally, although we adjusted for key confounders, our sample size limited our ability to explore potential interactions (e.g., between inflammatory indices and BMI or age), which should be investigated in larger cohorts. The exclusion of specific components (e.g., polyphenols in DII, alcohol in EDIP and LIS) may introduce bias by underestimating the anti-inflammatory or overestimating the pro-inflammatory potential of the diet and lifestyle. For instance, the absence of polyphenols could lead to a less nuanced DII. At the same time, the exclusion of alcohol in EDIP and LIS might weaken the association with inflammation, as alcohol is a known pro-inflammatory factor in other populations. However, our adaptations reflect the unique dietary and cultural practices of the Iranian population, ensuring the applicability of the indices to this context. Despite these limitations, the significant associations observed for DII and DLIS suggest that the adapted indices remain valid tools for assessing inflammation-related dietary and lifestyle patterns in PCOS. While FFQs are subject to inherent measurement error (e.g., recall bias), our rigorous data collection process, including interviewer training, cross-checks, and validation against biomarkers in Iranian populations supports the robustness of our dietary assessments. Similarly, physical activity quantification via IPAQ-SF, though less precise than accelerometry, aligns with large-scale epidemiological protocols and was administered uniformly by blinded staff to ensure consistency. We minimized reporting bias through interviewer training, standardized protocols, and portion-size aids, but self-reported dietary data remain vulnerable to recall and social desirability biases. Women with PCOS, particularly those with higher BMI, may underreport pro-inflammatory foods (e.g., processed meats, sweets), potentially attenuating the observed associations. Future studies could incorporate biomarkers (e.g., urinary nitrogen for protein intake) to validate self-reported data. Self-reported diabetes and hypothyroidism status may introduce misclassification bias, though we mitigated this by cross-checking available medical records. Sixth, these scores were validated for specific populations, and the weighting used may not apply to other populations. Finally, although our study evaluated dietary and lifestyle inflammatory patterns using validated indices, we were unable to measure circulating inflammatory biomarkers (e.g., CRP, IL-6) or cardiovascular risk factors (e.g., lipid profiles and fasting glucose and insulin levels) due to resource constraints. However, we adjusted for self-reported metabolic conditions; future studies should incorporate these biomarkers to elucidate how pro-inflammatory diets/lifestyles influence PCOS-associated dysmetabolism. In addition, as our study focused on Iranian women, generalizability to other ethnic groups may be limited due to potential differences in genetic predisposition, cultural dietary patterns, and lifestyle factors. Furthermore, since our participants were Iranian women attending an infertility center, our findings may not be generalizable to all women with PCOS, including those from different ethnic, socioeconomic, or reproductive health backgrounds.

Conclusions

In this study, pro-inflammatory diets (characterized by a higher DII) and combined inflammatory diet and lifestyle patterns (characterized by a higher DLIS) were associated with an increased odds of PCOS. While these results underscore the importance of addressing inflammation in PCOS management, the case-control design precludes causal conclusions due to potential reverse causation (e.g., undiagnosed PCOS influencing dietary habits). The wide confidence intervals for some estimates further underscore the need for larger studies to precisely quantify these associations. The findings highlight the need for a shift in PCOS risk assessment, emphasizing that combined diet-lifestyle inflammation (DLIS) is a stronger predictor than dietary inflammation alone (DII). This highlights the importance of integrated interventions that target both diet and physical activity, particularly in high-risk groups. Future research should validate DLIS in prospective cohorts and evaluate its role in personalized management of PCOS. Importantly, these findings should be confirmed in more diverse populations beyond infertile Iranian women to enhance generalizability and cross-cultural applicability. Studies should also assess dietary and lifestyle patterns before PCOS onset and track post-diagnosis changes to reduce reverse causation bias. Additionally, developing population-specific inflammatory indices that include regionally relevant foods (e.g., saffron and pomegranate in Iran) and lifestyle factors would improve accuracy in non-Western settings and enhance cross-study consistency.
