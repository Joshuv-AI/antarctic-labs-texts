Molecular Pathogenesis and Targeted Therapies for Myeloproliferative Hypereosinophilic Neoplasms

Introduction

Hypereosinophilic syndromes (HES) represent a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by the core features of persistent and significant peripheral blood eosinophilia, defined as an absolute eosinophil count of 1.5 × 10⁹/L or greater [1, 2]. The clinical manifestations of HES are diverse, reflecting the potential of eosinophils to infiltrate and damage tissues. Patients may present with constitutional symptoms, dermatologic findings, pulmonary infiltrates, gastrointestinal distress, or, most ominously, life-threatening cardiac complications such as endomyocardial fibrosis and thromboembolism [1, 3].

For decades, the majority of HES cases lacking a clear secondary cause were classified as “idiopathic,” leaving clinicians with limited, non-specific therapeutic options, primarily corticosteroids and cytotoxic agents, which carried significant toxicity and offered variable efficacy [2, 4]. This landscape was fundamentally transformed by the landmark discovery of a cryptic deletion on chromosome 4q12 that creates the FIP1L1-PDGFRA fusion gene and its resulting protein. The identification of this constitutively activated tyrosine kinase as the driver of the disease in a subset of patients with HES proved that these were not idiopathic conditions but rather clonal myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) [5, 6]. The dramatic and sustained hematologic and molecular responses to the tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) imatinib in patients with this fusion gene reclassified their disease from a chronic, often debilitating syndrome to a highly treatable form of leukemia [6, 7].

Following this breakthrough, subsequent research has identified other recurrent genetic rearrangements that also function as primary drivers of eosinophilic MPNs. This has led the World Health Organization (WHO) to formally establish a distinct category of neoplasms: “Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with eosinophilia and tyrosine kinase gene fusions”. This group is defined by rearrangements involving platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha (PDGFRA), platelet-derived growth factor receptor beta (PDGFRB), or fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1). A provisional entity driven by rearrangements of Janus kinase 2 (JAK2), such as PCM1-JAK2, has also been included [8–11]. The formalization of this classification emphasizes that these conditions are distinct hematologic malignancies where the specific molecular driver, rather than simply the presence of eosinophilia, dictates prognosis and is the primary target for therapy [8–12].

Given this molecularly oriented classification, a critical diagnostic challenge is the meticulous differentiation of these primary, clonal eosinophilias from the far more common secondary, or reactive, causes. Reactive eosinophilia can be triggered by a vast number of conditions, including parasitic infections, allergic reactions, drug hypersensitivity, autoimmune diseases, and other underlying malignancies. A thorough evaluation to exclude these causes before considering a diagnosis of a primary eosinophilic neoplasm commonly includes stool testing for parasites, autoimmune blood testing, and imaging studies as clinically indicated to evaluate for an underlying lymphoma or other malignancy [1, 2, 12]. This review will focus specifically on the myeloproliferative variants of HES and closely related overlap syndromes, such as atypical chronic myeloid leukemia (aCML)—also known as myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm with neutrophilia according to the WHO classification—which can feature eosinophilia as part of its clinical picture. We will synthesize the current understanding of their molecular basis and pathogenesis, detail the established and emerging targeted therapies, and explore the future directions and research gaps that are shaping the next generation of treatments for these rare diseases.

Molecular Landscape and Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of myeloproliferative HES is rooted in a landscape of specific, acquired genetic aberrations that confer a clonal advantage to hematopoietic stem or progenitor cells. These mutations lead to the constitutive activation of intracellular signaling pathways that drive cellular proliferation and survival, with a pronounced effect on the eosinophil lineage. Understanding this molecular basis is now central to the diagnosis, classification, and treatment of these disorders [12].

The most well-characterized genetic driver is the cryptic interstitial deletion on chromosome 4q12, which results in the FIP1L1-PDGFRA fusion gene [5]. The resulting fusion protein possesses constitutive, ligand-independent tyrosine kinase activity, leading to uncontrolled myeloid expansion, particularly of eosinophils. This aberration is found in a significant subset of patients previously diagnosed with idiopathic HES and is the defining molecular lesion of what is now most accurately termed chronic eosinophilic leukemia (CEL) [13]. Other clinically important rearrangements involve PDGFRB on chromosome 5q33 and FGFR1 on 8p11. While PDGFRB fusions also result in a myeloproliferative neoplasm that is highly responsive to imatinib, neoplasms driven by FGFR1 rearrangements are exceptionally aggressive [8]. This entity, formerly known as 8p11 myeloproliferative syndrome, frequently presents with features of MPN but carries a very high risk of rapid transformation to acute myeloid leukemia (AML) or T- or B-lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma [14]. Therefore, recognizing these rare entities on conventional karyotype (including rearrangements involving 4q12, 5q33, and 8p11) is essential to enable better prognostication and therapeutic decision-making. While conventional karyotyping can identify rearrangements involving 5q33 (PDGFRB) and 8p11 (FGFR1), it frequently fails to detect the cryptic 4q12 CHIC2 deletion underlying FIP1L1–PDGFRA rearrangements. Accordingly, targeted molecular testing using fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) or reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) is essential for the accurate identification of PDGFRA-driven disease and for guiding prognostication and therapeutic decision-making [12].

Beyond these three classic kinase-activating fusions, other mutations are increasingly recognized. The JAK2 V617F mutation, common in other myeloproliferative neoplasms, can be identified in a small subset of HES patients [9]. Furthermore, rearrangements involving JAK2, such as the PCM1-JAK2 fusion, define a distinct provisional WHO entity that can present with prominent eosinophilia [15]. In the related MDS/MPN overlap syndrome of aCML, which is defined by the absence of the BCR-ABL1 rearrangement, a different spectrum of mutations is common. These include activating mutations in CSF3R and prognostically significant mutations in genes such as SETBP1, ETNK1, and ASXL1, which contribute to the disease phenotype and are associated with a poor prognosis [16].

While cell-intrinsic kinase activation is the primary driver in myeloproliferative variants, eosinophilia is also modulated by cytokine signaling. Interleukin-5 (IL-5) is the principal cytokine responsible for the terminal differentiation, activation, and prolonged survival of eosinophils. In some HES variants, such as the lymphocytic variant, the underlying pathophysiology involves a clonal population of aberrant T-cells that overproduce IL-5, driving a reactive eosinophilia [17]. In myeloproliferative HES, the constitutively active kinases can amplify downstream signaling pathways, including the STAT5 and MAPK/ERK pathways, which can increase the sensitivity of myeloid progenitors to cytokines like IL-5, thereby synergistically promoting eosinophilic expansion [18].

The modern diagnostic workup for a patient with persistent and unexplained eosinophilia is a tiered process that relies on the identification of a clonal molecular marker. As shown in Fig. 1, systematic diagnosis includes bone marrow aspirate, cytogenetic evaluation, and targeted molecular testing to detect key rearrangements such as FIP1L1-PDGFRA, PDGFRB, and FGFR1. After meticulously excluding reactive causes, an investigation for a clonal disorder is initiated. A bone marrow aspirate and biopsy with morphologic review and cytogenetics is essential. This is followed by targeted molecular testing, typically using FISH or RT-PCR to detect the key rearrangements of PDGFRA, PDGFRB, and FGFR1 [19]. The use of next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels is becoming increasingly standard in the workup of suspected myeloid neoplasms [19, 20]. NGS can identify a broader array of somatic mutations (e.g., ASXL1, SETBP1), which can confirm the presence of a clonal hematopoietic process even when the classic fusions are absent and provide critical prognostic information. Increasingly, RNA-based NGS fusion panels are incorporated into diagnostic workflows to identify cryptic, complex, or novel kinase fusion events involving PDGFRA, PDGFRB, FGFR1, JAK2, and related genes, particularly when conventional cytogenetics and targeted FISH assays are unrevealing [10, 12]. Risk stratification is almost entirely dependent on the specific molecular driver identified. Patients with FIP1L1-PDGFRA-positive CEL have an excellent prognosis with imatinib therapy, whereas FGFR1 rearrangements are associated with an aggressive disease biology and a high risk of transformation [8, 21]; however, selective FGFR inhibition, particularly with pemigatinib, has substantially improved outcomes in patients with chronic-phase FGFR1-rearranged disease. In addition to molecular characterization, comprehensive diagnostic evaluation should include formal disease staging and lineage assessment. This involves evaluation for myeloid, lymphoid, or mixed-phenotype involvement using bone marrow morphology, flow cytometry, cytogenetics, and appropriate imaging studies, as recommended by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network Clinical Practice Guidelines for myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with eosinophilia and tyrosine kinase fusion genes. Accurate lineage assignment and staging are critical, as they influence prognosis, risk of transformation, and therapeutic decision-making, including the timing of intensive chemotherapy or allogeneic stem cell transplantation [22].

Established Therapies and Unmet Needs

The therapeutic strategy for myeloproliferative HES and related neoplasms is now fundamentally dictated by the underlying molecular abnormality, a direct consequence of the shift towards a genetically-defined classification of these diseases [8, 12]. For patients harboring rearrangements of PDGFRA or PDGFRB, the treatment landscape has been transformed by the availability of targeted therapy.

Imatinib, a first-generation BCR-ABL1 TKI, is the first-line standard of care for patients with proven PDGFRA or PDGFRB rearrangements [6, 23]. The FIP1L1-PDGFRA fusion kinase, in particular, is considerably sensitive to imatinib, a finding that has transformed the prognosis for this disease from a life-threatening condition to a manageable chronic illness [5, 23]. Patients often achieve rapid, complete, and durable hematologic and molecular remissions with relatively low doses of imatinib, typically 100 mg daily, with long-term follow-up studies confirming sustained efficacy and a favorable safety profile over many years. In contrast, PDGFRB-rearranged neoplasms typically require higher doses, most commonly 400 mg daily, to achieve and maintain optimal hematologic and cytogenetic responses [12, 23–25].

In stark contrast, for patients lacking an imatinib-sensitive mutation, therapeutic options are far more limited and less effective [4, 12]. The initial treatment for idiopathic HES and some myeloproliferative variants without a defined target is typically high-dose corticosteroids, which can induce remission but are associated with significant long-term toxicity and often fail to produce durable responses [2, 17]. For patients who are steroid-refractory or steroid-dependent, second-line therapies include cytoreductive agents like hydroxyurea or immunomodulators such as interferon-alpha [4, 17]. While these agents can control eosinophil counts in a subset of patients, responses are often partial or transient, and treatment can be limited by side effects. For patients with aggressive disease, particularly BCR-ABL1-negative aCML or FGFR1-rearranged neoplasms, the only potentially curative modality is allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) [14, 26]. HSCT is often considered for eligible patients with high-risk disease features, but its application is limited by patient age, comorbidities, and the availability of a suitable donor [26].

Despite the remarkable success in a subset of patients, significant unmet needs persist across the spectrum of these disorders. A primary challenge is the development of resistance to imatinib in PDGFRA-rearranged disease. Resistance can be primary (lack of initial response) or secondary (relapse after an initial response) and is often driven by the acquisition of secondary point mutations within the PDGFRA kinase domain, such as the T674I gatekeeper mutation, which prevent imatinib from binding effectively. The PDGFRA T674I substitution is structurally and functionally analogous to the BCR–ABL1 T315I gatekeeper mutation, as both alter a conserved residue within the ATP-binding pocket, sterically hindering imatinib binding and conferring high-level resistance to first-generation TKIs [27, 28]. Structural analyses have also detailed how PDGFRA activation loop mutations like D842V prevent imatinib binding, explaining the efficacy of avapritinib as a rational next-generation therapy [29, 30].

The most notable therapeutic challenge lies in the management of patients with FGFR1-rearranged neoplasms. These diseases are inherently aggressive and completely unresponsive to imatinib [14, 31]. Although patients may achieve transient responses to intensive multi-agent chemotherapy, relapse is nearly universal, and overall survival remains dismal [14]. Even when HSCT is performed, relapse rates are high, highlighting the urgent need for more effective bridging therapies and post-transplant maintenance strategies [31, 32]. Finally, a major unmet need exists for patients with aCML and other myeloproliferative HES forms that lack a clear, actionable driver mutation. The prognosis of these patients is poor, with a median survival of a few years, highlighting a critical lack of effective, disease-modifying therapies beyond HSCT [33, 34]. This “targetless” patient population represents a key area for ongoing clinical and translational research.

Novel Therapies in Development

Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors

The remarkable success of imatinib has catalyzed the development of next-generation TKIs designed to overcome mechanisms of resistance and to target the diverse array of other oncogenic kinase fusions found in HES and related neoplasms. This research has led to a growing collection of potent agents that offer new hope for patients with molecularly defined diseases who do not benefit from standard therapies.

A key area of development has been addressing imatinib resistance in PDGFRA-rearranged disease. While the T674I gatekeeper mutation is a known mechanism of resistance, mutations in the activation loop, such as D842V, also confer complete resistance to imatinib and other conventional TKIs [29]. Avapritinib is a highly potent, type I kinase inhibitor specifically designed to target and inhibit KIT and PDGFRA activation loop mutants [29]. Its efficacy was demonstrated in the pivotal NAVIGATOR trial in patients with gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST) harboring the PDGFRA D842V mutation, where it induced durable responses in the majority of patients, leading to its approval by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for this indication [30]. Although these mutations are rare in the context of myeloid neoplasms, studies have demonstrated the successful use of avapritinib in patients with PDGFRA-mutant systemic mastocytosis with eosinophilia and other myeloid neoplasms, establishing a mechanistic and clinical rationale for its use in select patients with imatinib-resistant PDGFRA-mutant neoplasms, recognizing that evidence in hypereosinophilic neoplasms remains limited [35].

For the notoriously aggressive and chemo-refractory FGFR1-rearranged neoplasms, several TKIs have been investigated. Ponatinib, a potent multi-targeted TKI with activity against ABL, PDGFR, FGFR, and VEGFR, was an early agent of interest [32]. Preclinical studies provided a strong rationale, demonstrating that ponatinib could effectively suppress the growth of cell lines driven by various FGFR1 fusion proteins and prolong survival in mouse models [36]. However, clinical experience has been mixed. While ponatinib can induce hematologic responses, and in some cases, complete remissions in patients with FGFR1-rearranged disease, these responses are often transient, and the drug carries a significant risk of serious vascular adverse events [37]. Its role is therefore limited, often serving as a potential bridge to HSCT in select patients [26, 37].

The challenges with ponatinib have highlighted the need for more specific and potent FGFR inhibitors. Pemigatinib is a selective inhibitor of FGFR1, 2, and 3 that was granted an FDA approval for treating cholangiocarcinoma with FGFR2 fusions [38]. Its activity against FGFR1 fusions has been demonstrated in myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms, most notably in the phase 2 FIGHT-203 study, where it induced high rates of complete response, leading to its recent approval for this specific indication [39]. In this pivotal trial, pemigatinib produced complete hematologic responses in nearly 80% of patients with chronic-phase disease and durable cytogenetic remissions, which directly supported its FDA approval in 2022 for myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with FGFR1 rearrangement [39]. Long-term follow-up has demonstrated sustained efficacy, with many patients successfully bridged to HSCT [40]. This represents a major therapeutic advance, providing the first specifically approved targeted therapy for this high-risk patient population. Other investigational agents targeting the FGFR pathway, such as futibatinib and infigratinib, are also in various stages of clinical development, reflecting an active and promising area of research aimed at improving outcomes for these diseases [32]. Importantly, despite the marked activity of pemigatinib in FGFR1-rearranged neoplasms, allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation remains essential for durable disease control in patients presenting with accelerated-phase or blast-phase disease. In these settings, selective FGFR inhibition should be viewed primarily as a cytoreductive and disease-stabilizing strategy to achieve remission and optimize patients for timely transplantation, rather than as definitive therapy [22, 26, 40].

JAK Inhibitors

The Janus kinase (JAK)-signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) pathway is a critical signaling cascade for a multitude of cytokines and growth factors essential for hematopoiesis and immune regulation. Its aberrant activation is a central pathogenic mechanism in classical MPNs like myelofibrosis and polycythemia vera, making it a prime therapeutic target. This rationale extends logically to the subset of HES and related overlap neoplasms where dysregulation of this pathway is the primary oncogenic driver [12].

Ruxolitinib, a potent inhibitor of JAK1 and JAK2, has become a cornerstone of therapy for myelofibrosis and polycythemia vera, demonstrating significant efficacy in reducing splenomegaly and improving constitutional symptoms [41]. Its role in eosinophilic disorders is being actively explored, particularly in cases driven by specific JAK2 abnormalities. This includes the small subset of HES patients who harbor the canonical JAK2 V617F mutation, as well as those with the provisional WHO entity defined by JAK2 rearrangements, most commonly PCM1-JAK2 [8]. In these molecularly defined populations, ruxolitinib represents a direct targeted therapy. Available reports, primarily limited to small series and case-based studies, demonstrate that ruxolitinib can reduce eosinophil counts, alleviate disease-related symptoms such as pruritus and night sweats, and decrease spleen size in select patients with JAK2-mutated HES or PCM1-JAK2-positive neoplasms [42, 43]. These early signals of activity warrant further investigation in prospective clinical trials to better define its efficacy and safety in this specific patient population.

The therapeutic landscape of JAK inhibition has expanded beyond ruxolitinib with the approval of second-generation agents. Fedratinib, a selective JAK2 inhibitor, and momelotinib, an inhibitor of JAK1, JAK2, and ACVR1, are now established treatments for myelofibrosis, offering new options for patients who are intolerant to or have lost response to ruxolitinib [44, 45]. While clinical data for these newer agents specifically in HES are currently scarce, their proven efficacy in controlling the myeloproliferation and cytokine-driven symptoms of MPNs suggests they may have a future role in managing eosinophilic overlap syndromes, pending further investigation.

Monoclonal Antibodies and Immune Modulators

While TKIs target the intrinsic cellular drivers of myeloproliferation, a parallel and highly effective therapeutic strategy involves targeting the extrinsic factors that promote eosinophil survival and activation. Among them, the IL-5 signaling pathway is the most critical, acting as the master regulator of eosinophil differentiation, trafficking, and persistence. The development of monoclonal antibodies that disrupt this pathway has influenced the treatment of severe eosinophilic asthma and has now been successfully translated to the management of HES.

Mepolizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody that binds to and neutralizes circulating IL-5, was the first biologic agent to be extensively studied in this setting. A pivotal randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial demonstrated that mepolizumab significantly reduced the rate of disease flares and allowed for a substantial reduction in corticosteroid dosage in patients with FIP1L1-PDGFRA-negative HES [17]. These findings led to its approval by the FDA for this specific indication, providing the first approved targeted therapy for the non-clonal HES population. Although its primary indication is for non-myeloproliferative HES, mepolizumab is increasingly used off-label to manage severe, refractory eosinophilia in patients with myeloproliferative variants [13]. In this context, it can serve as a potent steroid-sparing agent or as a bridge to more definitive therapy, such as HSCT, by controlling the end-organ damage mediated by eosinophils [13].

Another highly effective agent is benralizumab, a monoclonal antibody that targets the IL-5 receptor alpha chain (IL-5Rα) expressed on the surface of eosinophils and basophils. Unlike mepolizumab, which sequesters IL-5, benralizumab induces direct, rapid, and near-complete depletion of eosinophils through antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity. Studies, including a randomized phase 2 trial and real-world series, suggest that benralizumab can induce rapid, deep, and sustained eosinophil depletion in steroid-refractory HES [46], although evidence in clonal myeloid neoplasms remains limited and such agents do not directly eradicate the malignant clone. More recently, results from the phase 3 NATRON study demonstrated that benralizumab significantly delayed time to disease worsening or flare and improved clinical outcomes in patients with HES, with a favorable safety profile [47]. Its use in HES remains off-label (approved for asthma and eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis), although its potent cytolytic activity has made it an attractive option. Although case-based evidence includes patients treated after mepolizumab failure and occasional reports with clonal/myeloid features, data in overt myeloid neoplasms are limited and management should be individualized [13, 46].

Other immune pathways are also being explored. Dupilumab is a monoclonal antibody that blocks the alpha subunit of the IL-4 receptor, thereby inhibiting both IL-4 and IL-13 signaling. These cytokines are central to type 2 inflammation and contribute to eosinophil recruitment and activation. While its role in HES is less established than the anti-IL-5 agents, studies have shown its efficacy in controlling eosinophilia and clinical symptoms, suggesting it may be a viable option for select patients [48].

The role of checkpoint inhibitors, such as anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4 antibodies, is purely investigational and must be approached with extreme caution. A well-recognized spectrum of immune-related adverse events is associated with these agents, including the development of significant eosinophilia [49]. Consequently, there is a strong theoretical concern that their use in a patient with an underlying eosinophilic neoplasm could trigger a dangerous “eosinophil flare,” potentially exacerbating the disease. Their use would only be contemplated in highly specific and rare clinical scenarios, such as transformation to a lymphoid blast phase where such therapies might be indicated, and only with careful risk-benefit analysis.

Epigenetic and Targeted Agents

Beyond targeting kinases and cytokine pathways, research is actively exploring other critical vulnerabilities in myeloid cancer cells, including epigenetic dysregulation and dependencies on specific metabolic and survival pathways. These approaches are particularly relevant for overlap MDS/MPN syndromes like aCML, which often lack a single, dominant kinase driver and are instead characterized by a more complex mutational landscape [16, 33].

Hypomethylating agents (HMAs), such as azacitidine and decitabine, are the cornerstone of therapy for higher-risk myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS). These drugs work by incorporating into DNA and inhibiting DNA methyltransferases, leading to global hypomethylation and the re-expression of silenced tumor suppressor genes. Given the significant clinical and molecular overlap between MDS and aCML, HMAs represent an important therapeutic option for these patients. While currently considered off-label and not standard of care for aCML, HMAs can provide transitory responses. They may serve a critical role as a bridge to HSCT for eligible patients requiring cytoreduction, or as an alternative therapy for transplant-ineligible patients who cannot tolerate standard agents like hydroxyurea [50].

The principle of precision medicine, focused on targeting a specific mutation with a tailored inhibitor, extends to this patient population. Although rare in HES and aCML, mutations in the isocitrate dehydrogenase genes, IDH1 and IDH2, can occur. Potent, specific inhibitors—ivosidenib (for IDH1) and enasidenib (for IDH2)—are approved for treating patients with IDH-mutant acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and have shown efficacy in other IDH-mutant myeloid neoplasms [51, 52]. For the rare HES or aCML patient found to harbor one of these mutations, these targeted agents represent a clear and compelling therapeutic option.

Furthermore, a strong biological rationale exists for targeting other crucial cell survival pathways. The anti-apoptotic protein BCL2 is frequently overexpressed in myeloid malignancies, conferring a survival advantage to leukemic cells. Venetoclax, a BCL2 inhibitor, has significantly impacted the treatment of older or unfit patients with AML, especially when used in combination with HMAs [53]. This highly effective combination is now being actively investigated in high-risk MDS and chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML), with promising results [54]. This provides a strong rationale for exploring venetoclax-based combinations in refractory aCML, particularly in patients already being treated with an HMA. Similarly, preclinical evidence suggests that the Hedgehog signaling pathway, which is critical for leukemic stem cell maintenance, may be a viable target. Glasdegib, a Hedgehog pathway inhibitor, is approved in combination with low-dose cytarabine for newly diagnosed AML in older adults, and its role in other myeloid neoplasms remains an area of active investigation [55].

Clinical Trials Landscape

The clinical trial landscape for myeloproliferative HES and related disorders directly mirrors the evolution of the field towards a molecularly driven framework. The era of empirical trials with broad cytotoxic or immunomodulatory agents is giving way to studies of targeted therapies in small, genetically-defined patient populations. This shift, while promising, brings a unique set of challenges related to the rarity and heterogeneity of these diseases.

The most significant recent progress has been in targeting FGFR1 rearrangements, culminating in the approval of the selective FGFR inhibitor pemigatinib. The pivotal, multicenter, single-arm phase 2 FIGHT-203 study was specifically designed for patients with myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with FGFR1 rearrangement. The trial demonstrated remarkably high rates of complete hematologic and cytogenetic response in this historically refractory population, leading directly to its accelerated approval by the FDA and providing a much-needed, effective targeted therapy [39]. More recently, the phase 2 FIGHT-207 basket trial expanded the role of pemigatinib to solid tumors with FGFR1–3 alterations, providing mechanistic insights and identifying TP53 and MAPK pathway co-alterations as predictors of resistance [56]. While outside eosinophilic neoplasms, these data reinforce the durability of FGFR inhibition across malignancies. This success has validated the approach of developing specific inhibitors for this molecular cohort, and trials of other potent FGFR inhibitors are ongoing.

For other molecular subsets, trial data are emerging. The rationale for using avapritinib in PDGFRA D842V-mutant neoplasms is largely extrapolated from its considerable success in GIST, as demonstrated in the NAVIGATOR trial [30]. Its activity in myeloid neoplasms has been confirmed in phase 1 and 2 studies in advanced systemic mastocytosis, which often features eosinophilia and PDGFRA mutations, thereby establishing a strong evidence base for its use in this rare context [35, 57]. In parallel, the efficacy of JAK inhibitors is being formally assessed. For instance, a phase 2 study sponsored by the National Cancer Institute (NCT03801434) is actively evaluating the efficacy of ruxolitinib in patients with various primary eosinophilic disorders, including those with defined JAK2 mutations, which will help to solidify its role beyond off-label use [58].

The successful registration trials for monoclonal antibodies in non-clonal HES, such as the pivotal phase 3 study of mepolizumab, have set a high bar for evidence generation in eosinophilic disorders [17]. While dedicated trials for these biologics in defined myeloproliferative cohorts are lacking, they are often included as eligible populations in broader HES registry studies, which will provide valuable real-world data on their utility.

A major challenge that pervades all research in this area is patient enrollment. The rarity of these conditions, further stratified into even rarer molecular subtypes, makes conducting traditional, large-scale randomized trials nearly impossible. Consequently, the field relies heavily on single-arm, multicenter studies and innovative trial designs, such as basket trials, which enroll patients with different types of cancer based on a shared molecular alteration (e.g., any FGFR1-rearranged malignancy). To overcome these logistical hurdles, international collaboration and the development of robust patient registries are crucial. Such initiatives are essential for pooling data, studying the natural history of rare subtypes, and providing a platform to successfully conduct meaningful clinical research that can lead to new therapeutic approvals.

Across molecular subtypes, the evidence base is largely derived from single-arm trials, expanded access programs, registry cohorts, and case series. Randomized comparative studies are rare or infeasible given the extreme rarity and molecular fragmentation of these disorders, and cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted with caution.

Future Directions and Research Gaps

Despite substantial therapeutic advances, current evidence is constrained by several factors, including the rarity of individual molecular subtypes, small patient cohorts, reliance on non-randomized single-arm studies, heterogeneous response criteria, and limited long-term follow-up for newer agents. Resistance mechanisms and treatment sequencing remain incompletely defined, and outcomes are often confounded by transplantation strategies and referral bias. These limitations highlight the need for cautious interpretation of efficacy signals and continued collaborative research.

The future trajectory of HES therapy will be shaped by a deeper integration of genomic technologies to enhance diagnostic precision, the adoption of sensitive monitoring techniques to guide treatment decisions, and the strategic development of rational therapeutic combinations to overcome resistance.

A primary future direction is the universal application of personalized therapy through comprehensive genomic profiling at diagnosis. The current standard of using targeted FISH or RT-PCR for a few select fusions will likely be supplanted by the routine use of NGS panels and RNA sequencing. These powerful technologies can not only identify the canonical fusions but also uncover rare or novel kinase rearrangements, cryptic insertions, and the complex landscape of co-mutations that may influence prognosis and response to therapy. This approach will be crucial for identifying targetable lesions in patients who are currently considered “driver-negative,” potentially expanding the population of patients who can benefit from a precision medicine approach [10].

A major research gap is the establishment and clinical validation of minimal residual disease (MRD) monitoring. For patients with defined molecular markers, such as the FIP1L1-PDGFRA fusion, highly sensitive techniques like quantitative PCR (qPCR) or digital PCR (dPCR) can detect residual disease far below the threshold of conventional methods. The systematic use of MRD could transform patient management by providing a tool to guide treatment de-escalation or even discontinuation in patients with deep, sustained responses, analogous to the successful treatment-free remission (TFR) strategies in chronic myeloid leukemia and FIP1L1-PDGFRA-positive myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with eosinophilia [25]. Conversely, a rising MRD level could serve as an early warning of impending relapse, allowing for pre-emptive therapeutic intervention before the onset of clinical symptoms. Defining the optimal MRD thresholds, the timing of monitoring, and the appropriate clinical actions based on these results are critical areas for future prospective studies.

To address the significant challenge of studying rare diseases, there is a pressing need for better classification systems and unified international registries. As more molecular drivers are discovered, the WHO classification will require continued refinement to ensure it accurately reflects biological and clinical heterogeneity [8]. Furthermore, establishing collaborative international registries is paramount. Such registries are essential for pooling data from a sufficient number of patients to accurately describe the natural history of rare subtypes, identify prognostic factors, and serve as a platform for conducting meaningful clinical trials that would otherwise be impossible at a single institution [5].

Finally, the potential of combination therapies remains largely unexplored and represents a vital future direction, particularly for aggressive diseases like FGFR1-rearranged neoplasms. Even with potent new single agents like pemigatinib, the risk of acquired resistance remains. Future trials should focus on rational combinations designed to enhance responses and prevent relapse. This could include combining a potent TKI with conventional chemotherapy or a hypomethylating agent. Another innovative approach could involve a dual-pronged strategy, combining a clone-directed TKI with an eosinophil-directed biologic, such as an anti-IL-5 antibody, to simultaneously eradicate the malignant clone and mitigate the immediate end-organ damage caused by mature eosinophils. Investigating synergistic combinations, such as TKIs with BCL2 inhibitors like venetoclax, also represents a biologically plausible, hypothesis-generating strategy for overcoming resistance, warranting evaluation in carefully designed prospective trials. Recent translational studies highlight potential strategies, such as combining TKIs with venetoclax in high-risk AML and MDS, or dual targeting with anti-IL-5 biologics to control eosinophil-mediated organ damage alongside clonal inhibition. Basket trial designs, exemplified by FIGHT-207, demonstrate the feasibility of pooling rare molecular subsets to accelerate drug development [56].

Expert Commentary and Recommendations

In clinical practice, the immediate and comprehensive molecular characterization of any patient presenting with persistent, unexplained eosinophilia is imperative. The clinical approach must move beyond simply controlling the eosinophil count and focus on identifying and targeting the underlying clonal driver. This necessitates a workup that includes a bone marrow examination with cytogenetics and, at a minimum, a targeted FISH or RT-PCR panel for rearrangements of PDGFRA, PDGFRB, and FGFR1. The increasing accessibility and utility of broad, NGS panels can further clarify the diagnosis, identify co-mutations of prognostic relevance, and uncover rare, targetable alterations in patients who are negative for the classic fusions [12, 19].

The integration of emerging agents into real-world practice requires careful, evidence-based patient selection. For a patient with PDGFRA-rearranged disease who develops imatinib resistance via a known mutation like T674I or D842V, a switch to a next-generation inhibitor such as avapritinib is supported by mechanistic rationale and clinical experience in PDGFRA-mutant disease [29, 30]. For the historically dismal diagnosis of an FGFR1-rearranged neoplasm, the recent approval of pemigatinib represents a fundamental shift in the standard of care [39, 40], and it should be considered early in the treatment course for eligible patients, frequently as a bridge to allogeneic stem cell transplantation when appropriate. This approval, based on the FIGHT-203 trial, establishes pemigatinib as a preferred targeted option early in the treatment course, with additional long-term data (2025) confirming its role as a durable bridge to curative transplant. In a patient with refractory HES where a JAK2 mutation is identified, a therapeutic trial of ruxolitinib represents a rational, molecularly guided approach, preferably within a clinical trial setting [42, 43]. For patients with severe eosinophilia causing end-organ damage but without a defined kinase target, biologics can be steroid-sparing and organ-protective. Mepolizumab is on-label for HES and reduces flares and steroid exposure. Benralizumab—approved for asthma and eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis but not for HES—may be considered off-label in refractory cases, supported by clinical trial and real-world data, ideally in consultation with centers experienced in eosinophilic disorders [17, 46].

Looking forward, a key recommendation is the centralization of care for these rare diseases at, or at least in consultation with, specialized centers of excellence. This facilitates patient access not only to a multidisciplinary team but also to advanced molecular diagnostics and, most critically, to clinical trials. The design of these future trials must adapt to the reality of small patient populations. Innovative designs like basket trials, which enroll patients based on a shared molecular target regardless of histology, and platform trials are more efficient and appropriate than traditional randomized studies. For these trials, biomarker selection is paramount. We must move beyond simple response assessment to incorporate serial MRD monitoring as a primary endpoint, with the goal of establishing its utility in guiding treatment duration and predicting relapse, as has been successfully done in chronic myeloid leukemia.

Conclusions

The therapeutic landscape for myeloproliferative hypereosinophilic syndromes and related disorders has been transformed, shifting from an era of empirical, non-specific treatment to one defined by molecular precision. The substantial and durable success of imatinib in patients with FIP1L1-PDGFRA-positive disease serves as a model for modern oncology, demonstrating how discovering a core pathogenic driver can lead to a highly effective, life-altering therapy. This success, however, has not been universal. The field has been simultaneously defined by the persistent and considerable challenges posed by other molecular subtypes, particularly the aggressive and historically chemo-refractory FGFR1-rearranged neoplasms, which have long represented a significant unmet need. Nevertheless, much of the therapeutic evidence for rarer molecular subtypes remains derived from non-randomized studies and small cohorts, emphasizing the ongoing need for international registries, innovative trial designs, and long-term follow-up.

The future, however, holds significant promise. The recent approval of pemigatinib for FGFR1-rearranged disease marks a critical breakthrough, finally providing a targeted and effective option for these high-risk patients. This progress, alongside the development of other next-generation inhibitors and novel therapeutic combinations, reflects ongoing innovation. A truly collaborative, research-oriented approach will be essential to build on this momentum. The path forward requires the routine integration of comprehensive molecular diagnostics like next-generation sequencing to identify all potential targets, the clinical validation of minimal residual disease monitoring to guide and personalize treatment intensity, and the expansion of international registries and innovative trial designs to accelerate progress for all patients with these rare hematologic malignancies.
