Causal relations of trace elements and nutrients with insomnia: A Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Lei Luo, Desheng Zhou, Qihou Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study assesses causal relationships between serum trace elements/nutrients and insomnia using Mendelian randomization (MR) methods. Data was collected from genome-wide association studies, single-nucleotide polymorphisms associated with serum trace elements (iron, zinc, selenium, copper, calcium, potassium, magnesium) and nutrients (carotene, folate, vitamin A, vitamin B12, vitamin B6, vitamin C, vitamin D, vitamin E) were selected as instrumental variables for a 2-sample MR analysis, using insomnia genome-wide association study summary statistics from FinnGen. The primary analysis employed the inverse variance weighted method, supplemented by MR-Egger regression (MR-Egger), weighted median (WME), simple mode, and weighted mode approaches. Causal effects were estimated using inverse variance weighted-derived P-values, odds ratios (ORs), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Sensitivity analyses evaluated pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept test), heterogeneity (Cochran Q test), and robustness (leave-one-out analysis). The OR and 95% CI indicated a possible causal link between magnesium levels and insomnia risk (OR = 0.869, 95% CI = 0.763–0.990, P < .05). No significant causal associations were observed for other serum trace elements or nutrients. Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using MR-Egger and Mendelian Randomization Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO), with no evidence of bias (MR-Egger intercept P > .05; MR-PRESSO global test P > .05). Cochran Q test revealed no heterogeneity, and leave-one-out analysis confirmed the stability of the causal effect. These sensitivity analyses collectively support the robustness of the Mendelian randomization results. MR analysis indicated a potential association between magnesium concentration and insomnia risk. However, further research is needed to elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms and establish the clinical significance of these associations.

1. Introduction

Insomnia is primarily characterized by difficulties in sleep initiation, sleep maintenance, early morning awakenings, and non-restorative sleep.[1] Evidence indicates that chronic insomnia may elevate risks for multiple adverse health outcomes. It induces sympathetic nervous system hyperactivity, resulting in increased heart rate, elevated blood pressure, and accelerated atherosclerosis progression.[2] Furthermore, chronic insomnia upregulates proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., C-reactive protein, interleukin-6), which may promote vascular endothelial dysfunction and thereby exacerbate atherosclerotic processes.[3,4] Concurrently, Insomnia can also over-activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, leading to a disruption in the secretion rhythm of cortisol.[5] Through these interconnected immunologic-endocrine-metabolic pathways, insomnia ultimately increases susceptibility to various chronic diseases including hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular disorders.[6]

Current research on diet and sleep primarily focuses on macronutrients, fatty acids, and carbohydrates.[7] With advances in metabolomics, investigators have begun exploring novel approaches to modulate insomnia through metabolic regulation.[8,9] Notably, although trace elements are required in smaller quantities than other essential nutrients, they play critical roles in maintaining physiological homeostasis.[10,11] Existing studies on trace elements and insomnia remain limited, largely due to challenges in conducting large-scale clinical trials and the susceptibility of trace element levels to confounding environmental factors.[12] These limitations highlight the potential utility of Mendelian randomization approaches in exploring possible causal associations between serum trace elements/nutrients and insomnia risk, while acknowledging the need for further validation.

Although rigorously designed randomized controlled trials are considered the gold standard for establishing causality by effectively controlling potential confounders, they face substantial practical limitations. These include ethical constraints, limited external validity, challenges in maintaining double-blinding, uncontrollable behavioral or environmental factors, insufficient statistical power, and prohibitive time and financial costs.[13,14] The accumulation of large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) data has established Mendelian randomization (MR) as a novel approach for causal inference.[15] As a research method that integrates epidemiology and genetics, MR leverages naturally occurring genetic variations as instrumental variables to simulate randomized controlled trials. This approach allows for a more accurate assessment of the impact of specific factors on outcomes, effectively circumventing the confounding biases commonly encountered in traditional epidemiological studies.[16] In MR analyses, single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with the exposure of interest can serve as instrumental variables. Given that these instrumental variables are independent of other confounding factors, they can be used to evaluate the causal relationship between exposure and outcome events.

Using a 2-sample MR analysis, we evaluated 7 trace elements and 8 nutrients in relation to insomnia. These findings may offer preliminary insights into the possible role of dietary factors in insomnia.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design

A 2-sample MR analysis was performed to assess causal relationships, with serum trace elements (iron, zinc, selenium, copper, calcium, potassium, magnesium) and nutrients (carotene, folate, vitamin A, vitamin B12, vitamin B6, vitamin C, vitamin D, vitamin E) as exposures and insomnia as the outcome. The analysis was based on 3 core MR assumptions: Relevance assumption: The selected SNPs must be strongly associated with the exposure factors (serum trace elements and nutrients). Independence assumption: The SNPs must be independent of any potential confounders between exposures and outcome. Exclusion restriction assumption: The SNPs must influence the outcome (insomnia) only through the exposure factors, with no direct pleiotropic effects.

2.2. Data sources

All data used in this study were obtained from publicly available GWAS. Serum trace element (iron, zinc, selenium, copper, calcium, potassium, magnesium) and nutrient (carotene, folate, vitamin A, vitamin B12, vitamin B6, vitamin C, vitamin D, vitamin E) data were sourced from the IEU Open GWAS project (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). Insomnia GWAS summary statistics were obtained from FinnGen (https://r10.finngen.fi/)https://www.finngen.fi/en;downloadlink:https://storage.googleapis.com/finngen-public-data-r10/summary_stats/finngen_R10_SLEEP.gz. 2.3. Selection of instrumental variable

In accordance with STROBE-MR guidelines,[17] SNPs for each serum trace element and nutrient were selected through the following quality control steps: A genome-wide significance threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻⁶ was applied.[18,19] Linkage disequilibrium pruning was performed using the clump function with parameters set at r² < 0.001 and the kb = 10 000.[20] Calculate the F-statistic for each SNP and exclude SNPs with F < 10 to avoid bias due to weak instrumental variables. We quantified the strength of genetic instruments by calculating the proportion of variance explained (R²) using the following formula: R² = [2×β²×(1-EAF) × EAF]/[2×β²×(1-EAF) × EAF + 2 × SE²×N × (1-EAF) × EAF],where β is the estimated genetic effect size, EAF the effect allele frequency, SE the standard error, and N is the sample size.[21,22] Instrument strength was further assessed through the F-statistic: F = [R²×(N-k-1)]/[k × (1-R²)], where R² is the cumulative variance explained by all instrumental variables, N the sample size, and k the number of SNPs. SNPs with F < 10 were excluded to eliminate weak instrument bias.[23,24] For the assessment of pleiotropy, we conducted MR-PRESSO analysis to identify and remove outliers that exhibited horizontal pleiotropy (P < .05). The remaining valid instrumental variables were retained for the primary Mendelian randomization analysis.[25]

2.4. MR analysis

The R software package “Two Sample MR” was used for 2-sample MR analysis, We performed allele alignment using the “harmonise_data()”function from the TwoSampleMR package. The conservative mode (“action = 2”) was applied to handle strand orientation issues, and the following 5 methods were used: IVW, MR-Egger, WME, simple mode (SM), and weighted mode (WM). The IVW method is the predominant analytical method and it assumes that all Instrumental Variables (IVs) are valid. The WME method gives more weight to effective IVs and still provides a valid estimate when a subset of IVs are invalid.[26,27] The intercept P-value in the MR-Egger analysis was used to assess horizontal pleiotropy.[28,29] The simple mode represents an unweighted empirical density function for causal estimation.[30] The results of above analysis can be expressed by forest plot, OR value and 95% CI, and the results have statistical significance when IVW P < .05.[31]

Sensitivity analyses included Cochran Q test for heterogeneity (reported for IVW), the MR‑Egger intercept test and MR‑PRESSO for directional horizontal pleiotropy, and leave‑one‑out analysis to assess the influence of individual SNPs. Cochran Q P‑value < .05 was considered evidence of heterogeneity; in results Q P > .05, indicating no significant heterogeneity. Directional pleiotropy was assessed by the MR‑Egger intercept and by the MR‑PRESSO global test, which also detects and corrects outlier SNPs.[32,33] Leave‑one‑out analyses were performed by iteratively excluding each SNP and re‑estimating the effect; minimal change in the overall estimate indicates no single SNP drives the association. If heterogeneity and directional pleiotropy are not detected and estimates from multiple MR methods are consistent, the causal inference is more credible, although these tests have limited power and cannot fully exclude all sources of bias.

3. Results

3.1. Instrumental variable of expose

Based on the specified criteria, SNPs that satisfied all 3 hypotheses were selected, and the F-value of the instrumental variable exceeded 10 for all cases. However, when applying a screening criterion of P < 5 × 10-8, the number of available SNPs was insufficient for further analysis. Therefore, in accordance with STROBE-MR research specifications and literature review, the threshold was adjusted to P < 5 × 10-6. The final analysis included the following number of SNPs for each exposure: copper (6), selenium (6), zinc (8), folate (12), carotene (15), potassium (14), vitamin D (13), vitamin C (10), vitamin B12 (8), iron (11), vitamin E (12), magnesium (17), vitamin B6 (17), calcium (18), and vitamin A (11). (Supplementary Materials 1–2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q728).

3.2. Causal relations between expose and outcome

A total of 178 SNPS were retained after harmonise_data () function coordination, and 6 SNPs were eliminated due to palindromic or strand orientation issues. IVW, MR Egger, WME, SM, WM methods were employed to investigate the relationship between copper, selenium, zinc, potassium, iron, magnesium, calcium, folate, carotene, vitamins D, C, B12, E, B6, A and insomnia (Supplementary Material 2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q728).

According to the above analysis results, IVW method showed that a possible causal link between magnesium levels and insomnia risk (odds ratio [OR] = 0.869, 95% CI = 0.763–0.990, P < .05), indicating that magnesium concentration may be a potential regulatory factor in insomnia (Fig. 1). The results of IVW analysis of the remaining serum trace elements and nutrients were P > .05, indicating that there was no causal relation between the remaining serum trace elements/nutrients and insomnia. The causal association estimates of the 5 methods are similar and the direction is similar, which also suggests a potential causal relationship between magnesium concentration and insomnia (Supplementary Materials 1–2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q728).

3.3. Sensitivity analysis

Horizontal pleiotropy test: The results of MR-Egger and MR-PRESSO test show that there is no horizontal pleiotropy in the correlation between magnesium and insomnia (Table 2). To avoid excessive bias, a series of sensitivity analyses were performed to test the reliability of the MR Analysis. Cochran Q test showed no heterogeneity in the results, and the funnel plot showed basic symmetry. Through Leave-one-out analysis (Fig. 2A–D), each SNP was eliminated one by one, and no SNP was found to have a significant impact on the results, indicating that the observed causal effect was stable.

Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the MR‑Egger intercept test and MR‑PRESSO. MR‑Egger intercept and the MR‑PRESSO global test showed no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (Table 2). To evaluate robustness of the MR findings, we performed a series of sensitivity analyses. Heterogeneity among SNP‑specific estimates was tested using Cochran Q statistic in the IVW framework, which did not indicate statistically significant heterogeneity. The funnel plot showed no obvious asymmetry. Leave‑one‑out analyses were conducted by iteratively excluding each SNP and re‑estimating the causal effect (Fig. 2A–D); exclusion of any single SNP did not materially change the overall estimate, suggesting that the results were not driven by an individual variant. Taken together, these sensitivity analyses support the robustness of the observed association, although statistical tests for pleiotropy and heterogeneity have limited power and cannot completely rule out all potential biases.

4. Discussion

This study utilized publicly available GWAS data to investigate causal relationships between serum trace elements/nutrients and sleep disorders. The Mendelian randomization analysis provided evidence supporting a causal relationship between magnesium levels and sleep disorders. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings: Cochran Q test and leave-one-out analysis indicated no significant influence from individual SNPs; MR-Egger intercept and MR-PRESSO global test showed no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy. No significant causal associations were observed between other serum trace elements/nutrients and insomnia.

The pathogenesis of insomnia involves multiple factors including genetic predisposition, stress triggers, and maladaptive sleep behaviors.[34] In recent years, there has been a growing recognition of the importance of genetic factors in the etiology of insomnia. Lane et al identified 57 risk loci associated with insomnia symptoms through genome-wide association analysis of self-reported data from the UK Biobank cohort (n = 453,379), demonstrating significant genetic correlations with coronary artery disease, depressive symptoms, and subjective well-being.[35] In a larger-scale study of 1331,010 individuals with insomnia, Jansen et al detected 202 genomic loci encompassing 248 independent SNPs.[36]several candidate genes within these loci have established biological relevance to sleep regulation. For example, DMRT1 influences circadian rhythms by regulating clock genes (CLOCK and BMAL1), thereby indirectly affecting sleep cycles.[37,38] CACNA1C modulates neuronal excitability and neurotransmitter release, impacting sleep-wake transitions through calcium influx regulation.[39] Kantojärvi K et al demonstrated that mutations in CACNA1C may alter sleep onset time by modifying neuronal excitability.[40] UFL1 may protect neuronal cells by participating in the regulation of intracellular protein homeostasis, thereby indirectly modulating sleep rhythms.[41,42] Amin et al discovered that variations in the RBFOX3 gene are associated with sleep latency. [43] Plasil SL et al Further revealed that RBFOX3 may regulate the splicing or expression of γ-aminobutyric acid–ergic (GABAergic) neuron-related genes, such as γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptor subunit-encoding genes, thereby influencing the composition and function of γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptors.[44] Notably, GABAA receptors serve as the primary target for benzodiazepines and other sleep-inducing medications.[45] These findings provide new molecular-level evidence for the genetic mechanisms underlying insomnia.

These studies on genetic mechanisms have elucidated the pivotal role of neurotransmitter systems in sleep regulation, while recent advances in nutrigenomics reveal that such neurochemical balance can also be modulated by micronutrient homeostasis. As essential micronutrients, metabolic dysregulation of trace elements/nutrients is closely associated with various health issues.[46,47] Magnesium, the fourth most abundant trace element in the human body, plays a critical role in key physiological processes, including neural transmission and energy metabolism.[48,49] Although the Dietary Guidelines for Americans recommend a daily magnesium intake of 320 mg for adult women and 420 mg for adult men,[50,51] modern dietary patterns have led to widespread magnesium insufficiency.[52] This chronic suboptimal magnesium status may impair sleep quality by disrupting neurotransmitter homeostasis.[53]

GABA is the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system, which functions to suppress neuronal overexcitation and thereby reduce brain activity.[54] During sleep, increased activity of GABAergic neurons and activation of GABAA receptors facilitate faster sleep onset and maintenance of deep sleep.[55] Studies have shown that magnesium ion deficiency decreases membrane expression of GABAA receptors,[56] Additionally, novel magnesium ion -L-theanine complexes can improve sleep quality by enhancing GABAA receptor clustering.[57] Meanwhile, magnesium also affects glutamate release. Glutamate, an excitatory neurotransmitter, can cause neuronal overexcitation and disrupt sleep when overly active.[58] Studies indicate that magnesium modulates glutamic acid metabolism by upregulating the expression of glutamate decarboxylase 67.[59] Additionally, magnesium exerts an inhibitory effect on glutamatergic signaling by antagonizing N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors through voltage-dependent blockade of their associated calcium channels.[60] These findings suggest that magnesium may regulate sleep homeostasis through multiple pathways, including modulation of GABAergic activity and glutamatergic neurotransmission.

A Japanese study investigating low-energy intake and sleep quality in young women revealed significant correlations between sleep quality indices and multiple micronutrient levels, including magnesium.[61] Similarly, U.S. NHANES 2005–2014 data demonstrated a dose-dependent relationship between magnesium insufficiency (which is prevalent among adults aged ≥ 20 years) and sleep disturbances.[62] Furthermore, a randomized controlled trial examining the effects of nutritional interventions on sleep parameters (including latency, duration, and efficiency) demonstrated that a magnesium-containing multivitamin supplement can improve sleep quality in subjects.[63] Although observational studies have shown an association between magnesium levels and sleep disorders, the current evidence only tentatively supports a causal relationship. The generalizability of this association across diverse populations requires further investigation.

This study employed Mendelian randomization to elucidate the potential mechanisms by which magnesium concentration acts as a regulatory factor in improving insomnia, providing references for maintaining optimal magnesium levels to alleviate insomnia and proposing novel approaches for cost-effective and safe insomnia treatment through trace element supplementation. However, it should be noted that the current evidence is derived exclusively from European populations, given the substantial differences among populations in dietary habits, genetic background, and nutrient metabolism, the generalizability of these findings to other ethnic groups remains unclear. To strengthen the evidence base, future research could be enhanced by conducting multi-ethnic, multicenter cohort studies to systematically evaluate population differences in the magnesium-insomnia relationship. Future studies also should include well-designed randomized controlled trials comparing different magnesium formulations and dosing regimens for insomnia management, with standardized assessments of both sleep parameters (including latency, duration, and efficiency) and metabolic biomarkers. Develop personalized magnesium supplementation strategies based on individual nutritional status, genetic background, and comorbidity profiles, thereby generating stronger evidence for clinical practice.
