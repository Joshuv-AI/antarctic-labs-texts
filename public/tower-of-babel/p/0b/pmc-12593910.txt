Traumatic spigelian hernia in an adolescent following a blunt abdominal trauma: a rare case report and literature review
Authors: Mahdi Aljamal, Zainah Amjad Issa, Ali Shakhshir, Omar Abu-Zaydeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionspigelian hernias are rare anterior abdominal wall hernias, accounting for less than 2% of all hernias. Introduction

Traumatic spigelian hernia is an uncommon subtype of traumatic abdominal wall hernia involving an anterior abdominal wall defect bounded medially by the rectus abdominis muscle and laterally by the semilunar line, usually caused by blunt trauma to the abdomen. Although blunt abdominal wall trauma has a high incidence, cases of traumatic spigelian hernia were first described in 1933 [1]. spigelian hernias account for 0.12% to 2% of total abdominal hernia cases, making traumatic spigelian hernias even rarer [2]. Injuries to the spigelian aponeurosis area are highly associated with motor vehicle collisions, increased pressure intra-abdominally, and an already weakened spigelian fascia. The usual clinical presentation may differ among patients; some cases may be asymptomatic, while others present with dull abdominal pain with a visible bulge [3]. spigelian hernias after trauma can co-occur with several other mesenteric injuries, further complicating the overall case, such as bowel strangulation and perforation with potential fecal contamination, retroperitoneal involvement, and hematoma formation [4]. This case is of an adolescent male who suffered blunt trauma to the abdomen after falling onto bicycle handlebars, resulting in a traumatic spigelian hernia with an ileal mesenteric defect. In acknowledging the rarity of traumatic spigelian hernias in terms of incidence and the usual injured location, this case deems reporting necessary as it contributes to raising awareness among physicians regarding such rare, traumatic incidents with the potential of delayed or entirely missed diagnoses.

Case presentation

After suffering trauma from a bicycle handlebar injury, a 15-year-old male patient with a history of cerebral palsy and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) arrived at the emergency room complaining of right lower quadrant (RLQ) abdominal pain. Upon examination, the patient was found to have stable vital signs in the ER, with a localized bruise (2*2 cm) in the RLQ and swelling in addition to abrasions (Fig. 1), associated with RLQ abdominal tenderness, but without other signs or nausea, vomiting, or other positive findings. Two large-bore cannulas were inserted, and an intravenous (IV) fluid bolus was given. An IV antibiotic dose was given in the emergency room, blood tests (liver function tests, CBC, and kidney function tests) were sent, and the results returned were normal.

The patient was initially evaluated by abdominal ultrasound, which found a fascial defect in the anterior abdominal wall lateral to the right rectus abdominis muscle with herniated bowel loops, without free fluid or bowel wall edema. As the patient was stable, he underwent an abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan with IV contrast and was found to have a right lower quadrant abdominal wall defect consistent with a spigelian hernia (Fig. 2).

The decision was made to go for an exploratory laparotomy, which was done through a 5 cm right RLQ oblique incision along the linea semilunaris. Intraoperatively, a traumatic spigelian (ventral) hernia with a fascial defect of 2–3 cm was found along with an ileal mesenteric defect, with no bowel injury or perforation. Primary repair of the defect was performed, and bowel resection was not necessary. After the surgery, the patient started on an oral diet and was discharged on day 2 post-operation in excellent general condition.

Discussion

Identifying traumatic abdominal wall hernias meets the criteria for the immediate appearance of an abdominal hernia following blunt trauma, which involves disruptions of muscle and fascia without skin penetration, as defined by Damschen et al. As mentioned before, traumatic spigelian hernias constitute the lowest proportion of total traumatic abdominal wall hernias, accounting for less than 2% [5]. The usual location of spigelian hernias involves the spigelian aponeuroses between the semilunar line and rectus abdominis muscle, laterally and medially, respectively. Other than an underlying traumatic scenario, underlying conditions that raise the intra-abdominal pressure or deteriorating abdominal wall aponeurotic layers due to aging predispose to spigelian hernia.

In the traumatic sense, the underlying pathophysiology of spigelian hernia formation involves a rapid increase in intra-abdominal pressure following an abrupt abdominal blow [6]. The blow does not always directly impact the spigelian aponeurotic area; it may even occur far from the hernia itself. The relation of the impact’s location in regard to the spigelian hernia on the impact’s nature: diffuse or focal. Studies have more commonly reported low correlations between the site of abdominal impact and the herniated site [7]. Thus, traumatic spigelian hernias have typically arisen in a common location, between the semilunar line and the rectus abdominis muscle, in 90% of cases [8]. The repetition in the anatomical site of spigelian hernias adds to the significance of the clinical setting in the diagnosis process, as the typical presentation includes pain and immediate bulging in the spigelian aponeurosis area of the abdomen.

The reviewed literature includes 31 case reports as shown in Table 1, ranging in publication years from 1956 to 2025. However, only 22 of the 31 cases were specifically published as reports of traumatic spigelian hernia. The rest of the cases were not reported as traumatic spigelian hernias, despite sharing a similar mechanism of injury in motorcycle/bicycle handlebar accidents. Therefore, the report will only discuss the 22 case reports published specifically as cases of traumatic spigelian hernia. Twenty-one out of twenty-two cases involved males, and 1 case was reported of a female, the only female case ever reported [9]. Out of the 22 cases, 9 were pediatric, involving patients under the age of 18 years, including the first-ever case to report a traumatic spigelian hernia in a pediatric patient in 1967 [10]. Although traumatic spigelian hernias are rare, children are found to be more vulnerable due to not having a fully developed abdominal wall yet, along with a smaller overall anteroposterior diameter when compared to fully grown adults [11]. spigelian hernias have more commonly been congenital, but a few cases have been reported following a traumatic abdominal blunt injury [12].

Almost all of the twenty-two reports followed a similar manner in diagnosis that began with history and physical examination, which recognized an immediate abdominal bulge after the traumatic incident, followed by ultrasound imaging, and then intravenous contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) that confirmed the presence of spigelian hernias. Thakur et al. (2013) [13] described ultrasonography as the gold standard for spigelian hernia identification. However, in more recent reports, Singh and Kaur (2019) [14], Tinney et al. (2019) [15], and Yee et al. (2019) [16] all argued that, despite ultrasound imaging accurately defining defect boundaries, it carries the disadvantage of being operator dependent. They further emphasized the higher accuracy, reliability, and ability of CT scans to decrease misdiagnoses preoperatively. The importance of CT scans was demonstrated in Egea et al.’s (2006) [17] report of a patient who was diagnosed with a traumatic spigelian hernia identified by a CT scan conducted 12 months after the patient’s initial visit to the emergency department, where an ultrasound only determined an abdominal hematoma, thereby missing the hernia. The urgency of early imaging was further highlighted in the 2022 report by Kropilak et al. [18], stating that it is a “critical” step in any traumatic abdominal injury’s diagnosis process. Another 2022 report challenged the accuracy of CT scans, which was argued in previous years, reporting a case of a pediatric patient whose spigelian hernia went undiagnosed and was completely missed by a CT and was only visible in a standing position for a short 15 s. This illustrates the value of conducting a physical examination and simple observation before imaging (Mueller et al. 2022) [19]. In this case, physical examination followed by ultrasound and CT were conducted, confirming a spigelian hernia with associated ileal mesenteric defect and ruling out free air or fluid.

In terms of management approach, a spigelian hernia can be repaired through an exploratory laparotomy or laparoscopically, with or without mesh, depending on the case [20]. This case treated the spigelian hernia and ileal mesenteric defect by primary repair through an exploratory laparotomy without the use of mesh, considering the emergency nature of the case and its co-occurrence with an internal injury. Although laparotomies have been recommended, and a range of 31% to 56% of all TAWHs require surgical repair, spigelian hernia recurrence rates are increased. For that, studies have recently begun recommending nonoperative approaches, as a study showed that 71% of patients who suffered TAWHs were repaired nonoperatively and have had no reported recurrences [16]. Other surgeons have suggested using an extraperitoneal laparoscopic technique with mesh [21]. With the increasing number of cases of traumatic spigelian hernia being repaired laparoscopically, only recently have they reported its implantation in pediatric cases, portraying potential in its use in all age groups (Lopez et al. 2010) [21].

Conclusion

Traumatic spigelian hernias have a typical presentation of an immediate abdominal bulge after blunt trauma to the abdomen. They may occur solely or co-occur with other visceral injuries in the abdomen, including injuries to the bowel and small intestinal loops. In most cases, traumatic spigelian hernias develop in a similar location, regardless of where the abdominal trauma occurs. High intra-abdominal pressure is the driver of the injury to the spigelian aponeurosis, leading to herniation. Identification of a spigelian hernia starts with an ultrasound, providing dynamic information regarding the location of the visible abdominal swelling. While contrast-enhanced CT scans confirm the spigelian hernia, they can also lead to missed diagnoses. There is no treatment of choice; the patient’s presentation, extent of injury, and comorbidities determine the approach. Approaches range from invasive laparotomies to less invasive laparoscopic procedures and other non-operative methods of repair. However, surgery is the definitive approach to cases involving intra-abdominal injuries.
