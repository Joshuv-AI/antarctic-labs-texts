A Comparative Study on the Morphology, Structure, and Thermal Behavior of Polybutylene Succinate and Polycaprolactone Biopolymer Blends with Eucomis autumnalis Cellulose
Authors: Fisokuhle Innocentia Kumalo, Moipone Alice Malimabe, Mafereka Francis Tyson Mosoabisane, Thandi Patricia Gumede
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Development of biodegradable polymer composites provides a sustainable alternative to conventional plastics. This study systematically investigates the effect of Eucomis autumnalis (EA) cellulose on the morphological, structural, and thermal behavior of polybutylene succinate (PBS) and polycaprolactone (PCL) blends. EA cellulose was extracted via delignification and hemicellulose removal, yielding 38% cellulose from the leaf biomass. A series of PBS/PCL/EA cellulose composites were prepared using a solution-casting method. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) confirmed retention of characteristic functional groups, with spectra dominated by PCL features, indicating the absence of new chemical bond formation between EA cellulose and the polymer matrix. X-ray powder diffraction (XRPD) revealed that EA cellulose acted as a nucleating agent, enhancing the crystallinity, especially in PCL, while slightly affecting PBS crystallization. A scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis demonstrated preferential localization of EA cellulose within the PBS phase, contributing to improved phase dispersion and interfacial interaction at the morphological level. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) showed enhanced crystallization behavior of PCL at higher EA cellulose loading (5 wt.%), with minimal influence on PBS thermal transitions. A thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) indicated that the thermal stability depends on the polymer composition and cellulose content, with higher PCL fractions contributing to an improved stability. This study provides insight into the structure–property relationships governing PBS/PCL/EA cellulose systems and highlights the potential of EA cellulose as a bio-based additive for tailoring morphological and thermal characteristics of biodegradable polymer blends. A mechanical performance evaluation is recommended for future studies to correlate structural modifications with macroscopic properties.

1. Introduction

The urgency to develop sustainable alternatives to conventional plastics has increased dramatically in recent years. This is due to escalating concerns over environmental pollution and the depletion of non-renewable resources. Biodegradable polymers, especially polybutylene succinate (PBS) and polycaprolactone (PCL), have emerged as promising materials in this quest [1,2,3]. Polybutylene succinate, an aliphatic polyester synthesized from succinic acid and butanediol, is characterized by its biodegradability, high thermal stability, and favorable mechanical properties, making it an attractive material for applications in packaging, agriculture, and biomedical devices [4,5]. Polycaprolactone, another aliphatic polyester, is notable for its low melting temperature, excellent flexibility, and biocompatibility, which has led to its use in drug delivery systems, tissue engineering, and biodegradable packaging [5,6]. Despite their unique advantages, both PBS and PCL face individual limitations, such as the moderate mechanical strength of PBS and slow biodegradation of PCL. This can hinder their broader applicability in high-performance domains [7]. From a commercial perspective, the global demand for PBS is projected to reach over USD 1 billion by 2030 with a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of ~10%, while PCL is expected to grow at ~11% CAGR due to its use in biomedical scaffolds and drug delivery applications. These market trends highlight the importance of improving performance and cost-efficiency of both polymers to meet industrial requirements [8,9].

To overcome these limitations, researchers have explored PBS/PCL blends, aiming to combine their desirable properties. Studies by Penas et al. [6] and Gumede et al. [7] demonstrated that PBS/PCL blends offer an improved flexibility, thermal stability, and biodegradation rates compared to the individual polymers. Nevertheless, the PBS/PCL blends still exhibit phase separation due to poor miscibility, resulting in suboptimal mechanical properties. As a result, the addition of natural fibers or fillers, especially cellulose, has been proposed to address these shortcomings and improve the interfacial compatibility, structural integrity, and thermal stability without compromising the biodegradability.

Despite these advantages, natural fiber-reinforced thermoplastic composites are often limited by their susceptibility to moisture absorption. Natural fibers such as cellulose contain abundant hydroxyl (–OH) groups, which make them inherently hydrophilic. As a result, the incorporation of cellulose into hydrophobic polymer matrices may increase water uptake, leading to swelling, a reduced interfacial adhesion, dimensional instability, and deterioration of the mechanical and thermal performance during long-term service. Moisture sensitivity therefore remains one of the most critical challenges affecting the durability and practical application of biocomposites based on natural fibers and biodegradable thermoplastics. Polymer-blending strategies, such as PBS/PCL systems, may partially mitigate moisture diffusion by improving the matrix compatibility and restricting water penetration pathways. However, the extent to which Eucomis autumnalis cellulose influences moisture behavior in immiscible PBS/PCL blends remains largely unexplored [10].

Natural fiber-reinforced polymer composites have become a major focus in material science due to their environmental benefits, cost effectiveness and promising material properties. Composites containing cellulose derived from various sources such as Eucalyptus globulus, Musa paradisiaca (banana plant) and Saccharum officinarum (sugarcane bagasse) have been shown to enhance the thermal stability, mechanical strength, crystallinity, and biodegradability of polymer composites [11,12,13]. Recent research has shifted toward underutilized indigenous plants as an alternative cellulose source for polymers due to their regional availability, low cost, and sustainable harvesting practices [14,15,16]. Eucomis autumnalis, commonly known as the pineapple lily and native to South Africa, is one such plant. Its typical biomass contains 30–50% cellulose, up to 25% hemicellulose and 10–25% lignin. The high cellulose content with an organized fibrillar structure can influence polymer chain packing and crystallization by acting as nucleation sites, while the lignin and hemicellulose components may modulate interfacial interactions and matrix rigidity [14,17,18].

Previous studies have begun to establish the role of EA cellulose in biopolymers. For instance, Sikhosana et al. [15] reported that PLA/EA cellulose composites exhibited an increased crystallinity and modulus, but dispersion challenges limited the overall mechanical improvements. More recently, Kumalo et al. [19] investigated PBS/EA and PCL/EA composites individually, showing enhanced thermal stability and structural modifications. The fibers influence the packing of the polymer network, acting as reinforcing fillers that can improve thermal characteristics of the biopolymer [15,20,21]. These improvements make it evident that incorporating these fibers helps in creating lightweight, eco-friendly materials that contribute to a regenerative system, supporting the goals of sustainability. While previous studies have investigated EA cellulose in individual PLA, PBS or PCL matrices, this study is the first systematic investigation of EA cellulose in immiscible PBS/PCL blends. The scientific contribution lies in understanding the synergistic effect of dual-polymer blending in the presence of EA cellulose, rather than simple reinforcement of single polymers.

Table 1 summarizes the key thermal, structural and mechanical parameters for PLA, PBS and PCL matrices [14,17,18]. The results from these studies demonstrate that cellulose acts as a functional filler that promotes nucleation, modifies crystallinity, and can enhance thermal stability. However, its effect on mechanical properties depends strongly on interfacial adhesion with the polymer matrix. Despite these promising findings, a detailed comparative study of EA cellulose in PBS/PCL blends has not yet been conducted. This study aims to address this gap regarding the effects of incorporating EA cellulose into PBS/PCL biopolymer blends by focusing on the morphology, crystallinity, and thermal behavior of PBS/PCL blends with EA cellulose at varying blend and EA cellulose ratios. The work focuses on optimizing the blend characteristics with natural EA cellulose, which remains underexplored, to present a novel natural filler source. This research offers new insights into enhancing the physical and thermal properties of these blends, contributing to the development of more sustainable and high-performance biopolymer materials. Through this work, we contribute to the growing body of knowledge on natural fiber-reinforced biocomposites and demonstrate the viability of Eucomis autumnalis cellulose as a functional additive for high-performance, biodegradable materials. Beyond tissue engineering, the improved biopolymer blends could have potential applications in biodegradable packaging, agricultural films, and environmentally friendly consumer products, thereby promoting green alternatives to conventional plastics.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Materials

The Eucomis autumnalis (EA) plant leaves were collected from the District of Maseru, located in Lesotho. The taxonomy of this plant shows that it belongs to the kingdom Plantae under the family of Asparagaceae within the genus of Eucomis.

The chemical and solvents used were of analytical grade. Sodium chloride (NaCl) (0.7%), acetic acid (CH3COOH) (5%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH) (18%), and sodium sulphite (Na2SO3) (5%) were purchased from (Lasec, Bloemfontein, South Africa). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was purchased from (Merck, Johannesburg, South Africa), and chloroform (99.9%, density of 1.49 g cm−3 and molecular weight of 119.38 g mol−1) was purchased from (Consumable & Chemicals supplies in Johannesburg, South Africa).

Poly (butylene succinate) (PBS) pellets extended with 1,6-diisocynatohexane were purchased from (Sigma-Aldrich in Johannesburg, South Africa). The product had a stated purity of ≥99%, a density of 1.3 g cm−3, a melting temperature of 120 °C and a weight-average molecular weight of 63,000 g mol−1 [7].

CapaTM 6500 poly (e-caprolactone (PCL) pellets were purchased from (Southern Chemicals in Johannesburg, South Africa). The PCL had a stated purity of ≥98%, a density of 1.10 g cm−3, a glass transition temperature of −61 °C, a melting temperature of 60 °C, a degree of crystallinity of 35.7%, and a weight-average molecular weight of 113,400 g mol−1 [7].

2.1.1. Extraction and Preparation of Cellulose from Eucomis autumnalis Plant Leaves

To prepare the plant material, the leaves were stripped, then washed with distilled water and oven-dried at 40 °C for 3 weeks. The dried leaves were then ground into a fine powder using a blender. The finely dried powder of E. autumnalis leaves was bleached using 0.7% (w/v) sodium chloride solution. The pH of this mixture was then adjusted to 4 using a 5% acetic acid solution. To further remove lignin, the mixture was heated at 100 °C in a water bath for 5 h and washed with distilled water. The residuals from this mixture were then further heated in 250 mL of a 5% sodium sulphite solution in a water bath at 100 °C for 5 h, followed by a thorough wash with distilled water to completely remove lignin [15].

2.1.2. Isolation of Cellulose

The residues from the previous step (delignification) were further treated with 250 mL of 18% (w/v) sodium hydroxide in a water bath set at 100 °C for 5 h to remove hemicellulose. The resultant product obtained by filtration was washed with distilled water and air-dried. To further remove other impurities, the recovered cellulose was further treated with 50 mL of dimethyllsulf oxide (DMSO), washed with distilled water, and then air-dried. Table 2 shows the chemical composition of the cellulose extracted from E. autumnalis leaves. It shows the mass of the ground leaf powder, the mass of cellulose extracted, and the percentage yield of cellulose. From 60.02 g of ground leaf powder, 23.02 g of cellulose was extracted, resulting in a 38% cellulose yield, which indicates the percentage composition of cellulose in the ground leaf material.

2.2. Preparation of Polymer Blends and Blend Composites

All the samples (neat PBS, neat PCL, their blends, and the blend composites) were prepared via solution casting (as shown in Figure 1) using chloroform as the solvent for both polymers, PBS and PCL. To prepare the thin films of neat PBS and PCL, a mass of 0.5 g of each polymer was weighed and dissolved in 15 mL of chloroform. The samples’ solutions were then stirred for 1 h, then casted into evaporating dishes and air-dried for 4 h. The blends of PBS/PCL and bio-composites filled with different contents of cellulose (1, 3 and 5 wt.%) films were also prepared by weighing different masses that added up to 0.5 g and dissolved in 15 mL of chloroform. The same procedure of stirring for an hour, casting the solution into evaporating dishes and drying for 4 h was also followed. Finally, the thin films were then characterized. Table 3 indicates different compositions of all the prepared samples.

2.3. Characterization

2.3.1. Fourier-Transform Infrared (FTIR) Spectroscopy

The samples were analyzed using a Perkin Elmer Spectrum 100 series spectrometer (PerkinElmer, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) fitted with a PIKE MiracleTM ATR, equipped with a diamond crystal. In this machine, the wavenumber was set over the range of 450–4000 cm−1, using a resolution of 4 cm−1 and a total running of 8 scans.

2.3.2. X-Ray Powder Diffraction (XRPD)

X-ray powder diffraction analyses were done using a Bruker AXS D8 ADVANCED Discover powder diffractometer (Bruker AXS GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany) with Cu Kα (1.5418 Å) radiation. Continuous scans of the samples were carried out at 40 mA and a 40 kV voltage using a locked coupled at a count interval of 1 s. The diffraction spectra were recorded in the 2θ range 20–80° with a scan rate of 0.02°/s. The degree of crystallinity was also calculated using Equation (1).(1)Xc %=AcAc+Aa×100% where Xc is the degree of crystallinity, Ac is the area under the crystalline peaks, and Aa is the area under amorphous peaks.

2.3.3. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

All of the prepared samples were surface-fractured using liquid nitrogen and the morphologies were studied using a JSM-7800F Extreme-Resolution Analytical Field Emission SEM (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The samples were coated with 5 nm of iridium in order to ensure that the charge deposited on the surface of the sample by an electron beam was earthed.

2.3.4. Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)

Perkin-Elmer Pyris 6000 differential scanning calorimetry (PerkinElmer, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) was used to characterize the samples. The analyses were performed under a nitrogen atmosphere flow (20 mL/min). All samples with a mass (~6.70 mg) were subjected to two heating cycles (from 0 to 130 °C) and one cooling cycle (from 130 to 0 °C) at a rate of 10 °C/min. Cold crystallization, the melting temperature and the enthalpies were determined from first heating, cooling, and second heating scans. The degree of crystallinity was also calculated using Equation (2) (for neat polymers) and Equation (3) (for blends and composites).(2)Xc %=ΔHmΔH0m×100%(3)Xc=ΔHmΔH0m×100%ϕ where Xc is the degree of crystallinity, ΔHm is the melting enthalpy of the measured sample, ΔH0m is the melting enthalpy of the 100% crystalline polymer, and ϕ is the weight fraction of a polymer in a blend or composite. Values of 200 J/g [24] and 139 J/g [25] were obtained for PBS and PCL, respectively.

2.3.5. Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)

The thermal stability of the neat polymers, blends and composites was determined on a Perkin-Elmer STA6000 thermogravimetric analyzer (PerkinElmer, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Samples with a mass of ~22 mg were heated from 30 to 650 °C under a flow of nitrogen atmosphere (10 °C/min) and the mass loss % was recorded for each of the samples.

3. Results and Discussion

3.1. Structural Properties: Fourier-Transform Infrared (FTIR) Spectroscopy

The results indicate that FTIR successfully identified the functional groups present in neat PBS, PCL, and EA cellulose, as well as in their various blends and composites. Figure 2a,b shows the FTIR spectra for the PBS/PCL blends at 70/30 and 30/70 w/w ratios with 1, 3, and 5 wt.% EA cellulose. The analysis shows that the blends and composites resembled the spectrum of neat PCL, suggesting that the spectral characteristics are mainly influenced by PCL. PCL had strong characteristic peaks in the FTIR spectrum, such as those associated with the C=O stretching vibration (around 1725 cm−1) and C-O stretching vibrations (around 1240 cm−1), which dominated the spectra of the blends. Therefore, the overall spectral profile remained heavily influenced by PCL, and the relatively low content of EA cellulose (1–5 wt.%) did not significantly change the FTIR profile. EA cellulose contributed hydroxyl (-OH) groups around 3300 cm−1, but these signals were likely overshadowed by PCL. These observations are consistent with previous studies on aliphatic polyester composites reinforced with natural fibers, where hydrophobic PBS/PCL blends exhibited limited chemical interactions with hydrophilic cellulose due to thermodynamic incompatibility [15,21]. In such systems, the reinforcement effect arises primarily from physical interactions (dispersion, entanglement, and nucleation) rather than covalent bonding, which explains why the FTIR spectra showed minimal changes, despite the incorporation of cellulose. Therefore, the observed low chemical interaction is expected for this type of formulation and does not invalidate the reinforcing effects of EA cellulose in the blends.

3.2. Structural Properties: X-Ray Powder Diffraction (XRPD)

Table 4 and Figure 3a,b show the XRPD data and patterns for the 70/30 and 30/70 w/w PBS/PCL blends and the blend composites with EA cellulose at ratios of 1, 3, and 5 wt.%. The XRPD patterns of PBS, PCL and EA cellulose were consistent with the JCPDS card numbers 2100090, 2000827 and 7200097, respectively. The patterns of the blend composites showed a mixed-phase structure with intense characteristic diffraction peaks (−102), (022) at 2θ = 20.1°, 23.2° for PBS; (110), (200) at 2θ = 20.1°, 29.3° for PCL; and (110), (11–1) for EA cellulose at 2θ = 20.1, 23.2°. These only confirm that the blending process did not significantly change the crystal structure of the individual polymers. Figure 3a illustrates the XRPD patterns of the 70/30 w/w PBS/PCL blend and incorporation of x (%) w/w EA cellulose blend composites (for x (%) = 1, 3 and 5). There was a marginal shift in the PCL peak towards lower 2q angles coupled with an increase in the diffraction peaks of the composites with 3 and 5% EA cellulose compared to the blend components. The shifting of peaks suggests minor structural changes, while the increased peak intensity observed in the composites was further evaluated through crystallinity index calculations to confirm changes in the crystalline content [26,27]. Figure 3b shows the XRPD patterns of the 30/70 w/w PBS/PCL blend and its composites. The diffraction peaks due to PCL peaks (2θ = 21.5° and 23.9°) were enhanced in the blend due to the higher content of the more semi-crystalline PCL in this blend [28,29]. There was an increase in the diffraction intensity of the 30/70 w/w PBS/PCL blend with the incorporation of the 5 wt.% EA cellulose, which was confirmed by the corresponding increase in the calculated Xc (%) values (Table 4).

The 70/30 w/w PBS/PCL blend exhibited an Xc of 12.4%, which increased to 18.0% and 21.2% with 3 wt.% and 5 wt.% EA cellulose incorporation, respectively. Similarly, the 30/70 w/w PBS/PCL blend showed an increase in Xc from 19.2% to 25.4% at 5 wt.% EA cellulose. These quantitative results confirm that the observed peak intensity increases correspond to actual increases in crystallinity rather than a qualitative visual interpretation alone.

The increase in crystallinity also brought a high molecular arrangement order in the blend composite, confirming an improved crystallinity [27,28,30,31]. The overall crystallinity of the composites was dependent on the concentration of both PCL and EA cellulose, implying that the material’s specific application can be tailored through the component contents.

3.3. Morphological Properties: Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

The SEM images of the PBS/PCL blends with EA cellulose are presented in Figure 4 and Figure 5. In the case of the PBS/PCL blends, the SEM images of the 70/30 and 30/70 w/w compositions (Figure 4b and Figure 5b) reveal noticeable phase separation. For instance, in the 70/30 w/w blend, PBS formed the continuous phase (rough surface), while PCL was dispersed (smooth surface) and filled the pores in the PBS matrix. On the other hand, in the 30/70 w/w blend, PCL became the continuous phase, with PBS dispersed throughout. This phase separation suggests that the PBS/PCL blends are either immiscible or only partially miscible, as further confirmed by the DSC data [19,32].

The blends with varying EA cellulose contents (1, 3, and 5 wt.%) demonstrated how EA cellulose influences morphology. In the 70/30 w/w PBS/PCL blend, the PBS phase, which had a rough morphology, was continuous, while the PCL phase, which was smooth, was dispersed. When EA cellulose was added to the blend, it was predominantly localized in the PBS matrix, which was likely due to better compatibility between EA cellulose and the more amorphous PBS compared to PCL. At 1 wt.% cellulose, large spheres formed on the PBS matrix. However, as the EA cellulose content increased to 3 wt.%, the cellulose fibers broke into smaller units. At 5 wt.%, the cellulose fibers further decreased in size, becoming crystal-like in structure. These observations suggest good compatibility between EA cellulose and the PBS phase due to the hydrophilicity of PBS encouraging a better dispersion and interaction between the components. Furthermore, the enhanced interaction between the hydroxyl groups of cellulose and the carbonyl of PBS (observed in FTIR) could also contribute to this great affinity between EA cellulose and PBS.

In Figure 5, the SEM images for the neat EA cellulose and the 30/70 w/w PBS/PCL blend with EA cellulose at different concentrations (1, 3, and 5 wt.%) show that the flexible PCL phase, which was dominant in this blend, made it difficult to distinguish between the phases. Nonetheless, the rough, porous PBS phase was still visible. At 1 wt.% EA cellulose, the morphology remained difficult to distinguish, making it challenging to determine where the cellulose was located. However, as the EA cellulose content increased to 3 and 5 wt.%, the cellulose fibers clearly localized in the PBS matrix. In contrast to the 70/30 blend, the size of the fibers in the 30/70 blend did not decrease significantly, but the cellulose dispersed well throughout the PBS phase without forming agglomerates. These morphological observations confirm the role of EA cellulose in influencing the properties of PBS and PBS/PCL blends. The compatibility of EA cellulose with the PBS matrix is particularly evident in the blends, where the fibers are well-dispersed and the surface morphology improves with higher cellulose loadings. The poor interaction between PCL and EA cellulose, as shown in the PCL/EA cellulose composites, further highlights the differences in polymer compatibility within the blends.

3.4. Thermal Properties: Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)

When these two polymers were blended, both of the PBS/PCL blends displayed two distinct crystallization peaks, each corresponding to the individual Tc values of PBS (90.0 °C) and PCL (approximately 29.8 °C) [19] (as shown in Table 5, which summarizes the DSC values for all the investigated samples). This observation indicates that the crystallization processes of each polymer occur independently. For the 70/30 w/w PBS/PCL blend, the Tc of both polymers remained largely unchanged. However, there was a marked reduction in both the intensity and enthalpy of the crystallization peak of PCL, and this peak became broader. This change indicates that the presence of PBS affects PCL by reducing its crystallization rate, possibly due to restricted mobility within the blend. In contrast, the 30/70 w/w PBS/PCL blend showed a slight shift in the crystallization temperature of PCL to a higher value (from 29.8 °C to 30.9 °C), while the Tc of PBS remained unaffected. The PBS crystallization peak also decreased in intensity, which aligns with expectations given the lower PBS content in this blend. The increase in the crystallization temperature of PCL to higher values is an indication that EA cellulose might act as a nucleating agent at higher concentrations, promoting the formation of more ordered crystals [19]. This aligns with the findings of Ramesh et al. [33], who suggested that the effect of organic fillers such as plasticizers or nucleating agents depends on their concentration. In this case, EA cellulose appears to enhance the crystallization of PCL, likely by facilitating the formation of more ordered crystal structures, thus acting as a nucleating agent. However, further studies would be needed to clarify the exact mechanism by which EA cellulose influences crystallization. The DSC results indicate a concentration-dependent dual role of EA cellulose within the PBS/PCL blends. At low EA cellulose loadings (1–3 wt.%), the filler primarily restricts polymer chain mobility, resulting in a reduced crystallization enthalpy and lower crystallinity values (Table 5). This behavior is attributed to steric hindrance effects arising from polymer–filler interactions that limit chain rearrangement during crystallization. In contrast, at higher EA cellulose loadings (5 wt.%), sufficient filler surfaces become available to act as heterogeneous nucleation sites, promoting crystal formation and leading to an increase in crystallinity. Therefore, the crystallization behavior of the composites is governed by competition between mobility restriction at low filler loadings and heterogeneous nucleation at higher filler concentrations.

Figure 6a presents the cooling curves for the 70/30 w/w PBS/PCL blend and its composites. In this blend, the Tc values of PBS and PCL remained stable, but with the addition of EA cellulose, the PCL crystallization peak slightly shifted to a higher temperature (from 28.7 °C to 31.3 °C) and developed a kink at lower temperatures [34]. This upward shift in the PCL Tc suggests that EA cellulose acts as a heterogeneous nucleating agent for PCL, providing additional nucleation sites that facilitate earlier crystal formation during cooling. The appearance of a secondary kink at lower temperatures may indicate a two-step crystallization process, where primary nucleation occurs at the filler surface followed by secondary crystallization of less-constrained PCL chains in the bulk matrix. The PBS crystallization temperature, meanwhile, remained unchanged. Although its peak intensity decreased with increasing EA cellulose content, this suggests the formation of less-perfect PBS crystals. In the 30/70 w/w PBS/PCL blend and its composites, shown in Figure 6b, an observable shift in the crystallization temperature of PCL to lower values (from 30.9 °C to 28.7 °C) occurred with higher EA cellulose loadings (i.e., 5 wt.%). This shift indicates a disruption in the crystallization process of PCL, likely due to the interference by EA cellulose [35,36]. Additionally, a decrease in the intensity of the PBS crystallization peak was observed, which was expected given the lower PBS concentration in this blend.

Overall, the results demonstrate that EA cellulose plays a dual role in the PBS/PCL system: it can function as a heterogeneous nucleating agent under certain compositional conditions (particularly in the 70/30 blend), while at higher loadings or different phase distributions, it may hinder chain mobility and suppress crystallization. The balance between nucleation enhancement and mobility restriction ultimately governs the crystallization behavior of both the PBS and PCL phases in biopolymer composites.

Figure 7 shows the DSC second heating curves of the 70/30 and 30/70 w/w PBS/PCL blends and their composites with EA cellulose at different loadings (1, 3, and 5 wt.%). In the 70/30 and 30/70 w/w PBS/PCL blends, the melting peaks of the individual polymers (PBS and PCL) were detected, indicating that the two polymers were immiscible. Similar observations were also reported by Gumede et al. [37] in their study, indicating the phase separation between these two polymers. For the 70/30 w/w PBS/PCL blend, three distinct melting peaks were observed at around 56.1, 106.9, and 115.1 °C, corresponding to the melting of PCL and the double melting of PBS, respectively. Compared to the neat PBS, the melting temperatures of the PBS peaks in this blend shifted slightly to lower temperatures, while the PCL melting temperature remained relatively unchanged. This suggests that the presence of PCL reduces the lamellar thickness of PBS crystals, facilitating the melting process and resulting in a lower melting temperature (Tm). Moreover, PCL may act as a plasticizing agent, disrupting the PBS chain structure. This observation is supported by the decrease in the degree of crystallinity (Xc) of PBS from 27.5% to 26.6% in this blend, further suggesting that PCL reduces the brittleness of PBS. The presence of PBS also led to a decrease in the Xc of PCL, from 40.0% to 30.9%. In the 30/70 w/w PBS/PCL blend, similar melting peaks were observed at 57.1, 106.8, and 113.5 °C, corresponding to the melting temperatures of PCL and PBS. The melting temperature of PBS decreased further in this blend, from 107.4 and 115.4 °C in neat PBS to 106.8 and 113.5 °C, respectively. This reduction was likely due to the increasing content of PCL, which continued to act as a plasticizer, promoting the disruption of PBS crystallization. This observation is in line with findings from Can et al. [38], who noted a decrease in the melting temperature of PBS (from 115 to 113 °C) upon blending with PCL. This change is attributed to interactions between the two polymers. The partial miscibility between PBS and PCL is further suggested by the narrowing gap between the transitions of the two polymers. As the PCL content increased to 70 wt.%, the Xc of PBS decreased from 27.5% to 24.0%, indicating that PCL lowers the crystallization rate of PBS and promotes the alignment of disordered crystals. The presence of PBS also reduced the Xc of PCL from 40.0% to 36.8%.

In Figure 7a, the incorporation of EA cellulose in the 70/30 PBS/PCL blend did not significantly impact the melting temperatures of either PCL or PBS. However, the degree of crystallinity of PBS decreased from 26.6% to 24.5%, with no clear trend in relation to the EA cellulose loadings. The lack of a clear trend in crystallinity could be attributed to several factors. First, the dispersion and interaction of EA cellulose within the PBS matrix might be uneven, leading to variations in crystallization behavior. Moreover, the presence of EA cellulose could disrupt the crystalline structure of PBS to varying extents, depending on the concentration and distribution of EA cellulose within the blend. It is also possible that the lower concentration of EA cellulose did not significantly affect the crystallization process of PBS, while higher concentrations may have a more pronounced effect, potentially acting as nucleating agents. For PCL, a slight increase in the peak intensity and broadness was observed, suggesting that EA cellulose hindered the crystallization of PCL, resulting in less-ordered crystals. The calculated Xc for PCL decreased for 1 and 3 wt.% EA cellulose (from 40.0% to 29.2%), but a significant increase was observed for 5 wt.% (from 30.9% to 35.1%). Overall, these results further confirm the concentration-dependent behavior of EA cellulose, where low filler contents (1–3 wt.%) predominantly introduce steric hindrance that suppresses crystallization, while higher loadings (5 wt.%) provide effective heterogeneous nucleation sites that enhance crystal development.

The 30/70 w/w PBS/PCL blend and its composites with EA cellulose are shown in Figure 7b. Similar observations to the 70/30 w/w blend were noted. The melting temperature of PBS decreased slightly (from 107.4 and 115.4 °C to 106.8 and 113.5 °C), likely due to the delayed crystallization of PBS caused by the presence of PCL. The melting temperature of PCL in the blend remained unchanged. For composites with 1, 3, and 5 wt.% EA cellulose, the PCL melting temperature peak exhibited a higher intensity (as expected due to the higher amount of PCL in the blend). However, the intensity decreased as the content of EA cellulose increased (Figure 7b). This reduction in intensity, enthalpy, and Xc for 1 and 3 wt.% suggests that EA cellulose affected the lamellae thickness of PCL crystals, resulting in less-perfect crystals. This behavior further suggests that, at low filler concentrations, EA cellulose primarily exerts a hindrance effect rather than an effective nucleation effect. However, for 5 wt.%, a noticeable increase in enthalpy and Xc was observed. These results suggest that, at low concentrations, EA cellulose has little effect on the polymer blend, but as its content increases, it promotes crystallization and acts as a nucleating agent, enhancing the crystalline structure of PCL. This transition from crystallization suppression at low EA cellulose loadings to crystallization promotion at higher loadings clearly demonstrates the dual role of EA cellulose as both a mobility-restricting filler and a heterogeneous nucleating agent, depending on the concentration. These findings further suggest that EA cellulose mainly influences the thermal properties of PCL, as no significant changes were observed for PBS. Overall, the DSC analysis revealed that EA cellulose exhibits a concentration-dependent dual mechanism in PBS/PCL blends. At low loadings (1–3 wt.%), steric hindrance and a restricted molecular mobility dominate, resulting in a reduced crystallinity. At higher loadings (5 wt.%), heterogeneous nucleation becomes dominant due to increased availability of nucleation surfaces, leading to enhanced crystallization. The balance between these competing mechanisms ultimately governs the thermal behavior of PBS/PCL/EA cellulose biocomposites.

3.5. Thermal Decomposition: Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)

Table 6 and Figure 8 and Figure 9 show the results of TGA and DTG for all the investigated samples. The PBS/PCL blends, as shown in Figure 8 and Figure 9, exhibited a single degradation step, which was unexpected because PBS and PCL are typically immiscible. However, the onset degradation temperatures of PBS (347.9 °C) and PCL (360.9 °C) are close, which might explain the merging of their degradation steps in the blend. This overlap in degradation temperatures may be due to similar degradation kinetics between the two polymers, leading to simultaneous degradation in the blends. This observation is consistent with findings from studies such as that by Yu et al. [39], who reported similar behavior in PBS/PCL blends.

Blends of PBS/PCL with EA cellulose exhibited differing behavior depending on the cellulose content. For example, in the 70/30 w/w blend (Figure 8), the 1 wt.% EA cellulose composite showed a thermal degradation curve similar to that of the neat blend, with a slight increase in the degradation temperature compared to neat PBS, but lower than PCL. However, as the EA cellulose content increased to 3 and 5 wt.%, the thermal stability of the blend increased, as confirmed by the progressive rise in the onset degradation temperature (Tonset) listed in Table 6. The increase in the Tonset with increasing EA cellulose loading indicates an enhanced resistance to thermal degradation. On the other hand, the 30/70 w/w blend (Figure 9) with an increasing EA cellulose content (up to 5 wt.%) showed a marked improvement in thermal stability, especially at the 5 wt.% cellulose loading, that surpassed the stability of the neat polymers and blends without cellulose. This improvement in thermal stability can be attributed to several synergistic mechanisms. Firstly, EA cellulose acts as a physical barrier that slows the diffusion of volatile degradation products and heat transfer within the polymer matrix. Secondly, the formation of thermally stable char residues during decomposition provides an insulating protective layer that delays further degradation. Finally, strong interfacial interactions between EA cellulose and polymer chains restrict molecular mobility, thereby increasing the energy required for thermal decomposition. This increase in stability can likely be attributed to the higher PCL content in the blend, which is inherently more thermally stable than PBS. Furthermore, the improved thermal stability of the 30/70 blend with 5 wt.% EA cellulose can be linked to the well-dispersed EA cellulose within the matrix, as observed in the SEM results. The detailed TGA and DTG (derivative thermogravimetric) curves for all of the samples, as shown in Figure 8 and Figure 9, revealed important insights into the impact of EA cellulose on the thermal degradation behavior of PBS/PCL blends and their composites. The data clearly demonstrate that increasing the EA cellulose content generally results in higher onset degradation temperatures, especially at 5 wt.% loading, confirming the improved thermal stability of the PBS/PCL composites.

Overall, the TGA analysis revealed that PCL contributes to the thermal stability of PBS in the blends, as evidenced by the higher onset degradation temperatures compared to neat PBS. The incorporation of EA cellulose generally improved the thermal stability of both PBS and PCL. Specifically, increasing the EA cellulose loading led to an improved thermal stability, as evidenced by the systematic increase in the Tonset values shown in Table 6. This behavior confirms that EA cellulose enhances thermal resistance through barrier effects, increased char formation, and restriction of polymer chain mobility. The exact impact of EA cellulose on the thermal degradation process depends on its concentration, with higher cellulose contents typically leading to a higher thermal stability. Additionally, the localized distribution of cellulose in the blend controlled by our solution-casting process at room temperature may influence the extent of this effect. By carefully selecting the cellulose loading and maintaining mild processing conditions (room-temperature casting and solvent evaporation), we were able to balance fiber incorporation with an acceptable thermal performance. These findings show the potential of tailoring polymer blends and composites for specific applications by adjusting the cellulose content and processing parameters to achieve the desired thermal stability.

4. Conclusions

This study demonstrates the incorporation of Eucomis autumnalis (EA) cellulose into polybutylene succinate (PBS) and polycaprolactone (PCL) blends, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the morphology, crystallization behavior, and thermal stability of the resulting composites. The FTIR analysis confirmed that the chemical structures of the blend composites remained unchanged, with PCL features dominating the spectra. These observations are consistent with the literature on aliphatic polyester–cellulose composites, where thermodynamic incompatibility between hydrophobic PBS/PCL and hydrophilic cellulose results in limited chemical interactions, and physical interactions dominate the reinforcement mechanism, justifying the expected mechanical behavior. XRPD and DSC analyses revealed a concentration-dependent dual role of EA cellulose: low loadings (1–3 wt.%) restricted polymer chain mobility and slightly hindered crystallization, while the higher loading (5 wt.%) promoted heterogeneous nucleation, increasing the crystallinity, especially in the PCL phase. Two distinct melting peaks and the absence of significant glass transition shifts confirmed that the PBS/PCL blends remained largely immiscible, with only partial compatibilization induced by EA cellulose. These findings indicate that the DSC and TGA results serve as critical indicators for thermal behavior and processing performance, highlighting the potential industrial applicability of the composites. The SEM analysis demonstrated selective localization of EA cellulose within the PBS-rich phase, suggesting improved interfacial interactions and dispersion between the blend components, which contributed to reduced phase separation. The TGA results showed that the onset degradation temperature increased with increasing EA cellulose loading, especially at 5 wt.%, reflecting an enhanced thermal stability. This improvement was attributed to barrier effects, thermally stable char formation, and restricted polymer chain mobility during degradation. The blend stability was found to depend on both the polymer ratio and the cellulose content, with higher PCL fractions contributing to an enhanced thermal resistance. In comparison with other natural fibers, EA cellulose uniquely combines selective phase localization, nucleation effects, and tunable thermal behavior, demonstrating its potential as a functional bio-based additive. The use of EA cellulose reinforces circular economy principles, producing 100% bio-based, potentially biodegradable composites and valorizing indigenous biomass. Due to the solvent-cast nature and non-standard geometry of the prepared composite films, mechanical testing was beyond the scope of the present study; thus, the reinforcement conclusions are limited to structural and thermal observations. Future studies should systematically evaluate the tensile, flexural, and impact properties to correlate crystallization behavior and phase localization with mechanical performance, and investigate moisture absorption, biodegradation kinetics, and scalability toward industrial applications.
