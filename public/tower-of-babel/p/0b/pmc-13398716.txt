Clinical and Radiological Outcomes of Monosegmental Minimally Invasive Transforaminal Lumbar Interbody Fusion for High‐Grade Spondylolisthesis: A 3‐Year Observational Study
Authors: Chao Liu, Ya Wang, Changqing Li, Piming Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveHigh‐grade isthmic spondylolisthesis remains surgically controversial, and evidence on minimally invasive, single‐level reduction and fusion with mid‐term follow‐up remains limited. This study aimed to investigate the efficacy and safety of minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (MIS‐TLIF) and percutaneous pedicle screw fixation for high‐grade (Meyerding Grade III) spondylolisthesis.MethodsThis prospective observational case series included 14 consecutive patients with Meyerding Grade III isthmic spondylolisthesis treated at a single institution between 2014 and 2019. All patients underwent monosegmental MIS‐TLIF with percutaneous pedicle screw fixation. Outcome measures included VAS (back and leg pain), ODI, slip percentage, Dubousset lumbosacral angle, and other spinopelvic parameters. Pre‐ and postoperative comparisons were performed using paired t‐tests (p < 0.05).ResultsFourteen patients had a mean age of 54.9 years with a diagnosis of isthmic spondylolisthesis, Meyerding Grade III. The average estimated blood loss was 273.93 mL (range, 197–380 mL). The average operating time was 142.14 min (range, 125–165 min). The average follow‐up duration was 43.5 months, with a range of 38–60 months. In most cases, radicular pain was significantly relieved postoperatively. The mean ODI scores decreased from preoperative 65.56% to postoperative 7.93%. All cases achieved near‐anatomic reduction, and lumbosacral lordosis was significantly corrected. The average slip percentage improved from 64.36% (preop) to 5.88% (postop), and the average Dubousset‐LSA increased from 81.96° (preop) to 105.93° (postop). The overall sagittal profile improved dramatically.ConclusionsMIS‐TLIF and percutaneous pedicle screw fixation may offer a safe and effective treatment option for high‐grade spondylolisthesis, with satisfactory clinical and radiological outcomes.

Introduction

The incidence of spondylolisthesis in the general population is around 4% to 8%. It can cause disabling mechanical low back pain and/or radicular leg pain. The isthmic or lytic type of spondylolisthesis is one of the most common [1]. It is now generally accepted that low‐grade isthmic spondylolisthesis (Meyerding Grades I and II) can be managed with conservative measures such as activity modification, bracing, exercises, and regular six‐monthly radiographs to evaluate progression [2, 3]. If surgery is required, the choice of surgical approach is relatively recognized, and posterior reduction and fusion are the most widely used options [4]. However, due to the complexity of the anatomical structure, the treatment of high‐grade isthmic spondylolisthesis (Meyerding Grade III and above) continues to remain controversial. Various methods of surgical treatment ranging from posterior decompression alone, posterior decompression, and posterolateral fusion without reduction, isolated in situ posterior spinal fusion, staged anteroposterior circumferential arthrodesis in situ, combined anterior reduction and posterior stabilization, L5 vertebrectomy and stabilization from L4 to S1, posterior instrumented reduction, and decompression with posterior arthrodesis have been recommended [5, 6, 7, 8]. Varying rates of success in terms of radiologic and functional results and patient‐based outcomes have been reported. However, the best treatment option for high‐grade spondylolisthesis remains uncertain. More recently, in an effort to further reduce operative morbidity, minimally invasive posterior fusion techniques have been introduced [9]. These techniques essentially utilize a muscle‐splitting approach with specialized retractors and instruments to perform standard open procedures in a less invasive manner. To our knowledge, however, there have been sparse reports describing complete reduction and monosegmental transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion in the treatment of high‐grade spondylolisthesis by minimally invasive techniques. The purpose of this study is to report a new surgical technique to facilitate the safe reduction and stabilization of high‐grade spondylolisthesis.

However, several important limitations remain in the existing literature on high‐grade isthmic spondylolisthesis. First, many previous studies have focused on open reduction and fusion, multi‐segment fixation, or in situ fusion, which may increase surgical trauma or sacrifice additional motion segments. Second, reports specifically evaluating monosegmental minimally invasive reduction and fusion for adult high‐grade slips are still sparse. Third, detailed descriptions of intraoperative reduction strategies tailored to different reduction scenarios are limited, and the available evidence on spinopelvic correction and mid‐term outcomes after such minimally invasive procedures remains insufficient. Therefore, the present study aimed to report our experience with monosegmental MIS‐TLIF and percutaneous pedicle screw fixation in patients with high‐grade isthmic spondylolisthesis, with particular attention to clinical outcomes, radiological correction, fusion status, and the technical feasibility of two stepwise reduction strategies.

The aims of this study were to: (i) evaluate mid‐term clinical outcomes after monosegmental MIS‐TLIF for high‐grade spondylolisthesis; (ii) quantify radiological correction of key sagittal spinopelvic parameters following reduction and fusion; and (iii) assess the feasibility and perioperative safety profile, including complications and fusion status.

Materials and Methods

Ethics approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee of Xin Qiao Hospital (Approval No. 2026‐Research‐140‐01). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Patient Selection and Demographic Information

This study was designed as a prospective observational case series rather than an interventional clinical trial. Therefore, a clinical trial registration number was not applicable. Fourteen consecutive patients with single‐segment high‐grade lumbar isthmic spondylolisthesis (Meyerding Grade III) underwent monosegmental MIS‐TLIF at Xin Qiao Hospital between 2014 and 2019, and all patients were followed up for at least 3 years. Patients presented with low back pain with or without sciatica, and symptoms refractory to conservative treatment with corresponding imaging findings. The exclusion criteria were: (1) revision surgery; (2) multi‐segment disease; and (3) pathological conditions such as spinal malignancy, infection, or trauma.

Clinical and Radiological Evaluation

The data collected for analysis included operating time between open and closure of the skin incision, intraoperative estimated blood loss, postoperative drainage volume, clinical outcome, and complications. The Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) and Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores for back, hip, and lower extremity pain were collected by independent assessors before and after surgery, and updated results were obtained by telephone. The ODI, version 2.0, was used to assess the clinical outcomes of five patients. The sex question (Section 8) is unacceptable in our culture, and most patients are reluctant to answer this section; therefore, it was omitted in this study [10].

Radiological data include measurement of preoperative sagittal balance parameters on x‐rays, Meyerding grading, percentage of slip (%), lumbar lordosis (LL), sacrum inclination (SS), pelvic tilt (PT), pelvic incidence (PI), sagittal vertical axis (SVA), and Dubousset lumbosacral angle (Dubousset‐LSA) [11, 12, 13]. Anteroposterior, lateral, oblique, and flexion‐extension plain radiographs, CT (computerized tomography) scans, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were all performed prior to surgery. After operation, the anteroposterior and flexion‐extension plain radiographs were routinely reviewed, and a CT scan was needed if the fusion could not be determined. Using conventional x‐rays in both static and motion, an impartial radiologist evaluated the fusion rates at the most recent follow‐up. (i) Trabecular bone bridges between adjacent vertebral bodies at the instrumented levels served as proof of a full fusion; (ii) there were no signs of pseudarthrosis, such as translucent shadowing of the fused segments, subsidence of the graft, gaps at the interface between the final plate and the graft, or loosening of internal fixation; (iii) less than 4° segmental movement in dynamic x‐ray.

All radiologic assessments were made by having one independent observer execute the same task twice while recording the plain radiograph pictures produced on a digital radiograph image shown on a Picture Archives and Communication System terminal.

Surgical Technique

Patient Positioning and Fluoroscopic Localization

After induction of general anesthesia, patients were positioned prone on a radiolucent frame. The C‐arm was slightly angled (approximately 20°–25°) to obtain optimal visualization of the target pedicles and disc space on an anteroposterior view of the operative level. Bi‐planar fluoroscopy was used to identify and mark the skin overlying the L5/S1 facet complex.

Tubular Exposure and Bilateral Decompression

A 35‐mm longitudinal skin incision was made approximately 30 mm from the midline, covering the upper and lower levels of the target pedicles. The incision was deepened through the superficial fascia of the paraspinal muscles. Using progressively larger dilators, a muscle‐sparing surgical corridor was established, and an expandable quadrant retractor system was inserted under fluoroscopic guidance. A complete facetectomy was first performed on the side with relatively milder radicular symptoms to identify Kambin's triangle. After removal of the ligamentum flavum, the traversing and exiting nerve roots were decompressed. On the contralateral side, complete facetectomy, partial laminectomy, and flavectomy were performed to achieve adequate bilateral foraminal decompression. Bilateral decompression was routinely performed in all patients to reduce the risk of nerve root tethering during reduction, even in the absence of bilateral symptoms.

Disc Preparation and Cage Placement

After identification of the lateral border of the dura and the traversing and exiting nerve roots, aggressive discectomy was performed through Kambin's triangle. An appropriately sized interbody distractor was introduced to restore disc height and facilitate reduction. Autologous bone graft harvested from the resected facet was packed into the anterior disc space, and a single Capstone cage was inserted obliquely across the disc space. Particular attention was paid to placing the cage as anteriorly as possible in order to improve endplate contact area, restore lumbosacral lordosis, and help reduce shear stress on the slipped vertebra.

Percutaneous Pedicle Screw Insertion

After removal of the quadrant retractor system, the pedicle entry points were identified manually. A modified cannulated needle was advanced through the pedicle into the vertebral body under fluoroscopic guidance, followed by insertion of a blunt‐tipped guidewire into the ventral third of the vertebral body and preparation of the screw tract. Reduction was then performed using the Sextant II pedicle screw system on both sides. A representative case is shown in Figure 1.

Reduction Strategy A: Direct Reduction After Bilateral Release

In patients in whom approximately 50% reduction could be achieved after bilateral posterior release and intervertebral distraction, a cage was placed as far anteriorly as possible, followed by bilateral pedicle screw insertion and definitive reduction under fluoroscopic guidance. Reduction Strategy B: Staged Reduction for Difficult Slips

In cases where the slip could not be reduced to approximately 50% after bilateral release and distraction, staged reduction was performed. First, the pedicle screw on the initial side was inserted and preliminary reduction was achieved while the contralateral disc space remained distracted. The screw was not completely locked at this stage. A slightly larger cage was then inserted on the contralateral side. After that, the contralateral pedicle screw was inserted and a second‐stage reduction was performed, followed by final locking of all nuts. This staged strategy was particularly useful in severe slips that were difficult to reduce safely using a single maneuver. A representative case is shown in Figure 3.

Key Technical Points and Precautions

The critical technical points of this procedure include adequate bilateral decompression before reduction, anterior cage positioning to enhance correction of lumbosacral kyphosis, and staged reduction in difficult cases to reduce nerve root tension. These steps are essential to achieving satisfactory reduction while minimizing neurological complications. A detailed schematic of the operative workflow is shown in Figure 4.

Schematic diagram of Plan A and Plan B. After a facetectomy on one side and the release of the posterior structure of the vertebral body, the intervertebral space was treated. After that, the intervertebral space placed by the test model was extended, and the contralateral operation was performed after maintaining the extended intervertebral space. After the release of the contralateral bone and soft tissue, if a 50% reduction could be achieved according to intraoperative findings or x‐ray fluoroscopy, a cage would be implanted and placed in the anterior column of the lower vertebral body as far as possible. After that, bilateral pedicle screws were placed, and reduction was completed under the guidance of an x‐ray. Plan A is the schematic diagram of this surgical procedure. If the reduction rate of the slip vertebra was not 50% after bilateral posterior structure release and intervertebral space distraction, the pedicle screw on the first side was implanted and the first reduction was completed while the contralateral intervertebral space was distracted; the screw was not completely locked, and a slightly larger model of cage was implanted on the other side. After that, the contralateral pedicle screw was implanted, and the second reduction was completed with all nuts locked. Plan B is the schematic diagram of this surgical procedure.

Statistical Analyses

Clinical and radiological results were analyzed preoperatively, and each patient was reviewed postoperatively (immediate, 3 months, 1 year, and 3 years). Results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical differences were analyzed using paired t‐tests in SPSS Statistics 26. A difference was considered significant when p < 0.05.

Operative Data, Clinical Outcomes, and Complications

The estimated blood loss was 273.93 ± 54.10 mL (range, 197–380 mL). The operating time was 142.14 ± 10.87 min (range, 125–165 min). Discussion

Main findings: In this observational series with a minimum 3‐year follow‐up, monosegmental MIS‐TLIF achieved substantial improvements in pain and disability, near‐anatomic reduction with significant correction of lumbosacral alignment, and a high fusion rate with a low incidence of transient neurological symptoms.

Reduction and Spinopelvic Balance

An abnormal sagittal sequence of the spine with high spondylolisthesis frequently results in changes in the morphology of the spine and pelvis [14]. Therefore, one of the surgical decisions is whether or not surgical reduction is necessary to restore normal spinal pelvic morphology. In situ posterolateral fixation and fusion are widely accepted for the treatment of low‐grade spondylolisthesis; they are also generally considered safe and have good long‐term outcomes [7]. There were also early reports of high‐grade spondylolisthesis; however, in addition to failure to restore normal lumbosacral sequence, in situ fusion may be associated with the progression of the deformity, especially the progression of the slip angle [15]. Hresko et al. divided high spondylolisthesis patients into balanced pelvis and backward‐tilted pelvis according to the morphological characteristics of the pelvis in patients with severe slippage, especially the relationship between PT and SS, in which the overall spinal sequence of the former was close to normal, while the overall spinal sequence of the backward‐tilted pelvis was obviously abnormal, and the compensatory manifestations of lumbosacral kyphosis and trunk were more obvious [16]. Then, on the basis of that, SDSG proposed the SDSG classification of L5/S1 slippage based on the degree of slippage, pelvic parameters, and sagittal balance of the spine, of which 4–6 type was severe slippage. And the severe slippage of the posterior PT is divided into the sagittal balance type (type 5) and the imbalance type (type 6). In SDSG typing, the authors recommend surgical reduction in patients with types 5 and 6 to correct the abnormal sequence of the spine [17].

Alzakri et al. explored the effectiveness of surgical reduction of severe spondylolisthesis in maintaining or restoring normal pelvic balance and its association with improved quality of life. The results suggest that surgical reduction in patients with severe slippage helps correct pelvic imbalance, while patients with postoperative pelvic balance have a better quality of life [18]. Mac‐Thiong et al.'s research also show that the key to improving the quality of life in patients with severe slippage is to achieve a balanced pelvis. One of the best ways is to reset to a low slip [19]. In this study, all slippage rates fell from 61.2% to 10.7%, and balanced pelvic cases rose from 59% to 100%. At 3 years after surgery, the ODI value dropped from 38.13 to 10.32. Reduction, in our opinion, is also advantageous for the restoration of a balanced pelvis and the enhancement of the patient's quality of life.

At the same time, many studies have shown that, in addition to abnormal pelvic parameters, patients with severe slippage have mostly lumbosacral kyphosis, which compensates for increasing LL and reducing thoracic kyphosis [20]. The heavier the lumbosacral kyphosis, the larger the LL and the smaller the TK; the more pronounced the sagittal imbalance in the patient, and even the lumbosacral kyphosis, through a series of compensatory mechanisms, can affect the overall spinal sequence curvature. To improve the abnormal sequence of the spine, reduction of severe spondylolisthesis should include spondylolisthesis and correction of lumbosacral kyphosis [21]. Tanguay et al. found that the degree of lumbosacral kyphosis in patients with lumbar spondylolisthesis was significantly associated with quality of life, and the quality of daily life in patients with a ≥ of 80° in the preoperative Dubousset‐LSA was higher than that in patients with a < of 80° in the preoperative [22]. The Li Weishi study found that correcting Dub‐LSA above 90° and correcting slippage to within I degree significantly improved spine‐pelvic parameters in HDDS patients and improved partial backward‐tilted pelvis to balanced pelvis [23]. In our case, Dubousset‐LSA increased from 78.8° preoperatively to 100.2° after surgery, and we think compression of the posterior column, a bigger cage placed in the anterior center column, and reduction are important strategies to correct kyphosis.

Correction of Lumbosacral Kyphosis

However, reducing the position of patients with high‐grade spondylolisthesis is the most important surgical challenge. Many previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of low‐grade slippage, but surgical reduction is difficult due to the complexity of its anatomy. Ruf et al. [24] earlier reported single‐segment fixation fusion in open surgery for severe lumbar spondylolisthesis, and most previous reports were in situ fixed fusion or fused segment elevation to lumbar 4, but this would increase surgical trauma and sacrifice more motion segments. Therefore, single‐segment fixation, reduction, and fusion should be the best surgical options for patients with high‐grade spondylolisthesis. Since then, many studies have also shown that the fixed fusion of a single segment can achieve a good reduction and correct the clinical effect of lumbosacral kyphosis. Quraishi and Rampersaud first reported the use of MIS technology in the treatment of severe lumbar spondylolisthesis, and for a 24‐year‐old patient undergoing MISTLIF at grade 3 slippage, the slip percentage improved from 68% (preop) to 28% (postop) and the focal alignment to 20° lordosis (SA = 110°) [25]. Later, Deshpande et al. reported that the “rocking” technique is a minimally invasive reduction technique that has a good effect on patients with moderate to severe lumbar spondylolisthesis [26]. The key to this technique is to loosen and lift the pedicle screw by “rocking” during the lifting process. However, most cases are grade 2 slippage (80%), and degenerative slippage is the majority (58%), and it is unknown whether this technique can achieve the same reduction effect for severe slippage. We chose different treatments in all of our cases based on the reduction of the slip vertebral after the second stretch, especially for patients with severe lumbar spondylolisthesis that is difficult to reduce, and the choice of two lift‐up reductions, which proved to be very effective.

Technical Considerations for Minimally Invasive Reduction

Compared to traditional posterior lumbar interbody fusion, the use of a minimally invasive surgical technique called MIS‐TLIF, first described by Holly, represents a recent modification of the approach used to achieve lumbar interbody fusion and is based on a less invasive technique. The incision and approach provide better recovery and clinical outcomes. Wang compared mis and open TLIF procedures for patients with grade 1 and 2 spondylolisthesis, with intraoperative bleeding of 264 mL and 673 mL, respectively, and postoperative drainage of 39 mL and 158 mL, respectively [27]. Lamberg and colleagues conducted a long‐term analysis of different techniques for severe slippage in adolescents [5]. They reported mean ODIs of 9.7 (poster‐lateral fusion group), 8.9 (anterior fusion group), and 3.0 (circumferential group), with a mean follow‐up of 17.2 years. In this study, our patients' mean ODI improved from 62% to a mean of 22%. However, there were no comparisons of preoperative ODI scores in the aforementioned studies, and based on different patient populations (most literature on severe lumbar spondylolisthesis involves pediatric populations), any comparisons should be interpreted with caution. In our case, the ODI score decreased by 70%, reflecting a significant improvement in clinical outcomes. The volume of blood loss (EBL) (261.28 ± 55.72 mL), operative time (168.90 min), and postoperative hospital stay (5 days) in our case were comparable to those in the study by Shufflebarger and Geck [28].

Comparison With Previous Techniques and Clinical Outcomes

Fusion is the most important indicator of long‐term outcomes in lumbar fusion surgery. Especially for HGS patients, due to the large shear force of the slipped vertebral body and the small contact area of the cage, non‐fusion and pseudoarthrosis are two of the challenges of surgery. Previous literature has used multiple approaches to facilitate fusion: anterior surgery, extension of the in situ fusion one or two levels above the slip, specialized lumbo‐sacral instrumentation (transvertebral screw fixation, intrasacral rod, and iliac wing fixation), and/or reduction techniques, and so on. These same methods have brought greater trauma. In our case, the fusion rate was 94.1%, and we believe that the main factors that promoted fusion were: anatomical reduction, reduced shear force on the lumbar 5 vertebral body, and increased cage‐endplate contact area. At the same time, for some patients with severe osteoporosis, we use bone cement screw fixation to better increase stability and promote fusion.

Fusion and Long‐Term Stability

Foot drop or numbness caused by L5 nerve injury is a characteristic neurological complication in HGS. Neurological deficits such as nerve root, cauda equina, and peripheral nerve palsy have been reported in up to 45% of cases, but 90% eventually improve during follow‐up [29, 30]. In addition to traction of the L5 nerve root during reduction, other possible mechanisms of L5 palsy include neurovascular dysfunction, foraminal stenosis, temporary displacement of the L5 nerve root during decompression, and patient hyperextension during positioning [31]. Some authors suggest partial reduction, whereas others believe that gradual restoration of the flexed hip and knee to the extended position over several days may be helpful [32]. Tender et al. reported in two cases of severe spondylolisthesis that slow, staged lifting and reduction, with 3‐min pauses after each 2 mm reduction, may help avoid postoperative L5 nerve injury [33]. In the present study, transient postoperative paresthesia occurred in 3 of 14 patients (21.4%); symptoms were sensory in nature, no motor deficit occurred, and all resolved within 1–2 weeks without special treatment. No permanent neurological deficit or clear adverse effect on final functional recovery was observed. We believe that bilateral decompression and exposure of both L5 nerve roots before reduction are important in severe spondylolisthesis, regardless of whether preoperative L5 symptoms are present. The benefit of exposing the L5 spinal nerves is the release of adhesions between the nerve and the vertebral body, thus allowing the nerve to glide over the vertebral body during reduction. Moreover, we advocate the use of an osteotome to remove hyperplastic osteophytes in the isthmus together with scar ligament tissue, which can quickly and safely expose the L5 nerve root.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths of this study include the focus on a monosegmental minimally invasive reduction‐and‐fusion strategy and the availability of mid‐term follow‐up with comprehensive radiographic assessment. Limitations include the small sample size, single‐center design, lack of a comparative control group, and potential selection bias inherent to an observational series. In addition, the relatively wide age range may have introduced heterogeneity in bone quality, comorbidity burden, and functional expectations. Finally, although the maximum follow‐up reached 60 months, this duration remains insufficient to fully evaluate longer‐term segmental stability, adjacent segment degeneration, and other delayed outcomes after lumbar fusion.

Conclusions

MIS‐TLIF and percutaneous pedicle screw fixation may offer a safe and effective treatment option for high‐grade spondylolisthesis with satisfactory clinical and radiological outcomes. Larger cage placement, bilateral foraminal decompression, and stepwise reduction strategies tailored to the intraoperative reduction scenario appear to be key technical points of the operation.
