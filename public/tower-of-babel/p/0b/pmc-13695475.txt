Epilepsy educational curriculum for nurses: A report from the Nursing Section of the International League Against Epilepsy
Authors: Patricia O. Shafer, Sandra Dewar, Ariane Bernier, Ludivine Rohrer, Nancy Santilli, Carmela Redhead, Tammy Gravel, Gagandeep Singh, Jane R. von Gaudecker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA comprehensive, competency‐based epilepsy curriculum is needed to strengthen nurses' capacity to care for people with epilepsy worldwide. Such a curriculum should be adaptable across nursing roles, practice settings, and geographic regions. This report describes the development of a nursing curriculum for the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE). The Curriculum Workgroup of the Nursing Section of the ILAE developed two educational levels: a core level essential for all nurses and a proficient level for nurses who regularly care for people with epilepsy. Each level comprises competencies across five domains: Role of the Nurse, Epilepsy Basics, Nursing Assessment, Nursing Care, and Self‐Management Education and Care. Competencies were evaluated through an online survey assessing agreement using a five‐point Likert scale. Competencies were accepted if ≥60% of respondents rated them as “extremely important.” Items not meeting consensus were reviewed by the Nursing Workgroup and revised, merged, or rejected based on expert opinion. Overall, 55.8% of competencies met acceptance criteria by survey. Remaining competencies were refined through expert review to finalize the curriculum. More than 90% of respondents supported the curriculum, indicated they would use it in practice, and expressed interest in ILAE courses or programs based on the curriculum. This is the first competency‐based epilepsy curriculum for nurses from an international epilepsy organization for worldwide use. It will form the basis of future online and in‐person educational programs for nurses. The curriculum can be adapted to regional contexts worldwide, helping to address gaps in basic nursing education and continuing professional development.

INTRODUCTION

The World Health Organization (WHO) highlighted epilepsy as a global public health imperative in 2019 with a mandate to reduce the burden of epilepsy around the world. The global incidence of epilepsy varies by country, with low‐ to middle‐income populations experiencing the highest rates of disease and modifiable risk factors. 1 As a spectrum disorder, the epilepsies encompass different seizure types, syndromes, etiologies, and comorbidities. 2 Each can yield different clinical outcomes and challenges. While some epilepsies can be treated by primary care providers, many require specialized care and multiple types of health care professionals (HCP). Unfortunately, specialized epilepsy care is not found in many regions. To address this dilemma, the WHO suggested that a stepped model of epilepsy care be expanded. 3 This entails educating HCP at the primary care and community level, in specialty settings, and in multidisciplinary teams for care of people with epilepsy. Improving the education and competency of nurses in epilepsy at each level of care is needed to accomplish this.

The WHO Intersectoral Global Action Plan on Epilepsy and Other Neurological Disorders 2022–2031 (IGAP) outlines strategies for improving the health and quality of life for people living with epilepsy and their families. 3 Strategic objectives of IGAP include: improving access to epilepsy care; providing effective, timely, and responsive diagnosis, treatment and care; and implementing strategies for promotion and prevention of epilepsy and its consequences. As such, these goals have specific implications for nursing practice. Nurses play a pivotal role in the management of epilepsy. As the largest group of HCP worldwide, they are uniquely positioned to potentially bring about meaningful and wide‐ranging improvements in care. 4 In many regions, nurses serve as frontline providers and may represent the first or sometimes the only HCP encountered by individuals with epilepsy. Their responsibilities span diverse settings, including hospitals, outpatient clinics, community care environments, general medical, and surgical units, as well as specialized epilepsy services.

Nurses contribute substantially across the continuum of epilepsy care. They are integral to the management of acute conditions such as status epilepticus, support patients during diagnostic evaluations and epilepsy surgery, and play a key role in epilepsy monitoring units. Beyond clinical settings, nurses also provide essential care in schools, residential facilities, rehabilitation units, and other community‐based environments. Education of patients and their families constitutes a central component of nursing practice, irrespective of the care setting. Nursing care for people with epilepsy can be inherently holistic and patient‐centered, encompassing not only the diagnostic and therapeutic aspects of disease but also the broader impact of epilepsy on individuals' physical, psychological, and social functioning. 5 , 6 Ultimately, nursing practice is directed toward promoting health and well‐being for patients, their families, and the wider community.

A review of treatment gaps in epilepsy identified the need for education of healthcare providers to decrease delays in diagnosis and treatment of seizures. 7 Further, a needs assessment conducted by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) Nursing Section in 2022 highlighted educational gaps as the greatest barrier for nurses affecting their ability to care for people with epilepsy. Educational needs of nurses may vary due to the diversity of nursing roles, education, and licensure requirements in some areas. For example, advanced practice nursing (APN) roles with prescriptive authority are found more often in high‐income countries as compared to low‐income ones. Manzanares et al. examined differences in APN roles for epilepsy in the United Kingdom and Spain. 8 APNs in the United Kingdom had more advanced postgraduate education and training, broader knowledge of the APN role for epilepsy, and greater participation in nursing research and disease‐specific conferences than nurses in Spain.

It is widely acknowledged that nurses are disadvantaged by limited training in the neurosciences and an absence of formal epilepsy education. In the United States, the American Association of Neuroscience Nurses (AANN) offers a knowledge‐based certificate program for epilepsy nurses, while the Epilepsy Foundation offers a standardized program for school nurses about epilepsy. In the United Kingdom, the Epilepsy Specialist Nurses Association (ESNA) has recently expanded their role‐based competencies for epilepsy nurses. 9 , 10 , 11 China has developed a competency‐based training content system for new neurology nurses that is not specific to epilepsy. 12 Unfortunately, no globally relevant competency‐based curriculum on epilepsy is available to address the gaps in nursing education.

While the ILAE has developed curricula tailored for specialists, neurosurgeons, and primary care providers, a comparable tool for nurses was not established until now. 13 , 14 , 15 The development of a consensus‐based, epilepsy curriculum for nurses that can be used worldwide is therefore considered a critical first step toward strengthening epilepsy education for the nursing workforce. The initiative would provide a structured foundation for knowledge and skill development, enhance the quality and consistency of care, and support the integration of nurses more effectively into multidisciplinary epilepsy management worldwide.

In 2018 a nursing task force began examining the literature for nursing roles, impact and educational needs in epilepsy and established the framework for the nursing competencies in epilepsy. 16 In 2021, the Nursing Section was established and began developing a competency‐based curriculum aligned with ILAE's roadmap for epilepsy education 13 and incorporating the findings of a needs assessment conducted by the section. The curriculum can be adapted to meet regional needs across the globe, availability and access to resources, as well as different nursing roles. This report presents the curriculum development process, including consensus on a tiered approach to competencies and objectives.

METHODS

The Curriculum Workgroup consisted of approximately 20 nurses with expertise in epilepsy and/or nursing education. While some changes in membership of this group have occurred over the past 5 years, core members have remained stable. Nurses from five of the six ILAE regions, representing high‐, middle‐, and low‐income countries, participated in the curriculum development. Additional nurses and stakeholders reviewed the curriculum at different phases of development. An integrative approach was used including review of published literature and existing educational programs, expert opinion, and survey evaluation.

Learning domains

The workgroup designed the curriculum for nurses practicing in any setting. The domains were designed to ensure relevance for nurses across all specialties, settings, and practice types. Therefore, they focus on broad foundational knowledge and skills rather than specific topics. The final curriculum domains include Role of Nurse, Epilepsy Basics, Nursing Assessment, Nursing Care, and Self‐Management Education and Care.

Curriculum level

Two levels of curriculum were iteratively developed: core and proficient. The core curriculum was designed for all nurses regardless of their years of experience or the care setting. The proficient curriculum was designed for nurses who regularly provide care to people with epilepsy and other neurological diseases. A proficient nurse would be expected to complete both curriculum levels to ensure comprehensive knowledge and skill development. The frequency and type of care provided by nurses can vary across domains, depending on the type of nurse expertise and setting. For example, a proficient nurse in a hospital setting would manage acute seizures and administer or adjust medications, while an outpatient nurse may focus on chronic care management. A community‐based nurse may provide frontline care in a specific setting (i.e., school, residential or long‐term care, public health setting), manage resources and referrals, and provide public education on epilepsy. And yet, all these types of nurses must understand the use of antiseizure medications and rescue therapies. The core curriculum primarily targets knowledge and comprehension, while the proficient curriculum includes additional dimensions such as application, synthesis, analysis, and evaluation.

Competencies and objectives

Initially the workgroup developed competencies and objectives for the core and proficient levels for each domain. These underwent multiple revisions following review by the workgroup and external experts until consensus was achieved. Terminology for the objectives followed the recommendations from the Einstein Montefiore Center for Professional Development (https://montefioreeinstein.org/patient‐care/for‐professionals/continuing‐medical‐education), a program aimed at improving competence, performance, and patient outcomes for HCP. The survey to evaluate the curriculum was translated to French, Portuguese, and Spanish by bilingual native speakers and examined for ease of use and clarity. Following survey evaluation of the competencies, expert opinion from the Curriculum Workgroup was again used to edit and refine the competencies and objectives.

Evaluation of competencies

An online survey was conducted in English, Spanish, French, and Portuguese from November 2023 through February 2024 to examine agreement with competencies from nurses and other HCP within ILAE, other epilepsy and nursing organizations, and key nursing stakeholders. A convenience sampling approach was used. The survey was disseminated to nurses in the Nursing Section database and was included in ILAE's newsletter, e‐mail, and social media feeds. Regional leaders of the Nursing Section were asked to disseminate it to nurses, nursing groups, and key stakeholders in their regions. The survey was structured to first collect basic demographic and practice information. Respondents were then asked to review and rate each competency using a five‐point Likert scale from “extremely important” to “not at all important.” Respondents could write comments or edits to competencies if desired and submit any areas they believed were missed. They were also asked about their willingness to support and use the curriculum, possible barriers, and if they would recommend an ILAE course based on the competencies.

Descriptive statistics were used to evaluate the ratings by respondents for each item. Competencies were considered to have achieved consensus and were accepted for inclusion in the curriculum if 60% or more of respondents rated an item “extremely important.” Items that received less than 60% endorsement underwent further review and deliberations by the workgroup. Expert opinion and consensus from the workgroup were then used to revise some items for clarity and relevance or to remove them from the curriculum. The tiered curriculum was approved by the ILAE Education Council and the Executive Committee.

RESULTS

Sample

The survey was completed by 415 respondents, 86.5% of whom responded in English. Respondent demographics were broken down by profession, years of experience, practice setting, location, and World Bank Ranking (WBR) (Table 1). Nurses comprised 83.4% of respondents across all six ILAE regions. The largest group of respondents was from Europe, followed by North America and Asia Oceania. Africa, Latin America, and Eastern Mediterranean regions together accounted for only 17.11% of the sample. Most (79.8%) were from 34 high‐income countries in five regions (Asia Oceania, Eastern Mediterranean, Europe, Latin America, and North America). Twenty respondents (4.8%) were from five low‐income countries in Africa. Urban areas predominated (74.9%) and only 6.8% of the sample were from rural areas. Practice settings for nurse respondents ranged from epilepsy inpatient or outpatient units (46.9%), neurology or neurosurgery units (19.1%), combined neuroscience and epilepsy units (7.8%), general medical or surgical units (6.1%), primary care or community‐based (4.6%), academia (3.5%), critical care and emergency units (2.9%), and school nursing (2.3%). The majority of nurses (69.7%) reported working in epilepsy‐specific settings (center, clinic, or monitoring unit). Respondents reported substantial experience, with 61.7% having 11 or more years in their profession.

Support for curriculum

The survey responses demonstrated robust support for the nursing competencies. Most respondents (94.8%) expressed support for the curriculum to determine the competency of nurses who care for people with epilepsy in their country. A similar response (92.6%) was seen regarding taking or recommending an ILAE course based on these competencies, and 90.4% reported that they would follow the curriculum in their own practice. A total of 299 or 72% of respondents reported 783 barriers. The most frequently cited barriers were cost (24.4%, N = 191), time away from work to complete the curriculum (19.3%, N = 151), mode of delivery (13.8%, N = 108), and access to mentors (12.3%, N = 96).

Competency ratings

Based on survey results, 55.8% (N = 24) of 43 competencies achieved consensus with ≥60% of respondents rating the items as “extremely important.” Of these items, 16 or 37.2% were kept as competencies, while eight were moved to objectives or merged with another competency. After analyzing responses to open‐ended questions, the workgroup refined the remaining competencies. Of note is that survey respondents had agreed on the majority of core competencies, while some of the proficient items required more deliberation and minor revisions. Fourteen competencies that achieved ratings between 47% and 59% were accepted as competencies after revisions, and two were changed to objectives. Three competencies were excluded from the curriculum based on low consensus scores and expert opinion. These included: working knowledge of epidemiology and etiology (51.4% extremely important), nursing care for nonmedical or alternative treatments (50.9% extremely important), and incorporating EEG into assessment (39.7% extremely important). Content on etiologies is covered in other Epilepsy Basics competencies. Variability in the use of nonmedical or alternative treatments in different regions likely contributed to the lack of consensus. The workgroup felt that this topic could more appropriately be covered as content under treatment and education competencies. While EEGs are a critical part of epilepsy care, most nurses are not involved in the technical aspects of EEG testing. Thus, EEG content can be incorporated under other competencies as appropriate to nursing practice in different parts of the world. Epilepsy curriculum for nurses: domains, competencies, and learning objectives.

Note: Level 1 = Core level. Level 2 = Proficient level.

Domains 3 and 4 focus on skills that nurses need regardless of practice setting. Nursing Assessment emphasizes areas that may contribute to different types of care. For example, recognizing and assessing what happens during a seizure is important for seizure first aid, developing seizure action plans, treating acute seizures, keeping a person safe, and preparing patients for surgical evaluations. Seizure assessments at the core level focus on knowing what to observe, recognizing triggers, and differentiating seizure patterns (i.e., single, cluster, prolonged seizures). However, the proficient nurse should be able to conduct a more interactive seizure assessment to identify changes in responsiveness, cognition, behavior, or movement. Unique areas of assessment for different populations are highlighted and covered in more depth at the proficient level. For this domain, a nurse's practice setting will dictate how the findings are used.

The Nursing Care domain builds on the assessment skills and addresses basic care needs for people with epilepsy (i.e., seizure first aid and safety, responding to emergencies, managing medications, care for hospitalized patients). While proficient objectives are identified for almost all care competencies, care of patients with drug‐resistant epilepsy, care coordination, and resource management are unique to the proficient nursing curriculum.

The Self‐Management Education and Care domain encompasses the knowledge, skills, and resources that patients and families need to manage their epilepsy. 2 While many disciplines contribute to education of patients and families, nurses consider this aspect fundamental to their practice. Although group education can be beneficial in certain contexts, nurses focus on individualizing and tailoring education to meet the specific needs of each patient. These competencies focus on the role and responsibilities of nurses, rather than the patient outcomes associated with epilepsy self‐management. A comprehensive group of competencies is included in this domain, such as educational needs for any person with epilepsy (i.e., seizure recognition and first aid, safety, tracking seizures and managing triggers, using seizure action plans and rescue therapies) and those that focus on treatments, mortality, comorbidities, psychosocial challenges, and women's health. It is incumbent upon the healthcare team to help patients and families identify their needs and ensure that these needs are met.

DISCUSSION

This epilepsy curriculum for nurses, developed with survey consensus and expert opinion from the Curriculum Workgroup, offers a framework that can be used and tailored to any nurse or practice setting. The core curriculum will fill gaps in basic nursing education and can be used by practicing nurses worldwide. The proficient level will provide guidance for nurses specializing in epilepsy and related fields, addressing the knowledge and skills that are developed through informal or “on‐the‐job” training. This curriculum will be a living document, evolving as our knowledge about epilepsy, its treatment, and nursing practice grows.

The majority of competencies in this curriculum achieved consensus from respondents to the survey; however, it is interesting to note the lower agreement for some of the proficient competencies. Initially, curriculum working group members anticipated differences among types of nurses. However, further analysis of survey responses found more differences among ILAE regions than between advanced practice nurses and other nurses. 17 There are many factors that could contribute to this finding, including the reach of survey dissemination in some regions (Africa, Eastern Mediterranean, and Latin America), a low number of responses from low‐income countries and rural areas, and available languages for the survey. Yet nursing practice and the availability of epilepsy care have regional variations as well. Regions with limited epilepsy specialists or comprehensive epilepsy centers will have fewer nurses proficient in epilepsy, potentially affecting evaluation of these competencies. Also, some of the proficient competencies are geared to care of specific groups of patients with epilepsy or topics not encountered in all settings. These factors underscore the need for standardized training of nurses and for flexibility and tailoring of the curriculum across regions.

This curriculum does not offer a specific pathway for advanced practice nurses specializing in epilepsy. However, completion of the core and proficient nursing levels, along with select epileptology competencies, may fulfill needs of advanced practice nurses working in epilepsy. This nursing curriculum is not intended to replace country‐specific programs or role competencies. However, it can fill gaps for those countries, schools of nursing, and professional education organizations and increase the capacity of nurses able to provide care to people with epilepsy.

The concept of self‐management has been a longstanding part of nursing care. However, the application of self‐management and patient/family education in the context of epilepsy has varied considerably. While several evidence‐based programs have been developed, many are focused on managing comorbidities of epilepsy and have demonstrated limited long‐term impact on seizure control or quality of life. 18 , 19 , 20 To comprehensively address multifaceted aspects of epilepsy, self‐management has been systematically organized into 11 competencies under the Self‐Management Education and Care domain. This framework is designed to emphasize the goal of helping patients and families effectively manage epilepsy and overall health. By emphasizing the nurse's role in individualizing self‐management education, these competencies can serve as the basis for the epilepsy nurse educator role.

The barriers to use of the curriculum were expected and include cost, time to complete, and method of delivery. Unfortunately, these barriers are common in nursing education because nurses are usually not compensated in terms of time or funds to attend or participate in professional education. Thus, any nursing education program needs to consider these factors as well as regional differences and the ability to provide low‐cost or remote programming. Webinars and telementoring programs may overcome these barriers and allow adaptability among countries.

Limitations

Fewer responses from low‐income countries were received which may affect the generalizability to nurses in some countries. However, the curriculum can be tailored to the needs of different countries and types of nurses. For example, countries with no specialized epilepsy care, access to surgery or neuromodulation could choose not to use those competencies.

SIGNIFICANCE

As the largest healthcare workforce globally, nurses play a pivotal role in improving patient care outcomes. It is critical that professional nurses have the competency to care for people with seizures and their families. Training nurses in epilepsy care and management can significantly improve outcomes and increase capacity of the healthcare workforce. As a living document, the epilepsy curriculum for nurses will provide a framework to move forward and enhance professional nursing practice. Investing in education and resources for nurses in epilepsy care is crucial to advancing healthcare delivery, reducing the treatment gap, and promoting health and wellness for patients, families, and communities.
