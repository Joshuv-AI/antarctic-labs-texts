Constructing a biomimetic ECM protective barrier: a strategic interface design for urethral repair to mitigate foreign body reaction
Authors: Yuqing Niu, Jixian Liu, Xiuhong Sun, Peihong Han, Junjie Liu, Kai Fu, Rui Zhou, Xinyu Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrethral reconstruction often fails due to the foreign body reaction (FBR) triggered by the graft, leading to chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and lumen obstruction. To address this challenge, we developed an innovative strategy: a 3D-printed polylactic acid (PLA) scaffold was pre-implanted subcutaneously for 14 days to recruit host-derived fibroblasts, monocytes, and adipose-derived stem cells. These cells secreted a biomimetic extracellular matrix (ECM) rich in collagen, elastin, and glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), resulting in a proteoglycan (PG)-enriched, bioactive tissue-engineered urethral graft (TEUG). This PG-rich matrix interface, characterized by a high density of proteoglycans and glycosaminoglycans that mimic the native urethral microenvironment, closely mimicked native urethral tissue in terms of hydrophilicity, surface topography, roughness, and mechanical compliance. More importantly, compared with commercially available small intestinal submucosa (SIS)-based grafts, it significantly enhanced urothelial cell adhesion, spreading, and oriented spatial organization, effectively recapitulating the functional role of the native PG matrix. In a rabbit anterior urethral replacement model, TEUG not only provided essential structural support and elasticity but also maintained the tubular architecture and physiological distensibility of the urethra to accommodate pressure changes during voiding. Furthermore, it acted as a functional bioactive interface that modulated the local immune microenvironment, attenuated inflammatory responses, and resisted FBR. As a result, tissue homeostasis was preserved and regenerative capacity was promoted, leading to early and orderly re-epithelialization with successful formation of a continuous epithelial layer and contractile smooth muscle tissue. In contrast, SIS-mediated regeneration resulted in aberrant epithelial hyperplasia, ultimately causing luminal narrowing or complete occlusion. Throughout the postoperative observation period, TEUG-reconstructed urethras consistently maintained patent lumens and demonstrated superior voiding function. In summary, by engineering an ECM interface with immunomodulatory and anti-FBR properties, our approach offers a simple, efficient, and clinically translatable strategy to achieve functional regeneration in urethral reconstruction.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04649-y.Keywords: Urethral reconstruction, Foreign body reaction, 3D-printed biodegradable scaffolds, Biomimetic ECM coating, Immune-modulatory

Introduction

The male urethra, as a critical conduit of the urogenital system, serves the dual physiological functions of urine excretion and reproductive fluid transport, with its structural integrity directly determining the homeostasis of the urogenital system. However, this organ is susceptible to congenital malformations, age-related degenerative changes, and mechanical injuries, leading to pathological alterations such as urethral defects or strictures [1, 3]. Due to the significantly greater anatomical length of the male urethra compared to that of females, the incidence of urethral injury is much higher in males, resulting in prominent clinical diagnostic and therapeutic demands [1]. In terms of disease burden, the treatment cost for urethral injuries, particularly urethral strictures, is substantial. Although specific, up-to-date national estimates remain scarce due to measurement challenges, a seminal 2009 U.S. study reported annual expenditures approaching $200 million, with over $10 million solely for urethroplasty [4]. Given that broader non-cancerous genitourinary conditions now exceed $11 billion in annual costs [5], the true socioeconomic impact of urethral strictures likely far surpasses these historical figures.

Currently, clinical repair strategies for urethral injuries primarily rely on flap grafts or vascularized grafts. However, autologous grafts have notable limitations: donor site morbidity, limited availability, increased surgical complexity and duration, and potential donor-site complications such as scarring and functional impairment. These drawbacks make it difficult to meet the demands of repairing complex or long-segment (i.e., > 2 cm) urethral defects [6, 9]. Although researchers continue to explore optimized approaches—such as using oral mucosal grafts for strictures unsuitable for excision and primary anastomosis, the survival and efficacy of grafts are still significantly reduced in cases with poor recipient bed conditions (e.g., local tissue fibrosis) or underlying diseases such as lichen sclerosus, further exposing the clinical application bottlenecks of current therapies [10, 11]. Therefore, developing novel, efficient, and clinically adaptable technologies for urethral defect repair has become a critical challenge in urological surgery.

Tissue engineering offers a promising alternative, with decellularized matrices (e.g., small intestinal submucosa, SIS; bladder acellular matrix) and synthetic polymers (e.g., polylactic acid, PLA; poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid)) widely explored for urethral graft development [12, 20]. However, these materials often trigger a foreign body reaction (FBR), characterized by chronic inflammation, pathological fibrosis, and luminal narrowing, which critically compromises sustained patency and functional outcomes [10, 11, 21, 23].

The detrimental effects of FBR are particularly significant in the unique urethral microenvironment: FBR-driven local fibrosis can directly obstruct urine flow, potentially causing complete urethral obstruction [11]. Moreover, early postoperative urine leakage can exacerbate local inflammation and scar formation, significantly increasing the risk of severe complications such as fistulae [10, 24, 25]. Notably, FBR is essentially a non-specific host immune response to “foreign implants,” characterized by massive recruitment of monocytes/macrophages, differentiation into foreign body giant cells, and the fibrous capsules deposition [26, 27]. While this process may partially isolate the implant, it severely disrupts functional integration between the graft and host tissue, representing a major technical barrier in urethral tissue engineering.

To mitigate FBR, researchers have developed various scaffold surface modification strategies, including drug-eluting coatings, zwitterionic coatings, and bioactive polymer coatings. However, these approaches face multiple challenges in clinical translation: poor coating durability, which leads to shedding under physiological fluid flow; complex manufacturing processes that are difficult to scale up for clinical batch production; and delamination risks of certain coatings in physiological environments, potentially triggering secondary inflammation [28, 33]. More critically, many scaffold materials have not yet received approval from regulatory authorities such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), impeding the translational pathway from research to clinical application. Furthermore, cell-based tissue-engineered grafts, although possessing ideal regenerative potential, require lengthy in vitro maturation—typically involving two stages: “cell sheet cultivation on scaffolds to form tubular structures” and “perfusion culture to enhance extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and mechanical strength”—with a process duration ranging from several weeks to months [34, 37]. This inefficient production model is fundamentally incompatible with the “timeliness” required in emergency surgeries (e.g., traumatic urethral rupture) and the “standardization” needed in elective procedures, further limiting their clinical applicability.

Therefore, there is an urgent need for a next-generation urethral graft that combines excellent biocompatibility, pro-regenerative capacity, and rapid clinical availability. In this study, we propose a novel strategy based on host-mediated in situ ECM remodeling: subcutaneously pre-implanting a 3D-printed, biodegradable PLA scaffold for 14 days to recruit host-derived cells, including fibroblasts, monocytes, and adipose-derived stem cells. These cells secrete a biomimetic ECM rich in collagen, elastin, and glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), forming a proteoglycan (PG) matrix-enriched bioactive tubular structure (hereinafter referred to as TEUG), which essentially acts as a protective barrier mimicking the native urethral PG matrix. This engineered PG matrix coating is designed to recapitulate the biochemical and biophysical properties of natural urethral tissue, creating an immunomodulatory interface that suppresses FBR and supports organized tissue integration. This strategic interface design, precisely matching the functional characteristics of native PG matrix, enhances the targeting of FBR regulation. We hypothesize that this pre-vascularized, PG matrix-rich graft can achieve rapid host integration and functional re-epithelialization post-implantation without requiring prolonged in vitro maturation. This approach offers a simple, scalable, and clinically translatable pathway for developing high-patency, off-the-shelf engineered urethral substitutes.

Scaffold fabrication

The fabrication of urethral defect scaffolds was based on the method described in a previously published study [38], with the same design parameters retained to suit the hollow anatomical characteristics of the urethra during urination. A cylindrical porous scaffold was designed using SolidWorks software (Dassault Systèmes, France) with an inner diameter of 2 mm, matching the typical clinical urethral lumen dimensions, and a wall thickness of 0.5 mm. The scaffold featured a grid-like porous architecture to facilitate tissue ingrowth and nutrient transport, while mimicking the three-dimensional structure of native urethral tissue and providing appropriate mechanical support. Upon completion of the design, the model was exported in standard tessellation language format for subsequent 3D printing (Figure S1).

A bio-based high-precision resin, eResin-PLA Pro, was selected as the printing material due to its excellent biocompatibility, favorable mechanical properties, and suitability for micro-scale 3D printing. According to the manufacturer’s documentation, this material has passed biocompatibility testing in accordance with ISO 10,993 standards, making it suitable for in vivo implantation. Fabrication was carried out using an M-dental U60 DLP-based 3D printer (Ningbo Inteplast, China) equipped with a 405 nm light source. The STL file was imported into the slicing software, and the following printing parameters were set: layer thickness = 25 μm, exposure time per layer = 8–10 s, bottom exposure time = 60 s, with support structures automatically generated. Prior to printing, the build platform was calibrated to ensure geometric accuracy and surface quality, meeting the requirements for urethral repair applications.

After printing, the scaffolds were subjected to ultraviolet irradiation for 30 min on each side for sterilization prior to biological use.

Animal ethics and surgical procedures

All experiments were approved by the Medical Laboratory Animal Center of Dongguan University of Technology and conducted in strict accordance with the institutional guidelines for animal care and use. Prior to the study, all thirty male New Zealand white rabbits (1.5 months old, weighing 1.5–2.0 kg) were confirmed to be in good health, free from urinary tract infections or systemic diseases, and were acclimatized to the laboratory environment for one week. The animals were randomly assigned as follows: 10 rabbits were subcutaneously implanted with 1 scaffold each, yielding 10 TEUGs for autologous in vivo urethral defect replacement surgery; 10 rabbits received commercial SIS and served as the in vivo control group; the remaining 10 rabbits were subcutaneously implanted with 2 scaffolds each, obtaining 20 TEUGs for in vitro biophysical, chemical, and other characterizations, as well as characterization experiments of interactions between in vitro urethral epithelial cells and TEUG.

Animals were anesthetized via intravenous injection of 2% pentobarbital sodium (45 mg/kg) through the marginal ear vein. Anesthesia depth was monitored by assessing reflexes, and body temperature was maintained with a heating pad. To prevent post-surgical infections, all animals undergoing urethral surgery received enrofloxacin (5 mg/kg/day). Postoperatively, meloxicam (0.2 mg/kg) was administered subcutaneously 10 min after surgery for pain relief, with daily supplementation as needed for the first 3 days.

Post-operatively, rabbits were housed individually to prevent wound contamination. Wounds were daily disinfected with iodophor, and health status (including appetite, weight, and urination patterns) was monitored twice daily to detect complications such as stricture or infection. Animals showing severe distress were managed according to humane endpoint guidelines, with ad libitum access to food and water maintained throughout.

Preparation of TEUG

TEUGs were prepared by implanting sterile PLA scaffolds (inner diameter: 2 mm, wall thickness: 0.5 mm, length: 2.2 cm) subcutaneously into the ventral abdominal wall of rabbits. Under anesthesia, a small skin incision was made along the paramedian line, and the scaffold was inserted into a prepared subcutaneous pocket. The wound was closed with intradermal sutures.

After 14 days, the implant was retrieved under anesthesia. The scaffold, together with the surrounding tissue capsule, was excised en bloc. A cylindrical segment (~ 0.2 mm in length) was cut from the middle of the construct and longitudinally sectioned to obtain inner and outer surface samples.

For scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis, samples were fixed in electron microscopy fixative for 2 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, critically dried, and sputter-coated with gold (HITACHI MC1000). Surface morphology was examined using a HITACHI Regulus 8100 scanning electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan).

Body weight and body temperature were monitored before surgery and at regular intervals post-implantation to assess systemic responses. Unused samples were stored at − 20 °C for future use.

Urethral defect modeling and reconstructive surgery

Fourteen days after TEUG implantation, rabbits in both the TEUG and SIS groups underwent anterior (pendulous) urethral reconstruction surgery under intravenous anesthesia. A midline abdominal incision was made to expose the urethra extending from the bulbar region to the suspensory ligament. A 2.2 cm full-thickness circumferential segment of the pendulous urethra, starting 1 cm distal to the urethral meatus (glans), was excised to create a long-segment urethral defect. The surgical site was continuously irrigated with sterile saline to prevent tissue desiccation.

The distal regions (~ 5 mm from each end) were used for qPCR, and the distal-most regions (~ 2 mm from each end) were processed for TEM. Remaining paraffin sections were stored as unstained slides in the institutional raw data repository.

The excised native urethral tissue was immediately placed in sterile physiological saline, maintained at 4 °C during handling, and subsequently transferred to a − 20 °C freezer for storage until further analysis. These samples were used for subsequent characterization by atomic force microscopy (AFM), SEM, and histological evaluation.

For reconstruction, the pre-formed TEUG was used directly for end-to-end anastomosis. In contrast, the SIS patch was first manually rolled into a tubular structure (inner diameter ~ 2.5 mm) and sutured with 6 − 0 absorbable sutures under sterile conditions before implantation. Anastomosis was performed in an end-to-end fashion using 8 − 0 polydioxanone (PDS-II) sutures in a continuous running pattern, ensuring a watertight, tension-free closure with maintained luminal patency.

An F8 silicone catheter was inserted intra-luminally and left in place for 10 days to provide structural support, prevent stricture formation, and facilitate urinary drainage. The catheter was exteriorized through the distal urethra and secured in place. The surrounding soft tissues were repositioned, and the abdominal wall and skin were closed in layers.

Postoperatively, all animals received subcutaneous meloxicam for analgesia and were monitored daily for urination patterns, wound healing, and potential complications such as infection or urinary leakage. Animals were housed under standard conditions with free access to food and water.

AFM analysis

AFM was used to assess surface topography, roughness, and mechanical properties of TEUGs and native urethral tissues. Native tissue samples, previously stored at − 20 °C, were thawed and equilibrated in physiological saline for 2 h before testing.

Measurements were performed in saline using an AFM (Bruker Dimension ICON, Bruker, Germany) with a silicon nitride cantilever (spring constant ~ 0.58 N/m, tip radius ~ 20 nm). Surface morphology and roughness (Ra) were analyzed in tapping mode over 20 × 20 μm² areas.

Nanomechanical properties were evaluated in Indentation Retrace (IdR) mode. Young’s modulus was calculated from force–distance curves using the Hertz model, and spatial maps of mechanical response (including elasticity and adhesion) were generated across the inner and outer surfaces of the samples.

Data were analyzed using standard software (e.g., NanoScope Analysis). At least five regions from three independent samples per group were measured.

Surface and mechanical characterization

To evaluate the surface chemistry, wettability, and viscoelastic properties of the TEUGs and native urethral tissues, the following analyses were performed.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha 250Xi (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (1486.6 eV). Survey and high-resolution scans (C 1s, O 1s, N 1s) were acquired at a pass energy of 100 eV and 30 eV, respectively. Data were analyzed using Avantage software, with charge correction referenced to the C–C/C–H peak at 284.8 eV.

Attenuated Total Reflectance–Fourier Transform Infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy was performed on a Bruker TENSOR27 spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) equipped with a diamond ATR crystal. Spectra were collected over the range of 4000–400 cm⁻¹ at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹, with 32 scans averaged per sample. Background correction was performed prior to each measurement.

Static Water Contact Angle (WCA) was measured using a Dataphysics OCA Pro15 instrument (DataPhysics Instruments GmbH, Germany). A 5 µL droplet of deionized water was dispensed onto the flat surface of the sample (inner and outer layers separately), and images were captured immediately after droplet stabilization. Contact angles were determined by the sessile drop method using SCA20 software. At least five measurements were taken from different locations on each sample (n = 3 per group).

Cyclic Stress-Relaxation Testing was performed using a TA Instruments Q800 dynamic mechanical analyzer (TA, USA) in tension mode. Rectangular strips of TEUG and native urethral tissue (n = 5 per group) were preconditioned in physiological saline at 37 °C. Tests were conducted under 25% strain amplitude for 10 consecutive cycles at a frequency of 1 Hz. After the 8th, 9th, and 10th cycles, the samples were held at zero stress for relaxation periods of 15, 20, and 30 min, respectively, while the decay in restoring force was recorded. The hysteresis loops and residual stress after each relaxation phase were analyzed to evaluate energy dissipation, elastic recovery, and viscoelastic stability.

All samples were equilibrated in physiological saline for 2 h before testing. At least three independent samples per group were analyzed for each assay.

Histological analysis

For sample allocation, the middle 10-mm segment of the 2.2-cm graft was used for histological and immunofluorescence staining. Histological analysis was performed on paraffin-embedded tissue Sect. (5 μm) from TEUG obtained via subcutaneous self-assembly, and replacement samples harvested at 1-, 3-, and 6-months post-surgery. Sections were stained with H&E, MTC, EVG, PAS, AB (pH 2.5), SR, and Von Kossa following the manufacturers’ instructions.

For immunofluorescence staining, deparaffinized sections were subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval using a citrate-based buffer (pH 6.0) in a pressure cooker, followed by blocking with 5% normal goat serum in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in antibody diluent, washed, and incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. All slides were imaged using a whole-slide scanner (3DHISTECH, Hungary), except for SR-stained sections, which were examined under both bright-field and polarized light microscopy (PM). Semi-quantitative analysis of positive staining areas was performed using CaseViewer v2.4 and Fiji ImageJ-win64 (NIH, USA) after thresholding and background subtraction. Remaining paraffin sections were stored as unstained slides in the institutional raw data repository.

Isolation of urethral epithelial cells and evaluation of their interaction with TEUGs

Urethral epithelial cells (UECs) were isolated from rabbit urethral mucosa using a previously described enzymatic digestion and explant culture method [39]. In brief, the epithelial layer was separated and sequentially digested with dispase II at 4 °C overnight and collagenase Type I at 37 °C. Released cells were dissociated with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA, seeded in dulbecco’s modified eagle medium supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum, and cultured under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Cells from passages 2–4 were used for experiments, with epithelial identity confirmed by immunofluorescence staining for CK20 in preliminary batches.

For interaction studies, sterile TEUGs and SIS scaffolds were cut into 8 mm diameter discs and pre-wetted in culture medium to ensure full hydration prior to cell seeding. UECs were seeded onto the luminal surface at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells per scaffold. Cell adhesion, spreading, and monolayer formation were evaluated by SEM at 12, 48, 96, and 168 h post-seeding, with SIS serving as the control.

At 168 h, TEM was performed to examine focal adhesions and cytoskeletal (microfilament) organization of UECs on the luminal surface of TEUGs. At 6 months post-operation, the distal-most regions (~ 2 mm from each end) were processed for TEM. Samples were fixed, embedded, and ultrathin-sectioned (~ 50 nm) using a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome. Sections were analyzed under a HT7700 TEM (Hitachi, Japan) at 100 kV.

Additionally, AFM was used in Dissipation Retrace (DsR) mode (Bruker Dimension ICON, Germany) to map the spatial distribution of energy dissipation during probe retraction, reflecting nanoscale variations in cell-surface interaction dynamics. Dissipation maps were acquired at high resolution, with color coding indicating local differences in energy loss across the luminal surfaces of TEUGs and SIS.

Immunofluorescence staining for FAK and F-actin/DAPI was performed to visualize the spatial organization of focal adhesions and the actin cytoskeleton. Fluorescence images were analyzed using Fiji ImageJ software to quantify FAK expression intensity on the inner surfaces of TEUGs versus SIS scaffolds.

Postoperative monitoring in animal models

To evaluate the structural and functional outcomes of urethral replacement, rabbits were subjected to multimodal follow-up assessments at predetermined time points after implantation. At 1 month postoperatively, cross-sectional computed tomography (CT) was performed using a TC-460 CT scanner (Shenzhen TUTOM Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) to visualize the morphology and luminal patency of the implanted TEUGs in vivo. Concurrently, calcium wave imaging was conducted to assess intercellular signaling and physiological responsiveness of epithelial cells at the graft site.

At 3 months, urethral pressure profilometry was performed to evaluate urinary continence function and lumen patency. A pediatric urodynamic catheter was inserted retrogradely into the urethra under anesthesia, and continuous saline perfusion was maintained while slowly withdrawing the catheter. The BL420 Biological Signal Acquisition System (Chengdu Taimeng Science and Technology Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China) was used to record the maximum urethral closure pressure and pressure profiles along the urethral axis, allowing assessment of urethral closure capacity, smooth muscle contractility, and overall patency.

At 6 months, retrograde urethrography was carried out using the Elite Urethrogram System (Shenzhen TUTOM Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) to assess the anatomical structure and morphological integration of the regenerated urethra. After catheterization via the external urethral orifice, iopamidol contrast agent was injected retrogradely, and radiographic images were acquired in anteroposterior and oblique views to evaluate lumen continuity, diameter uniformity, and the presence of strictures or diverticula.

All procedures were performed under intravenous anesthesia, and animals were monitored for general health and voiding behavior throughout the 6-month follow-up period.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

TEUG and SIS grafts (mung bean-sized each) were harvested from the urethral defect sites of New Zealand rabbits at 6 months post-operation, along with healthy posterior urethral tissue from rabbits without urethral replacement surgery (served as positive control). The distal regions (~ 5 mm from each end) were used for qPCR, and the distal-most regions (~ 2 mm from each end) were processed for TEM. Tissues for qPCR were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C, while grafts for ELISA were homogenized on ice with radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected to determine protein concentration.

For qPCR, total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent. After verifying RNA purity (A260/A280 ratio: 1.8–2.0) with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (UH5210, Hitachi, Japan), 1 µg of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the SweScript RT II Reagent Kit. Amplification was performed on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Touch™ System (Bio-Rad, USA) with TB Green™ Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, Japan) in a 20 µL reaction system. The protocol included initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s, followed by melting curve analysis (65–95 °C). Relative mRNA expression levels of CD206, CD86, CD27, CD28, and CD4 were calculated via the 2 − ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the endogenous reference; results were presented as fold changes relative to the positive control.

The rabbit-specific primer sequences were as follows:

GAPDH: Forward: 5′-TGAAGGTCGGAGTGAACGGAT-3′ Reverse: 5′-CGTTCTCAGCCTTGACCGTG-3′; CD206: Forward: 5′-GCTAAATGGGAGAATCTGGAATGT-3′ Reverse: 5′-GACTAGGACAGTTGGTGGGCA-3′; CD86: Forward: 5′-GGACTGAGTGTCACGGTCTTTG-3′, Reverse: 5′-GATACACGCCCTTGTCCTTGA-3′; CD27: Forward: 5′-GTCTCGGAGACAGAAACAAGCAA-3′, Reverse: 5′-TTTCTTGTAGCAGCAACCTCCAC-3′; CD28: Forward: 5′-ACGATGATTCTCAGGCTGCTC-3′, Reverse: 5′-GCTCAGGTTGACCTCGTTGT-3′; CD4: Forward: 5′-AACATGCGAACCAGGTCAAGA-3′, Reverse: 5′-CCGAGTCGTCCATCCTGAGAT-3′.

ELISA was conducted following the instructions of Rabbit iNOS and Arg-1 ELISA Kits. 100 µL of standards or samples was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After discarding the liquid, detection antibody was added for another 30-minute incubation at 37 °C. The plate was washed 5 times, then HRP-conjugated secondary antibody was added and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by 5 more washes. Substrate chromogenic solution was added for 15-minute light-protected incubation at 37 °C. Stop solution was then added, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to calculate the target protein expression levels.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test. For three or more groups, one-way ANOVA was used followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Preparation and biochemical activity characterization of TEUG

In this study, a novel strategy combining “3D-printed biodegradable scaffold subcutaneous pre-implantation” with “host cell self-assembly and ECM secretion” was employed to engineer TEUGs. A 3D-printed biodegradable tubular scaffold (inner diameter: 2 mm, wall thickness: 500 μm, length: 2.2 cm) was subcutaneously implanted into New Zealand rabbits for 2 weeks (Figure S2). During this period, host-recruited cells, including fibroblasts, monocytes, and adipose-derived stem cells, migrated into the scaffold and actively secreted and organized ECM through autocrine signaling, with fibroblasts and stem cells specifically contributing to the synthesis of PG matrix (a core functional component of ECM), ultimately forming a bioactive tubular graft rich in integrated PG matrix. The experimental workflow is illustrated schematically in Fig. 1A. Body temperature and body weight of animals with and without subcutaneous TEUG implantation were comparable at all postoperative time points (Figure S3), with no statistically significant difference (p > 0.05). The resulting TEUG (Fig. 1B) exhibited a well-defined tubular morphology, meeting the macroscopic requirement for maintaining luminal patency during urination.

To characterize the cellular composition of the TEUG, triple-label four-color immunofluorescence staining was performed (Fig. 1C). Co-staining for Vim (a fibroblast marker), CD11b (a macrophage marker), CD44 (a cell surface molecule), and DAPI (nuclear staining) revealed abundant host-derived cells within the TEUG. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 1D) showed the following cell proportions: fibroblasts (Vim⁺ cells: 48.55% ± 7.55%), macrophages (CD11b⁺ cells: 12.75% ± 3.12%), and CD44⁺ putative stem/progenitor cells (3.62% ± 0.76%). These cell populations collectively constitute the primary cellular basis for ECM production.

Histological and quantitative analyses demonstrated that the TEUG closely recapitulates the biochemical characteristics of native urethral ECM, with PG matrix serving as a key bridge between structural components (collagen, elastin) and functional properties. EVG staining and quantitative analysis revealed no significant difference in elastin content between TEUG and native urethra (Fig. 1E). MTC and SR staining, combined with PM (Fig. 1F), showed that the collagen architecture in TEUG closely resembles that of native urethra: under PM, type I collagen exhibited bright yellow birefringence with an orderly arrangement (predominant component), while type III collagen displayed green birefringence with a fine mesh-like distribution (supporting component). Quantitative analysis of collagen I/III gray values showed no significant difference between TEUG and native urethra (p > 0.05, Fig. 1E).

AB staining (targeting aGAGs) and PAS staining (targeting nGAGs) (Fig. 1H) confirmed the presence of both aGAGs and nGAGs in both TEUG and native urethra, these GAGs are the major carbohydrate moieties of PG matrix, indicating consistency in the fundamental types of polysaccharides within PG matrix and associated functional properties such as water retention and structural support. However, quantitative analysis using the rose plot in Fig. 1I revealed notable differences in polysaccharide proportions: in TEUG, aGAGs accounted for 23%–36% and nGAGs for 50%–67%, whereas in native urethra, aGAGs constituted 50%–59% and nGAGs 31%–45%. This indicates significant differences in the relative abundance and total amount of GAGs (and thus PG matrix subtypes) between the two tissues. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the TEUG accurately reproduces the biochemical composition, spatial organization, and relative abundance of major ECM components, such as collagen, elastin, and PG matrix (via its GAGs components), present in native urethral tissue.

Characterization of ECM ultrastructure and nanoscale mechanical properties of TEUG and native urethral tissue

To evaluate the structural and mechanical biomimetic properties of TEUG, we employed AFM imaging and force mapping techniques to characterize the ultrastructure and nanomechanical properties of its ECM. Commercial SIS was not used as a control in the experiment; instead, native rabbit urethral tissue was selected for comparison. The reasons are as follows: SIS is a decellularized dense laminate that lacks the critical PG matrix of native urethral tissue, retaining only hydrophobic collagen and elastin fibers. Additionally, its dense and heterogeneous structure easily causes AFM probe slippage and unreliable measurement results, failing to meet the requirement for data accuracy. In contrast, native rabbit urethral tissue contains a complete PG-collagen-elastin network, and its structural and mechanical properties can serve as a physiological benchmark, directly verifying the ability of TEUG to replicate native tissue and providing reliable data for subsequent studies. As shown in Fig. 2A, low-magnification and high-magnification SEM morphological images of cross-sectional samples revealed that TEUG exhibited a continuous and dense tubular structure with uniform ECM distribution, which was highly similar to the overall structural organization of native urethra. Figure 2B further displayed high-magnification views of the inner and outer surfaces of TEUG, showing a porous network composed of interwoven nanofibers—this structure was highly biomimetic to the topological characteristics of native ECM. Quantitative analysis of the diameter of cross-sectional nanofibers (Fig. 2C) showed that the average diameter of TEUG was (73.21 ± 21.4) nm, while that of native urethra was (81.26 ± 18.9) nm, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (p > 0.05). This indicated that TEUG successfully replicated the nanofiber scale characteristics of native urethral ECM.

AFM topographic imaging (Fig. 2D), presented in both 2D and 3D formats, combined with quantitative analysis (Fig. 2E), reveals a surface roughness Ra of (65.33 ± 24.3) nm for TEUG and (54.33 ± 26.1) nm for native urethra, with no significant difference, suggesting excellent topographical biomimicry of TEUG. Further AFM-based Young’s modulus mapping (performed in physiological liquid at 37 °C using the Indentation-Retrace mode to probe nanoscale mechanics) reveals that TEUG exhibits a modulus range of (10.4–27.2) GPa (Fig. 2F). The predominantly yellow-green color distribution indicates relatively uniform stiffness at the level of individual nanofibers. In contrast, the native urethra displays a modulus range of (12.6–25.4) GPa, with localized red regions (> 25 GPa) likely corresponding to dense collagen bundles or nuclear structures. Notably, these GPa-level values represent the intrinsic local stiffness of the load-bearing ECM skeleton (e.g., mineralized or densely packed collagen fibrils) rather than the bulk macroscopic compliance of the hydrated tissue. Analysis of 3D mechanical topography indicates that while the native urethra exhibits higher local rigidity in specific dense domains, its structure is more orderly, whereas TEUG, though slightly stiffer on average at the nanoscale, shows greater surface irregularity and some mechanical heterogeneity. Force–displacement curve analysis (Fig. 2G) further reveals that the modulus distribution in native urethra is more concentrated, while that of TEUG is broader, suggesting microstructural inhomogeneity due to incomplete ECM maturation. Collectively, despite these nanoscale differences in distribution width and average stiffness, the elastic modulus mapping (Fig. 2F) demonstrates a spatially analogous pattern of mechanical properties between TEUG and native tissue. Both exhibit distinct regions of varying stiffness that mimic the natural elastic heterogeneity, highlighting their fundamental mechanical compatibility. Concurrently, the surface topography maps (Fig. 2H) exhibit analogous nanoscale morphological features, underscoring the favorable structural biomimicry of the TEUG scaffold. These data demonstrated that the TEUG closely recapitulates the ECM ultrastructure, surface morphology, and nanoscale mechanical behavior of native urethral tissue.

Biochemical and biophysical matching of TEUG to native urethral tissue

XPS analysis (Fig. 3A) revealed that both the TEUG and native rabbit urethral tissue exhibit dominant C1s, N1s, and O1s peaks on their surfaces, these elements are the primary components of PG matrix (via its protein core and GAG sugar chains), indicating that both materials are primarily composed of organic components rich in carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen. High-resolution deconvolution of the C1s spectrum identified characteristic functional groups associated with proteins and polysaccharides, including aliphatic carbon chains, carbonyl (C = O), and hydroxyl (C–OH) moieties. The relative abundances of these components are highly similar between TEUG and native tissue, suggesting good consistency in surface chemical composition.

In the ATR-FTIR spectra (Fig. 3B), both TEUG and native urethral tissue display a broad and intense absorption band around 3300 cm⁻¹, attributed to overlapping O–H and N–H stretching vibrations from hydrogen-bonded water, proteins, and GAGs (the carbohydrate component of PG matrix). Both samples show characteristic ECM signatures: amide I and II bands at 1654 cm⁻¹ and 1750 cm⁻¹ (indicative of peptide backbone –CONH–), alkyl chain vibrations at 1381 cm⁻¹ and 1358 cm⁻¹ (characteristic of elastin-like hydrocarbon structures), C–O–C stretching vibrations at 1181 cm⁻¹ (assigned to GAG sugar ring motifs), and = C–H bending vibrations at 870 cm⁻¹ (suggestive of aromatic glycoproteins). These shared molecular features indicate that TEUG effectively recapitulates the biochemical molecular structure of the native urethral ECM.

Static WCA measurements (Fig. 3C) show that the contact angle of TEUG is 57.6° ± 2.0°, which is not significantly different from that of native tissue (55.6° ± 2.1°), indicating comparable surface wettability and similar hydrophilic character. The moderate contact angles suggest favorable cell adhesion potential, likely due to the presence of hydrophilic polysaccharide components within the ECM-mimetic architecture, reflecting a good reproduction of the native tissue’s surface physicochemical properties by TEUG.

Cyclic tensile-relaxation testing (Fig. 3D) further compares the dynamic mechanical behavior of TEUG and native urethral tissue. Under 10 consecutive cycles at 25% strain, with stabilization over the first seven cycles and relaxation periods of 15 min (cycle 8), 20 min (cycle 9), and 30 min (cycle 10) applied at 37 °C, the stress-strain hysteresis loops of TEUG gradually narrow and stabilize, with minimal residual strain, demonstrating good elastic recovery and mechanical integrity. Notably, TEUG maintains a certain level of stress responsiveness and exhibits a relatively rapid strain recovery trend after relaxation, this elastic performance is partially supported by the cross-linking between PG matrix and collagen/elastin networks, whereas the native urethral tissue shows moderate stress softening and a reduced hysteresis loop, suggesting viscoelastic relaxation and mild energy dissipation under repeated loading. Combined findings indicate TEUG matches native tissue in static biochemical/surface characteristics and dynamic viscoelastic properties.

TEUG promotes adhesion, spreading, and functional assembly of UECs

To assess TEUG’s ability to regulate UEC behavior, New Zealand rabbit UECs were seeded on TEUG and commercial SIS scaffolds and monitored at 12, 48, 96, and 168 h (Fig. 4A). In contrast, cells on SIS remained sparsely distributed throughout the culture period and failed to reach confluence even after 168 h, indicating poor support for sustained proliferation and spreading. TEM analysis at 168 h (Fig. 4B) revealed that UECs on TEUG exhibited well-defined nuclei, abundant mitochondria, microfilaments aligned along matrix fibers, and mature focal adhesions, consistent with stable integrin-mediated anchoring. On SIS, organelles were sparse, actin filaments lacked directional organization, and focal adhesions were weak or absent, reflecting inadequate cytoskeletal assembly.

TEUG achieves structural compliance and functional integrity in urinary tract reconstruction in vivo

Figure 5A shows that the suture retention strength of TEUG was (2.0 ± 0.7) N, which is not significantly different from the tensile strength of natural urethral tissue ((1.5 ± 0.4) N; ns), indicating that its mechanical properties are within the normal physiological range of the urethra. In contrast, the suture strength of commercial SIS was (2.7 ± 1.2) N, significantly higher than both TEUG and natural urethral tissue (*p < 0.05), suggesting a potential risk of postoperative stress concentration. The intraoperative suture images shown in Fig. 5B indicate that TEUG exhibits good flexibility and can be uniformly sutured without significant tissue deformation. Conversely, due to its greater rigidity, SIS tends to cause local wrinkling and pulling deformations during suturing.

Discussion

Over the past decade, the development of bioengineered urethral grafts has primarily followed two distinct pathways: one relying on scaffold-mediated structural stabilization, and the other based on scaffold-free approaches such as cell sheets or spheroids that undergo self-assembly [12, 16, 18, 28, 31, 33, 34, 40]. In this study, we present a novel strategy that integrates the advantages of both: a transient, 3D-printed, biodegradable PLA scaffold combined with a 14-day subcutaneous pre-implantation period to actively recruit host-derived fibroblasts, monocytes, and adipose-derived stem cells. These cells drive the in-situ secretion of a biomimetic ECM rich in collagen, elastin, and GAGs. Critically, during the 14-day subcutaneous pre-implantation, these components integrate to form a biomimetic ECM centered on a PG matrix, a core constituent mediating structural adhesion and biological signaling. This process significantly accelerates the in vivo self-assembly of bioactive tubular tissue. The resulting TEUG not only provides a stable three-dimensional physical support for UECs and SMCs, facilitating their adhesion, proliferation, and directional alignment, thereby maintaining tubular integrity and promoting functional tissue regeneration, but more importantly, the engineered ECM with PG matrix serving as its functional active interface, closely mimics the biochemical composition and biophysical properties of native urethra. This creates an immunomodulatory interface that effectively suppresses the FBR and promotes orderly integration between the graft and host tissue (Fig. 8). Here, “PG-rich” is defined functionally and architecturally as a high-abundance, organized matrix of diverse proteoglycans (e.g., decorin, heparan sulfate) rather than a single species. Literature indicates that such specific PG composition and distribution are critical for modulating macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype and inhibiting TGF-β-driven fibrosis [41, 42]. This mechanism addresses the concern regarding how an immunologically inert material induces functional tissue: by leveraging the “dual nature” of the FBR. Instead of allowing the initial host response to progress into a pathological fibrous capsule, the PG-rich matrix spatially guides the reaction, transforming the provisional ECM into a dynamic, pro-regenerative template. Consequently, the “fibrous” tissue formed during pre-implantation serves not as a barrier, but as a biodegradable scaffold that supports smooth muscle differentiation and prevents the chronic inflammation typically associated with synthetic implants [43, 44], thereby underpinning the immunomodulatory mechanism observed in our study.

Schematic diagram of tissue regeneration induced by the TEUG bioactive interface at the urethral defect site in young New Zealand rabbits. The interface regulates the local immune microenvironment, inhibits the foreign body reaction, and guides the infiltration of surrounding tissues into the graft tube wall, thereby achieving tissue integration and functional repair

The architecture of the 3D-printed scaffold plays a pivotal role in enabling this specific tissue formation and GAG deposition. The internal 3D-printed scaffold plays a critical role in maintaining initial mechanical stability and tubular architecture. While conventional scaffold-based strategies can provide mechanical support, they often compromise smooth muscle compliance due to excessive stiffness, leading to diminished responsiveness to hormonal and electrophysiological stimuli [13, 20, 22]. In contrast, we specifically designed our PLA scaffold with high porosity and an open, interconnected architecture. This design is significant because it maximizes the surface area available for host cell infiltration and creates spacious micro-environments that facilitate the robust deposition and organization of biomimetic ECM, particularly GAGs and PGs. TEUG achieves enhanced mechanical strength while preserving excellent elasticity and physiological distensibility, key indicators of functional smooth muscle integration. Functional assessments further validate its superior performance: at one-month post-implantation, TEUG exhibits stronger contractile reactivity, rhythmic calcium signaling, and effective capture and response to electrical stimulation, whereas the commercially available SIS control group lacks these functional characteristics. By three months, TEUG generates sustained, rhythmic spontaneous contractions, while the SIS group develops early luminal narrowing due to surrounding fibrotic tissue proliferation, resulting in impaired contractile function. At six months, TEUG maintains effective contrast agent transport, indicating patent lumina and preserved function; in contrast, the SIS group progresses to complete luminal occlusion due to concentric fibrotic ingrowth, ultimately leading to graft failure. These results underscore that persistent inflammation and FBR are key barriers to achieving functional integration of urethral grafts, barriers which our architected scaffold and pre-conditioning strategy successfully overcome.

We selected PLA as the scaffold material to facilitate clinical translation. PLA has been approved by the U.S. FDA for clinical applications such as absorbable sutures (e.g., Vicryl, which contains PLGA), orthopedic fixation devices, drug delivery systems, and tissue engineering scaffolds [45]. However, the primary challenge of using PLA in urethral repair lies in its inherent rigidity [39, 46]. Our 3D printing approach directly addresses this by allowing precise control over pore size and strut thickness, balancing the need for initial mechanical stability with the requirement for rapid cellular invasion and GAG-rich matrix deposition [47]. As the ECM gradually remodels, the micro- and nanomechanical properties, macroscopic elastic behavior, and suture retention strength of the graft progressively approach those of native urethral tissue. Notably, the GPa-level stiffness detected by AFM reflects the intrinsic rigidity of the nanoscale ECM skeleton, which coexists with the graft’s overall macroscopic compliance governed by its porous, hydrated architecture. Critically, the interaction between scaffold degradation and ECM maturation is modulated by our pre-implantation strategy. While PLA hydrolysis can release acidic oligomers that potentially lower local pH and trigger inflammation, the 14-day subcutaneous pre-implantation phase allows for the rapid formation of a dense, vascularized host-derived ECM envelope prior to urethral implantation. This pre-formed biological barrier serves as a physiological buffer, effectively neutralizing local acidification and shielding the urethral lumen from direct exposure to degradation products. Consequently, the mature ECM mitigates the risk of sterile inflammation often associated with synthetic polymer degradation, ensuring a smoother transition from scaffold support to tissue autonomy. Importantly, the PLA scaffold is not intended to remain permanently; it degrades via hydrolysis into lactic acid, a natural metabolite cleared through the Krebs cycle as and water. This design ensures the scaffold provides temporary mechanical support during healing and fully resorbs as the neo-urethra matures, leaving no long-term foreign residue. Notably, we deliberately retained these autologous cells rather than applying decellularization to create an ‘off-the-shelf’ product. Since the recruited cells are host-derived, they pose no immunogenic risk; conversely, harsh decellularization would strip the labile bioactive cues and disrupt the delicate ECM architecture essential for our graft’s superior immunomodulatory function. Thus, this ‘living autograft’ strategy offers a more potent regenerative signal than any acellular alternative.

From a surgical perspective, TEUG demonstrates superior handling characteristics compared to SIS. SIS, due to its relatively stiff texture, tends to cause local tissue distortion during anastomosis. In contrast, TEUG exhibits excellent tissue compliance, enabling seamless integration with native urethra. In a model of anterior urethral defect, TEUG outperformed SIS in both load transmission and overall functional performance. Notably, SIS is composed of decellularized, densely laminated tissue that fails to recapitulate the compositional, structural, and mechanical complexity of native urethra, which is likely a fundamental reason for its propensity to trigger inflammatory responses and poor integration [13, 48]. It is crucial to acknowledge that TEUG and SIS begin in distinct mechanical states: TEUG is implanted as a pre-formed, self-supporting living tube with mature ECM, whereas SIS requires intraoperative manual tubularization. This procedural difference inevitably affects early compliance, anastomotic stress, and leakage risk. However, our long-term data demonstrate that TEUG’s biomimetic composition and immunomodulatory properties not only mitigate these initial mechanical challenges but also drive superior tissue remodeling, effectively preventing the fibrotic occlusion observed in the SIS group. Thus, despite differing starting conditions, the trajectory of functional integration strongly favors the TEUG strategy.

One of the key challenges in translating urethral tissue engineering to the clinic is the prolonged fabrication time. Although engineered grafts based on PLA nanofiber scaffolds seeded with autologous urothelial cells have been tested in a few clinical studies, there in vitro culture period extends up to approximately two months, making them unsuitable for acute trauma cases requiring immediate surgical intervention [14]. The strategy presented here eliminates the need for in vitro expansion of autologous cells and leverages subcutaneous pre-implantation to enable rapid ECM deposition, reducing the construction time of functional grafts by approximately 70%. Furthermore, compared to electrospinning, 3D printing reduces scaffold fabrication time by nearly 99% [40, 49, 52]. The synergy of these two innovations dramatically shortens the overall manufacturing timeline, bringing the concept of “off-the-shelf” functional urethral grafts closer to clinical reality.

Another critical factor for clinical adoption is scalable manufacturing. Functional urethral substitutes typically require a lumen diameter ≥ 3 mm and sufficient tissue thickness and mechanical strength. By precisely controlling 3D printing parameters, we can fabricate high-fidelity tubular scaffolds and regulate the number of host cell infiltration and matrix deposition layers to achieve the desired tissue thickness, meeting the dimensional and performance requirements for human urethral reconstruction. Looking toward clinical translation, scaling this approach to human-sized grafts introduces complexities in the degradation-remodeling balance. Larger grafts possess a reduced surface-area-to-volume ratio, which may slow down cell infiltration and extend the time required for complete ECM replacement. To address this, future iterations for human application may require tuning the PLA molecular weight or increasing scaffold macroporosity to synchronize the degradation rate with the slower pace of tissue ingrowth in larger defects. Ensuring that mechanical integrity is maintained by the nascent tissue before the scaffold loses significant strength will be paramount to prevent anastomotic failure or late-stage stenosis in human patients.

A technical limitation of this study is the extremely limited cross-reactivity of commercially available flow cytometry antibodies with immune cells from New Zealand rabbits, which restricts precise quantitative analysis of the host immune response. Although we systematically evaluated the local immune environment using multiple complementary methods—including histological staining (H&E, MTC, EVG), immunofluorescence, TEM, and qPCR, we observed that the engineered graft significantly reduced foreign body giant cell formation, minimized fibrous capsule thickness, and promoted an immunomodulatory microenvironment characterized by upregulation of anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage markers (e.g., CD206). This suggests strong immunomodulatory capacity. However, due to the lack of rabbit-specific surface marker antibodies, we remain unable to perform precise, high-throughput spatiotemporal tracking of infiltrating immune cell subsets, their population dynamics, and phenotypic transitions. This technical bottleneck is common in current rabbit-based tissue engineering studies, and future efforts must focus on developing more molecular tools for rabbit immune cell characterization. Furthermore, while our study confirms a pro-regenerative environment at 6 months, it did not capture the kinetics of the acute inflammatory phase immediately post-implantation. As foreign body reactions initiate early, the temporal evolution of immune responses prior to long-term remodeling remains uncharacterized. Future investigations should therefore prioritize high-resolution profiling of these early dynamics to fully elucidate how TEUG modulates the initial host response toward long-term tolerance.

Additionally, due to the predetermined animal protocol and limited tissue availability at each time point, we were unable to perform comparative mechanical property testing between explanted TEUG and native urethra. All harvested tissues were fully utilized for in vivo functional assessments and histological characterizations, leaving no remaining samples for mechanical analysis. This comparison will be included in future dedicated studies.

Additionally, a limitation of this study is that immunostaining was only performed on the middle segment of the TEUG. Although the distal regions were used for qPCR analysis and preliminary observations suggest good graft uniformity, we were unable to provide immunostaining data from the distal ends to further confirm graft uniformity and patency. This will be addressed in future studies by conducting full-length immunostaining analysis.

Notably, the “temporary scaffold + subcutaneous pre-implantation + host-driven remodeling” strategy developed in this study demonstrates strong expandability. In SD rats, a 3D-printed tubular construct was implanted subcutaneously and, after 14 days of pre-conditioning, formed a living tubular tissue enriched with host-derived cells and a nascent PG-rich ECM. When transplanted into an abdominal aortic defect in the same animal, it successfully achieved vascular reconstruction [38]. Decellularized matrices rich in glycosaminoglycans and PGs have been shown to effectively mitigate foreign body reactions and support functional integration in soft tissue regeneration [53], and similar advantages have been widely demonstrated in tissue engineering applications for nerves, myocardium, and trachea [54]. These findings not only confirm the structural stability, mechanical integrity, and biocompatibility of the engineered tissue under high hemodynamic stress but also highlight the platform potential of this strategy for regenerating other load-bearing or non-load-bearing hollow organs such as the urethra, trachea, and esophagus.

Conclusions

In summary, this study presents a novel strategy that integrates scaffold-guided structuring with host-driven self-assembly, utilizing 3D-printed biodegradable PLA scaffolds combined with 14-day subcutaneous pre-implantation to recruit host cells, which then secrete of a biomimetic ECM rich in collagen, elastin, and GAGs in situ. These components further integrate to form a bioactive interface centered on PG matrix, ultimately enabling the rapid fabrication of bioactive TEUGs. Relying on this PG matrix-based bioactive interface, the engineered ECM effectively mitigates foreign body reaction, supports the functional regeneration of urothelium and smooth muscle, and achieves long-term patency and physiological integration in a rabbit anterior urethral defect model. Notably, the same strategy has been successfully applied to abdominal aortic repair in SD rats, demonstrating its structural stability and broad applicability in load-bearing tubular organs. By eliminating the need for in vitro cell expansion and significantly shortening the fabrication timeline, this strategy further enhances the integration efficiency between the graft and host tissue through the regulatory role of the PG matrix bioactive interface. It thus provides a scalable, clinically translatable, and “off-the-shelf” solution for urethral regeneration. Our findings establish a new paradigm for regenerative therapies targeting not only urethral reconstruction but also other hollow organ systems.
