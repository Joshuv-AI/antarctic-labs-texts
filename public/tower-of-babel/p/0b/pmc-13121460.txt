Mechanical, tribological, and physical performance of sisal fiber reinforced epoxy composites filled with Aningeria, Cordia Africana, and Cedar Sawdust
Authors: Jemanesh Girma Abay, Besufekad Negash Fetene, Gadisa Sufe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Composites are increasingly employed in aerospace, automotive, construction, sports, and defense industries owing to their high strength-to-weight ratio, corrosion resistance, and customizable properties. This study focuses on sisal fiber reinforced epoxy composites with sawdust fillers from three Ethiopian hardwood species: Aningeria (Kerero), Cordia Africana (Wanza), and Cedar (Tid). Using Taguchi design of experiments (L9 orthogonal array), nine composite samples were fabricated by the hand lay-up method with fiber orientations of 0/90°, 0/45°, and random. Mechanical (flexural, compression, impact), tribological (wear), physical (water absorption), and morphological (SEM) properties were systematically evaluated. Results revealed that Aningeria sawdust composites with 0/90° fiber orientation and 10 wt% filler exhibited the highest flexural strength (90.08 MPa), superior impact strength (78.4 kJ/m2), and lowest water absorption (0.53% after 72 h). Cordia Africana sawdust composites with 0/90° orientation and 20 wt% filler demonstrated the highest compressive strength (87.97 MPa) and optimal wear resistance (18 μm). Statistical analysis identified fiber orientation as the most significant factor (p < 0.05), contributing 73.27% to flexural strength and 90.12% to wear resistance. The study demonstrates that sisal fiber composites reinforced with locally available sawdust fillers provide an eco-friendly alternative to conventional synthetic composites, with promising applications in structural and defense components where balanced mechanical performance and sustainability are required.

Introduction

The increasing global emphasis on environmental sustainability and economic viability has driven researchers to develop new materials based significantly on natural renewable resources, thus addressing environmental issues through alternative composite materials1–3. The composite industry, particularly the wood-based sector, faces challenges in sourcing solid wood due to environmental impacts such as deforestation, drought, and global warming4,5. Consequently, there is a growing demand for materials that can partially replace wood and other non-renewable resources. Sawdust, a valuable by-product of wood production, offers a sustainable alternative. It acts as a filler, enhancing material weight and reducing the consumption of natural plant fibers6,7 In Ethiopia, the primary wood species used in the furniture industry include Cordia Africana (wanza), Cedar (Tid), and Aningeria (kerero). Cordia Africana, a fast-growing and highly valued timber tree, is hard and durable, making it ideal for high-quality furniture8,9. Cedar, known for its resistance to fungal diseases and weather durability, is preferred for construction and furniture. Aningeria wood, also durable, is used in various applications, including furniture and construction10,11. Despite the utility of these woods, significant amounts of sawdust are discarded as waste, contributing to environmental pollution Natural fibers, such as sisal, are eco-friendly alternatives due to their availability, lightweight, high specific strength, recyclability, and biodegradability12,13. Sisal fiber, from Agave sisalana, is robust and used in various industries due to its excellent properties14. Although not as strong as carbon fibers, sisal is less brittle and more affordable15,16.

Natural fiber reinforced polymer composites have gained significant attention as sustainable alternatives to synthetic materials due to environmental concerns and the depletion of petroleum-based resources. Fibers such as sisal, jute, flax, and kenaf offer low density, biodegradability, cost effectiveness, and adequate mechanical performance, making them suitable for lightweight engineering applications17,18. When embedded in polymer matrices, these fibers enhance stiffness, impact resistance, and energy absorption, while recent studies on agro waste derived lignocellulosic fibers have further confirmed their potential for automotive and structural components.

The incorporation of bio based particulate fillers provides an additional pathway to tailor composite performance. Fillers such as wood flour and sawdust improve stiffness, wear resistance, and interfacial stress transfer while reducing material cost and moisture sensitivity. Their effectiveness depends on particle size, dispersion, and compatibility with the matrix, with well dispersed fillers shown to delay crack initiation and enhance compressive and tribological behavior through load sharing mechanisms. Recent research has increasingly focused on optimizing fiber architecture and hybrid reinforcement strategies19,20. Woven natural fiber mats demonstrate superior flexural, impact, and compressive performance compared with randomly oriented fibers due to improved load transfer and reduced void content. In parallel, lignocellulosic fillers have been successfully integrated to enhance stiffness and sustainability without compromising mechanical integrity. Sisal fiber is particularly attractive due to its favorable balance of mechanical performance, renewability, and wide local availability. Previous studies report good interfacial bonding with epoxy matrices and improved properties when fiber orientation is controlled. The combined use of woven sisal fibers and sawdust fillers therefore represents a promising approach for developing cost effective and sustainable composites suitable for semi structural automotive and defense applications21,22.

Researchers have explored developing composite materials from sisal fibers by hybridizing them with other materials. For instance in ref23,24, reported on the characteristics of sisal fiber as a composite material for light-body vehicles. Sisal fibers were extracted from plant leaves and treated with sodium hydroxide to improve surface roughness. Composite specimens were prepared using general-purpose resin as the matrix and sisal fibers as the reinforcement, oriented at (0/90)° and (0/45)° using the hand layup technique. The tensile, compression, flexural, and water absorption tests revealed that bidirectional (0°/90°) treated sisal fiber reinforced composites had better tensile and flexural strength, with minimal water absorption suitable for auto body panels25,26. reported optimized the mechanical properties of sawdust/woven jute fiber/polyester composites. They used polyester as a matrix with jute fiber and teak sawdust as reinforcement and filler. The study evaluated the impact of factors such as sawdust density and weight ratio, woven jute grade, number of jute layers, and treatment durations on the composites’ mechanical properties. Results showed significant enhancements in impact strength, flexural strength, tensile strength, and hardness with specific treatments and compositions. Similarly, other studies, such as those27,28, investigated the mechanical, thermal, and water absorption properties of various composite materials, demonstrating their potential for applications like boat hulls and low-cost construction work. These studies underscore the versatility and effectiveness of using natural fibers and fillers to enhance composite materials’ properties for diverse applications.

Although natural fiber–reinforced polymer composites have been extensively investigated, recent reviews in Composites Part B: Engineering, Journal of Cleaner Production, and Polymers confirm that research has primarily emphasized generic natural fibers and conventional lignocellulosic fillers. Studies addressing region-specific resources and their integrated optimization remain limited. In particular, no systematic investigation has examined the hybridization of Ethiopian sisal fiber with locally available hardwood sawdusts Aningeria (Kerero), Cordia africana (Wanza), and Cedar (Tid) under unified fabrication and statistical optimization frameworks.

Recent contributions, including the study reported in Journal of Materials Research and Technology39, have demonstrated the potential of hybrid fillers in enhancing composite performance. Similarly, the incorporation of sawdust as a sustainable filler has been documented in Polymers40–42 However, these studies do not address the combined influence of specific Ethiopian hardwood species, woven sisal fiber orientation, and multi-response optimization of mechanical, tribological, and physical properties. Moreover, the role of distinct chemical compositions and microstructural morphologies of Aningeria, Cordia africana, and Cedar sawdust in governing interfacial bonding, wear behavior, moisture uptake, and machinability has not been elucidated.

Despite the broad literature on sisal-reinforced composites, the characteristics of Ethiopian sisal fiber have not been systematically evaluated. Ethiopian sisal exhibits elevated cellulose content (~ 78%) relative to typical values reported for sisal from other regions (63–65%), attributable to local agro-climatic conditions. The implications of this compositional variation for stiffness, interfacial adhesion, and energy absorption in polymer matrices remain unexplored. Concurrently, substantial quantities of sawdust generated from Ethiopian furniture industries represent an underutilized lignocellulosic resource whose reinforcing potential in thermoset composites has not been assessed.

Accordingly, this study establishes a structured experimental and optimization framework to address these gaps. First, Ethiopian sisal fiber and the three hardwood sawdust fillers are characterized in terms of chemical composition, morphology, and relevant physical properties. Second, hybrid epoxy composites are fabricated via hand lay-up with controlled fiber orientation. Third, mechanical (flexural, impact, compression), tribological (dry sliding wear), physical (water absorption), and microstructural (SEM) responses are systematically evaluated using a Taguchi L9 orthogonal array. Fourth, multi-response performance optimization is conducted through Grey Relational Analysis to identify parameter combinations that maximize overall performance. Finally, machinability characteristics relevant to semi-structural applications, including rifle buttstocks, are assessed.

By integrating region-specific raw materials with statistical design of experiments and multi-criteria decision analysis, this work provides quantitative insight into filler–fiber synergy, orientation effects, and property trade-offs. The outcomes establish design guidelines for sustainable, high-performance composites derived from Ethiopian renewable resources and support their deployment in cost-sensitive semi-structural applications.

Materials and methods

Accordingly, this study examines sisal fiber reinforced epoxy composites incorporating locally available sawdust fillers from Aningeria, Cordia Africana, and Cedar. The effects of filler type, filler content, and fiber orientation on mechanical, tribological, surface, and physical performance are systematically evaluated. The findings provide clear insight into the potential of these sustainable composites for structural and semi structural applications where strength, durability, and environmental compatibility are essential.

For each experimental condition, three specimens were tested and the results are reported as mean values. These averages were used for statistical evaluation within the Taguchi framework, following established practice in multi factor composite optimization studies, while maintaining an appropriate balance between experimental rigor and resource efficiency.

Materials used for the experiment

Sisal fibers were extracted from fresh leaves of Agave sisalana harvested from local plantations in Bishoftu. Mature leaves were cut at the base and manually decorticated using a stainless-steel knife to separate fibers from the parenchymatous tissue. The fibers were washed with running water to remove chlorophyll and residual cellular matter, then sun-dried for 48 h. Manual extraction was adopted to maintain traceability of processing history, ensure uniform quality control, and document the complete chain from plant source to composite fabrication. All extraction and subsequent treatments were conducted in the materials laboratory of the Defence University Engineering College. For this study, sisal leaves were collected from Bishoftu, Ethiopia, harvested at the base of the plant. The fibers were manually extracted using a knife and subsequently treated with sodium hydroxide to improve mechanical performance, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Composite fabrication followed a sequential protocol comprising raw material preparation, fiber surface treatment, matrix preparation, laminate stacking, curing, and specimen machining. After extraction, sisal fibers were subjected to alkali treatment to enhance interfacial adhesion. Epoxy resin and hardener were mixed according to the manufacturer’s stoichiometric ratio and mechanically stirred to ensure homogeneity. Treated fibers and selected hardwood sawdust fillers were arranged within the mold according to predefined orientation angles. The laminate was consolidated using rollers to expel entrapped air and promote uniform resin impregnation. Curing was performed at ambient temperature, after which hardened laminates were machined into standard specimens in accordance with relevant ASTM procedures.

Sisal fibers were selected based on renewability, biodegradability, and regional availability. In Ethiopia, sisal cultivation supports fiber production for agricultural and industrial applications. The physicochemical characteristics of Ethiopian sisal fibers are strongly influenced by agro-climatic conditions and extraction methodology. Teklu43 reported a cellulose content of approximately 78% for Ethiopian sisal treated with 5% NaOH, exceeding values documented for sisal from Morocco (63.33%) and other tropical regions (64–65.2%). Elevated cellulose content enhances stiffness and tensile strength, with reported tensile strength reaching 387 MPa, compared to 278 MPa for water-retted fibers and 362 MPa for mechanically scraped fibers. The relatively low lignin content (~ 2.5%) facilitates improved fiber–matrix interaction after alkali treatment by exposing cellulose hydroxyl groups that participate in interfacial bonding with epoxy systems. Alkaline treatment was performed using a 5% (w/v) sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution at 25 °C for 2 h to remove hemicellulose, lignin, waxes, and surface impurities. Fibers were subsequently neutralized in 1% acetic acid, rinsed with distilled water until neutral pH was attained, and oven-dried at 60 °C for 24 h. The selected NaOH concentration aligns with optimization studies indicating that concentrations between 0.5% and 5% effectively enhance surface roughness and fibrillation without inducing significant cellulose degradation, whereas concentrations above 7% may reduce tensile strength due to chain scission and depolymerization.

The alkaline modification mechanism involves disruption of hydrogen bonding within the lignocellulosic network and partial removal of amorphous constituents (hemicellulose and lignin). This process increases surface roughness, reduces fiber diameter, and improves mechanical interlocking with the polymer matrix. Simultaneously, exposure of reactive hydroxyl groups enhances physicochemical adhesion at the fiber–epoxy interface, thereby contributing to improved load transfer efficiency and overall composite performance.

The chemical composition and morphology of wood sawdust fillers critically influence composite stiffness, strength, moisture uptake, and interfacial bonding. Comprehensive characterization of Aningeria (Kerero), Cordia africana (Wanza), and Cedar (Tid) sawdusts was conducted, and the results are summarized in Table 2. Aningeria exhibits the highest cellulose content (48.98%) and crystallinity index (52.8%), together with a higher aspect ratio (5.8), indicating elongated particle morphology conducive to improved load transfer within the epoxy matrix. Cordia africana presents intermediate cellulose content and aspect ratio (3.9), whereas Cedar shows the lowest aspect ratio (1.5) and the highest lignin content (28.54%). Elevated lignin content contributes to intrinsic weather resistance but may limit interfacial bonding due to reduced availability of hydroxyl groups. Filler density further influences composite performance; higher-density particles such as Aningeria reduce void formation and promote more efficient stress distribution, resulting in enhanced flexural strength.

Collectively, these compositional and morphological differences establish a mechanistic basis for interpreting the comparative mechanical, tribological, and physical performance of the developed hybrid composites.

A thermosetting epoxy system (HY556 resin with HY951 hardener) was employed as the matrix material. The resin was procured from World Glass Fiber Company, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Epoxy matrices are widely used in structural composites due to their high stiffness, low shrinkage during curing, chemical resistance, and strong interfacial adhesion with lignocellulosic reinforcements, enabling efficient stress transfer from matrix to reinforcement.

The resin and hardener were mixed in a 10:1 weight ratio (epoxy: hardener) in accordance with the manufacturer’s specifications, producing a cured density of approximately 1.4 g/cm3. Accurate stoichiometric proportioning is essential to ensure complete cross-linking of epoxide groups with amine curing agents; deviations from the recommended ratio may result in incomplete curing, reduced mechanical performance, or matrix embrittlement. The mixture was stirred gently to achieve homogeneity while minimizing air entrapment. HY951 acts as an amine-based curing agent, initiating a polyaddition reaction that transforms the low-viscosity resin into a three-dimensional cross-linked network. This curing process enhances mechanical strength, dimensional stability, and chemical resistance of the composite. To facilitate demolding and prevent adhesion to the mold surface, a thin layer of paste wax was applied as a release agent prior to laminate fabrication.

Distilled water was used during fiber and sawdust washing procedures to prevent ionic contamination. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) was employed as the alkaline treatment agent for surface modification of sisal fibers. Hardwood sawdust fillers, including Cedar (Tid) and Cordia africana (Wanza), were sourced from the Gaft Armament Industry woodworking facility and incorporated as particulate reinforcement in combination with woven sisal fibers. This matrix–reinforcement configuration was selected to ensure physicochemical compatibility between the thermoset resin and the chemically treated natural fillers while preserving manufacturing simplicity appropriate for semi-structural composite applications. The morphology and particle characteristics of the processed sawdust fillers are illustrated in Fig. 2, where (a) Aningeria (Kerero) sawdust, (b) Cordia africana (Wanza) sawdust, and (c) Cedar sawdust are presented for comparative visualization.

Sisal fibers were treated with an aqueous sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution to improve interfacial bonding. The treatment was carried out using a 5 wt% NaOH solution at room temperature for 2 h, followed by thorough washing with distilled water until neutral pH was achieved and subsequent air drying. This treatment protocol is commonly reported for natural fiber surface modification.

Preparation of composite samples

The composite specimens were fabricated using the hand lay-up method as illustrated in Fig. 3 because of its simplicity, low cost, and suitability for prototype composite development. Nine composite formulations were prepared following the Taguchi L9 orthogonal array design. The epoxy matrix system consisted of HY556 resin and HY951 hardener mixed in a 10:1 weight ratio (epoxy: hardener) and gently stirred for approximately 5 min to obtain homogeneous mixing while minimizing air bubble formation.

A rectangular steel mold with dimensions of 300 mm × 300 mm × 5 mm was prepared, and a silicon-based release spray was applied to facilitate demolding. Composite fabrication proceeded sequentially by first applying a thin epoxy–hardener mixture layer onto the mold surface. Subsequently, the first layer of woven sisal fiber was placed according to the specified orientation (0/90°, 0/45°, or random). The predetermined quantity of sawdust filler was then uniformly distributed over the fiber layer, followed by additional resin application. A hand roller was used after each layer to ensure complete wetting, improve fiber–matrix contact, and remove entrapped air. This stacking sequence was repeated until three laminate plies were formed for all composite specimens.

After lay-up, a flat plate with approximately 5 kg load was applied to maintain uniform consolidation pressure. The laminates were cured at room temperature (25 °C) for 24 h under static pressure to promote initial cross-linking. Post-curing was subsequently performed at 80 °C for 2 h to complete polymer network formation and improve thermal and mechanical stability.

Following curing, composite plates were trimmed to remove edge irregularities and cut into standard test specimens using a diamond-tipped saw according to relevant testing standards. Five replicate specimens were prepared for each experimental condition to ensure statistical consistency and reliability of results. Care was taken during fabrication to prevent fiber distortion and displacement, thereby preserving laminate structural integrity.

The fiber length and woven architecture were kept consistent across all samples, with the sisal fiber fraction fixed at 10 wt% to isolate the effects of filler type and fiber orientation. Void content and fiber volume fraction were not measured quantitatively; instead, SEM imaging was used to qualitatively evaluate filler dispersion and fiber–matrix interfacial bonding. Cedar sawdust was included in the study and assessed for mechanical and physical performance, but its properties were generally lower than those of Aningeria and Cordia Africana fillers and are discussed comparatively.

Prior to composite fabrication, sawdust fillers were dried and sieved to remove oversized particles, ensuring relatively uniform size suitable for hand lay-up processing. Although precise particle size distribution was not determined, future studies will include detailed characterization to better understand its impact on mechanical and tribological behavior.

Experimental design

The experimental design was developed using Taguchi’s method to evaluate the influence of three control factors, each at three levels, on composite mechanical, tribological, and physical responses. The L9 orthogonal array (33 design) was selected to provide efficient parameter screening while reducing experimental runs from 27 in a full factorial design to 9 trials. Three filler loading levels (10 wt%, 15 wt%, and 20 wt%) were investigated. These levels were selected based on typical reinforcement ranges reported for natural fiber–epoxy composites, where moderate filler content promotes effective stress transfer, whereas excessive filler addition may induce particle agglomeration, void formation, and matrix brittleness.

Three fiber orientation configurations were considered: 0/90°, 0/45°, and random orientation. The 0/90° arrangement represents orthogonal laminate alignment promoting bidirectional load resistance, 0/45° provides mixed shear–tension response, and random orientation simulates isotropic reinforcement behavior. These configurations were chosen to analyze the relationship between fiber alignment, interfacial stress distribution, and damage tolerance characteristics. The Taguchi design facilitated systematic assessment of parameter effects while maintaining experimental efficiency and statistical robustness for multi-response composite optimization.To isolate the effects of filler type and fiber orientation, the sisal fiber content was maintained at a constant 10 wt% for all specimens. This fixed reinforcement level ensures direct comparability among samples and provides a balanced configuration suitable for hand lay-up processing and semi structural applications.

A Taguchi L9 experimental design was adopted to investigate three factors at three levels, resulting in nine experimental combinations. Statistical evaluation of the Taguchi L9 orthogonal array and Grey Relational Analysis was conducted using Minitab Statistical Software, Version 21 (Minitab LLC, State College, PA, USA), the software is available at https://www.minitab.com/en-us/. to identify significant factors and optimal parameter settings. Each experimental run corresponds to a unique material composition, with sawdust filler, sisal fiber, and epoxy resin weight ratios of 10:10:80, 15:10:75, and 20:10:70, respectively as illustrated in Table 3. All laminates consisted of three plies, as summarized in Table 1, which presents the selected process parameters and their corresponding levels.

The rationale for selecting these factor levels was established through preliminary experimental screening and an extensive literature review. The 10–20 wt% range for sawdust filler was adopted based on prior findings indicating that lower filler loading (10 wt%) generally enhances flexural and impact performance, whereas higher filler loading (20 wt%) contributes to improved compressive strength and wear resistance. The signal-to-noise ratio was also used as a performance measure to assess the robustness of the process and evaluate deviations from the desired values46. Noise refers to uncontrollable factors associated with the experiment that need to be eliminated to reduce variability in the experimental or actual process variables47 The goal is to reduce noise and decrease variance in the process variables. To improve the effectiveness of this technique for multi-response optimization problems, the Taguchi method can be combined with Grey Relational Analysis (GRA), which was developed by48. First, the response parameters were converted into an S/N ratio. The S/N ratio was cooperated into grey relational analysis and converted into the normalizing sequence, the deviation sequence which was used to calculate the grey relation coefficient was calculated from the normalizing sequence46. Finally, the GRG value which is used on the taguchi optimization for optimization purposes was calculated from the gray relation coefficient.

Grey Relational Analysis identified the optimal parameter combination as 0/90° fiber orientation with 10 wt% Aningeria sawdust and 10 wt% sisal fiber reinforcement, corresponding to Sample 1. This configuration provides a balanced overall performance by simultaneously enhancing flexural strength, impact resistance, wear behavior, and moisture resistance. Although higher filler contents improved certain individual properties such as compressive strength, the GRA approach emphasizes overall multi response optimization rather than maximizing a single property. No separate validation experiment was conducted; however, the predicted optimum is consistent with the experimentally observed superior combined performance of Sample 1.

The Taguchi method employed an L9 orthogonal array to evaluate three control factors at three levels, yielding nine experimental conditions. Signal to noise ratios were calculated using the larger the better criterion for mechanical properties and the smaller the better criterion for wear and water absorption. Normalized responses with equal weighting were then used in Grey Relational Analysis to assess the overall performance of the composites.

Results and discussion

Flexural test

The flexural behavior of the hybrid sisal fiber–epoxy composites is governed by synergistic interactions among filler type, filler concentration, fiber orientation, and interfacial bonding quality, as illustrated in Figs. 12 and 13. The superior flexural strength observed for Aningeria sawdust–reinforced composites can be explained through microstructural reinforcement mechanisms associated with its intrinsic physicochemical properties. Among the evaluated fillers, Aningeria sawdust demonstrated the highest flexural performance, reaching approximately 90.08 MPa at 10 wt% filler loading with 0/90° sisal fiber orientation. This behavior is primarily attributed to its relatively high cellulose content (48.98%), crystallinity index (52.8%), and aspect ratio (5.8). Cellulose-rich lignocellulosic particles provide enhanced stiffness because cellulose microfibrils function as load-bearing structural elements within the composite system. The high crystallinity index indicates a more ordered polymeric structure, reducing molecular chain mobility and increasing resistance to elastic deformation under bending stress.

The aspect ratio of reinforcement particles plays a critical role in stress transfer efficiency. Higher aspect ratio fillers promote better mechanical interlocking and facilitate shear stress transmission from the epoxy matrix to the reinforcement phase. Aningeria sawdust particles exhibit elongated morphology compared with Cordia africana (aspect ratio 3.9) and Cedar (1.5), enabling more effective load distribution across the composite interface. Fiber orientation significantly influenced bending performance. The 0/90° woven sisal fiber configuration aligned reinforcement fibers parallel and perpendicular to the principal stress direction during three-point bending loading. This arrangement enhances resistance to tensile stress on the lower bending surface and compressive stress on the upper surface, thereby maximizing flexural load-bearing capacity. In contrast, 0/45° orientation introduces mixed shear–tension stress states, while random orientation reduces directional reinforcement efficiency, leading to premature microcrack initiation. The observed reduction in flexural strength beyond 10 wt% sawdust loading is primarily associated with particle agglomeration and matrix discontinuities. Excess filler content increases viscosity during fabrication, which restricts resin flow and reduces fiber wetting efficiency. Consequently, microvoids are formed within the matrix structure. These voids act as stress concentration sites where crack nucleation and propagation occur under bending load.

Void fraction analysis presented in microstructural results supports this mechanism, showing an increase in void content from approximately 2.03% at optimal composition to 4.58% at higher filler loading conditions. Increased porosity reduces effective load transfer area and promotes interfacial debonding between fiber, filler, and matrix phases.

Comparison with previously reported natural fiber composites indicates that the optimized hybrid system provides superior bending resistance. Pure epoxy matrices typically exhibit flexural strength in the range of 55–65 MPa. The incorporation of 10 wt% Aningeria sawdust and controlled fiber orientation enhanced flexural strength by approximately 38–64%, exceeding performance improvements commonly reported for randomly oriented short fiber composites.

From a structural mechanics perspective, the hybrid reinforcement system improves composite modulus and fracture toughness by simultaneously exploiting matrix stiffness, fiber tensile strength, and particulate filler load-sharing effects. The alkaline treatment of sisal fibers further contributed to performance improvement by removing hemicellulose and lignin components, increasing surface roughness, and promoting hydrogen bonding interactions between cellulose hydroxyl groups and epoxy polymer chains.

Overall, the flexural response demonstrates that optimal mechanical performance is achieved when reinforcement morphology, chemical composition, orientation architecture, and filler concentration are simultaneously controlled. The findings confirm that Aningeria sawdust combined with 0/90° woven sisal fiber orientation represents the most efficient configuration for bending load resistance in the developed hybrid composite system.

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to quantify the relative influence of control factors on flexural strength variation, as summarized in Table 8. The statistical evaluation was performed using signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio data obtained from the Taguchi L9 orthogonal array design. The results indicate that sisal fiber orientation was the most dominant parameter affecting flexural strength, contributing approximately 73.27% of the total variation.

The p-value associated with fiber orientation was 0.033, which is below the significance threshold of 0.05, confirming that fiber alignment has a statistically significant effect on bending performance. The dominant contribution of fiber orientation can be explained by directional stress transfer mechanisms in woven reinforcement structures. Proper alignment of fibers with principal loading directions enhances tensile resistance on the outer bending surface and compressive load distribution on the opposite surface during flexural deformation. However, the ANOVA model has certain statistical limitations due to the experimental design. Because the L9 orthogonal array provides only two degrees of freedom for residual error, the statistical power of hypothesis testing is relatively constrained. Consequently, the ANOVA results primarily indicate optimization trends rather than providing exhaustive predictive inference. Further experimental replication with expanded design matrices would improve parameter estimation accuracy and reduce uncertainty.

The significance of fiber orientation over filler type and loading level suggests that structural architecture plays a more critical role than particulate reinforcement concentration in determining flexural response for woven natural fiber composites. This finding is consistent with mechanics-based composite theory, where load-bearing fiber continuity and orientation efficiency govern macroscopic bending stiffness.

Overall, the statistical analysis confirms that controlled sisal fiber orientation is the primary factor governing flexural strength enhancement in the developed hybrid composite system, while filler type and content provide secondary optimization effects.

The hybridization of sisal fibers with Aningeria sawdust filler at 10 wt% loading and 0/90° woven fiber orientation produced the optimum flexural performance of the developed composites. This result highlights the critical role of reinforcement architecture and filler selection in governing bending resistance in natural fiber–reinforced epoxy systems.

The superior flexural strength of approximately 90.08 MPa obtained for Aningeria sawdust composites can be explained by the synergistic contribution of chemical composition, microstructural order, and particle morphology. Aningeria sawdust contains relatively higher cellulose content (48.98%), which enhances reinforcement efficiency because cellulose microfibrils serve as the principal load-bearing phase in lignocellulosic materials. The comparatively lower cellulose content in Cordia africana (45.23%) and Cedar (42.67%) reduces effective stress transfer capability within the matrix–filler network. In addition, the crystallinity index of Aningeria sawdust (52.8%) indicates a more ordered cellulose molecular structure, which restricts polymer chain mobility and increases elastic modulus under bending deformation. High crystallinity generally correlates with improved stiffness and resistance to microstructural shear deformation. Furthermore, the aspect ratio of Aningeria particles (5.8) is significantly higher than that of Cordia africana (3.9) and Cedar (1.5), promoting efficient load redistribution by facilitating mechanical interlocking and shear stress transfer across the epoxy interface.

The observed performance trend is consistent with previously reported findings in natural fiber composite research. For example, flexural strengths of 75–85 MPa were reported for optimized sisal–glass hybrid composites in Composites Part B: Engineering studies. The present hybrid composite system demonstrates higher flexural resistance than conventional pure sisal or randomly oriented short fiber composites, which typically exhibit flexural strengths in the range of 65–85 MPa.

The effectiveness of 0/90° fiber orientation can be explained using classical laminate mechanics. Bidirectional woven reinforcement aligns fibers along the principal tensile and compressive stress directions generated during three-point bending. Consequently, bending-induced tensile stress at the lower surface and compressive stress at the upper surface are more efficiently resisted. In contrast, 0/45° and random orientations introduce mixed stress states that reduce effective load-bearing continuity.

Flexural strength reduction observed when sawdust content exceeded 10 wt% is primarily associated with particle agglomeration and resin wetting limitations. Increased filler loading increases melt viscosity during fabrication, restricting resin flow and promoting void formation. Microstructural analysis confirms this mechanism, showing void fraction increase from approximately 2.03% at optimal composition (A-10-90) to 4.58% at higher filler loading conditions. These voids function as stress concentration zones that accelerate crack initiation and propagation under bending load.

For reference, unreinforced epoxy resin generally exhibits flexural strength between 55 and 65 MPa. The optimized hybrid composite system achieved flexural strength enhancement of approximately 38–64% relative to neat epoxy, exceeding performance improvements commonly reported for randomly oriented natural fiber composites. This improvement demonstrates the effectiveness of combining controlled fiber architecture with chemically compatible lignocellulosic particulate reinforcement.

Overall, the results confirm that mechanical performance is governed by the combined effects of filler chemistry, microstructural crystallinity, particle morphology, and reinforcement orientation. The hybrid composite configuration developed in this study provides a promising material candidate for semi-structural applications requiring high bending stiffness and sustainable resource utilization.

Compression

Compressive strength behavior of sisal fiber–reinforced epoxy composites containing Aningeria, Cordia africana, and Cedar sawdust fillers was evaluated using a Universal Testing Machine following general compressive testing principles consistent with composite material characterization standards. The results are presented in Figs. 14 and 15. The highest compressive strength, approximately 87.97 MPa, was obtained for composites containing 20 wt% Cordia africana sawdust with 0/90° sisal fiber orientation. This performance can be attributed to the relatively dense microstructure and higher lignin content of Cordia africana hardwood filler. Lignin enhances rigidity by reinforcing the lignocellulosic cell wall structure, thereby increasing resistance to compressive deformation.

Fiber orientation significantly influenced compressive response. The 0/90° woven fiber configuration provided superior compression resistance compared with 0/45° and random orientations. In this arrangement, fibers are positioned perpendicular to the compressive loading direction, forming a structural architecture analogous to column reinforcement. This geometry improves micro-buckling resistance and promotes efficient vertical load transfer across the laminate thickness.

Impact testing

The impact behavior of the hybrid composites is governed by the combined effects of reinforcement chemistry, microstructural architecture, and dynamic fracture energy dissipation mechanisms, as illustrated in Figs. 16 and 17. Impact toughness is a critical property for semi-structural materials because it reflects the ability of the composite to absorb and dissipate sudden mechanical energy without catastrophic failure.

The composite containing 10 wt% Aningeria sawdust filler and 0/90° woven sisal fiber orientation exhibited the highest impact strength, approximately 210 J/mm2 (≈ 78.4 kJ/m2). This superior performance is primarily attributed to the synergistic interaction between high cellulose-content particulate reinforcement and chemically treated fiber–matrix interfaces. Cellulose microfibrils serve as energy-absorbing structural elements because their semi-crystalline molecular structure allows limited elastic deformation prior to fracture.

The dynamic fracture resistance of the Aningeria hybrid composite can be explained through multiple energy dissipation mechanisms. During impact loading, crack propagation is resisted by fiber pull-out, fiber fracture, matrix yielding, and interfacial frictional sliding. The alkali treatment of sisal fibers improved interfacial adhesion by removing lignin and hemicellulose components, thereby increasing surface roughness and exposing hydroxyl functional groups capable of hydrogen bonding with the epoxy matrix. This improved interfacial chemistry enhances stress transfer efficiency and delays crack initiation. The aspect ratio of Aningeria sawdust particles (5.8) significantly contributed to impact toughness. Higher aspect ratio fillers promote effective mechanical interlocking and provide longer crack propagation pathways. When dynamic load is applied, cracks must circumvent elongated reinforcement particles, which increases fracture surface energy requirements and improves toughness.

The woven 0/90° fiber orientation forms a multidirectional reinforcement network that functions as a crack arresting structure. Under impact loading, propagating cracks encounter fibers oriented perpendicular to crack growth direction. This induces crack deflection, crack branching, and interlaminar shear stress redistribution. Such structural architecture enhances progressive failure behavior rather than sudden brittle fracture.

The reduction in impact strength observed beyond 10 wt% filler loading is associated with microstructural instability induced by particle agglomeration. Increasing filler concentration increases melt viscosity during fabrication, which restricts resin infiltration into fiber bundles. As a result, microvoids are formed within the matrix. These voids act as stress concentration points that facilitate crack nucleation under impact loading. Void fraction measurements confirmed this mechanism by showing increased porosity at higher filler loadings.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to investigate the microstructural morphology, fracture characteristics, and interfacial bonding quality of the hybrid composite specimens. SEM is a high-resolution imaging technique that utilizes a focused electron beam to interact with the specimen surface, generating secondary and backscattered electron signals that provide information on surface topology, phase distribution, and defect morphology.

SEM micrographs were captured at multiple magnification levels ranging from 50× to 1000× to evaluate fiber dispersion, matrix continuity, void formation, and fracture surface characteristics. The analysis was conducted on fractured composite surfaces to elucidate failure mechanisms under mechanical loading. Because the composite plates were fabricated using the hand lay-up open molding technique, the material system was inherently susceptible to air entrapment and void formation during resin impregnation.

Microstructural observations presented in Fig. 18 reveal clear relationships between morphology and mechanical performance. The A-10-90 composite exhibits relatively uniform fiber distribution and minimal porosity. At intermediate magnifications (200×), strong fiber–matrix interfacial adhesion is evident, indicating effective chemical and mechanical bonding after alkaline fiber treatment. At higher magnification (500×), fiber pull-out and localized matrix plastic deformation are observed, suggesting ductile fracture behavior and efficient energy absorption during mechanical loading.

The high-resolution image in Fig. 18i demonstrates the effect of 5% NaOH surface treatment on sisal fibers. Alkali treatment removed surface impurities and amorphous lignocellulosic components, exposing cellulose microfibrils and increasing surface roughness. The resulting fibrillated morphology provides mechanical anchoring sites that promote hydrogen bonding and interfacial mechanical interlocking between fiber and epoxy matrix, thereby improving load transfer efficiency.

In contrast, the C-20-R composite exhibits microstructural heterogeneity associated with higher filler loading. SEM images show void regions with estimated void fraction approximately 4.07%. These voids originate from resin viscosity increase and incomplete wetting during fabrication. Voids act as stress concentration zones that accelerate crack initiation under flexural and impact loading conditions, explaining the reduced toughness of high filler-content samples.

Machinability processing by image J soft ware

Delamination in fiber-reinforced polymer composites has traditionally been evaluated using digital image analysis of light microscopic images. However, many existing methods suffer from incomplete documentation, require manual interventions, and are difficult to automate or reproduce. ImageJ software provides a robust platform for automated image analysis and is widely used across scientific fields for quantitative measurement of image-based data. In this study, an automated methodology was developed using ImageJ to measure delamination areas, as illustrated in Fig. 19. The delaminated regions were quantified by the image-processing algorithm, with area expressed in pixels or other units, as summarized in Table 12. Visual inspection confirmed that upon completion of drilling, single-fiber pullout occurred near the bottom of the hole. ImageJ analysis verified these observations, revealing that 10 mm drill bits induced delamination at the top surface and slight fiber pullout at the bottom, while 12 mm drill bits caused more pronounced fiber pullout due to the composite’s layered structure. In contrast, 8 mm drill bits did not produce measurable delamination or fiber pullout, indicating that smaller drill diameters are preferable for maintaining composite integrity.

Compared with traditional manual measurement techniques, ImageJ-based digital analysis offers enhanced repeatability, reduced operator bias, and faster quantification of delamination areas. Manual approaches rely on visual estimation and hand-drawn measurements, which are time-consuming and inconsistent. ImageJ allows automated thresholding, edge detection, and pixel-based area calculation, providing objective and reproducible assessments across multiple samples.

The developed method effectively distinguishes between delamination and fiber pullout using contrast differences, edge discontinuities, and geometric characteristics of the drilled surfaces. Delaminated regions appear as irregular peripheral damage zones surrounding the drilled hole, while fiber pullout is identified as elongated protrusions aligned with the fiber direction. Although the approach reliably quantifies delamination, microscopic overlap between damage modes may require higher-resolution imaging or hybrid image-analysis techniques for comprehensive damage classification.

Chemical characterization of Sawdust fillers using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR)

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to characterize the chemical functionalities of Aningeria (Kerero), Cordia africana (Wanza), and Cedar (Tid) sawdust fillers and to establish correlations between chemical composition and composite performance. The analysis was conducted using an FTIR spectrometer (model IS50AXB) with a spectral resolution of 4 cm−1 across the mid-infrared region of 4000–500 cm−1, which is suitable for identifying lignocellulosic functional groups.

The FTIR spectra of Aningeria and Cordia africana sawdusts, presented in Fig. 20a and b, reveal dominant absorption bands associated with cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin constituents. A broad and intense absorption band observed around 3330 cm−1 corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations, originating from hydroxyl groups in cellulose and hemicellulose. The abundance of hydroxyl groups is critical because these functional sites promote hydrogen bonding and interfacial adhesion with epoxy matrices after alkaline surface treatment. Peaks observed near 2893 cm−1 are attributed to C–H stretching vibrations in aliphatic structures of cellulose and lignin. A spectral band appearing around 1595 cm−1 corresponds to aromatic skeletal vibrations associated with lignin macromolecular structures. Additional peaks between 1504 and 1506 cm−1 confirm the presence of guaiacyl-type lignin units. The absence of strong absorption peaks within the 1650–1740 cm−1 region indicates relatively low carbonyl functional group concentration, suggesting limited ester and ketone structures in Aningeria and Cordia africana sawdusts.

The chemical composition of Aningeria sawdust is particularly favorable for composite reinforcement because cellulose represents the primary load-bearing phase in lignocellulosic materials. High cellulose fraction enhances tensile stiffness and interfacial stress transfer efficiency due to the semi-crystalline structure of cellulose microfibrils. Consequently, Aningeria sawdust exhibits superior mechanical bonding compatibility with epoxy resin systems compared with other fillers investigated. FTIR analysis of Cedar sawdust, reveals similar functional group distributions but with notable intensity differences. Cedar sawdust exhibits a pronounced absorption band at 1735 cm−1, corresponding to C=O stretching vibrations of acetyl and uronic ester groups in hemicellulose.

Higher hemicellulose concentration is generally associated with increased hydrophilicity because hemicellulose contains amorphous polysaccharide structures rich in accessible hydroxyl groups. This structural feature contributes to higher moisture absorption tendency and reduced interfacial bonding strength with hydrophobic epoxy matrices. These chemical characteristics explain the comparatively lower mechanical performance observed in Cedar-based composites.

Lignin-associated absorption bands observed around 1595 cm−1 and 1506 cm−1 are more pronounced in Cordia africana and Cedar sawdusts, indicating higher aromatic polymer content. Lignin contributes to structural rigidity and compressive resistance because it functions as a natural binding matrix within plant cell walls. The presence of guaiacyl lignin units enhances stiffness and wear resistance but may reduce interfacial bonding efficiency due to limited reactive hydroxyl accessibility. The comparative FTIR peak intensity distribution summarized in Table 13 demonstrates distinct chemical signatures among the three hardwood fillers. Aningeria sawdust exhibits strong cellulose-associated peaks at 1035 cm−1, corresponding to C–O–C stretching vibrations of polysaccharide chains, which supports effective polymer matrix interaction.

Discussion outcomes

The main results demonstrate that the mechanical and environmental performance of the hybrid sisal–sawdust epoxy composite system is governed by hierarchical reinforcement mechanisms involving fiber architecture, filler chemistry, and matrix–reinforcement interfacial integrity. Among all formulations, the S1 composite (0/90° Aningeria, 10 wt% filler) exhibited the best overall performance, achieving flexural strength of 90.08 MPa, impact strength of 210 J/m2 (78.4 kJ/m2), compressive strength of 81.2 MPa, and water absorption of 0.53% after 72 h immersion. The high flexural strength can be attributed to efficient stress transfer across bidirectional woven sisal fibers, which act as load-bearing reinforcement networks. Under bending load, tensile stresses develop on the lower laminate surface while compressive stresses dominate the upper region, and the 0/90° fiber arrangement enables balanced resistance to both stress modes.

Increasing filler content beyond 10 wt% resulted in progressive mechanical degradation. For example, composites with 15 wt% and 20 wt% Aningeria filler showed flexural strengths of 49.44 MPa and 45.2 MPa, respectively, indicating nearly 45–50% reduction compared with the optimized S1 configuration. This reduction is mainly associated with resin viscosity increase during fabrication, which restricts matrix penetration into fiber bundles and promotes microvoid formation. Void-induced discontinuities generate localized stress concentration zones that accelerate crack initiation under mechanical loading. Similarly, impact strength decreased from 210 J/m2 to 145 J/m2 as filler loading increased, reflecting reduced fracture energy dissipation capacity.

Compressive strength exhibited a different trend, particularly for hardwood Cordia Africana composites. Sample S6 (20 wt% Cordia Africana, 0/90°) achieved the highest compressive strength of 87.97 MPa, representing the upper performance limit among tested samples. High-density hardwood particles enhance resistance to axial deformation by constraining polymer chain mobility and improving particle packing stiffness. Under compression loading, failure is governed more by matrix densification and micro-buckling inhibition rather than tensile fiber continuity.

Moisture absorption behavior followed diffusion-controlled kinetics consistent with quasi-Fickian transport. The lowest equilibrium water uptake of 0.53% was observed in S1 composite, while the highest absorption reached approximately 3.94% in random orientation Cordia composites. The superior moisture resistance of optimized composites is attributed to alkaline treatment of sisal fibers, which removes hemicellulose and lignin surface impurities, reduces hydroxyl group availability, and strengthens fiber–matrix interfacial adhesion. Woven reinforcement architecture further restricts capillary diffusion channels, limiting water penetration into internal microstructural voids.

Tribological behavior indicates that composites containing Cordia Africana filler demonstrate improved wear resistance due to higher lignin content and intrinsic particle hardness. Lignin-rich fillers enhance surface rigidity and reduce abrasive particle embedding during sliding contact. When compared with other natural fiber composites, the developed Ethiopian sisal hybrid system exhibits competitive performance, with flexural strength approaching the lower range of glass fiber–polyester composites (120–150 MPa) while maintaining lower density and biodegradable reinforcement advantages. Overall, the results confirm that optimal hybrid composite performance is achieved through synergistic regulation of fiber orientation, filler concentration, and interfacial bonding quality rather than increasing reinforcement quantity alone.

Potential applications, practical implementation, and sustainability aspects of sisal fiber–sawdust epoxy composites

Based on the measured mechanical, tribological, and physical performance, the sisal fiber–sawdust reinforced epoxy composites demonstrate clear potential for a range of semi-structural applications where moderate load-bearing capacity, impact resistance, wear resistance, and dimensional stability are required. Applications include rifle buttstocks, furniture and cabinetry components, interior automotive panels, tool handles, load-bearing boards, and lightweight structural panels. The combination of high flexural strength, competitive impact resistance, low wear rate, and reduced water absorption supports their use in components subjected to repeated handling, localized impacts, and mild environmental exposure.

From a practical implementation perspective, the incorporation of sawdust as a filler contributes to material cost reduction and resource efficiency by valorizing wood-processing waste. Sisal fibers are locally available, renewable, and biodegradable, and their use minimizes dependence on synthetic reinforcements while supporting regional supply chains, particularly in resource-limited contexts. Compared with conventional hardwood materials used in applications such as rifle buttstocks, the developed composites offer improved resistance to moisture uptake, enhanced design flexibility, and more consistent mechanical performance. They also remain significantly lighter than metallic alternatives, offering advantages in handling and ergonomics.

Although large-scale manufacturing was not addressed in this study, the hand lay-up process employed provides a clear proof of concept for material feasibility. The composite system is compatible with scalable manufacturing routes such as compression molding, resin transfer molding, or vacuum-assisted processes, which could further improve fiber wetting, reduce void content, and enhance repeatability. Overall, the developed sisal fiber–sawdust epoxy composites represent a technically viable and environmentally sustainable material option for semi-structural applications, with strong potential for further optimization and industrial adoption.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and characterized hybrid epoxy composites reinforced with Ethiopian sisal fiber and hardwood sawdust fillers derived from Aningeria, Cordia africana, and Cedar species. The results demonstrated that composite performance is governed by the synergistic interaction among filler chemistry, fiber architecture, and interfacial bonding quality. Ethiopian sisal fiber, characterized by high cellulose content and tensile strength, provided an efficient primary reinforcement phase, while hardwood sawdust fillers contributed secondary structural enhancement through particle packing and matrix constraint mechanisms.

The experimental findings confirmed that fiber orientation is the most dominant factor controlling mechanical and tribological behavior. The 0/90° woven sisal fiber configuration consistently improved stress transfer efficiency, crack resistance, and wear protection by forming multidirectional reinforcement networks that distribute applied loads along principal structural axes. Among the investigated formulations, the composite containing 10 wt% Aningeria sawdust and 0/90° fiber orientation achieved the best overall balance of properties, exhibiting flexural strength of 90.08 MPa, impact strength of 210 J/m2, and minimum water absorption of 0.53% after 72 h of immersion. These results are attributed to high cellulose content, favorable aspect ratio morphology, and strong fiber–matrix interfacial adhesion following alkali treatment.

Compression and wear performance were enhanced in composites containing higher-density hardwood fillers, particularly 20 wt% Cordia africana sawdust, which restricted matrix deformation and improved abrasion resistance under sliding contact. However, excessive filler loading beyond 10 wt% generally increased void formation, particle agglomeration, and moisture diffusion pathways, leading to reduced flexural and impact properties. Void fractions ranging from 1.14% to 4.58% indicated acceptable fabrication quality for hand lay-up processing, and the strong correlation between porosity and water absorption confirmed that moisture transport primarily occurs through capillary diffusion and microstructural defects.

Comparative analysis showed that the developed hybrid composites exhibit mechanical performance approaching lower ranges of synthetic glass fiber composites while maintaining sustainability advantages such as biodegradability, low density, and utilization of locally available agricultural and industrial waste resources. The material system demonstrated acceptable machinability characteristics, including controlled drilling-induced delamination suitable for assembly operations in defense-related components.

Despite the promising results, limitations related to fabrication variability, limited statistical degrees of freedom, and absence of long-term durability, fatigue, and ballistic resistance testing were identified. Future research should focus on hybrid filler optimization, nano-reinforcement integration, advanced fabrication methods, and comprehensive environmental aging studies to further improve performance reliability.

Overall, the study establishes Ethiopian sisal fiber–hardwood sawdust epoxy composites as viable sustainable material alternatives for semi-structural applications requiring balanced strength, impact resistance, wear durability, and moisture stability. The optimized composite formulation provides a practical foundation for the development of lightweight, environmentally friendly engineering materials utilizing locally sourced renewable resources.
