Additive‐Free Edge‐Functionalized Graphene Dough
Authors: Ashley Walker, Shaikh Nayeem Faisal, Gregory Ryder, Ali R Jalili, Sanjeev Gambhir, Luke O'Dell, Sepidar Sayyar, Gordon G Wallace, David L Officer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe exploitation of graphene's extraordinary properties depends on finding new ways to synthesize and process it without disrupting its fundamental structure. Here, we demonstrate the synthesis of highly processable and dispersible edge‐functionalized graphene (EFG) directly from graphite by a combination of selective oxidation and physical exfoliation. Microscopy and spectral characterization reveals few‐layer graphene nanoplatelets with defect‐free basal planes and carboxylate and phenolic edge‐functionalization. Its defect‐free nature is reflected in its high conductivity (900 S cm−1). The EFG has excellent dispersibility (100 mg mL−1) in aqueous and organic solvents and forms a soft graphene dough that can be molded to any geometric shape. The dried dough can readily reabsorb a wide variety of solvents indicative of a highly porous and amphiphilic material. The unique properties of the graphene dough are demonstrated with the formation of a 3D‐printed conductive scaffold, a printed circuit on paper, and the fabrication of a paramagnetic graphene dough. Significantly, free‐standing and 3D‐printed supercapacitors can be fabricated from ionic liquid‐impregnated dough that gives an excellent gravimetric capacitance of 210 F g−1 and a high energy density of 262.5 Wh Kg−1. This high capacitance is synonymous with efficient pore utilisation during charge storage and supports the unique amphiphilic, nanoporous EFG structure.Keywords: dough, edge‐functionalized, few‐layer graphene, organic dispersion, selective oxidation

Introduction

The development of doughs or clays using conductive low‐dimensional materials has created a new class of soft, moldable conductive materials that have demonstrated outstanding properties in sensing, energy harvesting and storage [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. While the majority of materials have comprised nanocarbons and their composites, one of the earliest examples was a titanium carbide (MXene) clay that could be easily shaped and rolled into films and, when used as an electrode material in supercapacitors, gave excellent volumetric capacitances [1]. Carbon nanotube (CNT) doughs have been prepared with only solvent processing [6] or with additives such as liquid surfactants or graphene oxide (GO) that could be fabricated into shape‐deformable thermoelectric generators [8] or ultrasensitive strain sensors [5].

Additive or binder‐free GO doughs or clays have also been produced by controlling the water or solvent content in the GO [2, 7, 9]. The GO dough can be molded into a wide variety of shapes and structures or easily mixed with other materials such as metal oxides like RuO2, a prototypical oxygen evolution reaction (OER) catalyst, which, on thermal annealing to give reduced GO (rGO), gave a superior OER electrocatalyst [7]. In addition, thermally annealed pristine GO clay [2] or GO dough composites with RuO2 and carbon nanotubes [9] give excellent electrode materials for supercapacitors. Another GO dough or putty, fabricated by precipitating GO from an aqueous dispersion using aniline, was able to be 3D printed and laser ablated to form interdigitated electrodes [3]. While all of these GO doughs could ultimately result in conductive 3D graphene‐based structures using suitable annealing or chemical reduction processes, the flexibility of the structures, the type of composites that could be made and the properties of the resulting graphene were inherently limited by the reduction method that determined how defective the graphene was. The availability of a dough made from graphene itself would resolve these issues and offer a highly conductive, flexible and deformable electrode material that could have applications across numerous areas in materials science and technology. The potential of such a dough is hinted at by the work of Boland et al., who showed that a composite of up to ∼15 volume % of graphene in crosslinked polysilicone or “Silly Putty” created a high‐performance sensing material as a result of its deformability and graphene mobility [4].

Here, we demonstrate that, by selectively edge‐oxidising graphite with mechanical exfoliation, we can synthesize an edge‐functionalized graphene (EFG) without graphite pre‐treatment or extensive synthesis post‐treatment. The EFG has remarkable and unprecedented processability such that doughs can be prepared with up to 70% water, organic solvents, oil and ionic liquids without the use of additional binders or extensive mechanical processing. Increasing the amount of solvent used leads to paste formation (>75%) and high concentration graphene dispersions of up to 100 mg mL−1 (>90% solvent). These surprising hydrophilic and organophilic properties result from the unique few discontinuous layered structure of EFG with hydrophobic, defect‐free basal planes and hydrophilic edge functionality. In addition, the graphene dough maintains an outstanding conductivity reflective of its defect‐free basal planes. The versatility of the structure and solvent affinity of EFG is demonstrated by the fabrication of a 3D printable, water‐based conductive ink that can be used to form a conductive circuit, the formation of a paramagnetic iron oxide‐containing EFG that can be made into magnetic dough, and the formation of moldable electrodes for variably shaped 3D supercapacitors. As a result, this unique form of graphene and the ease with which it can be processed hold great promise for its future application in a wide variety of material, energy and electronic applications.

Results and Discussion

EFG Synthesis, Dough and Dispersion

In this work, EFG nanoplatelets (NPs) were synthesized directly from graphite using a well‐known catalytic oxidation procedure involving ruthenium oxide (RuO4) (Figure 1a), a strong but selective oxidant commonly used in organic synthesis [10]. Following a significant improvement in the efficacy of the RuO4 oxidation procedure by Sharpless and co‐workers [11], it has been shown that it has an oxidative specificity for aromatic rings [12] and has been used for the efficient cleavage of the end caps of carbon nanotubes [13]. We anticipated that this same type of reaction might produce a form of graphene functionalized only around the edges of the sheets, providing a stable, dispersible material of largely defect‐free sheets, thereby conserving the important properties of graphene.

Treatment of natural graphite powder using the Sharpless oxidation procedure [11] with rapid homogenisation followed by ultrasonication of the resulting dispersion led to a high yield (92%) of a black, EFG powder with quite unexpected properties. It was immediately apparent that the EFG powder absorbed even small amounts of water and, as more water was added, a putty‐like material or dough was formed. At a 1:1 weight ratio of dried EFG powder and water, a rigid, clay‐like solid was obtained, whereas, as shown in Movie S1, simply mixing a 1:2 ratio of EFG to water by hand at room temperature resulted in a malleable, soft EFG dough (Figure 1b). This EFG dough could be cut or molded into any arbitrary 3D shape (Figure 1c), and simple air‐drying resulted in hard, albeit brittle, structures (Figure 1d). This behaviour is in stark contrast to GO dough formation, which requires special processing to wet the material [7]. In addition, the dried EFG dough could readily reabsorb water, or remarkably, a variety of organic solvents, silicon oil and ionic liquids, as illustrated in Movie S2, suggesting both a highly porous and amphiphilic material. The highly porous nature of the dough was confirmed by SEM (Figure 1e) and its amphiphilic nature became strikingly apparent as doughs were able to be formed from polar (EtOH, EtOAc, acetone, dimethylformamide, dimethyl carbonate) and non‐polar (toluene, hexane, dichloromethane) organic solvents, and ionic liquid (EMI TFSI), as well as with acidic (1 m HCl) and basic (1 m NaOH) aqueous solutions (Figure S1).

The amphiphilic character of EFG, along with a reduced tendency to aggregate, was also reflected in its continued uptake of both water and organic solvents to form an extrudable thick paste at around 75% solvent and highly concentrated dispersions (>90% solvent), as depicted for water in Figure 1b. Furthermore, in stark contrast to other graphenic materials [14, 15, 16, 17] that typically form dispersions at less than 1 mg mL−1, EFG dispersions up to 100 mg mL−1 could be readily prepared by simply mixing the EFG powder with water (pH 7) without sonication; all the resulting dispersions remained dispersed for 24 h (Figure S2). Surprisingly, EFG showed better dispersibility than GO under these simple mixing conditions, that is without sonication. Compared to 1 mg mL−1 GO that sedimented within one hour, the same concentration of EFG remained dispersed for 72 h, while reduced graphene oxide (rGO) [15] did not disperse at all (Figure S3). Such aqueous graphene dispersibility is unprecedented, although Dong et al., have reported [17] ultra‐high concentration aqueous graphene slurries (50 mg mL−1) made by the physical exfoliation of partially oxidised graphite at a high pH of 12. While the slurries could be diluted to create highly stable dispersions of this material at pH 12, the dispersion concentration was much lower (0.55 mg mL−1) and it became unstable at neutral pH, limiting its application. In contrast, EFG dispersions show better stability at pH 7 than either at pH 2 or 12 (Figure S4).

The enhanced amphiphilic nature of EFG is also apparent in comparison to other edge‐functionalized graphenes such as those produced by controllable edge oxidation [17, 18, 19], ball milling [20] or thermal functionalization [16]. While they exhibited either low concentration dispersions in organic solvents or did not report their formation, EFG formed 10 mg mL−1 dispersions in a range of different polarity organic solvents (toluene, DMF, ethanol and NMP) that were stable for over 48 h (Figure S5).

The results of viscosity measurements of the EFG dispersions, pastes and doughs are shown in Figure S6 and are similar to those reported for binder‐free graphene oxide doughs [7]. All samples show a shear‐thinning behaviour that is characterised by a viscosity decrease under shear strain. Significant increases in viscosity were observed when the EFG concentration increased from 50–100 mg mL−1 and then 100–300 mg mL−1 (Figure S6), representing transitions from dispersion to paste and paste to dough, respectively.

EFG Characterization

The extraordinary amphiphilic behaviour of EFG suggested a graphene structure that had extensive hydrophilic edge functionality while retaining the hydrophobic basal planes of typical graphene, a structure that was supported and further elaborated by electron and atomic force microscopies and other characterization. Low‐resolution transmission electron microscope (TEM) micrographs (Figure 2a,b) show that EFG NPs range from 100 nm to 2 µm with folds and wrinkles characteristic of very thin and flexible sheets. The darker areas on the single NP shown in Figure 2b suggest multilayers, and this is evident in the high‐resolution TEM images (Figure 2c,d). Islands of up to 2–3 graphene layers are apparent in the vicinity of the edge of the EFG NP (Figure 2c), whilst a closer look at the edges themselves (Figure 2d) shows that the NP base is a single or double layer. The basal plane of this base layer looks perfect and suggests very few defects.

(c) and (d) High‐resolution images of the edge of a typical sheet confirming the presence of 1–4 layers. Note the edge of the EFG in c), which shows a monolayer, while in (d) 2 layers can be seen. (e) Bright field (BF) image and (f) annular dark‐field image (HAADF) of a typical EFG platelet at the edge; (i), (ii) and (iii) show mono, double, and three layers, respectively, while (iv) represents an oxygen‐rich area on the edge. From (i) to (iii) the diffraction is increased due to the increase in the number of layers, while at the edge (iv), higher diffraction is as a result of the presence of heavier atoms, presumably two oxygen atoms on top of each other to form a COOH group.

To better understand the edge functionalization characteristics, we have performed high‐resolution high‐angle annular dark field (HAADF) STEM imaging of the EFG platelet at relatively low voltage (80 kV) (Figure 2e,f). An annular dark field image is formed only by very high‐angle, incoherently scattered electrons, as opposed to Bragg‐scattered electrons (bright field) and is highly sensitive to variations in the atomic number of atoms in the sample (Z‐contrast images). Therefore, in contrast to conventional bright field images, higher atomic number atoms are brighter (as for example O vs. C), and the edges of the functionalized graphene appear brighter in the HAADF image, which shows the presence of higher atomic number atoms (oxygen) rather than only carbon. Moreover, as the RuO4 oxidation might be expected to introduce carboxylate groups on the edges, the diffraction intensity is likely higher due to the two oxygens of each group rather than the single and double layer carbon atoms that are present in the plane. This effect is largely invisible in the bright‐field image.

Both SEM and AFM provide further evidence for a few‐layer, island‐type EFG NP structure. As can be seen in the SEM image of an aggregate of EFG NPs up to 3 µm (Figure S7), islands of <200 nm appear to be distributed across the larger base layers. AFM images and their cross‐sectional measurements (Figure S8) support this few‐layer island structure in which the number of layers varies from one to three across the measured EFG NPs. Given that the layer height may vary across a platelet by as little as 5 nm, as can be seen in the TEM image in Figure 2c, the height may be an average of the number of layers across the measurement line.

Raman spectroscopy further highlighted the differences between EFG and other chemically converted graphenes (CCGs) and edge‐functionalized graphenes. Raman spectra were obtained from the centre (Figure 3a) and edges (Figure 3b) of the EFG NPs. Both types of spectra show Raman peaks typical of defect‐free, few‐layer graphene nanoplatelets that have been synthesized by liquid phase exfoliation and whose Raman spectra have been discussed in detail by Coleman and co‐workers [21]. Indeed, very small D bands (1332 cm−1) and sharp G bands (1580 cm−1) are seen in both EFG spectra with D: G band intensity ratios (ID/IG) of 0.04 (centre) and 0.08 (edge), even smaller than those reported by Coleman (0.17–0.37) [21] and fully consistent with defect‐free basal planes in EFG as seen in the TEM images (Figure 2d). In contrast, CCGs typically exhibit large D bands, broad G bands and high ID/IG ratios (>1.50), indicative of a substantially defective basal plane [15]. Other edge‐functionalized graphenes, such as those produced by ball milling [20] or thermal functionalization [16], also exhibit small D bands due to low defect basal planes. The lack of defects in the EFG basal planes is further supported by the D' band that is evident on the edge of the D band in the EFG edge Raman spectrum (Figure 3b) but not present in the centre spectrum. The ID/ID' ratio of ∼3 confirms that the defects are largely edge defects, not basal plane vacancy defects [21, 22].

Raman spectra of EFG at (a) nanoplatelet centre and (b) edges obtained using 632 nm laser excitation. (c) XRD spectrum of EFG. (d) Comparison of XRD peaks of graphite and EFG. Deconvoluted XPS spectra of EFG for e) C1s and f) O1s.

The flake thickness or number of layers can be measured from the shape and position of the 2D Raman band using the method developed by Coleman and co‐workers [21] and summarised in the Experimental Section. This suggests that the EFG basal layers consist of <5 layers and the edges of 1 to 2 layers (Figure S9), consistent with the AFM results (Figure S8) and what is observed in the TEM images (Figure 2c).

The X‐ray diffraction (XRD) spectrum (Figure 3c) shows typical graphitic 002 and 004 crystalline phases. The shift of the 002 peak (25.71°) compared to that of graphite (26.35°) indicates a slightly increased d‐spacing (0.346 nm) in EFG over graphite (0.338 nm), and the peak sharpness confirms the layered nature of the EFG NPs. The comparison of the EFG XRD spectrum with graphite (Figure 3d) highlights the significantly smaller peak intensities of EFG that result from the much smaller crystallite size of the graphenic layered structures of the EFG NPs [19, 23].

The X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) survey spectrum of EFG (Figure S10) reveals that the major change in EFG atomic composition over the pristine graphene is a substantial increase in oxygen content (6.2 vs. 2.3 at.%, respectively). Deconvolution of the XPS C1s peak (Figure 3e) shows that the C═C peak at 284.8 eV is predominant, as expected with substantially smaller contributions from phenolic C─OH at 286.4 eV and O═C─OH at 288.9 eV, a similar spectrum to that reported by Ding et al., for an edge‐functionalized graphene [16]. However, a somewhat more prominent shoulder at 300 nm (Figure S11) in the UV–vis spectrum of EFG suggests a higher carboxyl content [15]. The carboxyl O1s peak is at 531.8 eV, with the remaining oxygen band (534.2 eV) due to chemisorbed water [14] as observed in the thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (Figure S12). The TGA also supported the presence of thermally stable functionality, showing only a 1.5 wt.% overall mass loss up to 900°C.

The presence of phenolic groups on the EFG was confirmed chemically using FeCl3, a well‐known test for phenols [24]. As can be seen in Figure S13, a colour change to green was observed when an EFG dispersion was added to the orange FeCl3 solution, suggesting Fe(III) complexation with phenolics.

The solid‐state 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectrum was acquired to obtain further evidence for the functionality on the EFG, but only a broad signal spanning almost the entire carbon spectral range was observed (Figure S14), typical of graphitic carbon [25]. While this prevented the identification of any EFG phenolic and carboxyl carbon signals, it confirmed the layered nature of the EFG platelets. However, the presence of the acidic phenolic and carboxylic functionalities was confirmed by titration experiments that showed two clear equivalence points at pKa ∼8.0 and ∼4.5 corresponding to the phenolic and carboxylic functionalities, respectively, similar to those of liquid crystalline GO (Figure S15). No pKa2 (COOH) value for the carboxyl groups at basal planes at 6.14 was observed as previously reported for GO and rGO [26], supporting the position of carboxyl groups at the edges rather than on the basal planes of EFG.

The picture that emerges from this characterization data is of a few‐layer graphene NP in which there are stacks of up to three to four discontinuous layers on one or two base layers of the platelet, with phenolic and carboxylic functionality on the edges of the discontinuous layers as well as the base layers, as depicted in Figure 4a. Notably, the discontinuous layers appear to be largely on only one side of the base layers, creating an island‐like structure. This is reflected in the fractured surface of an EFG paper (Figure 4b,c) in which the EFG NPs do not meld seamlessly together as is typically seen in a graphene paper [27].

The mechanism of formation of such a structure is not at all obvious. Nonetheless, it is clear that the combination of homogenisation and RuO4 oxidation, followed by ultrasonication is essential to produce it. Experiments were undertaken in which either the homogenisation or ultrasonication was omitted or the order in which they were carried out, together with the oxidising medium, was changed. No variations in the synthesis procedure led to an edge‐functionalized graphene with the properties described here. Paton et al., have demonstrated that defect‐free few‐layer graphene can be produced by shear exfoliation in liquids [21]. The homogenisation conditions used here exceed the shear exfoliation conditions required for the formation of defect‐free few‐layer graphene. Therefore, it is likely that few‐layer graphene is initially formed, which is subsequently oxidised by the RuO4 oxidant [11], which has an oxidative specificity for aromatic rings, thus leading to the phenolic and carboxylic acid functionalities on the graphene edges. It should be noted, however, that the oxidant needs to be present during the course of the homogenisation and that the following ultrasonication is essential for product formation. It may be that ultrasonication assists in the further degradation of the few‐layer graphene to give the island‐like structures that are subsequently edge oxidised.

This model of a broad, layered distribution of edge functionality across the NPs does provide a rationale for dough formation. Given that EFG has a high zeta potential of −49.9 mV and readily forms doughs and stable dispersions at neutral pH (Figure S4), both ionic and hydrogen bonding of the phenolic and carboxylic groups on neighbouring NPs might be expected to occur. In addition, the unobstructed underside of the EFG NPs would provide significant π–π interactions leading to a 3D structure with both hydrophobic (purple) and hydrophilic (red) pores as depicted in Figure 4d, accounting for the surprising amphiphilic properties of the EFG dough. The hydrophilic character of the EFG NP would also account for the significant increase in solvation and, therefore, dispersibility of EFG compared to other edge‐functionalized graphenes [14, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. It is worthy of note that it has been reported that the aqueous dispersibility of graphene has been enhanced with the addition of functionalized graphene quantum dots, creating a structure not unlike that proposed here [28].

The structure and morphology of EFG result in a graphene with properties quite different from any graphenic material previously reported. Aside from its unique processibility, EFG has outstanding conductivity. The sheet resistances of dried aqueous doughs were measured by four point conductivity probe and found to be around 4–6 Ω/sq at various points of the dough. In addition, the electrical conductivity of the EFG paper shown in Figure 4b was 9 × 104 S m−1, considerably higher than previously reported for graphene paper [17, 29]. As a result of these excellent conductivities and the outstanding processability of EFG, we explored the development of a variety of conductive 3D structures and devices.

EFG Applications

Conductive 3D Printed Dough Structures

An EFG scaffold (Figure 5a) was 3D (extrusion) printed using the binder‐free graphene dough (∼250 mg mL−1 of EFG in water) to demonstrate the fabrication of regularly structured conductive geometric shapes. As expected, the scaffold showed the same resistivity as the dough after drying, and, to the best of our knowledge, the highest conductivity of any 3D printed scaffold formed from either dispersions of processed graphene nanoplatelets or CVD grown graphene aerogel that has been reported [17, 28, 30, 31]. Although dried EFG dough structures were subject to dissolution when exposed to water, the mechanical properties of the EFG dough could be improved, while retaining its excellent sheet resistance, by adding Xanthan gum to an EFG paste (1:9 xanthan gum:EFG in water), which was then used to extrusion print a water‐resistant circuit on paper, as shown in Figure 5b.

Conductive Magnetic Graphene Dough

Taking advantage of the complexation of the EFG phenolic groups with FeCl3 (vide supra), Fe(III) complexed EFG could be thermally annealed to grow iron oxide nanoparticles inside the EFG layers, leading to a paramagnetic EFG (Fe‐EFG) powder (Figure 5c). The XRD spectrum demonstrated the presence of iron oxide in the Fe‐EFG [Fe3O4 (311 crystalline phase)] (Figure S16) [32], and the SEM images showed the well‐distributed dispersion of the iron oxide nanoparticles in the graphene layers (Figure 5d). The Fe‐EFG nanocomposite exhibited a typical hysteresis loop of a soft magnet with a saturation magnetization value of 10 emu g−1 (Figure 5e), comparable to other reported iron oxide graphene nanocomposites [33]. This paramagnetic Fe‐EFG was able to form a dough that was attracted to a magnet (Figure 5f), creating the possibility of graphene‐based magnetic switches or self‐healing conductive materials for future soft electronics, or new biomedical and environmental applications [34, 35].

EFG Dough Electrical Double Layer Capacitors

The excellent conductivity, porosity and remarkable solvent affinity of EFG dough pointed to its potential as an electrode material for electrical double layer capacitors (EDLCs) or supercapacitors [36]. To demonstrate this and exploit the EFG dough moldability, free‐standing solid‐state EDLCs were fabricated by pressing two balls of dough (∼200 mg), containing the ionic liquid (IL) N‐methyl‐N‐propylpyrroldinium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (C4mpyrFSI), against a PVDF membrane on a glass slide (Figure 6a). The C4mpyrFSI was chosen for its efficient electrical conductivity, high electrochemical stability, wide potential window, low viscosity and low melting point [37]. The dough EDLC was charged by simply inserting the copper leads of the charger into each side of the dough EDLC. Up to four light‐emitting diodes (LEDs) could then be powered by the dough EDLC (Figure 6b).

To quantitatively evaluate the EFG dough performance as active electrode material for a typical EDLC, a symmetric two‐electrode EDLC (2032‐type coin cell) was fabricated using two copper disk electrodes coated with electrolyte‐impregnated (C4mpyrFSI) dough separated by a Celgard membrane. This binder‐free fabrication method is much simpler than a typical device fabrication procedure [38]. The electrochemical cyclic voltammetry (CV) and galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) results were remarkably similar to those reported by Li et al., who prepared outstanding nanocarbon‐based EDLCs using mixtures of reduced graphene oxide (rGO) and exfoliated graphene (EG) [39]; the CVs (Figure 6c) and GCD curves (Figure 6d) showed quasi‐rectangular and isosceles triangular shapes, respectively, indicative of close to ideal capacitive behaviour. The gravimetric capacitance (C wt) calculated from the GCD curve (Figure 6d) was 210 F g−1 at 1 A g−1 current density, a value comparable to that of Li et al., (C wt = 231 F g−1 for 75% EG‐rGO at 1 A g−1) [40, 41]. Li et al., ascribed the high efficiency of their EG‐rGO material to exceptionally efficient pore utilisation, suggesting efficient pore utilisation in the EFG supercapacitors, although remarkably, the specific surface area of EFG is eight times lower (76 m2 g−1) than that of 75% EG‐rGO (612 m2 g−1) [39].

The energy densities of graphene‐based supercapacitors can range from 70 to 150 Wh kg− 1 [42, 43, 44, 45], although values five times higher than this can be achieved using additives such as silica microparticles [46]. Using nanoporous graphene electrodes made from the thermal annealing of GO electrodes in hydrogen and an ionic liquid electrolyte, Yang et al., achieved a high energy density of 148.8 Wh kg− 1 at a current density of 1 A g−1 [45]. The energy density of the analogous symmetric two‐electrode EDLC produced here is calculated to be 262.5 Wh Kg−1, almost twice that of Yang et al. at the same current density, and better than many of the graphene‐based supercapacitors reported to date. This is likely due to the highly porous nature of the graphene dough electrodes and supports the efficient pore utilisation proposed above.

As discussed by Li and co‐workers, the capacitance contribution per specific surface area (C ssa) is very useful for determining how efficiently pores are utilised for charge storage in carbon‐based electrodes [39]. Based on an EFG BET value of 75.6 m2 g−1 (see Supporting Information), the C ssa value for this EFG EDLC was 280 mF cm−2, significantly higher than that reported by Li et al., for their EG‐rGO EDLC (47 mF cm−2) [39]. The high C ssa demonstrated here is synonymous with a highly efficient pore utilisation during charge storage, likely due to the confined nanoporous EFG structure proposed here (Figure 2a). Such a structure could lead to monolayer confinement of the ionic liquid, resulting in the breaking of its Coulombic ordering [47], providing improved capacitance as has been reported for other nanoporous carbons [39, 48]. High capacitance values have also been reported for graphene materials with similar low BET values [49, 50].

The moldability of the EFG dough opened up the possibility of fabricating EDLCs that could be integrated into variously shaped devices. To explore this, we fabricated four different‐shaped capacitors, albeit with the same volume (4 cm3), using 3D printed plastic (polypropylene) housings with inserted copper wire as current collectors (Figure 6e). Ionic liquid (C4mpyrFSI) impregnated doughs (500 mg) were pasted into each half of the cases, which were screwed together with a PVDF membrane separating them (see Figure S17 for the assembly of the square capacitor). The CVs of these different‐shaped devices (Figure S18) show similar current responses albeit with some differences shown by the conical device, likely reflecting a resistivity difference as a result of its asymmetry relative to the other three capacitors. This is confirmed by the EIS spectra of the four devices, which all show in their Nyquist plots (Figure 6f) the same series resistance at high frequency and nearly vertical lines at low frequency characteristic of capacitive behaviour [51]. In the case of the conical device, the low‐frequency capacitive behaviour is shifted along the real axis toward more resistive values. This is a result of a change in the middle frequency range, which is affected by changes in the migration rate of the ions in the electrolyte due to the electrode thickness [51].

Given that the capacitances of the four 3D printed EFG EDLCs were similar as seen in the CVs in Figure S18, the C wt of the square device was determined as representative of all the devices and found to be 36 F g−1 at 2.5 A g−1 current density from the galvanostatic charge discharge curves (Figure 6g). Note that the capacitance mentioned here is measured by a two‐electrode cell based on total electrode mass. While this C wt value is seven times lower than that of the coin cell, it is a significant result given the simplicity of fabrication and that the mass loading of the square device was three hundred times higher than the coin cell, with substantially larger charge transfer pathways.

The stability of such a device to higher voltages was also examined. The CVs of the square device were measured in which the total voltages (starting at −0.5 V) were increased stepwise from 3 to 5 V by 0.5 V (100 mV s−1). The oxidation current remained relatively unchanged from 3 to 4.5 V (Figure 6h). At 5 V, a sharp peak of oxidation current occurs due to the corrosion and possible oxidation of the copper wire used as a current collector [52]. From this study, it was found that this device is stable up to 4.5 V, which is comparable to the EG‐rGO EDLCs reported by Li et al. [39] and the edge‐free graphene supercapacitors of Nomura et al. [52], despite the presence of edge functionality on the EFG.

The energy density of the square device is calculated based on the galvanostatic charge discharge curve at a potential range of 5 V (Figure S19). The EFG dough‐based EDLC shows a high energy density of 71.7 Wh kg−1 at 10 kW kg−1 which is three times higher than the energy density (∼20 Wh kg−1) of commercial activated carbon‐based supercapacitors (Figure 6i (inset)) [41]. Given the unoptimised nature of this EDLC, it is not surprising that its energy density is significantly lower than that of the coin cell. However, it is still comparable to energy densities of other graphene supercapacitors [42, 43, 44].

Given the functionalized nature of the EFG dough, the EFG dough EDLCs proved to be surprisingly robust. The capacitance versus cycle number of the square device was carried out for 10 000 cycles at a current density of 5 A g−1. The capacitance retention remains unchanged even after 10 000 cycles with 95% columbic efficiency, as shown in Figure 6i. This not only highlights the stability of the EFG dough for use in electrochemical devices but a major advantage over other EDLC materials; despite fabricating devices with large amounts of active material (∼500 mg) in a single cell, the ion transfer or diffusion is not significantly hampered as observed in commercial pouch cells with a mass loading greater than 15 mg [39].

The ease of use and moldability of the EFG dough suggested the possibility of developing customized structural supercapacitors in the voids of structures for applications such as emergency lighting. To demonstrate this, we charged the conical 3D printed EFG EDLC with a small portable charger (5 V and 1 A) for 2 min and then connected it to a LED push light (30 lumens) typically used in a store room or garage. The EDLC powered the light for 15 min (Figure 6j), highlighting the potential of these EFG‐based energy storage devices.

Conclusion

In summary, an edge‐functionalized graphene (EFG) with very low defects and exceptional solvent affinity and dispersibility was synthesised directly from graphite using a well‐known catalytic oxidation procedure involving ruthenium oxide (RuO4) together with rapid homogenisation and ultrasonication. Unlike typical single‐layer graphene that has a low aqueous dispersibility of less than 5 mg mL−1, EFG was found to have excellent dispersibility of up to 100 mg mL−1 in both aqueous and organic solvents. Unexpectedly, simply mixing by hand of 60%–65% of water or solvent led to the formation of a soft graphene putty or dough or a paste at 75% solvent. The aqueous‐based EFG dough could be molded to any arbitrary 3D or geometric shape, which could then be dried and re‐impregnated with a wide variety of solvents.

Microscopy and spectral characterization of EFG revealed a largely defect‐free few‐layer graphene with carboxylate and phenolic edge functionalisation that accounted for its extraordinary dispersion and amphiphilic properties. The absorption of hydrophobic solvents into the aqueous‐based dough suggested the presence of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic channels in the dough with the interacting basel planes in the few‐layer nanoplatelets creating the hydrophobic channels, and the interaction of the edge functionality between sheets forming the hydrophilic channels. The defect‐free nature of the basal planes of the nanoplatelets resulted in a high dough conductivity and an outstanding EFG paper conductivity of 900 S cm−1.

The nanoporosity and high conductivity of the EFG dough provided the opportunity to demonstrate the potential uses of this unique graphene. A soft EFG dough was 3D printed to form a water‐soluble conductive 3D scaffold whilst the addition of Xanthan gum to the EFG allowed the extrusion printing of a water‐resistant circuit on paper. The fabrication of an iron oxide functionalized paramagnetic graphene dough was achieved with the formation and subsequent sintering of iron phenolic complexes on the EFG nanoplatelets.

The formation of an ionic liquid impregnated dough allowed the fabrication of functional free‐standing electrical double‐layer capacitors (EDLCs). An excellent gravimetric capacitance of 210 F g−1 was obtained from the binder‐free ionic liquid EFG dough electrodes in a coin cell, along with a correspondingly high specific surface area capacitance (280 mF cm−2). An energy density of 262.5 Wh Kg−1, higher than that of many reported graphene supercapacitors was obtained. These high capacitances were synonymous with efficient pore utilisation during charge storage and supported the unique amphiphilic nanoporous structure of the EFG. As an example of the potential of the EFG dough to be used as variably shaped EDLCs, differently shaped 3D printed EDLCs were fabricated and compared. They were found to have similar electrochemical characteristics and capacitances, and to be remarkably stable, remaining unchanged after 10 000 cycles with 95% columbic efficiency. A 3D printed EFG dough EDLC gave a three times higher energy density (71.7 Wh kg−1 at 10 kW kg−1) than that of commercial EDLC.

One of the major challenges in realising the potential of graphene in a wide variety of applications has been in finding ways to process it for each application. The EFG that we have described here opens the way for graphene processing as a powder, or dispersion, paste and dough with aqueous, organic solvents and ionic liquids in ways that were not previously possible. In particular, this unique nanoporous and amphiphilic EFG dough offers new opportunities in composite development, 3D printability of scaffolds for medical applications, novel conductive magnetic materials, binder‐free and variably structured electrodes for energy storage devices and other graphene‐related applications that have not been possible to date.

Experimental Section

EFG Synthesis

Graphite (20 g, 1.67 atom) was suspended in MeCN (400 mL), EtOAc (400 mL) and water (600 mL). NaIO4 (71.2 g, 333 mmol) and RuCl3·xH2O (820 mg, 3.4 mmol, ∼0.2 mol%) were added, and the resulting mixture was cooled in an ice bath and homogenised (∼20 000 rpm) for 1 h, then the homogeniser was removed, and the mixture was ultrasonicated for 2 h. The suspension was filtered, then the filtrate was washed with water, 1 m HCl until the filtrate was colourless, then water until the filtrate was neutral. The filtrate was dried in vacuo to yield the product as a black powder [53].

Preparation of EFG Doughs

EFG doughs were prepared by mixing a 1:2 weight ratio of EFG and water or other solvent by hand, as shown in Movie S1. As a result of the excellent water absorbance property of EFG no sonication or mechanical mixing was required for dough formation, and the process was complete in a minute. EFG doughs were made with EtOH, EtOAc, acetone, DMF, toluene, hexane and silicon oil, as well as with acidic (1 m HCl) and basic (1 m NaOH) aqueous solutions. EFG doughs could also be made with ionic liquids such as N‐methyl‐N‐propylpyrroldinium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (C4mpyrFSI).

Synthesis of Paramagnetic Fe‐EFG

An EFG dispersion of 10 mg mL−1 was mixed with 20 wt.% of FeCl3, and the solution was stirred for 30 min. After that, the solution was evaporated to a paste and placed in a tube furnace at 450°C for 1 h under argon. The thermal annealing reduced the iron salts to iron oxide nanoparticles. The Fe‐EFG powder was then mixed with water or canola oil to form paramagnetic doughs.

Free‐Standing EFG Dough EDLC

A moldable free‐standing EFG dough supercapacitor was assembled by placing a PVDF separator between two 200 mg quantities of dough. These doughs are reconfigurable and can be shaped to any 3D configuration. Due to high electrical conductivity and a nanochannel network for electrolyte and ion transportation, without any current collector, it behaved like a supercapacitor by storing the charge.

Coin Cell EDLCs

Binder‐free EFG paste in ionic liquid was prepared by mixing EFG and ionic liquid (PY13FSI) in a 1:2 mass ratio. Electrodes were fabricated by rolling the binder‐free paste onto copper foils. The electrodes were then cut into circular discs suitable for coin cells with a controlled mass loading of ∼1 mg cm−2. CR 2032 coin‐type cells were assembled with the circular electrodes using a porous Celgard film as a separator and C4mpyrFSI as electrolyte. The gravimetric capacitance of the cells were calculated from galvanostatic charge/discharge using the formula for capacitance measurement [40].

3D Geometric Shaped Supercapacitor Devices

Four different geometrically shaped device cases were 3D printed in two halves from commercial black filament (Markforged's Onyx), a composite of tough nylon with micro‐carbon reinforcement. The internal volume of the devices were kept the same (4 cm3) with an inserted copper wire as current collector (Figure 6e). Each half of the case was filled with EFG dough prepared with ionic liquid (PY13FSI) (at a ratio of 1:4 of EFG and PY13FSI, respectively). The total weight of the dough to fill half of the case was 1.0 g. Then the PVDF membrane was placed in the middle. The halves were then bolted together to seal the device. The charging and discharging process initially were performed by a galvanostatic charge discharge experiment in a Biologic potentiostat at a range of current densities from 0.1 to 5 A/g for a wide potential window of 5 V. The devices were shown superb stability during the charge discharge process even at such an operating voltage in a single cell. The similar charge/ discharge properties of different shaped cell indicated the flexibility and moldability of the EFG electrode to develop any shaped devices. For practical application, a single device was charged by connecting the two ports of the devices with a DC adaptor operating at a 5 V range with a maximum current of 1 A and powered up light up led bulbs, rotating motors and high‐lumen push lights for demonstration.

Homogenisation

Homogenisation was carried out using an IKA T25 Digital Homogeniser fitted with an IKA S 25 N ‐ 25 F dispersal head (5–25 µm).

Ultrasonication

Ultrasonication was achieved using a Sonics and Materials Inc. VCX‐1500 1500 W sonicator probe using an ultrasonic tip (Sonics and Materials, VCX750) fitted with a flat‐head horn transducer.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

SEM was carried out using a JEOL JSM‐7500FA SEM. The sample was prepared by placing EFG powder onto carbon tape. The SEM image of bulk EFG powder was taken at a scale bar of 1 µm (Figure S7). The SEM image shows the few layers of EFG flakes in which small nanoplatelets of 100–300 nm are sitting on the top of large flakes 2–3 µm.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and Scanning Transmission Electron Microscope (STEM)

TEM and probe corrected STEM were carried out on graphene samples deposited on a holey carbon grid using an JEOL JEM‐ARM200f operated at 80 kV. The samples were pre‐irradiated with a beam shower by scanning the sample at the eucentric height with a defocused beam at a magnification of 50 000 times, with the purpose of avoiding contamination during subsequent imaging at high magnification.

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)

The AFM imaging of EFG platelets deposited on a Si substrate was obtained by tapping mode AFM (Park Systems, Korea). The images were constructed in air using a silicon AFM tip with a resonance frequency of 320 kHz (scan rate set as 0.5 Hz). AFM topography images of selected EFG NPs and cross sections along two directions on different locations marked in the topography images are shown in Figure S8. From these measurements, it is clear that the EFG NPs consist of rough surfaces where one to three small graphene layers sit on top of a large micron sized graphene platelet(s). This analysis further confirms the unique surface morphology of the few‐layer EFG flakes, which results in its unusual properties when compared to typical graphene NPs.

X‐Ray Diffraction (XRD)

XRD was performed using a GBC MMA XRD instrument with Cu K alpha radiation.

Raman spectra were obtained using a Renishaw Raman microscope equipped with a 632 nm excitation laser. The flake thicknesses at the centre and edges of EFG using Raman spectroscopy were measured following the method used by Coleman and his co‐workers [21]

X‐Ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)

XPS was carried out using a Thermo ESCALAB 250Xi spectrometer at a pressure of about 2 × 10−9 mbar with a monochromatic Al−Kα X‐ray source operating at a power of 120 W (13.8 kV × 8.7 mA) with 500 µm spot size. The deconvolution was calculated by CasaXPS software with the most accurate fittings.

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)

The thermogravimetric analysis was obtained using a NETZSCH TGA 209 instrument at a temperature ramp of 10°C min−1 under nitrogen and is shown in Figure S12. As the temperature reaches 200°C, a slight (∼0.4 wt.%) mass loss was observed due to the removal of adsorbed water, oxygen and labile oxygen‐containing functional groups like carboxyl groups. With the increment of temperature, an additional mass loss of (∼0.6 wt.%) is apparent from 400°C to 500°C, likely due to more stable carboxyl groups, with the majority of the phenolic groups resistant to thermal decomposition [54]. The overall mass loss under nitrogen at 900°C was 1.5 wt.%, indicating the remarkable thermal stability of EFG.

Ultraviolet‐Visible (UV–vis) Spectroscopy

The UV–vis characterization was performed on a Shimadzu UV‐1800 spectrophotometer. The UV–Vis spectrum of aqueous dispersed EFG (0.1 mg/mL) without any binder or surfactant was obtained. The maximum absorption peak was observed at 265 nm, which is closer to the typical UV–Vis peak of graphene (∼270 nm) [55].

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) Spectroscopy

A Bruker Avance III 11.7 T NMR spectrometer and a Bruker 4 mm double‐channel magic angle spinning (MAS) probe were used to obtain a 13C Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) MAS solid‐state NMR spectrum of the EFG (Figure S10). A powder sample of EFG was mixed with aluminium oxide (approximately 50% by volume), packed into a zirconia MAS rotor and spun at 10 kHz. 48 scans were acquired with a recycle delay of 2 min and 80 CPMG echoes acquired. The echo train was Fourier transformed directly, resulting in a spikelet spectrum with a spikelet separation of 500 Hz. The chemical shift scale is referenced to TMS at 0 ppm.

This MAS technique uses a train of refocusing pulses to record multiple spin echoes per scan (thereby giving a huge signal boost). When this line of spin echoes is Fourier transformed, a “spikelet spectrum” is obtained, consisting of multiple sharp lines that trace out the actual spectral line shape. This results in a broad signal spanning almost the entire carbon spectral range, which prevents the identification of any EFG phenolic and carboxyl carbon signals. The spectral broadness is likely due to anisotropic magnetic susceptibility effects as previously reported for graphitic carbon, albeit from static NMR data [25].

Titration

Metrohm 848 Titrino plus was used for the titration of all EFG samples. The equipment was calibrated using pH buffers 4.0, 7.0 and 9.0 with ±0.02 pH variation at 25°C. Three‐point calibration was done using these buffers, and a linear slope of 100±1.0% was observed after the calibration. Solutions of NaOH (0.1 m) and HCl (0.1 m) were prepared using AR grade reagents.

Reverse titration with 0.1 m HCl, following basification with 0.1 m NaOH found two equivalence points. Approximately 1.8 meq/g of carboxylic acids were found at a pH of ∼4.5, while 1.6 meq/g of phenolic residues were identified at a pH of ∼8.0. The EFG sample was titrated with both base and acid from pH 2.5 to pH 11.5 and back. During the reverse titration using 0.1 m HCl, two clear equivalence points of 7.26 and 6.64 mL were observed for EFG corresponding to the acidification of the carboxylate and phenolate functionality, respectively. These correspond to the analogous equivalence points for LCGO.

Rheological Behaviour of EFG Samples

Viscosity measurements were performed using an AR‐G2 rheometer (TA Instruments) equipped with a cone‐and‐plate geometry (40 mm diameter, 2° cone angle) over a shear rate range of 0.01–500 s− 1.

The rheological behaviour of EFG pastes (10 to 300 mg mL−1 aqueous dispersions) from low to high shear rates is shown in Figure S6. The viscosity of the dispersions/dough increased with the increment of the concentration. As shown in Figure S6a, it was observed that at shear rate of 10 s−1 the viscosity of the 300 mg mL−1 dough was ∼190 pa s−1 compared to ∼0.005 Pa s−1 for the 10 mg mL−1 dispersion. All samples showed a shear‐thinning behaviour that was characterised by a viscosity decrease under shear strain. By increasing the shear rate, the EFG sheets align, followed by breakage of sheet‐sheet and sheet‐water bonds that results in a decrease in viscosity. Comparing the rheological behaviour of the samples at low shear rates (Figure S6b), it is evident that shear thinning happens at a slower rate in the sample with the lowest concentration (10 mg mL−1). The bonds between the EFG NPs are weaker than those between EFG NPs and water molecules and break as soon as a shear strain is applied, resulting in the rapid viscosity decrease at low shear rates. By increasing the shear rates, the viscosity of the samples gradually plateau, indicating a Newtonian behaviour due to disentanglement of the sheets. However, the viscosity of the dough with high EFG content (300 mg mL−1) keeps decreasing at a rate higher than that of the other samples, which may be attributed to the interactions between the EFG NPs.

A sudden jump is observed in the viscosity of inks at a shear rate of 60–80 s−1 in samples with 10 and 50 mg mL−1 EFG content, being more pronounced at the lowest concentration. Sudden variation of viscosity occurs when the shear load is not consistent throughout the material, mainly due to inhomogeneity of the material, which can result in a phase separation [56]. Inhomogeneity and phase separation is less detectable at higher concentrations.

Conductivity and Resistivity

The conductivity and resistivity measurements were made on free‐standing graphene films and doughs using a four‐point probe JANDEL RM3000 Resistivity meter. The conductivity probe was placed on the flat surface of the dried dough over ten locations. A thin, flat rectangular‐shaped dough was used to calculate the surface resistivity. The calculated surface resistivity was an average of >10 readings.

Measurement of Magnetisation

The magnetisation property by hysteresis loop was conducted by a 9T cryogen‐free Physical Property Measurement System (DynaCool) instrument at room temperature.

Electrochemical Characterization

The electrochemical characterization and measurements of cyclic voltammetry (CV), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) were evaluated using a Biologic SP300 potentiostat.

Braunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) Surface Areas Measurement

The nitrogen sorption isotherms and BET surface areas were measured over P/Po = 0.05–0.30 at 77 K on an Autosorb IQ (Quantachrome, USA). For EFG, the multipoint BET surface area was found to be 75.6 m2 g−1 over P/Po = 0.05–0.30 with a total pore volume of 1.75 cm3 g−1 for pores smaller than 244.2 nm at P/Po = 0.99207.
