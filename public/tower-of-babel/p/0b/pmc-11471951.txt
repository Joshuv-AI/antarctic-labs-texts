Association Between Frailty Status and Readmissions in Hospitalized Patients With Systemic Lupus Erythematosus
Authors: Christopher Leung, Waqas Tahir, Yenny Rosli, Sarah B Lieber, Una E Makris, Jiha Lee, Hammad Ali, Radjiv Goulabchand, Siddharth Singh, Namrata Singh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe objective of this study was to evaluate the association between frailty status and risk of readmissions, inpatient death, and cost of admission among patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using the National Readmissions Database. Using International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision codes, we identified individuals >18 years of age who had a primary or secondary diagnosis of SLE and were hospitalized between January and June 2018. Using the validated claims‐based Hospital Frailty Risk Score, we categorized individuals as frail (score ≥ 5) or nonfrail (score < 5) at the time of index hospitalization. Our primary outcome was readmission rates post discharge from index hospitalization. Secondary outcomes were rates of inpatient mortality and the total cost of hospitalizations. Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate the association between frailty and risk of readmissions, with adjustment for age, sex, insurance type, household income, and Elixhauser Comorbidity Index score.ResultsA total of 39,738 patients with SLE met eligibility criteria. Over a median follow‐up of eight months, frail patients with SLE (n = 18,385) had higher Elixhauser Comorbidity Index scores and longer length of stay compared to nonfrail patients with SLE (n = 21,353). Frail patients with SLE had higher readmission rates, a higher proportion of prolonged hospitalizations, and higher costs per hospitalization. Frailty was independently associated with a 10% higher risk of readmission after adjustment for covariates.ConclusionAmong hospitalized adults with SLE, presence of frailty was associated with higher readmission and inpatient mortality rates. Our results highlight that frailty status can help risk stratify patients with SLE at increased risk for readmissions and other adverse health outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a systemic autoimmune condition affecting multiple organ systems, leading to widespread inflammation and a heterogeneous array of symptoms. Genetic, sociodemographic, and clinical factors affect individual susceptibility to SLE and subsequent health‐related outcomes. End‐organ damage, particularly renal and cardiovascular, resulting from chronic inflammation contributes to adverse disease trajectories and ultimately higher mortality rates. 1

Among adults with SLE, the posthospitalization period is associated with a high rate of readmission. In a prior analysis, SLE ranked among the top 10 medical conditions associated with readmissions. 1 , 2 In a previous study using a nationwide sample, 14% of adults with SLE were readmitted within 30 days of their index hospitalization. 3 Readmissions place a direct financial cost on both patients and hospitals. In 2004, prevalent SLE was associated with a mean total annual direct cost of $12,643 in the United States, and this cost increased with greater disease activity and organ damage.

Frailty is a state of physiologic vulnerability to worse health outcomes, such as hospitalizations, postoperative complications, and death. 4 Different ways to measure frailty have been proposed, although the two main frameworks are the physical phenotype and the deficit accumulation model. 5 According to the physical phenotype, frailty is conceived as a clinical syndrome. The most widespread physical phenotype approach was derived from the Cardiovascular Health Study and includes five components: weight loss, exhaustion, slow gait speed, poor handgrip strength, and low physical activity; frailty is present when at least three criteria are met. 6 In contrast, according to the deficit accumulation model, frailty is conceptualized as a state of accrued health deficits. Multiple frailty indices have been developed, reflecting the proportion of deficits present relative to the total number of possible deficits in the context of aging or a chronic health condition. In addition, claims‐based frailty tools have been developed to leverage the large volume of granular data available in administrative claims data sets, some based on existing frailty frameworks and others using distinctive approaches. Although the concept of frailty arose from the geriatric literature, it is applied increasingly in adults with chronic conditions, including SLE. 7 , 8 , 9

Multiple studies suggest that the prevalence of frailty is high in adults with SLE across the lifespan, exceeding the prevalence in community‐dwelling older adults. 10 , 11 In addition, given the overlap between SLE disease characteristics and features of the frailty phenotype, including muscle weakness and fatigue, disentangling phenotypic frailty from SLE disease characteristics remains an area of active investigation.

The Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics frailty index (SLICC‐FI) was developed to offer an additional health measure for patients with lupus, adopting a holistic approach through a clinical and laboratory assessment of patients. After controlling for confounding factors such as age, sex, and SLE‐specific treatment, and organ damage due to SLE, the frailty index was associated with increased mortality rates. 12 In two expanding studies performed by the same research group responsible for creating the SLICC‐FI, the frailty index emerged as a predictor for organ damage and hospitalizations in patients with SLE after controlling for demographic and pertinent clinical characteristics, including SLE disease activity and glucocorticoid and immunosuppressive therapy. 13 , 14 Additionally, elevated frailty levels in patients with SLE, as determined by the SLICC‐FI, have been linked to increased recurrent visits to the emergency department and worsened physical function and quality of life. 15 The creation and efficacy of the SLICC‐FI further emphasize that phenotypic frailty is distinct from SLE disease characteristics and can be used as a clinical tool to predict adverse outcomes in patients with SLE.

This study aimed primarily to understand the association between frailty status, measured using a claims‐based frailty index, and the risk of readmissions. Secondary aims were analyzing the inpatient mortality rate and characterizing the burden of hospitalization on patients and hospital systems by analyzing costs of admission and rates of severe hospitalizations (defined as length of stay [LOS] more than seven days) in patients with SLE.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Data source

The National Readmissions Database (NRD) is a nationally representative database designed to study national readmissions for all patients in the United States. 16 It was developed for the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project, which is sponsored by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality. Twenty‐eight geographically dispersed states contribute to the database and account for 58.7% of all US hospitalizations. The NRD was used to create a retrospective cohort to evaluate the association between frailty status and clinical outcomes among patients with SLE. Our study was deemed exempt from the University of Washington Institutional Review Board because the NRD is deidentified and fully publicly available. Informed consent was not needed.

Study population

Adult patients who had an index hospitalization between January and June 2018 with a primary or secondary discharge diagnosis of SLE were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) Clinical Modification/Procedure Coding System (codes M3210, M3211, M3212, M3213, M3214, M3215, M3219, M328, and M329). Admissions for lupus‐related pregnancies were included. After the first admission with a discharge diagnosis of SLE, readmissions and deaths were tracked until December 31, 2018, or patient death. Patients were excluded if they (1) were <18 years of age at index hospitalization, (2) died at index hospitalization (because our primary goal was to track readmissions), (3) were admitted for index hospitalization between July and December 2018 (because we would not be able to track six months of follow‐up), (4) were admitted initially for elective surgery (to focus on the impact of frailty) as described previously, 17 (5) were from out of state (the NRD is unable to follow patients admitted to a hospital in a different state), and (6) had missing data for the LOS or hospital charges for admission.

Exposure, covariates, and outcomes

Patients’ frailty risk scores were calculated at index hospitalization using the Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS). 18 Gilbert et al developed the score using a cluster analysis of more than a million older patients (≥75 years) admitted to hospitals with diagnoses associated with frailty development, such as dementia, delirium, and difficulty walking. 19 They validated their score by evaluating how well it predicted adverse outcomes in separate cohorts. The HFRS uses 109 ICD‐10 codes and awards points for each diagnosis to create a score (ranging from 0 to 99) that can be categorized as low risk (<5), intermediate risk (5–15), and high risk (>15), which maximizes distinction among groups. We only identified 3.7% of patients who would be categorized as having high‐risk frailty based on the HFRS, and so, we merged the medium and high‐risk frailty patients into the frail patient cohort. Low‐risk frailty patients were added to the nonfrail patient cohort.

Baseline demographics and comorbid conditions, including age, sex, median household income, and LOS, were collected. Various comorbid conditions (hypertension, heart failure, diabetes mellitus, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD]), leading causes of readmissions, and Elixhauser Comorbidity Index scores were calculated using relevant ICD‐10 codes. Insurance status was categorized as Medicare, Medicaid, commercial insurance, self‐pay, and other. Hospital characteristics, including urban location, teaching status, and bed size, were collected.

The primary outcome of this study was readmission rates following discharge from the index hospitalization. We tested the null hypothesis that there was no difference in readmission rates in frail and nonfrail patients diagnosed with SLE. Secondary outcomes were the burden of hospitalizations (including days to readmission, severe hospitalization [defined as LOS more than seven days], and cost of hospitalization) and inpatient death.

Statistical analysis

We compared baseline patient demographics at index hospitalization and admissions and hospital characteristics both at the index hospitalization and longitudinally in frail and nonfrail patients with SLE. Student's t‐test was used to compare continuous variables, and the Pearson χ2 test was used for categorical variables. All hypothesis testing was performed using a two‐sided P value with a statistical significance threshold of <0.01. To evaluate the independent effect of frailty on risk of readmissions, we performed a multivariable Cox proportional hazard analysis, adjusting for age, sex, median income range, Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, various comorbid conditions (obesity, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, and COPD), insurance type, hospital teaching status, and bed size at index hospitalization. To evaluate the independent effect of frailty on risk of death, we performed a logistic regression analysis, adjusting for age and various comorbid conditions (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, and COPD). Additional analyses were performed to evaluate the association between frailty and readmissions, stratified by age categories (<40, 40–64, and ≥65 years) and Elixhauser Comorbidity Index score (low complexity [<12] vs high complexity [≥12]). All statistical analyses were performed with Python 3.9.13.

RESULTS

In total, we identified 39,738 patients with SLE with index hospitalizations between January and June 2018. Among those with SLE, 18,385 (46.3%) were categorized as frail, and 21,353 (53.7%) were categorized as nonfrail (Figure 1). At index hospitalization, patients with SLE in the frail cohort were older (mean ± SD 71.13 ± 13.02 vs 64.52 ± 14.26 years; P < 0.01), less likely to be female (87.20% vs 89.50%; P < 0.01), and more commonly Medicare beneficiaries (vs other insurance types) (63% vs 43%; P < 0.01) and had higher Elixhauser Comorbidity Index scores (14.14 vs 8.53; P < 0.01) and longer LOS (5 vs 3 days; P < 0.01). Patients with SLE in the frail cohort also had more comorbid conditions, including hypertension (47.05% vs 43.40%; P < 0.01), heart failure (24.15% vs 12.81%; P < 0.01), diabetes mellitus (26.56% vs 19.11%; P < 0.01), and COPD (31.42% vs 26.24%; P < 0.01) (Table 1).

In subgroup analyses, we observed no difference in the association between frailty and risk of readmission by age groups (Supplemental Figure 1). Among the low‐complexity patients (Elixhauser Comorbidity Index score <12), frailty was associated with a higher risk of readmission (adjusted hazard ratio 1.10; 95% confidence interval 1.05–1.14). Similarly, among the high‐complexity patients, frailty was associated with a higher risk of readmission (data not shown).

The top five reasons for readmission in the frail cohort were sepsis (15.05%), acute kidney failure (4.39%), hypertensive heart and chronic kidney disease (3.78%), SLE (3.60%), and respiratory failure (2.78%). The top reasons for readmission in the nonfrail cohort were SLE (5.05%), COPD (2.52%), sepsis (2.44%), hypertensive heart disease (2.24%), and pneumonia (2.04%) (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first retrospective longitudinal cohort study of frailty and readmissions in hospitalized patients with SLE using a nationally representative sample. We found that frailty status is independently associated with an increased risk of readmission and inpatient death in patients diagnosed with SLE. Our findings add to a growing literature suggesting that frailty may be a valuable construct for identifying individuals with SLE prone to adverse health outcomes.

We also identified differences in demographics and clinical characteristics of the hospitalized frail and nonfrail patients with SLE. Frail patients with SLE were significantly older than nonfrail patients with SLE, corroborating prior studies in the general population that frailty is more prevalent in aging populations 20 and some 21 , but not all, 17 previous studies of frailty in adults with SLE. As expected, frail patients with SLE were also more likely to be insured by Medicare, given the age requirements for qualification. In both frail and nonfrail cohorts with SLE, over 85% of patients were female, as autoimmune diseases affect female patients more frequently. 22 However, we found that there was a higher percentage of male patients in the frail cohort compared to the nonfrail cohort with SLE. It is unclear if these sex differences are specific to the SLE population, as previous studies on sex differences in frailty have reported mixed results, potentially due to the different tools used for measuring frailty and the cohorts being comprised predominantly of female patients. 23

Our analysis indicates that frailty is associated longitudinally with higher rates of readmissions, inpatient mortality, and severe hospitalizations (more than seven days) and fewer days to readmission in patients with SLE, including after adjustment for relevant covariates. It is not clear whether this increased vulnerability to adverse hospital outcomes is related to frailty itself or other factors such as disease severity and/or long‐term management of SLE through the administration of glucocorticoids and other immunosuppressive medications, which may be interrelated with frailty; glucocorticoids, which are administered widely in treating systemic autoimmune diseases, such as SLE, have been shown to confer adverse health effects, including increased risk of osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease, and susceptibility to infection, all of which are elements of the HFRS and are associated with disease severity. Nevertheless, the HFRS incorporates elements reflective of functional domains (eg, abnormalities of gait and mobility, care involving use of rehabilitation procedures) that are not present in claims‐based disease severity scores, 24 suggesting that they are not synonymous constructs. Although we were unable to adjust models in this study for a claims‐based disease severity index in the absence of data on medication‐taking and given the similarity of our outcomes with claims‐based disease severity index domains, disentangling frailty from disease activity and organ damage represents an important future direction. Previous studies on frailty in SLE have shown that frailty is associated with adverse health outcomes, such as death and physical function, independent of SLE disease activity and organ damage. 12 , 15 In a previous study of adults with SLE ≤65 years of age enrolled in Medicaid, the risk of recurrent emergency department use was higher among frail (vs nonfrail) beneficiaries, including after adjustment for hydroxychloroquine, glucocorticoid, and immunosuppressive medication administration as proxies for disease severity. 25

SLE is associated with a direct burden on the health care system through expensive resource use. A systematic review of cost‐of‐illness studies on SLE found that inpatient care was the predominant direct cost for patients with SLE. 26 We identified that, on average, the frail cohort with SLE had more than $8,000 more costs of hospitalizations than the nonfrail cohort. Based on our analysis, we suspect that the increased costs of hospitalizations in the frail cohort may be partly related to the increased rates of readmissions and severe hospitalizations leading to longer hospital stays.

Our study has several limitations. First, we used ICD‐10 codes to identify patients with SLE, which is inherently prone to misclassification. However, two studies using ICD‐9 or ICD‐10 codes to identify patients with SLE found that the sensitivity of using one hospitalization record was low, whereas the specificity was very high. 27 , 28 This would suggest that by using ICD‐10 codes, our cohorts were unlikely to have mistakenly included patients without SLE; however, there may have been other patients with SLE within the database who were not coded for at the index hospitalization for various reasons, such as the SLE was erroneously not recorded, the SLE was not deemed relevant, or the SLE was inactive. Second, the NRD does not include patients’ social determinants of health, including race, ethnicity, education level, or socioeconomic status, which significantly contribute to worse outcomes in patients with SLE. Race and ethnicity have been shown to affect SLE incidence, course, and death. 29 , 30 Previous studies have found that African American and Hispanic patients with SLE are at greater risk of readmissions than White patients with SLE. 31 Third, we were not able to capture patients’ medication information because it is not available within this database. Fourth, the NRD is also limited in that it can follow a patient's admissions within one state, allows only short‐term follow‐up limited to one calendar year, and does not capture death outside an inpatient setting. Fifth, the NRD's purview on the cost and medical burden of SLE is limited to the cost of hospitalization and omits other significant resources, such as laboratory test results, radiology reports, medications, or other outpatient clinic visits, which limits more detailed analysis. Because of the difficulty in defining and categorizing frailty, our study has inherent limitations because of the use of the HFRS. The HFRS was originally developed based on a cohort of patients who were aged 75 or older, whereas the average age of the patients in our study was 68 years. Although there are no specific studies investigating the effectiveness of the HFRS in younger adult patient populations, numerous studies have employed the HFRS in comparable contexts. 19 , 32 We calculated the frailty risk score at only the index hospitalization, a single time point, and dichotomized scores into a frail or nonfrail group. However, frailty is a dynamic, multisystem illness that a binary rating cannot fully represent. Furthermore, it has to be noted that indices such as the HFRS do not take patient perspectives regarding their disease activity or frailty into account.

Research on the relationship between frailty and readmissions in patients with SLE is in its early stages, and additional investigation will be needed for further risk stratification and identification of patients at higher risk for adverse hospitalization outcomes, including readmissions and deaths. Despite the limitations, our study's major strengths are its large representative population and relatively long follow‐up time, which allows us to study various longitudinal risk factors and outcomes for frailty and SLE after hospitalization. Future studies could assess frailty status longitudinally in patients with SLE and ascertain how different treatments impact frailty level. Further research is also needed to determine strategies to reduce frailty in patients with SLE to decrease readmission rates and other adverse health outcomes.

Among hospitalized patients with SLE, frailty is associated with longer LOS at initial hospitalization. Frail patients with SLE were more likely to be male, older, and insured with Medicare. Longitudinally, frailty is associated with higher rates of readmissions, inpatient mortality, and severe hospitalizations and fewer days to readmission. Ultimately, frail patients with SLE also had a higher cost burden during hospitalization.
