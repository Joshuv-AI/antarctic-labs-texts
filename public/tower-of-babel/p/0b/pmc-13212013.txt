Does calculating the textbook outcome based on its negative predictors enhance the transparency of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma surgery assessment?
Authors: Mikhail Efanov, Natalia Britskaia, Denis Fisenko, Pavel Tarakanov, Yuliya Kulezneva, Olga Melekhina, Anna Koroleva, Andrey Vankovich, Dmitry Kovalenko, Nikita Solovyev, Victor Tsvirkun, Igor Khatkov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgrounds/AimsThe definition of textbook outcome (TO) for liver surgery in patients with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC)varies depending on the data and TO model utilized in the study. This study aimed to clarify the separate estimation of TO in relation to its negative predictors, using two validated TO models for ICC surgery.MethodsWe assessed the rates of achieving TO and failure of TO for both models in liver resection. Independent predictors of non-TO were identified through logistic regression and validated using AUC estimation.ResultsTO was achieved in a similar proportion of patients across both models: 40% and 43%. The TO models did not differ in preoperative data affecting TO achievement. Independent predictors of non-TO for both models included tumor size > 10 cm,, open surgical approach, and biliary resection. TO achievement significantly differed among sub-groups that were homogeneous concerning the opposing values of the independent predictors, regardless of the TO model. The ratio of the frequency of negative predictors in the TO and non-TO groups, referred to as the TO coefficient, determines the likelihood of achieving TO given the presence of that predictor. Predictors were ranked by their negative impact on TO achievement, from the highest risk of TO failure (tumor size > 10 cm) to the lowest risk (open approach).ConclusionsIndependent predictors of non-TO may improve the transparency of TO assessment and reduce biases related to unaccounted negative prognostic factors. The impact of these independent predictors on TO realization can be estimated and ranked using TO coefficient calculations.Keywords: Cholangiocarcinoma, Textbook outcome, Biliary resection, Minimally invasive liver resection, Minimally invasive biliary resection

INTRODUCTION

The use of composite models to assess surgical outcomes following liver resection, referred to as textbook outcome (TO), is becoming increasingly prevalent in clinical practice. In one of the first multicenter studies on TO in liver surgery, Görgec et al. [1] reported an achievement rate of 69.1%. This topic has garnered significant attention from various authors. A recent systematic review of 32 observational studies found that TO consists of five discrete parameters (ranging from four to seven), with achievement rates varying between 11.2% and 77.0% [2]. Nevertheless, many researchers continue to rely on the models proposed by Görgec et al. [1] or similar TO models, which yield achievement rates of approximately 59% to 69% [3,4].

In recent years, individual TO models tailored to specific tumor types have emerged. Over the past five years, there has been growing experience in applying the TO model to evaluate surgeries for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC). However, the findings from studies utilizing TO for ICC remain contentious, despite several large multicenter analyses [3-5]. In the two largest international studies, the rate of achieving TO was reported to be between 25.5% and 60.2% [4,5].

In addition to the estimated surgical outcomes incorporated within the TO framework, the achievement rate of TO is influenced by various associated factors that have an independent predictive impact on the realization of TO. Some studies account for these factors in predictive models, such as nomograms or equations, which calculate the risk of TO failure for individual patients. However, these factors are often overlooked in TO estimations for entire patient cohorts or in comparisons across different studies [4-6]. Factors associated with the failure to achieve TO carry prognostic significance independent of the TO structure and can therefore be identified as independent predictors of TO. Currently, no studies have justified the need for or proposed a mechanism for evaluating TO while considering the influence of these independent predictors.

In ICC surgery, bile duct resection is a consistent predictor of failure to achieve tumor resection (TO) [7]. The low TO achievement rate of approximately 25% is linked to bile duct and liver resection in cases of perihilar cholangiocarcinoma [8,9]. Some surgical benchmark studies have adopted a cautious approach to biliary resection, excluding patients with bile duct resection from the benchmarking category [3]. However, when evaluating TO achievement in liver resections without specifying tumor type or in cases involving bile ducts, authors often fail to discuss the separate outcomes of liver and bile duct resections. This oversight is notable given the lower TO achievement rate associated with combined liver and bile duct resections [1,3]. In a recent large study involving 1,556 ICC patients, which reported a 60.2% TO achievement rate, the authors acknowledged that bile duct resection might contribute to the lower TO rate compared to another study, but did not clarify its specific impact [4].

Moreover, the method of surgery—open versus minimally invasive—significantly influences the TO achievement rate. A 2019 study by Görgec et al. [1] found that TO was achieved in 74.8% of laparoscopic resections, compared to 61.9% for open resections (p < 0.001). The authors applied the same model to both approaches. Subsequent studies from other centers confirmed a high TO implementation rate of 80.5%, suggesting that laparoscopic resections may warrant separate assessment. Some authors have proposed developing a distinct TO model for laparoscopic resections, despite the minor differences between these models and those for open resections [10].

Several external factors associated with achieving tumor organ preservation (TO) include age, T stage, and vascular invasion. The number and type of these factors vary across studies, and they have not been validated as independent predictors of TO achievement or failure in other research [5].

Consequently, achieving TO in ICC surgery relies on the criteria outlined in the TO model and on external factors that independently influence TO success. Numerous studies utilize different TO models or propose new ones without adequately accounting for these external factors, which diminishes the clarity of TO assessment. It remains uncertain whether new TO models for ICC surgery enhance the quality of evaluation.

In this context, the aim of the study was to justify the use of independent predictors for separate TO assessment in ICC surgery, based on two validated TO models.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The research ethics committee approved the study protocol (1/2025, 22.01.2025). Informed consent was obtained from all patients before surgery, which included permission to use de-identified clinical data for this and other retrospective scientific studies. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study setting and design

The study was conducted at a high-volume tertiary center specializing in hepatobiliary surgery, with particular expertise in minimally invasive procedures such as laparoscopic and robotic liver and bile duct resections. It was designed as a single-center retrospective cohort study. The primary aim was to provide evidence for the hypothesis that the quality of TO estimation relies on independent predictors of TO failure as much as on the TO models themselves. Notably, independent predictors of TO failure are not included in the estimated surgical outcomes that comprise the TO models.

Efforts to enhance TO models by incorporating additional estimated outcomes often complicate the composite assessment, making it less transparent and predictable. In contrast, the use of validated TO models standardizes comparisons, suggesting that further improvements in TO assessment quality may arise from utilizing predictors not currently included in TO models. These independent predictors, assumed to be categorical, facilitate TO estimation in two subgroups that are homogeneous concerning the binary values of the predictor. This approach enhances the transparency of the TO assessment.

To test the hypothesis, we deliberately selected two TO models that differ in design and validation methods, both supported by a robust evidence base for their validity. One model is the most widely used TO for various liver tumors, while the other, the ICC TO, was developed from the largest multicenter study. We compared the two models to ensure that any differences between them do not significantly affect the rate of TO achievement. If no significant differences are found, it would indicate that the validated TO models are roughly equivalent regarding the rate of achieving the composite outcome and share similar estimated criteria.

If both models show no differences in the rate of achieving TO, we will assess the influence of independent predictors on TO implementation. Finding a similar negative impact of independent predictors on the TO rate in both models would support the need for a separate evaluation of TO in patient subgroups defined by contrasting values of these predictors. Such evaluations would help eliminate confounding relationships between independent predictors, their random allocation within the sample, and TO as the outcome. Additionally, we will compare the impact of different TO criteria on the failure to achieve TO to identify the criteria through which independent predictors exert their negative influence.

Data on immediate outcomes were collected from a prospective database of patients treated for ICC from October 2013 to December 2024, with all included patients having a pathologically confirmed diagnosis.

Procedure technique

The procedure type included R0 liver resection, which aimed for parenchyma-sparing surgery. Major resection was performed when hemihepatectomy or extended hepatectomy could not be avoided. R1 vascular resection was deemed acceptable if no vascular invasion by the tumor was confirmed intraoperatively. Lymph node dissection from stations 8a,p; 12 a,b,c, p; and 13a (according to Japan Pancreas Society nomenclature) was a standard step in all procedures except stage T1. Extrahepatic bile duct resection with Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy and caudate lobectomy were performed when preoperative biliary invasion with obstructive jaundice was present. A future liver remnant volume of less than 40% was considered an indication for portal vein embolization (PVE).

Data collection and study objects

Two models of TO were selected for comparative analysis. Model 1, based on the TO proposed by Görgec et al. [1], is a widely recognized standard developed by numerous international experts through Delphi voting, particularly in the context of liver surgery [4]. The criteria for Model 1 include the absence of intraoperative incidents of grade 2 or higher, postoperative bile leaks classified as grade B or C, severe postoperative complications, readmission within 30 days of discharge, in-hospital mortality, and the presence of an R0 resection margin.

Model 2 corresponds to the TO definition derived from the largest recent multicenter study involving 1,556 patients with ICC [3]. In Model 2, TO is achieved if all of the following criteria are met: absence of post-hepatectomy liver failure, intraoperative blood loss of less than 750 mL, absence of major infectious complications, and R0 resection.

In both models, demographic data were compared between the TO and non-TO cohorts to identify differences in initial conditions that could influence the achievement of TO. The demographic variables analyzed included age, sex, body mass index, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, tumor characteristics (such as TNM stage, size, and local spread), history of preoperative chemotherapy, and type of resection. Morbidity was classified according to the Clavien-Dindo scale (CD), with grade V complications defined as in-hospital mortality from any cause.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data (mean values) were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using either the two-tailed Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square test. The Shapiro-Wilk test and Q-Q plots were used to assess the normality of continuous data distribution. Normally distributed data were compared using the Student’s t-test, and continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean and standard deviation. Continuous data that did not follow a normal distribution are expressed as median and interquartile range. A logistic regression was performed to evaluate the predictive value of non-achievement of TO. The predictive value of the independent predictors identified in the logistic regression analysis was estimated by calculating the area under the ROC curve (AUC). An AUC of 0.60–0.69 was considered poor, 0.70–0.79 fair, and 0.80–0.89 good discriminative power. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The presence of significant differences was tested using the Z-score calculation, with differences considered statistically significant if Z ≥ 2. The statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp.). The Z-score calculation was performed using the online analytical tools provided by ListenData (https://www.listendata.com).

RESULTS

Data from 106 patients with resectable ICC were analyzed. Among these, 68 patients (64%) underwent liver resection alone, while 38 patients (36%) underwent a combined liver and bile duct resection. Minimally invasive liver resections were performed in 37 patients, including 10 robotic liver resections that also involved bile duct resection. Laparoscopic liver resections were conducted in 27 patients without bile duct resection.

TO were achieved in 42 patients (40%) using Model 1 and in 46 patients (43%) using Model 2. This analysis encompassed both open and minimally invasive resections, as well as liver resections performed alone and those combined with bile duct resection.

Significant differences were observed between the TO and non-TO subgroups across all initial data, regardless of the TO model. These differences included T-stage, tumor size, the prevalence of bilobar tumors, preoperative biliary drainage, major resection (involving more than three segments), vascular resection, biliary resection, and the use of minimally invasive techniques. The most pronounced differences (p < 0.001) were noted in the rates of bilobar tumors, preoperative biliary drainage, major resections, and the use of minimally invasive approaches. Differences in TNM stage (p = 0.049) and the rate of cholangitis (p = 0.007) were significant between the TO and non-TO subgroups in Model 1 but did not reach statistical significance in Model 2 (p = 0.071 and p = 0.067, respectively). Differences approaching statistical significance were also noted in the ASA and N stage assessments for both models.

Finally, three independent predictors of non-TO prognosis were identified, each demonstrating a negative influence in the multivariate analysis for both models: T-stage, open approach, and bile duct involvement. The latter was defined as biliary resection in Model 1 and cholangitis in Model 2. To simplify the description of this last predictor, it will be referred to as “biliary resection” hereinafter, since all patients with cholangitis due to biliary drainage underwent bile duct resection.

The binary nature of independent predictors of TO failure allows for the separate assessment of TO in patients with or without these predictors, specifically those with or without biliary resection, an open surgical approach, and tumor size ≥ 10 cm. Conducting separate assessments enhances the homogeneity of the data within the cohort and improves the prediction of TO achievement. To test this hypothesis, we compared data from patients with and without the identified definitive predictors of TO failure. Table 3 indicates a significant difference in TO achievement and failure rates among subgroups that are homogeneous regarding each independent predictor value for both TO models. In Model 1, TO was achieved in 5 (13%) patients with biliary resection compared to 37 (54%) without biliary resection (p < 0.001). For the open and minimally invasive approaches, TO was accomplished in 17 (25%) and 25 (68%) patients, respectively (p < 0.001). Among patients with tumors larger than 10 cm, TO was realized in 3 (12%) patients, while it was achieved in 39 (48%) patients with smaller tumors (p < 0.001). Similarly, in Model 2, TO was achieved in 8 (21%) patients with biliary resection and in 38 (56%) without biliary resection (p < 0.001). Regarding the type of surgical approach, TO was achieved in 29 (29%) patients with open surgery and in 26 (70%) patients with minimally invasive surgery (p < 0.001). For tumor size, TO was realized in 5 (20%) patients with negative predictor values and in 41 (50%) patients with positive predictor values (p = 0.007). These findings reinforce the importance of separately evaluating TO outcomes in cohorts of patients with opposing values of binary independent predictors of TO failure.

The distribution of independent predictors among patients who achieved TO and those who did not reveals an uneven influence of various factors on the success or failure of TO (Fig. 4). As illustrated in Fig. 4, the distribution of these independent predictors is largely consistent between models 1 and 2. This distribution is characterized by the ratio of each predictor’s presence in patients who achieved TO compared to those who did not, and this ratio remained stable across both models. This consistency applies to both the magnitude of these ratios for each predictor and their distribution among different predictors within each model. In this context, the ratio—reflecting the frequency of a negative predictor’s occurrence in the TO group—holds independent significance and influences the likelihood of achieving TO when that predictor is present (referred to as the TO coefficient). A smaller ratio indicates a stronger negative impact of the independent predictor on TO success. In other words, a lower coefficient value for an independent predictor suggests a reduced likelihood of contributing to TO, while conversely indicating a greater likelihood of contributing to TO failure. Different predictors have distinct TO coefficients.

Contribution of independent predictors (tumor size, biliary resection, and open approach) to textbook outcome (TO) achievement and failure in Models 1 and 2.

The TO coefficient (TO/non-TO ratio) was lowest for tumor size greater than 10 cm (0.14 and 0.23 in Models 1 and 2, respectively), indicating the highest risk of TO failure. Conversely, the TO coefficient was highest for open resection in both TO models (0.33 and 0.41), suggesting a less negative impact of this approach on achieving TO. An intermediate coefficient value was noted for biliary resection (0.15 and 0.27). Although the differences in TO coefficients among the various independent predictors did not reach statistical significance, they serve as indirect evidence of differing contributions to the risk of TO failure in this series. The most significant negative effects were linked to tumor size greater than 10 cm and biliary resection, and this trend was consistent across both TO models.

The estimated outcomes included in the TO models and significantly linked to limited TO achievement were identified as reasons for non-TO. These reasons varied based on the TO model and were associated with the presence of independent predictors of non-TO. These reasons for non-TO showed significant differences between the entire series and the cohort of patients treated with biliary resection, particularly concerning the rate of major infectious complications (p = 0.004), a criterion in Model 2, as well as the rates of severe morbidity (> 2 CD) (p < 0.001) and biliary fistula B, C (International Study Group for Liver Surgery) (p = 0.010), which were criteria in Model 1. Additionally, the rate of TO achievement was significantly lower in patients with large tumors for both models compared to the whole series (p = 0.009 for Model 1 and p = 0.031 for Model 2).

DISCUSSION

Over the past decade, TO has been integrated into clinical practice as a reliable method for the comprehensive evaluation of liver surgery. Numerous studies have examined the structure of TO and the surgical outcomes estimated within TO models [1-5]. In a landmark study by Görgec et al. [1], the first to present a TO model without considering liver tumor type, the TO achievement rate was reported at 69.1%.

Several studies have concentrated on TO in specific tumor types [3-5,11-13]. Some researchers utilized established models from previous studies, while others proposed new models that generally showed minimal differences from existing ones. The TO achievement rates exhibited slight variations across different tumor types. For instance, in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the TO achievement rate ranged from 50.9% to 77.7% [11]. In surgeries for colorectal liver metastases, TO implementation was reported between 71.8% and 72.2% [12,13]. Significant variability in TO achievement has been noted in studies on ICC. For example, Merath et al. [5] reported a TO achievement rate of only 25.5%, whereas a multicenter study by Altaf et al. [4], which analyzed 1,556 patients with ICC, found a TO achievement rate of 60.2%. One key reason for this disparity is that the earlier study applied more stringent criteria for TO achievement, including the absence of any complications and a hospital stay longer than the 50th percentile as the most limiting outcomes.

Efforts to adapt TO models to the specifics of cholangiocarcinoma surgery often lead to a reduced achievement rate of TO. In the study by Atyah et al. [14], which evaluated surgical outcomes for various biliary malignancies, incorporating laboratory parameters (such as rapid decreases in total bilirubin, international normalized ratio, and white blood cell counts) into standard surgical assessments resulted in a further decline in the TO achievement rate, which was recorded at 25.58%. Similarly, in the study by Hobeika et al. [15], adjusting the TO for ICC by including lymphadenectomy in the assessment criteria resulted in a low TO implementation rate of 22.1%.

This trend of decreasing TO achievement rates is also evident in studies proposing oncological models that consider cancer-specific parameters. For instance, Munir et al. [16] conducted a retrospective study using the National Cancer Database and introduced the textbook oncologic outcome (TOO) for ICC, achieving a rate of only 11.2%. However, when the criterion for “adequate” lymphadenectomy was removed from the model, the TOO achievement rate rose to 50.5%. These findings were corroborated in a subsequent study by Munir et al. [17], which reported TOO rates of 6.2% for robotic resections, 8.1% for laparoscopic, and 12.5% for open resections in ICC. Additionally, lymph node harvesting emerged as a predictor of TOO failure.

In the present study, two TO models were utilized. Model 1, developed by Görgec et al. [1], is widely applied across various liver tumors. Model 2, proposed by Altaf et al. [4], was specifically designed for assessing surgical outcomes in ICC and was validated in a recent large multicenter study. While Model 2 differs from Model 1 in the number and types of surgical outcomes evaluated, the TO rates were comparable in both models, at 40% and 43%, respectively. This suggests that many studies are now reporting similar TO rates despite variations in their models.

The comparable TO results can be attributed to the fact that many models assess the same or similar outcomes, typically focusing on major complications, mortality, readmission, residual tumor (R1), and length of stay, often based on the 50th or 75th percentile [1,2,11,12]. Minor variations in clinically significant criteria, such as blood loss and infectious complications, do not significantly affect TO achievement. These models have been validated through numerous studies and large multicenter international projects, making them relatively reliable tools for composite outcome assessment with predicted TO achievement rates. The current study confirmed the lack of differences in composite outcomes between these two validated models, finding that the TO achievement rates and factors influencing TO implementation were comparable or identical, despite structural differences in the TO models. While some studies have noted the similarity of validated TO models in terms of oncologic and perioperative quality parameters, no direct comparisons of TO achievement rates and influencing factors between models have been conducted [1,4].

As a result, it is now clear that well-established validated TO models have an average achievement rate of 40% to 70%, primarily varying by tumor type. These models are similar in the number and types of evaluated factors. However, increasing the complexity of the models by adding specific factors results in a significant decrease in the TO achievement rate and complicates the comparison of results.

The achievement of TO is clearly influenced not only by established criteria but also by independent predictors that are absent from current models yet significantly affect TO outcomes.

Researchers have highlighted that TO achievement can be affected by the complexity of resection, which is determined by factors such as liver condition (e.g., Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer stage for HCC), tumor type (e.g., lymphadenectomy for ICC), tumor size and number, blood loss, and PVE, particularly in colorectal metastases and other tumors [11,12]. In studies focusing on TO in ICC surgery, various factors impacting TO achievement have been identified, including preoperative jaundice, T-stage, N-stage, neoadjuvant chemotherapy, bile duct resection, and vascular resection [4-6]. The contribution of these independent predictors for TO failure was estimated in nomogram and realized in prediction model for achievement of TO for ICC, calculating chances for individual patients [4-6]. Notably, a large multicenter study published in 2024 reported a TO achievement rate of 60.2% and identified independent predictors through multivariate regression analysis [4]. The authors presented the distribution of these predictors in both TO and non-TO groups. However, the study lacks data on TO achievement rates within subgroups that are homogeneous regarding these independent predictors, leaving unclear how the proposed TO model operates in the absence of confounding influences. The reported distribution of independent predictors in the TO and non-TO groups does not adequately address this issue, a concern also noted in a 2019 study by the same research team [5].

Consequently, the initial variability of independent predictors contributes to data heterogeneity, complicating the assessment of TO realization and the comparison of TO rates across different centers. In the present study, we aimed to objectively evaluate the rate of TO achievement while minimizing the confounding effects of independent predictors. Our analysis identified three independent predictors of TO failure: an open surgical approach, biliary resection, and tumor size greater than 10 cm.

The type of approach adds heterogeneity, as the implementation of TO after minimally invasive resections is typically higher due to various factors, including patient selection and a lower degree of surgical trauma. To enhance the transparency of TO assessment, it may be beneficial to evaluate open and minimally invasive resections separately, as suggested by numerous researchers [1,17,18]. Görgec et al. [1] also demonstrated a significant difference in achieving TO: 61.9% for open resection and 74.8% for laparoscopic resection. In the current study, the TO rate for ICC after minimally invasive resection was significantly higher compared to open resection, even though the minimally invasive cohort included liver and bile duct resections performed using a robotic approach.

There is currently no discussion in the literature regarding the need for separate TO assessments in patients who have undergone liver resection with and without bile duct resection. Large series evaluating TO in ICC patients report a wide range of implementation rates, from 11% to 60%, with some studies indicating a negative impact—either indirect or direct—of bile duct resection [4,5,16]. In the study by Merath et al. [5], bile duct resection was performed in 7.4% of patients who achieved TO, compared to 24% in those who did not (p < 0.001). The absence of bile duct resection was independently associated with achieving TO. In the current series, bile duct resection was performed in 11.9% and 17.4% of patients with TO, according to Model 1 and Model 2, respectively. For patients who did not achieve TO, the rates of biliary resection were 51.6% and 50.0%, respectively.

In the study by Altaf et al. [4], the authors highlighted biliary resection and reconstruction as inherent challenges in ICC surgery, attributing the decreasing rate of achieving TO to these factors. However, they did not specify how many cases in the analyzed data involved biliary resection. As a result, many authors consider biliary resection an independent confounder when calculating TO in ICC surgery, suggesting that removing its impact could enhance the clarity of the assessment.

Evidence that bile duct resection significantly reduces the achievement of TO is found in studies assessing outcomes of resections for perihilar cholangiocarcinoma, which report a TO implementation rate of 24%–25.3% [8,9]. Efforts to develop a specific model for biliary cancers by incorporating criteria relevant to biliary surgery into the most commonly used TO models have resulted in a projected decrease in TO achievement: an overall rate of 25.6% and 11.2% specifically for ICC [14].

Tumor size is identified as an independent factor associated with TO achievement in some studies [12,19]. However, no research has examined the application of a critical tumor size for separate TO assessments.

Therefore, by analogy to open surgical approaches, it seems appropriate to evaluate liver resection outcomes in homogeneous cohorts based on combined liver and biliary resection, as well as critical tumor size, both of which are considered independent predictors of failure to achieve TO. These factors have a binary nature, allowing for division into two cohorts for separate TO assessments. This study reveals significant differences in the TO achievement rates not only between these homogeneous cohorts with opposing binary predictor values but also between each cohort and the entire series. Consequently, utilizing independent predictors for separate TO assessments may enhance the transparency of TO comparisons across different series or surgical centers.

The use of independent binary predictors, regardless of the approach type, has not been previously proposed as a standard method to enhance the transparency of TO assessment and comparison. In two studies, independent predictors of TO failure in ICC surgery were primarily limited to evaluating the prognosis of TO achievement in individual patients, either through nomogram calculations or by developing prognostic models using machine learning [5,6].

In this study, independent predictors significantly reduced the rate of TO achievement, irrespective of the TO model used. These findings underscore the utility of validated TO models, which reliably predict the rate of TO achievement.

However, the contribution of different independent predictors to TO failure varied, even though all predictors had a significantly negative impact on TO achievement. The extent of this negative impact can be estimated by the ratio of predictor presence in patients who achieved TO compared to those who did not. In the current study, this ratio was defined as the TO coefficient. A lower TO coefficient indicates a lesser contribution of the independent predictor to achieving TO. For instance, the coefficient was significantly lower for tumor size greater than 10 cm (0.14 for Model 1 and 0.23 for Model 2) compared to the open approach (0.33 for Model 1 and 0.41 for Model 2), highlighting a more pronounced negative impact of tumor size on the composite outcome in this series. Therefore, by examining the TO coefficient, independent predictors can be ranked according to the degree of their negative impact. This ranking may be practically important when comparing TO realization across different patient samples.

The current study shows that independent predictors negatively affect various outcomes within the TO structure. Biliary resections consistently led to a higher incidence of complications, including biliary and severe complications in Model 1, and infectious complications in Model 2. The open surgical approach was linked to increased rates of major morbidity in Model 1, but it did not significantly alter outcomes in Model 2. This suggests that the open approach has a comparatively less pronounced negative effect on achieving TO, a conclusion primarily supported by the TO coefficient calculations.

Tumor size influenced outcomes in two ways: through R1 and major morbidity in Model 1, and through R1 alone in Model 2. The low rate of clear margins is expected in larger tumors. In clinical practice, understanding how predictors relate to specific outcomes sheds light on the reasons for failing to achieve TO; for example, a higher rate of R1 resections may be linked to larger average tumor sizes. Additionally, a combination of high major morbidity and biliary fistulas indicates an increased rate of biliary resections.

In this context, the use of validated TO models that account for independent predictors raises questions about the necessity of creating new TO models. Further development of the TO concept in liver surgery may be warranted by establishing a universal, validated TO model applicable across diverse patient samples with varying predictors. For ICC, such predictors include bile duct resection and surgical approach. Tumor size is a significant but variable predictor, with a threshold that may depend on the specific patient population. Therefore, the effect of continuous independent predictors on TO should be explored in larger studies to identify critical values for predicting TO failure. Independent predictors can be ranked based on their influence on TO achievement using the TO coefficient, which aids in identifying the most valuable predictors. This approach enhances the clarity of TO assessment and provides a foundation for objective comparisons between treatment centers.

The limitations of this study include its retrospective design and the relatively small sample sizes of both the overall series and the cohorts used for subgroup analysis, which diminish the reliability of statistical calculations. Additionally, potential selection biases are inherent due to factors such as institutional experience, surgical decision-making pathways, and the availability of minimally invasive techniques. Notably, tumor size emerged as the independent predictor with the most significant negative impact in our study, with its threshold value being more influenced by the specifics of the analyzed sample than by binary predictors. Nevertheless, both the overall series and the subgroup analysis produced statistically significant results for many of the compared data points, supporting the validity of the study hypothesis.

In conclusion, the study confirmed the initial hypothesis that the impact of independent predictors on TO failure is critical and should be included in TO assessments. This finding supports the use of validated TO models in patient cohorts that share similar values for these independent predictors. Such an approach enhances the transparency and predictability of TO assessments, ultimately leading to greater accuracy in comparative evaluations of TO. However, the list of independent predictors for ICC surgery requires validation through multicenter studies involving larger patient samples.
