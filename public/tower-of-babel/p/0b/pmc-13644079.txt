Assessing the geographical variation of antenatal care and maternal mortality rates among pregnant women in Sierra Leone based on DHIS2 data from 2020–2022: a retrospective, descriptive, ecological study
Authors: Morlai Sesay, Wenyi Lai, Pavan Kumar Sah, Guan Ying
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSierra Leone reported a high maternal mortality rate (MMR) of 717 per 100,000 live births in 2019 due to disparities in use, quality, and access to skilled birth attendants and antenatal care (ANC) services. Our study aimed to assess the geographical variation in MMR and ANC among pregnant women in Sierra Leone, based on District Health Information System-2 (DHIS2) data from 2020 to 2022.MethodsThis retrospective ecological study analysed aggregated DHIS2 data from health facilities across all five administrative regions of Sierra Leone from 2020–2022. The data analysis included all pregnant women and maternal deaths captured in DHIS2. The MMR was evaluated using descriptive statistics, while the geographical disparity between the MMR and ANC was assessed through factorial analysis. Multivariate regression and bivariate association analysis were performed to determine the relationship between ANC and MMR across the five administrative regions of Sierra Leone.ResultsThe MMR reported in 2020 was 760 per 100,000 live births, which significantly decreased to 648 in 2021, and the lowest rate (288 per 100,000 live births) was reported in 2022. However, regional disparities persisted, particularly in the northern, western, and northwest regions. This study found that infection deaths, haemorrhage, and malaria were the significant causes of maternal deaths from 2020–2022. The correction coefficient for the causes ranged from 0.627 to 0.960. This study found that at the 1st ANC visiting time point, all regions, excluding the southern region, showed a non-significantly decreased relative number of screened pregnant women in 2021 and 2022 compared to 2020. However, at the 4th and 8th ANC visits, all regions, excluding the western region, showed a significantly increased relative number of screened pregnant women in 2021 and 2022 than in 2020. This pattern is consistent with growing awareness and adaptive strategies, though temporal trends alone cannot establish causality among pregnant women and healthcare systems in Sierra Leone. This study found that regional differences were less pronounced each year despite significant increases over time in haemoglobin testing, syphilis screening, and IPT doses. The multivariate regression results indicated that the IPT-1st dose showed the strongest negative association (standard regression coefficient = -1.307, P < 0.001) with MMR. This study found a significant negative association between ANC-1st visit, iron folate, and malaria in the 2nd or 3rd trimester in pregnant women treated with artemisinin-based combination therapies.ConclusionsThis ecological analysis revealed significant regional disparities in MMR and ANC utilisation in Sierra Leone. Regions with lower ANC utilisation rates were associated with higher MMRs, highlighting the need for targeted interventions. However, these associations do not imply causation, and individual-level studies are needed to confirm these relationships. Improving access to and quality of ANC services in the northern, northwest, and western regions may be critical for efforts to reduce maternal mortality and for progress toward Sustainable Development Goal 3.1. However, the ecological associations reported here require confirmation through individual-level intervention studies.Keywords: Geographical variation, Maternal mortality rate, Antenatal care, Pregnant women, DHIS2, Sierra Leone

Background

Maternal mortality is still more significant in the world’s poorest countries than in the wealthiest countries, more than a century ago, making maternal mortality a severe and continuous global health concern for more than a century. In 2020, the global maternal mortality rate (MMR) was 223 per 100,000 live births [1]. The WHO reported that MMR stagnated or worsened in many regions between 2015 and 2020. As previously reported, the MMR in Australia and New Zealand had 100 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births in 2016, and in 2020, it had 65.4 per 100,000 live births, a 34.6% decrease from the 2016 rate. The MMR decreased by 15.7% in Central and Southern Asia, whereas the sub-Saharan African region continues to bear the highest risk burden, with 201,000 per 100,000 live births in 2015 [1, 2]. Sierra Leone is located in the western part of the sub-Saharan African region, where the maternal mortality ratio remains among the highest in the world, at 1,360 and 717 per 100,000 live births in 2015 and 2019, respectively. The high maternal death rates in Sierra Leone were due to inequitable access to healthcare, geographical disparity, and sociocultural backgrounds, which include customary practices, such as dependence on traditional birth attendants (TBAs), gender disparities that limit the decision-making power of women, fear of stigma associated with certain conditions (e.g., teenage pregnancy) may prevent women from utilising ANC services and socio-economic factors that affect access to healthcare [3–5]. The decrease in MMR from 2015 to 2019 was primarily due to the enforcement of the Free Health Care Initiative (FHCI) in 2010, which provided free ANC and delivery services, and the initiation of the Maternal Death Surveillance and Response (MDSR) system in 2016, which improved data collection and response to maternal deaths [6, 7]. Research has shown that unsafe abortion, haemorrhage, and sepsis are significant causes of maternal death.

In contrast, factors such as delays in obtaining timely quality care from proximity and infrastructure resources are prominent within regions of the country [8, 9]. Skilled birth attendants (SBAs) are significant in managing complications during childbirth, such as haemorrhage, sepsis, and obstructed labour, which are leading causes of maternal deaths in Sierra Leone. The lack of SBAs in rural and underserved areas exacerbates these disparities, leading to higher maternal mortality rates (MMR). This is supported by studies from sub-Saharan Africa, which highlight the importance of SBAs in improving maternal outcomes [10, 11].

The global ambitions under Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 3.1 aim to reduce MMR by 70% by 2030. Sierra Leone piloted maternal death surveillance and response (MDSR) in 2016 to review maternal death cases as well as to collect and analyse data on maternal deaths to identify causes and prevent future deaths [7]; all the data collected were later sent to the District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2) for informed decision-making. To reduce the MMR, the government of Sierra Leone also provided free antenatal care (ANC) for all pregnant women since 2010 through the implementation of the Free Health Care Initiative (FHCI), which was essential for the prevention of maternal death [6, 12, 13]. ANC included ANC visits and ANC services. ANC visits included regular skilled healthcare professionals [10]. In our country, the 1st ANC occurs between 6–13 weeks of pregnancy [14–17]. The 1st ANC visit confirmed the pregnancy, assessed the mother’s overall health, and provided essential medical information about antenatal care [17]. Healthcare professionals provided services to pregnant women, such as medical history collection, physical examination, haemoglobin (Hb) testing, syphilis infection testing, intermittent preventive treatment (IPT) with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP), malaria treatment with artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), and the initiation of dietary supplementation (nutrition counselling). In most African countries, policies are available, although coverage might vary [18–20]. The 4th ANC visit occurred between 24 and 28 weeks of pregnancy [21]. The purpose was to monitor the progress of the pregnancy and identify any potential complications during the pregnancy. Services such as baby growth monitoring, screening for gestational diabetes, ultrasound and health education were provided to the pregnant woman during the 4th ANC visit. According to the WHO’s Global Health Observatory, 86% of women in high-income countries receive their 4th ANC visit. However, this was significantly reduced in low-income regions, with only approximately 50% coverage [22]. The 8th visit (usually at approximately 36–38 weeks of pregnancy) was the last visit in the standard ANC schedule and usually occurred in the third trimester to check the babies’ development and mothers’ health and to plan for delivery. Reaching the 8th visit was challenging in places with fewer resources. Only 40% of women have completed all eight ANC visits worldwide, but the number of ANC visits is more significant in developed countries (60–80%) [18, 23, 24].

Quality indicates the availability and effectiveness of extensive antenatal care (ANC) services, consisting of timely access to skilled healthcare providers, adequate medical supplies, and the ability to control complications effectively. This links with the WHO’s definition of quality ANC, highlighting the importance of access and the provision of evidence-based interventions [17]. ANC services include Hb testing, syphilis screening, the distribution of iron folate, IPT with SP, and malaria in the 1st and 2nd or 3rd trimesters treated with ACTs [25, 26]. Testing haemoglobin levels helped identify women at risk for anaemia. As previous research revealed, there could be a significant reduction in the number of cases of preterm birth and low birth weight with adequate screening and treatment of anaemia [27]. According to the WHO, syphilis during pregnancy causes more than 300,000 deaths annually among fetuses and neonates [28]. Syphilis screening at the first antenatal care visit is recommended to prevent transmission and improve pregnancy outcomes. The WHO recommends IPT during pregnancy with SP to prevent malaria and reduce adverse birth outcomes due to the condition in sub-Saharan Africa, the region predominantly afflicted by Plasmodium falciparum [29]. Prevention via IPT provided intermittent chemoprophylaxis and presumptive treatment of malaria and cleared the asymptomatic infection. The current WHO recommendations for the treatment of uncomplicated Plasmodium falciparum malaria recommend ACTs during the second and third trimesters (during the 4th and 8th ANC visits, respectively) but quinine during the first trimester (1st ANC visit) because of concerns over embryo toxicity with early exposure to artemisinin [30]. Studies from Rwanda and Ethiopia reported that enhanced ANC services utilisation and the influence of SBAs were associated with significant reductions in MMR [31, 32]. While our findings align with studies from Rwanda and Ethiopia showing ANC-maternal mortality associations, the ecological design shared across these studies precludes causal inference.

However, Sierra Leone had a low ANC attendance rate (24% had four or more visits from 2008 to 2013). Problems in service delivery and poor access to health insurance limit ANC usage in Sierra Leone [25, 26]. Geographical disparities in ANC emphasise the differences between rural and urban areas. For example, rural areas face challenges such as limited healthcare facilities, inadequate transportation, cultural preferences for traditional birth attendants, and lower ANC attendance rates [9, 33, 34]. The COVID-19 pandemic has also affected the country’s ANC attendance rate among pregnant women [35]. Lockdowns, transportation issues, and fear of infection discouraged pregnant women from joining ANC visits, especially in the early stages of the pandemic. However, the following years (2021 onwards) showed restoration as healthcare systems adapted and awareness increased [35, 36]. ANC was underutilised, especially among geographically isolated pregnant women. Therefore, gaps still need to be filled in assessing geographical disparities in ANC and MMR based on the DHIS2 database, which requires data-driven strategies for equitable healthcare.

Despite the implementation of DHIS2 in Sierra Leone since 2008, no prior study has utilised this national database to assess regional variations in ANC utilisation and MMR across all five administrative regions. Existing studies have relied on Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data or facility-specific assessments, which lack the comprehensive, routine coverage that DHIS2 provides for health systems monitoring [34, 37]. This gap limits the evidence base for regional resource allocation and targeted interventions. Therefore, this study seeks to close that gap based on the DHIS2 data from 2020–2022, which provides an integrated database.

Potential policy relevance

These findings might contribute to sound policy-making and the dissemination of information at the global and regional levels. These findings will help shape policies and programs to overcome barriers to maternal health and provide a prototype for more extensive assessment in similar lower-resource settings, improving the understanding of maternal health epidemiology.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective, descriptive, ecological study utilising aggregated health facility data from the District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2) for the period January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2022. The ecological design was necessitated by the use of regionally aggregated data rather than individual-level records.

Data source and study population

Administratively, Sierra Leone is divided into five regions (Northern, Southern, Eastern, Western, and Northwest area); each region is subdivided into a few districts, and each district is further divided into chiefdoms. There are 16 districts and 190 chiefdoms in the country [33]. This structure was relevant to our study because it highlights disparities in healthcare access and ANC utilisation, particularly between regions. Routine program data from the District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2), sourced from all five administrative regions in 16 districts and health facilities in Sierra Leone via this link: https://sl.dhis2.org (anyone should be authorised and have access to reproductive health data). DHIS2 is a national database established in 2008 [34, 38, 39]. The data were collected by healthcare providers and recorded in eight different forms, representing the different morbidities and services at all major health facilities. They were entered into the DHIS2 monthly and synced into the national for informed decision-making. The data collection process follows standardised protocols established by the Ministry of Health and Sanitation (MoHS) in Sierra Leone, ensuring consistency and reliability. Data integrity checks are performed regularly by the district monitoring and evaluation officers and the Directorate of Policy, Planning, and Information (DPPI) in the Ministry of Health and Sanitation to minimise errors [34, 40–42].

The MoHS uses the DHIS2 data to inform policy decisions, allocate resources, and monitor the impact of interventions nationwide, such as the Free Health Care Initiative (FHCI) and the Maternal Death Surveillance and Response (MDSR) system. This data is critical for identifying high-risk regions and targeting interventions to reduce maternal mortality [6, 43]. The DHIS2 is recognised as a reliable source of health data, as it integrates data from all five administrative regions and 16 districts, and is used for national policy-making [43]. Potential underreporting that occurred due to some maternal deaths occurring outside health facilities (e.g., at home) may not be fully captured, and there is a need for community-based surveillance to complement facility-based data [7].

This data was chosen for its reliability as a national dataset and integration into the country’s disease and surveillance system. Since Sierra Leone established the MDSR system in 2016, this study targeted the 2020–2022 timeframe because, by that time, Sierra Leone’s MDSR, which had been put in place in 2016, had developed and produced more trustworthy maternal health data. The data integrity was good from 2020 to 2022, which we used in our latter study.

Sample size justification

The study included all available data points from the 16 districts across 5 regions for the 3-year study period, yielding 15 region-year ecological units (5 regions × 3 years). This constitutes a census of available DHIS2 data for the study period rather than a sample, eliminating sampling error concerns. The sample size of 22,908 pregnant women and maternal deaths represents the completely captured population in DHIS2 during this timeframe.

Study variables

The variables were extracted from the DHIS2 within the period under review. There were 3 ANC visiting time points (1st, 4th, and 8th); 8 ANC services, including haemoglobin (Hb) testing, syphilis screening, 3 IPT doses, iron folate supplementation, 2 malaria doses, and maternal deaths. Operational definitions

Maternal Mortality Rate (MMR)

Number of maternal deaths per 100,000 live births recorded in DHIS2.

1st ANC visit

First antenatal care attendance recorded between 6–13 weeks of gestation.

4th ANC visit

Fourth antenatal care attendance recorded between 24–28 weeks of gestation.

8th ANC visit

Eighth antenatal care attendance recorded at approximately 36–38 weeks of gestation.

Hb testing

Haemoglobin level testing performed during any ANC visit.

Syphilis screening

Rapid plasma reagin (RPR) or equivalent testing for syphilis during ANC.

IPT-1st, 2nd, 3rd doses

Administration of sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) for malaria prophylaxis at scheduled intervals.

Iron folate supplementation

Provision of iron and folic acid tablets during ANC.

Malaria Treatment (ACTs)

Treatment of malaria in the 2nd or 3rd trimester with artemisinin-based combination therapies.

Data analysis

Data was analysed using SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Version 25, IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, and U.S.A). Inferential statistics (chi-square tests, ANOVA, correlation analyses) were applied to test for significant differences and associations across time periods and regions, treating the aggregated region-year units as the analytical units. While we acknowledge the absence of a traditional control group, the temporal comparison (2020 versus 2021 versus 2022) and regional comparisons serve as analytical contrasts. We recognise that p-values in this ecological context indicate the probability of observing the reported associations by chance, given the data structure, but do not imply causality or individual-level relationships [45, 46]. The significance level was set as 0.05, and any P-value less than 0.05 was statistically significant.

The MMR calculation used live births recorded in DHIS2 as the denominator. Based on the reported total study population (N = 22,908), the estimated live births were 7,237 in 2020 (55 maternal deaths), 7,562 in 2021 (49 maternal deaths), and 7,639 in 2022 (22 maternal deaths). These denominators represent facility-reported births and may underestimate total population births if home deliveries were not captured. These denominators are explicitly reported in Tables 1 and 2. Multivariate regression diagnostics

Independent variables were selected a priori based on WHO recommendations for antenatal care services (IPTp, Hb testing, malaria treatment with ACTs) and on prior ecological evidence from sub-Saharan Africa linking these services to maternal mortality outcomes. To avoid overfitting given the small number of ecological units, the final model was restricted to five predictors, consistent with the rule of thumb of at least 10 observations per predictor. Cluster-robust standard errors were computed with region as the cluster variable to account for intra-region correlation across the three study years (2020–2022).

The variation of MMR in Sierra Leone from 2020–2022

Based on the DHIS2 database, we found that the overall MMR reported in 2020 was 760 per 100,000 live births; in 2021, it dropped to 648 per 100,000 live births, and the lowest rate (288 per 100,000 live births) was reported in 2022. The variation of MMR in Sierra Leone had a regional distribution from 2020–2022, and the highest MMR was reported in the northern region, followed by western, northwest, eastern, and southern regions from 2020 to 2022, as Fig. 2 shows. Compared to 2020, the MMR in the northern region dropped, but it still had a relatively high rate (1,323 per 100,000 live births) in 2021. The western, northwest, and southern regions showed considerable reductions in maternal deaths in 2021 compared to 2020. During 2022, the northern region, which had the highest in 2020, dropped sharply to 524 per 100,000 live births (P = 0.025). The northwest, southern, and western regions maintained relatively low MMR, ranging from 436, 131, and 349 per 100,000 live births, respectively, compared to previous years, as displayed in Fig. 2. These findings reflect ecological patterns and should not be interpreted as causal relationships at the individual level.

Geographical disparities in ANC utilisation

This study involved different time points (2020–2022) and regions, and we employed factorial analysis to determine the influence of those two significant effects. We used relative frequency to present the results of ANC visits because the original frequency of pregnant women varied greatly in different regions, and the results were organised in Table 3. In total, there were three time points (1st, 4th, and 8th) for ANC visits. We found that a significant difference existed in periods in all ANC visiting time points (F = 4.240, 12.237, and 73.823, respectively, all P < 0.05) and no significant difference in the number of examinations among regions (F = 2.152, 1.941, and 2.148, respectively, all P > 0.05) based on the factorial analysis results. At the 1st ANC visiting time point, all regions, excluding the southern, showed a non-significantly decreased relative number of screened pregnant women in 2021 and 2022 compared to 2020. In the 4th and 8th ANC visits, all regions, excluding Western, showed a significantly increased relative number of screened pregnant women in 2021 and 2022 compared to 2020. All results indicated a growing awareness of the importance of pregnancy screening. However, in the same year, there was no difference in the frequency of examined pregnant women among the five regions in all 3 ANC visiting time points. These findings reflect ecological patterns and should not be interpreted as causal relationships at the individual level.

All regions showed at least a two-fold increased relative frequency of screened pregnant women in 2021 and 2022 compared to 2020 in syphilis screening. The Eastern region showed a significantly increased relative frequency of screened pregnant women in 2021 and 2022 compared to 2020 in IPT 2nd and 3rd doses. The Southern region showed a significantly increased relative number of screened pregnant women in 2021 and 2022 compared to 2020 in iron folate supplementation, as Table 3 indicates. Western regions showed a significant increase in the relative number of pregnant women who took 2 doses of malaria treatment (F = 205.544 and 208.300, P = 0.001). Also, the northwest showed an overall 1.2-fold change in the relative number of pregnant women who took 2nd or 3rd doses of malaria treatment (F = 11.724, P = 0.008). However, in the same year, there was no difference in the frequency of examined pregnant women among the five regions in 6 ANC services, except for IPT 3rd dose and Iron folate. These findings reflect ecological patterns and should not be interpreted as causal relationships at the individual level.

The association analysis of ANC and MMR in Sierra Leone

In order to test the association between ANC visits, ANC services, and total MMR in pregnant women in Sierra Leone, we employed a Pearson correlation analysis. During the association analysis, we controlled the potential confounding factors such as region and socio-economic status. This ensures that the observed associations are robust and meaningful. From Table 5, we found that the 8th ANC visit had a significant negative association with total MMR (Pearson’s coefficient = −0.601, P < 0.05), indicating that an increasing number of pregnant women attending ANC visits was associated with a decreasing MMR. Also, haemoglobin testing and iron folate supplementation showed a significant negative correlation with MMR (Pearson coefficients = −0.619 and −0.515, respectively; P < 0.05), suggesting that increasing haemoglobin levels or supplementing iron during ANC could decrease MMR in pregnant women in Sierra Leone. Fulfilling the 2nd or 3rd doses of malaria treatment with ACTs had a negative association with MMR in pregnant women (Pearson coefficient = −0.345, P = 0.016), indicating the treatment in the 2nd or 3rd trimester could decrease total MMR in pregnant women. These correlations suggest that regions with higher ANC visit coverage and greater provision of essential services such as Hb testing and malaria treatment were associated with lower reported maternal mortality in Sierra Leone, particularly in high-risk regions. These findings reflect ecological patterns and should not be interpreted as causal relationships at the individual level.

Discussion

The variation of maternal mortality rates in Sierra Leone

The findings (Table 1) illustrate a significant decrease in MMRs in Sierra Leone from 2020 to 2022. The MMRs reported in 2020 were high at 760 per 100,000 live births, and later decreased further to 288 by the end of 2022. Such trends were found in other sub-Saharan African countries such as Rwanda and Ethiopia. The observed reductions in maternal mortality were temporally associated with the implementation of healthcare service delivery interventions [48], the policies that increased ANC access, skilled birth attendance (SBAs), and facility-based delivery [49]. In Ethiopia, access to ANC and region-specific interventions [32], especially in rural areas, closely resembled Sierra Leone’s efforts to reduce maternal mortality rates in high-risk areas. Increased ANC utilisation allows for early detection and management of complications, while SBAs are trained to handle emergencies during childbirth [17]. ANC services, such as Hb testing, iron folate supplementation, and IPT doses, are plausibly linked to lower maternal mortality at the population level, though this ecological study cannot establish causality. These interventions are hypothesised to improve maternal health outcomes by addressing common causes of maternal deaths, such as anaemia and malaria [17, 29].

While these positive trends align with global efforts to reduce maternal mortality, the study also highlighted uneven regional disparities. The regions that had consistently higher MMRs at first were the northern, western, and northwest regions, at 1,473, 921, and 1,105 in 2020, but they fell considerably to 524, 349, and as low as 436 in 2022, respectively. The results were consistent with other previous studies. A substantial regional variation was observed in India. MMR was higher in central and northern states at 700 per 100,000 live births, while southern states had less than 100 per 100,000 live births [50]. Another study showed that Sierra Leone cited regional differences where the western region had the highest number of maternal deaths (n = 227, accounting for 35% of the total number), followed by the northern region with 162 (25%) pregnant women who died during pregnancy or childbirth. In contrast, the eastern region had the fewest maternal deaths (n = 98, 15.1%) [37]. The factors contributing to this were literacy rate, limited healthcare accessibility, and attending ANC frequency.

On the other hand, based on the causes or aetiologies of MMR, the majority of MMR cases occurred due to malaria, haemorrhage, hypertension, infections, and other complications developed during pregnancy. This was consistent with the global and regional (SSA) causes of maternal deaths. The causes contributing to maternal deaths region-wise, as identified by the UN’s Maternal Mortality Estimation Inter-Agency Group, indicate that postpartum haemorrhage contributed to approximately 34% of the overall maternal deaths in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). In contrast, in Southern Asia, the figure was closer to 30%. Pre-eclampsia and eclampsia were two health conditions that were complications of high blood pressure (hypertension), resulting in around 10–12% of the deaths in SSA and Southern Asia. Sepsis (infection death) was responsible for approximately 10–15% of maternal deaths all over the world, but it had a higher incidence in areas with deficient healthcare infrastructure [51]. In women, malaria serves as a background illness that is exacerbated by pregnancy, resulting in approximately 20% more maternal deaths [1, 52, 53]. The correlation between MMR values for all causes was significant and positive.

The geographical disparities in ANC services and maternal mortality rates (Tables 3 and 4)

This study found a non-significantly decreased relative number of screened pregnant women in 2021 and 2022 compared to 2020 at the 1st ANC visiting time point in most regions, as illustrated in Table 3. This result aligned with other previous studies. During the initial COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, most regions experienced a tremendous decline in ANC attendance, partly due to lockdowns, fear of infection, and disruption to health care [36]. The study further reported that regions with better healthcare infrastructure and social support systems recovered faster from the pandemic’s impact on ANC attendance. This explains why ANC 1st visits remained low in some regions while recovering in others, as revealed in this current study. Another study also reported significant regional differences due to geographical, financial, social, and cultural factors in utilising maternal health services during the COVID-19 pandemic. Recovery was faster in some regions with good health infrastructure and social participation than in others [36]. This study found that later ANC visits (4th and 8th) were more consistently attended, indicating growing awareness and adaptive strategies among pregnant women and healthcare systems. The findings were in agreement with previous studies. A study reported that while maternal health services were severely disrupted in 2020, most regions saw ANC visits recover in 2021 and 2022 as pregnant women and healthcare systems adapted to improve community awareness [27, 36, 54]. Another study also reported that although early ANC visits were more likely to be missed during the pandemic, adherence to later check-ups, such as the 4th and 8th visits, was relatively consistent, as these women sought vital care just before delivery [27]. While ANC visits declined in 2020 due to the pandemic, recovery trends in 2021 and 2022 indicate resilience and increased awareness of ANC’s importance.

The factorial analysis of this study indicated that regional differences were less pronounced in each given year despite significant improvement over time on several ANC service indicators, such as Hb testing, syphilis screening, and IPT doses. By 2022, however, regional variation in iron folate and malaria treatment had become more pronounced, showing where focused regional health strategies and policies were necessary, as highlighted in Table 4. The results of the antenatal care increase in syphilis screening were similar to a study done in Zambia that also identified similar improvements in prenatal syphilis screening due to more public awareness and policies [55]. However, their study identified disparities such as inadequate ANC services delivered to pregnant women and a low ANC turnout rate. Current evidence suggests that re-implementing periodic screening during ANC improves maternal health outcomes and reduces congenital syphilis. Studies conducted in Malawi and Uganda reported that early ANC visits were crucial for lowering maternal and newborn risks. However, there was limited (initial) management capacity, and the intervention was not contested and was seen as compatible with (policy) priorities at the national level [56]. The significant increase over time in Hb testing and iron folate supplement by geographical region of Sierra Leone was consistent with improved maternal outcomes often associated with a reduction in maternal anaemia and improved birth outcomes, according to studies from Ethiopia and India [20, 57]. However, the study reported differences in early ANC visits, educational status, and residency. Studies conducted in Tanzania and Nigeria demonstrate the beneficial effects of IPT on mother and newborn health outcomes [58], similar to the noted increases in IPT dosages with time in Sierra Leone. The association between increased IPT coverage and fewer malaria-related complications supports these findings of better IPT 3rd dose administration in some regions over time.

The association between ANC and maternal mortality rates

In this study, bivariate associations revealed six statistically significant negative associations for most of the variables observed, such as prenatal client’s first visit, haemoglobin screening, prenatal client for below 12 weeks, 8th prenatal client visit, antenatal client receives iron folate (iron supplementation), and treating malaria in the 2nd or 3rd trimesters treated with ACTs.

The multivariate regression analysis showed that antenatal Hb testing, antenatal client eighth visit, antenatal two IPT doses, and malaria prevention had a significant negative effect on MMR in Sierra Leone. Research conducted in Uganda found that enhancing maternal outcomes required comprehensive ANC care, which included preventive malaria medications [59]. The IPT for malaria with SP (first and second dosages) consistently reported significant reductions in maternal mortality. Moreover, routine Hb testing at prenatal visits was crucial in reducing the risk of anaemia, thus promoting a reduction in maternal mortality [59]. Maternal mortality and the frequency of prenatal visits were also found to be negatively correlated in an Ethiopian study [60]. The negative association between Hb testing and MMR is consistent with the hypothesis that early detection and treatment of anaemia may contribute to reduced maternal mortality, though causality cannot be inferred from this ecological analysis [17, 27]. Similar to this study, there was a substantial association between significantly reduced MMRs and multiple ANC visits, especially four or more ANC visits. This study found that regular and quality ANC service utilisation was associated with lower maternal mortality at the regional level; however, whether ANC directly reduces individual risk requires individual-level study. A Nigerian study demonstrated a reduction in MMR, attributed to multiple ANC visits and targeted prevention against malaria and event management of anaemia [61]. In the study, regions with higher proportions of pregnant women receiving malaria prophylaxis medicine were less likely to experience maternal morbidity and death due to malaria infection. The role of each antenatal care service concerning MMR is independent of the others; some interventions are more successful in this respect.

Several other studies have also shown the significance of ANC service utilisation. Research conducted in Uganda in 2021 reported that fewer malaria-related problems were connected to lower maternal death rates when long-lasting insecticide-treated nets (LLITNs) and ACTs were distributed during the second or third trimester [62]. This supports our conclusion that a negative association exists between maternal mortality in Sierra Leone and the use of malaria prevention. A study in Ethiopia reported that higher ANC visits, especially four or more, have been linked to lower maternal death rates. An Ethiopian study showed that higher interactions with ANC negatively affect maternal mortality. It was noted that ANC registration was as early as possible, and interventions such as iron supplementation and anaemia screening helped boost the outcomes [63].

There is an ecological fallacy consideration. This analysis examined regional-level associations between ANC indicators and MMR. The observed negative correlations between ANC utilization and MMR at the regional level do not necessarily reflect individual-level relationships. It is possible that within high-ANC regions, individual women who do not access care still experience high mortality risk, while in low-ANC regions, women accessing care may have better outcomes than the regional average suggests. This ecological fallacy limits our ability to infer that ANC interventions directly cause mortality reduction at the individual level [45]. Future individual-level cohort studies are needed to confirm these associations.

Possible health system explanations for the observed regional variation

The observed regional differences in the use of antenatal care (ANC) services and maternal mortality could be attributed to existing health system inequalities. For instance, the poor road infrastructure and uneven distribution of health facilities could be contributing to the observed regional differences. The Northern and Northwest regions experience geographic inaccessibility, which affects the use of ANC services and emergency obstetric care [33, 64]. The uneven distribution of skilled birth attendants could be another contributing factor to the observed regional differences. The Western Area in Sierra Leone, which accounts for 17% of the population, has 34% of skilled birth attendants [9, 65]. The rural population faces a critical shortage of qualified midwives and obstetric nurses. The inconsistent supply chain could be another contributing factor to the observed regional differences in the use of intermittent preventive treatment (IPT) and iron-folate supplements. For instance, the observed regional differences in the use of IPT could be attributed to the reported stockouts of sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine in peripheral facilities [66]. The observed differences in the quality of care could be attributed to the competence of healthcare providers in the management of haemorrhage and sepsis. The observed regional differences in the use of ANC services could be attributed to the effectiveness of the services, with some women dropping out due to the ineffectiveness of the services [34]. The COVID-19 pandemic affected the health systems in the country unevenly. The lockdown and transportation challenges in 2020 worsened the existing barriers to healthcare services, while the recovery trajectories varied by the resilience of the healthcare systems and the use of CHWs [36, 66]. The observed regional differences in the use of ANC services and maternal mortality could be attributed to the overall health system, which requires strengthening beyond the expansion of ANC services.

Study limitations

Instead of using individual-level records, this study used an ecological design with aggregated regional data. Consequently, the analysis focused on regional associations, which might not accurately represent individual relationships. This raises the possibility of ecological fallacy, which states that it is not possible to infer that women who received antenatal care (ANC) services on their own were less likely to die as mothers. Rather, the results only show that maternal mortality rates (MMRs) were generally lower in areas with greater ANC coverage [45, 46]. Additionally, the analysis was unable to control individual-level confounding variables like maternal age, parity, and comorbidities.

There was no conventional control or comparison group in the study. The analysis relied on regional contrasts and temporal comparisons across 2020, 2021, and 2022 instead of comparing exposed and unexposed populations. Broader temporal trends, such as the consequences of COVID-19 recovery, and structural regional variations in health infrastructure and service delivery that are unrelated to ANC utilisation, may complicate these comparisons.

The study relied solely on the completeness and quality of the current DHIS2 records because it was a retrospective analysis of regularly collected data. The initial collection and reporting of the data were not within the researchers’ control. As a result, the results are susceptible to information bias, especially if maternal deaths in particular areas or years were routinely underreported.

Significant restrictions exist on the completeness and quality of the data. Home births and maternal deaths that take place in the community might not be included in DHIS2, which mainly records events that take place in facilities. Maternal deaths in these settings might go unreported, which could result in an underestimation of the true MMR, particularly in rural areas with lower facility delivery rates, since an estimated 30–40% of births in Sierra Leone take place outside of medical facilities [7]. Regional comparisons may become systematically biased if this underreporting differs by region.

Additionally, the denominators used to compute the maternal mortality rate (MMR) are based on reported live births. If home births are not well-reported within the system, it is possible that the total number of reported births could be low. This could then overestimate MMR rates in areas with low rates of facility births.

The analysis is limited by the lack of availability of important individual-level covariates within the DHIS2 system. Important covariates such as maternal age, parity, gravidity, socioeconomic status, level of education, distance to the nearest health facility, quality of antenatal care services beyond mere attendance, co-morbidities such as HIV, anaemia, malaria, and the availability of skilled birth attendance during delivery were not available. The lack of these covariates limits the capacity to adjust for confounders using multivariable analysis.

From an analytical perspective, inferential statistical methods such as chi-square tests, analysis of variance (ANOVA), and regression analysis were conducted. Although these methods are appropriate for analysing patterns within grouped data, the results obtained must be interpreted as descriptive of the data pattern. However, the results must not be interpreted as causal. According to Morgenstern [45], the results obtained from these methods are descriptive of the data pattern. However, the results must not be interpreted as causal.

Another notable issue is the temporal confounding effect. The period under consideration (2020–2022) coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic. The pandemic caused unprecedented health system disruption globally. The observed trend of ANC utilisation and maternal deaths may be a result of the health system disruption. For example, the observed decline in the maternal mortality rate (MMR) may be due to improvements in reporting systems rather than the actual reduction of maternal deaths.

Given the relatively small number of ecological units, the regression estimates should be considered exploratory and potentially unstable.

Finally, the results obtained may not be generalised to other settings. The results were obtained from facility-reported data. The results may therefore not apply to other settings. Other low-resource settings may have different health system structures.

Conclusions

Regional-level trends in this retrospective ecological analysis of DHIS2 data from 2020–2022 indicated that higher facility-based maternal mortality rates were associated with regions with lower ANC utilisation, particularly the Northern, Northwest, and Western regions. These correlations do not prove causation. To ascertain whether the observed ecological correlations between ANC services (specifically IPT-1st dose, Hb testing, and attendance at the 8th ANC visit) and lower MMR at the regional level are actually causal effects, more research using individual-level cohort studies is necessary. We cannot conclude that ANC interventions by themselves will lower mortality, even though our data indicate that improving ANC utilisation in high-MMR regions may be linked to better results. Strengthening the health system requires addressing several issues at once, such as transportation infrastructure, skilled birth attendance, and emergency obstetric care. This study’s main contribution is the identification of regional differences in ANC coverage and facility-reported maternal mortality in Sierra Leone during the COVID-19 pandemic. These descriptive patterns serve as a basis for developing hypotheses and focused research, but they shouldn’t be the only source of information for intervention design in the absence of additional individual-level studies. Policymakers should prioritise equal resource allocation and bridge the infrastructural gaps to ensure universal access to quality maternal health care, which could help achieve Sustainable Development Goal 3.1.
