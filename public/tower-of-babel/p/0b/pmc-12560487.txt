2-chlorofatty acid modification of neutrophil proteins: identification, localization and role in NETosis
Authors: Haley L. Carlson, Reagan M. McGuffee, Rakesh P. Patel, David A. Ford
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We previously demonstrated neutrophil MPO derived HOCl targets the vinyl ether bond of plasmalogens resulting in the Liberation of 2-chlorofatty aldehydes (2-ClFALDs) and their oxidation products, 2-chlorofatty acids (2-ClFAs), which elicit neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation. In this study, the click chemistry analog of 2-chlorohexadecanoic acid (2-ClHA) was utilized to identify 127 proteins covalently modified by 2-ClHA in human neutrophils. Bioinformatics revealed that multiple proteins modified by 2-ClHA are related to protein modification and binding as well as metabolite interconversion. Three key proteins involved in NET formation and function were modified by 2-ClHA including peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4), neutrophil defensin alpha 3 (DEFA3), and neutrophil collagenase (MMP8). PAD4 activity was shown to be increased by 2-ClFA treatment. Further studies investigated 2-ClFA modified protein localization over time during NET formation. Initially PAD4 and 2-ClFA-modified proteins were extranuclear but over time they both localized to distinct nuclear regions. Following DNA release from neutrophils, 2-ClFA-modified proteins were found throughout the neutrophil and DNA strands. In summary, multiple neutrophil proteins are modified by 2-ClHA, including PAD4. 2-ClHA modification and activation of PAD4 is suggested as a key component of 2-ClHA elicited NET formation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02435-1.

Introduction

Neutrophils are the first responders to infection [1]. Neutrophils combat bacteria and viruses by phagocytosis, degranulation, respiratory burst and release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [2, 3]. Neutrophil myeloperoxidase (MPO) converts hydrogen peroxide to hypochlorous acid (HOCl). HOCl is a potent two-electron oxidant that reacts with viral as well as host DNA, proteins, and lipids [4–7]. HOCl targets the plasmalogen phospholipid vinyl ether bond liberating the vinyl ether Masked aldehyde as 2-chlorofatty aldehyde (2-ClFALD) (Fig. 1) [8]. 2-ClFALD is subsequently oxidized to 2-chlorofatty acid (2-ClFA) [9, 10]. MPO is a mediator of NETosis and our previous studies suggest MPO’s role in NET formation (NETosis) may be through the production of 2-ClFA [11, 12].

The mechanism responsible for 2-ClFA eliciting NETosis is only partially known. The peptidyl arginine deiminase-4 (PAD4) inhibitor, Cl-amidine inhibits 2-ClFA elicited NETosis [12]. PAD4 citrullinates histones enabling chromatin unpacking and NET formation [13, 14]. Since the chlorinated carbon of 2-ClFA is electrophilic, an attractive hypothesis is that the electrophilic chlorinated carbon is targeted by protein nucleophilic amino acids leading to 2-ClFA protein modification that leads to NETosis (Fig. 1). To test this hypothesis neutrophil proteins modified by 2-ClFA were identified using the click chemistry analog of 2-ClFA (2-chlorohexadec-15-ynoic acid, 2-ClHyA) [15]. Several neutrophil 2-ClFA proteins were identified using shotgun proteomics that potentially have roles in 2-ClFA-elicited NETosis including PAD4, MMP-8 and α-defensin.

Methods

Materials

Click chemistry reagents were purchased from Click Chemistry Tools, (Scottsdale, AZ, USA). All other chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) or Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). The alkyne analog of 2‐ClHA (2‐ClHyA) was synthesized according to previous protocols [15]. Cell culture supplies were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), Cell Applications Inc. (San Diego, CA, United States), or Lonza (Basel, Switzerland). Click-It Cell Reaction Buffer Kit was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, United States; catalog no. C10269). Rabbit polyclonal anti-PAD4 antibody was purchased from Genetex (catalog no. GTX113945). Rabbit polyclonal anti-neutrophil defensin antibody was purchased from Antibodies.com (catalog no. ABIN7362851). Rabbit monoclonal anti-MPO antibody was purchased from Abcam (catalog no. ab208670). Secondary antibody for all conditions was goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (1:300).

Neutrophil preparation and treatments with click analogs

Human neutrophils were isolated from healthy human donors as previously described and approved under Saint Louis University IRB protocol 9952 [11]. The isolated neutrophils were suspended in HBSS to a final concentration of 2 × 106 cells/ml. Neutrophils in HBSS were then treated with either 10 µM 2-ClHyA, 10 µM hexadec-15-ynoic acid (HyA) or vehicle (DMSO only) in 5mL HBSS for 1 h at 37 °C.

Click reactions of 2-ClHyA-modified proteins with biotin-azide

After treatment, cells were centrifuged at 5,000 g for 8 min and cell pellets were lysed with 200 µl RIPA buffer containing 1X cOmplete EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail and 400 µM PMSF. Lysates were then processed as described previously [16]. In brief, cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with Pierce high‐capacity streptavidin‐agarose beads, then supernatant protein concentrations were assessed using the Pierce BCA protein assay kit. Supernatant proteins (500 µg in 470 µl) were then diluted with 280 µl PBS. For the click reaction, 100 µM azide‐biotin, 90 µl water, 40 µl 150 µM THPTA, and 60 µl of a 1:5 Mixture of 150 µM THPTA: 30 mM Cu(II)SO4 were added sequentially, with vortexing between. Then 50 µl of 400 mM sodium ascorbate was added to start the click reaction and samples were mixed at room temperature for 30 min. Zeba 7 K MW cutoff desalting columns (10 ml, Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. 89893) were then used to remove excess click reagents. Next, NP‐40, SDS and PMSF were added to samples at final concentrations of 1%, 0.1%, and 400 µM, respectively. cOmplete Mini protease inhibitor cocktail was also added to each sample at a final concentration 1X to prevent protein degradation and precipitation.

Immunoprecipitation of biotin-azide clicked proteins with streptavidin-agarose beads

Samples were incubated with streptavidin-agarose beads as described previously [16]. In brief, the proteins clicked to biotin-azide were centrifuged and the supernatant was diluted to 2500 µl. A total of ~ 175 µg of proteins were rotated overnight with 30 µl of high-capacity streptavidin‐agarose beads at 4 °C. The bead solutions were then washed with 0.15% NP‐40 (4x on ice), 6 M urea in 50 mM Tris pH 8.0 (4x, 15 min, with rotation at 4 °C), 2 mM CaCl2 in 50 mM Tris pH 8.0 (4x, 15 min on ice). The proteins bound to streptavidin-agarose beads were subsequently used for shotgun proteomics.

LC-MS/MS analysis of proteins bound to streptavidin-agarose beads

The proteins clicked to biotin-azide and bound to streptavidin-agarose beads, as well as additional control samples consisting of beads only, were processed as follows. In brief, proteins were reduced using 500 mM DTT for 30 min and then alkylated with 1 M iodoacetamide (IAA) for 30 min in the dark. 500 mM DTT was then added again to quench excess IAA for 5 min at room temperature. The proteins were then digested while still attached to the beads by incubating with 500 ng trypsin, 20 h, 1400 rpm, 37 °C. The tryptic peptide samples were then cleaned up using C18 tips (Thermo Fisher Scientific). In brief, tryptic digest was mixed 1:4 with a solution of 2% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) in 20% acetonitrile (ACN). Tips were wetted with 50% methanol, then equilibrated with a solution of 0.5% TFA in 5% ACN. Peptides were eluted from tips with 0.1% formic acid in 70% acetonitrile, then dried and resuspended in 40ul 0.1% formic acid in 2% ACN, 98% water (all reagents HPLC-grade). Peptide samples were then analyzed using a Thermo Q Exactive orbitrap MS/MS equipped with a nanospray emitter (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Nanospray parameters in positive ion mode were as follows: spray voltage, 2.0 kV; capillary temperature, 320 °C; S‐lens RF level, 55.

A Dionex UltiMate 3000 RSLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to perform Liquid chromatography. The mobile phase solution A consisted of 0.1% formic acid (FA) in water, and mobile phase B was 0.1% FA, 80% acetonitrile in water. Loading solution was 0.1% FA, 2% acetonitrile in water. All solvents used were HPLC grade. The samples were then separated by reversed-phase chromatography on an Acclaim PepMap C18 HPLC column, 100 Å pore size, 15 cm × 75 μm, 2 μm particles (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 35 °C and a flow rate of 300 nl/min. Samples were injected onto the column at 99% mobile phase A, held for 3 min, then underwent a gradient from 1% to 65% mobile phase B (linear) over 90 min. Hydrophobic peptides were then eluted for 1 min with 90% B, and held at 90% B for 4 Min. The column was then re-equilibrated using 99% mobile phase A for 22 min. The top 10 peptides were selected in a data-dependent mode for MS/MS fragmentation by collision-induced dissociation. The automatic gain control (AGC) target was set to 3 × 106 with resolution 70,000 and maximum injection time 100 ms. For MS2 analysis, the AGC target mode was set to 5 × 104 with a Maximum injection time of 100 ms and a resolution of 17,500. The isolation window was set to 1.8 m/z. Collision energy of −28 eV was used to fragment ions. Analysis was performed with a dynamic exclusion of 40 s and charge exclusion of unassigned, 1, 6–8, and > 8.

Proteomic analysis of peptides of captured proteins

The MS/MS spectra were analyzed in Proteome Discoverer 2.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), using both Sequest HT and MSPepSearch nodes. The Swiss-Prot database for Homo sapiens (version: 2017‐10‐25, accessed 07‐07‐2020) was the protein database used to search Sequest HT. The spectral libraries used to search MSPepsearch were ProteomeTools _HCD30_PD version 1.0 (accessed 10/16/2018) and NIST_Human_Orbitrap_HCD_20160923 version 1.0 (accessed 09/07/2018). All programs were accessed from Saint Louis, MO, USA. The precursor mass tolerance was set to 10 ppm and fragment mass tolerance was set to 0.02 Da for both MSPepSearch and Sequest HT. Other parameters for the Sequest HT node include enzyme name: trypsin, max missed cleavages: 2, maximum peptide length of 150 and minimum peptide length of 6. The dynamic modifications allowed were oxidation, N‐terminal acetyl, N‐terminal Met‐loss, and N‐terminal Met‐loss + acetyl. The static modification allowed was carbamidomethyl. The percolator node was used for statistical analysis, with the false discovery rate (FDR) calculated using a decoy database search, and only high confidence peptide identifications with FDR < 0.01 were included.

Bioinformatic analysis

Only master proteins (proteins with at least one unique peptide) with more than five peptide spectral matches (PSMs) and more than one peptide per protein were used in these analyses. Protein Analysis Through Evolutionary Relations (PANTHER) was used to identify the protein classes of the 2-ClHyA proteins [17, 18]. The Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) analysis tool (version 12.0) was also used to analyze the list of protein IDs, with homo sapiens as the organism and confidence set to medium (0.400).

Detection of 2-ClHyA in human neutrophils by immunofluorescence

Isolated human neutrophils at 1 × 106 cells/ml in HBSS, final volume 2 ml, were adhered to fibrinogen-treated coverslips in wells of 6-well plate for 30 min at 37 °C. Neutrophils were then treated with either 10 μm ClHyA, HyA or DMSO (vehicle) for indicated amounts of time. Solution was removed from well and cells were fixed with 10% formalin for 15 min at room temperature. To permeabilize, 1 ml of chilled 1:1 methanol: acetone was added to each well, then the plate was placed at −20 °C for 5 min. Cells were then washed 3x with 2% BSA (in PBS). Click reaction was then performed using Click-it Cell Reaction Buffer Kit (Thermo), according to manufacturer protocol. In brief, 440 µl 1X reaction buffer, 10 µl CuSO4, 50 µl cell buffer additive, and 0.5 µl TAMRA-azide were combined to make 500 µl of click reaction cocktail. Eighty microliter drops of the reaction cocktail were placed onto parafilm, coverslips were placed face-down onto the drops and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Next, coverslips were washed 3x with 2% BSA solution. The following steps were performed in the dark. Cells were blocked with 2% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, rocking. The primary antibody was then added (MPO (1:500), PAD4 (1:500), neutrophil defensin (1:300)). Primary antibodies were diluted in blocking solution and incubated with coverslips overnight in a humidified chamber at 4 °C. The next day, the coverslips were washed 3X with 2% BSA for 5 min each, then incubated with secondary antibody (donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (Cat. No. ab150107, Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom)) diluted 1:300 in blocking solution at room temperature in the dark for 1 h followed by three consecutive wash steps with 2% BSA. Finally, the coverslips were DAPI stained and mounted. Images were obtained using a Keyence BZ-X800 microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan) at 60x magnification, with Haze Reduction setting 1 applied to all images by the BZ-X800 software (Keyence). Colocalization analysis was performed using ImageJ/Fiji [19]. Background was subtracted (rolling ball radius 60), and then colocalization analysis was performed using Coloc2, with PSF 10 and Costes randomization 10.

SDS-PAGE and western blot

Proteins were eluted from streptavidin beads by addition of 60µL NuPAGE LDS buffer at 96 °C for 10 min. The supernatant containing the eluted proteins was then mixed with NuPAGE reducing agent and heated at 70 °C for 10 min. The proteins for the MMP-8 western blot were then separated on a 4–12% Bis-Tris polyacrylamide gel using MOPS buffer over 55 min at 200 V. Proteins were then transferred to Immobilon-P PVDF membranes (Millipore Sigma, Saint Louis, MO, USA). Membranes were then blocked with 5% BSA in TBS with 0.05% Tween-20 ® (TBS-T) at room temperature for 1 h, then incubated with the primary antibodies (MMP-8: R&D systems MAB908 1 µg/mL) in 2% BSA in TBS-T overnight. After washing in TBS-T, the membrane was incubated in secondary antibodies (Li-Cor 926−68070, 1:10,000) in 2% BSA at room temperature for 1 h (in the dark), washed again in TBS-T, and imaged using the Li-Cor Odyssey CLx Near-IR Scanner. Images were analyzed using ImageStudio software.

Extracellular DNA detection

Neutrophils were suspended in HBSS (106 cells/ml) and treated with 2-chlorofatty acids delivered in DMSO in the presence of 10 µM Sytox Green. Cells were then incubated at 37 °C in a 96-well black plate with clear bottom. Extracellular DNA was detected by fluorescence at 523 nm. The heterogeneous nature of NET formation throughout the well was minimized by averaging the fluorescent signal detected in 21 different spots using a SpectraMax i3 Multi-Mode Detection PlatForm (Molecular Devices). The average of 21 readings was used as a single value for n = 1. Triton X-100 (0.1%) was used to lyse the cells to measure total DNA (which represents 100% extracellular DNA release). The ratio to that value was used to calculate the percentage of extracellular DNA in response to treatments.

PAD4 activity assay

PAD4 activity was assayed using a kit from Cayman Chemical, (Ann Arbor, Mi, USA, Item No. 701320) following the kit protocol. 2-ClHA and HA were assayed at 10 µM final concentration in each well. Vehicle was DMSO. Fluorescence was read using an EnSpire 2300 multimode plate reader (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA) at an excitation wavelength of 360 nm and an emission wavelength of 450 nm. Results were analyzed in Prism, using one-way ANOVA.

MMP-8 activity assay

MMP-8 activity was determined using the Sensolyte 520 MMP-8 Assay Kit from AnaSpec (San Jose, CA, USA) according to manufacturer instructions. In brief, 10 ng of MMP-8 protein (AS-72008, AnaSpec) per well was activated according to kit instructions, loaded into a black 96-well plate, incubated with treatment in the dark at 37 °C, and activity was measured at 60 min. Fluorescence intensity measurements were made using a PerkinElmer EnSpire multimode plate reader (Excitation at 490 nm, Emission at 520 nm).

Results

Protein targets of 2-ClHyA in human neutrophils

The α-chlorinated carbon of 2-ClHA is electrophilic and can alkylate cysteine residues similar to that previously described for 2-bromohexadecanoic acid in HEK293 cells [20]. In these studies we compared proteins alkylated by 2-ClHA to those modified by hexadecanoic acid through thioesterification. Human neutrophils were treated with either 2-ClHyA, HyA, or vehicle (DMSO) for 1 h at 37 °C (n = 3 biological replicates). The click chemistry analog treated neutrophil protein lysates were subsequently clicked with biotin-azide, and biotinylated proteins were purified using streptavidin agarose beads. On-bead trypsin-generated peptides were then analyzed by LC-MS/MS. Peptides and their corresponding proteins were identified with Proteome Discoverer 2.4.1.15, using the Sequest HT and MSPepSearch databases. A total of 127 proteins were modified by 2-ClHyA (Fig. 2A). Among these 127 proteins, 117 proteins were also modified by HyA thioesterification. Proteins modified by 2-ClHyA were analyzed in the Panther database to determine their distribution in protein classes (Fig. 2B). The most targeted protein classes modified by 2-ClHyA were metabolite interconversion enzyme and protein binding activity modulator groups. Cytoskeletal and defense/immunity protein classes were also represented, which potentially may have roles in 2-ClFA elicited NETosis. A STRING diagram of the proteins modified by 2-ClHyA revealed complex relationships between proteins (Fig. 3). Proteins in the “Response to Stress” pathway are indicated by red nodes and “Neutrophil Degranulation” pathway are indicated by blue nodes in the STRING diagram. Two proteins in the response to stress pathway include peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4, indicated as PADI4 in the STRING diagram) and neutrophil defensin alpha 3 (DEFA3), which are key proteins in the NETosis pathway [14, 21]. The complete List of the human neutrophil proteins modified by 2-ClHyA alone is shown in Table S1. Proteins modified by both 2-ClHyA and HyA, and HyA only are listed in Tables S2 and S3, respectively.

2-ClHyA elicited NETosis

2-ClHA has previously been shown to elicit NETosis [12]. Data shown in Figs. 4A & B demonstrate the click chemistry analog, 2-ClHyA, causes NETosis with a similar concentration-dependence and time course to that elicited by 2-ClHA, supporting the use of 2-ClHyA to identify protein targets of this halogenated lipid. Images shown in Fig. 5 further demonstrate the network of DNA strands decorated with MPO which are present 120 min following 2-ClHyA. The nuclear decondensation, association of MPO with DNA following NETosis, and the delay in NETosis (observed at 120 min) all suggest 2-ClHyA elicits suicidal NETosis [22].

Temporal course of 2-ClHyA localization in human neutrophils during NETosis

The localization of 2-ClHyA in neutrophils was subsequently monitored over time by performing click reactions with TAMRA-azide and localization overlap with key mediators of NETosis was assessed. Initially, following a 30 s treatment, 2-ClHyA has a perinuclear localization that has some overlap with the distribution of neutrophil defensin alpha 3 (DEFA3), one of the proteins exclusively modified by 2-ClHyA (Fig. 6A). DEFA3 is stored in the azurophilic granules [23]; consistent with this, staining for 2-ClHyA modified proteins appears to be in granules also (Pearson’s R = 0.88). The colocalization of 2-ClHyA with DEFA3 continues as distribution of defensin becomes more diffuse throughout the nucleus following a 30 min treatment (Pearson’s R = 0.62). Finally, at 120 min post 2-ClHyA treatment, formation of NETs is observed with strands of DNA decorated with both 2-ClHyA and DEFA3 (Pearson’s R = 0.79). In contrast, the localization of DEFA3 over time in the absence of 2-ClHyA does not change and remains in the azurophilic granule (Fig. 6B). Additionally in the absence of 2-ClHyA there is no NETosis as shown by the maintenance of the trilobular characteristic nuclei of neutrophils.

PAD4 Localization with 2-ClHyA and Activation by 2-ClHA

PAD4 was also modified by 2-ClHyA (Table S2), and we previously demonstrated that the PAD4 inhibitor Cl-amidine blocks 2-ClHA-elicited NETosis [12]. Accordingly, we compared the distribution of 2-ClHyA and PAD4 over time. PAD4 had a perinuclear distribution (e.g., outside of the DNA staining DAPI region) prior to the progression of neutrophils toward NETosis (Fig. 7A, Pearson’s R = 0.91 for 2-ClHyA and PAD4 localization in the image taken following 30 s treatment) and remained perinuclear in neutrophils that were not forming NETs (in the absence of 2-ClHyA, Fig. 7B). Treatment of neutrophils with 2-ClHyA for 30 min leads to the progression of changes leading to NETosis with PAD4 localized in the nuclei (Fig. 7A, Pearson’s R = 0.68 for 2-ClHyA and PAD4 localization). Over-exposure of the fluorescence signal under these conditions (Fig. 7A) also reveals 2-ClHyA in the nuclei, with colocalization with PAD4 (Pearson’s R = 0.75 for 2-ClHyA and PAD4 localization).

MMP-8 modification and activation by 2-ClHyA and 2-ClHA

To further support the significance of proteins modified by 2-ClFA in our proteomics analysis we performed a Western Blot for one of the identified proteins, MMP-8 following its pulldown with strepavidin-biotin. As determined in the proteomics study, MMP-8 is modified by both 2-ClHyA as well as HyA (Fig. 9A). Furthermore both of these lipids led to increased MMP-8 activity (Fig. 9B). Discussion

2-ClFALD was first discovered as a product of MPO-generated HOCl targeting the vinyl ether bond of plasmalogens [24]. 2-ClFALD accumulates in activated neutrophils, infarcted rodent myocardium and human atherosclerotic tissue [8, 25, 26]. 2-ClFALD is oxidized to 2-ClFA, which is also produced during neutrophil activation [9, 11]. Several key studies indicate 2-ClFA may be an important biomolecule produced in vivo. Plasma levels of 2-ClFA are increased in rodents exposed to chlorine gas [27]. Additionally, plasma 2-ClFA levels increase in both rodent and human sepsis [28, 29] and in the latter associates with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)-caused 30-day mortality [28] suggesting a role for 2-ClFA in the pathogenesis of sepsis. This concept is supported by studies showing 2-ClFA elicits microvascular endothelial cell activation resulting in Weibel-Palade body mobilization, P-selectin surface expression and von Willebrand factor release [15, 28]. Intravital microscopy studies have also shown superfusion of 2-ClFA onto the mesenteric circulation leads to increased leukocyte rolling and adherence [30]. Furthermore, 2-ClFA causes neutrophils to produce extracellular traps [12]. Collectively, the activation of endothelium and activation of NETosis by 2-ClFA could have profound effects on the integrity of the microvasculature during sepsis. The role of NET formation in the microcirculation during sepsis is a double edged sword. While NETs potentially aid in killing bacteria, they may also provide a matrix for platelet trapping and microvascular plugging [31].

2-ClFA-caused NETosis is inhibited by the PAD4 inhibitor, Cl-amidine [12]. Since the α-chlorinated carbon of 2-ClFA is electrophilic, we investigated the possible role of 2-ClFA modification of neutrophil proteins in NETosis and found PAD4 is one of the proteins modified. PAD4 is also modified by HA. It is possible that alkylation of cysteine residues by 2-ClHA may share targeting by thioesterification by HA. Alternatively there may be different cysteine residues that are modified by alkylation compared to those modified by protein palmitoylation (HA modification). To date PAD4 has not been reported to be palmitoylated, but the related skin protein PAD3 has been shown to be modified by palmitate [32]. Our previous studies showed only 2-ClHA, but not HA, elicits NETosis [12] and similarly in the present studies only 2-ClHA activates PAD4. PAD4 has a nuclear localization signal, which facilitates binding to the nuclear transport factor importin-a3 [33]. Movement of PAD4 into the nucleus promotes histone citrullination and subsequent chromatin de-condensation [34]. However, the mechanism responsible for PAD4 localization to histones is not known. Since both PAD4 and 2-ClHyA are similarly dispersed in the nucleus (Fig. 7), we speculate that the modification by 2-ClFA may have a role in the localization process. PAD4 converts peptide arginine residues to citrulline through a tunnel structure with active site residues of D473, D350, H471, and C645 [35]. Conversion of the positively charged arginine to a neutral citrulline has profound effects on histone structure leading to de-condensation of histones. It appears that 2-ClHyA does not modify the active site cysteine of PAD4 since 2-ClHA increased PAD4 activity. The active site cysteine is however modified by Cl-amidine resulting in PAD4 inhibition [35]. We previously showed Cl-amidine inhibits 2-ClHA elicited NETosis [12], which also suggests 2-ClFA does not modify C645. These previous studies, in conjunction with the results herein suggest 2-ClHA modifies PAD4 potentially increasing PAD4 localization to the histones, and 2-ClHA modification does not occur at the active site but does have a role in enhancing PAD4 activity.

Neutrophil defensin alpha 3 (DEFA3) is one of several cationic proteins and peptides stored in the azurophilic granules of neutrophils that are released extracellularly during NETosis and bind to the anionic strands of DNA which are the scaffold for NETs. 2-ClHyA initially localized with DEFA3 in azurophilic granules prior to NET formation. When NETs formed, both DEFA3 and 2-ClHyA modified proteins were observed associated with NETs. DEFA3 belongs to a family of defensins with a conserved cysteine motif and possesses antimicrobial and antiviral properties [36, 37]. We speculate that one or more of the three cysteine residues of DEFA3 are modified by 2-ClHyA to mediate NETosis. Neutrophil defensin may be relevant to 2-ClHA’s effects in sepsis, as defensin levels were significantly higher in septic patient plasma compared to healthy controls [38]. Additionally, increased copy numbers of the encoding gene for neutrophil defensin associate with severe sepsis in human sepsis patients [39]. Studies using a genetically modified mouse model found that this effect is due to neutrophil defensin inducing endothelial barrier dysfunction, and that blocking defensin’s functional interactions ameliorated mortality in septic mice (with high copy numbers of the gene) [40]. Further studies to assess the impact of 2-ClFA modification of DEFA3 on antimicrobial and antiviral activity are of interest and may lead to the development of treatments to reduce the microvascular dysfunction resulting from NETs observed in sepsis.

Our study has also shown MMP-8 is both modified by 2-ClFA and activated by 2-ClFA. It should be noted that MMP-8 is a NET-associated protein [41, 42]. In sepsis, MMP-8 contributes to tissue damage and sepsis severity [43, 44]. The role of MMP-8 in sepsis coupled with the levels of 2-ClFA observed during sepsis and MMP-8 modification and activation by 2-ClFA support the need to further investigate the MMP-8/2-ClFA/NETosis relationships as a potential target for sepsis treatments. Several additional proteins that were shown to be modified by 2-ClFA should be further evaluated in future studies including NCF4, SEPTIN6 and CTSS. NCF4 is a neutrophil cytosolic factor that is a component of the NADPH oxidase system [45, 46]. SEPTIN6 is a cytoskeletal protein and along with the GTPase enrichment group observed in our bioinformatics analysis suggests it may have a role in vesicle and granule mobilization. CTSS is cathepsin S, which is an elastase. Although cathepsin S has not been detected in the neutrophil extracellular trap proteome [47], it is released from neutrophils and has been implicated as a mediator of acute respiratory distress syndrome [48].

NETs were originally described as a mechanism that neutrophils deploy to kill bacteria [49]. The role of NETs has evolved over the past two decades, and it is recognized that they have a much greater role in disease progression and pathophysiology including conditions of sterile inflammation [50, 51]. During sepsis, neutrophil activation occurs resulting in both 2-ClFA accumulation and NET formation. NET formation during sepsis is detrimental leading to endothelial injury, inflammation and coagulation [52, 53]. Since DNase treatment reverses NET-elicited injury during sepsis [54], understanding mechanisms responsible for NET-elicited injury represents an attractive target for therapies. Upstream from NET elicited injury it is feasible that mechanisms leading to NETosis could be targeted. The present study provides additional mechanistic insights into the complexity of neutrophil activation leading to NET formation by identifying neutrophil proteins that are modified by 2-ClFA, a lipid that is produced from plasmalogen oxidation by HOCl.
