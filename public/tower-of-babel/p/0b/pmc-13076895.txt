NvashA function reveals temporal differences in neural subtype generation in cnidarians
Authors: Jamie A Havrilak, MingHe Cheng, Layla Al-Shaer, Whitney B Leach, Mia Yagodich, Dylan Faltine-Gonzalez, Michael J Layden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding how cnidarians pattern their nervous systems can provide insight into the ancestral mechanisms of neurogenesis that are shared with bilaterians, shedding light on the evolution of nervous systems. While previous studies revealed conserved mechanisms for neural induction, progenitor selection, and spatial patterning mechanisms between cnidarians and bilaterians, less is known about how distinct neuronal subtypes are specified over time in cnidarians. We utilized single-cell mRNA sequencing to profile NvashA-expressing cells across embryonic and planula-larva stages of Nematostella vectensis neurogenesis, and functional experiments to demonstrate that different neuronal fates are arising in a temporal pattern during embryonic and larval stages. These data demonstrate that there is a temporal component to neuronal patterning in Nematostella, which is consistent with mechanisms of neurogenesis observed in bilaterian model systems. These data further strengthen the conclusion that cnidarian nerve nets and complex bilaterian centralized nervous systems evolved from a common ancestor that utilized similar spatiotemporal patterning strategies to generate neuronal cell type diversity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41460-z.Keywords: Neurogenesis, Nematostella, NvashA, Neuronal patterning, Nerve-net, Neural developmentSubject terms: Developmental biology, Evolution, Neuroscience

Introduction

Cnidarian and bilaterian nervous systems evolved from the nerve net found in their last common ancestor1. This shared ancestry has sparked an interest in studying cnidarian neurobiology for comparative analyses with bilaterians and to provide insights into the evolution and function of nervous systems2–5. Cnidarians and bilaterians use a conserved set of genes to regulate neurogenesis and pattern their nervous systems. For example both groups utilize Notch signaling to select neural progenitors, regulate progenitor identity with sox and atonal family transcription factors, utilize achaete-scute homologs (ash) genes to regulate differentiation, have similar expression of terminal neuronal selector genes such as the pou class transcription factors, and utilize the same gene programs to spatially regulate neuronal subtype specification6–15. To date the majority of studies have focused on interrogating neuronal development at early embryonic stages. Here, we aim to gain insight into how neuronal subtypes emerge during embryonic and larval development, using the model cnidarian Nematostella vectensis.

Initial estimates from single-cell transcriptomic studies suggest that there are at least 35 neuronal cell-states in Nematostella, although the exact number of distinct differentiated neuronal types remains unknown16,17. Neurogenesis initiates during the blastula stage when coordinated Mek and Notch signaling begin specifying neuronal progenitors, which express the genes NvsoxC, NvsoxB(2), and Nvath-like3,18–22. The progenitors arise in a scattered pattern throughout the entire body18,19,22. At gastrula and early planula stages at least some neuronal subtypes arise within the domains of striped gene expression along the oral-aboral axis. The striped expression of the oral-aboral domain genes act as spatial cues that pattern different neuronal subtypes within the domains they specify2. Neurogenesis likely continues beyond the gastrula stage, based on the presence of proliferating neuronal progenitors19,22,23. However, neurogenesis beyond the gastrula stage has not been explored in detail.

In bilaterians, spatial and temporal cues work together to pattern neural subtypes24–27. Spatial cues confine subsets of neuronal progenitors and their progeny to defined regions. Temporal cues then act to pattern the order in which specific neuronal progenitors are born within each domain, the order in which different neuronal subtypes arise from a specific progenitor, or both28–30. This strategy enables a single domain to produce the many distinct neuronal subtypes needed to build the complex architecture of bilaterian nervous systems. While we and others have shown that spatial programs that pattern nervous systems predate the origin of bilaterians2,31–33, the evolutionary origins of temporal regulation of neurogenesis remain unresolved. This is, in part, because the molecular mechanisms that pattern the temporal axis vary across many of the current model systems, which makes it difficult to easily test if a core conserved program is present elsewhere in the phylogeny27,34. Thus, it is unclear whether mechanisms that temporally specify neuronal progenitors and neuronal fates emerged several times independently or whether they were likely present in their common ancestor and the molecular programs have diverged over time. Investigating whether temporal patterning mechanisms are present outside the bilaterian lineage can provide clues about the likelihood that temporal patterning was present in the stem bilaterian, or evolved independently in bilaterian lineages.

In previous work, we tracked neuronal subtypes labeled by the NvLWamide::mcherry reporter construct throughout larval stages35, but this analysis could not determine if the later detected neurons were born at the same time as earlier detected neurons but matured more slowly, or if they did in fact arise at later time points. Some cell types, such as cnidocytes, require days to mature22,36,37. Similarly, single-cell transcriptomic atlases have identified neuronal cell-states specific to early or late developmental stages, yet they have not determined when any of the later detected cell-states were born16,22. Thus, it remains unclear if temporal specification contributes to the formation of the Nematostella nerve net.

Because temporal lineage tracing of neuronal progenitors in Nematostella has not yet proven feasible, we used an alternative strategy to infer temporal patterning by examining NvashA expression and function across development. NvashA was chosen because it is expressed after the terminal division of many immature neurons16,22, and it persists through maturation, but is not maintained indefinitely in mature neurons3,23. Thus, examining NvashA-expressing cells captures naïve neurons after the terminal division from the progenitor throughout differentiation into a mature neuron. We reasoned that we could leverage single-cell atlases to identify later-born naïve NvashA-positive cell-states and the differentiated neurons that arise from them that are absent at earlier stages, which would provide evidence for a temporal component to nerve-net patterning in Nematostella. Our results show that NvashA-expressing cells are present in both early and late neuronal populations representing immature to mature neurons, and that there are unique NvashA+ cell-states that represent mature neurons at mid-planula larva stages. These findings suggest that neural subtype specification involves an as-yet unknown temporal patterning component and raises interesting questions about the evolutionary relationships of temporal patterning mechanisms. Considering that there is strong support that similar spatial cues pattern cnidarian and bilaterian nervous systems12, the temporal programs acting within those conserved domains offer interesting areas for further research about mechanisms that alter neuronal fates from common ancestral domains. Similarly, it seems unlikely that the molecular basis of temporal patterning is conserved, however, the regulatory programs have not been studied in detail in enough organisms to rule out a possible core program.

Results

Single-cell atlas of NvashA-expressing cells identifies embryonic and larval-born neuronal subtypes

One indicator of temporal patterning is a shift in the identities of neurons born over time. To determine if distinct neurons are generated at different times during development in Nematostella, single-cell mRNA sequencing was conducted on gastrula through late-planula stage embryos (~ 24 to 96 hours post fertilization (hpf)) (Supplemental Figs. 1 and 2), and then NvashA+ cells from all time points were subset and re-clustered (Fig. 1). NvashA was chosen because it is expressed following the cell’s terminal division (when immature neurons still express some neuronal progenitor markers)18 through maturation to distinct neuronal subtypes20,21,23. Thus, examining NvashA-expressing cells provides a window into neurons from a naïve to fully differentiated state. In total, the NvashA-expressing cells in our data represent 23 cell-state clusters representing neurons (fourteen clusters), cnidocytes (seven clusters), gland cells (one cluster), and early secretory cells (one cluster) (Fig. 1A–C). To determine the identity of each cluster, the top five genes whose expression was uniquely overrepresented in our clusters (hereafter referred to as differentially expressed genes or DEGs) (Supplemental Fig. 3), were mapped onto a published neuroglandular cell type single-cell atlas16(Supplemental Fig. 4A). Similarly, the top five DEGs from that same neuroglandular atlas38 and the top five genes that identify cnidocyte subtypes6 were mapped onto our NvashA atlas (Supplemental Figs. 4B and 5A, respectively, see Materials and Methods). These analyses largely confirmed that the same cell-state identities could be recovered from completely independent data sets (Supplemental Figs. 4 and 5). Second, we were able to assign identities to most of our clusters and have adopted the same naming conventions used in the previously published neuroglandular single-cell atlas16,22. The N1.L1, N1.L2, N1.L3, N1.g.early, N1.2, NGD.1, and N2.g1 neural cell-states are identifiable in our atlas (clusters 13, 11 + 19, 5, 18, 22, and 17, respectively), and a single NvashA cluster (cluster 14) mapped to several of the previously described N2 neural subtypes (N2.2-N2.4, henceforth grouped and called solely N2) (Fig. 1C; Supplemental Fig. 4). Cluster 8 is likely the SGD.early secretory cell type (Supplemental Fig. 4). Five remaining clusters could not be given a specific cell-state identity. Of those five, clusters 2, 6, 7, and 9 express progenitor markers (NvsoxB(2), NvsoxC, and/or Nvath-like), and 2 and 7 also express less of the differentiated neuronal markers (Nvelav1, NvTBA1-like7, and/or Nve429) (Fig. 1B; Supplemental Fig. 2A-F). Because previous studies failed to detect proliferative NvashA positive cells18,19, we infer that clusters 2 and 7 are likely early differentiating post-mitotic neurons since they are not definitive progenitors, and do not have a signature of a specific neuronal type yet.

Interestingly, the neuronal clusters fall into two large groups; group 1 contains six clusters that range from gastrula through late planula stages, and group 2 contains eight clusters composed of cells from the larval stages only (Fig. 1D; Supplemental Fig. 2G–J).

Group 1 contains clusters 6 and 9, which we infer are immature neurons as well as the previously identified neuronal subtypes N1.L1, N1.L2, and N1.L316 (Fig. 1C, D; Supplemental Fig. 4). We also looked at the locations of known neural markers and NvashA targets: NvLWamide-like, NvglraA-like, Nvserum amyloid A-like, and Nvabcc4-like (Supplemental Fig. 6). We found that NvLWamide-like is expressed in N1.L1-N1.L3, while Nvserum amyloid A-like and Nvabcc4-like are only expressed in N1.L2 (Supplemental Fig. 6). The N1.L1-N1.L3 clusters contain cells from all four developmental stages included in our atlas (Fig. 1; Supplemental Fig. 2), and interestingly the cells are arranged by the timepoint at which they were collected, with the oldest (late-planula stage) cells clustered distally to the younger and immature cells, potentially highlighting the fact that these cell types are born at gastrula stages but take multiple days to fully mature. This is consistent with our previous characterization of NvLWamide-like::mcherry expressing cells requiring multiple days to fully extend neurites35,39.

Group 2 contains cluster 2, which has a gene expression profile consistent with early differentiating neurons (Fig. 1B; Supplemental Fig. 3). Stemming out from the predicted immature cells present in clusters 7 and 0 are multiple clusters that have been previously identified as neuronal subtypes: N1.g.early, NGD.1, N1.2, N2.g1, and N216 (Fig. 1C; Supplemental Fig. 4). The arrangement of mature cell-states radiating out from a common pool of presumed immature, newly-differentiated neurons (clusters 2 and 7), and the finding that there is a late pool of immature cells which are distinct from those found in gastrula stage embryos (clusters 6 and 9) argues that some neuronal fates are generated exclusively at the larval stage.

To ensure the early and late neuronal clusters were not an artifact resulting from the inclusion of non-neuronal cells and to examine potential trajectory structure within the neural lineage, the cnidocyte, gland, and secretory cell populations were removed and the neuronal cells were re-clustered (Fig. 2A, Supplemental Fig. 7A). The same neuron.gastrula-planula (group1) and neuron.planula (group2) groups observed in Fig. 1D were recovered when only the neuronal cells were clustered (Fig. 2B), and the two groups had the same neuronal cell types and immature cell clusters grouping together as were previously identified in Fig. 1C (Supplemental Fig. 7A, B). However, without non-neuronal cells, more cell clusters were recovered, one of which was identified as S2.tll.3 (Fig. 2A; Supplemental Fig. 7). However, the other new clusters (Clusters 1–3. 5, 7, 9–11, and 17) could not be assigned a definitive identity because their DEGs overlapped with multiple known clusters or did not have DEGs from any known neuroglandular states in the published atlas (Fig. 2A; Supplemental Fig. 7). All of the ambiguous clusters are from earlier pseudotime positions (Fig. 2D), consistent with their being representative of less differentiated cell states, which is further supported by the observation that many of the new “more naïve” cell states share marker expression with the previously identified cells states that are adjacent to them and older in pseudotime (Compare Fig. 2A and D). Similarly, the new clusters seem to result from further resolution of the presumed “immature or early-differentiating” cell states in Fig. 1. For example, cluster 2 from the NvashA subset became two clusters (3 and 9), and cluster 9 became three separate clusters (10, 11, and 17) (Fig. 2A, B). Regardless, the same clusters representing both immature and mature neural cell states were recovered between both analyses, and their assignements to group 1 (early-born neuron.gast-pl) or group 2 (later-born neuron.pl) remained unchanged between analyses.

Putative neuronal specification trajectories revealed by pseudotime analyses

To further validate the presence of putatively early born and late born neuronal subtypes in our NvashA+ cells a pseudotime analysis with trajectory inferences were performed. Partitioning in Monocle 3 sorted the neural subset into 2 major partitions that corresponded precisely to the two transcriptionally different groups identified in Figs. 1 and 2A and B: a gastrula-planula lineage (group 1; Fig. 2C, red) and a planula-only lineage (group ; Fig. 2C, green). Three additional partitions contained very few cells, lacked connections to the principle graph, and exhibited undefined pseudotime values. The smaller partitions are composed of larval cells and we are unable to determine how or if they are related to the cells in the two major groups. Regardless, this analysis supports our previous conclusion that there are two main neuronal clusters representing cells that are born early in gastrula and larval stages. The inferred trajectories also suggest that the immature neural cell populations in each group give rise to multiple neuronal subtypes, with the “older” clusters representing distinct differentiated neural identities located distally to “younger” clusters of cells that could not be assigned an identity and thus represent a more naïve cell population (Fig. 2C, D). Importantly, the separation between the two groups and their subsequent trajectories is inferred by gene expression profiles recovered by Monocle and supports our initial conclusion that there is a temporal axis to neuronal fate specification.

Larval neurons spatially overlap with previously identified neuronal domains along the oral-aboral axis

To gain additional insights about the specification of neuronal subtypes, expression patterns of markers for the neuronal subtypes were assessed by whole-mount in situ hybridization (ISH). Marker genes whose expression was enriched in the specific later-born neuronal clusters but not in any other cell type were identified (see Materials and Methods; Supplemental Figs. 3 and 4). Markers for the putatively later-born neurons were detected in a scattered expression patterning in larval but not gastrula stage animals (Fig. 3A–F’). Expression was observed in both the ectoderm (Nv2.7753, Nv2.1205, Nv2.2620, Nv2.15362, and Nv2.11930) and the endoderm (Nv2.1999). Double fluorescent ISH confirmed that the expression of these unique cell-state markers is mostly non-overlapping (Fig. 3G–H’’).

We noticed that the spatial expression of markers for the larval-born neurons coincides with previously characterized early axial domains, specifically the aboral and trunk domains established at gastrula stages2. Nv2.7753, a marker for the N2 neural subtype, is expressed predominantly in the aboral domain (Fig. 3A’). Nv2.1205 and Nv2.2620 (markers for N1.2 and N1g.early neural subtypes, respectively) are expressed across both the aboral and trunk domains (Fig. 3B’, C’, G, G’, H’). In contrast, Nv2.15362, Nv2.11930, and Nv2.1999 (N1.L1, NGD.1, and N2.G1, respectively) are restricted to the trunk domain (Fig. 3D’–F’, H). The observation that larval-specific neuronal subtypes arise in spatial stripes that correspond to the aboral and trunk domains that arise in gastrula stages40–43, and the fact that these domains are known to organize the Nematostella nerve net10,12 suggests that temporal cues acting within the spatial domains patterns distinct neuronal subtypes from the same region at different times.

NvashA functions throughout the gastrula and planula larva stages

Prior studies only examined the functional role of NvashA at the gastrula stage21,23. To determine whether NvashA also functions at later stages, shRNA-mediated knockdown and NvashA knockout mutants were used to assess changes in the expression of neuronal subtype markers that overlap with NvashA+ cells (Fig. 4). Consistent with previous findings using an NvashA morpholino23, the known early NvashA target genes NvLWamide-like, Nvserum amyloid A-like, NvglrA3-like, and Nvabcc4-like had reduced expression following shRNA knockdown of NvashA in gastrula stage embryos (Fig. 4A–E’). However, by the mid-planula stage, these genes did not show reduced expression by ISH (Fig. 4F–J’) or quantitative PCR (qPCR) (Fig. 4K). Importantly, expression of genes that are upstream of NvashA activation21 were not affected by shRNA knockdown (Supplemental Fig. 8). Despite the sustained knockdown of NvashA observed by qPCR, a recovery, albeit slight, of NvashA ISH signal was observed in mid-planula stage embryos (Fig. 4K, and compare A–A’ to F–F’). To determine if the lack of a phenotype at the mid planula stage was due to the low levels of NvashA expression or reflected the true phenotype, we generated a NvashA null allele using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing. The NvashA coding sequence was replaced with a Nvubiquitin::egfp transgene (Supplemental Fig. 9A). To validate the null mutant, an ISH approach was used. Only 2% of offspring from wild-type crosses have no detectable NvashA expression throughout the gastrula and planula stages (Supplemental Fig. 9B). Progeny from crossing heterozygous NvashA+/Ubi::eGFP− mutants show no NvashA expression in approximately 25% of embryos from gastrula through planula stages, suggesting that homozygous NvashaUbi::eGFP−/Ubi::eGFP− mutant offspring are present at expected Mendelian ratios (Supplemental Fig. 9B). Next, the expression of known NvashA targets Nvabcc4-like and Nvserum amyloid A-like was assessed at gastrula and mid-planula stages. At the gastrula stage, the number of animals with low expression of Nvabcc4-like and Nvserum amyloid A-like was increased in offspring of heterozygous mutant animals compared to those from wild type crosses (Fig. 5A–A’, C–C’). These results align with our expectation that the number of animals with no or lower target gene expression would increase in progeny resulting from crosses of heterozygous mutants. It is interesting to note that the severity of the phenotype for the two markers is different, because these genes are both expressed in the same subtypes (Supplemental Fig. 6A). These findings are also consistent with the observation that target gene expression is decreased in gastrulae when NvashA is knocked down via shRNA (Fig. 4D–E’, K) or morpholino23.

At the mid planula stage, progeny from heterozygous mutant crosses also showed an increase in the percentage of animals that had reduced Nvserum amyloid A-like and Nvabcc4-like expression when compared to wild-type controls of the same stage (Fig. 5B–B’, D–D’). However, for Nvserum amyloid A-like this reduced expression phenotype was weaker than that observed at gastrula stages (Fig. 5A–B’’). One explanation for the weaker phenotype in older animals is that loss of NvashA results in a delay in development of NvashA dependent neurons. Because Nematostella development is not synchronous, and the range of stages present in similarly aged progeny expands over time, the reduced phenotype observed when comparing gastrula to planula stages would look weaker as older mutants would show a less severe phenotype as delayed expression recovered. Another possible explanation for the weaker phenotype in larval stages is that these markers are also expressed in non-NvashA dependent neurons. However, when Nvserum amyloid A-like expressing cells were subset and clustered across our gastrula through late-planula stage single-cell atlases, we found that a majority of the clusters express NvashA (Supplemental Fig. 10). We did observe a single NvashA negative cluster, which could either represent “older” cells that have since turned off their NvashA expression, or a NvashA-independent line of Nvserum amyloid A-like expressing neurons. Nevertheless, we did not see overwhelming evidence for populations of Nvserum amyloid A-like+ neurons that do not express NvashA during planula stages that could explain the expression of Nvserum amyloid A-like+ neurons in planulae. We also did not see any evidence for delays in transitions through developmental stages, which argues that the NvashA phenotype is likely the result of a delayed gene expression phenotype in NvashA+ developing neurons and not a general delay in development that effects the whole animal.

To test for the possibility of the delayed expression phenotype, we focused on the first 48 hours of development, when we were confident that shRNA knockdowns were strong by both ISH and qPCR. Animals injected with either shRNA to knockdown NvashA or a scrambled shRNA control44 were fixed every six to ten hours beginning at the gastrula stage (24 hpf), and expression of NvashA markers was tested (Fig. 6). The expression of Nvserum amyloid A-like and Nvabcc4-like in NvashA shRNA knockdown animals increases over time by ISH (Fig. 6A–H’) and qPCR (Fig. 6I, J), supporting the delayed expression phenotype. However, given the nature of shRNA-mediated gene knockdowns, we cannot rule out other explanations for the weaker phenotype in planulae compared to gastrulae.

To further test if NvashA also functions in regulating gene expression in later-born neural subtype fates, the expression of the putative late neural subtype marker genes Nv2.2620 and Nv2.1205 (representing N1.g.early and N1.2 cell-states, respectively) was tested in NvashA mutant larvae at the mid-planula stage (Fig. 7). For both Nv2.1205 and Nv2.2620, we observed an increase in the percentage of animals with no or low expression of the target genes in the progeny from heterozygous NvashA+/Ubiq::eGFP− mutant crosses compared to progeny from wild type crosses (Fig. 7A–B”). Similar to what was observed for the previously identified gastrula targets (Fig. 5), we did not observe a total loss of expression in 25% of the embryos, and we saw substantial variability in the decrease in expression of Nv2.2620 between two replicate experiments (Fig. 7A–A’’, note error bars). Regardless, reduced NvashA function results in a disruption of gene expression for neuronal subtype markers that represent both embryonic and larval-born neuronal subtypes. However, it is clear that the NvashA loss of function phenotype is not a total loss of neuronal subtypes, but rather a disruption to their development.

NvashA likely functions in the specification of cni.nem1 cnidocytes

Our analysis of NvashA+ cells from gastrula through planula stages revealed NvashA expression within cnidocyte clusters (Fig. 1). The identifiable NvashA+ cnidocyte cell-states were comprised of early differentiating subtypes derived from gastrula stage embryos (‘cni.gast’) and differentiating and mature cell-state clusters from the three planula stages (‘cni.nem1’, ‘cni.maturing’, and ‘cni.mature’) (Fig. 1A–C; Supplemental Figs. 2G–J and 5). Because the only cnidocyte subtype represented was the cni.nem1 nematocytes, and the remaining clusters are specifically maturing/mature cnidocyte clusters without a specific subtype identity, we suggest that NvashA may function to specially promote the development of the cni.nem1 cnidocyte subtype rather than act as a broad pro-cnidocyte gene during development22 (Supplemental Fig. 5).

Discussion

Temporal patterning contributes to neuronal diversification in Nematostella

Our investigation of neurons regulated by NvashA argues that different neuronal subtypes are generated at distinct times during development (Fig. 3). The generation of different neuronal fates at different times is the hallmark of temporal specification. Although the molecular cues are not yet known, we are confident that there is a temporal component to Nematostella nerve net patterning during development. The next question is, what is the mechanism? The limited proliferative potential of NvsoxB(2)+ progenitors argues that neuronal progenitors are being generated throughout development. Thus, we argue that it is likely that external cues present at the time a NvsoxB(2)+ progenitor is born would be the most likely source for temporal cues. However, recent studies suggested, but did not demonstrate, that neuronal stem cells expressing NvsoxC are upstream of the NvsoxB(2)+ progenitors with limited proliferative potential22. If true, it would argue that the NvsoxB(2)+ cells are analogous to ganglion mother cells19,45–47, and suggest that it is possible some temporal transcription factor code could be enacted in neuronal stem cells in Nematostella. Either way, an important first step in determining the mechanism is to establish the existence, or lack thereof, of the NvsoxC+ neuronal stem cells.

Implications for nervous system evolution

The common strategy to pattern bilaterian nervous systems is to first neuralize cells using a generic neurogenic program, then subdivide the neuronal tissue into spatial domains that will give rise to distinct neuronal subtypes. Then, within each domain, a temporal program allows for a single domain to give rise to more neuronal subtypes by adding time as an axis of patterning. Previous efforts showed that Nematostella and other cnidarians use conserved generic neurogenic programs to establish and maintain neuronal progenitor cells, and we demonstrated that spatial programs that pattern cnidarian nerve nets are conserved with those that pattern bilaterian brains2,26,31–33,48. The data here suggest that temporal patterning is also an ancient component of neuronal patterning. Within the bilaterian model systems the molecular mechanisms of temporal patterning are wildly different. Thus, it is unlikely to link a particular molecular program to the cnidarian-bilaterian ancestor. Regardless, the fact that temporal programs exist in Nematostella implies that a mechanism to alter neuronal fates from the same spatial domain over time is an ancient feature of nervous system patterning. Given that we and others have shown that the spatial domains that pattern neuronal fates are conserved across bilaterians and cnidarians, this study indicates that like traditional model systems temporal programs overlaid onto the spatial domains allow for an increase in neuronal diversity generated by a small number of spatial domains. We hypothesize that the divergence of temporal patterning mechanisms offer an exciting avenue to investigate the diversification of neuronal fates generated from the conserved spatial domains across animals. Thus, evolution of temporal programs might serve as a key factor in evolving neuronal identities within nervous systems.

Conclusion

Our single-cell transcriptomic analysis of NvashA-expressing cells during embryonic and larval development, coupled with functional studies, reveals that temporal patterning plays a key role in specifying neuronal subtype identity in Nematostella. These results suggest that Cnidaria use both spatial and temporal mechanisms to drive neural diversification. Our findings demonstrate that temporal patterning is an evolutionarily conserved strategy for generating neuronal diversity. Future work will aim to identify the upstream temporal cues and downstream effector molecules that together define specific neural trajectories throughout Nematostella neurogenesis.

Materials and methods

Animal care and microinjection

Nematostella were maintained in 11–13 parts per thousand Nematostella Medium (NM) (Tropic Marin Pro-Reef Sea Salt) with a pH of 8.1–8.2 and kept in the dark at 17 °C. Animals received weekly NM changes and were fed Artemia sp. four times per week. Spawning was induced as previously described49. Microinjections were performed on wild-type embryos at the single-cell stage as previously described49,50 and then kept at 24 °C until the desired developmental stage was reached50. Before fixation, embryos were cleaned and checked for normal morphology, and planula were relaxed with 7.14% MgCl2.

Colorimetric in situ hybridization

Embryos were fixed and stored as previously described49,51. Colorimetric in situ hybridization experiments were performed using previously published methods51. Probe primers used in this study can be found in Supplemental Table 1. All colorimetric in situ hybridizations that compare two conditions at the same stage (i.e. control shRNA vs. NvashA shRNA injected embryos) were developed for the same amount of time. Figures depicting an expression phenotype represent the wild type expression profile after observing 200–400 stage-matched individuals from two or more technical replicates. Population-level quantifications were performed by counting the number of embryos in each sample with wild-type, high, low, or no expression for each gene as viewed under a dissection microscope (Nikon SMZ1270). To minimize bias, quantifications were performed by a researcher blind to the identity of the treatment group. Lastly, embryos were placed in glycerol and mounted for DIC imaging using a Nikon NTi compound microscope with a Nikon DS-Ri2 color camera and Nikon Elements software. Gene-specific digoxigenin riboprobes were prepared as previously described. Probes were denatured (80 °C for 10 min), then snap cooled (ice 2 min), then brought to hybridization temperature (60 °C for 10 min) before they were added to the samples.

Double fluorescent in-situ hybridization

Samples were photobleached with 3% H2O2 in MeOH using a light box (1 h), rehydrated through 75%, 50%, and 25% MeOH in PTW (PBS + 0.1% Tween-20), and then washed in PTW (5 × 5 min). Samples were then treated with 60 µg/µL proteinase K (2 min), post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PTW (1 h), and washed again in PTW (5 × 5 min). Samples were then incubated in 50:50 PTW with pre-hybridization buffer (10 min, room temperature (RT)), then 100% pre-hybridization buffer (10 min, RT), followed by overnight incubation in hybridization buffer at 60 °C. Gene-specific digoxigenin and fluorescence riboprobes (Supplemental Table 1) were diluted to 1.0 ng/µL in hybridization buffer, denatured at 80 °C for 10 min, snap-cooled on ice for two minutes, then incubated at 60 °C for 10 min. Samples were hybridized at 60 °C for ~ 60 h. After probe recovery, samples were washed in hybridization buffer (10 min), twice in pre-hybridization buffer (20 min ea., 60 °C), then stepped into 2X SSC through 25%, 50%, and 75% 2X SCC in pre-hybridization buffer washes (30 min ea.). Next, samples were incubated in 100% 2X SSC (2 × 30 min ea., 60 °C), followed by 0.1X SSC (3 × 20 min ea., 60 °C). Samples were stepped into TNT through 25%, 50%, 75%, TNT in 0.1X SSC washes (5 min ea., RT). Samples were blocked in TNT + 5% sheep serum (Sigma, S3772) + 1% Roche blocking reagent (11096176001) for 2 h (RT, rocking) and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-digoxigenin-POD Fab fragments (Roche, 11207733910) in blocking buffer [1:500] on a rocker The next day, samples were washed in TNT (8 × 20 min ea., RT, rocking), the digoxigenin signal was developed using Cy3-TSA (AbexBio #K1051), and POD activity was quenched with three TNT/0.3% H2O2 washes in the dark without rocking. Samples were blocked again in TNT + 5% sheep serum + 1% Roche blocker (2 h, RT, rocking), then incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-fluorescein-POD Fab fragments (Roche, 11426346910) at [1:500] in blocking buffer. Samples were again washed in TNT (8 × 20 min ea., RT, rocking), the fluorescein signal was developed with HyperFluor 488 (AbexBio #4301), TNT washes (10 min ea.), followed by PTW washes (6 × 20 min, RT). Samples were washed in fresh PTW overnight (in the dark), incubated in Scale A2 at 4 °C, then mounted and imaged on a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope. The phenotypes were determined from imaging eleven (Nv2.1205 + Nv2.2620) or eight (Nv15362 + Nv2.2620) individual embryos over two technical replicates. Images were processed using Imaris (Bitplane) imaging software.

Generation and genotyping of NvashA null mutant

The NvashA mutant was generated using CRISPR/Cas9 following a previously published protocol for Nematostella52, except that homologous recombination was directed to occur within the NvashA locus. Two guide RNAs (gRNAs) were generated to target PAM sites in the NvashA locus (Supplemental Table 1), one in the 5’ UTR (-33 bp) and the other at the end of exon 1 after the conserved bHLH domain, (+ 395 bp) (Supplemental Fig. 9A). gRNAs were designed, cloned, and synthesized as previously described52. The donor vector contained a Nvubiquitin enhancer upstream of the enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) coding sequence, which together are flanked by 1Kb long homology arms (HA) that match the NvashA locus sequence up and downstream from the two PAM sites (Supplemental Fig. 9A, Supplemental Table 1). The donor vector was generated by modifying the vector from Ikmi et al.52 to replace the existing enhancer and homology arm sequences. Microinjection was used to introduce a mixture of both gRNAs, Cas9 enzyme, and donor vector plasmid into wild-type embryos. Mutants were confirmed by PCR genotyping using two primer sets that share the same forward primer (upstream of NvashA exon 1) but have different reverse primers that match the sequence in NvashA exon 1 or the Nvubiquitin enhancer (Supplemental Fig. 9C, Supplemental Table 1). In combination, the presence or absence of bands using these two primer sets can be used to distinguish between individuals with the following genotypes: NvashA+/+, NvashA+/Ubiq::eGFP−, and NvashAUbiq::eGFP−/Ubiq::eGFP− (Supplemental Fig. 9C).

Genomic DNA for genotyping was collected from either whole animals or tissue fragments. First, samples were treated with 200 µl of 100% EtOH for 5 min, then the EtOH was removed and residual EtOH was evaporated in a hood. Next 50 µl of 50mM NaOH was added to each sample followed by a 30 min incubation at 95 °C. Afterwards the samples were neutralized by adding 5µL of 1 M Tris, pH8.0.

shRNA preparation and delivery

shRNAs were designed and synthesized as described44, and stored at − 80 °C in single-use aliquots. To achieve a sufficient knockdown, a combination of two shRNAs targeting NvashA were combined (500 ng/µl of each) for a final concentration of 1000 ng/µl. A previously published scrambled sequence shRNA was used as the control for all shRNA injections44 and prepared to a total concentration of 1000 ng/µl. Similar volumes of shRNA were injected for the control and NvashA-knockdown groups. The primers used to synthesize the two shRNA sequences targeting NvashA in this study can be found in Supplemental Table 1.

Quantitative PCR

For qPCR experiments, pools of ~ 300–800 embryos were collected per sample, with similar numbers of embryos pooled per treatment group in each technical replicate. Total RNA was extracted from embryos at several developmental stages using phenol-chloroform phase separation and isopropanol precipitation as previously described21,53. 1 µg of total RNA per sample was used to generate cDNA using the qScript cDNA SuperMix kit (Quantabio). qPCR was performed on a Rotor-Gene Q and a master mix was made for each sample using the PerfeCTa SYBR Green FastMix ROX kit (Quantabio). Relative gene expression (fold change) was quantified using the comparative CT method54. qPCR primers used in this study were previously published23 or included in Supplemental Table 1.

Cell dissociation and FACS

Dissociation protocol was modified from Torres-Mendez et al.55, and all solutions were filter sterilized with 0.2 μm syringe filters (VWR) before use. Nematostella embryos at the desired developmental stages were collected in 15 mL falcon tubes. After removal of NM, embryos were acclimated into calcium and magnesium free Nematostella media (CMF/NM, pH ~ 8) for 5 min at RT, then washed in CMF/NM + EDTA for 10 min at RT. After removal of all but 0.5 ml of CMF/NM + EDTA, 4.5 mL of pre-warmed CMF/NM + EDTA + 0.25% Trypsin (pH 7.4–7.6) was added, for a total of 5 mL volume, and then samples were placed at 37 °C for enzymatic digestion. The tissue was gently pipetted up and down ~ 10x every 5–10 min with a p1000 pipette tip to help dissociate the tissue. Total time for enzymatic and mechanical dissociation was between 20 and 40 min, depending on the stage of the animals. After cells were fully dissociated, 5 mL (or equal vol.) of cold CMF/NM + 0.5% BSA (pH 7.8) was added to stop the digestion, and then the samples were kept on ice. Cells were then filtered through a 30 μm filter (CellTrics) and spun at 800 g for 10 min at 4 °C to pellet the cells. Cells were washed with cold CMF/NM + 0.5% BSA and pipetted gently up and down with a cut pipette tip, spun again at 800 g for 7 min at 4 °C, and resuspended in 2 mL cold CMF/NM + 0.5% BSA.

To assess cell viability and to allow for the acquisition of live cells and exclusion of dead cells, cells were stained with Hoechst 33,342 (2 drops/mL, incubated 15–30 min on ice in the dark) and Sytox green (1 µL/mL, incubated 20 min on ice in the dark). A subset of the cells was aliquoted into 1.5 mL tubes and stained with either Hoechst 33,342 as a live cell-labeled control, microwaved for 10 s (in an open tube) and stained with Sytox green as a dead cell-labeled control, or left unstained as a negative control for both stains.

Sorting of cells from early- mid-, and late-planula stage animals was performed on a Sony SH800S. The machine and collection chamber were pre-chilled to 4 °C prior to sorting. FACS was used to obtain 100,000 Hoechst positive/Sytox negative cells (while also setting gates to exclude doublets and debris). After sorting, cells were spun at 600 g for 5 min at 4 °C and resuspended to a targeted single cell suspension between 700 and 1200 cells/µl (1000 cells/µl optimal) in CMF/NM + 0.5% BSA. Cell concentration and viability were determined by visual inspection on a hemocytometer. Four, two, one, and two libraries were generated and sequenced for gastrula (24 hpf), early- (48 hpf), mid- (72 hpf), and late-planula (96 hpf) stages grown at 22 °C, respectively. Sequencing data from the same developmental stages were aggregated using the merge function during Seurat analysis. Data from gastrula-stage animals were previously published2 and reanalyzed for this study.

Single-cell RNA sequencing and analysis

The 10X genomics protocol was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the Chromium Next GEM single cell 3’ Reagents Kits v3.1 (Dual Index). To prepare the single-cell suspension for GEM generation and barcoding, a single-cell master mix was assembled for a targeted cell recovery of 10,000 cells and loaded onto a 10X Genomics Chromium Chip, then run on a 10X Chromium Controller. Post GEM-RT cleanup, cDNA amplification, and library construction were conducted according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Library quality was assessed on an Agilent Bioanalyzer. Illumina sequencing (NovaSeq 6000, single end reads) and processing of the raw data through Cell Ranger were performed by the Johns Hopkins Single Cell and Transcriptomics Core. Raw sequencing processed in Cell Ranger was aligned to the Nematostella vectensis genome56, and filtered count matrices were used for downstream analysis.

All downstream analyses were performed in R/RStudio. Low-quality cells were identified as cells containing > 10% mitochondrial counts, or cells expressing < 250 or > 10,000 genes, or cells containing < 500 or > 50,000 unique molecular identifiers, and those cells that failed to meet quality control parameters were excluded. Potential doublets were removed with DoubletFinder57. Filtered datasets were analyzed using standard Seurat pipelines58,59 and modifications of previous publications2,22. Clusters were determined and visualized with runUMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) and DimPlot functions in Seurat. Clusters were then annotated by plotting the expression levels of known cell type marker genes using Seurat DotPlot and FeaturePlot functions16,22(Supplemental Figs. 3–5; Fig. 1C). Markers used in this study for the annotation of clusters: Gland cell markers: Nvmucin and Nvnot-likeE; Cnidocytes: Nvcnido-fos1, Nvncol3; Neural Progenitor Cells: NvsoxC, NvsoxB(2), Nvath-like; Neurons: Nvelav1, NvTBA-like1, Nve490; Pharyngeal Ectoderm; NvfoxA; Mesendoderm; NvsnailA; Trunk/Aboral Ectoderm; Nvwnt2, NvSix3/616,22 (see Supplemental Table 1 for gene IDs).

To investigate only the cells that express NvashA, the subset function in Seurat was used on each developmental stage dataset to subset cells based on their expression level of NvashA. The NvashA subset datasets from each developmental timepoint were then combined using the merge function and reanalyzed with the standard Seurat pipelines. The NvashA+ neural dataset was generated by further sub-setting only the neural clusters and then reanalyzing.

Cluster-specific markers were computed using the FindAllMarkers function. Mapping the expression of the top 5 markers from each neural cluster back onto the NvashA dataset allowed for the identification of those clusters with differentially expressed genes, which we hypothesized could represent markers for those potential unique cell states (Supplemental Fig. 3A). Clusters 11, 13, 5, 7, 14, 18, 17, 22, 19, and 21 had specific expression of their top markers to their own cluster (Supplemental Fig. 3A) and restricted expression in neural cells (Supplemental Fig. 3B), identifying them as potentially representing neuronal subtypes. Clusters with markers that were not restricted to their respective cluster (0, 2, 6, and 9) were excluded from later neuronal subtype analyses. Sub-annotations of cnidocytes and neurons into particular subtypes with the NvashA+ subset was accomplished in two ways: (1) by mapping the identified cluster markers onto the Cole et al. neuroglandular single-cell atlas16(Supplemental Fig. 4A), and (2) by assessing whether our clusters were enriched for genes found to be to enriched in the previously identified neural and cnidocyte subtypes16,22(Supplemental Figs. 4B and 5A).

Trajectory inference and pseudotime

To reconstruct cell trajectories within the NvashA+ subset, the Seurat subset object was converted to a cell data object using SeuratWrappers: as.cell_data_set and used with Monocle 3. Cells were re-clustered and graph partitions were defined with cluster_cells function, and the trajectory (principle graph) was predicted independently within each partition using the learn_graph function. Roots for pseudotime were manually selected interactively with order_cells by clicking nodes corresponding to the most transcriptionally immature cell states within each partition.

Pseudotime was estimated by the pseudotime function with non-finite values set to NA to exclude those cells in disconnected partitions relative to the 2 major ones.
