Authorship asymmetry at the Women in Thoracic Surgery Inaugural Annual Conference: Gender pairings and senior status disparity
Authors: Nicole Lin, Mariya Samoylova, Rebekah Boyd, Busra Cangut, Rajika Jindani, Marianna Papageorge, Tanisha Rajah, Sara Sakowitz, Madigan Stanley, Kaitlin Bevers, Laura DiChiacchio, Nimrat Grewal, Ammara A Watkins, Stephanie Worrell, Mara Antonoff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Central Message

The WTS Inaugural Annual Conference successfully amplified women's voices, yet success at trainee level did not translate to the highest level of research seniority: Last authorship remained skewed.

Perspective

The senior authorship gap, which is a key metric for academic promotion, highlights the need for intentional mentorship and sponsorship by senior faculty of all genders to equitably elevate mid- and late-career surgeons.

Representation of women in academic cardiothoracic surgery has steadily increased over time, reflecting a changing landscape of the surgical workforce. However, this progress has not translated across all metrics of career advancement, recognition, and leadership roles. Despite the encouraging growth in women trainees and practicing surgeons, gender disparities continue at senior levels of academic leadership and scholarly work.1 For example, 1 analysis of US cardiothoracic surgery journals revealed that although women may constitute a growing percentage of first authors, their representation declines in the senior author position.2 The same findings were observed when analyzing cardiothoracic surgery randomized controlled trials.3 Gender gaps also extend to professional visibility, with women historically underrepresented among session moderators and invited research speakers compared with men.4 Although recent data show improvement, women remain markedly underrepresented, particularly in roles of seniority and leadership.

Given that academic promotion and leadership are heavily reliant upon recognized research productivity and professional involvement, the persistent lack of representation in the senior authorship position indicates systemic barriers that ultimately exclude the career advancement of women cardiothoracic surgeons. In alignment with the core mission and vision of the Women in Thoracic Surgery (WTS) organization, which is to provide networking, mentorship, sponsorship, and educational opportunities to optimize the advancement of women in cardiothoracic surgery, we aimed to characterize authorship patterns and scholarly output from the WTS Inaugural Annual Conference, which took place during 2024. Therefore, the primary aim was to characterize the gender distribution and concordance of first and senior authors at the 2024 WTS Inaugural Annual Conference. Secondary aims included comparing concordance rates across subspecialties and between WTS-member and nonmember submissions. We hypothesized that despite the WTS conference's mission to advance women in cardiothoracic surgery, gender disparities would persist in senior authorship positions, reflecting broader systemic barriers in academic advancement.

To our knowledge, authorship trends in women-focused surgical meetings have not been previously analyzed. By analyzing the composition of presenters, authors, and senior roles at this meeting, specifically designed to champion women in the specialty, we can identify current research collaborations and networks, as well as opportunities for intervention to further promote career growth among women trainees and junior faculty.

Methods

This study utilized a retrospective analysis of the submission database of all 98 abstracts that were submitted to the 2024 WTS Inaugural Annual Conference. Abstracts were categorized into 4 preestablished subspecialties (thoracic, cardiac, congenital, and workforce/other). For each submission, the presenting author of each submission was considered the first author, and the name listed in the last author block was considered the senior author. Therefore, in cases where multiple senior authors were listed, only the final author was analyzed. The gender of first and senior authors was inferred using the validated Genderize.io algorithmic tool. Genderize.io is an application programming interface that predicts the likely gender of a first name by analyzing large datasets of public profiles and social media records. It provides a probability score and a count for each query, allowing researchers to estimate gender distribution across large lists of names in an automated fashion. A 95% confidence threshold was utilized to minimize the risk of misclassification, ensuring that gender assignments were only made when the probability reached a level of high statistical certainty. An author's gender could not be determined if the name either did not exist within the Genderize.io database or failed to meet the 95% probability threshold. The names failing this score were designated as “Undetermined” to ensure objective inference.

Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were employed for summarization, and the Pearson χ2 test was used to assess differences in gender distribution between first and senior authors and variation in concordance across subspecialties. This study was exempt from institutional review board review and consent given that we analyzed publicly available data.

Results

Of the 98 submitted abstracts, 47 (48%) focused on thoracic surgery topics, 31 (32%) on adult cardiac surgery, 2 (2%) on congenital cardiac surgery, and 18 (18%) on workforce/other themes. Nearly half of submissions (48 of 98 [49%]) were from WTS members. Fifty-four abstracts (55%) were presented by resident or fellow trainees, 24 (24%) by medical students, 9 (9%) by early-career surgeons (0-7 years in practice), 2 (2%) by midcareer surgeons (8-15 years in practice), 2 (2%) by practicing surgeons (>15 years in practice), 1 (1%) advanced practice provider, and 6 (6%) unknown. Institutions from the United States accounted for 83 submissions (85%), whereas 15 (15%) originated internationally, representing 9 countries (Brazil, Canada, China, India, Mexico, Netherlands, Senegal, Turkey, and the United Kingdom).

Gender inference was possible for 97 of 98 first authors and all senior authors. Among first authors, 81 (83%) were identified as women and 16 (16%) as men. Among senior authors, 55 (56%) were men and 43 (44%) were women, representing a statistically significant difference in gender distribution (P = .015) (Figure 1). Among woman first authors (n = 81), 47 (58%) listed a man as senior author and 34 (42%) listed a woman senior author, whereas among men first authors (n = 16), 9 (56%) listed a woman as senior author and 7 (44%) listed a man senior author, which was not significantly different (P = .409) (Figure 2). Overall gender concordance between first and senior authors was 42% (41 out of 97). Half of the abstracts with senior women abstracts were thoracic (50% [21 out of 42]), followed by abstracts on workforce/education/miscellaneous (31% [13 out of 42]), and adult cardiac (19% [8 out of 42]). Abstracts with senior men abstracts were 49% thoracic (27 out of 55), 42% adult cardiac (23 out of 55), 5% workforce/education/miscellaneous (3 out of 55), and 4% congenital focused (2 out of 55). The overall median Scopus H-index for the senior authors was 22.0 (interquartile range [IQR], 15.0-44.0). The median Scopus H-index for women senior authors was 24.0 (IQR, 14.0-44.0), compared with 22.0 (IQR, 16.0-46.0) for men senior authors, which was not significantly different (P = .509).

When stratified by subspecialty, thoracic abstracts demonstrated the highest rate of gender concordance (22 out of 47 [47%]), followed by adult cardiac (6 out of 31 [19%]), congenital cardiac (0 out of 2 [0%]), and workforce/other (6 out of 18 [35%]) (P = .025) (Figure 3). Among WTS-member submissions, 26 out of 48 (54%) had woman senior authors, compared with 17 out of 50 (34%) among nonmembers (P = .046), indicating higher gender alignment within WTS-affiliated collaborations. Across all submissions, 58% (57 of 98) involved at least 1 inferred woman senior author or coauthor, reflecting meaningful but incomplete gender representation among senior contributors.

Discussion

The WTS Inaugural Annual Conference successfully achieved its goal of amplifying women voices in cardiothoracic surgery research, as demonstrated by the overwhelming proportion of abstracts (83%) featuring a woman as the presenting author. This finding confirms the WTS's effectiveness as a platform to provide opportunities for presentation and research engagement. Most abstracts were submitted by trainees and early-career surgeons, reflecting the strong engagement of emerging women scholars in cardiothoracic surgery. However, men remained the majority in senior author positions, resulting in limited gender concordance between first and senior authors. Thoracic submissions showed the greatest alignment between first and senior author gender, whereas cardiac and congenital submissions demonstrated wider gaps.

Despite success at the trainee level, our study reveals that the disparities persist at the highest level of research authority: the senior author position. Whereas the 44% women senior authorship observed is a significant achievement compared to the 11% national workforce, it is important to remember that these data are derived from a conference specifically designed to empower women.5 This gender gap in senior authorship within a meeting specifically dedicated to advancing women is a critical finding. These findings mirror broader trends reported in academic surgery, with previous investigators showing that women's authorship representation declines at higher authorship ranks and leadership roles despite overall increases in participation.6,7 Prior analyses of cardiothoracic surgery journals and randomized controlled trials have shown similar asymmetry, with women representing an increasing proportion of first authors but a persistently small fraction of senior authors.3 This pattern is consistent across multiple surgical subspecialties, suggesting that structural barriers—rather than specialty-specific factors—contribute to the gap in academic advancement.8 Continued progression of women in thoracic surgery requires more than a passive waiting period of workforce entry, it demands consistent sponsorship and continued structural changes in the present to ensure that the entry of women surgeons translates into sustained leadership and professional development.

The prevalence of male senior authors reflects a significant cohort of allies who are actively supporting and mentoring women, a partnership that is essential for the growth of women in thoracic surgery. However, distinguishing mentorship from sponsorship is critical in interpreting these results. Mentorship provides guidance, but sponsorship actively uses influence to elevate protégés into leadership positions and authorship prominence. The lack of formal, intentional sponsorship of mid- and late-career women faculty as well as competing professional and personal demands disproportionately limit the time available for sustained research productivity required for senior authorship. Because senior authorship is a key metric for academic promotion, this imbalance likely highlights the enduring challenge of translating participation into permanent leadership position for women surgeons.9 The predominance of male senior authors on women-led abstracts may therefore reflect both the limited pool of women in senior roles and insufficient sponsorship structures to ensure gender-equitable advancement. Implementing sponsorship initiatives through WTS and institutional programs could help ensure that early engagement translates into long-term academic leadership.

Targeted interventions are therefore necessary not only to create support systems for career development but also to intentionally elevate mid- and late-career women faculty into senior academic positions. The observed higher rate of gender concordance among WTS-member submissions suggests that professional affinity networks can positively influence authorship equity. Expanding inter-institutional collaborations within WTS may help foster senior mentorship opportunities and increase the visibility of mid-career women researchers.10 Future initiatives should include longitudinal analyses of subsequent WTS meetings, specifically tracking of authorship trends over time. In addition, comparison with broader professional meetings such as the Society of Thoracic Surgeons and the American Association for Thoracic Surgery can determine whether women-focused environments accelerate progress toward equity.11

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, because this analysis was restricted to the WTS Inaugural Annual Conference, a highly specialized event intentionally designed to foster women participation, the findings may be subject to selection bias and may not be fully generalizable to gender dynamics at larger, mainstream cardiothoracic surgery meetings. Second, the study relied exclusively on abstract authorship position as a proxy for research authority. This method does not capture other crucial metrics of advancement, such as grant funding, institutional leadership roles, or overall publication volume. In addition, due to the cross-sectional design of this retrospective study, findings identify an association rather than a causal link because we cannot definitively distinguish whether the observed gender disparity stems from a lack of available women mentors versus a preferential selection of men mentors. Future studies on funding, leadership roles, and publication data could further elucidate gaps in gender equity within academic cardiothoracic surgery. While the senior author designation typically implies primary project supervision, the abstract format does not require a formal disclosure of individual author contributions, making it impossible to definitively assess the precise level of mentorship from each listed author. Finally, the ability to draw longitudinal conclusions is constrained by the lack of follow-up data on the presenting authors, preventing a direct assessment of whether presentation opportunities like this translate into increased publications or permanent senior faculty status over time.

Although the WTS Inaugural Annual Conference served as a platform that enabled strong representation of women first authors, senior authorship remains skewed toward men. This gender disparity in senior authorship highlights ongoing gaps in academic advancement pathways. The results of this study provide a clear call to action, which should guide both WTS and the broader cardiothoracic surgical community to translate women trainee representation into sustained academic leadership. Future initiatives must evolve beyond traditional mentorship to incorporate intentional sponsorship, specifically through the prioritization of women for senior authorship roles and the active appointment of female faculty to influential leadership positions to ensure equitable career accession. In addition, further studies on identifying barriers could guide policy that provide tools and space for longitudinal success of women trainees in the later stages of their career. Our findings underscore the critical importance of intentional mentorship and sponsorship by senior faculty of all genders to support the equitable progression of women in academic cardiothoracic surgery. By combining mentorship, sponsorship, and institutional accountability, the field can convert opportunity into authorship and authorship into leadership. The WTS Inaugural Annual Conference represents an important milestone in visibility and inclusion. Continued progress will depend on sustained structural investment in women's academic development and recognition at every career stage.
