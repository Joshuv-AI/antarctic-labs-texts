Association between lactate dehydrogenase-to-albumin ratio and oxygen therapy requirement in hospitalized patients with COVID-19: A retrospective observational study
Authors: Hongsheng Deng, Kun Chen, Xinyong Liu, Jirun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although pulse oximetry (SpO2) is useful for real-time monitoring of oxygen saturation, it may not predict which patients will subsequently develop hypoxemia and require oxygen therapy. Laboratory biomarkers collected at admission may therefore facilitate earlier risk stratification and guide clinical decision-making. This study aimed to evaluate the association between the lactate dehydrogenase-to-albumin ratio (LAR) and oxygen therapy requirement in hospitalized patients with COVID-19. This retrospective study included hospitalized patients with COVID-19 admitted to the ICU of the Cancer Center of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, between February and March 2020. Patients were categorized into a non-oxygen therapy group (n = 40) and a group requiring oxygen therapy (n = 21) according to the requirement for supplemental oxygen therapy (SpO2 < 93%). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with oxygen therapy requirement. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to assess the predictive performance of the identified factors. Sixty-one hospitalized patients with COVID-19 (mean age 63.18 ± 11.01 years; 22 males [36.07%]) were included, among whom 21 required oxygen therapy. Multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that the lactate dehydrogenase-to-albumin ratio (LAR; OR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.00–4.21, P = .049) was independently associated with oxygen therapy requirement. ROC analysis demonstrated that LAR had an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.762 with 81.0% sensitivity and 72.5% specificity at an optimal cutoff value of 5.240. A combined model incorporating LAR, lymphocyte count, cough, d-dimer, bilateral lung involvement, chest tightness, and shortness of breath achieved an AUC of 0.818 with 76.2% sensitivity and 80.0% specificity. LAR was independently associated with oxygen therapy requirement in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 and may help clinicians identify patients at higher risk of requiring oxygen therapy, thereby supporting early triage and optimal allocation of medical resources.

1. Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) is caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2).[1] COVID-19 has caused a substantial global burden in terms of morbidity and mortality.[2] The disease mainly affects the respiratory system and causes symptoms like pyrexia, dry cough, dyspnea, headache, dizziness, general malaise, vomiting, and diarrhea.[1] The respiratory symptoms caused by COVID-19 can range from mild symptoms to severe hypoxemia with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), with a minimum of 9 days from the onset of symptoms to the development of ARDS, indicating that respiratory symptoms can progress rapidly.[3]

The blood oxygen saturation level is typically >95% in healthy individuals,[4] but patients with COVID-19 may develop decreased oxygen saturation as a manifestation of lung injury,[5] even in the absence of obvious respiratory symptoms.[6,7] This phenomenon has been described as “silent hypoxemia,” although its definition remains variable across studies.[8,9] Because early hypoxemia may be overlooked during the initial stages of the disease, rapid clinical deterioration may occur, leading to delayed intervention and increased mortality.[10]

Due to the lack of evident respiratory distress, hypoxemia may remain undetected and worsen the patient’s prognosis. The exact prevalence of hypoxemia without proportional dyspnea among patients with COVID-19 remains uncertain, with reported rates ranging from 3.0% to 56.5%.[11] Understanding the predictors and mechanisms underlying hypoxemia is important for the appropriate management and early identification of at-risk patients with COVID-19.[9]

Previous studies have also investigated factors associated with hypoxemia or the requirement for supplemental oxygen in patients with COVID-19, including older age, pulse oximetry findings, new-onset headache, preexisting cardiovascular disease, number of comorbidities, higher body surface area, higher body mass index, low lymphocyte (LYM) count, elevated C-reactive protein (CRP), and hospital admission within the first 8 days after symptom onset.[12-15] However, the factors associated with oxygen therapy requirement in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 remain incompletely understood.

Early identification of patients at risk of subsequent oxygen therapy requirement is essential for clinical triage and resource allocation. While pulse oximetry provides real-time assessment of current oxygen saturation, it cannot predict which patients will subsequently deteriorate and require supplemental oxygen. Furthermore, continuous 24-hour SpO2 monitoring is not always feasible in clinical practice, particularly during periods of high patient volume. Laboratory biomarkers measured at admission may therefore offer complementary predictive value for identifying patients who are likely to require oxygen therapy, enabling earlier and more targeted clinical decision-making.

Although the COVID-19 pandemic has largely subsided, the virus continues to circulate, and patients with COVID-19 requiring supplemental oxygen are still frequently encountered in clinical practice. In the present study, patients were categorized based on the requirement for oxygen therapy, which represents a clinically relevant outcome reflecting disease severity. We therefore aimed to evaluate the lactate dehydrogenase-to-albumin ratio (LAR) as a potential predictor of oxygen therapy requirement in hospitalized patients with COVID-19.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This retrospective observational study included hospitalized patients with COVID-19 from the Z7 Intensive Care Unit (ICU), Cancer Center, Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology between February and March 2020. Inclusion criteria: patients from Wuhan; with physical discomfort symptoms such as fever, cough, fatigue, etc; imaging findings suggest COVID-19; and RT-PCR or viral gene sequencing was positive for SARS-CoV-2. Exclusion criteria: patients requiring invasive mechanical ventilation at admission; with symptoms of shock; and with organ failure. The Ethics Committee of the affiliated Jinhua Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine approved this study [(2021) NO. 199]. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the committee because of the retrospective nature of the study.

2.2. Data collection

The investigators analyzed the patient’s medical records and recorded basic clinical data including sex, age, comorbidities including history of diabetes mellitus, history of heart disease and history of surgery, and symptoms including fever, cough and fatigue, imaging characteristics including bilateral lung involvement and chest tightness and shortness of breath, and whether or not the patient received oxygen therapy. Laboratory test biomarkers collected within 48 hours of admission were analyzed, including LYM (G/L), CRP (mg/L), white blood cell (WBC) (G/L), cardiac troponin I (CTNI, ng/L), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, U/L), creatinine (CR, μmol/L), albumin (ALB, g/L), creatine kinase (CK, U/L), d-dimer (DIMER, mg/L), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, U/L), and lactate dehydrogenase to albumin ratio (LAR). Two trained and experienced investigators double-checked the extracted data to ensure accuracy and completeness. COVID-19 was diagnosed according to the Guidance for Coronavirus Disease 2019: Prevention, Control, Diagnosis, and Management (Trial Sixth Edition).[16] Common COVID-19 is defined as the presence of fever, respiratory symptoms, and imaging evidence of pneumonia. Severe COVID-19 is defined when patients meet any of the following criteria: respiratory distress, a respiratory rate (RR) ≥ 30 breaths/min, resting-state oxygen saturation (SpO2) ≤ 0.93, or an oxygenation index (PaO2/FiO2) ≤ 300 mm Hg (1 mm Hg = 0.133 kPa). Critical COVID-19 is defined when patients meet any of the following criteria: respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation, shock, or organ failures necessitating ICU monitoring and treatment. When the patient’s peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) decreased to below 93%, supplemental oxygen therapy was administered according to clinical guidelines.[16] Based on the requirement for oxygen therapy, patients were categorized into a non-oxygen therapy group (n = 40) and a group requiring oxygen therapy (n = 21).

2.2.1. Outcome definition and terminology

In the original manuscript, the term “silent hypoxemia” was used to describe patients who required oxygen therapy. However, silent hypoxemia is generally defined as hypoxemia occurring in the absence of subjective dyspnea. Therefore, we have revised the terminology to “oxygen therapy requirement” to better reflect the grouping strategy used in our dataset.

2.3. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 24.0 (IBM, Armonk). Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SDs) and compared using Student t test; otherwise, they were presented as medians (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were expressed as n (%) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test. Optimal cutoff values were determined using Youden index of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with oxygen therapy requirement in hospitalized patients with COVID-19. Variables with P < .20 in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model (enter method) to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). CRP was additionally included in the multivariable model based on its recommendation as an early warning indicator in the Chinese Clinical Guidance for COVID-19 Diagnosis and Treatment (sixth edition). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC) were used to evaluate the predictive performance of the identified factors for oxygen therapy requirement. A 2-sided P value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

A total of 62 hospitalized patients with COVID-19 were initially enrolled, including 40 patients in the non-oxygen therapy group and 22 in the group requiring oxygen therapy. One patient in the group requiring oxygen therapy was excluded because positive pressure ventilation was administered using a noninvasive ventilator. Finally, 61 patients (mean age 63.18 ± 11.01 years; 22 males, 36.07%) were included in the analysis.

Compared with the non-oxygen therapy group (n = 40), the group requiring oxygen therapy (n = 21) showed a higher proportion of patients with chest tightness and shortness of breath (52.38% vs 22.50%, P = .018), lower lymphocyte count (1.24 ± 0.43 vs 1.45 ± 0.36 G/L, P = .045), higher CRP (8.65 [4.45–14.50] vs 3.14 [3.14–5.96] mg/L, P = .004), higher d-dimer (1.18 [0.51–1.55] vs 0.49 [0.32–0.93] mg/L, P = .007), higher LDH (225.00 [191.00–265.00] vs 184.50 [153.00–206.75] U/L, P = .002), and higher LAR (5.80 [5.46–7.58] vs 4.60 [3.76–5.67], P = .001). Age, sex, history of diabetes mellitus, history of heart disease, history of surgery, fever, cough, fatigue, bilateral lung involvement, WBC, CTNI, ALT, AST, CR, ALB, and CK were comparable between the 2 groups (Table 1).

4. Discussion

The present study suggests that LAR is independently associated with oxygen therapy requirements in hospitalized patients with COVID-19. Furthermore, a combined model incorporating laboratory biomarkers and clinical characteristics, including LAR, lymphocyte count, cough, d-dimer, bilateral lung involvement, chest tightness, and shortness of breath, may help identify patients at higher risk of requiring oxygen therapy.

4.1. Terminology and patient phenotype

The group requiring oxygen therapy was defined by the need for supplemental oxygen when peripheral oxygen saturation decreased below 93% during inpatient care (see Section 2). As shown in Table 1, 52.38% of patients in this group reported mild chest tightness and shortness of breath, in addition to other symptoms. This phenotype therefore does not correspond to strict “silent” hypoxemia (hypoxemia without perceived dyspnea). Recent work has highlighted variability in how silent hypoxemia is defined and operationalized across studies and clinical settings.[11,17] Our results should be interpreted as pertaining to hospitalized patients with COVID-19 who required oxygen therapy – including heterogeneous respiratory symptom profiles – rather than exclusively to those with asymptomatic hypoxemia.

The optimal LAR cutoff of approximately 5.2 identified in the present study is consistent with the value of 5.22 reported by Avcioglu et al[18] for predicting COVID-19 severity. The consistency of this threshold across studies with different outcomes suggests that a LAR of approximately 5.2 may represent a clinically meaningful inflection point in COVID-19 disease severity. The 34% rate of oxygen therapy requirement in our cohort is also consistent with rates reported in similar hospitalized populations.[19,20]

LAR integrates 2 important indicators, LDH and ALB, that can evaluate patients’ conditions from different perspectives and provide more clinical guidance information than individual indicators.[21] LDH and ALB are widely available and inexpensive routine biochemical tests, and their ratio is straightforward to calculate. Studies of acute respiratory distress syndrome in children found that LDH and ALB had limited prognostic value as individual indicators but showed improved accuracy when combined,[22-24] providing an early rationale for composite indices such as LAR.

LDH is released by hypoxic and damaged cells and is elevated in conditions of tissue injury, necrosis, and hypoxemia.[25,26] Elevated LDH in the early stages of COVID-19 may reflect subclinical tissue damage before clinical deterioration becomes apparent, which may explain its predictive value for subsequent oxygen therapy requirement.[27,28] Poggiali et al[28] reported that LDH is related to respiratory function (PaO2/FiO2) and predicts respiratory failure in patients with COVID-19, while Shi et al[27] demonstrated that elevated LDH was an independent risk factor for disease deterioration in patients with mild COVID-19. Previous studies have also demonstrated that LDH is an independent predictor of severity and mortality in other respiratory infections, including SARS and H1N1.[28-33] Han et al[34] further showed that LDH outperformed CRP and lymphocyte count in predicting COVID-19 severity, supporting the rationale for incorporating LDH into the LAR composite index.

ALB is a marker of nutritional status, inflammation, and vascular integrity.[35,36] In COVID-19, reduced ALB may reflect increased pulmonary vascular permeability and endothelial dysfunction rather than hepatic insufficiency alone, as intravascular albumin may be lost through capillary leakage.[37-39] As an anti-inflammatory and antioxidant protein, ALB protects against cytokine storm and oxidative stress-related lung injury; hypoalbuminemia may therefore impair these protective mechanisms and contribute to disease progression.[40] Violi et al[40] demonstrated that reduced ALB was associated with higher mortality in 319 patients with COVID-19, supporting its role as a clinically meaningful marker of disease severity. Together, the opposing directional changes of LDH and ALB in severe COVID-19 provide a mechanistic basis for the predictive value of their ratio.

In addition, several organ-specific biomarkers, including CRP and d-dimer, were significantly elevated in the group requiring oxygen therapy, whereas lymphocyte counts were lower, which is consistent with previous findings of multi-organ involvement in patients with COVID-19. CRP and lymphocyte count have been reported as important prognostic biomarkers of COVID-19 and are recommended as early warning indicators for critical illness in the Chinese Clinical Guidance for COVID-19 Diagnosis and Treatment (sixth edition).[16]

Furthermore, the present study showed that the combined model incorporating LAR, lymphocyte count, cough, d-dimer, bilateral lung involvement, chest tightness, and shortness of breath achieved an AUC of 0.818, which may compare favorably with other COVID-19 risk prediction tools reported in the literature. Importantly, all variables included in the model are routinely collected at hospital admission, making this approach clinically practical without requiring additional or specialized testing. Hence, integrating biochemical, hematological, and clinical characteristics may represent a useful and readily implementable approach for identifying hospitalized patients at risk of requiring oxygen therapy.

Previous studies have identified factors associated with hypoxemia or oxygen requirement in COVID-19; however, few have developed predictive models. Epidemiological and clinical studies have also characterized disease burden and care pathways across the severity spectrum. Shahsavarinia et al[41] conducted an umbrella review on the cardiac safety of hydroxychloroquine/chloroquine in COVID-19, highlighting the complexity of pharmacological management and the importance of accurate early triage to ensure appropriate treatment allocation. Bagi et al[42] demonstrated that even patients with mild COVID-19 discharged from the emergency department faced a 5.8% mortality rate during the first week of follow-up, emphasizing that risk stratification is important across all disease severities, not only in hospitalized patients. Shadvar et al[43] and Mahmoodpoor et al[44] addressed hemoperfusion and critical care management strategies for severe COVID-19, representing the escalation of care that early LAR-based identification may help anticipate and prepare for. Our LAR-based approach complements these studies by providing a simple, admission-based biomarker that may help identify patients at increased risk of requiring oxygen therapy before clinical deterioration occurs. Nevertheless, studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate and refine this model.

There are several strengths in this study. First, the study population was limited to patients with common and severe COVID-19, which may enhance the robustness and consistency of the findings compared with more heterogeneous patient populations. Second, all laboratory parameters were collected within 48 hours of admission and were derived from the first baseline measurements after hospitalization.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single center with a limited sample size (n = 61 overall, including 21 patients in the group requiring oxygen therapy), which may limit the generalizability of the findings and may have reduced statistical power. Consequently, the study may be statistically underpowered to detect more modest associations or to support broad generalizations across all COVID-19 populations. The present results should therefore be interpreted as exploratory, pilot data, generating hypotheses regarding the potential role of LAR in this cohort of inpatients with common and severe COVID-19. Second, this was a retrospective study, and patients were directly admitted from cabin hospitals or isolation facilities. Patients with SpO2 < 93% were managed with supplemental oxygen therapy after admission.

Third, due to the special circumstances in Wuhan during the early phase of the pandemic and the limited availability of monitoring equipment, continuous 24-hour SpO2 monitoring and arterial blood gas analyses were not consistently performed. Fourth, some inflammatory biomarkers, such as interleukins, were not routinely measured. In addition, other potential predictors did not reach statistical significance in the multivariable logistic regression model, which may be related to the relatively small sample size and may have reduced the statistical power to detect additional predictors.

Finally, external validation is warranted, particularly considering that the study period (February–March 2020) occurred before the introduction of vaccination, antiviral therapy, and the emergence of viral variants. Further studies are therefore required to confirm and refine these findings.

5. Conclusion

LAR at admission was independently associated with oxygen therapy requirements in hospitalized patients with COVID-19. A combined model incorporating LAR, lymphocyte count, cough, D-dimer, bilateral lung involvement, chest tightness, and shortness of breath may help identify patients at increased risk of requiring oxygen therapy and support early clinical decision-making and resource allocation.
