Comparison of Healthcare Resource Utilization and Disease Outcomes in Adults Hospitalized with Human Metapneumovirus and Respiratory Syncytial Virus
Authors: Emily Rayens, Lina S Sy, Lei Qian, Bradley K Ackerson, Jaejin An, Yi Luo, Xuan Huang, Jennifer H Ku, Punam P Modha, Radha M Bathala, Sudhir Venkatesan, Lisa Glasser, Daniel Molnar, Richard McNulty, Chengbin Wang, Hung Fu Tseng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman metapneumovirus (hMPV) and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) are among the leading causes of acute respiratory infection. We evaluated the risk of severe outcomes in adults hospitalized with hMPV compared to RSV at Kaiser Permanente Southern California.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort analysis in adults aged ≥18 years who tested positive for hMPV or RSV from 7 days prior to 2 days after the date of hospital admission between 2011 and 2024. Outcomes included length of hospital stay, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, occurrence of complications, readmission, and mortality. Risk ratios (RR) and hazard ratios (HR) were estimated for hMPV compared to RSV hospitalizations.ResultsThis study included 2859 hMPV-associated and 3029 RSV-associated hospitalizations. Approximately 30% of hMPV and RSV hospitalizations had an extended hospital stay (≥7 days) and about 15% were admitted to the ICU. The risk of an extended hospital stay was lower in hMPV than RSV hospitalizations (RR: 0.91 [95% CI: 0.84–0.99]). Over half of hMPV and RSV hospitalizations had pneumonia diagnoses; the risk was higher in hMPV hospitalizations (RR: 1.17 [1.12–1.23]). 1-year mortality risk was lower in hMPV compared to RSV hospitalizations (HR: 0.84 [0.74–0.94]). The risk of most outcomes, including the occurrence of complications, exacerbation of chronic diseases, in-hospital mortality, and readmission was comparable between hMPV and RSV hospitalizations.ConclusionsBoth hMPV and RSV hospitalizations were associated with substantial and similar healthcare resource utilization and morbidity, highlighting the importance of clinical appreciation of severe outcomes associated with hMPV in hospitalized adults.Keywords: human metapneumovirus, respiratory syncytial virus, healthcare resource utilization, hospitalization, adults

Human metapneumovirus (hMPV) and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) are among the leading causes of acute respiratory infection (ARI), with substantial impacts on young children, older adults, and those who are immunocompromised or who have chronic underlying conditions [1–4]. hMPV and RSV have similar clinical presentations, ranging from mild symptoms such as sore throat and cough, to severe and life-threatening pneumonia [5]. In addition, hMPV and RSV often exhibit seasonal patterns, with hMPV infections peaking in late winter or early spring in the Northern Hemisphere, while RSV infections peak in early winter [6–8].

Despite the similarities between these viruses, the disease burden and epidemiology of hMPV are poorly characterized, especially compared to the decades of data on community infection, outpatient visits, hospitalization, mortality, and healthcare utilization in different populations, healthcare settings, and countries documented with RSV infections [9–11]. This gap is due, in part, to the identification of hMPV in 2001, nearly 50 years after the identification of RSV. As testing for hMPV has been introduced into clinical practice, annual incidence of hMPV infection in the United States (US) has been reported between 2.2% and 10.5% among adults in the outpatient setting across four respiratory virus seasons [12]. During the same period, hMPV was associated with 8.5% of adult hospitalizations due to acute cardiopulmonary illness [12]. In a study of hospitalized patients with ARI in three hospitals in New York State during 2017–2019, hMPV was detected in 8.1% of adults with the hospitalization rate increasing by patient age [13]. Additionally, many markers of disease severity, including length of hospital stay, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and mortality, were comparable between hMPV- and RSV-associated hospitalizations.

The epidemiology of hMPV and RSV was substantially impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic. There was a decline in hMPV circulation with atypical peaks during the 2020–2021 season, followed by a resurgence and shift back to typical circulation patterns in the 2022–2023 season [14]. RSV incidence also decreased dramatically, although resurgent RSV epidemics were reported after the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, with out-of-season peaks, high volumes of cases, and heterogeneous resurgence times [14–17]. RSV seasonality is also returning to typical patterns, and emerging evidence suggests that hMPV and RSV cocirculation is expected, underscoring the need to better understand the epidemiology and outcomes of these infections [14].

While emerging evidence has revealed similarities between hMPV and RSV epidemiology and risk of severe outcomes, options for the prevention of hMPV are limited compared to RSV. There are currently no vaccines specifically licensed for hMPV, while vaccines and monoclonal antibodies are licensed for RSV prevention [18]. Most studies on hMPV and RSV have focused on children, older adults, and the immunocompromised population, which substantially limits understanding of the disease burden in the general adult population. Further, the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the epidemiology of both hMPV and RSV is not well understood. The purpose of this analysis was to evaluate the healthcare resource utilization and risk of disease outcomes in hospitalizations associated with hMPV compared to RSV in adults aged ≥18 years between 2011 and 2024.

METHODS

Study Setting

Kaiser Permanente Southern California (KPSC) is a large integrated healthcare system with about 4.9 million members. The demographic and socioeconomic characteristics of KPSC members are representative of the general Southern California population [19, 20]. Comprehensive patient health information, including demographics, diagnoses, laboratory results, vaccinations, and pharmacy records of KPSC members are stored in electronic health records (EHR). This study was approved by the KPSC Institutional Review Board and informed consent was waived.

Study Design

We conducted an observational retrospective cohort study among KPSC members aged 18 years and older. KPSC members who were tested for hMPV and RSV by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) respiratory pathogen panel between 7 days prior to and 2 days after the date of hospital admission (index date) between 1 July 2011 and 30 June 2024 with at least 1 year of continuous KPSC membership before the index date (allowing for a 31-day gap in membership) were eligible for inclusion in the analysis.

Study Exposures and Outcomes

The exposure of interest was hospitalization with hMPV, identified by positive RT-PCR, while those hospitalized with RSV were the comparator group. The outcomes of interest were: (1) hospitalization outcomes; (2) readmission to the hospital within 30 days of discharge; and (3) all-cause mortality within 30 days and 1 year of the index admission date. The hospitalization outcomes included length of hospital stay, extended hospital stay (≥7 days), use of respiratory support (receipt of invasive mechanical ventilation, noninvasive positive pressure ventilation, and/or high-flow nasal cannula oxygen), admission into the ICU, length of stay in the ICU, occurrence of complications (pneumonia, bacteremia/sepsis, septic shock, cardiac complications, neurovascular complications, and acute renal failure) based on diagnosis codes, exacerbation of chronic conditions (asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD], and bronchiectasis) based on diagnosis codes (Supplementary Table 1), and discharge disposition (in-hospital death, hospice, skilled nursing facility, home health service, home/self-care, and other).

Statistical Methods

Demographics and baseline comorbidities associated with hMPV and RSV hospitalizations were summarized using descriptive statistics. Absolute standardized difference (ASD) was calculated to assess the balance of covariates (Supplementary Methods, Supplementary Tables 2–4) between the two groups, with an ASD <0.1 considered a negligible difference [21]. Variables including age, sex, self-reported race/ethnicity, month of admission date of index hospitalization, year (seasonal; defined as 1 July–30 June) of admission date of index hospitalization, Charlson comorbidity score, and immunocompromised status were considered important covariates and were kept in adjusted models. Other potential confounders included in the adjusted models were determined by an ASD >0.1, except for the presence of “any coinfection” detected by respiratory pathogen panel testing which was added in post hoc analysis for descriptive purposes only. When model convergence issues were encountered, covariates were re-categorized or removed from the model.

Hospitalization outcomes including extended hospital stay (≥7 days), use of respiratory support, ICU admissions, occurrence of complications, exacerbation of chronic conditions, and in-hospital death associated with hMPV-hospitalizations were compared with RSV-hospitalizations. Risk ratios (RR), including both unadjusted and adjusted (aRR), with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated using Poisson regression models with robust error variance, without and with covariate adjustment. This model employed the generalized estimating equations approach to account for within-subject correlation (due to possible multiple hospitalizations from the same individual or coinfections for both hMPV and RSV) and to analyze common binary outcomes [22].

Analysis of 30-day readmission after discharge was conducted among patients who were alive at discharge. Individuals were followed from the discharge date to 30 days after the discharge date, readmission, death, or disenrollment, whichever came first. We calculated the readmission rate by dividing the number of hospitalizations with readmission by the total follow-up time. Unadjusted RR and aRR were estimated using robust Poisson regression to compare the readmission rates between hMPV hospitalizations and RSV hospitalizations, with follow-up time included as an offset.

For analysis of all-cause mortality within 30 days and 1 year of index admission date, individuals were followed from the admission date to the end of follow-up (30 days or 1 year, respectively), death, or disenrollment, whichever came first. We calculated the crude 30-day mortality rate and 1-year mortality rate by dividing the number of deaths by the total follow-up time. The cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality for patients hospitalized with hMPV and those hospitalized with RSV was estimated and plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Unadjusted and adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) with 95% CIs for all-cause mortality after hMPV and RSV hospitalizations were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression.

Hospitalization outcomes, readmission, and all-cause mortality were similarly analyzed in the same population stratified by age (18–59, ≥ 60 years), with additional stratification of the older age group (60–74, ≥ 75 years). Additionally, to assess the impact of COVID-19 on clinical detection and management of hMPV and RSV, these analyses were also conducted on the population stratified by preappearance and postappearance of COVID-19 (July 2011–June 2020 vs July 2020–June 2024).

RESULTS

Among 2 052 083 individuals with 3 935 819 hospitalizations documented between 1 July 2011 and 30 June 2024, 75,596 individuals (102 220 hospitalizations) were aged ≥18 years with ≥12 months of KPSC membership and were tested for hMPV and RSV. Among 2849 patients (2986 hospitalizations) and 3013 patients (3216 hospitalizations) with positive tests for hMPV and RSV, respectively, we included the first hMPV and RSV test-positive hospitalization per season per patient (2859 hMPV-associated and 3029 RSV-associated hospitalizations) in this analysis (Supplementary Figure 1; Table 1). Compared to RSV-associated hospitalizations, hMPV-associated hospitalizations involved adults who were younger (10.7% vs 8.2% 18–49 years), more likely to have received an RSV vaccine (1.0% vs 0.2%), less likely to have kidney disease (39.6% vs 44.6%), and less likely to be immunocompromised (19.1% vs 23.2%). hMPV-associated hospitalizations were documented later in the season (87.2% of cases occurring between January and June) compared to RSV (93.8% of cases occurring between October and March).

Hospitalization Outcomes

Approximately 30% of hMPV- and RSV-associated hospitalizations had a length of hospital stay ≥7 days, 25% utilized respiratory support, and 15% were admitted to the ICU (Table 2). The risk of an extended hospital stay (≥7 days) was lower in hMPV than in RSV hospitalizations (aRR: 0.91 [95% CI: 0.84–0.99]; Figure 1). There were no significant differences between hMPV and RSV hospitalizations in the use of respiratory support or ICU admission. Pneumonia was the most frequent complication, diagnosed in 66.1% and 55.9% of hMPV and RSV hospitalizations, respectively. The risk of pneumonia was higher in hMPV than in RSV hospitalizations (aRR: 1.17 [95% CI: 1.12–1.23]). There were no significant differences between hMPV and RSV hospitalizations in the risk of other complications or exacerbation of chronic conditions. Bacteremia/sepsis, cardiac complications, and acute renal failure were observed in 21.4%–38.3% of hospitalizations. The risk of in-hospital death was similar between the cohorts at 3.5% in hMPV-associated hospitalizations and 4.0% in RSV-associated hospitalizations (aRR: 1.00 [95% CI: 0.77–1.31]). For both hMPV- and RSV-associated hospitalizations, most individuals were discharged to home/self-care (50.1% vs 44.4%) or a home health service (32.2% vs 35.8%; Table 2).

The age-stratified analyses (18–59 years and ≥60 years) showed that cardiac and neurovascular complications were more frequently observed in older adults with either hMPV or RSV, as was the exacerbation of COPD (Supplementary Table 5). In younger hospitalized adults (18–59 years) who tested positive for hMPV or RSV, ICU admission was more frequently observed, as was sepsis/bacteremia, septic shock, and exacerbation of asthma (Supplementary Table 5). In individuals aged ≥60 years, hMPV hospitalizations were associated with an elevated risk of pneumonia (aRR: 1.15 [95% CI: 1.09–1.21]) and reduced risk of extended hospital stay (aRR: 0.90 [95% CI: 0.83–0.99]) and septic shock (aRR: 0.72 [95% CI: 0.53–0.98], Figure 2). Elevated risk of pneumonia associated with hMPV hospitalizations was also observed in the 18–59-year group (aRR: 1.29 [95% CI: 1.17–1.42], Figure 2). In secondary stratification of adults aged ≥60 years (60–74 and ≥75 years), the risk of hospitalization outcomes was similar compared to the combined older (≥60 years) cohort (Supplementary Table 6).

Hospital Readmission

Overall, the rate of readmission in hMPV-associated hospitalizations within 30 days of discharge was 12.45 (95% CI: 11.16–13.89) per 100 person-months, compared to 15.64 (95% CI: 14.20–17.22) per 100 person-months in RSV-associated hospitalizations (Supplementary Table 8), although the risk of readmission was not significantly different between groups (aRR: 0.86 [95% CI: 0.74–1.00]). Risk of 30-day readmission was not significantly different in the younger age group; however, in the group aged ≥75 years, risk of readmission was lower in hMPV-associated hospitalizations compared to RSV-associated hospitalizations (aRR: 0.77 [95% CI: 0.62–0.96]; Figure 4). Prior to the appearance of COVID-19, the risk of readmission was lower in hMPV-associated hospitalizations compared to RSV-associated hospitalizations (aRR: 0.81 [95% CI: 0.68–0.97]; Figure 4). Risk of readmission was not significantly different between hMPV- and RSV-associated hospitalizations after the appearance of COVID-19.

All-cause Mortality

Within 30 days of the index admission date, there were 184 deaths (mortality rate: 6.78 [95% CI: 5.87–7.83] per 100 person-months) among hMPV-associated hospitalizations and 232 deaths (mortality rate: 8.12 [95% CI: 7.14–9.24] per 100 person-months) among RSV-associated hospitalizations (Supplementary Table 9). All-cause mortality within 30 days of the index admission date was not significantly different between the two groups, with an aHR of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.74–1.12; Figure 5) and as demonstrated by the cumulative incidence curves (Supplementary Figure 2).

Within 1 year of the index date, the risk of mortality was lower in individuals with hMPV-associated hospitalizations than those with RSV-associated hospitalizations (aHR: 0.84 [95% CI: 0.74–0.94]; Figure 5). A lower risk of mortality associated with hMPV hospitalizations was also observed in adults aged ≥60 years (aHR: 0.85 [95% CI: 0.75–0.96]) and during the prepandemic period (aHR: 0.85 [95% CI: 0.75–0.98]). The cumulative incidence of 1-year all-cause mortality was lower (log-rank P = .0002) in individuals with hMPV-associated hospitalizations (19.5%) than in those with RSV-associated hospitalizations (23.6%; Supplementary Figure 3).

DISCUSSION

In this large cohort study of US adults aged ≥18 years hospitalized with hMPV or RSV, we observed that 30% of hMPV and RSV hospitalizations had an extended hospital stay and 15% were admitted to the ICU, suggesting substantial disease burden from both hMPV and RSV. While there were some differences in extended hospital stay, pneumonia, and 1-year mortality between hMPV and RSV hospitalizations, most hospital outcomes, readmission, and in-hospital mortality were comparable.

Our results are in line with previous comparisons of hMPV and RSV. In a study of adults aged ≥18 years hospitalized between 2017 and 2019 across three hospitals in New York State there were no observed differences in length of hospitalization (median: 4.8 vs 4.4 days), ICU admission (16.5% vs 16.1%), length of ICU stay (median: 4.4 vs 4.0 days), and in-hospital mortality rate (3.9% vs 5.2%) between individuals hospitalized for hMPV versus RSV, respectively [13]. In the current study, we observed a median length of hospitalization of 5 days for both hMPV and RSV. Additionally, our observed frequency of ICU admission (14.6% vs 16.2%) and in-hospital mortality (3.5% vs 4.0%) was similar to that previously reported [13]. In other hospitalization outcomes, our analysis supports previous findings of comparable risk of respiratory support [25] and other complications besides pneumonia [25–27].

Previous evaluations of chronic condition exacerbations associated with hMPV and RSV are limited, although a few have reported RSV as more prevalent when testing for viral pathogens in adults during COPD exacerbations or in children during asthma flares [28, 29]. We found the risk of chronic condition exacerbation to be comparable between groups, but this difference from previous literature may be explained by the construction of our research cohort and focus on hospitalized patients that tested positive for the virus as opposed to evaluation of patients tested during a flare.

While most outcomes were comparable, we observed some differences in the hMPV and RSV groups. Adults hospitalized with hMPV had a greater risk of a pneumonia diagnosis compared to those with RSV; elevated risk of pneumonia in hMPV patients has been previously documented in pediatric cohorts [30]. In the same prospective pediatric cohort study, length of hospitalization and frequency of respiratory support and ICU admission were similar between hMPV and RSV groups [30]. We further observed that hMPV-associated hospitalizations were at lower risk of extended hospital stay and 1-year mortality compared to RSV-hospitalizations. Additionally, adults aged ≥75 years hospitalized with hMPV had a lower 30-day readmission rate. Previous comparisons of mortality in hMPV- and RSV-associated infections have focused on in-hospital and 30-day mortality and have found those rates to be comparable between groups, as we did in this analysis [13, 26]. It is worth noting that the observed differences in extended hospital stay, readmission, and 1-year mortality were small and may have minimal clinical significance. We also observed that hMPV peaked later in the respiratory virus season compared to RSV; however, there was still notable cocirculation of both viruses between January and March [14, 31]. Of the 13 seasons included in this analysis, there were 5 that had a greater number of hMPV cases detected by respiratory pathogen panel testing in hospitalized adults; these seasons were not consecutive and occurred both prior to and following the COVID-19 pandemic. During the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic, the numbers of hospitalized hMPV and RSV cases were small, but by the 2022–2023 season, they have appeared to return to prepandemic levels. Hospitalization outcomes associated with hMPV and RSV were similar prior to and following the appearance of COVID-19.

This study has several strengths. First, KPSC's comprehensive EHR system enabled accurate capture of hMPV and RSV testing, course of hospitalization, mortality in the year following admission, and demographic and clinical covariates. Second, the size and diversity of our study cohort increased the generalizability of our findings. This study also had several limitations. First, misclassification of hMPV or RSV status was possible. However, RT-PCR diagnostic tests were highly sensitive and specific, and bias resulting from misclassification due to false positive or negative test results was expected to be minimal. Second, misclassification of hMPV or RSV status as negative was also possible if patients were admitted late in the course of infection when viral shedding was low [32]. Third, respiratory pathogen panel testing was generally reserved for critically ill inpatients in KPSC, and some hospitalized patients might have been tested using the respiratory pathogen panel only after testing negative for influenza and/or SARS-CoV-2. Therefore, it is possible the current study might not be representative of all hospitalized patients with hMPV or RSV. However, hMPV and RSV were tested concurrently on the respiratory pathogen panel, and tested populations should have been comparable. Fourth, coinfections could have impacted the severity of patient outcomes that were attributed to hMPV- and RSV-associated hospitalizations; however, coinfections did not frequently occur in adults hospitalized with hMPV or RSV.

In conclusion, hMPV- and RSV-associated hospitalizations were associated with substantial and similar healthcare resource utilization and morbidity. The risk of complications and in-hospital mortality in hMPV and RSV hospitalizations was comparable, highlighting both the importance of clinical appreciation of severe outcomes associated with hMPV in hospitalized patients and the need for preventive and therapeutic interventions for both viruses.
