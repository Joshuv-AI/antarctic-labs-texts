Seraph® 100 Microbind® utilization in immunocompromised toddler with adenovirus viremia in conjunction with continuous renal replacement therapy and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation
Authors: Guyu Li, Christian Hanna, Laura M. Dinnes, Wendy A. Allen-Rhoades, Mira A. Kohorst, Emily R. Levy, Charlotte S. Van Dorn, Ziyuan Zhang, James T. Gaensbauer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The Seraph® 100 Microbind® is an extracorporeal, sorbent hemoperfusion device intended to adsorb pathogens when integrated into extracorporeal circuits. We describe the use of Seraph 100 in an 18-month-old immunocompromised toddler with adenovirus viremia in conjunction with continuous renal replacement therapy and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation.

Introduction

The Seraph® 100 Microbind® (Seraph 100), (ExThera Medical Corporation, Martinez, CA), is an extracorporeal, sorbent hemoperfusion device intended to adsorb pathogens when integrated into extracorporeal circuits, such as extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) and hemodialysis [1], [2], [3]. In the United States, it received emergency use authorization from the Food and Drug Administration as an adjunctive treatment for COVID-19 complicated by severe pulmonary disease or life-threatening condition [4]. Heparan sulfate on the human cell surface are highly negatively charged, allowing indiscriminate attachment of intravascular viruses. This device incorporates immobilized polyethylene beads bound with heparin to replicate this electrostatic interactions [5]. Here, we describe the use of three sequential treatments to reduce severe adenovirus (AdV) viremia in an 18-month-old toddler with malignant rhabdoid tumor of the kidney (MRT) following autologous hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT).

Case presentation

An 18-month-old, 10 kg boy with a metastatic MRT underwent left radical nephrectomy followed by chemotherapy and autologous HCT. Prior to the HCT (Fig. 1), he developed febrile neutropenia accompanied by an increasing CMV viral load. Given his CMV IgG positivity and concurrent chemotherapy, this was considered symptomatic CMV reactivation. On post-HCT day 6, the patient developed fever and upper respiratory symptoms and tested positive for adenovirus on the BioFire respiratory panel via nasopharyngeal swab. He subsequently developed AdV viremia (4.25 log copies/mL) and pneumonia, with a positive adenovirus PCR from bronchoalveolar lavage. He was started on weekly cidofovir 5 mg/kg/dose with probenecid (Fig. 1).

He engrafted on day +10 with resolved neutropenia but persistent profound lymphopenia. On day +17, his plasma CMV viral load increased to 2.5 log IU/mL (318 IU/mL),and ganciclovir was restarted.

On day +34, the patient's condition deteriorated with respiratory failure and acute kidney failure requiring extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), despite treatment with weekly cidofovir and daily ganciclovir 2.5 mg/kg/dose adjusted for CRRT. The AdV viral load reached the assay's upper limit of detection (>2 million copies/mL), and the CMV viral load increased to a maximum of 3.2 log IU/mL (1590 IU/mL), accompanied by elevated ferritin. His condition was further complicated by hepatic veno-occlusive disease, thrombocytopenia-associated multiple organ failure and possible secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis due to the AdV and CMV infections. To manage pathologic inflammation, he received IV dexamethasone, weekly IV immunoglobulin, and monoclonal immunomodulatory agents targeting gamma-interferon (emapalumab) and terminal complement (eculizumab). Due to significant auto-anticoagulation observed in thromboelastograms and coagulation studies, neither heparin nor bivalirudin were utilized during ECMO.

On day +38, his AdV viral load increased by 2.6 log, accompanied by persistent hyperinflammatory syndrome and respiratory failure. As a result, alternative treatment with Seraph 100 was initiated. Following the approval from the institutional ethics committee and informed consent from the patient's family, the patient underwent three Seraph 100 treatment on post-HCT days 39, 40, and 42, each lasting approximately 24 h (a total of 68 h) while continuing simultaneous CRRT and ECMO support. In the CRRT setting, the Prismaflex HF 1000 Set filter was used (Fig. 3). Citrate was administered at a rate of 150 mL/hr, with a blood flow rate of 150 mL/min. The total extracorporeal volume of the combined circuit was 325 mL, including 160 mL for the Seraph 100, which was more than 10 % of the patient’s total blood volume. The circuit was primed with blood. An AdV quantitative PCR test with a higher upper limit, collected before the first treatment, reported 8.94 log copies/mL (890 million copies/mL). After approximately 68 h of treatment, the AdV viral load dropped to 7.79 log copies/mL (62 million copies/mL), the CMV viral load became undetectable (Fig. 1). and repeat ferritin levels decreased from a peak of 30825 mcg/L to 10032 mcg/L (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Laboratory parameters pre- and post-seraph 100 treatments. He received cefepime 50 mg/kg/dose every 12 h over 3 h and ganciclovir 5 mg/kg/dose every 12 h over 60 min (this dose was adjusted for CRRT in combination with ECMO as our standard ECMO dose is 10 mg/kg/dose q12h). Therapeutic drug monitoring for cefepime and ganciclovir was performed during Seraph 100 to assess drug clearance impacts. The target reference ranges are a Cmin of 8–50 mg/L for cefepime (the minimum concentration of a drug reached in plasma during a dosing interval) and a trough of 1–3 mg/L and peak of 3–12 mg/L for ganciclovir. An initial extrapolated Cmin for cefepime was 14.7 mg/L had been obtained a week before the first treatment as part of routine cefepime monitoring on ECMO and CRRT. The Cmin increased to 18.3 mg/L 36 h after start of first treatment (approximately 12 h into second treatment) and reached 30.9 mg/L 60 h after start of first treatment (approximately 12 h after completion of the second treatment). A pre-Seraph 100 treatment level of ganciclovir was unavailable for comparison, but a trough level of 3.1 mcg/mL was recorded 8 h after starting the second treatment, followed by a peak level of 9.3 mcg/mL 10 h later. No dosing adjustments were needed for either drug due to adequate concentrations.

Our patient experienced large-volume hematochezia and G-tube bleeding within five minutes of starting the first treatment. He developed intermittent bradycardia three hours after starting the second treatment. The third treatment was halted after about 20 h due to hematochezia and brief asystole episodes requiring chest compressions. During each treatment, coagulation studies including activated partial thromboplastin (aPTT) were assessed. His aPTT was elevated at 153, 90, and 176 s (reference range: 25–37 s) after first, second, and third Seraph 100 treatments, respectively (measurement occurred one hour after each treatment) (Fig. 2). Due to recurrent bleeding, an upper endoscopy with biopsy revealed vesicular mucosal changes and presence of fungal elements adjacent to the G-tube site in the stomach, without AdV and CMV inclusions. No further treatment was attempted due to concerns about severe bleeding events. On post-HCT day 45, the patient received viral-specific T-cell therapy, and AdV viral loads reduced to 7.17 log copies/mL (15 million copies/mL) and CMV levels remained undetectable. However, on post-HCT day 49, the patient's blood cultures returned positive for Candida albicans with subsequent hemodynamic deterioration. The patient died from multi-organ failure one day later.

Discussion

We describe utilization of Seraph 100 in conjunction with CRRT and ECMO in an immunocompromised toddler with severe Adv viremia. While we observed a demonstrable improvement in viral load and ferritin, the patient also experienced severe coagulopathy with each treatment. Our patient had a 1.77 log reduction in AdV viral load and an undetectable CMV viral load after three treatments, accompanied by a 72 % decrease in ferritin levels. We maximized the duration of the treatment to 24 h, which is the maximum allowed duration per treatment according to the manufacturer's guidelines. Limited clinical data exist on Seraph 100 use in children with severe AdV viremia, with no data for young children. A case report described the effective reduction of AdV load from over 10 million copies/mL to 150,000 copies/mL in a teenager with severe disseminated AdV infection following kidney transplant, achieved through 9.5 h of treatment over two days [3]. Additionally, in vitro studies have demonstrated a 62 % and 79 % reduction in AdV and CMV loads, respectively [5], [6].

We observed no significant drug clearance by Seraph 100 in conjunction with CRRT and ECMO throughout the treatments, as Cmin of cefepime and peak and trough of ganciclovir remained within our institutions target reference ranges. This was in alignment with the limited information published thus far that discussed minor impact on antimicrobial exposure with the filter. An in vitro study revealed that there were no changes in plasma levels of 18 different antimicrobials 60 min after initiation with the exception of aminoglycosides [7]. Also, a recent multi-site prospective study with 34 participants demonstrated that seven antibiotics-azithromycin, cefazolin, cefepime, ceftriaxone, linezolid, piperacillin, and vancomycin-are not removed by the filter [8].

In this case, the patient developed worsening coagulopathy and hemodynamic instability during treatment. The contribution of anticoagulants to this adverse event remains unclear. We postulate that his small body size (10 kg) may have contributed to an increased susceptibility to the anticoagulation within the filter, potentially through release of heparin from the filter into the blood. This connection is suggested by a significant increase in aPTT within four hours after treatment initiation, which then gradually returned to normal over the following 12 h. The treatment, which utilizes heparin-coated polymer beads has shown in vitro data that only insignificant amounts of heparin are released into the systemic circulation during extracorporeal blood purification [5], [6]. The device is only available in a single, fixed concentration of heparin-coated polymer beads for all age groups, thus, weight-based formulations of heparin for pediatric patients are not available for this therapy. The development of a smaller device incorporating a small amount of heparin-coated polymer beads in its filter may be necessary for use in young children with smaller body weight.

In contrast to our case, severe adverse effects in adults and adolescents, including circuit clotting, hypotension, and fever are only rarely reported [9]. Prospective studies in adults have not shown severe adverse effects or device-related complications with Seraph 100 during CRRT and ECMO, noting median treatment durations of 4 and 10 h, respectively [10], [11]. Similarly, case reports of adolescents undergoing two to three treatments within a 24-hour period each did not report severe coagulopathy or significant clinical changes [3], [12]. This limited evidence suggests that adverse effects may be less common in older children, particularly in those with shorter treatment times and fewer treatments.

In conclusion, we have demonstrated that the Seraph 100 in conjunction with CRRT and ECMO, provides promising clinical potential for managing AdV viremia and controlling inflammation without impacting cefepime and ganciclovir concentrations. However severe coagulopathy with secondary bleeding resulting in hemodynamic instability was observed with each of our serial treatments. Further research is needed to define safe application of the device and explore the balance between therapeutic benefits and the risk of adverse effects, particularly in young children.
