A new approach to antiphospholipid antibody syndrome
Authors: Mark Crowther, Doruk Erkan
Published: 2023

Antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APS) continues to pose challenges in clinical practice, mainly due to the multifactorial nature of thrombosis and pregnancy morbidity and the lack of reliable laboratory tests that predict its various clinical manifestations. In the absence of APS diagnostic criteria, for over 20 years, the revised Sapporo classification criteria [1,2] has provided guidance for clinicians, although the main purpose of “classification criteria” is to capture a heterogeneous group of patients for research and not to confirm diagnosis or guide therapy [3].

The formulation of the revised Sapporo APS classification criteria was originally based on expert consensus, and they have not evolved to adequately address the current understanding of the disease, for example, recognition of the diverse manifestations of APS such as thrombocytopenia or valvular heart disease or addressing our improved understanding of the obstetrical manifestations of the condition.

Researchers led by coprincipal investigators Drs Doruk Erkan and Stephane Zuily, representing a multidisciplinary group of approximately 100 international physicians, have recently developed and published new classification criteria for APS [4]. These criteria are to be used to define patients with this disease better for research studies rather than to be used as clinical classification criteria. Supported by the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR), these criteria are considerably more complex than the prior ones. However, they will quickly become the standard for defining this disease to improve APS research quality and inform patient outcomes.

The development of the 2023 ACR/EULAR APS classification criteria was significantly different than the process used to define APS criteria previously. Specifically, the current process was a comprehensive, data-driven, and consensus-based 4-phase methodology: a) criteria generation by surveys and literature review; b) criteria reduction by modified Delphi and nominal group technique exercises; c) criteria definition, further reduction with the guidance of real-world patient scenarios, weighting via consensus-based multicriteria decision analysis, and threshold identification; and d) validation using independent adjudicators’ consensus as the gold standard.

To illustrate the value of these new criteria, let us examine a putative patient. Ms L.S. is a 36-year-old woman who presented a week ago because of the detection of a new cardiac murmur on the background of an unexplained platelet count of 29 × 109/L, livedo racemosa, and a history of a single prior unexplained pregnancy loss at 8 weeks. There was no history of confirmed large-to-moderate vessel arterial or venous thromboembolism. She underwent transthoracic ultrasound demonstrating sessile vegetation; serial blood cultures were negative. Laboratory examination performed before initiating anticoagulation confirmed the presence of a lupus anticoagulant. Anticardiolipin (aCL) and anti-B2 GP1 immunoglobulin G (IgG)/immunoglobulin M (IgM) antibody levels were below the lower limit of detection of the commercial assay. She was stabilized with unfractionated heparin and transitioned to warfarin anticoagulation. Her thrombocytopenia was treated with corticosteroids and rituximab as her platelet count had not returned to a safe level for long-term anticoagulation. She returned to the clinic after 4 months. She had persistent mild thrombocytopenia (a platelet count of 97 × 109/L), but her other manifestations had resolved. Her lupus anticoagulant remained positive (tested when her international normalized ratio was normal and more than 24 hours after her last anticoagulant dose).

There was little doubt that this patient had APS; yet, using the revised Sapporo APS classification criteria, she did not technically meet the APS classification. Using the new classification criteria, the patient had APS. Specifically, her livedo racemosa, valvular vegetation, and thrombocytopenia gave her 8 clinical points (3 are required for classification). Her laboratory criteria (persistently positive lupus anticoagulant) met the criteria (providing 5 points compared with the required 3).

Let us then examine a second patient. Ms L.T. is a 32-year-old woman who presented with 4 consecutive pregnancy losses, all occurring before 8 weeks of gestation. Her laboratory testing demonstrated a high titer (>80 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay units) of IgM aCL; IgG test result was negative, as was the aB2GPI IgG/IgM test result. She did not have lupus anticoagulant positivity. She had no history of small or large vessel thrombosis or systemic lupus erythematosus. IgM aCL was persistently positive at 14 weeks after the first assessment.

Based on the 2023 ACR/EULAR APS classification criteria, this patient achieved 1 clinical point and 1 obstetrical point and, thus, did not meet the classification criteria for APS. This person would be defined as having APS using the prior criteria.

What is the value of these new weighted criteria for researchers? Most importantly, new clinical manifestations are added, clinical and laboratory definitions are updated, thrombosis risk stratification is incorporated, and a threshold for APS classification is provided. In the new criteria, a score above a certain value indicates that a large group of clinical and laboratory experts would feel comfortable classifying patients as having “APS”—this is supported by case 1 above, which previously would not have met the criteria. Many marginal cases that met prior criteria, particularly obstetrical cases and cases with less clear serology, will be excluded from the classification. This is illustrated by case 2, where multiple recurrent miscarriages early in pregnancy, viewed with skepticism by many experts as a manifestation of APS, had their significance downgraded, as did the IgM isotype positivity of any 1 diagnostic test, even if persistent. As a result of these improvements, researchers will have more confidence that those patients deemed by the criteria to have the disease do, in fact, have the disease.

What is the value of these new criteria for clinicians? As mentioned above, classification criteria are not diagnostic tools—as “diagnosis” is a complex equation requiring incorporation of a broad range of antiphospholipid antibody (aPL)-related clinical features, aPL profile, concomitant risk factors, and differential diagnoses about the epidemiology in a specific region. However, the 2023 ACR/EULAR APS classification criteria can guide evaluating aPL-positive patients, especially to prevent the overdiagnosis of APS, a common clinical practice problem.

The new criteria are not a panacea; many questions remain about this disease. Importantly, the criteria need to be open to further refinement, as encouraged by ARC/EULAR, as time passes and experience is gained—particularly as new diagnostic tools and methodologies develop. Second, these criteria do not address therapy; however, by excluding marginal cases, the hope is that future studies based on these criteria will have a more refined population, leading to a more reliable study design and outcomes. Third, and perhaps most importantly, these criteria do not address the underlying biology of the disease. Fundamentally, like many immune diseases, the underlying biology of this disease remains unclear. Understanding the disease’s biology will allow tailoring of therapy and should be the goal for research into APS.
