Evaluating coastal urban underground space for ecological and marine-pollution suitability using AHP–TOPSIS
Authors: Zhiwen Zhao, Zhaoping Tian, Guohui Wang, Ying Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn integrated Analytic Hierarchy Process–TOPSIS framework is developed to evaluate the ecological and marine-pollution suitability of urban underground space (UUS) projects in coastal cities. The AHP–TOPSIS hybrid approach is particularly suitable for coastal infrastructure evaluation because it integrates structured expert judgement with transparent aggregation of distance-to-ideal performance, enabling balanced consideration of ecological, marine, technical, and social objectives in complex planning environments. A structured hierarchy of 10 criteria, grouped into Environmental/Marine, Economic/Technical, and Social/Governance dimensions, is weighted based on judgements from 50 experts in planning, underground engineering, coastal and marine environments, and infrastructure finance. Environmental/Marine factors receive the highest aggregate weight (0.53), ahead of Economic/Technical (0.25) and Social/Governance (0.22), with energy efficiency, air quality, stormwater management, marine impact, and lifecycle cost emerging as the most influential criteria. Five representative UUS alternatives—underground transit hub, subsurface retail complex, underground green park, multi-level underground parking, and subterranean mixed-use complex, are assessed using TOPSIS. The underground green park and transit hub have the highest closeness coefficients (0.72 and 0.65), while retail and parking options systematically underperform due to weaker Environmental/Marine and social profiles. Sensitivity and scenario analyses confirm the robustness of the rankings to substantial variations in criteria weights, supporting the prioritisation of ecologically oriented and transit-supportive UUS concepts in coastal planning. Introduction

Rapid urbanisation and land scarcity in many metropolitan regions have led to growing interest in urban underground space (UUS) as a strategic resource for transport, utilities, public facilities, and mixed-use development [1,2]. Early work on UUS planning already highlighted its potential to relieve surface congestion, support compact urban forms, and contribute to low-carbon development when integrated into master planning processes [3–5]. Subsequent reviews and conceptual frameworks have reinforced the idea that underground space should be treated as part of a three-dimensional urban system [6,7], with explicit links to sustainability objectives and the Sustainable Development Goals [8–10]. Despite these linkages, UUS research on the implications of marine pollution remains limited [11,12]. Reviews of UUS planning and value evaluation emphasise economic benefits, spatial efficiency, and generic environmental indicators, but rarely consider explicit marine outcomes such as changes in runoff quality, combined sewer overflows, or saline–freshwater interactions [13–15]. Environmental risk assessments of underground public spaces have begun to catalogue potential impacts, such as air pollution, noise, vibration, and resource use, and some studies employ structured techniques, such as EFMEA and TOPSIS, to prioritise risks [16–18]. Yet these applications typically address internal environmental quality and local urban impacts rather than downstream effects on marine ecosystems, and they are seldom tailored to the specific hydro-climatic conditions of coastal cities [19–21]. Methodologically, the assessment of UUS options has increasingly turned to multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) methods to integrate environmental, economic, technical, and social considerations in a transparent manner [22,23]. Reviews of MCDM applications in infrastructure management and sustainability assessment show widespread use of analytic techniques such as AHP, TOPSIS, PROMETHEE, and others to handle conflicting objectives and stakeholder preferences [24–26]. In the broader built-environment literature, AHP has been employed to prioritise infrastructure projects, energy options, and planning strategies, including urban transport investment, renewable energy deployment, and sustainable construction material selection. Likewise, TOPSIS and related distance-to-ideal techniques have been widely used to rank alternatives based on multi-dimensional environmental and economic performance indicators in infrastructure and environmental planning contexts [27]. Hybrid AHP–TOPSIS frameworks have become particularly prominent in sustainability-oriented decision problems [28]. Recently, generating environmental improvement options for industrial processes, sustainable material selection for low-carbon buildings, social sustainability in construction supply chains, and strategic infrastructure choices, such as hydrogen refuelling station types [29–31]. In these applications, AHP is typically used to derive criteria weights from expert judgements, while TOPSIS aggregates the weighted performance scores to obtain a ranking of options. The combination has been shown to offer a practical balance between transparency, computational simplicity, and the ability to incorporate expert knowledge [32–34].

Within the UUS domain, MCDM methods have been applied to land-use allocation, design optimisation, and environmental risk evaluation, but integrated AHP–TOPSIS approaches remain relatively rare. They are mostly confined to specific risk or suitability issues that do not explicitly address marine pollution outcomes [35,36]. Park et al. [32] used “green UUS” and sustainability frameworks to propose indicator systems that are highly relevant for coastal contexts, emphasising energy efficiency, carbon footprint, air quality, and climate resilience, yet there is still little evidence of these indicators being systematically combined with marine-focused criteria such as stormwater management performance and potential impacts on coastal waters [37–39].

Despite increasing interest in underground ecological performance, marine-linked externalities, such as pollutant loading to coastal waters, saline–freshwater interactions, and downstream ecosystem impacts, remain largely unquantified in current UUS evaluation frameworks. Existing assessment systems typically prioritise terrestrial sustainability indicators without systematically embedding marine pollution pathways within structured decision models. While AHP–TOPSIS models are increasingly used in other areas of infrastructure and environmental decision-making, their potential to structure expert knowledge and to rank UUS alternatives in coastal settings has not yet been fully explored [32,40]. Among available MCDM approaches, the AHP–TOPSIS combination offers a balance of transparency, internal consistency testing, computational simplicity, and practitioner accessibility. Compared with outranking techniques such as PROMETHEE or compromise-based approaches such as VIKOR, AHP enables explicit verification of judgement consistency, while TOPSIS provides an intuitive distance-to-ideal ranking structure suitable for communicating results to planning authorities and infrastructure stakeholders in coastal contexts.

The present research addresses these gaps by developing a dedicated AHP–TOPSIS framework to assess the ecological and marine-pollution suitability of UUS projects in coastal cities. A criteria set is created that combines widely recognised UUS sustainability indicators, such as energy efficiency, air quality, and structural resilience, with explicitly marine-oriented factors related to stormwater management and impacts on coastal waters. Expert judgements from a geographically diverse panel are used to derive criteria weights and evaluate the performance of representative UUS typologies, and the resulting rankings are subjected to systematic sensitivity and scenario analysis. In doing so, the work contributes to three strands of literature: (i) UUS planning and sustainability, by foregrounding marine-pollution considerations within underground development; (ii) coastal urbanisation and marine ecology, by linking stormwater- and infrastructure-related drivers of pollution to specific spatial planning decisions; and (iii) MCDM methodology in the built environment, by demonstrating how an AHP–TOPSIS framework can be deployed to support strategic choices among competing UUS concepts under ecological and marine-protection priorities.

The Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) decomposes the decision problem into a hierarchy of goals, criteria, and (where relevant) sub-criteria, and relies on pairwise comparisons provided by experts to quantify their preferences on a consistent ratio scale. Developed by Saaty, AHP offers an internally coherent way to estimate criterion weights and assess the consistency of expert judgements, which has led to its widespread use in complex planning and resource-allocation problems [41] In this study, the hierarchy places overall UUS suitability at the top, followed by environmental and marine-pollution-related criteria and sub-criteria, which are then linked to the set of candidate UUS alternatives shown in Fig. 1. TOPSIS (Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to the Ideal Solution) is a compensatory MCDM method introduced initially by Hwang and Yoon, which ranks alternatives according to their geometric distance from an ideal (best) and a nadir (worst) solution in the criteria space [42]. After normalising the decision matrix and applying the AHP-derived weights, TOPSIS computes a closeness coefficient for each UUS alternative that reflects its simultaneous proximity to the ideal solution (maximum ecological performance, minimum marine pollution risk, etc.) and remoteness from the nadir solution. Alternatives with higher closeness coefficients are considered more suitable. TOPSIS has been extensively applied across engineering, environmental management, maritime systems, and infrastructure planning domains, where it has been integrated with SWOT, DEMATEL, fuzzy logic, and other decision-support tools to evaluate strategic alternatives under uncertainty [28]. Combining AHP and TOPSIS leverages the strengths of both methods. AHP captures expert knowledge through a transparent weighting process and enables consistency checks. At the same time, TOPSIS provides a straightforward ranking mechanism that can handle both benefit- and cost-type criteria without requiring strong distributional assumptions. Recent applications have shown that AHP–TOPSIS frameworks perform well in sustainability-oriented decision-making problems, such as water and environmental resource management and sustainable supplier selection.

Formulation of evaluation criteria and UUS alternatives

The first step of the AHP–TOPSIS framework is to define a coherent set of criteria and a representative group of urban underground space (UUS) alternatives. A structured literature review of more than 150 articles on sustainable underground infrastructure, coastal urban planning, and multi-criteria decision-making was conducted and complemented by consultations with domain experts. Building on recent work on green UUS assessment, underground-space sustainability, and environmental risk evaluation in underground public spaces, the criteria system was designed to capture ecological performance, marine-pollution risk, economic and technical feasibility, and social acceptability [43]. While the present study evaluates representative UUS typologies rather than site-specific parcels, future operational applications of the framework can integrate GIS-based spatial overlays to incorporate hydrogeological constraints, proximity to marine receptors, and coastal flood exposure into the suitability assessment.

Environmental and marine-related criteria focus on how each UUS alternative interacts with urban and coastal ecosystems. Key aspects include energy efficiency and associated greenhouse-gas emissions, air quality and ventilation conditions, integration of green and blue infrastructure, control of stormwater and runoff pathways, and potential impacts on groundwater and adjacent marine waters. These dimensions reflect the argument that underground developments in coastal cities must simultaneously improve urban sustainability and mitigate loading of pollutants to the marine environment [44]. Economic and technical criteria are included to ensure that ecologically preferable projects are also feasible and resilient. They cover construction and lifecycle maintenance costs, expected return on investment, robustness of structures under coastal hydro-geological conditions, and land-use efficiency in terms of floor-area yield and potential for mixed use. Similar multidimensional criteria sets have been recommended in recent MCDM applications to sustainable infrastructure planning and urban systems [45]. Social and governance-related criteria complement the framework by assessing public acceptance and usability of underground spaces, perceived safety and comfort, accessibility and connectivity to public transport, and the contribution of each project to broader urban-resilience and policy goals.

Based on these dimensions, eleven operational criteria were finalised and validated through an expert survey, which also confirmed their relevance for UUS planning oriented toward ecological and marine pollution. The decision hierarchy links these criteria to five real-world UUS alternatives located in coastal Chinese cities: (i) an underground transit hub integrating metro, bus and pedestrian networks; (ii) a subsurface retail complex in a dense commercial district; (iii) an underground green park providing additional recreational and ecological space; (iv) a multi-level underground parking facility in a high-traffic business area; and (v) a subterranean mixed-use complex combining residential, commercial and public functions. These alternatives form the lowest level of the AHP hierarchy and constitute the option set that will later be evaluated and ranked using the AHP-derived criteria weights and the TOPSIS procedure (see Fig. 2).

Expert survey design and data collection

A structured expert survey was implemented to obtain the judgments required for both the AHP weighting stage and the TOPSIS performance evaluation. The panel comprised 50 experts selected through purposive sampling. All invitees had at least five years of experience in one or more of the following fields: urban and regional planning, underground space engineering, coastal and marine environmental science, hydrology and water resources, transportation planning, and sustainability assessment. Zhejing Guangsha Vocational and Technical University of Construction confirmed that all questionnaires and the online protocol were completed, and that the obtained data are confidentially retained to preserve the anonymity of the respondents. Similar sample sizes have been recommended or adopted in survey-based AHP studies, which emphasise expertise and diversity over purely statistical representativeness [46]. Geographical coverage was treated as a key design requirement. The final sample included experts based in multiple coastal regions, including East and Southeast Asia, the Middle East, and Northern Europe, with professional experience in major coastal cities and port agglomerations. This spatial diversity was intended to capture different regulatory frameworks, hydro-geological settings, and patterns of UUS development, thereby reducing the risk that the criteria weights or performance scores would reflect only a single national context. Recent AHP applications in sustainability and resource management similarly stress the value of geographically heterogeneous panels when decisions have broad environmental implications [47]. All experts were treated equally during aggregation. This decision was based on the minimum eligibility threshold (≥ 5 years of relevant professional experience) and the observed high concordance of judgments. Differentiated weighting schemes were considered; however, no statistically significant performance differences were detected between subgroups when tested using independent-sample rank tests (p > 0.05). The questionnaire was divided into three sections. The first collected background information on each expert’s discipline, years of experience, and primary geographic focus. The second section contained pairwise comparison questions for the criteria and sub-criteria, using the standard 1–9 AHP scale to express relative importance [48]. Clear textual definitions and examples were provided for each criterion, and experts were asked to base their comparisons on the perspective of long-term ecological performance and marine-pollution risk in coastal UUS projects. The third section required experts to rate each of the five UUS alternatives against each criterion on a 1–9 semantic scale (“very poor” to “excellent”), generating the raw data for the TOPSIS decision matrix. Data were collected using an online survey platform, supplemented by targeted video-conference sessions for experts who preferred a guided completion. After the survey closed, responses were exported to a spreadsheet for processing. For the AHP part, individual pairwise-comparison matrices were constructed for each expert and checked for logical consistency using the consistency ratio; matrices with CR > 0.10 were returned to respondents for clarification or excluded from aggregation, following standard practice. Consistent matrices were then aggregated across experts using the geometric mean at the level of each matrix element (Aggregation of Individual Judgements, AIJ), which preserves the reciprocal property of AHP comparisons and is widely recommended for group decision-making. The resulting group matrices were used to derive the final priority vectors (criteria weights) using the principal eigenvector method [49].

For the TOPSIS stage, the performance ratings of each alternative on each criterion were first coded numerically (1–9) and averaged across experts to obtain a single representative score per alternative–criterion combination. These scores formed the initial decision matrix, which was then normalised and combined with the AHP-derived weight vector in subsequent steps of the TOPSIS procedure. The careful design of the expert panel, combined with transparent data-extraction and aggregation procedures, provides a robust empirical foundation for the multicriteria evaluation of UUS alternatives described in the following subsections (see Fig. 3).

Handling large-scale expert panels and aggregation strategy

While the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) was originally developed for small decision-making groups, its application has expanded to larger expert panels in sustainability and infrastructure assessment. When the number of evaluators increases, issues of response heterogeneity, consensus formation, and aggregation method selection become particularly important. Recent research on large-scale AHP and TOPSIS applications highlights that simple averaging may conceal divergent judgment patterns if heterogeneity is not assessed beforehand. To address this, the present study conducted a heterogeneity screening prior to aggregation. First, dispersion measures were calculated for each pairwise comparison element across experts. The standard deviation of judgement ratios remained within acceptable ranges (average SD = 0.84 on the 1–9 scale), indicating moderate, rather than extreme, disagreement. Second, Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) was computed for the aggregated pairwise comparison rankings, yielding W = 0.76 (p < 0.01), suggesting substantial agreement among experts.

Given this relatively high concordance and the absence of extreme outlier matrices (after consistency filtering), aggregation of individual judgements (AIJ) using the geometric mean was adopted. This method preserves reciprocal properties and is widely recommended for group AHP. Alternative large-scale extensions, including consensus-based clustering, fuzzy aggregation, and interval AHP formulations, were reviewed but deemed unnecessary due to the observed convergence of expert evaluations. Similarly, for the TOPSIS stage, arithmetic averaging of performance ratings was applied only after verifying that interquartile ranges for criterion ratings did not indicate multimodal clustering. The mean interquartile range across criteria was 1.9 on the 1–9 scale, suggesting controlled dispersion rather than structural fragmentation of opinion. Therefore, the chosen aggregation model is considered methodologically appropriate for the scale and consensus level of the expert panel.

The approach is consistent with recent AHP-based evaluations of urban and underground space planning, where hierarchical criteria systems are translated into quantitative priorities to support multi-criteria assessment [50].

TOPSIS procedure for ranking UUS projects

Sensitivity and robustness analysis

To assess the stability of the AHP–TOPSIS results and the reliability of the recommended UUS alternative, a systematic sensitivity and robustness analysis was conducted. Sensitivity analysis is widely recognised as a key step in multi-criteria decision-making, as small changes in criteria weights or performance scores can sometimes alter the ranking of alternatives; neglecting this step may lead to over-confident conclusions.

First, one-way (local) sensitivity tests were performed on the global criteria weights obtained from AHP. Each criterion weight was perturbed in increments of ± 5–10%, while the remaining weights were proportionally rescaled to maintain . For each perturbation scenario, the TOPSIS procedure was rerun, and the resulting closeness coefficients and rankings of the UUS alternatives were recorded. The extent to which the position of the top-ranked and second-ranked alternatives changed across scenarios was used as an indicator of ranking robustness.

Second, a scenario-based analysis was implemented to reflect plausible shifts in planning priorities. Three policy-oriented scenarios were considered: (i) an “environment-first” scenario that emphasises ecological and marine-pollution criteria; (ii) an “economic-feasibility” scenario that increases the weight of cost and land-use efficiency; and (iii) a “social-focus” scenario that strengthens the importance of accessibility, safety, and public acceptance. For each scenario, an adjusted weight vector was constructed within reasonable bounds of the original AHP weights, and TOPSIS rankings were recalculated. Comparing these rankings with the baseline case helps to identify UUS alternatives that perform consistently well under changing value structures, a practice recommended in recent MCDA applications in infrastructure and environmental planning.

Finally, robustness was assessed using ranking correlation and dominance checks. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were computed between the baseline ranking and each perturbed or scenario-based ranking; coefficients close to 1 indicate high robustness. In addition, alternatives that never dropped below a given position (e.g., consistently ranked within the top two) across all tested scenarios were identified as robustly preferable options. This combination of local sensitivity tests, policy scenarios, and rank-correlation analysis provides a transparent assessment of how sensitive the conclusions are to uncertainty in expert judgements and planning priorities, and increases confidence in the use of the AHP–TOPSIS framework to support sustainable UUS decisions in coastal cities.

Results and discussion

Descriptive profile of the expert panel and survey responses

A total of 50 fully completed questionnaires were retained for analysis. All invited experts responded, and after removing a small number of incomplete or inconsistent AHP matrices, 50 valid sets of judgements remained for both the weighting and performance‐rating stages. The overall response quality was high: for the group-comparison matrices derived from individual inputs, all consistency ratios were below the 0.10 threshold, indicating that the pairwise judgements used to calculate the criteria weights were logically coherent. In terms of professional background, the panel was dominated by specialists in underground space and urban planning (around two-fifths of respondents), followed by experts in coastal and marine environmental science, water resources and hydrology, transportation planning, and sustainability assessment. This mix ensured that both engineering and environmental management perspectives were represented when evaluating the ecological and marine pollution implications of UUS projects.

Regarding institutional affiliation, approximately half of the respondents were based in universities or research institutes, one-third worked in public agencies or municipal planning departments, and the remainder were practitioners from consulting firms and infrastructure operators. This balance between academic and non-academic experts helped to combine conceptual, research-based knowledge with practical experience from real projects and regulatory processes. The panel’s experience profile was skewed towards senior practitioners: most respondents reported more than 10 years of professional experience in urban infrastructure or environmental planning, and a substantial share had been directly involved in the design, assessment, or regulation of underground space projects in coastal settings. Only a small minority had fewer than 5 years of relevant experience, and these were typically early-career researchers working under the supervision of senior colleagues.

From a geographical perspective, the experts were distributed across several coastal regions, with the largest groups in East and Southeast Asia, followed by the Middle East and Northern Europe. Many reported having worked in more than one country or coastal city, thereby broadening the range of hydrogeological and institutional contexts reflected in the judgements. This spatial diversity is significant for the present study, as it reduces the risk that the resulting criteria weights and performance scores merely reflect the conditions of a single national planning system. The response patterns in the rating section of the questionnaire showed that experts made full use of the 1–9 scale, suggesting they could clearly discriminate between the performance of UUS alternatives across criteria. Environmental and marine-related criteria tended to receive higher mean importance scores than purely economic considerations, while social and governance criteria occupied an intermediate position. At the same time, no single criterion dominated the others, which indicates that the panel recognised the need to balance ecological protection, economic feasibility, and social acceptability in coastal UUS planning. These descriptive findings provide the empirical basis for the subsequent presentation of AHP-derived weights and TOPSIS rankings in sections “AHP-derived criteria weights and relative importance of factors” and “TOPSIS-based suitability scores and ranking of UUS alternatives”. AHP-derived criteria weights and relative importance of factors

Applying the AHP procedure to the 50 expert judgements yields a consistent set of weights for the ten criteria in the evaluation hierarchy. At the highest level, the three main dimensions receive clearly differentiated priorities: Environmental/Marine factors obtain the largest share of importance (weight 0.53), followed by Economic/Technical aspects (weight 0.25) and Social/Governance issues (weight 0.22). This pattern indicates that experts regard ecological performance and protection of coastal and marine environments as the primary concern in assessing UUS projects. At the same time, economic feasibility and social–institutional conditions play important but secondary roles.

Within the Environmental/Marine dimension, energy efficiency (C1) emerges as the most influential criterion. Its global weight is 0.159, making it the single most important factor in the hierarchy. Air quality (C2) and stormwater management (C3) follow with global weights of 0.138 and 0.127, respectively, while marine impact (C4) weighs 0.106. Taken together, these four criteria account for just over half of the total decision weight, confirming that experts prioritise both long-term operational performance (energy use and indoor environment) and control of runoff pathways that may affect coastal waters.

In the Economic/Technical group, construction and maintenance costs (C5) is the dominant criterion, with a global weight of 0.113, ranking fifth overall. This reflects strong concern with the whole-life affordability of UUS projects. Structural resilience (C6), which captures the ability of underground structures to withstand coastal hazards and complex subsurface conditions, is assigned a weight of 0.083, while land-use efficiency (C7) receives a weight of 0.055. Altogether, economic and technical indicators account for one-quarter of the total importance, underscoring their role as a feasibility filter after environmental requirements have been considered.

Social and governance-related criteria, though less heavily weighted, still account for more than one-fifth of the decision space. Public acceptance (C8) has a global weight of 0.088, indicating that societal support and perceived legitimacy of UUS projects are crucial for implementation. Safety and comfort (C9) follow with 0.077, reflecting concerns about user experience and perceived risk in enclosed underground environments. Accessibility (C10), which measures the degree of physical integration with public transport networks and the surrounding urban fabric, weighs 0.055, comparable to that of land-use efficiency.

Still, its environmental scores are slightly lower than those of A1 and A3, particularly in terms of stormwater management and indoor air quality. The subsurface retail complex (A2) and the multi-level parking facility (A4) show the lowest suitability values ( and , respectively), mainly due to weaker performance on key environmental/marine criteria and, in the case of A4, comparatively poor energy and carbon profiles coupled with limited social benefits. Overall, the TOPSIS results indicate a clear ordering of alternatives rather than a near tie, with the two top-ranked projects (A3 and A1) clearly separated from the remaining three options. This pattern suggests that UUS concepts that integrate ecological functions and public-space qualities (such as the underground green park) or that strongly support sustainable transport (such as the transit hub) are more compatible with the environmental and marine-protection priorities reflected in the AHP weights than car-oriented or purely commercial underground developments (see Fig. 5).

Sensitivity and robustness analysis of ranking outcomes

Sensitivity analysis was conducted to examine how stable the ranking of UUS alternatives remains when the criteria weights are perturbed and when planning priorities shift between environmental, economic and social objectives. Two complementary procedures were implemented: (i) one-way variation of individual criterion weights around their baseline AHP values, and (ii) three policy-oriented scenarios in which the dimension weights were systematically rebalanced towards environmental, economic or social goals. In all cases, the TOPSIS procedure was reapplied to obtain new closeness coefficients and rankings. In the one-way tests, each global criterion weight was increased and decreased by 20% in turn, with the remaining weights rescaled proportionally to maintain a unit sum. Across all such perturbations, the underground green park (A3) remained the top-ranked alternative, while the underground transit hub (A1) consistently occupied second place. Changes were observed mainly in the lower part of the ranking: the positions of the subsurface retail complex (A2) and the subterranean mixed-use complex (A5) occasionally swapped, and the multi-level parking facility (A4) sometimes moved from fifth to fourth place, but these shifts did not affect the identity of the best-performing options. This pattern suggests that moderate uncertainties in the experts’ weighting of individual criteria do not materially alter the core conclusion that A3 and A1 are the most suitable UUS concepts under the adopted framework.

Overall, the sensitivity results demonstrate that the identity of the best-performing alternatives is reasonably stable across a wide range of plausible weight changes. The underground green park (A3) and the transit hub (A1) dominate in the baseline and environment-first settings, while the mixed-use complex (A5) becomes competitive only when economic considerations are strongly prioritised. Implications for coastal planning practice

The results of the AHP–TOPSIS analysis have several implications for the planning and management of urban underground space in coastal cities concerned with marine-pollution risks. First, the dominant weight assigned to Environmental/Marine criteria and the strong performance of the underground green park (A3) and transit hub (A1) indicate that UUS concepts, which actively contribute to ecological functions and modal shift, are far more compatible with coastal protection objectives than projects driven mainly by private mobility or consumption. In practical terms, this suggests that city authorities should prioritise underground developments that integrate stormwater retention and treatment features, support public transport use, and reduce surface sealing, rather than expanding underground parking or stand-alone retail facilities that primarily induce additional traffic and energy demand.

Second, the relatively high weights assigned to stormwater management and marine impact, combined with the environmental profiles of A3 and A1, underscore the importance of hydrological design in UUS projects. Underground works in coastal zones can either exacerbate or mitigate pollutant pathways to receiving waters, depending on how drainage, infiltration, and connections to the sewer system are configured. The findings therefore support the incorporation of nature-based solutions, such as subsurface detention volumes, infiltration galleries, and connections to green–blue infrastructure, directly into UUS design guidelines. This would help ensure that underground interventions form part of a broader urban water strategy aimed at reducing combined sewer overflows, urban runoff peaks, and contaminant loads to estuaries and coastal waters.

Third, while economic and technical criteria are clearly secondary to environmental concerns, they still account for a substantial share of the decision space, and the good performance of the mixed-use complex (A5) in the economic-feasibility scenario demonstrates that financial viability and land-use efficiency can be aligned with environmental goals, provided that UUS schemes are carefully programmed. Mixed-use or transit-oriented underground developments can deliver high intensity of use and diversified revenue streams while also supporting compact, low-carbon urban forms. The implication for planners is not to abandon economically attractive underground projects, but to impose minimum environmental and marine-protection standards, for example, on energy performance, ventilation, materials, and water management, as preconditions for approval.

Fourth, the Social/Governance dimension, although numerically less influential, plays a critical enabling role. High weights for public acceptance and safety and comfort, combined with the strong social scores of A1 and A5, suggest that underground projects will only deliver their potential ecological benefits if they are perceived as safe, accessible and useful by residents. This calls for early stakeholder engagement, transparent communication about environmental risks and benefits, and strict attention to user experience (lighting, wayfinding, perceived security) in the design of underground spaces. Regulatory frameworks and planning policies should also explicitly recognise the contribution of UUS to coastal resilience, for example by integrating underground ecological functions and pollution control into coastal zone management plans and climate-adaptation strategies.

Finally, the sensitivity results show that the preference for ecologically oriented UUS concepts is robust across a wide range of plausible weight changes. Even when economic or social priorities are emphasised, purely car-oriented or narrowly commercial underground projects remain at the bottom of the ranking. This robustness strengthens the argument that long-term marine-pollution mitigation and ecological performance should be treated as non-negotiable design constraints rather than optional co-benefits. In practice, this implies that coastal cities considering new UUS investments should use decision-support tools similar to the AHP–TOPSIS framework developed here to systematically screen project proposals, discard options that perform poorly on key environmental and marine criteria, and focus resources on underground interventions that genuinely support sustainable coastal urban futures. From a regulatory perspective, the findings support the introduction of minimum environmental performance thresholds, particularly for stormwater control, marine impact mitigation, and energy efficiency, as screening criteria in coastal UUS approval processes. Projects failing to meet predefined ecological benchmarks should not proceed to economic evaluation stages, ensuring that marine protection remains a binding constraint rather than a secondary consideration.

Conclusions

This study developed and applied an AHP–TOPSIS multi-criteria framework to evaluate the ecological and marine-pollution suitability of urban underground space (UUS) alternatives in coastal cities. It introduces a marine-integrated AHP–TOPSIS framework for evaluating urban underground infrastructure, filling a critical methodological and practical gap by systematically embedding coastal pollution criteria into structured UUS decision-making. Based on judgments from 50 experts with backgrounds in planning, underground engineering, coastal and marine environment, and infrastructure finance, a hierarchy of 10 criteria was established and weighted. At the dimension level, Environmental/Marine factors received the highest importance with a weight of 0.53, followed by Economic/Technical (0.25) and Social/Governance (0.22). Within this structure, the most influential individual criteria were energy efficiency (global weight 0.159), air quality (0.138), stormwater management (0.127), marine impact (0.106), and construction and maintenance costs (0.113), indicating a clear emphasis on long-term environmental performance and whole-life affordability.

Using these weights, TOPSIS was applied to rank five UUS alternatives: an underground transit hub (A1), a subsurface retail complex (A2), an underground green park (A3), a multi-level underground parking (A4), and a subterranean mixed-use complex (A5). The underground green park achieved the highest closeness coefficient () and was identified as the most suitable option, followed by the transit hub () and the mixed-use complex (). The retail complex and parking facility showed lower suitability ( and , respectively), largely due to weaker performance on key Environmental/Marine criteria. Dimension-level performance scores reinforced these findings: A3 and A1 attained the highest environmental scores (0.85 and 0.78, respectively) and at least moderate economic and social scores, whereas A2 and A4 were penalised by comparatively poor environmental and social profiles despite acceptable economic results.

Sensitivity and robustness tests confirmed that these conclusions are not overly dependent on a specific weighting scheme. One-way perturbations of individual criteria weights by ± 20% did not change the identity of the top two alternatives: A3 remained first and A1 second in all tested cases. Scenario analysis, in which dimension weights were shifted towards environmental (S1), economic (S2), or social (S3) priorities, yielded Spearman rank correlations ranging from 0.70 to 0.95 relative to the baseline ranking. Although the mixed-use complex rose to first place under a strongly economically oriented scenario, the parking and retail alternatives consistently ranked lowest, indicating that they are structurally less compatible with ecological and marine-pollution objectives.

Overall, the results suggest that UUS concepts which integrate ecological functions (e.g. stormwater retention, green–blue connectivity) and support sustainable mobility are systematically favoured when coastal and marine protection is treated as a central objective. The framework demonstrates how expert knowledge can be structured to discriminate between functionally similar but environmentally divergent UUS projects, and provides quantitative evidence that Environmental/Marine criteria should be treated as binding constraints rather than optional add-ons in coastal underground planning.

Furthermore, while heterogeneity screening indicated sufficient consensus to justify geometric-mean aggregation, future research may explore alternative large-scale AHP formulations, including consensus clustering, interval-valued aggregation, or fuzzy-based extensions, to further refine group-decision modelling under higher divergence conditions. As with all expert-based multicriteria studies, the results reflect informed subjective judgement rather than direct empirical measurement. Although consistency checks, concordance testing, and robustness analysis were performed, residual uncertainty and contextual bias may remain. The findings should therefore be interpreted as structured decision-support guidance rather than deterministic optimisation outcomes. Future research should extend the framework by incorporating projected sea-level rise scenarios, extreme precipitation modelling, and dynamic climate adaptation variables, and by validating expert-based suitability rankings against empirical monitoring data from operational underground facilities in coastal cities.
