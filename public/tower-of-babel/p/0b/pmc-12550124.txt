Introduction

Diaphragmatic rupture is a rare condition that accounts for less than 1 % of all trauma cases [1], with the majority resulting from a penetrating mechanism (67 %), and the remainder are secondary to blunt injuries (33 %) [2]. The most commonly injured side is the left side, with the stomach being the most common organ to herniate, and the most common cause of blunt diaphragmatic injuries is motor vehicle collisions [3].

A diaphragmatic rupture associated with spleen injury is very rare and can be difficult to diagnose even with advanced imaging [4]. We report a case of a 28-year-old male with a left diaphragmatic rupture associated with a spleen injury encountered in a road traffic accident. The case has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [5].

Case presentation

We report a case of a 28-year-old African male who presented with a history of chest pain two hours before admission, sharp in nature, which increased with breathing in and reduced with breathing out. The pain was radiating to the left shoulder and was associated with difficulty in breathing and chest tightness. The history of coughing blood was negative. These symptoms were associated with frontal and occipital scalp lacerations, bleeding, and a brief loss of consciousness after being involved in the RTA. He reported that he was a passenger in a car when they were hit by a truck. The history of convulsions was unremarkable, as well as the history of vomiting, bleeding from the ears, nose, or mouth.

He was managed according to the ATLS protocol, and was found to have a patent airway, tachypneic with RR-24 bpm, bilateral chest movements, reduced air entry on the left side with SPO2–88 % on room air, and a deviated trachea to the right side. A bleeding scalp laceration of about 5 cm long and 0.5 cm deep on the frontal region, and another of about 4 cm long and 1 cm deep in the occipital region were primarily sutured. The BP was 140/80 mmHg and the PR-120 bpm (rapid, regularly regular). He was confused on arrival with a GCS of 14/15(E4, V4, M6) with no other neurological deficit. Analgesics and oxygenotherapy (3 L/min) were given to the patient as well as broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Discussion

Diaphragmatic injuries are relatively rare, accounting for less than 1 % of all traumatic injuries [6]. They occur in approximately 5 % of patients who undergo laparotomy or thoracotomy due to trauma [7]. The first reported cases of diaphragmatic perforations were documented by Ambroise Paré in a series of autopsy findings [8].

Early diagnosis of diaphragmatic rupture is crucial, as complications like visceral herniation can impair lung inflation and lead to respiratory issues [7]. Symptoms are often minimal or overshadowed by other injuries, complicating clinical diagnosis. Understanding the mechanism of injury can aid in evaluation. Blunt diaphragmatic injuries commonly result from sudden abdominal pressure increase, with motor vehicle accidents [2] being the primary cause. These injuries often accompany other traumas [9], as seen in our case, where the patient had a mild head injury alongside the diaphragmatic injury. The liver and lung injuries are the most common in blunt traumatic diaphragmatic injuries. The spleen injury is encountered in 32 % of cases, with a mortality rate of 13 % [10]. We believe that the grade 3 splenic injury encountered in our case did not lead to hemodynamic instability quickly because it was compressed by the herniated abdominal organs into the chest.

A plain chest X-ray (CXR) is a crucial initial diagnostic tool for any patient with a torso injury. However, its sensitivity varies, ranging from 27 % to 62 % for left-sided injuries and 18 % to 33 % for right-sided injuries [11]. An abnormally shaped diaphragm on CXR should raise suspicion with some cases showing herniation of the abdominal organs into the chest as it was in our case where the left hemidiaphragm was ruptured with abdominal organs herniating into the chest, but in others it might be difficult to distinguish diaphragmatic injury from hemothorax and/or pulmonary contusion and an the only leading signs are non-specific such as loss of diaphragmatic contour, ipsilateral pleural effusion, ipsilateral elevated hemidiaphragm, or mediastinal shift [12].

The nasogastric tube position has been used to diagnose the diaphragmatic rupture by identifying the herniated stomach into the chest; however, few are those that are diagnosed by that method, as demonstrated in the Wisconsin series [13]. The ultrasonography, which is mainly used in trauma patients in emergency departments, may detect the diaphragmatic rupture as a curved hyperechoic structure, and its movement can be observed with respiration [14,15]. The CT scan reduces the rate of missed diagnoses with a sensitivity of 71 % to 100 % and specificity of 75 % to 100 % in blunt trauma [16], and the MRI provides excellent resolution of the diaphragm; however, the signs seen are the same as those seen on a CT scan. In patients with hemodynamic instability, the diagnosis is often made intraoperatively.

Diaphragm injuries are much more easily treated in the acute phase rather than the late [4] and the reduction of herniated organs from the chest to the abdominal cavity, coupled with watertight closure of the diaphragmatic defect, are the cornerstone of acute diaphragmatic hernia repair.

Laparoscopic approach is an option in a stable patient in whom the laparoscopic surgery does not have any contraindications, as demonstrated by Khan et al., who managed laparoscopically a right diaphragmatic rupture secondary to a stab wound [17]. In the non-stable patients or when there is a contraindication for laparoscopic surgery, open surgery becomes the option, and a full midline laparotomy is the most used approach; however, there have been successful operations via a right thoracotomy as reported by Alves and colleagues [18].

Because many abdominal injuries can happen along with blunt traumatic diaphragmatic injury, it is best to repair an acutely injured diaphragm using exploratory laparotomy. This method is also suitable for unstable patients with penetrating injuries [19]. To check the right hemidiaphragm, the falciform ligament ought to be cut and the liver gently pulled down. For the left hemidiaphragm, the spleen and the greater curvature of the stomach should be pulled down to inspect for better inspection.

During the acute phase, it's relatively straightforward to reduce the intra-abdominal contents. In contrast, if this is not possible, a phrenotomy is recommended, taking care to avoid damage to the phrenic nerve. If the defect size permits, the pleural cavity should be examined to identify the source of ongoing bleeding within the ipsilateral (same side) pleural cavity and to evaluate the extent of contamination if the intestines have been perforated. This should be followed by thorough irrigation [20].

Small defects are typically associated with penetrating injuries, while larger defects are more often linked to blunt trauma. These larger defects can be repaired in several ways, including the figure-eight stitch, horizontal mattress sutures, a running hemostatic suture line, or a two-layer repair. For this, a nonabsorbable suture, either monofilament or braided, in sizes 0 or 1, is used. The running nonabsorbable monofilament suture in size 1 is preferred for traumatic diaphragmatic injuries. Care must be taken to avoid injuring the heart or lungs during the repair process.

After the repair, the integrity of the suture line may be tested by increasing intrathoracic pressure with the administration of large tidal volumes and assessment of diaphragmatic motion. This maneuver is repeated with the field flooded with sterile saline to determine if there is escape of air through the suture line.

Conclusion

Traumatic diaphragmatic injury is frequently overlooked or diagnosed too late in patients who have suffered blunt abdominal trauma. A thorough clinical examination, along with appropriate investigations, can aid in diagnosing this condition. In cases where the patient's condition is worsening, surgical exploration is recommended to identify and address the underlying cause of the instability. Additionally, some injuries may be discovered during surgical exploration. Clinicians should maintain a strong suspicion of traumatic diaphragmatic injury in patients with blunt abdominal trauma when the clinical examination reveals abnormalities. This injury can often be obscured by other injuries, such as head trauma, or additional injuries that typically accompany blunt abdominal trauma.
