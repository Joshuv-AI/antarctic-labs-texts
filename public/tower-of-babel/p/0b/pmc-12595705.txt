Respective characteristics and contributions of two cataract surgery delivery models to the eye health national action plan in Rwanda
Authors: Jean Bosco Ndayambaje, Christian Mazimpaka, Jean d’Amour Ndahimana, Theoneste Mutsindashyaka, Innocent Habimana, Jean de Dieu Harerimana, Tiva Kananura, Janvier Mungarulire, Hirwa Sylvain Muzungu, James Humuza, Thierry Claudien Uhawenimana, Robert Kabanda, Jean de Dieu Ndagijimana, Henry Mwangi, Angeline Mumararungu, Ntagara Ngabo Donatien, Caroline Ndolo, Damien Kirchhoffer, Muhammed Semakula
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCataract is the leading cause of avoidable blindness worldwide, and limited surgical access continues to affect elimination efforts, especially in low- and middle-income countries. In line with this burden, Rwanda implemented two complementary models for cataract surgery services: The Mobile Teams Outreach Cataract Surgery Model (MTO-CSM) and the Continuous Hospital-Based Cataract Surgery Model (CHB-CSM). This study compared the performance, quality adherence, efficiency, and cost-effectiveness of the two models.MethodsA retrospective, cross-sectional study was conducted across 10 health facilities (6 implementing MTO-CSM and 4 implementing CHB-CSM) between October 2022 and May 2023, covering data from October 2021 to September 2022. Data sources included hospital surgery registers, the Health Management Information System (HMIS), facility assessments, staff interviews, and financial records. Key indicators included the number of cataract surgeries (CS) performed, adherence to quality standards, total surgery time (in person-minutes), and the cost per CS.ResultsA total of 10,584 CS were performed. District hospitals contributed 2,337 surgeries, mainly under MTO-CSM. Kabgayi District Hospital and Rwanda Charity Eye Hospital, both implementing CHB-CSM, conducted 4,297 and 3,668 surgeries, respectively, accounting for 74% of the total national CS output. Quality compliance was higher at CHB-CSM facilities (an average of 88%, with 100% and 94% at Kabgayi and Rwanda Charity, respectively) compared to 58% in MTO-CSM sites. The average time per surgery was 801 person-minutes for CHB-CSM versus 1,950 person-minutes for MTO-CSM. The cost per surgery was RWF 207,224 (USD 193.7) for CHB-CSM and RWF 349,566 (USD 326.7) for MTO-CSM, making the outreach model 69% more expensive.ConclusionsThe CHB-CSM had a higher surgical volume, better quality adherence, greater time efficiency, and a lower cost per case. In contrast, the MTO-CSM, although more expensive, played an essential role in expanding access to remote areas. A hybrid strategy, integrating the efficiency of continuous services with the outreach model’s equity focus, is recommended to achieve Rwanda’s national cataract surgery targets and the WHO’s 2030 goal for effective cataract surgical coverage (eCSC).Keywords: Cataract surgery, Outreaches, Eye health services, Blindness, Rwanda

Introduction

Globally, 2.2 billion people live with vision impairment or blindness, of whom at least 1 billion have conditions that are preventable [1, 2]. The burden of visual impairment is particularly high in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. Cataract is the leading cause of avoidable blindness globally, accounting for approximately 47.9% of cases [3]. Cataract can be treated through a straightforward, 20-minute surgical procedure; however, barriers such as shortages of trained personnel, limited surgical supplies, inadequate infrastructure, and low patient awareness affect access, particularly in developing countries [4].

To reach its goal of performing 1,200 cataract surgeries per million people by 2024, the Rwanda Ministry of Health adopted two complementary service delivery models: the continuous hospital-based cataract surgery model (CHB-CSM) and the mobile teams outreach cataract surgery model (MTO-CSM). The CHB-CSM is a facility-based program operated within specialized and teaching hospitals, where cataract surgeries are regularly conducted during normal working days. Conversely, the MTO-CSM involves expert surgical teams from these hospitals traveling to district hospitals upon invitation. In the MTO-CSM, district hospitals identify and list cataract patients, schedule surgical sessions, and host visiting ophthalmologists to perform surgeries onsite. Combining these two models is a strategic approach to addressing shortages of skilled eye health personnel, including ophthalmologists, cataract surgeons, optometrists, optical technicians, and low vision specialists, and to increase access to cataract surgery in remote districts.

However, according to the 2022 Rwanda Annual Health Sector Performance Review Report, the CSR remained at 600 per million, roughly half of the national target. Additionally, there is limited evidence on the comparative performance and efficiency of these two models. This study was conducted to compare the performance of CHB-CSM and MTO-CSM, focusing on surgical output, efficiency, cost, and compliance with quality standards, in order to inform evidence-based strategies that will support the country in achieving the national CSR target and the WHO’s 2030 goal to increase effective cataract surgical coverage (eCSC) by 30 percentage points by 2030 (WHO, 2022) (See Fig. 1).

Methods

Study setting

Rwanda’s health system operates through a decentralized, pyramid-shaped model that extends from the community to the national level. It includes 1280 health posts, 516 health centers, 56 district hospitals, four provincial hospitals, and seven national referral hospitals. The system is supported by over 50,000 community health workers at the village level who provide services to households. Community health workers (CHWs) and health posts focus on eye health and making referrals. Health centers offer integrated primary eye care, led by PEC-trained nurses, which includes examinations, visual acuity testing, simple management, presbyopic correction, and referrals. District hospitals diagnose and treat common eye conditions, perform refractions, conduct minor procedures, and provide outreach services. Provincial and teaching hospitals offer comprehensive secondary services, and national referral hospitals deliver tertiary care, including subspecialty diagnostics, low-vision rehabilitation, and cataract surgery. All services follow established referral pathways and are primarily funded by community-based health insurance (Fig. 2).

Cataract patients are diagnosed at the PHC or DH level and referred to the DH or RH level for surgery

As of 2022, Rwanda had 29 ophthalmologists working in both public and private hospitals, with only eight qualified as cataract surgeons. Consequently, most district hospitals are unable to perform cataract surgeries. Only nine hospitals, including Ruhengeri RH, Kibuye RH, Kabgayi TH, Rwanda Charity Eye Hospital, Rwanda Military Hospital, Rwamagana TH, CHUB, Agarwal Eye Hospital, and King Faisal Hospital, are equipped to perform cataract surgeries on a regular basis. Other hospitals schedule outreach cataract surgical sessions once they have at least 30 patients on their waiting list requiring surgery. Outreach teams usually consist of one ophthalmologist, two ophthalmic clinical officers, and two nurses, with sessions often held over three consecutive weekend days.

Study design

The study employed a quantitative, retrospective, cross-sectional design, analyzing data collected from October 2021 to September 2022.

Sampling strategy

We used purposive sampling to capture national practices across various delivery models, capacities, and geographic areas. Ten hospitals were selected based on predefined criteria: active implementation of either MTO-CSM or CHB-CSM during the study period, and documented cataract surgical activity. We ensured representation from various provinces and included different facility levels (district, referral, specialized). Facilities without recent cataract surgeries, those performing only ad hoc missions, or with incomplete records were excluded. The final sample included six district hospitals implementing MTO-CSM and four hospitals (district, referral, specialized) using CHB-CSM (see Table 1).

Data collection

Data were collected between October 2022 and May 2023 using a standardized data collection form and semi-structured questionnaire via Open Data Kit (ODK). Sources included: surgery ward patient records, HMIS, staff rosters, accounting and financial records, and interviews with key personnel (optometric nurse, ophthalmology head, ophthalmic clinical officer, and head of finance/administration) (Table 2).

Variables and outcome definitions

Key indicators assessed included: surgical output (number of CS performed), compliance with quality standards (presence of preoperative testing, infection control, and protocols), time efficiency (average total person-minutes per surgery), waiting time (days from diagnosis to surgery), and cost per surgery (RWF, USD, PPP-$). Surgical outcomes, such as postoperative visual acuity, were not included, as this study focused on service delivery efficiency and cost analysis.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics to compare CHB-CSM and MTO-CSM. Costing was calculated in Rwandan Francs (RWF), US Dollars (USD), and Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) Dollars. The analysis included human resources, equipment availability, consumables, and protocol adherence. Equipment functionality (such as operating microscopes, phaco machines, and intraocular lenses) and staffing levels were reviewed. Waiting time was measured from initial diagnosis to the surgery date. Costing included all direct and indirect expenses: salaries, equipment depreciation, supplies, freight, insurance, import duties, administrative costs, and revenue sources (CBHI, RAMA, private insurance, and out-of-pocket).

Cost analysis: An ingredient-based costing method was used. Direct costs included human resources, supplies, and consumables. Indirect costs covered equipment depreciation, maintenance, and administrative overhead. Depreciation was calculated based on the assets’ useful life, considering annual utilization rates. Salaries were estimated from the national payroll system. Results were summarized per facility and reported in RWF, USD, and PPP-$.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted as part of an umbrella study entitled “Integrating eye health into Rwanda Performance-Based Financing (PBF): A mid-term review,” which was approved by the University of Rwanda’s institutional review board with approval number 119/CMHS IRB/2025.

Results

Total number of cataract surgeries performed

During the study period (October 202-September 2022), a total of 10,584 cataract surgeries were performed across all facility types. About 75% of those surgeries were conducted through the Continuous Hospital-Based Cataract Surgery Model (CHB-CSM) (See Table 3).

Average time spent on cataract surgery

The total person-minutes per cataract surgery varied among different staff levels and hospital types. Ophthalmologists averaged 109 minutes per case, mostly spent on theatre preparation (29 min) and surgery (22 min). Ophthalmic Clinical Officers (OCOs) averaged 417 minutes, mainly on theatre preparation (94 min) and logistics (120 min). Nurses averaged 201 minutes, mostly on theatre preparation (65 min). Anesthesia technicians averaged 82 minutes, primarily spent on preoperative and intraoperative care (17 minutes each). Overall, CHB-CSM required 801 person-minutes per surgery, while MTO-CSM required 1,950 person-minutes, reflecting a 59% difference in time between the two models. (Figs. 4, 5.)

Waiting time between cataract diagnosis and surgery

CHB-CSM facilities have markedly shorter waiting times, with surgery often performed the same day or within one week of diagnosis. By contrast, MTO-CSM facilities frequently delay surgery, sometimes for more than seven weeks (Fig. 5)

Cost per cataract surgery (MSICS procedure)

The CHB-CSM had an average cost of RWF 207,224 per surgery (USD 193.7; PPP-$627.0), compared to RWF 349,566 (USD 326.7; PPP-$1,057.6) under the MTO-CSM (Table 5). This represents a 69% higher cost in the MTO-CSM, with a cost-efficiency ratio of 0.59, in favor of CHB-CSM. Drugs and supplies represented the largest portion of costs (37.7%) in CHB-CSM, whereas in MTO-CSM, equipment accounted for 41.1% of total costs (RWF 143,639 vs. RWF 50,325). Equipment costs were estimated using depreciation and allocated per case; since MTO-CSM produces fewer surgeries, the per-surgery share appears proportionally higher. Personnel costs also vary; MTO-CSM estimates included allowances for visiting staff, whereas base salaries for CHB-CSM teams participating in outreaches, incurred in both models, were fully allocated to continuous settings but not duplicated in outreach (See Table 6).

Discussion

This study assessed the performance, quality, efficiency, and cost-effectiveness of two cataract surgery delivery models in Rwanda. Results highlight significant differences between the two models with important implications for policy, clinical practice, and resource allocation.

Most cataract surgeries were performed at two specialized centers: Kabgayi District Hospital and Rwanda Charity Eye Hospital, both of which operate under the CHB-CSM. These centers accounted for about 74% of all CS performed nationwide. This performance reflects variations in surgical capacity, infrastructure, and resource availability, as well as the organizational benefits of continuous service delivery models, which include dedicated staff, permanent equipment, and efficient workflows. These findings align with studies from other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), which show that high-volume, continuous surgical models are more efficient and productive [5, 6]. Additionally, adherence to quality standards was higher in CHB-CSM settings compared to MTO-CSM settings. This likely reflects resource limitations, sporadic staff availability, and weaker supervision mechanisms during outreaches. Similar findings have been reported in other LMICs, where outreach programs often face trade-offs between coverage and quality [6].

The average total time per actual surgery was lower in CHB-CSM settings (801 person-minutes) compared to MTO-CSM settings (1,950 person-minutes). This difference results from better coordination, routine workflows, and team familiarity within CHB-CSM hospitals. CHB-CSM handles a high volume of CS and typically achieves efficiencies due to the learning curve, shortened cycle, and reduced unit cost as seen in other LMIC contexts [5]. MTO-CSM typically bears setup, transport, and ad-hoc team coordination, which lengthens the procedure and increases cost. Additionally, CHB-CSM facilities had shorter waiting times between diagnosis and surgery, suggesting that routine staffing, dedicated theatre time, and stable patient flow translate into faster treatment after diagnosis.

A cost analysis showed that CS under CHB-CSM was more cost-effective (RWF 207,224; USD 193.7) than those under MTO-CSM (RWF 349,566; USD 326.7), with a 41% cost difference. This suggests that CHB-CSM hospitals benefit from fixed infrastructure and economies of scale, resulting in a lower cost per case. These findings align with studies from India and sub-Saharan Africa, which may indicate that high-volume, hospital-based models using Manual Small Incision Cataract Surgery (MSICS) are more efficient and have lower unit costs [5].

Our findings highlight the complementary roles of CHB-CSM and MTO-CSM in achieving Rwanda’s national cataract surgery target. To accelerate progress, CHB-CSM services should be strengthened and expanded to reliably deliver high-volume, cost-effective surgeries. Although contributing fewer surgeries than CHB-CSM, MTO-CSM must be maintained and improved to ensure equitable access to cataract surgery. This model remains crucial for promoting equity by serving remote and underserved populations [7]. Additionally, improving the quality of outreach cataract services is important. Evidence shows that standardized surgical protocols, structured follow-up systems, and integration with local referral pathways improve safety and outcomes. Efficiency can be strengthened by advanced scheduling, bulk procurement of consumables, and clear task allocation within teams. Costs may be reduced by sharing equipment across facilities, using mobile surgical units, and optimizing patient flow to maximize daily surgical volume [8].

This study has some limitations. First, it did not include patient-incurred costs, nor did it account for postoperative and follow-up costs in either model. We also did not evaluate clinical or patient-reported outcomes, which would have helped us assess differences in effectiveness and safety. Future research should include both direct and indirect patient costs, cover postoperative and follow-up expenses, and compare outcomes across models. Despite these limitations, the study offers actionable insights into system-level organization and access, which can inform near-term strategies to enhance cataract surgery service access and coverage in Rwanda and similar settings.
