Effects of ohmic heating–modified okara on the structural characteristics and quality properties of heat-induced myofibrillar protein gels
Authors: Zhongwen Cao, Chengcheng Xie, Xingyu Liu, Guangyi Bie, Xiangren Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effects of ohmic-heated and untreated okara, incorporated at different concentrations, on the physicochemical properties, structural characteristics, and functional performance of myofibrillar protein (MP) gels. Compared with the control MP gel without okara addition, moderate incorporation significantly improved gel quality (p < 0.05), with water-holding capacity increasing from 61.32% to 69.78% and gel strength enhanced by 37.81%. Among all treatments, ohmic-treated okara exhibited superior performance, as evidenced by a denser gel microstructure, higher storage modulus, and reduced protein carbonyl formation. Spectroscopic analysis revealed that ohmic-modified okara promoted a decrease in α-helix content accompanied by an increase in β-sheet structures, indicating alterations in secondary and tertiary structures. The incorporation of okara influenced intermolecular interactions, including hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, contributing to improved MP network formation. Overall, ohmic heating represents a potentially effective approach to enhance the functional properties of okara for its application in meat gel systems.

Introduction

Myofibrillar protein (MP) is the major structural protein in meat, accounting for approximately 50–60% of total muscle proteins. It plays a pivotal role in thermally induced gelation, significantly influencing key quality attributes of meat products, such as gelation, emulsification, and water-holding capacity (Li, Wang and Chen, 2021, Wang et al., 2023). The gelation process primarily involves the denaturation, aggregation, and cross-linking of myosin, wherein the head region initially aggregates to form a protein network, followed by the unfolding of the tail to establish a compact three-dimensional gel matrix (Bai et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2023). Due to its excellent gelling properties, MP is considered a critical functional component that governs the texture and sensory characteristics of emulsified meat products (Cen et al., 2022). However, the gelation behavior of MP is susceptible to various factors, including raw meat quality, heating conditions, pH, and ionic strength. To overcome these limitations, researchers have explored the use of exogenous additives such as hydrocolloids and enzymes, as well as emerging processing technologies including ultrasound, microwave, and pulsed electric fields, to improve gel quality (Choi and Chin, 2024, Guo et al., 2024). Among these strategies, the incorporation of dietary fibers has also proven effective in enhancing the physical, chemical, and functional properties of MP gels. Previous studies have demonstrated that plant-derived fibers such as sugarcane, citrus, and oat fibers—rich in soluble dietary fibers and bioactive components—can significantly improve the water-holding capacity and gel strength of MP gels. These fibers have also been reported to affect nutrient digestion and satiety, although such effects were not investigated in the present study and are mentioned here for background context only. (Wang et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2023; Zhuang et al., 2017). Similarly, okara is abundant in dietary fiber, particularly insoluble fractions; however, its limited solubility and functional properties–including psize, surface chemistry, and hydration capacity–restrict its direct application. Therefore, structural modification is necessary to enhance its interactions with myofibrillar proteins and unlock its potential to improve gel quality.

Okara, the insoluble residue obtained after the extraction of the soluble fraction during soymilk and tofu production, is produced in large quantities—with approximately 1.2 kg generated for every 1 kg of tofu produced (Privatti & Rodrigues, 2023; Mateos-Aparicio et al., 2010). It is rich in proteins, dietary fiber, polysaccharides, isoflavones, and bioactive peptides, and exhibits notable antioxidant activity and potential health benefits (Vong & Liu, 2016; Nagano et al., 2020). Nevertheless, the practical application of okara in the food industry remains limited due to its high perishability, beany flavor, and coarse texture (Feng et al., 2025). To address these challenges, various modification techniques—including physical, chemical, biological, and combinatory methods—have been employed to enhance the physicochemical properties and bioactivity of okara (Fan et al., 2021; Lazarin et al., 2023; Yoshida & Prudencio, 2020; Lei et al., 2025; Zhan et al., 2023). Among them, physical modification has attracted increasing attention owing to its high efficiency and eco-friendly nature (Lin et al., 2025).

Ohmic heating (OH), an emerging internal heating technology, offers advantages such as rapid and uniform heating and high energy conversion efficiency. In recent years, OH has been widely applied in the extraction and structural modification of bioactive components in food systems. Compared with conventional thermal processing, OH has been reported to cause less damage to bioactive compounds (Salari & Jafari, 2020; Al-Hilphy et al., 2020), achieve higher extraction efficiency (Cabas & Icier, 2021; Álvarez-Chávez et al., 2025), shorten processing time (Talha et al., 2024), and exhibit better environmental sustainability. Prior to this study, a preliminary experiment was conducted to optimize the ohmic-heating (OH) conditions for okara modification. Okara samples were treated at electric field strengths of 40, 45, and 50 V/cm and held at 100 °C for 3, 6, or 9 min, with untreated and water-bath-heated okara as controls. The results demonstrated that OH treatment significantly improved the physicochemical properties of okara, including water-holding capacity, oil-holding capacity, swelling capacity, microstructure, and secondary structure, compared with untreated and water-bath-heated samples. Furthermore, OH treatment enhanced the retention and content of bioactive components, increased soluble dietary fiber and total sugar content, and improved antioxidant activity. Among all tested conditions, okara treated at 45 V/cm with a holding time of 6 min exhibited the most desirable overall properties, justifying its selection for incorporation into myofibrillar protein gels (detailed data are provided in Appendix 1) (Cao et al., 2025). Notably, ohmic modification not only alters the physicochemical properties of okara, but also changes its fiber network, water-binding capacity, and surface hydrophobicity/hydrophilicity. Such structural changes may facilitate interactions between okara fibers and myofibrillar proteins during gelation, leading to a more uniform and denser gel network. Additionally, the retention of bioactive components, increased soluble dietary fiber, and total sugar content in ohmic-modified okara may further enhance the functional properties of MP gels. Therefore, this study investigates both the macroscopic properties and mechanistic effects of ohmic-modified okara on MP gel structure and functionality, providing a scientific basis for its application in meat product functional improvement (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Thermally Induced Gelation of Myofibrillar Protein (MP).Fig. 1

Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the effects of OH-modified okara on the thermally induced gelation properties of pork myofibrillar protein (MP) gels. Different levels of OH-modified okara were incorporated into MP gels, and their impact on protein oxidation, water-holding capacity, gel strength, intermolecular interactions, secondary and tertiary structure, and microstructure was evaluated. The objective of this study was to identify optimal incorporation levels of OH-modified okara and provide a novel approach for enhancing the functional and structural properties of meat gel systems, thereby broadening the potential applications of okara.

Materials and methods

Materials

Crude okara was purchased from Qilu Flavor Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). It was dried in a 60 °C oven for 6 h, then ground and passed through an 80-mesh sieve. Pork hind leg meat (large white crossbred, within 2 days post-mortem) was obtained from Metro Supermarket (Yangzhou, China). All chemical reagents used in this study were of analytical grade and purchased from Nanjing Basite Chemical Co., Ltd.

Sample preparation

Ohmic heating modification

Ohmic heating was performed using a self-developed laboratory-scale OH device. The system consisted of an AC power supply (ANJ13-1kVA, Anais Power Supply, Jiangsu, China), a needle-type thermocouple (HY005, Fuyang Instrumentation, Guangdong, China), a control computer, and a quartz heating chamber (inner dimensions: 7.6 × 7.6 × 7.8 cm) equipped with two parallel stainless steel electrodes. Okara and deionized water were mixed at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:10 (w/v) and placed into the chamber, which was set on a magnetic stirrer to ensure sample homogeneity. Based on preliminary experiments (Cao et al., 2025), the treatment conditions selected were a 45 V/cm voltage gradient, 2000 Hz frequency, heating to 100 °C, followed by a 6 min holding period. The untreated and treated samples were freeze-dried and passed through an 80-mesh sieve for future use.

MP extraction

To extract MP, 2 g of minced pork was mixed with 8 mL of pre-chilled phosphate buffer (PBS, pH 7.2–7.4). The mixture was homogenized twice for 30 s each at 12,000 rpm in an ice bath, followed by centrifugation at 2000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet was washed twice with the same buffer under the same conditions, then washed twice more with 0.1 M NaCl solution. The final precipitate was collected as MP and stored at 0–4 °C for use within 48 h. The concentration of extracted MP was determined to be approximately 70 mg/mL using the biuret method (Jiang et al., 2024).

Preparation of MP-okara gel system

Both OH-treated and untreated okara powders were added to MP solution at concentrations of 0.2%, 0.4%, 0.6%, and 0.8% (w/w). The mixtures were homogenized at (TGL16M, Shanghai Luxiangyi Centrifuge Instrument Co., Ltd.) 10,000 rpm for 30 s at 4 °C to ensure even dispersion. Samples were centrifuged at 3000 ×g for 2 min at 4 °C to remove air bubbles. Thermal gelation was then induced by heating from 20 °C to 80 °C at a rate of 2 °C/min in a water bath and holding for 30 min. The resulting gels were cooled rapidly in ice water and stored at 4 °C for 12 h before analysis.

Carbonyl content

Protein carbonyl content was determined using the 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) method with absorbance measured at 370 nm. Briefly, 1 mL of 2 mg/mL MP solution was mixed with 1 mL of 10% trichloroacetic acid (TCA), centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The pellet was treated with 1 mL of DNPH solution (0.2% DNPH in 2 M HCl) and incubated at room temperature for 1 h, with 2 M HCl as a blank. After incubation, 0.5 mL of 20% TCA was added, followed by centrifugation and three sequential washes with ethanol: ethyl acetate (1:1, v/v) until colorless. The final pellet was dissolved in 1 mL of 6 M guanidine hydrochloride, incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, centrifuged, and absorbance was read at 370 nm (Sun et al., 2024).Carbonyl contentnmol/mg=106×A/C×ε×bwhere A = absorbance, b = path length (0.54 cm), C = protein concentration (mg/mL), ε = molar extinction coefficient (22,000 L·mol−1·cm−1).

Total sulfhydryl content

Total sulfhydryl groups were determined using 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) with absorbance read at 412 nm. A 1 mL aliquot of 2 mg/mL MP was mixed with 9 mL of solution B (50 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.0, containing 10 mM EDTA, 0.6 M KCl, and 8 M urea). Then, 4 mL of this solution was reacted with 0.4 mL of 0.1% DTNB at 40 °C for 25 min. Surface hydrophobicity

MP solution (2 mg/mL) was prepared in phosphate buffer (pH 6.5). A volume of 200 μL of bromophenol blue (BPB) solution was added to 1 mL of MP, mixed, and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. After centrifugation (8000 rpm, 10 min), the supernatant was diluted 10-fold and absorbance was measured at 595 nm. Water-holding capacity (WHC)

Gel samples were placed in centrifuge tubes with filter paper and centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. WHC was expressed as the ratio of the weight after centrifugation to the initial weight (w/w) (Jiang et al., 2021).

Gel strength

After equilibrating at room temperature for 2 h, gel strength was measured using a P/0.5 cylindrical probe. The pre-test, test, and post-test speeds were set at 2.0, 1.0, and 2.0 mm/s, respectively. A trigger force of 5 g and a compression ratio of 50% were used (Gao et al., 2023).

Intermolecular forces

Approximately 1 g of gel sample was dispersed in 5 mL of each of the following solvents:

A (0.05 M NaCl), B (0.6 M NaCl), C (0.6 M NaCl +1.5 M urea), D (0.6 M NaCl +8 M urea), and E (0.6 M NaCl +8 M urea +0.5 M β-mercaptoethanol). Each mixture was homogenized for 15 s and incubated at 4 °C for 60 min. After centrifugation at 10,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C, protein content in the supernatant was determined (Lv et al., 2021).

Rheological properties

A rheometer equipped with parallel plates (diameter: 40 mm, gap: 1 mm) was used to measure thermal gelation behavior. The MP solutions were heated from 20 °C to 80 °C at a rate of 2 °C/min under oscillatory shear at a fixed frequency of 0.1 Hz and strain of 0.02. This strain value was selected based on preliminary strain sweep tests to ensure that measurements were conducted within the linear viscoelastic region (LVR). A thin layer of silicone oil was applied to the sample edge to prevent dehydration (Jiang et al., 2020).

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

Freeze-dried samples (1 mg) were ground with 100 mg KBr and pressed into pellets. FTIR spectra were recorded from 4000 to 450 cm−1 using a Frontier FTIR spectrometer (PerkinElmer, Boston, MA, USA). Background spectra were collected and subtracted prior to analysis. All spectra were subjected to baseline correction and normalized before further interpretation to minimize potential scattering artifacts associated with the KBr pellet method (Sow et al., 2019).

Tertiary structure analysis

Lyophilized and powdered samples were analyzed using a UV–V is spectrophotometer. Scans were performed from 200 to 400 nm at a speed of 10 nm/s. UV–V is spectra were used to provide indirect information regarding changes in the microenvironment of aromatic residues, which may reflect alterations in protein tertiary structure. (Wang et al., 2022).

Microstructure observation

Gel samples (10 × 5 × 5 mm) were freeze-dried and gold-coated before being examined using a scanning electron microscope (S-3400 N, Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at 300× magnification (Kong et al., 2023).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Results were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan's multiple range test (p < 0.05) were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 27. Graphs were created using Origin 2021.

Results and discussion

Protein oxidation

Protein oxidation is a critical factor affecting the structural integrity and functional stability of proteins. The total sulfhydryl content reflects the integrity and reducing status of thiol groups within protein molecules (Li et al., 2020), whereas the carbonyl content indicates the accumulation of oxidative products derived from protein side chains (Pan et al., 2021). As shown in Fig. 2, the total sulfhydryl content showed higher values at 0.4% and 0.6% compared with other groups. Higher sulfhydryl levels were observed at 0.4% and 0.6% addition levels, suggesting lower oxidative damage of myofibrillar proteins at these concentrations. The subsequent decrease at 0.8% may be attributed to the higher proportion of okara particles in the gel network, which could lead to uneven protein-fiber interactions and local microenvironments that slightly favor oxidative reactions. Notably, in all corresponding addition levels, the B group displayed significantly higher total sulfhydryl contents than the A group (p < 0.05), indicating that ohmic heating treatment could effectively mitigate the oxidation of sulfhydryl groups. This effect can be attributed, at least in part, to the enhanced antioxidant properties of ohmic-heated okara. As shown in the Supplementary Data, ohmic treatment significantly increased total antioxidant capacity, total phenolic content, and total flavonoid content compared with untreated okara, which may contribute to the reduced formation of protein carbonyls and better preservation of sulfhydryl groups in myofibrillar proteins (Barrón-García et al., 2021). In terms of carbonyl content, the B group exhibited less fluctuation across different addition levels and showed significantly lower values than the A group at 0.4%, 0.6%, and 0.8% inclusion levels (p < 0.05). These results indicate that the enhanced antioxidant properties of ohmic-treated okara may play a role in suppressing carbonyl formation in proteins, consistent with the observed lower carbonyl content and higher sulfhydryl levels in the treated gels. The antioxidant effect of ohmic treatment is likely due to the enhanced release of functional components such as phenolics and polysaccharides, which contributed to oxidative resistance and protein protection (Ramírez-Jiménez et al., 2019).In summary, compared with untreated okara, ohmic-treated okara more effectively maintained the reducing status of protein sulfhydryl groups and reduced the formation of carbonyl compounds at the same inclusion levels. Analysis of surface hydrophobicity

Fig. 3 illustrates the changes in surface hydrophobicity of pork myofibrillar protein under different treatments. Surface hydrophobicity reflects the degree of exposure of hydrophobic residues within protein molecules and serves as an important indicator for assessing conformational changes and aggregation behavior (Li et al., 2025). The results showed that the control group exhibited the lowest surface hydrophobicity. With the addition of okara, the surface hydrophobicity of proteins increased significantly, reaching higher levels at 0.4% and 0.6%. Among them, group B exhibited the highest surface hydrophobicity, peaking at 0.4% and being significantly higher than group A (p < 0.05). This result suggested that moderate addition of okara promoted partial relaxation of protein structures, leading to the exposure of hydrophobic groups and enhanced hydrophobic interactions among protein molecules (Wei et al., 2025). These structural alterations may have contributed, at least in part, to the improved gel strength observed in this group. However, gel formation is a complex process regulated by multiple intermolecular forces, including hydrophobic interactions, disulfide bonds, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions (Cheng et al., 2025). Therefore, surface hydrophobicity should be considered as one of the contributing factors rather than the sole determinant of gel strength. When comparing different addition levels, it was observed that increasing the okara content to 0.8% resulted in a certain decline in surface hydrophobicity, particularly in group A, where the reduction was more pronounced. This phenomenon might be associated with the potential aggregation or heterogeneous dispersion of excessive okara within the system, which could limit effective protein–protein interactions and reduce the exposure of hydrophobic groups (Coelho et al., 2023). However, this assumption requires further verification through psize distribution or dispersion analysis in future studies. Overall, group B demonstrated a more pronounced improvement compared with group A, further confirming that modification treatment improved the interaction characteristics between okara and proteins. In combination with rheological results, the increase in surface hydrophobicity was consistent with the enhancement of gel elasticity, indicating that moderate OH-modified okara enhanced gel quality by regulating protein conformation and hydrophobic interactions.Fig. 3Surface hydrophobicity of myofibrillar protein under different treatments MP: untreated myofibrillar protein gel; A: gel with untreated okara added; B: gel with ohmic-treated okara added; 0.2%, 0.4%, 0.6%, 0.8%: levels of okara addition. Although these correspond to relative increases of 60.71% and 54.51%, respectively, it should be noted that the baseline WHC of the MP group was comparatively low. In terms of gel strength, B-0.4% also showed the highest value (70.55 g), compared with 51.19 g in the MP group. This represents an increase of approximately 19 gThese findings are in agreement with previous studies. For instance, Lu et al. (2023) reported that the WHC of pork MP gels increased from 56.68% to 89.84% and gel strength from 11.57 N·mm to 23.89 N·mm as the addition of insoluble dietary fiber from Lentinus edodes stipes increased from 1.0% to 2.5%. Similarly, Pereira et al. (2021) found that the addition of moderate levels of coconut kernel fiber significantly enhanced the structural and water-retention properties of MP gels. Okara is rich in insoluble dietary fiber and possesses a highly porous structure that contributes to water absorption, thus reducing free water and improving WHC (Fan et al., 2020). Within a suitable concentration range (0.2–0.6%, w/w), okara fibers may function as structural fillers within the predominantly protein-based gel matrix. At this relatively low incorporation level, the fiber-to-protein ratio remains limited, allowing okara particles to be embedded within the continuous protein network and contribute to mechanical reinforcement. However, when the addition level increases further (≥0.8%), the relative proportion of fiber becomes higher compared to the protein matrix, which may disturb the continuity of the gel network. Excessive fiber incorporation could introduce steric hindrance and intensify competition for water, thereby impairing protein aggregation and gelation, ultimately leading to reduced WHC and gel strength (Zhang, Duan, et al., 2023).

It is noteworthy that at the same concentration, the B group consistently showed significantly higher WHC and gel strength than the A group (p < 0.05). This could be attributed to the structural modifications induced by OH, such as increased surface area, which enhanced the interaction between okara fibers and myofibrillar proteins and facilitated the formation of a more compact gel matrix (Barrón-García et al., 2021). Therefore, the addition of moderate levels (0.4% and 0.6%) of ohmic-treated okara exhibited a greater improvement in the gel quality of MP systems.

Analysis of intermolecular interactions

The formation of MP gels involves multiple chemical forces, including ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, and disulfide bonds. These interactions work synergistically to induce protein structural rearrangements, thereby influencing the gelation properties of the protein (Mi et al., 2022). However, as the addition level increased, a decreasing trend in various chemical forces was observed, which may be attributed to excessive okara competing with proteins for water and forming physical barriers between protein molecules, thereby limiting their effective interactions and orderly alignment (Wang et al., 2021). Among all treatment groups, Group B exhibited a more pronounced enhancement effect than Group A at most concentrations, particularly at the addition levels of 0.4% and 0.6% (p < 0.05). This phenomenon may be related to structural or surface property modifications of okara induced by ohmic treatment (He et al., 2020), such as partial disruption of the cell wall structure and increased exposure of functional groups. These structural alterations might enhance the potential for intermolecular interactions between okara components and proteins, including ionic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic associations (Yin et al., 2025). However, it should be noted that these interaction changes were inferred indirectly based on physicochemical and structural analyses rather than directly quantified. Therefore, they are proposed as possible contributing mechanisms. Among these interactions, enhanced hydrophobic associations may partially contribute to gel reinforcement and stabilization of the gel network (Chu et al., 2022). Meanwhile, the significant increase in disulfide bond content observed in Group B was accompanied by a simultaneous increase in total sulfhydryl groups. This phenomenon suggests that protein structural unfolding may have exposed previously buried sulfhydryl residues, leading to an overall increase in detectable sulfhydryl content. Under these conditions, partial oxidation and thiol-disulfide interchange reactions may occur concurrently, resulting in enhanced disulfide bond formation without overall sulfhydryl depletion (Yang et al., 2021). Disulfide bonds and hydrophobic interactions are the predominant chemical forces within the gel matrix, as they facilitate the formation of a stronger and more elastic three-dimensional protein network, thereby effectively promoting gel formation (Wei et al., 2024). Therefore, ohmic-treated okara significantly enhanced the cross-linking density and structural stability of the protein gel network by strengthening its chemical interactions with proteins. Analysis of rheological properties

Fig. 6 presents the changes in storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) of myofibrillar protein (MP) gels under different treatments as a function of temperature. Overall, all samples exhibited a typical thermally induced gelation process. At the initial stage (20–40 °C), both G′ and G″ showed little variation, indicating that the system remained in a sol state (Wang et al., 2018). Subsequently, in the range of 50–60 °C, both G' and G" decreased markedly, corresponding to the dissociation of the myosin head-tail structure and partial unfolding of the head domain, which temporarily disrupted the protein gel network (Zhang et al., 2025). As the temperature increased to 65–75 °C, G' rapidly rose and reached a plateau, whereas G" peaked and then slightly declined. This phase corresponds to the unfolding of the myosin tail region, which exposes hydrophobic residues and promotes disulfide bond formation, enhancing intermolecular interactions and facilitating the reconstruction and stabilization of the gel network (Ma et al., 2025). These observations align with literature describing the distinct thermal denaturation behavior of myosin head and tail domains.Fig. 6Effects of okara under different treatments on the temperature-dependent changes of G′ (A) and G″ (B) of myofibrillar protein gels.Fig. 6

Effects of okara under different treatments on the temperature-dependent changes of G′ (A) and G″ (B) of myofibrillar protein gels.

In terms of group comparison, the control group exhibited the lowest G′ and G″ values, suggesting a relatively loose gel network with weak elastic and viscous contributions. The moduli of group A were generally higher than those of MP, indicating that okara particles enhanced intermolecular interactions among proteins to some extent (Nie et al., 2025). Notably, groups A2 and A3 showed markedly higher G′ values during the high-temperature recovery stage, along with more pronounced G″ peaks compared with A1, suggesting that an appropriate amount of okara facilitated uniform gel network construction and improved energy dissipation (Nutter et al., 2025). However, when the addition level reached 0.8%, both the final G′ values and G″ peaks decreased, implying that excessive okara disrupted the protein-to-fiber ratio in the system, thereby hindering further cross-linking.

In comparison, group B exhibited more pronounced improvements. Specifically, B2 and B3 demonstrated higher G' recovery and final values in the later heating stage, accompanied by prominent G" peaks, outperforming both group A and the control. These rheological behaviors may reflect enhanced energy dissipation and viscoelasticity, which are often associated with gel toughness (Huang et al., 2020; Li et al., 2020). Such effects are also supported by mechanical measurements, including gel strength and WHC, indicating that the modification treatment improved the dispersibility and interfacial properties of okara, facilitating its interaction with proteins to construct a denser and more stable three-dimensional network (Nie et al., 2025). Nevertheless, B4 showed a decline in moduli, confirming that excessive addition impaired the uniformity of the gel system.

In summary, the moderate incorporation of OH-modified okara effectively improved the rheological properties of MP gels, endowing them with both higher strength and greater toughness. An addition level of 0.4%–0.6% proved optimal. These findings provided a theoretical basis for the functional application of okara in meat product processing.

For instance, in group B2, β-sheet increased to 39.97% and α-helix decreased to 19.17%, accompanied by minor decreases in β-turn (15.59%) and random coil (25.27%). Similar trends were observed for B1, B3, and B4. These quantitative results confirm that okara addition promotes α-helix-to-β-sheet transitions, which may contribute to a more compact and stable gel network (Xiao et al., 2020). The region at 1540–1515 cm−1 (Amide II) is another important marker of protein secondary structure. After the addition of okara, particularly in the B group, the absorption peaks became more pronounced, suggesting the formation of new hydrogen bonds or interactions between proteins and okara (Lin et al., 2019). The absorption band at 1075–1078 cm−1 is related to C—O and C–O–C bending vibrations, characteristic of polysaccharides and cellulose. The enhanced peaks in this region indicate the involvement of polysaccharides from okara, with stronger absorption observed in the B group. This suggests that OH treatment strengthened the structural contribution of okara, as previous studies have shown that OH treatment can accelerate the release of carbohydrates from okara (Zhang, Xu, et al., 2023). Compared with the A group, the hydrogen bonding region in the B group showed stronger absorption, indicating that OH-treated okara enhanced hydrogen bonding either among proteins or between proteins and fibers (Yin et al., 2019). Compared to the control group, the β-sheet content increased across all treatment groups, suggesting that the addition of okara is associated with the formation of more ordered protein structures and potentially enhanced structural stability (Yang et al., 2023). Meanwhile, α-helix content showed a decreasing trend, particularly in the B group, indicating that OH treatment may have modified the structural characteristics of okara and influenced its interaction with myofibrillar proteins, which is correlated with the observed α-helix to β-sheet transition (Feng et al., 2020). These structural changes are consistent with the macroscopic gel properties observed in the previous sections: the gels in the B group exhibited higher gel strength, storage modulus (G'), and improved water-holding capacity. This suggests that ohmic-treated okara not only altered its molecular structure but also promoted protein-fiber interactions, forming a more compact and stable gel network, thereby directly enhancing the rheological and textural properties. In summary, OH treatment enhanced the structural features and reactivity of okara, facilitating more effective interactions with proteins and thereby improving the conformational characteristics and structural stability of the gel system.Fig. 7FTIR spectra of okara-pork myofibrillar protein gels (a), secondary structure fitting curves (b), and distribution of secondary structure components (c).Fig. 7

Tertiary structure

The tertiary structure plays a crucial role in maintaining the functional activity and structural stability of proteins (Álvarez-Machancoses et al., 2020). As shown in Fig. 8, all samples exhibited a characteristic absorption peak near 280 nm, which corresponds to the absorbance of aromatic amino acid residues in proteins (Kim et al., 2024). Compared with the control group, the absorbance of samples increased after the addition of okara, indicating that okara may alter the protein conformation and expose more hydrophobic groups (Zaman et al., 2020). Notably, the B group exhibited significantly higher absorbance than the A group, suggesting that ohmic-heated okara more readily interacts with proteins, promotes partial unfolding, and enhances the exposure of aromatic residues (Jiang et al., 2025). As the addition level increased, the absorbance gradually rose and tended to approach a plateau at higher addition levels (0.6–0.8%), indicating a possible threshold in the conformational impact of okara. In summary, the addition of okara, especially after ohmic heating, can significantly affect the tertiary structure of myofibrillar proteins and enhance their UV absorbance properties.Fig. 8Effect of different treatments and levels of okara addition on the UV absorbance spectra of pork myofibrillar proteins.Fig. 8

Microstructure analysis

As shown in Fig. 9, the scanning electron microscopy images illustrate the effects of differently treated okara on the microstructure of MP gels. The control group exhibited a typical porous network structure; however, the pore distribution was uneven, and some regions showed collapse, indicating a loose and less stable gel structure (Zhou et al., 2019). In the A group, as the addition level increased, the gel network became increasingly rough and disordered, with significantly enlarged pores. In particular, samples A3 and A4 exhibited thickened pore walls and more frequent network breakage, suggesting that a high amount of untreated okara, acting as an unstable filler, may interfere with protein–protein crosslinking and weaken the integrity of the gel matrix (Wu et al., 2018). These structural differences are observed qualitatively in the micrographs, likely due to the presence of large fiber particles in untreated okara, which possess poor surface hydrophobicity and low dispersibility (Sun et al., 2014). In contrast, the B group displayed a more compact, regular, and homogeneous porous structure. Especially in samples B1 and B2, the pore walls were thinner and the network structure more continuous, suggesting that a moderate amount of ohmic-heated okara may be associated with the promotion of protein network formation, rather than implying direct causation by the electric field. This may be due to microstructural changes induced by the electric field during ohmic heating, which improve the dispersibility and surface activity of okara (Cokgezme & Icier, 2022), thereby enhancing its interaction with proteins through non-covalent forces and facilitating gel network construction. Importantly, these microstructural observations are consistent with the previously reported macroscopic gel properties. Compared with the control and A groups, the more compact and continuous network in the B group is associated with higher gel strength, greater water-holding capacity, and improved rheological performance. This indicates that the structural reinforcement of the gel network by ohmic-treated okara directly contributes to its superior functional properties. Although pore size slightly increased in B3 and B4, the overall structure remained superior to that of the untreated group, suggesting that ohmic heating can serve as an effective modification strategy to reinforce the structural functionality of okara in protein-based gel systems.Fig. 9Effects of differently treated okara on the microstructure of myofibrillar protein gels.Fig. 9

Effects of differently treated okara on the microstructure of myofibrillar protein gels.

Conclusion

This study was conducted at the laboratory scale under controlled OH treatment conditions. While the findings provide valuable mechanistic insights, the effects of OH-modified okara in complex food systems or at industrial scales remain to be investigated. Additionally, only selected physicochemical and structural parameters were measured; other functional properties, such as sensory attributes or long-term storage stability, should be explored in future work.

This study systematically investigated the effects of OH treatment and different addition levels of okara on the physicochemical properties, structural characteristics, and functional performance of pork myofibrillar protein (MP) gels. The conceptual mechanism of OH-modified okara interacting with myofibrillar proteins is summarized in Fig. 10. Compared with previous studies using untreated okara, our results show that OH-treated okara not only enhances water-holding capacity and gel strength, but also promotes favorable secondary and tertiary structural transitions, improves storage modulus, and reduces protein oxidation. Notably, the most desirable gel properties were generally observed at moderate addition levels (approximately 0.4–0.6%), suggesting that this range may represent the optimal incorporation level of OH-treated okara in MP gel systems. These findings provide new insights into the mechanisms by which OH treatment modifies okara functionality and protein-okara interactions. While the results suggest potential for application in meat products, further studies are needed to evaluate scale-up feasibility and formulation constraints before practical industrial implementation.Fig. 10Schematic illustration of the interaction mechanism between OH-modified okara and myofibrillar protein.Fig. 10
