Clinical validation of MyCog Mobile: development of a parsimonious and clinically interpretable prediction model for mild cognitive impairment
Authors: Stephanie Ruth Young, Elizabeth M Dworak, Katherina Hauner, Julia Yoshino Benavente, Michael S Wolf, Cindy J Nowinski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo develop and validate a prespecified logistic model for detecting mild cognitive impairment (MCI) using MyCog Mobile, a self-administered smartphone-based screening application, and to evaluate a structured simplification that reduces patient burden while maintaining diagnostic accuracy.Materials and MethodsWe analyzed data from 277 older adults (100 electronic health record-confirmed MCI; 177 normal cognitive aging). Guided by the Harrell/Regression Modeling Strategies framework, a prespecified 10-predictor model was compared against reduced models using Wald χ2 partitioning. Internal validation used 2000 bootstrap (BS) resamples to calculate optimism-corrected C-statistics/area under the receiver operating characteristic, calibration, and clinical utility via decision curve analysis. Sensitivity analyses compared the primary model to bootstrapped and cross-validated regularized regression approaches (LASSO, Ridge, Elastic Net) to confirm model stability.ResultsThe final parsimonious model included 6 predictors and achieved an optimism-corrected C-statistic of 0.812 with excellent calibration (slope = 0.92). Detection accuracy was 75% (BS 95% CI, 69%-80%), consistent with penalized regression models in sensitivity analyses (accuracy 72%-73%), with overlapping CIs confirming predictive stability. Decision curve analysis showed the model provides net benefit over both “refer-all” and “refer-none” strategies across all examined thresholds, capturing ∼55% of the net benefit achievable by a theoretically perfect screener.ConclusionThe final model prioritized parsimony to reduce patient burden while maintaining clinical accuracy to detect MCI. Stability across traditional regression and regularized regression approaches from the statistical learning literature indicated a robust predictive signal. Findings support MyCog Mobile as an accurate and accessible cognitive screener able to detect the earliest signs of cognitive impairment in primary care.

Introduction

Background and significance

Cognitive impairment in older adulthood (65 years and older) is a critical global health crisis,1 and it is important to differentiate pathological decline from normal cognitive aging (NCA). Early detection of cognitive impairment is important to identify potentially reversible causes, manage symptoms and comorbidities, determine appropriate clinical care and caregiver involvement, and help families plan for the future.2,3 Primary care visits offer an excellent opportunity to identify cognitive impairment early, as these are the most common settings where older adults receive care4 yet up to 90% of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) cases are missed in these settings.5–8

Primary care clinicians face significant barriers to cognitive screening that contribute to missed diagnoses, especially limited time and resources in busy clinics.9 Clinicians’ lack of training and confidence in administering cognitive tests, interpreting results, making an MCI-related diagnosis, or engaging in subsequent care planning and management further hinder early detection.5,10 Another underlying barrier to early detection efforts is the lack of standardized, minimally burdensome cognitive assessments that are both sensitive to early indicators of impairment and appropriate for use in resource-constrained primary care settings.5,8,11,12 Remote screening applications offer an opportunity to streamline data collection, standardize assessment, and improve cognitive care efficiency while meeting clinical and billing requirements.

MyCog Mobile is a novel NIH-funded cognitive screening system enabling patients to self-administer measures on their smartphones prior to primary care visits,13 thereby increasing the flexibility of screening options (Figure 1). Scores can be automatically sent to the patient’s electronic health record (EHR) and trigger appropriate clinical decision-making support recommendations.13 MyCog Mobile has demonstrated evidence of reliability and validity compared with gold standard neuropsychological assessments,14,15 as well as preliminary evidence of clinical accuracy when compared with the Mini-Cog.16 A major goal of MyCog Mobile is accessibility for older adults self-administering the battery on their smartphones. We aimed to identify the most parsimonious combination of cognitive tasks that maintains diagnostic accuracy while minimizing patient burden. This emphasis on brevity is critical for remote screening, where completion rates decline with assessment length and participant burden.17,18

MyCog Mobile patient journey.

Objective

The goal of this study was to determine the most effective clinical prediction model for detecting the earliest signs of cognitive impairment in older adults, MCI, while prioritizing both parsimony (to minimize patient testing burden) and diagnostic accuracy. Following the Harrell Regression Modeling Strategies (RMS)19 framework, we evaluated a prespecified model to identify robust MyCog Mobile-derived predictors. Our primary aim was to validate a parsimonious model that achieves high diagnostic discrimination (C-statistic/area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUROC] ≥ 0.80) and stable performance through optimism-corrected bootstrap (BS) validation, ensuring the tool is both clinically reliable and efficient for primary care implementation.

Materials and methods

Ethics and dissemination

The Institutional Review Board at Northwestern University (STU00214921) approved this study protocol. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation and were compensated for their participation. Participants in the MCI group were given an adapted form of the Brief Assessment of Capacity to Consent to determine eligibility to consent to participate.20

Sample

This cross-sectional validation study employed convenience sampling of patients from 2 groups: participants diagnosed with MCI and participants from the general population without suspected impairments (Table 1). The MCI subsample (n = 101) was recruited from Northwestern Medicine General Internal Medicine, Geriatrics, and Neurobehavior and Memory Clinics. Diagnostic data were collected via a review of electronic medical records in the institution’s electronic data warehouse. Inclusion criteria for the MCI subsample required that participants: (1) were 65 years or older; (2) spoke English; (3) had a documented diagnosis of MCI (International Classification of Diseases [ICD]-9 331.83; ICD-10 G31.84; ICD-11 6D71); (4) determined to have capacity to consent to participate. Exclusion criteria were: (1) diagnosis of any form of moderate or advanced cognitive impairment or (2) severe, uncorrected vision or hearing impairment that precluded meaningful participation in the study.

The general population sample (n = 177) was a subset of a sample recruited via a market research agency for the MyCog Mobile construct validation study.14 We aimed to recruit a general population sample of older adults that would mirror the population of older adults who attend annual primary care visits. The Mini-Cog, one of the most commonly used cognitive screeners in primary care,21 was used as a proxy to determine who would be flagged for suspected impairment in a typical primary care visit. Inclusion criteria for the nonimpaired subsample required that participants: (1) were 65 years or older; (2) spoke English; (3) obtained Mini-Cog score ≥3; (4) completed at least the MyCog Mobile portion of the testing battery. Exclusion criteria included: (1) Mini-Cog score <3 and (2) severe, uncorrected vision or hearing impairment that precluded meaningful participation in the study.

Procedure

All participants self-administered MyCog Mobile in dedicated clinical research facilities between spring 2024 and fall 2025. MyCog Mobile was self-administered on study-provided iPhones under standardized conditions, with research staff present to supply devices and provide technical support as needed. Staff did not assist with cognitive tasks but instead oversaw participants as they completed the tasks independently. For the normal cognitive aging sample, the Mini-Cog was administered by a trained examiner to determine eligibility for inclusion.

Measures

The 4 MyCog Mobile measures deployed in this study (Figure 2) are adaptations of tasks from the NIA-Funded Mobile Toolbox,22 a validated smartphone-based cognitive assessment system. Two tasks, MyPictures and MySorting, are also used in the tablet version of MyCog Mobile, MyCog.23

MyCog Mobile example screens. Example screens from each of the MyCog Mobile measures from left to right: MyFaces, MySorting, MyPictures, MySequences.

MyFaces

MyFaces is an associative memory test originally developed by Rentz et al.24 to predict cerebral amyloid beta burden. The MyCog Mobile version was adapted from the Mobile Toolbox Faces and Names test.25 Participants view 12 face-name pairs, then after a 5-10 min delay, complete 3 memory subtests: Recognition (select the person from 3 options), First Letter (indicate first letter of the person’s name; Figure 2), and Name Matching (select the correct name from 3 options). Raw accuracy scores are calculated for each subtest.

MySorting

MySorting is an executive function and cognitive flexibility measure adapted from MyCog Dimensional Change Card Sorting26 and Mobile Toolbox Shape-Color Sorting.27 Participants sort images by shape or color based on cue words appearing on screen. The task includes practice trials, single-dimension blocks (shape-only, then color-only), and mixed trials requiring flexible switching. The primary score reflects accuracy adjusted for response speed.

MyPictures

MyPictures is a measure of episodic memory adapted from MyCog Picture Sequence Memory26 and the Arranging Pictures task in the Mobile Toolbox.28 A series of 14 images depicting independent, nonsequential activities is presented in a specific order and placed in specific, sequential locations on the screen. Following this presentation, the images are scrambled, and the participant is asked to replace the images in the original order (Figure 2). There are 2 trials. We examined exact match accuracy on the 8-item practice as well as trials 1 and 2.

MySequences

MySequences is a measure of working memory adapted from the Mobile Toolbox Sequences test.29 MySequences requires participants to remember strings of letters and numbers and arrange them in order, with the letters in alphabetical order first and then the numbers in ascending numerical order (Figure 2). Trials begin with strings of 3 alphanumeric characters and increase in length, reaching a maximum difficulty of 10 characters. Raw scores reflect the number of correct trials.

Analyses

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.0) using established packages for regression modeling and penalized regression. Baseline participant characteristics—including age, education, sex, and race/ethnicity—were summarized descriptively by diagnostic group. Group differences were characterized using standardized differences except for race and ethnicity as several subgroups had very small cell counts. Age, sex, and education were included as candidate predictors along with the cognitive task variables. Missing data were minimal, with only one MCI participant missing task scores, and analyses were conducted using complete cases (N = 277; 100 MCI, 177 NCA). All bootstrapping used 2000 resamples.

Analytic framework

Model development followed the Harrell/RMS framework,19 using BS resampling for internal validation with an additional nested BS procedure to account for variable selection optimism. With 100 events and 10 candidate predictors (events-per-variable [EPV]=10), our sample was within the commonly cited EPV heuristic for logistic regression stability,30 but below Riley et al.’s recommended minimum sample size requirements for development of a multivariable prediction model.31 We proactively addressed this limitation through: (1) prespecification of the full candidate predictor set based on domain knowledge; (2) structured variable reduction with prespecified stopping rules; (3) extensive BS validation (2000 resamples) with selection-aware optimism correction; and (4) comprehensive sensitivity analyses. We report optimism-corrected C-statistic/AUROC as our primary discrimination measure.

Parsimonious model development

We started with 10 prespecified predictors: age, sex, years of education, MyFaces First Letter, MyFaces Matching, MyFaces Recognition, MySequences, MySorting Rate Correct, MyPictures Practice, and MyPictures Total Score (trial 1+trial 2). Structured backward elimination was used to simplify the model to reduce assessment burden while maintaining interpretability, following prespecified stopping rules focused on preserving optimism-corrected discrimination and calibration.19 Predictor contributions were quantified using partial Wald χ2 statistics from the ANOVA decomposition (χ2 minus degrees of freedom). At each step, the lowest-contributing predictor was removed, and the reduced model was refit and internally validated with 2000 BS resamples to obtain optimism-corrected performance estimates. Predictors were removed only if all criteria were met: (1) reduction in optimism-corrected C-statistic ≤0.02; (2) proportional loss of explained variation (R2) ≤5%; (3) optimism-corrected calibration slope ≥0.85; and (4) Wald χ2 contribution <1. To account for optimism introduced by the variable-reduction process itself, we used a nested BS (2000 outer resamples) in which the entire reduction algorithm was repeated within each BS sample using apparent performance criteria (without inner BS validation for computational feasibility); the resulting BS-fitted model was then evaluated on the original dataset to estimate selection-induced optimism.

Model evaluation

Performance was evaluated using 2000 BS resamples. Discrimination was quantified via the optimism-corrected C-statistic/AUROC, with both naive (standard BS) and selection-aware (nested BS) estimates reported. Calibration was assessed graphically and quantitatively via calibration slope (values near 1.0 indicate strong agreement between predicted and observed probabilities; values <1.0 suggest overfitting, values >1.0 suggest underfitting). Explained variation was measured using Nagelkerke R2. Predictor importance was evaluated using Partial Wald χ2 statistics adjusted for degrees of freedom. Predictor effect size was characterized using bootstrapped odds ratios (ORs) with percentile BS 95% CIs obtained by refitting the final model across BS resamples. Clinical utility was evaluated using decision curve analysis (DCA) across threshold probabilities from 0 to 0.90. Net benefit was compared against refer-all and refer-none strategies.32 Relative utility, defined as the ratio of the model’s net benefit to the prevalence (ie, the net benefit of a theoretically perfect model), was calculated to provide a prevalence-standardized measure of clinical value on a 0-1 scale.33

Threshold optimization

To facilitate real-world primary care application, a classification threshold was selected using a constrained optimization procedure. Recognizing that a screening tool must maintain high rule-out utility to be clinically viable, we enforced minimum performance constraints of sensitivity ≥0.70 and specificity ≥0.70. Within the constrained feasible region, the threshold maximizing Youden’s index (sensitivity + specificity −1) was selected. Threshold selection was performed post hoc on the finalized model and evaluated using BS resampling (2000 resamples) rather than data splitting, consistent with the goal of estimating optimism-corrected performance rather than deployment-ready operating characteristics. Bootstrap 95% CIs were calculated for sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) at the optimized threshold.

Sensitivity analyses

We compared the parsimonious model with the full 10-predictor logistic regression model and 3 penalized regressions (Ridge α = 0, LASSO α = 1, Elastic Net α = 0.5). Penalized models selected λ via 10-fold cross-validation. To obtain optimism-corrected C-statistic and pseudo-R2 for penalized models, we used double-resampling: for each BS resample, λ was reselected using internal 10-fold cross-validation, the model was refit on the BS sample, and performance was evaluated in the BS sample (apparent) and on the original dataset (test-on-original). Mean optimism (apparent − test) was subtracted from full-sample apparent performance to yield optimism-corrected estimates. For all models, we reported sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, and NPV at optimized thresholds with BS 95% CIs; ΔC ≥0.02 was considered meaningful.34

Results

Only one participant in the MCI group was missing data and was therefore excluded from analyses. The final analysis included 277 older adults with complete data (100 MCI, 177 NCA; Table 1). Group differences are described using standardized differences rather than hypothesis tests. The MCI group was older and included a higher proportion of males. Racial and ethnic composition differed between groups, although both cohorts were predominantly White and non-Hispanic. Education levels were similar across groups.

Parsimonious model development

Structured backward elimination was applied to the 10-predictor set (Table 2), revealing 4 variables that failed to contribute significant predictive information and were subsequently removed from the model: age, MyFaces Recognition, MySequences, and sex. Their removal slightly improved the optimism-corrected C-statistic (from 0.804 to 0.812), indicating these predictors added overfitting rather than meaningful signal. Elimination stopped when MyPictures exact-match practice reached a Wald χ2 contribution of 1.95, exceeding the prespecified threshold of 1.0, yielding a 6-predictor final model.

Model evaluation

The final 6-predictor model demonstrated strong discrimination (apparent C-statistic = 0.827; 95% CI, 0.775-0.874) with minimal optimism after BS validation (optimism-corrected C = 0.812). Overall accuracy was good with a Brier score of 0.158 compared with 0.231 for a noninformative model predicting the base rate, representing a 32% reduction in prediction error. Calibration was excellent, with a BS-corrected slope of 0.92. Apparent R2 was 0.404 and optimism-corrected R2 was 0.369. As shown in Table 3, cognitive measures accounted for most predictive signal: MySorting contributed the largest proportion of discriminative information, followed by MyFaces and MyPictures tasks, while education provided a smaller but meaningful contribution. Although some predictors’ CIs included 1, all retained variables demonstrated positive Wald χ2 values and consistent BS retention, supporting their inclusion in the final model. A nested BS repeating the elimination within each resample yielded a selection-aware corrected C-statistic of 0.80 and a selection-aware calibration slope of 0.86, consistent with robust performance after accounting for selection.

Threshold optimization and clinical performance

A constrained threshold-optimization procedure (minimum sensitivity ≥0.70 and specificity ≥0.70) identified an optimal classification threshold of 0.328 for the final 6-predictor model. At this threshold, the model achieved good accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity (Table 4). Corresponding predictive values indicated that ∼62% of individuals screening positive were truly impaired (PPV), while ∼85% of individuals screening negative were cognitively normal (NPV).

Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate whether increased model complexity improved performance beyond the parsimonious 6-predictor model, we compared it with expanded unpenalized logistic models and penalized regression approaches using the full 10-predictor set (Table 4). All models satisfied the same sensitivity and specificity constraints at their optimized thresholds, and CIs for sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, and NPV overlapped across approaches, indicating no meaningful performance differences. The parsimonious model maintained strong discrimination and competitive explained variance, performing as well as or better than more complex unpenalized models. Penalized approaches demonstrated lower explained variance and slightly reduced discrimination without corresponding gains in classification performance. Overall, added model complexity did not improve predictive accuracy or clinical utility relative to the parsimonious solution.

Clinical utility

Decision curve analysis (Figure 3) suggests the model demonstrates positive net benefit across the full range of displayed thresholds, outperforming both “refer-all” and “refer-none” strategies at every threshold examined. At the optimized classification threshold of 0.328, the model achieved a net benefit of 0.197, compared with 0.046 for refer-all, an improvement of +0.150. Relative utility was 0.55, indicating the model captures ∼55% of the net benefit achievable by a perfect theoretical screener. At this threshold, the model identifies 28.2 true positive MCI cases per 100 patients screened, while avoiding 46.6 unnecessary referrals per 100 patients relative to refer-all. The sustained net benefit at higher thresholds reflects the model’s ability to identify high-confidence cases with few false positives, supporting its utility across a range of clinical decision-making contexts from broad screening to selective referral.

Discussion

This study developed and validated an interpretable prediction model for detecting MCI using MyCog Mobile. The final 6-predictor model achieved strong discrimination and excellent calibration, and results were consistent across regularized regression approaches, supporting a robust predictive signal. To transparently account for variable selection, a nested BS that repeated the reduction procedure within each resample produced selection-aware performance estimates (optimism-corrected C-statistic=0.80, calibration slope=0.86), reinforcing the model’s robustness. Decision curve analysis indicated substantial clinical utility; at a ∼33% risk threshold, the model identifies an estimated 28 true MCI cases and avoids an estimated 47 unnecessary referrals per 100 patients compared with referring everyone for a full cognitive workup.

The executive functioning test, MySorting, emerged as the strongest individual predictor, accounting for ∼30% of the model’s total discriminative information (Wald χ2 contribution). This aligns with recent evidence suggesting that digital assessments of processing speed and executive function capture early cognitive inefficiencies that precede memory decline in prodromal dementia.35,36

The remaining tests are memory measures: MyFaces (First Letter and Matching subtests) and MyPictures (Practice and Total Score), which collectively explained over half of the model’s predictive power. Across all predictors, cognitive task performance accounted for ∼87% of the model’s discriminative capacity, demonstrating that digital biomarkers drive MCI detection in this screening paradigm.

Education provided a modest contribution to the model (13.2% of total Wald χ2), though its individual effect was uncertain (OR = 1.157 [0.996, 1.363]). The positive direction likely reflects a calibrating effect: because the cognitive task scores are not adjusted for educational attainment, older adults with more education who score equivalently to less-educated peers are likely performing further below their own expected baseline, consistent with greater underlying decline.37 Education’s role may also be partly attributable to the highly educated MCI sample (31.7% with graduate degrees) than the community-recruited NCA group (18.6% with graduate degrees), which would require validation in an external sample to confirm. Regardless, education is not weighted heavily in the model.

We were able to remove 4 noncontributing variables from the model without sacrificing discrimination: age, MyFaces Recognition, MySequences, and sex. Notably, removing these variables improved the optimism-corrected C-statistic, suggesting they contributed primarily to overfitting rather than meaningful prediction. Removing 2 cognitive measures (MyFaces Recognition and MySequences) while maintaining strong performance supported our goal of parsimony. Furthermore, both LASSO and Elastic Net retained MySequences as a seventh predictor, yet neither approach achieved higher discrimination than the 6-predictor model (corrected C = 0.804 for both vs 0.812), providing converging evidence that MySequences does not contribute incremental predictive value beyond the other retained measures in this sample. Qualitatively, MySequences is a longer and more frustrating task, and given it did not demonstrate strong predictive power its removal is clinically supported. This reduction will decrease testing time and patient burden, supporting broader engagement and adherence when MyCog Mobile is self-administered remotely.

Age and sex were noncontributing despite being known risk factors for MCI.38 Age’s minimal contribution is likely attributable to the restricted age range in our sample (65 years and older), which mirrors the target population for MyCog Mobile but also limits age-related variance. Additionally, within older adults, age may primarily serve as a proxy for underlying cognitive changes that our direct performance measures capture more precisely.39 It is possible sex showed no contribution because sex-related differences in MCI risk often operate through domain-specific cognitive profiles40 which may also be measured directly by our individual cognitive tasks.

Our findings align with recent findings using similar self-administered smartphone-based assessments. Namely, Jutten et al.35 found that performance on the Mobile Toolbox measures, from which the MyCog Mobile measures were adapted, was significantly associated with early to intermediate tau burden as well as with in-clinic cognitive assessments used to detect preclinical Alzheimer’s disease. Equally as important as the predictive power of the tests was the completion rate; Jutten et al.35 found that 80% of their participants (n = 100) were able to complete the Mobile Toolbox measures on their own smartphones at home unassisted. Although the participants in our study completed the measures on study-provided devices, the high completion rate (<1% missing) and the consistency with previous studies that used MyCog Mobile remotely15,41 supports the feasibility of self-administration of MyCog Mobile by older adults.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several methodological strengths. First, we prioritized clinical realism by focusing specifically on individuals with MCI and excluding those with moderate and advanced dementias. Many validation studies aggregate impaired groups, which can artificially inflate performance by leveraging larger cognitive differences. By targeting MCI specifically, a stage where functional abilities are largely preserved, we evaluated MyCog Mobile under conditions aligned with its intended real-world use for early detection of subtle cognitive impairments. This focus is particularly important for remote, self-administered smartphone measures, as individuals with more advanced impairment may be unable to complete assessments independently. MyCog Mobile is designed to detect the earliest cognitive changes and establish a baseline while individuals remain functionally independent, enabling longitudinal monitoring of subtle intraindividual decline before progression to stages requiring in-person testing.

Second, we prioritized transparency and clinical interpretability by employing traditional unpenalized logistic regression following the Harrell RMS framework. This allowed us to quantify the specific diagnostic weight of individual digital biomarkers through Wald χ2 partitioning rather than relying on “black box” ML approaches. We implemented rigorous internal validation using 2000 BS resamples throughout model development, with additional sensitivity analyses using nested cross-validation for regularized ML comparisons. Consistency across 5 model specifications, predictor sets, and regularization strategies provides convergent evidence of predictive stability.

Despite these methodological safeguards, several important limitations warrant consideration. A key consideration is the use of 2 distinct recruitment pipelines: MCI participants were recruited from clinical settings with EHR-confirmed diagnoses, whereas cognitively normal participants were recruited through a market research panel. Because recruitment source and diagnostic status overlapped completely, cohort effects cannot be empirically separated from diagnostic differences, introducing potential selection bias and dataset confounding. The 2 groups may differ in health-seeking behavior, technology familiarity, or socioeconomic factors unrelated to cognition. Although we adjusted for age, sex, and education and conducted extensive sensitivity analyses, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (eg, comorbidities, medication use, health literacy) cannot be ruled out. External validation in prospectively enrolled cohorts with uniform diagnostic procedures is therefore essential.

Although racial and ethnic differences existed between the MCI and NCA groups, these subgroups were too small and uneven for robust statistical inclusion. Including them would have compromised model stability without offering mechanistic insight. Furthermore, disparities in cognitive impairment are better explained by associated social determinants of health (eg, education, experienced racism) rather than racial/ethnic identity itself.42 Future research should prioritize more diverse samples and focus on proximal, modifiable risk factors.

Another limitation is our relatively small sample size of 277 participants (100 events). Although structured backward elimination reduced the predictor set from 10 to 6 variables, improving the events-per-variable ratio from 10.0 to 16.7, our final model still falls short of the Riley et al.31 minimum of 330 participants required for precise R2 estimation and reliable shrinkage for a 6-predictor model. We proactively addressed this through several strategies: (1) prespecification of the full model based on domain knowledge rather than data-driven feature discovery, (2) formal model comparison with prespecified, clinically relevant stopping criteria, (3) extensive BS validation (2000 resamples) to quantify optimism, and (4) rigorous sensitivity analyses demonstrating consistency across multiple specifications. The observed minimal optimism (0.015 using naïve BS, 0.027 using selection-aware nested BS) and excellent calibration (slope = 0.92) suggest adequate internal validity despite the modest sample size. Nevertheless, external validation in larger, independent cohorts remains essential, and wider CIs on individual predictor effects limit our ability to make strong inferences about specific task contributions.

For the normal cognitive aging group, we relied on a well-established cognitive screener (Mini-Cog) to classify normal cognition. Although EHR data were not available to independently verify the absence of cognitive diagnoses in these participants, the Mini-Cog is the same instrument commonly used in primary care to identify patients without evidence of impairment. Thus, our control inclusion procedure closely mirrored real-world primary care screening practices, suggesting patients in our normal group would likely not be suspected of MCI or referred for further diagnostic work up at a routine visit. However, we cannot rule out that some of the patients in the normal cognitive aging group indeed had mild impairments without a formal evaluation.

For the MCI group, however, we relied on clinical diagnoses documented in the EHR and we did not have access to a continuous cognitive outcome measure (eg, Mini-Cog score, detailed neuropsychological battery) that would have enabled ordinal modeling across the full cognitive spectrum from normal aging through varying degrees of impairment. This binary classification may have reduced statistical power and limited our ability to detect nonlinear relationships between predictors and cognitive status. However, the binary outcome used in this study directly reflects the clinical decision point MyCog Mobile is designed to support: identifying individuals who require further evaluation versus those who do not. This classification mirrors the actual primary care workflow, where a brief screening tool triggers a “flag for follow-up” rather than staging the severity of impairment.

Finally, although MyCog Mobile was self-administered, assessments were conducted in a controlled research setting using study-provided devices rather than remotely on participants’ personal smartphones. This may limit generalizability to real-world deployment, where device heterogeneity, internet variability, and unsupervised environments are factors. Ongoing pragmatic implementation trials in primary care will evaluate remote self-administration, EHR integration, clinical decision support, and adoption by patients and providers, providing critical evidence on scalability, feasibility, and ecological validity.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that MyCog Mobile, a brief self-administered smartphone-based cognitive assessment can achieve clinically relevant performance for detecting mild cognitive impairment. The model’s clinical accuracy, automated scoring, EHR integration capability, and intuitive interpretation address multiple barriers to cognitive screening in primary care settings. These findings support the feasibility of deploying MyCog Mobile in primary care as a scalable solution to improve early detection of cognitive impairment.
