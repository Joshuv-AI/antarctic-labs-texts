Dataset of cortical and subcortical single neuron activity during value-based tasks in macaque monkey
Authors: Liza London, Marques Love, Zachary R Zeisler, Peter H Rudebeck, Frederic M Stoll
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA key challenge in cognitive neuroscience is to provide an account of how the brain guides choice behavior toward rewarding outcomes in uncertain environments. Interaction between the frontal cortex and subcortical structures is critical for this type of decision-making. The frontal cortex is, however, not a homogeneous structure and is composed of subregions that are distinct based on their cytoarchitecture. How these distinct subregions uniquely contribute to decision-making is not well established. Further, little is known about how neurons in each subregion interact with each other, meaning that the cross-areal dynamics of reward-guided decision-making in frontal cortex are yet to be fully revealed. Here we present a dataset of 16,495 neurons recorded from 22 anatomically verified areas in 2 macaque monkeys across 340 behavioral sessions. Monkeys performed single- and two-alternative probabilistic value-based decision-making tasks where they made responses to stimuli that were associated with distinct probabilities of receiving different flavored juice outcomes. Our dataset includes behavior, neuronal spike times, and electrode locations based on cytoarchitectural features of Nissl and immunohistochemically stained tissue. Investigators can use this extensive dataset to further resolve the functions of, and interactions between, frontal and subcortical brain regions during reward-guided decision-making.

Background & Summary

Higher cognitive function, such as multi-attribute decision-making, involves coordinated activity between prefrontal cortex (PFC) and subcortical brain areas. Dysfunction or damage to these areas results in deficits in decision-making1. For instance, patients with ventromedial PFC lesions are unable to make beneficial choices in situations where they have to integrate multiple attributes, such as each option’s cost and benefits2,3. However, PFC is not a single entity and the frontal cortex is composed of anatomically and functionally heterogeneous areas4–7. Functional neuroimaging of humans performing economic decision-making tasks has shown that discrete frontal areas encode distinct decision-related attributes8 and reward-related processes9. In addition, lesion studies in non-human primates have further shown that specific subdivisions of ventral frontal cortex are critical to different aspects of decision-making. For example, lesions to orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) impair the ability to update the sensory-specific value of a potential option that can be chosen, whereas lesions to the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (vlPFC) impair the ability to make decisions involving reward availability10. Thus, there is emerging evidence that distinct parts of frontal cortex make specific contributions to reward-guided decision-making.

Despite such insights from lesion and functional neuroimaging, these levels of analysis are unable to reveal how the activity of single neurons in frontal cortex relates to the evaluation and selection of different options based on their attributes. Similarly, although single-neuron recordings in humans offer remarkable opportunities to study PFC function, this research is limited to patients with severe neurological conditions, and recording sites are chosen according to clinical, not research, criteria11. In particular, of the available animal models to study human PFC, only the Euarchonta lineage including tree shrews, lemurs and all primates have prefrontal cortical areas resembling those in humans, and of this lineage, primates including humans evolved highly differentiated PFC7,12. Consequently, the cortical organization of PFC in humans and old-world primates such as macaques is largely anatomically homologous, which cannot be said for other species13. Because single neuron recordings in macaques can be localized to specific areas, they provide significant explanatory power toward understanding the functional circuitry involved during higher-order cognition.

Previous single neuron recordings in macaques have successfully revealed feature-specific encoding in isolated areas; for example, neurons in OFC encoded the attributes associated with different choice options including the flavor, size, and probability of receiving a reward as well as the integrated value of each option14–16. Frontal cortical areas such as OFC are, however, made up of cytoarchitecturally distinct subregions that each have unique connectivity patterns5,17–20, and their precise functional interactions during cognitive processes remain poorly understood.

Here we present a dataset that includes recordings of the activity of 16,495 single neurons from 22 anatomically defined frontal and subcortical areas in two macaque monkeys21. Only putative single neurons meeting strict isolation criteria are included in this dataset. Recordings were made during 340 sessions where subjects performed a set of multi-attribute decision-making tasks, and individual sessions include recordings from multiple areas simultaneously, meaning that both features encoded by individual areas as well as functional interactions between areas during decision making can be assessed. Specifically, in our experiments, we recorded the activity of single neurons across most frontal cortex subdivisions as well as in the striatum and amygdala (Tables 1, 2) during reward-based decision-making tasks (Fig. 1). During each session, monkeys performed trials where either a single choice option was presented (one-forced choice or 1FC) or two options were presented and subjects could choose between them (two-alternative forced choice or 2AFC). In all types of trials, options were associated with one of two flavors of fruit juice associated with different probabilities of delivery. The specific juice flavors were changed from session to session. Following recordings, each neuron was assigned to a distinct area of frontal cortex, amygdala, or striatum based on distinguishable cytoarchitectural features observed using Nissl, Calbindin and SMI-32 tissue staining (Fig. 2).

Parts of the present data have been published before, with a particular focus on activity patterns within ventral frontal cortex and amygdala. In brief, we previously reported that the activity of single neurons in vlPFC and OFC exhibit dissociable encoding properties during 2AFC trials; OFC preferentially encodes outcome qualities such as flavor and vlPFC preferentially encodes reward probability22. Moreover, we found that the activity of neurons within cytoarchitecturally defined subdivisions within vlPFC and OFC exhibit distinct encoding of different task variables23 as well as distinct patterns of functional communication24 related to different aspects of choice behavior. We further found that the cytoarchitecture of ventral frontal areas was not sufficient to explain systematic cortical variation in neuronal timescales25. This unique and large-scale dataset will allow investigators to analyze the neurophysiological activity of numerous frontal and subcortical areas during reward-related behavior, offering the possibility to further resolve the finer-grained neural mechanisms that contribute to reward-guided decision making.

Methods

Subjects

Subjects were two adult rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta), referred to as monkey M and X. At the beginning of neurophysiological recordings, monkeys were 8 and 5.5 years old and weighed 11.9 and 7.9 kg respectively. All procedures were reviewed and approved by the Icahn School of Medicine Animal Care and Use Committee (PHS D16-00069), followed the National Institute of Health standards for experiments involving non-human primates and in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Monkeys were group-housed, maintained on 12-hour light and dark cycles and had continual access to food. Subjects were water-controlled 5 days per week during the duration of training and testing. Fluid control followed IACUC guidelines and protocols (minimum of 10 ml/kg/day) and was implemented to motivate subjects without impacting physiological and psychological well-being26,27. Monkeys frequently received more than the minimum requirements (median and [5th 95th] percentiles, monkey M: 20.8 [14.4 25] ml/kg/day; monkey X: 50 [40.6 52.4] ml/kg/day) as well as fruit rewards following testing.

Apparatus

Monkeys were head restrained while seated in a custom primate chair. The primate chair was positioned 56 cm from a 19-inch monitor screen. Monkeys reported choices using their gaze, which was monitored and acquired at 90 frames per second using an Infrared oculometer (PC-60, Arrington Research, Scottsdale, AZ). A custom-made air pressure-driven juice dispenser system28 delivered juice rewards directly to the monkey’s mouth. We used MonkeyLogic behavioral system to monitor trial events, reward delivery and timings (https://monkeylogic.nimh.nih.gov), operating in MATLAB (version 2014b, The MathWorks Inc). An Omniplex data acquisition system (Plexon, Dallas, TX) recorded raw electrophysiological activity (sampled at 40 kHz resolution). Spikes from putative single neurons were automatically clustered offline using the MountainSort plugin of MountainLab29. Raw signals were bandpass filtered between 300–6000 Hz with a 1000 Hz transition band and spatial whitening was applied. Events exceeding 3 standard deviations above baseline were detected on each electrode independently with a minimum inter-event interval of 0.33 ms, and 67-sample clips were extracted, projected onto 10 principal components, and recursively clustered using the nonparametric ISO-SPLIT method. Results were then manually curated based on principal component analysis, inter-spike interval distributions, visually differentiated waveforms, and objective cluster measures (Isolation > 0.75, Noise overlap < 0.2, Peak signal-to-noise ratio > 0.5, Firing Rate > 0.05 Hz). Note that the firing rate considered here reflects the total number of spikes recorded during each session divided by the session duration and therefore includes both periods of task engagement and rest.

Behavioral tasks

Monkeys performed three closely related probabilistic tasks: a single option forced choice task (1FC), a two-alternative forced choice task (2AFC), and a dynamic version of either the 1FC or 2AFC task (referred to as 1FC_dyn or 2AFC_dyn respectively) (Fig. 1a). Monkeys performed trials from all three tasks during most of the 340 sessions (monkey M: 133/147, monkey X: 193/193). In the remaining sessions (14/147), monkey M only performed the 1FC and 2AFC tasks. Most sessions (monkey M: 104/133, monkey X: 193/193) started with a defined number of trials of the 1FC task (monkey M, n = 94–115; monkey X, n = 300 trials), followed by blocks containing four 2AFC trials followed by one dynamic trial (i.e., 4x 2AFC - 1x 2AFC_dyn - 4x 2AFC - 1x 2AFC_dyn - etc.). In 13/133 sessions of monkey M, a single block of 1FC trials was followed by alternating blocks containing 50 2AFC trials followed by 30 or 35 2AFC_dyn trials. In the remaining 16/133 sessions, monkey M first performed 1FC trials, followed by one block of 234–530 2AFC trials, followed by one final block of 9–332 2AFC_dyn trials.

Stimuli used were common across tasks and contained two features: an external-colored rectangle indicating which outcome flavor monkeys could earn (9 possible colors, 2 chosen at random per session), and a second central rectangle, more or less filled, indicating the probability at which this particular outcome flavor would be delivered at the end of the trial. During a given session and across tasks, monkeys were shown options containing one of two possible colors directly corresponding to one of two juice flavors (randomly picked from a set of 5, which included apple, cranberry, grape, pineapple and orange juices, diluted to 50% in water). The probabilities ranged from 10% to 90%, with increments of 20% for monkey M and 10% for monkey X.

During 1FC trials, monkeys were shown a single option, after which they could select it by fixating a response box on the side of the presented option. On 2AFC trials, monkeys were prompted to choose between two options shown simultaneously on the right and left sides of the screen. The two options presented were associated with 2 possible outcome flavors (flavor J1 vs J2, different-flavor trials) in 50% of trials for monkey M and 75% of trials for monkey X. In these trials, the probabilities were always different for monkey M but could be either different or similar (e.g., J1 at 70% vs. J2 at 70%) for monkey X. For the rest of the trials (50% for monkey M and 25% for monkey X), the options presented had the same flavor outcome but with different probabilities (e.g., J1 at 50% vs. J1 at 70%, same-flavor trials).

In the dynamic trials, monkeys were presented with a sequence of stimuli presented on each side of the screen and could earn reward at the average probability of the options shown on the chosen side. The version of the task differed between the subjects. For monkey X, two single options of identical flavor but different probabilities were sequentially presented on the same side of the screen. The subject was then forced to choose this presented pair (single option context, 1FC_dyn). By contrast, monkey M was sequentially presented with three pairs of options associated with different probabilities and allowed to choose between them (2AFC_dyn). After their choice, monkeys were shown a stimulus with the average probability of the options presented on the corresponding side, indicating their likelihood of being rewarded in that trial. Given that both monkeys performed different versions of the dynamic task, we only provide limited behavioral metrics, namely the juice flavor monkeys could receive on each trial, the side of their response and whether they received a reward (parameters that could be included as confounding factors when analyzing 2AFC trials occurring after these dynamic trials, see Data Record for details on the provided behavioral variables and Usage Notes for further guidance on how to use these trials).

To begin each trial, monkeys fixated on the central fixation cross for 0.7 to 1.3 s (steps of 0.3 s). Monkeys were allowed to break fixation during the first 0.4 s, in which case they were asked to regain fixation within 6 s and maintain it for another 0.3 to 0.9 s (steps of 0.3 s). One (1FC trials) or two (2AFC trials) options then appeared pseudorandomly on the left and/or right side of the screen and monkeys could freely look at them. Stimuli stayed on the screen for 0.4 to 0.8 s (steps of 0.2 s, pseudorandomly) before being turned off for 0.2 s. In dynamic trials, two sequential stimuli were presented for the same duration and separated by 0.2 s. One or two response boxes then appeared on the sides of the previously presented options (3 possible locations equidistant to the options’ locations; bottom left/right, middle left/right, top left/right, Fig. 1b). Monkeys indicated their choice by fixating a response box on the side of the desired stimulus within 8 s. In 2AFC trials, monkeys were asked to fixate one of the response boxes for at least 0.25 s to validate their choice, at which point the other response box would disappear. The dwell time allowed animals to change their choice within the trial. Monkeys were required to fixate the selected response box for another 0.6 to 1.2 s (steps of 0.3 s). Responses boxes would then disappear for 0.3 to 0.7 s (steps of 0.2 s). The same stimuli were then shown again during the feedback period. The selected stimulus was first presented flashing (5 times 0.1 s ON followed by 0.1 s OFF), before staying on the screen for the duration of the reward (if delivered) and an additional 0.5 s. The non-selected stimulus (during 2AFC trials) would remain on the screen for the same duration. On rewarded trials, subjects received 2–3 pulses of 0.03–0.06 s of fluid (separated by 0.1 s each, 0.25–0.36 ml total reward per trial of the juice flavor and at the probability indicated by the selected option). Non-rewarded trials were matched in time to trials that included reward delivery. Finally, rewarded trials were followed by a 2 s intertrial interval while non-rewarded trials were followed by 3.5–4 s. On trials when monkeys failed to maintain required fixation on the fixation cross or the response box (referred as break of fixation), a large red circle was shown on the center of the screen for 1 s followed by a longer intertrial interval (4–6 s for monkey M, 3–4 s for monkey X). The same circle and intertrial interval were given if the monkey failed to initiate a trial within 6 seconds of the appearance of the fixation cross or failed to choose an option within the allotted time (8 s).

Surgical procedures and electrophysiological recordings

All surgical procedures were carried out under aseptic conditions in a dedicated operating suite. General anesthesia was induced with ketamine hydrochloride (10 mg/kg, i.m.) and maintained with isoflurane (1.0–3.0%, adjusted to effect). Animals received intravenous isotonic fluids throughout the procedure, and physiological parameters including heart rate, respiration, blood pressure, expired CO2, and body temperature were continuously monitored. Perioperative medications included dexamethasone sodium phosphate (0.4 mg/kg, i.m.) and cefazolin (15 mg/kg, i.m.), which were administered starting one day prior to surgery and continuing for one week post-operatively. Analgesia was provided with ketoprofen (10–15 mg/kg, i.m.) for three days, followed by oral ibuprofen (100 mg) for an additional five days.

Monkeys were first implanted with a custom titanium headpost, secured to the skull with titanium screws. Based on structural MRI scans, a form-fitted plastic recording chamber was then positioned over the right frontal lobe and affixed with dental acrylic (C&B Metabond, Parkell; Ortho-JET BCA, Lang Dental). To aid in planning electrode trajectories, the chamber was subsequently filled with diluted gadolinium (Magnevist, Bayer; 1:1000 saline solution) and imaged with MRI using external fiducials. In a later surgery, a craniotomy was made within the chamber boundaries. After ensuring sterile conditions (confirmed by negative aerobic and anaerobic cultures, IDEXX BioAnalytics), the dura was punctured at the intended entry points of each electrode, and a 157-channel semi-chronic microdrive (Gray Matter Research, Bozeman, MT) was implanted (Fig. 2a). The microdrive housed glass-coated tungsten electrodes (1–2 MΩ at 1 kHz; Alpha Omega Engineering, Nazareth, Israel), which were immediately advanced through the dura to prevent tip damage. The electrodes were then lowered gradually over weeks to months to target frontal cortex, striatum, and amygdala (Fig. 2b). Only electrodes with impedances greater than 0.2 MΩ at 1 kHz were used for recordings.

Across sessions, electrodes were adjusted daily to sample new neurons, with a median of 55 electrodes moved per day in monkey M and 104 in monkey X. Typical advances were 125 µm and 62.5 µm, respectively. Electrodes were occasionally retracted by 2–5 mm, allowed to stabilize for at least one week, and then advanced again. This incremental approach minimized tissue damage and avoided pre-selection of neurons. However, it is possible that some units were recorded more than once across days. Because it is difficult to ascertain if multiple recorded neurons represent one neuron from waveform parameters alone (see Technical Validation and Usage Notes), we are treating all neurons as distinct units.

Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, recordings in monkey X were temporarily paused, after which the initial microdrive was explanted and replaced with an identical 157-channel system. Prior to removal, CT images were collected with several electrodes left in place, and an additional MRI was acquired post-explantation to check for possible brain displacement and to map our electrode locations. The second implant contained 137 electrodes that were subsequently lowered and recorded.

Electrode locations were confirmed using multiple complementary approaches. First, electrode depth was tracked continuously during lowering, noting changes in noise level and multiunit activity indicative of transitions between white and gray matter. Second, CT images acquired at different time points throughout the experiment were co-registered with the post-chamber implant MRI and an additional MRI taken at the conclusion of the experiment after removal of the microdrive. Finally, after completion of recordings, brains were fixed and block-face photographs were taken during sectioning, showing clear marks of the electrodes’ track. Using Free-D software30, electrode trajectories were then reconstructed and assigned to specific cytoarchitectonic areas (see next sections).

Tissue preparation and immunohistochemistry

When recordings were completed, the monkeys were deeply anesthetized (ketamine, 10 mg/kg and sodium pentobarbital, 100 mg/kg) and euthanized via transcardial perfusion with 4% formaldehyde (EMS, Hatfield, PA) in Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Fisher Scientific, Fair Lawn, NJ). After extraction, the brain was post-fixed in buffered 4% formaldehyde for 24–48 h. Then, the brain was placed in solutions of increased sucrose concentration (10%, 20%, 30% w/v in PBS), making sure the brain sank before transitioning to higher concentrations. Cryopreservation, sectioning, mounting, tissue staining, and immunohistology services were all performed by FD NeuroTechnologies, Inc (Columbia, MD). Specifically, the recorded brain hemispheres were cryoprotected in FD Tissue Cryoprotection SolutionTM for 10 days before being rapidly frozen in isopentane pre-cooled to −70 °C with dry ice. Serial sections (50μm) were cut coronally through the right hemispheres, approximately from Bregma 20.25 mm to −15.75 mm31. Every 1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th section of each series of 4 sections (interval: 200μm) were collected and stained separately. The first series was stained with FD Cresyl Violet SolutionTM (Nissl staining, 1 section every 200μm). The second and third series were processed for Calbindin (using mouse monoclonal anti-Calbindin-D-28K antibodies, 1:1000 dilution; Millipore Sigma, St. Louis, MO) and SMI-32 (using mouse Purified anti-Neurofilament H nonphosphorylated antibodies, 1:12000 dilution; Biolegend, San Diego, CA) immunohistochemistry (note that only sections adjacent to every 3rd Nissl-stained sections were processed, meaning 1 section every 600μm). Specifically, after inactivating the endogenous peroxidase activity with hydrogen peroxide, sections were incubated separately with avidin and biotin solutions (Vector Lab, Burlingame, CA) for blocking nonspecific binding of endogenous biotin, biotin-binding protein and lectins. Subsequently, sections were incubated, free-floating, in 0.01 M PBS containing Triton X-100, blocking serum, and the primary antibody for 67 hours at 4 °C. The immunoreaction product was then visualized according to the avidin-biotin complex method32 using the Vectastin elite ABC kit (Vector Lab., Burlingame, CA) and 3′,3′-diaminobenzidine as a chromogen. After thorough washes, all sections were mounted on microscope slides, dehydrated in ethanol, cleared in xylene, and coverslipped in Permount (Fisher Scientific, Fair Lawn, NJ).

Defining neuroanatomical boundaries

To accurately define the neuroanatomical areas in which neurons were recorded from, we parcellated the frontal cortex based on cytoarchitectural features that have been described previously5,33,34 (Table 1). Coronal sections stained for either SMI-32, Nissl, and Calbindin were examined using a Zeiss Apotome 2 light microscope. Based on the variation in patterns of staining that we outline below, we drew boundaries of relevant brain areas. Representative examples of these histological boundaries are shown in Fig. 2c–o.

Area 24c was defined as the dorsal and ventral banks of the cingulate sulcus. Area 6DR was identified as being anterior to the genu of the arcuate sulcus and the appearance of a densely stained layer II, while area 6VaVb is posterior to the lower limb of the arcuate sulcus, and area 6DC is posterior to the genu of the arcuate33. Area 8B was distinguished by a small sparsely stained cell body population with little obvious organization in layers IV-VI. Area 8A was separated by its distinctive long neurite projections in layers II and III in conjunction with less stratified layers IV-VI. Area 46d was defined in the coronal plane throughout the anterior to posterior axis by its distinct stratification in layers II-V and long dendritic processes in layers II-III34. An anatomical area included within area 46d, area 46df has been described in previous literature as an isolated region localized closer to the fundus of the principal sulcus34. We differentiated area 46df by identifying a significantly darker layer V as well as a more obvious separation of layers (IV-VI) than the rest of area 46d. Matching previous work, the boundary between area 46d/46df and area 46v was defined as the middle of the fundus of the principal sulcus. Area 46v typically had characteristic layer separation that complemented previous literature identifying a prominent layer II34. Area 46v is also distinguishable by the scattered elongated dendrites in layers II & III. Previous literature described an area 46vf within area 46v34; however, we were unable to reliably identify this area and as a result did not include it here. Area 45 could be identified through the presence of dark soma staining in layer III and dense dendritic arbor staining in layer II. Area 44 can be dissociated from area 45 by its dysgranularity and can be found in the bank of the lower limb of the arcuate sulcus.

On the ventral surface of the brain, we defined four subdivisions of area 12, as first parcellated by Carmichael and Price5. Between subregions of area 12, we could characterize the difference between area 12r and 12m by the existence of distinct vertical processes in layer II in area 12r, compared to the diffuse and dense cell body organization of superficial layers in area 12m. More posteriorly, area 12l differentiated from other subregions of area 12 due to significant SMI-32 staining and reduced stratification between layers. Area 12o was distinguished from area 12l due to the presence of dense vertical projections in layer III. Moreover, we could discriminate area 12o due to lighter neurite projection staining as compared to the adjacent areas 12l and 13l. Using SMI-32 staining we could identify area 13 l through densely stained cells in layers III and V and the presence of long axonal projections in layer III, contrasting adjacent area 13 m. We found that area 11 ml was distinguished by dense SMI-32 staining, specifically in layers III, creating a distinction between adjacent areas 13b and 12m. We did not distinguish between areas 11m and 11l as the medio-lateral boundary of these areas varied appreciably between the two subjects and could not be consistently defined. Lastly, agranular insula (AI) is agranular and defined by distinct dense vertical arbors in layer V. Table 1 lists the key structural characteristics that were used to define each area.

Data Records

Behavioral variables

The behavioral data is organized in 2 elements, t_evt and trialtype (Fig. 3b). First, t_evt is a structure which contains arrays of timestamps (in seconds from recording start at t = 0) for each of the event epochs per trial (fixation cross on/off, stimulus on/off, response fixation, etc.). Each entry in t_evt is the length of the number of trials completed in that session (across all tasks). As we show in the Technical Validation section, any of these arrays can then be used to align the spike timestamps to a particular event and generate a peri-event time histogram. The other behavioral variable is the trialtype table, of size [nbTrials x nbVariables], which provides trial-wise behavioral data, including the task name, the flavor and probability associated with each stimulus shown, the subject’s choice and whether they received a reward or not. All variables are described in detail in Table 3. Stimulus-related parameters are separated by task (e.g., proba_1FC vs probaL_2AFC and probaR_2AFC), so parameters for 2AFC are set to NaN or <undefined> when the monkey is in a 1FC trial, and vice versa.

Neural variables

The neural data is contained in a Matlab cell array called unit (Fig. 3c), which has length of the number of neurons recorded during that session. The main parameters for each unit are the session name, the electrode on which the neuron was recorded (ch ∈ [1,157]), the cluster ID from the sorting routine (clust_id ∈ [1,10]), the depth (in mm) of the electrode tip along its track when the neuron was recorded (depth ∈ [0, −40] with zero representing the bottom of the microdrive and negative values the distance in the ventral direction), the reconstructed 3D location of the neuron (loc_xyz, along the AP, ML, and DV axes), its area (e.g. “12r”, “24c”, “amygdala”) and the average firing rate across the entire recording session (firing_rate). Due to differences in drive placement, electrode insertion angles, and individual neuroanatomy structure, the depth and reconstructed 3D locations should not be used to directly compare recording locations across subjects and implants. Similarly, depths should not be used to infer specific cortical layers from which neurons were recorded because electrodes frequently traverse cortical layers at non-perpendicular angles. Moreover, it should be noted that the cluster ID is an arbitrary index which can be used to dissociate neurons if more than one neuron was recorded per electrode. Spike sorting metrics are also available, namely the isolation probability, the noise overlap probability, peak amplitude (sd), peak noise (sd) and peak signal-to-noise (SNR, sd) (see Fig. 4b, Table 4 and Methods for further details). The average waveform (from −0.75 to 1.25 ms, at 40 kHz sampling rate) and standard deviation are under the variables avg_waveform and std_waveform, respectively, and of size [1 x nbTimebin]. Finally, the timestamps of every spiking event from the corresponding neuron are in the timestamps array (size of [1 x nbSpike]).

Technical Validation

Behavior

Monkeys engaged readily with both tasks, maintaining stable fixation and performing consistently across sessions. Indeed, they exhibited a high level of motivation across both 1FC and 2AFC tasks, completing on average more than 95% of all trials in each session (Fig. 1c). Sessions were typically long, with a median duration of 81 minutes for monkey M and 227 minutes for monkey X.

Importantly, monkeys adjusted their behavior according to the offered contingencies. In the 1FC task, this was reflected in their reaction times, where monkeys directed their gaze to the response box more quickly when the associated probability of reward was higher (Fig. 1d, top panels). To quantify this effect, we fit linear models separately for each session, with log-transformed reaction times as the dependent variable and both outcome probability and flavor as fixed effects. The distribution of t-statistics for the probability regressor was strongly shifted toward positive values across sessions, reaching significance in 35% of them (p < 0.05, monkey M: 60/147 sessions; monkey X: 59/193 sessions) (Fig. 1d, bottom). These results indicate that probability information systematically influenced monkeys’ action selection, even when only a single option was available and no explicit deliberation was required.

Choice behavior in the 2AFC task further demonstrated that monkeys relied on both probability and flavor information (see also22). For each session, we modeled the probability of choosing a given flavor using a logistic regression with the log ratio of the two offered outcome probabilities as predictor. To ensure balanced sampling, the analysis was restricted to a random subset of 200 trials per session (when more were available) and included only trials in which the two options differed in flavor. As expected, the fitted logistic functions revealed a characteristic sigmoidal relationship where monkeys were increasingly likely to choose the option associated with flavor J1 as its relative reward probability increased (Fig. 1e, top panel). Beyond probability-driven choices, both monkeys also showed stable but idiosyncratic preferences for specific juice flavors, expressed as horizontal shifts in the psychometric functions across sessions. At the population level, the main effect of probability ratio was highly significant in both monkeys, with t-statistics consistently exceeding chance expectations (all sessions p < 0.002; Fig. 1e, bottom panel).

Taken together, these behavioral analyses demonstrate that both monkeys not only understood the task structures but also used both flavor and probability information to guide their decisions.

Neuronal isolation and example responses

Putative single neurons were isolated from wideband recordings using the offline sorting toolbox MountainSort29 and later manually curated for isolation and stability. Electrodes were slowly lowered over the duration of the experiment, so earlier sessions were more likely to have more neurons from dorsal areas while later sessions yielded neurons from more ventral areas (Fig. 4a). From MountainSort, we extracted estimates for each neuron of its isolation quality (scaled from 0-1 from least to most isolated), noise overlap (probability), peak signal to noise ratio, and peak noise (Fig. 4b and Table 4). We only provide neurons considered well-isolated (Isolation > 0.75, Noise overlap < 0.2, Peak signal to noise ratio > 0.5, Firing Rate > 0.05 Hz) and exhibiting stable waveforms across the entire recording session such that every neuron was collected across all tasks. This corresponds to a total of 8,168 single neurons from monkey M and 8,327 from monkey X during both 1FC and 2AFC tasks. The total number of neurons recorded from each subdivision is reported in Table 2 and we provide larger areas into which the smaller subdivisions can be combined (e.g. cytoarchitectonic subdivisions of areas 11 and 13 together comprise what is generally referred to as OFC). As described earlier, the isolation metrics are available for each individual neuron and could be used to further restrict the neurons to consider for a given analysis. Note, however, that our manual curation could be considered stringent, and we do not believe much can be gained from further restrictions.

Finally, we perform example peri-event time histograms to demonstrate this workflow on our dataset (Fig. 5; spk_peth.m and spk_peth.py). In the 1FC task, we could identify single neurons encoding probability (Fig. 5a), response side (left or right; Fig. 5b), and flavor (Fig. 5c) around the time of stimulus presentation. In the 2AFC task, we identify similar neurons encoding the probability of the chosen stimulus (Fig. 5d), chosen side (Fig. 5e), and reward receipt (Fig. 5f). These figures were generated directly on the uploaded data with helper functions we provide and can be used as an initial step in any further analysis of these data.

Usage Notes

Dynamic task trials

While both monkeys completed identical versions of the 1FC and 2AFC tasks, the dynamic task differed substantially across monkeys, limiting the feasibility of systematic behavioral or neural analyses of these trials. Accordingly, we strongly recommend against using dynamic task trials for standalone analyses. Instead, a restricted set of behavioral variables (juice flavor, response side, response location, and reward outcome, Table 3) is provided to allow users to model potential carryover effects on surrounding 2AFC trials, such as choice history or reward-dependent modulation. These trials should therefore be used primarily to regress out immediate contextual influences on subsequent choices and neural activity.

For simplicity, dynamic trials share the same event structure as 1FC and 2AFC trials. We therefore provide the timestamps of the different events in the t_evt matrix. One difference is the stimulus onset and offset times, which correspond only to the onset of the first stimulus and the offset of the final stimulus within each dynamic trial. Individual onsets and offsets for each stimulus in dynamic trials are not explicitly represented in order to preserve a consistent tabular structure of the t_evt matrix. The timing of sequential stimuli can nevertheless be inferred from the interval between stim_on and stim_off, together with the known stimulus durations (each stimulus in a sequence is presented on screen for the same duration of either 0.4, 0.6, or 0.8 s, followed by a blank screen for 0.2 s). For example, a time difference of 1.4 s for monkey X (1FC_dyn) means that each of the two stimuli was presented 0.6 s (stim1 for 0.6 s, off for 0.2 s, stim2 for 0.6 s; total time of 1.4 s). Although approximate stimulus timing can thus be reconstructed, detailed information about the probability associated with each individual stimulus was not provided to avoid single-subject analyses related to decision-making or value-based processes in these trials. Nevertheless, dynamic trials may be suitable for analyses of general sensory responses to visual stimuli.

Repeated neuron recordings

Given the semi-chronic nature of our recordings, it is possible that a given neuron might have been counted more than once if it was recorded on consecutive days. Two possibilities can be considered if one wishes to remove potential duplicates. First, users can attempt to identify individual neurons by using their firing characteristics (average waveform, inter-spike intervals, response profiles and/or firing rates). Note that this approach usually works best when applied to other types of neurophysiology recordings using high-density recording probes35,36, as even a small amount of electrode drift can strongly influence firing characteristics and could result in erroneous classifications. Alternatively, users could use the provided recording depth to only consider neurons recorded at locations that are separated by a distance considered to be enough to ensure that the neuron is different. We previously used such a conservative approach in a prior publication and only considered neurons that were recorded at more than 75um apart. This analysis revealed that our results were consistent whether or not this additional threshold was applied22. This is likely due to the large number of neurons recorded in the present dataset, which reduces the impact of any potential duplicate counts on downstream analyses.
