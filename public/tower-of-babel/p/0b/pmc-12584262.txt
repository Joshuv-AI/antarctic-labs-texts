Diagnostic value of AHNAK2 immunohistochemical expression in papillary thyroid carcinoma: an immunohistochemical study
Authors: Xinyi Zhang, Lingli Chen, Minying Deng, Huaiyu Liang, Dongxian Jiang, Huimei Wang, Yufeng Liu, Rui Zhang, Lei Xu, Jia Liu, Akesu Sujie, Chen Xu, Yalan Liu, Yingyong Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultiple immunohistochemical markers including galectin-3 and BRAF V600E have been used to identify benign and malignant thyroid tumors. AHNAK nucleoprotein 2 (AHNAK2) is a novel oncogene that belongs to the AHNAK protein family and is upregulated in many malignant tumors, including thyroid cancers. However, its actual value in pathological diagnosis remains unknown. Therefore, we utilized a set of well-studied markers to further confirm the diagnostic role of AHNAK2 in differentiating thyroid tumors. Two hundred ninety-six papillary thyroid carcinomas (PTCs), 167 follicular adenomas (FAs), 36 follicular thyroid carcinomas (FTCs), and 11 non-invasive follicular thyroid neoplasms with papillary-like nuclear features (NIFTPs) were collected. Markers including AHNAK2, galectin-3, BRAF V600E, HBME-1, Thyroid Peroxidase (TPO), CD56, and cyclin-D1 were used simultaneously for immunohistochemistry (IHC). The positivity rates of the PTC markers were as follows: AHNAK2 (95%), galectin-3 (89.5%), BRAF V600E (82.4%), HBME-1 (84.1%) CK19 (99.7%), cyclin-D1 (98.0%), TPO (61.1%), and CD56 (47.3%). AHNAK2 (4.2%), galectin-3 (6.0%), BRAF V600E (0.0%), HBME-1 (16.8%); CK19 (88.6%), cyclin-D1 (78.4%), TPO (94.0%), and CD56 (94.6%) were identified in FA. The areas under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for each marker in distinguishing PTCs from FAs were AHNAK2 (0.964), galectin-3 (0.935), BRAF V600E (0.907), HBME-1 (0.882), cyclin-D1 (0.776), CK19 (0.752), CD56 (0.053), TPO (0.004), and AHNAK2 in combination with galectin-3 and BRAF V600E (0.992). We firstly found that AHNAK2 was highly expressed in PTC and could be a new promising diagnostic marker for distinguishing thyroid tumors. The combination of AHNAK2 with galectin-3 and BRAF V600E can serve as an efficient diagnostic IHC panel for thyroid cancer, with great clinical potential.Keywords: Papillary thyroid carcinoma, AHNAK2, Immunohistochemistry

Introduction

Thyroid cancers include papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC), poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma, and undifferentiated carcinoma, of these, PTC and FTC, accounting for 90% of all malignant thyroid tumors, are also referred to as differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC) [1]. Benign thyroid tumors comprise follicular nodular lesion, follicular adenoma (FA), and acidophilic cell adenoma. Currently, the classification of benign and malignant thyroid tumors relies mainly on histological assessments. PTC, growing in an invasive manner, is characterized by papillary structure and psammoma bodies. Morphologically, it exhibits acidophilic or clear cytoplasm, oval or ground-glass nuclei with nuclear grooves, and pseudo-inclusions. Diagnosis with histology alone can have potential pitfalls, as the characteristic ground-glass nuclei in PTC can also occasionally be found in benign thyroid diseases, such as thyroid nodular hyperplasia, FA, and lymphocytic thyroiditis, while papillary structures can also appear in FA and acidophilic cell adenoma. In clinical practice, a set of immunohistochemical markers including galectin-3, BRAF V600E, Thyroid Peroxidase (TPO), CD56, cyclin-D1, CK19, etc. have been proposed and widely employed for differential diagnosis [2–7]. However, current choices each have their own limitations in distinguishing between benign and malignant thyroid tumors.

AHNAK2 is a novel oncogene belonging to the AHNAK protein family that has been reported to play a pro-oncogenic role in many cancers, including PTC [8]. Through an experiment using 467 samples of thyroid neoplasms, we discovered that AHNAK2 is highly expressed in PTC, and barely expressed in FA, FTC, non-invasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary-like nuclear features (NIFTP), lymphocytic thyroiditis, and normal thyroid tissue. We believe that this disparity is of great clinical value. Therefore, this exploratory study utilized this set of immunohistochemical markers to evaluate the diagnostic value of AHNAK2 in PTC.

Materials and methods

Tissue Specimens

A total of 463 thyroid neoplasm patients, including 296 PTC patients, 36 FTC patients, 11 NIFTP patients, and 167 FA patients, were recruited from January 2018 to December 2022 in the Pathology Department of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University. All patients underwent total thyroidectomy or unilateral thyroidectomy with isthmic thyroidectomy, followed by histopathological diagnosis. Ethical approval was obtained from the ethics committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (B2024-169). H&E-stained microscope glass slides were reviewed by two experienced pathologists to confirm the diagnosis of thyroid tumors according to the criteria described by the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of thyroid tumors, 5th edition [9]. Unstained sections (3 μm-thick) were prepared from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples, including one section stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and eight for subsequent immunohistochemical staining experiments. Ten pairs of PTC and adjacent tissues were selected for RT-pCR experiments.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracting from paraffin samples of 10 pairs of PTC and corresponding peritumoral tissues using MagPure FFPE RNA/DNA extraction Kit (Guangzhou Magen Biotechnology Co., Ltd). Total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (RR047A, Takara). Quantitative PCR was performed using Applied Biosystems 7500 Real Time PCR System (Roche) and SYBR™ Green Realtime PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO, Japan). The PCR procedure was performed according to standard procedure for two-step PCR amplication (Stage 1: predegeneration 95 °C, 30 s. Stage 2: qRT-PCR 95 °C, 5 s; 60 °C, 34 s; 40 cycles. Stage 3: Dissociation). The primer sequences were AHNAK2-F: CAAGAGGCAACCACACCACTG, AHNAK2-R: TGGAATCAACACCTGTCTCA TCAAC; ACTB-F: GCACTCTTCCAGCCTTCCTTCC, ACTB-R: GCGGATGTCC ACGTCACACTTC. Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−△△CT method.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Representative slides were subjected to IHC staining on the BenchMark XT automated stainer (Ventana Medical Systems, Inc., Tucson, AZ, USA). The immunohistochemical markers applied in this study were AHNAK2, galectin-3, BRAF V600E, HBME-1, TPO, CD56, CK19 and cyclin-D1. The primary antibodies are listed in Table 1. IHC assay was performed with OptiView Universal DAB Detection Kit (Ventana, Tucson, AZ) for AHNAK2 mouse monoclonal antibody, OptiView Universal DAB Detection Kit and OptiView Amplification Kit (Ventana, Tucson, AZ) for BRAF V600E rabbit monoclonal antibody, and ultraView Universal DAB Detection Kit (Ventana, Tucson, AZ) for other antibodies. The specific steps were conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Negative control sections were treated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) instead of the primary antibody. Vascular endothelial cells or nerve fiber tissue in the sections served as positive control for AHNAK2, normal tonsil for galectin-3 and cyclin-D1, melanoma cells for BRAF V600E, colonic epithelial cells for CK19, thyroid epithelial cells for TPO, small-cell lung cancer cells for CD56, and mesothelioma for HBME-1.

The following staining patterns were considered positive: AHNAK2 cytoplasmic or membranous staining, galectin-3 and BRAF V600E cytoplasmic staining, membranous staining of CK19, HBME-1, CD56 and TPO, and nuclear expression of cyclin-D1, respectively. Positive staining of ≥ 1% of the tumor cells was considered a positive result, whereas staining of < 1% was regarded as negative [10]. The proportion of positively stained cells for each marker was divided into grades of every 10%, and percentages < 10% were further divided into 1–5% and 6–10%. A positivity rate >50% in tumor cells was regarded as diffusely positive staining [11]. Two experienced pathologists independently evaluated the immunohistochemical results for each case. The interpretation of the results contained the positive/negative status of the cells, and the range of staining in tumor/non-tumor areas. A diagnosis was confirmed only if two pathologists had consistent results. When there were discrepancies in rating, a third experienced pathologist was invited to make a final judgement.

Statistical analysis

The accuracy of AHNAK2 and other immunohistochemical markers in diagnosing PTC and FA was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC). The Youden Index was applied to determine the optimal diagnostic threshold. Based on this threshold, the specificity, sensitivity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of each marker and their combinations as diagnostic biomarkers were analyzed using a cross-tabulation table. Chi-square test was used to analyze the distribution of various immunohistochemical markers in thyroid tumors. SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was performed for statistical analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical and pathological information

Two hundred and ninety-six patients diagnosed as PTC were enrolled, including 204 females and 92 males, with a median age of 45 years (range: 19–80 y) and an average tumor diameter of 1.2 cm (range: 0.1–3.5 cm). Histologically, there were 207 (69.9%) classical PTCs, 69 (23.3%) infiltrating follicular PTCs, 7 (2.4%) hobnail PTCs, 5 (1.7%) solid PTCs, 3 (1.0%) tall cell PTCs, 4 (1.4%) warthin-like PTCs, and 1 (0.3%) diffuse sclerosis PTC. One hundred and sixty-seven FA patients were collected, including 114 females and 53 males, with a median age of 50 years (range: 16–79 y) and an average tumor diameter of 3.7 cm (range: 1–11 cm). A total of 11 NIFTP patients were enrolled, including 3 males and 8 females, with a median age of 41 years (range: 31–62 y). Thirty-six FTC patients were collected, including 9 males and 27 females, with a median age of 45 years (range: 14 ~ 69 y). Among the surrounding tissues, 385 cases showed normal thyroid tissue and 125 cases presented lymphocytic thyroiditis.

Comparison of diagnostic efficacy of each immunohistochemical marker

By analyzing the ROC curve, we found that the AUC for AHNAK2 in distinguishing PTC from FA was 0.964 (95%CI:0.947–0.982, p < 0.001), higher than that of galectin-3 (AUC = 0.935, 95%CI:0.911–0.960, p < 0.001), BRAF V600E (0.907, 95%CI:0.879–0.935, p < 0.001), HBME-1 (0.882, 95%CI:0.851–0.914, p < 0.001), cyclin-D1 (0.776, 95%CI:0.733–0.819, p < 0.001), CK19 (0.752, 95%CI:0.700–0.805.700.805, p < 0.001), CD56 (0.053, 95%CI:0.027–0.078, p < 0.001), and TPO (0.004, 95%CI:0.000–0.009.000.009, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). When the AHNAK2 immunohistochemical staining score was 2.5%, the sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing thyroid tumors was 94% and 96.3%, respectively, the Youden index was 90.3%, the PPV was 97.2%, and the NPV was 91.4%. When the galectin-3 immunohistochemical score was 7.5%, the sensitivity and specificity was 89.1% and 96.9%, respectively, the Youden index was 86.0%, the PPV was 96.4%, and the NPV was 83.5%. When the BRAF V600E immunohistochemical score tumors was 5%, the sensitivity and specificity was 81.4% and 100%, respectively, the Youden index was 81.4%, the PPV was 100%, and the NPV was 76.3%. When the HBME-1 immunohistochemical score was 15%, the sensitivity and specificity was 77.5%, and 90.7%, respectively, the Youden index was 68.2%, the PPV was 89.9%, and the NPV was 74.7%. When the cyclin-D1 immunohistochemical score was 65%, the sensitivity and specificity was 56.5% and 83.2%, respectively, the Youden index was 39.7%, the PPV was 69.0%, and the NPV was 85.7%. When the CK19 immunohistochemical score was 95%, the sensitivity and specificity was 90.5% and 44.1%, respectively, the Youden index was 46.4%, the PPV was 66.6%, and the NPV was 95% (Table 5). Among these, AHNAK2 had the highest Youden index for the diagnosis of PTC.

Diagnostic efficacy of combined AHNAK2, galectin-3 and BRAF V600E

We divided the proportion of positively stained tumor cells into segments of 0%, 1–5%, 6–10%, 11–20%… 91–100%, and counted the distribution of AHNAK2, galectin-3, and BRAF V600E expression in PTC. AHNAK2 and galectin-3 accounted for the largest proportion when 81–90% of tumor cells were stained (14.5% and 22.3%, respectively), while BRAF V600E was highest in 91–100% of positive cells (49.3%). The expression rates of the three markers were AHNAK2: 95.0%, galectin-3: 89.5%, and BRAF V600E: 82.4%, in the case of positively stained tumor cells ≥ 1%. The expression rates of the three markers were AHNAK2: 73.0%, galectin-3: 75.7%, and BRAF V600E: 79.4%, in the case of positively stained tumor cells > 30%. The expression rates of the three markers when diffusely positive staining (positively stained tumor cells > 50%) were AHNAK2: 59.1%, galectin-3: 68.6%, and BRAF V600E: 77.7%. The positive distributions of AHNAK2 and galectin-3 were more consistent, whereas BRAF V600E was more likely to exhibit a diffusely positive distribution. When negatively expressed, the proportion of AHNAK2 (5.0%) was lower than that of galectin-3 (10.5%) and BRAF V600E (17.6%) (Fig. 4).

Positive distribution ranges of AHNAK2, galectin-3, and BRAF V600E in PTC. (The positive rate of AHNAK2, galectin-3, and BRAF V600E in PTC were 95%, 89.5%, and 82.4%, respectively. The diffuse positive rate were 59.1%, 68.6%, and 77.7%, respectively.)

With respect to the expression status of the three-marker combination in PTC, 222 PTCs (75.0%) were positive for all the three markers. Fifty-five PTCs (18.6%) expressed the two markers simultaneously, and co-expression of AHNAK2 and galectin-3 was observed in 33 PTCs (11.1%). Seventeen PTCs (5.8%) exhibited only one marker positivity, of which AHNAK2 positivity accounted for 3.7% (11 PTCs). Only two PTCs (0.9%) were negative for all three markers (Figs. 5 and 6).

ROC curve analysis showed that the AUC of the three-marker combination was the highest for diagnosing PTC at 0.992 (95% CI: 0.977–0.996, p < 0.001), followed by the combination of AHNAK2 and BRAF V600E (AUC = 0.989, 95% CI: 0.979–0.998, p < 0.001), AHNAK2 and galectin-3 (AUC = 0.986, 95% CI: 0.977–0.996, p < 0.001), and galectin-3 and BRAF V600E (AUC = 0.969, 95% CI: 0.953–0.985, p < 0.001) (Fig. 7). The Youden index for the three markers combined for diagnosing thyroid tumors was 93.3%, sensitivity 96.3%, specificity 97.6%, PPV 95.1%, and NPV 98.7% (Table 6).

Discussion

Given the unique histological features of PTC, namely papillae, ground-glass nuclei, and nuclear pseudo-inclusions, it is easy to reach a correct histopathological diagnosis using H&E staining. However, when thyroid lesions lack papillary structures microscopically, the diagnosis can be challenging without IHC results. In addition, benign conditions such as FA and lymphocytic thyroiditis can also present with papillary morphology and ground-glass nuclei, often leading to misdiagnosis of malignancy. To help confirm the diagnosis, IHC is important in these scenarios to confirm the diagnosis. Many researchers have explored diagnostic markers with better specificity and sensitivity for differentiating between benign and malignant thyroid tumors, and a group of immunohistochemical markers for this purpose has been accumulated in clinical practice, including galectin-3, BRAF V600E, cyclin-D1, CK19 CD56, and TPO [2–7]. In our study, we found that AHNAK2 was overexpressed in PTC, barely expressed in FA, FTC, NIFTP and not expressed in lymphocytic thyroiditis or normal thyroid tissue, indicating a certain diagnostic value. However, no study has yet applied this marker for the differential diagnosis of thyroid tumors. This study explored the expression of AHNAK2 and other widely used immunohistochemical markers in PTC, FTC, NIFTP, FA, lymphocytic thyroiditis, and normal thyroid tissue, and comprehensively evaluated the advantages and disadvantages of each marker, thereby exploring the value of AHNAK2 in differential diagnostic applications.

AHNAK2 is a large protein with a molecular weight of over 600 kDa, which was first discovered by Schtivelman et al. [12] in 1992. Currently, research on AHNAK2 in thyroid cancer is limited. However, some studies have found that the expression of AHNAK2 is increased in thyroid cancer cells at both the mRNA and protein levels, which is consistent with our research [13]. AHNAK2 is also involved in migration, invasion, and metastasis of thyroid cancer cells. Specifically, downregulation of AHNAK2 inhibits the migration and invasion of thyroid cancer cells by suppressing the NF-kB pathway [14]. In addition, AHNAK2 activates the Wnt/B-catenin pathway to promote distant metastasis and epithelial-mesenchymal transition in thyroid cancer, and the expression of β-catenin and cyclin D1 is correlated with the increased expression of AHNAK2 [13]. AHNAK2 may also promote the metastasis and proliferation of thyroid cancer cells through the PI3K/AKT pathway [15]. To date, no study has explored the expression of AHNAK2 in thyroid cancer using IHC. In this study, we tested AHNAK2 expression using both RT-qPCR and IHC assay and found that AHNAK2 had a positivity rate of 95.0% in PTC, which was higher than that of other markers, such as galectin-3, BRAF V600E, and HBME-1. The diffusely positive rate exceeded 50%. The positivity rate in FA was lower than that of other markers, and there was no expression in lymphocytic thyroiditis and normal thyroid tissue. ROC curve analysis showed an AUC of 97% and a Youden index of 91.2% at the optimal cutoff value, which was higher than that of other markers. AHNAK2 demonstrated a high specificity and diagnostic efficiency for differential diagnosis with great clinical value. Furthermore, AHNAK2 was barely expressed in FTC and NIFTP with focal and weak immunohistochemical staining, which can be well differentiated from follicular PTC.

Galectin-3 is associated with thyroid tumor cell migration, adhesion, and apoptosis [16]. In most studies, galectin-3 was a sensitive marker for differentiation between malignant and benign thyroid tumors, with a positivity rate of 64.8% ~ 100% in PTC, 92.3% in FTC, and 0 ~ 11.1% in FA [10,17, 18]. According to present study similar to the literature, the positivity rate of galectin-3 in PTC was 89.5%, lower than that of AHNAK2, CK19, and cyclin-D1. The use of galectin-3 allowed a diagnostic efficiency of 86.0%, second only to that of AHNAK2, and had considerable value in differential diagnosis.

BRAF is a serine-threonine kinase of the RAF protein family, and an effector of the MAPK cascade [19]. Among the numerous molecular changes in the BRAF gene, the most common is the BRAF V600E mutation, where a single-base change from thymine to adenine at position 1,799 in exon 15 results in glutamine replacing valine [20]. Numerous studies have shown that the BRAF V600E mutation activated the MAPK pathway, leading to follicular cell transformation and involvement in the development of PTC [21, 22]. Marchetti [23] and Zatelli [24] combined traditional cytology with molecular detection of the BRAF-V600E mutation, improving the diagnostic accuracy of PTC from 62.3% to 77.3% and 77.3% to 86.7%, respectively. Previous reports have shown that the mutation rate of BRAF V600E in PTC is less than 50% [25], thus the sensitivity of BRAF V600E has certain limitations in the diagnosis of PTC. In this study, BRAF V600E in the diagnosis of PTC exhibited a sensitivity of 81.4% and a specificity of 100%, respectively. In 52 cases of BRAF V600E-negative PTC, we found that AHNAK2 was negative in six (11.5%) cases, expressed in 20 (38.5%) cases with ≤50% of tumor cells stained, and positive in 26 (50%) cases with >50% of tumor cells stained. Therefore, AHNAK2 may serve as a supplementary diagnostic marker for BRAF V600E-negative cases.

HBME-1, a mouse monoclonal antibody, was originally applied to stain malignant mesothelioma. The expression of HBME-1 in PTC varies greatly among studies, with positive rates ranging from 72.3% to 95.5% [26, 27]. In our study, the positive rate of HBME-1 in PTC was 84.1%, which was lower than that of AHNAK2 and galectin-3. However, HBME-1 was also highly expressed in FTC, NIFTP and follicular adenoma, with a low specificity of PTC diagnosis. Moreover, in view of the incomplete staining of the cell membrane and mostly focal positive expression by HBME-1 staining, it may be misdiagnosed as negative in the daily diagnosis process, especially in the diagnosis of cytology.

CK19, a low-molecular-weight cytokeratin, is expressed in tumor cells and the normal thyroid epithelium. Many studies have combined CK19 with other markers, such as galectin-3, as diagnostic markers for thyroid tumors [28]. Here, we found that CK19 was almost completely expressed in tumor cells and that the normal thyroid epithelium also showed a positive reaction. Though the staining intensity in tumor cells was stronger than that in the surrounding normal thyroid epithelium, the specificity was not high, especially in cytological specimens obtained by fine-needle aspiration (FNA). Therefore, its efficacy as a diagnostic marker may be limited.

Cyclin-D1 is a regulatory factor in the G1/S phase of the cell cycle. It is deregulated in cancer and has become a biological marker of cancer phenotype and disease progression [29]. Several studies have confirmed the overexpression of cyclin-D1 in thyroid cancer and its potential as a diagnostic marker for distinguishing benign from malignant thyroid tumors with the PPV of 100% and NPV of 57.1% for diagnosing malignant thyroid tumors in FNA specimens [30, 31]. In our practice, there are limitations to using this marker to diagnose thyroid tumors. First, because of the nuclear staining of cyclin-D1 in both tumor cells and lymphocytes, it is difficult to visualize the overall tumor extent by staining only with this marker, especially in the context of lymphocytic thyroiditis. Besides, cyclin-D1 was highly expressed in both PTC (98%) and FA (78.4%), with a sensitivity of 71.5% and a specificity of 68.4%. It also showed some expression in the normal thyroid tissue and lymphocytic thyroiditis.

CD56 is a neural cell adhesion molecule that is typically expressed in thyroid follicular epithelial cells but not in thyroid cancer. Its expression may affect the migration ability of tumor cells [32]. Loss of CD56 and TPO expression is considered a diagnostic marker for PTC. Different studies have shown that retained CD56 expression in PTC is less than 10%, whereas that in FA is 88.9% [33], which is a good negative diagnostic marker. Unexpectedly, based on this study, retained CD56 expression was seen in 47.3% of PTC and 94.6% of FA with inferior PPV (18.4%) and NPV (5.5%), which were not considered reliable markers for diagnosing PTC. Similarly, many studies have shown that TPO does not have satisfactory diagnostic sensitivity for thyroid lesions and is often used in combination with other molecules such as galectin-3 and CK19 [34]. In this study, the PPV and NPV of TPO were both less than 50%, and the ROC curve displayed almost no ability to distinguish between benign and malignant thyroid tumors.

On the basis of the high diagnostic efficacy of AHNAK2, galectin-3, and BRAF V600E individually, we explored the combined value of the trio for diagnosing thyroid tumors. The ROC curve showed that the AUC of the three markers combined for diagnosis was the highest, reaching 99.2%, and the Youden index was 93.9%, representing a 3.6% improvement over the use of AHNAK2 alone, 7.9% improvement over the use of galectin-3 alone, and 12.5% improvement over the use of BRAF V600E alone, demonstrating better diagnostic efficacy than the individual use of each marker. Furthermore, the combined diagnosis of these three markers effectively supplements the sensitivity of BRAF V600E and galectin-3. The expression distribution of the three markers showed that the range of IHC staining for AHNAK2 and galectin-3 in PTC was relatively consistent, although BRAF V600E expression was generally diffuse, with a lower positivity rate (82.4%) than that of AHNAK2 (95%) and galectin-3 (89.5%). We also observed that 75.0% (222 cases) of PTCs expressed all three markers simultaneously, whereas only 0.7% (two cases) of PTCs were negative for all three markers, further proving the accuracy of the combined diagnosis of AHNAK2, galectin-3, and BRAF V600E for PTC. Among the 5.8% (17 cases) PTCs that were positive for only one marker, AHNAK2 accounted for 3.7% (11 cases), indicating the importance of using AHNAK2 for diagnosing PTC when other markers are not supported. Among the 18.6% (55 cases) of PTCs that were positive for the two markers, AHNAK2 and galectin-3 positivity accounted for 11.1% (33 cases), and AHNAK2 and BRAF V600E positivity accounted for 5.4% (16 cases), reflecting the importance of AHNAK2 in combination diagnosis over the other two markers.

In addition, this study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center investigation with a limited sample size, especially for FTC and NIFTP. Future multi-center studies with large cohorts are warranted to further validate the clinical value of AHNAK2 and its combined application with other markers. Secondly, while the current study focuses on the diagnostic utility of AHNAK2 in thyroid tumors, subsequent research will expand to pan-cancer analyses to explore its potential application in a wider range of malignancies.

Conclusion

In summary, we found that AHNAK2 was highly expressed in PTC but almost not expressed in FTC, FA, normal thyroid tissue, or thyroiditis for the first time. Thus, it may be a highly promising diagnostic marker for various pathological applications, including distinguishing PTC from FA. The combination of AHNAK2 with galectin-3 and BRAF V600E can be recommended as a supplement to morphological assessment for diagnosing thyroid cancers.
