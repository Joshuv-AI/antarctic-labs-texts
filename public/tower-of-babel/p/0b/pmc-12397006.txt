Outcomes of Dialysis Patients Living With HIV in Australia and New Zealand: A Cohort Study Using the Australia and New Zealand Dialysis and Transplant Registry (ANZDATA)
Authors: Mitchell Hunter‐Dickson, Andrea Huang, Douglas Drak, Catherine Zheng, David Gracey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo describe the demographics and comorbidities of people living with HIV (PLWH) starting on dialysis in Australia and New Zealand and to assess mortality outcomes and rates of infective complications.MethodsRetrospective population‐based cohort study of between 1996 and 2022. The primary outcome was the difference in survival between PLWH and HIV‐negative patients started on dialysis. Other outcomes included peritoneal dialysis peritonitis rates and causative organisms.ResultsA total of 82 739 patients were included in the study; 95 (0.1%) were HIV‐positive. The median age at first dialysis was lower in the PLWH group at 53 years (IQR 44–60) compared to 61 years (IQR 49–71) in the HIV‐negative group (p < 0.001). PLWH had higher rates of tubulointerstitial disease (15% vs. 10%) and glomerular disease (29% vs. 23%) than the HIV‐negative cohort (p = 0.030). There were similar patterns of other comorbidities. Median survival from commencement of dialysis was similar between PLWH and HIV‐negative patients at 7.1 years (95% CI 6.1–10.3 years) and 6.3 years (95% CI 6.2–6.4), respectively, (p = 0.34); the younger age at commencement for PLWH meant that median lifespan was approximately 7 years shorter. Causes of death were similar between groups, with cardiovascular death being most common at 43%, followed by withdrawal at 24% and then infection at 17% (p = 0.64). There was no clinically significant difference in peritonitis rates or causative organisms.ConclusionPLWH had a shorter median lifespan as compared to HIV‐negative patients on dialysis; however, this was entirely a consequence of earlier renal failure in PLWH, with on‐dialysis mortality similar between the two groups.

Introduction

Modern antiretrovirals (ARVs) have improved the life expectancy of people living with HIV (PLWH) to near that of the general population [1]. This has allowed a shift in clinical focus towards the management of non‐infective complications of HIV, including chronic kidney disease (CKD) [2].

CKD is increasingly common in PLWH due to both HIV‐related risk factors and higher prevalence of traditional risk factors [3]. HIV‐related risk factors include toxicity from ARVs, immune‐mediated glomerulonephritis, and HIV‐associated nephropathy, although the latter is now rare with modern ARVs [3, 4]. Diabetes, hypertension, and hepatitis C infection are all more common than in the general population and may have an additive effect in the progression of CKD in PLWH [3]. HIV infection has also been shown to be an independent risk factor for more rapid progression of CKD, potentially mediated by the chronic inflammatory state created by HIV, as well as direct viral effects on the kidney [5, 6, 7].

There are conflicting data on outcomes for PLWH started on dialysis, with early studies reporting higher mortality rates compared to HIV‐negative patients, primarily due to bacterial infections and cardiovascular complications [1, 8, 9]. More recently, a large retrospective cohort study from the United States, including over 5000 PLWH on dialysis, found similar mortality rates for Caucasian patients who were HIV‐negative. However, for non‐Caucasian patients and patients co‐infected with Hepatitis C, mortality rates were significantly higher in PLWH [8]. There are no data on outcomes for Australian and New Zealand patients with HIV who receive dialysis. Given differences in patient demographics and models of healthcare in America as compared to Australia and New Zealand, it is important to assess outcomes locally.

Our study aims to investigate the characteristics and outcomes of patients with HIV in Australia and New Zealand using the ANZDATA registry from the start of the widespread availability of ARVs in Australia from 1996 to 2022 [3].

Methods

All adult patients aged ≥ 18 years with kidney failure who commenced dialysis in Australia and New Zealand between 1 January 1996 and 31 December 2022 registered in the ANZDATA registry were included.

Patients were included in the PLWH group if they were ever coded as having HIV. Patients coded in ANZDATA as having ‘suspected’ diagnosis were also included with the confirmed diagnosis group for the analysis.

The primary outcome of interest was the difference in survival between PLWH and HIV‐negative patients. Patient demographics and comorbidities were reported as mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range as appropriate. Wilcoxon rank sum test, Fisher's exact test, and Pearson's Chi‐squared test were used to compare groups.

Ethics approval was granted by the Sydney Local Health District Human Research Ethics Committee‐CRGH (2021/ETH12167). Analysis was completed on de‐identified data extracted from ANZDATA, and individual written consent was not required.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was completed using ‘R’ (2024.12.1 + 563). Patients with missing BMI values (0.03%, n = 2667) had values imputed using the median as it was a predominantly left skewed distribution. One patient's gender was classified as ‘Intersex’; due to rarity, they were also excluded from analysis. Complete case analysis was performed thereafter.

Time‐to‐event analysis was conducted using Kaplan–Meier models. Risk factors for increased mortality were assessed by use of a stratified cox proportional hazards model using propensity matched data to account for unmatched data and potential confounding. Patients were stratified by HIV status to preserve non‐violation of the proportional hazards assumption in the non‐ stratified model. Propensity score matching was performed using nearest neighbour matching (1:1 ratio) with a 0.05 calliper width. Matching was based on age, gender, BMI, smoking status, diabetes, cancer diagnosis, primary kidney disease group. Balance was assessed using standardised mean differences, with a value < 0.1 considered as well‐balanced.

Results

Between 1 January 1996 and 31 December 2022, 83 760 patients commenced dialysis in Australia and New Zealand, of which 95 had a diagnosis of HIV infection. After applying exclusion criteria 82 645 HIV‐negative patients and 94 PLWH were included in the analysis.

Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. Median age at first dialysis was lower in PLWH (53 years, IQR 44–60 years) compared to those in the HIV negative cohort (61 years, IQR 49–71 years); (p < 0.001). A greater proportion of patients in the PLWH group were male compared to HIV‐negative patients (88% vs. 61%, respectively; p < 0.001). Comorbidities were similar between groups with no significant difference in rates of coronary artery disease, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease or malignancy (p > 0.100 for all). The primary kidney disease diagnosis is shown in Table 1. The most common cause in both groups was diabetic kidney disease, at 38% in PLWH and 36% in HIV‐negative patients. A higher proportion of PLWH had underlying tubulointerstitial disease at 15% compared to 10% for HIV‐negative patients; similarly, higher rates of glomerular disease were found in PLWH at 29% compared to 23%. Hypertensive/renovascular kidney disease was less common in PLWH at 3.2% compared to 13% in the HIV‐negative cohort (p = 0.030).

To assess for differences in outcomes at the extremes of the study period patients were stratified into three groups by year of commencement of dialysis. Time periods correspond to major changes in HIV management in Australia, with the introduction of ART in 1996 and the adoption of the Fourth and Eighth Australian National HIV Strategies in 2000 and 2018, respectively. Demographics in different time periods (1996–2000, 2000–2018, 2018–2022) are shown in Table 2. Median age at first renal replacement therapy was lower for those who commenced dialysis in 1996–2000 (54 years old, IQR 40–66 years) compared to those who commenced in between 2018–2022 (63 years old, IQR 51–72 years) (p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in mortality in time periods two and three, no analysis was possible in time‐period one as there were too few PLWH on dialysis. In time‐period two median survival was 7.67 years (95% CI 6.32–11.8) for PLWH, and 10.13 years (95% CI 10.02–10.3) for those who were HIV‐negative. There was no significant difference in survival (p = 0.41). In time‐period three there was again no significant difference in survival (p = 0.22), with a median survival of 3.34 years (95% CI 2.99–4.32) for PLWH and 2.21 years (95% CI 2.17–2.24) for those without HIV. Lifetime prevalence figures of chronic lung disease, coronary artery disease, peripheral vascular disease and cerebrovascular disease were higher although this was likely explained by the older average age and duration of the study. Likewise, the prevalence of cancer and diabetes were also higher. Hepatitis C antibody positivity remained constant over the course of the study (2.8% vs. 2.1%).

Peritoneal Dialysis Outcomes

Peritonitis rates and causative organisms are shown in Table 4. In PLWH requiring dialysis, 41 (43.6%) had peritoneal dialysis (PD) compared to 34 701 (42%) in the HIV‐negative group. In patients on PD, 37 (90.2%) and 28 788 (83.0%) in the PLWH and HIV‐negative patient groups, respectively, had an episode of peritonitis, with median of three episodes for PLWH (IQR 1.5) and two episodes in HIV‐negative patients (IQR 1.4, p = 0.01). The end date of PD was not included in the data. During the study period, only 11 (29.7%) PLWH and 12 594 (43.7%) HIV‐negative patients in total had a single episode of peritonitis. There was a maximum number of 17 repeated peritonitis episodes reported in one HIV‐negative patient. The maximum number of repeated episodes for PLWH reported was nine in one patient. For PLWH and HIV‐negative patients, the most common organisms causing PD peritonitis were Staphylococcus species (including coagulase positive and negative staphylococcus) at 38.2% and 31.3% of PLWH and HIV‐negative patients, respectively. This was followed by gram negative bacteria at 20.6% and 23.4% of PLWH and HIV‐negative patients, and culture negative peritonitis at 20.6% and 17.9%.

Discussion

PLWH required dialysis at a younger age than HIV‐negative patients but had similar mortality outcomes to the HIV‐negative cohort after commencing dialysis. This aligns with recent studies which have similarly found no differences in survival [8, 9].

The younger age at commencement of dialysis in PLWH than in HIV‐negative patients is in keeping with trends in previously reported American and French cohorts. While the age of commencement of dialysis in those studies (46 and 45 years, respectively) is considerably lower than 53 years in our study, this may be more a reflection of the earlier time period in which those studies were performed [8, 10]. In our own study, we noted a delay of first dialysis over time, potentially due to improvements in HIV management.

The increased proportion of patients with tubulointerstitial disease and glomerular disease in PLWH may suggest antiretroviral therapy or direct viral injury contributing to earlier development of end stage renal failure (ESRF) [5]. However, renal biopsy rates were relatively low in PLWH, particularly in those diagnosed with tubulointerstitial disease (Table S2), potentially leading to confirmation bias as these patients may have been assumed to have complications of HIV.

There was no significant difference in mortality outcomes for PLWH compared to the HIV‐negative cohort once on dialysis. This was unexpected given PLWH started dialysis approximately 9 years earlier than the HIV‐negative patients, and younger age at dialysis initiation is typically associated with prolonged survival on dialysis [11]. This seems to be a reproducible trend for PLWH, however, with a large US cohort study with a similar age difference between PLWH and HIV‐negative cohorts, finding a 1‐year survival rate of 83%–92% for PLWH and no association between HIV status and increased mortality in Caucasian patients [8]. Similarly, a prospective French cohort study followed 164 dialysis patients on ARVs between 2002–2004, finding no significant difference in mortality when compared to an HIV‐negative cohort, reporting a 1‐year survival rate in PLWH of 93.8% and a 2‐year survival rate of 89.4% [10].

In the ANZDATA cohort, there was an increased mortality in hepatitis C positive patients compared to those with negative hepatitis C serology. Although there were divergences detected in the trend over time, there was no clinically significant difference in median survival. Increased mortality in dialysis patients with positive hepatitis C serology was also identified in a large meta‐analysis of 574 000 dialysis patients from nine countries, finding an adjusted mortality risk of 1.26 [12, 13]. We did not find a significant difference in mortality of HIV and hepatitis C co‐infected patients; however, this was a small subgroup of only 13 patients. Increased mortality in patients co‐infected with HIV and hepatitis C was reported in the American cohort study, finding a HR for mortality of 1.38–1.67 [8]. The excess mortality in patients with hepatitis C may be explained by limited access to direct acting antivirals for hepatitis C as they were only introduced in Australia in 2016, midway through our study period, and were introduced after many of the studies included in the metanalysis were published [14]. The similar mortality rate seen in co‐infected patients may suggest that PLWH may have received treatment for hepatitis C with resolution of infection. Inclusion of hepatitis C viral load to confirm active infection would be important in future studies assessing outcomes of hepatitis C infection in dialysis patients.

In both groups, the most frequent cause of death was cardiovascular, followed by dialysis withdrawal and then infection. This is in keeping with the US cohort study, although rates of withdrawal from dialysis in that study were not reported [8]. The proportion of cardiovascular death was higher in our population, though this may reflect a relative reduction in infective complications rather than worsening of cardiovascular disease. Previous studies have reported higher infective mortality rates, from 30% to 54% in German, Spanish and San Francisco cohorts of PLWH on dialysis; however, these studies included patients treated prior to the widespread use of ARVs and had lower patient numbers [15, 16]. In a propensity matched Cox regression, age was found to be a predictor of mortality (HR 1.07, p < 0.05) which suggests that older patients at dialysis initiation have worse outcomes. This may represent increased cardiovascular risk in older PLWH with an increasing burden of traditional cardiovascular risk factors in combination with increased cardiovascular risk of HIV infection [17].

The significant burden of cardiovascular mortality in our patient cohort reinforces the importance of managing non‐infective complications of HIV. There is an increasing array of medications to treat cardiovascular disease and slow the progression of CKD, including SGLT2 inhibitors; however, medications for cardiovascular disease tend to be prescribed less frequently in PLWH [18]. There are risks of drug–drug interactions and, particularly for SGLT2 inhibitors, concerns about the potential increase in urogenital infections in PLWH. Small retrospective studies have shown lower rates of prescription for SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP1‐receptor agonists in PLWH compared to HIV‐negative patients, potentially due to concerns about safety or a lack of guidance on their use in this population [19, 20]. There is a need for further studies to assess the safety of these medications and to help with guideline development given the increasing burden of cardiovascular disease in PLWH.

There was no clinically significant difference in PD peritonitis rates with a median of three per patient in PLWH compared to two in the HIV‐negative cohort. Previous studies have shown similar mortality and PD failure rates for PLWH and HIV‐negative patients [8, 21]. However, there has been variability in relative peritonitis rates for PLWH. Two small single centre studies from South Africa found no increased risk of peritonitis in PLWH, but a larger prospective cohort study, also in South Africa, reported significantly higher rates of peritonitis in PLWH when compared to HIV‐negative patients [21]. The prospective study was completed in a resource‐poor setting with a high baseline rate of PD peritonitis, and despite increased rates of peritonitis, there was no difference in the risk of PD catheter failure at 18 months when compared to HIV‐negative controls [21]. We found no significant difference in causative organisms for PD peritonitis; there were similar rates to those reported in the PDOPPS study for Australia, Canada and Thailand [22]. Most organisms were bowel or skin flora, with no episodes of mycobacterial peritonitis in PLWH and only one episode of fungal peritonitis. This result confirms peritoneal dialysis as a safe and effective renal replacement modality for PLWH in Australia and New Zealand.

Limitations

Our study had a number of limitations; ANZDATA does not report HIV‐specific data, including viral load, CD4 counts, or antiretroviral therapy, all of which may impact infection rates and survival outcomes. Further, ARVs changed throughout the study period, which may also affect prognosis. There is no requirement by ANZDATA for renal biopsy to assess the cause of kidney disease, potentially confounding diagnosis, as multiple potential factors can contribute to the development of end‐stage kidney disease in PLWH. A potential lack of consistency in registry data may lead to discrepancies in diagnosis coding and accuracy of outcome data. Despite including all PLWH who required dialysis in Australia and New Zealand over a 26‐year period, the sample size for HIV‐positive patients was small.

Conclusion

There was no difference in mortality outcomes for PLWH and HIV‐negative patients in Australia and New Zealand after commencing dialysis. However, as PLWH presented earlier with end‐stage kidney disease, their median lifespan was approximately 7 years shorter. Cardiovascular disease was the most common cause of death in both groups, and no excess of infective complications was identified in PLWH on dialysis. This study demonstrates the importance of addressing non‐infective complications of HIV and confirms that both peritoneal dialysis and haemodialysis are suitable for PLWH.
