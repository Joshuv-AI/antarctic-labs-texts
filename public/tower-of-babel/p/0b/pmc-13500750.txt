Life’s Essential 10 and brain health: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Yang DU, Feiran YANG, Qing YE, Jeffrey W Ripley-Gonzalez, Liwen HE, Fangkun LIU, Ray W Squires, Thomas P Olson, Xinyin WU, Siyao GAO, Yaoshan DUN, Jian XIA, Suixin LIU, Xufang WU
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMaintaining brain health while extending life expectancy is an important public health challenge in aging societies. However, the association between cardiovascular health (CVH) and brain health has not been fully established. This study aims to develop a Life’s Essential 10 (LE10) framework for assessing CVH and to evaluate the effects of different CVH levels on life expectancy free of stroke and dementia.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included adults aged 37-73 years from the UK Biobank who had no history of stroke or dementia at baseline and had complete data on LE10 metrics and covariates. The LE10 framework developed in this study was based on Life’s Essential 8 (LE8), which comprises eight domains, diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep duration, body mass index, blood lipids, blood pressure, and blood glucose, and additionally incorporated alcohol consumption and sedentary behavior. A total score was calculated by assigning a score to each domain, and participants were categorized as having low, moderate, or high CVH. The primary outcome was life expectancy free of stroke and dementia. Secondary outcomes included the risk of the composite endpoint of stroke or dementia, the risk of all-cause mortality, and total life expectancy.ResultsAmong 122 953 participants, with a mean age of 55.7 years and 48.4% being women, 13.2%, 60.0%, and 26.8% had low, moderate, and high CVH, respectively. During a median follow-up of 13.8 years, 4 776 composite endpoint events, 3 317 cases of stroke, 1 697 cases of dementia, and 7 825 deaths were recorded. Compared with low CVH, high CVH was associated with a lower risk of the composite endpoint in both men (HR=0.59, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.67) and women (HR=0.50, 95% CI 0.42 to 0.59). High CVH was also associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality in men (HR=0.44, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.48) and women (HR=0.35, 95% CI 0.31 to 0.40). At 50 years of age, men with high CVH had a stroke- and dementia-free life expectancy of 31.8 years (95% CI 31.1 to 32.5), compared with 24.9 years (95% CI 24.5 to 25.2) among those with low CVH. The corresponding estimates for women were 35.5 years (95% CI 34.7 to 36.2) and 28.4 years (95% CI 27.8 to 28.9), respectively. Analyses of stroke- and dementia-free life expectancy at 60 and 70 years of age and comparisons of total life expectancy across CVH groups consistently showed that higher CVH was associated with longer disease-free survival and total life expectancy.ConclusionHigher CVH, as assessed using the LE10 framework, was closely associated with better preservation of brain health and a lower risk of all-cause mortality. As global population aging accelerates, maintaining brain health has become a critical public health priority. The American Heart Association (AHA)/American Stroke Association (ASA) related scientific statement proposed that optimal brain health at the clinical level can be defined as the absence of cognitive impairment or dementia, stroke, and other brain diseases, and identified stroke and dementia as important clinical outcomes for monitoring brain health[1-2]. In 2019, stroke and dementia accounted for 87.2% of neurological deaths and 61.7% of neurological disability-adjusted life years globally[3]. In the same year, 57.4 million people were living with dementia, a figure projected to rise to 152.8 million by 2050[4], and the number of people worldwide with stroke reached 101 million[5]. Despite their distinct underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, stroke and dementia are both major adverse brain health outcomes that lead to cognitive impairment, functional dependence, decreased quality of life, and increased disease burden[6-7]. Early identification and tailored prevention are essential to delay onset and reduce the burden of poor brain health.

Cardiovascular health (CVH) is closely associated with stroke and dementia[3]. Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) provides a framework for assessing CVH by integrating lifestyle factors associated with cardiovascular well-being[8]. Higher CVH has been linked to a reduced risk of poor brain health-related diseases[9], and may attenuate stroke and dementia risk[10-11]. Furthermore, recent evidence[12] has shown higher CVH to be associated with increased life expectancy free of common non-communicable chronic diseases. However, research on the relationship between CVH and life expectancy with preserved brain health remains limited, restricting the evidence base needed to guide targeted prevention strategies and limiting the application of CVH. Additionally, LE8 does not consider sedentary behavior or alcohol consumption, despite their recognition as key risk factors for both cardiovascular and brain diseases[13-14] and inclusion in prevention guidelines[7, 15]. It is crucial to note that sedentary behavior and physical activity are distinct, independent behavioral constructs with different underlying biological mechanisms. Sedentary behavior is not simply a lack of physical activity; in fact, individuals can meet the recommended guidelines for moderate-to-vigorous physical activity but still face elevated health risks due to prolonged sedentary time. For instance, recent study[13] has demonstrated that high amounts of sedentary behavior are significantly associated with an increased risk of incident dementia among older adults, regardless of their physical activity levels. Therefore, separating these 2 indicators in our analysis provides a more precise and scientifically justified evaluation of their independent health impacts.

To address these gaps, this study introduced Life’s Essential 10 (LE10), an expanded CVH model that adds sedentary behavior and alcohol consumption into LE8 to support a more comprehensive strategy for brain health management. In this study, brain health was defined as the absence of stroke and dementia, in order to evaluate the association between CVH and poor brain health outcomes from a holistic clinical perspective. We assessed the associations between LE10 and life expectancy with preserved brain health.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The UK Biobank study was approved by the North West Multicentre Research Ethics Committee (Approval number: 16/NW/0274). All participants provided written informed consent. In addition, this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (Approval number: 202307141) and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400083272). We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Study design and population

Participants were screened from a prospective cohort of more than 500 000 individuals within the UK Biobank, aged 37 to 73, between 2006 and 2010 across 22 assessment centers in England, Wales, and Scotland[16]. We included participants with complete data on the LE10 metrics. Individuals who were lost to follow-up, pregnant, in poor health, or had a baseline diagnosis of stroke, dementia, or cancer were excluded. Additional exclusions were made for those missing data on covariates. The study protocol is available online[17].

Assessment of LE10

LE10 metrics, including alcohol consumption, sedentary behavior, physical activity, diet, tobacco/nicotine use, and sleep duration, were collected through questionnaires. Blood pressure was measured by trained nurses, and blood samples were collected to measure non-high-density lipoprotein (non-HDL) cholesterol and blood glucose.

Alcohol consumption was classified into 6 categories: 0-100 g/week, >100-130 g/week, and >130 g/week subdivided into quartiles based on previous criteria[18] and population distribution[19]. Sedentary behavior was self-reported as daily hours spent in activities like TV watching, computer use, or driving, and scored by deciles[20]. Data collection and scoring methods for LE10 metrics are provided in Supplementary Method 1 (https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134).

The standardized LE10 score (range 0-100) was derived by weighting each metric using β coefficients from covariate-adjusted Cox regression models, summing, normalizing by total weights, and scaling to 100. Higher scores indicate better CVH.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was life expectancy free from stroke and dementia. Secondary outcomes included the composite or singular incidence of stroke or dementia, all-cause mortality, and overall life expectancy.

Mortality data were obtained from NHS death registries, and stroke and dementia incidence from primary care, hospital records, and death registries. Diagnoses were based on the earliest recorded date, classified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes (Supplementary Method 1, https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134). Follow- up was from enrollment to diagnosis, death, or censoring on 30 November 2022, corresponding to the latest mortality data linkage available at the time of analysis.

Covariate assessment

Covariates included age, sex, ethnicity, Townsend deprivation index (TDI), education levels, annual household income, and family history[12].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean±standard deviation. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages). Participants were stratified into 3 CVH levels based on their LE10 scores: Low (<60), moderate (60-<80), and high (≥80). The data-driven turning points were initially identified using restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression analysis[21]. To ensure clinical utility and ease of application, these mathematical cut-offs were subsequently rounded to practical integers, conceptually mirroring the classification framework established by the AHA guidelines[8] and restricted cubic spline regression analysis[21] (Supplementary Figure 1, https://doi.org/10. 57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134). The LE10 scoring algorithm is detailed in Supplementary Method 2 (https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134).

We used Cox proportional hazards regression to estimate hazard ratio (HR) for disease incidence and all-cause mortality. Potential confounders, including age, sex at birth, ethnicity, TDI, education level, annual household income, and family history of disease, were adjusted. The proportional hazards assumption was tested by calculating scaled Schoenfeld residuals and no violations were observed. Interactions between CVH and polygenic risk scores (PRS) for stroke and dementia were assessed using relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and likelihood ratio tests[22].

Life expectancy was estimated using multistate life tables[23]. Monte Carlo simulations were used to estimate 95% confidence interval (CI) via the R package elect[24]. To assess the potential increase in life expectancy by improving LE10 metrics, we estimated overall life expectancy and additional life-years gained from enhancing low-risk CVH metrics. Detailed statistical methods for these calculations are outlined in Supplementary Method 3 (https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134).

Subgroup analyses were performed for sex, age, socioeconomic conditions, and family history. Sensitivity analyses were conducted, including landmark analyses excluding events occurring within the first 2 years of follow-up, and multiple imputation of missing data using chained equations[25]. Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) were used to quantify and rank the associations between LE10 metrics and brain health outcomes, leveraging a machine learning approach[26]. Further details are provided in Supplementary Method 4 (https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134).

To quantify the incremental predictive value of LE10 to LE8, we calculated concordance index (C-index), continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). To address potential overfitting from deriving and evaluating the LE10 score within the same cohort, we performed internal validation using 5-fold cross-validation. The study population was randomly divided into 5 approximately equal-sized folds stratified by outcome status. In each iteration, 4 folds were used to fit the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model, with β coefficients re-estimated in the training set, and the remaining fold was used for validation. This process was repeated until each fold had served once as the validation set. Finally, cross-validated C-index and pooled HR were obtained to assess model stability.

All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2 and Python version 3.9.7, with statistical significance set at P<0.05.

Results

Demographics and characteristics

A total of 122 953 participants (48.4% women) with complete LE10 data were included after excluding those lost to follow-up (n=398), pregnant or in poor health (n=5 408), with prevalent stroke or dementia (n=13 080), or missing covariates (n=13 868; Supplementary Figure 2, https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134). The age was (55.7±8.1) years, and LE10 score was 72.5±10.9. CVH was classified as low in 13.2%, moderate in 60.0%, and high in 26.8%. Higher LE10 CVH scores were more common in younger individuals, women, and those with higher education and income. Over a median of 13.8 years (interquartile range 13.1 to 14.5; 1 658 965 person-years) a total of 4 776 (3.88%) composite events of stroke or dementia were recorded (3 038 men, 1 738 women), equating to 2.9 events per 1 000 person-years; 3 317 (2.70%) initial stroke events (2 166 men, 1 151 women) and 1 697 (1.38%) initial dementia events (1 044 men, 653 women) corresponding to 2.0 and 1.0 events per 1 000 person-years, respectively. Additionally, there were 7 825 deaths (6.36%) reported during the follow-up period (5 253 men, 2 572 women) equating to 4.7 events per 1 000 person-years.

Associations of LE10 CVH with brain health

Restricted cubic spline analysis consistently demonstrates that prolonged daily sedentary time and excessive weekly alcohol consumption correlate with elevated risks of composite incidence of stroke or dementia, stroke incidence, dementia incidence, and all-cause mortality, with statistically significant overall and non-linear dose-response relationships (all overall and non-linear P<0.001; Supplementary Figures 3 and 4, https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134). Higher alcohol consumption and sedentary behavior scores were associated with reduced composite incidence risks compared to low scores, with HRs of 0.88 (95% CI 0.80 to 0.96) and 0.75 (0.69 to 0.82), respectively (Supplementary Table 4, https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134). This trend extended to individual incidence of either stroke or dementia (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6, https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134).

Stroke incidence rates per 1 000 person-year were 1.5 (1.3 to 1.7) versus 3.8 (3.5 to 4.2) among men with high versus low CVH, and 0.8 (0.7 to 0.9) versus 2.7 (2.3 to 3.1) among women. The corresponding dementia incidence rates were 0.8 (0.6 to 0.9) versus 1.6 (1.4 to 1.8) among men, and 0.5 (0.4 to 0.6) versus 1.3 (1.0 to 1.6) among women.

Participants with the most low-risk CVH metrics had the lowest composite and disease-specific incidence risks (Supplementary Figure 5, https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb. xbyxb.00134). Kaplan-Meier analyses revealed participants with high CVH demonstrated the highest survival probabilities free of disease incidence throughout the follow-up (Figure 1A-1C).

Association between LE10 CVH and all-cause mortality

Restricted cubic spline analysis demonstrated an inverse association between LE10 score and all-cause mortality (Supplementary Figure 1, https://doi.org/10. 57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134). Participants with high CVH had the lowest all-cause mortality risk (men: HR=0.44, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.48, P<0.001; women: HR=0.35, 95% CI 0.31 to 0.40, P<0.001) compared to those with low CVH (Table 2, Figure 1D). Additionally, participants with the highest number of low-risk CVH metrics had the lowest all-cause mortality (Supplementary Figure 5, https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134).

High or moderate alcohol consumption scores were associated with lower all-cause mortality, compared to low scores, with HRs of 0.65 (0.61 to 0.70) and 0.76 (0.69 to 0.83), respectively. Lower sedentary time was also associated with reduced mortality risk, with HRs of 0.78 (0.73 to 0.83) and 0.91 (0.86 to 0.97) for high and moderate sedentary behavior scores, respectively (Supplementary Table 7, https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb. xbyxb.00134).

Association between CVH and overall life expectancy

At age 50, men with low, moderate, and high CVH were projected to live 29.1 (28.7 to 29.5), 33.3 (32.9 to 33.7), and 36.2 (35.5 to 36.9) years, respectively; women, 32.8 (32.2 to 33.4), 37.1 (36.5 to 37.7), and 40.1 (39.4 to 40.9) years, respectively. High CVH was associated with longer life expectancy compared to low CVH, with an increase of 7.1 (7.1 to 7.2) years in men and 7.3 (7.3 to 7.4) years in women. Similar trends were observed at ages 60 and 70 (Supplementary Table 8,https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134).

Life expectancy increased with the number of low-risk LE10 metrics (Supplementary Table 9,https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134). Among men, it increased from 27.0 (26.3 to 27.6) years in those with 0-1 low-risk metrics to 38.9 (38.1 to 39.7) years in those with all 10 (Supplementary Figure 6A, https://doi.org/10. 57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134), while for women, it rose from 30.1 (29.4 to 30.9) years to 42.2 (41.4 to 43.1) years (Supplementary Figure 6B, https://doi.org/10. 57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134). Achieving complete low-risk LE10 metrics was associated with gains of 12.0 years in men (Supplementary Figure 6C, https://doi.org/10. 57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134) and 12.1 years in women (Supplementary Figure 6D, https://doi.org/10. 57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134).

Association between CVH and life expectancy with preserved brain health

At age 50, men with low, moderate, and high CVH were estimated to live 24.9 (24.5 to 25.2), 28.8 (28.4 to 29.2), and 31.8 (31.1 to 32.5) years free of stroke and dementia, respectively. Women were estimated to live 28.4 (27.8 to 28.9), 32.4 (31.9 to 33.0), and 35.5 (34.7 to 36.2) years, respectively (Figure 2A). Estimates of life expectancy free of stroke and free of dementia are presented separately in Figures 2B and 2C, respectively. Life expectancy with preserved brain health increased with the addition of further low-risk metrics (Supplementary Table 11,https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134). Participants with all 10 low-risk CVH metrics had a life expectancy with preserved brain health of 33.7 (33.0 to 34.5) years for men and 37.5 (36.7 to 38.3) years for women, while participants with 0-1 low-risk metric were 22.2 (21.7 to 22.7) years for men and 25.6 (25.0 to 26.3) years for women (Figure 3A and 3B). Improving all 10 metrics to low-risk status at age 50 was associated with disease-free life-years gained of 11.5 (11.5 to 11.5) years for men and 11.8 (11.8 to 11.9) for women (Figure 3C and 3D). Similar trends were observed for stroke or dementia individually (Supplementary Figures 7 and 8; Supplementary Tables 10 and 11; https://doi.org/10. 57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134).

At age 50, men with the highest alcohol consumption scores gained 2.6 (2.6 to 2.6) years free of stroke and dementia compared to those with the lowest scores (Supplementary Figure 9, https://doi.org/10. 57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134); women gained 2.7 (2.6 to 2.7) years (Supplementary Figure 10, https://doi.org/10. 57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134). Comparable trends were observed for sedentary behavior (Supplementary Figures 9 and 10, https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134).

Subgroup and sensitivity analysis

In subgroup analyses, the inverse association between LE10 and composite disease incidence (Supplementary Figure 11, https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb. 00134), individual incidence of stroke or dementia (Supplementary Figures 12 and 13, https://doi.org/10. 57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134) and all-cause mortality (Supplementary Figure 14, https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb. xbyxb.00134) remained consistent. Similar patterns were observed for life expectancy stratified by socioeconomic factors and by family history (Supplementary Figure 15-18, https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134).

Sensitivity analyses incorporating multiple imputation and 2-year landmark analyses supported the robustness of the findings with consistent results across analyses for outcomes including disease incidence and mortality (Multiple imputation: Supplementary Figure 19; Landmark analysis: Supplementary Figure 20; https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134), and overall and disease-free life expectancy (Multiple imputation: Supplementary Figure 21; Landmark analysis: Supplementary Figure 22; https://doi.org/10. 57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134).

Genetic risk levels did not alter the protective association between higher CVH and reduced incidence of both stroke and dementia. This inverse relationship remained consistent across 3 genetic risk strata (Supplementary Tables 12 and 13, https://doi.org/10. 57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134).

Discriminative performance and internal validation

For all-cause mortality, the C-index for LE8 was 0.731 (0.725 to 0.736), whereas for LE10 was 0.736 (0.730 to 0.741), representing an increase of 0.005 (P<0.001). For composite incidence of stroke or dementia, the C-index was 0.739 (0.732 to 0.746) for LE8 and 0.742 (0.735 to 0.749) for LE10, representing an increase of 0.003 (P<0.001).

Additionally, the LE10 yielded a statistically significant improvement in continuous NRI across all outcomes, including all-cause mortality (0.026, 95% CI 0.007 to 0.043, P<0.001), the composite outcome of stroke and dementia incidence (0.070, 95% CI 0.045 to 0.089, P<0.001), stroke incidence (0.073, 95% CI 0.037 to 0.095, P<0.001), and dementia incidence (0.052, 95% CI 0.012 to 0.095, P=0.020). Similarly, the IDI showed significant improvements for all-cause mortality (P<0.001), the composite of stroke and dementia incidence (P<0.001), and stroke incidence (P<0.001). However, the IDI improvement for dementia incidence alone did not reach statistical significance (P=0.119).

Internal validation using 5-fold cross-validation showed stable model discrimination across all outcomes (Supplementary Table 14, https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb. xbyxb.00134). The cross-validated C-indices were 0.724± 0.005 for the composite incidence of stroke and dementia, 0.696±0.004 for stroke, 0.784±0.007 for dementia, and 0.727±0.008 for all-cause mortality. Furthermore, moderate and high CVH levels consistently predicted significantly lower risks for all outcomes compared to low CVH (all P<0.001).

Online platform

To facilitate the application of the LE10 model, we developed MyLifeEssential (https://brain.mylifeessential.com), a web-based risk assessment tool. Users input demographic and LE10 data to generate aggregate and individualized LE10 scores, alongside personalized incidence risk estimates for stroke and dementia. The platform also projects potential risk reductions achievable by improving CVH metrics. Prioritizing data security, the tool operates without collecting personally identifiable information (PII). All inputs are processed transiently for real-time calculations and are never stored or shared, as detailed in the confidentiality statement provided prior to use (Supplementary Figure 23, https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134).

Discussion

This study developed the LE10-based CVH by incorporating sedentary behavior and alcohol consumption into the LE8 framework. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that a higher LE10 score is associated with more favorable brain health outcomes.

A growing body of research has independently linked CVH to increased lifespan[27-28], reduced risk of stroke[10], and lower incidence of dementia[29]. In this study, we found that individuals with optimal CVH profiles not only lived significantly longer but also experienced delayed onset of stroke and dementia compared with those with suboptimal CVH. The results of this study showed that higher CVH was inversely associated with the risk of stroke incidence, dementia incidence, and composite incidence of stroke or dementia. These consistent findings suggest that the association observed for the composite outcome was unlikely to be driven solely by either stroke or dementia, but may reflect a broader and relatively consistent relationship between optimal CVH and reduced risk of brain health-related adverse outcomes. These results underscore the clinical importance of evaluating and managing CVH and extend its implications for promoting healthy aging and preserving brain health.

Previous evidence[30-31] demonstrated higher CVH, as measured by LE8, is associated with a reduced risk of noncommunicable diseases. However, LE8 does not account for alcohol consumption or sedentary behavior, despite both being evidence-based predictors of stroke and dementia. LE10 addresses this gap and aligns with international guidelines, including the AHA’s 2024 guideline for the primary prevention of stroke[15] and 2024 report of the Lancet standing commission for dementia prevention[7], which emphasize achieving core CVH metrics and reducing alcohol intake and sedentary behavior. Moderate to high alcohol consumption is associated with a higher risk of stroke and dementia[32-33]. Furthermore, sedentary time is associated with a 44.0% greater incidence of stroke and greater dementia risk, especially when total sedentary time exceeds 10 hours per day[13, 34]. In our study, both alcohol consumption and sedentary time were associated with the composite incidence of stroke or dementia, and with longer life expectancy. SHAP analysis confirmed their significant contributions (Supplementary Figure 24, https://doi.org/10. 57760/sciencedb.xbyxb.00134).

The inclusion of alcohol consumption and sedentary behavior in the LE10 framework is strongly supported by well-established biological and molecular mechanisms linking these risk factors to stroke, dementia, and reduced life expectancy. Excessive alcohol intake contributes to cerebrovascular pathology through multiple pathways, including sustained hypertension, dysregulated lipid metabolism, chronic systemic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction[35-36]. These alterations not only increase stroke risk but also promote neurotoxicity, white matter degradation, and cortical atrophy, accelerating cognitive decline and dementia onset[37-38]. Similarly, heightened sedentary behavior induces adverse physiological changes such as insulin resistance, disrupted glucose homeostasis, elevated inflammatory cytokine production, and impaired cerebral perfusion[39]. These factors synergistically promote vascular injury and neurodegeneration, contributing to both increased stroke susceptibility and progressive cognitive impairment[40].

Although the incremental improvement in predictive discrimination (i.e., the C-index) from LE8 to LE10 observed in our study was modest, this reflects a well-recognized phenomenon in epidemiological modeling: It is methodologically challenging to achieve large absolute increases in discrimination when adding variables to an already robust baseline model like LE8. Therefore, we emphasize that the primary clinical value of transitioning to LE10 lies in guiding holistic risk modification rather than purely enhancing risk prediction. By integrating alcohol consumption and sedentary behavior—both highly prevalent and modifiable independent risk factors for cardiovascular and brain health—LE10 provides a more comprehensive framework for primary prevention. Crucially, capturing these 2 additional metrics introduces negligible operational or financial burden to the healthcare system. Unlike physiological biomarkers, they require no laboratory tests, physical examinations, or specialized equipment, and can be efficiently assessed through routine verbal inquiries or simple self-reported questionnaires during standard clinical encounters. Consequently, LE10 facilitates more integrated and streamlined behavioral counseling in primary care without adding practical complexity.

Nevertheless, several limitations merit consideration. First, despite our adjustment for multiple confounders and the consistent findings from the 2-year landmark analysis, residual confounding (e.g., unmeasured socioeconomic trajectory or psychosocial stress) and reverse causality remain plausible alternative explanations that cannot be entirely excluded. Second, individuals with high CVH may differ in other health-related or socioeconomic attributes, which potentially introduces residual confounding. Third, CVH metrics and lifestyle factors were measured exclusively at baseline. Given the dynamic nature of lifestyle behaviors, our inability to account for longitudinal changes over the follow-up period is a notable limitation. Such reliance on baseline data may lead to non-differential misclassification, which typically biases the results toward the null and potentially attenuates the true strength of the observed associations. Future studies incorporating repeated measurements of CVH metrics and lifestyle behaviors are warranted to better capture their dynamic changes over time and to further refine the assessment of their associations with health outcomes. Fourth, the study cohort may not be fully representative of the general population. Because our sample was predominantly of European ancestry and generally healthier than average, the absolute life expectancy estimates may not generalize to more diverse or higher-risk populations. Future studies in diverse, multi-ethnic, and higher-risk cohorts are warranted to validate these findings and improve generalizability. Nonetheless, the relative comparisons between CVH categories should be internally valid. Fifth, MyLifeEssential online tool only serves as a theoretical derivation model based on a single cohort and is strictly intended as an academic demonstration platform rather than a mature tool for clinical medical decision-making. Future multi-center external validation studies are essential and planned before recommending its broader clinical application. Finally, assessments of lifestyle factors, particularly alcohol consumption and sedentary behavior, were self-reported in the UK Biobank and may be prone to measurement or reporting bias.

In conclusion, LE10 represents a novel tool for comprehensive CVH assessment. Higher LE10 CVH was strongly associated with lower risks of composite stroke or dementia, and reduced all-cause mortality, suggesting that improving CVH may serve as a practical clinical and public health strategy to prevent major chronic neurological diseases and to promote healthy longevity and brain health. Additionally, we developed MyLifeEssential online platform as a research demonstration and health education tool to visualize the associations of LE10 CVH with related health outcomes and life expectancy.
