Effect of transcutaneous electrical acupoint stimulation on major complications after cerebral endovascular procedures (ASSET): study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Kai-li Qu, Jing-Wen Fu, Tao He, Min-Juan Zhang, Yan Huo, Zhi-Hong Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:To evaluate the effect of transcutaneous electrical acupoint stimulation (TEAS) administered during surgery on major morbidity after cerebral endovascular procedures.Methods:A single-center randomized controlled trial was conducted in Xijing hospital. 158 participants scheduled for cerebral endovascular procedures are randomly assigned to the TEAS group and the control group in a 1:1 ratio, receiving TEAS and no stimulation, respectively. The primary outcome is the incidence of in-hospital major complications after surgery. The secondary outcomes include the VAS score of pain and the QoR-40 score at 1 day and 3 days after surgery, the incidence of major complications and mortality by 30 days and 90 days after surgery, and the length of postoperative hospital stay.Ethics and Dissemination:This study protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Xijing hospital. All participants are required to provide written informed consent. This study will be published on completion in peer-reviewed publications.Conclusion:The results of this study will provide evidence of TEAS as a standardized perioperative adjunctive therapy for patients undergoing cerebral endovascular procedures.Trials Registration Number:NCT05413460.

Introduction

Acupuncture, a key therapeutic modality of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), involves the insertion of fine, sterile needles into specific anatomical points. Supported by a growing body of clinical evidence, acupuncture is recommended for a spectrum of conditions, most notably chronic pain, migraine prophylaxis, and nausea/vomiting associated with chemotherapy and pregnancy[1]. When applied perioperatively, acupuncture-related techniques can offer multiple therapeutic benefits, including reduced anesthetic requirements[2], prevention of anesthesia-related complications[3–5], enhanced postoperative analgesia, and improved recovery of gastrointestinal function[3,4]. These multimodal effects position acupuncture as a valuable adjunctive therapy in comprehensive stroke management and perioperative care[6]. Acupuncture could be of particular value in neurosurgical patients, especially those undergoing supratentorial craniotomy, by mitigating postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV)[7], which is a critical concern due to its potential to increase intracranial[8] and intraocular[9] pressure after surgery. Furthermore, vasospasm and ischemic events are not rare after cerebral endovascular procedures[10,11]. In patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage, acupuncture was proved effective in preventing cerebral vasospasm[10–12], indicating that acupuncture may help to decrease vascular complications after surgery. Besides, acupuncture was reported to induce lower levels of proinflammatory cytokines and lower incidence of fever after craniotomy, indicating an anti-inflammatory effect of acupuncture[13], which is in accordance with findings in experimental research[14].

However, whether acupuncture-related techniques could decrease major morbidities after cerebral endovascular procedures is still unclear. The major morbidity after cerebral endovascular procedures was reported to be as high as 15%[15,16] and mortality as high as 1%[16]. In patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage, the morbidity of pulmonary infection can be as high as 58%, and the incidence of cerebral infarction was 40%[17]. Furthermore, based on our team’s previous findings that TEAS could decrease the use of anesthetics including opioids and propofol, and maintain intraoperative hemodynamic stability, TEAS potentially reduces the risk of cerebrovascular complications by minimizing blood pressure fluctuations[1,2,18]. Considering the above multiple benefits of acupuncture related techniques, they may help to improve the postoperative outcomes after cerebral endovascular procedures. Thus, in this study, we hypothesize that electrical acupoint stimulation before and during surgery would decrease the in-hospital morbidity of major postoperative complications after cerebral endovascular procedures. The article is compliant with the Transparency In The reporting of Artificial Intelligence (TITAN) Guidelines[19].

Methods and design

Ethical issues

The study protocol and statistical analysis plan was approved by the ethics committee (Xijing hospital). The study was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05413460).

Design

The study design adhered to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines in the preparation of this protocol. The SPIRIT Figure and Checklist are shown in Table 1 and Supplemental Digital Content file 1, available at: http://links.lww.com/ISJP/A27, respectively). The study is an investigator-initiated single-center randomized clinical trial held in **. The study compares TEAS versus no stimulation control in patients undergoing cerebral endovascular procedures. A total of 158 participants will be enrolled in this study and randomly allocated to two groups in a 1:1 ratio using computer-generated randomization sequences created by PASS software. To ensure allocation concealment, the randomization sequence will be securely sealed in sequentially numbered, opaque envelopes. An independent research assistant, who is not involved in either the administration of anesthesia or postoperative follow-up assessments, will open the corresponding sealed envelope only after the participant has been admitted to the operating room, thereby implementing the allocation process. According to the assignment, the patients will receive TEAS or no stimulation from 30 min before anesthesia induction to the end of the surgery. The follow-up period lasts until 90 days after surgery. Patients

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria included patients scheduled for elective cerebral endovascular procedures under general anesthesia and aged 18 years or older.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with any of the following conditions will be excluded: contraindications to the use of electro-acupuncture (including those with infection or injury of the skin to attach electrodes, and those with implanted electrophysiological devices); difficulty in communication; American Society of Anesthesiologists class III or higher; Hunt-Hess class III or higher; body mass index < 18 kg/m2 or > 30 kg/m2; large or giant cerebral aneurysms with diameters greater than 15 mm; minor cerebral aneurysms with diameters less than 3 mm; recurrence of an cerebral aneurysms after endovascular procedures or surgical clipping; severe abnormal coagulation function, severe liver and kidney function damage, or combined with heart and respiratory system failure; history of neurological or psychiatric diseases; use of sedative or analgesics before surgery; and participation in other clinical researchers within 3 months.

Interventions and comparison

Rationale for acupuncture protocol

On the day of surgery, patients are randomly assigned to the TEAS group or the control group. For all the patients, the electrodes (5 × 5 mm2) are placed at DU20 (Baihui), bilateral PC6 (Neiguan), and ST36 (Zusanli) and are connected to the Hwato Electrical Acupoint Stimulator (model No. SDZ-V; Suzhou Medical Appliances Co, Ltd, Suzhou, China). Thirty minutes before surgery, intervention is performed by an investigator who is not involved in anesthesia and follow-up. For the TEAS group, DU20, ST36, and PC6 are stimulated. For the control group, electrodes are attached, but no stimulation is given.

DU20, ST36, and PC6 are chosen and identified according to the TCM theory. DU20 is most often used for the treatment of neurologic disorders, including brain ischemia. ST36 is most often used acupoint for improving gastrointestinal function and alleviating inflammation. PC6 may benefit the patient by preventing PONV, improving analgesia and enhancing hemodynamic stability. DU20 is located at the midpoint of the line connecting the apexes of the two auricles. ST36 is located in the anterior aspect of the leg, at 3 cun (Chinese inch) below the kneecap and one cun from the anterior crest of the tibia. PC6 is located at 2 cun proximal to the transverse wrist crease, between the tendons of palmaris longus and flexor carpi radialis. One cun is defined as the width of the patient’s thumb (see Supplemental Digital Content material 2, available at: http://links.lww.com/ISJP/A27). The patients in the TEAS group receive electrical stimulation with the “disperse-dense” waves of alternating frequencies of 2 and 10 Hz for 2-second cycle. The threshold intensity is defined as perception of heaviness, numbness, and swelling in the stimulated location. The lower and upper safety limits of the threshold intensity are 4 mA and 11 mA for DU20 and PC6, and 4 mA and 20 mA for ST36. Electrical stimulation is administered 30 minutes before anesthesia induction and continues until the end of the surgery. Patients in the control group receive the same treatment without electrical stimulation.

Blinding

Blinding of the patients is difficult because the electrical stimulation starts when the patients are awake. To minimize the related potential bias, in patients of the control group electrodes are attached to the same acupoints as the TEAS group but no stimulation is given. Moreover, we test the stimulation threshold of all the participants and tell them that they may or may not feel the electrical stimulation. The intervention is administered by a specific investigator who is not involved in anesthesia and follow-up. The stimulator is placed in an opaque box to keep the surgical team and the anesthesiologists blinded to the group allocation. Treatment assignments are concealed from data collectors, research staff, and the statisticians. All statistical analyses will be performed with the intervention groups, named A and B. Unmasking will be done when severe adverse events occur.

The success of the blinding of the patients will be validated at 24 h after surgery by the Bang blinding index[17]. The participants are systematically interviewed regarding their perceived group allocation and asked to provide rationale for their belief. Based on these responses, a blinding index was calculated and scaled along a continuum ranging from −1 (complete incorrect guessing) to 1 (complete correct guessing), with 0 indicating random guessing (perfect blinding).

Operative details

All surgeries will be done under general anesthesia with tracheal intubation. The following parameters will be monitored during surgery: ECG, HR, ABP, RR, SpO2, EtCO2, temperature, urine volume, and blood gas analysis. Propofol, sufentanil, and rocuronium will be used for anesthesia induction. Propofol and remifentanil infusion will be used for anesthesia maintenance. Protective lung ventilation with a tidal volume of 6–8 ml per kilogram of predicted body weight and a positive end-expiratory pressure of 6–8 cm of water will be used. Postoperatively, patients will be transferred to the post-anaesthesia care unit or intensive care unit (ICU) depending on the condition.

At the end of the surgery and predefined time points, the patients are followed up. The investigators recorded all data into case report forms. The case report form will be monitored by a clinical research associate and will be forwarded to the principal investigator for inspection. When follow-up finished, the patient records are transmitted to the statistician for locking and analysis.

Outcome measurement

The primary outcome is the incidence of in-hospital major complications after surgery. Postoperative major complications include myocardial infarction, heart failure, need for mechanical ventilation, stroke, delirium, and coma.

Key secondary outcomes include the incidence of major complications and mortality by 30 days, 90 days, and 6 months after surgery, and the length of postoperative hospital stay.

Other secondary outcomes include episodes of hypotension during surgery, serum levels of interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α at the end of the surgery, the VAS score for pain, MMSE and the QoR-40 score at 1 day and 3 days after surgery, and the scores of the Rankin’s scale at 3 days after surgery.

Sample size calculation and statistical analysis

The primary outcome is the incidence of in-hospital major complications after surgery. According to previous reports, the incidences of postoperative complications after cerebral aneurysm surgery vary from 15% to 58%[17]. Assuming a decrease in postoperative morbidity from 40% in the control group to 20% in the TEAS group, with a power of 80% and α level of 5%, 158 patients (N =79 for each group) are needed.

The modified intention-to-treat (mITT) population is defined as the patients who are randomized and receive at least one treatment session. The per-protocol (PP) population is defined as the patients who completed follow-up for the primary endpoint. All analyses are based on the mITT population.

The descriptive analysis is used for the baseline characteristics of the patients in each group. For the primary outcome and category secondary outcomes, the absolute risk difference (RD) and associated two-sided 95% confidence interval CI are estimated using the Newcombe–Wilson score method. For continuous secondary outcomes, normally distributed data are compared between groups using the independent, unpaired, two-tailed t test, whereas non-normally distributed variables are compared using the Mann–Whitney test. Where appropriate, we express statistical uncertainty with 95% CIs. Kaplan–Meier estimates of survival curves and log-rank tests are used to compare survival distributions between the two groups. The multiple imputation method will be used to account for missing data on morbidity and mortality after surgery. A two-sided P<0.05 is considered statistically significant. A Bonferroni correction is used to account for multiple comparisons.

Sensitivity analysis will include analysis of the primary outcome in the ITT set (those who are randomized) and PP set, subgroup analysis of the primary outcome based on type of surgery (coiling or stent), gender (male or female), and age (less than 65 or 65 years or older), and analysis of individual complications. Interim analysis is not designed. All statistical analyses will be conducted with SPSS, version 26 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and R 4.3.3 software (The R Project for Statistical Computing, www.r-project.org).

Discussions

The result of this trial is expected to provide evidence that TEAS is effective for decreasing major postoperative complications in hospital in patients undergoing cerebral endovascular procedures.

Cerebral endovascular procedures carry significant risks of major complications including myocardial infarction, heart failure, mechanical ventilation requirement, stroke, delirium, and coma[17,20]. Mechanisms include anesthetic-induced cardiovascular suppression, hemodynamic instability, surgical stress-induced inflammation, and impaired organ perfusion. TEAS, a non-invasive acupuncture alternative, shows promise in mitigating these complications through multiple mechanisms. Previous research demonstrates that perioperative TEAS application reduces anesthetic requirements, stabilizes hemodynamics, enhances organ perfusion, and attenuates inflammatory responses across various surgical settings[1,2,4,6,18,21,22]. Specifically, in ambulatory surgery patients, TEAS decreased opioid and propofol consumption while reducing related adverse events[23,24], and improved postoperative cognitive function was observed in both heart valve replacement and elderly abdominal surgery patients[25,26]. In both adult and pediatric patients undergoing cardiac surgery, TEAS has shown reduced myocardial injury indicated by less cardiac events, lower postoperative cardiac troponin I levels, and shorter ICU stays with TEAS[27,28]. Preclinical evidence from animal and healthy volunteer studies reveals that preemptive electroacupuncture protects against ischemic injury and cerebral hypoperfusion[29,30]. Clinical study further proved that TEAS reduces neuronal injury markers (S100β and NSE) in cranial surgery patients[22]. These cumulative findings strongly suggest that TEAS exerts significant protective effects against both surgical stress and cerebral injury, positioning it as a valuable adjunctive therapy for cerebral endovascular procedures.

A major issue in the design of this trial is setting of the control group. Since the objective of this trial is to clarify the effectiveness of TEAS, we choose no-stimulation as the control group. In studies in which a group receiving acupuncture is compared with another group, the control or comparator can be sham acupuncture, usual care, active treatment, a waiting list, or no treatment[31,32]. Previous studies have shown the significant difference between the TEAS group and the no-stimulation control group as to the consumption of opioids and postoperative nausea and vomiting[23,24].

Another major concern of this trial is the blinding of the participants. TEAS starts from 30 min before anesthesia, indicating that the participants are awake during the first 30 min of TEAS. There were studies in which acupuncture was started after the induction of anesthesia and revealed reduced efficacy or a lack of efficacy, although the mechanism underlying this phenomenon is not known[33]. To minimize the potential bias, we tell the patients they may or may not feel the stimulation during the intervention. The effects of blinding are evaluated after intervention.

Recent developments of artificial intelligence (AI) have provided extremely promising applications in molecular biology, drug discovery[34], patient-specific diagnosis and treatment[35,36]. AI is revolutionizing TCM as well[37]. In TCM practice, AI transforms diagnostic approaches by processing multimodal data from modern instruments, enhancing the objectivity and consistency of the four diagnostic methods while maintaining TCM’s holistic essence[38]. It systematically evaluates formula-syndrome relationships and herbal combination rules, supporting standardized protocol development and optimizing pharmaceutical formulations through advanced data analytics[37,39,40]. However, integration challenges remain in data standardization, algorithmic transparency, and clinical validation. In acupuncture, AI has achieved breakthroughs in acupoint selection, manipulation identification, and efficacy prediction[41,42]. The integration of modern instruments with AI techniques improves quantification and personalization of acupuncture therapy. Particularly promising is the future integration of TEAS with AI. Combining TEAS’s neuroprotective effects with machine learning could elucidate the “acupoint-brain function” relationship and enable real-time, adaptive stimulation systems. This advancement would shift treatment from standardized protocols to personalized regimens, particularly in perioperative management. Neuroimaging studies combined with machine learning have begun revealing how different acupoint stimulations modulate brain plasticity, with baseline neural features predicting treatment outcomes[43]. While these developments face challenges including the need for better integration of TCM theory, comprehensive databases, and clinical validation, it bridges ancient wisdom with modern science. This interdisciplinary convergence marks a critical pathway for achieving both precision medicine and the modernization of TCM practices.

In conclusion, the results of this trial are expected to clarify whether TEAS is effective in decreasing the major complications after cerebral endovascular interventions.
