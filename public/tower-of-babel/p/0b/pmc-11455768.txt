Clinical Assessment of Breast Cancer Resistance Protein (BCRP)-Mediated Drug–Drug Interactions of Sepiapterin with Curcumin and Rosuvastatin in Healthy Volunteers
Authors: Lan Gao, Diksha Kaushik, Kimberly Ingalls, Sarah Milner, Neil Smith, Ronald Kong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveSepiapterin, also known as PTC923 and CNSA-001, is a synthetic form of endogenous sepiapterin being developed as a novel oral treatment for phenylketonuria. Sepiapterin is a natural precursor of tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) and, when orally administered, is converted to BH4 via the pterin salvage pathway. In vitro studies have demonstrated that both sepiapterin and BH4 are both substrates and inhibitors of the breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP) transporter. This phase I study investigated BCRP-mediated drug–drug interactions of sepiapterin as a victim and as a perpetrator.MethodsAn open-label, fixed-sequence, four-period, crossover, single-dose study was conducted in adult male and female healthy volunteers (18–55 years of age). In a given treatment period, subjects received a single oral dose of sepiapterin (20 mg/kg), sepiapterin (20 mg/kg) plus curcumin (2 g), rosuvastatin (10 mg), or rosuvastatin (10 mg) plus sepiapterin (60 mg/kg). The pharmacokinetics of sepiapterin, its metabolite BH4, and rosuvastatin were studied, and geometric mean ratios of exposures in the presence and absence of the BCRP inhibitor curcumin or sepiapterin were estimated. The presence of the BCRP c.421C>A polymorphism was evaluated in all subjects.ResultsA total of 29 subjects were enrolled and included in the safety analysis. Among them, 26 subjects were included in the pharmacokinetic and drug–drug interaction analyses. Following oral administration 20 mg/kg sepiapterin, sepiapterin was rapidly and extensively converted to BH4, and BH4 maximum observed concentration (415.0 ng/mL) was observed 4.95 h (time to maximum observed concentration) post-dose. Sepiapterin maximum observed concentration and area under the concentration–time curve from time 0 to time of the last quantifiable measurement or the last sample collection time (AUClast) were <1% of BH4 values. Coadministration of the BCRP inhibitor curcumin (2 g) increased BH4 maximum observed concentration, AUClast, and area under the concentration–time curve from time 0 extrapolated to infinity by 24%, 21%, and 20%, respectively. When sepiapterin was coadministered with the BCRP substrate rosuvastatin, there was no effect on the pharmacokinetics of rosuvastatin. BCRP c.421C/A carriers (n = 4) had higher plasma exposures of BH4 (1.39 × for AUClast) and rosuvastatin (1.61 × for AUClast) than c.421C/C carriers (n = 22). Greater increases in BH4 exposures (1.33 vs 1.18 for AUClast) were observed in c.421C/A carriers compared with c.421C/C carriers when sepiapterin was coadministered with curcumin. All treatments were well tolerated during the study.ConclusionsOral coadministration of the BCRP inhibitor curcumin slightly increased the plasma exposure of sepiapterin and its metabolite BH4 in healthy volunteers. This modest increase was deemed not clinically meaningful. Sepiapterin did not alter the pharmacokinetics of the BCRP substrate rosuvastatin.

Key Points

Coadministration of the breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP) inhibitor curcumin with sepiapterin increased exposure to the major active metabolite of sepiapterin, tetrahydrobiopterin, by 1.24-fold, 1.21-fold, and 1.20-fold for maximum observed concentration, area under the concentration–time curve from time 0 to time of the last quantifiable measurement or the last sample collection time, and area under the concentration–time curve from time 0 extrapolated to infinity, respectively.Oral coadministration of sepiapterin had no impact on the pharmacokinetics of the BCRP substrate rosuvastatin.Higher plasma exposures of tetrahydrobiopterin and rosuvastatin were observed in subjects with BCRP c.421C/A compared with those with the c.421C/C genotype, and greater increases in tetrahydrobiopterin exposures were observed when sepiapterin was coadministered with a BCRP inhibitor.

Introduction

Sepiapterin (also known as PTC923 or CNSA-001) is a synthetic form of endogenous sepiapterin, which is a natural precursor for the biosynthesis of tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) [1, 2]. Tetrahydrobiopterin serves as a cofactor for phenylalanine hydroxylase, which is an essential catalyst for the conversion of phenylalanine to tyrosine [3, 4]. Exogenously administered oral sepiapterin significantly reduced blood phenylalanine in subjects with phenylketonuria (PKU), including those with classical PKU and those who are not responsive to sapropterin dihydrochloride treatment, in phase II and phase III studies [5, 6].

The pharmacokinetics of sepiapterin and its metabolite BH4 were previously investigated in adult healthy volunteers in a first-in-human study following single and multiple ascending oral doses of sepiapterin [1] and in a relative bioavailability and food effects study [7]. Following oral administration, sepiapterin is quickly absorbed, and rapidly and extensively converted to BH4 via the pterin salvage pathway. The sepiapterin maximum observed concentration (Cmax) and area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) from time 0 to 24 h are generally <1% of the BH4 values in healthy volunteers, and plasma concentration declines to below the lower limit of quantitation (0.5 ng/mL) by 8 h. For BH4, the plasma concentration reaches the maximum at approximately 5 h (time to Cmax [Tmax]) and returns to a level close to the endogenous level by 24 h. Administering sepiapterin with low-fat and high-fat diets increased BH4 AUC from time 0 extrapolated to infinity (AUCinf) 1.8-fold and 2.8-fold, respectively, at a 60-mg/kg dose. No apparent accumulation of BH4 was observed after 7-day repeated daily dosing at doses up to 60 mg/kg with a high-fat diet. Tetrahydrobiopterin exposures increased approximately dose proportionally within the dose range of 5–20 mg/kg and far less than dose proportionally in the dose range of 20–60 mg/kg when administered with a high-fat diet. The highest doses in healthy volunteers and subjects with PKU are a single dose of 80 mg/kg under fasted conditions and a repeated dose of 60 mg/kg with a high-fat diet.

Sepiapterin is metabolized by sepiapterin reductase into dihydrobiopterin, which is then reduced by dihydrofolate reductase to form BH4 [1]. When sepiapterin is exogenously administered, the resulting BH4 is believed to follow the same pathway as endogenous BH4, being oxidized to form BH4-4α-carbinolamine while serving as a cofactor for aromatic amino acid hydroxylases (such as phenylalanine hydroxylase and tyrosine hydroxylase) and regenerated to BH4 by pterin-4α-carbinolamine dehydratase and dihydropteridine reductase [2]. It is unlikely that cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes make any significant contribution in the process. While in vitro studies indicate that BH4 is a substrate of efflux transporter P-glycoprotein, sepiapterin is not a substrate of P-glycoprotein (data on file). Neither sepiapterin nor BH4 is an inhibitor of the hepatic uptake transporter organic anion transporting polypeptide 1B1 (OATP1B1) (data on file). In vitro investigations reveal that both sepiapterin and BH4 are substrates of the human breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP) and at the same time weak inhibitors of BCRP (in vitro half maximal inhibitory concentration [IC50] values of sepiapterin and BH4 estimated to be 647.7 and 778.4 μM, respectively [data on file]).

Breast cancer resistance protein constitutes a major efflux transporter of the adenosine triphosphate-binding cassette subfamily G member 2 (ABCG2) affecting the transport of structurally diverse endogenous and exogenous molecules across cellular membranes [8]. Breast cancer resistance protein is expressed in various tissues, such as the gastrointestinal tract, liver, kidney, brain endothelium, mammary tissue, testis, and placenta, and acts as a barrier to drug entry and disposition [9]. The importance and associated toxicity of transporter-mediated drug–drug interactions (DDIs) has long been recognized [10–13]. Clinically, BCRP regulates intestinal absorption and biliary excretion [14]. Inhibiting intestinal BCRP often results in increased plasma concentration for orally administered BCRP substrates, while inhibition of hepatic BCRP has effects on hepatobiliary clearance that are best assessed indirectly through a hepatic pharmacodynamic biomarker [14]. A genetic polymorphism in transporters may lead to variability in drug exposures and associated toxicity and/or efficacy [11, 15]. The most frequent single nucleotide polymorphism of BCRP, c.421C>A (rs2231142), harbors at higher frequencies in Asian than in Caucasian or African American populations, and often higher plasma concentrations of a BCRP substrate are observed in subjects with the c.421C>A mutation due to reduced BCRP activity [16]. However, a comparison of BCRP polymorphism on DDI effects has been reported infrequently, often because of an insufficient number of subjects in typical DDI studies carrying BCRP c.421A/A or c.421C/A genotypes [14].

Pharmacokinetic (PK) properties of sepiapterin and consequently its metabolite BH4 may be impacted when sepiapterin is orally coadministered with BCRP inhibitors. In the recently completed, phase III APHENITY study (NCT05099640), adult subjects with PKU were treated with orally administered sepiapterin at 60 mg/kg/day, a dose level at which the gastrointestinal sepiapterin concentration is estimated to be more than 100-fold of the IC50 values. Both sepiapterin and BH4 are highly soluble in aqueous solution; solubility of sepiapterin is estimated to be greater than 2.95 mg/mL (12.4 mM) at pH 1.0 at 37 °C in aqueous solution, and much higher aqueous solubility is estimated for BH4 (~20 mg/kg) [data on file]. As the solubility versus IC50 ratios are greater than ten-fold for both sepiapterin and BH4, coadministration of sepiapterin with a BCRP substrate may impact the pharmacokinetics of such a drug, warranting clinical investigation. The present study was designed to evaluate BCRP-mediated DDIs of orally administered sepiapterin with sepiapterin as a victim and as a perpetrator, by coadministering sepiapterin with a BCRP inhibitor and with a BCRP substrate, respectively.

Curcumin, a naturally occurring polyphenol from the root of the plant Curcumin longa Linn, was used as the BCRP inhibitor. The source of curcumin is turmeric, a frequently used spice with a long history and medicinal properties as an antioxidant agent that have been well recognized [17]. No curcumin-related toxicity was reported at doses up to 8 g/day during the phase I clinical trial testing curcumin as a chemopreventive agent in high-risk or pre-malignant conditions, in which subjects received oral curcumin for 3 months [18]. Curcumin potently inhibits BCRP in vitro (inhibition constant, 0.70 ± 0.41 μM) [19], and clinically, coadministration of curcumin (2-g tablets) increased BCRP substrate sulfasalazine exposure 3.2-fold [19].

Rosuvastatin was used as the BCRP substrate. Rosuvastatin is a prescription medication for the treatment of dyslipidemia with a recommended dose range of 5–40 mg and an accumulation ratio of approximately 1.3–1.4 [20]. Rosuvastatin is frequently used as the indicative victim of an intestinal BCRP substrate and hepatic uptake transporter OATP1B1 at 10 mg in clinical DDI investigations [14]. Oral coadministration of eltrombopag (75 mg), a BCRP inhibitor with in vitro IC50 of 2.7 μM, with rosuvastatin (10 mg) increased rosuvastatin AUCinf by 55% and Cmax by 103% [21]. A single oral dose of 50 mg of omecamtiv, a BCRP inhibitor with in vitro IC50 of 2.9 μM, increased rosuvastatin (10 mg) AUCinf by 27.1% and Cmax by 54.2% [22].

Here, we report the results of a clinical investigation of the DDIs of sepiapterin, evaluated both as a substrate of BCRP when coadministered orally with the BCRP inhibitor curcumin and as an inhibitor of BCRP when coadministered orally with the BCRP substrate rosuvastatin, in healthy adult male and female volunteers. All enrolled subjects were assessed for the presence of the BCRP c.421C>A (rs2231142) polymorphism to investigate the potential impact of BCRP polymorphisms on the pharmacokinetics and DDIs of sepiapterin.

Subjects and Methods

Study Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the International Council for Harmonization ethical principles of good clinical practice and the Declaration of Helsinki. The final protocol and informed consent document were reviewed and approved by an institutional review board (Advarra Institutional Review Board, Columbia, MD, USA). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to enrollment.

Study Design and Subjects

A fixed-sequence, open-label, four-period, crossover, single-dose study was conducted in healthy volunteers at a single site in the USA (Fig. 1). Healthy adults aged 18–55 years and with a body mass index between 18.5 and 30.0 kg/m2 were screened to assess eligibility within 28 days prior to the study. Eligible subjects checked into the clinical research facility on day −1 and received a single oral dose of sepiapterin (20 mg/kg) on day 1 (Period 1), sepiapterin (20 mg/kg) administered simultaneously with curcumin (two 1-g tablets, Doctor’s Best® Curcumin C3 Complex and BioPerine) on day 5 (Period 2), rosuvastatin (10-mg tablet, Crestor®) on day 9 (Period 3), and rosuvastatin (10 mg) administered simultaneously with sepiapterin (60 mg/kg) on day 15 (Period 4). All study drugs were administered within 30 min of starting a high-fat, high-calorie breakfast following an overnight fast. Food intake restriction continued until 4 h post-dose. Consumption of grapefruit, orange, and/or apple juices was not allowed from 2 h prior to dosing to 2 h post-dose. Consumption of cranberry juice was prohibited from day −1 through to discharge. Pharmacogenomic Profiling

A 2.0-mL blood sample was obtained on day −1 for BCRP c.421C>A (rs2231142) polymorphism analysis from subjects enrolled in the study. Samples were collected in tubes containing dipotassium ethylene diamine tetra acetic acid (K2EDTA) and immediately stored at −70 °C prior to shipment to Erasmus MC (Rotterdam, the Netherlands) for BCRP genotyping analysis. DNA isolation was performed using the MagCore® Nucleic Acid Extraction kit (Atrida, Amersfoort, the Netherlands) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. BCRP SNP c.421C>A analysis was carried out using a validated fluorogenic 5′-nuclease assay (TaqMan ABI 7500; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), real-time polymerase chain reaction, and DNA sequencing including three control samples (wild type, heterozygous, and variant) as previously described [23].

PK Analysis

Whole blood samples (4.0 mL each) were obtained pre-dose (≤60 min) and 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 12, 24, 32, and 36 h post-dose for sepiapterin and BH4 (day 1 and day 5), and pre-dose and 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 36, 48, 72, and 96 h post-dose for rosuvastatin (day 9 and day 15) via venipuncture into Vacutainer® tubes containing K2EDTA. Detailed procedures regarding the collection, storage of plasma samples, and quantitative determination of sepiapterin and BH4 in plasma using a validated high-performance liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry method were described previously [24]. Plasma rosuvastatin concentrations were determined using a validated high-performance liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry method [25]. The lower limit of quantitation for sepiapterin, BH4, and rosuvastatin were 0.75 ng/mL, 0.5 ng/mL, and 20 pg/mL, respectively.

Non-compartmental analysis of plasma concentration–time data was performed using Phoenix® WinNonlin, version 8.3 or higher (Certara, Princeton, NJ, USA). Area under the concentration–time curve was calculated using the linear-up, logarithm-down trapezoidal method. Regression analysis was performed on the terminal elimination phase of individual semi-logarithm concentration–time data. At least three non-zero post Tmax, the span ≥1.5-fold of the estimated half-life (T1/2), and adjusted R2 ≥ 0.80 were required for the regression. Area under the concentration–time curve from time 0 extrapolated to infinity was estimated only if the T1/2 could be reliably determined and the extrapolated AUC was ≤20%.

To determine the DDI effects, the geometric mean ratio (GMR) and 90% confidence interval (CI) of the log-transformed PK parameters Cmax, AUC from time 0 to time of the last quantifiable measurement or the last sample collection time (AUClast), and AUCinf were calculated. For Tmax, a non-parametric analysis was used to calculate median differences between treatments using the Hodges–Lehmann method [26, 27].

Safety and Tolerability

Treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) and drug-related adverse events were assessed daily through the duration of the study, starting from signing of the informed consent and continuing until the safety follow-up. Evaluations included clinical laboratory tests (chemistry, hematology, and urinalysis), physical examinations, 12-lead electrocardiograms, and vital signs (blood pressure, respiratory rate, and heart rate). Treatment-emergent adverse events were coded according to the System Organ Class and Preferred Term using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities version 26.0.

Results

Demographics

A total of 29 adult healthy subjects enrolled in the study and received at least one dose of the study treatments. All 29 subjects were included in the safety analysis set. Of these, three subjects were excluded from the PK and DDI assessments because of possession of food and drink items prohibited by the study protocol. The PK analysis set (N = 26) consisted of comparable numbers of male (n = 15, 58%) and female (n = 11, 42%) subjects with a mean age of 38 ± 9 years (Table 1). The majority were White (46%) and Black or African American (42%). Among the subjects included for the PK analysis, the majority were identified as carriers of the BCRP genotype c.421C/C (n = 22, 85%), and a few had the genotype c.421C/A (n = 4, 15%). Both sepiapterin and BH4 represent endogenous compounds. At pre-dose, the sepiapterin plasma concentration was below the quantitation limit (0.75 mg/mL), while the mean endogenous BH4 plasma concentration was 2.38 ng/mL. Following oral administration, sepiapterin was quickly absorbed and rapidly and extensively converted to its active metabolite BH4. Sepiapterin reached Cmax with a median time of 4.00 h and was quickly eliminated and dropped below the quantitation limit by 8 h. Because of the fast elimination, sepiapterin T1/2 and AUCinf could not be reliably estimated. Sepiapterin Cmax and AUClast were both <1% of its metabolite BH4. Tetrahydrobiopterin plasma concentration reached Cmax with the median time of 4.95 h and declined in a biphasic manner post Tmax. By 36 h, BH4 concentrations were close to endogenous levels.

Coadministration of curcumin with sepiapterin increased sepiapterin Cmax and AUClast 1.14-fold and 1.36-fold, respectively, and increased metabolite BH4 Cmax 1.24-fold, AUClast 1.21-fold, and AUCinf 1.20-fold (Table 3). Coadministration with curcumin had a negligible impact on the absorption rate of sepiapterin, with a 0.03 h (90% CI −0.48, 0.56) change of Tmax by the Hodges–Lehmann method, and coadministration with curcumin slightly delayed the absorption of BH4, with 0.50 h (90% CI 0.06–0.56) as the Hodges–Lehmann estimate of the Tmax shift (Table 4). Tetrahydrobiopterin terminal T1/2 values were 4.60 and 4.39 h in Periods 1 and 2, respectively, with no apparent effect from coadministration of curcumin (Table 2). Coadministration with sepiapterin (60 mg/kg) did not significantly alter rosuvastatin exposures (Table 3). When coadministered with sepiapterin, the GMRs of rosuvastatin Cmax, AUClast, and AUCinf were 1.13, 1.03, and 1.01, respectively, compared to rosuvastatin alone. The absorption was slightly faster, with a Hodges–Lehmann estimated median Tmax change of −0.73 h when coadministered with sepiapterin (Table 4).

Of the total 26 subjects included for the PK analysis, 22 carried the BCRP c.421C/C gene and four carried the c.421C/A gene. Significantly higher BH4 and rosuvastatin exposures were observed in subjects with the c.421C/A gene (Tables 2 and 5). In subjects with the c.421C/A gene, compared with subjects with the c.421C/C gene, the BH4 mean Cmax, AUClast, and AUCinf were 1.36-fold, 1.39-fold, and 1.38-fold higher, respectively (Table 2), while rosuvastatin mean Cmax, AUClast, and AUCinf were 1.60-fold, 1.61-fold, and 1.52-fold higher, respectively (Table 5). In contrast, sepiapterin Cmax and AUClast values in subjects with c.421C/A were 0.76 and 0.75, respectively, of the Cmax and AUClast values in subjects with the c.421C/C gene (Table 2).

When coadministered with a BCRP inhibitor, greater increases in BCRP substrate exposure were observed in subjects with the c.421C/A gene. In subjects with the c.421C/A gene, the BH4 Cmax, AUClast, and AUCinf GMRs were 1.39-fold, 1.33-fold, and 1.33-fold, respectively, when sepiapterin was coadministered with curcumin as compared with sepiapterin administered alone; in contrast, these values were 1.22-fold, 1.18-fold, and 1.18-fold, respectively, with curcumin coadministration in subjects with the c.421C/C gene (Table 3). Similarly, sepiapterin Cmax and AUClast GMRs were 1.34-fold and 1.51-fold, respectively, in subjects with the c.421C/A gene compared with GMRs of 1.10-fold and 1.33-fold , respectively, in subjects with the c.421C/C gene. Greater increases in sepiapterin and BH4 exposure were observed in subjects carrying the c.421C/A gene. When rosuvastatin was coadministered with sepiapterin, no apparent difference was observed in rosuvastatin exposure GMRs in subjects with the c.421C/A gene (GMR for AUClast 1.127) versus subjects with the c.421C/C gene (GMR for AUClast 1.007) (Table 3).

Safety and Tolerability

Adverse events are summarized in Table 6. Overall, 16 unique TEAEs were reported by 12 subjects across the treatment periods. All TEAEs were transient and mild. Of these, only two were considered probably or possibly related to the study drug (rosuvastatin); namely, one subject experienced one event of mild constipation and another subject experienced one event of mild nausea. There were no clinically relevant changes in physical examinations, laboratory evaluations, vital signs, and 12-lead electrocardiogram measurements. No serious TEAEs or events leading to the discontinuation of study treatment were reported. Discussion

Studies have shown that sepiapterin and its metabolite BH4 are both weak in vitro inhibitors of BCRP (data on file). The projected gut concentrations (Igut) of sepiapterin and BH4 are expected to be over ten-fold of the IC50 values because of the high target therapeutic dose of sepiapterin (60 mg/kg) and high aqueous solubility. Furthermore, both sepiapterin and BH4 are also substrates of BCRP. Therefore, the potential BCRP-mediated DDIs of sepiapterin as both the perpetrator and the victim were investigated in healthy volunteers as described here.

Rosuvastatin is frequently utilized as a substrate for clinical investigation of gastrointestinal BCRP-mediated and OATP1B1-mediated DDIs [14, 28, 29]. Though CYP2C9 is the major enzyme for rosuvastatin metabolism in vitro, clinical evidence suggests that the role of CYP2C9 in humans is minimal [14, 16]. While it would be challenging to differentiate the effects of BCRP inhibition from other DDI effects if the perpetrator is simultaneously an inhibitor of OATP1B1, such as when using sulfasalazine as the clinical inhibitor [14], the results from in vitro studies indicate that this is not the case for sepiapterin. In vitro results demonstrated that neither sepiapterin nor BH4 is an inhibitor of OATP1B1. Our data from in vitro studies indicate that sepiapterin does not inhibit CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C8, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, or CYP3A4 in pooled human liver microsomes and does not induce CYP1A2, CYP2B6, or CYP3A4 activity in human hepatocytes (data on file).

Given that sepiapterin did not affect rosuvastatin exposures, results from this study clearly demonstrate that sepiapterin does not inhibit the intestinal BCRP efflux transporter. There is also no apparent difference observed in rosuvastatin exposure GMRs for subjects with c.421C/C and c.421C/A genes when coadministered with sepiapterin as compared to rosuvastatin alone. This specific observation further strengthens the conclusion that sepiapterin does not inhibit intestinal BCRP.

Following oral administration of sepiapterin, BH4 is the major metabolite in systemic circulation. Sepiapterin Cmax and AUClast are <1% of those of BH4. Hence, a change in BH4 exposure is considered the primary endpoint to assess the DDI. Coadministered curcumin increased BH4 only slightly (1.21-fold for AUClast). However, this modest change is deemed not clinically relevant and does not warrant a dose adjustment.

In this study, a dose of 20 mg/kg, lower than the phase III sepiapterin dose (60 mg/kg), was selected for a clinical DDI investigation of sepiapterin as the victim. This was mainly based on the fact that an approximately linear increase in BH4 exposure was observed in the dose range of 5–20 mg/kg, while a far less than dose-proportional increase in BH4 exposure was observed when the dose was above 20 mg/kg [1]. In a previous study, BH4 Cmax and AUClast increased 1.54-fold and 1.67-fold, respectively, with a three-fold increase in sepiapterin dose from 20 to 60 mg/kg administered with a high-fat diet [1, 7]. The highest sepiapterin doses that have been studied in healthy subjects prior to this study are a 80-mg/kg single dose under fasted conditions and repeated doses of 60 mg/kg with a high-fat diet. Therefore, sepiapterin 20 mg/kg with a high-fat diet was selected, with the expectation that it would be more sensitive to potential intestinal BCRP inhibition and safer in the case of any significant increase in BH4 exposure.

Polymorphisms in BCRP contribute to interindividual differences in exposure for orally administered BCRP substrates [11, 16]. Clinical studies have demonstrated higher peak plasma concentrations, lower clearance, and an increased risk for adverse events with rosuvastatin in individuals carrying BCRP alleles c.421C/C, c.421C/A, and c.421A/A, in that ascending order [16, 30–32]. When a BCRP substrate was coadministered with a BCRP inhibitor, greater increases in BCRP substrate exposure were observed [21]. Moreover, the allele frequency of the BCRP c.421C>A polymorphism varies among ethnic groups, with a significantly higher frequency in Asian subjects (35%) compared with Caucasian subjects (14%) [16, 28, 32, 33]. In this study, there were a few subjects (n = 4, 15%) identified with the BCRP polymorphism c.421C/A and the majority with c.421C/C (n = 22, 85%), which is consistent with the average population frequency of c.421C/A and c.421C/C reported by others [31, 34]. Despite the small sample size of carriers with the genotype c.421C/A, comparisons of this group to the group harboring genotype c.421C/C were consistent with reports in the literature, with elevated plasma concentrations for BH4 (1.39×) and rosuvastatin (1.61×). It is expected, based on observations in this study, that there would be higher plasma concentrations of BH4 in Asian subjects because of the higher frequency of the BCRP c.421C>A polymorphism, though the increase in BH4 exposure in Asian populations is expected to be no greater than 1.6-fold given the magnitude of the increase observed for rosuvastatin. The findings also suggest that, for a substrate of a polymorphic enzyme or transporter, genetic data should be collected in an ethnobridging or pharmacogenetic study to provide fundamental insights into ethnic variability.

Evaluating the genotype in DDI assessments could help to elucidate differences in exposure for substrates of a polymorphic enzyme or transporter, such as CYP2C19 and BCRP, and guide personalized dosage adjustments. However, the low frequency of BCRP c.421C>A variants could result in greatly increased study costs and a prolonged study duration owing to the greater number of subjects needing to be screened. Indeed, because of a lack of a sufficient number of subjects with the c.421C>A polymorphism in DDI studies, post hoc comparisons of DDI effects for different BCRP genotypes are rarely conducted [14]. It is generally acceptable and considered scientifically robust for patients carrying the c.421C/C genotype to be enrolled in BCRP-mediated DDI studies, as this exemplifies the most extreme scenario wherein a higher BCRP substrate plasma concentration is expected [14].

For sepiapterin and BH4, higher GMRs were observed in subjects with the c.421C/A genotype than in subjects with the c.421C/C genotype when sepiapterin was coadministered with curcumin compared with administered alone. However, there was no difference in the GMRs of rosuvastatin in subjects with c.421C/A and c.421C/C genotypes, which is likely owing to the fact that sepiapterin does not inhibit rosuvastatin absorption as found in this study. The higher GMRs of sepiapterin and BH4 in subjects with c.421C/A compared with those with c.421C/C is contradictory to the conceived “worst-case scenario for c.421C/C carriers” [14]. Greater increases in rosuvastatin plasma concentrations were observed in Asian subjects (11 c.421C/C, 9 c.421C/A, and 1 c.421A/A) compared with non-Asian subjects (13 c.421C/C, 5 c.421C/A, and 0 c.421A/A) when rosuvastatin was coadministered with eltrombopag, which implied greater DDI effects in subjects with the c.421C>A polymorphism, similar to the results seen in this study [21]. However, the findings from eltrombopag could not rule out a potential confounding effect from the inhibition of the hepatic uptake transporter OATP1B1. These findings strengthen the necessity of monitoring genotypes for substrates of a polymorphic enzyme or transporter. Such genetic data should be collected during phase II and phase III clinical studies to facilitate a post hoc population PK analysis for a pharmacogenetic comparison.

Conclusions

The results demonstrate that oral coadministration of BCRP inhibitor curcumin with sepiapterin slightly increases plasma exposure of sepiapterin metabolite BH4. However, the magnitude of the 1.24-fold change is not considered clinically relevant. At the highest dose (60 mg/kg), sepiapterin does not impact the pharmacokinetics of the BCRP substrate rosuvastatin. The difference in exposures for BH4 and rosuvastatin, as well as the difference in magnitude of inhibition of sepiapterin absorption by curcumin, in subjects with different BCRP c.421 genotypes strengthen the necessity and importance of collecting genetic data during clinical studies involving a substrate for a polymorphic enzyme or transporter. The greater magnitude of increase in BH4 exposures in subjects with the c.421C/A genotype as compared with subjects with the c.421C/C genotype is unexpected and contradictory to the concept of a “worst-case scenario” of DDIs for subjects with c.421C/C.
