Evaluation of Social Media Utilization Among Members of the Musculoskeletal Tumor Society: Implications in Practice
Authors: Mark P. Youssef, Andrew F. Ibrahim, Alexandria Giese, Jake A. Fiore, Paul G. Guirguis, Mina Botros, Susan McDowell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Social media plays an increasing role in healthcare, influencing patient education, provider selection, and physician marketing. Previous studies show mixed results on its impact on physician review scores, and limited data exist within orthopedic oncology. The purpose of this study was to quantify social media utilization among Musculoskeletal Tumor Society (MSTS) members as well as identify any differences by physician sex or practice type.Methods:All active MSTS members were searched for common social media accounts. Account activity and engagement data were acquired. Publicly available ratings from popular physician review websites were compiled. Data were collected on each member's demographic details including sex, geographical location, type of practice, and h-index. The association between social media activity and online review scores was analyzed using chi-square tests for categorical data and t-tests for continuous variables.Results:A total of 247 MSTS members, comprising 199 academic and 48 private surgeons, revealed significant differences in orthopedic surgeon social media engagement and online presence. At least 1 social media account was used by 178 (72.1%) of MSTS members with LinkedIn most used (57.9%) followed by ResearchGate (39.7%). Surgeons with 3 or more active social media accounts demonstrated significantly higher ratings on practice-affiliated scoring sites compared with those without any social media presence (4.88 ± 0.10 vs. 4.82 ± 0.13, p = 0.017), and a significant difference was observed in practice-affiliated scoring, with higher h-index surgeons receiving better ratings (4.84 ± 0.10 vs. 4.79 ± 0.11, p = 0.009).Conclusions:Most MSTS members do not have professional social media accounts geared toward the patient population (i.e. Instagram, TikTok). Although social media demonstrates benefits of patient engagement, education, and recruitment, there was only significant difference in ratings on practice-affiliated scoring sites with over 3 social media accounts compared with surgeons without any accounts.Level of Evidence:Level IV. Introduction

The use of social media has continued to grow1, including within health care, increasing patient autonomy when selecting providers. In addition, physician review websites (PRWs) are another important tool for patients, with studies showing that PRW ratings can influence a patient’s selection of a provider2. For providers, social media offers a platform to disseminate accurate medical information and present professional qualifications for patient outreach and marketing3-5.

Within orthopedic surgery, Earp et al. reported that greater than 90% of surgeons have at least 1 online profile, but many do not actively use professional social media5,6. Studies have demonstrated this trend across many orthopedic subspecialties7-18, with some suggesting that professional social media use may correlate with higher PRW ratings9,12,13,17,19. However, the current literature has mixed results with other studies reporting that social media use does not significantly affect the PRW scores10,16,20-22.

There is limited research on the impact of social media within the subspecialty of orthopedic oncology. One study by Gusho et al. reports that social media plays a larger role for younger patients when selecting an orthopedic oncologist23.

The purpose of this study was to quantify social media utilization among musculoskeletal tumor society (MSTS) members as well as identify differences according to physician sex or practice type. In addition, we examined the relationship between different MSTS member characteristics and their impact on online review scores.

Methods

This cross-sectional study assessed social media utilization among MSTS members and its influence on online physician review scores. We included all surgeons listed as active members in the MSTS directory as of February 2025 (n = 247). Associate, Affiliate, Candidate, and Resident members were excluded to focus on established orthopedic oncologists with independent clinical practices. Members without MD or DO credentials were also excluded. This study used publicly accessible data, exempting it from institutional review board approval.

Data Collection

Internet searches were conducted through Google.com by entering each surgeon's full name and medical degree. Searches were limited to the top 20 results to capture relevant professional information, including practice type (academic or private) and associated websites. Websites were categorized as group (listing multiple clinical partners), hospital (affiliated with a hospital), or personal (dedicated to a single physician). Surgeons with both academic and private profiles were classified as private because the presence of a private practice website suggests active participation in a private clinical setting despite an academic affiliation. In addition, private practice websites frequently appeared higher in search results and often contained more detailed physician biographies, reflecting how patients are likely to encounter physicians’ online. Demographic data collected included sex, geographical location, practice type, and h-index (obtained from Google Scholar). US states were grouped into regions according to the US Census Bureau classifications: West, Midwest, South, and Northeast. An h-index cutoff of 5 was used to define a threshold of scientific impact and divide the MSTS surgeons into 2 cohorts. This cutoff was chosen based on findings of a median h-index of 5 among orthopedic academic faculty24.

Social Media and Review/Rating Analysis

The social media platforms analyzed included Facebook, X, Instagram, LinkedIn, ResearchGate, YouTube, and TikTok. Profiles were included if publicly viewable, professionally oriented toward orthopedic oncology, and active within the last 6 months. Metrics such as followers, subscribers, and reads were recorded when available. Publicly available ratings were collected from Healthgrades.com, Vitals.com, and hospital-affiliated physician pages when available. The study focused on the number of reviews and overall rating scores to assess the impact of social media engagement on patient feedback.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Means and standard deviations were calculated for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Associations between social media activity and online review scores were analyzed using chi-square tests for categorical data and t tests for continuous variables. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

A comparative analysis of 247 MSTS members, comprising 199 academic and 48 private surgeons, revealed significant differences in social media engagement and online presence.

Demographics and Professional Characteristics

Among MSTS members, 81% were male surgeons and 19% female surgeons, with no significant differences between academic and private practice settings (p = 0.382; Table I). Most MSTS surgeons were affiliated with hospital systems (89.9%), followed by group settings (7.7%), and personal practice (2.4%). Practice setting varied significantly between academic and private surgeons, with most academic surgeons practicing in a hospital setting compared with private surgeons (99.5% vs. 50.0%, p < 0.001). Most surgeons practiced in the United States (91.9%), with no significant geographic variations. The mean h-index among MSTS surgeons was 18.3, with academic surgeons possessing a significantly higher value compared with their private counterparts (19.8 vs. 10.9, p = 0.002)​.

Social Media Presence and Engagement

Social media presence varied considerably across platforms (Table II). LinkedIn was the most used platform (57.9%), with no significant differences between academic and private surgeons (58.3% vs. 56.3%, p = 0.797). ResearchGate was next most prevalent (39.7%), followed by X (11.7%), Facebook (9.7%), and Instagram (7.7%). TikTok and YouTube were least used. TikTok and YouTube accounts were only observed among private surgeons (4.2%, p = 0.037 and 6.3%, p = 0.007, respectively). No significant differences in social media engagement (followers, reads, or subscribers) between academic and private surgeons were found for any social media platform.

Physician Reviews and Ratings

MSTS members were most likely to have at least 1 review submitted on Healthgrades.com (79.4%), followed by Vitals.com (58.7%), and practice-affiliated scoring (52.6%). The number of reviews present on a practice-affiliated website was significantly higher than the number of reviews on both Healthgrades.com and Vitals.com (278.3 vs. 25.2 and 21.2, respectively, p < 0.001), by more than ten-fold on average. Patient-reported physician ratings (on a scale of 0-5) were also significantly higher on practice-affiliated websites than both Healthgrades.com and Vitals.com (4.8 vs. 4.3 and 4.1, respectively, p < 0.001; Table III). Private surgeons had a higher number of reviews on Vitals.com compared with academic surgeons (32.1 ± 28.4 vs. 18.7 ± 19.7, p = 0.004) and were less likely to have reviews on Healthgrades.com (68.8% vs. 81.9%, p = 0.043), although ratings were otherwise similar across platforms.

Discussion

This study found that there is a stark contrast between the social media platforms used by MSTS members and the social media platforms used by the public and more specifically by patients. The most used social media sites among MSTS members were LinkedIn (57.9%), followed by ResearchGate (39.7%), X (11.7%), Facebook (9.7%), Instagram (7.7%), YouTube (1.2%), and TikTok (0.8%). These figures are especially notable when compared with the average usage rate of the most popular platforms used by US adults today including 85% for YouTube, 70% for Facebook, 50% for Instagram, and 33% for TikTok1. Similar to this study’s findings, Richman et al. analyzed 1,465 trauma surgeons of which only 0.7%, 2.4%, 2.2%, and 5.9% used YouTube, Facebook, Instagram, and X, respectively, with LinkedIn representing the highest usage at 48.7%18. A lack of social media activity within the MSTS membership community and a demonstrated preference toward LinkedIn and ResearchGate may be revealing of greater engagement online when networking among colleagues and a lower comfort level toward developing an online patient-forward persona.

This study’s findings did suggest a possible association between greater use of social media (≥3 accounts) and increased ratings on practice-affiliated scoring sites; however, the absolute difference in rating was small, suggesting that the impact on patient perception may be limited. MSTS members primarily used social media platforms not accessed by the public, most likely resulting in the absence of patient engagement and education. One important consideration is that when surgeons have 3 or more accounts, at least 1 platform is geared toward patients rather than academics (e.g., X, Facebook, or Instagram rather than LinkedIn or ResearchGate). Thus, when the 3 account threshold is reached, 1 account is most likely geared toward patients such as X, Facebook, or Instagram which the patient population uses more frequently.

The most significant finding in this study was that surgeons with higher h-indexes received better ratings on practice-affiliated scoring. This association can be explained by more evidence-based practices being implemented, more expertise in a practice, and/or more patient confidence in a provider well-established in their field. While the increase in physician rating is statistically significant, the absolute difference may not result in a clinically meaningful difference in how patients assess surgeon quality. Online physician ratings have been found to skew toward the upper end of a 5-star rating scale, which can make small differences statistically significant in large data sets25,26. Future studies are needed to determine whether small differences in surgeon rating impact patient decision making.

Orthopedic oncology may also have different social media correlations to patient ratings in comparison with other orthopedic subspecialties because of the nature of the field. Orthopedic oncology has fewer elective procedures and deals more often with life and limb threatening conditions. This creates a unique experience for cancer patients when deciding who they want to perform their care. According to Ejaz et al., the most important factors for cancer patients when selecting their surgeon were surgeon case-specific experience, surgeon specialized training, and the case-specific volumes for the hospital27. As this information can be found on hospital websites, this suggests less need for a social media presence for oncologic surgeons.

This study did have limitations. One of these limitations was not being able to find social media accounts that are under handles that are not the surgeon’s name. In addition, several ratings on Healthgrades.com and Vitals.com contained a small sample size that could be skewed by very low ratings from a few patients. Practice affiliated scoring may also vary depending on the institution, making it a difficult metric to compare across multiple practices. Future research efforts may need to collect ratings of physicians directly from patients to standardize the process. In addition, physician ratings shown on hospital-affiliated platforms may be moderated to highlight positive reviews while removing negative reviews. This may introduce a selection bias into these ratings, possibly limiting comparisons made to third-party physician rating pages. Furthermore, physicians who practice for longer are more likely to have a higher H-index, more online reviews, and a more robust digital presence. Moreover, as physicians practice for longer, they are often perceived to be more knowledgeable and confident in their decision making, which may represent an important confounding variable that can influence online rating. Future studies should focus on controlling for number of years in practice to more accurately ascertain the effect this may have on physician scoring. In addition, this study did not evaluate whether certain social media metrics, such as follower count, impact physician ratings, which may represent another important factor to understanding how social media impacts physician scores. Our study cohort also contained a small proportion of MSTS members that practiced outside the United States. Differences in healthcare systems and their impact on the need for physician self-promotion may influence social media presence and physician scores. While the relatively small number of surgeons within our study cohort limited our ability to evaluate these differences, future efforts should be made to assess the impact healthcare systems may have on both social media utilization and surgeon scores.

Conclusions

The findings demonstrate that most orthopedic oncologists do not maintain social media accounts, particularly on the most widely used platforms. While social media platforms serve as a method to share scholarly work and provide patient education, there was no significant correlation between active social media accounts and physician ratings, except when comparing 3 or more active accounts and zero active social media accounts. However, this study did find that h-index was positively correlated with practice-affiliated scoring. Given the growing role of social media in healthcare communication, there may be an opportunity for orthopedic oncologists to use these platforms to educate patients and direct them to appropriate educational content about their disease, ultimately enhancing patient understanding and engagement.
