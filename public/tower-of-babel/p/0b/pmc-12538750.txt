Determinants of hepatitis C virus treatment completion among Los Angeles County residents
Authors: Cassidy Hernandez-Tamayo, Chrysovalantis Stafylis, Lokesh Bhardwaj, Riya K Shah, Tatiana Becerra, Dara Bruce, Roopkamal Saini, Natalie Saremi, Immanuel Thomas, Krzel Manansala-Tan, Arjun P Vij, Alison Li, Nathan Sudeep, Jacob Gizamba, Bijan Hosseini, Sabrina Navarro, Sofia Ufret-Rivera, Albert J Farias, Jeffrey D Klausner

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatitis C viral infection is curable, yet only about one-third of identified cases have been cleared nationally. We evaluated determinants of treatment completion in a sample of newly reported hepatitis C cases among Los Angeles County residents.MethodsUsing information from the Los Angeles County Hepatitis C Case Registry, we contacted reported hepatitis C ribonucleic acid (RNA) positive cases diagnosed from January 2021 through April 2022. We used Pearson’s Chi-Square Tests and multivariable logistic regression to identify demographic and clinical characteristics associated with self-reported hepatitis C virus treatment completion.ResultsAmong 2,992 reported RNA-positive cases, 619 (21%) were successfully contacted. Among those, most respondents were untreated (72%), male (65%), and tested by primary care providers (50%). Respondents who reported symptoms in the past six months had 0.2 (95% CI: 0.1, 0.4) times the odds of completing treatment compared to respondents with no symptoms. Characteristics of respondents were the same as those diagnosed with hepatitis C in Los Angeles County.ConclusionsWe verified low hepatitis C treatment completion at the population-level. Background

Hepatitis C viral infection is readily diagnosed and curable, yet the United States is not on track to reach the World Health Organization’s 2030 national Hepatitis C elimination goal which aims to reduce new infections by 90% and related deaths by 65% from 2016 to 2030 [1]. Based on current estimates, the United States is not expected to meet these goals until 2037. In 2020, California was one of three states with the highest number of deaths associated with hepatitis C [2]. It also has the highest annual number of individuals who need to be treated to meet the 2030 elimination goal, yet it is not expected to reach this benchmark until sometime before 2040 [3]. Safe and effective direct-acting antiviral medications are curative and decrease hepatitis C-associated morbidity and mortality [4]. While everyone who is infected should be offered treatment, in a study of 1.7 million individuals identified as infected during 2013 to 2022, only 357,000 (34%) cleared their infection [5]. Our prior work has shown that during the severe respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 pandemic there was a 37% decrease in total prescriptions for hepatitis C treatment further hindering progress toward elimination [6]. Commonly reported personal barriers to completing hepatitis C treatment include fear of side effects, stigma, limited access to healthcare, and having other urgent health issues [7]. At the system level, obstacles such as provider reluctance to initiate treatment, coexisting medical conditions, potential drug interactions, and concerns about patient adherence have been documented [8]. Insurance-related hurdles, particularly prior authorization requirements, have significantly delayed or hindered the initiation and completion of treatment [9, 10]. California presents additional challenges with retreatment restrictions that hinge on criteria such as medication adherence, ongoing substance use, or documentation of sustained virologic response [11]. These policies effectively limit patients to a single opportunity for treatment regardless of circumstances [11].

Los Angeles County has similar trends. It is estimated that among the 32,980 residents who tested hepatitis C virus ribonucleic acid- (RNA) positive between 2017 and 2021, 42% were reported as having cleared their infection either spontaneously or by treatment [12]. More effort in increasing treatment is needed for Los Angeles County to achieve hepatitis C elimination goals [13]. To understand the factors associated with treatment completion at the population-level, we undertook an evaluation of treatment determinants upon initial outreach among recently reported hepatitis C virus RNA positive cases in Los Angeles County.

Methods

We conducted a secondary data analysis of data collected in an ongoing linkage-to-cure project, Project Hepatitis C Virus Connect, a public-academic partnership between the Los Angeles County Department of Public Health and University of Southern California [14]. The data were limited to cases reported to the Los Angeles County Hepatitis C Case Registry with Hepatitis C viral RNA infection between January 2021 and April 2022, involving non-incarcerated and housed Los Angeles County residents [14]. This time period was selected to ensure that adequate programmatic data were available [14]. We also restricted data to those cases reported infected at least twelve months prior to the contact attempt date. Project Connect [14] caseworkers reached out to cases, notified them about their result, offered education and completed a brief quality improvement survey consisting of 12 to 30 questions to evaluate infection awareness, treatment completion, and relevant demographic and clinical characteristics during the initial patient contact (Appendix A). Data collection was conducted via telephone, with each interview averaging 15 min. To optimize patient outreach, case workers attempted up to three phone calls before categorizing individuals as unreachable and moving on to the next contact [14]. These outreach efforts took place between April 2023 and January 202414. Data were entered into Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) version 13.9.1.

Hepatitis C virus treatment completion status was the primary outcome of interest, evaluated with the question “Did you complete treatment for Hepatitis C?” (Yes/No). The independent variables of interest were age group (18–29, 30–44, 45–64, ≥ 65 years), sex assigned at birth (male, female), race/ethnicity (Hispanic, Black, Asian, White, Other or Unknown), health insurance type (private, Medicare, Medicaid/MediCal, other public-sponsored, and none/unknown), provider type (primary, specialty, other or unknown), and symptom status (potential symptoms of chronic liver disease in the past six months).

Respondents were grouped into age groups according to their age in years at specimen collection date. Using the testing provider name, we searched public records to identify their medical specialty. “Primary care” physicians were internal medicine, family medicine, and osteopathy physicians. “Specialty care” physicians included infectious disease, gastroenterology, and hepatology specialists; all other specialists and non-primary care physicians were categorized as “Other”. Respondents who did not have ordering physician names in their record were classified as “unknown provider”. We inquired about the following potential symptoms of chronic liver disease in the past 6 months: yellowing of the skin or eyes, swelling of the stomach, spider-like spots on the skin, easy bruising on the skin, prolonged bleeding when brushing teeth, throwing up blood, passing blood in stool, swelling of legs, redness in palms of hands, and itchy skin.

We calculated counts with percentages of demographic and clinical characteristics. We explored differences in the treatment completion status of respondents among the same categorical demographic and clinical characteristics using Pearson’s Chi-Square Tests. For our multivariable logistic regression model, variables were included if they were statistically significantly related to treatment completion status in bivariate analyses, had a biological basis for confounding, or were important risk factors of hepatitis C according to a priori hypotheses [15]. The final model assessed independent determinants of demographic and clinical factors (age group, race/ethnicity, health insurance type, provider type, and symptom status) and treatment status with mutually adjusted prevalence odds ratio associations and 95% confidence intervals. We performed sensitivity analyses by including respondents currently in treatment into the treated instead of the untreated category.

We assessed the representativeness of the sample of respondents by comparing sample demographic characteristics to the demographic characteristics of the total population of all the hepatitis C virus RNA-positive cases reported to Los Angeles County between January 2021 and April 2022 using Pearson’s Chi-Square Tests. For all analyses, a two-sided p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed in SAS Software Version 9.4 (Cary, NC, USA).

Results

The case management team reached out to 2,992 residents with a reported case of hepatitis C infection. We were unable to contact 2,081 (70%) residents and 272 (9%) declined interviews resulting in 639 (21%) persons successfully surveyed (Fig. 1). Data from twenty respondents who had cleared infection on their own (n = 19) or had a liver transplantation (n = 1) were excluded from further analyses. Among 619 respondents, 517 (84%) were aware of their hepatitis C virus infection and 446 (72%) of the total were untreated for hepatitis C virus or did not complete hepatitis C virus treatment.

Discussion

We analyzed determinants of treatment completion status among hepatitis C virus RNA-positive Los Angeles County residents reported to the Los Angeles County reportable disease Hepatitis C Case Registry between January 2021 and April 2022. Contacted persons exhibited similar race/ethnicity, sex, and age distributions as all residents with a hepatitis C virus RNA-positive reported case in that period, suggesting our results were representative of the population of those diagnosed with hepatitis C in that same period. Overall, the percentage of hepatitis C treatment completion was low. Respondents tested by specialists, those 30–44 years of age, and with no current chronic liver disease symptoms had higher frequencies of recent treatment completion compared to those tested by primary care physicians, those 18–29 years of age, and currently symptomatic.

There are limited data on population-level characteristics of those who complete and do not complete hepatitis C treatment. Our results are similar to the 2016 to 2018 California Department of Public Health report that analyzed hepatitis C virus testing and linkage to care demonstration projects and found similarly low treatment completion proportions [16]. Of the 2,064 people who tested positive for hepatitis C virus RNA, only 657 (32%) were known to have completed treatment [16]. Our research in the same population showed potential reasons for low treatment completion. In a survey of 245 individuals with untreated HCV infection [17]participants did not perceive treatment as a priority, or prioritized other heath priorities, and reported frequently obstacles related to insurance or the medical provider not recommending treatment.

We also found that treatment completion was lower in those individuals aged 18–29 years compared to other age groups. That association is similar to another study which found that among United States Medicaid enrollees, there was a 0.65 significantly lower odds of hepatitis C virus treatment initiation among those 18–29 years old compared to those older [18]. The recent rise in the incidence of hepatitis C virus infection in individuals younger than 40 years old who inject drugs and the documented lower treatment rates present an ongoing challenge for the effective control of hepatitis C virus transmission [2].

Treatment completion was similar across race/ethnicities. Our results differ from other studies that show minority status and lower treatment completion. A study evaluating patients with chronic hepatitis C infection who received treatment between 2002 and 2018 showed lower percentages of treatment completion in Blacks compared to Non-Hispanic Whites [19]. Among adults with chronic hepatitis C at four healthcare systems in California, Louisiana, Texas, and Virginia, there were significantly lower odds of treatment in Hispanic compared to non-Hispanic Whites [20].

While we found in the bivariate analysis that insurance type was associated with treatment completion, in the multivariate analysis, private insurance was not an independent determinant of treatment completion. Other studies using administrative data claims and encounters of insured adults aged 18–69 years infected with hepatitis C found Medicaid/Medi-Cal patients were less likely to receive treatment compared to those with private insurance [21].

Respondents tested by specialist physicians were more likely to have completed treatment compared to those tested by primary care physicians. This likely reflects the fact that patients tested by specialists were more likely to be treated because they were referred by primary care physicians to get treated. Specialist physicians typically order pretreatment hepatitis C RNA tests to confirm infection prior to starting treatment. The lower frequency of treatment completion among those tested by primary care physicians suggest that if primary care physicians had more capacity to treat patients with hepatitis C care, treatment completion might increase. California state law requires patients receiving primary care services in health facilities to be offered hepatitis B and hepatitis C screening tests and if the test result is positive, primary care providers must ensure treatment [22]. With these recent updates to hepatitis C treatment guidelines, there is greater potential for primary care providers to manage more cases. Increasing awareness among these providers about relevant state laws is essential. Streamlining the guidelines further could enable even more individuals to receive treatment by equipping primary care providers with the tools and confidence to manage hepatitis C effectively.

The survey population was restricted to those with adequate contact information and diagnosed hepatitis C infection reported to Los Angeles County. Furthermore, survey responses were self-reported, and participants may have provided inaccurate information, although the impact of self-report on the accuracy of treatment completion would be expected to be minimal given that typical treatment duration is between eight and twelve weeks of therapy. Potential chronic liver disease symptoms were also assessed by self-report which may introduce inaccuracy. While only 21% of those contacted participated in the survey, respondents were similar in characteristics to the total population. Unmeasured bias still may occur. Finally, we were unable to establish causal inference and temporal relationships due to the cross-sectional nature of the analysis. Some unmeasured confounding variables that could have biased the observed associations include injection drug use, alcohol use disorder, socioeconomic status, and comorbidities.

Conclusions

We found low hepatitis C treatment completion rates at the population level, underscoring the need to expand both medical and public health efforts to promote timely treatment. Key demographic and clinical factors associated with treatment completion were identified, with individuals aged 18–29 years and those diagnosed in primary care settings being least likely to complete treatment. These findings highlight opportunities for targeted interventions to improve care and treatment uptake among these groups. The data can inform policy initiatives aimed at optimizing linkage to and completion of hepatitis C treatment, as well as supporting advocacy for additional resources to achieve the 2030 elimination goal. Public health departments should directly use reported case-registry data or create public-private partnerships to ensure those reported with hepatitis C infection are timeously treated.
