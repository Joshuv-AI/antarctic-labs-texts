Clinical characteristics and risk factors for poor prognosis in children with complicated acute hematogenous osteomyelitis treated with vaccum sealing drainage
Authors: Jiale Guo, Wei Feng, Baojian Song, Danjiang Zhu, Yuwei Wen, Qiang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to analyze the clinical characteristics of children with complicated acute hematogenous osteomyelitis treated with vacuum sealing drainage (VSD) in our institution, and to explore the risk factors for poor prognosis.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on the medical records of patients diagnosed with acute hematogenous Osteomyelitis from December 2015 to December 2022. The demographic profiles, treatment procedures, examination results, and follow-up information of the patients were meticulously recorded. Patients were categorized into two groups based on the occurrence of treatment failure, deformity, limb length discrepancy, growth arrest, osteonecrosis, chronic osteomyelitis, pathological fracture, chondrolysis, and recurrence: good prognosis group and poor prognosis group. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent risk factors associated with adverse prognosis. The performance of the regression model was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve.ResultsA total of 150 patients who met the inclusion criteria were followed up for an average of 3.4 years (range: 1.0–7.0 years). There were 31 patients (21%) in the poor prognosis group. In all cases, after 7 days of continuous saline irrigation and 2 surgical debridement, 75% of the infections were completely controlled, and the local abscess disappeared in 86% of the patients. By regression analysis, leukocyte admission (adjusted odds ratio (aOR) 1.2; 95% confidence interval (CI) [1.0–1.4]; P = 0.04), first postoperative C-reactive protein level (aOR 1.04; 95% (CI), [1.0–1.07], P = 0.034), time to diagnosis of osteomyelitis (aOR 1.3; 95% (CI), [1.1–1.6], P = 0.012), and days of fever with antibiotics (aOR 1.5; 95% (CI), [1.1–2.2], P = 0.024) were independent risk factors for poor outcomes. The four variables combined to construct an efficient prediction model, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.85.ConclusionThe innovative application of vacuum sealing drainage technology in the management of complicated patients with substantially localized abscesses achieved significant efficacy, while one-third of children still had unfavorable outcomes. High-risk groups with recurrence and sequelae necessitate regular follow-up and even long-term orthopedic intervention.Keywords: Acute hematogenous osteomyelitis, Risk factors, Prognosis, Children

Background

Acute hematogenous osteomyelitis (AHO) varies widely in clinical severity and could lead to severe complications and permanent disability in complicated cases if not treated promptly and effectively, with incidence rates ranging from 1 to 13 per 100,000 [1, 2]. The metaphysis of the long bone is the most vulnerable location. Recently, due to the rise of drug-resistant bacteria, musculoskeletal infections have become more complicated and the prognosis worsened [3, 4]. Therefore, the diagnosis and treatment of AHO must be individualized and multidisciplinary cooperation [5]. Studies have shown that 90% of patients obtained full remission using antibiotics just, while 10% had serious long-term sequelae such as avascular necrosis, chronic osteomyelitis and pathologic fractures [1, 6]. Thus, during the last twenty years, most practitioners have conducted comprehensive investigations to forecast negative consequences for individuals with AHO [7–11]. Alhinai et al. and McNeil et al. [7, 10] developed new models to predict groups with a heightened risk of developing acute and chronic complications. Copley et al. [12] developed a severity of illness score using early clinical signs and laboratory indicators to categorize patients’ prognosis risk and utilized it as a tool for treatment decision-making. Vorhies et al. [13], on the other side, assessed the risk of severe sequelae according to the severity of the disease during hospitalization. However, their studies were mostly based on populations who are treated conservatively. Surgery is indicated for individuals with rapidly progressive disease and a large number of local abscesses, especially involving drug-resistant bacterial infections caused by CA-MRSA, septic arthritis, disseminated sepsis, and multifocal abscesses [1, 14–16]. Although relevant guidelines and expert consents have been published in the United States, China, and Europe, the details and indications of surgical intervention [4, 17, 18] have not been widely elucidated and agreed upon due to the lack of reliable evidence-based medical research. The main objective of this study is to analyze the clinical features of patients with complicated AHO treated with vacuum sealing drainage (VSD) in our hospital, to identify the risk factors linked to unfavorable results, and to offer guidance for clinical surgical interventions.

The VSD device (VSD Medical Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) was initially developed for managing soft tissue wound infections. In 2006, we pioneered its adaptation for surgical treatment of pediatric AHO and septic arthritis [19]. Compared to conventional approaches, the VSD cohort demonstrated superior outcomes: shorter febrile duration (11 vs. 15 days, P = 0.003), fewer dressing changes (1 vs. 4 times, P < 0.001), reduced hospitalization (26 vs. 29 days, P = 0.047), and fewer surgical interventions (1 vs. 2 times, P = 0.003) [19]. Following these findings, we subsequently expanded its application to a broader AHO population and standardized protocols that have now been adopted across many Chinese medical institutions [20, 21].

Materials and methods

Study population and data collection

After approval by the ethics committee, all patients with a discharging diagnosis of acute Osteomyelitis in our hospital from December 2015 to December 2022 were consecutively enrolled. The diagnosis of osteomyelitis was confirmed by clinical criteria (localized tenderness, redness, swelling, or reduced mobility) together with one or more of the following: positive blood culture or pus culture; the imaging manifestations indicated local spread, joint effusion, bone invasion, and deep abscess; histopathology demonstrating acute inflammation. Data collected from the hospital’s electronic medical record system included pre- and post-admission antibiotic therapy details, chief complaints, physical examination, weight-bearing status, vital signs, C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), procalcitonin (PCT), white blood cell count, platelet count, coagulation function index, recovery time of inflammatory indicators, bacterial culture results, pathological reports, imaging findings, surgical times and approaches, length of hospital stay, complications, intensive care unit days, and follow-up data. The data was anonymized before the examination.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: 1) age range from 1 month to 18 years old; 2) duration of disease ≤ 2 weeks; 3) complicated course of disease, as follows: slow, prolonged response, or lack of clinical response after appropriate antibiotic therapy ≥ 48 h; radiologically confirmed abscess progression [17]; 4) follow-up time ≥ 1 year.

Exclusion criteria: 1) a history of penetrating trauma or prosthesis implantation within the past 30 days; 2) underlying systemic diseases such as immunodeficiency, malignant tumors, or diabetes; 3) severe acute infectious diseases like varicella; 4) noninfectious inflammatory responses such as rheumatic fever, eczema, or juvenile idiopathic arthritis.

AHO treatment

The indications for surgical intervention in our hospital include the following criteria: ①Antibiotics therapy for more than 96 h proved ineffective; ② Intermuscular or joint cavity abscess >2cm3, subperiosteal abscess >2 mm; ③ Shiny sequestrum. The imaging data was conveyed through reports prepared by a senior radiologist. The chief orthopedic surgeon reevaluated the patient’s imaging and laboratory results prior to surgery to decide if surgical procedures were necessary. For patients with substantial bone deterioration, it is recommended to implant vancomycin-mixed artificial bone particles during surgery. Our empirical antimicrobial selection adheres to international guidelines and local microbial epidemiology. Given the 30% MRSA culture positivity rate in Beijing pediatric populations, where MRSA universally exhibits resistance to lincomycin, our institutional protocol recommends vancomycin combined with third-generation cephalosporins (e.g., ceftriaxone) for critically ill children presenting with high fever (≥ 38.5 °C), altered mental status, and severe localized swelling/pain. Conversely, for subacute presentations with insidious onset and mild local symptoms, we employ second-generation cephalosporins (e.g., cefuroxime, cefazolin) as first-line therapy, pending culture confirmation [17, 22]. Following discharge, the children received oral antibiotics (cefazolin or linezolid) until their erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) returned to normal and underwent regular follow-up evaluations at our hospital’s outpatient department or local medical institutions. Follow-up information was obtained from outpatient records, through telephone, or internet platforms.

Definition of terms

Recurrence was defined as readmission within 3 months after resolution of the initial infection, characterized by new-onset osteoarticular symptoms accompanied by elevated inflammatory markers, and either positive diagnostic biopsy or culture-confirmed recurrent infection. The time to diagnosis of AHO was determined as the period between the appearance of symptoms and the detection of positive blood cultures or the emergence of distinctive imaging alterations. The presentation of chronic osteomyelitis, treatment failure, growth arrest, and limb length difference aligns with the findings of Alhinai et al. [10]. Poor prognosis (PP) was defined as such clinical events as treatment failure, deformity, limb length discrepancy, growth arrest, osteonecrosis, chronic osteomyelitis, pathological fracture, chondrolysis, and recurrence.

Surgical approach

①Patients were placed in a supine position with general anesthesia. ② The incision was located based on the MRI findings (plentiful subperiosteal pus). Soft tissue and periosteum were dissected to expose the infected area and then aspirate pus with a 5-ml syringe for bacterial identification and antibiotic susceptibility testing. The polluted area was irrigated using a water jet, followed with the cortical bone perforated with an electric drill and a “fenestration” of 0.5*1.0 cm created, obtaining the pus from the bone marrow cavity preserved a sample again. The marrow cavity was washed out with pressurized saline until clear fluid drained out. ④The VSD foam material was precisely cut by the size and shape of the cortical window and carefully inserted into the cavity. ⑤Meticulously suture the periosteum, muscle, and subcutaneous soft tissue layer by layer and securely fix foam to both skin. ⑥Application of biological semipermeable membranes to close half sponge outside the skin and surrounding area. If septic arthritis was combined, the procedures would be repeated with a single VSD inserted into the joint cavity similarly. 2000 ml saline irrigating and drainage every day postoperation with a controlled pressure ranging from 26.6 to 39.9Kpa for one week was performed, and then the surgical incision was reopened to detect whether there was still purulent accumulation surrounding the foam to determine whether to replace or remove the VSD (Fig. 1).

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous data were expressed by or median and interquartile range (IQR) according to the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test. Categorical variables were described by frequencies with proportions (%). Univariate analysis and multivariate logistic regression were used to identify related independent risk factors associated with poor prognosis, and calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. The model’s fitting effect and goodness of fit were assessed and validated through the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and Hosmer-Lemeshow test.

Results

Demographics

A total of 278 children with a diagnosis of acute hematogenous Osteomyelitis were included in the study. Following the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 150 cases were included in the analysis, of whom 15 patients received treatment with vancomycin calcium sulfate/calcium phosphate mixed artificial bone powder (Fig. 2). There were 82 (54.7%) males and 68 (45.3%) females, with an average age of 7.5 years (IQR, 2.6–10.4). Arthralgia pain and fever occurred in 144 (96%) patients before admission. Deep local tenderness was the only symptom in 6 (4%) patients. MRI was performed in 129 (86%) patients, including 35 patients completed in other hospitals. Twenty patients underwent lesion biopsies to confirm acute infection. Before the diagnosis of AHO, ultrasonography was conducted in 124 (82.7%) patients, with cloudy effusion detected in the soft tissues, subperiosteal space, and joint cavities in 100% of cases. Among 100 patients who underwent local lesion radiography within 14 days of symptom onset, osseous destruction was identified in 18 (18%) cases.

Patient flow chart

Microorganism

Bacterial cultures were obtained from 147 (98%) patients. Isolates grew from both pus and blood in 45 (30.6%) cases, pus only in 57 (38.8%), and blood only in 18 (12.2%). The positive rate of bacterial culture was 81.6% (120/147), among which methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus accounted for 62.5% (75/120), methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus accounted for 32.5% (39/120), followed by S pyogenes 1.7% (2/120), S pneumoniae 1.7% (2/120), Klebsiella pneumoniae 0.8% (1/120), and Salmonella enteritidis 0.8% (1/120). Due to the lack of corresponding PCR molecular detection methods, the incidence of kingella kingae was seriously underestimated, which is the most common cause of bone and joint infection in preschool children. The femur and tibia are the most common sites of infection.

Antibiotic therapy

Every patient was administered a minimum of 4 days of intravenous antibiotics prior to undergoing definitive surgery. The predominant antibiotics utilized for empirical treatment before hospitalization were vancomycin (19%), moxalactam (14%), ertapenem (12%), and ceftriaxone (12%). The most often administered antibiotics post-admission were vancomycin (40%), cefuroxime (36%), cefamandole sodium (13%), and cefazolin sodium (10%). Most patients received intravenous antibiotics for a period of 2 to 4 weeks, with the duration mostly determined by the pathogen’s susceptibility to the antibiotics and the host’s immune capability. Oral antibiotics were administered to 125 (83%) patients for a duration of 2 to 4 weeks, mostly determined by the pace of decrease in the ESR. 78% (n = 117) of the youngsters received oral antibiotics upon discharge without retaining a PICC. The decision to transition to oral antibiotics was based on CRP findings and clinical symptoms.

Characteristics of patients with or without poor prognosis

On admission, thirty-six percent of patients (n = 54) presented with a body temperature >38.5℃. CRP level at admission was twice higher in the poor prognosis (PP) group than in the good prognosis (GP) group (P = 0.013), but it was not an independent risk factor in the model (OR = 0.99, P = 0.95). CRP level returned to normal (< 10 mg/L) in 83 (55%) patients, of which only 7 patients (8.4%, P < 0.001) were in the PP group. After the second operation, 75% (112/150) of patients with good prognosis achieved CRP normalization, while only 10 cases (10%) with poor prognosis normalized (P < 0.001). Compared with the GP group, the PP group had a longer CRP recovery time (22 days vs. 15 days; Median difference: 6.0 mg/L; 95%CI: 2.0–9.0 mg/L, P = 0.002). Eighty-seven cases (58%) had a white blood cell count higher than the normal value (10 × 109 cells/L) on admission, and the difference between the two groups was statistically significant (median difference:3.72 × 109/L; 95%CI: 1.20–6.56 × 109/L; P = 0.003). This parameter was an independent risk factor for poor prognosis in the prediction model, but its reliability was low (AUC = 0.69). Although the PP group required longer total hospital stay (38 days vs. 42 days, P = 0.097) and duration of intravenous antibiotic therapy (19 days vs. 21 days, P = 0.076), there was no statistically significant difference. Patients with adverse prognosis were more likely to have a higher positive bacterial culture (73% vs. 97%, P = 0.002), intensive care unit (3% vs. 11%, P = 0.01), septic arthritis (33% vs. 55%, P = 0.02), and multiple surgeries (9% vs. 35%, P = 0.001).

Prediction of poor outcomes

Due to the nature of the retrospective study, it was not possible to preset the date for monitoring laboratory indicators. Therefore, we could utilize CRP and ESR within 3–5 days post-surgery to reflect the regression of inflammation. However, univariate statistical analysis revealed some significant covariate differences between patients with and without adverse prognosis (Table 1). In particular, postoperative CRP level was more than 5-fold higher in the PP group compared with the GP group (Z = 4.8, P < 0.001). We included these covariates in a multivariate binary logistic regression model. The final analysis included 134 patients with complete data (Good Prognosis [GP] group: n = 107; Poor Prognosis [PP] group: n = 27). Four independent predictors of adverse prognosis were identified, as follows (Fig. 3): Elevated white blood cells (aOR 1.2; 95% CI [1.0-1.4], cutoff value = 15 [Youden index], sensitivity 63%, specificity 75%); First postoperative CRP (aOR 1.04; 95% CI [1.0-1.07], cutoff = 17 mg/L, sensitivity 78%, specificity 73%); AHO diagnosis time (aOR 1.3; 95% CI [1.1–1.6], cutoff = 7.5, sensitivity 63%, specificity 71%); Days of antibiotic-based fever (aOR 1.5; 95% CI [1.1–2.2], cutoff = 7.5, sensitivity 89%, specificity 61%). The area under the curve (AUC) for WBC was 0.69, for first postoperative CRP was 0.80, for AHO diagnosis time was 0.68, and for antibiotic-based fever timing was 0.77 (Fig. 4). Using multivariate regression analysis, the probability formed by the above four parameters was incorporated into the SPSS software to draw the joint ROC curve, and this integration significantly improved the reliability of the model (AUC=0.85, cutoff=0.15, sensitivity of 89%, specificity of 77%, positive predictive value {PPV} of 52%, negative predictive value {NPV} of 95%).

Discussion

Over seven years, our institution encountered 278 instances of acute hematogenous Osteomyelitis, with 162 cases (58%) requiring surgical treatment. 70% (105/150) of patients had an onset time of more than 6 days before admission, primarily due to delays in diagnosis and ineffective inventions, culminating in a complicated course, often accompanied by local abscess and persistent hyperthermia. Patients typically get conservative treatment prior to surgery in emergency care, infectious medicine, or other hospital settings. If clinical symptoms did not improve, inflammatory markers did not diminish sufficiently, and a subperiosteal abscess was identified using MRI or B-ultrasound, patients were referred to the orthopedic unit for further assessment of surgical necessity. Surgical techniques are chosen based on the lesion’s location and size, including placing a rubber drainage tube, using a vacuum sealing drainage device (VSD), or performing basic decompression and debridement.

VSD technique facilitated wound healing by establishing a closed drainage environment and utilizing biological materials as channels to continuously attract wound exudates. This technology has found extensive clinical applications, including but not limited to open wounds. In the local treatment of acute osteomyelitis, compared with the traditional method involving frequent dressing Changes and surgical puncture drainage, the introduction of VSD technology has revolutionized the surgical treatment of AHO by effectively removing wound exudates and reducing bacteria load through continuous irrigation and drainage for 7 days [23, 24]. In addition, it promotes angiogenesis and resolution of tissue edema.

In this study, we initially assessed the effectiveness of vacuum sealing drainage in managing complicated AHO. A significant disparity in CRP levels between pre- and post-initial surgery was observed (58.5 mg/L vs. 9 mg/L, median difference 39.6 mg/L, 95%CI 28.0 to 52.0 mg/L, P < 0.001). The proportion of patients with preoperative CRP > 50 mg/L decreased from 59 to 17.3% after the procedure. The percentage of patients with body temperature > 38℃ one day before surgery was 54.6% (82/150), and it decreased to 14.6% (22/150) one day after surgery (P = 0.001). According to the records of the second operation, 86% (129/150) of the patients had no pus accumulation after continuous irrigation for 7 days, however, 14% (21/150) of the patients needed to replace the VSD to continue irrigation and drainage. While we appreciate the efficacy of intravenous antibiotics given during two surgical procedures, it is equally critical to further consider the influence of ongoing irrigation and drainage in patients with a lengthy medical background and substantial abscess development. Research has demonstrated that using both medical and surgical treatments is more beneficial than solely relying on antibiotics for treating acute hematogenous osteoarticular infections [7]. This study is the most extensive retrospective analysis of surgical intervention in children with complicated AHO known to us. This study’s results offer healthcare professionals a new treatment option for managing specific patient groups. It improves understanding of surgery’s effects, deepens cognition of AHO’s tricky course, and enables timely prediction and communication of prognostic risks to families, potentially reducing medical disputes.

The primary objective of this study was, without a doubt, to forecast the risk factors linked with an unfavorable prognosis. We found the longer to diagnose acute osteomyelitis, the higher the risk of recurrence and sequelae. With the increasing comprehension of the pathophysiology of osteomyelitis and substantial breakthroughs in pharmacology, landmark progress has been made in the treatment of this field. The concept of death is outdated; However, what poses a challenge to the prognosis of AHO are diagnostic delays and the emergence of multiple drug-resistant strains [2, 25]. In certain underdeveloped rural areas with limited access to medical resources, challenges in symptom recognition and delays in seeking appropriate medical attention have resulted in the widespread dissemination and persistent bone devastation, which predisposed to the form the extensive abscesses around the lesion before arrival at the tertiary hospital. Several studies have also proven the association between early detection of osteomyelitis and improved clinical prognosis [1, 2, 26].

Currently, the CRP level and the duration of fever after the administration of antibiotics are reliable predictors of acute or chronic complications of AHO [7, 9–11]. Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies. Hunter et al. [9] found that failure to reduce CRP levels by 50% on day 4/5 predicted the development of chronic complications. In addition, our study highlights the significant association between CRP levels after initial surgery and late sequelae and a high rate of recurrence, as indicated in the guidelines: the absence of a rapid decrease in CRP concentration within 48 h after the start of treatment or the absence of a decrease of 50% or more from the peak CRP concentration within 3–5 days after admission define the complexity of the disease course [17]. However, the persistence of fever during antibiotic therapy also indicates inadequate control of the source of infection. Chase McNeil et al. [7] suggested that prolonged fever days and delayed source control were independently associated with orthopedic complications (OC). Both our findings and predictive models developed by other investigators emphasize the prognostic importance of timely control of the source of infection. A 50% reduction in CRP or maintenance of a level of ≤ 17 mg/L within 4 days after initial surgery, along with a duration of antibiotic therapy ≤ 7 days, has shown promising results [9].

After controlling for confounding variables, MRSA co-infection was not identified as a predictor of poor outcome, consistent with the results published by Alhinai et al. and McNeil et al. [7, 10]. Some studies indicate that AHO caused by MRSA may lead to more severe acute complications (such as subperiosteal abscess, deep vein thrombosis, and multiorgan failure) and chronic consequences compared to infections caused by other organisms besides MSSA or Staphylococcus aureus [3, 4, 13, 14]. In a study by Gafur et al. [27], it was found that children infected with MRSA needed more surgical procedures compared to children with non-MRSA infections. However, in our study, only 28% of MRSA cases (11/39) required multiple surgical procedures. Variations in the pathogenicity of bacteria in different areas or the prompt administration of vancomycin may have played a role in this.

As a polypeptide hormone, PCT has a short half-life of only 22 h and decreases rapidly within 48 h of antibiotic treatment, typically elevated in the presence of bacterial infections [28]. In addition, Butbul-Aviel proposed [29] that PCT has particular utility in accurately distinguishing Osteomyelitis from arthritis and other inflammatory and reactive diseases. The median PCT level was 14-fold higher in the PP group than in the GP group (median difference:1.16 ng/mL; 95%CI: 0.59–3.18 ng/mL; P < 0.001). However, this variable was excluded from the regression model due to the high number of missing values. However, further studies are needed to determine its significance in the diagnosis of osteomyelitis and its subsequent complications.

Among the 31 adverse prognosis events (Table 2), the majority of recurrent cases and pathologic fractures were managed through long-term anti-infection therapy and reoperation. Among them, 80% of patients had complete clinical recovery, and only 20% of patients had permanent sequelae. Patients who failed treatment eventually developed chronic osteomyelitis and were treated surgically. Patients with mild leg length discrepancy (1 to 2 cm) can use specially tailored augmentation shoes for normal recreational activities. However, inclusion and long-term monitoring were driven by concern about the potential development of a greater length discrepancy. Adverse outcomes were generally associated with high healthcare utilization and included prolonged hospital stay, repeat imaging and laboratory tests, continued antibiotic use, and additional VSD replacement (52512 vs. 36403 RB, P = 0.004).

The use of artificial bone powder mixed with vancomycin topically to treat AHO is a source of controversy. While proven effective in treating chronic osteomyelitis, the application of this approach in acute osteomyelitis has yet to be investigated. Wang et al. [30] compared the use of vancomycin-loaded calcium sulfate beads after surgical debridement with conventional surgical therapy and found that the former significantly shortened the time to CRP reduction to 50% and recovery to normal, and may have reduced the duration of intravenous antibiotic. Zhang et al. [31] performed a descriptive study on 22 patients with AHO, finding that 15 patients (68.18%) had positive outcomes and deemed this treatment approach satisfactory. The clinical practice recommendations of the American Academy of Pediatric Infectious Diseases and the Infectious Diseases Society advise against using antibiotics at the surgical site due to the potential harm and added cost [10]. Only one case (6.7%) of poor outcomes was seen in this investigation, and the difference was not statistically significant when compared to the VSD alone group (P = 0.28). It is yet to be determined if this technique can notably enhance the outlook for patients with complex acute AHO or accomplish swift inflammation management.

Limitations

In addition to the inherent limitations of the retrospective study, this study still has five shortcomings. Firstly, the absence of a surgical control group precludes definitive assessment of VSD’s comparative advantages in treating complex AHO, despite our objective to validate its therapeutic efficacy, as standardized surgical protocols for benchmarking remain unavailable. Secondly, Our surgical criteria were established based on extensive clinical expertise, thus their applicability to other areas is yet questionable. And further discussion and clarity are needed to determine if the abscess size can indicate the severity of the disease and be used as a criterion for surgery. Thirdly, the hypothesized benefits of surgical intervention, including reduced hospitalization duration, decreased intravenous antibiotic dependence, accelerated normalization of inflammatory biomarkers, and clinical symptom resolution, lack objective substantiation within our dataset. Fourthly, the efficacy of bone substitutes combined with vancomycin for controlling infection sources is not proven and is outside the scope of this study, suggesting a need for further research in this area. Lastly, constraints inherent to conventional bacterial culture methodologies prevented molecular detection of Kingella kingae colonization, representing an additional technical limitation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, VSD technology itself is not considered innovative. However, we innovatively applied it to the treatment of AHO. After 1 week of continuous saline irrigation and 2 debridement procedures, we completely controlled more than 75% of the infection. However, the specific role of antibiotics in this process is unknown. For patients with a high risk of poor prognosis, it is imperative to allocate more attention, implement corresponding preventive measures promptly, and provide follow-up care after the infection subsides.
