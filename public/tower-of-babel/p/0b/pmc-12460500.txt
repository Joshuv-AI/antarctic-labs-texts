Temporal Dynamics and Adaptive Mechanisms of Microbial Communities: Divergent Responses and Network Interactions
Authors: Shengjie Sun, Zhiyi Qiao, Denis V Tikhonenkov, Yingchun Gong, Hua Li, Renhui Li, Kexin Sun, Da Huo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrobial communities are vital to aquatic ecosystems, driving biogeochemical cycles, nutrient recycling, and overall ecosystem functioning. However, their instant feedback, particularly in response to environmental fluctuations, remain insufficiently understood. In this study, we investigated the interaction of prokaryotic and eukaryotic microbial communities in riverine ecosystems under temporal dynamics using high-throughput sequencing and co-occurrence network analysis. We observed distinct patterns, with eukaryotic communities showing a consistent increase in alpha diversity, while prokaryotic communities exhibited more variable and directional shifts over time. Two key phases were identified: a dynamic phase characterized by rapid changes in both alpha and beta diversity and a stabilization phase where community composition became more stable, with increased evenness. Co-occurrence network analysis revealed a transition from a modular structure in the dynamic phase to a more centralized and highly connected network in the stabilization phase. While modularity can enhance stability by localizing perturbations within distinct subnetworks, increased centralization and connectivity may weaken this stabilizing effect, potentially making the network less resilient to environmental fluctuations. Our findings provide new insights into the adaptive mechanisms that sustain microbial community stability and resilience in dynamic aquatic ecosystems, emphasizing the importance of diversity and network structure in maintaining ecological stability. Introduction

Microbial communities, encompassing both prokaryotic and eukaryotic microorganisms, play a fundamental role in aquatic ecosystems, serving as essential contributors to biogeochemical cycles, nutrient recycling, and overall ecosystem functioning [1]. Amidst the ongoing challenges posed by global warming and anthropogenic pressures, the vulnerability of microbial communities has increasingly come to the forefront [2]. Due to their small size, rapid growth rates, and metabolic flexibility, microbial communities can exhibit rapid turnover, often within hours to days, allowing them to respond swiftly even to minor disturbances [3]. Such rapid and spontaneous responses to environmental fluctuations often result in significant variations in community dynamics, with microbial abundances fluctuating notably between measurement time points. [4]. Recent studies have demonstrated that microbial communities can undergo substantial compositional changes over short time periods. For example, in estuarine and lacustrine systems, microbial communities have been shown to exhibit rapid shifts in composition within 1–3 weeks, suggesting continuous reassembly of these communities [5, 6]. Notably, the extent of such short-term temporal variation can even exceed spatial differences: in marine eukaryotic communities, compositional changes observed over a few days were greater than those seen across several kilometers [7]. Recent research suggests that short-erm community dynamics may have profound and lasting impacts on both community structure and function [8]. Therefore, understanding the temporal dynamics of these microbial communities is crucial for predicting the resilience and adaptability of aquatic ecosystems under changing environmental conditions.

Biotic factors are crucial in driving microbial community dynamics [9]. As key components of microbial communities, the coexistence of prokaryotic and eukaryotic communities, with their distinct diversity and abundance patterns, plays a pivotal role in shaping microbial community structure and function through their interactions. Research has demonstrated that prokaryotic and eukaryotic communities exhibit distinct temporal variations. For instance, a study conducted in a freshwater lake in the subtropical region of eastern China found that prokaryotic communities are more strongly affected by seasonal changes than eukaryotic communities. This difference reflects their distinct adaptive strategies, where prokaryotes exhibit higher sensitivity to variations in temperature, total nitrogen, and dissolved oxygen, whereas eukaryotic communities maintain a more complex and interconnected co-occurrence network, which enhances their resilience and ecological stability in response to environmental fluctuations (W. [10, 11]). Additionally, it has been shown that eukaryotes positively influence prokaryotic diversity through multiple predation, enhancing species complementarity (i.e., more efficient resource use among taxa) and evenness (i.e., more balanced abundances), by suppressing dominant taxa and promoting coexistence [12]. Furthermore, various environmental conditions have been observed to influence the interaction strength between eukaryotic and prokaryotic organisms, leading to variations in microbial diversity, structure, and stability [13]. Therefore, understanding the interactions between eukaryotic and prokaryotic communities, as well as how these interaction strength vary under different environmental conditions, is essential for comprehensively understanding microbial community dynamics and their ecological implications.

These intricate associations between eukaryotic and prokaryotic communities, along with the environmental pressures they face, are often reflected in the structure and dynamics of co-occurrence networks, which can provide insights into potential ecological interactions and shared environmental responses. These networks, which are constructed based on correlations between species abundances, provide insights into the structural properties of microbial communities, including their stability, robustness, and the potential for species to co-adapt or compete within their environments. Although current co-occurrence networks cannot directly measure biotic interactions, they remain valuable tools for uncovering ecological patterns and hypothesizing the mechanisms underlying microbial community assembly. By integrating environmental metadata and functional information, co-occurrence analyses can help disentangle the relative contributions of abiotic filtering in potential species selection [14, 15]. Previous studies have indicated that the stability of microbial co-occurrence networks is not solely determined by biodiversity, but rather by the nature and architecture of species interactions. In particular, networks dominated by weak or negative interactions—such as competition—tend to exhibit higher temporal stability and resilience, whereas strong positive feedback may destabilize the system [16]. Among various topological features of ecological networks, modularity has received particular attention due to its close relationship with community organization and ecosystem stability. Modularity quantifies the extent to which taxa are grouped into interacting or coexisting units, known as modules. These modules are typically composed of taxa that are positively correlated, indicating shared ecological functions, spatial proximity, or similar habitat preferences. However, modules may also contain negatively correlated taxa, which could reflect competitive interactions or ecological niche differentiation. Modularity can significantly impact community stability [17]. Recent research indicates that structural characteristics of ecological networks, such as nestedness and modularity, can effectively distinguish between antagonistic and mutualistic interactions, particularly when contextualized with local environmental information [18]. Moreover, high modularity stabilizes communities by containing the effects of a taxon’s loss within its module, preventing the disruption from spreading throughout the entire network [19]. However, higher environmental pressures may lead to lower network modularity and an increase in positive associations, which could be key processes that disrupt community stability and ultimately destabilize microbial networks [20]. This underscores the critical need for further research into the interplay between biodiversity, network structure, and environmental pressures to safeguard microbial community stability and ecosystem health.

This study focuses on the temporal dynamics of prokaryotic and eukaryotic microbial communities in a riverine ecosystem. By employing high-throughput amplicon sequencing of 16S rRNA and 18S rRNA genes, coupled with co-occurrence network analysis, we aim to comprehensively examine microbial diversity and community structure. Two distinct phases were identified: a dynamic phase, marked by pronounced fluctuations in both alpha and beta diversity, followed by a stabilization phase, during which the community composition exhibited greater stability and an increase in evenness. Specifically, we seek to address three key questions: (1) Do prokaryotic and eukaryotic microbial communities exhibit distinct temporal response patterns that reflect their sensitivity to environmental fluctuations? (2) How do the structural characteristics of microbial co-occurrence networks vary across different periods, and how do environmental factors shape the properties and stability of these networks? (3) How do structural changes in eukaryotic and prokaryotic communities shape the topological properties of microbial co-occurrence networks? Through this study, we aim to gain a deeper understanding of how microbial community dynamics are reflected in co-occurrence networks and how environmental factors shape both community composition and network properties in aquatic ecosystems.

Material and Method

Sample Collection and Environmental Attributes

The Haihe River, a critical water resource in Tianjin, China, serves multiple functions including drainage, water storage, supply, shipping, tourism, and environmental protection. The study area is located at Lishunde Wharf (117.22°E, 39.12°N) on the Haihe River in Tianjin (Fig. S1). This region is predominantly influenced by the East Asian monsoon and exhibits a temperate monsoon climate. Sampling at the same site was conducted over 88 days, from May 28, 2021, to August 23, 2021, with samples collected in every 3 days, resulting in a total of 30 samples. In total, 10 L of in situ surface water (about 0.5 cm depth) was collected using a plexiglass water collector. Surface water (500 mL) from the sampling site was immediately filtered using a 0.22-μm polycarbonate membrane (Millipore, USA). The water samples were kept at 0–4 °C in insulated containers with ice packs and transported to the laboratory for analysis as soon as possible. The water quality parameters analyzed included total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), ammonium (NH4-N), nitrate (NO3-N), total suspended solids (TSS), and pH [21]. Daily meteorological data, including temperature, relative humidity, and wind speed, were obtained using the nasapower R package, which accessed the NASA POWER database based on the study area’s coordinates for analysis [22]. These environmental variables are summarized in their temporal variation in the study area (Fig. S2).

Amplicon Sequencing and Processing

The microbial DNA was extracted using the Magen HiPure Soil DNA Kit (Magen, Guangzhou, China). The concentration of the extracted DNA was measured with a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), and its purity was assessed using a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA). The hypervariable region V3–V4 of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primer pair 338F/806R [23–25], while the hypervariable region V4 of the eukaryotic 18S rRNA gene was amplified with the primer pair 528F/706R [26]. The PCR products were first evaluated via 1% agarose electrophoresis and then purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Germany). The purified PCR products were pooled in equal amounts and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using the PE250 mode at Frasergen (Wuhan, China).

The raw FASTQ files for both 16S and 18S rRNA gene sequences were processed using the same workflow in QIIME2 v2023.2. The paired-end reads were processed using the DADA2 pieline, which included quality filtering, denosing, and the generation of amplicon sequence variants (ASV) [27]. Taxonomic classification of each ASV was carried out using the Silva database v138.1 [28]. For the 16S rRNA gene data (prokaryotic), chloroplast and mitochondrial sequences were removed to avoid potential contamination from eukaryotic organelles, thereby ensuring a more accurate representation of the prokaryotic microbial community. To ensure comparability across samples, microbial profiles were rarefied to the same sequencing depth using the “Rarefy” function in the “GuniFrac” package in R [29]. The prokaryotes retained 34,342 merged sequences per sample, which were clustered into 8439 ASVs, and the eukaryotes retained 47,089 per sample, which were clustered into 5454 ASVs. All downstream diversity and network analyses were performed using the rarefied raw abundance data, not relative abundances, unless otherwise stated. All subsequent analyses were conducted in R v4.2.1 unless otherwise specified.

Statistical and Ecological Analysis

The α-diversity index (Shannon, Simpson, and richness) for each sample and the β-diversity index (“Bray–Curtis” distance) for each pairwise sample both were calculated using the “vegan” package. The sum of the total phylogenetic branch length (phylogenetic distance, PD) was calculated using the “picante” package [30]. To investigate the temporal dynamics of microbial diversity, we performed a quadratic polynomial regression analysis on the α-diversity and phylogenetic distance. Additionally, to evaluate the temporal dynamics of microbial community structure, two approaches were employed. First, the time decay relationship (TDR) was analyzed using a quadratic polynomial regression to capture potential non-linear relationships between community dissimilarity and time intervals between sampling points. Second, Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was calculated between each time point and the initial time point to assess the changes in community structure over time relative to the baseline sample. Microbial community stability was evaluated using the average variation degree (AVD) [31]. To examine the relationships between physicochemical properties and the microbial community structure, Mantel tests were performed. The hierarchical partitioning (HP) was used to further analyze the significance of individual environmental factors and their combined effects by “rdacca.hp” package [32]. In the HP analysis, variables were grouped as follows: weather (temperature, humidity, wind speed), physicochemical (TSS, pH), nutrients-N (NH₄⁺, NO₃⁻, TN), P (TP), and eukaryotes and prokaryotes (PC1 and PC2 from PCoA).

All co-occurrence network analysis was done using the “meconetcomp” package [33]. Co-occurrence networks were constructed based on Spearman correlation coefficients. To ensure the robustness of the network, only species that appeared in at least four samples were included in the analysis. A correlation was considered robust when the absolute value of the correlation coefficient (r) was greater than 0.85 and the p-value was less than 0.01. The p-values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. To assess the stability and robustness of the networks under species extinction scenarios, simulations were conducted by randomly removing nodes in each network. The average degree and natural connectivity were then computed to evaluate network stability. We generated sub-networks for each time-series sample from meta-community networks by preserving ASVs presented in each time point sample. Network-level topological features provided in “subnet_property” function were calculated for each sub-network. In addition, node vulnerability (Eff) was calculated to assess each node’s contribution to global network efficiency. Specifically, the vulnerability of a node reflects the loss in network efficiency when that node and all its edges are removed [34]. The visualization was carried out using the Gephi v0.10.1 [35]

We used partial least squares path modeling (PLS-PM) to evaluate the interactions between eukaryotic and prokaryotic communities and their impact on co-occurrence networks [36]. The complexity of the network was characterized using latent variables constructed from density, average degree, average path length, and complexity heterogeneity [37]. The goodness of fit (GoF) was used to assess the overall fit and robustness of the model.

Result

Dynamics of Microbial Community Composition and Diversity

To examine temporal patterns in microbial community structure, we first analyzed changes in α-diversity (Shannon, Simpson, and richness) and phylogenetic distance over time. These metrics were fitted using a quadratic model that incorporated both time and its squared term to capture potential nonlinear trends, providing insights into ecological and evolutionary processes throughout the study period (Fig. 1A). For prokaryotes, the diversity metrics generally exhibited a nonlinear pattern, with initial increases followed by decreases over time. The Shannon diversity showed an initial increase followed by a decrease in prokaryotes (R2 = 0.25, pquadratic < 0.01, plinear = 0.93), while it increased significantly in eukaryotes (R2 = 0.47, plinear < 0.001, pquadratic = 0.93). The Simpson diversity also exhibited a nonlinear trend in prokaryotes (R2 = 0.32, pquadratic < 0.01, plinear = 0.85) and increased significantly in eukaryotes (R2 = 0.41, plinear < 0.001, pquadratic = 0.99). The phylogenetic distance exhibited both linear and nonlinear changes in prokaryotes (R2 = 0.39, plinear < 0.01, pquadratic < 0.05), and a moderate linear increase in eukaryotes (R2 = 0.17, plinear < 0.05, pquadratic = 0.43).

Temporal dynamics of microbial community diversity and composition. A Temporal trends in microbial community diversity, including Shannon diversity, Simpson diversity, richness, and phylogenetic distance for both eukaryotic and prokaryotic communities. B Temporal trends in the taxonomic composition of the microbial community for eukaryotes and prokaryotes

The community composition of eukaryotes and prokaryotes was analyzed over time (Fig. 1B). The eukaryotic community was predominantly composed of Cryptophyceae (36.62%), Chytridiomycota (25.17%), and SAR (11.32%), while the prokaryotic community was primarily composed of Proteobacteria (31.39%), Actinobacteriota (25.83%), and Bacteroidota (20.33%). These percentages represent the cumulative relative abundances across all samples. Variance analysis based on relative abundance values was used to evaluate temporal fluctuations of microbial phyla. Among prokaryotes, Actinobacteriota (0.0119), Proteobacteria (0.0085), and Cyanobacteria (0.0074) exhibited the highest variances. For eukaryotes, the greatest fluctuations were observed in Cryptophyceae (0.0400), Chytridiomycota (0.0220), and SAR (0.0096).

Dynamics of Microbial Community Structure and Environmental Influences

The time-decay relationships (TDRs) for microbial communities were modeled by fitting the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity of prokaryotic and eukaryotic communities as a function of temporal distance, using both linear and quadratic terms (Fig. S3). For the prokaryotic community, the fitted quadratic model reveals a significant linear increase in dissimilarity with increasing time intervals (plinear < 0.001, pquadratic = 0.082), suggesting a continuous directional change in community structure as time gaps widen. This is further supported by the high density of dissimilarity values near 1, indicating that prokaryotic communities experienced substantial structural shifts over time, with increased variability between more temporally distant samples. In contrast, the eukaryotic community displayed a more complex, nonlinear trend. Initial decreases in Bray–Curtis dissimilarity were followed by subsequent increases as the time gap between samples widened (plinear = 0.47, pquadratic < 0.05). This indicates that while short-term fluctuations may have led to more similar community structures, longer temporal gaps resulted in greater community divergence. The turning point at approximately day 40.81 occurs near the midpoint of the time series, indicating a key transition in the community’s temporal dynamics. We conducted a principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distance to examine the temporal dynamics of microbial communities (Fig. S4). The analysis reveals a clear separation of samples along the primary coordinate axis, indicating significant shifts in both eukaryotic and prokaryotic community structures over time (p < 0.001). Notably, the samples divide around the midpoint of the time series, supporting the earlier findings of a key transition in community structure.

Furthermore, to directly quantify the temporal dynamics in community structure before and after the midpoint of the time series, Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was calculated between each time point and the initial sample, followed by a quadratic polynomial regression analysis (Fig. 2A). For eukaryotes, the model exhibited a strong fit (R2 = 0.76), with both the linear and quadratic terms highly significant (plinear < 0.001, pquadratic < 0.001). Prokaryotic communities also showed a significant linear trend (R2 = 0.6, plinear < 0.01). This analysis revealed two distinct phases of community dynamics. During the dynamic phase, covering approximately the first half of the time series, both eukaryotic and prokaryotic communities displayed increasing dissimilarity from the initial sample, indicating rapid shifts and higher variability in community composition. As the time series progressed into the stabilization phase, changes in dissimilarity plateaued, particularly for prokaryotic communities, suggesting a period of relative stability in community structure. These phases highlight notable differences in the temporal responses between the two microbial groups, reinforcing earlier observations of divergent community dynamics surrounding the midpoint of the study. Meanwhile, we assessed the community stability of eukaryotic and prokaryotic communities across two phases using the average variation degree (AVD). From the dynamic phase to the stabilization phase, the average variation degree (AVD) of microbial communities decreased, indicating an increase in overall community stability. This decline was particularly evident in prokaryotic communities, whose AVD significantly decreased during the stabilization phase, as determined by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (p < 0.05; Fig. S5). Based on the results shown in the Pielou’s evenness, both eukaryotic and prokaryotic communities exhibited a significant increase in evenness during the stabilization phase compared to the dynamic phase (p < 0.01, p < 0.0001; Wilcoxon test), indicating a more balanced distribution of species as community composition became more stable over time (Fig. S6).

The unitary environmental drivers of the variation of microbial community were analyzed using Mantel test (Fig. 2B). The result revealed that the NH4+ had a significant impact on the prokaryotic community (p < 0.001), as well as TP and TN (p < 0.001). NO3− also significantly influenced the prokaryotic community structure (p < 0.01). For the eukaryotic community, TP and TN were significantly correlated with community structure (p < 0.001), along with wind Speed (p < 0.001) and time (p < 0.001). Among these environmental factors, TN exhibited the highest Mantel’s r value for the prokaryotic community, signifying it as the most influential factor. For the eukaryotic community, TP had the highest Mantel’s r value, indicating its strong association with community structure. Next, we employed hierarchical partitioning (HP) to address the contributions of multiple variables to community variation and their combined effects, effectively mitigating multicollinearity issues and identifying the most influential factors. For the eukaryotic community, factors collectively explained 57.5% of the variation, with weather factors having the strongest impact at 10.21%. The combined effects of all factors, including weather, prokaryotic community influences, and time, explained an additional 3.05% of the variation (Fig. 2C). For the prokaryotic community, the factors explained a total of 46% of the variation, with nitrogen (N) having the strongest impact at 10.46%. The combined effects of nitrogen, weather, and phosphorus (P) explained an additional 1.27% of the variation in the prokaryotic community structure (Fig. 2D).

Co-occurrence Pattern of Microbial Community

To further elucidate the community dynamics during the dynamic and stabilization phases, we analyzed the microbial co-occurrence networks. The analysis was based on Spearman correlation coefficients, allowing us to construct comprehensive co-occurrence networks for both phases (Fig. 3A). Both networks contained a similar number of nodes, with 722 nodes in the dynamic phase and 742 nodes in the stabilization phase. However, the proportion of prokaryotic nodes decreased significantly from 38.09% in the dynamic phase to 23.32% in the stabilization phase. The stabilization phase network exhibited twice as many edges (6429) compared to the dynamic phase network (3209), resulting in an average degree of 17.33, almost double that of the dynamic phase (8.89). Centralization, which measures the dominance of key nodes, was higher in the stabilization phase (0.098) than in the dynamic phase (0.036), indicating a more prominent role of key nodes or hubs. Conversely, the dynamic phase exhibited higher modularity (0.920) compared to the stabilization phase (0.746), suggesting a more distinct community structure in the early phase. Overall, as the system progressed from the dynamic phase to the stabilization phase, the networks became more interconnected and centralized, with greater diversity in node connectivity and increased complexity. These findings reflect the transition from a less connected, more modular network in the dynamic phase to a highly interconnected and centralized structure in the stabilization phase (Table S1).

Next, we analyzed the overlap of nodes between the two phases. The results showed that the networks shared 316 nodes, accounting for 27.5% of the total nodes in both networks. The dynamic phase network had 406 unique nodes (35.4% of its total), while the stabilization phase network contained 426 unique nodes (37.1% of its total) (Fig. 3B). Among these unique nodes, the number of prokaryotic nodes decreased from 223 in the dynamic phase to 121 in the stabilization phase, while the number of eukaryotic nodes increased from 183 to 306, respectively. To identify keystone taxa, we examined node metrics such as degree and betweenness centrality. In the dynamic phase, multiple ASVs affiliated with Cryptomonas lundii exhibited high centrality, indicating their important ecological role. In contrast, the stabilization phase network was dominated by ASVs affiliated with uncultured_Chytridiomycota, a member of the fungal phylum Chytridiomycota, which exhibited the highest topological centrality. These taxa may function as keystone species, playing critical roles in structuring microbial interactions during different temporal phases. The stabilization phase network exhibited a higher average degree and natural connectivity compared to the dynamic phase network, reflecting a more interconnected and cohesive structure. These higher values indicate that the microbial community was more densely connected during the stabilization phase, with more frequent associations between taxa. Although the stabilization phase network was initially more connected, it was somewhat less robust to node loss compared to the dynamic phase network, where the average degree and natural connectivity were more concentrated during the early stages of node removal (Fig. 3C). This suggests that while the stabilization phase network had higher connectivity overall, the dynamic phase network retained greater resilience under perturbations. Additionally, an analysis of node vulnerability (Eff) showed that the median vulnerability was lower in the dynamic phase (0.009) compared to the stabilization phase (0.283), further highlighting the stabilization phase’s increased fragility, despite its more connected structure. However, this difference was not statistically significant based on the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (p > 0.05; Fig. S7).

We also constructed subnetworks for each sample to examine the influence of environmental factors on network properties. Each subnetwork contained nodes present in the respective sample, enabling us to calculate network attributes. Spearman correlation was then applied to assess the relationship between subnetwork properties and environmental factors (Fig. 3D). The analysis revealed distinct environmental influences on network properties in both phases. In the dynamic phase, key correlations include a negative relationship between vertex count and wind speed, and a positive correlation between clustering coefficient and both NH₄⁺ and time. Additionally, centralization showed a negative correlation with NH₄⁺ and TP, while heterogeneity was positively correlated with NO₃−. In the stabilization phase, NH₄⁺, TP, and TN all showed significant negative correlations with multiple network metrics, including vertex count, edge number, average path length, and network diameter, indicating a strong influence of these environmental factors on network structure. Humidity and wind speed were also negatively correlated with several network properties, such as network diameter and heterogeneity. Additionally, time showed a positive correlation with network diameter and vertex count, suggesting an increasing complexity in the network over time. Overall, the stabilization phase network exhibited a stronger and broader correlation with environmental variables, suggesting higher complexity and increased sensitivity to environmental factors compared to the dynamic phase.

Potential Drivers of the Microbial community Structure and Network Properties

We further explore the relationships between eukaryotic and prokaryotic communities, as well as the inter-domain co-occurrence network properties, using partial least squares path modeling (PLS-PM). The PLS model’s goodness of fit (GoF) score of 0.60 indicates a good explanatory power, capturing significant patterns within the dataset (Fig. 4). The analysis reveals a strong positive relationship within the eukaryotic community. Eukaryotic community diversity strongly positively influences eukaryotic stability (coefficient = 0.751, p < 0.001) and eukaryotic community structure (coefficient = 0.708, p < 0.001). Similarly, prokaryotic community structure (coefficient = 0.842, p < 0.001) and diversity (coefficient = 0.857, p < 0.001) have a strongly positive effect on stability. Additionally, the eukaryotic community strong influence on the prokaryotic community, including a notable positive correlation of eukaryotic community structure on prokaryotic community structure (coefficient = 0.613, p < 0.001) and stability (coefficient = 0.565, p < 0.01). However, there are also significant negative relationships, such as between eukaryotic diversity and prokaryotic diversity (coefficient = − 0.647, p < 0.01), and between eukaryotic community structure and prokaryotic stability (coefficient = − 0.442, p < 0.05). The eukaryotic community structure shows a strong positive association with network Complexity (coefficient = 0.959, p < 0.001) and centralization (coefficient = 0.772, p < 0.05), indicating that more diverse and stable eukaryotic communities contribute to more complex and centralized network structures. Additionally, the eukaryotic community structure negatively impacts network modularity (coefficient = − 0.852, p < 0.05), suggesting that as the eukaryotic community becomes more interconnected, the network becomes less modular. On the prokaryotic side, diversity harms network complexity (coefficient = −0.421, p < 0.05).

Discussion

Distinct Response Patterns in Eukaryotic and Prokaryotic Community Diversity

Our findings indicate that microbial communities were distinctly separated at the midpoint of the time series, forming two phases: the dynamic phase and the stabilization phase. During the dynamic phase, both eukaryotic and prokaryotic communities exhibited similar adaptive patterns, with significant increases in alpha diversity (Shannon, Simpson, richness indices, and phylogenetic distance), suggesting active community expansion and diversification. Concurrently, Bray–Curtis dissimilarity increased, particularly in the eukaryotic community, indicating substantial shifts in composition. This rise in diversity suggests the introduction and establishment of new species, increasing community heterogeneity over time [38]. Environmental factors such as nitrogen, phosphorus, and weather fluctuations likely played a role in shaping resource availability and species interactions [39]. Overall, the rapid increase in diversity contributed to community restructuring and enhanced resilience to environmental variability. During the stabilization phase, Bray–Curtis dissimilarity fluctuations diminished, indicating more stable community composition, but eukaryotic and prokaryotic communities exhibited divergent responses. Eukaryotic α-diversity continued to increase, suggesting ongoing species establishment and niche differentiation, likely driven by complex interactions such as predation and symbiosis [40, 41]. In contrast, prokaryotic α-diversity initially increased and then declined over time, likely reflecting the combined effects of early diversification followed by competitive exclusion and environmental filtering. [42]. Increased competition from eukaryotic communities may have further contributed to this decline [43], leading to a more streamlined prokaryotic community dominated by species better adapted to stable conditions. This shift highlights the adaptive strategies of microbial communities while also revealing the potential consequences of reduced diversity for ecosystem stability.

Network Centralization from Dynamic Phase to Stabilization Phase

We further explored the differences in microbial co-occurrence networks during the dynamic phase and stabilization phase. We generated comprehensive co-occurrence networks for both phases, providing deeper insights into the structural dynamics of prokaryotic-eukaryotic communities. The analysis revealed several key differences between the two networks. Both the dynamic and stabilization networks have a similar number of nodes, with 722 and 742 nodes, respectively. However, the proportion of prokaryotic nodes decreased significantly from 38.09% in the dynamic phase to 23.32% in the stabilization phase. This shift indicates that prokaryotic communities were more affected by the environmental changes, likely due to more diverse selection pressures during the heterogenization stage, which made certain prokaryotes unable to adapt and led to their exclusion. In contrast, the proportion of eukaryotic nodes increased significantly from the dynamic phase to the stabilization phase, consistent with the continuous rise in eukaryotic α-diversity. This pattern suggests that an increasing number of eukaryotic taxa were recruited over time, which may have enabled them to exploit a wider range of resources and ecological niches, thereby demonstrating greater adaptability and competitiveness during this stage (D. [10, 11, 44]). Notably, the stabilization network had twice as many edges (6429) compared to the dynamic network (3209), resulting in a higher average degree of 17.33 versus 8.89. The more stable environmental conditions and balanced species abundance distributions during the stabilization phase likely facilitated stronger species interactions and niche partitioning, leading to a more tightly connected and structurally complex co-occurrence network. Such increased connectivity and network complexity have been associated with enhanced community stability and resilience under stable environmental conditions [34]. Additionally, the centralization metric, which measures the dominance of key nodes, was higher in the Stabilization network (0.098) than in the dynamic network (0.036), indicating that certain keystone or “pivotal” species became more dominant in the network structure during the stabilization phase. In contrast, the modularity was higher in the dynamic network (0.920) compared to the stabilization network (0.746), suggesting a more distinct and compartmentalized community structure in the dynamic phase, with clearer boundaries between different microbial subgroups. To explore potential keystone taxa, we examined node-level centrality. In the dynamic phase, ASVs affiliated with Cryptomonas lundii showed high centrality, suggesting a pivotal ecological role. In the stabilization phase, ASVs affiliated with uncultured_Chytridiomycota dominated, indicating a shift in network structure and possibly in key functional groups. The rapid diversification during the dynamic phase likely contributed to a more segmented and independent community structure, with distinct ecological niches forming in response to fluctuating environmental conditions.

Ecological interaction networks exhibit a coherent structure characterized by well-defined patterns that contribute to network stability. Network stability is influenced by species connectivity, the presence of negative interactions, and a balance of a few strong and many weak interactions [16]. Additionally, the clustering of species into subgroups, known as modularity, involves numerous connections within species subgroups and fewer connections between them, effectively decoupling the subgroups within the community. This modularity minimizes the risk of systemic change during perturbations by impeding dependencies between subgroups, thereby promoting compartmentalization and buffering against the propagation of disturbance effects across the network [45]. On the other hand, centralization, which measures the dominance of key nodes, can lead to a network where certain keystone or “pivotal” species become more influential. While centralization can enhance network efficiency and robustness by facilitating quick responses to changes, it also makes the network more vulnerable to the loss of these key species, potentially leading to significant disruptions in network stability [46]. We observed similar findings in our study. To investigate the diversity of nodes and network robustness, we simulated species extinction by randomly removing nodes. The results showed that although stabilization networks have higher connectivity, these metrics decrease faster compared to dynamic networks. This suggests that dynamic networks are more stable and resistant to node loss. The scatter density function further supports this, showing higher robustness in the dynamic network, whereas the stabilization network is more prone to abrupt changes. We found similar observations by constructing subnetworks for each sample and using Spearman correlation analysis to examine the influence of environmental factors on network properties in both dynamic and stabilization networks. The stabilization networks showed stronger and broader correlations with environmental variables, suggesting that they are more fragile to environmental factors with higher complexity and dynamics compared to Dynamic networks.

Eukaryotic Fluctuations Drive Integrate Network Centralization

The relationships between eukaryotic and prokaryotic communities, as well as their inter-domain co-occurrence network properties, were explored using partial least squares path modeling (PLS-PM), with the model achieving a GoF score of 0.60, indicating strong explanatory power. Previous research has shown that significant interactions exist between eukaryotic and prokaryotic communities, including important interactions such as predation and competition [47, 48]. Recent studies have also hinted that dissolved organic matter (DOM) may play a role in mediating the interactions between eukaryotic and prokaryotic communities, potentially influencing their ecological dynamics [49, 50]. Our results indicate a strong correlation between eukaryotic and prokaryotic community structures, suggesting reciprocal interactions between these groups. Changes in either community may influence the other, possibly through direct interactions or by altering shared environmental conditions. One of the significant findings is the negative relationship between eukaryotic diversity and prokaryotic diversity (coefficient = − 0.647, p < 0.01). This suggests that the observed inverse relationship between eukaryotic and prokaryotic diversity may partly stem from competition for resources, but it is also likely influenced by shifts in the relative abundances of photosynthetic groups, such as eukaryotic algae and cyanobacteria. For example, in river systems, increases in cyanobacterial abundance have been associated with declines in eukaryotic algal diversity, while prokaryotic diversity remained high, indicating differential niche occupation and potential competition between these groups [41, 51]. Eukaryotic community structure has a notable positive correlation with prokaryotic community structure (coefficient = 0.613, p < 0.001) and stability (coefficient = 0.565, p < 0.01), highlighting the close interdependence of these groups. When eukaryotic communities are well-structured, they can create stable environments that promote the coexistence of various prokaryotic communities. Conversely, significant structural changes in eukaryotic communities can disrupt prokaryotic communities, either through direct interactions or by altering the habitat [52, 53].

Meanwhile, our model sheds light on how the characteristics of eukaryotic and prokaryotic communities influence co-occurrence network properties. The eukaryotic community structure shows a strong positive association with network complexity (coefficient = 0.959, p < 0.001) and centralization (coefficient = 0.772, p < 0.05). Additionally, the eukaryotic community structure negatively impacts network Modularity (coefficient = −0.852, p < 0.05). This phenomenon can be caused by several reasons. First, environmental stressors, such as pollution, nutrient limitation, or weather change, can induce niche compression, reducing the available resources and habitats within a microbial community [54]. Consequently, only species that are best adapted to the novel environmental conditions can survive and reproduce. These species often emerge as keystone or hub species, assuming more central and influential roles within the network, leading to network centralization [55]. Additionally, increasing environmental pressures can intensify competition within microbial communities. In response, certain taxa may gain competitive advantages—such as through predation, resource competition, or symbiotic interactions—allowing them to dominate others and establish themselves as keystone species. As these taxa become increasingly central to community functioning, the overall network structure tends to become more centralized [56]. Furthermore, in response to environmental pressures, eukaryotic communities may reorganize their ecological networks to optimize function. For instance, by reducing redundant interactions or functions and strengthening key interactions, communities can improve resource use efficiency and overall resilience. This reorganization process can result in certain nodes (i.e., species) becoming more central and critical, thereby contributing to network centralization [57]. Different species employ varying strategies to cope with environmental stressors. Some may enhance mutualistic and symbiotic relationships to improve survival chances, while others may adopt competitive and exclusionary tactics to maintain their niches [58]. Overall, the dynamics of eukaryotic communities reflect this diversity of coping strategies, which can influence network structure and drive network centralization. While centralized networks may enhance functional stability in the short term due to the stabilizing effects of keystone species, excessive centralization can increase vulnerability to the loss of these critical species. Therefore, maintaining moderate diversity and network modularity is crucial for long-term ecological stability, a notion that is also supported by previous studies on microbial community resilience under stress [20]. In summary, eukaryotic communities often respond to environmental stressors by centralizing their networks to optimize survival and resource utilization. This process involves niche compression, competitive pressures, ecological network reorganization, diversity of coping strategies, and the trade-offs between biodiversity and stability. These factors collectively contribute to the centralization phenomenon observed in co-occurrence networks.

Conclusions

In this study, we comprehensively analyzed the temporal dynamics of microbial communities, revealing distinct response patterns between eukaryotic and prokaryotic communities. Our findings highlight the distinct temporal dynamics of microbial diversity and community structure, with eukaryotic communities showing gradual increases in diversity and stability, while prokaryotic communities exhibited more rapid and directional changes. The co-occurrence network analysis further illustrated how changes in network structure reflect community responses to environmental fluctuations, with modularity and centralization serving as key indicators of ecosystem stability. These results contribute to our understanding of the adaptive mechanisms underlying microbial community dynamics, providing valuable insights into how biodiversity and network properties interact to sustain ecosystem functions.
