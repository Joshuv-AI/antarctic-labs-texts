ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThis study evaluates plasma‐based proteomic profiles for predicting amyloid positivity in adults with Down syndrome (DS) and examines the impact of apolipoprotein E ε4 (APOE ε4) on test performance.METHODSCross‐sectional data from 290 adults with DS were analyzed using single molecule array (SIMOA) technology to measure plasma amyloid beta (Aβ)42, Aβ40, neurofilament light chain (NfL), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), tau phosphorylated at threonine 181, and total tau. Amyloid burden was quantified using Pittsburgh Compound B and (18)F‐florbetapir Aβ positron emission tomography. Support vector machine analyses were conducted with biomarkers as predictors and age, sex, and APOE ε4 carrier status as covariates.RESULTSAge, GFAP, and NfL contributed the most to the model performance. The proteomic profile achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 96% in models with and without APOE ε4.DISCUSSIONThese findings suggest that plasma proteomic biomarkers can effectively identify amyloid positivity in adults with DS and may support clinical triage, monitoring, and selection for clinical trials, independent of APOE ε4 status.

BACKGROUND

Individuals with Down syndrome (DS) are at high risk for the development of Alzheimer's disease (AD). 1 This increased risk is related to complete or partial trisomy of the amyloid precursor protein in DS, resulting in the overproduction of amyloid beta (Aβ). 2 , 3 Consequently, nearly all adults over the age of 40 years with DS will develop amyloid plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles, the hallmarks of AD. 4 The lifelong increase of Aβ in those with DS makes potential treatments targeting amyloid to stop or slow conversion to AD particularly relevant in this population. 5 , 6 , 7 Positron emission tomography (PET) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis are costly and invasive AD diagnostics. However, plasma biomarker tests offer a practical alternative for characterizing amyloid levels in DS, 8 , 9 , 10 helping to guide screening and enrichment strategies for the inclusion of individuals with DS in future AD clinical trials. 10

Studied most extensively are Aβ isoforms 40 and 42, and phosphorylated tau (p‐tau). P‐tau has several protein epitopes, including 181, 217, and 231; plasma phosphorylated tau 181 (p‐tau181) and p‐tau217 have a high sensitivity and specificity in detecting AD neuropathological changes in blood, correlating with amyloid and tau PET outcomes. 10 , 11 , 12 When predictive models include age, plasma p‐tau181, and neurofilament light chain (NfL), a blood test can accurately differentiate adults with DS classified as asymptomatic from those with clinical diagnoses of prodromal AD or AD dementia. 8 , 13 , 14 , 15 Markers of neurodegeneration including NfL, a polypeptide cytoskeletal scaffolding protein and total tau (t‐tau), another microtubule‐associated protein marker of neurodegeneration, follow an age‐related increase. These markers, along with levels of p‐tau make them appropriate measures to track neuronal death associated with neurofibrillary tau tangles in this group. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 In a study by Petersen and colleagues, 8 NfL showed better discriminatory ability across disease states of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) from cognitively stable (CS) DS, producing an area under the curve (AUC) of 65%, while t‐tau reached 56%. Together with age and sex, modeling produced an AUC of 87%, underscoring the importance of age as a driving factor of DS‐associated Alzheimer's disease (DSAD). Finally, plasma glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), an astroglia cytoskeleton marker of neuroinflammation, has been extensively studied in DS. Astrogliosis is a key component in DSAD pathophysiology that increases incrementally with amyloid positivity, prior to tau pathology, suggesting GFAP's role in mediating tau phosphorylation. 20 , 21

Plasma protein biomarker concentrations can categorize AD‐related diagnoses cross‐sectionally in people with DS with adequate accuracy (AUC > 0.80). 6 , 22 , 23 However, the number of studies examining the effect of AD risk gene, apolipoprotein allele 4 (APOE ε4), in predictive modeling remain limited. Bejanin et al. 24 completed a comprehensive report of biomarkers in DS and the effect of APOE ε4, showing reduced p‐tau181 and smaller hippocampal and right superior parietal cortical volumes in carriers versus non‐carriers starting around age 40 years, coinciding with symptom onset. Further, APOE ε4 carriership is associated with increased mortality and earlier age of onset of AD in those with DS, though its role in dementia onset have been inconsistent. 24 , 25 , 26 Regarding CSF biomarkers, amyloid ratio (Aβ42/40) was reduced in APOE ε4 carriers by their early 20s, but no differences were seen for t‐tau or neurofilament light chain (NfL). 26 , 27 This unexpected finding suggests that APOE affects risk to conversion involving pathways independent of neurodegeneration. There is also evidence that APOE ε4 carriers with DS experience an exacerbation of amyloid and tau pathology and immune and monoamine dysfunction. 28 , 29 Still, findings on PET positivity are inconsistent, despite APOE ε4 association with earlier clinical and biomarker changes of DSAD. 20 , 28 , 30

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We reviewed prior studies on AD biomarkers in individuals with DS, focusing on plasma‐based proteomic markers and the role of APOE ε4. Plasma markers such as p‐tau181, NfL, and GFAP have shown promise in cross‐sectional classification, but the effect of APOE ε4 on predictive models of amyloid positivity in DS remains unclear. Interpretation: Our study shows that a plasma proteomic profile accurately predicts amyloid PET positivity in cognitively stable adults with DS (AUC = 96%) and that APOE ε4 status does not significantly enhance model performance. Future directions: Future longitudinal studies should examine how proteomic trajectories relate to disease progression in DSAD. Our study should be validated in larger cohorts of varying diagnostic classification to further determine the role of APOE ε4 in amyloid positivity in DSAD and risk of amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities in DSAD.

Here, we conducted a cross‐sectional analysis to determine whether a plasma‐based proteomic profile of Aβ40, Aβ42, p‐tau181, NfL, t‐tau, and GFAP can predict amyloid PET positivity in individuals with DS. We conducted a sensitivity analysis to determine the effect of APOE ε4 carriership on model performance. We also explored the predictive accuracy of the protein panel among APOE ε4 carriers and non‐carriers.

METHODS

The Alzheimer's Biomarker Consortium‐Down Syndrome

Alzheimer's Biomarker Consortium‐Down Syndrome (ABC‐DS) is an ongoing cohort study among adults (25 and older) with DS, with the aim of recruiting a total of 550 individuals and 50 sibling controls. ABC‐DS is a multidisciplinary, multisite investigation. All ABC‐DS sites operate under Institutional Review Board‐approved protocols. All study participants and their legally authorized representatives provided informed consent for participation in the ABC‐DS cohort study. The goals of ABC‐DS include identifying sensitive neuropsychological measures of cognitive decline, PET neuroimaging, and measurement of blood‐based proteins and metabolites and genetic biomarkers, along with the characterization of neuropathological features associated with transition from CS to MCI to clinical dementia due to AD in adults with DS.

ABC‐DS study procedures

The complete ABC‐DS procedures are detailed elsewhere 31 , 32 and are briefly stated here. Data are acquired using direct cognitive and functional measures, caregiver questionnaires, medical and neurological assessments, neuroimaging biomarkers, blood‐based biomarkers, genetic information, and neuropathology for those willing to participate in brain donation. The cognitive test battery was designed for use with individuals with DS with varying degrees of premorbid intellectual functioning. Behavioral and psychiatric conditions and overall health status are also assessed. Caregiver interviews are structured to capture information on changes in cognition, day‐to‐day functioning, adaptive behavior, and medical status. Each participant obtains a baseline visit, followed by subsequent assessments every 16 months over the 5‐year study. However, only certain neuroimaging procedures are included in select cycles.

Data availability

ABC‐DS provides public access to clinical, cognitive, and biomarker (fluid and imaging) data through the Laboratory of Neuroimaging (LONI), documentation, and deidentification; biospecimen samples are transferred and managed by the National Centralized Repository for Alzheimer's Disease and Related Dementias (NCRAD), and the ABC‐DS biospecimen bank distributes CSF. Qualified investigators submit requests for access (https://www.nia.nih.gov/research/abc‐ds#data) to data and samples. All requests are reviewed by ABC‐DS investigators and National Institutes of Health (NIH) staff.

Participants

The demographic characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Table 1. The current analysis included 290 adults with DS (60% CS; 18% MCI‐DS; 16% AD‐DS, and 6.6% classified as “other”). Due to the nearly 100% amyloid positivity rate in the MCI and DSAD groups, analysis was not stratified by diagnostic group. Instead, data analyses were conducted among the entire sample, including those with impairment or “other” cognitive status, and then among CS participants (n = 198). Data from this study include blood samples collected at the ABC‐DS baseline visit.

Diagnostic classification

Dementia status, subtype, and age at onset were determined by consensus conferences that integrated information from available sources, including medical, clinical, and cognitive tests; however, ratings were blind to AD biomarker status. Consistent with the recommendations of the American Association on Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities and the International Association for the Scientific Study of Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities 33 , 34 working group for the establishment of criteria for the diagnosis of dementia in individuals with developmental disability. Participants were classified as CS, MCI, DS‐AD, or other. Participants were classified as CS if they were without cognitive or functional decline and as MCI if they demonstrated evidence of cognitive and/or functional decline beyond what would be expected based on their age based on their performance on neuropsychological measures and a documented caregiver report of decline. Participants were categorized as having dementia (DSAD) if there was evidence of substantial progressive declines in cognitive functioning and daily living skills. The “other” category was used to indicate when declines were observed but symptoms could be attributed to factors or conditions unrelated to AD.

Assays

Plasma samples were analyzed at the University of North Texas Health Science Center, Institute for Translational Research Omics core. Automation of the proteomic assay preparation was conducted using a customized Hamilton robotics system. Aliquoting was conducted via the Hamilton easyBlood robotics system. Assays were obtained from the commercially available kits from Quanterix utilizing single molecule array (SIMOA) technology. Proteomic assays were conducted using a multiplex biomarker assay platform. Pooled plasma control samples were included on each plate run. A total of 500 µL of plasma was used to assay the following markers: NfL, p‐tau181, t‐tau, GFAP, Aβ40, and Aβ42. Coefficient of variation, lower limit of detection, and the highest level of detection for each assay are published elsewhere. 35 , 36 These data were downloaded from the LONI website in December 2024. P‐tau 217 was not available at the time the data release was downloaded and so was not included in the current analysis.

PET

To quantitate the Aβ burden, PET scans for use with close adherence to the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) specifications for uptake period and scanning duration for the Aβ ([C‐11] Pittsburgh Compound B [PiB] – four sites, and florbetapir/[F‐18] AV‐45 – three sites). For regional analysis, PiB standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) values were calculated from the 50‐ to 70‐min after injection with reference to the cerebellum. 32 The maximum injected dose is specific to each participating site as required by local restrictions; however, the following targeted injected doses are as follows: an uptake period of 0 to 40 min and a PET acquisition period of 50 to 70 min were used for [C‐11] PiB at a targeted injection dose (10% tolerance) of 15 mCi (555 MBq). An uptake period of 0to 40 min and a PET acquisition period of 50 to 70 min was used for florbetapir ([F‐18] AV45) at a targeted injection dose (10% tolerance) of 10 mCi (370 MBq). Harmonization processes for the PET procedures across all sites require consideration of initial protocol variations in the order of scans at participant visits and the use of different PET radiotracers for measuring Aβ (PiB) at baseline and 32‐month visits. For harmonization across sites, the reconstruction parameters of the PET images followed the specifications defined by the ADNI trials for specific PET scanner models. 32 The Centiloid (CL) global amyloid burden SUVR values for florbetapir and 40‐ to 70‐min PiB scans were converted to the CL scale using previously published methods. 37 CL values were calculated using a level 2 calibration equation based on the PiB calibration dataset available through GAAIN: Centiloid=99.44×SUVRPiB,50−70min−109.3.

The 18 CL threshold for amyloid positivity was selected based on previously published samples of participants with DS and was found to be the optimal threshold compared to lower CL thresholds (e.g., 12 CL) and more traditional thresholds (e.g., 24 to 30 CL). 38 , 39 These estimates demonstrate that a threshold of 20 CL can detect A+ status at the earliest age group (age ≤ 39) and that ≥60% of individuals with DS over the age of 45 years are A+ across thresholds of 20, 25, and 30 CL. 38

Apolipoprotein E

Two target apolipoprotein E (APOE) single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) (rs429358 and rs7412) were genotyped from DNA samples with the KASP genotyping system by LGC genomics. Using these two SNP genotypes, APOE 𝜀2, 𝜀3, and 𝜀4 alleles were defined for analysis. The distribution of APOE genotypes differed modestly between amyloid‐negative (A−) and amyloid‐positive (A+) groups (p = 0.057). The ε3/ε3 genotype was the most prevalent across all participants (63%), with similar frequencies in the A− (67%) and A+ (58%) groups. The ε3/ε4 genotype was observed in 15% overall (13% A−, 19% A+), while the ε4/ε4 genotype occurred in 2.4% overall and was more frequent in the A− group (3.6%) compared to A+ (0.8%). Genotypes containing the ε2 allele (ε2/ε2, ε2/ε3, ε2/ε4, ε3/ε2, ε4/ε2) were relatively uncommon (approximately 14% overall) and were slightly more prevalent among A+ individuals (19%) than A− (11%). Individuals with at least one copy of the APOE 𝜀4 allele were identified as APOE 𝜀4 carriers. APOE 𝜀4 status is included in the description of the sample.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.2) and SPSS (version 28). All continuous variables were log‐transformed before model fitting, and missing values were imputed using predictive mean matching implemented in the mice package. Descriptive statistics were captured using mean (M) and standard deviation for demographic and clinical variables. We used a support vector machine (SVM), a supervised machine learning algorithm that constructs a hyperplane in a high‐dimensional space to classify data by maximizing the margin between different classes. Model parameters and tuning were performed using caret, 40 including hyperparameter optimization of C and gamma via an inner 10‐times repeated five‐fold cross‐validation to identify the best‐performing model settings. The optimal probability threshold for calculating sensitivity (SN), specificity (SP), positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) was then determined from the repeated cross‐validation using the Youden index, and the final model was subsequently trained on the full dataset using these optimized parameters. In the 10‐times repeated five‐fold cross‐validation, the dataset was divided into five subsets, with four used for training and one for testing in each iteration, and this process was repeated 10 times to ensure robust model evaluation. Performance (AUC, SN, SP, PPV, NPV) across all folds and repetitions was averaged to minimize bias and assess the generalizability of the SVM model. There were five models reported: 1 including and 2 excluding APOE 𝜀4 as a factor, 3 among carriers and 4 non‐carriers, and 5 among those with stable cognition (CS)‐DS. The data were insufficient (low power) to run the model on those with MCI or DSAD. Covariates included age, sex, and APOE 𝜀4 carrier status. Significance was set at p < 0.05.

Models 1 and 2: proteomic profile for detecting amyloid PET (+) with and without inclusion of factor APOE ε4 carriership

Overall, excluding or including APOE ε4 resulted in no difference in the model's performance (Figure 1). Both models reflected variable importance plots (Figure 1) demonstrating that age, GFAP, and p‐tau181 contributed the most to both model performances. When APOE ε4 was included, the model achieved a SN of 86.07%, SP of 94.05%, and AUC of 96.73%; NPV was 90.29% and PPV was 91.30%. Excluding APOE ε4 resulted in a SN of 90.98%, a SP of 92.26%, and an AUC of 96.62%; NPV was 93.37%, and PPV was 89.52%.

Models 3 and 4: proteomic profile for detecting amyloid PET (+) among APOE ε4 carriers and non‐carriers

Reflected in Table 1, there were n = 67 participants that were APOE ε4 carriers of which 28 were amyloid PET (+). Figure 2 demonstrates the ROC curve and variable importance plots for both models, one run in APOE ε4 carriers and the other run among APOE ε4 non‐carriers. In both models, p‐tau181, age, and NfL, respectively, contributed most to each model's performance. Among APOE ε4 carriers, the model achieved a SN of 85.71%, SP of 100%, and AUC of 97.71%; the NPV was 90.70% and the PPV was 100.00%. Among APOE ε4 non‐carriers, the SN was 90.43%, and there was a slight decrease in SP (93.02%) and AUC (96.64%); the NPV was 93.02% and the PPV was 90.43%.

Model 5: proteomic profile for detecting amyloid PET (+) among CS‐DS participants

Among CS‐DS participants (n = 198) (Figure 3) the blood‐based proteomic panel demonstrated strong performance in identifying amyloid PET (+), with an overall accuracy of 90.91%, SN of 91.67%, and SP of 90.48%. GFAP, p‐tau181, and NfL contributed most to the model performances. The PPV was 84.62%, and the NPV was 95.00%, suggesting the test is especially reliable for ruling out amyloid pathology.

Incremental values

Each model was performed by incrementally adding variables, showing how sequentially adding predictors improved model performance (e.g., AUC) and quantified the change in performance (∆AUC) at each step. These tables (Tables S1–S5) highlight each variable's incremental contribution and enhance the interpretability and clinical relevance of the predictive models. Sequentially adding variables to quantify ∆AUC reflects that age, followed by NfL and GFAP, then p‐tau181 contribute most to the performance of the protein panel across the total sample including or excluding APOE. Among APOE ε4 non‐carriers; however, GFAP contributed more strongly to the predictive model followed by p‐tau181 and NfL. Among APOE ε4 carriers, p‐tau181 showed acceptable performance alone (AUC 91.67%), with age, NfL, and GFAP contributing increasing change within the model. Among the CS‐DS population, age alone demonstrated acceptable model performance (AUC 93.09%), followed by NfL and p‐tau181.

DISCUSSION

Using a protein panel analyzed through a 10‐fold cross‐validation machine learning approach, we conducted a cross‐sectional study of blood‐based protein biomarker panel in 290 individuals with DS to classify amyloid PET positivity. In the overall sample, age, GFAP, and p‐tau181 were the strongest contributors to classification. Across the entire sample, the panel achieved an AUC of 96% in both models with and without APOE ε4 included as a factor. Among CS‐DS, GFAP, p‐tau181, and NfL contributed most, suggesting that amyloid positivity in DS is associated with biological signatures of neuroinflammation, astrogliosis, and tau‐related processes, even before clinical symptoms emerge. Previous research showed that the age at which an individual becomes amyloid PET positive was strongly associated with the timing of subsequent cerebral tau positivity in DS. 41 Increments in tau correlate with cognitive symptom onset and can be identified during the first 2.5 to 5 years after Aβ PET positivity in those with DS, 30 , 41 underscoring the utility of our proteomic profile to identify persons eligible for secondary prevention trials aimed at reducing the prevalence of conversion to MCI. Plasma NfL was consistently represented across models and shows reasonable evidence to stage the AT(N) framework in those with DS and may serve as outcome measures in clinical trials for disease‐modifying drugs. 1 , 42 , 43 Plasma Aβ40/Aβ42 have not yet proven to be useful for diagnosing, predicting, and assessing the risk of conversion to DSAD, 44 , 45 consistent with our variable importance findings. The amyloid ratio Aβ42 /Aβ40 is regarded as a better indicator of amyloid positivity than either 40 or 42 isoforms, 46 , 47 which may explain our feature importance plots. However, amyloidosis is expected in DS individuals after 35 years of age, and Aβ42 /Aβ40 declines steeply after 45 to 50 years of age in DS. 48 , 49 Therefore, the addition of Aβ42 /Aβ40 to the model may have limited utility in the detection of early‐stage changes in CS‐DS individuals.

We also assessed how the panel performed among DS individuals who were APOE ε4 carriers and non‐carriers. Among carriers, the protein panel underdetected amyloid positivity. In non‐carriers, the model was more sensitive, identifying more A+ individuals, but with slightly reduced precision. In both groups, p‐tau, age, and NfL emerged as the strongest correlates. Age is the strongest common predictor in DS individuals who are APOE ε4 carriers and non‐carriers, and variable importance plots indicate shared biological mechanisms in both groups. Despite evidence that APOE 𝜀4 influences the clinical expression and biomarker profile of AD in DS, 42 , 50 , 51 our analysis did not support an association between APOE ε4 and PET positivity among those with DS. Instead, other factors such as age, neuroinflammation, and neurodegeneration were more strongly related to PET amyloid positivity. The APOE 𝜀4 group is small and omission of prodromal and more advanced DSAD cases (e.g., older individuals) in our analyses may have contributed to the observed null effect. The influence of APOE ε4 on PET amyloid still warrants further study and clinical trials including individuals with DS may still benefit from stratifying based on APOE genotype to track risk associated with anti‐amyloid medications and associated amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities.

Earlier work from our group and others 6 , 22 , 52 have demonstrated the utility of inflammatory cytokines, along with neurodegeneration markers, to serve as a blood‐based biomarker test for detecting diagnostic states (e.g., MCI and AD) among adults with DS with excellent model performance across SN (>0.90) and SP (>0.85). Notably, the current panel includes GFAP and among CS‐DS individuals, the protein panel was acceptable. Age is a strong predictor across all models, and the incremental values table reflects that biological factors NfL, GFAP, and p‐tau181 add meaningful change to predicting Aβ status. Neuroinflammation precedes overt neurodegeneration and cognitive changes in the AD continuum, 14 and GFAP has growing support in the literature as a key inflammatory marker in DSAD. Multiple processes contribute to the initiation and exacerbation of neuroinflammation in individuals with DS. This is reflected in the elevated expression of various cytokines and chemokines 53 , 54 and elevated microglial activation and dysfunction due to chronic inflammation. 55 , 56 In addition, multiple mechanistic pathways contribute to elevated neurodegeneration in DS, related to inflammatory, oxidative, metabolic, and vascular processes. 55 , 57 Additional inflammatory proteins available within ABC‐DS can further enhance characterization, especially given accumulating evidence linking inflammation to the pathogenesis of DSAD. In this context, the suggested use of the current protein profile among CS‐DS individuals would be beneficial in routine physical examination and laboratory assay for rapid or subacute presentation as well as slow and progressive insidious onsets of AD dementia. Among CS‐DS individuals, age offers the strongest predictive ability to detect amyloid status across all models, suggesting that age alone can help identify individuals eligible for screening. If a positive test is found using the current protein profile, the addition of inflammatory markers would provide further clinical staging utility.

Anti‐amyloid treatments for early symptomatic AD are clinically available in some countries, increasing our need for confirmation of amyloid pathology through blood‐based biomarkers. The Global CEO Initiative on Alzheimer's disease 58 recommended that if blood‐based biomarkers are to be used for triaging, they should meet SN thresholds of ≥90% and SP of ≥85% in primary care and ≥75% to 85% in secondary care, depending on follow‐up testing availability. Blood‐based biomarkers used without confirmatory follow‐up testing should meet the ∼90% SN and SP benchmark of CSF testing. The robustness of models 1 and 4 (Figures 1 and 3) highlights the potential clinical utility of our blood test in early and accurate detection of amyloid positivity. These results suggest that a plasma‐based proteomic profile can confidently rule out those who are not likely to be amyloid PET positive, helping to optimize resource allocation for PET imaging. Our test offers a promising solution enabling early identification for annual monitoring, baseline cognitive assessment, and timely engagement with primary care resources. When implemented with robust quality control measures, this test has the potential to accelerate access, inform treatment decisions, and facilitate clinical trial enrollment in a cost‐effective and scalable manner.

This study is limited by its cross‐sectional design, which prevents assessment of within‐person biomarker trajectories and their predictive validity for clinical progression. Longitudinal data will be essential to determine whether the observed biomarker patterns predict future cognitive decline or AD onset in individuals with DS. While SIMOA provides a high SN for detecting plasma analytes, it may lack the analytical SP of newer platforms. For example, p‐tau217's performance is superior to that of p‐tau181 when it comes to predicting AD pathology in other populations, and its inclusion in future studies could improve model performance and help clarify the relative contributions of different phospho‐tau species. The limited sample size of APOE ε4 carriers also reduces power to detect stratified effects and may contribute to unstable importance rankings in subgroup analyses. Larger, longitudinal cohorts incorporating more comprehensive biomarker panels, including p‐tau217, are needed to validate and expand upon these findings.
