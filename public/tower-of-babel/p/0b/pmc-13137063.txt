From fixed points to probabilistic maps: A spatial framework for acupoint localization
Authors: Da-Eun Yoon, Dha-Hyun Choi, Yeonhee Ryu, Younbyoung Chae
Published: 2026

Acupuncture points are conventionally represented as discrete locations on the body surface. Both classical descriptions and modern atlases typically define each acupoint by a single canonical coordinate based on proportional measurements or anatomical landmarks.1,2 This representation implicitly assumes that acupoints have fixed spatial locations and that deviations from these coordinates primarily reflect practitioner error. However, this assumption may oversimplify how acupoints are localized in clinical practice, where practitioners often search within a small anatomical region through palpation and exploration. This simplification matters because fixed-point representations can obscure clinically meaningful variation in localization, complicate the definition of sham sites, and overstate the precision of anatomical correlations.

In practice, acupoint localization often involves palpation and exploration within a limited anatomical region, where needling sites are identified through local examination. Practitioners often palpate the surrounding tissue and select insertion sites based on local tenderness or tissue characteristics, resulting in small yet consistent variations in needling location.3 Empirical studies have likewise reported substantial dispersion in acupoint localization and only moderate agreement among trained practitioners.4,5 Importantly, such variability should not be interpreted solely as imprecision or error, but may partly reflect the clinically embedded process of palpation-based site selection within a constrained anatomical region. Despite this variability, most research still treats acupoints as fixed spatial coordinates. As a result, variability in acupoint localization is rarely incorporated into experimental design or anatomical interpretation.

This commentary offers an alternative view: acupoints may be more appropriately understood as spatial probability distributions rather than fixed points. This perspective acknowledges observed clinical variability while preserving identifiable spatial centers. We discuss how spatial mapping approaches, particularly kernel density estimation (KDE) may offer a more realistic framework for representing acupoint localization and a quantitative basis for integrating clinical practice with contemporary spatial analysis methods in acupuncture research. Similar spatial representations are widely used in other biomedical fields, suggesting that comparable approaches may help refine how acupoints are represented and investigated.

In conventional representations, an acupoint is modeled as a single coordinate on a body surface. In this context, when multiple practitioners attempt to locate the same acupoint, the resulting needling sites typically form a cluster of nearby locations instead of a single identical point. Such patterns suggest that acupoint localization may be better understood as a spatial distribution rather than exact positional agreement. Spatial statistical methods offer a natural framework for modeling this variability by summarizing dispersed observations as a continuous probability surface. KDE is a widely used non-parametric method for estimating probability distributions from scattered spatial data.6 In KDE, each observed location contributes a kernel function, and the aggregation of these kernels yields a continuous probability surface representing where observations are most likely to occur. KDE has been widely used in spatial analysis and neuroimaging, including in the construction of probabilistic atlases of brain regions whose anatomical boundaries are not sharply defined but are instead represented as probability distributions.7 KDE is presented here as a practical and intuitive method for distribution-based mapping, although other spatial modeling approaches may also prove useful depending on the structure of the data.

In practical terms, an acupoint probability map can be constructed by plotting repeated localization or needling coordinates for a given acupoint on a common body surface, applying KDE to these observed points, and visualizing the resulting surface as a continuous probability map. Areas of higher density indicate locations more consistently selected across observations, whereas broader dispersion indicates greater variability in localization. Within such a map, the maximum-density region may be interpreted as the statistical center of the acupoint, while the surrounding lower-density regions represent its probabilistic spatial extent.

Applied to acupuncture, KDE can transform empirical needling locations into distribution maps of acupoints. Rather than being represented by a single coordinate, an acupoint can be modeled as a spatial distribution characterized by a central high-probability region. The extent and shape of such distributions may differ across acupoints, anatomical regions, and localization contexts. Reframing acupoints as graded spatial regions has several potential implications for acupuncture research and clinical practice. First, such maps may have important implications for education and training. In many curricula, acupoints are presented as precise dots that students are expected to memorize and reproduce. In practice, however, localization typically involves palpation and exploration within a limited anatomical region. Representing acupoints as graded probability maps could provide a more realistic depiction of clinical localization by emphasizing the identification of a high-probability region rather than a single coordinate. Quantitative comparisons between trainee placements and expert-derived distributions may also offer objective metrics for assessing localization skills.9 Second, distribution-based acupoint maps may help address challenges in clinical trial design, particularly the selection of sham points. In many randomized trials, sham sites are defined as locations placed at a fixed distance from canonical acupoint coordinates.10,11 If acupoints are better understood as spatial distributions, nearby sham sites may fall within the low-probability periphery of the target acupoint distribution. Probabilistic maps could therefore support a more principled definition of sham locations by distinguishing anatomically nearby but distributionally distinct sites and identifying regions outside the effective spatial envelope of a target acupoint. Third, this mapping approach may facilitate integration with anatomical and physiological research. Previous studies have suggested associations between acupoints and connective tissue planes or neural structures.12 Rather than evaluating these relationships at a single coordinate, probabilistic maps allow researchers to examine how different parts of an acupoint region overlap with anatomical features or functional responses. Such analyses may provide a more nuanced framework for investigating how variation in needling location relates to anatomical or physiological substrates.

In conclusion, acupoints are commonly represented as fixed locations, yet clinical observations indicate measurable variation in needling location. Conceptualizing acupoints as spatially distributed regions provides a framework that explicitly captures this pattern. Kernel density–based mapping offers a practical method for visualizing and quantifying these spatial distributions. Instead of redefining acupoints in purely abstract terms, this approach offers a way to translate observed localization differences into a spatially explicit and analytically tractable representation. This perspective may help align acupuncture research with contemporary spatial analysis methods while providing a quantitative bridge between traditional acupoint concepts and modern biomedical research. Future studies should test this framework across multiple acupoints and practitioners, compare probabilistic maps with conventional fixed-point representations, and evaluate whether such maps improve localization training, sham-point design, and anatomical interpretation.
