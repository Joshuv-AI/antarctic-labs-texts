Weight gain and brain development in the ABCD study cohort: A decade of insight and the road ahead
Authors: S Adise, N Buchbinder, RS Huber, O Kiss, J Ma, C Makowski, KH Leblanc, O Akande, C Lehrmann, D Yurgelun-Todd, KE Rhee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrently, childhood obesity affects 20% of US youth, and estimates are climbing. This suggests that risk factors contributing to its development and maintenance are poorly understood. As the brain plays an important role in food intake, it has emerged as a potential key driver of excess weight gain. Conversely, weight gain is thought to have detrimental effects on the brain particularly among regions that control food intake, thus, creating a vicious cycle of overeating. This review synthesizes recent findings from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study®, a large national, longitudinal, multimodal dataset. ABCD’s unparalleled longitudinal imaging, dense behavioral phenotyping, and multi-omic data have uniquely enabled researchers to move beyond cross-sectional correlations in small homogeneous samples, and test temporal, bidirectional, associations between neurodevelopment and weight gain. Studies have begun to suggest that structural and functional differences in the brain from ages 9-to-12-years-old relate to body mass index (BMI), weight gain, and cognitive outcomes, cross-sectionally and longitudinally. Here, we discuss potential mechanisms of these associations, including some environmental (e.g., socioeconomic status, resources), lifestyle (e., sleep, physical activity, diet), and heritable (e.g., genetics, prenatal programming) factors that may exacerbate risk and maintenance. While these studies begin to shed light on these associations, we also focus on gaps in knowledge and how the ABCD Study data can be further leveraged to probe into mechanistic pathways and inform prevention and intervention strategies. With this review, we highlight ways in which the scientific community can collaborate to continue to advance our knowledge on this topic.Keywords: Obesity, Neurocognitive development, Social determinants of health, Weight gain, Disordered eating, Neuroimaging

Introduction

Childhood obesity rates continue to climb in the United States, (Hu and Staiano, 2022) highlighting persistent gaps in understanding its driving mechanisms and, thus, limiting the development of effective prevention and intervention strategies. Identifying factors that contribute to the development and maintenance of obesity (e.g., weight gain) is critical given its association with adverse health outcomes, including cardiometabolic disease, cancer, psychiatric conditions, and cognitive decline (Gurnani et al., 2015, Kelsey et al., 2014). Increasing attention has focused on the brain’s role in excess weight gain and maintenance as the brain is the central regulator of food intake (Berthoud et al., 2017). Alterations in the brain’s neural systems, such as those involved in appetite and reward, have been hypothesized to contribute to overeating and subsequent weight gain in adults and youth (Adise et al., 2018, Adise et al., 2024a, Adise et al., 2025a, Berthoud, 2012, Davidson et al., 2019). This is concerning because obesity in adults has also been associated with cognitive decline and increased risk for dementia and Alzheimer’s disease, (Nguyen et al., 2014, Morys et al., 2021) and onset of obesity in childhood has been associated with earlier onset of negative health outcomes. This raises the concern that obesity onset during childhood may place individuals on trajectories towards earlier cognitive decline if left unaddressed. As such, understanding these neurobiological relationships in youth may be pivotal for prevention and treatment programs.

To date, cross-sectional studies using smaller cohorts have had difficulty defining the longitudinal relationship between adiposity, brain development, and cognition. The Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study® is a large, longitudinal cohort study of approximately 12,000 youth in the United States who were recruited at ages 9–10 years and followed over time. The study includes repeated assessments of brain structure and function, cognition, behavior, physical health, and environmental factors. As such, it provides an unparalleled opportunity to advance the field by examining longitudinal associations among adiposity, brain development, and cognition during late childhood and early adolescence. With its large sample size, longitudinal multimodal imaging, dense behavioral phenotyping, and biological/genetic data, the ABCD Study data enables researchers to move beyond small, cross-sectional studies and test bidirectional, temporal associations between neurocognitive development and weight gain.

In addition to addressing limitations related to sample size, the ABCD Study also provides a framework for improving replicability. Its multi-site design, spanning 21 research sites across diverse demographic and geographic populations, enhances the generalizability of findings across varied contexts. Furthermore, the publicly available and standardized nature of the ABCD Study data facilitates independent replication and large-scale validation of results. While replicability remains a broader responsibility of the scientific community, open-access datasets such as ABCD represent an important step toward ensuring that findings can be independently verified and extended. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in particular, enables researchers to quantify structural and functional alterations in neural systems involved in appetite, reward, and executive function, pathways long hypothesized to contribute to excessive weight gain and obesity. Now, with over 11,000 participants and several years of the ABCD Study data available, we are well-positioned to define current knowledge, identify remaining gaps, and leverage the ABCD Study data to inform prevention and treatment strategies for the future.

In this review, we synthesize findings from the ABCD Study on neurobiological, cognitive, genetic, and environmental contributors to obesity risk in youth. We emphasize longitudinal evidence linking brain development to weight-gain trajectories during late childhood and early adolescence, with a focus on bidirectional and developmental processes. We begin with early cross-sectional studies and then examine longitudinal analyses to clarify what has been done, what has been learned, and where the field should go next. We use the term “obesity” to refer to clinical classifications based on body mass index (BMI), “excessive weight gain” to refer to weight trajectories that exceed expected developmental patterns and may carry metabolic risk, and “adiposity” to refer more broadly to body fat and its distribution. Notably, where appropriate, the exact metric (e.g., BMI) will be utilized to reflect research findings. We review evidence spanning brain structure and function, neurocognitive processes, heritability and prenatal influences, and environmental and behavioral factors that may exacerbate or mitigate weight gain and obesity risk. Finally, we highlight key gaps in the literature and outline priorities for future ABCD-based research to inform prevention and intervention strategies. Across sections, we organize this review around a developmental, bidirectional framework in which neurocognitive differences may increase vulnerability to excess weight gain, while weight gain itself may further alter brain systems involved in appetite, reward, and executive function.

Is weight related to variation in brain-related measures?

Although various small-scale studies have shown associations between greater BMI and variation in the brain’s structure (Alosco et al., 2014, Mestre et al., 2017, Perlaki et al., 2018) and function, (Batterink et al., 2010, Black et al., 2014, Bruce et al., 2010) most findings have not been consistently replicable. The ABCD Study has begun to address these limitations by providing a large, well-powered dataset. One seminal study using the ABCD Study dataset identified robust and reproducible associations between variation in brain structure (e.g., cortical thickness, surface area, and subcortical volume) and BMI at 9-to-10-years-old (Adise et al., 2021). Similarly, these differences are thought to relate to variation in how the brain functions (Laurent et al., 2019, Ronan et al., 2019, Morys et al., 2023). Importantly, beyond these baseline associations, longitudinal analyses from the ABCD Study have begun to clarify whether these differences precede weight gain, result from weight gain, or reflect bidirectional processes. Together, these findings suggest that BMI-related variation in brain structure may reflect underlying biological processes that shape appetite, reward, and executive function systems across development.

It has been postulated that the relationship between the brain and higher BMI may be fueled by neuroinflammation, (Miller and Spencer, 2014) a process in which prolonged activation leads to demyelination, axonal degradation, and ultimately, cell death. Neuroinflammation can be triggered by various factors, such as injury, infection, and more importantly, diets high in fat/sugar and excess weight gain (as reviewed in (Miller and Spencer, 2014); Yang and Zhou, 2019; Guillemot-Legris and Muccioli, 2017). Animal models have indeed observed a relationship between markers of neuroinflammation and overeating a high fat/sugar diet even prior to weight gain (Hsu et al., 2015, Tsan et al., 2021). Yet, studies in humans have been more limited due to methodological limitations. However, new neuroimaging techniques have begun to surface, allowing insight into indirect assessments between proxies of markers of neuroinflammation and weight gain.

One such technique that may help us capture associations of neuroinflammation comes from the signal intensity of T2 images; greater intensity has been thought to be a marker of putative gliosis, (Schur et al., 2015) a signal of increased glial activity due to potential neuroinflammation. Largely, this technique has been used to examine gliosis in the hypothalamus, a key regulator of food intake. In a small sample of youth in ABCD (n = 407), greater putative gliosis in the hypothalamus was elevated in youth with obesity at 9-to-10-years-old, and this correlated with changes in adiposity one year later (Sewaybricker et al., 2021). This finding suggests that altered activity in the hypothalamus may disrupt feeding/eating circuits that could induce overeating and subsequent weight gain. Future research is needed to further explore this hypothesis.

A more popular technique to garner estimates of brain health is diffusion-weighted imaging. Multi-shell diffusion imaging acquisitions, such as Restriction Spectrum Imaging (RSI), can be applied to obtain estimates of brain microstructure. RSI derives metrics of diffusion within small cell bodies and cellularity (e.g., number of glial/neuronal cell bodies) inside a region (White et al., 2013). As such, studies utilizing the ABCD Study data with this method (RSI) have found that at 9-to-10-years-old, altered tissue microstructure in the nucleus accumbens, possibly due to neuroinflammation, was associated with greater BMI regardless of age, and weight gain over a two-year period (i.e., 9-to-12-years-old) (Rapuano et al., 2020). It is further thought that altered structural differences may predispose youth to the vicious cycle of overeating, specifically high-fat foods (Rapuano et al., 2022). Others have found that these associations extend beyond the accumbens to the basal ganglia, specifically among those who already have overweight/obesity at 9-to-10-years-old (Li et al., 2023). Of note, one study showed that at this age, aerobic physical activity may reduce these associations, (Ma et al., 2023) highlighting a potential intervention strategy.

Importantly, several studies using the ABCD Study data have begun to show that higher BMI was associated with continued changes in the brain over a two-year period (i.e., 9-to-12-years-old) (Adise et al., 2025a, Rapuano et al., 2022, Jiang et al., 2023, Adise et al., 2023a). Together, these highlight that sustained weight gain has been associated with changes in the brain during adolescence, which may then further exacerbate the risk of increasing weight gain or maintenance of obesity. These findings are consistent with the idea that adolescence is a critical period of growth and development that is subject to heightened vulnerability and increased risk for disease outcomes. Moreover, weight gain across these two-years has been associated with not only changes to the brain via micro and macrostructure, (Adise et al., 2024a, Adise et al., 2025a, Rapuano et al., 2022, Jiang et al., 2023) but may relate to greater intake of high fat/high sugar foods by 11-to-12-years-old because of differences in neurocognitive processes (i.e., greater baseline impulsivity and reward seeking behavior) (Adise et al., 2024b). Thus, this is beginning to raise the possibility that weight gain may contribute to changes in the brain, that in turn, could relate to differences in diet regulation by altering neural systems involved in decision-making and appetite control.

While neuroimaging studies provide some insight into potential brain differences and greater BMI, it is presumed that variation in the brain is related to differences in functional outcomes, such as cognitive processes and executive functioning. To fully understand the developmental dynamics of obesity, it is essential to examine how the brain and neurocognition influence behavior over time, and vice versa. To that end, several studies with the ABCD Study dataset have begun to study this relationship. For example, at age 11-to-12-years-old, a small sample of youth with overweight or obesity performed worse on a working memory task while also having decreased activation in corresponding brain regions typically linked to working memory (Zhang et al., 2023). Working memory is thought to be integral for diet maintenance. For example, this executive function may be important to remind individuals what they have just eaten and that they may not be hungry or need more. Thus, deficits in working memory may contribute to overeating. Others have argued that reward processing may also play a role in overeating and weight gain (as reviewed in (Hofmann et al., 2009, Hofmann et al., 2012, Kotabe and Hofmann, 2015, Stice and Burger, 2012, Kroemer and Small, 2016). To support this theory, data from the ABCD Study has shown that greater responsiveness to rewards was associated with greater BMI and stronger self-reported overeating on the Child Behavior Checklist at 9-to-10-years-old (Kulisch et al., 2023). These findings provide a potential behavioral pathway through which variation in brain systems supporting reward sensitivity and executive function may contribute to overeating and subsequent weight gain.

Recent studies have tried to address the fundamental ‘chicken or the egg’ question by exploring bidirectional relationships between executive function and adiposity. Across all youth of varying weight status, (Sakib et al., 2023) showed support for both the “brain-as-a-risk-factor” and “brain-as-an-outcome” models of BMI-brain relationships from 9-to-12-years-old. In particular, measures of inhibitory control, like performance on the Flanker Task, had bidirectional associations. However, these analyses included youth with obesity in the model suggesting that these associations may be biased towards differences that have already emerged. To address this gap, some studies have begun to examine these associations among youth who did not have overweight or obesity at the start of the ABCD Study (i.e., ages 9-to-10-years-old) (Adise et al., 2024a, Adise et al., 2025a, Adise et al., 2025b). These studies have shown that from the age of 9-to-12-years-old, alterations in the brain’s subcortical volume and microstructure (Adise et al., 2024a, Adise et al., 2025a, Adise et al., 2025b) as well as function (Adise et al., 2023b) among key appetite-regulating and decision-making regions of the brain have been observed prior to weight gain. These findings suggest that structural and behavioral alterations may precede weight gain.

Notably, some findings vary by sex, with stronger associations between the brain and weight gain emerging in females (Adise et al., 2024a). These sex differences may be due to differences in pubertal timing, and as such, suggest structural brain-weight associations may emerge with the onset of puberty (i.e., biological maturation). During this time, females undergo puberty at an earlier age, (Graber et al., 2010, Loomba-Albrecht and Styne, 2009) which also may accelerate neurocognitive development (Chaku and Hoyt, 2019, Beck et al., 2023, Blakemore et al., 2010, Goddings et al., 2014). Thus, the effect of hormones on brain structure and cognitive functions related to excess weight gain should be considered in all analyses. Yet, irrespective of pubertal onset, sex differences for weight gain and overeating may persist. For example, among males who were of a healthy weight at 9-to−10-years-old, those who scored higher on impulsivity (e.g., positive urgency, lack of planning, lack of perseverance, drive, and fun seeking) were more likely to have higher intake of fat and sugar by ages 11-to−12-years-old. This behavior coincided with two-years of weight gain, but not in females (Adise et al., 2024b). It is unclear why this relationship was observed in males but not females. However, across both sexes, those with heightened negative urgency (the tendency to act impulsively when upset), exhibited greater intake of high fat and sugar foods.

While pubertal timing (i.e., biological maturation) may play an important role in defining when these associations emerge (i.e., prior to or after excessive weight gain), neuroimaging techniques that allow us to observe finer-grained changes that may occur (e.g., changes in intracellular diffusion, increased glial cell activation) are needed. One study (Adise et al., 2025a) provided some evidence for this utilizing Restrictive Normalized Isotropic (RNI), a metric derived from RSI. RNI measures the proportion of intracellular unidirectional diffusion (e.g., neuronal cell bodies, glial cells), that largely captures diffusion within small cell bodies (White et al., 2013). In this study, there were subtle associations between this metric and BMI in both males and females from ages 9-to-12-years-old (Adise et al., 2025a). Notably, this study showed that associations may also be bidirectional across sexes, highlighting that this sensitive metric may pick up subtle differences in brain microstructure prior to pubertal onset. However, similar to the structural findings, (Adise et al., 2024a) these associations were more pronounced in females. All together, these studies are beginning to suggest that greater BMI may be associated with markers consistent with neuroinflammation. However, more work is needed to disentangle the timing, mechanisms, and developmental implications of these associations. It is important to note that emerging evidence provides a first glimpse that underlying brain differences may predispose certain youth to weight gain. Together, these findings are consistent with a bidirectional developmental model in which early differences in brain structure and function may confer risk for weight gain, while subsequent weight gain may further shape neurocognitive trajectories across adolescence.

Heritability and prenatal programming of obesity risk

A key remaining question is: what are the factors contributing to brain changes that are associated with increased risk of obesity? Several studies have highlighted that how much a child weighs is highly heritable (Silventoinen et al., 2007, Schrempft et al., 2018). In the ABCD Study, age- and sex- adjusted BMI percentile was found to be 89.5% heritable in a sibling sample of children aged 9-to-10-years-old, with the remaining variance attributable to unique environmental factors (Kulisch et al., 2023). This is consistent with, though higher than, the findings from the CODATwins study, a large longitudinal study across infancy and adolescence of nearly 88,000 twin pairs which found a peak in heritability values of ∼0.85 by age 10 in boys and by age 16 in girls (Silventoinen et al., 2016). While the heritability of weight is quite high, specific mechanisms remain underexplored. Kulisch and colleagues (Kulisch et al., 2023) used behavioral genetic modeling and found potential causal effects of obesity on eating behavior, cognition, cortical thickness, and cortical surface area. Additionally, they found some evidence for reciprocal relationships between, psychopathology and BMI and eating behavior, thus, partly supporting a “vicious cycle” or bidirectionality between eating behavior and BMI. Another study, (Morys et al., 2023) which calculated a polygenic score for BMI (BMI-PGS) for each ABCD Study participant, demonstrated that BMI-PGS was significantly associated with BMI z-score and that having more Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with BMI had an additive predictive effect on BMI z-score at baseline. They similarly found that BMI-PGS was associated with lower cortical volume and thickness in frontal and temporal areas, relative to age-expected values. Interestingly, these associations with lower cortical thickness in fronto-temporal regions seemed to persist across the lifespan, but other BMI-associated cortical thickness differences might be specific to early adolescence, as studies in older populations find different associations (Morys et al., 2024).

In addition to these genetic studies, other indicators of the heritability of BMI have been demonstrated. ABCD studies now have shown that prenatal gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is associated with reduced cortical gray matter volume. Of note, siblings who were discordant for GDM exposure showed differences in cortical gray matter volume: the GDM-exposed sibling had reduced cortical gray matter volume compared to their non-exposed sibling (Luo et al., 2023). Prenatal GDM exposure was also associated with higher adiposity measures (e.g., waist-to-hip, waist-to-height) at 9-to-10-years-old, and gray matter volume was found to partially mediate this association. Prepregnancy maternal BMI was not initially collected in the ABCD Study, so it is unknown whether maternal obesity would have similar or separate effects from those of maternal GDM. However, future releases of the ABCD Study may be able to address this as current maternal BMI was collected starting at year 6. Together these studies highlight how prenatal exposures and inherited genetic risk, both affecting brain and neurodevelopment, may program weight gain trajectories during adolescence.

Factors that may exacerbate risk and maintenance

Obesity is a multifactorial disease that is also influenced by environmental or other sociodemographic factors, as evidenced by the fact that some groups have disproportionately higher obesity rates, (Fradkin et al., 2015, Ogden et al., 2018, Ogden et al., 2020, Isong et al., 2018) possibly due to differences in environmental factors (Acevedo-Garcia et al., 2020). The advantages of the analyses conducted with ABCD Study data have included its large sample size, diverse sample population from many regions of the country, and vast number of variables that assess genetic, cognitive, behavioral, psychological, and environmental factors in conjunction with each other. This rich dataset allows insight into how this vast array of factors may exacerbate obesity risk and work to influence each other. One such study exemplifies this benefit as it included 43 demographic, cognitive, psychological, and behavioral variables associated with obesity risk to determine which factors had the greatest association with BMI. Gray et al., (2020) (Gray et al., 2020) used machine learning regression analyses and found that stimulant usage (e.g., not taking ADHD medications), Hispanic ethnicity, non-White race, males, lower income, and having an unmarried primary caregiver, in this order, had the strongest associations with BMI. Other cognitive variables (such as abstract reasoning) and environmental variables (such as screen time use and sleep initiation) had lower, albeit still significant, associations with BMI.

Many of the significant demographic factors highlighted in Gray et al., (2020) (Gray et al., 2020) have been tightly correlated with other social factors that may be contributing to one’s risk of obesity, such as poor access to healthy foods and green spaces, quality health care and education, and robust employment and housing opportunities (Capoccia et al., 2025). Fortunately, these types of measures are also available in ABCD Study dataset, such as neighborhood or community environment assessments (e.g., Area Deprivation Index [ADI], (Singh, 2003) Childhood Opportunity Index [COI] (Acevedo-Garcia et al., 2020, Acevedo-Garcia et al., 2014) and socioeconomic status (e.g., household income, parental education), and have also demonstrated an association with greater BMI and weight gain from 9-to−12-years-old (Adise et al., 2024a, Li et al., 2023, Adise et al., 2022a, Adise et al., 2022b, Dennis et al., 2022, Ertel et al., 2024). For example, children who experienced poverty exhibited both lower cognitive performance, and were more likely to have overweight or obesity, (Tomasi and Volkow, 2021) and these associations showed steeper negative brain-BMI associations among cortical thickness and volume.

Moreover, several environmental factors may alleviate or moderate the relationships between BMI and the brain. For example, at 9-to-10-years-old, BMI mediated the relationship between ADI and total cortical volume, working memory and cognitive flexibility (Dennis et al., 2022). This result suggests that resources in the environment may first pose a risk for elevated BMI, which in turn may have an effect on the brain’s structure and function. Similar findings were also found when examining relationships between lower SES (measured by lower household income, higher neighborhood disadvantage, and lower parent educational attainment) and white matter microstructure (i.e., lower RSI-Restrictive Normalized Directional [RND] and greater RSI-RNI (Li et al., 2023)). These studies highlight the complex, inter-related relationship between social-environmental factors and its impact on weight and cognitive/brain health.

To better understand how different social factors impact weight status and brain development, Adise, et al., (2022) (Adise et al., 2022b) specifically examined three domains assessed by the COI: education (quality of schools and resources supporting academic achievement), health/environment (access to healthy foods, green spaces, and exposure to environmental toxins), and social/economic (access to employment and social/economic resources). They found that the health/environment domain moderated the relationship between BMI and many regions of the brain, more so than the education or social/economic domain (although these two domains contributed to greater weight gain trajectories over a two-year period). These findings held true independent of the impact of family income, suggesting that access to healthy environments and food/green space may be an area of focus for future public health interventions. Furthermore, measures of racial discrimination at 10-to-11-years-old have been associated with greater BMI z-score and waist circumference at 11-to-12-years-old, even after controlling for sex, household income, and parent education (Cuevas et al., 2023). Persistent discrimination is another stressor that has been linked to physiologic responses in the cardiovascular, neuroendocrine, and inflammatory pathways that result in blunted diurnal cortisol responses (as reviewed in (Brodish et al., 2011)) and may be contributing to poor BMI outcomes. Understanding the mechanism by which we can decrease the impact of personally mediated or systemic racism may help to address the obesity epidemic.

The importance of exercise and sleep on obesity risk and maintenance

The ABCD Study collected a wide range of data on activity levels, including both self-report measures and more objective assessments using the Fitbit device. Utilizing these data, several studies have confirmed that physical inactivity, indicated by low daily step counts, was associated with higher BMI and overweight or obesity risk (Kiss et al., 2024, Nagata et al., 2023a). Additionally, excessive screen time was associated with higher BMI, (Nagata et al., 2021) while replacing 30 min of screen time with physical activity was associated with healthier BMI outcomes (Zink et al., 2024). Although the links between physical inactivity and BMI are not new evidence, per se, it is important to note that these associations have been demonstrated in a large and diverse sample as well.

Importantly, data from the ABCD Study identified physical activity as a significant mediator of the bidirectional relationships between obesity and cognitive function, Sakib et al. (2023) highlighting that physical exercise and activity programs could be effective intervention strategies for mitigating obesity and its associated poor cognitive outcomes. In Sakib et al. (2023) moderate-to-vigorous physical activity mediated the association between baseline adiposity measures (e.g., waist circumference, BMI) at age 9-to-10-years-old and neurocognitive task performance at the 2-year follow-up. Physical activity also mediated the association between baseline cognitive function and adiposity measures (e.g., waist circumference, BMI) at the 2-year follow-up (Sakib et al., 2023). Though neuroimaging evidence explaining the brain mechanisms underlying such relationships has yet to be fully elucidated, physical activity, particularly aerobic-based exercise, is known to have beneficial effects on cerebrovascular function, and neurotrophic factor levels (Jia et al., 2022, Guadagni et al., 2020). Supporting this hypothesis, a cross-sectional analysis of the ABCD Study data revealed that high levels of aerobic physical activity at 9-to-10-years-old were associated with reduced hippocampal white matter microstructural alterations that were found in females with overweight or obesity (Ma et al., 2023).

Poor and insufficient sleep and obesity are also fundamentally linked (as reviewed in (Shochat et al., 2014); Norton et al., 2018). Each 1 h decrease in duration may increase the odds of obesity by 80%, (Gupta et al., 2002) and odds of shorter sleep is 1.16 times higher among individuals with obesity than their lean counterparts (assessed cross-sectionally when the cohort was 9-to-10-years-old) (Brooks et al., 2022). Sleep quality and timing are also related to obesity risk in youth, (Hart et al., 2011) with objective wearable data from the ABCD Study showing that in 11-to-12-years-old youth, late bedtimes, short and irregular sleep, and greater social jetlag, defined as the mismatch between endogenous circadian rhythms and socially determined schedules, are strong predictors of obesity (compared to healthy weight) in adolescents, alongside low step counts and elevated resting heart rate (Kiss et al., 2024). Importantly, two cross-sectional studies at 9-to-10-years-old in the ABCD Study dataset have shown that sleep disturbances partially mediated the relationship between BMI and cortical thinning and smaller cortical volume, (Cui et al., 2023) and the relationship between BMI and cognitive dysfunction (Mattey-Mora and Nelson, 2021).

Mechanisms for the relationship between sleep and weight gain are unclear, but could include effects of sleep restriction on energy intake (Dashti et al., 2015) or appetite-controlling hormones (leptin, ghrelin) (Hagen et al., 2015). Sleep affects an individual’s self-regulatory capacity (Hofmann et al., 2012) (as reviewed in (Palmer and Alfano, 2017)) and may alter brain circuits involved in obesity, (Jensen et al., 2022) especially reward circuits, (Nechifor et al., 2020) and inhibitory control-executive function networks (Telzer et al., 2013). Notably, having excess weight can cause or worsen sleep problems like obstructive sleep apnea by narrowing the airway, leading to insomnia, daytime fatigue, and disturbed sleep (Pennings et al., 2022). This could create a vicious cycle where poor sleep begets further weight gain, which then contributes to poor sleep quality. Further longitudinal studies are required to provide deeper insights into the intertwined relationships between sleep disturbances and obesity in adolescents. The ABCD Study dataset could provide a valuable opportunity to address such relationships and to further explore associated brain and cognitive function.

Disordered eating behaviors and risk for weight gain and obesity

Disordered eating, including binge eating behaviors early in development, may index later risk for unhealthy weight gain and/or obesity (Kessler et al., 2013). Within this realm, the ABCD Study captures subclinical-level eating disorders via self-report data. Emerging findings suggest neural signatures in reward-control circuits and executive function regions may predict early binge-eating behaviors (Shi et al., 2025, Steward et al., 2024, Martin et al., 2024, Murray et al., 2023a, Smith et al., 2025). For instance, individuals with binge eating disorder (BED) are 3–6 times more likely to have obesity compared to those without, and BED is also associated with an earlier onset of having/being overweight (Kessler et al., 2013, Hudson et al., 2007, Spitzer et al., 1993). Concurrently, the ABCD Study data indicate that screen time and problematic social media use at 9-to−10-years-old substantially predict eating disorder symptoms, including binge eating (Nagata et al., 2021, Shi et al., 2025, Al-Shoaibi et al., 2024). Other studies have investigated neurobiological underpinnings for binge eating, such as altered brain structure (Shi et al., 2025) and function (Steward et al., 2024, Martin et al., 2024, Murray et al., 2023a, Smith et al., 2025) in regions contributing to reward-control circuitry. There is also some evidence of sex-specific differences underlying binge eating-related alterations in brain structure-function of these circuits (Martin et al., 2024, Murray et al., 2023b, Murray et al., 2023c).

The large sample size of the ABCD Study also affords the opportunity to tease apart cognitive and neural mechanisms related to binge eating vs. weight status. For instance, some of the above-mentioned findings related to brain connectivity within the reward network were found to be specifically associated with binge eating, regardless of the individual’s weight, (Steward et al., 2024) emphasizing unique neurocognitive factors not solely due to anthropometric measures that could help identify children at risk for problematic weight gain in the future. Additionally, other studies have found brain correlates at 9-to−10-years-old that were associated with extreme weight gain likely secondary to binge eating disorder), one and two-years later, (Adise et al., 2021, Adise et al., 2023a suggesting that underlying circuitry in the brain has been associated with subclinical eating disorder risk.

Integration of genetic data to better understand premorbid biological risk factors for disordered eating behaviors and problematic weight gain represents a valuable avenue that the ABCD Study is well-positioned to address. In this vein, one paper using ABCD Study data at 9-to−10-years-old provided evidence for associations between BMI PGS and cortical morphology within regions comprising the ventral attention and frontoparietal networks even after controlling for participants’ BMI z-scores (Westwater et al., 2023). On the other hand, another study found positive associations between BMI PGS with a latent “eating” variable which included binge eating behaviors (Steward et al., 2024). Emerging genome-wide association studies (GWAS) on binge eating behaviors have demonstrated shared genetic architecture between binge eating and both metabolic and neuropsychiatric traits (Termorshuizen et al., 2025), highlighting key genes that have been previously linked to obesity and appetitive behavior such as FTO (Wardle et al., 2008; Wardle and Carnell, 2009). One of these GWAS found, independent of BMI, BED-associated SNPs near the HFE, MCHR2, and LRP11 genes in European ancestry, and replicated MCHR2 in the ABCD Study cohort (Burstein et al., 2023). MCHR2 encodes a G-protein-coupled receptor for melanin-concentrating hormone, which plays an important role in energy and glucose metabolism (Al-Massadi et al., 2021) and regulates impulsive eating (Noble et al., 2019). Future studies are encouraged to use the latest GWAS of binge eating behaviors from Termorshuizen and colleagues (Termorshuizen et al., 2025) to generate new PGS in the ABCD Study, especially as current releases include data on participants past the typical age of onset of BED (∼13 years old), (Swanson et al., 2011, Spurrell et al., 1997) and thus more participants are expected to meet criteria for BED compared to older studies that relied on the first few waves of ABCD Study data (Swanson et al., 2011, Spurrell et al., 1997). By integrating insights across external influences (e.g., social media exposure, lifestyle socioeconomic conditions) and both neural and genetic mechanisms, this body of work supports the identification of youth at risk, well before clinical onset, enabling earlier interventions and tailored prevention strategies.

Call to science - An actionable agenda for researchers and clinicians aiming to leverage ABCD data for obesity prevention

While these foundational studies have begun to reveal the associations with obesity, much progress is still needed to fully understand the underlying mechanisms and factors that sustain obesity over time. Future research leveraging ABCD Study data is well-positioned to explicitly test this bidirectional developmental model and to identify when, how, and for whom brain–behavior relationships most strongly influence obesity risk. This work is especially critical as childhood obesity rates continue to rise; (Hu and Staiano, 2022) a rise also exacerbated by the COVID19 lockdowns, especially among those from lower income families (Betts et al., 2023). In turn, obesity during the pandemic was also associated with higher blood pressure, Nagata et al. (2023)b demonstrating that short periods of rapid weight gain can have immediate and potentially serious health consequences. Understanding the consequences of rapid weight gain on the body and neurodevelopment is an underexplored area of research. Nonetheless, these findings highlight the importance of identifying not only the origins but also the maintenance of obesity, which is necessary in order to inform timely and effective interventions.

Many of the obesity findings from the ABCD Study dataset suggest markers consistent with neuroinflammation may be a key mechanism; however, these conclusions are primarily based on indirect proxies, such as imaging markers of gliosis on T2-weighted imaging and RSI. The specific mechanisms linking obesity and brain inflammation remain unclear. Future research should aim to identify direct biomarkers of inflammation and clarify the developmental timing and cognitive implications of these associations. Incorporating additional measures of inflammation such as inflammatory cytokines and additional MRI methods like magnetic resonance spectroscopy may provide deeper insight into the neuroinflammatory processes underlying obesity.

Additionally, studies using the ABCD Study data have illuminated the robust associations that brain anatomy and greater weight gain have on subsequent increases in body weight. The baseline data (9-to−10-years-old) and the longitudinal results (until age 12) elucidate that too much weight gain may stem from neurobiological variation in appetite control that may facilitate overeating, which may further contribute to a vicious cycle of continued weight gain (Adise et al., 2024a, Adise et al., 2025a, Adise et al., 2021, Rapuano et al., 2020, Rapuano et al., 2022, Adise et al., 2023a, Adise et al., 2025b, Adise et al., 2023b). The use of longitudinal data from baseline and year 2 follow up, rather than merely cross-sectional analysis, allows scientists to examine how specific structural differences and diffusion metrics lead to later weight gain. As researchers achieve further clarity regarding which brain structural and functional differences are most prominent in predating weight gain, scientists may improve early identification of youth at elevated risk of obesity. Executive function interventions have shown some promise but have been short-lived (Hayes et al., 2018). This is perhaps because it has been unknown as to which neurocognitive outcomes to target at specific developmental ages, which the ABCD Study is well-positioned to elucidate. Currently, while it is unclear the best predictor of weight gain is (e.g., brain structure or function), neurocognitive measures may pave the way for early identification methods and allow for excessive weight gain to be predicted before it is experienced.

Further research is also needed to understand the influence of puberty and sex-specific mechanisms in shaping these brain–obesity relationships. Early findings suggest that associations are stronger in females, likely due to earlier pubertal onset, yet few studies have integrated pubertal status or hormonal biomarkers. Future work should prioritize biological measures of puberty to assess how hormonal changes interact with brain development and weight gain. While early research has shown a heritable relationship with obesity, the prenatal origins of obesity risk remain another critical gap. Future research is needed to focus on the roles of maternal BMI and gestational risk on the heritability of obesity. Prenatal exposure to gestational diabetes has been linked to altered brain structure and higher adiposity; however, maternal BMI was not collected in the early waves of the study. The Healthy Brain and Child Development (HBCD) Study, a longitudinal study modeled after ABCD, follows pregnant mothers and their children through early childhood and may help overcome this limitation by capturing more detailed perinatal and maternal health data.

Modifiable lifestyle factors such as physical activity, sleep, and screen time have shown clear associations with obesity; yet the underlying brain-based mechanisms remain poorly understood. Although ABCD collected diet and nutrition data, few studies have examined these factors (Adise et al., 2024b, Akhmadjonova and Shearrer, 2025). A recent ABCD Study analysis has begun to address this gap by testing whether brain-based measures and known risk factors predict sugar-sweetened beverage intake in adolescents, highlighting the value of integrating dietary outcomes into neurodevelopmental models of obesity risk (Akhmadjonova and Shearrer, 2025). Future studies should examine access to healthy food, green spaces, and other environmental factors that influence the relationship between brain development and obesity. These represent critical areas for future investigation, particularly for developing environmentally informed prevention and intervention strategies. Nonetheless, these findings highlight the importance of identifying not only the origins but also the maintenance of obesity, which is necessary in order to inform timely and effective interventions. Limitations of the existing literature

Importantly, many of the studies reviewed utilized BMI (or derivatives [e.g., BMI z-score, %ile]) as either a predictor or outcome measure. Although BMI is a convenient proxy for adiposity, it does not accurately reflect body composition, which may be more relevant for understanding metabolic risk. Additional limitations arise from the use of BMI z-scores and percentiles. These metrics are often preferred in pediatric populations because they account for age- and sex-specific growth patterns. However, they were originally designed for cross-sectional clinical reference (e.g., to identify a child’s relative position within a population distribution), rather than for tracking within-person change over time. As a result, changes in BMI z-scores or percentiles do not necessarily reflect true change in adiposity within individuals and may misrepresent longitudinal weight trajectories, particularly at the extremes of the distribution.

While raw BMI can be used to assess within-person change when only anthropometric data are available, it does not account for normative developmental changes in growth during childhood and adolescence. Additionally, all BMI-based measures are subject to bias and may misclassify adiposity across racial and ethnic groups (Adise et al., 2024c, Flegal and HISTORY, n.d). A thorough discussion of the pros and cons of using BMI versus z-scores or percentiles when only anthropometric data are available is provided in Adise et al. (2024c) Despite these limitations, many studies included in this review relied on BMI z-scores and percentiles in longitudinal analyses, which may introduce additional bias and lead to over- or underestimation of associations with weight gain. Although the ABCD Study is currently constrained by these measures, future research should prioritize more precise assessments of adiposity that better capture body composition (e.g., whole body MRI, bioelectrical impedance, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA)), including measures of longitudinal within-person changes that are less susceptible to the biases and statistical limitations inherent in BMI-based measures and cardiometabolic risk. Beyond measurement limitations, relatively few studies have examined the neurochemical and functional/cognitive mechanisms that explain why differences in brain structure and microstructure are associated with obesity risk. An important avenue for future research is to clarify the directionality of these associations: for example, whether differences in executive function contribute to structural brain variation during adolescence, especially amid synaptic pruning and myelination, or whether structural variation in the brain drives behavioral outcomes. Addressing this question will be critical to understanding how neurocognitive development interacts with obesity risk.

Although the ABCD Study provides rich, multimodal data, certain measures remain underutilized or limited in scope. Objective physical activity and sleep data derived from wearable devices are only available for a subset of participants and time points. Similarly, while dietary and nutrition data are collected, relatively few studies have incorporated these measures, representing a missed opportunity to better understand how lifestyle behaviors interact with brain development and obesity risk. Finally, although longitudinal analyses from the ABCD Study provide important insight into the temporal ordering of brain and weight-related processes, these findings remain observational and do not establish causality. Unmeasured confounding and pre-existing differences between individuals may contribute to observed associations. Future studies leveraging genetically informed approaches (e.g., polygenic risk scores), quasi-experimental designs, and within-person analytic frameworks will be important for strengthening causal inference.

Conclusion

While much work remains, findings from the ABCD Study suggest that obesity is linked to widespread alterations in the brain, particularly within executive function and reward pathways that are critical for regulating food intake. Emerging longitudinal evidence indicates that these associations persist and evolve across development, raising important questions about how sustained weight gain shapes neurocognitive trajectories. The data also point toward a reciprocal relationship between brain and behavior, creating a potential feedback loop that reinforces obesity risk. At the same time, biological maturation (e.g., pubertal timing) may alter when and how these effects unfold, highlighting the need to identify critical and sensitive windows for sex-specific vulnerabilities. Contextual environments, ranging from socioeconomic status to family and neighborhood factors, further shape both obesity risk and brain development, underscoring the importance of integrating social context into mechanistic models (see Fig. 1 conceptual model). Finally, preliminary evidence that lifestyle behaviors such as sleep, diet, and physical activity can buffer the brain–obesity associations suggest promising targets for intervention. Together, the ABCD Study dataset provides an unprecedented opportunity to define when, how, and for whom obesity exerts its strongest neurodevelopmental effects, paving the way for more precise prevention and interventions.
