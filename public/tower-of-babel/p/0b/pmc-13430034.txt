Association of hypoalbuminemia with disease and survival in 1664 cats presenting to a tertiary teaching hospital: a retrospective case-control study
Authors: Conner C Hayes, Xiaojuan Zhu, Paolo Pazzi, Sarah M Schmid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypoalbuminemia (HA) is associated with a higher case fatality rate in people.Hypothesis/ObjectivesTo describe the affected organ systems and pathoetiologies in cats with mild (2.5-2.9 g/dL), moderate (2-2.4 g/dL), or severe (< 2 g/dL) HA, and compare hospitalization and survival times to cats without HA (wHA; ≥ 3 g/dL).AnimalsEight hundred thirty-two HA and 832 wHA cats.MethodsRetrospective case-control study of cats with plasma albumin concentrations measured between 2017 and 2023. Diagnoses were categorized by organ system and pathoetiology and subsequently compared among HA and wHA groups. Hospitalization and survival times were compared.ResultsHA prevalence was 32% (948/2927); 116 cats were excluded. Cats with HA were more often diagnosed with infectious (211/832, 25%) or neoplastic (198/832, 24%) pathoetiologies, and diseases affecting the hematopoietic (140/832, 17%) or multisystemic (71/832, 9%) organ systems compared to cats wHA (P < .001 for all). Cats with severe HA were more likely to have infectious diseases than all other severities (P < .001). Cats with HA had longer hospitalizations and shorter survival than wHA cats (P < .001). Cats with severe HA had significantly shorter restricted mean survival times from admission to 6 months (71 days, 95% CI 51-92 days) than wHA (144 days, 95% CI 138-149 days, P < .001), mild HA (119 days, 95% CI 111-127, P < .001), and moderate HA (108 days, 95% CI 95-122 days, P = .02) cats.Conclusions and clinical importanceIn cats with severe HA, investigation of infectious diseases should be prioritized.

Introduction

Hypoalbuminemia (HA) occurs in 21% to 53% of ill people and is associated with increased morbidity and higher case fatality rates in critically ill people, dogs, and cats.1–10 The complications of HA are numerous and include impaired wound healing and surgical site dehiscence, gastrointestinal dysfunction, altered drug pharmacokinetics, and reduced oncotic pressure resulting in intravascular fluid loss, interstitial edema, ascites, and hypovolemia.11–14 In dogs, HA is a negative prognostic indicator for heat stroke, chronic enteropathies, and recovery from surgery.7,11,15–17 Cats with moderate to severe HA have longer hospital stays, higher treatment costs, and higher case fatality rates compared to cats with mild HA.1

Several conditions can lead to HA, including increased catabolism, hepatic dysfunction, inflammation, loss of renal glomerular selectivity, gastrointestinal (GI) disease, and malnutrition.18 Few studies have examined HA in cats and its disease associations. One study found GI disease to be the most common, with nearly half of the affected cats having GI neoplasia.1 However, this study lacked a comparison group of cats with normal albumin concentrations. Given that signs of GI disease are a common presenting complaint among cats, this might reflect general disease prevalence rather than a specific link to HA.19 HA in cats is most frequently reported in infectious and neoplastic diseases, including feline infectious peritonitis (FIP), histoplasmosis, septic peritonitis, pyothorax, multiple myeloma, and lymphoma.1,20–30 These findings underscore the need for a large-scale study comparing cats with and without HA (wHA) to better characterize disease associations and prognostic implications.

This retrospective case-control study aimed to describe disease associations and outcomes in cats presenting to a tertiary referral teaching hospital with varying degrees of HA, compared with age-, breed-, and sex-matched cats wHA. The primary aim was to identify differences in the organ systems and disease pathoetiologies associated with HA compared to those wHA. Our secondary aim was to determine if organ systems and disease pathoetiologies differed in cats with mild, moderate, or severe HA compared to cats wHA. Our final aim was to assess the impact of HA and its severity on survival to discharge, admission to 6-month survival, and discharge to 6-month survival. We hypothesized that HA, and the severity of HA, would be associated with neoplastic and infectious diseases, longer hospitalization, and shorter survival.

Materials and methods

Animals

For this retrospective case-control study, the laboratory database of the University of Tennessee College of Veterinary Medicine was searched to identify cats with a plasma albumin (pALB) concentration measured in heparinized blood between August 2017 and 2023. Cats with a single pALB concentration below the lower reference interval (< 3.0 g/dL) were assigned to the HA group and included based on the first occurrence of HA. Cats wHA and no history of HA (pALB ≥3.0 g/dL) were randomly selected and frequency-matched to cats with HA based on age, breed, sex, and presenting service to achieve similar baseline distributions between groups.

Data collection and classification

Data collected from the medical records are summarized in the Appendix. Hypoalbuminemia was classified as mild (2.5-2.9 g/dL), moderate (2.0-2.4 g/dL), or severe (< 2.0 g/dL), and primary diagnoses were assigned to organ system and pathoetiology categories by a single author (S.M.S.).

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. The pALB concentrations were approximately normally distributed and reported as mean ± SD. Non-normally distributed variables (age and hospital duration) were compared using the Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. Associations between HA severity and categorical variables (sex, breed, service, organ system, and pathoetiology) were evaluated using chi-square or Fisher's exact tests. Differences in survival to discharge among albumin categories were assessed using chi-square tests, with adjustment for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method.

Two 6-month survival analyses were performed: overall survival from admission (all cats) and survival after discharge (cats surviving to discharge with ≥1 day of follow-up). Survival across albumin categories were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank testing, and pairwise comparisons were adjusted for multiple testing using the Sidak method. Because median survival time was not reached, 6-month restricted median survival time (RMST) and 95% CIs were reported. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to evaluate the effects of age, HA severity, and their interaction on survival.

The pALB concentrations were compared among organ system and pathoetiologies using 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey Karmer adjustment for multiple comparisons. Normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed using Shapiro-Wilk testing with Q-Q plots and Levene’s test, respectively. SAS, version 9.5, release TS1M8 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) was used for all analyses. P < .05 was considered significant.

Further details are provided in the Appendix.

Results

Evaluation of cats with and without hypoalbuminemia, regardless of severity

Cats

A total of 2927 cats had a pALB concentrations measured within the study period, and 948 (32%) had HA. Of cats with HA, 116 were excluded for incomplete medical records (n = 98) or having an unknown age (n = 18), leaving 832 cats with HA for evaluation. An additional 832 age-, sex-, breed-, and service-matched cats without HA were identified from the remaining cats to form the wHA group. The mean ± SD pALB concentration for all 1664 cats was 3.0 ± 0.6 g/dL. The mean ± SD pALB concentrations for the 832 cats with HA and 832 matched cats wHA were 2.5 ± 0.4 g/dL and 3.5 ± 0.5 g/dL, respectively (P < .001).

Demographic variables and presenting service of cats with HA and cats wHA are presented in (Supplemental Table S1; see online supplementary material for this table). The mean ± SD age of cats with HA was 9.4 ± 5.2 years. Of the cats with HA, 55% (460/832) were male (430 neutered, 30 intact) and 45% (372/832) were female (340 spayed, 32 intact). Domestic shorthair cats comprised the majority of cats with HA (531/832, 64%), followed by purebred (103/832, 12%), domestic longhair (86/832, 10%), mixed-breed (63/832, 8%), and domestic medium hair (49/832, 6%) cats. Among the purebred cats, 22 breeds were represented, with Siamese (25/103, 24%) and Maine Coon (18/103; 17%) breeds being most common (Supplemental Table S2; see online supplementary material for this table). There were no significant differences in age (P = .90), sex and neuter status (P = .75), or breed (P = .99) between cats with HA and cats wHA. Cats with HA most commonly presented through the emergency service (552/832, 66%), and there was no significant difference in presenting service between cats with HA and cats wHA (P = .99).

The 3 organ systems most commonly affected in cats with HA were the hematopoietic (140/832; 17%), GI (135/832; 16%), and urogenital (126/832; 15%) systems. Hospitalization duration and survival to discharge

Median hospitalization time for cats with HA was significantly longer, 2 (range: 1-16) days, than cats wHA, 1 (1-15) days (P < .001, Supplemental Table S1; see online supplementary material for this table). Compared to cats wHA (755/832; 91%), significantly fewer cats with HA (660/832; 79%) survived to discharge (P < .001).

Survival at 6 months

Between admission and 6 months after admission, death occurred in 33% (275/832) of cats with HA and 16% (136/832) of cats wHA. Kaplan-Meier curves illustrating admission to 6-month survival for cats with HA and cats wHA are shown in Figure 1, with differences between groups assessed with the univariate log-rank test (P < .001). The MST was not met for either group, and so RMSTs were calculated. The RMST was 111 days (95% CI, 104-117 days) for cats with HA and 144 days (95% CI, 138-149 days) for cats wHA.

Of the cats that survived to discharge, 82% (542/660) of cats with HA and 75% (564/755) of cats wHA had follow-up beyond 1 day of discharge and were included in the discharge to 6-month survival analysis. Between discharge and 6 months after admission, death occurred in 32% (173/542) of cats with HA and 21% (120/564) of cats wHA. Cats by severity of HA

Cats

Of the 1664 cats with a pALB concentration measured within the study period, 832 had HA, which was classified as mild in 65% (540/832), moderate in 24% (200/832), and severe in 11% (92/832). The mean ± SD pALB concentrations for mild, moderate, and severe HA were 2.7 ± 0.1 g/dL, 2.2 ± 0.3 g/dL, and 1.6 ± 0.3 g/dL, respectively.

Demographic data by severity group is available in Supplemental Table S3 (see online supplementary material for this table). Cats with severe HA were significantly younger than those with mild HA (P = .01) or wHA (P < .001). There were no significant differences among sex/neuter status or breed among groups.

In the cats with mild HA, the most commonly affected organ systems were the GI (97/540, 18%), hematopoietic (92/540; 17%), and urogenital (78/540; 14%) systems. Compared to cats wHA, cats with mild HA were more likely to have multisystemic (P = .01) or hematopoietic (P < .001) diseases and less likely to have upper respiratory (P = .01) or neurologic conditions (P = .01). The most common disease pathoetiologies in cats with mild HA were neoplastic (137/540, 25%), infectious (112/540, 21%), and inflammatory (84/540, 16%). Compared to cats wHA, cats with mild HA were more likely to have neoplastic (P < .001) and infectious (P < .001) disorders and less likely to have open/idiopathic (P = .01) or toxic/dietary indiscretion (P = .01) diagnoses. The most common diagnoses among cats with mild HA were intraabdominal large cell lymphoma (22/540, 4%), chronic enteropathy (21/540, 4%), and intraabdominal small cell lymphoma (21/540, 4%).

Diagnoses among cats with moderate HA most commonly affected the urogenital (38/200, 19%), hematopoietic (29/200, 15%), or GI (27/200, 14%) system. Compared to cats wHA, cats with moderate HA were more likely to have multisystemic disease (P = .01) or have a primary diagnosis affecting the hematopoietic system (P = .01) and less likely to have disorders affecting the neurologic (P < .001) or endocrine (P = .01) system. The most common disease pathoetiologies in cats with moderate HA were infectious (56/200, 28%), neoplastic (46/200, 23%), and inflammatory (25/200, 13%). Compared to cats wHA, cats with moderate HA were more likely to have neoplastic (P = .04) or infectious (P < .001) disease pathoetiologies and less likely to have an open/idiopathic diagnosis (P < .001). The most common diagnoses among cats with moderate HA were FIP (14/200; 7%), intraabdominal large cell lymphoma (13/200; 7%), and polytrauma (9/200; 5%).

Among cats with severe HA, primary diagnoses were most frequently classified as hematopoietic (19/92, 21%), multisystemic (15/92, 16%), or hepatobiliary/pancreatic (12/92, 13%). Compared to cats wHA, cats with severe HA were more likely to have multisystemic or hematopoietic disorders (P < .001 for both). Cats with severe HA were also more likely to have multisystemic diseases compared to cats with mild HA (P = .04), but not cats with moderate HA. The most common primary disease pathoetiologies in cats with severe HA were infectious (43/92, 47%), neoplastic (15/92, 16%), and traumatic (9/92, 10%). Cats with severe HA were more likely to have infectious diseases compared to cats with mild HA (P < .001), moderate HA (P = .01), and wHA (P < .001). Compared to cats wHA, cats with severe HA were less likely to have inflammatory (P = .01) or open/idiopathic (P = .02) diagnoses. The most common diagnoses among cats with severe HA were FIP (15/92; 16%), polytrauma (8/92; 9%), and intraabdominal large cell lymphoma (7/92; 8%).

Survival to discharge

Survival to discharge was 91% (755/832), 84% (453/540), 78% (156/200), and 55% (51/92) of cats wHA, with mild HA, moderate HA, and severe HA, respectively, and differed significantly across groups. Cats with severe HA were significantly less likely to survive to discharge compared to cats wHA, cats with mild HA, and cats with moderate HA (P < .001 for all). Survival was significantly higher in cats wHA than cats with mild or moderate HA (P < .001 for both). No significant difference in survival to discharge was detected between cats with mild and cats with moderate HA (P = .07).

Discussion

This study evaluated the prevalence, disease associations, and prognostic implications of HA and its severity in cats presenting to a tertiary referral teaching hospital. Nearly one-third of cats evaluated were found to have HA, with mild HA being most common. Cats with HA were significantly more likely to have neoplastic and infectious diseases, particularly those affecting the hematopoietic, hepatobiliary/pancreatic, or multiple (ie, multisystemic) organ systems. In contrast, HA was less frequently associated with neurologic, endocrine, or upper respiratory diseases. Additionally, cats with HA had a worse prognosis, characterized by longer duration of hospital stays, lower survival to discharge, and reduced 6-month survival. Stratification by HA severity revealed that cats with severe HA were younger and more likely to present with infectious pathoetiologies compared to all other severity groups. These findings suggest that HA is more common in cats presenting to teaching hospitals than anecdotally recognized and is associated with survival outcomes.

The prevalence of HA in the cats that were presented to the teaching hospital within the study period was 32%, which is nearly identical to that reported in a UK study.1 The distribution of HA severity was also comparable, with 65%, 24%, and 11% of cats classified as having mild, moderate, and severe HA versus 63%, 23%, and 13% in the UK study.1 These reproducible findings across 2 large study samples suggest that the prevalence and severity distribution of HA are likely consistent across geographic regions, reinforcing the notion that HA represents a common condition in feline referral populations. However, it is important to acknowledge the potential for referral bias, as tertiary care centers often manage complex or critically ill cases, frequently with multiple comorbidities. This could have led to an overestimation of HA prevalence relative to the general feline population presented to nonreferral hospitals. Notably, in our study, the majority of cats with HA (66%) presented through the emergency service, suggesting these cases were likely more acutely or severely ill. This pattern mirrors trends in human medicine, in which HA is most prevalent among hospitalized and critically ill people, where prevalence rates range from 30% to 70%.9,10

The data in the current study were analyzed both by the presence or absence of HA and by HA severity. Although pALB concentrations also were evaluated by organ system and disease pathoetiology, substantial overlap between cats with HA and cats wHA was observed across all groups, limiting the utility of pALB concentrations to distinguish among affected organ systems or underlying pathoetiologies. Among cats with HA of any severity, the most commonly affected organ systems were the hematopoietic, GI, and urogenital systems. However, only the proportions of cats with hematopoietic, multisystemic, and hepatobiliary/pancreatic disease were significantly higher in the HA group compared to the wHA group. Stratification by disease severity showed that hematopoietic and multisystemic diseases remained significantly different across all severities of HA when compared to cats wHA. Within the HA cohort, only multisystemic diseases varied by severity, with cats with severe HA being more likely to have multisystemic diseases. In agreement with the hematopoietic and multisystemic organ system findings, neoplastic and infectious pathoetiologies affected nearly half of the cats with HA. Furthermore, nearly half of the cats with severe HA had infectious pathoetiologies, which was a higher proportion than cats with mild HA, cats with moderate HA, and cats wHA. Infectious but not noninfectious inflammatory conditions were more common in cats with HA compared to cats wHA in the current study. Among the infectious diseases identified in cats with HA, FIP was most common, affecting 21% of cats within the infectious pathoetiology category, and was the most common diagnosis among cats with severe HA. Hypoalbuminemia is reported to occur in 60% to 90% of cats with FIP, is more common in cats with effusion, and is likely secondary to severe systemic inflammation, vasculitis, and in effusive cases, third spacing.24–26 During systemic inflammation, albumin behaves as a negative acute phase protein, meaning that its synthesis decreases in response to proinflammatory cytokines, allowing for use of amino acids in the production of positive acute phase proteins.31 This reduced synthesis of albumin, along with increased vascular permeability during inflammation, can contribute to HA in both acute and chronic inflammatory conditions.32 The overrepresentation of FIP among cats with severe HA in the current study underscores the importance of evaluating for FIP in cats with HA, particularly when the degree of HA is severe. Pyothorax was among the most common infectious conditions noted in a UK study,1 and in the current study pyothorax occurred in 8 of the cats with HA and none of the cats wHA. Furthermore, pyothorax showed a disproportionate number of cats (6/8) with severe HA. Hypoalbuminemia is a common finding among cats with pyothorax and is likely attributable to increased vascular permeability, loss of protein into the pleural effusion, and reduced hepatic synthesis due to preferential synthesis of acute phase proteins.28,33,34

Within the hematopoietic organ system category, 69% of the diagnoses were neoplastic. Neoplastic pathoetiologies were identified in 24% of cats with HA, of which the most common diagnosis was intraabdominal large cell lymphoma (42 cats with HA). The majority of cats with intraabdominal large cell lymphoma (35/42) had mild or moderate HA. HA is reported to occur in 24% to 75% of cats with large cell lymphoma and is associated with a shorter survival time.20–22 Although intraabdominal small cell lymphoma is the second most common diagnosis among the neoplastic pathoetiologies group (27 cats with HA), in contrast to large cell lymphoma, the majority of cats with small cell lymphoma (37/43) had either normal pALB concentrations or mild HA. Hypoalbuminemia occurs in 14% to 49% of cats with low grade intestinal lymphoma.20,35 Although the exact mechanisms underlying HA in cats with lymphoma remain unclear, possible mechanisms include increased cytokine expression from tumor cells and systemic inflammation causing suppression of hepatic albumin synthesis, increased uptake of albumin by tumor-associated macrophages, and blood loss from ulcerated or bleeding GI masses.36–40 In 1 study of 9 cats with large cell lymphoma that occurred after treatment of small cell GI lymphoma, pALB concentrations were significantly lower at the time of large cell lymphoma diagnosis compared to the time of small cell lymphoma diagnosis.41 The authors proposed monitoring albumin trends in cats undergoing treatment for small cell GI lymphoma as a potential indicator of disease transformation. In the current study, although neoplastic pathoetiologies were more common in cats with mild and moderate HA compared to cats wHA, there was no difference in the proportion of cats with neoplastic pathoetiologies between severe HA and wHA groups. This could suggest that severe HA is less commonly associated with neoplastic conditions.

In contrast to the findings in a UK study,1 the present study did not identify a significant difference in the occurrence of GI disease between cats with HA and cats wHA.1 This is most likely because a diagnosis of alimentary lymphoma was assigned to the hematopoietic system in the current study, separating lymphoma from other causes of GI disease such as chronic enteropathy. Therefore, although GI disease is a common complaint for cats presenting to a tertiary center, GI disease is not associated with an increased incidence of HA compared to cats wHA when alimentary lymphoma is excluded. Previous studies have reported HA in 5% to 83% of cats with chronic enteropathy, and the in current study, HA occurred in 24/63 (38%) of cats with unbiopsied chronic enteropathy or biopsy-confirmed chronic inflammatory enteropathy.42,43 Of cats with HA diagnosed with chronic enteropathy, the majority (21/24, 88%) had mild HA.

Urinary obstruction was diagnosed in 42 cats with HA. Although obstructive disease was not significantly more common in cats with HA compared to cats wHA, the presence of HA in this subset was unexpected. Cats with feline lower urinary tract disease have signs of systemic inflammation, including higher concentrations of positive acute phase proteins such as serum amyloid A and alpha-1-acid glycoprotein and lower albumin concentrations. Albumin concentrations in these cats were mildly lower and showed improvement 48 hours after resolution of the obstruction.44 In the current study, serial albumin concentrations were not evaluated, so it remains unclear whether a similar recovery occurred. Additionally, it is unknown whether cats with ureteral obstructions, which were also part of the obstructive pathoetiology category, exhibit the same inflammatory profile.

Hypoalbuminemia is a well-recognized marker of increased morbidity and higher case fatality rates in critically ill people.2–4 Lower albumin concentrations are associated with higher case fatality rates in cats with hepatic lipidosis, large granular lymphocyte lymphoma, acute kidney injury, panleukopenia, and bite wounds.21,45–49 The results of this study suggest that HA might have a similar prognostic role across multiple feline diseases. In this study cohort, cats with HA had significantly longer hospital stays, with a median duration that was 1 day longer than that of cats wHA. This extension in hospitalization times contributes to greater treatment costs, a finding documented in cats with moderate and severe hypoalbuminemia.1 Survival to discharge and at 6 months were also significantly lower in cats with HA compared to cats wHA. Furthermore, when cats were stratified by HA severity, those with severe HA had the poorest outcomes, with 45% dying prior to discharge. When all deaths (before and after discharge) were included in the analysis (eg, admission to 6-month survival analysis), cats with severe HA had the shortest RMST at 71 days, which was statistically different from all other groups. However, when the analysis was limited to cats that survived to discharge, this difference in RMST was attenuated and remained different only compared to cats with mild HA and cats wHA. These findings suggest that although overall survival probability is the lowest in cats with severe HA, conditional survival improves substantially among those that survive to discharge, narrowing the survival gap between severity groups. However, it should be noted that the small sample size could have downplayed any significance, because only 38 cats with severe HA were included in the discharge to 6-month survival analysis, with numbers dropping to 11 cats by day 50. Given the diagnosis of FIP in 16% of the cats with severe HA, it is worth noting that the prognosis presented in the current study might differ now that antiviral therapies are more readily available. These findings, corroborated by a UK study,1 indicate that serum albumin concentrations likely have a role in feline prognostic scoring systems.

This study was strengthened by the inclusion of a large cohort of cats with HA and a matched group of cats wHA, enhancing the validity of our comparative analyses. Nevertheless, several limitations inherent to its retrospective design should be acknowledged. Diagnoses were based on the medical record and the clinical judgment of the attending clinician at the time of presentation, which could have introduced variability in diagnostic accuracy. In some instances, cats were treated symptomatically without a definitive diagnosis, and the lack of standardized diagnostic protocols could have resulted in missed subclinical comorbidities or misdiagnoses. Misclassification of primary organ system or pathoetiology also could have occurred. Although classification was performed by a single author to maintain consistency across cases, this might have introduced subjective bias. To match the degree of illness among the 2 study groups, an equal number of cats wHA were selected from the same presenting service (eg, emergency, internal medicine). The use of clinical severity scores, such as the acute patient physiologic and laboratory evaluation (APPLE) score could have facilitated comparison of disease severity between wHA and HA groups; however, inconsistences in the information available in the record precluded their use. Additionally, more than half of the cats were still alive at the time of last follow-up and subsequently lost to further tracking, as is often the case in referral practice. This high degree of right censoring precluded the calculation of median survival times and necessitated the use of restricted median survival times. Finally, the University of Tennessee College of Veterinary Medicine functions primarily as a secondary and tertiary referral center, therefore the study cohort presented here might not accurately represent causes of HA in cats observed in general clinical practice.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that HA is a common and clinically relevant finding in cats presenting to a referral hospital, affecting one-third of cats, and is most often mild in severity. Compared to a cohort of cats wHA, cats with HA were more likely to have neoplastic or infectious diseases, most often affecting multiple organ systems or the hematopoietic system. Infectious diseases were more common in all HA severity subgroups compared to cats wHA; however, cats with severe HA most commonly had infectious pathoetiologies. This finding suggests that cats with severe HA should undergo a comprehensive infectious disease evaluation to exclude potential infectious etiologies. In contrast, cats with mild or moderate HA, but not severe HA, were more likely to have neoplastic conditions compared to cats wHA. Cats with severe HA were also younger than all other groups and had the worst prognosis. Finally, cats with HA experienced poorer outcomes, including longer hospital stays, lower survival to discharge, and reduced long-term survival. These findings support an association between pALB concentration and clinical outcomes in cats and underscore the need for further prospective studies.
