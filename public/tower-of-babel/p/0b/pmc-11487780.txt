Association between exposure to proton pump inhibitors and delirium: a descriptive and disproportionality analysis of VigiBase
Authors: Alexandre Decros, Elise-Marie Minoc, Véronique Lelong-Boulouard, Basile Chrétien, Alexandre Meurant, Joachim Alexandre, Charles Dolladille, Cédric Villain
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesProton pump inhibitor (PPI) exposure can lead to hyponatraemia, which is a common cause of delirium. An association between PPI exposure and delirium without hyponatraemia has been suggested in the literature. We aimed to describe the association between reports of delirium and PPI exposure and to assess the association between PPI and delirium with and without hyponatraemia.DesignA descriptive and disproportionality analysis of claims data.SettingWorld pharmacovigilance database VigiBase between 1 January 1991 and 9 February 2022.Primary and secondary outcome measuresIn the first part, we described reports of delirium for which involvement of a PPI or interactions of PPIs with other drugs were suspected. In the second part, delirium cases and non-cases were used to assess the disproportionality signal using the reporting OR (ROR) for the associations of PPI exposure with delirium or delirium/hyponatraemia co-events.ResultsWe identified 2395 reports of delirium in which involvement of PPI exposure was suspected. Omeprazole, esomeprazole and pantoprazole were the most frequently reported PPIs. Hyponatraemia was present in 11% of the reports. The disproportionality analysis included 1 264 798 reports of adverse drug reactions in patients using PPIs, including 19 081 reports of delirium. We did not find a disproportionality signal for the association between PPI use and delirium (ROR 0.89, 95% CI 0.87 to 0.91). We detected an association of PPI use with delirium/hyponatraemia co-events (ROR 1.53, 95% CI 1.41 to 1.65).ConclusionsMost reports of delirium in which the involvement of PPIs was suspected did not include concomitant hyponatraemia. However, no significant signal of disproportionate reporting of delirium was observed for PPIs compared with other drugs, except in cases of delirium associated with hyponatraemia. Hyponatraemia may be the main mechanism linking PPI exposure with delirium, and this possibility should be further explored in prospective studies.Trial registration numberNCT05815550.Keywords: CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY, Delirium & cognitive disorders, Adverse events, Delirium

Introduction

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are used widely worldwide to treat peptic ulcers, Helicobacter pylori infection, and gastro-oesophageal reflux and to reduce the risk of ulcers caused by non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.1 Guidelines for these indications recommend a short prescription duration (4–8 weeks of treatment in most cases). However, in real life, PPI prescriptions are frequently continued for a longer period.1 Moreover, they are sometimes used for non-validated indications, such as non-specific abdominal pain.1 According to the 2023 Beers criteria (a list of potentially inappropriate drugs for older adults), PPIs are potentially inappropriate drugs when used in the absence of indication or when they are prescribed for longer than the recommended duration.2

Although most adverse drug reactions reported for PPIs are mild and reversible, frequent and often prolonged exposure to PPIs may raise the risk of uncommon but more severe adverse drug reactions and become a public health issue. Several studies have shown that prolonged PPI exposure poses significantly greater risk in patients with colitis caused by Clostridium difficile,3 hypomagnesaemia,4 fracture,5 hypovitaminosis B12,6 pneumonia7 or hyponatraemia.8 The prescription of PPIs in older adults is associated with significantly higher rates of hospitalisation and death.9 10

Delirium is common in older adults and can lead to significant morbidity and mortality.11 Up to 30% of cases of delirium are related to medication.12 In 2009, the French National Health Authority (Haute Autorité de Santé) identified PPIs as delirium-causing drugs.13 An observational study including 675 older adults revealed an association between PPI exposure and the occurrence of delirium without hyponatraemia.14 Another study of more than 3000 patients aged 65 and over in intensive care units found a significant association between PPI administration and delirium.15

Most case reports attribute delirium associated with PPI exposure to severe hyponatraemia related to a syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion.16 17 However, a few case reports have described the occurrence of delirium related to PPI exposure without hyponatraemia, raising the question of whether there are other mechanisms underlying the link between PPI exposure and delirium.18 19 This link might be attributable to acetylcholine, a protective neurotransmitter in the central nervous system that is involved in the occurrence of delirium.20 Indeed, some studies have shown that exposure to PPIs can inhibit choline acetyltransferase and reduce the synthesis of acetylcholine.21

The association between delirium and PPI exposure has not been well studied. Using data from the pharmacovigilance database of the WHO, our objective was to describe the characteristics of delirium reports in which the involvement of PPIs was suspected and to evaluate the associations of PPI exposure with delirium alone and with concomitant delirium and hyponatraemia.

Methods

Design

We used Vigibase, WHO’s pharmacovigilance database. All Vigibase reports are qualified as ICSR (individual case safety reports). To date, more than 30 million reports from 160 countries have been collected.22 Each report includes suspected drugs, their mode of administration and therapeutic indication and information on demographic and geographical characteristics, year of the report, associated illnesses, associated drugs, and characteristics of adverse drug reactions (ADRs). For each report, the level of clinical relevance and completeness of the information collected is assessed by the Vigigrade score. This score ranges from 0 to 1, and a report is considered well documented if the score is higher than 0.8. The required criteria for the completeness assessment include time to onset, age, sex, indication of the drug, outcome, report type, dose, country, reporter characteristics and various comments.23 The ADRs are coded according to the MedDRA (Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities) classification.24 The drugs are classified using the WHO ATC (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical) code.25 The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee (University of Caen Normandy, Caen, France; reference: 2646, dated 15 July 2021). The study protocol was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05815550).

Description of the variables

We extracted data from 1 January 1991 (PPI marketing date) until 9 February 2022. PPIs were coded according to the ATC code A02BC and included the following drugs: esomeprazole, lansoprazole, omeprazole, pantoprazole, rabeprazole, dexlansoprazole, dexrabeprazole, vonoprazan and tegoprazan.25 ‘Confusional state’, ‘delirium’ and ‘disorientation’ were the preferred terms of the MedDRA classification for identifying reports of delirium. A concomitant delirium-hyponatraemia event was defined as a report in which delirium (as defined above) and hyponatraemia (defined using the MedDRA classification) were reported at the same time. The other variables used were based on their presence at the time of the included reports and defined using the WHO ATC code for medication variables and the MedDRA classification for other health-related variables.

Description of delirium reports

We selected all reports of delirium in which the involvement of PPIs or interactions of PPIs with other drugs were suspected. Using VigiLyze, we first extracted several characteristics of these reports: year and country of reports, type of report, type of notifier and average Vigigrade score. We then summarised the other characteristics for all reports: type of PPIs according to the frequency of reports in the database (categorised as pantoprazole, omeprazole, esomeprazole and other PPIs); age (under 65 years old, between 65 and 74 years old, and over 74 years old); sociodemographic data; administration regimens (route of administration, dose, duration of exposure, decision on drug continuation after the adverse event); therapeutic indication; concomitant hyponatraemia; polypharmacy; if PPI was considered the only suspected drug; associated drugs known to cause delirium (opioids, anxiolytics, antipsychotics, antihistamines, hypnotics and corticosteroids); and data concerning delirium (severity and evolution after stopping or continuing the PPI). Delirium was considered severe if it resulted in hospitalisation, life-threatening conditions, disability or permanent damage, malformation, birth defects or death. Comparisons of the characteristics of reports according to subgroups of PPI type and age group were performed using x² tests with two-sided p<0.05 as the threshold for significance.

Disproportionality analysis

The association between PPI and delirium was evaluated by case/non-case disproportionality analysis. The case/non-case disproportionality method is recommended for detecting a signal for an association between a drug and an adverse event in a pharmacovigilance database. A signal is present when the number of reports of an adverse event is greater than expected. This is referred to as a disproportionate reporting rate of an adverse event relative to others. In the present study, the safety signal corresponded to a statistically significant difference in the distribution of reports of delirium (case) in PPI users (exposure of interest). Thus, this second analysis did not focus on delirium reports in which PPIs were suspected or interacted with other drugs (as in the first part of the study) but on every report of delirium in both PPI users (new and chronic users) and non-users. For this analysis, we also used data from 1 January 1991 to 9 February 2022. As the data extraction method was different from that of the descriptive part (VigiLyze was not used for the disproportionality analysis), numbers of reports of delirium in which PPIs are suspected to be involved differ from the first part of the study. Persons under the age of 18 were excluded from this analysis. The strength of the disproportionality was quantified by the reporting OR (ROR) with a 95% CI estimated with univariate and multivariate logistic regression models. An ROR was considered statistically significant if it was greater than 1 and if the lower boundary of its 95% CI did not include 1. An ROR lower than 1 was not considered to indicate statistical significance, as the method was unable to detect the absence of a signal.26 To validate the disproportionality analysis, we chose bisphosphonates as a negative control (drugs that are not known to trigger the ADR of interest) and opioids as a positive control (drugs that are known to trigger the ADR of interest) for delirium occurrence. The multivariate analyses were adjusted for the following risk factors for delirium and/or potential confounders: age, sex, geographic region, opioids, antipsychotics, anxiolytics, hypnotics, corticosteroids, antihistamines, drug misuse, undernutrition, dementia, depression, central nervous system vascular disorders, visual impairment, hearing impairment, alcohol use, drug abuse/dependence/withdrawal, acute urinary retention, constipation, infection, hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia, dehydration, seizure disorders and anticholinergic syndrome. The collinearity of the final models was assessed by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF, acceptable if <5, online supplemental table 1). The main analysis assessed the association between PPI exposure and delirium for all reports and according to subgroups of age and type of PPI. A secondary analysis assessed the association between PPI exposure and hyponatraemia/delirium co-events for all reports and according to age and type of PPI. A sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the associations of both delirium and hyponatraemia/delirium co-events with exposure to PPIs, which was the only drug suspected to be associated with delirium occurrence. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (V.4.0.2, R Studio v1.4.1717) and the following packages: rlang, dplyr, stats, base, fst, data.table, magrittr, openxlsx, carData, car, grid and checkmate.

Patient and public involvement statementPatientand Public Involvement statement

This study used a large pharmacovigilance database to achieve its objectives. The authors therefore did not directly involve patients or the public throughout the study.

Results

Description of delirium reports

Data were extracted from 2395 reports in which the involvement of PPIs or interactions of PPIs with other drugs was suspected. The mean age of the patients was 65.3 years; 771 (37%) patients were 75 years or older, and 1401 (60%) were women. The average Vigigrade score was 0.48, and 320 reports (13%) had scores above 0.80. In 1128 (47%) reports, PPIs were the only suspected drug. Hyponatraemia was reported in 259 (11%) reports. Among associated drugs likely to cause delirium, the presence of opioids was identified in 339 reports (14%), anxiolytics in 296 reports (12%), antipsychotics in 441 reports (18%), hypnotics in 149 reports (6%), corticosteroids in 263 reports (11%) and antihistamines in 89 reports (4%). The characteristics of all reports and PPI subgroups are shown in table 1. There were 989 (41%) reports involving omeprazole, 609 (26%) involving esomeprazole, 414 (17%) involving pantoprazole and 383 (16%) involving other PPIs. With respect to the administration regimens, the route was predominantly oral (95% of reports). PPIs were often used at full doses (67% of reports), and the duration of PPI exposure was often less than 30 days (85% of reports). Delirium was severe in 1231 (72%) reports. The percentages of resolving delirium were greater for reports in which PPIs were stopped than for those in which PPIs were continued (59% vs 30%, respectively).

Disproportionality analysis

By searching in Vigibase from 1 January 1991 to 9 February 2022, we identified 1 264 798 reports occurring in patients exposed to PPIs including 19 081 reports of delirium and 25 063 403 reports occurring in patients exposed to other drugs including 214 421 reports of delirium (online supplemental table 2). In 24% of the reports, the patients were 65 years of age or older, and women were described in 61% of the reports. The main disproportionality analysis did not find a disproportionality signal between PPI exposure and delirium (adjusted ROR 0.89, 95% CI 0.87 to 0.91). Secondary analyses by age subgroups and PPIs did not find a disproportionality signal between PPI use and delirium in multivariate analyses (figure 1).

The characteristics of the reports used for the analysis of delirium/hyponatraemia co-events are shown in online supplemental table 3. We identified 1101 reports of delirium with hyponatraemia in patients exposed to PPIs. A disproportionality signal was detected between PPIs and delirium/hyponatraemia co-events when all reports were analysed (adjusted ROR 1.53, 95% CI 1.41 to 1.65), in the group of omeprazole users (adjusted ROR 1.54, 95% CI 1.38 to 1.72) and in all age subgroups (figure 2).

Discussion

Our study summarised reports of delirium in Vigibase in which the involvement of PPIs was suspected. In half of the reports, a PPI was the only suspected drug. Hyponatremia was present in 11% of reports, and this proportion increased in older adults. Delirium resolved more frequently when PPIs were discontinued than when they were continued. The disproportionality analysis did not detect any significant safety signal between delirium and PPIs, except for delirium/hyponatraemia co-events. Among the most frequently reported PPIs, only omeprazole was significantly associated with hyponatraemia/delirium co-events. These results did not differ according to age.

We observed a disproportionality signal between PPI exposure and delirium/hyponatraemia co-events. A few reports of PPI-induced hyponatraemia have been described, with the average duration from the start of PPI treatment to the onset of hyponatraemia ranging from 7 to 15 days,16 17 27 28 and all patients with hyponatraemia in these reports met the diagnostic criteria for inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion syndrome.16 17 27 28 To date, no hypothesis has explained the association between PPI exposure and the occurrence of this syndrome. The percentage of reports that included hyponatremia was highest among patients aged 75 and older, which could be explained by a greater proportion of predisposing drugs and comorbidities in this age group. However, the disproportionality signal between PPI exposure and delirium/hyponatraemia co-events was the same in all age subgroups. In the other subgroup analyses, omeprazole was the only PPI associated with significant over-reporting of delirium/hyponatraemia co-events, which has not been reported previously; for the other studied drugs, the results approached but did not reach statistical significance. A difference in statistical power could explain the discrepancies between drugs, as the sample size of omeprazole reports was larger than those of other PPIs.

Despite the high number of reports of delirium suspected to be due to PPI exposure without concomitant hyponatraemia, the disproportionality analysis did not reveal a significant association between PPI exposure and delirium. Our results do not suggest a direct pathway linking PPIs and delirium except through hyponatraemia. This is in contrast to two previous observational studies that found a significant association between PPI exposure and delirium.14 15 One of these studies was performed in intensive care units, and residual confounding factors were not completely ruled out.15 The other study mostly included older adults experiencing multimorbidity and polypharmacy, a population in which pharmacokinetic drug interactions might be involved. For example, PPIs inhibit CYP2C19, which can lead to increases in the concentrations of drugs such as diazepam that are metabolised by this specific cytochrome.29 By contrast, our much younger study population likely experienced fewer drug interactions and only 52 of the reports specifically mentioned diazepam use, which is a known delirium trigger.

Surprisingly, we did not find wide variations in the number of reports of delirium by age category, even though very old adults are more prone to delirium. This could partly be explained by under-reporting, especially since delirium can be difficult to diagnose in older adults.30 Furthermore, as delirium is also often multifactorial in this population, it is possible that the involvement of PPIs was not suspected in some of the cases of delirium reported in Vigibase. Notably, compared with younger adults, PPIs were less likely to be continued after the occurrence of delirium and more frequently resolved when PPI exposure was continued in older adults. As the results of the disproportionality analysis were not modified by age, the greater proportion of resolution of delirium after PPI discontinuation in older adults is likely due to unreported factors that may not be related to PPI use. This observation also highlights the involvement of multiple factors in delirium in older adults, who frequently experience polypharmacy and multimorbidity. Physicians may be more likely to deprescribe inappropriate medications in older adults than in younger adults, which might explain the higher rate of PPI discontinuation in older adults. However, PPI prescription may be safe to pursue in older adults experiencing delirium if PPI prescription is appropriate and natraemia is within the normal range.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest study to assess the association between delirium and PPI use. Pharmacovigilance studies follow a robust methodological approach and allow large-scale analyses of drug safety data, which may be necessary for the detection of a safety signal.26 Vigibase is the largest pharmacovigilance database.26 The recorded data are representative of real life, and we adjusted our analyses for several variables, including known delirium-inducing drugs.26 ROR is a reproducible and easy-to-use tool for assessing disproportionality.26 The results for the positive and negative controls (online supplemental table 2) are consistent with the data in the literature and ensure that our study was not compromised by major bias. However, pharmacovigilance studies have intrinsic limitations.26 First, the level of evidence is lower than that of a randomised trial or a prospective observational study.26 A pharmacovigilance study cannot confirm a causal relationship between a drug and an adverse effect.26 Second, pharmacovigilance analyses are subject to information bias (i.e., misclassifications and missing data).26 The misclassification of reports according to the MedDRA classification could bias the results of analyses. Moreover, the diagnosis of delirium is sometimes challenging, and delirium events could therefore be misreported or under-reported.26 Third, missing data are frequent in Vigibase, especially for the duration of exposure, drug dosage and associated illnesses.26 In the descriptive part of this study, the distribution of Vigigrade scores was comparable to that in previous reports,23 suggesting that our results are at least as reliable as previous analyses of Vigibase.

Conclusion

Despite a large number of reports of delirium in which involvement of PPIs was suspected, we did not find a significant signal of disproportionate reporting of delirium for PPIs compared with other drugs. However, PPI exposure was associated with the occurrence of delirium in the presence of hyponatraemia. Our study suggests that the occurrence of delirium in patients treated with PPIs seems to be mediated mainly by the occurrence of hyponatraemia. Future studies could assess and compare the risk of occurrence of hyponatraemia between PPIs. Specific studies in older adults would also be of interest due to the suspected under-reporting of delirium in this population. Until more conclusive evidence related to associations with delirium can be obtained, prescribers should be mindful of other risks associated with PPI use and respect the validated indications and the recommended prescription durations.
