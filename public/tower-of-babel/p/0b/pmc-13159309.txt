Development and validation of a nomogram to predict the risk of cardiometabolic multimorbidity in older adults: data from the NHANES database
Authors: Jiao-yu Cao, Xiao-juan Zhou, Li-xiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop and validate a nomogram prediction model for predicting the risk of cardiometabolic multimorbidity(CMM) in older adults, providing a basis for early risk stratification and clinical intervention.MethodsData from seven survey cycles (2005–2018) of the NHANES database were integrated, incorporating 6,338 older adults aged ≥ 65 years who met the inclusion criteria. LASSO regression was employed to screen characteristic variables associated with CMM, followed by multivariate logistic regression analysis to identify independent risk factors and construct the model. The model’s performance was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) for discrimination, Spiegelhalter’s z-test for calibration, and Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) for clinical utility.ResultsLASSO regression identified 10 characteristic variables, all of which were confirmed as independent predictors via multivariate logistic regression: college education or above (OR = 0.731, 95% CI: 0.611–0.873, P = 0.001), waist circumference (OR = 1.026, 95% CI: 1.021–1.030, P < 0.001), poverty-income ratio > 3 (OR = 0.750, 95% CI: 0.622–0.904, P = 0.003), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) score (OR = 1.057, 95% CI: 1.041–1.073, P < 0.001), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (OR = 0.682, 95% CI: 0.585–0.796, P < 0.001), hemoglobin (OR = 0.884, 95% CI: 0.847–0.922, P < 0.001), red blood cell distribution width (OR = 1.052, 95% CI: 1.005–1.102, P = 0.030), blood urea nitrogen (OR = 1.104, 95% CI: 1.079–1.130, P < 0.001), globulin (OR = 1.026, 95% CI: 1.014–1.038, P < 0.001), and total cholesterol (OR = 0.644, 95% CI: 0.608–0.681, P < 0.001). The AUC of the nomogram was 0.735 (95% CI: 0.723–0.748), and internal 10-fold cross-validation yielded a consistent AUC of 0.736 (95% CI: 0.721–0.748). Spiegelhalter’s z-test (z=-0.399, P = 0.655) indicated good calibration. DCA showed a positive net benefit when the threshold probability for CMM was between 15% and 77%. ConclusionThe nomogram demonstrates satisfactory predictive performance CMM risk in older adults and serves as a valuable quantitative tool for individualized prevention and control.Keywords: Cardiometabolic multimorbidity, Elderly, Nomogram, Risk prediction, NHANES

Introduction

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), defined as the coexistence of two or more metabolic and cardiovascular diseases such as diabetes, coronary heart disease, and hypertension, has emerged as a major threat to the global health of older adults [1, 2]. Epidemiological studies indicate that the prevalence of CMM among adults aged ≥ 65 years ranges from 30% to 45%, with the risk increasing significantly with advancing age [3–5]. Compared with individuals suffering from a single chronic disease, patients with CMM exhibit a 2.3-fold higher all-cause mortality rate and a 3.1-fold higher cardiovascular mortality rate, accompanied by more severe organ dysfunction and reduced quality of life [6–9].

Current clinical cardiovascular risk assessment tools, including the Framingham Risk Score and ASCVD Risk Calculator, are primarily designed for single-disease endpoints [10, 11] and demonstrate limited effectiveness in predicting CMM. Additionally, these tools generally overlook geriatric-specific risk factors such as psychological status and novel biomarkers like red blood cell distribution width [12]. The rationale for integrating both psychosocial and biochemical factors lies in the complex, bidirectional nature of CMM in older adults. While biochemical markers provide a snapshot of current physiological and metabolic status, psychosocial factors such as depression act as critical modulators of disease progression through the psycho-neuroendocrine-immune axis and by influencing health-related behaviors. Therefore, a holistic prediction model that accounts for both dimensions is essential for capturing the multifaceted risk profile of the aging population.

While nomograms have shown utility in risk stratification for conditions like coronary heart disease [13] and diabetes [14], large-scale validated models for predicting CMM specifically in older populations do not yet exist. Using data from the NHANES database, this study combined LASSO regression with traditional logistic regression to systematically screen multidimensional predictors, including socioeconomic factors (education level, poverty-income ratio), anthropometric parameters (waist circumference), blood biomarkers (hemoglobin, blood urea nitrogen), and psychological assessments measured by the Patient Health Questionnaire-9(PHQ-9). By developing and validating the first CMM risk nomogram specifically for older adults, this research aims to provide a scientific and practical tool for individualized clinical risk assessment and address gaps in current geriatric comorbidity early warning systems.

Objects and methods

Research objects

Data for a total of 70,190 participants from seven survey cycles (2005–2018) were extracted from the United States NHANES database. Inclusion criteria consisted of: (1) aged ≥ 65 years; (2) complete data availability for both the outcome variable (CMM) and all 29 initial candidate predictors. In this study, “complete data availability” was defined as having no missing values for any assessed variables. Specifically, if a participant had missing data for any of the 29 variables, they were excluded. This complete-case analysis approach was adopted to ensure that all statistical processes—including the baseline comparison, dimensionality reduction via LASSO regression, and multivariate logistic modeling—were conducted on a consistent study population. This methodology helps maintain data integrity and avoids potential bias or noise introduced by data imputation in large-sample clinical prediction studies. The use of complete-case analysis in this study is based on the assumption that the missing data were Missing At Random (MAR). This assumption is considered reasonable for the NHANES database, as missingness in socioeconomic or clinical variables often relates to observed demographic factors (such as age or education level) rather than the unobserved values themselves.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) missing age data or age < 65 years; (2) concurrent malignant tumors. After applying these criteria, 6,338 individuals included in the final analysis. The participant screening workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1. All participants were categorized into a CMM group (2,436 cases) or a non-CMM group (3,902 cases). CMM was defined as the coexistence of two or more of the following clinically diagnosed conditions [15, 16]: heart disease (e.g., myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, or other heart diseases), diabetes, stroke, and hypertension. NHANES data were obtained from the National Center for Health Statistics and are publicly available.

Methods

Comprehensive clinical data were collected, including age, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, total PHQ-9 score, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), hemoglobin, lymphocyte percentage, monocyte percentage, red blood cell count, red blood cell distribution width(RDW), white blood cell count, albumin, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), globulin, total bilirubin, uric acid (UA), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), total cholesterol (TC), education level (below high school, high school/equivalent graduate, some college or associate degree, college graduate or above), gender (male, female), poverty-income ratio (< 1, 1–3, > 3), race (Mexican American, other Hispanics, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, other races), and marital status.

The PHQ-9 is a validated self-report scale widely used to screen for and assess the severity of depressive symptoms. It comprises 9 items, each corresponding to core depressive symptoms (e.g., depressed mood, loss of interest, sleep disturbance, fatigue), with scores ranging from 0(“not at all”) to 3(“nearly every day”). Total scores range from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating greater severity. Severity categories are defined as: mild depression (5–9), moderate depression (10–14), moderately severe depression (15–19), and severe(≥ 20) [17]. The scale demonstrates good reliability and validity [18].

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 software and R software (R 3.6.1) were used for statistical analyses. Normally distributed Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, with intergroup comparisons performed using the t-test. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as the median and interquartile range [M (P25, P75)], and the Mann-Whitney U test was employed. Categorical data were presented as case counts and percentages, analyzed with the chi-square test.

LASSO regression was utilized to screen for characteristic variables and minimize the impact of multicollinearity. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was then applied to identify independent predictors, followed by the construction of a nomogram model. The model’s discriminative ability was evaluated using the ROC curve, calibration was assessed via the calibration curves, and clinical utility was determined using decision curve analysis(DCA) curve. To mitigate overfitting, internal validation of was performed using 10-fold cross-validation. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Screening of characteristic variables

To manage the high dimensionality of the 29 candidate variables and mitigate multicollinearity, LASSO regression was employed. Using a 10-fold cross-validation approach, Lambda.1se (0.031) was selected as the optimal tuning parameter. This process identified 10 variables with non-zero coefficients: education level, waist circumference, poverty-income ratio, PHQ-9 score, HDL-C, hemoglobin, RDW, BUN, globulin, and total cholesterol. The LASSO regression selection process is visualized in Fig. 2.

Logistic regression analysis

The 10 variables identified by LASSO regression were further analyzed logistic regression. The linearity assumption for continuous predictors was rigorously confirmed using the Box-Tidwell test, with all interaction terms yielding P > 0.05.

Univariate (unadjusted) analysis showed that all 10 variables were significantly associated with CMM(P < 0.05). Subsequent multivariate (adjusted) logistic regression confirmed them as independent predictors: college graduate or above (OR = 0.731), waist circumference (OR = 1.026), poverty-income ratio > 3 (OR = 0.750), PHQ-9 score (OR = 1.057), HDL-C (OR = 0.682), hemoglobin (OR = 0.884), RDW (OR = 1.052), BUN (OR = 1.104), globulin (OR = 1.026), and total cholesterol (OR = 0.644). Establishment of the nomogram model

A nomogram for predicting CMM risk was established incorporating the 10 independent predictors. The model was developed using the ‘rms’ package in R, with points assigned to each variable based on their respective regression coefficients. No additional variable selection was performed to maintain the integrity of the prior screening results. As visualized in Fig. 3

Performance and clinical utility

The nomogram demonstrated good discriminative ability with an AUC of 0.735 (95% CI: 0.723–0.748). Internal validation via 10-fold cross-validation confirmed model stability, yielding a consistent AUC of 0.736. Spiegelhalter’s z-test (z = -0.399, P = 0.655) and calibration curves indicated excellent agreement between predicted and observed probabilities. Furthermore, DCA showed that the nomogram provides a significant net benefit within a threshold probability range of 15% to 77%. As visualized in Fig. 4.

Discussion

Leveraging large-scale data from the U.S. NHANES, this study is the first to develop and validate a nomogram prediction model for CMM specifically in the older population. By innovatively integrating multi-dimensional predictors—including socioeconomic status (education level, poverty-income ratio), psychological assessment (PHQ-9 score), and novel hematological markers (RDW, globulin, among others)—the model achieved an AUC value of 0.735 and demonstrated significant clinical net benefits within a risk threshold of 15%-77%. This provides a robust quantitative tool for early stratified intervention in geriatric comorbidity.

Key findings and mechanism discussion

The 10 independent predictors identified were categorized into three dimensions. The selection of these variables followed a rigorous, two-stage statistical process to ensure model stability and reproducibility. Initially, LASSO regression was applied to 29 candidate variables, where Lambda.1se was utilized as the optimal tuning parameter to identify 10 characteristic predictors. These variables were subsequently entered into a multivariate logistic regression model, where all 10 variables demonstrated statistical significance as independent factors.

Metabolic indicators: Waist circumference (OR = 1.03) and low HDL-C (OR = 0.68), align with traditional cardiovascular risk models, supporting the pivotal role of central obesity and lipid metabolism dysregulation in CMM [19, 20]. Notably, although BMI was initially assessed, it was excluded during the LASSO phase, whereas waist circumference remained a significant independent predictor, suggesting that central adiposity may be a more sensitive indicator for CMM risk in older adults than general obesity. Total cholesterol showed a protective correlation (OR = 0.64), which may reflect the “cholesterol paradox” in the elderly, where lower cholesterol levels often indicate malnutrition or chronic frailty [21, 22].

Novel biomarkers: RDW (OR = 1.05) and globulin (OR = 1.03) are significant additions. Beyond their statistical correlation, these markers have distinct biological relevance to CMM. RDW is a robust indicator of systemic inflammation and oxidative stress; elevated RDW reflects impaired erythropoiesis and accelerated vascular aging, which are foundational to atherosclerosis and metabolic disorders [23]. Globulin, comprising various inflammatory proteins, serves as a surrogate for chronic immune activation, signaling a pro-inflammatory milieu that exacerbates insulin resistance and endothelial dysfunction [24].

Socio-psychological factors: The PHQ-9 score (OR = 1.06) and low education level (OR = 0.73) support the regulatory role of the “psycho-neuroendocrine” pathway in comorbidity [25]. Crucially, the relationship between these factors is increasingly recognized as a “vicious cycle” driven by shared inflammatory mechanisms. While our study is cross-sectional, prospective cohort studies have provided longitudinal evidence that depressive symptoms can precede and predict the future development of CMM [4, 6]. This potential causal link is likely mediated by the chronic activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and the sympathetic nervous system due to prolonged psychological stress, which triggers a sustained release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and accelerates metabolic decline [9, 23]. By integrating these markers, our model captures a “psycho-inflammatory-metabolic” axis, highlighting how psychological distress and systemic inflammation synergistically drive the progression of metabolic outcomes in older adults.

Therefore, these associations should be interpreted with caution and should not be construed as evidence of a definitive causal relationship between the predictors and CMM.

Model advantages and clinical value

Compared with traditional tools such as the Framingham score, the advantages of this model are as follows: (1) Comprehensive comorbidity assessment: It enables the holistic prediction of the coexistence of multiple metabolic and cardiovascular diseases, addressing the limitation of tools that focus on a single endpoint. (2) Geriatric-specific optimization: It incorporates sensitive indicators like PHQ-9 scores and RDW, which align with the unique pathophysiological characteristics of older populations. Specifically, this model differs from existing CMM risk models in its holistic variable selection and precise population focus. While previous studies often relied on purely metabolic or clinical factors (e.g., TyG index or single-disease history), our model integrates socioeconomic (poverty-income ratio) and psychological (PHQ-9) dimensions, which are pivotal but often overlooked in geriatric care. Unlike models developed for general adult populations, our study focuses exclusively on adults aged ≥ 65 years, accounting for the unique aging-related physiological shifts such as the “cholesterol paradox”. (3) Enhanced practicality: Based on clinical variables obtained from routine physical examinations, risk assessment can be completed without the need for specialized or costly testing.

Research limitations and improvement direction

There are several limitations in this study:

Cross-sectional design: The reliance on a cross-sectional study design is a primary limitation, as it precludes the ability to establish temporal precedence or determine causal links between the identified risk factors and the occurrence of CMM. While our nomogram provides a valuable tool for risk stratification, the results must be interpreted cautiously as observed correlations rather than causal effects. Future research utilizing longitudinal cohorts is essential to track the progression of these variables over time and confirm the potential causal pathways suggested by our analysis.

Missing data handling: We excluded all participants with missing information to ensure a complete-case analysis across the 29 candidate variables. While this approach maintains the integrity of the data used for model construction, it may introduce selection bias if the characteristics of excluded individuals differ systematically from those included in the final sample. Furthermore, although we assumed the data were Missing At Random (MAR), we cannot entirely rule out the possibility of Missing Not At Random (MNAR). If the missingness was related to unobserved health statuses, it could further bias the risk estimates and limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the reduction in sample size due to these exclusions could potentially decrease the statistical power and affect the generalizability of the findings. Suggestions for follow-up research include: 1) Establishing multinational, multi-center cohorts for external validation; 2) Integrating genomic and proteogenomic data to enhance prediction depth; 3) Developing a dynamic risk assessment system to track intervention effects.

Additionally, the reduction in sample size due to these exclusions could potentially decrease the statistical power and affect the generalizability of the findings. Suggestions for follow-up research include: 1) Establishing multinational, multi-center cohorts for external validation; 2) Integrating genomic and proteogenomic data to enhance prediction depth; 3) Developing a dynamic risk assessment system to track intervention effects.

Clinical application prospect

This model can be applied to: (1) Community screening: It facilitates the quick identification of high-risk individuals to enable accurate secondary prevention; (2) Clinical decision-making: It guides priorities for individual intervention, such as strengthening psychological intervention for those with high depression scores; (3) Health policy: It supports optimizing resource allocation for the prevention and control of comorbidity in the elderly. It is suggested that the model be embedded into electronic health record systems in combination with mobile health technology to enable dynamic risk monitoring.

Conclusion

The nomogram model constructed in this study is the first multi-dimensional prediction tool specifically for CMM in older adults, demonstrating acceptable discrimination (AUC > 0.70) and clinical practicability. It serves as a valuable quantitative tool for the individualized prevention and control of CMM. In the future, further refinement of the model through multi-center collaboration is needed to promote its translation and application amid global aging.
