Effect of COVID-19 NHS breast screening pause on breast cancer presentation and management: an insight into overdiagnosis
Authors: Rachel Aquilina, Jajini Varghese, Jayant S Vaidya, Uma J Vaidya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:To ascertain the impact of a 3-month pause in the NHS Breast Screening Programme (NHSBSP) during COVID-19 on numbers, characteristics and outcomes of breast cancer patientsDesign:We compared numbers, referral sources (NHSBSP/GP), tumour characteristics, treatments, and survival before and during COVID-19 using chi-square tests and Kaplan-Meier estimates. Patients were grouped into: (a) all cases from 2019 to 2021 (n = 3353) and (b) six selected months in 2019 and 2020 (n = 325).Setting:The study was conducted with the breast units of two large London NHS hospitals as a local audit to understand the impact of COVID-19 on breast cancer management.Participants:Patients diagnosed with breast cancer from 2019 to 2021 were analysed.Main outcome measures:Temporal variation in the number of breast cancer cases by referral source and the impact of the pause in screening mammography due to the COVID-19 pandemic on changes in tumour characteristics, treatments and survival.Results:Overall, there were 31% fewer patients in 2020 vs. 2019 (886 vs. 1281, p = .002), due to fewer screen-detected (851 vs. 398) rather than symptomatic cases (432 vs. 459), p < .00001), with no corresponding increase in 2021 (n = 1186) In the detailed analysis, there were significantly fewer grade 1 cancers (11/120 vs. 37/204, p = .028) in 2020 vs. 2019, but there was no stage shift from smaller or less advanced (node-negative) tumours to larger or more advanced tumours, and no difference in extent of surgery, i.e., ratios of breast conservation to mastectomy, or sentinel node biopsy to axillary clearance. There was no difference in time from referral to diagnosis between the two time periods. The median follow-up was 3.8 years; K-M survival curves overlapped, with no difference in 3-year survival (90.5% vs. 91.7%, HR=1.09, p = .83).Conclusions:During the COVID-19-induced NHSBSP pause, good-prognosis (grade 1) cancers reduced without an increase in larger, or node-positive cancers, or more extensive surgery, and 3-year survival remained unchanged, suggesting that pausing NHSBSP may have avoided overdiagnosis without causing harm.Keywords: Breast cancer, royal free hospital, Barnet and chase farm hospitals, national health service breast screening programme, COVID-19, pandemic, screening

Key messages

What is already known: The NHS breast cancer screening programme (NHSBSP) was paused during the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic and it was suggested that this pause should lead to a higher number of advanced breast cancers and increased breast cancer mortality.

What this study adds: We found that in the months after the NHSBSP pause during the early part of the COVID-19 pandemic, there was no increase either in number of breast cancers, or number of more advanced breast cancers, and breast cancer survival up to 3 years did not change, but detection of good prognosis cancers – contributing to overdiagnosis – was reduced.

How this study might affect research, practice or policy: The finding that COVID-19 induced pause seems to have avoided overdiagnosis without jeopardising outcomes from breast cancer should be taken into consideration when developing further research in other centres in the UK and for strategies to improve the balance of benefit versus harm of cancer screening

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic was declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern on the 20th of January 2020.1,2 Following the suspension of non-urgent medical treatment during the pandemic, screening for breast cancer was deprioritised – redirecting staff and resources towards COVID-19 management. 3 With breast cancer being one of the most common causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, contributing to over 2.3 million new cases annually, 4 it is vital to assess the impact of this disruption on breast cancer treatments and mortality rates. Breast cancer screening programmes aim to find cancer at an earlier stage and can reduce breast cancer mortality (relative risk reduction 15%). 5 Hence, it was expected that following the screening interruption, patients may require more extensive treatments with a potential increase in mortality.6,7

However, it is well known that screening mammography leads to overdiagnosis – diagnosis and treatment of cancers that may never have become clinically apparent.8–10 One illustration of this phenomenon is figure 1.8,10 In the UK, screening is offered every 3 years. In the Fife region in Scotland, the screening van visits all areas of Fife from January to December every 3 years; after a particular year of screening, no screening occurs in the intervening 2 years. This unique method of delivering screening makes it easy to plot the numbers of patients diagnosed, when the population was being screening versus when there was no screening, exactly coinciding with a calendar year. In Figure 1, we can see that on introduction of screening mammography in 1991, there was a clear increase in the number of invasive breast cancers in the Fife region in Scotland (from the background of 90–150, per 100,000 population). However, despite allegedly diagnosing all these cancers cancer earlier than their clinical presentation, we see no dip in the incidence in the subsequent years, when these cancers would have otherwise presented. This pattern keeps repeating demonstrating that amongst the patients diagnosed during the screening years, a large number of patients may never have presented clinically – they were being overdiagnosed.

The duration of the interruption of breast cancer screening programmes varied widely between and within countries, but in most cases, screening programmes were suspended for up to 6 months. 5 Modelling studies have suggested that a 3-month interruption is likely to result in missing 7% of invasive cancers.11,12 Numerous models predicted a major backlog resulting in a substantial increase in cases following the resumption of the National Health Service Breast Screening Programme (NHSBSP) – causing significant delays in diagnosis and treatment.13,14

In this context, we anticipated a greater proportion of clinically advanced tumours during the interruption as smaller tumours would go undetected. These latter previously undetected cancers would be expected to present months later, in a more clinically advanced stage. We might expect a higher mortality from breast cancer for patients diagnosed during and after the period of pause.

Our research aims to assess the validity of this hypothesis and analyse the impact of this pause in breast cancer screening in the real-world clinical setting.

Methods

To determine the impact of COVID-19 on breast cancer presentation, diagnosis, treatment, and outcomes, we analysed a large data set inclusive of all patients admitted with breast cancer between 2019 and 2021 at the Royal Free Hospital (RFH) and the Barnet and Chase Farm Hospitals (BCFH). We analysed data on age, ethnicity, referral date, and diagnosis date on all patients diagnosed with breast cancer during these 3 years.

For a more detailed analysis of the impact of the first UK lockdown and the associated pause in the NHSBSP on the individual patient and tumour characteristics, treatments as well as their outcomes, a smaller data subset was used to explore 6 months in 2019, before the pandemic began, and 6 months in 2020, during the pandemic. We chose the months of April to August of 2020 because these were the months when the COVID-19 pandemic had caused the largest disruption. In addition, we chose the month of October because it is celebrated as the ‘breast cancer awareness month’ and referrals usually peak in this month. As we had chosen these months during 2020, we also chose the same months during 2019 so they would be the most comparable periods.

For this smaller cohort of patients, electronic patient records, imaging and pathology reports, and clinic letters were reviewed and data about tumour size, grade, type, receptor status, lymph nodes status, date of surgery, surgery type, cause of death, and date of death (where applicable) were extracted. The last follow-up status for all patients was determined in August 2023.

The chi-square test was used for statistical analysis, apart from time to diagnosis for which two-sample Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann–Whitney) test was used. All p-values were obtained at the standard 5% significance level. Kaplan–Meier graphs were plotted to estimate 3-year survival rates.

This study has been registered as a local audit to understand the impact of COVID-19 on breast cancer management.

Results

From the larger dataset, between January 2019 and December 2021, a total of 3353 patients were diagnosed with breast cancer at the Royal Free and Barnet and Chase Farm Hospitals. Of these, 1281 were diagnosed in 2019, 886 in 2020, and 1186 in 2021. Figure 2 shows how the numbers were greatly reduced in 2020, mainly between April and August 2020 but did not increase in the following months.

The data from the larger dataset, depicted in Figure 2 also show a significant decrease in NHSBSP-referred patients in 2020 (p < .00001, compared with 2019), while there was no change in GP referred patients across the 3 years. One would have expected those patients who ‘missed’ being diagnosed in 2020 would have presented ‘late’ in 2021, but there was no increase in numbers during 2021, either in symptomatic GP referrals or NHSBSP.

For the smaller data-set of patients diagnosed in the chosen 6-month periods 204 patients were diagnosed in 2019 and 121 patients in 2020.

Between 2019 and 2020, the number of GP-referred patients reduced from 142 to 109, but NHSBSP-referred patients reduced by a much greater level from 60 to 12 (23% reduction vs. 80% reduction, chi2 = 17.1, p < .0001 for the difference in reduction).

There was a lower proportion of patients with grade 1 tumours in 2020 (n = 11/121) compared with 2019 (n = 37/204, chi2 = 4.93, p = .026).

There was no statistically significant change in the proportion of patients receiving surgery between 2019 and 2020 (197/204 vs. 113/121, i.e. 96.6% vs. 93.3%, chi2 = 1.75, p = .187). The total number of surgeries was reduced in 2020, but there was no significant difference in the proportion of patients having breast conservation (vs. mastectomy) between 2019 and 2020 (chi2 = 0.3719, p = .542): breast conservations were 81/121 patients in 2020 versus 134/204 in 2019, and the number of mastectomies were 32/121 in 2020 and 62/204 in 2019. Similarly there was no difference in the ratio of sentinel node biopsy: axillary clearances (chi2 = 0.0288, p = .865) (Figure 3). Both these data suggest that there was no stage shift from smaller or less advanced tumours to larger more advanced tumours that would have need larger surgery (mastectomy rather than breast conservation or more axillary clearances rather than sentinel node biopsies).

Discussion

Following the initiation of the first nationwide COVID-19 lockdown on the 23rd of March 2020, 15 along with the paused breast screening by NHSBSP from March to May 2020 at Barnet and Chase Farm and the Royal Free Hospital, the number of breast cancer patients being diagnosed saw a decline of over 30% (from 1281 to 886 cases).

Pausing breast cancer screening programmes during COVID-19 has been predicted to have a deleterious impact on breast cancer patients.15,16 However, there have not been any studies suggesting that by pausing screening programmes we may be avoiding the diagnosis of low-grade, good-prognosis cancers. These are the types of cancers that contribute most to the prevalence of overdiagnosis, that is well recognised today.

The COVID-19 pandemic offered a unique opportunity to explore the effect of the pause in the NHSBSP on breast cancer presentation and outcomes. Our study took place across two London NHS trusts and included a large number of breast cancer patients (n = 3353).

Amongst the tumour characteristics studied, the grade of the tumour is indicative of aggressiveness the tumour cells, while the stage (size and lymph node involvement) is more indicative of how advanced the tumour is i.e. how long it has been in the body and grown or spread. In the more detailed analysis of our smaller cohort (n = 325), we found a decrease in diagnosis of cancers associated with an inherently good prognosis (grade 1), with no change in diagnosis of more clinically advanced cancers, as judged by size, nodal status, either during these months or subsequently. This observation is strengthened by the fact that there was no increase in the number of mastectomies or axillary clearances during or subsequent to the COVID-19-induced pause in screening.

The absence of a great increase in the number of patients being diagnosed with breast cancer following the resumption of the NHSBSP, coupled with the lack of increase in more advanced cancers during or after the COVID-19 pandemic offer us some insight into overdiagnosis. The data show that the pause during the pandemic led to reduced diagnosis of the ‘good prognosis’ grade 1 cancers that contribute most to overdiagnosis, a well recognised effect of screening mammography: that is, the diagnosis by screening of slow-growing tumours which may not become apparent in the patient's lifetime. Without a routine screening programme in place, these patients may not present to the clinic.

It is important for policymakers and clinicians to recognise the potential of overdiagnosis by screening as avoiding it could prevent unnecessary emotional distress and reduce unnecessary treatment. Patients have been shown to face substantial anguish if they are not informed about the potential for overdiagnosis in advance of attending screening and discover it after their diagnosis. 17 Although we know that some patients diagnosed with screening mammography will have been overdiagnosed, the main obstacle in trying to prevent overdiagnosis is that we still do not know how to separate potentially fatal and non-fatal cancers once they are diagnosed. However, as screening inevitably leads to overdiagnosis, its effects could be reduced by adopting treatments that are more convenient and safer for patients as suggested by Prof Michael Marmot's committee who produced a comprehensive report on screening commissioned by the Department of Health – treatments such as using targeted intraoperative radiotherapy (TARGIT-IORT) during lumpectomy surgery rather than a post-operative radiotherapy course.18–24

The strength of this study is the large complete data set from two major NHS hospitals. For the smaller data set, we were able to analyse near complete clinicopathological data (only 4% unknown) as well as survival outcomes for up to 3 and 4 years for the two sets of patients from 2020 and 2019. A longer follow-up would be desirable; however, if there was a great impact on mortality, we could expect it to start becoming apparent in the first 3–4 years. One weakness of this study is that we did not assess how the number of patients receiving chemotherapy changed during the period, however, if the absolute number of patients reduced and those with more advanced tumours did not increase, then it is unlikely that more patients received chemotherapy.

Sud et al. anticipated an additional 734 breast cancer related deaths a because of the 3-month delay in patient presentation, 16 while Duffy et al. anticipated an excess of up to 647 breast cancer related deaths. 15 This would amount to a 7% (647/11,500 deaths per year in the UK) relative increase in deaths.

In reality, in our study the actuarial estimated 3-year mortality was 9.5% and 8.3% (p = .83) of patients diagnosed in 2020 and 2019. However, the denominator – the number patients diagnosed were nearly half of 2019 in 2020 (121 vs. 204). So, the total number of patients who died in 2020 versus 2019 was actually much lower (11 vs. 20), and the absolute proportion was also not different – 11 of 121 diagnosed in 2020 died, and 20 of 204 diagnosed in 2019, died during the whole follow-up period.

Conclusion

There was widespread expectation that the lack of breast cancer screening between March and May 2020 would cause a large increase in the number of advanced breast cancer diagnoses. We found that such an increase was not evident in the year following the pause in breast cancer screening. The pause in NHSBSP reduced the proportion of good prognosis (grade 1) cancers, but did not have a significant impact on the proportion of clinically advanced cancers and those requiring more extensive surgery, suggesting that the pause in screening avoided overdiagnosis of cancer in some women.
