The gut commensal Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron harbors prevalent class A and D β-lactamases with cross-taxa dissemination potential
Authors: Tao Song, Zhangqun Hou, Chuan Zhang, Tong Luo, Peiting Wen, Ling Mei, Dezhi Yuan, Xue Shen, Hongbo Dong, Kelei Zhao, Yiwen Chu, Xinrong Wang, Fengzheng Chen, Wenxia Sun, Jiafu Lin, Wenjun Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The abundant gut commensal Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron is repeatedly challenged by β-lactam exposure in the human intestine, yet its β-lactamase repertoire and dissemination potential remain incompletely characterized. Here, we screened 626 publicly available B. thetaiotaomicron genomes and identified 1059 putative β-lactamase homologs, all belonging to class A or class D families. Four highly prevalent representatives—BTA-1 and CfxA-3 (class A) and OXA-347 and OXA-1327 (class D)—were prioritized for experimental validation. Heterologous expression in Escherichia coli revealed heterogeneous resistance phenotypes, with ones conferring only modest MIC increases (e.g., OXA-347, ~2-fold for amoxicillin) and others producing strong resistance to penicillins (e.g., BTA-1caused a 256-fold increase in amoxicillin MIC). Purified BTA-1 and OXA-347 were active under gut-relevant conditions, with pH optima at 7 and 8 and temperature optima at 40 °C and 30 °C, respectively. Notably, OXA-347 hydrolyzed representatives of penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, and monobactams, and mass spectrometry confirmed β-lactam ring opening. Beyond B. thetaiotaomicron, these β-lactamase alleles were detected across multiple gut commensal genera as well as taxa annotated as opportunistic pathogens. Genomic context analyses showed similar gene-cluster patterns in B. thetaiotaomicron, across gut commensal genera, and in opportunistic-pathogen–associated taxa. Collectively, our findings clarify the diversity, activity, and distribution of B. thetaiotaomicron–associated β-lactamases across the gut microbiome and clinically relevant taxa.

Introduction

Bacteroides species are dominant and functionally important members of the human gut microbiota, widely recognized for their central roles in polysaccharide degradation, nutrient metabolism, and host–microbiota interactions1–3. Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron is a dominant keystone member of the human gut microbiota, recognized for its remarkable ability to degrade a wide range of complex dietary polysaccharides and host-derived glycans through extensive glycan utilization loci (PULs)4. This metabolic activity allows B. thetaiotaomicron to generate short-chain fatty acids which serve as energy sources for host epithelial cells, contribute to intestinal homeostasis, and support the overall stability and resilience of the gut microbial community5,6. Beyond its metabolic role, B. thetaiotaomicron also interacts with the host immune system7, modulating inflammatory responses and promoting regulatory T cell populations, thereby supporting immune balance and contributing to tolerance against potentially harmful antigens8. Together, these features underpin B. thetaiotaomicron’s central role in maintaining gut health and highlight its potential as a next-generation probiotic, while also emphasizing the need to consider its broader ecological and clinical implications9.

Although B. thetaiotaomicron shows great promise as a next-generation probiotic, its role as a reservoir for antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) presents significant safety concerns10. The human gut microbiota is a major hotspot for ARG accumulation and horizontal gene transfer (HGT), providing a dynamic environment in which resistance determinants can spread among commensals and under certain conditions, to pathogenic bacteria11,12. Within this context, B. thetaiotaomicron has been reported to harbor several clinically relevant ARGs, including β-lactamases (cfxA), macrolide resistance genes (ermF), and tetracycline resistance genes (tetQ/tetX), some of which are associated with mobile genetic elements capable of mediating HGT13,14. These observations highlight the potential for B. thetaiotaomicron to contribute to the dissemination of resistance traits within the gut microbial community. However, systematic studies evaluating the diversity, substrate specificity, and risk of B. thetaiotaomicron-derived ARGs remain limited, underscoring the need for a comprehensive assessment before their therapeutic application.

Beta-lactam antibiotics, commonly used in human medicine, apply strong selective pressure on the gut microbiota, driving the evolution and adaptation of resident bacterial populations15. In the 1980s, researchers discovered that B. thetaiotaomicron produces β-lactamases, which were later identified as Ambler class A enzymes conferring resistance to cefoxitin and other β-lactam antibiotics. This enzyme is also referred to as BT_4507 in genomic studies16. Subsequent work demonstrated that deletion of this gene imposes a fitness cost on B. thetaiotaomicron in the presence of β-lactam antibiotics, highlighting its functional importance for bacterial survival17. Moreover, BT_4507 can be packaged into outer membrane vesicles, which are capable of protecting other gut commensals and potentially pathogenic bacteria from β-lactam-mediated killing18. To date, only a class A β-lactamase (BT_4507) has been functionally characterized in B. thetaiotaomicron, and it remains unclear whether additional β-lactamases of other Ambler classes are present, or what their potential for HGT and contribution to antibiotic resistance might be.

Despite its recognized probiotic potential, B. thetaiotaomicron harbors β-lactamases whose diversity, substrate specificity, and capacity for HGT remain largely unknown, representing a critical gap in understanding their ecological and clinical implications. In this study, we systematically identify and characterize B. thetaiotaomicron-derived β-lactamases, investigate their biochemical properties and genetic contexts, and assess their potential for dissemination, providing a comprehensive framework to evaluate both gut microbial ecology and health risk.

Methods

Screening for β-lactamases in B. thetaiotaomicron

Genome sequences of B. thetaiotaomicron were retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) on June 15, 2024. All predicted protein-coding sequences were extracted and compiled into a nonredundant FASTA dataset to enable large-scale homology screening. Experimentally validated β-lactamase sequences were obtained from the β-Lactamase Database (BLDB: http://bldb.eu/), and only entries supported by peer-reviewed literature were included to ensure annotation reliability.

Sequence searches were conducted using DIAMOND19 with curated β-lactamase sequences as queries. An E-value cutoff of 1 × 10−10, ≥40% amino-acid identity, ≥80% alignment coverage was applied to minimize false positives. Resulting hits were manually inspected to remove incomplete or truncated sequences, producing a high-confidence dataset suitable for downstream phylogenetic, clustering, and functional analyzes.

3D structure prediction

The 3D structure of β-lactamase was predicted using ColabFold, along with the 3D structure prediction of His-tagged β-lactamase. The parameter settings were as follows : number_recycles 50, max_msa 512–1024, number_relax 5, rank_plddt, and number_models 520. The protein structures were visualized using ChimeraX21.

Gene synthesis and plasmid construction

Open reading frames encoding four β-lactamases—BTA-1 (protein ID: RGY21831.1), CfxA-3 (protein ID: WP_004348227.1), OXA-347 (protein ID: RHD78488.1) and OXA-1327 (protein ID: KAB4306926.1)—were codon-optimized for Escherichia coli expression and synthesized commercially (GenScript, China). Each gene was cloned into the pET-28a(+) vector using NdeI and XhoI restriction sites, resulting in constructs encoding C-terminal His-tagged proteins. Plasmid integrity was verified by Sanger sequencing.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination

MICs were determined for ten β-lactam antibiotics—penicillin K (PG), ampicillin (AMP), amoxicillin (AMX), cefalexin (CLX), cefonicid sodium (CNS), cefotaxime (CTX), ceftazidime (CAZ), cefepime (FEP), imipenem (IPM), and aztreonam (AZT)—using the broth microdilution method following CLSI guidelines. Overnight cultures of E. coli BL21(DE3) expressing recombinant enzymes were diluted 1:1000 in fresh Mueller–Hinton (MH) broth and inoculated into 96-well plates containing twofold serial dilutions of each antibiotic. Wells without antibiotics served as positive growth controls, and sterile medium served as negative controls. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 20 h, and bacterial growth was quantified by measuring OD600nm with a microplate reader. MIC was defined as the lowest antibiotic concentration that completely inhibited visible growth. All susceptibility assays were performed in recombinant E. coli as a standardized heterologous host background.

Spot assay for antibiotic resistance verification

Recombinant E. coli strains were cultured overnight at 37 °C to an OD600 ≈ 1.0. Serial tenfold dilutions (10⁻¹–10⁻⁵) were plated on LB agar containing AMX (0.2 μg/mL), CTX (0.02 μg/mL), IPM (0.04 μg/mL), or AZT (0.02 μg/mL). Growth patterns were compared with the empty-vector control (pET-28a) to validate resistance phenotypes.

Protein expression and purification

Recombinant E. coli BL21(DE3) strains were cultured overnight in LB medium containing kanamycin (100 μg/mL) at 37 °C. Cultures were diluted 1:100 into fresh LB and grown to OD600nm = 0.6, followed by induction with 1 mM IPTG at 25 °C for 12 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), resuspended in 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.0), and lysed by sonication on ice. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 10 min), and supernatants were loaded onto HisTrap affinity columns (GE Healthcare). Proteins were eluted with an imidazole gradient, and purity and molecular weight were assessed by SDS–PAGE. Protein concentrations were determined spectrophotometrically at A280.

Enzyme activity assay

Enzymatic activity was defined as the amount of enzyme that catalyzed the hydrolysis of substrate equivalent to the formation of 1 μM penicilloic acid per minute under the assay conditions. Reactions were performed in 1 mL containing 200 μg/mL AMP in 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.0) at 37 °C for 15 min and were terminated by boiling for 5 min. The substrate-only control was subjected to the same boiling treatment in parallel to evaluate the effect of heating alone on AMP, and boiling for 5 min showed negligible effects under these conditions. Negative controls contained enzyme plus buffer without substrate, and substrate-only controls contained substrate plus buffer without enzyme.

Agar diffusion assay for hydrolyzed products

The antibacterial activity of β-lactam antibiotics before and after enzymatic hydrolysis was evaluated using agar well diffusion with E. coli ATCC 25922 as the indicator strain. A mixture of 400 μL overnight culture and 100 mL molten LB agar was poured into 9 cm Petri dishes. Wells (4.5 mm diameter) were loaded with enzyme–antibiotic reaction mixtures (20 μg/mL antibiotic, incubated at 37 °C for 3 h). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and zones of inhibition were measured to assess residual activity.

Mass spectrometric confirmation of hydrolysis

Hydrolysis of PG, AMP, AMX, CLX, CNS, CTX, CAZ, FEP, IPM and AZT was confirmed by electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI–MS). Reaction mixtures (100 μL purified enzyme + 900 μL 50 μg/mL antibiotic) were incubated at 37 °C for 3 h and filtered through 0.22 μm membranes. Mass spectra were acquired in both positive (ESI⁺, 5500 V) and negative (ESI⁻, 4500 V) ionization modes with m/z 10–1000, curtain gas 40 psi, ion source gases 1 and 2 at 30 psi, drying gas 450 °C, and flow rate 5 L/min.

Effect of temperature and pH on enzyme activity and stability

Enzymatic activity was measured using AMP as the substrate. Optimal temperatures were determined at 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 °C, with relative activity normalized to the maximum (100%). Thermal stability was assessed by pre-incubating enzymes at each temperature for 1 h, followed by cooling on ice and residual activity measurement. Optimal pH was determined using Britton–Robinson buffers (pH 3–11). pH stability was evaluated by incubating enzymes in the same buffers at 4 °C for 1 h before measuring activity. Relative activities were expressed as percentages of maximal activity.

Enzyme reaction kinetics

Under optimal conditions, the effects of varying substrate concentrations (S) of nitrocefin (19.4–193.6 μM) on the kinetic parameters of β-lactamase, including the Michaelis constant (Km) and the maximum reaction rate (Vmax), were investigated. The initial reaction rates (V) were determined, and the kinetic parameters were obtained by linear regression analysis using the Lineweaver–Burk double-reciprocal plot.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyzes were conducted using one-way analysis of variance in GraphPad Prism (version 9.0), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Screening of β-lactamases in B. thetaiotaomicron genomes

We retrieved 626 publicly available B. thetaiotaomicron genomes comprising 3,195,751 predicted proteins. Protein sequences were searched against experimentally validated β-lactamases curated in CARD and BLDB using thresholds of ≥40% amino-acid identity, ≥80% alignment coverage, and an E-value < 1 × 10⁻¹⁰. This screen returned 1059 putative β-lactamase-like hits, all assigned to class A or class D families, whereas no class B or class C candidates met these criteria (Fig. 1A). Clustering at 80% sequence identity grouped the hits into 55 clusters (Supplementary data 1), dominated by BTA-1 (48.8%, n = 517), CfxA-3 (9.6%, n = 102), OXA-347 (15.7%, n = 166), and OXA-1327 (3.8%, n = 40). BTA-1, CfxA-3, and OXA-347 were identical to previously characterized β-lactamases, whereas OXA-1327 shared 46% identity with its closest curated homolog (Supplementary material 1A). Domain annotation indicated that the candidates contained canonical class A or class D β-lactamase domains and were predicted to lack signal peptides (Supplementary material 1B). Despite moderate sequence identity between BTA-1 and CfxA-3 (42%), structural alignment of predicted models showed a high fold similarity (TM-score 0.92) (Fig. 1B, C, and Supplementary material 1C, D). Likewise, OXA-347 and OXA-1327 displayed conserved class D folds (TM-score 0.85) (Fig. 1D, E, and Supplementary material 1C, D), supporting OXA-1327 as a divergent member of the β-lactamase family.

Phenotypic and biochemical validation of β-lactamase activity

The four candidate β-lactamase genes (blaBTA-1, blaOXA-347, blaOXA-1327, and blaCfxA-3) were expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3), and resistance phenotypes were quantified by MIC assays. In contrast, CfxA-3 conferred only modest (~2-fold) increases against penicillin G, ampicillin, and amoxicillin, consistent with a narrower phenotypic spectrum. Spot assays on antibiotic-supplemented agar supported these trends, with all four β-lactamase-expressing strains growing on amoxicillin-containing plates while the empty-vector control failed to grow (Fig. 2B). Notably, growth advantages in spot assays were not always reflected in broth MIC measurements; for example, BTA-1 enabled visible growth on aztreonam- and cefotaxime-containing plates despite little or no MIC shift under standard microdilution conditions. These phenotypes were measured in a heterologous E. coli background and thus reflect resistance-associated effects in a surrogate host rather than direct quantitative estimates in native B. thetaiotaomicron.

Enzymatic hydrolysis and substrate specificity

To further examine substrate specificity, crude extracts were incubated with representative β-lactam antibiotics and residual activity was assessed by inhibition-zone assays. CfxA-3 and OXA-1327 predominantly reduced inhibition zones for penicillin-type substrates, consistent with preferential activity toward penicillins. In contrast, BTA-1 (class A) and OXA-347 (class D) displayed broader activity profiles in this assay. Notably, OXA-347 reduced inhibition zones for representatives of penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, and monobactams, consistent with a broad hydrolytic spectrum (Fig. 2C, D).

Biochemical analysis of β-lactamases

We attempted to purify all four enzymes; however, only BTA-1 and OXA-347 were obtained in soluble form at sufficient yield for biochemical analyzes (Fig. 3A, and Supplementary material 2). Purified BTA-1 and OXA-347 retained >80% of maximal activity between 20 and 40 °C, with temperature optima at 40 °C and 30 °C, respectively (Fig. 3B). Thermal stability differed: BTA-1 retained ~80% activity after 1 h at 50 °C, whereas OXA-347 was largely inactivated under the same condition (Fig. 3C). Both enzymes were active over a broad pH range (pH 6–11), with pH optima at 7 (BTA-1) and 8 (OXA-347) (Fig. 3D, E). The enzymatic kinetic properties of BTA-1 and OXA-347 were compared. The results showed that the Vmax of BTA-1 (15.61 μM·min−1) was markedly higher than that of OXA-347 (6.858 μM·min−1), whereas their Km values were comparable (258.9 μM and 273 μM, respectively). These findings indicate that BTA-1 showed a higher apparent maximal reaction rate under the assay conditions, while the two enzymes displayed similar substrate affinities (Supplementary material 3). Taxonomic distribution and ecological sources

We investigated the taxonomic distribution of homologs to four representative β-lactamase genes (blaBTA-1, blaCfxA-3, blaOXA-347, and blaOXA-1327) across publicly available bacterial genomes. A total of 3385 bacterial genomes were found to carry β-lactamase genes. The bacteria carrying lactamases were predominantly from phylum Bacteroidota (98.3%), with minor contributions from Bacillota (0.7%) and Pseudomonadota (0.2%). Within Bacteroidota, the class Bacteroidia was dominant, with the order Bacteroidales further subdivided into multiple families, among which Bacteroidaceae and Prevotellaceae were most abundant. At the genus level, Bacteroides accounted for 51.8% of genomes with genus-level assignments, followed by Phocaeicola (11.6%) and Prevotella (4.3%). Gene-specific host-range analysis indicated marked differences among alleles. blaBTA-1 was largely restricted to Bacteroides (99.4%, n = 945), whereas blaCfxA-3, blaOXA-347, and blaOXA-1327 were detected across a broader set of genera, including Phocaeicola, Segatella, Parabacteroides, and Riemerella (Fig. 5B). At the species level, blaCfxA-3 was most frequently detected in Riemerella anatipestifer (n = 176), B. thetaiotaomicron (n = 164), and Bacteroides ovatus (n = 35). In contrast, blaBTA-1 was most common in B. thetaiotaomicron (n = 579), B. ovatus (n = 198), and Bacteroides xylanisolvens (n = 89). blaOXA-347 was predominantly associated with R. anatipestifer (n = 176), B. thetaiotaomicron (n = 164), and B. ovatus (n = 35), whereas blaOXA-1327 was more frequently found in B. thetaiotaomicron (n = 41), B. ovatus (n = 23), and Elizabethkingia anophelis (n = 20) (Fig. 5C). Notably, B. thetaiotaomicron appeared as a recurrent host across all four alleles, serving as a primary or secondary source in most cases. Together, these host-distribution patterns suggest that the relevance of B. thetaiotaomicron-associated β-lactamase alleles extends beyond a single species and should be considered in the broader context of Bacteroides and related gut-associated bacteria.

Across the 3385 genomes, blaCfxA-3 was the most prevalent allele (n = 1724), followed by blaBTA-1 (n = 951), blaOXA-347 (n = 537), and blaOXA-1327 (n = 173). Co-occurrence analysis showed that blaBTA-1 and blaCfxA-3 co-occurred in 206 genomes, whereas blaOXA-347 and blaBTA-1 co-occurred in 175 genomes. Instances of multi-allele carriage were also observed; for example, blaOXA-1327, blaBTA-1, and blaCfxA-3 were simultaneously present in 32 genomes (Fig. 5D).

Ecological distribution of β-lactamases

Isolation-source metadata indicated that most β-lactamase–carrying genomes were recovered from human-associated samples (Homo sapiens; 81.3%, n = 2416), with smaller contributions from ducks (5.5%, n = 163), pigsty-associated environments (2.2%, n = 64), Pongo pygmaeus (1.4%, n = 42), and records lacking clear source annotation (6.8%, n = 203) (Fig. 5E, and Supplementary data 3). Among human-associated genomes with available body-site annotations, the majority were derived from gastrointestinal sources, with fecal and intestinal samples accounting for 92.4% (n = 908) of annotated occurrences. Other body sites, including the oral cavity (3.2%, n = 31), blood (3.1%, n = 30), and lung (0.7%, n = 7), were less frequently represented in the available metadata (Fig. 5F, and Supplementary data 3).

Geographic metadata further showed that β-lactamase–carrying genomes were reported across six continents, with the largest numbers originating from North America (n = 1784), followed by Asia (n = 659) and Europe (n = 315) (Fig. 6A, and Supplementary data 3). In total, genomes were assigned to 33 countries; seven countries contributed more than 50 genomes, including the United States, China, Australia, Canada, Slovenia, Germany, and Japan. The United States and China accounted for the largest shares (n = 1699 and n = 552, respectively) (Fig. 6B, and Supplementary data 3). A quantitative summary of flanking-gene composition showed that resistance-associated genes comprised >50% of neighborhood annotations for all four loci, with blaOXA-347 exhibiting the highest proportion (62.6%) (Fig. 7B). Mobility-related signatures were detected around each locus and were most abundant in blaOXA-347 neighborhoods. Regulatory/DNA-binding–related annotations were mainly observed near blaCfxA-3 and blaOXA-1327, whereas transporter-associated annotations were primarily associated with blaOXA-347 (8.9%) (Fig. 7B). Together, these patterns indicate locus-specific differences in neighborhood composition and suggest varying degrees of association with mobility-related genetic features among the four β-lactamase alleles.

Discussion

β-lactamases in B. thetaiotaomicron

This study expands the catalog of β-lactamases in the gut commensal B. thetaiotaomicron and adds functional context to prevalent alleles. By screening 626 B. thetaiotaomicron genomes, we identified a large set of β-lactamase homologs that, under our search criteria, were assigned to class A and class D families. Notably, we detected a divergent class D candidate (OXA-1327) that shows low sequence identity to curated enzymes yet retains a conserved β-lactamase fold based on structural comparisons, highlighting that sequence similarity alone may underestimate β-lactamase diversity in commensal genomes. Together with three highly prevalent alleles (BTA-1, CfxA-3, and OXA-347), these findings refine the current view of the gut resistome by linking genomic prevalence to experimentally testable enzyme candidates22. While the forces shaping this diversity remain to be established, the observed allele heterogeneity is consistent with dynamic genetic turnover in gut-associated bacteria and suggests that β-lactamase diversity in B. thetaiotaomicron should be interpreted within the broader ecological and evolutionary context of the genus Bacteroides23–25.

Functional plasticity and ecological adaptation of β-lactamases

Functional assays revealed pronounced heterogeneity among the four prevalent β-lactamases, ranging from narrow penicillin-biased activity to broader hydrolytic profiles. In heterologous E. coli expression, BTA-1 conferred high-level resistance to penicillin-type antibiotics, whereas CfxA-3 and OXA-1327 produced more modest, substrate-restricted phenotypes, consistent with differences in catalytic scope and/or expression-dependent effects. OXA-347 showed the broadest activity in our assays, with hydrolysis detected for representatives of multiple β-lactam subclasses, including penicillins, cephalosporins, monobactams, and carbapenems; establishing the magnitude of this breadth will benefit from quantitative kinetics using purified enzyme and clinically relevant substrates. We also observed that agar-based spot assays sometimes revealed growth advantages not captured by broth MIC measurements, which may reflect inoculum effects, local antibiotic degradation, or condition-dependent expression and localization differences in the heterologous host. Collectively, these results point to substantial functional variability among prevalent commensal β-lactamases and motivate mechanistic work to define how enzyme activity, expression, and cellular context jointly shape resistance phenotypes26.

Dissemination patterns of β-lactamase alleles

Our comparative analyzes outline a hierarchical dissemination framework for B. thetaiotaomicron–associated β-lactamase alleles. First, allele distributions and co-occurrence patterns are consistent with circulation among B. thetaiotaomicron strains. Second, several alleles show a broader host range across gut commensal genera, supporting inter-genus spread within commensal communities. Third, alleles were enriched in taxa annotated as opportunistic pathogens, and their loci frequently co-localized with mobility-related signatures in resistance-enriched neighborhoods, a pattern consistent with cross-species dissemination potential27,28. Although these observations do not by themselves demonstrate transfer events, they provide a data-driven basis to prioritize β-lactamases candidates for risk-aware surveillance and for targeted mobility testing in gut-relevant models.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be noted. First, resistance phenotypes were assessed in a heterologous E. coli system rather than in native B. thetaiotaomicron. Although native-host validation would enhance the physiological relevance of these findings, such analyzes would require additional development of anaerobic, host-adapted expression and susceptibility-testing approaches and were therefore beyond the scope of the current study. Future work should address this issue through native-host characterization in Bacteroides. Second, the breadth of OXA-347 activity and substrate preferences across the enzyme set would be strengthened by quantitative kinetics on purified proteins and standardized carbapenem hydrolysis assays. Third, ecological and geographic patterns derived from public genomes are sensitive to sampling and metadata availability; future work should incorporate de-redundancy, explicit denominators for metadata completeness, and, where possible, prospective sampling. Finally, while neighborhood analyzes implicate mobility-related signatures, direct transfer assays (e.g., conjugation in gut-relevant communities or model systems) and phylogenetic incongruence tests are required to demonstrate horizontal transfer and to quantify dissemination rates in situ.

In summary, this study links genome-scale discovery to experimental validation to refine the role of B. thetaiotaomicron in the gut resistome. By screening 626 B. thetaiotaomicron genomes, we identified prevalent class A and class D β-lactamase alleles and validated representative enzymes with distinct resistance phenotypes and hydrolytic activities. Notably, OXA-347 exhibited broad activity in our assays, with hydrolysis detected for representatives of multiple β-lactam subclasses, and mass spectrometry supported β-lactam ring opening. Integrating taxonomic profiling with genomic neighborhood analyzes showed similar gene-cluster patterns in B. thetaiotaomicron, across gut commensal genera, and in opportunistic pathogens, although the direction of dissemination remains unclear. Collectively, these findings support incorporating gut commensals into resistance surveillance and motivate targeted follow-up studies to quantify enzyme kinetics, resolve cellular localization and expression in native hosts, and directly test mobility in gut-relevant models.
