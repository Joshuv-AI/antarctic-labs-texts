Near-cognate tRNAs enhance tRNA rejection and prime the ribosome for rapid subsequent tRNA testing
Authors: Clark Fritsch, Arpan Bhattacharya, Martin Y Ng, Hong Li, Philip C Nelson, Barry S Cooperman, Yale E Goldman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe ribosome plays a central role in translating the genetic code into amino acid sequences during polypeptide synthesis. In each cycle of peptide elongation, the ribosome discriminates between correct and incorrect aminoacyl-tRNAs based on the codon present in its A-site. To ensure high fidelity, ribosomes employ multiple proofreading mechanisms that reduce the selection of incorrect aminoacyl-tRNAs. Initial proofreading of incorrect tRNAs (near-cognate or non-cognate) is well understood in prokaryotic ribosomes but incompletely understood in eukaryotic systems.To investigate tRNA selection and accommodation for both cognate and near-cognate tRNAs, we employed single-molecule fluorescence resonance energy transfer with an in vitro eukaryotic translation system assembled on an error-prone mRNA sequence. We compared tRNA binding and accommodation rates of tryptophan-aminoacyl-tRNA in the ribosomal A-site containing either cognate or near-cognate codons. Although initial sampling of near-cognate tRNAs was slower than for cognate tRNA, subsequent near-cognate sampling events proceeded more rapidly than the initial near-cognate event. Increasing the concentration of near-cognate aminoacyl-tRNA surprisingly decreased its accommodation efficiency, a phenomenon not seen with cognate tRNAs. These findings suggest that rejection of a near-cognate tRNA induces an altered ribosomal conformation that enhances discrimination against further errors while simultaneously accelerating the tRNA sampling rate.

Significance

Accurate synthesis of proteins by the ribosome is essential for life. Understanding how ribosomes discriminate between cognate and near-cognate aminoacyl-tRNAs during translation can provide important insights into how this high level of fidelity is achieved. Here, we use single-molecule fluorescence resonance energy transfer and an in vitro eukaryotic translation system assembled on an error-prone mRNA to measure the structural dynamics of ribosomes during the rejection or misincorporation of near-cognate aminoacyl-tRNAs. Our results show that initial rejection of a near-cognate tRNA alters the ribosome to both accelerate further tRNA sampling and enhance ribosomal proofreading. This dynamic proofreading behavior highlights a previously unrecognized mechanism of fidelity control in eukaryotic translation.

Introduction

Accurate protein synthesis requires the efficient delivery of cognate aminoacyl tRNAs (aa-tRNAs) to the ribosomal A-site, where they base pair with their complementary mRNA codons (1,2,3,4). To facilitate this process, aa-tRNAs form ternary complexes (TCs) with GTP and an elongation factor (EF-Tu in bacteria and eEF1A in eukaryotes), which sample the ribosomal A-site until a codon-anticodon interaction is established and tRNA accommodates into the A-site. The ribosome then catalyzes peptide-bond formation between the A-site amino acid and the P-site nascent peptide, extending the growing polypeptide chain by one residue (1). The translocase (EF-G·GTP in bacteria, eEF2·GTP in eukaryotes) then catalyzes translocation, moving the tRNAs from the A- and P- sites to the P- and E-sites, respectively, followed by dissociation of the deacylated tRNA from the E-site to complete the elongation cycle (2).

Initial binding of the 40–60 different TCs to the A-site of the ribosome occurs in a codon-independent manner, necessitating robust selection mechanisms to reject incorrect (near-cognate and non-cognate) TCs (3). Failure to exclude these tRNAs can lead to the incorporation of incorrect amino acids and potentially lead to protein aggregation and loss of proteostasis in cells (4,5). Consequently, ribosomes achieve high fidelity during tRNA selection with an error rate of approximately one in 10,000 codons in Saccharomyces cerevisiae (6,7). Most of these errors are due to the misincorporation of near-cognate tRNAs that have one incorrect base among the three anticodon bases (6).

This high level of fidelity is established through kinetic steps during tRNA selection. Stable Watson-Crick pairing between the mRNA codon and the tRNA anticodon triggers EF-Tu·GTP hydrolysis and dissociation of EF-Tu·GDP, whereas non-cognate aa-tRNAs rarely trigger GTP hydrolysis and instead dissociate rapidly from the ribosome (2,8,9). Near-cognate TCs often undergo GTP hydrolysis when bound to the ribosome but are typically rejected in a subsequent proofreading step, possibly due to the failure of the 30S subunit to undergo domain closure (10).

The process by which ribosomes discriminate between cognate and near-cognate tRNAs has been studied in depth in prokaryotes and to a lesser degree in eukaryotes (1), where the proofreading process may be more complex due to differences in ribosome composition and the decreased rate of peptide elongation compared to prokaryotic ribosomes (11,12,13,14,15,16). The full range of conformational changes that the ribosome undergoes to either reject or accommodate near-cognate tRNAs remains uncertain. This is partly due to the rarity of near-cognate and non-cognate misincorporation events, which limits study of the interplay between non-productive tRNA selection events and tRNA misincorporation.

Here, we characterize the selection and accommodation of cognate and near-cognate tRNAs in a reconstituted in vitro eukaryotic system assembled on an error-prone mRNA transcript. Dynamics were observed using fluorescently labeled tRNAs in single-molecule fluorescence resonance energy transfer (smFRET) experiments (17). Using this approach, we found that the initial TC binding event for near-cognate tRNAs, mispaired at the third (wobble) base of the codon, occurs much more slowly than subsequent rebinding events. Unexpectedly, the number of non-productive sampling events before successful accommodation of near-cognate tRNAs, but not for cognate tRNAs, increases significantly with TC concentration. We propose a kinetic model that is consistent with these results.

We also examined eEF2-catalyzed translocation of the mRNA and tRNAs in the A- and P-sites ribosomes containing either cognate or near-cognate aa-tRNA in the A-site. Our results on translocation with cognate peptidyl-tRNA were similar to those of others (18,19,20), except that tRNA dissociates promptly from the E-site after translocation in the present conditions, without requiring prior binding of the next TC into the A-site, as shown before for prokaryotic ribosomes after the first few elongation cycles (21).

Materials and methods

Preparation of labeled tRNA

Bulk Escherichia coli and yeast tRNA were purchased from Roche. Specific non-labeled tRNA isoacceptors were prepared from bulk E. coli tRNA (tRNAGln, tRNALys) or bulk yeast tRNA (tRNAArg, tRNAPhe, tRNATrp, and tRNAVal) via hybridization to bead-immobilized DNA oligos which were complementary to each specific tRNA isoacceptor and linked to the column beads by a 3′ biotin and streptavidin linkage, as described previously (22,23). To minimize non-specific binding of labeled tRNAs to the PEG-passivated slides during single-molecule experiments, fluorescent labeled tRNAGln and tRNATrp were isolated instead using 5′-CAA AAA CCG GTG CCT TAC CGC TTG GCG-3′/3AmMO/and 5′-TTT GGA GTC GAA AGC TCT ACC ATT-3′/3AmMO/DNA oligos, respectively, with 3′ amino-modifications (3AmMO, IDT) conjugated to N-Hydroxysuccinimidyl-Sepharose 4 Fast Flow beads (H8280, Sigma-Aldrich).

To conjugate the DNA oligos, the N-Hydroxysuccinimidyl-Sepharose 4 Fast Flow beads were washed and pelleted extensively with 1 mM HCl. 500 nmol of amine-derivatized oligo was added to 15 mL of packed bead slurry, mixed, and incubated for 2 h at room temperature followed by an overnight incubation at 4°C on a rocking platform. Excess supernatant containing unreacted DNA oligo was removed by vacuum filtration. The beads were washed three times with 0.1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.5, buffer A), resuspended in buffer A, incubated at room temperature for 4 h to block unreacted succinimide, and then washed four times with buffer A and four times with 0.1 M Na-acetate, 0.5 M NaCl (pH 5.0; buffer B). Wash steps with buffer A and buffer B were repeated twice as described previously, and beads were incubated overnight at 4°C in buffer B. The supernatant was then removed, and beads were washed with buffer A repeatedly until the A260 value of the supernatant no longer decreased with subsequent washes. Using these amine-conjugated beads, tRNAGln and tRNATrp were then purified from bulk tRNA, reduced, and labeled with either Cy3-or Cy5-fluorescent dyes as described previously (22,23,24,25).

Preparation of mRNA sequences

All mRNAs used in these studies were transcribed from plasmids with the HiScribe T7 Quick High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (E2050S, New England Biolabs). Transcripts contained a T7 promoter sequence (5′-TAA TAC GAC TCA CTA TAG GG-3′) followed by a sequence encoding the cricket paralysis virus internal ribosome entry site (IRES), which allows for initiation of the elongation cycle without the need for canonical initiation factors (26) (5′-AGA CCG GAA TTC AAA GCA AAA ATG TGA TCT TGC TTG TAA ATA CAA TTT TGA GAG GTT AAT AAA TTA CAA GTA GTG CTA TTT TTG TAT TTA GGT TAG CTA TTT AGC TTT ACG TTC CAG GAT GCC TAG TGG CAG CCC CAC AAT ATC CAG GAA GCC CTC TCT GCG GTT TTT CAG ATT AGG TAG TCG AAA AAC CTA AGA AAT TTA CCT-3′) and a coding sequence containing one of three codons at the sixth position (underlined) of the coding segment (26): 1) a tryptophan codon (5′-TTC AAA GTG AGA CAA TGG CTA ATG ACA TTT CAA GAT ACC – 3′), 2) a stop codon (5′-TTC AAA GTG AGA CAA TGA CTA ATG ACA TTT CAA GAT ACC-3′), or 3) a cysteine codon (5′-TTC AAA GTG AGA CAA TGC CTA ATG ACA TTT CAA GAT ACC-3′). This was followed by a partial Fluc+ gene sequence (5′-ATG GAA GAC GCC AAA AAC ATA AAG AAA GGC CCG G-3′) used as a spacer between the coding sequence and the 3′ end, which terminated with biotin for attachment to the streptavidin-coated microscope slides used in single-molecule experiments.

Addition of a 3′-biotin modification to each mRNA was performed as previously described (27,28).

Preparation of ribosome complexes

40S and 60S ribosomal subunits were purified from shrimp (Artemia salina) cysts and eEF1A and eEF2 were purified from yeast (S. cerevisiae), as described previously (29). Purified 40S and 60S ribosomal subunits were incubated with eEF1A, eEF2, Phe-tRNAPhe, Lys-tRNALys, Val-tRNAVal, and Arg-tRNAArg, all at 2 mM, 2 mM GTP and the 3′-biotinylated mRNA IRES containing either a UGG codon (cognate to Trp-tRNA), a UGA codon (stop, near cognate to Trp-tRNA), or a UGC codon (cysteine, near cognate to Trp-tRNA) at the sixth position of the coding sequence. Post-translocation complexes positioned at the fourth codon of the mRNA coding sequence (POST4 complexes) with a nascent peptide (FKVR) in the P-site were then formed (halted there by lack of the next ternary complex in the sequence, Gln-TC) and purified as described previously (28). Aliquots were then flash frozen in LN2 and stored at −80°C.

Preparation of PEG-passivated flow chambers

PEG-passivated slides were prepared as previously described, with some minor modifications (21). In brief, microscope slides (25 × 75 × 1 mm3, Globe Scientific) were drilled with a diamond-tipped bore producing two rows of five equally spaced holes for inflow and waste. Slides were washed thoroughly with deionized water and sonicated at 40°C 1) once for 25 min in deionized water, 2) twice for 15 min in acetone, and 3) once for 25 min in methanol. They were then washed with deionized water, sonicated in 5 M KOH at 40°C for 1 h, and washed 20 times with deionized water and three times with 200-proof EtOH. Slides were next dried using N2 gas, plasma cleaned at 800–1000 mTorr air pressure, and incubated overnight at room temperature in a silanization solution containing 1 mL of 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane, 5 mL of acetic acid, and 94 mL of methanol. After washing an additional 20 times with deionized water, slides were sonicated at room temperature for 10 min and washed with EtOH five times before being dried with N2 gas. A polyethylene glycol (PEG) solution (Laysan Bio; Arab, AL) containing 20% (w/w) methoxy polyethylene glycol (methoxy PEG) succinimidyl valerate (molecular weight 2000) and 1% biotin-PEG-SC (molecular weight 2000) dissolved in 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate (pH 8.3) was then sandwiched between the salinized glass slide and a coverslip and incubated for at least 16 h in a humidified chamber. Excess PEG solution was then washed off with deionized water, and the glass slides and coverslips were then immediately used to make five-channel stopped-flow chambers with double-sided tape, as described previously (28), and stored in N2-purged Falcon tubes.

Immobilization of ribosome complexes on PEG-passivated slides

Immediately before the start of the experiment, 0.5 mg/mL streptavidin was injected into a stopped-flow chamber and incubated for 6 min. Unbound streptavidin was washed out from the flow chamber with buffer C (40 mM HEPES, 80 mM NH4Cl, 5 mM Mg(OAc)2, 100 mM KOAc, and 3 mM 2-mercaptoethanol). For experiments measuring different aspects of tRNA selection and translocation in actively translating ribosomes, biotinylated POST5 complexes containing FKVRQ-Cy3-tRNAGln in the P-site of the ribosome were formed fresh at room temperature by incubating 0.01 μM POST4 complexes with 0.08 μM eEF1A, 1 μM eEF2, 2 mM GTP, and 0.03 μM Gln-tRNAGln (Cy3) for 6 min. Formation of POST5 complexes positioned the UGG codon (cognate to Trp-tRNA), UGA codon (stop, near cognate to Trp-tRNA), or UGC codon (cysteine, near cognate to Trp-tRNA) in the ribosomal A-site according to the mRNA sequences described above. Alternatively, for experiments measuring translocation in stalled ribosomes, eEF1A, eEF2, and Gln-tRNAGln (Cy5) were incubated with POST4 complexes for 6 min to form biotinylated POST5 complexes containing FKVRQ-tRNAGln (Cy5) in the P-site. The biotinylated POST5 complexes were then injected into the streptavidin-coated flow chamber and incubated at 24°C for 6 min. Unbound ribosomes were washed out using buffer C. Formation of POST5 complexes was verified by imaging the fluorescent tRNA at position 5 as described below. Specific binding to the surface was verified in control experiments on slides lacking added streptavidin, which retained less than 10% of fluorescent particles relative to slides prepared as described above.

Single-molecule fluorescence imaging

smFRET studies were carried out at 24°C. Unless otherwise stated, all fluorescence recordings were carried out in buffer C supplemented with an enzymatic oxygen-scavenging system of 2 mM protocatechuic acid, 50 nM protocatechuate 3,4-dioxygenase (Sigma-Aldrich), 1 mM cyclooctatetraene (Sigma-Aldrich), 1 mM 4-nitrobenzyl alcohol (Sigma-Aldrich) (30), and 1.5 mM 6-hydroxy-2,5,7,8-tetramethyl-chromane-2-carboxylic acid (Trolox, Sigma-Aldrich).

Image stacks were recorded at 25 s−1 frame rate on a custom-built objective-type total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscope (21) based on a commercial inverted microscope (Eclipse Ti-E, Nikon) with a 532-nm TIRF excitation laser. For measurements of tRNA selection, video recording was started and then various concentrations of Trp-tRNATrp(Cy5) in complex with eEF1A and GTP (Trp-TC (Cy5)) were injected under program control 1 s later. Co-injection experiments with Trp-tRNATrp(Cy5)·eEF1A·GTP and eEF2·GTP (Figs. S1 and S2) to study actively translating ribosomes were performed similarly using 25 nM Trp-tRNATrp(Cy5)·eEF1A·GTP and 5 μM eEF2·GTP. In experiments to study translocation in stalled ribosomes, Trp-tRNATrp(Cy3)·eEF1A·GTP was first injected into flow chambers and incubated with previously assembled Cy5-labeled POST5 complexes for 6 min to form PRE6 complexes. Excess Trp-tRNATrp(Cy3)·eEF1A·GTP was then washed out of the flow chamber, and various concentrations of eEF2·GTP were injected 3 s after starting the recording. Measurements of nonspecific (E-site) binding of uncharged tRNATrp(Cy5) to POST5 complexes containing Cy3-labeled glutamine tRNA in the P-site were done using 9.3 nM uncharged tRNATrp(Cy5) (Fig. S1).

A deadtime of 0.7 s between the start of the injection and arrival of fluorescence substrates at the sample surface was recorded for our injection system. This deadtime, plus the 1- or 3-s recorded time before injection, was subtracted from relevant measured waiting times (e.g., delay before Trp-TC (Cy5) binding). Single-molecule traces, unless otherwise stated, were filtered using a generalized Chung-Kennedy filter (31). Custom LabView scripts were used to trigger laser shutters and pumps.

Cumulative distributions of waiting times were fitted to single- or double-exponential functions using MEMLET (32). 95% confidence intervals were generated for these fits using bootstrapping with 500 iterations in MEMLET. TLow Binding, τLow Binding, THigh Binding, τHigh Binding, and TPre Binding for cognate and near-cognate conditions (Fig. 2 A; Tables S1 and S3) were measured manually by selecting the start and/or end times for each state. Likewise, TSB-No Sampling, TSB-With Sampling, and TSB-All Traces (Tables S2 and S4) were measured manually by selecting the start times for stable binding events for cognate and near-cognate conditions. Ns was measured by counting by eye the number of sampling events occurring before stable binding. Measurements of TSubsequent Sampling, τInitial, and τSubsequent were performed using the ebFRET analysis package (33). Hidden Markov models were generated in ebFRET by fitting data with a two-state model, with 10 restarts and precision set to 10−8.

Results

To obtain insights into the relationship between tRNA accommodation and rejection for cognate and near-cognate TCs using smFRET, we first assembled eukaryotic post-translocation complexes containing a P-site pentapeptidyl-tRNA (FKVRQ-tRNAGlu (Cy3)) on 3′-biotinylated mRNA (see materials and methods). The sixth codon of each mRNA was either cognate (UGG; Trp-POST5) or near-cognate (UGA; Stop-POST5) to Trp-tRNATrp. The sequence context flanking the A-site, including codons CAA (Gln) in position 5 and CUA (Leu) in position 7, was selected to obtain an error-prone message that increases the likelihood of near-cognate accommodation (17,34,35,36,37,38).

Ensemble radioactivity assays confirmed formation of these post-translocation complexes and showed that ∼80% of complexes containing a cognate UGG codon at position 6 could translate through the eighth codon position encoding methionine used to detect octapeptide (Fig. S3 A and B), aligning with prior findings by our group (17,29). Verification of specific surface binding of properly formed POST5 complexes is described in materials and methods.

Kinetics of formation of the cognate Trp-PRE6 complex

Previous studies have shown that TCs often bind non-productively and dissociate before accommodation and peptidyl transfer can occur, a behavior observed for non-cognate, near-cognate, and occasionally cognate TCs (39,40). To investigate the kinetics of cognate tRNA selection in our eukaryotic in vitro system, we immobilized Trp-POST5 complexes on streptavidin-coated glass coverslips in stopped-flow chambers and washed away unattached materials. The tRNA selection process was initiated by flowing cognate Trp-tRNATrp (Cy5)·eEF1A·GTP ternary complex (Trp-TC (Cy5)) into the chambers to form stable PRE6 complexes containing hexapeptidyl-tRNA FKVRQW-tRNATrp (Cy5) in the A-site and tRNAGln (Cy3) in the P-site (Fig. 1 A and B). smFRET traces were generated by recording Cy3 and Cy5 fluorescence under TIRF illumination.

Binding of Trp-TC (Cy5) to the Trp-POST5 complexes led to the transition from background FRET (E = 0) to an initial transient low-FRET state of ELow = 0.23 ± 0.10 (SD, n = 539 traces; Figs. 2 A, S4 A and B) with a mean lifetime of τLow = 4.4 ± 0.2 s followed by a transient high-FRET state with EHigh = 0.5 ± 0.2 (SD, n = 494 traces) and a mean lifetime τHigh = 5.0 ± 0.2 s (n = 960 traces; Figs. 2 A, S4 C and D). Based on previous studies in bacterial and eukaryotic translation systems, we attribute the ELow state to the formation of a codon decoding complex in which the Trp-TC (Cy5) interacts with the A-site codon and the EHigh state to a subsequent activated state associated with accommodation but before peptidyl transfer (18,41,42). The proportion of accommodated ribosomes exhibiting the ELow, EHigh, or both ELow and EHigh states before forming the accommodated EPre state (Fig. S5) remained consistent across Trp-TC (Cy5) concentrations ranging from 6.2 to 50 nM Trp-TC. The absence of detectable ELow or EHigh states from many (∼58%) individual traces may reflect noise or intrinsic differences between ribosomes.

We attribute the subsequent transition to a FRET state with an overall average FRET efficiency of EPre = 0.34 ± 0.16 and a lifetime limited by the photobleaching rate to Trp-PRE6 formation after accommodation and peptidyl transfer (Fig. 2 A). This attribution is based on our prior ensemble results (17), using the same experimental system, showing that Trp-TC binding to Trp-POST5 will rapidly result in Trp-PRE6 formation (k = 0.3 s−1, 37°C). Thus, ribosomes that persisted in the EPre state for at least 10 s before the disappearance of the FRET signal were categorized as long-term accommodation events (stable binding events). We found such accommodation events in most single-molecule recordings (∼82%; Fig. S6 A and D). The remaining traces either showed at least one non-productive Trp-TC (Cy5) binding/dissociation (sampling) event before accommodation (∼10%; Fig. S6 C and D) or failed to undergo productive accommodation (∼8%; Fig. S6 B and D), showing only brief non-productive sampling events before photobleaching of the tRNAGln (Cy3) in the P-site. Dissociation of stably bound tRNAs (lasting >10 s) followed by non-productive sampling events, indicative of tRNA proofreading, was extremely rare for cognate tRNAs (Fig. S7 A–C).

Background fluorescence due to the presence of free fluorescent TC in the buffer in these experiments limited the reliability of detecting oscillations between classic (A/A and P/P) and hybrid (A/P and P/E) states in ribosomal PRE complexes, which were expected based on earlier studies (43,44,45). To improve the quality of measuring the dynamics of cognate tRNAs in Trp-PRE6, we pre-assembled Trp-PRE6 complexes with tRNAGln(Cy5) in the P-site and FKVRQW-tRNATrp (Cy3) in the A-site, which were prevented from undergoing translocation (stalled) by the absence of eEF2. Labeled tRNAs were washed out of the flow cell for these experiments. Stalled Trp-PRE6 complexes did indeed fluctuate between a low-FRET and a higher-FRET state (Fig. S2 A). The high-FRET stalled state had a mean lifetime of τClassic = 0.90 ± 0.04 s and a mean EClassic = 0.45 ± 0.12 (SD, n = 535 traces; 2175 events), whereas the stalled low-FRET state had a mean lifetime of τHybrid = 2.1 ± 0.1 s and mean FRET efficiency of EHybrid = 0.30 ± 0.11 (SD, n = 535 traces; 2284 FRET transitions). These measurements are qualitatively similar to values previously determined in studies using stalled E. coli ribosomes: EClassic = ∼0.65 and τClassic = ∼3 s, EHybrid = ∼0.3, τHybrid = ∼2 s (21,46), albeit with a larger difference between the EClassic and EHybrid values in the E. coli case.

Time intervals between TC injection and stable binding, irrespective of the initial FRET value (ELow, EHigh, or EPre) or presence of sampling events before stable binding, exhibited fast and slow phases (Figs. 2 and S8 A). We fitted these traces with double-exponential functions over a range of Trp-TC (Cy5) concentrations (6.2–50 nM TC; Fig. S8 A). Maximum-likelihood estimation (32) produced apparent rate constants for Trp-TC (Cy5) arrival (Fig. 2 C). 80%–90% of binding events exhibited the faster phase of stable binding time, a percentage that hardly varied as a function of TC concentration (Fig. 2 B). The dependence of this faster component on Trp-TC (Cy5) concentration followed Michaelis-Menten kinetics (Fig. 2 C), yielding the kcat, Km, and kcat/Km values listed in Table 2. The saturation of the fast component at high TC concentrations suggests the presence of a transient intermediate that does not generate a detectable FRET signal before the first observed FRET increase. The slower component of the stable binding-time distribution accounted for ∼10%–20% of binding events (Fig. 2 B) and had a rate constant of 0.04 ± 0.01 s−1, which was independent of TC concentration (Fig. 2 C). Temporal measurements and additional kinetic parameters are provided in Table S2.

Kinetics of near-cognate tRNA selection on error-prone mRNA

As mentioned previously, the mRNA sequence used for our Stop-POST5 complexes contained a premature stop codon that differed from the cognate tryptophan codon by a single mismatch at the third (wobble) position, making it near-cognate to Trp-tRNATrp but still promoting some accommodation of near-cognate tRNAs due to its flanking nucleotide context. Consistent with previous studies (1,15), the majority of error-prone Stop-POST5 complexes exposed to Trp-TC (Cy5) underwent non-productive sampling within the duration of the recordings (Figs. 1 C and D, S9 B and D), with only ∼8% showing long-term accommodation after their first contact with TC (Fig. S9 A and D). An additional 16% of traces exhibited sampling and stable binding (Fig. S9 C and D), whereas the remaining ∼76% failed to undergo stable accommodation within the duration of the recordings (Figs. 1 D, S9 B and D). The proportion of traces with only sampling events decreased as Trp-TC (Cy5) concentration increased, correlating with a rise in stable complex formation (Fig. S9 D). Notably, ∼40% of near-cognate complexes that remained bound for over 10 s dissociated within the recording time and were followed by further Trp-TC (Cy5) sampling events, a behavior largely absent in the cognate complexes. This suggests that tRNA proofreading can still occur after the formation of the EPre state (Fig. S7 D–F).

The proportion of stable binding events preceded by ELow, EHigh, or both states before transitioning to the PRE state was similar between cognate and near-cognate conditions (Fig. S5), as were the FRET efficiencies of these states (Table 1). However, durations of τLow and τHigh for the near-cognate case were significantly shorter than for the cognate condition (Tables 1 and S3). Stop-PRE6 complexes, stalled by absence of eEF2, oscillated between a high-FRET state with EClassic = 0.45 ± 0.12 (SD, n = 142 traces; 2019 events) and τClassic = 0.70 ± 0.01 s, and a low-FRET state with EHybrid = 0.30 ± 0.11 (SD, n = 142 traces; 2019 events) and τHybrid = 1.09 ± 0.03 s (Fig. S2 B and C).

Because most near-cognate traces exhibited multiple non-productive sampling events before either the end of the trace or stable entry into the EPre state, we further measured the time between sampling events (TSubsequent Sampling; Fig. 3 A) that occurred after the initial nonproductive event. According to the simplest models of tRNA selection, the initial and subsequent sampling events should be essentially identical, as non-productive sampling events are presumed to leave the ribosome in the same (naive) state that it was in before contact with the TC. Surprisingly, at all tested Trp-TC (Cy5) concentrations, TSubsequent Sampling was significantly shorter than TInitial Sampling (Fig. 3 B and C). Rate constants generated from TSubsequent Sampling increased linearly with Trp-TC (Cy5) concentration, yielding a second-order rate constant of 4.4 ± 0.30 μM−1s−1 (Fig. 3 D) that was approximately 3.5-fold faster than the nearly saturated rate for the initial event at 40 nM TC. In traces that contained both sampling events and stable binding, the time between the final sampling event and stable binding (TFinal Binding) fell between TInitial Sampling and TSubsequent Sampling and also generated rate constants that followed Michaelis-Menten kinetics (Tables 2 and S4).

The lifetimes (durations) of initial and subsequent sampling events were the same: 0.44 ± 0.06 s (n = 315 events) and 0.44 ± 0.03 s (n = 936 events), respectively. Both demonstrated distributions with clear delayed rising values at short durations that suggest two or more kinetic steps in series before tRNA dissociation (Fig. S10 A and B). The FRET distribution for these sampling events was best described by the sum of two Gaussian components, with E values of 0.21 ± 0.08 and 0.48 ± 0.18 (n = 438 traces; 1383 sampling events; Fig. S11 A). These values closely aligned with the mean FRET efficiencies of the ELow and EHigh states for stable cognate and near-cognate binding.

A POST5 complex containing a different near-cognate codon (UGC, cognate to cysteine tRNA) in the A-site exhibited similar behavior: TC binding during subsequent sampling events was faster than for initial non-productive events (Fig. S12 A), supporting the corresponding result with the UGA near-cognate stop codon. However, a more detailed study for this codon was not pursued due to the significantly lower number of stable binding events obtained relative to the UGA near-cognate sequence (Fig. S12 B).

We investigated whether the basis for these surprising results might be caused by delayed equilibration of the injected TC concentration at the coverslip surface. Injection of uncharged Cy5-labeled tRNATrp to measure interactions with the ribosome in the absence of eEF1A (likely at the E-site) resulted in TInitial Sampling and TSubsequent Sampling values that were nearly identical to each other (Fig. S1 A). This result, combined with the observation that cognate TC binding is also faster than the initial near-cognate interaction (compare TInitial Sampling in Tables S1 and S3), indicates that the difference between near-cognate TInitial Sampling and TSubsequent Sampling cannot be explained by inhomogeneous TC concentration (i.e., lower at the coverslip surface) shortly after injection. Furthermore, sample heterogeneity among individual ribosomes is unlikely to account for the differences in binding kinetics, as most traces used for our analysis contained both initial and subsequent sampling events.

We next considered whether the difference in binding-rate constants between initial and subsequent events may be due to pre-binding of TCs to an accessory site such as the ribosomal P-stalk after the initial sampling event. This might allow for subsequent TCs to sample the ribosome immediately after rejection of the first TC. Contrary to this hypothesis, however, we found that pre-incubation of the Stop-POST5 complex with 5 μM eEF2·GTP to saturate G-protein factor binding sites (e.g., on the P-stalk) before simultaneous addition of near-cognate TC and 5 μM eEF2·GTP failed to eliminate the difference between TInitial Sampling and TSubsequent Sampling (Fig. S1 B). This result rules out the possibility that the phenomenon is caused by a substrate “channeling” mechanism, as has previously been hypothesized (39,47,48,49).

Cognate tRNA exhibits faster subsequent TC binding when accommodation is blocked

Tigecycline is an antibiotic that inhibits aa-tRNA accommodation within the A-site of prokaryotic ribosomes without directly affecting GTP hydrolysis. Although primarily targeting bacterial protein synthesis, tigecycline also reduces aa-tRNA accommodation in eukaryotic ribosomes, albeit to a lesser degree than in prokaryotic ribosomes (43). To investigate whether cognate TCs in the presence of tigecycline exhibit sampling dynamics similar to the near-cognate TCs, we pre-incubated Trp-POST5 complexes with 100 μM tigecycline before injection of Trp-TC (Cy5) and tigecycline. As expected, although accommodation was still common (data not shown), tigecycline induced frequent cognate TC sampling events, mimicking what is observed with near-cognate TCs.

TInitial Sampling and TSubsequent Sampling (Fig. S13 A and B) arrival times for cognate TCs in the presence of tigecycline were fitted by single-exponential functions giving observed rate constants that again indicated that subsequent binding was faster than initial binding (Fig. S13 C), although the difference was smaller than with the near-cognate codon in the absence of the antibiotic. We observed strong similarities between cognate tRNA in the presence of tigecycline and near-cognate tRNA for the lifetimes of the sampling events (τInitial and τSubsequent; Fig. S10 C and D) and the FRET efficiency distributions for the sampling events, with components at E = 0.21 ± 0.08 and E = 0.48 ± 0.18 (SD, n = 393 traces; 2121 sampling events; Fig. S11 B). Excursions to a high-FRET state associated with GTP hydrolysis were also observed in prokaryotes after tigecycline treatment (41) and again suggest that inhibition of later stages of tRNA selection and accommodation was incomplete under the conditions tested here. Overall, these results suggest that the mechanism underlying faster subsequent sampling compared to initial sampling is similar regardless of whether accommodation is inhibited by exposure to antibiotic or near-cognate TC.

Dependence of near-cognate accommodation on TC concentration

Due to the low probability of accommodation of near-cognate tRNAs into the ribosome, previous single-molecule studies of near-cognate tRNA dynamics after selection are more limited than for the cognate case (18). Use of our error-prone mRNA, though, allowed us to compare the kinetics of cognate and near-cognate tRNA selection further, as ∼20% of our near-cognate ribosomes underwent accommodation within the length of our recordings.

In the near-cognate condition, traces where the first binding event led directly to stable binding showed arrival times comparable to those of initial sampling events across various TC concentrations (Tables 2 and S4). However, traces containing nonproductive sampling events before stable binding exhibited significantly slower accommodation times. The time distributions, when fitted by single-exponential functions, yielded rate constants approximately half as fast as the slow component of stable binding observed in the cognate condition (Fig. S8 B). Notably, these rate constants showed little dependence on Trp-TC (Cy5) concentration (Fig. 4 D and E; Table S4).

Under the most straightforward hypothetical model for tRNA selection, the probability, Pa, that any given TC—whether cognate or near-cognate—will accommodate into the ribosome rather than be rejected would be constant, resulting in a mean number of sampling events before stable binding (Ns = 1/Pa) that is independent of TC concentration. To test this assumption, we measured Ns for the near-cognate TC condition and found, surprisingly, that Ns varied strongly with Trp-TC (Cy5) concentration (Fig. 4 A and C). In contrast, Ns for the cognate TC condition remained consistently low (∼0.1 sampling event per stable binding) and was unaffected by Trp-TC (Cy5) concentration, as initially expected (Fig. 4 B and C). These findings suggest that increasing near-cognate Trp-TC (Cy5) concentration reduces the probability of accommodation after a sampling event. A revised kinetic mechanism consistent with this observation and previous information on near-cognate tRNAs is presented in the discussion.

eEF2-catalyzed translocation for cognate and near-cognate mRNA

Previous studies have noted that misincorporated near-cognate tRNAs translocate more slowly than properly accommodated cognate tRNAs (17,50). To test this point in the present system, we compared the dynamics of eEF2-dependent translocation of stalled Trp-PRE6 and Stop-PRE6 complexes prepared by pre-incubating Trp-POST5 and Stop-POST5 complexes containing FKVRQ-tRNAGln (Cy5) in the P-site with Trp-TC (Cy3) for ∼8 min before injection of eEF2·GTP.

A representative recording of FRET dynamics during Trp-PRE6 translocation is shown in Fig. S14 A. Before eEF2 injection, the Trp-PRE6 exhibited an equilibrated mixture of the EClassic and EHybrid FRET states with an overall average FRET efficiency of EPre = 0.34 ± 0.16 (SD, n = 497 traces, Fig. S14 B). Upon eEF2·GTP injection, the FRET signal transitioned to a high-FRET state (Etrans = 0.59 ± 0.04, SD, n = 1154), followed by complete loss of signal (Fig. S14 A and B). Approximately 70% of traces occupied the EHybrid state immediately before the transition to the Etrans state. This FRET increase aligns with structural studies (51,52,53,54) showing closer spacing of P- and E-site (POST) tRNAs compared to A- and P-site (PRE) tRNAs and is consistent with previous single-molecule studies on human ribosomes (20,52).

The apparent rate constant for high-FRET-state formation increased with eEF2 concentration, reaching ktrans = 0.68 ± 0.06 s−1 (Fig. S14 C) at 5 μM eEF2, consistent with previous ensemble measurements (17). The subsequent FRET loss likely reflects E-site tRNA dissociation or photobleaching. By analyzing the high-FRET state at varying laser intensities and eEF2 concentrations (Fig. S15), we estimated E-site tRNA dissociation rate constant, corrected for photobleaching, as kdiss = 0.67 ± 0.02 s−1 at 5 mM Mg2+, which was largely independent of eEF2 concentration (Fig. S14 D).

Notably, the tRNA dissociation rate constant at 5 mM Mg2+ is ∼30-fold faster than was earlier reported for human ribosomes (20).The large difference in E-site tRNA dissociation rates between these studies is likely due to several factors. First, previous studies relying on human ribosome complexes used initiation complexes that were prepared non-enzymatically using 15 mM Mg2+and in the presence of polyamines, which possibly led to conformational changes that were not readily reversible when the Mg2+ concentration was reduced. Additionally, the human ribosome complexes contained nascent peptides that were only one or two amino acids long in comparison to the five- and six-amino-acid-long nascent peptides considered here. We previously showed that delayed exit of deacylated E-site tRNA, prompted allosterically by A-site TC binding, is exhibited by bacterial ribosomes but only for short one- to three-residue nascent peptides, after which the E-site tRNA dissociates promptly, as found in the present experiments (21).

For Stop-PRE6, only 30% of complexes were converted to Stop-POST6 upon addition of eEF2·GTP, whereas ∼60% of Trp-PRE6 complexes were converted to Trp-POST6 upon addition of eEF2·GTP. Despite this difference, the transient high-FRET state for Stop-POST6 (Etrans = 0.55 ± 0.20) was formed with a similar rate constant (ktrans = 0.78 ± 0.03 s−1 at 5 μM eEF2), and tRNA dissociation rate constant (kdiss = 0.41 ± 0.05 s−1) from the Trp-POST6 complex. These findings for cognate and near-cognate translocation are summarized and compared in Table 3. Our finding that cognate and near-cognate translocation occurred at similar rates was unexpected given our earlier ensemble studies (17) showing 20-fold slower translocation of Stop-POST5 compared to Trp-POST5, similar to earlier E. coli in vitro reconstitution studies (50). However, those experiments were conducted by adding Trp-TC and eEF2 together without the 8-min incubation period in between as done here. smFRET experiments under conditions more comparable to the ensemble ones (i.e., adding the Trp-TC and eEF2 together) were not successful due to very low yields of Stop-POST6 formation (≤10% of Stop-POST5). Due to the instability of nascent peptide binding in the Stop-PRE6 complexes, it is possible that poorly formed/slowly translocating complexes were depleted during the long incubation period used in experiments employing stalled Stop-PRE6 complexes. Further experiments are needed to resolve this discrepancy.

Discussion

In the genetic code, the second position of a codon is the most stringent selector of the amino acid, possibly because it specifies the type of residue: U for hydrophobic amino acids, A for hydrophilic, and G or C for semipolar residues with mixed character (55). Position 1 is the next critical determinant (56) and the third, wobble, position is most degenerate (57,58). Mismatches at the first or second nucleotide position of the codon generally produce stronger rejection of non- and near-cognate tRNAs than at the wobble base (56,59). Here we use smFRET to determine the relationship between cognate and near-cognate tRNA selection and accommodation at the third position in a reconstituted in vitro eukaryotic translation system. Ribosomes were programmed with error-prone mRNA transcripts to increase the likelihood of near-cognate accommodation events to quantify. We primarily used a Trp-tRNATrp with a CCA anticodon that was near-cognate to an UGA stop codon due to a single mismatch at the third position. We also tested near-cognate (UGC, Cys) and with the cognate codon (UGG, Trp) when accommodation was blocked by tigecycline. The stop codon was flanked upstream by a CAA codon and downstream by a CUA codon, a combination known to promote misincorporation of near-cognate tRNAs (34,35,36,37,38).

Most of the present findings on the kinetics of cognate and near-cognate tRNA selection align with previous studies from our lab on peptide elongation, as compared in Table S5 (17,18), and are largely consistent with prior work using purified yeast elongation factors and ribosomes (60,61). However, recent studies conducted with human ribosomes and elongation factors reported significantly faster rate constants for tRNA selection and accommodation (18). Although the source of this discrepancy is not certain, it likely arises from differences in buffer composition (notably, the use of buffers containing polyamines and much higher Mg2+ concentration, which have extensive effects on ribosome function (62)). Additionally, the use of non-essential elongation factors (e.g., eIF5A) likely contributed to the faster measured rate constants.

Several of our results are inconsistent with common models of tRNA selection, which assume that dissociation of a near-cognate aa-tRNA before peptide bond formation (i.e., the result of proofreading) results in simple reformation of the initial POST complex. Specifically, the following three observations are incompatible with proofreading that results in the reformation of an identical POST complex after near-cognate tRNA rejection. First, subsequent sampling events occur more rapidly compared to the initial non-productive sampling event on exposure of post-translocation ribosomes to near-cognate TCs. Second, the average number of non-productive sampling events that occur before a near-cognate tRNA is successfully accommodated into the ribosome unexpectedly increases with near-cognate TC concentration (Fig. 3 B–D). Third, the overall rate of stable binding (accommodation) of the near-cognate tRNA does not vary significantly with the concentration of TC, despite faster TC binding at higher TC concentrations.

Taken together, the results suggest that, as the concentration of near-cognate TC is increased, an inhibitory mechanism related to incorrect base-pairing decreases the likelihood of stable binding, leading us to propose the new hypothetical proofreading scheme, shown in Fig. 5 A (Scheme 1). The essential concept of Scheme 1 is that dissociation of a near-cognate tRNA produces a ribosome structure that binds TCs faster than the naive one but is slow to accommodate. The initial POST complex, state 1, binds a near-cognate TC in two steps. After the arrival of the TC into the flow chamber, the rate of appearance of the first FRET-producing interaction (ELow) follows a hyperbolic relationship vs. TC concentration with Km = 26 nM and a Vmax of 0.08 s−1. The cumulative distribution of the arrival times (Fig. 2 B) shows no lag phase, even at low TC concentrations, indicating that step 1 (states 1 ↔ 2) is a rapidly equilibrating reversible collision with k-1 >> k2. State 2 could be a “testing site” in which the TC is not making full contact with the mRNA (10), or it could correspond to binding at the P-stalk (47), neither of which comes close enough to transfer excited-state energy to the P-site tRNA (FRET E = 0). State 3 is the first intermediate that produces FRET (ELow) and is converted to state 4 relatively rapidly. k4 and k5 are rate constants for eEF1A dissociation, accommodation, and peptide transfer, respectively.

Kinetic reaction scheme that predicts the experimental results. (A) The top line of the scheme represents a standard or typical model of tRNA selection (states 1–6). The present experimental results imply that ribosomes after dissociation of a near-cognate TC retain a structural conformation (state F) that binds TCs faster than state 1 and, after TC binding (state R), exhibits inhibition of accommodation relative to states 3 and 4. States 3 and 4 are considered together. Step 4b is dashed because it is uncertain and not required to explain the results. See text for more explanation. (B) Fits from the analytical model (Appendix A) to the observed rate constants for initial sampling events (data, red squares; model, red line), subsequent sampling events (data, blue triangles; model, blue line), and final sampling events before stable binding (data, green circles; model, green line). (C) Model fit (red line) to the mean number of sampling events before stable binding at each TC concentration (black circles). Error bars in (B) and (C) represent 95% confidence intervals generated by bootstrapping. Gray shaded regions around each model fit represent 95% confidence intervals determined from the analytical model.

Accommodation of a near-cognate tRNA via steps k3 and onward would result in a misincorporation event that is typically prevented by the ribosome’s proofreading activity. The more likely event for a near-cognate TC is to non-productively sample the ribosomal A-site and dissociate from either state 3 or state 4 before peptide-bond formation. Because states 3 and 4 are indistinguishable from each other with the current experimental results, we have combined them (dashed box) for the purposes of modeling the overall reaction scheme. According to the simplest model of tRNA selection, once a tRNA fails to undergo accommodation and dissociates from the ribosome, the ribosome returns to the same state that it had initially, when it originally encountered the tRNA. However, because our results indicate that subsequent non-productive sampling events bind to the ribosome more rapidly than the initial sampling events, the data imply that the ribosome assumes an altered (fast-binding) state after near-cognate tRNA rejection.

Therefore, ribosomes in states 3/4 after tRNA rejection do not simply return to state 1 but instead proceed to the altered conformation, state F, via a reaction step with rate constant of k-2a. State F then either binds a near-cognate TC again (step 6) to form state R, a FRET-detectible complex, or converts in a first-order reaction to state E (step 7). We posit that state R is refractory, or at least slow, toward undergoing GTPase activation or tRNA accommodation, which are necessary for peptide elongation, thereby delaying the ribosome in functionally processing a near-cognate TC. This echoes prior results with near-cognate TCs or with inhibitory codon pairs that have provided evidence for persistent and essentially irreversible changes limiting the stoichiometry of peptide elongation (17,63). Reversible near-cognate TC binding to form state R thus results in repeated unsuccessful sampling events. In competition with step 6, step 7 forms state E, which binds near-cognate TC (step 8) to reform state 3, providing a renewed opportunity to activate GTP hydrolysis, fully accommodate aa-tRNA within the A-site, and form the next peptide bond, finally inserting the near-cognate amino acid into the peptide chain. We considered whether state F could convert directly to state 1, obviating the need to invoke state E, but rejected this possibility because we find the final transition to stable binding of near-cognate TC after sampling events to proceed more rapidly than the initial transition (Fig. 5 B; Tables 2 and S4).

In Scheme 1, the relative rates of near-cognate TC binding resulting in state conversion falls in the order state F to state R (k6) > state E to state 3 (k8) > state 1 to state 3 (k1·k2/k-1). This order of reactivity accounts for the observations that subsequent sampling events proceed more rapidly than the initial sampling event and that this difference increases with increasing near-cognate TC concentration, which favors state F partitioning toward state R over state E. It also is consistent with our observation that the average number of near-cognate sampling events occurring before stable binding increases with TC concentration, reaching an average of 3.5 ± 0.3 sampling events per stable binding event at 40 nM TC (Fig. 5 C).

Although other kinetic diagrams might explain the unusual kinetic behavior of near-cognate selection, the consistency of Scheme 1 with the experiments is demonstrated by its ability to quantitatively fit the near-cognate kinetic data of Figs. 3 D, 4 B and C using maximum-likelihood optimization (Appendix A; Figs. S16 and S17). This permits estimation of some of the rate constants in Scheme 1, which are presented in Table 4 along with those determined directly from experimental data (see materials and methods). With these values, Scheme 1 predicts kInitial Sampling, kSubsequent Sampling, kFinal Binding, the durations of the observed FRET states, and number of sampling events (Ns) quite well (Fig. 5 B and C). Parameter confidence intervals (gray bands in Fig. 5) were determined by bootstrapping. We also programmed a Monte Carlo simulation of Scheme 1, which confirmed the analytical equations (Fig. S18).

The great majority of tRNAs in a cell are non-cognate or near-cognate to any given codon, so the fast-TC-binding conformation in our model (state F) is expected to be the predominant post-translocation intermediate for ribosomes containing only one tRNA. This is in accord with cryo-electron microscopy images of ribosomes in fast-frozen cells (64) showing a unique post-translocation single-tRNA state. This structure presumably corresponds to state F, rather than state 1. The apparent inhibition of accommodation in state R, indicated by an increased number of sampling events that occur before stable binding as near-cognate TC concentration is increased, likely facilitates the rejection of near-cognate and, potentially, non-cognate TCs. The durations of the first sampling event and subsequent ones are the same, indicating that the near-cognate TC rejection rate from state R (k-6) is approximately the same as from state 3/4 (k-2a). This equivalence leads to normal exchange between bound and free TCs in states F and R. The present data do not provide information for cognate TCs on whether state R would activate GTP hydrolysis and accommodation (state R → state 5, which we did not include for the near-cognate pathway). If further reactions from state R are not inhibited for cognate TCs, then this novel mechanism could contribute prominently to fidelity of tRNA selection in eukaryotic elongation by facilitating accommodation of cognate tRNAs relative to the near-cognate ones.
