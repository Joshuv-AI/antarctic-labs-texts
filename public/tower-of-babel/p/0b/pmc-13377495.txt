Multiplex Panel Detects Glial and Inflammatory Biomarker Signatures in Sporadic and C9orf72-ALS
Authors: Karthik Baskar, Christina Steffke, Sarah Bernsen, Zeynep Elmas, Jasper Hesebeck-Brinckmann, Joachim Schuster, Thomas Meyer, Patrick Weydt, Jochen Weishaupt, David Brenner, Alberto Catanese
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesCSF proteomics has emerged as a valuable strategy for identifying diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). However, the limited availability and volumes of CSF samples restrict the broader clinical application of CSF-based biomarker panels. To address this challenge, we investigated whether the novel nucleic acid-linked immuno-sandwich assay (NULISA) multiplex platform—capable of quantifying multiple neural, glial, and inflammatory markers from minimal biofluid volumes—could validate previously proposed biomarkers and identify additional candidates relevant to ALS.MethodsUsing this platform, we measured a targeted panel of 131 biomarkers in cohorts of patients with C9orf72-associated ALS, sporadic ALS (sALS), and matched healthy controls.ResultsThe 6 markers neurofilament heavy chain (NEFH) and neurofilament light chain (NEFL), chitinases—particularly chitotriosidase-1 (CHIT1) and chitinase-3-like protein-1 (CHI3L1), and chemokines CCL2 and CCL3 were significantly elevated in both ALS groups compared with controls. These biomarkers correlated with disease progression and demonstrated strong diagnostic performance when combined into aggregate scores, as reflected by a high area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for ALS. Notably, C9orf72-ALS patients exhibited higher levels of the oxidative stress-related markers PRDX6 and ENO2, compared with sALS patients, suggesting a genotype-specific molecular signature.DiscussionOverall, our findings support the use of a multiplexed panel of diverse, inflammatory, glial, and neurodegeneration-associated biomarkers as a complementary diagnostic and prognostic tool alongside established measurements of neurofilaments. This approach may enhance biomarker robustness while minimizing CSF volume requirements, thereby improving clinical feasibility in ALS research and care.

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a relentlessly progressive neurodegenerative disease characterized by the degeneration of motor neurons in both the brain and spinal cord. This leads to muscle weakness, paralysis, and ultimately respiratory failure, contributing to a generally poor prognosis with a median survival of 3–5 years after symptoms manifestation.1 The pathogenesis of ALS is complex and multifactorial, involving an interplay of genetic, environmental, and cellular mechanisms. Among these, neuroinflammation has emerged as a critical factor in ALS progression, contributing not only to neuronal damage but also influencing the onset and trajectory of disease.2

Neuroinflammation is primarily driven by the activation of glial cells, particularly microglia and astrocytes, within the CNS. Microglial activation is one of the earliest responses to neuronal injury in ALS, with these cells becoming overactivated and releasing a cascade of proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and neurotoxic molecules such as glutamate and reactive oxygen species.3 This activation exacerbates neuronal injury and accelerates motor neuron degeneration. Astrocytes, which normally provide metabolic and structural support to neurons, become reactive in ALS and release additional inflammatory mediators that contribute to the pathologic cycle.4,5 Collectively, this chronic inflammatory state in the CNS is thought to contribute to the self-perpetuating cycle of neurodegeneration, with inflammation acting as both a consequence of neuronal damage and a mediator of further motor neuron sufferance.6 This neuroinflammatory response is detectable in the serum and CSF of ALS patients through the presence of various biomarkers. Increased levels of proinflammatory molecules such as ICAM-1, IFN-γ, and MCP-1 have been indeed documented in ALS patients.7,8 Elevated concentrations of these proteins were shown to positively correlate with disease severity and progression,8,9 suggesting their utility as diagnostic and prognostic tools. Another group linked to glial inflammation is the chitinases. Chitinases—particularly chitotriosidase-1 (CHIT1) and Chitinase-3-like protein 1 (YKL-40/CHI3L1)—have emerged as promising biomarkers in ALS, reflecting microglial and astrocytic activation in the CNS. Elevated levels of these proteins in CSF and blood correlate with faster disease progression, greater neuroinflammatory activity, and shorter survival, making them useful for prognostic stratification.10-15

In fact, an early and accurate diagnosis of ALS remains a considerable challenge, largely due to the absence of definitive biomarkers and the overlapping clinical symptoms with other neurodegenerative conditions. For these reasons, the identification of CSF biomarkers, particularly those linked to neuroinflammation, might offer promising adjuncts to clinical diagnostics. Neurofilament light chain (NEFL), a marker of axonal damage, is the most used and reliable biomarker for ALS diagnosis and disease monitoring.16,17 However, to further enhance diagnostic accuracy, the combination of NEFL with other proteins, such as cardiac troponin T,18 may represent an effective strategy to improve the sensitivity and specificity of ALS diagnostics, particularly in the early stages when clinical symptoms are less pronounced. In addition, this might have significant implications into clinical trials for ALS. As novel drugs and treatments are developed, biomarkers that can monitor disease progression and treatment effects are becoming essential endpoints. For example, the antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) Tofersen, which was developed for the treatment of SOD1-ALS,19 has highlighted the importance of biomarkers to document early treatment effects. In fact, NEFL could predict a clinical benefit earlier than clinical outcome parameters. Tofersen treatment has also highlighted novel therapy-responsive biomarkers (such as the NPTXs) and has been shown to influence the levels of neuroinflammatory molecules including IL-6, tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α) and IL-1β.19,20 These findings suggest that Tofersen's efficacy may involve modulation of different cellular features that contribute to disease progression. Therefore, identifying additional biomarkers to complement neurofilaments could enhance the early diagnosis of ALS, provide better insights into the neuroprotective effects of emerging therapies, and improve the ability to predict clinical outcomes.

Methods

Participants

Patients were recruited through the German Network for Motor Neuron Diseases (MND-NET) at the ALS centers in Ulm, Bonn and Berlin (see affiliations above).14 All patients were diagnosed with definite, probable, or laboratory-supported ALS according to the revised El Escorial criteria and underwent genetic screening for ALS-associated variants. Apart from riluzole, no additional disease-modifying therapies were administered to the enrolled patients.

The study cohort included 27 patients carrying a pathogenic C9orf72 repeat expansion, 35 patients classified as sporadic ALS (sALS) without evidence of ALS-associated mutations, and 38 age-matched and sex-matched control individuals with non-neurodegenerative neurologic conditions, including tension headache, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, and idiopathic facial nerve paralysis. The 3 groups were comparable with respect to age and sex. The mean age (±SD) was 56.6 (±7.8) years in the C9orf72-ALS group, 60.0 (± 11.5) years in the sALS group, and 61.0 (±8.6) years in controls.

In both ALS groups, the ratio of bulbar to spinal disease onset was approximately 1:2, reflecting the typical epidemiologic distribution of ALS. CSF samples were obtained by lumbar puncture, and all samples were processed and stored at −80°C within 2 hours of collection until further analysis.

NULISAseq Assay

The NULISAseq CNS Disease Panel 120 enables highly sensitive and specific multiplex detection of 131 proteins associated with neurologic function and inflammatory responses in a single analysis. This panel includes both well-established biomarkers linked to neurodegenerative disorders and potential candidate markers. Several of these proteins have not previously been measurable in body fluids such as serum or CSF because of earlier methodological constraints. NULISAseq analyses were conducted at Alamar Biosciences following previously described protocols.21 In brief, CSF samples stored at −80°C were thawed on ice and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 minutes. A 10 µL aliquot of the resulting supernatant was transferred into 96-well plates and analyzed using Alamar's CNS Disease Panel, which primarily targets proteins related to neurodegeneration as well as cytokines and chemokines involved in inflammation and immune responses.

The NULISAseq workflow was conducted using a Hamilton automated platform. The process began with the formation of immunocomplexes using DNA-barcoded capture and detection antibodies. These complexes were then isolated and washed using paramagnetic oligo-dT beads. Subsequently, the immunocomplexes were released into a low-salt buffer and recaptured on streptavidin beads for additional washing. The adjacent DNA ends within each immunocomplex were then ligated, generating DNA reporter molecules that contained both target-specific (TMI) and sample-specific (SMI) barcode sequences. The resulting reporter molecules were pooled, PCR-amplified, purified, and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 system. Proteins that met quality control criteria were determined based on target detectability, defined as the percentage of samples with signal levels above the established limit of detection for each protein target.22

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis Functional Rating Scale-Revised Analysis and Correlation With Biomarkers

The monthly slope of Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis Functional Rating Scale-Revised (ALSFRS-R) until the time point of sampling was calculated by subtracting the ALSFRS-R at sampling date from the maximal score of 48 and dividing the difference through the disease duration since symptom onset (in months): (48-ALSFRS-R-Score at BL)/disease duration in months since symptom onset.20,22 The observed ALSFRS-R-Score was documented during the regular patient visits. The levels of biomarkers at the sampling timepoint were correlated with the respective ALSFRS-R-Slope at same time point.

Data Analysis

Sequencing outputs were analyzed using the NULISAseq computational pipeline provided by Alamar Biosciences. Both SMI and TMI barcode sequences were quantified, permitting up to 2 base mismatches or a combination of 1 insertion/deletion and 1 mismatch. Normalization within each plate was carried out by dividing the target counts in each sample well by the counts of its internal control. Subsequently, normalization across plates was conducted using interplate control (IPC) normalization, where counts were adjusted by dividing them by the TMI median values calculated from the 3 IPC wells present on each plate. The resulting values were then rescaled, incremented by 1, and log2-transformed to generate NULISA Protein Quantification values.

The processed data sets were imported into Python (version 4.0.11) within the JupyterLab environment using the pandas library. Venn diagrams were created using Matplotlib. Two-dimensional principal component analysis (PCA) and k-means clustering were performed using NumPy, Seaborn, scikit-learn, and Matplotlib libraries. Principal component loadings derived from the PCA were extracted using pandas and visualized as bar charts with Matplotlib. Pearson correlation analyses were conducted using NumPy, and scatter plots illustrating these relationships were generated with Seaborn and Matplotlib.

For additional statistical analyses, including differential expression (DE) analysis and volcano plot generation, the NULISAseq-R and ggplot2 packages in R were used. Data organization and supplementary statistical testing were performed using Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism (version 10). A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied for statistical testing. Final visualizations, including bar graphs and box plots, were produced using GraphPad Prism (version 10).

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

Biosampling of CSF samples was performed in the context of the German Network for Motor Neuron Diseases (MND-NET) cohort study, for which informed consent from all donors was obtained. The MND-NET cohort study was approved by the ethics committee of Ulm University (application number 19/12).

Data Availability

The raw NULISAseq data are available on request.

Results

In this study, we included 27 individuals carrying a pathogenic repeat expansion in the C9orf72 gene and 35 patients with sALS and compared them to 38 age-matched individuals without neurodegenerative disorders (hereafter referred to as controls; eTable 1). The distribution of male and female patients was comparable among groups: women represented the 48.14% of the C9orf72-ALS, 48.57% of the sALS, and 50% of the control individuals. The distribution of onset types in both ALS groups was comparable with that reported in European cohorts, with bulbar onset accounting for 37% of C9orf72 cases and 31.4% of sALS cases (eTable 1). None of the patients included in this study fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for frontotemporal dementia at the time of recruitment. CSF proteomes obtained from these cohorts were analyzed using the NULISAseq CNS Disease Panel, which comprises 131 protein targets. Of these, 117 proteins passed quality control criteria and were retained for downstream analyses (eTable 2).

We first analyzed the CSF proteome considering all ALS patients (sALS + C9orf72-ALS) as a single group and compared them with controls (Figure 1A). Based on the 117 proteins detected above the limit of detection, PCA revealed a partial separation between ALS patients and controls. Although PC1 did not separate disease and control groups (eFigure 1), the combination of PC2 and PC3 more effectively distinguished ALS from control individuals (Figure 1B). In particular, the separation between ALS patients and controls was observed along PC3. Inspection of the PC3 loadings indicated that this separation was primarily driven by the neurofilaments NEFL and NEFH, together with inflammatory markers including CHIT1, CCL2, CCL3, and CHI3L1 (Figure 1C). Consistent with this observation, DE analysis showed that NEFH, NEFL, CHIT1, CHI3L1, CCL2, CCL3, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), TNF, UCHL1, and IL7 were significantly upregulated in the ALS cohort compared with controls (Figure 1D). These results confirm that the ALS CSF proteome is characterized by a combined signature of neuronal injury and inflammatory activation.

We next sought to identify proteomic signatures specific to ALS subgroups and first compared sALS patients with controls (Figure 2A). Similar to the analysis performed in the combined ALS cohort, PCA revealed a separation between sALS patients and controls along PC3 (Figure 2B and eFigure 2). Examination of the PC3 loadings indicated that this separation was largely driven by neurofilaments (NEFL and NEFH) and CHIT1, together with several inflammatory mediators including IL10, IL18, CCL3, and CCL13 (Figure 2C). Consistent with these observations, DE analysis identified NEFH, NEFL, CHIT1, CCL2, CHI3L1, CCL3, IL7, GFAP, TNF, and UCHL1 as significantly increased in sALS CSF relative to controls, whereas S100A12 levels were reduced (Figure 2D and eFigure 3).

We then focused on the C9orf72-ALS cohort (Figure 3A). PCA analysis again revealed a separation between C9orf72-ALS patients and controls along PC3 (Figure 3B and eFigure 4), which was mainly driven by the levels of CHIT1, NEFH, NEFL, kinase insert domain Receptor (KDR), and alpha-synuclein (SNCA) (Figure 3C). Differential expression analysis showed that NEFH, NEFL, CHIT1, CHI3L1, CCL2, CCL3, TREM1, and ENO2 were significantly upregulated in C9orf72-ALS CSF compared with controls. By contrast, the levels of IL12p70 and TIMP3 were reduced in C9orf72 patients (Figure 3D and eFigure 5). Overall, these analyses suggest a substantial overlap between the proteomic alterations observed in sporadic and genetic ALS. Indeed, when comparing the proteins significantly altered across ALS genotypes, we observed that 6 markers—NEFH NEFL, CHIT1, CHI3L1, CCL2, and CCL3—were commonly upregulated in both ALS groups (Figure 4, A and B). This finding suggests the presence of a shared molecular signature characterized by markers of neuronal injury and inflammatory activation.

To further explore potential genotype-specific features, we directly compared the CSF proteome of C9orf72-ALS and sALS patients (eFigure 6A). This analysis revealed a significant upregulation of HBA1, ENO2, and PRDX6 in C9orf72-ALS samples, whereas IL12p70, GFAP, interferon gamma (IFNG), TIMP3, and placental growth factor (PGF) were higher in sALS CSF (eFigure 6, B and C). These results suggest the presence of partially distinct inflammatory and oxidative stress-related signatures between ALS subtypes. Importantly, the disease progression rate—estimated using the ALSFRS-R slope—was comparable between sALS and C9orf72 patients (eFigure 6D), indicating that the observed proteomic differences are unlikely to be driven by differences in disease progression between the 2 groups.

We next asked whether the combination of the 6 commonly altered proteins might improve biomarker-based readouts in ALS. Correlation analyses revealed that increased levels of NEFL and CHIT1 significantly correlated with the ALSFRS-R slope in both sALS and C9orf72 patients (eFigure 7, C and D, and eFigure 8, A and B), while CHI3L1 showed a significant correlation only in C9orf72 patients (eFigure 8, C and D). By contrast, correlations between NEFH (eFigure 7, A and B), CCL2, and CCL3 (eFigure 9, A–D) levels and the ALSFRS-R slope did not reach statistical significance in either ALS group.

In agreement with the trends observed for the individual markers, the aggregate score of CHIT1, CHI3L1, CCL2, and CCL3 improved the association with disease progression in both sporadic and C9orf72 patients (Figure 5, C and D), showing stronger performance than when neurofilaments were included (Figure 5, A and B) or when neurofilaments were considered alone.

Finally, we evaluated whether combining the 6 commonly altered proteins could improve biomarker-based discrimination of ALS patients from controls. Receiver operating characteristic analyses indicated that, compared with neurofilaments alone, the combination of all 6 markers increased diagnostic sensitivity in C9orf72-ALS, whereas no substantial improvement was observed in sALS (Figure 6, A and B). Notably, this improvement in predictive performance was not observed when analyzing the combined ALS cohort (i.e., merging C9orf72 and sALS cases; eFigure 10). Together, these findings highlight the importance of genotype-specific stratification in ALS biomarker analyses.

Discussion

ALS is a complex, heterogeneous disease with various clinical subtypes and distinct molecular underpinnings. The variability in disease progression, clinical presentation, and underlying genetic causes complicates its diagnosis, prognostication, and treatment. The integration of emerging therapeutic strategies—particularly approaches based on RNA interference—alongside the validation of neurofilaments as pharmacodynamic biomarkers has opened new avenues for targeting ALS at the molecular level and for monitoring treatment response. However, it remains unclear whether and how novel neuroprotective therapies influence other key aspects of ALS pathophysiology, especially glial inflammation. Technological advances including NULISA-based targeted proteomics allow to address the interplay between neurodegeneration, glial inflammation and drug therapy by simultaneously monitoring the levels and dynamics of proxy markers of neurodegeneration and (neuro)inflammation within a single assay, thereby facilitating the development of biomarker panels and composite marker scores.

In this study, we applied the NULISA multiplex proteomics platform to characterize CSF biomarker profiles in sporadic and C9orf72-associated ALS, identifying both shared and genotype-specific molecular signatures. Our results confirm that, beyond neuroaxonal damage, a prominent and convergent proinflammatory signature characterizes the CSF of ALS patients, supporting the relevance of inflammatory biomarkers not only for diagnosis and prognosis but also for therapeutic monitoring.

Consistent with prior literature, NEFL and NEFH were robustly elevated in both ALS groups compared with healthy controls and contributed strongly to group separation in PCA analyses. Neurofilaments remain the most validated CSF biomarkers in ALS, reflecting the extent of neuroaxonal degeneration and correlating with disease progression.23,24 However, our data clearly show that neurofilaments alone do not fully capture the complexity of ALS-associated molecular changes.

A key finding of this study is the consistent upregulation of the inflammatory and glial-associated markers CHIT1, CHI3L1, CCL2, and CCL3 in both sporadic and C9orf72-ALS patients. CHIT1 and CHI3L1 are established indicators of microglial and astrocytic activation and have previously been associated with disease severity and survival in ALS, as well as other diseases.13,25,26 Of note in this context, a recent study has demonstrated that hat chitinase upregulation in ALS is not only evident in glial cells but also in neurons.27 Similarly, chemokines such as CCL2 and CCL3 play central roles in monocyte recruitment and innate immune activation and have been reported to be increased in ALS samples.28,29 These observations are consistent with previous CSF biomarker studies in ALS,12,30-32 which highlighted the relevance of neurofilaments and inflammatory markers such as CHIT1 and CHI3L1 as indicators of neurodegeneration and glial activation. In line with these reports, our data confirm that NEFL, NEFH, CHIT1, CHI3L1, CCL2, and CCL3 represent key markers distinguishing ALS patients from controls. Notably, although these biomarkers were significantly altered in both sporadic and C9orf72-associated ALS, their association with disease progression (which should interpret with caution because of the low, but still significant R2 values) and their performance in discriminating ALS patients from controls appeared more pronounced in the C9orf72 group. This observation may reflect a more homogeneous molecular signature within genetically defined ALS compared with the broader clinical and biological heterogeneity observed in sALS. Importantly, the use of the highly multiplexed NULISA platform enabled the simultaneous assessment of a substantially larger panel of proteins than typically analyzed in earlier studies, allowing a broader characterization of the inflammatory landscape in ALS CSF. Together, these findings further support the central role of neuroinflammation in ALS pathophysiology and highlight the potential added value of multiplex biomarker profiling for improved disease characterization, patient stratification, and potentially monitoring of disease-modifying treatments in the future. In fact, while the overall inflammatory profile was shared between ALS subtypes, we also identified C9orf72-specific elevations in ENO2 and PRDX6, markers linked to neuronal injury and oxidative stress. Oxidative stress and altered redox homeostasis have been repeatedly implicated in C9orf72-associated ALS, likely reflecting mitochondrial dysfunction and toxic dipeptide repeat accumulation.33,34 In contrast, higher levels of GFAP, IL12p70, IFNG, and TIMP3 in sALS suggest subtle differences in astrocytic and immune signaling pathways between ALS subtypes, independent of disease progression rate.

A potential translational implication of our findings lies in the relevance of proinflammatory biomarkers for monitoring therapeutic responses. RNA interference-based therapies, including those targeting the C9orf72 repeat expansion, represent one of the most promising disease-modifying strategies in ALS. However, intrathecal ASO administration has also been associated with inflammatory adverse events, including activation of innate immune pathways and cytokine release in the CSF.19,20,35,36 In this context, biomarkers such as CHIT1, CHI3L1, CCL2, and CCL3 may be particularly valuable as pharmacodynamic and safety readouts, enabling discrimination between treatment-related inflammatory responses and disease-driven neuroinflammation during postmarket surveillance.

Notably, the aggregation of inflammatory markers with neurofilaments significantly improved diagnostic sensitivity in C9orf72-ALS and enhanced correlations with disease progression in both ALS subtypes. The superior performance of the inflammatory aggregate score compared with neurofilaments alone underscores the added value of capturing immune-related processes alongside neurodegeneration. This multidimensional biomarker strategy may be especially relevant in clinical trials, where distinguishing target engagement, treatment-induced inflammation, and true disease modification is critical. Monitoring these parameters as part of a risk mitigation plan in early clinical development could help identify a biologically effective dose level with manageable side effects during Phase 1b/2a enabling further successful development.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that the CSF of ALS patients is characterized by a robust and shared glial and inflammatory signature that complements established neurodegeneration markers. Multiplexed measurement of inflammatory biomarkers using low-volume platforms such as NULISA offers a powerful approach for patient stratification, disease monitoring, and, importantly, safety and response assessment in interventional clinical trials. Future longitudinal studies will be essential to validate these markers as dynamic indicators of treatment response and inflammatory adverse effects.
