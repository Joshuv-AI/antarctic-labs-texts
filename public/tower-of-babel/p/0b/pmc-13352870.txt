Genomic dynamics of antimicrobial resistance transmission between bacteria from intensive care unit surfaces and from critically ill patients
Authors: Cristel Iona Kennedy Cuevas, Valdir Marcos Stefenon, Gladys Mercedes Estigarribia Sanabria, Fernando José Florentín González, Adriana Raquel Rinflerch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Intensive Care Unit (ICU) is an environment where multiple factors that predispose to the transmission of Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) coexist. Genomic sequencing is a technology that enables a better understanding of how AMR genes are transmitted within the ICU environment.ObjectiveTo evaluate the genomic dynamics of the transmission of antimicrobial resistance genes between bacteria from ICU surfaces and bacteria from critically ill patients.MethodsA cross-sectional analytical study. Surface swab samples were collected from the ICU, including suction units, infusion pumps, stethoscopes, dialysis machines, monitors, ventilators and gas valves, as well as samples from critically ill patients with infections. Species were identified using microbiological microplate panels; antibiotic susceptibility testing was carried out to assess AMR; and whole-genome sequencing was performed on isolates that were multidrug-resistant (MDR). The sequencing was carried out using a nanopore long-read sequencer and the results were analysed using specialised bioinformatics tools.ResultsA total of 130 positive isolates were identified, of which 96 (73.85%) were from environmental samples and 34 (26.15%) from patient samples. Forty (30.76%) MDR species were identified. The most common MDR species were Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii. The most frequently detected AMR genes were blaNDM-5 and blaOXA-23. The most common plasmids were IncFIB(K)_1_Kpn3, Col440I and IncL/M(pMU407)_1_pMU407. A clonal outbreak of Acinetobacter baumannii ST 2 was detected on the dialysis machine, stethoscopes and patient samples. Evidence of horizontal transfer of resistance genes via plasmids conjugation was found in samples taken from suction units, oxygen valves and tracheal secretion in three cases.ConclusionThe findings of this research show that antimicrobial resistance genes are transferred between bacteria on surfaces and bacteria in critically ill patients, and that this transfer occurs in both directions.

Introduction

According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a phenomenon that occurs when microorganisms such as bacteria, fungi, parasites and viruses change over time and the medicines used to treat them lose their effectiveness [1].

A multidrug-resistant (MDR) strain is one that is resistant to at least one agent from three or more antimicrobial categories. In Intensive Care Units (ICUs), the prevalence of patients with infections caused by MDR bacteria ranges from 45.2% to 69.6%, and MDR infections are associated with longer ICU stays and high mortality rates [2, 3, 4].

For a bacterium to be transmitted within an ICU, three elements must come together: a reservoir (source of the microorganism), a susceptible host (a patient who is immunocompromised or has an acute injury), and a route of transmission (contact with infected individuals or carriers and/or contact with contaminated objects). Reservoirs of microorganisms may be human (patients, healthcare staff or visitors) or may be fomites (inert surfaces, such as instruments or ICU equipment) [5, 6].

International studies have identified MDR microorganisms on inert surfaces in ICUs, such as MDR Acinetobacter baumannii on keyboards, bed sheets, ventilators, washbasins and infusion holder; Alcaligenes spp. with high antibiotic resistance on door handles and medical record folders, and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) Acinetobacter baumannii complex on bedside table, floor and monitor [7, 8, 9].

In order to investigate bacterial antimicrobial resistance in depth, one of the most informative methods is genomic sequencing. Bacterial genomic sequencing involves determining the sequence of the nucleotide bases in the bacterial chromosome, including its plasmids. This method enables the detection of the genes responsible for antimicrobial resistance and also allows the identification of their mobile genetic elements [10].

With regard to molecular biology studies for the detection of AMR genes carried out using isolates from ICU surfaces, a study using short-read sequencing identified genes such as blaKPC, responsible for resistance to carbapenems, mecA, responsible for resistance to most beta-lactam antibiotics, including methicillin and vanA, responsible for resistance to glycopeptides, on ICU surfaces such as bed rails, infusion pumps, electrocardiographs, stethoscopes, dialysis equipment, oximeters, respirators and gas valves [11]. Another study, which used multiplex PCR amplification to identify AMR genes, detected the blaIMP, blaKPC and blaOXA-48 genes which confer resistance to carbapenems in swabs taken from ICU beds [12].

Furthermore, there are few studies that have compared the genetic data from isolates taken from the environmental and from patients; the study by Sukhum K, et al. is one of the few to suggest the movement of clones between the environmental and patients, but it did not include an analysis of genetic elements such as plasmids and prophages [13].

Although it is known that the genes responsible for antimicrobial resistance can be spread via vertical and horizontal transfer, current studies do not specifically reflect how or in which direction the transfer of antimicrobial resistance genes occurs between patients and ICU surfaces. Consequently, our understanding of how to predict and combat such transfer remains limited [14, 15, 16].

In light of the above, the aim of this study is to analyse the whole genome of isolates from inert surfaces in the ICU and from critically ill patients with infection, using long-read genomic sequencing, in order to demonstrate the dynamics of transmission of antimicrobial resistance genes between surface bacteria and patient bacteria.

Methods

Design

Analytical, cross-sectional study.

Sample and sampling

The sample size was calculated using the Epi Info™ application, based on a total of 200 samples and an expected frequency of positive isolates of 12%, taken from the study by Kennedy, et al. [17] with a 95% confidence interval and a margin of error of 5%, resulting in a minimum sample size of 90 samples.

The sampling method was non-probabilistic, involving consecutive cases. The collection period ran from January to July 2024. The study samples were collected from the ICU at the Coronel Oviedo Regional Hospital (HRCO) in Paraguay.

The study included samples from the surfaces listed in Sect. "Surface sampling procedure", taken from a 10-bed ICU, as well as biological samples from patients with a confirmed bacterial infection, during the period in which the surface samples were collected.

Samples that were compromised in any way, such as through dropping or spillage during transport to the laboratory, and samples that became contaminated upon re-isolation were excluded.

Surface sampling procedure

Samples were collected using sterile swabs (3 M Quick Swab) with Letheen broth, free from sodium bisulphite, as the transport medium.

Swab samples were collected from ICU surfaces such as suction devices, infusion pumps, stethoscopes, dialysis machine, monitors, ventilators and gas valves (oxygen and medical air valves).

Sampling was performed twice, once early in the morning (at 7:00 am) and a second time upon the admission of a new patient to the recently disinfected ward.

The swabs were transported to the laboratory facilities at a controlled temperature (18 °C) and processed within a maximum of 24 h after sample collection.

It should be noted that the gas valves were swabbed around their edges and not inside the valves. Both the suction units and the dialyzer were swabbed around the edges of their connectors; at no point were areas inside their tubes or connections swabbed.

Inoculation and incubation of samples

Surface samples were inoculated onto rehydratable plates (3 M Petrifilm) containing different nutrients for each bacterial group. The rehydratable plates were incubated for the periods and at the temperatures listed below:

Plates for Enterobacterales for 24 h ± 2 h at 35 °C ± 1 °C.Plates for Coliforms for 24 h ± 3 h at 37 °C ± 1 °C.Plates for Staphylococci for 24 h ± 2 h at 37 °C ± 1 °C.

Plates for Enterobacterales for 24 h ± 2 h at 35 °C ± 1 °C.

Plates for Enterobacterales for 24 h ± 2 h at 35 °C ± 1 °C.

Plates for Coliforms for 24 h ± 3 h at 37 °C ± 1 °C.

Plates for Coliforms for 24 h ± 3 h at 37 °C ± 1 °C.

Plates for Staphylococci for 24 h ± 2 h at 37 °C ± 1 °C.

Plates for Staphylococci for 24 h ± 2 h at 37 °C ± 1 °C.

When colony growth was observed on the plates, re-isolation was carried out on Levine, MacConkey, Müller-Hinton, Mannitol Salt and Blood, followed by identification of the isolated species using Liofilchem’s Integral System Enterobacteria and Integral System Staphylococci panels, along with additional biochemical tests such as Triple Sugar Iron (TSI) and Voges-Proskauer (VP).

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

The identified isolates were inoculated onto Müller-Hinton agar for antibiotic susceptibility testing, in accordance with the Kirby-Bauer method, using antibiotic discs appropriate for each species: amoxicillin with clavulanic acid, boronic acid, aztreonam, amikacin, ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid with sodium acetate, cefoxitin, cefepime, cephalothin, clindamycin, ciprofloxacin, colistin, erythromycin, gentamicin, imipenem, levofloxacin, meropenem, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, tigecycline, oxacillin, piperacillin-tazobactam and vancomycin.

The results were interpreted in accordance with the parameters of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) [18].

For the detection of enzyme production, the double-disc and combined-disc methods were used for Extended-Spectrum Beta-lactamases, the Carbapenem Inactivation Method (CIM) and the boronic acid test for Carbapenemases, and the EDTA test for Metallo-beta-lactamases.

Procedure for collecting patient samples

The researcher did not take samples directly from patients; instead, she re-isolates bacteria from the hospital’s biobank. The re-isolates were taken from patient samples collected routinely in the ICU during the same period as the surface sample collection (from January to July 2024), and for which species identification and susceptibility testing results were available.

Selection of isolates for sequencing

Surface and patient isolates that were MDR, i.e. resistant to at least one agent from three or more antimicrobial categories.

The selected isolates were stored in a freezer at -80 °C until DNA extraction.

DNA extraction and quantification

The stored isolates were thawed in incubators at 37 °C for 40 to 60 min and then re-inoculated onto the same agar plates mentioned in Sect. Inoculation and incubation of samples. Thawing took place in December 2025.

For extraction, 200 mg of culture from each isolate (the size of a grain of rice) was taken using a sterile loop and placed into a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube containing 1 mL of isotonic buffer (saline solution). This mixture was vortexed for 1 min and centrifuged for 1 min to obtain a semi-homogeneous suspension.

DNA extraction was then carried out using the Zymo BIOMICS DNA Miniprep kit, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions [19].

Upon completion of the extraction, DNA quantification was performed using a fluorometer (Quantus-Promega), verifying a concentration of 200 ng for each isolate.

Genomic sequencing

Immediately after DNA quantification, the library was prepared and the isolates were sequenced using a nanopore long-read sequencer (MinION MK1B), following the protocol by González Escalona N, et al. [20].

Two flow cells (FLO-MIN114) and one 24-barcoding kit (SQK-RBK114.24) were used for sequencing.

Sequencing was performed using MinKNOW software, with two runs of 48 h each, comprising 20 isolates per run.

Bioinformatic analysis

The bioinformatic analysis was carried out from January to March 2026. Various programmes were used, such as Nanoplot to verify read length, read quality and estimated coverage; Filtlong for filtering low-quality reads; Flye for bacterial genome assembly; Bakta for bacterial and plasmid genome annotation; ABRIcate for screening specific resistance genes and other genetic elements, MLST for species verification, and comparison of sequence types (STs) between isolates, Minimap2 to determine plasmid identity percentage and for comparing similarity between plasmids, PHASTER for identifying phages, MUMmer for genome alignment, and Snippy for checking distances between SNPs and Indels.

The PubMLST and BIGSdb-Pasteur databases were used to complete the assignment of the sequence types. The database used for antimicrobial resistance genes was Resfinder and CARD, whilst the database used for plasmids was Plasmidfinder.

For the classification of plasmid similarity, the cut-off points were > 70% for high similarity and ≥ 95% for very high similarity or identical plasmids [21, 22].

Ethical approval declarations

The protocol for this study was reviewed by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medical Sciences at the National University of Caaguazú and was approved (CEI No 19/2023).

In order to carry out this study, approval was obtained from the director of the hospital concerned and the head of its Bacteriology Department.

After reviewing the protocol and the director’s letter of authorisation, the head of the Bacteriology Department (responsible for biological samples) authorised the collection of re-isolates from the ICU biobank, in accordance with guidelines 10 and 11 of the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS).

All data relating to the re-isolates from ICU patients were collected from an anonymised database, without access to identifying information, in order to respect confidentiality and anonymity, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki on medical research involving human subjects.

With regard to biosafety and the management of waste from sample processing, procedures were carried out in accordance with the hospital waste management manual of the General Directorate of Health Surveillance (DGVS) of the Ministry of Public Health and Social Welfare (MSPBS) [23].

Results

Total of 130 positive isolates were identified, of which 96 (73.85%) were from surface samples and 34 (26.15%) from patient samples.

The species identified on surfaces were as follows: 25 (19.23%) Klebsiella pneumoniae, 19 (14.61%) Staphylococcus saprophyticus, 14 (10.77%) Staphylococcus epidermidis, 12 (9.23%) Enterobacter cloacae, 6 (4.61%) Escherichia coli, 4 (3.08%) Klebsiella aerogenes, 3 (2.31%) Klebsiella oxytoca, 3 (2.31%) Acinetobacter baumannii, 2 (1.54%) Staphylococcus haemolyticus, 2 (1.54%) Staphylococcus hominis, 2 (1.54%) Cronobacter spp., 1 (0.77%) Klebsiella michiganensis, 1 (0.77%) Staphylococcus simulans, 1 (0.77%) Pseudomonas aeruginosa and 1 (0.77%) Stenotrophomonas maltophilia.

The species identified in patients were 11 (8.46%) Klebsiella pneumoniae, 10 (7.69%) Escherichia coli, 8 (6.15%) Enterobacter cloacae, 3 (2.31%) Acinetobacter baumannii and 2 (1.54%) Pseudomonas aeruginosa.

Of the total isolates, 40 (30.76%) were MDR; of these, 23 (57.5%) were from surfaces and 17 (42.5%) were from patients. Species isolated from surfaces were: 10 (25%) Klebsiella pneumoniae, 3 (7.5%) Acinetobacter baumannii, 3 (7.5%) Enterobacter cloacae, 2 (5%) Staphylococcus haemolyticus, 1 (2.5%) Cronobacter spp., 1 (2.5%) Klebsiella michiganensis, 1 (2.5%) Pseudomonas aeruginosa, 1 (2.5%) Staphylococcus epidermidis, 1 (2.5%) Stenotrophomonas maltophilia.

In patients, the species identified were 6 (15%) Klebsiella pneumoniae, 4 (10%) Escherichia coli, 3 (7.5%) Acinetobacter baumannii, 2 (5%) Enterobacter cloacae and 2 (5%) Pseudomonas aeruginosa.

The complete genome sequencing of all MDR isolates was successfully carried out. An example of the circular genome of one of the isolates is shown in Fig. 1.

The genomic characterisation of surface isolates, together with their collection dates, is detailed in Table 2, whilst the genomic characterisation of patient isolates, together with their isolation dates, is set out in Table 3.

Genomic sequencing identified 31 prophages (77.5%) in isolates from surfaces and patients, of which 21 (67.74%) were fossil prophages and 10 (32.26%) were prophages with a complete cluster consistent with integrated temperate prophages.

In addition, of the 23 MDR surface samples, 56.52% carried extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) genes and 39.13% carried carbapenemase genes. Of the 17 patient samples, 64.71% carried ESBL genes and 41.17% carried carbapenemase genes. The details of the ESBL and carbapenemase genes, classified by sample type, are set out in Table 4.

Of the plasmid types detected on surfaces and in patients, only 4 plasmid types exhibited the characteristic that similarity occurred between a surface sample and a patient sample, a finding that occurred on 15 occasions. The plasmid type, the average percentage of identity and the source of the isolates for these 15 occasions are detailed in Table 5.

SNPs and Indels were examined among isolates with the same sequence type (ST), such as Klebsiella pneumoniae ST 39, Klebsiella pneumoniae ST 7229 and Acinetobacter baumannii ST 2. It was found that, in the case of Klebsiella pneumoniae ST 39, the distances ranged from 31 to 57 SNPs and from 16 to 28 Indels, indicating an expanded clonal lineage or related subpopulation. In the case of Klebsiella pneumoniae ST 7229, the distance ranged from 2721 to 31,510 SNPs and 5127 to 16,869 indels, indicating that, although they belong to the same sequence type, their genetic distance is inconsistent with strict clonality. Whereas for Acinetobacter baumannii, distances ranging from 4 to 8 SNPs and from 16 to 32 Indels were found, implying direct clones.

Discussion

In this study, the most prevalent AMR genes across all surfaces were blaOXA-1, blaCTX-M-15 and blaNDM-5. Of this list, blaCTX-M-15 and blaNDM-5 are considered the most dangerous, as they are genes responsible for the production of extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) and New Delhi metalloβ-lactamases (NDMs).

This detection of AMR genes on surfaces is similar to the study by Nieto M, et al. which analysed AMR genes using short-read genomic sequencing and identified blaCTX-M-15 and blaNDM genes in swabs taken from surfaces in the neonatal units of ten hospitals across six low and middle income countries, reporting a high prevalence of bacteria carrying AMR genes in the facilities studied [24]. It is also similar to the study by Christoff A, et al. which used short-read sequencing frequently identified blaCTX-M-1 group, blaSHV-like and blaKPC-like genes in environmental samples from ICU and neonatal ICU [11].

On the other hand, in patients, the most common AMR genes were aph(3’’)-Ib, aph(6)-Id, blaADC-25, blaOXA-23 and sul1, of which the one with the greatest potential for danger would be blaOXA-23, as it is responsible for hydrolysing penicillins, broad-spectrum cephalosporins and carbapenems.

The genes identified in patients are comparable to those in the study by Hussein JM, et al. which analysed samples from hospitalised patients with urinary tract infections using PCR and isolated Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Proteus mirabilis and Pseudomonas aeruginosa carrying the blaOXA-23 gene, and also with the study by Sánchez S, et al. which detected Acinetobacter baumannii carrying the aph(3’’)-Ib, aph(6)-Id, blaOXA-23, blaADC-73 and sul1 genes via short-read and long-read sequencing in patients admitted to the ICU [25, 26].

The high prevalence of genes responsible for ESBLs and carbapenemases is consistent with other studies conducted in ICUs and is a constant cause of serious infections resistant to multiple antibiotics [11, 25, 26, 27].

Although studies have detected antimicrobial resistance genes on hospital surfaces and in patients [11, 12, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28], the most significant findings of this research focus on the genomic relationship between antimicrobial resistance in surface isolates and patient isolates, and these findings are detailed below.

There were three findings in which clonal dissemination or a related lineage was identified. The first finding, involved the isolation on five occasions of Acinetobacter baumannii ST 2, carrying the blaADC-25, blaOXA-23 and blaOXA-66 genes, with no plasmids and with only 4 to 8 SNPs and 16 to 32 indels of distance, in a dialyzer sample from January, in two stethoscope samples from January and April, and also in two patient samples; one of tracheal secretions from January and one of blood culture from April. Genetic characteristics such as ST, repeated AMR genes, the absence of plasmids, short distances between SNPs and indels, and the presence of an initial prophage are consistent with a clonal outbreak, where the absence of plasmids, the change in the presence of the prophage, the number of AMR genes with variations, and the short distances between SNPs and indels, reveal a stable chromosome with intra-outbreak evolution involving degradation of the prophage, few point mutations in the genome, and a sequence length with low insertion and/or deletion rates.

This finding is consistent with the study by Nateghi M et al., which identified carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) isolates on hospital surfaces that were clonally related, using multiplex PCR. It is emphasised that sequence type 2 accounts for more than 70% of CRAB isolates and undoubtedly constitutes a threat to the survival of ICU patients [28].

The second finding, involved three isolates of the species Klebsiella pneumoniae ST 7229, obtained from tracheal secretion and urine samples in January and from an oxygen valve sample in July. The presence of the blaSHV-145 gene and the IncR and IncFIB(K)_1_Kpn3 plasmids, with high percentages of IncR similarity (93.68% and 92.63%) between the two patient samples and the oxygen valve, and also with high percentage of IncFIB(K)_1_Kpn3 similarity (88,36% and 92,13%) between the two patient samples and the oxygen valve. These isolates showed a related lineage, but the genetic distances of SNPs (2721 to 31510) observed and the similarity of the plasmid replicons suggest that antimicrobial resistance likely spread through a combination of clonal expansion and horizontal plasmid transfer, rather than through strict clonality among all isolates.

This finding is consistent with the study by Luo K, et al. who found that IncFIB(K)-1-Kpn3 plasmids are more common in patients with ICU infections, using clinical data obtained from short-read sequencing results [29].

The third finding, involved two isolates of the species Klebsiella pneumoniae ST 39, obtained from a patient urine sample in April and from an oxygen valve in July; both possessed the blaKPC-2 and blaSHV-187 genes, as well as the Col440I and IncL/M(pMU407)_1_pMU407 plasmids, with a very high percentage of similarity (95.94%, 99.12%). The coincidences between AMR genes, the similarity of the plasmids and the distance between SNPs (31 to 57) appear to indicate that the spread of AMR genes was due to an expanding clonal lineage.

This finding can be compared with the study by Puraswani M, et al. which detected a predominance of the Col440I plasmid through short-read and long-read sequencing of patient and environmental samples, and carried out phylogenetic analysis, concluding that the environment plays a crucial role in the spread of infectious strains [30].

With regard to the findings on horizontal transfer, three cases have come to light. The first case, was observed in an isolate of Klebsiella michiganensis ST 138 from the surface of the suction device in February and another isolate of Enterobacter cloacae ST 53 from the patient tracheal secretion in March; both isolates carried the blaCTX-M-15 gene and the IncL/M (pMU407)_1_pMU407 plasmid with a very high percentage of similarity (99.31%). This finding would suggest that medical devise act as a reservoir for MDR bacteria and facilitate the horizontal transfer of AMR genes between bacteria of different species via plasmid conjugation.

It should be noted that Klebsiella michiganensis is a species rarely reported in the literature, as it is virtually impossible to distinguish it phenotypically from the other species within the Klebsiella oxytoca complex; consequently, it is only reported when genomic analyses are carried out [31]. Furthermore, this would be the first time this species has been identified in Paraguay. Although studies from other countries have isolated it in tracheal secretions from patients and in samples from infants with meningitis [32, 33].

The second case, was observed among three isolates, an isolate of the species Pseudomonas aeruginosa ST 319 from a tracheal secretion in April and an isolation of Klebsiella pneumoniae ST 39 from the oxygen valve in July, which carried the blaKPC-2 gene and the Col440I and IncL/M(pMU407)_1_pMU407 plasmids, with a high percentage of similarity (84.12% and 87,21%). These findings indicate that MDR microorganisms from patients are capable of contaminating the hospital environment, facilitating direct horizontal transfer via conjugation and generating MDR environmental species that survive daily disinfection and cleaning processes.

Of particular note in these case is the isolation of Pseudomonas aeruginosa ST 319, a sequence type which, according to case reports such as that by Nishihara Y, et al. is rare and can rapidly lead to a fatal outcome [34].

The third case, was observed in an isolate of Klebsiella pneumoniae ST 39 detected on 1 July in the oxygen valve, an isolate of Klebsiella pneumoniae ST 278 detected on 5 July in the patient’s tracheal secretion. The oxygen valve possessed cat and blaSHV-145 genes and the tracheal secretion had cat and blaSHV-27 genes, both with the IncFIB(K)_1_Kpn3 plasmid, showing high similarity (70.21%) to each other and also to the Siphovirus prophage (integrated cluster). This finding suggest, through horizontal transfer, the patient’s isolate acquired the cat gene via a conjugative plasmid (IncFIB(K)_1_Kpn3) from the surface of the oxygen valve. The differences in the blaSHV gene indicate post-transfer recombination, and the identical presence of the prophages in both isolates illustrates how, during the transfer of genetic information, plasmids are also capable of transferring the accessory environment. Furthermore, the fact that the Siphovirus cluster is complete demonstrates how prophages are capable of being stably transferred via plasmids.

The detection of prophages, most of which are of Siphovirus morphology, indicates the strategic adaptation of MDR isolates, as lysogenic bacteria have a competitive advantage in terms of survival, as temperate prophages are thought to prevent bacterial lysis; furthermore, the presence of prophages, even if defective, can significantly contribute to microorganisms being more efficient in biofilm formation and in the development of resistance to drugs and disinfectants [35].

This last case could be linked to the study by Pacífico C, et al. which was one of the first studies to find an association between bacteriophages and ICU bacteria, identifying phages from the Myoviridae and Siphoviridae families [36].

Every ICU has standardised disinfection protocols, and it is usually the national health ministries that determine the composition and frequency of these disinfection strategies. The detection of MDR microorganisms carrying beta-lactam and carbapenem resistance genes via highly conjugative plasmids on highly critical surfaces such as the edges of oxygen valves, and the repeated isolation of CRAB from stethoscopes, highlights the importance of ensuring comprehensive disinfection, suggesting that certain surfaces and medical instruments may be overlooked. Furthermore, it is of vital importance that each healthcare facility assesses whether these protocols are effective based on its own environmental microbiological analyses.

Based on the AMR genes detected, the mcr-1 colistin resistance gene was absent from surfaces and was observed in only one patient, whilst the tet(X) tigecycline resistance gene was absent not only from surfaces but also from patients; which would indicate that these two antibiotics would be the most effective against the majority of the isolates analysed, with the obvious exception of those species that are intrinsically resistant to them.

It should be noted that this study takes a novel approach by determining the genetic distance between MDR isolates from surfaces and patients, and that it is the first to classify cases consistent with the transfer of AMR genes between environmental and patient samples into horizontal transfer and vertical dissemination.

Among the limitations of this study is the view that the findings could have been broadened by including more types of surfaces and samples from healthcare staff. Nevertheless, the results meet the study’s objective, being representative of the dynamics of AMR transfer, highlighting the type of AMR transfer mechanism, the genetic information involved, the SNP distance between isolates and the chronology of transmission.

Conclusion

The genomic findings show that antimicrobial resistance genes are transferred between bacteria on ICU surfaces and bacteria on critically ill patients, and that this transfer occurs horizontally via conjugative plasmids and vertically via clonal spread. Furthermore, transmission occurs in both directions; from bacteria on patients to bacteria on surfaces and from bacteria on surfaces to bacteria on patients.
