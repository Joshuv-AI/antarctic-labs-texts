Eating Experiences of Patients With Cancer in the Hospital – A Qualitative Study
Authors: Jip M. van Elst, Kris Beukers, Sandra Beijer, Maryska Janssen‐Heijnen, Anna K. L. Reyners, Harriët Jager‐Wittenaar, Jacco J. de Haan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsEnjoying food is related to the taste, smell and texture of food, ambience during meals, presentation of the meals, and freedom to choose meals. A decrease in food enjoyment can lead to decreased food intake, undesired weight loss, and reduced quality of life. Cancer and cancer treatment can induce symptoms that affect food enjoyment. To optimise food enjoyment and intake, insight in the experiences and preferences of hospitalised patients with cancer is essential. This qualitative study aimed to explore the perspectives of patients with cancer on the experience of food and drinks, ambience during meals, timing of the meals, and the freedom to choose one's own meals, during hospital admission.MethodsIn hospitalised patients with cancer treated with systemic therapy, 12 semi‐structured interviews were performed. Nutritional status was subjectively assessed, and taste and smell objectively to characterise the study population. Thematic analysis was used to identify key themes.ResultsThree selective codes emerged: (1) the quality of hospital food and drinks, (2) the presentation of hospital food and drinks, and (3) meeting patients' preferences. Patients described hospital food, and in particular main meals, as bland, overcooked, and repetitive. Autonomy in meal choices and flexibility in portion sizes were appreciated but inconsistently facilitated. Ambiance was moderately important, with suggestions for communal dining areas. Presentation issues, including plastic odours and inconsistent meal temperatures, were mentioned. Patients valued personalised options such as additional seasonings or sauces.ConclusionsThe eating experience of hospitalised cancer patients is shaped by various factors including quality of food, its presentation, and the ability to cater to individual preferences. By addressing these aspects and implementing a patient‐centred food service, care could be optimised in oncology wards.

Introduction

Enjoying food is related to the taste, smell and texture of food, ambience during meals, timing of the meals, presentation of the meals, and the freedom to choose one's own meals [1, 2, 3]. Patients with cancer can have difficulties to maintain food enjoyment, especially if they are hospitalised. Cancer and systemic therapy can induce symptoms negatively related to food enjoyment, including mucositis, changes in taste and smell, early satiety, nausea, diarrhoea, pain, fatigue, and dyspnoea [4]. These symptoms can fluctuate in patients with cancer; they often experience variations in taste preferences and satiety levels, making it essential to provide them with flexibility in both the types of food available and the timing of meal serving [5, 6]. Hospitalised patients with cancer are particularly vulnerable to a reduced food enjoyment due to the intensity of treatments, long hospital stays, and limited access to familiar foods. Furthermore, the hospital environment could decrease food enjoyment due to factors such as noise, unpleasant odours, or the lack of a pleasant dining atmosphere [3]. The decrease in food enjoyment can lead to a reduced food intake, which can result in undesired loss of weight and muscle mass [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. At last, a reduced food enjoyment can enhance negative emotions regarding food and eating, and therefore may decrease quality of life [12, 13].

Understanding the factors that influence food enjoyment in hospitalised patients with cancer is necessary for developing targeted interventions to improve this enjoyment and therefore nutritional intake, enhance patient satisfaction, and optimise treatment outcomes [14]. Previous studies have examined patients' eating experiences in a healthcare setting and highlight the importance of patient‐centred approaches to improving food enjoyment and nutrition in these settings [15, 16, 17, 18]. However, these earlier studies have not studied specifically hospitalised patients with cancer, in which symptoms negatively related to food enjoyment are highly prevalent. Moreover, food enjoyment varies significantly across cultures, reflecting diverse culinary traditions, dietary preferences, and social norms surrounding food consumption [19]. Therefore, these studies should be performed in various cultures, as the factors influencing food enjoyment and preferences may differ greatly depending on local values and expectations regarding eating.

The aim of this qualitative study was to explore how hospitalised patients with cancer experience the food and drinks, ambience during meals, timing and presentation of the meals, and the freedom to choose one's own meals. Furthermore, patients' suggestions about possible improvements on eating experience during hospital admission were collected.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Study Population

This qualitative study was conducted at the University Medical Center Groningen (UMCG), the Netherlands, in patients with cancer admitted to the medical oncology and haematology wards. Participants were recruited between December 2022 and November 2023. Patients with pathologically confirmed diagnosis of any cancer type, and treated with systemic therapy, were included. Furthermore, patients had to be hospitalised for more than 2 days, and had to have consumed multiple meals in the hospital environment. By purposive sampling, the included participants varied with regard to age, gender, days of hospitalisation, type of tumour, and type of treatment. Participants were excluded if contra‐indications for oral food intake existed, or if oral food intake was perceived as too painful to eat orally by the patient. This study was approved by the Medical Ethical Committee of the UMCG (METc UMCG no. 202200345), and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki for Medical Research involving Human Subjects. The study was conducted and reported according to the qualitative reporting guideline COREQ [20].

Procedure

Screening was conducted by the head nurse, who reviewed the list of admitted patients. Potential participants received verbal and written information about the study during hospitalisation by the investigators. They were given 3 days to consider participation; however, if participants indicated that they wished to take part immediately, this was also permitted. In all cases, participation took place during the same hospital admission. Following the provision of written informed consent, a date for the interview was scheduled. The interview was conducted in a quiet room on the oncology ward. To characterise the study population, the Patient‐Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG‐SGA) Short Form was filled in by the participants and checked by the researcher [21]. This questionnaire includes four boxes on weight and weight change, food intake, nutrition impact symptoms, and activities/function. The PG‐SGA Short Form has a numerical scoring range from 0 (no problems) to 36 (severe problems). The maximum scores for each box are as follows: weight and weight change (5 points), food intake (4 points), nutrition impact symptoms (24 points), and activities/function (3 points). The risk of malnutrition was classified as low (0–3 points), moderate (4–8 points), or high (≥ 9 points). Thereafter, a semi‐structured in‐depth interview was performed. All interviews were conducted by the same researcher (JvE, female, MD/PhD student). Participants were not informed about the researcher's background, personal goals, or reasons for conducting the study.

The interview started with questions about their personal life, to make the participant feel more comfortable, and questions regarding the cancer diagnosis and treatment. The addressed topics related to patients' experiences with various aspects of eating, including challenges related to eating, perceptions of the taste and smell of hospital food, the available food choices, the timing and presentation of meals, the ambiance during meal times, the use of protein‐enriched snacks, and the differences between eating at home and in the hospital. Furthermore, personal tips‐and‐tricks provided by the patients to maintain adequate nutritional intake, and how patients' think that food enjoyment can be improved in the hospital were addressed. The topics were established in advance, based on literature, by researcher JvE and reviewed with researcher JdH, SB, AR and JdH (see supplement A for questionnaire). It was not pilot tested, nor did it involve formal patient or public involvement. The interview took a maximum of 1 h. No field notes were made during or after the interviews.

Subsequently, taste and smell were objectively determined with respectively taste strips and Sniffin' Sticks [22, 23]. Alterations in taste and smell can significantly influence the enjoyability and acceptability hospital food, yet patients are not always aware of these changes [24]. Including taste and smell tests in this study allowed to capture the diversity of taste and smell alterations, contributing to more effective purposive sampling.

Taste strips with an impregnated taste solution were placed in the participants' mouths. The taste solutions contained the basic tastes bitter, sweet, sour and salty, with each taste presented at four different concentrations, ranging from weakest to strongest. Thus, a total of 16 tests were performed, with each taste being presented once at each concentration level. This method assessed taste perception (i.e., the ability to identify the four basic tastes) at varying concentrations. The strips were administered in a standard random order predetermined by the manufacturer, with the researcher aware of the sequence but the participants blinded to it. After each strip, participants were asked which taste they noticed. A correct answer earned one point, and a total of 16 points could be obtained. The scoring was done after the test, with participants' answers later compared to the correct. A taste score of < 9 was considered to indicate hypogeusia, based on the manufacturer's guidelines. Sniffin' Sticks are pens filled with liquid odorants, which were presented to the participants' nose. Participants smelled 12 different scents with an interval of minimal 30 s to prevent desensitisation. For each scent, participants chose between four options from a multiple choice card, and earning one point with a correct answer. Participants earned one point for each correct answer, with a maximum of 12 points achievable. The responses were scored after the test by comparing them to the correct answers. A score below 10 on the smell test was classified as hyposmia, based on the manufacturer's established cut‐off value.

Finally, the participants were asked whether topics were missed and whether the questions or topics were clear. After the interview, the socio‐demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants were collected from the patients' electronic file, including stage and location of the primary tumour, type and status of treatment, duration of treatment, age, gender, days of hospitalisation, current diet (including tube feeding and oral nutritional supplements), weight (change), length, and medical history affecting taste, smell or mouthfeel.

Data Analysis

Participants were included until data saturation was reached, that is, no new insights were reported by the participants. All interviews were conducted by JvE, who completed training in qualitative methods through certified university courses. These courses included practical training in interviewing and qualitative data analysis. The interviews were audio recorded, transcribed verbatim by JvE and KB, and personal details were removed. Transcripts were not returned to participants for comment or correction, and participants did not provide feedback on the findings. The data were analysed with a thematic approach with inductive coding, guided by the framework described by Boeije in ‘Analysis in Qualitative Research’ [25]. The first two transcripts were independently open coded by three researchers (KB, JvE and SB) and afterwards, consensus on the codes was reached. The other transcripts were independently open coded by KB and JvE. Every two interviews, the codes were discussed and the code tree adjusted as needed. After eight interviews, data saturation was reached. However, as eight interviews were considered too few to capture sufficient variation in the sample, four additional interviews were conducted. The other four interviews were coded with the code tree [25]. Next, the subsequent phase involved axial coding through categorisation to organise and connect the emerging codes into meaningful groups. Axial coding was conducted inductively, by categorising and connecting the codes that emerged from the data into meaningful groups. Following axial coding, selective coding was employed to identify core themes. The categories from axial coding and the selective codes were discussed with all involved researchers [25]. All data has been coded and categorised in ATLAS.ti (version 24.1.1.).

Results

Study Population

In total, 14 potential participants were asked to participate in this study, of whom two declined participation due to fatigue and lack of interest. Therefore, 12 patients participated in this study, and no participants were excluded. The median interview duration was 36:10 min (IQR 21:45‐49:17).

Median age was 49 years (IQR 43–64). Most participants were male, former smokers, had testicular cancer, and were treated with chemotherapy. No participants had a medical history affecting taste, smell or mouthfeel. Characteristics of the study population are summarised in Table 1. A significant proportion of the participants is at risk of malnutrition, with 33% at high risk and 58% at intermediate risk. Of the 12 participants, three had objective hypogeusia (reduced sense of taste), and one did not complete the taste test due to mucositis. Furthermore, seven of the twelve participants had objective hyposmia (decreased ability to smell), and six of the twelve of participants experienced some form of subjective taste alteration, with hypogeusia and dysgeusia (altered taste) being the most common. Subjective smell changes were reported by two participants. A significant number of participants reported additional symptoms related to eating and drinking, particularly reduced appetite and dry mouth. Thematic Analysis

A total of 11 axial codes were identified, which were subsequently grouped into three selective codes: the quality of hospital food and drinks, the presentation, and meeting patients' preferences. These selective codes and the axial codes are presented in Figure 1 and will be further elaborated in the subsequent sections.

Selective and axial codes.

Selective Code 1: Quality of Hospital Food and Drinks

The experienced quality of hospital food and drinks emerged as a critical selective code, with the axial codes breakfast and lunch, main meal, and snacks and drinks.

Overall, breakfast and lunch were deemed adequate and generally perceived as satisfactory, though some participants desired higher quality bread options to better suit their preferences. Cultural differences were also noticeable. For example, breakfast and lunch were viewed as reflecting typical Dutch preferences and the meals were considered too average and unremarkable. Furthermore, the bread was perceived as dissatisfactory and sourdough bread was preferred instead by a non‐Dutch participant.

Participants were most critical about the hospital main meals. Participants perceived the main meal offerings as subpar, primarily due to the cooking and steaming methods employed. These cooking methods were reported to significantly detract from the flavour, making the food appear less attractive, as if little care had been taken in its preparation, and the texture of the food often was perceived as too soft and moist. However, on the other hand, the texture of the food was described as overly hard, with food sticking to the plate. Using sauces, such as chicken curry sauce, was reported to be helpful in addressing this problem.And yes, the same goes for the texture of potatoes, so to speak, you can overcook mashed potatoes to the point of ruin, but you can also turn them into something delicious. This difference between making something tasty out of it and, well, not putting in any effort at all can sometimes be quite significant.(Participant 12)

And yes, the same goes for the texture of potatoes, so to speak, you can overcook mashed potatoes to the point of ruin, but you can also turn them into something delicious. This difference between making something tasty out of it and, well, not putting in any effort at all can sometimes be quite significant.

Regardless of the preparation method, taste was another major concern, with the main meal being described as bland and inadequately seasoned. It was suggested that the option to add herbs and salt according to personal preference would enhance the dining experience.

The importance of the meal's appearance varied among participants. It was expressed that an attractive presentation was especially beneficial when feeling unwell, making the food more palatable. However, the food did not always look appetising, appearing dry. In contrast, it was reported that appearance was not important and the presentation was satisfactory and consistent with participants' expectations.

Temperature issues, like meals being served too hot, hindered participants to remove the plastic cover. Moreover, participants reported the food being too cold when delivered, when they received the meal at a later moment than regular main meal serving time. The plastic covers used to serve the food were criticised for imparting a plastic odour and detracting from the overall presentation of the meal. Selective Code 2: Presentation

The second selective code that emerged was presentation, in the broadest sense of the word, including food service staff, food and drink provision, and ambiance. The food service staff encompasses all individuals involved in the food and drink provision, including nutrition assistants and dietitians. The food service staff was perceived as friendly and accommodating. It was appreciated that staff members remember preferences of participants during extended hospital stays. However, participants experienced a fine line between receiving gentle encouragement to eat and feeling pressured by overly persistent offers:There are just people who are constantly nauseous, and you can't force things down their stomach. Last year I was there, and I even had arguments with the dietitian, the people handling the food, because I had those [brand name nutritional drinks] on my list, but I couldn't get them down because I was just too nauseous, you know. And then they have the list: ‘yeah, he has to have this, and he has to eat that.’ Well, try keeping it down, you know, when you're really sick.(Participant 9)

There are just people who are constantly nauseous, and you can't force things down their stomach. Last year I was there, and I even had arguments with the dietitian, the people handling the food, because I had those [brand name nutritional drinks] on my list, but I couldn't get them down because I was just too nauseous, you know. And then they have the list: ‘yeah, he has to have this, and he has to eat that.’ Well, try keeping it down, you know, when you're really sick.

Furthermore, concerns were raised about the involvement of multiple disciplines in food management, with fears of conflicting advice, and potential errors in food handling. Such conflicting advices and errors could occur when information about the patient's dietary needs (e.g., type or amount of nutritional supplements) is not effectively communicated between dietitians, nursing staff, and food service personnel. Additionally, it was noted that the knowledge of food and drink among the nutrition assistants was insufficient, highlighting a need for improved training in this area.

The food and drink provision concerns the timing of the meals, how often the food staff offers food and drinks, and how it is offered. Participants either found the main meals delivered too early or at appropriate times. No participants reported that main meals were delivered too late. It was expressed that the regularity of meal times was appreciated, while it was also preferred for meals to be provided based on hunger rather than fixed schedules. According to the participants, the snack cart made frequent enough rounds for beverages and snacks. However, there was uncertainty about what the cart offered, with a participant suggesting that a physical display or up‐to‐date menu would be beneficial, noting that the existing menu included items that were not available. Additionally, it was mentioned that drinking enough could initially be difficult if a participant could not get out of bed, only later discovering that a pitcher could be requested. Overall, while the necessary provisions were present, it was not always clear to the participants what was available or how to access it.

Participants reported that the hospital's ambiance did not significantly affect their eating experience. However, it was also noted that while hunger overrides the influence of the environment, a pleasant atmosphere can be beneficial during episodes of nausea, helping to encourage eating. Participants sharing rooms appreciated the opportunity to dine with roommates at a shared table, finding it a pleasant social experience. A participant suggested the idea of having a communal lounge area on the ward where patients could gather to eat together buffet‐style, though they acknowledged the practical challenges of implementing such a setup.Well, I sometimes think, couldn't you just have some sort of buffet or something like that, where people can (help) themselves? Yeah, maybe that's a very revolutionary idea, but people who are mobile, like me and (name roommate) in my room, we can easily get things ourselves. If you put it up somewhere, the food is served, and then you can add things yourself. I think that's much more fun too. Then you can enjoy eating together with others at those tables.(Participant 8)

Participants also made suggestions for improving the overall dining experience during their hospital stay. They expressed a preference for larger rooms with amenities like a small refrigerator, and noted that having a window open during meals would improve comfort, as the hospital environment often has a chemical odour:There is a bit of a chemical smell. I do notice that it has a bit of an effect at some point. It feels as if it's just all inside your body, everywhere. So today I opened the window to let in some fresh air. Selective Code 3: Meeting Patients' Preferences

The selectiveN code ‘meeting patients' preferences’ essentially means ensuring that patients receive what they want, including axial codes such as food and drink selection, portion size, the possibility of bringing in outside food, sustainability, and logistical considerations.

Participants named some things they felt were missing from the selection, such as hamburgers, salami, or different types of pasta. A suggestion was the option to add personalised toppings to the main meal, such as extra salt, herbs, or a sauce. Participants who had been hospitalised multiple times noted that the main meal menus could vary more, as the same options tended to repeat every few weeks. The desire for more vegetable choices was also expressed. In contrast, a satisfaction with the assortment available for breakfast and lunch was reported. However, some additional items were named to be added to the breakfast and lunch menu, for example herring, salami, and more types of bread. Participants appreciated the small extras provided for special occasions or holidays, which gave them a more pleasant experience. The participants found the portion sizes to be good or too large, and as the quantity of food can be adjusted each time, participants appreciated to customise their portions based on their appetite that day.

It was noted that participants had outside food delivered for main meal, or asked family members to bring meals from home, primarily because they did not enjoy the hospital food. Reasons for not bringing in outside food included being unaware that it was permitted, and financial constraints.[…] sometimes there's only broccoli and spinach and something else. Well, those [two] aren't exactly my favorites, and then there's not much left (laughs). So then I'll send a quick message home like: can you bring something? It's, yeah I think, not really varied enough.(Participant 2)

[…] sometimes there's only broccoli and spinach and something else. Well, those [two] aren't exactly my favorites, and then there's not much left (laughs). So then I'll send a quick message home like: can you bring something? It's, yeah I think, not really varied enough.

Sustainability, encompassing both human health and environmental impact, was brought forward as a topic by participants. Participants valued healthy food, and generally believed that the hospital meals were sufficiently nutritious, with placing trust in the staff to ensure the healthiness of their diet. However, it was noted that the vegetables appeared to be less healthy due to being overcooked. The importance of the hospital's commitment to environmental sustainability was also emphasised, suggesting that the institution should focus on providing organic options and reducing food waste. For instance, a participant mentioned repeatedly receiving two margarine cups despite requesting only one, highlighting a potential area for improvement in reducing waste.

As for the logistical aspects of the food service, participants reported to not always having received the food they wanted, sometimes due to receiving an incorrect order, or not being able to choose their main meal on the first day of admission. However, participants expressed that they were impressed by the level of service provided by such a large hospital and acknowledged that the service, food selection, and overall patient satisfaction might not always be further improved. Participants recognised that their suggested improvements may not always be feasible due to the costs they would impose on the hospital. It was noted that the service might improve if meals were prepared on each ward rather than in a central kitchen, so meals can be cooked more specifically for that ward. It was also stated that an external company could provide the food for the hospital to improve the quality and keeping the costs low.

Discussion

This qualitative study explored the eating experiences of hospitalised patients with cancer. The three selective codes that emerged were: the quality of hospital food and drinks, the presentation, and the ability to receive desired preferences. These codes point out the multi‐factor facets involved in meeting the preferences of this patient group, which are influenced by nutrition impact symptoms such as taste alterations, nausea, or less appetite.

This study highlighted instances of dissatisfaction with the quality and taste of hospital food, particularly the main meals. In other studies, taste has been rated as one of the most important aspects of eating experience [26]. Participants in the current study described the food as bland, overcooked, or lacking in variety, which may suggest room for improvement in preparation methods. However, patients with cancer can have taste and smell changes due to cancer therapies, which can lead to changed preferences [9, 27]. Therefore, standard meals may not adequately meet the needs of these patients and might benefit from adjustments tailored to their specific taste preferences and dietary requirements. Participants in the current study suggest that enhancing flavour through the use of spices, herbs, and sauces could improve the eating experience for patients. Allowing patients to customise their meals might not only cater to individual taste preferences but also compensate for the taste and smell alterations common among this population. A recent and promising approach for managing taste and smell changes is called taste steering. A personalised taste profile is created with taste strips, Sniffin' sticks, and questionnaires, and with this profile, meals are adapted to patients' specific changes in taste and smell [28].

The presentation of food and the overall ambiance during mealtimes were reported by participants to be important. While most participants did not find the hospital environment itself to detract from their eating experience, those experiencing nausea noted that a pleasant ambiance could encourage eating. Other studies showed that an attractive meal presentation could improve food intake [18, 29]. Participants in the current study reported that the plastic cover is significant to them due to the smell it emanates. However, another study suggests that, in general, patients do not consider serving ware important to their eating experience, compared to other aspects of the meal, including taste, as serving ware typically does not emit unpleasant odours [30]. The suggestion to have a communal dining area or a buffet‐style service shows a desire for more social interaction and autonomy over meal choices, and has been raised in other studies in hospitalised patients [31]. Moreover, a study on offering a breakfast buffet in postoperative patients showed higher protein and energy intake compared to a regular bedside breakfast [32]. This indicates that improving the mealtime environment, perhaps by minimising the clinical feel and incorporating more home‐like elements, could positively influence food enjoyment and nutritional intake [33]. The feasibility of such improvements, that preferably should focus on patients who will benefit most, likely varies between hospitals based on local circumstances and resources.

Concerns about menu repetition and a desire for more choice suggest that while logistical efficiency is necessary in a large hospital setting, there is room for improvement in menu planning to better cater to patient needs. Additionally, the confusion around food availability and ordering processes indicates a need for clearer communication. Sustainability, both in terms of personal health and environmental impact, was a concern for participants. Nutritious food was valued and a preference for options that were organic and environmentally friendly was expressed. While hospital food services face challenges in balancing cost, sustainability, and patient satisfaction, incorporating more sustainable practices could be a valued addition for patients, and management strategies on food‐related waste in hospitals have been proposed [34, 35].

A strength of this qualitative study lies in the broad scope of topics asked about the eating experience. Furthermore, this study has, in contrast to other studies, a specific focus on hospitalised patients with cancer, with a diverse group in terms of cancer type, treatment, and the range of symptoms experienced. However, this study also has several limitations, including the fact that it represents a single point in time, and its conduct within a single hospital, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. Moreover, only one non‐Dutch participant was included. This reflects the demographic composition of the hospital's patient population in the Northern Netherlands, where the majority of patients are of Dutch origin. Another limitation relates to the potential for researcher bias, as researchers' perspectives and assumptions may shape interviews and data interpretation. To reduce this risk, we had frequent discussions throughout the research process and compared interpretations within the research team to the original transcripts, and the research process was protocolized.

This study has provided insights into the eating experiences of hospitalised patients with cancer, effectively mapping the problems and laying a foundation for future research aimed at exploring and evaluating potential solutions to improve eating experience. Future research should first consider a larger and more diverse sample across multiple institutions to validate these findings and make the results applicable to other (more diverse) areas. Next, quantitative studies could assess the impact of specific interventions, such as introducing flavour enhancers or communal dining areas, on patient satisfaction and nutritional outcomes.

Conclusion

The three selective codes that emerged in this quality study on eating experiences of hospitalised patients with cancer were the quality of hospital food and drinks, the presentation, and the ability to receive desired preferences. The results underscore the importance of a patient‐centred approach to hospital food services, which could significantly enhance patient satisfaction, support better nutritional intake, and improve the overall quality of life for patients undergoing cancer treatment.
