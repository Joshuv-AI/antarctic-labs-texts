The effect of body mass index on bone density by age distribution in women
Authors: Berna Sermin Kilic, Aysegul Yildirim, Nil Atakul
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Osteoporosis significantly affects public health, especially among postmenopausal women, by reducing bone mass and increasing the risk of fractures. While the influence of body mass index (BMI) on Bone Mineral Density (BMD) is acknowledged, the specific age range in which BMI most significantly affects bone density remains unclear. This study aims to explore the relationship between BMI and BMD in different age groups to identify potential age-specific risk factors for osteoporosis. In a retrospective cohort study at the University of Health Sciences Turkey, X Hospital, DXA scans of 240 postmenopausal women aged 40 to 80 were analyzed between February 2018 and February 2020. The study used statistical analyses, including ANCOVA and Pearson correlation, to investigate the effects of BMI on BMD, adjusting for age and other variables. Analysis revealed a complex interaction between BMI and BMD. Controlling for BMI showed a statistically significant difference in measurements of lumbar T, neck T, and trochanter T between age groups (P < .05). Specifically, bone density in the 40 to 49 age range was found to be lower when adjusted for BMI, suggesting that the protective effect of higher BMI is less pronounced in younger postmenopausal ages but decreases less negatively in older groups. The impact of BMI on trochanter T measurements was markedly different, increasing in the 40 to 49 age group, while decreasing in others. BMI, along with body weight and age, are important determinants of BMD, but not the sole factors influencing bone loss. The study highlights the need for a personalized approach to osteoporosis risk, considering a broader spectrum of genetic, hormonal and lifestyle factors. It recommends further evaluation of other risk factors in larger cohorts and emphasizes the importance of future longitudinal research to elucidate the dynamic relationship between BMI and BMD over time.

1. Introduction

Osteoporosis is a widespread systemic skeletal disorder that significantly affects the health of postmenopausal women, leading to reduced bone strength and a substantially higher risk of fractures. This debilitating condition is characterized by a marked reduction in bone mass in combination with a progressive deterioration of the microarchitecture of bone tissue. Such changes are instrumental in increasing the fragility of bones, making individuals more susceptible to fractures from minor falls or injuries.[1] Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA) scans serve as the gold standard in the diagnostic evaluation of osteoporosis. These scans are critical to accurately assess bone mineral density (BMD), which is critical for diagnosing osteoporosis, assessing fracture risk, and monitoring the effectiveness of ongoing treatments. DXA’s high-precision capabilities allow healthcare providers to detect early bone loss and initiate appropriate interventions to prevent further deterioration.[2,3]

The World Health Organization (WHO) has played a crucial role in the clinical management of osteoporosis by establishing comprehensive diagnostic criteria that are based on BMD levels obtained from DXA scans. These criteria categorize bone health status into several classifications, including normal, osteopenia, and osteoporosis, with a special emphasis on osteoporosis identified by the presence of 1 or more fractures. This classification system helps clinicians make informed decisions regarding patient care and tailor treatment plans that are most suitable for individuals based on their specific BMD readings. Furthermore, these criteria underscore the importance of early detection and intervention in the management of osteoporosis, highlighting the critical role of DXA scans not only in the diagnosis, but also in the strategic planning of treatment to mitigate the risk of fractures and improve patient outcomes.[4]

By expanding on the importance of osteoporosis as a health concern, particularly among postmenopausal women, and detailing the role and importance of DXA scans in its diagnosis and treatment, the passage now provides a more comprehensive overview.[5,6] The inclusion of WHO criteria for classifying bone health based on BMD levels further enriches the discussion, emphasizing the clinical approach towards diagnosing and managing osteoporosis.

Despite the well-documented impact of body mass index (BMI) on bone health, recent scientific inquiries have revealed a far more intricate relationship between these 2 factors than previously understood. Recent studies have revealed that a higher BMI and waist circumference do not necessarily offer protection against bone health problems.[7] In fact, they may be associated with an increased risk of fractures in postmenopausal women.[8] This finding challenges the long-held belief that increased body weight serves as a safeguard against bone loss, introducing a paradigm shift in our understanding of bone health dynamics. Furthermore, research has unveiled notable disparities in the influence of BMI on BMD in different living environments, indicating that urban versus rural settings might play a significant role in modulating the relationship between BMI and bone health.[9] These revelations underscore the critical need for personalized and nuanced approaches when assessing fracture risk and manage osteoporosis in diverse demographic cohorts.

Identification of this research gap highlights the urgent need for a more granular exploration of how BMI affects bone health across various demographic landscapes, particularly among postmenopausal women. Although the general consensus acknowledges a favorable relationship between BMI and BMD, it is clear that the scenario is complicated by age-related changes in BMI, which can inversely affect BMD and consequently the risk of osteoporosis.[10] Such complexities signal the intricate interplay between age, BMI, and bone health, necessitating a deeper dive into understanding these relationships. The purpose of the current study is to meticulously unpack the multifaceted role of BMI as a potential risk factor for osteoporosis among this demographic, with a particular focus on identifying the age range where BMI significantly affects the risk of osteoporosis. Through this investigation, the study strives not only to fill the existing gap in the literature gap, but also to improve the development of targeted prevention and treatment strategies against osteoporosis. Addressing this challenge is of paramount importance given the widespread prevalence of osteoporosis and its implications for public health globally.[11] This research effort is expected to contribute significantly to the refinement of osteoporosis risk stratification practices, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of preventive measures and therapeutic interventions for at-risk populations.

2. Methods

The meticulously designed retrospective cohort study aimed to explore the influence of BMI on BMD within a demographic of menopausal women aged 40 to 80 years. Ethical approval for this investigation was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Istanbul Teaching and Research Hospital (Approval No.: 2190, Date of Approval: 21.02.2020), ensuring compliance with the ethical standards established in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki of 1964. Conducted over the period of February 2018 to February 2020, the study centered on analyzing data from Dual-Energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scans. These scans are crucial to accurately assessing bone density, thus serving as an essential tool in the study methodology. All participants involved in the study received informed consent, aligning with the protocols established by the Declaration of Helsinki, further emphasizing the study’s adherence to ethical research practices.

Participant selection was carried out with precision, incorporating a comprehensive set of inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure a homogeneous study population and reliable results. Women subject to conditions or treatments that could independently influence BMD -such as recent pregnancy, lactation, postpartum period <12 months, history of hysterectomy, bone tumor, bone disease, ongoing treatment affecting BMD, or chronic diseases that impact bone health - were systematically excluded. This exclusion process was vital to minimize confounding variables and improve the study’s validity.

For data collection, demographic details – specifically age, height, weight, and BMI, were accurately measured accurately using a Seca 769 digital scale. BMI was calculated using the weight formula in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2), an essential step for subsequent analyses. DXA scans provided precise T scores for the lumbar vertebra, trochanter, and femoral neck, facilitating a nuanced investigation into the relationship between BMI and bone density in different age groups.

Statistical analyses were performed with the help of the SPSS version 23.0 programme. The conformity of the variables with the normal distribution was examined using histogram graphics and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. When presenting the descriptive analysis, the mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum values were used. Normally distributed (parametric) variables were evaluated between more than 2 groups using the 1-way ANOVA test. ANCOVA analysis was performed to control the effect of a second variable on the dependent variable. The assumption of equality of group variances was checked with the Levene test. Duncan and Benferroni multiple comparison tests were used to investigate the reason for the significant difference between the groups. A Pearson correlation test was used in the analysis of the measurement data with each other. Cases with a P-value <.05 were considered statistically significant results.

Given the retrospective nature of the study, where direct consent from participants was not feasible, the research design was carefully crafted to respect patient confidentiality and adhere to ethical guidelines. The explicit mention of the ethics committee’s approval and decision number provides an additional layer of transparency and accountability, reaffirming the study’s ethical foundation.

4. Discussion

BMD is influenced by multifarious elements, including age, genetic predispositions, and hormonal changes, particularly in the context of postmenopausal women. This demographic is particularly susceptible to osteoporosis, a condition characterized by diminished bone mass and structural deterioration, leading to increased fragility and risk of fracture. The association between BMI and BMD is of considerable public health interest. A higher BMI has traditionally been considered protective against osteoporosis, attributed to an increase in the mechanical load on the skeleton, which in turn stimulates osteoblast activity and improves BMD.[12]

Critical examination by Morin et al reveals that lower BMI serves as a predictor of osteoporosis among women aged 40 to 59 years, emphasizing the protective role of higher BMI against bone degradation.[8,13] This protective correlation is further supported by Saarelainen et al, who observed that a higher BMI protects against bone loss at pivotal skeletal sites such as the lumbar spine and femoral neck over an average follow-up period of 10.5 years.[9,14] Simonelli et al studied a group of White non-Hispanic women aged 30 and above using GELunar Prodigy densitometers. They found that TBS at L1 to L4 was poorly associated with BMI (R: 0.17), and that the trabecular bone score (TBS) decreased dramatically with age.[15] Furthermore, the analysis by Looker et al, which drew from 2 comprehensive US population surveys, demonstrated a consistent cross-sectional decline in TBS in various age groups, affirming that age significantly affects TBS irrespective of race or ethnicity in both men and women (P < .001), thus corroborating the age-related decrease observed in different demographic groups.[16]

Further research, specifically a study examining 4907 Lebanese women aged 20 to 90 years, underscored a negative relationship between the TBS and age (r = −0.39; P < .001), highlighting the age-dependent decline in bone density and strengthening the vulnerability to bone density reduction across a wide age spectrum.[17] In contrast to previous findings, Jain et al provided a distinct point of view by uncovering a positive correlation between the TBS and BMI among African Americans, suggesting that demographic-specific factors significantly influence this relationship.[18]

The study by Onat et al highlighted advanced age as a critical risk factor for low BMD, elucidating that this association is due to decreased absorption of calcium and vitamin D, along with decreased endogenous vitamin D. The resultant increase in parathyroid hormone, a response to lower calcium levels, leads to enhanced bone resorption, thus exacerbating the reduction in BMD.[19] The intricate relationship between BMI and BMD in postmenopausal women is underscored by various studies and physiological insights, revealing a multifaceted interplay of genetic, hormonal, and lifestyle factors. The MEDOS study highlights the increased risk of osteoporotic hip fractures among the elderly and women, pointing to gender and age as significant risk determinants.[20] This observation is complemented by Kumar et al, who discovered a substantial increase in BMD in the femoral neck and lumbar spine with increased BMI among postmenopausal Indian women, suggesting that a higher BMI may offer a protective shield against osteoporosis by accommodating greater mechanical loads, thus encouraging bone growth and reducing bone resorption.[21] This beneficial effect is potentially augmented by elevated levels of free sex hormones, such as estrogen, in women with a higher BMI, which contribute to the deceleration of osteoclast activity and bone resorption.[22,23]

Adding to the complexity, lifestyle modifications, including resistance exercises and regular physical activity, emerge as the key to boosting BMD levels in postmenopausal women, underscoring the value of such interventions in osteoporosis prevention.[24,25] Asomaning et al accentuate the critical influence of BMI on osteoporosis risk, positing that minor variations in BMI could exert a more pronounced impact on bone density compared to other modifiable risk factors.[26] The extensive study by Palermo et al further explores the association between low body weight, smoking, absence of estrogen use, and low BMD, while identifying high BMI, African descent, estrogen use, and regular exercise as factors associated with elevated BMD.[11]

The progression of bone loss exhibits a distinctive pattern, with the rate of reduction in BMD in the lumbar region being slower compared to the femoral region as age progresses. In particular, the initial decade of menopause marks the most significant bone loss in the lumbar spine, suggesting a complex interaction of factors that influence the progression of osteoporosis.[27] The findings of Choi et al elucidate an inverse relationship between BMD in vertebral and hip joints and age, challenging the presumption of a uniform decline in BMD at different skeletal sites with aging.[28]

In summary, the synthesis of these studies and observations provides a comprehensive understanding of the nuanced relationship between BMI and BMD in postmenopausal women. It highlights the importance of a holistic approach to osteoporosis management and prevention, considering the protective role of a higher BMI alongside the indispensable contributions of age, hormonal balance, and lifestyle factors in modulating osteoporosis risk and bone health.

5. Limitations

The primary limitation of this study lies in its retrospective design, which, despite providing valuable information on the relationship between BMI and BMD among postmenopausal women, limits the establishment of causality due to the reliance on historical medical records and DXA scan results. This design restricts the control of variables and the comprehensive accounting of confounders such as dietary intake, physical activity levels, genetic predispositions, smoking, alcohol consumption, and family history of osteoporosis, which could subtly influence bone health. The focus on a specific demographic narrows the generalisability of the findings, potentially excluding younger people or men who are also at risk of osteoporosis. Additionally, the exclusion criteria might have omitted individuals with conditions or treatments that significantly impact bone density, possibly affecting the representation of BMD variations.

6. Conclusion

This study intricately explores the relationship between BMI and BMD in postmenopausal women, revealing how BMI interacts with various determinants such as age, hormonal changes, genetics, and lifestyle changes to influence the risk of osteoporosis. It establishes that while a higher BMI generally acts as a protective factor against osteoporosis, this effect varies significantly between different age groups and is subject to the influences of demographic factors, age-related hormonal changes, and lifestyle practices. In particular, the research underscores that controlling for BMI between age groups demonstrates a pronounced positive impact on bone density in the 40 to 49 age bracket, with a diminishing effect observed in older age categories. The study highlights the critical role of BMI in enhancing BMD in all ages, albeit with varying degrees of impact, underscoring the importance of personalized strategies in osteoporosis management and prevention of osteoporosis. This underscores the need for targeted patient selection for screening and prophylaxis, advocating a comprehensive approach that integrates genetic, hormonal and lifestyle considerations to optimize bone health and mitigate osteoporosis risk.
