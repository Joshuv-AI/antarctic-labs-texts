Assessing Postpartum Levator Ani Muscle Recovery: A Feasibility Study on Automated Texture Analysis in Transvaginal Ultrasound
Authors: Haowei Tai, Fernanda Pipitone, Kourosh Kalayeh, Jennifer LaCross, James A Ashton-Miller, John O DeLancey, J Brian Fowlkes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:Levator ani (LA) muscle injury during childbirth is an important contributor to the development of pelvic floor disorders (PFDs). Understanding the mechanisms of LA muscle damage and healing is crucial for developing individualized recovery plans and preventive measures. Although muscle avulsion has been extensively studied, the internal rearrangement of muscle structure has not. Current evaluation by palpation or force measurement can be limited due to pain and motivation. An objective evaluation of internal muscle structure has the advantage of being independent of these subjective factors. The objective of this study was to evaluate the feasibility of using 3-D transvaginal ultrasound (US) imaging combined with quantitative texture analysis for assessing deep LA muscle injuries and monitoring tissue microstructural changes involved in healing over time in postpartum women. By providing insights into recovery mechanisms, this approach has the potential to track muscle repair and quantify the progression of tissue healing.Methods:This is a secondary analysis of 3-D transvaginal US images performed on women who were at high risk for LA muscle injuries following vaginal delivery but did not have muscle avulsion (n = 15), and on women who underwent cesarean section (C-section) before the second stage of labor (n = 10) as controls. Imaging data were collected at 2 wk, 6 wk and 6 mo postpartum for the vaginal delivery group, and at 6 wk and 6 mo for the C-section group. Texture “features” (93 in total) that result from image characteristics (i.e., local pixel-intensity differences and adjacency relationships) were extracted from the US images using six classical texture analysis techniques. Feature reduction was implemented by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) and removing features with high similarity (|PCC| >0.8). Three feature-selection methods were employed: linear mixed effects analysis of variance, receiver operator characteristic analysis and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression. Features selected by at least two methods were used to train a random Forest classification model, with the dataset split into 70% training and 30% testing sets.Results:Four texture features were identified as potential imaging biomarkers associated with structural changes in the LA muscle during recovery. Average percentage changes of these four features (12.2% ± 2.3%) were observed between 2 and 6 wk postpartum in the vaginal delivery group. In contrast, smaller changes between 6 wk and 6 mo were noted in the vaginal delivery group and C-section group over the same period (6.0% ± 1.2% and 6.6% ± 2.3%, respectively). The random Forest model achieved a classification accuracy of 78% at distinguishing different time points based on the selected features.Conclusion:US image texture analysis detected systematic changes in muscle echotexture between 2 and 6 wk postpartum, recognized as a crucial period for healing. This suggests that the proposed approach could meaningfully complement current evaluation techniques. By providing objective imaging metrics, this method may be useful in studying rates of recovery independent of current subjective methods that are based on voluntary contraction. It can also be used to compare different recovery protocols and ultimately improve patient outcomes.Keywords: Levator ani muscle injury, Texture analysis, Feature selection, Issue characterization, Transvaginal ultrasound imaging, Postpartum recovery

Introduction

Pelvic floor disorders (PFDs) affect millions of women worldwide and place a significant burden on health care systems [1]. In the United States, approximately 25% of women are affected by PFDs [2,3], and their prevalence increases with age [4]. The economic impact of PFDs is substantial, involving both direct and indirect medical costs [5,6]. The pubococcygeal portion of the levator ani (LA) muscle plays a crucial role in supporting the pelvic organs and maintaining continence [7,8]. It stretches to three times its length [9,10], which is unprecedented anywhere else in the body, during vaginal childbirth and can be damaged by fetal expulsive forces. Tears in the muscular complex [11] are well known, but the internal disruption of individual muscle fascicles and fibers is not well studied in parturient women.

Understanding the timing and manner in which the LA muscle heals is crucial for developing rehabilitation strategies [12]. By recognizing individual variations in tissue damage, clinicians can develop personalized approaches considering structural differences [13]. Current pelvic muscle recovery assessments rely on voluntary muscle activation, which can be limited by pain or a lack of patient motivation. Techniques such as transvaginal palpation primarily assess muscle bulk, integrity and strength [14], and may not adequately detect abnormalities within the structure of the pubococcygeus. This results in a research gap regarding the extent of muscle injury and the potential for recovery [15]. Medical imaging techniques that link imaging biomarkers to tissue anatomical structures hold promise in addressing this issue [16]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with fluid-sensitive sequences has been used to assess muscle injuries that do not exhibit gross disruption [15,17]. However, limited availability makes MRI assessment unsuitable for widespread clinical use.

To address this issue, a technique for characterizing LA tissue injury using quantitative texture analysis has been proposed. It is hypothesized that disruption of the normally well-organized muscle fibers during childbirth alters the ultrasound (US) image, enabling direct monitoring of disorganized muscle structure and its recovery process. In this study, texture analyses, including feature extraction, reduction and selection, were performed to identify potential microstructural indicators. Integrating these advanced US image analysis techniques provides an objective, readily accessible method for muscle assessment. The focus of developing this tool is to allow research on muscle healing based on what is going on inside the muscle. Once developed, the tool could be used in clinical research to assess tissue microstructure changes throughout different stages of healing. This holds potential not only to improve our understanding of muscle recovery and guide more personalized treatment strategies, but also to provide a solid foundation for future studies aimed at addressing key questions about the dynamics of muscle healing.

Materials and methods

US data collection

This study is a secondary analysis of a subset of scans from the POP-I study [18], which was a prospective cohort study evaluating the recovery of women after vaginal birth with risk factors for pelvic floor injury and those who delivered by cesarean section (C-section). A total of 112 cases were studied according to the established protocol to identify those with high-risk factors for pelvic floor injury [18,19] after approval from the Institutional Review Board. In the present study, a subset of this cohort (n = 15) was selected for further analysis based on pre-defined criteria: the presence of the levator muscle (i.e., no complete avulsion) and the availability of scans at all specified time intervals (2 wk, 6 wk and 6 mo). For comparison, a randomly selected cohort of women who underwent C-sections was included in the POP-I study as a control group in this study (n = 10). Note that imaging protocol and parameter settings were consistent for all participants, while the physician could adjust the US scanner controls (e.g., gain or imaging region) to maintain image quality. All US images were acquired and analyzed with identical voxel sizes (width, height and depth) for each subject and at every time point. The details of the imaging protocol can be found in our previous work [18] and are summarized briefly as follows: US data were collected at approximately 2 wk, 6 wk and 6 mo postpartum for the vaginal delivery group, whereas for the C-section group, data were collected at approximately 6 wk and 6 mo postpartum. A timeline of the experimental procedure is presented in Figure 1a. During the examination, patients were evaluated in the lithotomy position (Fig. 1b). US imaging was performed using a BK Medical Flex Focus system (BK Medical, Peabody, MA, USA) equipped with an endocavitary 8838 transducer (BK Medical, Herlev, Denmark). The transducer employs a mechanical 3-D imaging technique by rotating its internal transducer head through a full 360-degree rotation. This rotation captures a volumetric dataset of the pelvic floor by acquiring 2-D images at incremental angles throughout the rotation (Fig. 1c) without requiring manual movement by the operator. The volume was imported into the open-source software 3D Slicer (http://www.slicer.org) to sample the muscle belly. Depending on muscle thickness, three to five parasagittal slices from medial to lateral were identified and used in the analysis. The region of interest (ROI) was selected within the pubovisceral (=pubococcygeal) muscle in each slice by a single experienced observer (F.P.). ROI selection was based on MRI findings identifying locations prone to injury during vaginal delivery, which document persistent edema in this area [17] in the parasagittal plane (Fig. 2). The same region was identified in the C-section group for comparison.

US textural feature extraction

Texture analysis, including the use of several mathematical transforms [20], was conducted on the ROIs to extract features indicative of textural variation within the US image. Detailed processing methods for these techniques have been previously described [21]. Prior to feature extraction, the US images underwent normalization within each image to ensure consistency and comparability across samples. The analysis focused on examining local variations in image intensity using a 3×3 sliding kernel to assess the associations among neighboring pixel values, as indicated by the directional arrows in Figure 3. Characterizing these spatial patterns aims to quantify pixel-based variations that may reflect underlying changes in tissue microstructure [21,22]. The resulting pixel-based variations were quantified to generate textural ‘feature maps’ (Fig. 3). A total of 93 feature maps were derived from the following classical texture analysis techniques to represent structural patterns within the images [23,24]: first-order statistics, Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM), Gray Level Run Length Matrix (GLRLM), Gray Level Size Zone Matrix (GLSZM), Gray Level Dependence Matrix (GLDM) and Neighboring Gray Tone Difference Matrix (NGTDM). Feature maps were generated from the ROI in each slice and averaged across all selected ROIs at each time point for every subject before analysis.

A summary of the textural analysis approach. A 3×3 sliding window was used to quantify local pixel relationships that may indicate tissue microstructural changes. First-order and higher order textural metrics were implemented to provide a comprehensive evaluation of pixel variation. A suite of 93 features was extracted to form feature maps within localized regions. Subsequently, feature reduction was conducted using the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) to eliminate redundant features, followed by feature selection to identify features selected by at least two of the following methods: linear mixed effects analysis of variance (ANOVA), receiver operator characteristic (ROC) selection and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. Finally, the selected features were used to train a random Forest model for time classification.

A two-step approach was used for feature reduction and selection.

Step 1: To reduce model complexity by eliminating redundant features [25], those features that exhibited similar trends over time postpartum were removed based on a Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) [26]. The redundancies were removed by randomly discarding one feature from each pair that was highly correlated (|PCC| >0.8). The remaining feature values were normalized and expressed as a percentage of the maximum value for each feature across all patients and time points. This approach ensures a comparable scale and prevents features with larger values from dominating the model.

Step 2: To reliably select the most effective features that describe the muscle condition, three selection methods were employed: A linear mixed effects analysis of variance (ANOVA)-based filtering method [27], receiver operator characteristic (ROC) and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) analysis [28,29]. Recognizing that different methods may select features that work better for specific models, the selected features were combined, inspired by “bagging methods” in machine learning [30], which reduce model variance by training multiple models, thereby identifying those that best reflect temporal changes postpartum. Each method was applied separately to the reduced feature set obtained from Step 1. Note that analyses in Step 2 were performed using only data from the vaginal delivery group. The three feature-selection methods were as follows:

The ANOVA model was applied to account for both fixed effects (i.e., time) and random effects (i.e., subject) [31]. Features showing significant differences over time (p < 0.05) were selected for further analysis. This process resulted in five features remaining.

ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic ability of each feature in distinguishing between the three time points (i.e., 2 wk, 6 wk and 6 mo). Sensitivity and specificity were calculated, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each ROC curve by considering three pairwise comparisons: 2 wk versus 6 wk, 6 wk versus 6 mo and 2 wk versus 6 mo. The features with the highest AUC values in each comparison were selected. If the same feature appeared in multiple comparisons, the one with the next highest AUC was selected to ensure a set of five unique features.

Feature selection was conducted using LASSO regression, a statistical method that applies regularization and variable selection to enhance model accuracy and interpretability. Time labels for each data point served as the response variable in training the model. LASSO introduces a penalty term that shrinks the less important feature coefficients to zero. The coefficients from the LASSO model were ranked to identify the top five features contributing to the prediction of time points.

The final features were determined by selecting those identified by at least two of the three selection methods.

Finally, a random Forest classification model, a machine learning algorithm that uses a group of decision trees to assign samples to discrete categories [32], was trained using these features linked to the associated tissue changes over time. The dataset was divided into a training set (70%, 31 samples) and a testing set (30%, 14 samples) [33,34]. Note that each feature value was generated by averaging across three to five selected ROIs for each participant at each visit. Only data from the vaginal delivery group were used for model training and testing, while C-section data served exclusively as a comparison group and were not included in the random Forest classification model development.

Statistical analysis

To display the overall changes in extracted textural features at each postpartum time point, the final data across all subjects were summarized as the mean ± standard error of the mean. To identify features with significant postpartum changes, a paired t-test was applied to features selected by at least two of the three methods from Step 2. Comparisons were made between 2 and 6 wk, and between 6 wk and 6 mo in the vaginal delivery group, as well as 6 wk versus 6 mo in the C-section group. For all analyses, a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. During feature selection, data analysis was performed using R for ANOVA and the open-source Python library scikit-learn [35] for LASSO and AUC. Data visualization and related statistical computations were performed using Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

The process of PCC comparisons eliminated redundant features to reduce the feature set from 93 to 22. The PCC values for these 22 features are shown in Figure 4 and include features from first-order statistics, GLCM, GLSZM, GLDM and NGTDM. This reduced feature set was then further refined by applying the three selection methods (i.e., ANOVA, ROC analysis and LASSO regression). Each method independently identified five top features. Features selected by at least two out of the three methods were then retained, resulting in a final set of four texture features for analysis: Gray Level Non-uniformity (from GLSZM), as well as Small Dependence Emphasis, Small Dependence High Gray Level Emphasis and Large Dependence High Gray Level Emphasis (all from GLDM). The PCC values of these four final selected features are highlighted within the red boxes appearing on the diagonal in Figure 4. All absolute PCC values among the pairs were at or below 0.8, indicating that these features are sufficiently different from each other.

Discussion

The pelvic floor muscles undergo an unprecedented stretch during childbirth, elongating two to three times their original length – a degree not seen anywhere else in the body. Even without muscle avulsion, injury and repair remain active processes that influence recovery, as shown by localized persistent tissue edema in areas of damage observed on fluid-sensitive MRI scans [17]. Although pubococcygeal avulsion from the pubic bone has been studied extensively, less is known about smaller degrees of muscle damage and repair where the fibers remain intact. This study employed advanced image texture analysis of transvaginal US imaging to investigate how the LA muscle changes after childbirth. By integrating texture analysis and feature-selection methods, we found that tissue alterations impact the backscattered US signal. These alterations are visible on texture feature maps, highlighting tissue regions that may appear similar on traditional B-scans but exhibit differences under texture analysis. The significance of this study is in providing a research tool that could potentially assess the speed of LA tissue healing and facilitate more personalized or targeted treatment plans in the future.

Our results indicate that the LA muscle in the vaginal delivery group undergoes significant texture changes from 2 to 6 wk postpartum, whereas no further significant changes were observed at 6 mo postpartum. This absence of significant change between 6 wk and 6 mo was also true for the C-section group along with no significant difference when comparing feature values for vaginal delivery and C-section groups at 6 mo. This latter finding is consistent with a previous study where no significant difference in the Modified Oxford score was observed at 6 mo postpartum between women who underwent C-sections and those who had vaginal births [19]. Changes in the C-section group are expected as muscle alterations that occur prior to birth [36] still need to be reversed. Because 2-wk scans were not done on these subjects, the main function of the C-section scans was to determine whether there were differences at 6 wk and 6 mo between vaginal birth and C-section. Our feature analysis revealed that different features showed slightly varying sensitivity across time points, potentially reflecting phases of healing such as the inflammatory response, fibroblastic repair and maturation-remodeling that might differentially affect the features.

The variations observed in the feature maps may indicate localized differences in tissue organization. Although these features were selected for their ability to capture longitudinal changes in their values averaged over the ROI, further investigation is needed to understand the significance of these regional variations. Because direct histological assessment is not feasible in this context, texture-based US analysis offers a non-invasive surrogate for investigating underlying tissue processes. With further validation, this approach could serve as a practical alternative, providing valuable tissue information without the need for invasive procedures.

Currently, LA muscle recovery is assessed through physical examination [37], hiatus size [38], muscle shape (e.g., ballooning) [39], and muscle edema and morphology. Implementing a US image-processing strategy could complement postpartum recovery tracking and help assess the outcomes of various interventions aimed at accelerating muscle healing. It could also complement muscle strength testing, which is often limited by patient motivation and pain-related muscle inhibition. Because this method is independent of muscle activation, it provides objective, quantitative data that may enhance assessment accuracy. Muscle injury and recovery is widely studied in other body regions, and US is employed not only to evaluate injuries [40] but also to diagnose neuromuscular disease [41]. Applying these techniques to the pelvic floor could expand our understanding of this common injury and improve clinical assessments.

By quantifying changes in image biomarkers over time, this research holds promise in linking these biomarkers to specific healing stages. Our approach may complement these efforts by pinpointing subtle, localized changes that could help direct investigations more efficiently, similar to observations in other muscles with lower-grade injuries, such as the hamstrings or gastrocnemius [42,43]. Identifying such differences can guide the development of individualized treatment plans and provide insight into the characteristics of soft tissue injury associated with prolapse [44]. Additionally, capturing these subtle tissue changes may enhance our ability to objectively monitor recovery progress, allowing early detection of complications and timely adjustments to therapy.

The proposed methods should be robust to variations introduced by different operators and system noise because they are based on relationships between neighboring pixels and utilize features selected for their correlation with postpartum time. Consensus-based selection across three independent feature-selection methods further reduces susceptibility to such variations.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. As the LA muscle is fiber-rich, and despite selecting multiple ROIs per patient to mitigate the impact of beam angle, further studies are needed to use other transducers and data-collection methods (e.g., transperineal) to evaluate the proposed method. Additionally, the study focuses only on women with intact LA muscles because if there were avulsion, a visible muscle would not be present for analysis. This feasibility study included a small sample size, although the longitudinal design remains a strength. To minimize the risk of overfitting due to the limited sample size, only the final four selected features were used to train the network for tissue assessment. Validating these findings in a larger population will be essential to confirm their broader applicability and robustness. Other limitations include the unknown influence of image rendering and display performed using 3D Slicer, as well as uncertainty regarding whether alternative rendering software would modify the outcomes. Moreover, ROI selection was performed by a single reader to ensure consistency, but inter-reader reliability was not evaluated. Furthermore, although feature importance can be evaluated using the random Forest approach, the specific contribution and unique information provided by each selected feature remain unclear. The PCC threshold of 0.8 was chosen based on previous literature [45], although this threshold may still allow moderately correlated features to be included. To evaluate this choice, we compared random Forest model performance with and without the Small Dependence High Gray Level Emphasis feature, finding approximately a 3% difference in accuracy. This suggests that while features may be correlated, each could still reflect slightly different aspects of muscle texture. While it might seem counterintuitive for features (e.g., Small Dependence High Gray Level Emphasis and Large Dependence High Gray Level Emphasis) to increase simultaneously, they are not strictly mathematically opposed or perfectly anti-correlated, particularly in heterogeneous tissue. Subtle changes or broadening in the probability distribution can allow both feature values to increase under certain conditions. Additionally, the impact of gain or other imaging parameter changes on feature extraction and the reliability of extracted features was not assessed. Future work will employ more advanced machine learning models capable of direct image analysis or systematically examine whether different US system settings (e.g., gain) impact normalized feature values in larger study populations. Such approaches would clarify the influence of each selected feature on predictive outcomes, facilitating the extraction of robust and reliable features. This approach is a natural addition to current methods that measure muscle fiber length to understand muscle function [9]. However, future studies are needed to explore how these imaging biomarkers relate to actual anatomical changes. Establishing such correlations would strengthen the validity of using US texture analysis as a reliable tool for assessing LA muscle and guiding clinical interventions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that calculated image feature maps can be used to quantify systematic postpartum changes in LA muscle texture, likely reflecting internal abnormalities more subtle than gross disruptions. The proposed selection methods effectively identified image features that likely captured LA tissue injury. Furthermore, combining multiple selected features yielded a classification accuracy of 78% when distinguishing different postpartum time points in natural delivery. The findings from our texture analysis approach suggest that tissue microstructure changes alter pixel relationships in US images. These alterations, visualized via texture mapping, have the potential to detect subtle changes linked to recovery. This approach could address the challenges of assessing deep pelvic tissues and bridge gaps left by traditional evaluation methods. It may prove invaluable for understanding LA muscle repair mechanisms, ultimately contributing to improved prevention and management of PFDs.
