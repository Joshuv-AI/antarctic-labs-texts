Loop engineering improves prime editing efficiency
Authors: Bailun Li, Zhike Lu, Ting Zhou, Huilin Mou, Zhilin Zou, Keyi Yao, Zhenxing Yu, Wenyu Ma, Tingliang Wang, Miao He, Lijia Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrime editor (PE) offers significant translational potential due to its diverse editing outcomes. However, its editing efficiency is generally lower than that of wild-type Cas9 and base editor (BE). Previous investigations have suggested that the availability of the primer binding site (PBS) and reverse transcription template (RTT) within the pegRNA limits the installation of desired edits at target sites. In this study, we investigated whether the position and copy number of PBS-RTT sequences are important parameters influencing PE editing efficiencies. We inserted extra copies of the PBS-RTT sequence into the loop 2, tetraloop, or the 3′ end of pegRNA, as well as moved the 3′ PBS-RTT to the internal stem-loops of sgRNA. Incorporating an extra PBS-RTT copy into loop 2, but not tetraloop, increased PE editing efficiency by 40%–147% across various target sites and cellular contexts. This suggests that both the local concentration and steric hindrance of the PBS-RTT sequence appear to affect its accessibility to the DNA nicking end, ultimately influencing the editing efficiency of PE. By addressing the bottleneck of limited PBS-RTT availability, our strategy may broaden the wide applicability of PE, especially in translational medicine.Keywords: MT: RNA/DNA Editing, prime editing, pegRNA, PBS-RTT, local concentration, steric hindrance

Introduction

Applying the CRISPR systems to translational medicine requires high editing efficiency and diverse editing outcomes with minimal off-target editing.1,2,3,4 Prime editing (PE) holds great potential in CRISPR therapy as it mediates targeted insertions, deletions, and all 12 possible base-to-base conversions without introducing a double-strand DNA (dsDNA) break.4,5,6 PE typically employs a Cas9 nickase (Cas9n) along with a tethered or untethered Moloney murine leukemia virus (M-MLV) reverse transcriptase (RT), and a prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA).7 Cas12 nickase and other reverse transcriptases have also been developed to facilitate prime editing.8,9 The pegRNA is an engineered sgRNA with a 3′ primer binding site (PBS) that complements with the nicked non-targeting strand (NTS), and a reverse transcription template (RTT) to install the desired edits into the NTS catalyzed by M-MLV RT.7,10,11 The editing versatility of PE arises from the 3′ PBS-RTT of pegRNA, whose degradation causes a truncated pegRNA that occupies the editor protein but is incapable of installing the desired edits.12 Moreover, the 3′ PBS sequence is complementary not only to the NTS but also to the spacer sequence of pegRNA, which potentially compromises the proper formation of Cas9n-pegRNA complex.6,12,13,14 Together, the availability of pegRNA with accessible PBS-RTT is an essential parameter to PE editing efficiency, especially in clinical settings where the pegRNA expression is limited.

Previously, appending a structured RNA motif at the 3′ end of pegRNA has successfully increased PE editing efficiencies by preventing the PBS-RTT from 3′ exonuclease degradation.12 By screening a series of RNA motifs, an engineered pegRNA (epegRNA) was designed by appending an evopreQ1 to the 3′ of pegRNA to protect the PBS-RTT sequence.12 This design achieved 1.5- to 5.6-fold higher editing efficiencies in various cellular contexts relative to the canonical pegRNA.12 Another study changed the non-C/G pair to a C/G pair in stem-loop 2 of pegRNA, which induced a 2.77-fold increase of PE efficiency, presumably via stabilizing pegRNA secondary structure.15 Besides, chemical modifications of pegRNA, which can reduce its degradation, have also been used to enhance PE editing efficiency.12,16 All the optimizations increase the chance of forming a competent PE-pegRNA complex. However, the undesired sequence complementary between PBS and spacer in the pegRNA still limits the downstream reverse transcription and installation of desired edits.7,17

According to the high-resolution structure of the SpCas9/sgRNA complex and previous investigations on sgRNA engineering, extra sequences could be incorporated into the loop 2 or tetraloop of sgRNA without interrupting the formation of SpCas9/sgRNA complex as well as the downstream recognition and cleavage of the targeted dsDNA.17 Therefore, we inserted an additional PBS-RTT sequence into the loop 2 or tetraloop of epegRNA, which presumably could compensate for the consumed PBS due to PBS-spacer complementary and lead to higher editing efficiency. Biochemical assays demonstrated that inserting an extra PBS-RTT sequence into either the loop 2 or tetraloop of sgRNA or epegRNA won’t decrease their binding affinity with Cas9. Importantly, we observed a tendency of increase on PE editing efficiencies when an extra copy of PBS-RTT was incorporated into the loop 2, but not tetraloop, of epegRNA, demonstrating that the local concentration and steric hindrance of PBS-RTT sequence are other essential parameters to PE efficiencies apart from the epegRNA sequence stability. By systematically loop engineering, we develop an engineered epegRNA with universally enhanced editing efficiencies. This study provides an efficient PE, which is able to generate versatile editing outcomes at high efficiencies across various target sites and cellular contexts.

Results

Moving the PBS-RTT of pegRNA to the loop 2 retains PE editing efficiency

To improve the limited efficiency of PE resulting from the self-circularization and 3′ end degradation of pegRNA, we firstly tried to move the PBS-RTT of pegRNA to the loop regions of the pegRNA scaffold. Because the substitutions and deletions in loop 2 and tetraloop presumably won’t affect the Cas9 catalytic function,17,18 we chose tetraloop and loop 2 in sgRNA for PBS-RTT insertion (Figure 1A). Considering that the untethered RT might exhibit lower steric hindrance than the nCas9-RT fusion protein when using the loop-modified sgRNAs, we employed untethered RT in the loop-modified PE system.19 An all-in-one vector, including the expression cassettes of a modified sgRNA, a Cas9n (H840A), an untethered M-MLV RT,19,20 and a fluorescent protein (mKate2), was designed to target VEGFA or EMX1 in HEK293T cells (Figure 1B). Compared to the canonical pegRNA, the loop 2-modified sgRNA mediated similar prime editing frequencies (Figure 1C). However, the tetraloop-modified sgRNA lost the majority of its editing efficiency, likely because the PBS in the tetraloop is too far to be accessed by the nicked DNA strand in spatial position and thus fails to initiate reverse transcription. The result indicated that the PBS-RTT in loop 2 could successfully bind with the nicked DNA and subsequently initiates reverse transcription.

Incorporating an extra PBS-RTT copy into the loop 2 of the epegRNA scaffold enables robust prime editing

Having verified that the loop 2-modified sgRNA enables comparable editing efficiency to pegRNA, we introduced the same design to the epegRNA, in which the 3′ end PBS-RTT is protected by a structured evopreQ1 from degradation12 (Figure 2A). We reasoned that two PBS-RTT copies in the modified epegRNA might promote the interaction between PBS and the nicked DNA strand, resulting in improved editing efficiency of PE. Additionally, we also considered that the presence of two copies of PBS-RTT might change the secondary structure of epegRNA, which would impact its binding to Cas9n and decrease the PE editing efficiency. Therefore, we performed surface plasmon resonance (SPR) to assess the binding affinity of the modified epegRNA for Cas9. Inserting the PBS-RTT sequence into the loop 2 of epegRNA resulted in comparable binding affinity for Cas9 as the canonical epegRNA and a loop 2-modified sgRNA (KD = 5.40 nM for loop 2-modified sgRNA and 4.41 nM for loop 2-modified epegRNA versus 5.17 nM for unmodified epegRNA, Figures 2B and S1). Besides, the addition of PBS-RTT to the tetraloop of sgRNA and epegRNA even modestly increased the binding affinity for Cas9 (KD = 2.61 nM for tetraloop-modified sgRNA and 2.01 nM for tetraloop-modified epegRNA versus 5.17 nM for unmodified epegRNA, Figure S2). The results indicated that the incorporation of PBS-RTT into the loop 2 or tetraloop did not impede Cas9/epegRNA complex formation. Therefore, we proceeded to assess the editing efficiency of loop 2-modified epegRNA in HEK293T cells.

We transfected an all-in-one plasmid encoding loop 2-modified epegRNA and Cas9n with an untethered RT into HEK293T cells to validate whether the extra PBS-RTT in epegRNA could improve the PE2 editing efficiency. Compared to epegRNA, loop 2-modified epegRNA increased the editing efficiency across three gene loci (Figure 2C), especially at DNMT1 locus (2-fold increase), indicating that the local concentration of PBS-RTT sequence appears a critical parameter for PE efficiency. In contrast, inserting an extra PBS-RTT copy into the tetraloop of epegRNA decreased PE efficiency by 47% (Figure S3). The trend of decrease is the same as the tetraloop-modified sgRNA, which again implies that the position of PBS-RTT in the scaffold of sgRNA is a critical parameter for the editing efficiency of PE.

Besides, we also introduced multiple PBS-RTT sequences at the 3′ end of the canonical pegRNA tailed with an evopreQ1 motif12 (hereafter, tail-modified epegRNA) to increase the local concentration of PBS-RTT (Figure S4A). However, the tail-modified epegRNAs did not increase the editing efficiencies at the three genomic loci we tested (HEK4, VEGFA, and EMX1) in HEK293T cells (Figure S4B).

Exploring the design parameters of loop 2-modified epegRNA

We next sought to optimize the loop 2-modified epegRNA to further increase the editing efficiency of PE2 in HEK293T cells. We speculated that the PBS in an internal loop is less accessible by the nicked DNA compared to the PBS at the 3′ end of pegRNA, which might cause steric hinderance and diminish the editing efficiency of PE. To facilitate PBS accessibility for the nicked DNA, we added extended sequences at both the 5′ and 3′ ends of the PBS-RTT and the stem of loop 2 (Figure 3A), and these extension sequences were named RTT extension, PBS extension, and stem extension, respectively. We firstly evaluated the effect of different length of RTT extension, PBS extension (3 nt-24 nt), and stem extension (1 nt-7 nt) in loop 2-modified sgRNA on the PE efficiency at three targets (Figure 3B). The results demonstrated that the loop 2-modified epegRNA with a 12-nt RTT extension yielded higher PE efficiency. Besides, the PE efficiency appeared to positively correlate with the length of PBS extension. Among the parameters we tested, 18- to 24-nt PBS extension and 5- to 7-nt stem extension consistently resulted in superior editing efficiency (Figure 3B).

After establishing an appropriate range of extension length for designing loop 2-modified sgRNA, we then assessed the efficiency of loop 2-modified epegRNA with various extension lengths across six endogenous sites (Figures 3C–3H). Compared to canonical epegRNA, the loop 2-modified epegRNA with 24-nt PBS extension length remarkedly increased PE efficiency, resulting in a 431% improvement at DNMT1 locus (+2 C·G to A·T) and 19%–72% improvement at RNF2 (+4 A·T to C·G), HEK4 (+2 G·C to T·A), RUNX1 (+1 C·G to A·T), and VEGFA (+3 A·T to C·G) (Figures 3C–3H). At four of the six sites (DNMT1, RNF2, RUNX1, and EMX1), we further evaluated longer PBS extensions in the loop 2-modified epegRNA (Figures 3C–3H). The PBS extensions between 30 and 48 nucleotides did not further increase the editing efficiency at these tested sites except for DNMT1, at which we observed a remarkable increase in editing efficiency (710%), but the baseline for this benchmark is considerably low. We also noticed that the loop 2-modified epegRNA didn’t increase the PE editing efficiency at EMX1 locus, at which the baseline editing efficiency of epegRNA has already been close to 50% (Figure 3H). Overall, the loop 2-modified epegRNA resulted in a 53.5% average improvement in PE efficiency relative to that of canonical epegRNA across all tested sites in HEK293T cells (Figure 3I), with no apparent decrease in edit/indel ratios (Figures S5A–S5E). A similar assessment was conducted by using a series of tetraloop-modified sgRNAs with various extension lengths in the tetraloop. But these tetraloop-modified sgRNAs didn’t increase editing efficiency as expected (Figure S6).

To determine which RTT in loop 2-modified epegRNA dominates reverse transcription, we designed two distinct RT templates to introduce different edits and tested the performance across three genomic loci. We found that in loop 2-modified epegRNA, the RTT at 3′ end is more reverse transcribed and inserted in target sites than that in loop 2 (Figure S7). The result indicated that the RTT at 3′ end is more sufficient to bind with the nicked DNA than the RTT in loop 2.

Scaffold insertion is resulted from extended reverse transcription beyond the expected sites, which causes undesired edits at the target loci.9,21 To validate whether RTT in loop 2 would increase scaffold incorporation, we separately examined the PE2-mediated indels with epegRNA, loop 2-modified sgRNA and loop 2-modified epegRNA at three loci. We observed comparable scaffold insertion among the three gRNAs (Figure S8). The results revealed that introducing RTT to loop 2 would not elevate scaffold insertion.

The loop 2-modified epegRNA is compatible with the PE3 nicking sgRNA

To verify the broad applicability of the loop 2-modified epegRNA, we further applied this strategy in PE3, in which an additional sgRNA nicks the non-edited strand and improves the editing efficiency via inducing the endogenous mismatch repair pathway.7 The loop 2-modified epegRNA significantly improved the efficiency of PE3 compared to epegRNA across four loci (79% at FANCF, 87% at RNF2, 27% at HEK4, and 121% at RUNX1) (Figure 4A). We also noticed a slightly higher unintended indel frequency when using the loop 2-modified epegRNAs, but the edit/indel ratio remained comparable to that of the canonical epegRNA (Figure S9A). The result indicated that the incorporation of PBS-RTT into epegRNA is compatible with the nicking sgRNA and can enhance PE3 efficiency.

The loop 2-modified epegRNA improves the editing efficiency of PE2 across distinct edit types and multiple cell lines

Having validated the advantage of using loop 2-modified epegRNA to introduce single base substitutions, we evaluated the performance of loop 2-modified epegRNA for precise installation of small insertions and deletions. According to the result of design parameters exploration, we designed loop 2-modified epegRNAs to introduce 1- and 3-bp insertions or deletions in HEK293T cells (Figure 4B). We found that the loop 2-modified epegRNAs enhanced PE efficiency in three out of the eight tested edits. However, for the rest of edits, the improvement in editing efficiency mediated by loop 2-modified epegRNA was limited. We speculated that was because the extension length was designed based on the previous results for single-base substitution (Figure 3B), which may not be suitable for small insertions and deletions. Thus, the editing efficiency might be further improved by varying the extension length for the optimal design of loop 2-modified epegRNA. The edit/indel ratio of PE with loop 2-modified epegRNAs remained at a similar level to that of the epegRNAs (Figure S9B).

The previous studies demonstrated that PE editing efficiency varies between cell types,7,11 highlighting the need to test the improved PE system in a variety of cells. We thus evaluated the ability of loop 2-modified epegRNAs for installing single-base conversions at FANCF, RNF2, and HEK4 sites in A549 and HeLa cells. The epegRNA was used for comparison. The loop 2-modified epegRNAs improved the editing efficiency across all sites in both cell lines (Figures 4C and 4D), demonstrated by about 71% higher editing in A549 cells and 40% higher editing in HeLa cells, with no significant change in edit/indel ratio overall (Figures S9C and S9D). These results suggested that the loop 2-modified epegRNAs can enhance PE efficiency across distinct edit types and multiple cell lines.

Discussion

PE is a versatile gene editing tool to enable small insertions, deletions, and single-base conversions with low rate of undesired edits. However, the PE efficiency remains low at many target sites, hindering its potential in translational medicine. In this study, we present a novel strategy to improve PE efficiency by introducing an extra PBS-RTT sequence into loop 2 of the epegRNA scaffold. The addition of this extra PBS-RTT increases the local concentration of accessible PBS-RTT and improves the likelihood of PBS binding to the nicked DNA. Moreover, the loop 2-modified epegRNA contains two copies of PBS-RTT, allowing it to mediate prime editing even if the 3′ PBS-RTT is degraded. These factors contribute to the enhanced PE observed with the loop 2-modified epegRNA. We found that the loop 2-modified epegRNAs broadly improved PE2 efficiency compared to canonical epegRNAs across different cell lines. Additionally, we investigated the performance of loop 2-modified epegRNA in PE3 and observed an average improvement of 78% relative to the canonical epegRNA.

We explored various parameters for the loop 2-modified epegRNAs by altering the RTT extension, PBS extension, and stem extension. PE efficiency was effectively improved with PBS extensions of 18–24 nucleotides, stem extensions of 5–7 nucleotides, and RTT extensions of 12 nucleotides. This might be because that the extension of PBS, RTT and stem in loop 2 could reduce the steric hindrance of PBS-RTT sequence to the DNA nicking end, thereby increasing the PE efficiency.

Furthermore, although we have verified the efficiency of loop 2-modified epegRNA in PE3, further study should be performed to investigate the potential benefits of utilizing loop 2-modified epegRNA in combination with more advanced PE4,11 PE5,11 PE6,9 and PE722 to further improve the PE efficiency. This advancement would significantly improve the effectiveness and broaden the application scope of prime editing.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of plasmids

The modified sgRNAs and pegRNAs were synthesized by General Biosystems (Anhui) Corp. Ltd. and cloned into pU6-pegRNA-GG-acceptor (Addgene#132777) or pU6-tevopreq1-GG-acceptor (Addgene#174038). All the sequences of modified pegRNAs are listed in Tables S1 and S2. The pCMV-PE2 (Addgene#132775) was used to express prime editor protein.

Cell culture

HEK293T cells were a gift from Shang Cai Lab, Westlake University. HEK293T cells were cultured and passaged in dulbecco’s modified eagle’s medium (DMEM) with high glucose (Sigma), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gemini) and 1% penicillin streptomycin (Gibco). Cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. Cell line was authenticated by their respective suppliers and tested negative for mycoplasma.

Transfection and flow cytometry

Sixteen to twenty-four hours before transfection, HEK293T cells were seeded into 12-well poly-d-lysine coated plates (Corning). Cells were transfected at approximately 70% confluency with lipofectamine 3000 (ThermoFisher Scientific), 3249 ng all-in-one plasmid (PE2), and 1083 ng sgRNA plasmid (PE3) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Unless otherwise stated, cells were cultured for 2 days following transfection, after which cells were dissociated with 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (1X) (Gibco) and finally resuspended with 150 μl 1×PBS (ThermoFisher Scientific). Then the mKate2 positive cells were sorted by MULTI-APPLICATION CELL SORTER MA900 (Sony) and collected for deep sequencing analyses.

Genomic DNA preparation

The sorted cells were lysed by adding 20 μL of freshly prepared lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5; 0.05% SDS [ThermoFisher Scientific]; 25 μg/mL proteinase K [Tiangen]). The mixture was then incubated at 37°C for 1.5 h, followed by an additional incubation at 80°C for over 30 min to inactivate the proteinase K. Primers used for mammalian cell genomic DNA amplification are listed in Table S3.

High-throughput DNA sequencing of genomic DNA samples

Genomic sites of interest were amplified from genomic DNA samples and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq. In brief, amplification primers containing Illumina forward and reverse adapters (Table S3) were used for a first round of PCR (PCR 1) to amplify the genomic region of interest. PCR 1 reactions (50 μL) were performed with 0.25 μM of each forward and reverse primer, 1 μL genomic DNA extract and 25 μL NEBNext Ultra II Q5 Master Mix (M0544S). PCR reactions were carried out as follows: 98°C for 30 s, then 32 cycles of (98°C for 10 s, 62°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s), followed by a final 72°C extension for 2 min. Unique Illumina barcoding primer pairs were added to each sample in a secondary PCR reaction (PCR 2). Specifically, 50 μL of a given PCR 2 reaction contained 0.25 μM of each unique forward and reverse Illumina barcoding primer pair, 2.5 μL unpurified PCR 1 reaction mixture, and 25 μL NEBNext Ultra II Q5 Master Mix (M0544S). The barcoding PCR 2 reactions were carried out as follows: 98°C for 30 s, then 15 cycles of (98°C for 10 s, 61°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s), followed by a final 72°C extension for 2 min. PCR 2 products (pooled by common amplicons) were purified by 0.5× and 0.6× magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter), eluting with 25 μL water. DNA concentration was measured by microspectrophotometer (Nanodrop, ThermoFisher Scientific) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq instrument according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Analysis of genome editing outcomes from deep sequencing data

Sequencing reads were demultiplexed using Cutadapter. Alignment of amplicon sequences to a reference sequence was performed using CRISPResso223 (Table S4). Amplicon sequences were aligned to a reference sequence with CRISPResso2 in standard mode using the parameters “-q 30” and “-discard_indel_reads TRUE”. For each amplicon, the CRISPResso2 quantification window was positioned to include the entire sequence between pegRNA- and sgRNA-directed Cas9 cut sites. We set the maximum overlap for paired-end reads to 140 nucleotides and the minimum overlap to 10 nucleotides. The plot window size was set to 60 nucleotides, and only alleles with a frequency of at least 1% were included in the plot. CRISPResso2 provides information about reads, such as the number of valid reads input, the proportion of unmodified reads, the distribution of mutation sites, and the location of the quantization window with sgRNA and Reference. For all prime editing yield quantification, prime editing efficiency was calculated as: percentage of (number of reads with the desired edit that do not contain indels)/(number of total reads). For quantification of point mutation editing, CRISPResso2 was run in standard mode with “discard_indel_reads” on. Prime editing for installation of point mutations was then explicitly calculated as: (frequency of specified point mutation in non-discarded reads) × (number of non-discarded reads)/(total reads). To analyze scaffold incorporation, it was defined as the percentage of (number of amplicons with scaffold-templated bases)/(number of reads that align to the amplicon). For all experiments, indel yields were calculated as: (number of indel-containing reads)/(total reads). Due to the large number of experimental parameters, in order to compare the results of multiple experiments, we used epegRNA as a control to standardize the data, including editing efficiency and indel.

In vitro pegRNAs transcription assays

PegRNAs transcribed in vitro were obtained by HiScribe T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (E2040S).

Surface plasmon resonance binding assay

Binding affinity of transcribed pegRNAs and Cas9 were assessed using a Cytiva Biacore 8K instrument equipped with a CM5 sensor chip. The immobilization of Cas9 protein (IDT) onto the sample flow cell of the CM5 chip was achieved by employing standard amine coupling reagents, namely 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide and N-hydroxysuccinimide, resulting in approximately 5,000 resonance units. The reference flow chip remained unmodified. A single-cycle kinetics program was employed to serially dilute the transcribed pegRNAs and pass it over the immobilized Cas9 protein and the reference flow cell. Background subtraction was performed by injecting the running buffer, which consisted of PBS (pH 7.4) supplemented with 0.05% Tween 20, using the same program. All data were processed and analyzed using the Biacore Insight Evaluation Software (Cytiva) with a 1:1 langmuir binding model.
