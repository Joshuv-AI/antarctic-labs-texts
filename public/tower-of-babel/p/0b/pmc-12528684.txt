Modeling subsurface instabilities for urban infrastructure resilience via novel joint microgravity and geotechnical methods
Authors: Andy Anderson Bery, Joseph Gnapragasan, Adedibu Sunny Akingboye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn urban areas with complex geological formations, such as Petaling Jaya in western Peninsular Malaysia, assessing subsurface conditions is crucial for the safe and efficient design of infrastructure foundations. Traditional methods often fail to address the variability and heterogeneity of soil-rock profiles, which are key to predicting load-bearing capacity and preventing structural failures. This study introduces a novel microgravity–geotechnical approach, integrating Bouguer and residual with enhanced gravity-derivative and gravity anomaly depth-structure modeling as well as borehole data (SPT-N and RQD) to examine subsurface features and structural instabilities. The gravity models highlight significant low-density anomalies corresponding to weathered silty sands, sandy silts, and fractured limestone, while derivative and tilt angle maps reveal curvilinear structures, suggesting tectonic or karstic dissolution processes. These features, along with observed weak zones, exacerbate the risk of uneven settlement, subsidence, and structural damage, which are already evident in the area’s infrastructure. Borehole and depth-structure gravity models confirm variability in subsurface materials, with competent layers extending between 18 and 85 m in depth, underscoring the importance of precise foundation siting. Areas with low-density or fractured materials are evidently unsuitable for shallow foundations and require reinforcement, including deep foundation anchoring and specialized piling. The findings of this research provide critical insights for infrastructure design in regions with complex geological features, offering guidance on reliable siting and remediation of failing infrastructure. The approach established in this study can be applied to other terrains facing similar subsurface challenges, promoting safer and more resilient infrastructure development.Keywords: Microgravity crustal study, Subsurface gravity anomaly, Infrastructure instability, Rock quality designation (RQD), Standard penetration test (SPT-N), Petaling MalaysiaSubject terms: Geophysics, Engineering

Introduction

The stability of built infrastructure is fundamentally governed by the geological conditions at construction sites1–4. Subsurface irregularities, including voids, fractures, and density anomalies, frequently remain undetected during routine site assessments, ultimately predisposing engineering structures to subsidence, cracking, and deformation5–7. In regions undergoing rapid urbanization—where stratigraphy can be heterogeneous and faulted—these hidden discontinuities can significantly weaken foundational stability and threaten the longevity of infrastructure8–11. Identifying and mitigating such features early in the development process is essential for ensuring enduring infrastructure performance. This requires an efficient geological–geophysical (including geotechnical) approach capable of providing adequate subsurface competency and stability over a large area.

Several geophysical and geotechnical techniques have been used to investigate subsurface instability in various terrains, yet many face technical challenges, especially in dense urban areas3,12–15. Techniques like electrical resistivity, seismic refraction, and induced polarization are effective but often impractical in urban settings due to limited space and interference from electromagnetic and anthropogenic noise2,16,17. Ground Penetrating Radar (GPR) effectively detects shallow anomalies but struggles in conductive overburden such as clay-rich soils, limiting its penetration depth and resolution1,18. Geotechnical methods like borehole drilling, standard penetration tests (SPT-N), and rock quality designation (RQD) offer high-precision, direct subsurface characterization, yet they are invasive, labor-intensive, costly, and provide only point-source data, making them impractical for large-scale investigations3,5,16,19,20. These limitations underscore the need for a more efficient, cost-effective, and high-resolution approach to urban subsurface characterization.

In contrast, the microgravity method offers a noninvasive and precise alternative for subsurface characterization by detecting variations in the Earth’s gravitational field caused by density contrasts21–23. Its adaptability to confined urban spaces and sensitivity to subtle density anomalies make it particularly advantageous for geotechnical investigations in complex urban environments15,24,25. However, it requires highly sensitive instruments, careful data acquisition protocols, and advanced processing techniques, along with the integration of geological and geotechnical data, to ensure accurate results and meaningful interpretation26,27. Short-wavelength gravity anomalies, often associated with subsurface voids, cavities, or weakened zones, indicate localized density decreases relative to the surrounding host rock, making microgravity an effective tool for identifying and delineating geological instabilities7,23,28.

Microgravity surveys have become a critical tool, particularly for detecting mining-related voids, karst features, and structurally weak zones that pose significant risks to urban development12,26. The method’s ability to detect subtle density variations makes it highly effective in identifying subsurface anomalies that conventional geophysical and geotechnical techniques often miss27 provided a comprehensive review of advancements in gravity-based subsurface investigations, emphasizing the method’s precision in delineating weak zones that could compromise infrastructure stability. In Malaysia, where the study area is situated, microgravity studies have yielded promising results, particularly in sedimentary and karstic terrains prone to subsurface instabilities10 successfully identified sediment-filled cavities in Kuala Lipis, demonstrating the technique’s capability to characterize complex subsurface conditions13 employed land regional gravity surveys to assess Quaternary sediment thickness in the Muda River Basin, Kedah, further affirming its applicability in geological mapping. Similarly7, applied microgravity for subsurface cavity detection in Kuala Lumpur, emphasizing its effectiveness in highly urbanized environments. These studies collectively highlight the method’s reliability in subsurface characterization and its growing role in mitigating geotechnical hazards in urban infrastructure planning.

This study hypothesizes that integrating microgravity with borehole-based geotechnical methods enhances subsurface instability assessments compared to standalone techniques. Specifically, it posits that microgravity’s ability to detect density variations enables the effective identification of critical subsurface features such as cavities, fractures, and lithological transitions that influence engineering stability. The central research question is: Does integrating microgravity with geotechnical borehole data improve the detection and characterization of subsurface instability, thereby enhancing urban infrastructure resilience? Addressing this question contributes to a more robust geotechnical framework for mitigating urban geohazards.

Given the above, integrating microgravity with borehole-based geotechnical techniques is expected to significantly enhance subsurface geoengineering assessments by providing a more comprehensive understanding of lithostructural variability and depth distribution. This integration improves characterization modeling accuracy and enhances the reliability of foundation design, particularly in geotechnically sensitive areas such as Petaling Jaya, western Peninsular Malaysia. Subsurface characterization in this region is challenging due to site-specific constraints and geology dominated by limestone, which is prone to karstification under tropical conditions29,30. Prolonged chemical dissolution leads to the formation of voids, sinkholes, and extensive fracture networks—features that often remain undetected until they manifest as surface depressions, foundation settlements, or structural failures31,32. The visible structural distress observed at the study site underscores the need for detailed investigations to mitigate pre- and post-geo-hazard risks10,13,33.

Building on previous research, this study presents a novel microgravity–geotechnical framework that integrates enhanced gravity-derivative analysis, gravity anomaly depth-structure modeling, and borehole data (lithological logs, SPT-N, and RQD). Applied to a construction site in Petaling Jaya, where structural damage has raised serious concerns about subsurface instability, this approach aims to: (1) enhance subsurface characterization through gravity-derivative analysis and depth-structure modeling for improved detection of cavities, fractures, and lithological transitions; (2) delineate subsurface instability zones by integrating microgravity-derived density contrast analysis with borehole geotechnical parameters; (3) assess soil-rock competency and geotechnical integrity by correlating microgravity anomalies with SPT-N and RQD values to provide quantitative insights into subsurface strength variations; and (4) develop an optimized methodological framework that integrates geophysical and geotechnical datasets for improved subsurface characterization, with practical applications for urban infrastructure resilience and geohazard mitigation.

Unlike conventional geophysical–geotechnical studies, this methodology offers a first-of-its-kind synergy that enhances subsurface instability assessments by enabling high-resolution delineation of low-density anomalies, improving soil-rock variability characterization, and providing critical insights for remediation planning and foundation design. Given the tropical weathering in the study area, the integration of SPT-N and RQD—widely used for assessing subsurface strength and rock mass integrity3,5,34—effectively validates microgravity interpretations. By bridging gaps in existing methodologies, this study sets a new benchmark for proactive, high-resolution, multi-physics geotechnical assessments to mitigate infrastructure risks in geologically complex urban environments.

Location and geological settings

Southeast Asia comprises a mosaic of continental blocks and volcanic arc terranes, welded together along suture zones that preserve the remnants of former Tethyan ocean basins30,35. The regional geology of Peninsular Malaysia forms part of the continental core of Southeast Asia and is characterized by the Western Sibumasu Terrane and the Sukhothai Arc (or East Malaya Block) of the Indochina Terrane (Fig. 1a). These terranes amalgamated during the Late Triassic through a series of tectonic collisions that shaped the present-day geological framework35,36.

(a) Detailed regional geological map of Peninsular Malaysia (modified after43). (b) Geological map of Petaling Jaya and its environs, showing the study area (modified after29).

The Bentong-Raub Suture Zone (BRSZ) is a deep-rooted, 13 km-wide tectonic boundary formed during the Permian–Triassic, marking the convergence between the Gondwana-derived Sibumasu Terrane and the Sukhothai Arc31,37. The BRSZ, along with the Lebir Faults, subdivides Peninsular Malaysia into three north–south-trending tectonostratigraphic belts: the Western, Central, and Eastern Belts (Fig. 1a). This suture represents the collisional boundary between the Western Belt, dominated by the Sibumasu continental block, and the Central and Eastern Belts, which consist of (meta)sedimentary and magmatic rocks of the Sukhothai Arc30,36,38. These belts record tectonic–magmatic processes triggered by the partial melting of the Sibumasu and Indochina crustal blocks during their collision, resulting in extensive granitoid intrusions and regional metamorphism38.

The Central Belt predominantly features Carboniferous-Permian sedimentary formations, including limestone, sandstone, shale, siltstone, and conglomerate, reflecting diverse depositional environments30,39. Over 90% of the plutonic rocks in Peninsular Malaysia are granitic, forming two major granitic provinces: the Main Range Granite Province and the Eastern Granite Province31. Granitoids from the Permian to Late Triassic dominate the region’s igneous geology, with I-type granites prevailing in the Eastern Belt and S-type granites in the Western and Central Belts, highlighting differences in magma genesis38. The Paleozoic rocks have experienced multiple deformation cycles, folding, and uplift, resulting in complex fault networks, indicative of a dynamic tectonic history39. The region exhibits pervasive faults in various orientations, including N–S, NNW–SSE, NW–SE, and E–W, which resulted from complex repetitive movements and strike-slip faulting37,40–42. These fault systems played a pivotal role in shaping area regional geology.

Petaling, in Klang District, Selangor, western Peninsular Malaysia, is a major economic hub known for rapid urban growth and high population density. The Petaling area is characterized by diverse geological formations, including sedimentary rocks such as sandstone, shale, and limestone29,30, as shown in Fig. 1b. The limestone terrains, in particular, are highly susceptible to karstification under prolonged chemical dissolution, which leads to the formation of subsurface cavities, sinkholes, and zones of ground instability, posing significant geotechnical challenges. The investigated study site (Fig. 2a), located in Petaling Jaya, lies near the intersection of the Kuala Lumpur Limestone Formation and the Kenny Hill Formation, within a limestone-dominated geological setting (Fig. 1b). Hence, the geological complexity, coupled with rapid urbanization, presents significant risks to infrastructure and development. The terrain is relatively flat, with elevation variations primarily influenced by man-made structures. The investigated area covers approximately 17,020 m2.

Methodology

Field data acquisition procedure

The microgravity survey was meticulously conducted to ensure high-quality, reliable data. Variations in subsurface density produce measurable changes in gravity, making microgravity ideal for identifying near-surface karstic cavities. The strong density contrast between voids (~ 0 g/cm3) or water-filled cavities (~ 1 g/cm3) and the surrounding limestone (~ 2.7 g/cm3) facilitates their detection. Negative residual anomalies on the residual gravity map correspond to these cavities (or weak zones), enabling the modeling of their approximate geometry15.

Measurements were acquired on a 10 m × 10 m grid across 195 stations (Fig. 2b). Station positions were accurately determined using a local Cassini projection, with elevations reduced to a uniform gravity level under expert supervision. Data collection utilized the Scintrex AutoGrav CG-5 Gravimeter mounted on a tripod, offering ± 0.001 mGal resolution and advanced noise reduction features, including mitigation of locally induced shocks, vibrations, and micro-seismic noise, ideal for detecting subtle subsurface density variations15,45. A base station was established for drift monitoring, with measurements recorded every 2–3 h. Each station was measured in cycles of three repetitions lasting 30 s each, with 5–15 s delays for stability. Repeat measurements ensured deviations remained within acceptable limits (≤ 0.05 mGal error; ≤ 0.06 standard deviation), with stations exceeding these thresholds re-measured to uphold data accuracy.

The survey was divided into three stages to optimize conditions and minimize external disturbances. The first stage lasted five hours (12:00 am–5:00 pm) in an empty parking lot. The second stage spanned three hours (5:30 pm–8:30 pm) but was halted due to seismic activity from an earthquake in Indonesia. The final stage, lasting 11 hours (6:00 am–5:00 pm), was conducted to avoid heavy traffic disruptions. Measurements near sensitive areas, such as the Training Center, were taken during off-working hours on weekdays. To ensure consistency, repeat measurements were conducted at three previously occupied stations. Key parameters, such as instrument tilt (≤ 10°) and standard deviation (≤ 0.06), were continuously monitored to ensure high-quality gravity raw data. Gravity readings were manually documented and stored in the gravimeter’s internal memory, followed by immediate transfer to a laptop for validation and verification. These steps ensured robust data acquisition for subsequent processing and analysis.

Data processing and lithostructural-depth modeling

Bouguer and residual gravity anomaly modeling

The raw gravity data, corrected for tidal and drift effects, were rigorously validated before being organized into an MS Excel spreadsheet for further processing. Standard reduction formulae were applied to compute the Bouguer gravity anomalies. Terrain corrections were deemed unnecessary due to the flat nature of the survey area. Geosoft Oasis Montaj software was utilized for processing, gridding, and generating maps from the Bouguer gravity data.

The Bouguer anomaly map was generated using minimum curvature gridding, ensuring a smooth and continuous representation of gravity variations for detecting and interpreting subsurface anomalies. To enhance the visibility of shallow geological features (i.e., residual gravity anomalies), regional trends were removed using first-order polynomial fitting, a mathematical technique that models and subtracts large-scale gravity trends by fitting a linear surface to the Bouguer anomaly data. This was combined with a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) technique, which effectively filtered long-wavelength components and enhanced localized gravity variations. The resulting residual gravity field was then gridded with optimized cell spacing to improve resolution for further analysis. Beyond Bouguer and residual gravity mapping, advanced structural modeling techniques were applied to refine subsurface interpretations. These included analytic signal (AS) and derivative filtering (vertical and horizontal derivatives) for edge detection of lithological boundaries, source parameter imaging (SPI) for depth estimation, and Euler deconvolution (E-Decov) to determine the depths and geometries of density discontinuities. Additionally, 2D depth-structural modeling integrated residual gravity anomalies with borehole data, enhancing subsurface constraints and improving geological interpretations.

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the Bouguer and residual gravity maps, multiple validation steps were implemented. The geological map of the study area served as a corroborative reference, confirming the alignment of gravity anomalies with established stratigraphic and structural trends. Additionally, lithologic logs from the two existing boreholes (BHs) were used to cross-check gravity-derived depth variations and density contrasts, ensuring consistency with known subsurface conditions. SPT-N and RQD models further supported gravity interpretations. To enhance anomaly resolution, an optimized gridding technique was applied, and various polynomial fitting methods were tested for residual separation, with first-order polynomial fitting identified as the most effective.

Interpreting gravity anomalies requires accounting for geological and environmental factors that influence subsurface density variations, such as groundwater fluctuations and soil moisture changes, which can alter bulk density and affect gravity readings. To minimize these effects, surveys were conducted during stable climatic periods, reducing the likelihood of significant groundwater level changes. Borehole lithological logs, SPT-N values, and RQD measurements provided additional geological constraints to ensure that observed gravity anomalies were primarily associated with lithological variations and structural weaknesses rather than hydrological fluctuations. Furthermore, anthropogenic noise from urban infrastructure was mitigated using established filtering procedures. Residual gravity anomalies were cross-validated with E-Decov and SPI depth estimations, ensuring consistency across analytical techniques. This integrative approach enhances the reliability of gravity-derived subsurface models, allowing for more confident differentiation between true geological features and external influences, thereby improving the assessment of subsurface instability related to infrastructural risks.

Analytic signal (AS)

The analytic signal (AS) technique, or total gradient method, is a key enhancement technique for litho-structural studies, effective in mapping lineaments, geological trends, contacts, and edges of magnetic source bodies. It generates enhanced gravity anomaly maps to delineate density anomaly boundaries and estimate source depths46–49. Vertical derivatives

In areas with limited rock exposure and accessibility, such as the study region, first-order horizontal derivative in the y-direction (1HDy) and vertical derivative (1VD) filters are essential for mapping intra-bedrock structures50. 1HDy highlights lateral density variations, making it key to identifying horizontal geological shifts. It refines the definition of density contrasts, aiding in the detection of structural anomalies48. 1VD sharpens anomaly boundaries by reducing widths, which helps in detecting shallow geological formations and contacts49,51. Together, these filters enhance spatial resolution, crucial for lithostructural mapping.

Source parameter imaging (SPI)

The SPI technique, or local wavenumber method, utilizes the analytic signal of gravity data grids to estimate depth, edge locations, and dips of subsurface features without assuming source thickness50,52. This makes SPI effective for detecting faults, intrusions, and density anomalies in urban and geologically complex environments53. Despite its advantages, SPI has limitations. It assumes subsurface anomalies follow simple geometric models, which may not reflect actual geological complexity. Additionally, data noise, grid resolution, and anthropogenic or instrumental errors influence accuracy, with coarse grids oversimplifying structures, fine grids amplifying noise, and external disturbances introducing artifacts53,54. Despite these challenges, SPI remains a valuable tool for preliminary depth estimation, particularly when integrated with complementary geophysical datasets. Euler deconvolution (E-Decov) and 2-D depth-structural modeling

Euler Deconvolution (E-Decov) is a method for estimating the depth and location of subsurface anomalies using gravity data, based on Euler’s homogeneity equation (Eq. 5), which relates the potential field and its spatial gradients to the source location and structural index (SI)13. To mitigate errors in depth estimation, SI=0 was carefully selected based on model cells, window size, and target anomaly depth, ensuring that depth overestimation or underestimation was minimized50. This approach refined the reliability of the model, improving the accuracy of subsurface interpretations. For gravity anomalies, an SI=0 is suitable for point-like sources, effectively detecting localized density contrasts with structures13,57. This technique is particularly valuable for complex lithostructural regions, making it essential for geophysical–geotechnical urban studies where precise depth and geometry estimation is required.

Furthermore, an interactive 2-D depth-structural modeling was employed via the GM-SYS interface of Oasis Montaj software to model the soil-rock configurations, depths, and interfaces of Profile 1 near an existing borehole in the study area. This forward modeling technique calculates the gravity response of a user-defined geologic model and iteratively adjusts the model to match observed data by reducing mismatches. The 2-D model assumes a series of tabular prisms perpendicular to the profile, extending infinitely along the strike direction. This method effectively resolves subsurface structure depths, constrained by borehole data, enabling precise interpretation of geological features50.

Borehole-based SPT-N and RQD modeling

SPT-N is a cost-effective geotechnical test widely used in engineering construction to assess soil strength, competency, and penetration resistance [5,14,58]. It provides both disturbed and undisturbed samples, with in situ borehole-based SPT-N carried out during the drilling of two boreholes (BH1 and BH2) in this study. SPT-N represents the number of blows required for the sampler to penetrate 30 cm of soil, measured at 1.5 m depth intervals. A total of 16 values were recorded—10 for BH1 and 6 for BH2.

The lithologic rock quality, on the other hand, was assessed to determine factors such as foundation depth bearing capacity and potential differential settlement using the borehole-based RQD dataset from BH1. Core drilling at BH1 yielded a single limestone core sample at a depth of 12–13.5 m using a diamond drilling bit. No RQD results were obtained for BH2 due to the absence of hard rock mass penetration. RQD was calculated for the recovered limestone core as the ratio of intact core pieces longer than 100 mm to the total core run59. Topsoil and weathered residual soil profiles were unsuitable for RQD evaluation due to their incomplete recovery and low competency3,19,20. Graphical plots of SPT-N and RQD data were subsequently analyzed to assess variations in soil-rock quality with depth.

The integration of microgravity and borehole-based geotechnical data in this study follows a qualitative and semi-quantitative approach, given that the SPT-N and RQD datasets are localized and primarily serve to enhance geotechnical structural accuracy. The microgravity-derived models (Bouguer and residual gravity, gravity derivatives, and depth-structure anomalies) were interpreted in conjunction with borehole lithological logs, SPT-N values, and RQD measurements to constrain subsurface instability. This approach ensures that gravity anomalies indicative of density contrasts, cavities, or weak zones are corroborated with direct subsurface observations, thereby strengthening confidence in the geological interpretation. The validation process involved spatial correlation of identified anomalies with borehole data, facilitating a structured assessment of subsurface heterogeneities without reliance on predictive modeling or statistical interpolation.

Results

The Bouguer gravity anomaly map of the study area (Fig. 3a) reveals significant gravity variations that reflect the subsurface distribution of dense and less dense rocks. The Bouguer gravity anomaly values generally range from −13.97 to −12.68 mGal. High (negative) gravity anomalies are observed primarily in the southern and central sections of the map, with values between −13.01 and −12.68 mGal. These high anomalies suggest the presence of dense subsurface materials, likely linked to shallower, more compacted limestone bedrock13,15. The central part of the map shows intermediate gravity anomalies, ranging from −13.62 to −13.01 mGal, which may represent areas where subsurface geological formations transition between denser and less dense materials or possibly indicative of mixed lithological compositions. The gradual shift in anomaly values across this region may suggest lithologic heterogeneity or minor structural variations15. Conversely, low gravity anomalies are observed in the northern part of the map, with values as low as −13.97 and −13.62 mGal. These low anomalies indicate deeper, less dense materials such as weathered bedrock or cavities, and correspond to areas where the bedrock—possibly limestone—occurs at greater depth or has been reduced in density by weathering60.

(a) Bouguer gravity anomaly and (b) residual anomaly maps of the study area.

The residual gravity anomaly map (Fig. 3b), which isolates local variations from the regional gravity trend, provides a clearer picture of subsurface local geology. Positive residual anomalies, ranging from 0.006 mGal to 0.117 mGal, are seen in the southern and central areas, indicating denser geological formations close to the surface, possibly linked to basement uplift with intra-bedded fractures. The negative residual anomalies, ranging from −0.009 mGal to −0.111 mGal, are located primarily in the northern and northwestern sections of the study area. These negative anomalies suggest deeper or less dense rock formations, possibly pointing to thicker sedimentary layers, voids, or fractured basement rocks15. By integrating Bouguer and residual gravity data with depth-structure models and borehole-based geotechnical information, a comprehensive subsurface profile emerges. These models are significantly effective in delineating faults, basement depths, and sedimentary thicknesses, highlighting geological phenomena like sedimentary depressions or crustal thinning. The findings are critical for assessing subsurface stability and provide vital information for pre- and post-infrastructure construction planning and remediations, ensuring informed decision-making in the study area7,9.

Discussion

The Bouguer gravity anomaly map (Fig. 3a) reveals a prominent gravity gradient transitioning from low values in the northwest to high values in the southeast. This gradient highlights subsurface density contrasts associated with structural features such as faults or lithological boundaries. These variations suggest a complex subsurface with density differences reflecting diverse lithologic units, varying depths, and structural settings12,13,15. Such gravity gradients are indicative of geological processes, including faulting and lithological variation, which significantly influence groundwater flow, soil stability, and subsurface behavior9,10,21. These identified zones are crucial for urban planning and infrastructure projects, as they highlight regions of potential subsurface instability or significant structural features that could impact development7,22,61,62.

The residual gravity anomaly map (Fig. 3b) refines this understanding by emphasizing shallower, denser formations potentially linked to compact rocks, contact-metamorphic zones, or intrusive bodies22,63,64. Transitional zones marked by intermediate gravity values likely indicate fault systems, lithological boundaries, or stratigraphic contacts, further underlining the structural complexity of the region. The juxtaposition of deeper basement structures in the northern against denser, shallower formations in the southern section of the study area reflects possible tectonic activity, such as basement uplift or subsidence13,15. The correlation between the Bouguer and residual maps suggests that low-gravity anomalies correspond to deeper, loosely compacted lithological and karstified materials in the north, while the south exhibits shallower, denser formations in the study area. Subtle differences between these maps likely arise from variations in bedrock deformation effects and bedrock depth25,65.

Lithological characterization: Insights from analytic signal and derivative gravity modeling

The analytic signal (AS; Fig. 4a), first-order horizontal derivative in the y-direction (1HDy; Fig. 4b), first-order vertical derivative (1VD; Fig. 4c), and tilt angle derivative (Fig. 4d) maps enhance the understanding of the litho-structural framework of the study area, building on the insights from the Bouguer and residual gravity anomaly maps. These derivative maps emphasize local density contrasts and structural boundaries12,66, offering a refined view of subsurface geology and its implications for both pre- and post-infrastructure stability assessments.

The AS map (Fig. 4a) highlights significant density contrasts, identifying structural discontinuities such as faults, fractures, or lithological boundaries. High-amplitude anomalies (ranging from 0.0075 to 0.0105 mGal/m) in the the central–northern half of the study area explicitly depicts stronger amplitudes associated with transitional to deeper bedrock features and loosely compacted sediments, consistent with previous findings51,66. In contrast, low-amplitude zones from the center toward the southeastern Sect. (0.0014 to 0.005 mGal/m) correspond to the variability of the transitional to shallow, denser bedrock, with intra-bedded fractures reducing the amplitudes of the mapped sections. Meanwhile, the 1HDy map (Fig. 4b) accentuates lateral density variations and reveals strong gradients in the southeastern part of the map (up to 0.0057 mGal/m), indicative of active fault lines or lithological transitions13,48. These areas suggest tectonic shearing or structural complexity that may influence groundwater movement and subsurface stability. Weaker gradients in the northwest imply a uniformly deformed, extensive subsurface lithology with the potential for deeper depressions.

The 1VD map (Fig. 4c) enhances short-wavelength anomalies, revealing denser, near-surface materials such as uplifted basement rocks or shallow intrusive bodies67, while low vertical derivatives in the northwest highlight deeper depressions and less compact materials21,22. The tilt angle derivative map (Fig. 4d) reveals structural trends similar to those in Fig. 4c but places additional emphasis on curvilinear structures in the southeastern section and western edge with strong signals. These features suggest geological complexities, including faults, folds, or karstic dissolution15,67. These features indicate abrupt changes in subsurface density and structural stability, contributing to localized subsurface instability. The sharp gradients observed in Fig. 4d point to potential fault zones or fractured limestone rock masses, which could lead to differential settlement or ground subsidence in the study area7.

Together, these maps reveal the structural complexity of the subsurface, highlighting fault zones, lithological boundaries, and density contrasts crucial for assessing urban infrastructure hazards. Variations in density, particularly in fault zones or karstic features, can lead to differential settlement and subsidence, affecting infrastructure stability1,21. The size and intensity of gravity anomalies associated with cavities are influenced by surrounding structural factors, such as crack connectivity and formation mechanisms26. These variations in subsurface structure pose significant risks for infrastructure development, potentially contributing to structural cracks observed at the investigated sites10,21.

In Profile 1 (Fig. 5b), a significant depression-like feature is observed at the center of the model, with the deepest section reaching 14 m and residual gravity values ranging from −0.14 to −0.12. This observation supports earlier interpretations from the Bouguer gravity, residual gravity, and derivative maps (Figs. 3 and 4). The profiles also demonstrate complex subsurface lithological units, alternating between denser and less dense materials, which influence the observed density variations. While geological variability is expected in any study area, these boreholes provide critical ground-truthing points that corroborate microgravity-derived anomalies.

Borehole lithological data from BH1 and BH2 (Fig. 5d) further refine the profile models. BH1, reaching a depth of 18.5 m, and BH2, reaching 13.5 m, encountered moderately to highly weathered/fractured materials at depth. However, BH2 did not penetrate this limestone unit, indicating regional variation in subsurface composition. These findings confirm that the near-surface bedrock is weak limestone, which is prone to weathering and dissolution, as observed in the infrastructure cracks in the study area. Additionally, the presence of loose, unconsolidated soils, such as silty sand and sandy silt, further complicates the subsurface structure, contributing to infrastructure instability. While BH1 and BH2 provide critical lithological and geotechnical constraints, deeper structural features not captured by these boreholes or the residual gravity profiles (Fig. 5b–c) are effectively delineated in the depth-structure models, including SPI (Fig. 6a), E-Decov (Fig. 6b), and the 2-D iterative gravity anomaly depth-structure model of Profile 1 (Fig. 7). These models enhance the ability to characterize deeper geological features beyond the borehole depths, complementing the borehole data and improving the overall reliability of subsurface instability assessments. Thus, while additional boreholes could further refine spatial resolution, BH1 and BH2 are sufficient for validating key geotechnical characteristics and supporting the integrated geophysical–geotechnical interpretation of the study region.

2-D gravity anomaly depth-structure model along profile 1 within the study area.

In Fig. 6a, SPI, the depth to the top of the gravity sources reaches up to 26.81 m, while the E-Decov S.I. 0.0 has a depth of > 30 m, with intrinsic structural and curvilinear trends. Figure 6 also shows a similar depth range to the E-Decov, reaching up to 45 m for the unconsolidated silty sand and about 80 m for the sandy silt. Figure 7 clearly shows that the unconsolidated materials are deeper than depicted by the borehole logs (Fig. 5d), likely due to progressive weathering, which may have been enhanced by Malaysia’s climate, characterized by heavy rainfall and intense heat throughout the year61,68. This effect has compounded the weathering of the limestone rock, which is prone to chemical weathering due to the dissolution of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃)10,69. The structural features and trends depicted in Fig. 6b are likely responsible for the differential settlements affecting buildings in the area. These settlements are attributed to unstable foundations along these sections, further exacerbated by water seepage along the identified structural features10,15. Overall, the SPI and E-Decov results aligned well with gravity-derived models and borehole logs, effectively overcoming limitations in highly weathered or fractured zones, where gradual lithological transitions and overlapping anomalies can impact depth accuracy49,53. This confirms their robustness and reliability in subsurface instability mapping.

The implications of these findings for infrastructure development are significant. The varying depth and composition of subsurface lithologies can lead to uneven settlement, differential subsidence, and further cracking in structures, especially in ongoing projects, if appropriate competent subbases are not established for foundation support3,70. Areas characterized by weak limestone and unconsolidated materials are particularly vulnerable. The depression-like features observed in the residual gravity maps, along with the curvilinear structures indicated by the tilt angle derivative map (Fig. 4d), suggest areas of potential tectonic activity or karstic dissolution. These subsurface instabilities can exacerbate infrastructure damage, as cracks are likely a result of uneven load distribution and subsidence7,9,15.

Soil-rock quality assessment: insights from borehole-based SPT-N and RQD modeling

To further enhance the interpretation of the quality, competency, and foundation load-bearing capacity of the surface–subsurface soil-rock profiles in the study area, the SPT-N and RQD results for the two existing boreholes, BH1 and BH2, were integrated, as presented in Fig. 8. The borehole-based SPT-N and RQD model reveals variability in the recorded values: BH1 shows SPT-N values ranging from 6 to 50, while BH2 ranges from 8 to 12, with an RQD value of 47% for the depth interval of 12–13.5 m. Notably, as depicted in Fig. 5d, BH1 lacks a hard rock mass for an RQD estimate. At depths between 10.5 m and 15 m, no SPT-N measurements were recorded, as the penetration test terminated upon encountering moderately to highly weathered/fractured limestone at BH2. BH1 faced a similar challenge due to hammer weight limitations but eventually recorded its maximum SPT-N value of 50 at a depth of 18 m, indicating a fairly competent subsurface layer. The absence of recorded data at these depths in BH1 can be attributed to the high resistance posed by the highly weathered/fractured strata, which likely exceeded the energy capacity of the SPT-N hammer. Moreover, the presence of alternating very soft layers and fractured rock within this zone may have contributed to obstructions, underlining the limitations of the SPT-N method in accurately characterizing such complex subsurface conditions3,5,14.

Borehole-based SPT-N and RQD model for the study area’s soil-rock quality assessment.

The majority of the recorded SPT-N values of 6–12 correspond to unconsolidated, less competent soil units, aligning with established benchmarks for weak soil conditions5. Furthermore, the below-average RQD value of 47% suggests a weak limestone bedrock, consistent with prior interpretations and the global classification standards for rock quality34. Though the RQD is a single representative value, it still provided additional information on the likely depth of the studied site’s competent strata (limestone) and its weakness/strength in supporting pre- and post-engineering infrastructure. The borehole lithological logs, SPT-N values, and RQD measurements from BH1 and BH2 capture representative subsurface conditions, particularly in areas of concern, and align with broader regional geological trends. These findings agree with the results derived from gravity models and their enhanced depth-structural interpretations (Figs. 3–7), emphasizing the conclusion that the subsurface is characterized by weak, unconsolidated materials and weathered limestone. This highlights potential challenges for load-bearing capacity and structural stability in the study area, necessitating cautious geotechnical considerations for remediations and future infrastructure projects.

Implications of modeled soil-rock units and structural instabilities on infrastructure

The evaluation of subsurface soil-rock units and associated structural instabilities is essential for ensuring the safety and sustainability of infrastructure. This study highlights the importance of understanding the heterogeneities within soil and rock layers, as they directly influence their load-bearing capacities and susceptibility to structural failures5,12. Low-density zones observed in residual gravity maps (Fig. 3b) and weak zones identified in boreholes BH1 and BH2 (Fig. 5d) indicate significant variations in lithological units, such as unconsolidated silty sands, sandy silts, and weathered/fractured limestone. Overall, the study emphasizes the importance of integrating geophysical and geotechnical methods to inform foundation design, optimize load distribution, and ensure structural resilience in areas with complex geological formations.

Conclusions

This study, conducted in Petaling Jaya, Malaysia, presents a novel microgravity-geotechnical approach, integrating Bouguer and residual gravity modeling with lithostructure-depth gravity anomaly modeling and borehole data (SPT-N and RQD). The methodology provides comprehensive insights into subsurface structural instabilities, load-bearing capacity, and the suitability of soil-rock profiles for urban infrastructure development in a region with complex geological formations. By combining geophysical and geotechnical data, this study advances our understanding of subsurface conditions, offering practical implications for infrastructure design.

The gravity models revealed significant low-density anomalies, which were associated with weak subsurface materials, including weathered silty sands, sandy silts, and fractured limestone. The derivative results, including analytic signal and tilt angle maps, highlighted curvilinear features indicative of potential tectonic or karstic dissolution processes, further complicating subsurface stability. These weak zones increase the risk of uneven settlement, subsidence, and structural damage, as observed in existing infrastructure cracks. Such findings emphasize the critical need for careful foundation design in regions with low-density, weathered, or fractured materials to mitigate risks associated with subsurface instability. The borehole-based soil-rock quality models and lithological logs provided further validation, indicating that competent subsurface layers extend between 18 and 85 m in depth, possibly enhanced by progressive dissolution as a result of the area’s intense climates. The delineation of these subsurface materials is essential for infrastructure design, as competent layers provide a stable foundation for heavy structures, while weak or fractured materials necessitate additional geotechnical reinforcement to prevent structural failure.

The implications of these findings are profound for urban infrastructure in areas with complex subsurface geology. The study emphasizes the importance of integrating geophysical and geotechnical methods to enhance infrastructure resilience. For regions with weak subsurface materials, deep foundation anchoring, specialized piling, and sealing of water-filled voids (or cavities) are proposed to ensure reliable load-bearing capacity and structural stability. Additionally, infrastructure in areas prone to karstic dissolution or deep depressions should incorporate design features that accommodate these challenges, safeguarding against future structural failures.

Despite the valuable insights provided, this study could benefit from the incorporation of advanced deep geophysical imaging techniques to improve the understanding of deeper subsurface layers. Additionally, ongoing monitoring of infrastructure performance is essential to assess the effectiveness of applied designs and address emerging challenges. Overall, this research underscores the necessity of employing a multi-disciplinary approach, incorporating a novel methodological framework, to inform and optimize infrastructure development. By improving the understanding of subsurface conditions and addressing potential structural vulnerabilities, this study contributes to the development of safer, more resilient infrastructure in geologically complex areas.
