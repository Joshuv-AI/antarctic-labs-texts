Associations of air pollution and depression risk in older adults: an ML + SHAP + SEM framework
Authors: Shengxian Bi, Yingchun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDepression risk in older adults is a complex mental health issue influenced by multiple factors, including individual vulnerability and environmental exposure. Although many methods have been used to predict depression risk in this population, the causal relationships between these factors remain underexplored.MethodsThis study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and an environmental pollution dataset, comprising 2,086 older adults. Ten machine learning (ML) models were trained to predict depression risk, and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were applied to assess variable importance. A Structural equation modeling (SEM)-based approach was then used to examine the causal pathways and mediating mechanisms linking air pollution variables and individual variables to depression risk.ResultsAll ten ML models showed modest predictive performance, with area under the curve (AUC) values exceeding 0.6. SHAP analysis suggested pain, insomnia, and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) as the most important individual variables, while ozone (O3) and sulfur dioxide (SO2) were identified as key air pollution variables. Analysis within the SEM-based approach indicated that O3 indirectly increased depression risk through IADL and pain, while SO2 exhibited both direct and indirect negative associations with depression.ConclusionsThis study provides evidence supporting structural mechanisms linking environmental exposure and mental health. The combined ML and SEM-based approach was used to facilitate variable screening and pathway evaluation, and offers a complementary perspective for examining potential causal relationships. This integrative framework may provide a useful reference for future research on mental health and related interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26574-3.

Introduction

Depression is a common and disabling mental health disorder characterized by persistent sadness, hopelessness, and diminished interest in daily activities [1]. It often causes significant emotional and physical distress, impairing an individual’s ability to function effectively in personal, social, or occupational domains [2]. In severe cases, it can lead to suicidal thoughts and behaviors, contributing to high morbidity and mortality. Older adults are particularly vulnerable due to stressors such as physical decline, chronic illness, reduced social support, and role loss [3]. In this population, depression frequently coexists with somatic symptoms, which increases the burden of comorbid conditions, accelerates functional decline, and delays recovery [4]. Epidemiological studies have reported a prevalence of approximately 35.1% among older adults, with higher rates in rural areas and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups [5, 6]. Given the globally aging population, it is crucial to identify the key factors for depression risk in older adults to guide targeted prevention and intervention strategies.

The etiology of depression in older adults is multifactorial, shaped by complex interactions between demographic factors and broader environmental exposures [7]. Previous studies have reported that sociodemographic characteristics, health status, and psychosocial factors are closely associated with depression risk in this population [8]. Recently, more attention has focused on the impact of environmental exposures, particularly air pollution, on mental health [9–11]. Air pollution may influence the central nervous system by triggering inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and neurotoxic pathways [12]. These mechanisms may increase the risk of developing depression. However, most existing studies have relied on cross-sectional designs, which limit the ability to clarify the causal pathways linking risk factors to depression risk [13, 14]. Moreover, interactions between air pollution and individual vulnerability remain understudied. Advances in big data technologies have facilitated the application of ML algorithms in mental health research, given their strong modeling capabilities and flexibility [15, 16]. Integrating ML with causal modeling approaches shows promise in advancing the identification of risk factors and elucidating potential pathways underlying depression risk.

Building on this background, this study developed an integrated analytical framework combining ML and SEM-based approach to investigate how environmental and individual factors influence depression risk in older adults. Using a large-scale health tracking survey in China, the study addressed three objectives: (1) using predictive models for depression risk in older adults with ten ML models and comparing their performance; (2) applying SHAP to evaluate the importance of air pollution exposure and individual variables in predicting depression risk, highlighting their relative contributions and heterogeneous patterns; and (3) employing a SEM-based approach to examine the direct and indirect pathways linking air pollution and individual variables to depression risk, while identifying potential mediating mechanisms. This framework improved the early identification of depression risk in older adults and offered theoretical and empirical support for differentiated intervention strategies.

Methods

Data source and preprocessing

Individual data were obtained from CHARLS. CHARLS is a nationally representative longitudinal survey that systematically collects information on demographics, health status, income, employment, family structure, and mental health among middle-aged and older adults in China [17]. Since its launch in 2011, CHARLS has adopted a multistage, stratified sampling design and conducted follow-up surveys every two to three years. It has become a key resource for studying the health and socioeconomic conditions of China’s aging population. Air pollution data were obtained from the 0.1° gridded assimilation dataset for China. The annual mean concentration of air pollutants was defined as the exposure window, which temporally precedes the assessment of current depression risk. These data were then matched to individual survey records based on each respondent’s city and survey year [18]. During the sample selection process, the study first excluded 6,785 individuals lost to follow-up from the 17,705 respondents in the baseline survey. This ensured the inclusion of only those who completed all follow-up waves. Next, respondents under 60 years of age (n = 6,506), those with missing age information (n = 147), and those with missing depression symptom data (n = 2,181) were excluded. The final analytical sample consisted of 2,086 older adults (see Fig. 1).

Research variables

The predictor variables included individual variables and air pollution variables. Individual variables comprised region, gender, age, residential area, education, marital status, poverty, pension insurance, health insurance, parent-child communication, chronic disease, chronic disease comorbidity, social activities, smoking, alcohol consumption, insomnia, physical exercise, activities of daily living (ADL), IADL, cognitive ability, and pain [19–21]. Social activities were measured across eight dimensions: friend interaction, card/board games, helping friends, exercise, community activities, volunteer work, training classes, and internet use. The ADL were evaluated using the Katz Index, while the IADL were assessed using the Lawton scale [22, 23]. Difficulty in any task was classified as functional impairment. Cognitive function was measured by the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), which includes four domains: orientation, calculation, drawing, and episodic memory. In accordance with international consensus on cognitive aging, cognitive impairment was defined as a score that was ≥ 1 SD below the age-adjusted normative mean [24]. The air pollution variables included SO2, O3, nitrogen dioxide (NO2), carbon monoxide (CO), Particulate matter ≤ 10 μm (PM10) and ≤ 2.5 μm (PM2.5). These data were obtained from monitoring stations and aggregated annually to estimate regional exposure for each individual.

The outcome variable was measured using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10), which evaluates self-reported depression risk over the immediate past week prior to the interview date [25]. The scale includes the following items: (1) feeling bothered by things that usually do not upset you; (2) having trouble keeping your mind on what you were doing; (3) feeling depressed; (4) feeling that everything you did was an effort; (5) feeling hopeful about the future; (6) feeling fearful; (7) restless sleep; (8) feeling happy; (9) feeling lonely; and (10) having difficulty getting going. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from “rarely or none of the time” (0) to “most of the time” (3), with total scores ranging from 0 to 30. A total score of ≥ 10 was used as the threshold for high risk of depression.

Study design

This study used a multistage analytical framework that integrated ML, SHAP-based interpretability, and SEM-based approach to investigate the effects of air pollution and individual variables on depression risk in older adults. First, the predictor variables were screened using nonparametric tests and LASSO regression with L1 regularization. The penalty parameter was selected using 10-fold cross-validation, and ten individual variables that were significantly associated with depression risk were retained: pain, IADL, region, insomnia, chronic disease, gender, cognitive ability, residential area, ADL, and social activities. Second, ten ML models were applied to the selected variables to assess model robustness and generalizability. These models included logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT), XGBoost, gradient boosting machine (GBM), LightGBM, deep neural network (DNN), recurrent neural network (RNN), and long short-term memory (LSTM) [26]. A sensitivity analysis using SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) was performed on the training set to address class imbalance. Third, SHAP analysis was performed to interpret the model outputs and identify the relative importance of air pollution and individual variables [27]. Finally, a SEM-based approach was established based on the SHAP results to further explore the causal pathways linking air pollution and individual variables to depression risk [28]. In the SEM analysis, a mixed-effects logistic regression model was used to handle the hierarchical structure of the panel data. The 95% confidence intervals for the mediation effects were calculated using the parametric Bootstrap method. Additionally, cross-validation, Bootstrap accuracy analysis, outlier detection, and control variable analysis were employed to test the sensitivity of the model specification (see Fig. 2).

Results

Descriptive characteristics of depression risk among older adults

The baseline distribution of depression risk among 2086 older adults showed individual differences (Table 1). A higher prevalence was observed among those living in central regions (51.47%), females (52.40%), rural residents (48.71%), and individuals with primary education or below (49.68%). Depression risk was also greater among single or divorced adults, those living in poverty (64.42%), and those covered by resident pension insurance (48.56%) or resident health insurance (49.40%). In terms of family relationships, older adults who rarely communicated with their children had a risk rate as high as 68.18%. Regarding health status, individuals with chronic diseases (51.10%), multimorbidity (55.53%), insomnia (65.14%), ADL impairment (79.46%), IADL impairment (65.62%), or cognitive impairment (58.25%) faced higher risks. As for lifestyle behaviors, depression risk was more common among those who did not participate in social activities (51.11%) or engaged in little or no physical exercise (54.02%). Conversely, the prevalence of depression risk was lower among smokers (41.78%) and drinkers (44.10%). During the follow-up period, the exposure levels of air pollutants were summarized by median and interquartile range (IQR) for both the depression and non-depression groups. The depression group had a median CO concentration of 0.32 mg/m3 (IQR = 0.24). The median concentrations of O3 (83.88 [6.45] µg/m³), NO2 (14.03 [22.27] µg/m3), and PM2.5 (59.36 [27.81] µg/m3) were lower in the depression group. By contrast, median concentrations of PM10 (102.94 [45.82] µg/m3) and SO2 (7.89 [11.10] µg/m3) were higher in the depression group.

ML model evaluation

Ten machine learning models were applied to predict depression risk among older adults, with results shown in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 1. All models showed stable predictive performance, with AUC values above 0.6. LR achieved the highest AUC (0.6763), with modest differences observed across the other models. In addition to AUC, performance metrics varied within narrow ranges for accuracy (0.48–0.55), sensitivity (0.89–0.98), Youden’s index (0.10–0.14), and F1-Score (0.63–0.65), though overall distributions were largely comparable. A sensitivity analysis using SMOTE on the training set to address mild class imbalance showed no significant change in AUC (< 0.02).

Relative importance of variables

Figures 4 and 5 show the SHAP analysis results for the LSTM model. The remaining nine machine learning models are presented in Supplementary Figs. 1–9. The ranking of feature importance was generally consistent across all ten models. Among individual variables, pain, insomnia, and IADL contributed the most to depression risk. Among air pollution variables, O3 and SO2 had the greatest impact.

SEM-based approach analysis

The results of the SEM-based approach are shown in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 2. The model showed an AUC of 0.851, accuracy of 0.775, sensitivity of 0.654, specificity of 0.861, precision of 0.768, and an F1-score of 0.706. Neither O3 nor SO2 showed significant direct effects on insomnia, IADL, or depression. No significant indirect effects through insomnia or IADL were observed. In contrast, both pollutants exhibited significant direct effects on pain, with standardized coefficients of 0.115 (95% CI: [0.025, 0.205], p = 0.012) for O3 and 0.104 (95% CI: [0.042, 0.166], p = 0.001) for SO2. Significant path-specific indirect effects on depression through pain were identified for both pollutants. The standardized coefficients were − 0.105 (95% CI: [− 0.187, − 0.023], p = 0.012) for O3 and − 0.095 (95% CI: [− 0.152, − 0.037], p = 0.001) for SO2. The comprehensive decomposition of effects is presented in Supplementary Table 3. A statistically significant total effect was observed for SO2 (standardized coefficient = − 0.147, 95% CI: [− 0.247, − 0.047]), which was largely driven by its indirect effect (standardized coefficient = − 0.093, 95% CI: [− 0.176, − 0.010]). For O3, the total effect was − 0.130 (95% CI: [− 0.275, 0.014]), while the corresponding indirect effect did not reach statistical significance (standardized coefficient = − 0.099, 95% CI: [− 0.223, 0.021]). To facilitate interpretation of the standardized effect estimates, absolute risk differences were calculated. A one standard deviation increase in exposure was associated with a 0.61% point reduction in the predicted probability of depression for O3 and a 1.07% point reduction for SO2.

Potential multicollinearity and the robustness of the observed associations were assessed using variance inflation factors and the Pearson correlation matrix for the air pollutants (Supplementary Table 4). The correlation between O3 and SO2 was weak (r=-0.105). All variance inflation factors were well below conventional thresholds, with the highest value being 2.33 for O3, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity. A side-by-side comparison of single-pollutant models, in which each pollutant was entered separately, and the multi-pollutant model was then conducted (Supplementary Table 5). The estimated effects were highly consistent across model specifications. The total effect of SO2 differed only marginally between the single-pollutant model (β=-0.151) and the multi-pollutant model (β=-0.147). A similar pattern was observed for O3, with estimates of -0.144 and − 0.130, respectively. These results indicate that the reported associations are robust and unlikely to be driven by collinearity among pollutants.

The results of the sensitivity analysis are summarized in Table 2, and detailed metrics are provided in Supplementary Tables 6, 7, 8 and 9. The 5-fold cross-validation analysis showed that the coefficient of variation (CV) for SO2 effect estimates ranged from 8.2% to 16.0%. In contrast, the effect estimates for O3 were moderately sensitive to sample composition (CV: 52.7%-55.9%). Bootstrap accuracy analysis indicated that the standard error variation for all effect estimates of both pollutants remained below 2%. After excluding strong outliers, the changes in coefficients were less than 7%. Covariate control analysis showed moderate to high sensitivity in model specification. The O3 effect estimate varied between 48.2% and 65.4%, while the SO2 effect estimate ranged from 12.8% to 39.4% under different adjustment strategies.

Discussion

This study used nationally representative longitudinal survey data to develop a comprehensive analytical framework that integrated ML and SEM-based approach. In this framework, ML was applied primarily as an exploratory tool for variable screening, while the SEM-based approach was subsequently used for pathway inference. The study systematically evaluated the pathways through which air pollution and individual factors influence depression risk in older adults. By modeling these pathways, the study identified key risk factors and revealed how air pollution indirectly affects depression risk in older adults through mediators, including physical health and functional impairments.

ML was used to inform the selection and prioritization of variables for the causal pathway analysis. Across the ten ML algorithms applied in this study, pain, insomnia, and IADL were consistently identified as the most influential individual-level factors associated with depression risk, with pain emerging as the dominant contributor [29–32]. At the environmental level, O3 and SO2 were considered the primary air pollution exposures. This prioritization guided the subsequent SEM-based approach analysis, which focused on the interplay between these individual health-related factors and air pollution exposures.

Causal pathway analysis revealed that air pollutants influence mental health through different mechanisms. The direct effects of O3 and SO2 on insomnia, IADL, and depression risk were insignificant. However, both pollutants showed a significant positive direct effect on pain, suggesting that higher concentrations of air pollutants may worsen pain perception in older adults. This positive direct effect may be related to the oxidative stress and inflammation induced by air pollution [33]. These physiological reactions activate neuroinflammatory pathways, increasing pain sensitivity and potentially aggravating the physiological basis of chronic pain [33, 34]. Through the pain-mediated pathway, the effects of O3 and SO2 on depression risk were negatively correlated. This negative indirect effect suggests that higher air pollutant exposure increases pain perception but may lower depression risk through certain adaptive mechanisms [35, 36]. Long-term exposure to air pollution may cause individuals to develop greater pain tolerance or cognitively reframe pain as an environmental factor rather than an internal psychological issue. This reduces the likelihood that pain will lead to depression [37]. Additionally, some studies propose that attention-shifting mechanisms may be involved, whereby chronic pain occupies cognitive resources and diverts attention away from depressive emotions, thus weakening the direct pathway from pain to depression [38–40]. The negative correlation of pain as a mediator may also reflect a phenomenon in which emotional distress is misattributed to physical pain, leading individuals to overlook underlying depressive symptoms, thereby reducing the reported depression risk [41, 42]. It is important to note that while SO2 showed a significant mediating effect, its direct effects on pain and depression risk were relatively weak. This may be due to the low-concentration exposure characteristic of SO2. In the present study sample, SO2 concentrations did not reach a threshold required to trigger significant physiological impacts, and its effects were primarily mediated through cumulative indirect pathways. Short-term exposure may activate protective mechanisms, such as autophagy or anti-inflammatory responses, rather than directly inducing neurotoxicity [43, 44]. This results in weaker immediate effects on depression. However, through the pain-mediated pathway, its long-term cumulative effects gradually become apparent, amplifying the inflammatory cascade and indirectly regulating depression risk. Additionally, this weak correlation may be influenced by regional heterogeneity. For example, SO2 exposure may be associated with better economic resources, which could buffer its negative psychological impact, further diluting the significance of its direct pathway [45]. Meanwhile, the overall effect of O3 was not significant, likely due to the high sensitivity of the sample, suggesting that the effect of O3 is easily influenced by population heterogeneity, leading to instability in effect estimates [46].

The multistage methodological framework proposed in this study provides valuable insights. By integrating variable selection, ML modeling, SHAP-based interpretability analysis, and SEM-based causal validation, the framework shifts the focus from identifying correlations to inferring causal pathways. As an interpretability tool, SHAP was used to explore the relative importance of variables and showed a generally consistent pattern across models. This supports its use for preliminary variable screening and model refinement. The incorporation of the SEM-based approach further validated the causal pathways between variables and revealed mediating mechanisms, enhancing the understanding of complex psychological health issues. From a public policy perspective, the findings suggest that depression prevention and control should address both individual management and environmental interventions. Effective management of chronic pain, functional impairment, and sleep disorders is critical for strengthening the psychological resilience of older adults. Urban planning and environmental policies should consider mental health, especially the role of air pollution control in reducing mental health burdens. Primary prevention measures, such as air quality standards, seasonal warnings, and air filtration, must be prioritized. Support strategies for the elderly, including sleep improvement, pain management, and rehabilitation, are essential. For pollutants with well-defined causal pathways, region-specific, population-targeted interventions should be implemented to enhance living environments and health support systems for the elderly.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, depression is a dynamic psychological state, and there is a clear temporal lag between its onset and remission. The current analysis did not fully capture the temporal sequence among variables. Second, the ML algorithms showed modest predictive performance, limiting their applicability in high-precision clinical settings, though they remain suitable for large-scale population screening. Third, while the observed negative association between SO2 and depression risk is supported by adaptive response mechanisms in the literature, further empirical evidence from diverse cohorts is needed to validate its robustness and elucidate the underlying biological pathways. Finally, potential survival or selection bias may arise from excluding individuals lost to follow-up, which could affect the representativeness of the sample and bias the estimates toward healthier survivors.

Conclusion

This study developed a multi-stage analytical framework integrating ML and SEM-based approach to examine the pathways through which air pollution and individual factors may influence depression risk in older adults. ML was used to identify and prioritize key variables, followed by SEM-based approach as the primary tool for pathway inference and interpretation. The analysis indicated that pain, insomnia, and IADL were among the most significant individual factors associated with depression risk, underscoring the relevance of physical health and functional status in mental well-being. Regarding air pollution, pain was identified as a potential mediator of depression risk. The association with SO2 appeared to be largely driven by this pathway, with a relatively weak direct effect, while the association between O3 and depression risk was not statistically significant. Overall, these findings suggest that certain air pollutants may affect mental health predominantly through indirect pathways involving physical symptoms. The study employed a closed-loop methodology. It began with ML for variable screening and progressed to pathway validation using a SEM-based approach, allowing for a more structured assessment of the relationships between environmental factors, health, and psychological outcomes. This integrated framework may provide a useful reference for future research on complex mental health issues.
