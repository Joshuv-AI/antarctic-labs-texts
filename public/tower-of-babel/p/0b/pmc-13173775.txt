Burden, spectrum, and treatment gaps in cardiac diseases in Ethiopia: an 11-year analysis of 10,999 patients at a National Charity Cardiac Center
Authors: Mohammed Nasir, Sura Markos, Miklol Mengistu, Kefelegn Dejene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are major causes of premature mortality and disability, especially in settings where timely cardiac surgery or percutaneous interventions are limited. Globally, the prevalence of CVDs is rising, with significant regional differences. Africa bears a high burden of congenital heart disease (CHD) and rheumatic heart disease (RHD) but has limited access to surgical and interventional resources. This study describes the spectrum and management of cardiac diseases at the Cardiac Center of Ethiopia (CCE), highlighting treatment gaps.MethodsA hospital-based retrospective descriptive study was conducted from February 1 to June 1, 2023, reviewing records of 10,999 cardiac patients managed between 2012 and 2023. Variables included were demographics, type of heart disease, and management type (surgery or percutaneous intervention). Categorical data were summarized using frequencies and percentages, and continuous data using median and interquartile range (IQR).ResultsAmong 10,999 patients, the median age was 7.5 years (IQR 3–17.5), and 66.6% were younger than 18 years. CHD was the most common diagnosis (49.6%), followed by valvular heart disease (VHD) (35.4%) and hypertensive heart disease (HHD) (6.6%). Of 8,689 patients with guideline directed indications, only 2,900 (33.4%) received surgical or percutaneous intervention. Among patients with CHD, 950 (56.5%) underwent surgery and 730 (43.5%) received percutaneous treatment. The most common surgical procedures were ventricular septal defect (VSD) patch closure (26.2%), atrial septal defect (ASD) patch closure (19.4%), and patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) ligation (18.7%). The most frequent percutaneous procedures were PDA device closure (43.8%), ASD device closure (27.4%), and balloon pulmonary valvuloplasty (24.7%). Among patients with acquired heart disease, 23.5% underwent surgery and 76.5% received percutaneous intervention, most commonly percutaneous mitral commissurotomy (47.6%). Notably, 3.1% of patients with CHD progressed to an inoperable state, primarily due to delayed surgical or percutaneous intervention, necessitating exclusive medical management of their complications.ConclusionThe cardiac caseload was predominantly pediatric, with congenital heart disease as the most frequent diagnosis and rheumatic heart disease remaining a major acquired condition. Despite availability of surgery and percutaneous interventions, only one-third of patients received guideline directed care, and delays rendered some cases inoperable. These findings highlight the urgent need to expand access to timely cardiac interventions, implement early detection and screening programs, and strengthen prevention strategies for rheumatic heart disease in resource-limited settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07799-2.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) exemplify the ongoing epidemiological transition affecting both developed and developing nations [1–4]. In Africa, the CVD burden has risen by 50% over the past three decades [5], now accounting for 7–10% of hospital admissions [6]. However, cardiac disease patterns exhibit significant regional heterogeneity [7]. Notably, Africa has experienced a rising incidence of CHD from 1990 to 2017, contrasting with trends in high-income countries [8]. Concurrently, East Africa reports the world’s highest prevalence of rheumatic heart disease (RHD) (16.24 cases per 1,000 in 2020) [9].

CVDs substantially contribute to premature mortality and disability [10, 11]. Global CVD-related deaths surged from 12.1 million in 1990 to 18.6 million in 2019, while disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) doubled from 17.7 million to 34.4 million [10]. Despite this, an estimated 4.5 billion people lack access to life-saving cardiac surgery, with rates of 0.5 per million in developing countries and 1,300 per million in developed countries [12]. CHD affects 1 in 400 live births in resource-limited settings, while RHD requires 200–250 surgeries per million population to mitigate mortality and disability [13].

Resource constraints, including shortages of specialized personnel, equipment, and infrastructure, severely limit cardiac care in developing regions [14, 15]. Interventional cardiology services remain particularly scarce in Africa [16]. Competing health priorities, such as infectious diseases like Human Immunodeficiency Virus/Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (HIV/AIDS) and malaria, further divert attention from CVDs, exacerbating this dual disease burden [1, 17].

Despite the growing burden of cardiac disease in Sub Saharan Africa, data on treatment gaps, particularly access to surgery and catheter based interventions, remain scarce. This study aimed to characterize the spectrum of cardiac disease and quantify unmet treatment needs at Ethiopia’s national cardiac referral center over an 11-year period.

Methods and materials

Study area and period

This study was done at Cardiac Center of Ethiopia (CCE), a charitable tertiary cardiac facility in Addis Ababa, between February 1 and June 1, 2023. Since its establishment in 2012, the center has provided completely free cardiac care through donor funding, serving both pediatric and adult populations. The center operates a 10-bed inpatient ward, two fully equipped operating theaters, one catheterization laboratory, and a five-bed intensive care unit. Its outpatient department evaluates at least 50 patients daily while performing three cardiac surgeries and four interventional procedures weekly. During specialized cardiac mission periods occurring three to four times annually, the center significantly expands its capacity, delivering 50–70 combined interventions and surgeries through intensive 1–2-week campaigns. CCE’s clinical team comprises four cardiac surgeons, three pediatric cardiologists, two adult cardiologists, and twenty nursing staff. The facility maintains comprehensive diagnostic capabilities, including transthoracic/transesophageal echocardiography, electrocardiography, and pacemaker programming services.

Study design and population

This hospital-based retrospective observational cohort study with descriptive analysis was conducted at the CCE from February 1 to June 1, 2023. Medical records of 10,999 cardiac patients, including both pediatric and adult cases, who received inpatient or outpatient care at CCE between January 12, 2012, and January 12, 2023, were reviewed. The study population included all documented cases during this 11-year period, providing a comprehensive representation of the center’s cardiac patient population.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

All documented cardiac cases during the study period were included. Patients with incomplete medical records (missing diagnosis or treatment data) were excluded.

Diagnosis of cardiac disease

Experienced and certified pediatric and adult cardiologists diagnosed cardiac disease using transthoracic echocardiography and performed transesophageal echocardiography, CT scans, or coronary angiography when indicated. We did not assess inter-observer variability, which represents a study limitation.

Eligibility and operability assessment for cardiac management

Eligibility for cardiac surgery or intervention was assessed based on recommendations from international guidelines, including the Indian Guidelines, European Society of Cardiology Guidelines (ESC), and American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) Guidelines for various cardiac conditions. The term ‘guideline-directed recommendation’ refers to any situation in which at least one of these guidelines recommends the specific surgery or intervention. Operability was determined by cardiologists based on echocardiographic evidence of shunt reversal in patients with shunt lesions.

Study variables

The variables we used in this descriptive study were age at presentation, gender, place of residence, types of heart disease, and available management options (interventional procedure or surgery).

Definitions and operational definitions

Cardiac Disease:In this study, cardiac disease was defined as any condition, congenital or acquired, that impairs the structure or function of the heart. This includes congenital heart defects, as well as acquired conditions such as valvular heart disease, including rheumatic and degenerative, ischemic heart disease, hypertensive heart disease, cardiomyopathies, arrhythmias, and pericardial diseases [18–41].

Detailed operational definitions are provided in Supplementary 1.

Data collection and quality assurance

Data collection was conducted over five months, from February 1 to June 1, 2023. A structured questionnaire was used to systematically extract data from the medical records of patients who were either admitted to or had follow-up visits at the CCE. To ensure data validity and minimize potential bias, a comprehensive quality assurance protocol was implemented, including a two-day training program for all data collectors and supervisors. Before full-scale implementation, the data collection instrument was pretested on a 5% sample of cardiac patients at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital. Daily quality control procedures involved supervisors checking the completeness of collected data, followed by secondary validation performed by the principal investigator. Data abstraction was not blinded, as this was a descriptive study.

Data processing and analysis

All data entries underwent manual verification before cleaning in Epi Info 7 to ensure consistency. The validated dataset was subsequently analyzed using both Python 3.5 and Microsoft Excel 2013 for descriptive analyses. Age of the patient was described using median and interquartile range after a normality test was done using the Shapiro-Wilk test to determine the appropriate analytical methods. Results were presented using tables and graphical representations to facilitate a comprehensive interpretation of findings.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics and distribution of cardiac disease patterns among cardiac patients

Between January 12, 2012, and January 12, 2023, 11,120 patients were registered at the Cardiac Center of Ethiopia, either as inpatients or through outpatient follow-up clinics. Of these, 121 patients were excluded due to incomplete diagnosis or treatment data, resulting in a final cohort of 10,999 patients. The ages of the included patients ranged from 1 month to 92 years, with a median age of 7.5 years (IQR: 3–17.5 years). The age distribution was non-normal (Shapiro–Wilk test, p < 0.001). Patients under the age of 18 years constituted the majority, accounting for 7,322 cases (66.6%). Table 1; Fig. 1 summarize the sociodemographic characteristics and patterns of cardiac disease among the study population. The cohort was predominantly female, with 6,836 (62.2%) females, yielding a male-to-female ratio of 1:1.6. Rural residents comprised the majority of the study population, with 6,250 patients (56.8%) residing in rural areas. The most prevalent diagnosis was CHD, affecting 5,449 patients (49.5%), followed by VHD (3,892 patients; 35.4%) and HHD (723 patients; 6.6%).

Patterns of congenital and valvular heart disease

Among patients with CHD, the most common subtype was isolated VSD, occurring in 904 individuals (16.6%), followed by isolated PDA in 828 individuals (15.2%) (Table 2). Of the patients with VHD, 1,750 (43.7%) had multivalvular involvement, and 52.7% had severe valvular lesions. RHD was the leading cause of valvular pathology, identified in 2,350 patients (60.4%) (Fig. 2).

Management of cardiac diseases

During the study period, 1,680 procedures were performed in 950 patients with CHD, some undergoing multiple procedures, and 1,235 procedures were performed in 1,235 patients with acquired heart diseases, representing 33.4% of the total procedures required at the Cardiac Center of Ethiopia (Fig. 3).

Table 3 summarizes all types of surgery done for congenital and acquired heart diseases. Among patients with CHDs, surgical interventions were performed in 950 cases (56.5%), while percutaneous interventions were undertaken in 730 cases (43.5%). The most frequently performed surgical procedures were VSD patch closure (249; 26.2%), ASD patch closure (184; 19.4%), and PDA ligation (178; 18.7%). Among percutaneous interventions, the most common procedures included PDA device closure (320; 43.8%), ASD device closure (200; 27.4%), and BPV (180; 24.7%). Out of 5,449 patients with CHD, 170 (3.1%) were initially considered operable but, due to delayed surgical or percutaneous catheter interventions, progressed to an inoperable state according to guideline recommendations. The most frequent diagnoses among these patients were atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD, 45 cases), PDA (37 cases), VSD (36 cases), truncus arteriosus (23 cases), double outlet right ventricle (DORV, 18 cases), and dextro-transposition of the great arteries (Dextro-TGA, 11 cases). These patients remain under regular follow-up and receive medical management exclusively for the control of complications associated with their condition. Of those who underwent surgical or percutaneous interventions for acquired heart disease, 290 patients (23.5%) underwent surgery, and 945 patients (76.5%) had percutaneous procedures. Of those who underwent surgery, Valve surgery was performed in 270 patients (93.1%), while percutaneous mitral commissurotomy accounted for 450 of 945 percutaneous procedures (47.6%). All valve procedures were performed in patients with RHD.

Discussion

This hospital-based study described the spectrum and management of cardiac diseases among patients receiving follow-up care or admitted to the Cardiac Center of Ethiopia. Our findings identified CHD and RHD as the most prevalent conditions. Despite the high disease burden, only one-third of patients requiring guideline-directed surgical or percutaneous intervention received appropriate care.

These results align with reports from Tanzania, Uganda, and Cameroon, where CHD and RHDs predominated. However, contrasting data from Yemen and Somalia identified HHD as the most common acquired condition [42–47]. These regional variations likely reflect differences in sociodemographic profiles and risk factor distributions across populations.

Our findings demonstrate that VHD, particularly RHD, was the predominant cardiac condition among adults, consistent with prior reports from Malawi and Ethiopia [31, 48, 49]. Unlike CHD, RHD is entirely preventable, yet its disproportionate burden in this and other low-resource settings likely reflects systemic challenges: inadequate primary healthcare, poor living conditions, and overcrowding facilitating Group A Streptococcus transmission.

The persistent dominance of RHD as a leading cardiac etiology in our study population reveals fundamental failures in primordial prevention. While high-income countries have nearly eliminated RHD through systematic antibiotic prophylaxis and robust public health systems, our findings mirror the enduring burden observed across resource-limited settings. This disparity stems not from biological differences but from structural inequities: inadequate primary healthcare access, delayed streptococcal infection treatment, and suboptimal living conditions that facilitate disease transmission.

Consistent with the global epidemiological pattern [50–55], VSD emerged as the predominant CHD in our cohort. This finding contrasts with reports from Yemen and Somalia, where ASD demonstrates higher prevalence [32, 33], and with Jordanian data showing PDA as the most frequent CHD [56]. The observed geographical variations in CHD distribution may reflect differences in diagnostic capabilities, genetic predispositions, or environmental influences such as altitude-dependent ductal physiology. These disparities highlight the need for standardized, population-specific CHD surveillance systems to better understand the determinants of regional phenotypic variation.

Our hypertensive heart disease findings mirror patterns in Ethiopia, Yemen, and Cameroon [45, 57–60], underscoring hypertension’s growing burden in resource-limited settings. This trend correlates with increasing obesity, smoking, and alcohol consumption, revealing systemic gaps in preventive care. The data emphasize the urgent need for tailored interventions addressing both clinical management and socioeconomic determinants in vulnerable populations [60].

The findings of this study reveal significant gaps in cardiac care access within African healthcare systems, where only 33.4% of our patients received guideline-recommended interventions. This treatment disparity stems from systemic deficiencies, including chronic underfunding of health infrastructure, persistent medication shortages, and critical workforce limitations.

The 3.1% inoperability rate observed in our cohort aligns with Tanzanian data [5, 42], suggesting that delayed presentations and surgical backlogs in resource-constrained settings contribute substantially to poor outcomes. This highlights the pressing need for targeted interventions to strengthen cardiovascular infrastructure, improve medication availability, and optimize referral pathways. Addressing these challenges could mitigate treatment delays and improve outcomes for cardiac patients across similar resource-limited settings.

Limitations of the study

This study was hospital based and may not represent the general population, as referral patterns favored complex cases. The predominance of pediatric and congenital heart disease reflects the center’s specialized role rather than the national distribution of cardiac conditions. Inter-observer variability in diagnosis was not assessed, and operability was determined primarily using echocardiography without routine cardiac catheterization to measure pulmonary vascular resistance. Finally, retrospective data collection limited the ability to capture socioeconomic factors, comorbidities, and long-term outcomes.

Conclusion and recommendations

The cardiac caseload at Ethiopia’s national referral center was predominantly pediatric, with congenital heart disease as the most frequent diagnosis and rheumatic heart disease remaining a major acquired condition. Despite the availability of surgery and catheter based interventions, only one-third of patients received guideline directed care, and delays rendered some cases inoperable. These findings reveal critical gaps in access to timely cardiac treatment. Expansion of comprehensive cardiac services, including surgical and catheter based interventions, is urgently needed. Early detection and systematic screening programs should be implemented to identify patients before conditions become inoperable. Prevention strategies for rheumatic heart disease must be strengthened through public health interventions, and health system capacity, including workforce and infrastructure, should be enhanced. Addressing these gaps is essential to reduce morbidity and mortality from cardiac disease in resource-limited settings and to improve outcomes for children and adults with heart disease across Ethiopia and similar environments.
