Probabilistic carcinogenic and health risk assessment of heavy metals in lettuce and cabbage from Behbahan, Iran, using Monte Carlo simulation
Authors: Raham Armand, Lida Rafati, Hamed Mohammadi, Nezam Armand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough the recommendation to use vegetables in the diet and the public’s tendency to a healthy lifestyle has led to an increase in the use of lettuce and cabbage, pollutant such as heavy metals in them can lead to health risks for consumers. In this study, the concentration of heavy metals including lead, chromium, cadmium, and nickel in lettuce and cabbage in Behbahan, southern Iran, was investigated and the non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risk of exposure to them in consumers was assessed using Monte Carlo simulation. The results of the analysis of 10 separate samples showed that the average concentrations of lead, chromium, cadmium, and nickel in (lettuce, cabbage) were (0.134, 0.312), (0.306, 0.699), (0.0756, 0.0601), and (0.320, 0.633) mg/kg wet weight, respectively. The hazard quotient values ​​for all studied heavy metals in both cabbage and lettuce samples were less than 1. Also, the hazard index calculated for lettuce and cabbage was 4.58E-1 and 1.52E-1, respectively, which indicate no non-carcinogenic risk for consumers. However, the ELCR from consumption of lettuce and cabbage considering the total concentration of detected heavy metals was 4.14E-03 and 1.40E-03, respectively, indicating an unacceptable carcinogenic risk. Our study highlights the health risk from heavy metals in lettuce and cabbage in a developing region, which should be seriously considered by local policymakers to increase control measures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40958-w.Keywords: Heavy metals, Food contamination, Risk assessment, Lettuce, Cabbage, Monte Carlo SimulationSubject terms: Diseases, Environmental sciences, Risk factors

Introduction

Nutrition and diet are important factors that directly affect health1. The impact of food on health can be considered from two aspects, including the nutrients supply and possible contaminants2,3. Therefore, in addition to meeting the body’s needs, including essential nutrients, the diet should also be free of chemical or biological contaminants that lead to foodborne diseases4. Healthy nutrition to provide essential nutrients can be easily achieved by choosing a variety of foods and observing the proportion of consumption in a diet1, but controlling chemical and biological pollution requires planning and related policies that are not easily supported, especially in developing regions.

Vegetables are among the most important foods that are considered a major component of the diet in many food cultures5. Specifically, vegetables are a major source of dietary fiber, minerals, and many types of vitamins6. In addition, vegetables provide phytochemicals, including bioactive flavonoids, phenolic compounds, and peptides, which are of significant importance to the health of consumers7. Also, some studies have presented numerous therapeutic properties for some herbs in controlling diseases such as diabetes and modulate immune response, which highlights the importance of plants in nutrition8,9. In addition to the important role of vegetables in nutrition, lifestyle changes in recent decades that have led to an increase in consumers’ tendency towards a vegetarian diet have been effective in increasing vegetable consumption10. Therefore, the development of scientific evidence of the benefits of vegetables and the tendency resulting from cultural and social changes in recent decades have supported an increase in per capita vegetable consumption7,11. Also, the development of trade and globalization has facilitated citizens’ access to a variety of vegetables throughout the year, which is one of the reasons for the increase in vegetable consumption, especially in developing countries.

Although the nutritional benefits of vegetables, cultural support, and the recommendations of international organizations, including the World Health Organization, have led to an increase in the consumption of vegetables, the health risk caused by possible pollutants in vegetables is a concern, especially in developing areas12,13. Population growth in recent decades and the need for more agricultural products have led to the expansion of agricultural land14, which, with the increased use of pesticides and chemical fertilizers, can highlight the risk of increasing the concentration of various pollutants in food. Urbanization and industrial development have also increased the potential of pollutant transfer to the environment from sources such as industrial wastewater, solid waste, and air pollution15,16. Thus, a variety of nutritional, agricultural, and environmental factors have effectively increased the risk of exposure to food-borne contaminants. Heavy metals are one of the most important known pollutants in the environment that can enter the food chain and eventually cause various diseases including dyslipidemia and cardiovascular effects, neurological, musculoskeletal, liver and kidney disorders17–19. However, heavy metals in vegetables are classified into two main groups: essential metals and non-essential metals20. Essential metals such as iron and manganese are recognized as nutrients at low concentrations, but concentrations above health guidelines can lead to associated diseases21, while non-essential metals such as cadmium and chromium have health implications for the consumer even at low concentrations22.Therefore, in areas where agricultural lands have been affected by industrial development and urbanization, the concentration of non-essential metals in agricultural products should be considered as a health risk.

Khuzestan is one of the southern provinces in Iran that has experienced significant industrial development, including oil and petrochemical industries, in recent decades. In addition, the rapid population growth of this province, like other regions of Iran, in the last four decades has led to urbanization and an increase in pollutant sources, including solid waste23. Also, the presence of fertile land in Khuzestan and access to water resources make this region one of the main producers of agricultural products in Iran. Although numerous previous studies have considered pollutant sources and investigating the concentrations of pollutants resulting from them in the soil and water resources of Khuzestan24,25, the concentration of heavy metals in many agricultural products, especially vegetables, in Khuzestan and other regions affected by industrial development and urbanization is still a knowledge gap. This study is a response to this gap by evaluating the concentrations of heavy metals in lettuce, and cabbage, which are among the most consumed vegetables in Iran. This study was conducted in Behbahan, a city in Khuzestan Province, using Monte Carlo simulation to highlight the concentration of heavy metals in vegetables produced in agricultural lands affected by industrial development and urbanization and the health risks associated with their consumption.

Method

Study area

This study was conducted in Behbahan, Iran. As shown in Fig. 1, Behbahan is located at 30.59°N, 50.23°E, close to major oil and gas resources in the Middle East. The city has a population of 144,000 and is surrounded by fertile agricultural land. The presence of rivers with abundant water and a favorable climate supports the production of a variety of agricultural products, including vegetables, in this region. Agriculture in this region is based on traditional methods, and in recent years to increase agricultural production, the use of chemical fertilizers has increased. Also, the economic development of this region in recent decades has led to an increase in sources of heavy metal emissions, including industries.

Study area.

Sampling and analysis

In this study, ten vegetable stores (retail) were selected to collect vegetable samples based on the uniform geographical distribution of sampling locations in the studied city (S1-S10). Separate samples of lettuce and cabbage were prepared from each sampling location. Sampling was carried out two times with an interval of four weeks. Therefore, in this study, 20 separate samples of each of the vegetables studied were analyzed, which included a total of 40 samples. Finally, the concentrations of the studied heavy metals were reported based on the average concentration in 20 samples of each vegetable. Each sample prepared contained two kilograms of each of the studied vegetables, which were immediately transported to the laboratory26. The crushed or chopped parts were separated, then sample was washed and cleaned carefully using distilled water7. After washing, the samples were kept at room temperature for 24 h7. Then, 200 g of each sample was accurately weighed and after grinding and homogenization, dried at 105 °C for 24 h27. 500 mg of the dried samples were digested using 10 ml of HNO3 at 80 °C27,28. The digested samples were filtered and adjusted to a volume of 25 mL using deionized water26, and finally analyzed using ICP to detect the concentration of heavy metals including chromium (Cr), cadmium (Cd), nickel (Ni), and lead (Pb). The concentrations detected in dry weight were calculated as in wet weight based on the moisture content in the studied lettuce and cabbage26.

Risk assessment

As shown in Table 1, the health risk from consuming the studied vegetables with detected concentrations of heavy metals was assessed based on the calculation of the hazard quotient (HQ) and hazard index (HI)29. To assess health risk, the Estimated Daily Intake (EDI) was first calculated. To calculate the EDI, the parameters listed in Table 1 were used7. After calculating the EDI, the reference dose of each of the studied metals for each of the studied vegetables was used to calculate the HQ28. Finally, the sum of the HQs was calculated as the HI resulting from the consumption of each of the studied vegetables26. Also, the carcinogenic risk from the detected concentrations of heavy metals in the studied vegetables was assessed by calculating the Excess Lifetime Cancer Risk (ELCR)7. The ELCR was calculated by multiplying the Cancer Slope Factor (CSF) by the EDI30,31. If the calculated HI is less than or equal to 1, it indicates no significant non-carcinogenic risk associated with the studied contaminant28. Also, an ELCR less than 10− 6 indicates a negligible carcinogenic risk and an ELCR greater than 10− 4 indicates an unacceptable carcinogenic risk. If the calculated ELCR is in the range of 10− 6 to 10− 4, it indicates an acceptable carcinogenic risk7.

Results and discussion

The results of the analysis of studied heavy metals in cabbage and lettuce samples are shown in Fig. 2. The results showed that the total concentration of the total studied metals in cabbage was 2.09 times higher than the total concentration of the studied metals in lettuce. Also, the order of the concentration of the studied metals in both types of vegetables studied was chromium> nickel> lead> cadmium. The results showed that the concentration of lead in the studied vegetables was on average 3.28 times higher than cadmium. Although concentrations of the studied heavy metals were detected in lettuce and cabbage, these findings can be evaluated in comparison with the results of previous studies. The results of previous studies evaluating heavy metal concentrations in cabbage and lettuce in other cities in Iran are shown in Table 2. This comparison shows the spatial variations of heavy metal concentrations in vegetables studied in Iran. Also, Mi et al. 2019 reported the average concentrations of lead, chromium, and cadmium in 35 cabbage samples in China as 0.39, 0.16, and 0.61 mg/kg, respectively, indicating higher concentrations of chromium, lower concentrations of cadmium, and consistent concentration of lead compared to the detected concentrations in our work35. Fonge et al. 2021 reported a lead concentration of 1.36 mg/kg in studied cabbages in the savannah region, which was 4 times higher than the average concentration detected in our work36. In addition, Bassiri et al. 2025, reported the range of chromium and lead concentrations in lettuces obtained from different market regions of Lebanon as 0.24–3.66 and 0–0.42.42 mg/kg, respectively, indicating spatial variations in heavy metal concentrations in vegetables37. Also, a review of reports on heavy metal concentrations in lettuce from some African countries showed that the concentrations of cadmium, chromium, lead, and nickel were in the range of 0.03–0.25, 0.76–1.82, 0.3–1.24, and 16.81–17.7 mg/kg, respectively38.

Spatial variations in heavy metal concentrations in lettuce and cabbage in different cities of Iran and differences in pollution levels with concentrations reported in other countries could be due to differences in sources of heavy metal emissions and different levels of pollution. Sources of heavy metals in agricultural products include chemical fertilizers, soil pollution, and agricultural water pollution39. Agricultural development in recent decades in the studied city to meet the increased demand due to population growth has led to the growth of the use of pesticides and agricultural fertilizers in this region. Therefore, according to the available scientific evidence of the effect of chemical fertilizers on the increase of heavy metals40, one of the sources of pollution in cabbage and lettuce could be related to chemical fertilizers. However, the development of urbanization and industrialization of Khuzestan province in recent decades could also be one of the reasons for the detected concentrations in our work. Evidence of the potential for heavy metal leakage from municipal solid waste including cigarette butts, e-waste, and waste management by-products such as landfill leachate has been reported in previous studies41,42. The impact of heavy metal leakage from solid waste on increasing the concentration of these pollutants in soil and water resources43, considering the increase in solid waste production due to urbanization and population growth, is one of the main factors affecting the contamination of agricultural products.

Although the concentrations of heavy metals in lettuce and cabbage in the study area were different from some concentrations reported in previous studies, comparison of the detected concentrations with national and international standards can support appropriate interpretation. Some health guidelines recommended concentrations of lead, chromium, cadmium, and nickel in lettuce and cabbage as 0.3, 0.5, 0.2, and 2 mg/kg, respectively26. Therefore, as shown in Table 2, the average concentrations of lead and chromium in cabbage samples were higher than the standard, while the concentrations of cadmium and nickel in cabbage samples were lower than the health guidelines. However, all the metals studied in lettuce samples were lower than the guidelines. Therefore, the concentration of heavy metals, especially lead and chromium, in cabbage and lettuce samples can be a concern for public health and food security. Although food quantity and access to sufficient food support the concept of food security49, the removal of contaminated food from the food chain can lead to food loss and subsequent consequences due to increased food waste23. Therefore, ensuring acceptable risk from various contaminants, including heavy metals in food, in addition to meeting food security goals, can also prevent related environmental consequences.

The results of estimating the HQ and HI resulting from the concentrations of detected heavy metals in lettuce and cabbage samples based on the calculated EDI are shown in Table 3. These results, which were estimated by considering the average of the effective parameters, showed that the highest HQ was related to lead, while nickel resulted in the lowest calculated HQ. Considering the HI values ​​calculated for cabbage consumption (1.52E-01) and lettuce (4.58E-01) in the studied city was lower than 1, there is no non-carcinogenic risk. However, changes in per capita vegetable consumption and pollutant concentrations could lead to different results. For example, Khezerlou et al. 2021, evaluating the concentrations of heavy metals including lead, chromium, cadmium, nickel, zinc, arsenic, and copper in vegetables including cabbage, lettuce, carrots, tomatoes, and cucumbers in Tabriz, Iran, reported total target hazard quotient (TTHQ) values ​​for women and men in the range of 10.7–16.1, with the highest TTHQ associated with lettuce and cabbage in the range of 14.1–16.144. However, Rezapour et al. 2024, showed that wastewater irrigation increased cadmium concentrations in lettuce and cabbage grown in Urmia, northwestern Iran, by 68% and 220%, respectively, but the HQ was still less than one46. Jalil et al. 2022 also provided evidence that cabbage had a higher heavy metal uptake potential than other vegetables, but the heavy metal concentrations in the studied samples did not result in an HI higher than one50. Therefore, the non-carcinogenic risk from heavy metals in vegetables is affected by demographic characteristics, pollutant concentration, and per capita consumption, although some factors such as wastewater discharged into the environment and soil pollution can lead to increased concentrations of heavy metals in vegetables and increased associated risks.

Although the non-carcinogenic risk associated with heavy metals in cabbage and lettuce samples was not significant due to HI less than 1, the carcinogenic risk associated with the consumption of heavy metals in lettuce and cabbage was also assessed. The results of the assessment of the carcinogenic risk from the consumption of lettuce and cabbage in the study area are shown in Fig. 3. The results showed that the carcinogenic risk associated with lead, chromium, cadmium, and nickel was less than 4.56E-06, 9.08E-04, 1.51E-03, and 1.72E-03 for 95% of the probabilities in lettuce, respectively, and less than 2.05E-06, 3.78E-04, 3.67E-04, and 6.94E-04 for cabbage, respectively. However, as shown in the supplementary figures (SF1-SF8), the contribution of the studied metals to the carcinogenic risk was not the same. In recent years, concerns about the increasing concentration of pollutants such as heavy metals in the environment have led to the development of studies assessing the carcinogenic risk of exposure to contaminated food27–29. Comparing the situation assessed in our work with previous studies shows that in recent years, the carcinogenic risk has been reported in negligible and acceptable range in many Iranian cities. For example, Amarloei et al. 2024, by studying the concentrations of heavy metals including lead, chromium, arsenic, cadmium, and mercury in summer vegetables from Ilam province, Iran, calculated the highest contribution of carcinogenic risk to arsenic, although the concentration of cadmium in the vegetables studied was higher than that of other heavy metals. This study reported the carcinogenic risk due to heavy metal concentrations in vegetables in the unacceptable range, as the risk associated with arsenic was 0.819, which is much higher than 10− 4 34. Also, Abedi Sarvestani et al. 2024, evaluating the concentration of heavy metals in leafy vegetables in Kerman province, Iran, highlighted the carcinogenic risk associated with lead and cadmium concentrations in the studied vegetables in unacceptable range (10− 4−10− 6) and emphasized that the increased carcinogenic risk of vegetable consumption requires urgent control measures51.

Calculated ELCR resulting from to studied heavy metals.

Also, in this study, Margin of Exposure (MOE) for Pb in studied vegetables was calculated based on two parameters including Benchmark Dose Lower Confidence Limit (BMDL) and EDI. To calculate the MOE, MBDL value for cardiovascular effects in adults (1.5 µg/kg-BW/day), was divided by the calculated EDI. The MOE for cardiovascular effects in adults resulting from Pb concentration in cabbage and lettuce was 6.22 and 2.79, respectively, so interpreted as concern.

Based on the average parameters, the total ELCR in lettuce and cabbage was 4.14E-03 and 1.40E-03, respectively, indicating a higher carcinogenic risk for lettuce than for cabbage. Sensitivity analysis for Monte Carlo simulations showed that metal concentrations in vegetables and body weight were the parameters with the greatest impact on calculated risk. The reason for the higher carcinogenic risk could be due to the difference in heavy metal concentrations and higher per capita consumption of lettuce in Iran, because per capita consumption and pollutant concentrations are directly effective in EDI and the resulting carcinogenic risk26. Therefore, nutritional recommendations to increase vegetable consumption as an important source of nutrients7 can significantly increase the risk of carcinogenesis if heavy metal contamination exceeds national and international guidelines. This discrepancy in nutritional goals for contaminated agricultural products has also been highlighted in previous studies51. The results of our work highlighted the impact of heavy metal concentrations as one of the most common environmental pollutants on health risks resulting from food pollution. In recent decades, industrial development and urbanization in developing countries, including Iran, have led to an increase in the number of sources of environmental pollutants such as wastewater and solid waste. In addition, the widespread use of pesticides and chemical fertilizers to produce more agricultural products has led to an increase in the concentration of pollutants, including heavy metals, in agricultural soil and water. In this condition, the findings of our work indicate the impact of development on the pollution of agricultural products, which is a concern for the health of consumers.

Conclusion

The concentrations of heavy metals including lead, chromium, cadmium, and nickel in lettuce and cabbage as commonly consumed vegetables in the diet in Iran, as a developing country, were studied and the associated health risk for consumers was assessed. The results showed that the average concentrations of the total studied metals in lettuce and cabbage were 0.820 and 1.720 mg/kg wet weight, respectively, which compared with reports available in other cities in Iran, highlighting the spatial variations in the concentrations of heavy metals in vegetables. Differences in the sources of heavy metal emissions are the most important reason for the spatial variation in the concentrations of these pollutants in vegetables. Although only lead and chromium in cabbage samples were higher than the health guidelines, the estimated risk for lettuce was higher than that of cabbage due to its higher per capita consumption. The calculated hazard index due to exposure to the studied heavy metals in cabbage and lettuce was less than 1, indicating no non-carcinogenic risk to consumers. However, the carcinogenic risk (ELCR) higher than 10− 4 in the studied cabbage and lettuce indicated an unacceptable carcinogenic risk. Agricultural and industrial development in recent decades, along with the increase in urban population, has led to an increase in sources of heavy metal emissions and environmental pollution in developing countries including Iran, as our results showed, leading to an increase in the health risk of food consumers, especially vegetables. This study highlights the need to pay attention to the Sustainable Development Goals to reduce the transfer of pollutants to the environment and food. Controlling pollutant sources such as sewage and solid wastes with the aim of preventing heavy metal contamination of agricultural soil and water is recommended as the main measures by policymakers. Also, studying the health risks associated with the subsequent links of the food chain and processed foods with the studied vegetables is suggested for further studies.
