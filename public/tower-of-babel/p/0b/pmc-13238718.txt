A Preliminary Open‐Label Pilot Study of a Pillow‐Based ELF‐ELME (Extremely Low‐Frequency, Extremely Low Magnetic Environment) Intervention for Major Depressive Disorder
Authors: Ibuki Tomita, Masako Tachibana, Yoshihiro Nawa, Mikako Ito, Fumie Kinoshita, Yachiyo Kuwatsuka, Daisuke Mori, Masashi Ikeda, Kinji Ohno, Toshiya Inada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMitochondrial dysfunction has been implicated in the pathogenesis of depression. Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent condition with substantial individual and societal burden. A novel approach—exposure to an extremely low‐frequency, extremely low magnetic environment (ELF‐ELME)—has shown promise. This preliminary study builds on a previous trial using a head‐mounted ELF‐ELME device and explores the potential efficacy and safety of a pillow‐based ELF‐ELME device in patients with MDD.MethodsIn this preliminary open‐label pilot study, four patients with MDD were treated using a pillow‐based ELF‐ELME device (10 μTesla, 4 ms, 1–8 Hz/8 s) for 2 h daily over 8 weeks. The primary outcome was change in Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) scores from baseline to Week 8. Artificial Intelligence‐based MADRS (AI‐MADRS) scores were also assessed.ResultsTotal MADRS scores showed a unidirectional decrease from baseline (33.8 ± 7.3) to Week 8 (21.5 ± 8.7; p = 0.050, FDR = 0.067), and no time point reached statistical significance after FDR correction. In contrast, AI‐MADRS scores significantly decreased from baseline (34.0 ± 8.3) to Week 8 (19.8 ± 6.8; p = 0.040). No serious adverse events were reported.ConclusionThese preliminary findings suggest that an 8‐week course of pillow‐based ELF‐ELME intervention may have potential to alleviate depressive symptoms in patients with MDD. However, given the small sample size and open‐label design, the results should be interpreted with caution. Larger controlled studies are warranted to confirm these findings.Trial RegistrationjRCT number: jRCTs042240061; https://jrct.mhlw.go.jp/en‐latest‐detail/jRCTs042240061

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a common and debilitating psychiatric condition that imposes a significant burden on both individuals and society. Although various pharmacological and psychotherapeutic interventions are available, a substantial proportion of patients fail to achieve full remission or experience intolerable side effects. This underscores the need for alternative or adjunctive treatment strategies.

While repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) using high‐intensity magnetic fields has been employed for treatment‐resistant depression, its accessibility remains limited due to the need for specialized facilities [1]. Moreover, the standard rTMS protocol requires frequent sessions—typically five times a week for 4 to 6 weeks—posing a significant burden on patients. The cost of treatment can also be substantial. Although generally considered safe, rTMS may cause discomfort or pain at the stimulation site, headaches, facial muscle twitching, and in rare cases, seizures or mood switching. Long‐term safety data are still limited.

Extremely low‐frequency, extremely low magnetic environment (ELF‐ELME) intervention has emerged as a potential non‐invasive neuromodulation approach for major depressive disorder (MDD). In our previous studies, ELF‐ELME was shown to induce hormetic activation of mitochondria in mouse AML12 cells [2], human HEK293 cells [3], and human cybrid cells derived from a patient with neuropathy, ataxia, and retinitis pigmentosa (NARP) syndrome [4]. It also ameliorated depressive‐like behavior in a mouse model by enhancing mitochondrial energy production [5]. Clinically, our earlier open‐label trial using a head‐mounted ELF‐ELME device demonstrated a significant reduction in Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) scores in patients with MDD [6].

To improve user convenience and potentially enhance adherence, we developed a pillow‐based ELF‐ELME delivery system. The present open‐label trial aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of this novel device in patients with MDD, using the same sample size (N = 4) as in the previous head‐mounted device study. We hypothesized that the pillow‐based system would also result in a significant reduction in depressive symptoms. In addition, there is growing interest in non‐invasive neuromodulation approaches for depression [7, 8]. These approaches are increasingly being explored in relation to underlying biological mechanisms, including mitochondrial function, although clinical evidence remains limited [9].

Participants and Methods

Ethical Considerations

This study was initiated following approval by the Certified Review Board of Nagoya University, accredited by the Tokai‐Hokuriku Regional Bureau of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, Japan (Certification No. CRB4180004). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Clinical Trials Act. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants

The study participants were psychiatric outpatients from Nagoya University Hospital, aged 18–75 years, diagnosed with MDD according to the DSM‐5 criteria, and had been receiving a stable dose of antidepressant treatment for at least 4 weeks. Eligibility also required a total Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) score of 22 or higher at baseline. Patients with psychotic disorders, substance abuse or dependence, serious physical illness, epilepsy, organic brain disease, or significant suicidal ideation were excluded. They had not participated in any other clinical trials or received electroconvulsive therapy within the past 16 weeks.

Study Design

This was an 8‐week, open‐label, single‐arm trial. Each participant used the pillow‐based ELF‐ELME device (10 μTesla, 4 ms, 1–8 Hz/8 s) for 2 h daily over the 8‐week period (a timer was used to turn off the device after 2 h). The stimulation parameters were previously optimized to induce maximum mitophagy‐mediated enhancement of mitochondrial electron transport chain activities in cultured mammalian cells and in mouse liver [2]. We also confirmed that this stimulation ameliorated a mouse model of depression induced by chronic social defeat stress [5]. The pillow‐based ELF‐ELME device was custom‐built by Ourstex Corp (Osaka, Japan). A schematic of the pillow‐based ELF‐ELME device is shown in Figure 1B, and a heatmap illustrating its electromagnetic field intensities is shown in Figure 1D. Safety and efficacy were evaluated at baseline and at Weeks 2, 4, 6, and 8. The primary outcome was evaluated by trained psychiatrists using the Japanese version of the MADRS [10]. The Artificial Intelligence‐based MADRS (AI‐MADRS) was assessed as a secondary outcome at baseline and Week 8. The AI‐MADRS consists of nine structured interviews based on modified versions of MADRS items 2–10, allowing severity estimation via a machine learning model [11]. It was developed using structured clinical interview data and has shown a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.76 with clinician‐rated MADRS scores in our previous validation studies [11]. In the present study, AI‐MADRS was included as a secondary outcome to provide an objective and technology‐assisted assessment of depressive symptoms.

Outcome Measures

The primary efficacy outcome was the change in total MADRS score from baseline (Week 0) to Week 8. The MADRS was administered at Weeks 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8. The AI‐MADRS was also administered at baseline and Week 8. Safety and tolerability were monitored at each time point through clinical interviews.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size was based on estimates of patient eligibility and consent rate. Approximately 50 eligible patients were expected within 1 year with a projected consent rate of 10%; therefore, the sample size was set at four. All values are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Paired Student's t‐tests were used to compare MADRS and AI‐MADRS total scores at each time point relative to baseline (Week 0). For MADRS total scores, false discovery rates (FDRs) were calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to adjust for multiple comparisons across time points (https://tools.carbocation.com/FDR). A p‐value or FDR less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. There is no clear consensus as to whether parametric or non‐parametric tests are more appropriate for small sample sizes such as those used in this study, and both approaches have been reported to yield comparable results under certain conditions. The Shapiro–Wilk test often lacks the power to detect non‐normality in small datasets, frequently resulting in false negatives; thus, the application of normality tests in this context may be inappropriate [12]. In addition, as both parametric and non‐parametric tests have been shown to yield similar results for small sample sizes [12], and since our data were theoretically expected to follow a normal distribution, the use of parametric tests was suggested and approved by our Certified Review Board. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.3.1 and SPSS version 29.0.2.0. As this was a preliminary exploratory pilot study, the sample size was determined based on feasibility considerations rather than formal statistical power calculations. This limitation is acknowledged when interpreting the findings.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Figure 2 presents the flow diagram of study participants. A total of four Japanese patients were enrolled between August 2024 and February 2025. All participants were diagnosed with moderate major depressive disorder; three had a single episode and one had recurrent episodes (DSM‐5: 296.32). The cohort consisted of two males and two females, with a mean age of 42.0 years (standard deviation: 25.1; range: 18–72 years). The mean baseline MADRS score was 33.8 ± 7.3, consistent with the inclusion criteria for moderate depression. The mean body mass index (BMI) was 22.5 kg/m2 (range: 19.2–24.9). Information on participants' occupational background, as a proxy for educational and social functioning, was as follows: two were students, one was an office worker, and one was a housewife. Regarding medication, none of the participants were receiving antipsychotic drugs (chlorpromazine‐equivalent dose: 0 mg/day) or mood stabilizers or regular benzodiazepine treatment. All participants were on stable SSRI treatment prior to enrollment and continued treatment throughout the study. The mean daily imipramine‐equivalent dose was 112.5 mg/day (range: 75–150 mg/day) [13].

MADRS Scores

The MADRS score at baseline (Week 0) was 33.8 ± 7.3 (mean and standard deviation). Scores progressively decreased across the study period: Week 2 (31.0 ± 6.5, p = 0.334, FDR = 0.334), Week 4 (26.5 ± 4.4, p = 0.045, FDR = 0.067), Week 6 (23.0 ± 2.2, p = 0.032, FDR = 0.067), and Week 8 (21.5 ± 8.7, p = 0.050, FDR = 0.067) (Figure 3A and Table 1). Although MADRS scores showed a monophasic downward trend over the 8‐week period, none of the FDRs were below 0.05.

AI‐MADRS Scores

The AI‐MADRS score at baseline was 34.0 ± 8.3, which significantly decreased to 19.8 ± 6.8 at Week 8 (p = 0.040), yielding the reduction of AI‐MADRS scores by 14.3 ± 8.2 (Figure 3B and Table 1).

Safety and Tolerability

No serious adverse events were reported during the study, indicating that the pillow‐based ELF‐ELME device was well‐tolerated by all participants.

Comparison With the Head‐Mounted Model

Figure 3 shows the time course of (A) MADRS and (B) AI‐MADRS scores for both the head‐mounted model (previous study: Clinical Registration ID: jRCTs042220011) [6] and the pillow‐based model (this study). In the previous study, the head‐mounted model showed a reduction in MADRS scores from 27.0 ± 2.2 at baseline to 8.8 ± 8.3 at Week 8 (p = 0.021, FDR = 0.048) (Figure 3A). Statistical significance was also observed at Weeks 4 and 6. AI‐MADRS scores also decreased from 32.8 ± 2.9 to 12.5 ± 10.1 (p = 0.034) (Figure 3B). Although the degrees of reductions of MADRS and AI‐MADRS scores were more with the head‐mounted model compared to the pillow‐based model, there was no statistically significant difference in the degrees of reductions between the head‐mounted and pillow‐based models.

Discussion

This open‐label trial investigated the efficacy and safety of a novel pillow‐based ELF‐ELME device for the treatment of MDD in four patients. The primary outcome demonstrated a clear trend toward reduction in clinician‐rated MADRS scores; however, it did not remain statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR), underscoring the exploratory nature of these findings. Regarding the secondary outcome, a statistically significant reduction in AI‐MADRS scores was observed after 8 weeks of treatment. Therefore, the observed reductions in MADRS scores should be interpreted cautiously, and no definitive conclusions regarding efficacy can be drawn from the present study. While the present results suggest that the pillow‐based ELF‐ELME system may have potential antidepressant effects, they should be viewed as preliminary and hypothesis‐generating.

The findings of this study are consistent with our previous investigation using a head‐mounted ELF‐ELME device, which demonstrated significant reductions in both MADRS and AI‐MADRS scores in the same sized cohort [6]. While statistical significance was observed only in AI‐MADRS scores in the pillow‐based model, the degrees of reductions of MADRS and AI‐MADRS scores were not statistically different between the head‐mounted and pillow‐based models.

One possible explanation for the lack of statistical significance in the MADRS scores in the pillow‐based model is the consistency of the electromagnetic field. Although the pillow‐based model offers increased convenience, it may be susceptible to variability in effective dose delivery due to head movements during sleep. The orientation and distance between the user's head and the electromagnetic source embedded in the pillow‐based device can fluctuate, possibly altering the field strength reaching targeted brain regions. Additionally, the user's own pillow height and material may influence the electromagnetic field efficacy. In contrast, the head‐mounted device provides more stable and consistent delivery of the electromagnetic field. Despite these variables, the pillow‐based system still achieved clinically relevant symptom reduction. Its convenience and non‐intrusiveness may promote better long‐term adherence compared to wearable devices. Lack of serious adverse events in the currently reported pillow‐based model, as well as in the previously reported head‐mounted model [6], suggested that ELF‐ELME may be a safe intervention. However, these factors were not quantitatively measured in the present study and should be systematically evaluated in future research.

In a mouse model of depression by chronic social defeat stress, ELF‐ELME treatment for 6 weeks increased mitochondrial electron transport chain complex proteins and mitochondrial oxygen consumption rates in the prefrontal cortex [5]. In addition, ELF‐ELME activated antioxidative stress response and suppressed lipid peroxidation in the prefrontal cortex. Estimation of the mitochondrial energy production in the human brain is experimentally available in vivo by near‐infrared spectroscopy [14] or 31P‐magnetic resonance spectroscopy [15]. In future studies, these techniques will help identify whether ELF‐ELME ameliorates MDD by enhancing mitochondrial energy production as we observed in a mouse model of depression [5].

This study has several limitations. The open‐label design is subject to placebo effects and observer bias, and symptom improvement may have resulted from spontaneous partial remission or delayed effects of ongoing pharmacotherapy. Adverse events were assessed through clinical interviews rather than standardized rating scales, which may have limited the sensitivity and systematic detection of side effects. Additionally, the small sample size (N = 4) limits generalizability and reduces statistical power to detect smaller effects or between‐group differences. The 8‐week duration may be insufficient to evaluate the long‐term efficacy or sustainability of the antidepressant effects. Moreover, comparison with the head‐mounted model was indirect, based on historical data, and should be interpreted cautiously. Given that this was a non‐randomized open‐label exploratory study with a small sample size, the findings should be considered preliminary.

Future research should involve larger, randomized controlled trials to establish the efficacy of the pillow‐based ELF‐ELME device more definitively. Such studies could also investigate optimal stimulation parameters, the impact of head movement and pillow characteristics, and direct comparisons with other antidepressant treatments and the head‐mounted ELF‐ELME device.

In conclusion, this preliminary open‐label pilot study suggests that an 8‐week course of treatment using a pillow‐based ELF‐ELME device may have potential to reduce depressive symptoms in patients with MDD. However, given the small sample size and exploratory design, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The pillow‐based ELF‐ELME system may offer a convenient and low‐burden neuromodulation approach, although its clinical applicability remains to be established. Further large‐scale, randomized controlled studies are required to confirm efficacy and determine its role in clinical practice.
