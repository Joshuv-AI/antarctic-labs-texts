Long-Term Use of Cochlear Implants in the Older Adult Population
Authors: Maya Hatley, Emily R. Spitzer, Susan B. Waltzman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveHearing loss affects 60–70% of elderly people, with ∼10% of these potentially eligible for cochlear implantation (CI). While outcomes in older adults have been positive, concerns remain regarding long-term auditory performance due to higher incidence of comorbidities, reduced cortical plasticity, and cognitive decline. This study evaluates 10-year speech perception outcomes and potential mediating factors in adults ≥60 years old at implantation.MethodsA retrospective chart review identified 98 patients implanted between 2006 and 2014 who were ≥60 years old at surgery with ≥10 years of follow-up. The primary outcome was unilateral (CI-only) Consonant-Nucleus-Consonant (CNC) word scores in quiet at 10 years, also measured preoperatively and at one and 5 years postoperatively.ResultsMean age at implantation was 70.2 years (standard deviation [SD]: 6.4, range: 60.1–85.3), and mean CI experience was 12.6 years (SD: 2.5, range: 10.0–20.1). CNC scores improved significantly from preoperative levels at one (d: 54.2%, q = 31.70, p < 0.001), five (d: 56.5%, q = 31.8, p < 0.001), and 10 years (d: 60.0%, q = 34.67, p < 0.001), without significant differences among postoperative time points. CNC scores did not differ significantly between patients implanted ≥70 versus <70 years old (F(1, 96) = 1.1, p = 0.306). Greater average daily CI use at most recent appointment correlated with higher CNC scores at all postoperative intervals (r = 0.435–0.595, p < 0.001). Inability to live independently was associated with significantly lower scores at 5 years (d = 15.2, p = 0.003).ConclusionSpeech perception outcomes were stable in most elderly CI users 10 years post-implantation. Performance was not associated with age at implantation but was positively related to consistent device use and independent living. Limitations include some missing data, especially 10 years postoperatively, and inability to assess the impact of cognitive function on outcomes.

Introduction

It has been well established that hearing loss is a significant problem in the older adult population. The recent World Report on Hearing showed that the global incidence of hearing loss rises exponentially with age [1]. Unaddressed age-related hearing loss is the 3rd greatest cause of years lived with disability globally and the greatest cause for adults over 70 years of age [2]. Not only is hearing loss highly prevalent, untreated hearing loss in the older adult population may also lead to social withdrawal, decreased quality of life, and lost productivity resulting from early retirement [1]. Studies have demonstrated relationships between hearing loss and all-cause mortality, with the strength of this relationship increasing with increased severity of hearing loss [3].

Cochlear implantation (CI) is a safe and effective treatment for individuals with moderate to profound sensorineural hearing loss who receive limited benefit from hearing aids [4]. Importantly, previous studies have shown CIs to be effective in improving auditory perception, speech recognition, and quality of life in older adults [5–8]. The short-term speech perception outcomes of older adults after CI have also been shown in many studies to be similar to or slightly lower than those of younger adults [9–13]. Though cochlear implants have been shown to provide substantial benefit to users, there is significant variation in performance of CI users at all ages, with some studies suggesting variability in outcomes increases with age [12, 14–16].

Of particular interest are the variability and stability of long-term outcomes in older adult CI users, specifically device usage. In a retrospective cohort study by Choi et al. [17] rates of regular CI use (≥8 h/day) in adults implanted over the age of 60 were shown to exceed 80% at a mean of 13.5 years postoperatively. However, adults implanted under the age of 75 were shown to have significantly higher rates of regular use compared to those implanted over the age of 75.

Another concern regarding auditory outcomes in elderly CI users is the potential for their performance to decline over time due to age-related factors such as increased medical comorbidities, decreased cortical plasticity, or cognitive decline [16]. In a large multicenter study by Lazard et al. [18] nearly a quarter of adults implanted over the age of 67 were found to have declines in speech perception between 6 months and 2 years postoperatively. However, smaller studies by Dillon et al. [19] (n = 14) and Garcia-Iza et al. [20] (n = 39) both found word recognition scores to remain stable or improved between all postoperative time points up to 10 years postoperatively in adults implanted over the age of 60 [19, 20]. Dillon et al. [19] also found the same to be true for Hearing in Noise Test sentence scores in the same small cohort of older adult CI users. Additionally, Issing et al. [21] found gains made in hearing-related quality of life to be stable from 6 months to 6 years postoperatively in a group of adults implanted over the age of 70. The objective of this study was to evaluate long-term speech perception performance and the stability and potential mediating factors of this performance in a large cohort of CI users ≥60 years old at the time of implantation. This study will investigate the long-term outcomes of a large group of older adult CI users by comparing speech perception scores at ten years postoperatively to those at one and five years postoperatively as well as comparing scores at all time points to preoperative scores.

Materials and Methods

Demographics

On approval by our institutional review board (IRB #i23-01017), a single-center retrospective chart review was completed at a tertiary medical center with a dedicated CI center. Adults implanted for the first time from 2006 to 2014, greater than 60 years old at the time of implantation, and with speech perception scores for at least one time point pre- and postoperatively, and ten or more years of CI listening experience after initial CI implantation were included in this study. Each patient was individually evaluated for CI candidacy and medically cleared for surgery prior to implantation. Ninety-eight patients meeting inclusion criteria had relevant data collected from their charts.

Speech Perception Testing Procedures

Word recognition was measured using recorded Consonant-Nucleus-Consonant (CNC) word lists in quiet [22]. All unilateral speech perception testing was performed with a hearing aid preoperatively and with the CI during evaluations at one, five, and 10 years postoperatively. If a patient had residual hearing in the contralateral ear, it was plugged and muffed during unilateral speech testing. Speech perception measures were administered in a calibrated sound field at 60 dB A.

Variables of Interest

Demographic characteristics collected included age at the time of implantation, sex, duration of deafness, and the presence of medical comorbidities both preoperatively and at the most recent follow-up appointment. Preoperative speech testing scores and pure tone averages (PTAs) in the ear to be implanted, obtained by averaging aided thresholds at 500 Hz, 1,000 Hz, and 2000 Hz, were also recorded. Other information collected at the most recent appointment included residential circumstances, cognitive functioning, age, daily usage of CI, and employment status. When recording a patient’s residential circumstances, patients were classified as able or unable to live independently. Those who were classified as unable to live independently were those documented to require full-time home health care, live with a family member who must assist in daily functioning, or otherwise noted to require significant assistance from one or more caregivers in activities of daily living [23]. Average daily CI usage in hours was objectively measured and recorded using the data logging function of the CI sound processor.

The primary outcome measure was unilateral CNC word scores on speech discrimination testing at 10 years post-implantation, including evaluations conducted between eight and 12 years after implantation. In the case of patients who received a second CI within 10 years of the first CI, data from the first implanted ear were always used for unilateral speech perception testing. In simultaneously implanted patients, data from the poorer performing ear on preoperative testing were included in our analyses in order to align with the practice of implanting the poorer ear in unilateral CI.

Speech perception scores were also collected preoperatively and at 1 year (including evaluations conducted between 9 months and 1.5 years post-CI) and 5 years (including evaluations conducted between four and six years) postoperatively. These ranges of time since implantation were selected to accommodate real-world variability in clinical follow-up, while still allowing meaningful comparison across postoperative time points by maintaining a narrow window relative to elapsed time since implantation. These time frames, including that of the 10-year postoperative timepoint, are consistent with the commonly accepted distinctions between early, mid-term, and long-term outcomes in CI [24–26].

Changes in speech perception scores between postoperative time points were analyzed, with 12.8 percentage points being used as a critical difference. This threshold was determined by Hersbach et al. [27] to be the 95% critical difference threshold for two CNC lists of 50 words each, as done in this study. Speech perception scores were not collected or analyzed at the most recent appointment due to the variability in length of CI usage and inconsistency in available data at the most recent appointment, limiting the utility of this data for comparison amongst the cohort. Therefore, data collected at the most recent appointment was limited to demographic characteristics and data logging information.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical data were analyzed with percentages, and continuous data were analyzed using the mean, standard deviation, and range. The relationships between primary outcomes and demographic data, including age at implantation, duration of CI experience, and most recent daily usage data were analyzed using correlation matrices. Speech perception scores from multiple time points postoperatively were compared using one-way mixed effect ANOVA testing with post hoc comparisons using Tukey’s test. Speech perception scores from patient sub-groups, including factors such as age, residential circumstances, cognitive status, and medical comorbidities, were compared to one another using two-way mixed effect ANOVA testing with appropriate post hoc comparisons. Mixed effect models were used rather than repeated measures due to some missing data points at each time postoperatively.

Results

Study Population Characteristics

All patients implanted in the years 2006–2014 were screened for inclusion in this retrospective chart review. Of these patients, 98 patients met inclusion criteria of implantation at or greater than 60 years old and at least 10 years of CI listening experience after implantation (first implantation for bilaterally implanted patients). The mean age of these included patients at implantation was 70.2 years (standard deviation [SD]: 6.4 years, range: [60.1, 85.3]). Twelve of these subjects underwent revision, with a mean time to revision of 4.3 years (SD: 3.8, range: 1.2–13.1 years). The most common reasons for revision included device failure (n = 4), significant declines in speech perception (n = 2), and medical complications (n = 2). See Table 1 for further details.

Due to variation in patient follow-up and timing of annual evaluations performed, evaluations from 9 months to 1.5 years were included in the 1 year postoperative time point, evaluations from four to six years were included in the 5 year postoperative time point, and evaluations from eight to 12 years were included in the 10-year postoperative time point. Unilateral speech perception scores were available for 98 patients preoperatively, 81 patients at 1 year, 72 patients at 5 years, and 55 patients at 10 years postoperatively. All patients had at least 10 years of clinical follow-up in accordance with the inclusion criteria; however, speech perception data were unavailable for some patients within the predefined assessment windows. At the 10-year time point, missing data were due to patients not attending audiology follow-up at this time point (n = 3) or not completing formal speech perception testing within this interval despite continuing to attend audiology appointments (n = 40). At these appointments, other needs were addressed, including equipment troubleshooting, speech processor upgrades, programming adjustments, hearing aid checks for bimodal users, and informal speech perception testing.

Prior to implantation, unaided acoustic hearing was tested using insert earphones or circumaural headphones. The mean PTA of thresholds at 500 Hz, 1,000 Hz, and 2000 Hz in the ear to be implanted was 80.8 dB HL (median: 85 dB HL, range: 25.5–115 dB HL).

Improvements in Unilateral Speech Perception Scores

The mean aided preoperative unilateral CNC score was 7.2% (median: 2%, range: 0–48%), and the mean aided preoperative bilateral CNC score was 29.0% (median: 20%, range: 0–96%). Unilateral CNC scores were collected at 1 year (range: 0.8–1.4 years), 5 years (range: 4.0–5.9 years), and 10 years (range: 8.1–12.0 years) post-implantation. The mean unilateral CNC word scores at 1, 5, and 10 years postoperatively were 60.5% (median: 64%, range: 8–100%), 64.3% (median: 66%, range: 0–98%), and 66.1% (median: 68%, range: 18–98%), respectively. On one-way mixed effect ANOVA testing, the effect of time on unilateral speech recognition scores was found to be statistically significant (F (2.7, 185.2) = 374.0, p < 0.001). In post hoc comparisons with Tukey’s test, unilateral CNC scores were found to be significantly improved from preoperative scores at one (mean difference: 54.2%, q = 31.7, p < 0.001), five (mean difference: 56.5%, q = 31.8, p < 0.001), and 10 years (mean difference 60.0%, q = 34.67, p < 0.001) (shown in Fig. 2). Post hoc comparisons also revealed that there were no significant differences in unilateral CNC scores among any of the postoperative time points.

Mean unilateral CNC scores preoperatively and at 1, 5, and 10 years postoperatively.

Of the 43 patients with missing data at the 10-year postoperative time point, 40 patients did not complete formal speech perception evaluations during this time despite continuing to attend follow-up appointments. Reasons for this included other issues (such as equipment troubleshooting or upgrades) taking up most of the time in the appointments (n = 5), patients attending most appointments virtually or at another institution (n = 4), or patient refusal (n = 2) during this time. However, most often, a reason was not given, and patients were simply noted to be “overdue” for annual evaluations as they continued to attend other appointments (n = 29).

Fourteen of the patients with missing data underwent formal speech perception testing more than 12 years post-implantation (median: 15.2 years, range: 12.1–20.1 years), with an average unilateral CNC word score of 61.14% (median: 64%, range: 18–88%) at their most recent evaluation. The mean difference in CNC scores from 5 years post-implantation to over 12 years post-implantation was +0.2% (median: +6%, range: −24% to +18%). Two patients exhibited declines in CNC scores greater than 10% during this time frame, with score decreases since 5 years post-implantation of 24% and 16% at 14.6 and 18.4 years post-implantation respectively. The other 29 patients with missing data 10 years post-implantation did not have any documented speech perception testing performed at our institution thereafter.

Age and Long-Term Outcomes

Speech perception scores were further analyzed as a function of age at implantation. Analysis with correlation matrices did not show a significant relationship between age at implantation and CNC scores at one (r = 0.133, p = 0.237), five (r = 0.018, p = 0.882), or 10 years (r = −0.060, p = 0.668) postoperatively. Likewise, no significant correlation was found between age at implantation and the change in CNC scores from preoperative scores to 1 year post-implantation (r = 0.071, p = 0.531), 1 year to 5 years post-implantation (r = −0.247, p = 0.053), 5 to 10 years post-implantation (r = 0.067, p = 0.660), or 1 year to 10 years post-implantation (r = −0.261, p = 0.080).

Additionally, on two-way mixed-effects ANOVA testing examining the effect of both time point and age on speech perception outcomes, patients over the age of 70 at implantation were not shown to have significantly different CNC scores at any time point pre- or postoperatively from those of patients implanted younger than 70 years old (F (1, 96) = 1.1, p = 0.306). The age cutoff of 70 years old was chosen given the mean age in this cohort of 70.2 years old and the median age of 68.9 years. Additional Mediating Factors in Long-Term Outcomes

Demographic and audiologic data were collected from the most recent appointment for all patients. The relationship between the ability to live independently and speech perception scores was analyzed, with unilateral CNC words scores at each time point for both groups shown in Figure 4a. On analysis with two-way mixed-effects ANOVA, both timepoint (F (3, 183) = 220.9, p < 0.001) and the ability to live independently (F (1, 85) = 8.4, p = 0.005) were found to have a statistically significant effect on unilateral speech perception scores. On post hoc comparisons with Šídák’s multiple comparisons test, unilateral CNC scores were found to be significantly lower in individuals not living independently at the most recent appointment at 5 years (mean difference: 15.2%, p = 0.003) postoperatively. A significant difference was not observed between these groups preoperatively (mean difference: 1.8%, p = 0.987), at 1 year postoperatively (mean difference: 8.6%, p = 0.228), or at 10 years postoperatively (mean difference: 11.6%, p = 0.155).

Mean unilateral CNC scores preoperatively and at 1, 5, and 10 years postoperatively for those able to live independently at the most recent follow-up appointment compared to those unable to live independently at the most recent follow-up appointment.

At the most recent appointment, patients had an average length of CI usage of 12.6 years (SD: 2.5 years, range: 10.0–20.1 years) and had an average daily CI usage of 11.5 h (SD: 4.2 h, range: 0.1–19.0 h). The majority of patients (83.5%) in this study with available data were found to be using their CI for 8 h or more per day at the most recent follow-up appointment. On analysis with correlation matrices, shown in Figure 5, daily CI usage at the most recent appointment was shown to be significantly correlated with unilateral speech perception scores at one (r = 0.453, p < 0.001), 5 (r = 0.474, p < 0.001), and 10 years (r = 0.595, p < 0.001) postoperatively.

Effects of Comorbidities on Speech Perception

Patients presented with a variety of comorbidities at the most recent follow-up appointment. The number of comorbidities at the most recent appointment was not shown to be correlated with either the unilateral CNC scores at any time point or the change in unilateral CNC scores across any of the time points. Additionally, comorbidities found in 10 or more patients were analyzed with a two-way mixed effects ANOVA to determine if the presence of any of these comorbidities had a significant effect on speech perception outcomes. No one comorbidity was found to have a significant effect on speech outcomes at any time point postoperatively, with the most common comorbidities in this study population being hypertension (n = 39; p = 0.938), hyperlipidemia (n = 28; p = 0.070), and cancer (n = 24; p = 0.1804).

Discussion

The results of this study show that individuals implanted over the age of 60 demonstrated clinically and statistically significant improvements in unilateral speech perception scores following CI, between 50 and 60 percentage points, similar or superior to prior literature on short-term outcomes in older adults [10, 13, 20]. Notably, the majority of individuals maintained these significant gains, with no significant mean differences between 1 and 5 years postoperatively (d = −3.45%), 5 and 10 years postoperatively (d = 1.45%), or 1 and 10 years postoperatively (d = −2.04%). As noted previously, CI frequently results in variable speech perception outcomes, as shown in Figures 1 and 2.

Given this variability, we found it important to understand the scores and changes in scores for each individual subject. We found that just 12 patients of the 98 total patients in this study exhibited a clinically significant decline (>12.8 percentage points, as determined by Hersbach et al.) in speech perception performance on CNC word testing between consecutive postoperative time points, a relatively small fraction of the overall study population [27].

Though many have cited concerns over long-term decline of speech perception as CI users age, several studies have failed to find this outcome in older adults [19–21]. Larger studies such as the one by Lazard et al. [18] involving 699 adults implanted over the age of 60, focused only on short-term outcomes, but did find that almost a quarter of older adults exhibited speech perception decline within 2 years compared to just 11% of younger adults. Notably, the literature is lacking particularly in data on long-term speech perception in noise and of sentences in this older adult population. We were unable to evaluate long-term changes in speech perception of sentences in noise in this study due to a change in which sentence-in-noise tests were used at our institution during the study period (i.e., from Hearing in Noise Test (HINT) sentences to AZBio sentences) [28, 29].

Though this variability in outcomes after CI is well documented, the causes are much less well understood. This subset of patients who experienced decline was similar in age to the overall population (mean: 70.8 years old, median: 71.4 years old) and did not have a longer duration of deafness prior to CI (mean: 10.75 years, median: 12 years), or a greater incidence of revisions (n = 4). When looking at clinical notes from this subset of patients, some potential reasons for declines in speech perception postulated by providers included low daily usage and failure to attend follow-up appointments for multiple years in the setting of COVID, family issues, and health problems.

One proposed factor in differential outcomes, especially in the older adult population, is age at implantation [30]. However, our results show that age greater than 70 years old at the time of implantation did not result in significantly different speech perception outcomes compared to those implanted at younger than 70 years old, nor did age at implantation significantly correlate with unilateral CNC scores or changes in unilateral CNC word scores at any time. Therefore, age at implantation was not found to explain variability in speech perception outcomes seen in this group of older adult CI users.

Additional factors thought to influence speech perception outcomes are cognitive and functional status at the most recent postoperative appointment [31–33]. We were unable to assess the potential impact of cognitive decline on these outcomes because cognitive decline is not evaluated in a routine or standard fashion at follow-up audiology appointments at our institution. Though clinicians did document concern for cognitive decline for some patients (n = 13), these reports were highly variable in source and severity. Therefore, we did not include this data in our analysis.

In our analysis of residential circumstances, patients were classified as able or unable to live independently at the most recent follow-up, as described above. We found that individuals who were able to live independently had significantly higher speech perception scores than those unable to live independently at 5 years postoperatively (p = 0.003), though not at one or 10 years postoperatively.

Interestingly, no significant differences were identified between these groups at 10 years postoperatively, despite the most recent follow-up being closer to this time point. This could be explained by the potential positive impact on auditory rehabilitation of not being able to live independently, due to more frequent socialization by necessity. Studies by Tang et al. [33] and Cassazza et al. [34] have shown that living with others or engaging in certain social activities may be associated with improved speech perception outcomes [33, 34].

Of note, the group of 22 patients categorized as unable to live independently was moderately heterogenous in both the degree of assistance needed and the reasons for the assistance needed. Some individuals primarily had challenges with mobility requiring full- or part-time assistance in the home, while others required assistance due to new onset disabilities including blindness, and still others were very ill with complex medical conditions. The strength of these conclusions is somewhat limited by this heterogeneity but also represents an important consideration in this older adult patient population. Of note, no one comorbidity in this cohort was associated with differential speech perception outcomes, nor was the number of comorbidities present at their most recent appointment correlated with speech perception outcomes. Therefore, the findings in this analysis are unlikely to be explained by medical comorbidities alone.

In general, the patients served by our cochlear implant center are in good health. This could be partly explained by the fact that patients with more severe cognitive impairment, functional limitations, or illnesses may be less likely to return to our center for follow-up once implanted, due to difficulties with mobility, reliance on caregivers for transportation, and competing medical appointments [35]. Additionally, there were many older adult patients implanted after the age of 60 years old at our institution who passed away or were lost to follow up prior to reaching 10 years of cochlear implant experience, another factor potentially skewing the studied cohort to be healthier than the average older adult CI user.

In this study, one factor found to be associated with improved performance following CI in this population was daily CI usage. Greater daily usage of the CI at the most recent appointment was associated with higher speech perception scores at 1, 5, and 10 years postoperatively. This is in agreement with prior studies also showing an association between increased cochlear implant use and improved speech perception performance [36, 37]. It is also possible that individuals who experience greater early benefit from their implants are more likely to return for follow-up, while those who perceive limited benefit may be less likely to do so – especially ten or more years postoperatively.

As this study included only individuals with 10 years of documented listening experience, we may have inadvertently excluded poorer performers who were lost to follow-up prior to this time. However, the patients included in this study did range widely in their average hours of daily CI usage, with nearly 20% of patients wearing their CI for less than 8 h a day at 10 years postoperatively and the most recent follow-up appointment. Previous studies have shown a positive association between average CI daily use and speech perception performance [36, 38, 39]. However, a study by Schvartz-Leyzac et al. [38] showed that older adults (>80 years old) had significantly lower average daily CI use compared to younger adults, with other studies replicating this finding as well [17, 38]. Proposed reasons for low CI usage among older adults include less time spent on average in social situations, medical or cognitive comorbidities making CI usage challenging, or lack of awareness of the importance of consistent CI use [26, 36, 38, 40]. With the evidence that average daily CI use is associated with improved outcomes, patient education and encouragement on increasing CI use, especially for those with low daily CI usage is crucial. Fortunately, several studies have shown that patient education can improve average daily CI usage for many patients [36].

As with any single-center retrospective study, inherent limitations in this study design limit the strength of these conclusions despite the large sample size described. Especially in analyses of cognitive decline, medical comorbidities, and the ability to live independently, we were limited by the fact that many patients at our center receive other medical care elsewhere, limiting our ability to collect data on these characteristics from the electronic medical record. Additionally, these characteristics were noted based on clinical documentation in most cases, which may be subject to variability in provider practices, rather than standardized instruments, which are not routinely used at audiologic follow-up appointments. Future studies should prospectively investigate the impact of medical co-morbidities, cognitive decline, and residential circumstances on outcomes of CI in this older adult patient population using standardized evaluations for these factors to improve both internal and external validity. These studies should also seek to understand the impact of both the severity of cognitive impairment and the length of listening experience with cognitive impairment on speech recognition outcomes.

There were also missing data for some patients at each time point, potentially introducing bias. Most notably at 10 years postoperatively, formal speech perception data were missing for many patients despite continued follow-up (n = 43). Based on clinical documentation of appointments during this time frame, the lack of formal speech testing was often due to time limitations of appointments and audiologists prioritizing addressing any equipment issues, adjusting CI programming, and conducting subjective, informal speech perception evaluations. It is possible that patients with missing data were different than the overall study sample.

Finally, in order to maintain comparability between individuals included in this study, only the first implanted ear was analyzed, even in bilateral and bimodal cases. Therefore, the speech perception outcomes of these individuals likely underestimated the real-world listening experience and outcomes of bilaterally implanted or bimodal patients.

Conclusions

In patients who were implanted at ≥ 60 years old who had 10 or more years of CI experience, speech recognition scores were shown to be stable up to 10 years postoperatively, with relatively few individuals who demonstrated decline in speech perception scores between 1 and 10 years postoperatively. Age at implantation was not shown to have a significant effect on speech perception scores. Providers and older adult candidates for CI should note that performance generally did not decline over time in this large population of older adult cochlear implant users. However, factors associated with better long-term speech perception may include increased CI usage and the ability to live independently. When interpreting these findings, it is crucial to note that only individuals with 10 years of cochlear implant use who continued to attend follow-up appointments at this institution were included, which may have resulted in a healthier and higher performing study cohort as compared to the broader population of older adult CI users. Additionally, cognitive decline, medical comorbidities, and functional status were not assessed using standardized instruments, limiting our ability to fully evaluate the impact of these factors on long-term speech perception outcomes. Future, larger studies should seek to understand the relationship between outcomes of CI and cognitive decline, an especially important clinical factor in the older adult population.
