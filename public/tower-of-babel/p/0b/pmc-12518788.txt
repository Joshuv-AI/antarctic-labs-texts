miR398‐Dirigent Module Negatively Regulates Cassava Resistance to Cassava Common Mosaic Virus by Affecting the Cell Wall Lignification and Thickness
Authors: Abdoulaye Assane Hamidou, Jiming Song, Yuhua Chen, Liyun Yang, Linling Zheng, Xin Chen, Yinhua Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStudies have demonstrated microRNA398 (miR398) hyperaccumulation in plant species infected with viruses; however, its role in a viral context remains unexplored. Here, we identified cassava (Mes)‐miR398 as a regulatory miRNA responsive to cassava common mosaic virus (CsCMV) infection through small‐RNA analysis. Mes‐miR398 targets Dirigent (Dir; Manes.05G165700.1) associated with disease resistance. Transgenic cassava lines overexpressing Mes‐miR398 (OE398) showed downregulation of the Dir gene during CsCMV infection. A dual‐luciferase reporter assay confirmed the direct targeting of the Dir gene by miR398. Interestingly, OE398 lines exhibited enhanced vegetative growth. However, CsCMV accumulation was significantly higher in OE398‐CsCMV and Dir‐silenced (SDir)‐CsCMV lines and reduced in Dir‐overexpressing (OEDir‐CsCMV) lines, indicating a negative regulatory role of the Mes‐miR398/Dir module in antiviral defence. Transmission electron microscopy revealed a reduction in cell wall thickness in CsCMV‐infected plants compared with healthy controls. A similar decrease was observed in SDir lines, whereas OEDir lines maintained greater wall thickness. Furthermore, lignin content was significantly reduced in SDir lines relative to OEDir lines. Histochemical staining with Toluidine Blue O and Safranin O−Fast Green supported these findings, showing increased lignification in OEDir lines and reduced lignification in SDir lines. Subcellular localisation demonstrated that the Dir protein localises to the cell membrane, suggesting a role in regulating the cell wall membrane. These results revealed that the mes‐miR398/Dir regulatory module negatively affects cassava resistance to CsCMV by altering cell wall lignification. This study is the first to identify miR398 as a regulator of a Dir gene and provides critical insights into the molecular interactions between cassava and CsCMV.Keywords: cassava, cassava common mosaic virus, cell wall thickness, disease resistance‐responsive (Dirigent) gene, lignification, mes‐miR398

Introduction

Cassava (2n = 36, Manihot esculenta) is a staple food crop in tropical and subtropical regions, cultivated as a subsistence crop. Cassava has been in high demand since 2000, and production has consistently increased yearly, but potential yields are limited by susceptibility to viral diseases (Legg et al. 2015). The main viruses compromising cassava production include cassava common mosaic virus (CsCMV) in Asia and the Americas (Costa and Kitajima 1972; Silva et al. 2011). Recent studies have reported the re‐emergence of CsCMV (Tuo et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2020), and its potential threat requires further investigation. First identified in cassava in Brazil by Silberschmid in 1938 (Colariccio et al. 2021), CsCMV can cause yield losses of 30%–60% (Calvert et al. 1996; Costa and Kitajima 1972). CsCMV infection significantly impacts chloroplast function and plant metabolism (Zanini et al. 2024). In addition, CsCMV‐infected plants experience oxidative stress, diminished antioxidant capacity, reduced starch and maltose levels and an increased sucrose‐to‐starch ratio (Abdoulaye et al. 2025).

CsCMV, a potexvirus of the family Alphaflexiviridae (Kreuze et al. 2020), contains a positive‐sense (+) single‐stranded (ss)RNA genome of 5.5–9 kb with flexuous filamentous virions 470–800 nm long and 12–13 nm in diameter (Kreuze et al. 2020). The CsCMV genome has a 5′‐methyl guanosine cap and a 3′‐polyadenylated tail flanking five open reading frames (ORFs) (Kreuze et al. 2020). ORF1 encodes the viral replicase with methyltransferase, helicase and RNA‐directed RNA polymerase (RdRp) domains, followed by the triple‐gene block (TGB) protein, and is found in all plant‐infecting family members. The TGB proteins are movement proteins (MPs) important for virus cell‐to‐cell and long‐distance movement and necessary to invade the host plant and cause disease successfully (Lucas 2006; Waigmann et al. 2004; Stripe 2008). The potexvirus TGB1 protein contains an NTPase/helicase domain involved in ATP hydrolysis, RNA binding, homologous interactions and cell‐to‐cell movement (Kadaré and Haenni 1997). In addition, the TGB1 protein contributes to the variability in potexvirus RNA silencing suppression (Voinnet et al. 2016). For instance, the TGB1 protein encoded by Plantago asiatica mosaic virus (PlAMV), which has a strong suppressor activity, affects the accumulation of microRNA (miRNA), virus genomic RNA and virus‐derived small interfering RNAs (Senshu et al. 2009). TGB2 and TGB3 proteins of some potexviruses have been linked to the formation of perinuclear granular structures and peripheral bodies (Ju et al. 2005; Schepetilnikov et al. 2005). The coat protein (CP), required for virion assembly and cell‐to‐cell movement, is encoded by ORF5 (Cruz et al. 1998). CsCMV CP targets abscisic acid insensitive 3 (ABI3)/viviparous 1 (VP1) (MeRAV1) and MeRAV2 transcription factors to subvert cassava immunity, enabling systemic infection (Wei et al. 2023).

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are a class of short, single‐stranded RNA molecules that do not code for proteins, consisting of 21–24 nucleotides (Reinhart et al. 2002). miRNAs, discovered in 1993, are key plant growth and development regulators and play a crucial role in plant stress response by regulating gene expression through directing messenger RNA (mRNA) cleavage and/or inhibiting translation (Sunkar et al. 2012; Sunkar et al. 2007). Plant miRNAs can exert either a positive or negative regulatory effect on viral infection (Mengistu and Tenkegna 2021). For instance, miR528 exerts a negative regulatory effect on viral resistance in rice by cleaving the mRNA of L‐ascorbate oxidase (Wu et al. 2017; Yao et al. 2019). miR444 enhances rice resistance against rice stripe virus infection (Wang et al. 2016).

First reported in Arabidopsis thaliana (Sunkar and Zhu 2004), miR398 is a conserved miRNA in plants (Li et al. 2022) that responds to abiotic and biotic stresses, including viral infections (Beauclair et al. 2010; Sunkar et al. 2006; Tagami et al. 2007; Dugas and Bartel 2008; Jagadeeswaran et al. 2009; Jia et al. 2009; Li et al. 2010; Lu et al. 2010; Wu et al. 2011; Liang et al. 2012; Liu et al. 2020). It plays a key role in modulating the activity of superoxide dismutases (SOD) and participates in reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging (Abdel‐Ghany and Pilon 2008). For example, miR398 alleviates the oxidative damage caused by phenanthrene in the roots of wheat plants (Li et al. 2020). It has also been implicated in metal stress tolerance in various plant species, including Arabidopsis (Sunkar et al. 2006), alfalfa (Gu et al. 2017), castor bean (Çelik and Akdaş 2019) and grapevine (Leng et al. 2017) when exposed to copper, nickel and cadmium‐induced metal stress.

Notably, miR398 expression differs between plants and stress conditions (Li et al. 2022). In wheat, drought stress stimulates the expression of miR398 (Kantar et al. 2011), whereas in rice, it suppresses the expression of miR398 (Lu et al. 2010). In potato plants infected with potato spindle tuber viroid and genetically modified to have reduced levels of Dicer‐like2/4 protein, systemic necrosis occurs, associated with increased miR398 expression and an overproduction of ROS (Suzuki et al. 2019). In Nicotiana benthamiana, increased miR398 level and decreased expression of its target protein umecyanin triggers plant defence mechanisms to limit the spread of beet necrosis yellow vein virus (Liu et al. 2020). Similarly, in bamboo, miR398‐regulated antioxidants have a role in bamboo mosaic virus accumulation (Lin et al. 2022). Conversely, in rice, miR398 can regulate the functioning of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), therefore modulating ROS levels and enhancing resistance to blast disease (Li et al. 2019). In response to nitrogen stress, miR398‐3p was upregulated in both the roots and shoots. Conversely, miR398‐5p was upregulated in the roots but downregulated in the shoots (Javed et al. 2019).

Lignin, a complex heteropolymer of guaiacyl (G), syringyl (S) and hydroxyphenyl (H) monolignols, is polymerised in the apoplast through free radical‐mediated coupling (Lewis and Yamamoto 1990; Delmer et al. 2024). Alterations of lignin can negatively impact plant development (Muro‐Villanueva et al. 2019). Lignans, structurally related to lignin, serve key roles in plant defence and exhibit antioxidant activity (Ramsay et al. 2017). Their stereoselective biosynthesis is guided by Dirigent (Dir) proteins (Dalisay et al. 2015; Paniagua et al. 2017), which control radical coupling specificity and are localised to the cell wall (Delmer et al. 2024; Davin et al. 1997). Dir proteins are widely conserved across plant species and linked to disease resistance (Paniagua et al. 2017). Emerging evidence suggests that Dir proteins also contribute to lignification processes, such as forming the Casparian strip lignin‐based diffusion barrier in plant roots (Delmer et al. 2024). In maize, mutants deficient in specific Dir proteins exhibit impaired lignin deposition in the Casparian strip, leading to reduced salt tolerance (Wang, Cao, et al. 2022; Wang, Viljamaa, et al. 2022).

Despite multiple studies that have demonstrated the hyperaccumulation of miR398 in plant species infected with viruses (Tagami et al. 2007; Bazzini et al. 2011; Naqvi et al. 2010; Hu et al. 2011; Pacheco et al. 2012; Suzuki et al. 2019), the specific functions of miR398 in viral contexts remain unclear. In this study, we identified mes‐miR398 as a key regulator of cassava under CsCMV infection through miRNA prediction and reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) analysis. For the first time, we identified the Dir gene as a key target of miR398 under CsCMV infection using target prediction. A dual‐luciferase reporter assay confirmed the direct targeting of the Dir gene by miR398. Virus‐induced gene silencing (VIGS) and virus‐mediated protein overexpression (VOX) approaches, virulence assays, subcellular localisation, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of cell wall thickness, Toluidine Blue O and Safranin O − Fast Green staining, and lignin content showed that the miR398‐Dir module affects cell wall lignification and negatively regulates cassava resistance under CsCMV infection. These findings provide a role of miR398 in viral defence and offer strategies for engineering CsCMV‐resistant cassava.

Results

Virus Screening Revealed Abundant Viral Contigs Predominantly Related to CsCMV

Samples collected from Danzhou (Figure S1), Hainan, China, were surveyed for viral disease detection using high‐throughput sequencing (Illumina HiSeqTM2000/MiSeqTM), resulting in raw data (15,187,043 reads, 0.759 G bases, 0.01% error rate, 99.33% Q20, 97.20% Q30 and 50.45% GC content). A total of 15,187,043 (100%) reads were generated. After filtering out low‐quality reads, 14,921,043 (98.25%) clean reads remained (Table S1). The error rate of nucleotide sequences is generally < 0.025%, as shown in Figure S2. The clean reads screened for small RNAs (18–26 nt) resulted in 8,816,977 reads (Figure S3, Table S2). The latter reads were mapped to the Manihot esculenta genome and GenBank virus RefSeq, resulting in 6,951,329 (78.84%) and 313,610 (3.56%) reads, respectively (Table S3). Of the small RNAs mapped to the host genome, 41.5% were 24 nucleotides (nt) long (Figure S4), while 23.34% of those mapped to the virus RefSeq were 21 nt (Figure S5). This data can help scientists set appropriate nucleotide intervals for filtering small RNA data. The databases annotated a total of 174 contigs (Figure 1A). The viral screening identified most viral contig candidates related to CsCMV isolates, including the previously identified CsCMV isolate Hainan‐DZ (GenBank accession number: MN243731.1; Table S4) (Tuo et al. 2020), in Hainan, China. Therefore, the CsCMV was investigated in this study.

Mes‐miR398 Is a Potential Regulator Under CsCMV Infection

The clean reads screened for miRNAs (18–30 nt) resulted in 11,092,171 reads (Figure S6), with most reads being between 21 nt (13.31%) and 24 nt (12.62%), consistent with miRNAs being 21–24 nt (Reinhart et al. 2002). However, notable reads of 18–28 nt were also detected, suggesting a slightly extended interval may be valuable for identifying novel miRNAs relevant to plant–virus resistance. 8,603,360 (77.56%) reads were mapped to the M. esculenta genome (Figure S7), with a detailed summary of small RNA alignment and annotation in Table S5. The miRNA annotation percentage (0.99%) was lower than expected but comparable to similar data (manuscript in preparation), potentially indicating lower miRNA expression in these samples or cassava under virus infection. Sixty‐five known and 27 novel mature miRNAs were predicted (Tables S6 and S7). Differential expression analysis showed miR398 (Figure 1B) was highly upregulated in the Danzhou (DZH) sample and downregulated in the CsCMV‐free sample (Control; CK), along with miR408, miR172c, miR159a‐5p and novel_1 (Figure 1C). However, the expression level of miR398 was relatively higher than that of miR408, miR172c and miR159a‐5p (Figure 1C). Therefore, Mes‐miR398 was selected for further investigations. The fold change (FC) of TPM values confirmed miR398's differential and high expression in the DZH sample (Figure 1D), suggesting its potential importance in virus replication or cassava resistance to CsCMV infection. Based on these results, miR398 is a potential regulator for CsCMV infection.

Mes‐miR398 Regulates the Dir Gene in Response to CsCMV Infection

Target gene analysis was conducted for known and novel miRNAs to predict their potential regulatory targets. Results revealed that Mes‐miR398 targets Manes.05G165700.1 (Disease resistance‐responsive [Dir‐like protein]) with the lowest confidence score of 4.0 (Table S8), Mes‐miR172c targets Manes.04G016200 (Unknown function) with a score of 2.5 (Table S9) and Mes‐miR408 targets Manes.18G085600.1 (Basic blue protein) with a score of 3.5. Predicted target genes for Mes‐miR398, Mes‐miR408 and Mes‐miR172c are summarised in Tables [Link], [Link]. To validate miRNA‐target interactions, RT‐qPCR was performed to assess the relative expression of Mes‐miR398 and its target Dir gene in cassava cultivars SC9 (susceptible) and UG4 (resistant), under CsCMV‐infected (SC9‐CsCMV, UG4‐CsCMV) and control (SC9‐CK, UG4‐CK) conditions. Notably, we previously assessed the pathogenicity of CsCMV in UG4 and SC9, confirming that SC9 and UG4 are susceptible and resistant cultivars, respectively (manuscript in preparation and Figure 1E). In SC9‐CsCMV samples, Mes‐miR398 expression was significantly upregulated at 4 days post‐inoculation (dpi) compared to SC9‐CK, while Dir gene expression was significantly downregulated (Figure 1F). A similar expression pattern was observed in UG4‐CsCMV versus UG4‐CK (Figure 1G). The CsCMV coat protein gene (CP) was significantly upregulated in SC9 (Figure 1E), while it was downregulated in UG4 (Figure 1E), despite both cultivars displaying miR398 induction (Figure 1F–G). Therefore, increased miR398 alone does not drive higher viral load in UG4 (Figure 1E–G). Furthermore, a dual‐luciferase reporter assay in Arabidopsis protoplasts, cotransfected with Mes‐miR398 and the Dir gene, showed a significant reduction in luciferase activity, confirming that the Dir gene is a direct target of Mes‐miR398 (Figure 1H and Table S11). These results support the conclusion that Mes‐miR398 negatively regulates Dir expression during CsCMV infection in susceptible cultivar SC9.

The Overexpression of Mes‐miR398 (OE398) Increased CsCMV Viral Load and Increased Cassava Stem Growth

OE398 transgenic lines were developed to investigate the function of the miR398 family in cassava under CsCMV infection. The cassava cultivar SC9 was agro‐inoculated with VOX398 to generate OE398 lines, and three independent lines were successfully developed and confirmed by PCR (Figure 2A). No obvious symptoms were observed in these OE398 plants (Figure 2A). The expression of mature Mes‐miR398 was evaluated using RT‐qPCR, confirming overexpression in OE398 lines 1 and 2 (Figure 2B). However, the expression levels were lower than those observed in SC9‐CsCMV plants (Figure 1F), suggesting that CsCMV infection more effectively induces Mes‐miR398 expression than the current overexpression construct. OE398‐1 exhibited the highest Mes‐miR398 expression among the transgenic lines and was therefore selected for subsequent analyses. Interestingly, Dir gene expression was upregulated in OE398‐1 under CsCMV‐free conditions (Figure 2C) but downregulated in OE398‐1 under CsCMV infection (Figure 2D), indicating a potential regulatory interaction. This result suggests that cassava may modulate Dir gene expression in response to CsCMV infection, or that CsCMV may suppress Dir gene expression via Mes‐miR398‐mediated regulation to facilitate infection. To explore the potential direct interaction between CsCMV proteins (RdRp, CP, TGB1‐2‐3) and Dir, protein–protein interaction modelling was performed using AlphaFold3. The results showed no likely interaction between CsCMV and Dir proteins (Figure S8), consistent with the current understanding that miRNAs typically do not directly target viral proteins. Phenotypically, OE398 lines demonstrated enhanced growth compared to empty vector controls (NC2) (Figure 2E), suggesting additional roles of Mes‐miR398 in growth regulation. Furthermore, mature Mes‐miR398 expression was significantly upregulated in OE398‐CsCMV compared to OE398 control (OE398‐CK) plants (Figure 2F). The viral load was also quantified by measuring CP gene expression via probe‐based Taq qPCR, which showed increased CP levels in OE398‐CsCMV samples (Figure 2G), indicating enhanced CsCMV viral load in Mes‐miR398‐overexpressing plants compared to OE398‐CK. All experiments were independently repeated at least twice to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the findings.

Dir Proteins (A0A2C9VWW3) Contain the Conserved Motifs of the DIR Family and Are Clustered in the ‘Dir‐a’ Subfamily

Multiple sequence alignment was performed to identify the conserved domain motifs of the Dir (A0A2C9VWW3) protein. The DIR family contains five identified domain motifs in spruce (Picea spp.): I (LchYhHD), II (FGslsVhDDslT‐G), III (SshVGRAQGhY), IV (ThVFpsGcasGSTLslhG) and motif V (REhuVVGGTGcFphARGAph+T) (Ralph et al. 2007). The analysis confirmed that Dir (A0A2C9VWW3) contains characteristic DIR family motifs, although it displays distinct residue variations compared to other members of the family (Figure 3A). As previously reported, the overall sequence identity between DIR subfamilies is low, highlighting their evolutionary divergence (Ralph et al. 2006). When aligned with cassava Dir (A0A2C9VWW3) proteins, the protein of interest also exhibited conserved Dir motifs but with notable residue differences (Figure S9), supporting its annotation as a Dir‐like protein. To further investigate its evolutionary relationships, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using Clustal Omega for alignment and iTOL for visualisation (Figure 3B). The resulting tree identified the presence of the previous six distinct subfamilies (Dir‐a, Dir‐b/d, Dirc, Dir‐e, Dir‐f and Dir‐g) within the extended DIR and DIR‐like family (Figure 3B) (Ralph et al. 2006). The Dir (A0A2C9VWW3) protein clustered with members of the Dir‐a subfamily (Figure 3B), consistent with earlier classifications. Dir‐a is subdivided into two distinct groups: Dir‐a1 and Dir‐a2 (Meng et al. 2020), a pattern also observed in the current phylogeny (Figure 3B). For example, the Dir‐a1 subfamily includes proteins such as DRR206 (Figure 3B) involved in (+)‐pinoresinol‐forming Dir protein from pea ( Pisum sativum ) (Seneviratne et al. 2015; Kim et al. 2015), while Dir‐a2 includes members like AtDir6 (Figure 3B) involved in a (−)‐pinoresinol‐forming Dir protein from Arabidopsis thaliana (Kim et al. 2012; Pickel et al. 2010). The Dir‐g subfamily was also resolved into three distinct clades, Dir‐g1, Dir‐g2 and Dir‐g3, indicating further substructuring within this group (Figure 3B).

Efficient Silencing of The Dir Gene Increases CsCMV Infection

The Dir gene was silenced using the virus‐induced gene silencing (VIGS) approach to study the function of the Dir‐like protein family in cassava under CsCMV stress. The cassava cultivars SC9 and UG4 were agro‐inoculated with ViDir to generate the Dir‐silenced lines. Three Dir‐silenced lines were generated, and the silencing efficiency of the Dir gene was confirmed through RT‐qPCR (Figure 3C). The experimental group containing the ViDir recombinant plasmid showed no obvious changes in cassava leaves, and Dir expression was downregulated in the generated Dir‐silenced lines compared with the control (NC1), indicating efficient silencing of the Dir gene. Subsequently, the viral load was investigated to determine the function of the Dir gene in cassava under CsCMV stress. Silenced cassava plants were infected with CsCMV, and at 4 dpi, fresh leaves were subjected to RNA extraction and probe‐based Taq qPCR analysis. The relative expression showed that CP was overexpressed in OE398‐CsCMV lines (Figure 3D). Overall, silencing the Dir gene in cassava increases CsCMV infection. We speculated that the Dir gene could induce cassava resistance in response to CsCMV.

The Overexpression of the Dir Gene (OEDir) Reduces CsCMV Infection

Based on our previous hypothesis, the Dir gene was overexpressed to evaluate its role in conferring cassava resistance to CsCMV infection. Cassava cultivars SC9 and UG4 were agro‐inoculated with VOXDir to generate OEDir lines, resulting in three transgenic lines (Figure 3E). The expression levels of the Dir gene in these lines were confirmed by RT‐qPCR, showing successful overexpression in OEDir‐1, OEDir‐2, and OEDir‐3 (Figure 3E). Among them, SC9‐2 and UG4‐3 exhibited the highest Dir gene expression levels and were selected for further analysis. To assess the antiviral effect of the Dir gene, viral load in these lines was quantified using probe‐based Taq qPCR targeting the CP gene. Results showed significant downregulation of CP in both SC9‐2 and UG4‐3 lines (Figure 3F), indicating reduced CsCMV viral load. This result contrasts with previous observations where CsCMV infection was increased in Dir gene‐silenced lines (Figure 3D), reinforcing the conclusion that the Dir gene reduces CsCMV accumulation. These findings suggest that the Dir gene functions as a resistance‐related protein, contributing to cassava defence against CsCMV. Furthermore, the expression of the Dir gene remained elevated in OEDir lines (SC9‐2 and UG4‐3) following CsCMV infection, further supporting its active involvement in resistance mechanisms. The data strongly indicate that the Dir gene plays a key regulatory role in enhancing cassava resistance to CsCMV.

CsCMV Altered the Cell Wall Membrane: A Similar Phenotype Was Observed in ViDir Lines

It has been proposed that certain Dir genes of unknown function may play roles in secondary cell wall biosynthesis and plant defence (Corbin et al. 2018). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed on CsCMV‐infected and CsCMV‐free cassava leaves to observe cell wall structure. Symptomatic leaves exhibiting mosaic patterns were selected for infected plants, while healthy leaves were used for the CsCMV‐free controls. TEM analysis revealed notable differences in cell wall structure, particularly in the middle lamella (Figure 4A), between infected and uninfected samples. Dir gene‐overexpressing (OEDir gene) and Dir gene‐silenced lines were also subjected to TEM to explore Dirs role further. Distinct differences were also observed between these lines (Figure 4B). Cell wall thinning was significantly observed after CsCMV infection and in Dir‐silenced lines (Figure 4C,D). These observations suggest that CsCMV infection alters cell wall biosynthesis and that the Dir gene is likely involved in regulating this process, potentially contributing to structural defence mechanisms in cassava.

Dir Gene (Manes.05G165700) Is Involved in Cell Wall Lignification

Given the observed reduction in cell wall thickness in Dir‐silenced lines and the known involvement of Dir in cell wall biosynthesis, we hypothesised that Dir may contribute to cell wall lignification. To investigate this, lignin content was quantified in OEDir and Dir‐silenced lines. The results showed a significant decrease in lignin accumulation in the silenced lines compared with the OEDir lines (Figure 5A), supporting the role of Dir in lignin biosynthesis. Further evidence was provided by Safranin O–Fast Green and Toluidine Blue O staining, which revealed stronger lignin‐associated fluorescence in OEDir lines and weaker staining in Dir‐silenced lines (Figure 5B), consistent with reduced lignification. Subcellular localisation analysis indicated that the Dir protein is localised predominantly to the plasma membrane, with no signal detected in the nucleoplasm (Figure 5C). Chloroplast‐targeted fluorescence was examined to determine whether Dir might also localise to the chloroplast, given that CsCMV has been reported to affect chloroplast function. However, no Dir signal was observed in the chloroplast (Figure S10), confirming that Dir is primarily localised to the plasma membrane. These results demonstrate that the Dir gene (Manes.05G165700.1) plays a significant role in lignin accumulation and is likely involved in regulating cell wall biosynthesis at the plasma membrane level.

Discussion

Pandemics and climate change have intensified the impact of viral diseases, with rapidly evolving RNA viruses complicating long‐term control strategies (Biek et al. 2015; Jones 2006; Allem 1994). Chemical treatments remain ineffective in agriculture (Boualem et al. 2016; Anderson et al. 2004), and while sanitary measures help, they often pose environmental risks. In addition, CsCMV has been shown to invade meristematic cells in cassava, hindering complete CsCMV eradication (Collavino et al. 2023). Thus, developing virus‐resistant cultivars remains the most sustainable approach (Tilman et al. 2011; Meziadi et al. 2017). In line with a proactive approach, we conducted a large‐scale survey in Danzhou, China, to detect emerging viruses in cassava, a model that could be adopted globally to mitigate crop losses and reduce agricultural waste. As a result, CsCMV, a recently reported virus in China (Tuo et al. 2020), seems persistent throughout cassava plants as it has been detected in diverse cities of Hainan (Tuo et al. 2020), laying the foundation of the current research. The re‐emergence of CsCMV in diverse areas in the last decades requires particular attention to assess the potential risk of CsCMV infection in cassava cultivation (Tuo et al. 2020; Lozano et al. 2017).

After a fruitful discussion with prominent and outstanding scientists, it was concluded that small RNA remains a promising tool for controlling viral replication. Nevertheless, it also suggested that developing new strategies is mandatory (Wu et al. 2024). Studies have reported several cassava miRNAs in response to diverse environmental stress, such as miRNAs under heat and drought‐like stress (Ballén‐Taborda et al. 2013), cassava mosaic disease stress (Bizabani et al. 2021), cassava brown streak disease stress (Wagaba et al. 2016), anthracnose disease stress (Pinweha et al. 2015), Xanthomonas axonopodis pv. manihotis stress (Pérez‐Quintero et al. 2012) and chilling response and stress acclimation (Xia et al. 2014). However, miRNAs function in response to CsCMV infection in cassava and other crops remained largely unexplored. The current investigations showed that Mes‐miR398, a regulator of cassava in response to CsCMV infection, targets the Dir gene (Table S8, Figure 1H). More importantly, these findings suggest that the miR398‐Dir module negatively regulates cassava resistance to CsCMV by affecting the cell wall lignification and thickness.

Various virus‐infected plants have been found to demonstrate miR398 accumulation. For instance, Arabidopsis plants infected with oilseed rape mosaic tobamovirus showed an increase in the levels of miR398 (Hu et al. 2011). Under tomato leaf curl New Delhi virus infection, miR398 was significantly upregulated (Naqvi et al. 2010). Coinfection of N. benthamiana with potato virus X and potato virus Y or plum pox virus led to a higher abundance of miR398 compared with virus‐free plants (Pacheco et al. 2012). These findings are consistent with CsCMV triggering the regulation of Mes‐miR398 upon infection in cassava. However, the mechanism triggering the Mes‐miR398 expression needs to be further investigated, as to our best knowledge, viruses do not directly target miRNAs. Notably, the constitutive expression of ‘Viral Suppressor’ RNA silencing could modify the levels and function of miRNAs (Lewsey et al. 2007; Schott et al. 2012). For instance, the infection of Arabidopsis by turnip mosaic virus (TuMV) or the production of an RNA silencing suppressor (TuMV P1/HC‐Pro) has been demonstrated to induce developmental abnormalities and hinder the miRNA‐guided cleavage of mRNA targets that encode several families of transcription factors (Kasschau et al. 2003). In addition, Mes‐miR398 was predicted to target Manes.04G012850.2 [Superoxide Dismutase (CU‐ZN)2 (CSD2)] (Table S8), a stress‐tolerance‐related gene, under CsCMV infection. CSD2 is a key regulator in enhancing plant stress resilience by suppressing growth (Bonnet et al. 2004). This finding highlights the potential significance of CSD2 in CsCMV–cassava interactions, warranting further investigation.

While miR398 is well‐studied for its role in stress‐response regulation, its function in plant growth and development remains less understood (Li et al. 2022). Although miR398 and its known targets CSD1/2 are involved in developmental regulation (Sunkar et al. 2006), evidence suggests that miR398 may influence growth through additional, yet unidentified, target genes (Chu et al. 2005; Yamasaki et al. 2007). In rice, miR398 regulated rice growth and yield (Zhang et al. 2017). In this study, overexpression of miR398 was found to promote stem growth (Figure 2A–E), indicating its potential as a molecular tool for developing high‐yield, disease‐resistant crops. Nonetheless, further research is required to elucidate the underlying regulatory mechanisms.

Virus‐induced gene transcript levels are altered depending on different host–virus combinations. Under CsCMV infection, Mes‐miR398 altered Dir gene expression (Table S8, Figure 1H) and increased CsCMV infection in the SC9 cultivar (Figure 2G). Modifying lignin in transgenic plants can cause defence responses to be expressed while severely impacting growth (Liu et al. 2023). SDir lines increased cell wall lignification and thickness (Figures 4 and 5). In addition, subcellular localisation demonstrated that the Dir protein localised to the cell membrane (Figure 5C), suggesting a role in regulating the cell wall membrane. Similarly, Glycine max (Gm)Dir1‐GFP fusion proteins localised in the cell membrane (Chen et al. 2018). These results suggest the miR398‐Dir module negatively regulates cassava resistance to CsCMV by affecting the cell wall lignification and thickness. Most Dir genes lack introns, with some exceptions, such as Cucumis melo Dir06 possessing one intron (Yadav et al. 2021). Cassava Dir (Manes.05G165700.1) contains no intron.

The DIR proteins have been demonstrated to mediate the regio‐ and stereoselectivity of bimolecular coupling during lignan and lignin production (Paniagua et al. 2017). Lignification is commonly linked to the structural defence against infections, and it plays a vital role in preventing attacks from pathogens by blocking the activity of microbe‐produced enzymes that break down cellulose, glucose, laccase and polygalacturonase (Bagniewska‐Zadworna et al. 2014; Lee et al. 2019). Notably, higher expression in Dir proteins leads to pinoresinol accumulation upon pathogen invasion of plants (Corbin et al. 2018). For instance, Dir‐4 was upregulated upon infection by Colletotrichum gloeosporioides and Botrytis cinerea (Reboledo et al. 2015). Overexpressing the Dir gene in cassava increased lignin content (Figure 5A,B) and reduced CsCMV viral load (Figure 3F). To our best knowledge, no Dir genes in response to virus infection have been reported. Nevertheless, the Dir protein was reported to enhance plant resistance against environmental stress. For instance, the GmDir1 gene increased soybean resistance to Phytophthora sojae.

Upon CsCMV infection in cassava, Mes‐miR398 targeted and reduced Dir gene regulation, affecting the cell wall membrane lignification, thus facilitating CsCMV infection (Figure 6). Overexpressing the Dir gene increased cell wall lignification and induced cassava resistance to CsCMV. Based on the current results, we propose two hypotheses regarding CsCMV‐infected cells that warrant further investigation: (i) CsCMV replication may be enhanced by an unknown regulatory mechanism involving miR398 or (ii) reduced cell wall thickness may facilitate CsCMV movement between cells. Notably, we observed that UG4 restricts CsCMV infection despite the induction of miR398. Given that miR398 has been previously implicated in plant defence, we hypothesise that the latter variety might activate a defence response that leads to miR398 accumulation.

Proposed Mes‐miR398/Dir module model. miR398‐Dirigent module affects cell wall lignification and negatively regulates cassava resistance to cassava common mosaic virus (CsCMV) infection. Upon CsCMV infection in cassava, Mes‐miR398 targets and reduces Dir gene regulation, affecting the cell wall membrane lignification, thus facilitating CsCMV infection. Overexpressing Dir increases cell wall lignification and induces cassava resistance to CsCMV.

Experimental Procedures

Plant and Pathogen Materials, and Growth Conditions

Cassava cultivar South China 9 (SC9; Susceptible to CsCMV) and UG4 (Resistant to CsCMV), obtained from the National Cassava Germplasm Nursery (Danzhou, China) and Chinese Academy of Tropical Agricultural Sciences, were used in this study. The plant tissues (stems) were initially cultivated in a mixture of potting soil and vermiculite (at a ratio of 2:1) in a greenhouse, where they were exposed to natural environmental conditions. One‐month‐old healthy plants were moved to a growth chamber with a 16‐h light and 8‐h dark cycle at 25°C, 1 to 2 weeks before inoculation. CsCMV isolates Hainan‐CM (CsCMV‐CM; GenBank accession number: MW175326.1) were used in this study. All the primers used in this study are listed in Table S12.

Sampling and Nucleic Acid Purification

Leaves showing disease symptoms were randomly collected in a cassava field in Danzhou, Hainan, China. The leaves were first sterilised with 70% ethanol and cut using scissors. All the samples were directly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until use. High‐quality total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Universal RNA Reagent (DP424, TAINGEN), or RNAprep Pure Plant Plus Kit (DP441, TIANGEN), following the instructions provided by the manufacturer. DNA was extracted using an extraction buffer of cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), followed by phenol:chloroform and caesium chloride purifications for a high DNA quality necessary for the success of the downstream assay. The DNA was precipitated with ethanol, dissolved in an elution buffer (EB) and stored at −80°C until use. RNA and DNA were assessed for quality (A260/A280 ≥ 2, A260/A230 ≥ 2) and quantity (ng/μL) using a Nano‐300 microspectrophotometer (ALLSHENG). In addition, the nucleic acid integrity was verified through electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel.

Small RNA Library Preparation and Sequencing

The samples were sent to Beijing Novogene Technology Co. Ltd. for RNA purification, small RNA library preparation and sequencing. RNA contamination and degradation were assessed on 1% agarose gels before RNA library preparation. RNA purity was assessed using the NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (IMPLEN). RNA integrity was assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay kit of the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies).

The small RNA libraries were generated using 2 μg RNA. All the samples were mixed to generate a single sample, ‘cass_leaves’, for the library construction. The NEBNext Multiplex Small RNA Library Prep Set for Illumina (HiSeqTM2000/MiSeqTM; NEB) was employed for this purpose, following the manufacturer's recommendations. In summary, the purified RNA was mixed with NEB 3′ SR adapter (5′‐AGATCGGAAGAGCACACGTCT‐3′), and subsequently, the SR RT primer hybridised to the excess of 3′ SR adapter. Afterward, the ssDNA adapter was converted into a dsDNA molecule. This step is crucial to prevent adapter‐dimer formation. In addition, dsDNAs are not substrates for ligation mediated by T4 RNA ligase I and do not ligate to the 5′ SR adapter in the subsequent ligation step. The 5′ ends adapter (5′‐GTTCAGAGTTCTACAGTCCGACGATC‐3′) was ligated to the 5′ ends of siRNA, miRNAs and piRNA. The first strand of DNA was synthesised utilising M‐MuLV Reverse Transcriptase (RT; RNase H−). The PCR amplification was performed utilising LongAmp Taq 2 × Master Mix, SR Primer for Illumina, and index (X) primer. The PCR products were subjected to purification using 8% polyacrylamide gel at a voltage of 100 V for 80 min. DNA fragments of 140–160 bp length were recovered and dissolved in 8 μL EB. Finally, the quality of the library was assessed using DNA High Sensitivity Chips on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 instrument.

The index‐coded samples were clustered using a cBot Cluster Generation System with the TruSeq SR Cluster Kit v3‐cBot‐HS (Illumina), following the instructions provided by the manufacturer. Following the formation of clusters, the library preparations were sequenced using a Novaseq 6000 platform, resulting in the generation of 50 bp single‐end reads.

Data Analysis, Viral Screening and miRNA‐Target Predictions

The raw data were quality‐controlled using Perl and Python programs. First, reads with more than 30% of bases having a quality score (sQ) ≤ 20 were removed. Reads (i) of bases N ≥ 10%, (ii) containing 5′ adapter sequences, (iii) polyA/T/G/C and (iv) without 3′ adapter sequences and insert fragments were removed. And the 3′ adapter sequences were trimmed. The resulting readings were filtered for small RNAs (sRNAs) with a length ranging from 18 to 26 nt for further investigation (Aregger et al. 2012). Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome ( M. esculenta v8.1‐cassava retrieved from Phytozome v13) (Goodstein et al. 2012) using the Bowtie v. 1.3.1 program (allowing 1 mismatch) (Langmead et al. 2009). The clean reads were mapped with GenBank Virus RefSeq for viral screening. The clean reads were assembled using SPAdes (Bankevich et al. 2012) and Velvet (Zerbino and Birney 2008). Contigs classification and annotation were conducted to detect the species distribution through databases: Host Genome, NCBI Nr (NCBI nonredundant protein sequences), NCBI Nt (NCBI nonredundant nucleotide sequences), GenBank Virus RefSeq nucleic acid database (represented as ‘Virus RefSeq Nucleotide’), GenBank Virus RefSeq protein database (represented as ‘Virus RefSeq Protein’). The alignment algorithm used was the Blast algorithm (Korf et al. 2003), with parameter restrictions of E‐value (1e−6 for BLASTN and 1e−4 for BLASTX).

To predict potential miRNA candidates, the clean reads of length 18–30 nt were mapped to the known miRNA retrieved from the miRBase 22.0 database (Kozomara et al. 2019) using the Bowtie v. 1.3.1 program without allowing any mismatches. Subsequently, the predicted miRNA hairpins were compared with miRNA precursor sequences from miRBase using mirDeep2 (Friedländer et al. 2012). The miREvo program (Wen et al. 2012) and mirdeep2 were integrated to predict novel miRNA.

The miRNA targets were predicted using TargetFinder (Bo and Wang 2005). The DEGseq v. 1.38.0 (R package) (Love et al. 2014) was used for miRNA differential expression (DEM) analysis. The p value was corrected using the q value method. The miRNAs showing absolute log2‐fold change (FC) > 1 and p < 0.025 were considered statistically significant DEMs. The miRNA expression levels were normalised using the transcript per million (TPM) normalisation method (Li et al. 2022).

RT‐qPCR Analysis

For mRNAs, cDNA was synthesised from the extracted total RNA (1 pg–1 μg) using the HiScript IV RT SuperMix (+gDNA wiper) (R423, Vazyme), following the manufacturer's instructions. qPCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (RR820A, Takara). For miRNA, cDNA was synthesised from the extracted total RNA (5 μg) using TransScript Green miRNA Two‐Step RT‐qPCR SuperMix (AQ202, TRANS), following the manufacturer's instructions. The UBQ10 (Manes.07G019300) housekeeping gene was used as a reference as it had the most stable expression pattern in cassava‐infected tissues (Moreno et al. 2011). The target of the qPCR assay is determined by the design of the primers (and in viral assay cases an internal probe). The relative gene expression was determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method [ΔΔCt = (Cttarget gene − CtUBQ10)] (Schmittgen and Livak 2008). The relative gene expression was visualised using the Prism v. 8.0.1 program (GraphPad). Three sets of biological replicates and three sets of technical replicates were analysed.

Pathogenicity Assay

Viral load was quantified using Premix Ex Taq (Probe qPCR) Bulk (RR390L, Takara), following the manufacturer's instructions. Probes were designed based on previously described methods (Smith and Osborn 2009). The TaqMan probe assay detected fluorescence using a 5′ fluorophore (FAM) and a 3′ quencher (MGB). To assess CsCMV expression in samples containing both CsCMV and transgenic plasmids, a specific approach was developed: the Ct value of the CP gene and the Ct value of the transgenic gene from the same sample were first subtracted, followed by analysis using the 2−ΔΔCt method. As previously described, samples containing ‘CsCMV’; water or untreated samples were used as negative controls to assess the relative expression (Niño‐Jimenez et al. 2024). All samples were analysed 4 dpi.

Generation of CsCMV Agro‐Infectious Clone

CsCMV‐CM was amplified and linked into the backbone fragment of the pGreenII‐35S vector to generate pCsCMV‐CM using the Gibson Assembly Cloning Kit (NEB), as previously described (Tuo et al. 2021). Briefly, 10–80 ng of purified PCR amplicons and 5 μL of 2 × Gibson mix (NEB) were incubated at 50°C for 1 h and transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α cells. Positive clones were selected for Sanger sequencing.

Generation of miR398 and Dir Overexpression Plasmids

Firstly, a pair of primers F‐NC2.398/R‐NC2.398 (Table S12) was designed to amplify the precursor miR398 (MI0037907) from the genomic DNA (gDNA) using PrimeSTAR Max DNA polymerase (Takara). The Dir gene (Manes.05G165700.1) was amplified from the synthesised cDNA using the designed primer F‐NC2.Dir/R‐NC2.Dir. The resultant amplicons were purified using the FastPure Gel DNA Extraction Mini Kit (Vazyme). Subsequently, the purified amplicon was linked to the pCsCMV2‐NC (NC2) vector (Tuo et al. 2023), using the Nimble cloning kit (Yan et al. 2023). Briefly, 80 ng of purified amplicon was mixed with 120 ng of NC2 and 5 μL of Nimble mix and incubated at 50°C for 1 h. The recombinant plasmids pCsCMV2‐miR398 (VOX398) and pCsCMV2‐Dir (VOXDir) were transformed into competent E. coli DH5α cells. Positive clones were selected for Sanger sequencing.

Generation of pCsCMV‐Dir Plasmid Through VIGS Approach

The online software SGN VIGS Tool (Fernandez‐Pozo et al. 2015) was used to design the silencing target gene induced by the Dir gene. Subsequently, primers were designed and added to the NC adapter sequences in an antisense orientation (F‐NC1.Dir/R‐NC1.Dir; Table S12) to amplify the target gene from the synthesised cDNA. The purified amplicon was linked to the pCsCMV‐NC (NC1) vector (Tuo et al. 2021). The recombinant plasmid pCsCMV‐Dir (ViDir) was constructed using the Nimble cloning kit (Yan et al. 2023) and transformed into competent E. coli DH5α cells. Positive clones were selected for Sanger sequencing.

Agroinfiltrations

The plasmids were first extracted from the positive bacterial clones using RapidLyse Plasmid Mini Kit‐Box 2 (Vazyme) following the manufacturer's instructions. The purified plasmids were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 (pSoup19) cells. The positive clones were selected and cultured overnight in Luria Bertani (LB) broth supplemented with kanamycin and rifampicin (50 and 25 mg/L, respectively). Subsequently, the bacteria were centrifuged and collected at 4000 rpm. The suspension was washed three times with 10 mM MgCl2, then suspended in 1 M MES, 1 M MgCl2 and 200 mM acetosyringone (AS). The inoculum was adjusted to a final optical density of 600 nm (OD600) 0.8, 0.8, 0.5 and 0.5 for pCsCMV‐CM, ViDir, VOX398 and VOXDir, respectively. The empty vectors NC1 and NC2 were used as negative controls. Leaves of 1‐month‐old healthy plants were used to inoculate pCsCMV‐CM, VOX398, VOXDir and ViDir. The suspended bacterial solutions were inoculated into both sides of the main vein on the back of each cassava leaf using a 1 mL syringe. Each leaf was inoculated on 10 spots. The total RNA of cassava new leaves (Aapproximately 3–4 weeks after inoculation) of the negative control and experimental groups was extracted and reverse‐transcribed into cDNA to detect the target gene expression level.

Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay

Arabidopsis seedlings were grown for 25–30 days at 22°C under a 12 h light/12 h dark photoperiod. Protoplasts were prepared and transformed with plasmids using PEG, following a previously established protocol (Yoo et al. 2007). The 3′ untranslated region (UTR) sequence of Dir (Manes.05G165700.1) was cloned into the pGreenII0800‐35S‐LUC vector and cotransformed with Mes‐miR398, which was inserted into the pGreenII‐62SK‐319a vector (Liu et al. 2014). Arabidopsis leaves were transiently transformed with A. tumefaciens GV3101 (pSoup19) carrying the above constructs. Firefly luciferase (LUC) and Renilla luciferase (REN), both driven by the 35S promoter, were detected using a luminometer (PerkinElmer) (Liu and Axtell 2015). Primer sequences are listed in Table S12.

Subcellular Localisation

The cassava Dir (Manes.05G165700.1) gene without the codon stop was cloned into the pCSXN‐eGFP vector using the ClonExpress Ultra One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme). Fluorescence signals were detected using a confocal imaging reader (BioTek).

Histological Analysis and Microscopy Observation

One month after seed germination, stems from both wild‐type (WT) and transgenic cassava plants were fixed in formalin acetic acid alcohol (FAA) solution at 4°C. Tissue sections (5 μm thick) were obtained using an automatic microtome (Leica Microsystems). To visualise lignified cell walls, sections were stained with Toluidine Blue O and Safranin O–Fast Green, then examined using a TCS SP2 spectral confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems). Lignin autofluorescence was observed under ultraviolet light with an excitation wavelength of 355/25 nm. For transmission electron microscopy (TEM), stem samples were fixed in freshly prepared 2.5% (vol/vol) glutaraldehyde solution. Vacuum infiltration was applied to ensure complete fixative penetration for 12 h. Cell wall thickness was quantified using Image‐Pro Plus v. 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics Inc.).

Lignin Content

The Klason method was used to determine the lignin content, using 72% H2SO4 to dissolve away all of the polysaccharides (Hatfield and Fukushima 2005).

Bioinformatic Analysis

The AlphaFold3 database predicted protein interaction structures (Abramson et al. 2024). Protein interactions were visualised using the RCSB Protein Data Bank online viewer (https://www.rcsb.org/3d‐view). Annotated Dir protein sequences from A. thaliana , Forsythia intermedia, Glycyrrhiza echinata , Gossypium barbadense , Gossypium hirsutum , Hordeum vulgare , Linum, M. esculenta , Oryza sativa , Picea sitchensis , Podophyllum peltatum , Saccharum hybrid cultivar, Schisandra chinensis, Sesamum indicum , Sorghum bicolor , Tamarix androssowii, Thuja plicata , Triticum aestivum , Tsuga heterophylla and Zea mays were retrieved from UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/). Sequences lacking complete alignment coverage across all five conserved motifs were excluded, and duplicate entries were removed. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using Clustal Omega (EMBL‐EBI) (Li et al. 2015) and visualised with the Jalview tool. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL v. 6) online platform (Letunic and Bork 2024).

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted using Student's t test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. The different characters displayed significant variations (*p < 0.025, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001; ns, nonsignificant).
