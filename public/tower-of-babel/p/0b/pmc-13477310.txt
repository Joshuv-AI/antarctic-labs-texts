Association of the Discordance Between Apolipoprotein B and Low‐Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol With Cognition
Authors: Jiwei Jiang, Chao Li, Yanli Zhang, Jie Liu, Yang Liu, Wenyi Li, Qiwei Ren, Huiying Zhang, Yunyun Duan, Qianting Lv, Huaguang Zheng, Yuesong Pan, Yu Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate and validate the association of the discordance between apoB (apolipoprotein B) and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) or non–high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol with cognitive function and explore the mediating role of cerebral large‐ and small‐vessel diseases on this association, for which previous evidence was limited.MethodsCommunity‐dwelling adults aged >50 years from the Polyvascular Evaluation for Cognitive Impairment and Vascular Events study were included in this cross‐sectional study. Global cognitive function was evaluated using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment. The association of the residuals between apoB and LDL‐C or non–high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels with Montreal Cognitive Assessment score was explored using multivariate linear regression. The simple mediating effects of neuroimaging markers of cerebral large atherosclerotic disease and cerebral small‐vessel disease on this association were analyzed.ResultsThis study included 2916 participants (age: 61.01±6.63 years, female sex: 1553 [53.26%]). Total Montreal Cognitive Assessment scores and visuospatial/executive function, naming, attention, language, abstraction, delayed memory, and orientation subscores differed significantly among 3 groups stratified by the residuals between apoB and LDL‐C. Increased residuals between apoB and LDL‐C were associated with decreased Montreal Cognitive Assessment score postadjustment. Intracranial atherosclerotic plaque presence and burden mediated 13.36% and 10.87% of this association, respectively; the mediation effect of intracranial atherosclerotic plaque presence postadjustment was 5.67%. The validation study confirmed these findings, with 10.04% and 9.08% postadjustment mediation effects of the presence and burden of intracranial atherosclerotic plaque, respectively, on the association.ConclusionsHigh discordance between apoB and LDL‐C was associated with poor cognitive function, partially mediated by intracranial atherosclerotic plaque.

Traditionally, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) is the primary lipid parameter for the management of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD). 1 However, some statin‐treated patients with low LDL‐C, including those with diabetes or high triglycerides, still develop subclinical atherosclerosis and vascular inflammation, known as residual ASCVD risk. 2 , 3 The 2019 European Society of Cardiology/European Atherosclerosis Society Guidelines recommend apoB (apolipoprotein B) as an alternative to LDL‐C and potentially superior to non–high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (non–HDL‐C) in these individuals. 2 A longitudinal study demonstrated that excess apoB (the aforementioned value of apoB that contributed by LDL‐C levels alone) was dose‐dependently associated with an increased risk of myocardial infarction and ASCVD in women and men. 4 More interestingly, recent studies have found that cerebrospinal fluid apoB levels correlate with phosphorylated and total tau in presymptomatic Alzheimer's disease (AD), whereas cortical apoB is strongly associated with cortical amyloid‐β deposition in late‐stage AD. Moreover, baseline cerebrospinal fluid apoB levels were also associated with longitudinal visuospatial cognitive decline. 5 , 6 These preliminary findings suggest that apoB's involvement in lipoprotein metabolism could accelerate the progression of ASCVD and AD.

Lipid metabolism dysregulation contributes to vascular dysfunction and AD‐related cognitive decline. 7 , 8 Elevated LDL‐C is an established risk factor for dementia and stroke. 9 , 10 A cross‐sectional study found differential expression of ATP‐binding cassette transporter A1 and apolipoprotein H proteins, which are related to lipoprotein and cholesterol metabolism, with exacerbated alterations observed in patients with concomitant dementia and cardiovascular disease. 11 However, a 17‐year prospective study found that low and high levels of HDL‐C were associated with elevated dementia risk, whereas the association between LDL‐C and dementia risk was modest. 12 Existing evidence on the role of routine single‐component lipid profiles (total cholesterol [TC], triglycerides, LDL‐C, and HDL‐C) in the association with cognitive function is varied and inconsistent. 13 Our previous study linked discordantly high apoB with respect to LDL‐C or non–HDL‐C to greater intra/extracranial and coronary atherosclerotic plaque burden. 14 , 15 A study involving 12 688 statin‐naïve adults also found that metabolically healthy individuals could exhibit discordantly high apoB levels relative to LDL‐C or non‐HDL‐C levels. 16 Considering that apoB at given LDL‐C and non‐HDL‐C levels is characterized by high variability and improves ASCVD residual risk prediction, 17 we hypothesize that the discordance between apoB and LDL‐C or non‐HDL‐C could serve as a novel lipid biomarker for evaluating cognitive function.

Despite accumulating knowledge, previous evidence regarding the association between apoB and cognition has been limited to measurements of apoB in cerebrospinal fluid and brain tissue. Its adoption in clinical practice remains low. 5 , 6 Moreover, clear evidence on the relationship of the discordance between rather than the cumulative effect of apoB and LDL‐C or non‐HDL‐C with cognition, as well as the underlying mechanism, remain unclear. Therefore, this study primarily aimed to investigate and validate the association of the discordance between apoB and LDL‐C or non‐HDL‐C with cognition in a neurologically healthy population, and to further explore the role of cerebral atherosclerotic large and small vascular disease in this association.

METHODS

All data and methods that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Participants

This cross‐sectional study extracted data from the baseline survey of the PRECISE (Polyvascular Evaluation for Cognitive Impairment and Vascular Events) study conducted between May 2017 and September 2019. The PRECISE study was a population‐based prospective cohort study that consecutively enrolled 3067 community‐dwelling adults aged 50 to 75 years, who were cluster sampled from 6 villages and 4 communities in Lishui city, China. 18 The inclusion and exclusion criteria for participant enrollment in the PRECISE study have been described previously. 18 Ethical approval was obtained from Beijing Tiantan Hospital (KY2017‐010‐01) and Lishui Hospital (2016‐42). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03178448). The exclusion criteria in this study were: (1) contraindications to brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or cognitive assessments; (2) life expectancy ≤4 years due to advanced cancer and other diseases; (3) mental diseases; (4) missing lipid profile biomarkers—apoB, TC, LDL‐C, and HDL‐C; and (5) individuals on lipid‐lowering medication. This study finally included 2916 participants, after excluding those on lipid‐lowering medication (n=120), with missing lipid biomarkers (n=2), or brain MRI and cognitive assessments (n=29). This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for cohort studies.

Clinical Measures and Neuropsychological Tests

Collected data included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), education, and medical history (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, heart disease, smoking, and drinking). Global cognition was evaluated using the Beijing version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), 19 encompassing visuospatial and executive function, naming, attention, abstraction, language, delayed memory, and orientation. Lower MoCA scores indicate worse cognitive function.

The definitions and evaluation criteria of baseline clinical measures have been described previously. 20 BMI, commonly used as a predictor of nutritional status and overall adiposity, was calculated by dividing each participant's weight in kilograms by height in meters squared. 21 Education level was categorized as illiteracy, primary school, junior high school, senior high school, or bachelor's degree or higher. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, self‐reported hypertension previously diagnosed by a physician, or current use of antihypertensive agents. 22 , 23 Diabetes was defined as a fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL (to convert to mmol/L, multiply by 0.0555), 2‐hour postload glucose ≥200 mg/dL, hemoglobin A1c ≥6.5% (to convert to proportion of total hemoglobin, multiply by 0.01), self‐reported diabetes previously diagnosed by a physician, or current use of antidiabetic agents. 24 Dyslipidemia was defined as TC ≥240 mg/dL, LDL‐C ≥160 mg/dL, HDL‐C <40 mg/dL (to convert cholesterol to mmol/L, multiply by 0.0259), or self‐reported dyslipidemia previously diagnosed by a physician. 2 Heart disease was defined as a history of coronary artery disease, arrhythmia, primary valve disease, heart failure, cardiomyopathy, or endocarditis. Smoking status was defined as current smoking, referring to individuals who smoked at least 1 cigarette per day on average during the past month. 25 Drinking status was based on self‐reported frequency of alcohol intake (per week) and the typical number of standard drinks consumed per occasion. It was defined as consuming alcohol at least once per week. 26

Laboratory Lipid Profile Measurements and Discordance Definition

The TC, triglycerides, LDL‐C, HDL‐C, ApoB, Lp(a) (lipoprotein(a)), fasting blood glucose (FBG), creatinine, and serum urea nitrogen levels were measured at baseline.

According to standard operating procedures in clinical laboratories, fasting venous blood samples were drawn and collected into separator tubes at baseline. The samples were then centrifuged at 3000g for 10 minutes to separate the serum and stored at 4 °C for subsequent analysis. All biochemical parameters in this study were measured using the Abbott Architect C16000 automatic biochemical analyzer. The detailed testing methods are provided in Data S1.

Non‐HDL‐C was calculated as TC minus HDL‐C (non‐HDL‐C=TC – HDL‐C). Discordance/concordance between apoB and LDL‐C or non‐HDL‐C was evaluated using a residuals approach according to previous evidence. 14 , 27 First, residuals of expected versus observed apoB–LDL‐C or apoB–non‐HDL‐C values were calculated using linear regression. Thereafter, discordance between apoB and LDL‐C or non‐HDL‐C, measured by residuals, was defined as follows: discordantly low apoB: <25th percentile residual (apoB lower than expected based on LDL‐C or non‐HDL‐C); concordance: 25th to 75th percentile residual (apoB close to expected based on LDL‐C or non‐HDL‐C); and discordantly high apoB, >75th percentile residual (apoB higher than expected based on LDL‐C or non‐HDL‐C). A residuals plot to visualize the definition of discordance/concordance between apo B and LDL‐C or non‐HDL‐C evaluated using residuals is shown in Figure S1.

To evaluate the cumulative effect of apoB and LDL‐C or non‐HDL‐C on cognition, the population was divided into 3 groups using a cutoff value approach: low/low (apoB and LDL‐C or non‐HDL‐C were below their respective cutoff values), discordant (either apoB was ≥ the cutoff value and LDL‐C or non‐HDL‐C was < the cutoff, or apoB was < the cutoff and LDL‐C or non‐HDL‐C was ≥ the cutoff), and high/high (apoB and LDL‐C or non‐HDL‐C were ≥ their respective cutoff values). According to guideline recommendations, 28 , 29 the cutoff value of LDL‐C was 3.4 mmol/L, corresponding to 1.16 g/L of apoB at the equivalent percentile in the PRECISE study. Similarly, the cutoff value for non‐HDL‐C was 4.1 mmol/L, corresponding to 1.04 g/L of apoB.

Brain Neuroimaging Measures and Markers

Structural brain MRI, including 3‐dimensional T1‐weighted imaging, 2‐dimensional T2‐weighted, 2‐dimensional fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery, diffusion‐weighted imaging, susceptibility‐weighted imaging, and time‐of‐flight magnetic resonance angiography, was performed using a 3.0‐T MR scanner (Ingenia 3.0T, Philips, Best, the Netherlands). Neuroimaging markers of intracranial and extracranial large atherosclerosis and cerebral small‐vessel disease (CSVD) were evaluated. The former is characterized by the presence of intra/extracranial atherosclerotic plaque, the presence of intracranial atherosclerotic stenosis (ICAS)/extracranial atherosclerotic stenosis, and the burden of intra/extracranial atherosclerotic plaque, whereas the latter is marked by the severity of white matter hyperintensity, the presence of enlarged perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia, and the total CSVD score. Definitions and methods are shown in Data S2.

External Validation

A single‐center retrospective study involving 1216 neurologically healthy participants (aged ≥50 years) from Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University (January 2021–December 2023), which has been described previously, 30 was conducted to externally validate the association and mediation findings. This study followed the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Central Ethics Committee of Beijing Tiantan Hospital (KY 2024‐410‐03). A detailed flow chart of the eligibility criteria (identical to those used in the initial cohort study) at baseline in the independent database is presented in Figure S2.

The clinical assessments include age, sex, BMI, educational years, and medical history of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, chronic coronary disease, smoking, and drinking. Chronic coronary disease was diagnosed according to the 2023 guideline, 31 and included stable angina, silent myocardial ischemia, ischemic cardiomyopathy, and patients discharged after admission for an acute coronary disease event or after a coronary revascularization procedure following stabilization of all acute cardiovascular issues. Definitions of the other clinical assessments were the same as those in the initial cohort study. Global cognitive function was evaluated using the MoCA scores. Fasting venous blood samples were collected from the antecubital vein into serum separator tubes. The tubes were centrifuged for 10 minutes at 3000g to separate the serum, which was immediately used to measure laboratory biomarkers, including TC, triglycerides, LDL‐C, HDL‐C, Lp(a), FBG, creatinine, and serum urea nitrogen, using LABOSPECT008AS automatic biochemical analyzer (Hitachi, Japan).

Neuroimaging markers of intracranial large atherosclerosis and CSVD were evaluated using structural brain MRI, including 3‐dimensional T1‐weighted imaging, 2‐dimensional T2‐weighted imaging, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery, diffusion‐weighted imaging, susceptibility‐weighted imaging, and magnetic resonance angiography, performed using a 3.0‐T MR scanner (Siemens Prisma, Erlangen, Germany). Neuroimaging markers of extracranial large atherosclerosis were evaluated using high‐resolution ultrasonography (Aplio A500; Canon Medical Systems Corporation, Japan). The definitions and evaluation criteria were the same as those used in the initial cohort. Interobserver agreement for the detection of neuroimaging markers was assessed by replicating the assessment of all participants by the 2 raters. A senior neurologist (Duan Y) determined inconsistencies. Good reproducibility (all Cohen κ >0.85) was found for these neuroimaging biomarkers.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are expressed as raw numbers (percentages) and were assessed using the χ2‐test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as the mean±SD and were evaluated using univariate analysis of variance or independent t‐tests, whereas skewed data are presented as the median with interquartile range and were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis or Mann–Whitney U test. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were adjusted using Bonferroni correction.

Pearson or Spearman correlation was used to determine the correlation coefficients between both lipid biomarkers (TC, triglycerides, LDL‐C, HDL‐C, apoB, non‐HDL‐C, Lp(a), residuals between apoB and LDL‐C, and residuals between apoB and non‐HDL‐C) and clinical measures, total MoCA and domain scores, and neuroimaging markers (burden of intra/extracranial atherosclerotic plaque, and white matter hyperintensity, enlarged perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia, and total CSVD scores per the Rothwell criteria). Multiple linear regression models were generated to determine the relationship of the original residuals between apoB and LDL‐C or non‐HDL‐C with the total MoCA scores in all participants. For each outcome, we created 2 models: model 1 without adjustment (crude model) and model 2 adjusted for statistically significant indicators in the aforementioned univariate analysis, including age; sex; BMI; educational level; medical histories of hypertension, diabetes, smoking, and drinking; and serum urea nitrogen (creatinine in the validated database), FBG, and Lp(a) levels.

Simple mediation models (5000 bootstrapped iterations) assessed the neuroimaging effect on the associations between apoB–LDL‐C/non‐HDL‐C and MoCA. Under this framework, the total effect was divided into natural direct effect and natural indirect effect, and the mediation effect was quantified as a percentage (natural direct effect/total effect × 100). Natural direct effect represents the effect of the association between the residuals of apoB and LDL‐C/non‐HDL‐C with the MoCA scores, independent of these neuroimaging measures. Natural indirect effect represents the effect of the association between the residuals between apoB and LDL‐C/non‐HDL‐C and the MoCA scores, which could be explained by changes in neuroimaging measures. The confounders in the mediating analyses were the same as those in the multiple linear regression analyses. Statistical significance was set at 2‐tailed P values <0.05 for all analyses. All statistical analyses were performed from May 2024 to March 2025 using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and SPSS (version 29.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics of the Population in 3 Groups Based on Concordance/Discordance Between apoB and LDL‐C or Non‐HDL‐C

The mean age of the final sample of 2916 participants was 61.01±6.63 years, with 1553 (53.26%) being female. The total MoCA score was 21 (17–24). The median residual between apoB and LDL‐C was 4.28×10−3 (−0.04 to 0.05), whereas that between apoB and non‐HDL‐C was −7.83×10−3 (−0.08 to 0.07). Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study population stratified by the concordant/discordant categories between apoB and LDL‐C or non‐HDL‐C based on residuals. There were significant differences in BMI; education; medical histories of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia; drinking status; serum levels of TC, triglycerides, HDL‐C, Lp(a), and FBG; total MoCA scores and the MoCA subscores for visuospatial/executive function, naming, attention, language, abstraction, delayed memory; and orientation among the 3 groups defined by concordant/discordant apoB and LDL‐C based on residuals (all P<0.05). Compared with participants in the concordance group, those in the discordantly high apoB and LDL‐C group had higher BMI, a greater proportion with a medical history of dyslipidemia, higher serum levels of TC, triglycerides, Lp(a), and FBG, but lower serum HDL‐C levels and visuospatial/executive function subscores on the MoCA (all P false discovery rate<0.05); participants in the discordantly low apoB and LDL‐C group demonstrated lower BMI; a lower proportion with histories of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and drinking; lower serum levels of TC, triglycerides, Lp(a), and FBG but higher HDL‐C levels; and total MoCA scores and higher subscores in naming, attention, language, abstraction, and delayed memory (all P false discovery rate<0.05). Similar findings were also observed in participants between the discordantly high apoB and LDL‐C group and the concordant group (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that in a neurologically healthy population, increased apoB–LDL‐C residuals were independently associated with decreased cognitive function, which was partially mediated by cerebral large atherosclerotic plaque from a statistical perspective. These findings preliminarily reveal the potential value of large‐vessel residual risk in assessing cognitive function.

An increasing number of studies have demonstrated that apoB serves as a superior risk marker for ASCVD compared with LDL‐C, 32 , 33 because apoB is a defined molecule, whereas the cholesterol markers represent the mass of cholesterol within lipoprotein particles defined by their density rather than their molecular structure. It has become apparent that focusing solely on cholesterol concentrations may not capture the full extent of cholesterol‐related risk, as this approach overlooks factors such as the number and size of cholesterol‐containing lipoproteins, which play important roles in the entry and entrapment of lipoproteins within the arterial intima. 34 Generally, apoB and LDL‐C levels are closely correlated; however, discordance can exist between their measurements in individuals, especially in those with cholesterol‐depleted or cholesterol‐enriched LDL. 16 , 35 Indeed, the number of atherogenic lipoprotein particles circulating in the bloodstream can also vary significantly: a high number of small, cholesterol‐depleted particles is more atherogenic than a low number of cholesterol‐enriched lipoproteins at comparable cholesterol levels. 36 Similarly, in this study, we regressed apoB on LDL‐C and used the resulting residual to represent the amount of apoB not explained by LDL‐C in a model alongside LDL‐C. This approach revealed substantial variability in individual apoB values for a given LDL‐C value, suggesting that a strong correlation does not necessarily imply equivalence in absolute levels of different markers in individual cases.

LDL is the most abundant apoB‐containing lipoprotein particle in circulation. However, the association between single LDL‐C or single apoB levels and cognition varies and remains inconsistent. A longitudinal study demonstrated that compared with LDL‐C levels 70.0 to 99.9 mg/dL, low LDL‐C levels (<70 mg/dL, especially <55 mg/dL) were associated with slower cognitive decline in population‐based settings. 37 However, a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled trial found that the addition of evolocumab to maximally tolerated statin therapy had no impact on patient‐reported cognition after an average of 2.2 years of treatment. 38 The ARIC (Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities) study that enrolled 9350 participants revealed faster cognitive decline with the highest versus the lowest apoB quintiles over a 15‐year follow‐up. 39 A large‐scale cross‐sectional study included 1014 patients with mild cognitive impairment and found no significant association between apoB and MoCA scores. 40 Accordingly, a previous study demonstrated that elevated serum LDL‐C and genetic mutations in apoB were associated with a heightened risk of AD‐related cognitive impairment. 41 LDL particle size can be derived from the ratio of LDL‐C concentration to total apoB concentration (LDL‐C/apoB ratio), which has been found to independently predict major cardiovascular events in patients with established atherosclerosis 42 and can also predict cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality in the general population. 43 Moreover, our findings showed that the cumulative effect of apoB and LDL‐C was not associated with cognitive function based on the cutoff value approach. To our best knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate that increasing residuals between apoB and LDL‐C levels (ie, high discordant apoB), rather than the cumulative effect of lipid biomarkers, are independently associated with poor cognitive function in a neurologically healthy population. Therefore, the discordance between apoB and LDL‐C levels could be considered another important biomarker of atherosclerotic disease and its risk factors. However, this discordance represents merely an association and may not be specific to the development of cognitive decline, based on our findings. A prospective longitudinal study is needed to validate the predictive value of apoB–LDL‐C discordance for the early detection of cognitive decline in healthy populations.

More important, our study also found that the association of residuals between apoB and LDL‐C with cognition was partly mediated by the presence and burden of intracranial atherosclerotic plaque, providing preliminary evidence on the large vascular disease contributing to cognitive decline in healthy populations. Consistent with our findings, the ARIC study demonstrated that intracranial atherosclerotic disease was associated with an augmented risk of incident dementia and partially mediates the effect of cardiovascular risk factors on the brain, leading to dementia. 44 A recent review concluded that cholesterol accumulation, lipid aggregates, and vascular changes were common across neurodegenerative diseases, including cognitive impairment‐related disease. 45 Numerous lines of evidence have evinced the relationship between intracranial atherosclerotic disease and clinical, neuroimaging, and pathology‐based markers of AD‐related cognitive impairment, which could be related to hypoperfusion, arterial stiffness, and inflammation. 46 A cross‐sectional study also found that asymptomatic carotid stenosis was associated with cognitive impairment, independent of known vascular risk factors for vascular cognitive impairment. 47 The cholesteryl ester transfer protein and peripheral lipoproteins lead to cognitive impairment and dementia, and can potentially affect the cerebrovasculature from both sides of the blood–brain barrier. 48

However, the mechanism of intracranial atherosclerotic disease on the association between lipid and cognitive performance is incompletely understood. An observational study found that transient ischemic attack patients with hypoperfusion exhibited a more significant luminal stenosis degree than those in the group without hypoperfusion, suggesting the association between cerebral hypoperfusion and intracranial atherosclerotic disease. 49 Accordingly, chronic brain hypoperfusion may lead to slower cerebral ability to process information, poor cognitive performance, and dementia progression. 50 Moreover, microemboli originating from mechanically unstable areas of carotid plaque could also play a contributory role in the cognitive decline in patients who have undergone endarterectomy. 51 It suggested that the discordance between apoB and LDL‐C could be considered a novel potential point for intervention and prevention of intracranial atherosclerotic plaque and associated cognitive decline.

This study has some limitations. First, as a cross‐sectional study, it was impossible to evaluate the temporal relationship of apoB‐LDL‐C residuals with ICAS or cognitive function, as well as the association between ICAS and cognitive function. The mediating effect only suggests a potential mediation consistent with (but not proving) a theoretical model in which neuroimaging markers may lie on the pathway between apoB‐LDL‐C residuals and cognition. Second, the differences in spatial resolution of MRI cause discrepancies in the sensitivity of atherosclerosis detection, affecting comparisons between different vascular territories. Third, cognitive function was assessed solely using the MoCA, which cannot adequately represent all aspects of cognitive function and may be constrained by the ceiling effect. Finally, the participants in the validation sample were younger and healthier than those in the main study cohort and were derived from only a single center. The potential effects of this on the validation results include limited generalizability, unmeasured bias, and a restricted range of cognitive outcomes. However, the successful validation in this distinct sample indicates that the findings from our study were robust and powerful, and reduced survival bias.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in a neurologically healthy population, the residuals between apoB and LDL‐C are negatively associated with cognitive function, which may be partly mediated by large cerebral atherosclerotic plaque. This suggests the clinical value of discordance between apoB and LDL‐C associated with cognitive function and emphasizes a potential lipid‐related cerebral large vascular pathological mechanism underlying cognitive impairment. Future research should aim to explore the longitudinal association of apoB–LDL‐C residuals with the development of ICAS and the trajectory of cognitive decline, validate the mediating role of ICAS on this association between discordantly high apoB and cognitive decline using causal mediation analysis, and investigate the potential role of CSVD in the association between lipid biomarkers and cognitive function. This should be performed through larger, multicenter, and prospective cohort studies that include older populations, with extended follow‐up periods.
