Trait Dataset for 3,000 European Wild Bee and Hoverfly Species
Authors: Marija Miličić, Ahlam Sentil, Jelena Ačanski, Océane Bartholomée, Jordan Benrezkallah, Petr Bogusch, Thomas Brau, Luna Breuer, Frédéric Carion, Natasha de Manincor, Bastien De Tandt, Rémi Devorsine, Achik Dorchin, Aleksandra Đorđević, Luca Dorio, Ambre Dormal, Thierry Duez, William Fiordaliso, Simone Flaminio, Guillaume Ghisbain, Arthur Herinckx, Marina Janković Milosavljević, Max Kasparek, Michael Kuhlmann, Romain Le Divelec, Clément Lefebvre, Shawn Lemaire, Sebastien Patiny, Audrey Pinchon, Snežana Popov, Pierre Rasmont, Stuart P M Roberts, Laura Roquer-Beni, Paolo Rosa, Rémi Santerre, Jakub Straka, Adrien Terryn, Joffrey Thulier, Tamara Tot, Clément Tourbez

ABSTRACT
AbstractWild bees and hoverflies are key pollinators, but their populations are declining in Europe. As the monitoring of each species would be arduous to implement, a cost-effective solution could be a shift from species level monitoring to an evaluation of the trends of functional groups. However, the lack of centralized trait databases hampers progress, as data are often dispersed across literature, limited to some countries or remain as expert knowledge. In a comprehensive effort of scientists working on these two insect groups, data on traits of 3,052 European bee and hoverfly species (2139 and 913, respectively) was compiled. The dataset includes 18 shared traits, along with additional group-specific traits—13 for wild bees and eight for hoverflies. These traits span nesting ecology, larval biology, adult morphology, behavior, and distribution patterns. The primary data sources consist of literature reviews, experimental studies, and expert insights. By fostering collaboration among researchers, this dataset provides a new foundation for exploring the ecological roles of these vital insect pollinators, guiding targeted conservation strategies, and deepening our understanding of the mechanisms of pollinator decline in Europe.

Background & Summary

Pollinators represent a critical component of global biodiversity in terrestrial ecosystems. Their role in the pollination process is essential for sustaining both wild plant communities and agricultural crops1,2. However, the alarming decline in insect pollinator populations3, primarily driven by climate and land-use changes, with intensive land management practices, and pesticide applications4,5, as well as the spread of exotic invasive species6, poses significant threats to biodiversity, and can result in cascading disruptions to food webs and the ecosystem services on which human well-being depends. Recognizing these challenges, the European Commission (EC) introduced its first Pollinators Initiative in 2018, marking a strategic effort to address the decline of wild pollinators. This commitment was reinforced by the EU Biodiversity Strategy for 2030, adopted in 2020, which outlined targeted measures to safeguard pollinators. Building on these foundations, the EC launched an updated Pollinators Initiative in early 2023, designed to tackle the decline and help avert extinctions driven by anthropogenic activities.

Addressing the ongoing decline in pollinators requires moving beyond the comprehensive survey of taxonomic diversity and embracing a functional approach that may simplify monitoring and can offer insights into the consequence for ecosystem dynamics7. Wild bees (Hymenoptera: Anthophila) and hoverflies (Diptera: Syrphidae), as key insect pollinators, are excellent models for this approach due to their diverse life cycles, habitat requirements, and sensitivity to climate change and intensive land-use alterations. An increasing number of studies have examined the traits of pollinators from various perspectives, including plant–pollinator specialization, efficacy of pollination services, and responses to both climate and land-use changes8–11. Such studies often explore both historical scenarios and predictive models12–16 to better understand and address future challenges. However, remaining geographical and taxonomic biases in pollinator trait data collection17 are still limiting our ability to perform multi-taxon and multi-trait analyses. Such analyses are, however, vital for accurately predicting the impacts of global change at the community/regional level on biodiversity and ecosystem services18.

Recent efforts have outlined a roadmap for establishing community data standards in bee trait research, which includes a protocol for effective data sharing and an aggregated dataset comprising six traits over 1,600 bee species globally (i.e. less than 10% of the ca. 20,000 bee species recorded worldwide)19. New national initiatives, such as BeeFunc 1.0 (the first comprehensive trait database for wild bees in France) are compiling and expanding national trait datasets, including 26,176 trait data records20. For hoverflies, the Syrph the Net publication21 gathers 12 morphological, ecological, and behavioral traits for most European hoverflies and is currently the largest available data source on traits for this pollinator group. However, a comprehensive database that centralizes the traits of both bees and hoverflies at continental level is still lacking. Currently, this information is either dispersed and presented inconsistently across various publications or confined as expert knowledge. This fragmentation makes trait data difficult to access, compile, integrate effectively into new research and interpret at multi-taxon and global levels.

Here, data on traits of approximately 3,000 wild bee and hoverfly species have been compiled. The current database is a direct result of community efforts, deriving from multiple projects, i.e.: the H2020 project Safeguard (Safeguarding European wild pollinators), and the EC DG Env projects ORBIT (Taxonomic resources for European bees), Taxo-Fly (Taxonomic resources for European hoverflies), and PULSE (Providing technical and scientific support in measuring the pulse of European biodiversity using the Red List Index) (see acknowledgement for details). This work includes traits related to various life stages, morphological features, ecological requirements, and distribution patterns, which provide valuable insights into their diversity and ecological functions19. Besides offering trait data for a large number of European wild bee and hoverfly species, this work also highlights the potential of traits in addressing different research priorities. Certain traits may offer valuable insights into adaptive responses and species survival, aiding conservation efforts, while others could contribute to understanding broader evolutionary, biogeographical, biological and ecological patterns22–24. This comprehensive approach ensures that trait selection can align with both conservation needs and fundamental research objectives.

Methods

Taxonomic scope and trait selection

Regarding hoverflies, the recently published list of 913 European species was used25. For bees, the latest European checklist was considered, which includes a total of 2,138 wild bee species26. Additionally, Apis mellifera was included in our trait database, making a total of 2,139 bee species.

Traits were selected based on criteria designed to provide comprehensive insights into pollinator morphology, biology, ecology, and threat mitigation. First, we included traits that span the entire pollinator life cycle and capture key morphological characteristics, with emphasis on both larval and adult stages (e.g., adult body length, number of generations, and duration of larval development). Second, we selected traits that improve our understanding of pollinator biology (e.g., nesting behavior, adult and larval nutrition, and microhabitats), ecology (e.g., sociality and behavior), and spatial / temporal distribution (e.g., Area of Occupancy (AOO), Extent of Occurrence (EOO), flight period, and macrohabitat). These traits support the design and prioritization of targeted conservation actions. Third, we incorporated traits that reflect pollinator responses to major threats such as climate change and habitat loss (e.g., hairiness, Species Temperature Index (STI), Species Continentality Index (SCI), and habitat associations). Finally, by considering a maximum of traits shared between bees and hoverflies, covering both effect and response traits, we aim to support research across a broad diversity of pollinator species and research themes. Data collection

Definition of traits and methodology

The selected traits were grouped into five sections: (i) nesting, (ii) larva, (iii) adult morphology, (iv) adult ecology/behavior, and (v) distribution; each section included six to 10 sub-sections, i.e., traits. For traits in which a species can only fall into a single category or value (e.g., nest type and body length), the data were recorded in a single column. For traits with multiple states (e.g., nesting behavior, larval microhabitat, or number of generations), multiple columns were assigned for each trait state, with “yes” or “no” as categories. Functional traits used in the analyses are summarised in Table 1, while Supplementary Table S1 provides detailed information on the traits by sections and sub-sections, the states and categories, the types of variables (i.e., Boolean, text, real number, and integer number) with the corresponding units (e.g., mm and km²) and the data sources. Below, we define the operational meaning of each trait within the context of this dataset and provide supporting references. Although full trait state lists are provided separately (Table S1), specific states may be mentioned where relevant to clarify classification or usage. Additionally, where applicable, we outline the protocols to measure the traits. The pollinator groups associated with each trait are specified in brackets. Adult morphology:

Body length (categories) (bees and hoverflies): we defined categorical length classes to enhance comparability among taxa and to enable analyses that benefit from discretized trait values rather than continuous measurements alone. For bees, three body length categories were defined using the distribution of the species inter-tegular distance (ITD) values of females (i.e. workers for social bees): small (0.63 mm < ITD ≤ 3.09 mm), medium (3.09 mm < ITD ≤ 5.56 mm), and large (5.56 mm < ITD ≤ 8.02 mm). These thresholds were empirically derived from the observed ITD distribution in the dataset. For hoverflies, body length data were inferred based on the published information on species dimensions21,36 and classified into five categories: small (<7 mm), medium (7–12 mm), large (>12 mm), small or medium (if a species length range encompasses two categories, e.g., 5–8 mm), and medium or large. This approach allowed for consistent categorical representation of body size across both datasets, facilitating cross-taxon comparisons of size-related traits.

Inter-tegular distance (ITD) (bees): is an estimate of body length, corresponding to the length between the two points of wing insertion (tegulae)37. To estimate bee species ITD, we measured and averaged the ITD of minimum five individuals per sex as a general rule. However when the species was very poorly represented in entomological collections, we evaluated the species ITD based on 1 to 4 specimens. For eusocial species (e.g. Apis mellifera and Bombus), we measured the ITD mean for 10 individuals per caste (males, queen and worker), when possible, to take into account intra-specific variation. To measure specimen ITD, we used either a digital caliper, a digital microscope (KEYENCE, VHX-970F), an ocular micrometer, or length measurement software (i.e., ImageJ). Due to differences in tool accuracy, the overall measurement precision was estimated at roughly 0.1 mm. For each species, we recorded the number of individuals the average measure was based on.

Hair length (bees): represents the average hair length on the mesoscutum of three bees per species and sex39. To minimize potential bias due to hair abrasion or loss in older material, only well-preserved individuals with largely intact hair cover were included in the analysis. For each specimen, we measured the length of five randomly selected hairs on the mesoscutum. When hair length varied across different regions of the mesoscutum, we measured hair length for each distinct area separately. Then the proportion of the surface occupied by each area was recorded, and the overall mean hair length for the mesoscutum was weighted based on the surface occupied by each distinct area. We calculated hair length using a digital microscope (KEYENCE model VHX-970F). For each species, we recorded the mean hair length for each sex/caste and provided the corresponding number of individuals measured.

Hairiness index (bees): is a quantitative measure involving hair density and hair length. To measure this trait we used the protocol developed by Roquer-Beni et al.39. The protocol suggests the measurement of hair length and density on three body parts: the face, the ventral and the dorsal surface of the thorax. However, given the high and moderate correlations reported between these body parts for hair length and hair density, respectively39, we measured hair components on a single body part: the dorsal surface of the thorax, specifically the mesoscutum, due to its role in thermoregulation40. The hairiness index was calculated by multiplying hair density by hair length. For each species, we calculated the mean hairiness index for each sex/caste and provided the corresponding number of individuals we measured.

Adult ecology and behavior:

Sociality (bees and hoverflies): reflects the interaction between the individuals of the same species (i.e., cooperation, communication, and organization). For bees we applied the three categories described in Michez et al.27 and Michener41: solitary, parasocial and eusocial. In solitary bees each female constructs and provisions her own nest independently, without interacting with her adult offspring or other individuals of the same species, except for mating. Parasocial refers to individuals of the same species and generation that collaborate to build a shared nest. We distinguish four sub-categories of parasocial species: communal, quasisocial, semisocial, and subsocial species27. Eusocial species are characterized by colony-living groups, with a division of labour, morphological differentiation into castes, and overlapping generations. Since the sociality is connected to the nesting ecology, and brood parasites and inquiline species do not construct or partition their nests, we retained the associated parasitism categories (brood parasite, obligate inquiline and facultative inquiline). All studied hoverfly species are solitary.

Area of occupancy (AOO) (bees and hoverflies): defines the area occupied by a species, excluding cases of vagrancy. The measure reflects the fact that a species will not usually occur throughout the area of its extent of occurrence, which may contain unsuitable or unoccupied habitats44. The IUCN recommendation for the grid size used to calculate AOO is 2 × 2 km. This grid is placed over all selected taxon records within the user-defined area. AOO corresponds to the number of occupied cells in a uniform grid that covers the entire range of taxa (i.e., number of occupied cells * area of an individual cell)44. Data sources

The data on traits of hoverfly species were collected from the available literature sources13,21,29,30,36,46, and / or based on opinions of expert taxonomists and field observations (Table S1). Namely, the data extracted from the existing literature include: larval microhabitat, host, larval nutrition, duration of larval development, body length (mm, raw data), inundation tolerance of larvae, number of generations, flight period (months)21,29,30,49, AOO and EOO in Europe44. The indices STI and SCI were calculated using existing data. Information on the larval location, the number of larval microhabitats, body length (categories), spatial distribution in Europe and flight period (season) were inferred based on the available data36,43,46. The flight behavior, flight height, body hair (categories), body structure, adult macrohabitat and the tolerance to human influence were scored from field observations and based on opinions of expert taxonomists, as described in Miličić et al.36. Other traits assessed for hoverfly species, i.e., nest, sociality and buzz pollinator were provided based on experience / opinions of expert taxonomists and field observations.

For bees, the starting point was the personal database of Stuart Roberts. The database includes six traits: inter-tegular distance (ITD), sociality, nesting ecology, larval diet breadth, buzz pollinator and number of generations per year. All these traits, except ITD, are categorical and did not encompass the majority of European bee species. This database was designed, built, maintained and curated by Stuart Roberts, initially as part of the ALARM project (EU FP6 SUST-DEV) in 2005, and subsequently as an integral part of the STEP project (EU FP7 ENVIRONMENT). Since 2014, the database has been maintained entirely at personal expense. The database covers a wide-ranging suite of traits and deals with all bee taxa found within the boundaries of Europe as defined by IUCN44. The database is not publicly available as a published dataset; access to the underlying data can be obtained by contacting the data owner or via the corresponding author. In the present study, this resource was further curated, standardised and expanded. The list of references consulted for this database can be summarized accordingly. Material includes national and regional bee faunas, generic monographs, local reports, academic papers, taxonomic revisions and descriptions, ecological treatments, PhD theses (unpublished), Red Lists (local, national, regional and continental), ad hoc observations reported in the grey literature and social media along with unpublished personal observations and those of colleagues.

We extended the list of traits provided by Stuart Roberts to 31 bee traits (Table S1) and additional species. We categorized the new bee trait data into two categories: (i) empirical measurements and (ii) estimated data. Empirical datasets include existing data provided by colleagues (i.e. taxonomists and experts on national bee faunas) and literature, and new data acquired by the team of UMons. Data from taxonomists are based on their field observations, expertise, and private datasets. This data covers most of the bee clades found in Europe, including Melittidae (Denis Michez), Bombus (Pierre Rasmont and Guillaume Ghisbain), Anthophorinae (Pierre Rasmont), Eucerini (Achik Dorchin), Nomada (Jakub Straka), cuckoo bees (Petr Bogusch and Jakub Straka), Andrena and Panurgini (Thomas James Wood), Panurginae (Sébastien Patiny), Hylaeus (Romain Le Divelec), and Colletes (Michael Kuhlmann). Furthermore, we contacted the extensive expert network of the Laboratory of Zoology (UMons), including national and local experts and researchers across Europe to access private, unpublished data. Regarding literature, we gathered information from published literature, focusing on bee clades lacking taxonomic expertise. These groups include Megachilini, Osmiini, and Xylocopini. The references consulted for the trait data of each species are listed in the trait database. In addition to the existing data on bee traits, the UMons team conducted new measurements for ITD and hairiness traits on newly collected material and specimens from historical and reference collections. First, regarding new material, we organized targeted field trips across five European countries (Greece, Italy, Belgium, France, and Sweden) over two consecutive years, 2022 and 2023 to collect abundant and diverse fresh material. Second, to gain access to less common and rare species, we visited the collections of three European museums: the Biological Museum of the University of Lund (Sweden), the Stuttgart State Museum of Natural History (Germany), and the Biodiversitätszentrum Oberösterreich (Austria). We have also accessed the material of the reference collection of the University of Mons and private taxonomist collections, including the collection of Thomas James Wood, which covers different bee groups, Achik Dorchin (Eucerini), Christophe Praz (Megachilini) and Jakub Straka (Nomada).

Regarding the estimated/inferred data, seven bee traits were derived from existing data: tongue length, STI, SCI, EOO, AOO, flight period, number of larval microhabitats and larval location.

Data Records

The database presenting the trait data of European hoverflies and bees has been deposited in the repository Zenodo (folder output), under the name traits_raw_bees_hoverflies.xlsx and it is accessible from https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.1803235750. It is available for download as a Microsoft Excel Worksheet (.xlsx) file. For the 913 hoverfly species and 2,139 bee species the relevant traits (Table 1) have been classified into the five sections and 39 sub-sections, i.e., traits. Eighteen traits were mutual for hoverflies and bees, while eight traits were available only for hoverflies, and 13 only for bees (Fig. 2). The trait database consists of 3,055 rows and 108 columns. The first three rows define the column titles (i.e., section, sub-section, and possible trait states), while the remaining rows refer to the trait data of each species. The first five columns contain taxonomic ranks, i.e., orders, families, genera, species, and combination of genera and species names, and the last two columns indicate the validator and references, whereas the rest 101 columns relate to 101 trait modalities. In the cases of not applicable / not available information, the abbreviation “NA” is used. In total, 166,906 cells contain associated (non-NA) data.

Along with the traits database of European hoverfly and bee species, a corresponding metadata file has been deposited in the same repository (folder output), under the name metadata_traits_raw.xlsx. This metadata file is also available for download as a Microsoft Excel Worksheet (.xlsx). The table consists of 48 rows and nine columns. The first two columns contain names of the fields of the traits database table and their descriptions; the remaining seven columns refer to the information on traits and the structure of their presentation in the database: trait description, trait states, categories, pollinator taxa, type of variable, unit, data source. Along with the two title ones, the five rows represent taxonomic information, the 39 traits (classified into five sections, i.e., nesting, larva, adult morphology, ecology/behavior, and distribution), the validator and references.

To complement the original trait dataset, we additionally provide a GBIF-aligned version accompanied by corresponding metadata. This version consists of a Taxon Core table (dwc_taxon_core_bees_hoverflies.csv) and a MeasurementOrFact (dwc_MOF_bees_hoverflies.csv) table containing 11 columns and 72,196 records. The conversion was performed by systematically reshaping the original trait matrix into atomic measurement records, whereby each trait with an assigned value was transformed into an individual MOF entry, while “no” and “NA” fields were excluded. This dual provision enhances clarity, facilitates reuse, and supports integration into biodiversity data infrastructures. Dataset and metadata (metadata_traits_MOF.xlsx) are deposited in the output folder of Zenodo repository, and are accessible at: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.1803235750. Furthermore, the R code used to transform the original trait dataset into a GBIF-aligned (Darwin Core–compliant) version is provided in the folder code at the same repository link. All files required to reproduce the full workflow are stored in the folder input, including trait_data_input.csv, which contains the machine-readable version of the raw trait dataset used by the conversion script; trait_metadata.csv, which provides information on variable types, units, trait groups and definitions used to generate the MeasurementOrFact table; and taxonomic_backbone_bees_hoverflies.csv, which contains the taxonomic backbone used to retrieve authorship information for bees26 and hoverflies25.

Data Overview

A descriptive summary of the trait composition is presented in Table S2. The traits and trait states are presented by the share percentages of the number of species in the whole sample. For most of the traits the cumulative value is 100% because the choice of possible categories is mutually exclusive. However, as previously explained in the Methods section, some traits are defined with non-mutually exclusive choice, i.e., in these cases a species could be characterized with multiple trait states. For example, a hoverfly species may use several “larval microhabitats”, thus the given percentages are not cumulative.

Technical Validation

The validation of bee trait data was conducted through virtual or in-person workshops with expert taxonomists. Trait data for each taxonomic group (i.e., family, genus, or tribe) were extracted and assigned to one or more experts for review, validation, and correction. The traits of Megachilini, Osmiini and Xylocopini were validated based on available literature, while those of Melecta, Habropoda, and Thyreus remained unvalidated due to a lack of literature and expertise. The technical validation process followed two steps (Fig. 3): (i) validation of existing data and (ii) provision of missing data. During validation, experts assessed the data based on their expertise, literature sources, and material from their collections. They confirmed accuracy, corrected errors, or excluded unreliable data. When experts lacked evidence to verify the accuracy of certain information, it was retained but marked as unvalidated. Details on validated and unvalidated data are provided in the file “trait_validation_bees.xlsx” deposited in Zenodo (folder output), and are accessible from https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.1803235750. In the second step, when information for a particular species and trait was unknown, taxonomists supplied the missing data when possible. This was based on their expertise, available literature, or material from their private collections.

Validation workflow of the bee trait database.

Technical validation of the hoverfly database was performed using established best practices for these data types. All data were entered manually and cross-checked by all authors to examine the validity and assess the accuracy of the included trait information. Only the reliable sources were used, therefore the authors are confident of the correctness of the included data. In several cases where the trait state of a particular species was not available in the literature, the species was assigned the most common trait state observed in other species of the same genus, provided the authors had sufficient confidence in the assignment. In the case of doubtful information regarding a particular trait, this record was not included in the database. The author team comprises hoverfly experts thus ensuring high level of quality control while providing the best knowledge. The future users are encouraged to notify the corresponding author of any potential errors or additions to ensure the data are accurate and up to date; these updates will also be subject to the technical validation process.
