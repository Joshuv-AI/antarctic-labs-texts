Impact of Community‐Based Hypertension Screening on Blood Pressure Management and the Role of Socioeconomic Status
Authors: Hui Gao, Ying‐Quan Wang, Yang Zheng, Qun‐Di Yang, Yu Jiang, Xiao‐Xia Liu, Dan‐Ni Liu, Lei Zhang, Yu‐Zhuo Wang, Xue‐Na La, Qing‐Hua Xia, Yan Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA study was conducted to estimate the impact of hypertension screening on blood pressure measured 1, 2, and 3 years after baseline, as well as how its impact varies across socioeconomic statuses.MethodsEmbedded within a subcohort of the ChinaHEART (China Health Evaluation and Risk Reduction Through Nationwide Teamwork) project, blood pressure was measured both at baseline and follow‐up visits. Information about socioeconomic factors, including education, annual household income, occupational status, and urbanity, was also collected. A sharp regression discontinuity design was used to estimate the impact of hypertension screening on blood pressure measured 1, 2, and 3 years later. We also explored the role of baseline socioeconomic status by adding the interaction term.ResultsThe regression discontinuity analysis found no effect of baseline hypertension screening on the primary outcomes. When stratified by socioeconomic status, regarding systolic blood pressure measured 1 year later, the impact of screening was 5.52 mm Hg (95% CI, −1.55 to 12.59) for individuals with primary or lower education, 0.80 mm Hg (95% CI, −4.00 to 5.59) for middle education, and −7.39 mm Hg (95% CI, −12.03 to −2.75) for high school or above education, with a significant trend observed (P for interaction=0.002). In addition, among individuals with high school or above education, the impact on systolic blood pressure 1, 2, and 3 years later was −7.39 mm Hg (95% CI, −12.03 to −2.75), −8.30 mm Hg (95% CI, −15.79 to −0.81), and −4.63 mm Hg (95% CI, −12.71 to 3.44), respectively. These patterns, although insignificant, were also found for other socioeconomic factors. The impact on diastolic blood pressure was smaller and insignificant.ConclusionsThe impact of community‐based hypertension screening on systolic blood pressure was affected by individual’s education level and lasted only in the short term.Keywords: blood pressure, regression discontinuity design, socioeconomic statusSubject Categories: Epidemiology, Primary Prevention, High Blood Pressure

Hypertension is one of the most important and preventable risk factors for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and mortality in China. 1 Early detection and management of hypertension, which typically consists of lifestyle changes and blood pressure‐lowering medication treatment, are vital to ensure optimal cardiovascular health. However, only 45% of Chinese adults with hypertension were aware of their condition, only 30% were taking blood pressure‐lowering medication, and just 7% had achieved normal blood pressure levels. 2 Therefore, efficient and cost‐effective population‐wide prevention strategies are urgently needed to help detect individuals with hypertension and motivate them to take actions.

Community‐based screening is typically a population‐level prevention strategy, in which individuals are informed of their hypertensive status and encouraged to take related lifestyle changes or consult for medical treatment. However, a critical question is whether this “light touch” intervention is effective at improving blood pressure management, with mixed results reported. For example, studies conducted in China and South Africa found that hypertension screening reduced blood pressure at 2 years, 3 , 4 whereas only modest change in dietary pattern up to 2 years after hypertension diagnosis was observed in the United States. 5 In addition, a recent study conducted in Germany does not support any favorable health effect of the blood pressure screening. 6 These differences may due to the heterogeneity regarding the study context and also population characteristics. By now, few studies have explored the effect of community‐based screening at different yearly level follow‐up intervals, although this evidence can help tailor the timing and design of prevention strategy. Furthermore, socioeconomic status also affects health behavior change after noncommunicable disease diagnoses, 7 , 8 yet the effect of socioeconomic status on the impact of community‐based hypertension screening remains unclear.

In the context that blood pressure measurements are inevitably subject to random variability caused by measurement error, individuals with a blood pressure measurement just above the diagnosis threshold for hypertension would have similar baseline characteristics to people with a measurement just below the threshold. Therefore, by using the regression discontinuity design to compare outcomes in the groups with blood pressure just above and just below the threshold, 9 we aimed to investigate the causal impact of hypertension screening on blood pressure measured 1, 2, and 3 years later, as well as how the impact of this screening varies across different socioeconomic statuses.

METHODS

Data Availability Statement

The data are not publicly available. The ChinaHEART (China Health Evaluation and Risk Reduction Through Nationwide Teamwork) project provides conditional data access only for qualified researchers with legitimate requests; a formal application and research proposal is required. Please contact cvd-project@nccd.org.cn to seek approval for data access.

Study Population

This study was embedded within a subcohort of the ChinaHEART project. The ChinaHEART project is a government funded public health program designed for screening of cardiovascular disease risk and for intervention in community‐based populations throughout China, with detailed study design reported elsewhere. 10 Briefly, 351 county‐level regions in all 31 provinces in the mainland of China were selected as study sites, and participants recruited met the following inclusion criteria: aged 35 to 75 years, lived in the study site for >6 months within 12 months before baseline survey, and signed informed consent. The ChinaHEART project was approved by the ethics committee at the China National Center for Cardiovascular Diseases (approval number 2014‐574). All enrolled participants provided written informed consent.

Based on the baseline interview and examinations, participants were divided into 2 groups: high CVD risk group and non‐high CVD risk group. Individuals were classified as being in the high CVD risk group if they met at least 1 of 4 criteria: (1) major cardiovascular events history (myocardial infarction, percutaneous coronary intervention, coronary artery bypass graft or stroke); (2) a predicted CVD risk ≥20% based on World Health Organization/International Society of Hypertension cardiovascular risk prediction charts 11 ; (3) severely abnormal blood lipid levels (low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥4.14 mmol/L or high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol <0.78 mmol/L); or (4) severely high blood pressure (systolic blood pressure >160 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure >100 mm Hg). Then, those high CVD risk participants and also a randomly selected sample of non‐high CVD risk participants would receive a more comprehensive assessment of their cardiovascular health status (high‐risk questionnaire) and be regularly invited to active follow‐up visits on an annual basis.

The subcohort used here was conducted in Shanghai, China. From August 2017 to February 2023, a total of 111 218 residents were recruited at baseline survey with 17 182 attended any follow‐up survey. After exclusion of those with self‐reported hypertension or blood pressure‐lowering medication use (n=9876), unavailable blood pressure measurement at baseline (n=1), 7305 individuals remained with 4747 identified as high CVD risk and 2558 as non‐high CVD risk. Among those, participants with blood pressure reassessment at 1 (n=6466), 2 (n=3871), and 3 (n=2218) years after baseline survey were considered eligible for related analysis.

Data Collection and Variable Definition

Structured electronic questionnaires (baseline and high‐risk questionnaire) with a real‐time logical check function were administered by trained field workers through standardized in‐person interviews. The participants’ demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, including age, sex, education level, occupation, annual household income, medical insurance, and marital status, were collected. Health lifestyles (ie, smoking, alcohol consumption, leisure‐time physical activity) and health status (ie, medical history, medication use, family history) was also collected.

Regarding socioeconomic factors, education was categorized according to the International Standard Classification of Education scale: primary school or lower was equivalent to level 0 and 1; middle school was equivalent to level 2; high school or above was equivalent to level 3 to 8. Household income was measured by asking “What was your total household income in the past year (RMB)?” and was dichotomized as whether or not >50 000 RMB per year. Occupational status was dichotomized as retired or not. Regarding lifestyle factors, according to former study, 12 smoking status was categorized into whether or not currently smoking. Alcohol use was measured by asking the participants’ drinking frequency and the amount of alcohol consumption during a typical drinking day, and drinkers who drank no more than 25 g (3 units) for men or 15 g (2 units) for women per day on average were defined as having moderate alcohol use. Leisure‐time physical activity was quantified by types of typical activity at different intensity, frequency, and duration per week, with sufficient leisure‐time physical activity defined as at least 150 minutes moderate intensity aerobic activities or 75 minutes vigorous intensity aerobic activities per week. A qualitative food frequency questionnaire was used to collect habitual dietary intake by asking frequency of eating 12 food components over the previous year, and a healthy diet pattern was defined as having ≥4 based on weekly food intake of 6 components (daily intake of fresh fruit, fresh vegetables, and whole grains; eating fish and other seafood at least 1 day per week, beans and bean food at least 4 days per week, and red meat fewer than 7 days per week).

Physical examinations, including blood pressure, height, and weight, were conducted using standardized procedures and unified devices, both at baseline and follow‐up visits. Body mass index was calculated as body weight divided by the square of height (kg/m2). Blood pressure was measured twice in the right upper arm after 5 minutes of rest in a seated position using a standardized electronic blood pressure monitor (Omron HEM‐7430; Omron, Kyoto, Japan). If the difference between the 2 systolic blood pressure measurements was >mm Hg, a third measurement was obtained and the average of the last 2 readings was used. Levels of blood lipids (CardioChek PA Analyzer; Polymer Technology Systems, USA) and glucose (BeneCheck BK6‐20M Multi‐Monitoring System; Suzhou Pu Chun Tang Biotechnology, China) were also measured, with last meal before the tests recorded.

Regression Discontinuity Design

Based on their screening results, all the identified participants with high CVD risk and also randomly selected non‐high CVD risk participants would be intervened based on a prespecified strategy, including personalized lifestyle suggestions, primary prevention (eg, providing medical recommendations for those who already have dyslipidemia or diabetes), as well as secondary prevention (eg, providing treatment recommendations to improve the prognosis of cardiovascular patients). Regarding blood pressure management, a therapeutic lifestyle change (TLC) targeting hypertension would be recommended for those newly diagnosed as having hypertension. This TLC was drafted based on the 2010 Chinese guidelines for the management of hypertension, 13 and includes result notification, pamphlet distribution (to make individual aware of the dangers of hypertension), and individualized lifestyle suggestions (reducing salt intake, doing physical activity, maintain a balanced diet, weight management, smoking cessation, and lowering alcohol consumption). The detailed information was provided in Table S1.

The regression discontinuity design can be used when a threshold is applied to a continuous assignment variable to decide whether an individual receives an intervention or not. 9 In this study, we used a sharp regression discontinuity design (where everyone on one side of the threshold is treated and no one on the other side is treated) to establish the impact of this hypertension‐related TLC intervention. The continuous assignment variable here was the blood pressure measurement at baseline. The threshold used in this analysis was the hypertension threshold (systolic blood pressure of 140 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure of 90 mm Hg). The research workers used these thresholds to decide who should receive TLC. Our main outcome measures were the participant’s systolic and diastolic blood pressure measured 1, 2, and 3 years after baseline survey.

The local average effect of baseline screening on blood pressure 1 year after are given by β1. Then, we explored the heterogeneity in impact between different socioeconomic statuses, including education level (primary school or lower, middle school, and high school or above), annual household income (whether or not >50 000 RMB), occupational status (whether or not retired), and urbanity (rural versus urban), by adding an interaction term between individual socioeconomic variable and the indicator variable (τi).

The choice of bandwidth is critical given that using a smaller bandwidth will induce lower bias but higher variance, whereas using a larger bandwidth will cause higher bias but lower variance, here we comprehensively determined the optimal bandwidth around the threshold by using the data‐driven optimal bandwidth method, 14 based on former study, 3 and also instrument manual of the electronic blood pressure monitor. During the main analysis, the optimal bandwidth (rounded to the nearest integer) was 6 mm Hg for systolic and diastolic blood pressure.

In addition, we conducted 2 subgroup analyses (high versus non‐high CVD risk, male versus female) to check for any effect modification caused by baseline CVD risk status and sex. We also investigated subsequent metabolic changes potentially induced by the intervention, including effects on body mass index, waist circumference, lipid, and fasting blood glucose. In addition, sensitivity analyses were performed to test the robustness of our main results by excluding participants with prevalent diabetes, CVD, or cancer at baseline and by using a range of potential bandwidths.

The random forest method was used to deal with missing covariates (7.2%). Data were handled and analyzed with SPSS Statistics version 25.0.0.1 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R, CRAN version 4.3.2. All analyses were performed at the significance level of 0.05 (2 tailed).

Premises of the Regression Discontinuity Design

SinBecausece the regression discontinuity design requires no manipulation of the assignment variable around the threshold, we demonstrated the related histograms for baseline systolic and diastolic blood pressure (Figure 1) to visually check for the presence of manipulation and found no visual evidence of jumping around the threshold. In addition, the regression discontinuity design also requires that no other interventions or changes that affect the outcome were correlated with the selected threshold (140 or 90 mm Hg for hypertension). As shown in Table 1 and Table S3, almost all the characteristics were balanced around the threshold. Regarding systolic blood pressure analysis, only leisure‐time physical activity and prevalent cancer were borderline different between below‐ and above‐threshold groups within 1‐year sample. Regarding diastolic blood pressure analysis, there were slight imbalances in sex and diabetes‐related variables within the 1‐year sample. Therefore, in our regression analysis, we adjusted only for baseline age and sex.

Impact Estimates

Table 2 shows the results of the main outcome variables (systolic and diastolic blood pressure measured 1, 2, and 3 years after baseline survey). The regression discontinuity analysis found no effect of baseline hypertension screening followed by targeted TLC intervention on the primary outcomes considered. Interestingly, when we stratified participants based on their socioeconomic status, those with higher socioeconomic level tended to have a reduced systolic blood pressure afterward (Figure 2; Table S4). To be specific, regarding systolic blood pressure measured 1 year later, the impact of baseline screening was 5.52 mm Hg (95% CI, −1.55 to 12.59) for individuals with primary or lower education level, 0.80 mm Hg (95% CI, −4.00 to 5.59) for those with middle education level, and −7.39 mm Hg (95% CI, −12.03 to −2.75) for those with high school or above education level, and a significant trend was observed (P for interaction=0.002). In addition, when we compared the impacts across different follow‐up intervals, the significant protective effect induced by baseline intervention among higher socioeconomic level gradually decreased with longer follow‐up intervals (Figure 2; Table S4). For example, among individuals with high school or above education, the impact of baseline screening on systolic blood pressure 1, 2, and 3 years later was −7.39 mm Hg (95% CI, −12.03 to −2.75), −8.30 mm Hg (95% CI, −15.79 to −0.81), and −4.63 mm Hg (95% CI, −12.71 to 3.44), respectively. Among those living at urban area, the impact on systolic blood pressure 1, 2, and 3 years later was −3.49 mm Hg (95% CI, −6.69 to −0.29), −2.85 mm Hg (95% CI, −7.59 to 1.89), and 1.93 mm Hg (95% CI, −3.56 to 7.42), respectively. However, the impacts for diastolic blood pressure were relatively smaller compared with systolic blood pressure and insignificant.

Additional and Sensitivity Analysis

Results of subgroup analyses revealed no difference regarding the impact of baseline hypertension screening between individuals with high and non‐high CVD risk (Tables S5 and S6; Figure S1) or between male and female participants (Tables S7 and S8; Figure S2). In addition, metabolic outcomes measured 1 year later, including body mass index, waist circumference, and lipid, did not show any significant change, except for fasting blood glucose. To be specific, socioeconomic status affected the effect of baseline screening on fasting blood glucose, with significant impact observed for those who still employed (−1.01 mmol/L [95% CI, −1.66 to −0.35]) and living at rural area (−1.05 mmol/L 9[5% CI, −1.96 to −0.14]) (Tables S9 and S10).

Results remained robust when we excluded those with prevalent diabetes, CVDs, or cancer at baseline (Tables S11 and S12; Figure S3). Furthermore, we tested the robustness of our main findings by plotting the impact of baseline screening on systolic blood pressure 1 year later among individuals with different education levels against different bandwidths and extrapolating impact size to a bandwidth of 1. As the bandwidth shrinks, the impact size becomes larger for systolic blood pressure across different education levels (Figure 3), which supports our main results that the impact of hypertension screening on systolic blood pressure was affected by education level.

DISCUSSION

Embedded within a subcohort of the ChinaHEART project, our study found that the impact of baseline hypertension screening on later blood pressure was affected by an individual’s socioeconomic status and that the related targeted TLC intervention could result in a large and significant reduction in systolic blood pressure only for those with higher education level. More important, this protective effect was observed for only 1 year after baseline screening and would gradually fade with longer follow‐up periods. The lowering impact of screening on diastolic blood pressure was smaller and insignificant.

Although the prevalence of hypertension in China has slightly declined since 2010, its treatment and control remain low, 15 and the disease burden of CVD attributable to high systolic blood pressure still increased between 2005 and 2018. 16 Lack of awareness is a major obstacle to successfully reducing hypertension at the population level in China 2 ; community‐based hypertension screening can improve the awareness of hypertensive status and provide related lifestyle suggestions, which could, in turn, induce favorable behavioral changes. 3 However, socioeconomic inequality can hinder this change, as previous study found that low socioeconomic status was associated with continuing or initiating physical inactivity and continuing smoking after the diagnosis of noncommunicable diseases. 7 Here we found that the impact of hypertension screening on later blood pressure was affected by individual’s socioeconomic status, that the related TLC intervention could reduce later systolic blood pressure only for those with higher education level. This finding provides valuable implications for primary prevention prioritizing people with low education level and requiring more individualized and effective prevention strategy.

Across all main analyses, the reduction in systolic blood pressure 1 year later was ∼7 mm Hg among individuals with high education attainment, which is a quite large reduction for such an information intervention conducted at the community level. Given that former nationwide studies conducted among middle‐aged and older Chinese adults reported that hypertension was the predominant factor for cardiovascular mortality, 1 and that a 10 mm Hg reduction in systolic blood pressure corresponded to a 30% decrease in CVDs, 17 our findings highlight the clinical and population health relevance of the impact of community‐based blood pressure screening. Similar to a former study, 3 we did not observe any effect of baseline screening on diastolic blood pressure. Although both systolic and diastolic blood pressure have been considered for the hypertension management in current Chinese guideline and also for the prevention strategy in the design of the ChinaHEART project, systolic blood pressure is more closely associated with cardiovascular events than diastolic blood pressure, which is deeply rooted in people’s mind. Therefore, despite targeted lifestyle suggestions have been equally provided for those with higher systolic or diastolic blood pressure, it is plausible that those with raised diastolic blood pressure might be less likely to make required behavior change compared with those with raised systolic blood pressure.

Another factor worth noticing is that the impact of baseline screening on subsequent systolic blood pressure was observed only 1 year later and would gradually diminish with longer follow‐up periods. According to this, community‐based screening should be offered annually to maximize its effectiveness. In complement with aforementioned findings that only those with high education attainment would gain significant health effect from current intervention, the design of future primary prevention strategies may be tailored based on this evidence in terms of the follow‐up interval and the individuals who would benefit most.

Aside from our recommendations, the impact of community‐based screening could also be further improved, for example, through avenues for intervention improvement (ie, intensified training of health care workers who conduct screening interventions) and additional tools to help communicate the need for behavior change (ie, mobile apps). To be specific, results from the CRHCP (China Rural Hypertension Control Project) confirmed that compared with usual care, nonphysician community health care provider‐led intervention resulted in significant improvement in blood pressure control and also reduction in cardiovascular disease and death, among rural residents in China. 18 , 19 These health care providers (also known as village doctors) work on the front line of basic primary care and public health service in rural China, and most of them have only some medical training (ie, 3 years of vocational or junior medical education). 20 In the CRHCP, these health care providers in the intervention group were trained in standardized blood pressure measurement, protocol‐based antihypertensive treatment, and health coaching (which covered home blood pressure monitoring, medication adherence, and lifestyle modifications). 21 Meanwhile, the increasingly prevalent and capable smartphone technology also offers a promising means of delivering health care interventions to the general population. Mobile apps can deliver different behavioral interventions and help users lead healthier lives. However, there was currently less consistent evidence in support of the effectiveness of mobile apps in improving health behaviors or outcomes, 22 , 23 which may be due to the heterogeneity in study design, content, and delivery. What is more, a recent trial conducted in 48 Chinese residential older adult care facilities found that use of salt substitute (62.5% NaCl and 25% KCl), but not efforts to restrict salt supply, could achieve blood pressure lowering and deliver health benefits. 24 All these feasible, effective, and sustainable prevention strategies could be scaled up in rural or less developed areas where the residents with relatively lower education attainment mostly live.

The key strengths of this study include the prospective cohort design, yearly level follow‐up, and meticulous assessment of blood pressure. We also had detailed information regarding the potential confounders. However, our study has some limitations that need to be addressed. First, information on socioeconomic level and lifestyle was mainly self‐reported, thus measurement errors were inevitable. Second, data for socioeconomic status and behavioral and metabolic factors were collected at baseline. Apart from education level, other covariates might have changed during follow‐up, which could affect the elimination of confounding effect. Third, due to the study design of The ChinaHEART project, all recruited participants would be receive intervention based on a prespecified individualized strategy regarding their cardiovascular health status. Because lifestyle suggestions for those having dyslipidemia share similar components with having hypertension, therefore, although no difference regarding baseline metabolic characteristics was found between those above or below the threshold, our results might be underestimated considering that individuals with blood pressure below the threshold but with dyslipidemia or diabetes would also receive lifestyle suggestions. Fourth, data on adherence to lifestyle recommendations were not collected, which precludes further investigation of potential mediators. In addition, since this analysis was conducted during an early phase of the ChinaHEART study, clinical outcomes (such as coronary heart disease and stroke) were not yet available due to the relatively short follow‐up period; however, as the ChinaHEART study is an ongoing cohort, these outcomes will be incorporated in future updates. Fifth, despite the quasiexperimental nature of the regression discontinuity design, unmeasured confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Therefore, causal interpretation should be made with caution. Finally, this study recruited only local residents living in Shanghai, China and aged 35 to 75 years, limiting the generalizability of our findings to other populations or to other health care settings. For example, we could not conclude that the intervention would be effective in younger adults among whom hypertension has an increasing prevalence 25 and is associated with higher cardiovascular risks. 26 Future studies with more comprehensive measurements regarding adherence and relevant hard outcomes are warranted to validate our findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, community‐based hypertension screening and target TLC intervention reduced systolic blood pressure at the population level. Nevertheless, this impact was significantly affected by an individual’s education level and lasted only in the short term. Policy makers should consider implementing routine blood pressure screening for middle‐aged and older adults in China, and the role of social determinants of health should also be emphasized in primary prevention. More personalized and effective measures, instead of “one‐size‐fits‐all” lifestyle interventions, are needed to achieve the substantial reduction of the socioeconomic inequality in hypertension management.
