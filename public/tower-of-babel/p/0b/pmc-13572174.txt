Research note: Efficient preservation of genome-edited chicken germplasm via N-methylacetamide-based semen cryopreservation
Authors: Thirubasyini Songodan, Hong Jo Lee, Seung Je Woo, Nam Seok Kim, Jae Yong Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid development of genome-edited chicken lines for agricultural and biomedical use requires effective methods for long-term preservation. In birds, cryopreservation of oocytes and embryos is challenging due to the structure of the egg. This makes semen cryopreservation one of the most practical alternatives, however, this method has not been validated in genome-edited chickens. This study evaluated the feasibility of cryopreserving semen from genome-edited chickens using an N-methylacetamide (N-MA)-based protocol. Two genome-edited chicken lines were used in this study which are a DAZL-GFP germ cell reporter line and a RAG1 knockout line. Semen was stored for either one week or one month prior to assessing fertility and hatchability. The results demonstrated that cryopreserved semen from both genome-edited chicken lines maintained their fertilizing capacity, with no significant differences in fertility or hatchability compared to cryopreserved wild-type groups at either storage duration. Hatchability remained above 80% across all groups, indicating that semen cryopreservation did not significantly affect embryo development after successful fertilization. Furthermore, SYBR-14/PI staining revealed no significant differences in sperm viability between genome-edited and control groups, although both showed a significant decrease in viability compared to fresh semen. These findings demonstrate that N-MA-based semen cryopreservation provides a reliable and practical method for the preservation of genome-edited chicken lines.Keywords: Chicken, Genome editing, Germplasm preservation, N-methylacetamide, Semen cryopreservation

Introduction

Advances in poultry breeding and genome-editing technologies have led to the rapid expansion of elite breeding stocks and valuable research lines. Consequently, reliable ex situ conservation strategies are becoming increasingly essential to protect these genetic resources from disease outbreaks, facility closures, and unplanned genetic loss. In contrast to mammals, cryostorage of whole ova or fertilised embryos is constrained by the large and yolk-rich architecture of the avian egg, which has redirected avian biobanking towards germline cells such as primordial germ cells (PGCs) (Nakamura, 2016). While germline competent-cell approaches can enable broader genetic restoration, they require specialised isolation, culture, and transplantation workflows, often demanding substantial optimisation. By comparison, rooster semen can be collected repeatedly using a non-invasive abdominal massage method and then processed and cryopreserved in scalable formats, making semen freezing a practical first-line option for safeguarding valuable male germplasm (Donoghue and Wishart, 2000). This advantage is particularly relevant for genome-edited chicken lines, which require efficient and economically sustainable long-term preservation strategies.

Previous chicken semen cryopreservation studies have tested various freezing and semen handling conditions such as semen dilution ratio, equilibration time, types of packaging, various thawing methods, and usage of different cryoprotectants. Glycerol and amide-based compounds such as dimethylacetamide (DMA) and dimethylformamide (DMF) are some of the most commonly used cryoprotectants in chicken semen cryopreservation (Zong et al., 2023). In our previous study, we had shown successful cryopreservation of White Leghorn (WL) and Korea Ogye chickens using N-Methylacetamide (N-MA) (Lee et al., 2012). However, it is unclear whether post-thaw sperm viability, fertility, and hatchability differ between genome-edited and wild-type (WT) chickens.

In these regards, the present study evaluated whether the previously established N-MA-based semen cryopreservation protocol is applicable to genome-edited chickens. Two genome-edited chicken models developed in previous studies were used to assess the feasibility of semen cryopreservation. The first was a germ cell tracing model generated by inserting green fluorescent protein (GFP) into the last intron of the endogenous DAZL locus (DAZL-GFP). The DAZL-GFP model enables fluorescent tracking and isolation of germ cells throughout chicken embryonic development (Rengaraj et al., 2022). The second model was an immunodeficient chicken model generated through knockout (KO) of the RAG1 gene (RAG1-KO), which disrupts the development of mature B and T lymphocytes and serves as a system for studying immune cell development in chickens (Lee et al., 2022).

Therefore, this study was conducted to determine whether post-thaw reproductive performance, including sperm viability, fertility, and hatchability, differs between genome-edited and WT chickens. In addition, the study evaluated the ability of the N-MA-based protocol to preserve semen quality and fertilizing capacity following both short-term (1 week) and long-term (1 month) cryostorage. We hypothesized that the established N-MA-based cryopreservation protocol would preserve semen from genome-edited chickens as effectively as semen from WT chickens, resulting in comparable post-thaw sperm viability, fertility, and hatchability after both short-term and long-term cryostorage.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and animal care

The use of the WL chickens was approved by the Institute of Laboratory Animal Resources, Seoul National University (SNU-250226-4-1). The animals were maintained in a standard management program at the University Animal Farm, Seoul National University, Korea. Procedures involving animal management, reproduction, and embryo manipulation adhered to the standard operating protocols of our laboratory.

Semen collection and cryopreservation

Semen collected once a week from 10 randomly selected genome-edited chickens and WT roosters by abdominal massage for 3 consecutive weeks. WL chicken breeds of age 30 weeks and above were used in this study. Sasaki’s method was applied for semen cryopreservation (Sasaki et al., 2010). Briefly, ejaculates collected within each week were pooled together using a 1-mm syringe and transferred into precooled 1.5 ml tubes. Next, the semen was mixed with a precooled diluent (I) at a 1:1 ratio. After 30 minutes of incubation at 5°C, a precooled diluent (II) that included 9% N-MA was added to the mixture of semen and diluent I at a ratio of 1:1. About 500 μl of the mixture was packed into disposable straws (13408; MINITÜB, Tiefenbach, Germany) and pre-frozen in liquid nitrogen vapor 4.5 cm above the surface of liquid nitrogen for 30 minutes, then stored in a liquid nitrogen storage tank without sealing. The chemical composition of diluent I consisted of D(+)-glucose 0.2 g (G7528; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), D(+)-trehalose dihydrate 3.8 g (T9531; Sigma), l-glutamic acid, monosodium salt 1.2 g (G1126; Sigma), potassium acetate 0.3 g (P1190; Sigma), magnesium acetate tetrahydrate 0.08 g (MAG512; BioPure, Brampton, ON, Canada), potassium citrate monohydrate 0.05 g (P1722; Sigma), BES 0.4 g (B4554; Sigma), Bis-Tris 0.4 g (B4429; Sigma), and gentamicin sulfate 0.001 g (G3632; Sigma) in 100 ml distilled water. The pH was adjusted to 6.8, and osmotic pressure was adjusted to 360 mOsm/KgH₂O. Diluent II differed only slightly with the addition of 18% N-MA to the solution (M26305; Sigma) resulting in final cryopreserved concentrations of 9% N-MA. The concentration of 9% N-MA was chosen based on our previous study (Lee et al., 2012).

Cryopreserved semen thawing and artificial insemination

The cryopreserved semen was thawed at 5°C for 100 seconds. The thawed semen was then used immediately for artificial insemination in 6 WT WL hens in each experimental group. The volume of thawed semen used was 500 μl per artificial insemination. Artificial insemination was performed twice a week for 3 consecutive weeks. To further examine the long-term effects of stored semen on fertility and hatchability semen stored for 1 week and 1 month was used for artificial insemination. For fresh semen injection, 100 μl of the fresh semen was used.

Sperm viability analysis

To examine sperm viability, we used the LIVE/DEADTM Sperm Viability Kit (Thermo Fisher; L7011) on fresh and cryopreserved semen. After dilution the semen was double stained with SYBR-14 and Propidium Iodide (PI) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After staining, the samples were manually examined under a fluorescence microscope (TU-80; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

To determine whether significant differences in fertility, hatchability and sperm viability existed among fresh and cryopreserved semen groups, the data were first analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When significant main effects were detected, comparisons between individual groups were subsequently performed using Student’s t-tests. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Results and discussion

First, we conducted experiments to examine whether cryopreservation methods using N-MA are adaptable to genome-edited chickens for short term (1 week) and long-term (1 month). Fresh semen and cryopreserved semen of WT chickens were used as a control group. The fertility rate for 1 week of cryopreserved semen of WT, DAZL-GFP and RAG1-KO was 28.7%, 15.0%, and 20.0%, respectively, whereas the fresh semen was 89.7%. No significant difference in fertility rate was found between the cryopreserved WT chicken semen and the cryopreserved genome-edited chicken group (Fig. 1A). In 1 month, the respective fertility rates of WT, DAZL-GFP and RAG1-KO were 34.8%, 17.0%, and 22.3%, respectively, whereas the fresh semen was 93.7%. Again, no significant difference in fertility rate was found between cryopreserved WT and genome-edited chicken groups (Fig. 1B). The hatchability rate was above 80% in all groups for both short-term (Fig. 1C) and long-term (Fig. 1D) cryopreserved semen, indicating that cryopreservation did not significantly affect embryo development after successful fertilization. Collectively, these results confirm that semen cryopreservation using N-MA is applicable in genome-edited chicken lines without compromising embryonic development.

Next, we performed sperm viability analysis on fresh semen and cryopreserved semen to identify the percentage of live sperm cells and to detect how much cryopreservation affects the viability of sperm. The live sperm cells with intact membranes were stained with SYBR-14 and emitted green fluorescence, while sperm cells with damaged membranes were stained with PI, emitting red fluorescence (Fig. 2A and 2B). In 1 week of cryopreserved semen, the percentage of SYBR-14 stained sperm cells in WT, DAZL-GFP and RAG1-KO was 39.8%, 29.9%, and 39.6%, respectively, and fresh sperm was 87.6% (Fig. 2C), whereas after 1 month of cryopreservation, the percentage of SYBR-14 stained sperm cells was found to be 33.1%, 29.1%, and 31.3%, respectively, and fresh semen was 88.5% (Fig. 2D). We found no significant difference between cryopreserved groups. The viability of fresh semen group was significantly higher than the cryopreserved semen group in both short-term and long-term cryopreserved groups (Fig. 2C and 2D).

In this study, the principal reproductive parameters, including fertility, hatchability, and sperm viability, were comparable among cryopreserved groups. These findings are consistent with our previous study, in which fertility rates of 26.6% and 35.7% were achieved using WL semen cryopreserved for 7 and 60 days, respectively. In the same study, sperm viability decreased from 92.7% in fresh semen to 38.2% following cryopreservation (Lee et al., 2012). In contrast, Sasaki et al. (2010) reported a fertility rate of over 80% for four consecutive weeks after weekly insemination of cryopreserved semen of Yakido Roosters. However, differences in fertility rates can be attributed to breed-specific differences of chicken used in the previous study and the current study. As a result, optimization of freezing and thawing protocols may be necessary to accommodate breed-specific differences in cryotolerance and fertilization capacity (Sasaki et al., 2010). Comparable findings have also been reported in mammalian genome-edited models. In a Huntington's disease rhesus macaque model, post-thaw sperm motility and viability did not differ significantly between genome-edited and WT animals, suggesting that the presence of a transgene itself does not inherently reduce resistance to freeze–thaw stress (Putkhao et al., 2013). Although the rhesus macaque study reported higher post-thaw sperm viability in both WT (58.18 ± 1.74%) and genome-edited animals (61.74 ± 3.46%), these values should be interpreted cautiously because mammalian and avian sperm differ substantially in their structural and physiological characteristics, which influence their responses to cryopreservation. Therefore, the comparison is intended only to illustrate the similar trend in post-thaw sperm viability between genome-edited and WT animals, rather than to directly compare cryopreservation outcomes between species or genome editing approaches.

The low sperm viability recorded in this study correlated with the low fertility rate in across all the cryopreserved groups. Avian spermatozoa membranes are rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), making chicken sperm particularly susceptible to oxidative damage during cryopreservation. Excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during the freeze–thaw process initiate lipid peroxidation of membrane PUFAs, resulting in impaired membrane integrity and ultimately contributing to DNA fragmentation (Surai et al., 2001). Zong et al. (2023) reported that cryopreservation caused severe ultrastructural damage to chicken sperm, including swollen mitochondria, reduced mitochondrial matrix density, and enlarged gaps between the acrosome and perforatorium after thawing. Partyka et al. (2010) reported that frozen-thawed fowl semen exhibited significantly higher proportion of DNA-fragmented cells than fresh semen, suggesting that oxidative damage induced during cryopreservation extends beyond membrane injury to compromise genomic integrity. Collectively, these findings suggest that cryopreservation-induced oxidative and structural damage to sperm contributes to the reduced sperm viability observed in this study, which likely underlies the decreased fertility across all cryopreserved groups.

Nevertheless, several limitations in this study should be acknowledged. Firstly, although reduced sperm viability likely contributed to the lower fertility observed after cryopreservation, a more comprehensive evaluation of cryo-induced sperm damage is required to better understand the mechanisms underlying this decline in both genome-edited and WT chicken semen. Therefore, further analysis is needed apart from sperm viability analysis to deeply understand how cryopreservation affects other characteristics of sperm such as sperm motility, acrosomal integrity, reactive oxygen species, and DNA fragmentation index. Secondly, semen cryobanking preserves only male germplasm and cannot conserve female genetic components, particularly the W chromosome and mitochondrial DNA, due to the technical challenges associated with cryopreservation of the large, yolk-rich avian egg (Nakamura, 2016). Therefore, conservation strategies for genome-edited chickens should not rely solely on semen preservation. Greater emphasis should also be placed on the preservation of female germplasm such as PGCs as they can preserve the complete genetic composition of the donor line. An integrated preservation strategy combining semen cryobanking with PGC preservation would provide a more comprehensive framework for safeguarding genome-edited chicken lines and enabling more complete restoration of the original genotype.

In summary, this study represents the first report demonstrating successful semen cryopreservation in genome-edited chickens. The findings indicate that semen from genome-edited chickens can be effectively preserved using the same N-MA-based protocol applied to WT chickens, supporting the feasibility of extending conventional poultry cryobanking methods to genome-edited avian lines. This study provides a practical and cost-effective strategy for preserving valuable genome-edited chicken resources for long-term applications in research, biotechnology, and breeding.
