Ketogenic Diet as Protection for Deprescribing Antipsychotics
Authors: Jann E Schlimme
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeople with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder are almost always treated with continued use of antipsychotics. Tapering of antipsychotics is very often associated with a recurrence of symptoms within a year. This applies particularly to the final steps of reduction from very small doses. Ketosis seems to offer protection against psychotic relapses in these final steps. I report a case series that supports this thesis. Two effects of antipsychotics that disappear after discontinuation of the medication and can be replaced by ketosis could possibly be responsible for this: effects on the mesolimbic system against the background of insufficient GABAergic inhibition in the prefrontal cortex and mesolimbic system; effects on the microglia against the background of increased unspecific neuroinflammation.

Dear Professor Gold,

Over the past few years, in my psychiatric and psychotherapeutic practice, I have become aware of a potential protective effect of a ketogenic diet against recurrent psychotic crisis. The ketogenic diet, in which the body is forced to produce and metabolize ketone bodies instead of glucose through reduced carbohydrate intake, has been known in medicine since 1921 as an antiepileptic treatment.1 Benefits of ketosis have also been reported for neurodegenerative diseases and for people with schizophrenia in terms of cognitive symptoms and metabolic side effects.2 Some of my patients with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder wish to taper off their antipsychotics due to side effects or other personal reasons. However, tapering of antipsychotics is very often associated with a recurrence of symptoms within a year. This applies particularly to the final steps of reduction from very small doses.3 Surprisingly, my patients on a ketogenic diet were able to take these final steps without renewed psychotic crises, which they had not been able to do before.

Z, diagnosed with schizophrenia [ED 2017]. Three unsuccessful attempts at a stepwise reduction, each an individual reduction over about 1.5 years with the clear intention of mimicking the hyperbolic binding curve of antipsychotics. During the first reduction, I treated her as a second opinion psychiatrist parallel to trauma-sensitive psychotherapy, but lost contact after a few months without antipsychotics while she was in good health. Another psychotic episode after 10 months of antipsychotic abstinence (aripiprazole) and again after 6 months of antipsychotic abstinence (olanzapine) and within 3 months of continuing to take olanzapine at a dosage of 2.5 mg. For each psychotic episode she entered a psychiatric hospital, latest in October 2022. After demission in January 2023, she decided to abruptly reduce her antipsychotic (haloperidol), in parallel with 250 mg of quetiapine, which led to a one-week psychotic crisis. Immediately afterwards, she voluntarily induced ketosis (self-tested by blood samples) with intermittent fasting with the intention of achieving mental stability, which she managed for 11 months. While in ketosis and without antipsychotics she felt cognitively fitter, more energetic, and resilient. During this time, she moved from Germany to the United States for work and remained there until January 2024. During this time, she exercised regularly. In December 2023, she stopped the ketogenic diet due to externally induced sleep deprivation on a short trip abroad. Within a week, psychotic experiences recurred (main complaints: delusional relatedness experiences and mutism). She was admitted to a psychiatric hospital for a week and received lorazepam and quetiapine. She did not restart the ketogenic diet. In January 2024, she moved back to her family in Germany, continued treatment with quetiapine 200 mg, but had a psychotic episode in May 2024. She was admitted to a psychiatric hospital, re-diagnosed with catatonic schizophrenia, discharged after about 2 months on quetiapine 450 mg, then gradually reduced to 300 mg. At the same time, she moved to Berlin in the fall of 2024 and contacted me again. She had a mental crisis while on quetiapine that was most likely postpsychotic depression and driven by childhood adversities, with a renewed admission to the psychiatric hospital near her home in December 2024. She actually continues quetiapine and is planning to get back to ketosis.

K was diagnosed with schizoaffective disorder with usually schizomanic episodes and prolonged depressive after-effects [ED 2002; DD schizophrenia DD bipolar disorder]. Over the years, K has been prescribed various antipsychotics, at times in combination with lithium or valproate. Due to side effects (mainly cognitive impairment, feelings of alienation), he has almost annually reduced the medication very quickly within 2-3 weeks on his own initiative after stabilization. As a rule, a new schizomanic episode occurred within a month. In total, K had 14 compulsory admissions to psychiatric hospitals in this way by 2017. In contact with me since 2019, initially for a second opinion regarding his antipsychotics. At the time, K was taking aripiprazole 10 mg and valproate 1000 mg per day. We agreed to a significantly slower reduction of the antipsychotic under a maintenance dose of valproate if he absolutely wanted to. We reduced the dose of aripiprazole in 1 mg increments every 2 months until the dose was 2.5 mg. In December 2020, K had reached this dose, and mental stability was still maintained. In January 2021, a schizomanic episode recurred. The cause was a visit to his homeland, where long-simmering conflicts with his family erupted, as well as the diagnosis of a recurrence [December 2020] of his colon cancer, which was now inoperable at staging [ED February 2020]. K was able to stabilize in an outpatient setting without adjusting his medication. Despite an otherwise unfavorable prognosis, immunotherapy was administered by the oncology department of the Charité University Medicine in Berlin with lasting success. As of now, the patient is cured of his cancer [last check-up in February 2025]. Due to his cancer, K began a ketogenic diet on his own initiative in the summer of 2020, which he did not have serum-controlled. In the summer of 2021, the reduction of the antipsychotic continued with a previous minimal reduction of the valproate to 600 mg/day. In 0.5 mg steps every 2 months, K reached 1.0 mg aripirazole in March 2022 in a mentally stable state. Based on the strictness of his diet, it can be assumed that he has remained ketogenic since the summer of 2020. Subsequently This is followed by a reduction in 0.25 mg increments until he reaches 0.25 mg aripiprazole in January 2023. In February 2023 he has another schizomanic episode. The cause was again a visit home with an outbreak of unresolved inner-family conflict and a change of diet in the family’s context. Admission to a psychiatric hospital, stabilization within 3 months with olanzapine 10 mg and lorazepam 4 mg with reassessment of the diagnosis as possible bipolar disorder. The hospital had previously always diagnosed schizophrenia, although the condition after the patient’s report of delusional persecution, anxiety, abundance of meaning and verbosity, and erraticism always seemed schizomanic to me. During the inpatient stay, the patient was switched to aripirazole, valproate PLUS lithium. Aripirazole was discontinued at the patient’s request the day before discharge. K established ketosis immediately after discharge in mid-June 2023 and stopped valproate and lithium without further ado and of his own accord. He continued ketosis until September 2024, and mental stability continues.

H, diagnosis of schizophrenia [ED 2011], continued combination of antipsychotics over years, since 2016 monotherapy with aripirazole 15 mg. He attempted to gradual withdraw from the medication, but resumed taking it in 2019 after 6 months of being medication-free due to increased psychotic symptoms. In 2019, first contact with me and start of psychotherapy with trauma-therapeutic elements, including lifestyle changes with the omission of psychoactive substances (especially cocaine, alcohol, which had been used intermittently from time to time), increased resumption of the sport (tennis) that was practiced intensively in adolescence. Several unskilled jobs for a few months, then finally a job as a tennis coach. Repeated moves within Germany, continuation of therapy in video format. At the patient’s request, reduction of aripiprazole – roughly following the hyperbolic binding curve – to 1.8 mg within 2 years from 2022 to April 2024. At the beginning of 2024, discussion of ketosis as a potentially protective factor in the reduction of antipsychotics. H is extensively involved with this diet and decides, among other things for health reasons, to start a ketogenic diet. After establishing ketosis, checking with self-administered blood tests, he gradually tapered off the antipsychotic medication over 4 months starting in early summer. He experienced himself as cognitively fitter, with a little more energy (physical and mental) and more resilience. After the reduction, he moved to Spain, had a difficult relationship, returned to Germany in April 2025 and continued to engage in intensive sporting activities, and maintained mental stability. While in ketosis, H has now gone 9 months without antipsychotics.

The three case reports show that ketosis can be a factor in mental stability without antipsychotics. This corresponds to the well-known fact that schizophrenia is not known or cannot be found in hunter-gatherer cultures.4 Among other factors, diet is likely to play a role here, as it is usually ketogenic in hunter-gatherer cultures. After all, the Neolithic Revolution is intimately linked to the development of a predominantly carbohydrate-based diet, although other aspects relevant to the discussed topic (stockpiling and ownership of goods, expansion of social networks beyond close kinship, and development of more hierarchical community structures) also played an important role in this development (5p. 42f). Two scientific rationales could explain this effect: (1) increased GABAergic inhibition under ketosis6 with corresponding inhibitory effects on the mesolimbic system against a background of genetically determined and/or historically acquired insufficient GABAergic inhibition in the prefrontal cortex and mesolimbic system, as described in individuals with schizophrenia7 and (2) increased resilience to non-specific inflammation under ketosis,8,9 which compensates for the loss of the immunomodulatory effect of even low-dose antipsychotics,10 against a background of historically acquired increased non-specific neuroinflammation, as described in individuals with schizophrenia.11
