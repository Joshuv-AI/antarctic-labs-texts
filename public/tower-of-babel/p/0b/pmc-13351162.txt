Ultrasound-assisted cocrystal of emodin and matrine: An opportunity to improve synergistic bioavailability

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Research on natural compounds has always been a hot topic in medical research [1], [2], [3]. The active ingredients extracted from natural plants exhibit high activity and good curative effect on corresponding diseases [4]. However, the active compounds also present certain drawbacks, including low solubility and insufficient stability. These factors ultimately result in decreased bioavailability and reduced therapeutic effectiveness [5]. In recent years, pharmaceutical cocrystals have been developed to enhance the drug dissolution and bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs [6], [7]. Pharmaceutical cocrystals are crystalline solids consisting of an active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) and cocrystal formers (CCFs) bound in the same lattice by non-covalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonds, π-π stacking, van der Waals interactions and halogen bonds [8], [9], [10], [11]. Drug-drug cocrystal technology represents a significant approach for preserving the integrity of chemical structure and simultaneously enhancing the physicochemical properties of drugs at the molecular level [12], [13], [14], [15].

Emodin (EM, Fig. 1a) is a natural product derived from Rhubarb, commonly used in traditional Chinese medicine [16]. EM demonstrated versatile therapeutic and pharmacological activities, including antineoplastic [17], anti-inflammatory [18], anti-oxidant [19], and immunosuppressive effects [20]. Despite its potential, EM encounters challenges including low polarity and poor bioavailability, which have impeded its advancement as an anti-tumor agent [21]. The substantial first-pass metabolism of EM in the liver and intestine further led to its restricted oral bioavailability [22]. Matrine (MA, Fig. 1b) is a naturally occurring alkaloid that belongs to the group of important bioactive compounds derived from Chinese herbs like Sophorae Flavescentis Radix [23]. Numerous studies have shown that MA possesses anti-viral, anti-tumor, immune-suppressive and anti-inflammatory abilities [24], [25], [26], [27], [28]. However, MA has low bioavailability, poor chemical stability and toxicity to the central nervous system [29], and is prone to adverse reactions such as hepatotoxicity and neurotoxicity [30], [31], [32]. Therefore, improving the bioavailability of both EM and MA is crucial for further research and application. Cocrystal technology serves as an effective approach to address issues related to solubility, bioavailability, and toxicity [9], [33]. Cocrystals of EM or MA with other CCFs were separately prepared. It was observed that, compared with monotherapy, after the formation of cocrystals, the bioavailability of drugs increased, and their stability was improved with toxicity reduced [34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39].Fig. 1Molecular structures of (A) emodin (EM; MW = 270.237 g/mol) and (B) matrine (MA; MW = 248.37 g/mol).

Molecular structures of (A) emodin (EM; MW = 270.237 g/mol) and (B) matrine (MA; MW = 248.37 g/mol).

The objective of this study is to construct a novel drug-drug cocrystal by combining EM with MA, expecting a synergistic effect that enhances the bioactivities of both natural products while mitigating the adverse effects induced by high-dose single-drug administration.

Ultrasound-assisted crystallization was selected as the preparation method because it offers distinct advantages over conventional techniques such as solvent stirring or grinding. The key mechanism is acoustic cavitation, the formation, growth, and violent collapse of microbubbles in a liquid under high-intensity ultrasound [40], [41], [42]. Cavitation generates localized hot spots and high-speed microjets, and strongly depends on operating parameters (temperature, acoustic intensity, hydrostatic pressure) and reactor geometry [43]. Consequently, ultrasound-assisted crystallization significantly shortens the nucleation induction period, narrows the metastable zone width, and promotes uniform nucleation, enabling rapid cocrystal formation with high yield and quality [44], [45]. In this work, we systematically investigated the influence of key ultrasonic parameters (power, time, temperature) on cocrystal yield using a one-variable-at-a-time approach followed by response surface methodology [43] (RSM) and artificial neural network (ANN)[46] modeling.

Since the substrates must be dissolved in a suitable solvent before crystallization, drug solvated crystals may form and compete with the desired drug-drug cocrystal. Therefore, we first screened four solvents (ethanol, ethyl acetate, tetrahydrofuran, and methyl tert‑butyl ether) to identify one that dissolves both drugs without forming solvates (Section 2.2.1). Subsequently, three preparation methods (solution stirring, solvent-assisted grinding, and ultrasound-assisted crystallization) were compared, and the ultrasonic method was chosen for its high efficiency (<3 h) and product quality.

To systematically optimize the crystallization process, a Box‑Behnken design (BBD) from RSM was employed, and the experimental data were further modeled using three ANN architectures (BP, GA‑BP, and GA‑ACO‑BP). The detailed principles and parameter settings of these models are described in Section 2.2.5. The optimized EM‑MA cocrystal was then characterized by PXRD, DSC, FT‑IR, SCXRD, stereo microscopy, and SEM. In vitro cytotoxicity against 4T1 breast cancer cells was evaluated, and network pharmacology was used to elucidate the synergistic anti‑tumor targets and pathways.

Materials and chemicals

Preparation and Screening of Cocrystal

Solvent screening

Before preparation, the substrates should be dissolved in proper solvent. Solvent may become a competitor to form drug solvated crystals. To avoid the above problem, appropriate solvent screening should be conducted. EM or MA was dissolved in excess solvent respectively, such as ethyl acetate (EA), ethanol (EtOH), tetrahydrofuran (THF), and methy tert-buty ether (MTBE). The prepared clear solution was then stirred for 6 h at a temperature of 35°C. Subsequently, the solution was transferred to a glass bottle and left at room temperature until the solvent was almost completely evaporated. The crystals were collected and dried for further analysis.

Preparation methods screening

EM and MA with molar ratio of 1:1 were dissolved in the selected solvent MTBE. Then the mixed solution was treated using three crystallization methods at 35°C for 3 h. The solution stirring method involved stirring the mixture continuously at a steady speed. Ultrasound-assisted crystallization was performed using an SK8200HP power-adjustable benchtop ultrasonic bath (KUDOS, Shanghai Kedao Ultrasonic Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The bath had an internal tank size of 500 mm × 300 mm × 150 mm (volume 22.5 L) and operated at a fixed frequency of 53 kHz. The ultrasonic power was 500 W, and the reaction-absorbed acoustic power was determined by calorimetry (5.21 W). The crystallization temperature was maintained at 35°C, and the sonication duration was 3 h. Moreover, the solvent-assisted grinding was performed by placing EM and MA (total 20 mg) in an agate mortar. MTBE was used as the grinding solvent. A total of 5 mL of MTBE was added dropwise in ten portions (500 μL each) over the course of 3 h of continuous grinding with a pestle at room temperature. The grinding was paused briefly for each addition to ensure uniform wetting. The precipitate was analyzed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) to confirm whether the drug-drug crystal had been formed, and the optimal method was identified by comparing the quantity of cocrystal.

Calorimetry (under identical conditions) revealed that the reaction-absorbed acoustic power by the sample was approximately 5.21 W, despite the nominal electrical power of the device being 500 W. This large difference is typical for bath-type sonication systems and confirms that direct calorimetric power measurement is necessary. Notably, the measured 5.21 W power was sufficient to induce efficient cocrystal formation within 3 h, yielding a cumulative energy input of 56.268 kJ [47].

One-variable-at-a-time approach

The following parameters were systematically varied while keeping other conditions constant: ultrasonic temperature (25-50°C, 5°C intervals), sonication time (1-11 h), EM:MA molar ratio (1:1 to 1:3), and ultrasonic power (250-500 W). The choice of ranges was based on literature reports on thermal stability of the two compounds [48].

According to ultrasound-assisted crystallization method as described in 2.2.2, the reaction conditions for the formation of EM-MA cocrystal were optimized by employing a one-variable-at-a-time approach. Specifically, the influence of temperature (25 to 50°C) was assessed with the molar ratio and ultrasonic time fixed at 1:1 and 3 h, and the ultrasonic power was fixed at 500 W. Subsequently, the effect of ultrasonication duration (1 to 11 h) was evaluated at an optimal temperature of 35°C, with a power of 500 W, and the same molar ratio. The influence of the molar ratio of EM:MA from 1:1 to 1:3 on the reaction was investigated under the optimized temperature, time and power of 30°C, 3 h and 500 W. Finally, under the conditions of 35°C, 3 h and a molar ratio of 1:1, the effect of power (250-500 W) on the cocrystal formation was investigated. Then the crude cocrystal underwent recrystallization in MTBE, followed by filtration. Box-Behnken experimental design

Based on the results of one-variable-at-a-time approach, a three-factor, three-level Box-Behnken design (BBD) was conducted using Design-Expert 13 to investigate the interactive effects of ultrasonic time, temperature, and substrate molar ratio on the EM-MA cocrystal yield. Artificial neural networks

To systematically optimize the extraction conditions and improve the reliability of prediction results, this study established three artificial neural network models, namely backpropagation (BP) neural network, genetic algorithm-optimized BP (GA-BP) neural network, and genetic algorithm-ant colony optimization-optimized BP (GAACO-BP) neural network [50]. The experimental dataset employed for model training, validation, and testing was derived from Box-Behnken Design (BBD) experiments, which encompassed the entire scope of key extraction variables and their interaction effects.

Models were implemented using MATLAB R2024a software adopted a consistent three-layer network framework (input layer, hidden layer, and output layer). The input layer contained 4 neurons, corresponding to four critical extraction process parameters: ultrasonic time, ultrasonic power, ultrasonic temperature and the molar ratio of EM:MA. The output layer was designed with one neuron, which represented the comprehensive evaluation index of extraction efficiency. The R2, RMSE and MAE were calculated using the following formula to comprehensively evaluate the fitting performance of the model [51].(2)R2=1-∑i=1n(yi-yi^)2∑i=1n(yi-yi¯)2(3)RMSE=1n∑i=1n(yi-yi^)2(4)MAE=1n∑i=1nyi-yi^

Where yi represented the actual response value of the individual sample i,yi^ denotes the fitted outcome generated by the established regression model for the sample i, yi¯ signified the mean value of the experimentally observed response variables, and n corresponded to the overall quantity of samples involved inthe analysis [52].

Cytotoxicity Detection

MTS assay was conducted to assess cytotoxicity. 4T1 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1×104 cells/mL and incubated for 24 h under standard culture conditions (37°C and 5% CO2). The culture medium was aspirated, after which the EM-MA physical mixture (1:1 ratio) and EM-MA cocrystal formulations—both prepared in culture medium supplemented with 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)—were added to individual wells at serial concentrations of 0, 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100 and 200 μM, followed by a 48 h incubation period. Simultaneously, a blank and serum-free culture medium were added as a negative control. At the conclusion of the incubation period, after three washes with pre-chilled PBS, 100 μL of MTS was added to each well. Subsequently, the absorbance (OD) value at 490 nm was measured useing a Microplate reader. Each concentration was tested in three independent replicates (n = 3), and the results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The concentration-survival curve was plotted, and the IC50 values were calculated using GraphPad Prism 8.0.

The 2D molecular structures of EM and MA were downloaded from PubChem, and submitted to the Swiss Target Prediction (http://www. Swiss target prediction. The intersection of the obtained targets was determined to acquire the common target of the two drugs as the target of EM-MA cocrystal, which was then used in the following data processing step.

3.2.2 Collection of potential target genes for TNBC

With “Triple Negative Breast Cancer” as the keywords, the TNBC-related targets were predicted in the disease databases GeneCards (Relevance score > 10) (https://www.genecards.org/)[58], OMIM (https://www.omim.org/)[59], and Therapeutic Target patabase (https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/). Then, the online software mapping tool platform Venny 2.1.0 (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/) was used to obtain the intersection target gene of EM-MA against TNBC.

3.2.3 Construction of a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network

All the common targets between EM-MA and TNBC were retrieved in the STRING (http://string-db.org) database to obtain the protein-protein interaction network, the species were set as “Homo sapiens” and the “medium confidence” was set at 0.4. Simultaneously, disconnected nodes within the network were hidden. The visualization of the PPI network and topology analysis were performed via Cytoscape 3.8.0 software.

3.2.4 GO and KEGG pathway analyses

The obtained common targets were imported into the DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) database for GO annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, and the main biological functions and main signal pathways of EM-MA in the treatment of TNBC were studied. The species were selected as “Homo Sapiens,” and the threshold was set at P<0.05. The bubble chart of KEGG enrichment analysis and the column chart of GO enrichment analysis were made by SRplot (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/SRplot)[60].

Statistical analysis

All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The data were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Graphs were produced using Origin software, and pictures were generated on BioGDP.com.

Results and Discussion

Crystallization condition screening

Solvent screening

In this study, solvents need to be added to obtain cocrystal. The selected solvent should initially possess a certain solubility for the two drugs constituting the cocrystal to guarantee their complete dissolution and thorough mixing. Additionally, the solvent should not form solvated crystals with EM or MA, as it would have an impact on the formation of drug-drug cocrystal. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was employed to observe the subtle changes in crystal structure so as to determine whether new substance was formed (Fig. 2). EM showed two characteristic peaks corresponding to carboxylic O-H stretch at 3381 cm-1 and C=O stretch at 1664 cm-1 (Fig. 2A). In the case of MA, C-H stretching was assigned at 2925 cm-1, and at 1642 cm-1 originated from methylene saturated C=O stretching (Fig. 2B). By comparing infrared spectral curves of EM raw material and its mixtures with other four solvents, it observed that after EM was mixed with MTBE, all the characteristic peaks of EM remained intact, and no new absorption peaks appeared. However, upon mixing with EtOH, EA or THF, a prominent O-H stretching vibration peak emerged at 3300 cm-1, indicating that hydrogen bonds formed between the drug and solvent, implying that EM and solvent had generated solvated crystals (Fig. 2). Solvated crystal would preferentially occupy the hydrogen-bonding sites on drug molecules. This occupation impeded the non-covalent interactions necessary for the formation of target cocrystal between the two drug molecules, ultimately resulting in the failure of cocrystal preparation. Therefore, in the subsequent experiments, MTBE was chosen as the solvent.Fig. 2(A) FTIR spectra of EM in different solvents. (B) FTIR spectra of MA in different solvents.

Preparation methods screening

The effects of solution stirring, ultrasound-assisted crystallization and solvent-assisted grinding on cocrystal yield were compared. The results of obtained amounts of cocrystal prepared by different methods with various solvents were shown in Table 2. The cavitation effect of ultrasound is the formation, growth, oscillation and violent collapse of gas/vapor bubbles in a liquid under high‑intensity ultrasound, and could instantly generate local high temperature and pressure, while destroying the lattice structure of drug molecules and rapidly forming a great number of uniform crystal nuclei [61]. In contrast to the grinding method, which relied on mechanical force to slowly break the lattice and required a rather lengthy process, and the solvent stirring method characterized by sluggish natural nucleation and a prolonged crystallization, that the ultrasonic method would take no more than 3 h to achieve nucleation-crystallization and produce a larger amount of cocrystal. The vibrational effect of ultrasonic waves continuously dispersed the crystals within the system. This approach effectively circumvented the issues of non-uniform local grinding and crystal agglomeration inherent in the grinding method, along with the substantial discrepancies in crystal size and impurity inclusion tendency of the solvent stirring method. Moreover, ultrasound could promote the dissolution and diffusion of drugs in solvents, leading to a higher level of uniformity in the final product and a minimal amount of solvent was required to thoroughly mix the drugs, preventing substantial solvent residue and moisture interaction. Therefore, the ultrasound-assisted crystallization was chosen for preparation of EM-MA cocrystal.

One-variable-at-a-time approach

Fig. 3A illustrated the effect of ultrasonic temperature on the yield of cocrystal preparation. With the ultrasonic duration maintained at 3 h and the molar ratio of the two drugs fixed at 1:1, the influences of temperature in the range of 25 to 50°C on the cocrystal formation yield were systematically examined. As the temperature increased to 35°C, the cocrystal formation exhibited a continuous upward trend. An appropriate temperature regime could attenuate the intermolecular forces among drug molecules, thus facilitating the nucleation and growth of cocrystals [62]. When the temperature exceeded 35°C, the cocrystal formation underwent a gradual decline. Elevated temperature was prone to compromise the thermodynamic stability of the formed cocrystals, which might result in partial decomposition of the cocrystal phase or its transformation into individual drug crystal phases [63].

With a fixed ultrasound time of 3 h and a crystallization temperature of 35°C, the influence of molar ratios of different drugs on the cocrystal formation yield was investigated. As was demonstrated in Fig. 3C, a 1:1.5 molar ratio of EM to MA maximized cocrystal yield. It was speculated that in the recrystallization process, the system with a 1:1.5 molar ratio underwent more complete recrystallization with less loss. Therefore, the response surface analysis experiment was conducted using a 1:1.5 molar ratio as the optimal condition.Fig. 3Effects of different (A) ultrasonic temperature, (B) ultrasonic time, (C) molar ratio of EM:MA, (D) ultrasonic power were studied.

When the reaction temperature was maintained at 35°C, the reaction time was set at 3 h, and the molar ratio of EM to MA was fixed at 1:1, the ultrasonic power was adjusted within the range of 250 to 500 W. Experimental results demonstrated that the yield of the cocrystal exhibited an overall upward trend as the ultrasonic power increased from 250 W to 500 W. Specifically, under the aforementioned fixed conditions, the formation amount of the cocrystal attained its maximum when the ultrasonic power elevated to 500 W. Ultrasonic power is a key parameter determining cavitation intensity. Higher power increases both the number and the collapse energy of cavitation bubbles, thereby accelerating nucleation, shortening induction time, and promoting fragmentation and secondary nucleation, which ultimately improves yield and crystal uniformity [64].

Model performance comparison and validation experiments

RSM is based on statistical experimental design and polynomial regression, approximating the input‑output relationship with low‑order (first-or second‑order) polynomials [65]. However, it can only provide local optima and is constrained by predefined boundaries and polynomial order [66]. In contrast, ANNs simulate biological neural systems, establishing nonlinear mappings through multiple layers of neurons, weights, biases and activation functions [67]. Therefore, two artificial neural network models were implemented in MATLAB R2024a to characterize the interactions between the four input factors and the yield of EM-MA cocrystal . The results of model prediction were presented in Table 4. Fig. 5C depicted the performance of training and test sets under different numbers of epochs. The optimal number of neurons in the hidden layer was determined to be five. PXRD analysis

By comparing the powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns between raw materials and drug-drug cocrystal (Fig. 6B), distinct differences in diffraction peaks were observed. Specifically, the cocrystal exhibited more characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to c1, c6, and c9, which is in contrast to the raw material counterparts. When the cocrystal was compared with emodin, the characteristic peaks a1 and a3 originally present in raw emodin were no longer detectable. Additionally, the intensity of the c8 peak in the cocrystal was significantly enhanced relative to the a4 peak of emodin, while the c10 peak intensity was notably reduced compared to the a5 peak of emodin. It was observed that, in contrast, the cocrystal didn’t exihit the b4 peak, which was a characteristic feature of matrine. Furthermore, the intensity of c2 peak in the cocrystal was weakened in comparison to that of b1 peak in matrine, while the intensity of c4 peak was enhanced relative to that of b2 peak in matrine. A diffraction peak shift was observed for c5 when compared to the b3 peak of matrine; similarly, the c7 peak of the cocrystal underwent both a positional shift and an intensity reduction relative to the b5 peak of matrine. These aforementioned changes in PXRD characteristics collectively indicated the successful formation of a cocrystal between emodin and matrine. The PXRD pattern calculated from the single-crystal structure of EM-MA was in good agreement with the experimental pattern.

DSC Analysis

The DSC curves and onset melting temperatures of EM, MA and EM-MA were presented in Fig. 6C. The distinct endothermic peaks in EM and MA at 261.4°C and 88.5°C, respectively, represented the melting point. Specifically, the DSC thermogram of EM-MA cocrystal displayed an endothermic peak at 158.1°C, which fell within the rang of endothermic peaks between EM and MA. The emergence of a novel endothermic peak suggested the presence of an interaction between EM and MA, leading to the formation of a new phase.

Optical Microscope Characterization

Stereomicroscopic images of EM, MA and EM-MA cocrystal under natural light, GFP light, Cy3 light and mixed light demonstrated distinct color characteristics (Fig. 7C). Under natural illumination, the crystal of MA manifested a white and faintly transparent aspect, and no remarkable fluorescence was detected. EM presented a yellow hue under natural light, while under alternative lighting conditions, distinct vivid-yellow fluorescence was discerned. The EM-MA cocrystal exhibited a slightly transparent and bright orange-yellow appearance. The comparison results of color characteristics could suggest the formation of target cocrystal.

SEM Characterization

In electron microscopic observations, the EM particles manifested as slender, elongated strips (Fig. 7D). In contrast, the surface of MA particles displayed a rough texture, and no distinct morphology could be identified. However, the EM-MA cocrystal exhibited a completely different, block-shaped crystalline morphology. Notably, EM-MA cocrystal indicated the optimal stability without any degradation under conditions of 55°C and 75% relative humidity (RH), which might be attributed to the formation of stable intermolecular hydrogen bonds between EM and MA (Fig. S1).

Cytotoxicity detection

The EM-MA cocrystal displayed remarkable cytotoxicity against 4T1 cells at 48 h post-administration, with a concentration-dependent reduction in cell viability (Fig. 8A). Specifically, at a concentration of 100 μM, the cell survival rate was reduced to less than 25%. In contrast, EM+MA group exhibited substantially lower cytotoxic efficacy, as indicated by a cell survival rate exceeding 40% at the same 100 μM concentration (Fig. 8B). Analysis revealed that the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value of EM+MA was 68.41 μM, whereas the IC50 value of the EM-MA cocrystal was significantly lower at 42.07 μM. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using DAVID software on triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)-related targets intervened by the active components of EM-MA, with the species restricted to Homo sapiens, and subsequent visualization of the results was conducted. We obtained 127 statistically significant (FDR < 0.01) GO function enrichment entries, including 28 BP, 88 MF, and 11 CC terms in total. In the CC category, the target proteins were classified into cytosol and apical plasma membrane. Conclusion

In this study, the BBD method was integrated with the GA-BP model to establish an effective strategy for ultrasound-assisted crystallization of EM-MA. Systematic optimization revealed that ultrasonic time, temperature, and molar ratio of EM to MA jointly exerted a synergistic effect in regulating the formation of cocrystal EM-MA. When the molar ratio of EM to MA was 1:1.5, cross-linking for 3 h at 35°C under an ultrasound power of 500 W, the yield of cocrystal could reach up to 80.91%. It was also discovered that the ultrasounic method could remarkably cut down the preparation time and substantially enhance the efficiency of crystallization.

The EM-MA cocrystal, a novel drug-drug cocrystal, was synthesized and characterized by using PXRD, DSC, FTIR, SCXRD and SEM. The results showed significant differences between cocrystal and monomers such as thermal property, color and morphology. Single-crystal structure derived from SCXRD data demonstrated 1:1 cocrystal formation between EM and MA, wherein hydrogen-bonding played a stabilizing role. Subsequently, cellular experiments indicated that the inhibitory intensity of cocrystal on 4T1 cells was higher than that of monomer mixture administration. The aboved results illustrated that EM-MA cocrystal exerted synergistic and dual drug therapy effect.

Then, network pharmacological analysis showed both of EM an MA could be involved in multiple signaling pathways and biological processes, demonstrating the characteristics of their multi-target and multi-pathway synergistic effects in the treatment of triple-negative breast cancer. PPI analysis identified the core genes Bcl-2, ESR1 and EGFR. Enrichment analysis of GO and KEGG pathways indicated that PI3K-Akt pathway played a key role in the regulation of TNBC by EM and MA. Therefore, cocrystals would have potential for further development as combination drugs , to improve the bioactivity of natural products.

In summary, this study demonstrates that ultrasound‑assisted crystallization, when systematically optimized using RSM and ANN, is a rapid and efficient method for preparing drug‑drug cocrystals. The cavitation effects of ultrasound not only shortened the crystallization time to less than 3 h but also improved the yield and product uniformity.
