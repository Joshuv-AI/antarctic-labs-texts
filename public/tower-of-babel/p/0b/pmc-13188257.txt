Assessing the psychometric properties of the Youth Externalizing Problems Screener (YEPS) in peruvian adolescents
Authors: Denisse Manrique-Millones, Brian Peña-Calero, Gabriel Joel Paredes-Valencia, María Luisa Martínez-Camacho, Jesús Guerrero-Alcedo, David Villarreal-Zegarra, Nora Wiium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExternalizing problems represent a major public health concern during adolescence. Reliable and culturally valid screening instruments are essential for early identification, particularly in diverse sociocultural contexts such as Peru. This study aimed to analyze the psychometric properties of the Youth Externalizing Problems Screener (YEPS) in Peruvian adolescents. Specifically, we examined its factor structure, tested measurement invariance across ethnolinguistic and sex groups, and gathered evidence of validity based on relations to other variables, including anxiety and depressive symptomatology.MethodsParticipants were 1,233 adolescents (50.2% female; Mage = 14.50, SD = 1.50) recruited from secondary schools in Metropolitan Lima (Spanish-speaking, n = 705) and Cusco (Quechua-speaking, n = 528). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and item response theory (IRT) models were applied to evaluate structural validity and item-level properties. Measurement invariance across sex and ethnolinguistic groups was tested using multi-group CFA. Evidence of validity based on relations to other variables was examined via correlations with depression (PHQ-9) and anxiety (GAD-7) scores.ResultsFindings supported a bifactor structure, with a predominant general externalizing factor and specific dimensions reflecting hyperactivity–inattention and oppositional defiance. Bifactor indices indicated that the total score was psychometrically appropriate, and IRT analyses showed high discrimination parameters. Measurement invariance was upheld across sex at all levels (configural, metric, scalar, and strict), permitting meaningful comparisons of both latent means and observed scores. For ethnolinguistic groups, configural, metric, and scalar invariance were supported; however, strict invariance was not achieved, suggesting differences in residual variances. The YEPS general factor demonstrated moderate correlations with depression (r = .47, p < .001) and anxiety (r = .45, p < .001).ConclusionsThe YEPS demonstrates adequate psychometric properties in Peruvian adolescents, supporting its use as a reliable tool for assessing externalizing problems in research and applied settings. While the total score adequately captures the general tendency toward externalizing problems, subscale variance may also be clinically relevant. Strict invariance across sex supports its broad applicability, whereas partial invariance across ethnolinguistic groups highlights the importance of considering cultural context when interpreting raw scores. Overall, the YEPS offers a culturally sensitive and psychometrically sound screening option for adolescent populations in diverse Peruvian settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04495-5.

Introduction

Externalizing behavior problems

Externalizing behavior problems refer to a cluster of maladaptive, outwardly directed behaviors that violate social expectations and disrupt interpersonal or academic functioning [1, 2]. According to Renshaw and Cook (2018) [3], these problems typically include aggression, impulsivity, hyperactivity, and rule-breaking. Such behaviors not only compromise youths’ relationships with peers and adults but also hinder their engagement and success in school.

Within developmental and educational psychology, externalizing problems are often contrasted with internalizing difficulties (e.g., anxiety, depression) and are understood as emerging from interactions between individual vulnerabilities and contextual risk factors [4]. Early identification is particularly important, as these behaviors predict adverse outcomes in adolescence and adulthood, including academic failure, social rejection, and increased risk of delinquency [5–7].

Studying externalizing behavior problems during adolescence is important because these difficulties are among the most prevalent forms of youth psychopathology and are strongly associated with maladaptive developmental trajectories. Externalizing behaviors disrupt learning environments, compromise peer and family relationships, and predict long-term maladjustment.

Longitudinal research shows that externalizing problems emerging in adolescence increase the risk of school dropout, substance use, criminal involvement, and mental health disorders in adulthood [8, 9]. Moreover, adolescents exhibiting these behaviors often struggle to develop adaptive psychosocial resources. Research has shown that these behavioral difficulties are associated with lower levels of self-regulation, weaker social bonds, and a reduced sense of future orientation, all of which may undermine the capacity to construct a coherent purpose in life [10].

Sex differences and comorbidity with internalizing problems

Research demonstrates consistent sex differences in externalizing problems across adolescence, in terms of prevalence, developmental trajectories, and correlates. Males typically display higher rates of overt externalizing behavior (e.g., aggression, conduct disorder, antisocial behavior) compared to females, especially during mid to late adolescence [11]. However, this gap may narrow in domains, such as covert or relational behaviors among females [12, 13].

While these differences highlight the need to account for developmental variation between boys and girls, the co-occurrence of externalizing problems with internalizing difficulties remains a central concern. A substantial body of research shows that externalizing behaviors and depressive symptoms frequently co-occur during adolescence, forming complex patterns of mutual reinforcement. Longitudinal evidence indicates that aggression or delinquency may increase vulnerability to later depression by straining peer and family relationships and limiting opportunities for positive social experiences [14, 15].

Conversely, depressive symptoms may exacerbate externalizing tendencies by undermining emotion regulation and increasing irritability or hostility [16]. This reciprocal association reflects shared underlying mechanisms, including deficits in self-regulation, heightened stress reactivity, and adverse environmental contexts, such as family conflict or low parental support [17, 18].

Anxiety represents an additional pathway linking externalizing problems and depression. Anxiety symptoms frequently co-occur with externalizing disorders such as oppositional defiant disorder (ODD), conduct disorder (CD), and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Adolescents exhibiting externalizing tendencies are at elevated risk of later anxiety symptoms, while anxiety may intensify externalizing behaviors [19].

The peruvian context and the need for early identification

In Peru, this complex comorbidity presents a significant challenge. Many adolescents grow up in environments marked by socioeconomic inequality, family stressors, and limited access to mental health services, all of which may exacerbate the development and persistence of externalizing problems [20, 21].

Vulnerabilities are particularly pronounced in rural and indigenous communities, where poverty rates are higher and geographic isolation restricts access to educational and health resources. Structural inequalities further limit opportunities for positive youth development.

Peru’s linguistic and cultural diversity adds another layer of complexity to psychological assessment and early identification. The distinction between Spanish-speaking and Quechua-speaking populations is not merely linguistic but also cultural, reflecting differences in worldview, emotional expression, and help-seeking behaviors [22]. Quechua-speaking adolescents, especially in Andean regions, often live in communities where collectivist values and interpersonal harmony shape norms around emotion regulation and behavior. In such contexts, overt expressions of anger or defiance may be discouraged or interpreted differently by caregivers and teachers. This may lead to underreporting or misinterpretation in screening instruments developed for Spanish-speaking or Western populations [20, 23]. This is particularly relevant given that Quechua is spoken by approximately 7.8 million people worldwide, a large proportion residing in Peru [24].

Additionally, school dropout rates and exposure to community or family violence further interact with individual vulnerabilities to heighten risk for maladaptive outcomes.

At the institutional level, the National Secretariat for Youth (SENAJU) promotes policies and programs supporting adolescents in areas such as participation, education, and employment Peruvian legislation, including the Code of Children and Adolescents (Law No. 27337) [25], establishes rights and protections safeguarding adolescents’ wellbeing, educational access, and protection from violence. These frameworks, alongside strong family ties and community-oriented cultural values, may function as protective factors that buffer risk and foster resilience.

Taken together, these contextual factors highlight a critical need for efficient early identification mechanisms. Adolescence represents a developmental stage characterized by heightened neurobiological sensitivity and social transitions. Without timely intervention, disruptive behaviors may become entrenched and resistant to change [26]. Early screening is therefore essential for preventing escalation and promoting positive adjustment in this vulnerable population.

Screening tools and the youth externalizing problems screener (YEPS)

A major barrier to large-scale early identification lies in the nature of traditional assessment instruments. Tools such as the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL; [27]) or the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ; [28]) are comprehensive but lengthy. Their administration can be burdensome in school-based or large-scale research contexts, particularly in resource-limited settings.

In this context, the Youth Externalizing Problems Screener (YEPS) emerges as a valuable alternative. Developed by Renshaw and Cook (2019) [3], the instrument was designed to provide a brief, targeted, and psychometrically sound assessment of externalizing behaviors while minimizing respondent burden. Its brevity and ease of administration make it particularly suitable for school settings and large-scale epidemiological studies, where time and participant fatigue are critical concerns [29]. The YEPS was originally grounded in two core domains of externalizing behavior-conduct/oppositional defiance (C/O) and hyperactivity/impulsivity/inattention (H/I). Hereafter, the abbreviated labels C/O and H/I are used to refer to these domains. An initial pool of 14 pilot items was developed, seven representing conduct-related difficulties and seven reflecting impulsivity, hyperactivity, and attentional problems. All items were written in a straightforward, positively framed format that avoided the need for reverse scoring, and responses were collected on a 4-point frequency scale (1 = almost never, 2 = sometimes, 3 = often, 4 = almost always). Based on psychometric analyses, the final version was refined to 10 items and demonstrated a unidimensional structure, indicating that the YEPS effectively captures overall externalizing behavior problems within a single factor [3] .

Despite its promising features, the YEPS has undergone limited psychometric validation. However, its applicability in a distinct sociocultural context like Peru remains entirely unexplored. Weeks et al. (2022) [30] explored the YEPS in combination with the Youth Internalizing Problems Screener (YIPS) and showed that a bifactor model provided a good fit when both measures were integrated. Moreover, Jovanović (2024) [31] provided evidence of convergent validity and measurement invariance across gender, time, and cultural contexts in Serbia and the United States, thereby extending the instrument’s generalizability. Taken together, these studies suggest that the YEPS holds promise as a brief and psychometrically sound screener for externalizing behaviors, although further independent validation in diverse populations is needed.

The present study

The present study examines the psychometric properties of the Youth Externalizing Problems Screener (YEPS) in an understudied sociocultural context like Peru. Although the YEPS has demonstrated promising evidence of reliability and validity in previous research studies, its applicability in Latin American populations remains unexplored.

To address this gap comprehensively, we employ a multi-method analytical strategy. First, we use Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) within a Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) framework to test the hypothesized unidimensional factor structure and evaluate the model’s global fit to the data in our sample [32]. Subsequently, we employ an Item Response Theory (IRT) framework to conduct a detailed, item-level analysis, evaluating the performance and characteristics of each individual item [33]. This combined approach allows for a comprehensive evaluation of the YEPS at both the scale and item level, providing a thorough assessment of its validity and utility for use with Peruvian adolescents. Additionally, we assess reliability and its associations with other variables, including depressive symptoms and anxiety. Finally, we examine measurement invariance across ethnolinguistic groups (Quechua speakers vs. Spanish speakers) and sex (females vs. males). Through these analyses, the study aims to contribute to the cross-cultural validation of the YEPS and provide a reliable screening tool for Peruvian adolescents.

Methods

Participants

The study encompassed 1,233 Peruvian adolescents, of whom 621 were females (50.4%) and 612 were males, with a mean age of 14.56 years (SD = 1.54). Participants were enrolled in grades 1 through 5 of secondary education. A total of 541 adolescents (43.9%) were Quechua-speaking youth residing in the highlands of Cusco, while 692 (56.1%) were Spanish-speaking adolescents from Metropolitan Lima. Regarding school attendance, the vast majority (97.4%) reported attending classes five days per week, while 26 participants attended four days, and six reported attending between one and three days per week.

Instruments

Youth Externalizing Problems Screener (YEPS, [3])

The YEPS is a brief self-report instrument designed to assess externalizing difficulties in adolescents. It focuses on behaviors typically associated with conduct problems, hyperactivity, impulsivity, and oppositional tendencies that may interfere with youths’ academic, social, and emotional functioning. It is composed of 10 items that capture common externalizing behaviors in adolescents (i.e. “I break rules whenever I feel like it.”). Items are rated on a 4-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 = almost never to 4 = almost always, with higher scores reflecting greater levels of externalizing difficulties.

The adaptation process for the Spanish version involved a rigorous methodology to ensure linguistic and conceptual equivalence, consistent with established international guidelines for cross-cultural test adaptation [34–36]. This process involved several steps. First, a forward- and back-translation procedure was implemented to ensure linguistic equivalence between the original and translated versions of the instrument. Second, the translated items were pilot tested with 30 bilingual adolescents, which allowed us to assess the clarity, comprehension, and cultural resonance of the wording. Their feedback was used to refine the items and adjust any ambiguous or potentially confusing phrasing. Finally, a panel of experts in psychology and methodology reviewed the revised items to confirm both their semantic accuracy and their cultural appropriateness for the target population.

Following this initial stage, an identical adaptation procedure was carried out to produce a version of the measure suitable for Quechua-speaking adolescents, in line with international recommendations for ensuring linguistic and cultural equivalence [35]. This parallel process guaranteed that the instrument preserved its conceptual meaning while being accessible and relevant within a different linguistic and cultural context.

Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9 [37])

The PHQ-9 is a widely used and validated self-administered depression assessment tool. It has nine items. Participants rate the frequency of symptoms over the past two weeks on a four-point scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”, providing a total score (i.e., “Over the last 2 weeks, how often have you been bothered by any of the following problems? Thoughts that you would be better off dead or of hurting yourself in some way”). The scale reported adequate fit and reliability indices (PHQ-9 (χ² [27] = 144.92, CFI = 0.987, TLI = 0.983, RMSEA = 0.060, SRMR = 0.029, ω = 0.891).

Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7; [38])

It is a widely used and well-validated self-report questionnaire designed as a screening tool to assess the frequency of common anxiety symptoms over the past two weeks (i.e. “Over the last two weeks, how often have you been bothered by the following problems? Not being able to stop or control worrying”). It consists of 7 items (e.g., feeling nervous, not being able to stop worrying, trouble relaxing) rated on a 4-point scale from 0 (“Not at all”) to 3 (“Nearly every day”). The scale showed good fit and reliability indices (GAD-7 (χ² [21] = 53.36, CFI = 0.996, TLI = 0.994, RMSEA = 0.048, SRMR = 0.017, ω = 0.895).

Procedure

A cross-sectional design was employed in public schools from Lima and Cusco, where secondary students completed a paper-based self-report survey. Before data collection, approval was secured from the corresponding educational authorities. After authorization, coordination with schoolteachers was carried out to agree on suitable times for the assessments. Data was collected during regular class hours in a single session, conducted by a trained psychologist with the assistance of two research staff who were available to address any questions. Adolescents participated voluntarily after providing their assent, while their parents signed written informed consent forms. No compensation was given for participation. The data collection took approximately 50 min.

Ethical topics

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Universidad Científica del Sur (CIEI-CIENTÍFICA), under protocol number 101-2024-PRO99. Participation was entirely voluntary, and the survey was administered anonymously for research purposes only. Funding was provided by CONCYTEC through the PROCIENCIA program (call “E041-2024-04: Proyectos de Investigación en Ciencias Sociales 2024-04,” contract PE501088191-2024), with additional support from Universidad Científica del Sur via the DGIDI office.

Data analysis

Psychometric analyses were conducted in R [39] using the packages lavaan, semTools, psymetrics, and mirt. Before modeling, item responses were inspected descriptively (means, SDs, category endorsement) and for basic item quality using polychoric associations (corrected item–total correlations and the inter-item polychoric correlation matrix).

To evaluate the YEPS internal structure, three plausible models were compared, reflecting the instrument’s intended scoring and its item-development domains. Model 1 (M1) operationalizes the intended single-score interpretation, a unidimensional Externalizing factor. Model 2 (M2) tests a two-factor solution aligned with the content areas used to generate the items, conduct–oppositional defiance (C/O) and hyperactivity/impulsivity/inattention (H/I), treating them as correlated factors. Model 3 (M3) specified a bifactor structure, treating Externalizing as a general factor and the two domains (C/O and H/I) as specific factors.

Models were estimated for ordinal indicators using CFA with WLSMV. For comparison within an IRT framework, we also fitted Samejima’s graded response model (GRM) via marginal maximum likelihood. Model fit was judged against commonly used guidelines: CFI/TLI ≥ 0.95, RMSEA ≤ 0.06 (≤ 0.08 acceptable), and SRMR ≤ 0.08 [40]. Under the GRM–IRT framework, item-level fit was evaluated with Orlando and Thissen’s S–X² statistic [41], and summarized using RMSEA S–X² (≤ 0.06 close, ≤ 0.08 acceptable). Parameter interpretation followed standard IRT guidance for polytomous items [42, 43].

In the bifactor model, orthogonality between the general and the specific factors was imposed. Auxiliary bifactor indices were examined: ωH (omega hierarchical), ECV (explained common variance), PUC (percent uncontaminated correlations), H (construct replicability), and factor determinacy. Dominance of the general factor was considered supported when ωH ≥ 0.80, ECV_general ≥ 0.70, and PUC ≥ 0.70, together with H ≥ 0.70 indicating an interpretable and replicable general factor [44].

Measurement invariance for ordinal indicators followed Wu & Estabrook (2016) [45] identification scheme and constraint ordering under theta parameterization [43]: configural, thresholds, metric (loadings), scalar (latent response intercepts), and strict (residual variances).

In bifactor models with ordinal items, scalar invariance testing can face identification challenges because all items load on both the general and a specific factor, leaving no pure indicators to anchor factor means [45]. As a minimal identification constraint, we designated item 5 as the anchor by fixing its specific-factor loading to zero, following marker-item selection procedures [46, 47]. Item 5 was selected because it showed the lowest specific loading (λspec = 0.06) alongside a relatively high general loading (λgen = 0.79) and content judged broadly applicable across subgroups. A detailed explanation of the identification constraints and the anchor-selection rationale is provided in Additional file 1 (Text S1).

Given the sensitivity of Δχ² in bifactor models [47], invariance decisions relied on changes in fit indices (ΔCFI ≤ 0.010, ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015; [48–50] and were supplemented by the RMSEA for nested differences (RMSEA_D), considering invariance supported when the RMSEA_D 90% CI upper bound did not exceed 0.10 [51, 52]. Details on the computation and interpretation of RMSEA_D are provided in Additional file 1 (Text S2).

Differential item functioning (DIF) was examined as a complementary analysis across ethnolinguistic groups (Spanish vs. Quechua speakers; focal group: Quechua speakers) because strict invariance was not supported. DIF was evaluated primarily using a multigroup bifactor graded response model and an iterative all-others-as-anchors (AOAA) [53] procedure implemented in mirt package [54]; three seed anchors (YEPS8, YEPS1, YEPS2) were selected from the items with the smallest initial AOAA test statistics, and the anchor set was iteratively refined via drop and add-or-drop steps; at each step, likelihood-ratio tests (LRT) were Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted. DIF magnitude was summarized with differential response functioning (DRF) [55] indices (sDIF, uDIF, dDIF) with percentile bootstrap confidence intervals, and test-level DRF statistics (sDRF, uDRF) were computed for items flagged by AOAA. Sensitivity analyses replicated AOAA under a unidimensional model and applied polytomous logistic regression DIF (difR package) [56] with purification, uniform/nonuniform tests, BH adjustment, and Nagelkerke’s R² effect sizes [57, 58].

Evidence based on relations with other variables was examined using structural equation modeling (SEM), specifying a model that included the YEPS bifactor structure and estimating latent correlations between the YEPS general factor and the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 factors.

The higher endorsement of items 7 and 9 reflects that both target everyday attentional regulation, namely difficulty sustaining focus and distractibility. Compared with overt oppositional or aggressive behaviors, these experiences may be more common to report in adolescence and more likely to be endorsed even at relatively low levels of the underlying trait. Consistent with this, their first thresholds indicate earlier endorsement along the continuum (see GRM parameters in Table 3), suggesting higher sensitivity at the lower end of externalizing severity.

Evidence based on internal structure

CFA Results. Structural validity was first examined using confirmatory factor analysis. The one-factor specification (M1 [1 F]) showed acceptable incremental fit but elevated RMSEA (0.087). The two-factor solution (M2 [2 F]), aligned with item-generation domains (hyperactivity/impulsivity/inattention [H/I] and conduct–oppositional defiance [C/O]), yielded slight improvement while RMSEA remained above preferred thresholds (0.082). A bifactor model (M3) reconciling a general factor with domain-specific variance showed improved fit. Auxiliary bifactor indices suggested that the general factor captured most common variance (ωH = 0.88; ECV = 0.87), although PUC was below the prespecified 0.70 guideline (PUC = 0.53), so conclusions regarding essential unidimensionality were drawn with caution. Consistent with this pattern, construct replicability and factor determinacy were high (H = 0.93; FD = 0.96), supporting an interpretable total score (ω = 0.91). Hierarchical omega estimates indicated that reliable variance in the specific-factor scores was small after accounting for the general factor (Additional file 1, Table S1).

For invariance testing, a re-parameterized bifactor model (M4) was estimated with the specific loading of item 5 set to zero. M4 (Bifactor-[S·I–1]) was statistically and practically equivalent to M3 (Δχ² = 1.72, p = .189; ΔCFI < 0.001, ΔRMSEA < 0.001), justifying its use as a re-expression for subsequent invariance analyses.

MIRT/GRM results. Parallel analyses using Samejima’s graded response model (MIRT framework) replicated the CFA pattern. The one-factor MIRT model (M1) yielded good fit, the two-factor model showed marginal improvement, and the bifactor solution (M3) demonstrated the best fit among competing models. The constrained bifactor model (M4) showed comparably good fit with small practical differences relative to M3 (ΔCFI = 0.003, ΔRMSEA = 0.007; Δχ² = 4.76, p = .029), confirming convergence across estimation frameworks. Likewise, M4 (Bifactor-[S·I–1]) is supported for invariance testing, while substantive interpretations refer to the bifactor structure (M3; Table 2).

CFA and GRM parameter estimates converged (Table 3). All items showed substantial general-factor loadings (λ = 0.60–0.81) and high general-dimension discriminations, whereas specific-factor contributions were mostly small. The main exception was the H/I cluster, particularly items 7 and 9, which retained notable specific-factor variance, suggesting that attentional regulation captures meaningful covariance beyond the general externalizing tendency. GRM thresholds were ordered as expected, and item-level fit was adequate across all items (RMSEA S-χ² ≤ 0.04). Substantively, the pattern of specific-factor loadings was interpretable. The H/I items that capture sustained attention and distractibility, particularly items 7 and 9, showed the largest specific-factor contributions (Table 3), suggesting a component of attentional regulation that remains partially distinct from the broader externalizing tendency captured by the general factor. In contrast, most C/O items showed small or negligible specific-factor loadings, indicating that oppositional and rule-breaking behaviors were largely accounted for by the general factor. One exception was item 8, which showed a moderate C/O-specific component, possibly reflecting a provocative interpersonal style that is not fully explained by general externalizing severity.

Differential item functioning across ethnolinguistic groups

Because strict invariance was not supported, we conducted complementary DIF analyses across Spanish and Quechua speakers. Across methods, most items were statistically flagged after Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment, a pattern consistent with the high power afforded by the sample size (N = 1,233). Item-level DRF indices suggested that the largest effects were concentrated in a small subset of items, particularly YEPS7, YEPS3, YEPS9, and YEPS10 (dDIF = 0.44, 0.39, 0.38, and 0.35, respectively; Supplementary Table S3). However, test-level differential response functioning indicated negligible aggregate impact (sDRF = − 0.20, p = .694; uDRF = 0.46, p = .128), suggesting that item-level DIF effects partially cancel and remain small when scores are aggregated (Supplementary Tables S3–S4). Sensitivity analyses supported a more focused interpretation: polytomous logistic regression with purification flagged only three items after BH adjustment (YEPS2, YEPS7, YEPS9), all with negligible effect sizes (ΔR² ≤ 0.015; Supplementary Table S4). Overall, these results are consistent with the invariance findings in indicating statistically detectable item-level differences across ethnolinguistic groups, while suggesting limited practical impact on comparisons based on the total score.

Evidence based on relations to other variables

A structural equation model was estimated that combined the YEPS bifactor measurement (general factor and orthogonal group factors) with single-factor models for PHQ-9 and GAD-7, allowing the YEPS general factor to correlate with depression (PHQ) and anxiety (GAD) (see Fig. 2). The model showed good overall fit indices (CFI = 0.953, TLI = 0.946, RMSEA = 0.056 [0.053, 0.059], SRMR = 0.047). Standardized latent correlations indicated moderate and statistically significant associations between the YEPS general factor and PHQ (r = .47, p < .001) and GAD (r = .45, p < .001), while PHQ and GAD were strongly correlated (r = .85, p < .001). Overall, these findings provide validity evidence based on relations with other variables for interpretations of YEPS general-factor scores, consistent with convergent validity with depression and anxiety constructs.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the factorial validity of the Youth Externalizing Problems Screener (YEPS) in a sample of Peruvian adolescents. Although the original validation supported a unidimensional structure [3], our findings indicate that a bifactor specification provides a better fitting representation of the data. The results suggest a general externalizing factor, complemented by specific dimensions reflecting hyperactivity/impulsivity/inattention and conduct-oppositional defiance. However, the strength of the general factor should be interpreted with nuance, as auxiliary indices, particularly the PUC (0.53), was below commonly suggested guidelines and indicated that general and specific variance were not fully separable in a simple way [44]. The general factor captured variance common to all YEPS items, whereas the specific factors represented residual domain-specific covariance among item clusters after accounting for the general factor. Thus, the model support a practical total-score interpretation without implying that domain-specific variation was absent. This pattern aligns with conceptualizations of externalizing problems as heterogeneous but with interrelated domains that include aggression, rule-breaking, oppositionality, and hyperactivity [27, 60].

From a structural perspective, the bifactor solution showed improved fit relative to the unidimensional and correlated two-factor models. Auxiliary indices were generally consistent with a general factor structure (essential unidimensionality), supporting use of the total score as an adequate summary measure [44, 61]. Nevertheless, because PUC was below commonly suggested guidelines, ωH and ECV should be interpreted jointly rather than as standalone evidence of an overwhelming general-factor dominance [41]. The convergence of CFA and IRT supports the appropriateness of the bifactor representation while also highlighting the multidimensional nature of the construct.

GRM thresholds further clarified items behavior. Items 7 and 9 were endorsed at comparatively lower levels of the latent trait, which was consistent with attentional difficulties being reported even among adolescents with relatively low externalizing severity. In contrast, items capturing more socially undesirable behaviors required higher trait levels before endorsement increased, which was consistent with a severity gradient in which inattentive experiences reflected milder and more pervasive manifestations, while conduct-related behaviors reflected higher-severity expressions.

Beyond structural validity, this study examined measurement invariance. Across ethnolinguistic groups, evidence supported configural, metric, and scalar invariance, indicating that the factorial structure, factor loadings, and item intercepts were comparable for Spanish- and Quechua-speaking adolescents. This allowed for meaningful comparisons of the latent factor means between the groups, as the scale’s units were equivalent [62]. However, failure to achieve strict invariance indicated that the measurement error, or residual variance, associated with individual items differed across cultural contexts. This implies that while the construct is understood similarly in general, the specific manifestation of some of the indicators of the construct may be influenced by distinct ecological factors [63, 64].

Complementary DIF analyses across Spanish- and Quechua-speaking adolescents identified some statistically significant item-level differences. However, test-level effects were negligible, and sensitivity analyses showed only trivial impact. Overall, ethnolinguistic differences were unlikely to meaningfully affect total-score comparisons, particularly for screening purposes.

Differences in residual variances may be attributed to a confluence of contextual factors that might shape the expression of behaviors. In the Andean context, particularly in Quechua communities, cultural values often emphasize collectivism, reciprocity, and respect for community harmony, which can contrast with more individualistic orientations in urban Lima [65, 66]. Certain behaviors that might be labeled as “externalizing” in an urban setting (e.g., assertiveness, arguing) could be interpreted differently within a more collective context. Conversely, behaviors that disrupt community cohesion might be more salient and thus potentially reported with different levels of consistency across contexts. These contextual differences may contribute to item-level response non-equivalence; although our DIF magnitude results suggested limited impact once responses were aggregated into a total score.

Invariance testing across sex yielded clearer evidence of equivalence. Strict invariance was supported, with negligible changes in fit indices at all levels of restriction. This indicated that both latent means and observed scores were comparable across sex, supporting the use of the YEPS for examining sex-based differences in externalizing behaviors in Peruvian adolescents.

This finding aligns with a body of research suggesting that well-constructed measures of broad psychopathological constructs (e.g., conduct disorder; attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder) often demonstrate adequate measurement invariance across sex (e.g.) [67]. The fact that this holds within a Peruvian adolescent sample strengthens the claim that the YEPS captures core behavioral manifestations of externalizing problems that are consistently recognized and reported similarly for both boys and girls, despite potential differences in prevalence rates.

Finally, evidence of validity based on relations to other variables was supported through associations with internalizing symptoms. The analysis revealed moderate and statistically significant positive correlations between the general factor of the YEPS and both depression (r = .47, p < .001) and anxiety (r = .45, p < .001). These findings align with established psychopathological literature, which consistently documents a significant comorbidity between externalizing problems and internalizing disorders [68]. The moderate strength of these correlations confirms that the YEPS general factor captures a construct that is meaningfully related to, yet distinct from depression and anxiety. The observed moderate relationships suggest that while externalizing problems often co-occur with internalizing states, they represent a separable clinical domain.

Externalizing and internalizing problems may stem from common underlying risk factors. For example, deficits in emotional regulation or executive function can manifest as impulsive anger and aggression (externalizing) or as rumination and helplessness (internalizing) [69]. Research in Peru and similar contexts underscores the relevance of these relationships. Studies have shown that adverse environmental conditions, which are risk factors for both externalizing and internalizing problems, are prevalent. A study analyzing data from Peruvian adolescents found that exposure to family and community violence is a significant risk factor for a range of mental health issues, including both aggressive behavior and depressive symptoms [70]. This highlights a common environmental trigger for comorbid conditions. Analyses of adolescent mental health in Peru indicate that co-occurring emotional and behavioral problems are linked to socioeconomic adversity and lack of access to mental health services [68], reinforcing the interconnected nature of these problems in the local context.

Implications for screening, practice and multicultural assessment

The findings support the use of the YEPS scale as a brief and efficient screening instrument in school and community settings. Its brevity, acceptable psychometric performance, and general-factor predominance make it particularly suitable for contexts where time and resources are limited.

The demonstration of strict invariance across sex strengthens its applied utility, as observed total scores can be compared directly between boys and girls without substantial measurement bias. This is especially relevant for epidemiological monitoring and school-based screening programs.

Regarding ethnolinguistic comparisons, scalar invariance and negligible test-level DIF suggested that total-score screening comparisons between Spanish- and Quechua-speaking adolescents were unlikely to be meaningfully distorted. However, for research requiring precise latent mean comparisons, latent-variable modeling is preferable given the absence of strict invariance.

The Peruvian context provides an important contribution to the cross-cultural validation literature. The partial non-equivalence in residual variances across ethnolinguistic groups may reflect contextual differences in how behaviors were expressed, interpreted, or socially regulated.

In collectivist Andean contexts, behavioral norms emphasizing harmony and reciprocity may shape how externalizing behaviors were perceived and reported. These contextual nuances underscored the importance of culturally sensitive interpretation rather than assuming full equivalence across diverse groups.

At the same time, the overall structural stability of the measure across groups supports the broader applicability of the YEPS beyond its original validation context. This balance between structural robustness and contextual sensitivity is particularly important for multicultural assessment.

Limitations

Despite these contributions, several limitations should be acknowledged. Most notably, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about the temporal stability of the YEPS or its ability to predict future outcomes. Longitudinal research is needed to establish test–retest reliability, developmental invariance, and the predictive validity of the measure in Peruvian youth. Second, the sample, while diverse in including both urban (Lima) and rural (Cusco) adolescents, may not fully capture the heterogeneity of Peru’s broader adolescent population, such as those in the Amazon region or adolescents outside the formal school system. Third, while we examined convergent validity with depression and anxiety, the study did not assess external validity, such as school performance, peer relationships, or behavioral referrals, which would help clarify the applied significance of YEPS scores. Finally, although the adaptation process for both the Spanish and Quechua versions followed rigorous translation and expert review procedures, the present study did not include in-depth qualitative methods (e.g., cognitive interviewing or ethnographic approaches) specifically designed to explore how externalizing behaviors are conceptually understood within Quechua-speaking communities. While pilot testing supported clarity and comprehension, further qualitative research would help ensure a deeper cultural equivalence and contextual appropriateness of item interpretation in indigenous adolescents. Future studies should incorporate culturally grounded qualitative approaches to complement psychometric validation and strengthen cross-cultural applicability.

In summary, the YEPS scale demonstrated adequate psychometric properties for assessing externalizing problems in Peruvian adolescents. A bifactor model provided an acceptable representation of the data, supporting use of the total score for screening while acknowledging residual multidimensionality. The scale performed consistently across sex and broadly across ethnolinguistic groups, with limited practical impact of item-level differences. These findings support the YEPS scale as a brief, culturally adaptable screening tool suitable for research and applied contexts in diverse adolescent populations.
