Novel Pseudomonas putida-derived nanoliposomes enhance the inhibition of MCF-7 breast cancer cells by superb intracellular uptake of doxorubicin
Authors: Zahra Asadi, Elham Arkan, Ghazal Nowroozi, Faranak Aghaz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In this study, we aimed to utilize phospholipids from the bacterium Pseudomonas putida (PP) as a plentiful, safe, and accessible resource for creating nanoliposomes to deliver doxorubicin (Dox) to MCF-7 breast cancer cells. This bacterium provides a cost-effective source of phospholipids commonly used in nanoliposome production, with no toxicity or adverse environmental impact. To this end, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were first conducted to evaluate the feasibility of this approach and to analyze the behavior and interaction of Dox with the nanoliposomes. The phospholipids of PP were then extracted using Folch's technique. Subsequently, Dox-loaded PP-derived nanoliposomes (PNL-Dox) were developed using the thin-film method. The physicochemical properties of the fabricated nanocarrier were then investigated and the anticancer effects of this system were tested on MCF-7 cells. The results of the MD simulations indicated that Dox reacted with all of the phospholipids through hydrogen bonds without affecting the fluidity, stability, and thickness of the nanoliposome membrane. Additionally, a small number of Dox molecules interacted with the nanocarrier membrane, while the remaining were located in its interior. The physicochemical investigation results showed that PNL-Dox had an average particle size and zeta potential of 271.7 ± 7.1 nm and −8.8 ± 3.3 mV, respectively. Scanning electron microscopy revealed that the particles were spherical and did not show any signs of aggregation. Drug release of PNL-Dox was gradual at pH 7.4 and 6.5, with a significantly higher release at pH 6.5. In vitro studies demonstrated successful uptake of PNL-Dox by MCF-7 cells, resulting in cytotoxicity within 24 and 48 h of treatment. Also, it increased apoptosis and reduced the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in these cells. Our study showcased the potential of PP phospholipids to form a promising anti-cancer drug delivery system, opening up new possibilities for the treatment of all types of cancers.

Introduction

Doxorubicin (Dox), also known as Adriamycin, is an anthracycline antibiotic isolated from cultures of Streptomyces peucetius var, with a broad spectrum of chemotherapeutic activities. It is used for the treatment of various human malignancies, especially breast cancer [1]. However, despite having a strong anti-proliferative effect, chemotherapy with this agent can cause non-specific toxicity and adverse events, particularly cardiotoxicity, which have limited its usage. Additionally, resistance to Dox is another significant obstacle to successful chemotherapy with this drug [2,3]. The MCF-7 cell line is often used for research into breast cancer and chemotherapy resistance. It has characteristics of the breast epithelium that provide valuable insights into breast cancer [4]. MCF-7 cells express estrogen and progesterone receptors as well as human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 (HER2), which play a key role in breast cancer growth. The research conducted with MCF-7 cells in vitro closely mirrors findings from in vivo models [5].

Nanotechnology is an emerging tool that can enhance the effectiveness of drugs and mitigate the side effects of chemotherapeutic agents [6]. Nanoliposomes are nanoscale vesicles mainly composed of at least one phospholipid bilayer that encloses aqueous media [7]. The nano-scale size and large surface area of nanoliposomes make them excellent options in various fields, including drug delivery [8,9]. However, these properties can also lead to their accumulation in various organs, potentially causing systemic toxicity. Therefore, achieving a precise nanoliposome design involves adjusting their physicochemical properties and understanding their interactions with cell membranes to determine their fate within the body [10]. Liposomal formulations are helpful in correctly delivering Dox to cancer cells and reducing the risk of cardiotoxicity and other unwanted side effects such as nausea, vomiting, myelosuppression, and alopecia. Additionally, liposomes enable the administration of a greater cumulative dose of Dox over a lifetime [11,12]. Doxil, also known as liposomal Doxorubicin hydrochloride, is the first FDA-approved nano-drug used to treat ovarian cancer, breast cancer, multiple myeloma and Kaposi's sarcoma [13]. The Doxil liposome consists of cholesterol and phospholipids especially soybean phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) [14]. PE can easily cross the membrane of mammalian cells better than other phospholipids. The nanostructure containing PE can strongly transfer chemotherapeutic agents to cancer cells [15]. PE is the main phospholipid in the membrane of living organisms specifically the inner bacterial membrane [16].

Lately, there have been efforts to utilize natural sources like plants in the synthesis of nanoparticles, particularly silver nanoparticles [17,18]. Additionally, bacteria are increasingly being recognized as another valuable resource for the biosynthesis of nanoparticles. This approach is commonly known as bottom-up synthesis, where cell extracts and biomass of bacteria serve as an alternative to regular starting materials [19]. The bacterial outer membrane vesicles and ghosts are also used for nanoparticle preparation. Their biomedical applications are due to the capability of delivering the encapsulated drug to the cellular nucleus, cytoplasm, and membrane, crossing various cellular barriers [20]. Gram-negative bacterial ghosts have been applied as carriers due to their targeted drug delivery and immunogenicity, acting as natural adjuvants. They have been used in vaccines to induce a strong immune response [21]. Other applications of bacterial ghosts include gene delivery and chemotherapy, particularly with doxorubicin, due to their excellent loading capacity [22]. The benefits of bacterial-derived nanoparticles include cost-effectiveness, scalability, safety, biodegradability, and the abundance of biological resources in nature [23]. Bacteria provide sustainable and eco-friendly raw materials for the synthesis of nanoparticles compared to conventional approaches that use hazardous sources that have a negative impact on the environment [24].

Pseudomonas putida (PP) is a gram-negative and rod-shaped bacterium that is generally isolated from soil [25]. The surface structure of this bacterium has the potential for developing nanoliposomes. Two‐thirds of the total phospholipid of PP is PE, one-third is phosphatidylglycerol, and less than 5 % is cardiolipin (diphosphatidylglycerol) [26].

So, in this study, for the first time, we aimed to synthesize Dox-loaded nanoliposomes with superior physical and chemical properties using the PP membrane phospholipids rather than bacterial ghosts. First, we used the molecular dynamics (MD) models to investigate the mechanisms by which Dox may interact with the monomers and aggregates of the phospholipids of the nanoliposome. After evaluating the physicochemical properties, we did an in vitro study to investigate the anti-cancer properties of this nano drug delivery system on the breast cancer cell line, MCF-7.

Materials and methods

Materials

All reagents and chemicals were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. (St. Louis, USA) unless otherwise stated. Ultrapure water (UP-water) was generated by Milli-Q (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA).

Molecular dynamics simulations study

MD simulations were performed to evaluate the interactions between the membrane phospholipids of the synthesized carrier and Dox. The GROMACS 2021 package was used for the simulations, and the parameters were generated using the CHARMM force field [27]. The meropenem structure and the initial data of phospholipid components were obtained from the CHARMM-gui web server (http://www.charmm-gui.org/). To prepare the system, some Dox molecules were first randomly placed on the outer surface of the lipid bilayers and the simulations were started. Solvation of the system was performed by adding the water molecules to the simulation boxes based on the TIP3 model [28], and the whole system was neutralized by adding an appropriate number of ions. Then, the steepest descent algorithm performed the energy minimization [29] and the unfavorable interactions between the molecules were corrected. The system pressure was set to 1 bar using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat, achieved through the NVT and NPT ensembles, respectively. The V-rescale thermostat algorithm adjusted the system temperature to 298 K [30,31]. The long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method [32]. The LINCS algorithm was utilized to maintain bond lengths at their equilibrium [33]. The cutoff for computing short-range interactions was 1 nm, and the simulation was conducted for 100 ns using the leap-frog algorithm [34].

Bacterial growth conditions

PP (RUP1, PTCC No:1624) was obtained from the Department of Plant Protection of Razi University, Kermanshah, Iran. Firstly, the bacteria were cultured on a nutrient agar medium for 24 h. Then, the primary culture was transferred to the nutrient broth and incubated on a shaker (190 rpm) at 29 °C for 48 h. To monitor the bacterial growth, the culture's absorbance was measured at 600 nm (OD 600).

Lipid extraction

The bacterial lipids were extracted according to Folch method [35] with minor modifications. First, the bacterial culture medium was centrifuged at 4000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min. After discarding the supernatant, the bacterial pellet was mixed with 1 mL of methanol and stirred at 200 rpm for 10 min. Then 2 mL of chloroform was added and the solution was stirred for 30 min at room temperature. 3 mL and 2 mL of chloroform/methanol solution (2:1) were added and shaken for 10 and 60 min, respectively. Then 1.9 mL potassium chloride solution (0.88 %) was added and the solution was mixed well. Finally, another washing step was performed by adding 1.4 mL of methanol (50 % v/v in water). The resulting solution was centrifuged at 4000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min and the extracted lipids were collected in chloroform at the lower phase. The solvent was evaporated and the obtained lipids were collected and stored at 4 °C until use.

Nanoliposome preparation

Dox-loaded PP-derived nanoliposomes (PNL-Dox) were fabricated by the thin-film hydration method [36]. Briefly, 6 mg of bacterial lipid was dissolved in 1 mL of chloroform and this organic solvent was removed on a rotary evaporator (50 °C, 150 rpm) and a thin film layer was formed. Then 15 mL of UP-water (60 °C) containing 0.5 mg of Dox was added to the round-bottomed flask and mixed vigorously. After that, the sample was treated in an ultrasonic bath for three cycles of 5 min with 2 min rest in between. The sample was finally sonicated for 30 s to reduce the size of particles and filtered 20 times through a syringe filter (0.22 μm) [37]. The blank PP-derived nanoliposomes (PNL-B) were prepared using the same protocol without adding Dox.

Characterization of PNL-Dox

The mean particle size (Z-Average), polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of PNL-Dox and PNL-B were measured with Zetasizer (Nano-ZS, Malvern Co., United Kingdom), at 25 °C and a fixed angle of 90°. Before analysis, samples were diluted directly (1:10) with ultrapure water to obtain a 100–300 kcps count rate.

Nanoparticles' surface characteristics and morphology were evaluated using a scanning electron microscope (SEM). Initially, the samples were coated with gold-palladium membranes and then examined by SEM (SU3500, Hitachi Co., Japan).

To assess the chemical composition of PNL-Dox, bacterial phospholipids and Dox, the IR absorption of their functional groups was determined using Fourier-transformed infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy. Firstly, the samples were freeze-dried to obtain the solid form. Then, 5–6 mg of the sample was mixed with 100 mg of spectroscopy-grade potassium bromide (Sigma Aldrich) and condensed into a disk [38]. The chemical composition of the samples was analyzed using an FT-IR spectrophotometer (IR Prestige-21, Shimadzu Co., Japan) over a wavenumber range of 4,000–400 cm−1 at a resolution ratio of 4 cm−1.

Entrapment efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL)

First, the PNL-Doxs were separated from the sample through ultracentrifugation (Optimal L-90k, Beckman coulter Co., USA). This process was done at 16,000 rpm and 4 °C for 40 min. The light absorbance of the supernatant was then measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Mini 1240, Shimadzu Co., Japan) at a wavelength of 236 nm. After quantifying the free Dox using its standard curve, EE (%) and DL (%) were calculated according to the following equations:EE(%)=TotalDox−FreeDoxTotalDox×100DL(%)=TotalDox−FreeDoxTotalweightofnanoparticles×100

In vitro drug release

The in vitro pH-responsive release profile of the PNL-Dox was assessed using a dialysis method (cut-off = 12–14 kDa) [39]. Two experiments were conducted using media with pH values of 6.5 and 7.4 adjusted by HCL. Briefly, the freshly prepared PNL-Dox was added to a dialysis bag with sealed ends. Each bag was then placed in a separate beaker containing release media corresponding to one of the two pH values mentioned earlier. The beakers were agitated at 100 rpm and 37 °C on a magnetic stirrer for 40 h. At predetermined time intervals, 1 mL of media was withdrawn and replaced with its medium. At the end of the experiments, the collected media was analyzed for Dox concentration by measuring its absorbance at 236 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Mini 1240, Shimadzu Co., Japan) and quantified using its standard curve. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cytotoxicity assay

To determine the cytotoxicity of Dox, PNL-B and PNL-Dox against MCF-7 cells, an MTT assay was performed [40]. First, 3 × 103 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The cells were then treated with PNL-B (60 and 120 μg/ml), Dox, and PNL-Dox (3.7, 7.5, 15, 30, 60, 120 μg/ml) for 24 and 48 h at 37 °C. After treatment, the culture media was discarded and replaced with 100 μl of FBS containing MTT solution with a final concentration of 5 mg/ml. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h in the dark. The media was then replaced with DMSO (200 μL) and the cells were read by a microplate reader (LQ-350, Awareness Technology Co., USA) at 570 nm. The cell viability was calculated using the following equation:Cellviability(%)=OD570(test)OD570(control)×100

Intracellular uptake and DAPI staining

To assess the ability of PNL-Dox's cellular uptake in MCF-7 cells, the nanoliposomes were labeled with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC). First, PNL-Dox (5 mg/ml) was mixed with FITC (5 μg/ml) and incubated for 12 h. After this, the mixture was centrifuged at 10000 rpm for 10 min. The pellet was then washed with PBS and the labeled nanoliposomes were kept at 4 °C until use [41]. Next, MCF-7 cells were seeded in a 6-well culture plate at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. Then the cells were treated with 500 μl FITC-labeled PNL-Dox and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. After washing three times with PBS, the cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (100 μL), and the fluorescence intensity was assessed using a Cytation cell imaging reader (Cytatyon5, Biotek Co., USA).

Subsequently, to investigate the effects of PNL-Dox on DNA, MCF-7 cells were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (40 μM) and incubated for 30 min. Finally, the cells were studied using a fluorescence imaging system (Cytation 5, Biotek, USA) after being washed thrice with PBS [42].

Apoptosis

FITC-conjugated Annexin V/propidium iodide (PI) co-staining was performed to assess the type of cell death. The experiment involved growing MCF-7 cells (1 × 105) in 6 well culture plates for 24 h at 37 °C. After that, the cells were exposed to PNL-Dox (60 μg/ml), Dox (15 μg/ml), and PNL-B (120 μg/ml) for another 24 h. Trypsin was then added to detach the cells, and three parts of each sample were treated with Annexin V-FITC, PI, and Annexin V-FITC and PI, respectively. One part of each sample was kept as a control. The first two samples were treated with 3 μl Annexin V-FITC and incubated at 4 °C for 15 min. After adding 1 mL of binding buffer, the samples were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min. The pellet was then resuspended in 500 μl of binding buffer and 3 μl of PI was added to the PI-containing samples. The fluorescent intensity was measured with flow cytometry (BD FACS Calibur, BD biosciences Co., USA) using a wavelength of 617 nm for PI and 520 nm for Annexin V-FITC. Annexin V+/PI− and Annexin V+/PI+ were considered as early apoptotic and late apoptotic cells respectively [43].

ROS measurement

The ROS level was measured using the fluorogenic 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCF-DA) probe. First, MCF-7 cells were treated with PNL-Dox (60 μg/ml), Dox (15 μg/ml), and PNL-B (120 μg/ml) for 24 h. After washing three times with PBS and detaching with trypsin, the cells were collected by centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 5 min. Each sample was then divided into 4 parts, with 3 for staining with H2DCF-DA, PI, and both dyes (H2DCF-DA and PI), and 1 for control. 2 μl of H2DCF-DA was added to the assigned samples and incubated for 45 min at 37 °C. Before reading, 3 μl of PI was added to the assigned tubes, and the fluorescent intensity was then read by flow cytometer (BD FACS Calibur, BD Biosciences Co., USA) [44].

Synthesis of PNL-Dox and its physicochemical properties

Utilizing phospholipids extracted from microorganisms to fabricate nanostructure, is an efficient method to produce nanoliposomes worldwide [48]. This high-yield process helps to reduce the cost, energy consumption, and time needed compared to conventional methods and provides environmentally friendly nanoparticles with a wide range of applications in many fields, such as biomedicine [49]. They are safe, biocompatible, and biodegradable, and have been used as anticancer agents and drug delivery systems [50]. In the present study, we successfully synthesized PNL-B and PNL-Dox using the phospholipids extracted from PP for the first time after MD simulations. The characteristics of PNL-B and PNL-Dox are shown in Table 4. The average particle size for PNL-B and PNL-Dox was 217.3 ± 11.95 and 271.7 ± 7.1 nm respectively. PNL-Dox exhibited a zeta potential of −8.8 ± 3.3 mV and a PDI of 0.419 ± 0.06, whereas PNL-B had a zeta potential of −19.93 ± 4.2 mV and a PDI of 0.27 ± 0.03. The PNL-B's negative zeta potential was due to phosphatidylglycerol and cardiolipin, which are components of the PP plasma membrane that carry one and double net negative charges, respectively [26]. The negative zeta potential of PNL-Dox will decrease the possibility of its clearance in the body by reticuloendothelial system (RES) [51]. In Dox spectra, the peak at 3414 cm−1 represents the N-H stretch and 1575 cm−1 corresponds to the aromatic ring. The FTIR spectra of bacterial phospholipids have a peak at 3417 cm−1, which indicates the N-H stretch. The presence of phosphatidylethanolamine in extracted lipids was confirmed through the identification of this peak associated with its amine group. Another peak at 2852 cm−1 represents the methylene C-H stretch, whereas 1739 cm−1 corresponds to the ester group associated with phospholipids. The C-O stretch of primary alcohol appears at a peak at 1068 cm−1. Additionally, 1462 cm−1 is assigned to long-chain linear aliphatic compounds that indicate the fatty acids in phospholipids. Moreover, the peak at 1217 cm−1 corresponds to PO2− anti-symmetric stretching mode. The data confirmed the existence of phospholipids in the extracted lipids of P. Putida.

The PNL-Dox spectra showed a peak at 3429 cm−1 that is assigned to the N-H stretch and a peak at 1575 cm−1 corresponding to the aromatic ring. The cyclic ethers C-O stretch appeared at 1076 cm−1. The C–H stretching in aromatic methoxyl groups was detected at 2856 cm−1. The peaks at 1735 cm−1 are assigned to the ester group and 1226 cm−1 corresponds to PO2− anti-symmetric stretching mode. In general, the FTIR data confirmed the successful synthesis of PNL-Dox.

The in vitro drug release assessment

The release profile of PNL-Dox is represented in Fig. 7. The PNL-Dox demonstrated a pH-responsive behavior towards drug release. At the standard physiological pH, Dox was hardly released with a maximum release percentage of 8.97 % at the end of the experiment. However, when the pH level of the release medium changed by nearly 1 unit, there was a significant increase in the release percentage at each time point. The Dox release percentage reached 30.9 after 40 h of immersion in pH 6.5, which simulated the tumor condition. The design of drug delivery systems is crucial in targeting specific areas of the body, with the pH-specific release profile being a vital factor. Tumor tissues have an acidic pH, which affects the physical characteristics of nanocarriers such as aggregation and disruption, and alters the amount of drug release [54,55]. For instance, PNL-Dox releases a low amount of drug at normal physiological pH, making it safe for normal tissues. However, a high release occurs in acidic pH, dissolving the nanocarrier rapidly and causing it to lose its structure, which releases the entrapped drug. This specific characteristic makes it a suitable candidate for tumor tissues, reducing doxorubicin's adverse side effects. Our finding was consistent with previous studies, showing that nano-encapsulation of Dox resulted in a pH-dependent release profile and minimal side effects of free Dox [56]. Both release curves showed a rapid drug release within the first 4 h, especially at pH 6.5 with a steeper slope. After this initial period, the release of doxorubicin occurred more slowly and with a gentler slope. Drug release behavior is affected by drug absorption on the nanocarrier surface. The initial burst release appears to be due to the release of free and surface-absorbed Dox by diffusion through the membrane of dialysis bag [57].Fig. 7In vitro pH-responsive drug release of the PNL-Dox in different pH values to simulate the physiological (pH 7.4) and tumor (pH 6.5) conditions. It has been suggested that bacterial membrane vesicles with a high proportion of phospholipids are excellent candidates for delivering chemotherapeutic agents due to their natural targeting capabilities. Bacterial-derived nanocarriers can also be recognized and internalized by cancer cells without a specific ligand [58]. In our study, cancer cells gradually took nanoliposomes and the controlled leakage of Dox occurred slowly over time, resulting in greater toxicity at 48 h compared to 24 h. In addition, long-term exposure to PNL-B induced death in MCF-7 cells. The ultimate toxicity of our system will result from the synergistic action of PNL-B and Dox.

The main component of our nanoparticle is phosphatidylethanolamine, which has an isoelectric point of 4.18 [60]. It has been reported that PE-enriched nanoliposome exhibits pH-responsive characteristics [61]. In the acidic environment of tumors, the negatively charged PNL-Bs undergo a potential reversal phenomenon, which aids in their fusion with the negatively charged surfaces of tumor cells via electrostatic interaction inducing a high rate of late apoptosis (3.75 %) in these cells [62]. It appears that the PNL-B deposited on the MCF-7 cells causes hypoxia in these cells, contributing to the toxicity of PNL-Bs on MCF-7 cells [63].

It is worth noting that we tested PNL-B on normal fibroblast cells and observed no significant cell death (data not shown). It is indicated that PE has great potential to conjugate with tumor-specific ligands, inducing toxicity in cancer cells without harming normal cells [64]. Therefore, it can be concluded that the toxicity of our PNL-B is target-specific and in a low-pH environment. Treatment with PNL-Dox reduced the late apoptotic cells compared to PNL-B but led to more early apoptosis compared to the control, Dox, and PNL-B groups. Due to the presence of amino groups, Dox reduced the net negative charge of our nanoliposomes, which in turn reduces the pH responsiveness of the carrier [65]. It is anticipated that over an extended treatment period, Dox will gradually be released from the carrier as the early apoptotic cells progress into the late apoptosis phase. The widely used nano drug Doxil can cause side effects such as infusion reactions. One way to reduce these unwanted effects is by decreasing the half-life of the liposome, leading to a shorter circulation time of the entrapped drug in the body [66]. Research has shown that the diffusion constant of a pure PC monolayer is lower than that of PE monolayers, resulting in greater fluidity in PE monolayers. Additionally, vesicles of PE are stable at pH 8.1 but are destroyed in the acidic pH of a tumor [67,68]. This suggests that PNL-Dox, which contains about two-thirds PE, is a good alternative to common liposomal doxorubicin, as it has a shorter half-life and greater fluidity.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed novel nanoliposomes using PP-extracted phospholipids to load and deliver Dox to MCF-7 breast cancer cells. MD simulation demonstrated that loading Dox in this nanocarrier was energetically favorable and did not negatively impact its stability, fluidity, or thickness. Our results also showed that this system had suitable physicochemical properties with a size of 271.7 ± 7.1 and a zeta potential of −8.8 ± 3.3. Moreover, PNL-Dox showed a pH-responsive release profile, enabling the gradual release of Dox in a target-specific manner especially at pH 6.5. Treatment with this system resulted in decreased viability of MCF-7 cells with a half-maximum inhibitory concentration of 60 μg/ml. PNL-Dox was also found to reduce production of ROS in this concentration. Exposure to PNL-Dox led to an increase in late apoptosis. In summary, this study presents a promising new nanocarrier for the treatment of various types of cancer.
