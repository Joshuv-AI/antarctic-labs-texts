Posterior sternoclavicular dislocation in childhood – a case report about a rare injury
Authors: Bence Hajnal, Gábor Merényi, Tamás Mona
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sternoclavicular dislocation is a rare injury, especially in the pediatric population. These injuries are distinguished based on the direction of the dislocation, among which posterior dislocation is the most dangerous, mainly due to the proximity of vasculature and the trachea. Because of this, quick diagnosis and appropriate management are crucial in the treatment.Our report is about a 14-year-old boy, who was brought to our department after his friend twisted his arm and pulled it behind his back. The child had pain in the clavicular region, and with thorough examination, the medial end of the clavicle was not palpable as normally. Point-of-care ultrasound helped us with the diagnosis, and after X-ray, a CT scan was performed to visualize the location of the medial end of the clavicle relative to the vasculature and the trachea. The child underwent surgery, where the attempt of closed reduction was unsuccessful, so the operating trauma specialist opted for open reduction, and the fixation with a figure-of-eight suture. The postoperative days went uneventfully, and the child's full range of motion was acquired again after a few months.In case of posterior sternoclavicular dislocation, early diagnosis and thorough examination are crucial not just to plan the treatment, but also to avoid further, potentially life-threatening complications. In many cases closed reduction can be performed successfully, but in some instances open reduction and internal fixation is inevitable. With appropriate treatment and physiotherapy, the full range of motion can be achieved quickly in the pediatric population.

Introduction

Traumatic sternoclavicular dislocation (SCD), particularly with a posteriorly displaced clavicle, is a rare injury, especially in the pediatric population. In the whole population, SCD represents about 3 % of all shoulder girdle injuries, and less than 1 % of all skeletal dislocations [1]. Among SCDs, posterior dislocation is found only in 3–5 % of the cases, as the anterior dislocation is far more common [2]. As the ossification of the medial clavicular physis is expected around 21–24 years of age [3], true dislocations at this site are rare in children because of the physis being relatively more prone to injuries than the joint itself [2].

Although it is rare, posterior SCD can become a life-threatening injury given the proximity of the vasculature and the trachea, potentially causing compression of the airway, or giving rise to severe bleeding not just at the time of the trauma, but when reduction is attempted as well.

In our article, we present the case of a 14-year-old boy, who suffered the posterior dislocation of the right sternoclavicular joint while playing with his friend. In his case, after thorough examination and evaluation, closed reduction was attempted under general anaesthesia, but the attempt was unsuccessful, and open reduction along with internal fixation was inevitable. The child healed well, achieving full range of motion in months.

Case presentation

Patient information and clinical findings

Our patient was a 14-year-old boy, with no history of any significant injury or diseases in the past. He suffered an injury while playing with a friend, who twisted his arm and pulled it behind his back. Our patient heard a silent click-like noise from the right shoulder and experienced an immediate sense of pain along with that. The EMS was called, who put the patient's arm in a sling – as part of the first aid for the suspected clavicular fracture -, and transferred the child to our department. Upon arrival he was in a stable condition, complaining about serious pain in the right clavicular and shoulder region.

During the initial physical examination, with palpation no deformity or swelling could be found at the most common fracture site, in the midshaft [4], but we experienced moderate swelling and a palpable absence of the medial end of the clavicle in the sternoclavicular joint. To make our suspicion more grounded, we used point-of-care ultrasound as the most quickly accessible modality of imaging in our daily practice, with which the „empty” sternoclavicular joint could also be identified, with the volar cortical of the clavicle seen to continue behind the sternum.

As we made sure that the child had no airway, breathing or circulation problems or symptoms of any cardiorespiratory complaints, an X-ray was taken as well to make sure that we see a dislocation rather than a fracture (See Fig. 1). After we saw the aforementioned imaging results, we consulted with our hospital's trauma specialists treating mostly adults, who had some experience with injuries like ours. They recommended reduction, and internal fixation if needed. To ease the serious pain experienced by the child, with special care taken, 2 % Lidocaine solution was administered via injection into the articular space. After this, the child had no further complaints.

Treatment

3 days after his admission -as time was needed to make the necessary preparation both of equipment and personnel -, the child underwent operation, during which closed reduction was attempted under general anaesthesia with traction of the arm and dorsal push of the shoulder, but this attempt was unsuccessful. After this, our trauma specialist opted for open reduction, which was performed via a wide incision above the medial half of the clavicle with the line extended over the upper segment of the sternum.

The reduction was performed using point-ended reduction forceps while the arm was pulled, and the shoulder was pushed in a dorsal direction. As the medial end of the clavicle was unstable in the joint and a tear was detected on the articular disc, a figure-of-eight suture with polydioxanone absorbable suture was placed through drill holes to stabilize the position of the clavicle relative to the sternum in the joint (See Fig. 3). After the suture application, a stability test was performed to ensure sufficient fixation. Follow-up and outcomes

After the surgery, the child had uneventful recovery from the anaesthesia, and had no pain at the site of the injury. 2 days later he was discharged with good overall status. Physiotherapy was started right after the operation to maintain the function of the elbow and the wrist, initially along with the rotation and flexion of the shoulder without any elevation for 3 weeks, later with passive - from weeks 3 to 6 -, then active elevation as well. The child had no complaints, experienced no pain after the surgery, and got back to full range of motion regarding the shoulder and the arm in two and a half months. After that, he gradually went back to doing sports, starting contact sports after about 3 months, and had no further problems to date (See Fig. 4).Fig. 4The last X-ray taken of the site of the injury, 1 year after the surgical intervention. Discussion

Posterior dislocation of the sternoclavicular joint is a rare injury, posterior dislocation taking up only 3–5 % of all cases [2]. The mechanism is usually an indirect force causing compression from sideways and from behind to the posterolateral aspect of the shoulder, causing a leverage force on the sternoclavicular joint, transmitted via the clavicular axis, or a blow to the contralateral shoulder while the ipsilateral shoulder is immobilized. Only 10–25 % is caused by direct force to the medial side of the clavicle [2,5]. In children and adolescents, true dislocation is seen in an even smaller amount of cases, as the medial physis of the clavicle is more prone to injury, causing the epiphyseolysis - or metaphyseal fracture - of the medial end of the clavicle a more likely injury before the closure of the growth plate of the clavicle, which usually happens at 21–24 years of age [2,3,6].

Typical presentation of a posterior SCD is swelling of the sternoclavicular joint, along with severe pain of the shoulder increasing with movement of the extremity. Visible sign is the asymmetry of the sternoclavicular joints, with the medial end of the clavicle not being palpable next to the sternum. The patient may also experience dyspnea, dysphagia, or even numbness and weakness of the upper extremity – which all can be caused by the compression of the structures in the thoracic inlet. Venous congestion of the extremity may also be caused by the compression of the vascular structures. The patient typically supports and guards the upper extremity on the injured side, while tilting the head towards that side, relieving the injured joint from the force applied by the sternocleidomastoid muscle [7].

Although final distinction between a fracture and a true dislocation may only be achieved by MRI or during surgery, first imaging modality used to make the suspicion stronger is usually X-ray, which should be followed by contrast-enhanced CT, the gold standard of imaging, which is capable of visualizing the vascular structures. CT is also useful in assessment of the displacement and the position of the sternoclavicular joint. Ultrasound may also come handy in diagnosing and even for the first assessment of the proximity to the vascular structures, but is largely limited by the operator experience [8]. MRI could be used to distinguish between a fracture and a true dislocation, and may be able to assess the congruity of the sternoclavicular joint as well the mediastinal compression caused by the clavicle end. It can also give a picture of the condition of the ligaments around the joint. MRI may also be preferrable in the aspect of radiation exposure [2].

Preoperative planning should be able to shed some light on the potential risks of reduction along with the experience in the field of trauma needed for the capability of safe reduction of the dislocation. Because of the proximity of great vessels, serious vascular injury should be anticipated and prepared for, not just with equipment, but with experienced personnel as well, having cardiothoracic and vascular surgery support, intensive radiology, and intensive care standing by as well [8].

Treatment of posterior SCD mainly consists of reduction as soon as reasonably possible – considering the needs mentioned in the previous paragraph -, along with internal fixation if needed. With early reduction, major complications may be avoided [9]. Closed reduction should be preferred when possible, with a technique pulling or pushing back the shoulders of the patient, or pulling the clavicle directly back forward with the fingers or a sterile towel clamp or reduction forceps. Although, closed reduction's success rate is only between 30 and 50 %, the rate being higher when the reduction is performed within 10 days of injury [2,10].

Open reduction should be opted for in case of failure with closed reduction techniques, or in the presence of complications [11]. After open reduction, means of fixation may vary from team to team, meaning figure-of-eight sutures with absorbable or non-absorbable sutures and tendon sutures, placement of an external fixator, grafting with palmaris longus or tibialis anterior with the use of synthetic ligaments, or even the resection of the medial part of the clavicle. If necessary [2,11]. K-wires have a potential of migration, and have shown an approximate death rate of 40 % in fixation of SCDs, as a consequence of vascular complications due to wire migration [6].

Immobilization of the sternoclavicular joint is usually advised for 4 to 8 weeks, with early motion of the shoulder as soon as comfort allows, weight-bearing restrictions being in place generally for 6 weeks, and absence from contact sports for 3 months [2].

Conclusion

Posterior sternoclavicular dislocation is a rare entity, especially in the pediatric population, and diagnosis may be difficult at first. Thorough evaluation and imaging are particularly important regarding the probability of potentially life-threatening complications arising after the initial injury. Although X-ray and ultrasound may help with making a diagnosis, CT scan or MRI is needed to fully evaluate the dislocation and the status of large vessels and the trachea in the upper mediastinum and the lower neck region, and to help with planning the treatment. Before reduction, preparation is needed to address any potential complications. Closed reduction may be successful, but preparation for open reduction and internal fixation is needed as well. In order to reach full range of motion as soon as possible, physiotherapy should be started right after the procedure, paying utmost attention to gradual mobilization of the healing sternoclavicular joint. With all the aforementioned criteria incorporated in our therapy plan, the patient may be able to recover fully from the injury in months' time.
