Disseminated melioidosis—challenge to routine antibiotic therapy: a case report
Authors: Atish Mohapatra, Pragya Agarwala, Hari Prasad Sirigiri, Padma Das

Introduction

Melioidosis caused by Burkholderia pseudomallei is often referred to as “The Great Mimicker” because it has a wide range of clinical presentations that can mimic other common illnesses. This nickname stems from the fact that the symptoms and signs of melioidosis closely resemble those of various other infectious and noninfectious diseases. Diverse clinical presentations, nonspecific symptoms, resemblance to other infections, variable incubation period, geographic variations, and lack of pathognomonic features create an escape route for this bacterium to be either misdiagnosed or underdiagnosed.

Melioidosis is endemic in tropical regions and is particularly prevalent in Southeast Asia and Northern Australia. It is transmitted through percutaneous inoculation, inhalation, or aspiration and is usually associated with risk factors such as diabetes mellitus, alcoholism, chronic lung disease, and chronic kidney disease. Here, we present a case report of disseminated melioidosis with septic arthritis, whose pus culture isolate was initially misidentified as Acinetobacter baumanii and was clinicoradiologically suspected to be disseminated tuberculosis and for which antitubercular treatment had been initiated.

Case report

A 48-year-old Asian man, an electrician, chronic alcoholic, and smoker, with a known history of type 2 diabetes mellitus for the last 12 years, presented with left lower limb swelling for 40 days (after visit to a waterfall), high-grade fever for 4 days, and breathlessness for 4 days. There was no history of dental procedures or tooth caries; 8 days prior to presentation, he was admitted to a private hospital with same complaints where magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lower limb showed multiple abscesses with myositis with minimal knee joint effusion. Previous culture isolate from ankle synovial joint fluid was reported as Acinetobacter baumanii complex, which was sensitive to all antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam, amikacin, ampicillin-sulbactam, cefepime, ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, cotrimoxazole, imipenem, meropenem, gentamicin, tobramycin) tested, and he was treated with intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam (4.5 gm 8 h). High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) thorax showed bilateral consolidation with enlarged mediastinal lymph nodes, for which he was started on oral weight-based combination of antitubercular drugs (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol) and discharged. After 3 days, the patient condition deteriorated with sudden onset of breathlessness, and he was admitted to trauma and emergency of our hospital.

On admission, he was well built with body mass index (BMI) of 23.8 kg/m2, febrile (102 °F), tachycardic (pulse rate, 132 beats per minute), tachypneic (respiratory rate, 36 breaths per minute) supported with noninvasive ventilator, FiO2 of 60%, oxygen saturation 98%, and blood pressure (138/80 mmHg). High random blood sugar (337 mg/dL) and presence of urinary ketones denoted uncontrolled diabetes mellitus. Bilateral crepitations were present. Swelling, redness, and tenderness over left lower limb was noted. He was conscious, oriented, and able to obey commands, with Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 15. Abdomen was soft, nontender, and no organomegaly was noted.

Initial investigations revealed white blood cell count of 4810/μL, hemoglobin (5.9 mg/dL), platelet count (58,000/μL), creatinine at 0.73 mg/dL, urea at 25 mg/dL, C-reactive protein (CRP) 274.9 mg/L, and procalcitonin (5.74 IU/mL). Thoracic X-ray showed bilateral lower lobe consolidation and pleural effusion. Fluid from left ankle joint effusion was aspirated aseptically and sent for bacterial and mycobacterial culture while no fungal culture was requested for the same. Paired aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures were also sent before administrating empirical intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g stat dose followed by 4.5 gm every 6 hours, and clindamycin 600 intravenous mg every 8 hours. However, his condition worsened with development of shock and required inotropes and he was intubated.

On day 1, pus culture from ankle was received in bacteriology lab and was processed for Gram staining. It showed many pus cells and Gram-negative bacilli with characteristic safety pin appearance, as shown in Fig. 1. Paired blood culture were received and both the aerobic BacT/ALERT (Make-Biomerieux, France) blood culture bottles flagged positive with bacteria showing a similar Gram stain picture. On day 2, both pus and blood culture showed non-hemolytic, non-lactose fermenting, umbonate, late oxidase positive colonies (Fig. 1). The colony was identified to be Burkholderia pseudomallei by Vitek-II automated identification system sensitive to meropenem, ceftazidime, cotrimoxazole, and doxycycline. Meanwhile, the pus sample was subjected to cartridge-based nucleic acid amplification test (CBNAAT) testing and turned out to be negative for Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Treatment regimen for melioidosis was planned as 4 weeks of intensive therapy with meropenem (2 g intravenous every 8 hours) and oral cotrimoxazole (160/800 mg) twice daily as per the 2020 revised Darwin melioidosis treatment guideline.

On day 7, fever subsided and gradual improvement in breathlessness was seen, but consecutive synovial joint fluid from the knee and paired blood culture repeatedly revealed B. pseudomoallei. An endotracheal aspirate (ET) aspirate was sent at this stage, and did not show any growth of B. pseudomallei (Table 1). On day 10, cotrimoxazole was replaced with doxycycline (100 mg intravenous every 12 hours) as the patient was developing pancytopenia (Table 2). On day 15, partial reduction of swelling and redness in ankle and knee joint was noted along with reduction of procalcitonin (PCT) values to normal referral ranges, despite of raised CRP and repeated blood culture positivity for B. pseudomallei (Fig. 2). Repeat blood cultures sent on day 21 yielded a negative report showing bacteriological clearance (Table 1). After 4 weeks of intensive treatment with meropenem, patient showed marked clinical recovery and was stepped down to intravenous ceftazidime (2 g per 8 h) for 14 days. After completion of prolonged intensive phase, patient was discharged on day 43, with an advice to strictly adhere to oral eradication regimen with cotrimoxazole (160/800 mg) twice daily for 3 months and monthly follow-up for next 6 months to check recovery as well as adverse drug reactions such as pancytopenia. Follow-up after 1 month showed remarkable recovery of the patient with reduction of swelling and redness in both the lower limb and clearing of chest X-ray (Fig. 3). Consecutive monthly follow-ups showed gradual improvement of the patient condition with continued eradication regimen. Patient was asymptomatic with complete recovery and performing all his activities of daily living at completion of therapy.

Discussion

This is a case of misidentified disseminated melioidosis, initially suspected to be an Acinetobacter spp. infection, later as tuberculosis on the basis of clinicoradiological picture, resulting in misuse of piperacillin-tazobactam and antitubercular drugs. Delayed diagnosis due to misidentification and delayed treatment lead to dissemination of the original infection by Burkholderia pseudomallei. Being a sturdy and extensively antibiotic resistant bacteria, it could sustain itself in blood in spite of appropriate intensive antibiotic therapy at 21 days.

Whitemore’s bacillus not only escapes human immune system, but also manages to escape from view of clinicians, clinical microbiologists, and radiologist. Fortunately, in our case, clinicians were prompt in suspecting it to be probable melioidosis and appropriate specimen (blood, synovial pus) were sent to microbiology laboratory. However, endotracheal tube aspirate did not grow Burkholderia pseudomallei, which can be attributed to sending the sample 7 days after antibiotic therapy. Prompt identification and antibiotic susceptibility reports led to initiation of right regimen, and the patient survived. In this case, the most probable mode of transmission could have been skin inoculation during visit to waterfall, following which the patient developed nodular lesions in left leg. Added risk factors such as diabetes mellitus and chronic alcoholism might have accentuated the pathogenesis in our case [1, 2].

Oxidase positive isolates with metallic sheen are commonly reported as Pseudomonas spp. in low-resource laboratory settings due to nonavailability of automated identification system. However, the characteristic colony morphology coupled with biochemical reactions (oxidase positivity, arginine hydrolysis) and intrinsic resistance to ampicillin, first- and second-generation cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, and colistin may give important clues in the manual identification of the organism. There have been several reports of B. pseudomallei being misidentified as other non-fermenters, or reported broadly as Pseudomonas spp., leading to nonspecific medication, as the treatment regimen is strikingly varied from that for other non-fermenters [3]. Greer et al. attempted reidentification of 419 blood culture previously identified as Acinetobacter isolates, from which 5/419 (1.2%) of isolates turned out to be B. pseudomallei [4]. Moreover, in fine-needle aspiration cytology of enlarged lymph nodes in case of melioidosis, presence of caseous necrosis may mislead the diagnosis toward tuberculosis (TB). In a tuberculosis-endemic country such as India, this often leads to injudicious administration of anti-TB regimen, similar to what happened with our case.

The patient was put on combination antibiotic therapy as per 2020 revised Darwin’s guidelines for melioidosis. According to this guideline, patients with bilateral pneumonia and blood culture positivity or deep-seated infection such as septic arthritis should be treated with at least 4 weeks of intensive therapy and 3 months of eradication therapy [5]. Meropenem, having a lower minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) value for B. pseudomallei, and quicker time-kill effect as compared with ceftazidime, proves superior for intensive phase regimen [6]. However, in our case, step down from meropenem to ceftazidime was done after observing major clinical improvement evident with resolution of symptoms, that is, subsiding fever and improvement in breathlessness along with significant reduction in redness and swelling in ankle and knee joint. However, it took around 21 days of intensive phase therapy to establish bacterial clearance (Fig. 2). Adherence to long-term eradication phase with cotrimoxazole resulted in gradual recovery of the patient, failing which could have led to relapse. However, it is important to monitor leukocyte counts when administrating cotrimoxazole in these cases to watch for pancytopenia during the long course of treatment.

Conclusion

The tenacious nature of this infection and its ability to cause multisystem involvement in high-risk patients pose a serious challenge for infectious disease physicians. Targeted antibiotic therapy with right dose and duration coupled with patient compliance to the long course of treatment and periodic clinical follow-up will ascertain cure in laboratory-confirmed melioidosis cases. Clinical suspicion must be communicated to the microbiology laboratory, and the laboratory in turn should be cautious during manual identification of non-fermenters.
