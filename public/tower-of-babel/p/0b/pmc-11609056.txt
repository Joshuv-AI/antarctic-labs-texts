Population Pharmacokinetics Modeling and Simulation of Deutenzalutamide, A Novel Androgen Receptor Antagonist, in Patients With Metastatic Castration‐Resistant Prostate Cancer
Authors: Yixian Liu, Yongji He, Xiaohui Qi, Xinghai Li, Yi Zhou, Yuanwei Chen, Zhenlei Wang, Li Zheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeutenzalutamide is a new molecular entity androgen receptor antagonist. The primary aim of this study was to develop a population pharmacokinetic model of deutenzalutamide and evaluate effects of intrinsic and extrinsic factors on pharmacokinetics. A nonlinear mixed‐effects modeling approach was performed to develop the population pharmacokinetic of deutenzalutamide using data from 1 Phase I trial of deutenzalutamide. Goodness‐of‐fit plots, prediction‐corrected visual predictive check, and bootstrap analysis were carried out to evaluate the final model. Simulation for the developed model was used to evaluate the covariate effects on the pharmacokinetics of deutenzalutamide. A 2‐compartment model with first‐order absorption and elimination from the central compartment was established for deutenzalutamide. The final covariate included body weight on peripheral compartment volume. This is the first research developing the population pharmacokinetic model of deutenzalutamide in patients with metastatic castration‐resistant prostate cancer, and it is expected to support the future clinical administration of deutenzalutamide.Keywords: androgen receptor antagonist, deutenzalutamide, HC‐1119, modeling and simulation, population pharmacokinetics

Prostate cancer is the most common malignancy in men worldwide and is the second leading cause of cancer‐related deaths in men worldwide, only behind lung cancer. 1 In China, prostate cancer ranks 6th in incidence of male malignant tumors and is the fastest growing male tumor in terms of incidence and mortality in China. Androgen‐deprivation therapy, achieved through surgery or chemotherapy, is the standard treatment for advanced prostate cancer. 2 , 3 , 4 Although this treatment strategy is highly effective in the initial phase of treatment for the majority of patients, almost all patients gradually progress to develop castration‐resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) with a median survival time of 14‐30 months, which has a limited treatment options and poor prognosis. 5 , 6 Metastatic castration‐resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) is a worse condition, with median overall survival of less than 2 years or even less than 19 months. 7 , 8

Current chemotherapy for CRPC treatment includes androgen synthesis inhibitors (eg, abiraterone) or androgen receptor antagonists (eg, enzalutamide). Abiraterone can block androgen synthesis from testicular, adrenal, and prostate sources, while enzalutamide inhibits androgen receptor binding to androgens, blocking the androgen receptor signaling pathway. Both can reduce androgenic activity in vivo and even in tumor cells to the greatest extent possible. 7 , 9 , 10 As a second‐generation androgen receptor antagonist, enzalutamide has a much higher affinity for androgen receptors than the first generation. It can inhibit the signaling of androgen receptors in cells with overexpression of androgen receptor, which significantly prolongs the survival of patients with CRPC who have previously received chemotherapy. 5 , 10 Compared with abiraterone, enzalutamide does not need to be combined with glucocorticoids, which can avoid side effects caused by glucocorticoid. However, adverse events associated with the central nervous system of enzalutamide, such as epilepsy and posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome, have been observed during clinical treatment. 11

Deutenzalutamide is a novel small‐molecule androgen receptor antagonist. It is a deuterated agent of enzalutamide, intended for the treatment of mCRPC, which can also competitively inhibit androgen binding to receptors, block the transmission of androgen receptor signaling pathways, inhibit prostate cancer cell proliferation, and engage in the induction of apoptosis. 12 Deutenzalutamide exhibits metabolic stability within hepatic microsomes, with urine serving as the principal route of excretion. Deutenzalutamide undergoes metabolism predominantly via deuteromethylation, hydrolysis of N‐methyl benzamide, and cyanide hydrolysis. In plasma, it primarily manifests as the parent compound, the carboxylic acid derivative (M1), and the N‐deuteromethylated metabolite (M2), shown as Figure S1. Cytochrome P450 2C8 serves as the principal enzyme involved in biotransformation of deutenzalutamide, with a secondary role played by cytochrome P450 3A4/5. Preclinical studies have shown that deuteration does not significantly change the physicochemical properties and biological activity of compounds, but increases the stability of compounds to metabolic enzymes, consequently increasing drug exposure in vivo, prolonging half‐life, and decreasing the production of metabolites. 13 , 14 , 15 Early‐phase clinical research demonstrated that deutenzalutamide was safe, well tolerated, and efficacious at a range of the given doses, and it had a lower effective dose compared with the enzalutamide when both are at the same level of exposure. 12 , 16 , 17 , 18 The primary aim of this study was to develop a population pharmacokinetic model of deutenzalutamide, which can quantitatively evaluate the covariates affecting the pharmacokinetic characteristics of deutenzalutamide, supporting the clinical studies and application of deutenzalutamide.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Population

Data from 1 Phase I trial of deutenzalutamide were included in this analysis. The trial followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice and was approved by the ethics committee at the study center. All subjects gave a written, signed informed consent prior to the trial.

The clinical trial of deutenzalutamide 12 (registration number NCT03774056) was an open‐label, dose‐escalation Phase Ia trial to evaluate the tolerability, safety, pharmacokinetics, and dose‐limiting toxicity of deutenzalutamide in patients with mCRPC. The study used a standard 3 + 3 dose escalation design and all eligible subjects were separated into 4 dose‐escalation cohorts: 40 mg (N = 3), 80 mg (N = 9), 160 mg (N = 9), and 200 mg (N = 3). Specifically, participants in each cohort should take the capsules of corresponding dose once daily for 12 weeks. The first medication administration was under fasting conditions, and after this, there was no dietary restriction per se. At the initial single‐dose stage, blood samples for pharmacokinetic analysis were collected within 0.5 hours before dosing and at 0.5, 0.75, 1, 2 (±5 minutes), 4, 8, 12 (±15 minutes), and 24 hours (±30 minutes, prior to the following administration) after deutenzalutamide administration. At the subsequent continuous dosing schedule, blood samples were collected within 0.5 hours before dosing on Days 7, 21, 35, 42, 56, 70, and 84 and at the 0.5, 0.75, 1, 2 (±5 minutes), 4, 8, 12 (±15 minutes), and 24 hours (±30 minutes) on Day 84 after the last dose of deutenzalutamide.

Bioanalytical Methods

The concentrations of deutenzalutamide in plasma samples were determined using a validated liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry method, which was developed by the Xihua Scientific Co, Ltd. [2H6]‐enzalutamide was used as internal standard to quantify deutenzalutamide, and [2H6]‐desmethyl‐enzalutamide to quantify M1 and M2. An Venusil XBP C18 column (50 mm × 2.1 mm, 3 µm; Agela Technologies, Inc.) was used to complete chromatographic separation at 32°C. Elution was accomplished using the mobile phase of 0.1% formic acid and 1 mmol/L ammonium acetate in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in methanol (B) at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The analytes were eluted using the following gradient elution method: 0‐2.50 minutes, 55% B; 2.50‐4.20 minutes, 55%‐70% B; 4.20‐4.30 minutes, 70%‐95% B; 4.30‐5.00 minutes, 95% B; 5.00‐5.01 minutes, 95%‐55% B; 6.00 minutes, stop. The settings for the mass spectrometry instrument were detailed in Table S1, while Table S2 provided information on the extraction methodology. The calibrated regression curves showed a good linear relationship ranging from 40 to 40,000 ng/mL for deutenzalutamide, 10‐10,000 ng/mL for M1, and 30‐30,000 ng/mL for M2. The lower limits of quantitation were 40.0, 10.0, and 30.0 ng/mL for deutenzalutamide, M1, and M2, respectively. The intra‐assay precision was 0.6%‐4.7% for deutenzalutamide, 0.6%‐5.3% for M1, and 0.7%‐5.1% for M2. The intra‐assay accuracy was 88.5%‐111.9% for deutenzalutamide, 90.2%‐113.5% for M1, and 91.0%‐114.6% for M2. The interassay precision was 3.4%‐4.9% for deutenzalutamide, 4.1%‐6.7% for M1, and 3.4%‐6.0% for M2. The interassay accuracy was 95.8%‐103.4% for deutenzalutamide, 94.7%‐103.5% for M1, and 95.9%‐103.2% for M2. The results of long‐term stability showed that deutenzalutamide and its metabolites M1 and M2 were stable in human plasma for 223 days when stored under −80°C. 16

Modeling Software

The population pharmacokinetics modeling and simulation of deutenzalutamide was characterized by using a nonlinear mixed‐effects modeling approach with NONMEM Version 7.5 (ICON Development Solutions). Perl‐speaks‐NONMEM Version 3.4.2 (Uppsala University, Sweden) and Pirana Version 2.8.0 (Certara Inc.) were performed as auxiliary tools for NONMEM. R software Version 4.2.1 (https://www.r‐project.org/) and R studio (https://www.rstudio.com) were used to preprocess the output of NONMEM and prepare the figures.

Structural Model

The structural model was performed using the ADVAN6 subroutine in NONMEM software, and the first‐order conditional estimation with interaction estimation method was used to build the population pharmacokinetics model.

Initially, classical linear 1‐ and 2‐compartment models were tested for deutenzalutamide in the model development. Different absorption models were investigated to describe the absorption process after oral administration, including 0‐order absorption, first‐order absorption, and 0‐first mixed absorption. Furthermore, Michaelis‐Menten elimination was also attempted to interpret nonlinear pharmacokinetic characteristics of deutenzalutamide when administrated under a higher dosage. Then, model convergence, objective function value (OFV), goodness‐of‐fit (GOF) plots, and parameter plausibility were used to discriminate the model.

Statistical Model

Random effect is a kind of observation factors that is difficult or impossible to measure. The statistical model in this population pharmacokinetics analysis included 2 parts: estimation of between‐subject variability and residual variability. Covariate Model

Covariate analysis in this population pharmacokinetic model was mainly tested to explore the source of interindividual variation in pharmacokinetic parameters. R software was first used to map the interindividual variation values of the pharmacokinetic parameters estimated by the final basic model, preceding the formal covariate modeling. The correlation between interindividual variation and covariates was explored through the scatter plot matrix, and covariates that might affect the model parameters were initially screened on the basis of the clinical significance and the scientific beliefs. After the covariates of correlation analysis, the stepwise covariate model was adopted to screen covariates. In order to prevent the collinearity of covariables and the influence of interference factors on the model, covariates with obvious correlation were added to the model separately for evaluation, rather than at the same time. The establishment of a covariate model consisted of 2 steps: the first step is to gradually include covariates in accordance with the criteria of covariate inclusion to obtain the full model, namely, forward inclusion. The second step is to obtain the final model according to the proposed standard, namely, backward elimination.

The specific criterion was that when a covariate was added to the model and the OFV decreased by more than 3.84 (χ2, df = 1, P < .05), the covariate would be included. If there were multiple covariates that met the requirements of correlation analysis, each covariate was separately added into the model, and then the covariate that met the inclusion criteria and had the largest OFV decline would be put into the model first as the basis model for the next round. The step of forward inclusion would be completed until no covariates were added, which formed a full model. The backward elimination of covariates was adopted under a stricter criterion. If the OFV value increased by more than 6.63 (χ2, df = 1, P < .01) after the elimination of a covariate in the full model, the covariate would be reserved; otherwise, the covariate would be removed from the model until all the covariates meeting the exclusion criteria were eliminated to obtain the final model.

Baseline age, height, body weight, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status grade, serum creatinine, leukocytes, neutrophils, platelets, hemoglobin, total bilirubin, aspartate aminotransferase, and alanine aminotransferase of subjects were included in this covariate analysis. Model Evaluation

Model evaluation was carried out to judge the stability and predictive robustness of the final population pharmacokinetic model, which included GOF, prediction‐corrected visual predictive check (pcVPC) and bootstrap analysis. For the pcVPC, 1000 simulations of the deutenzalutamide concentration were performed on the basis of the analysis data set. Nonparametric bootstrap analysis was used to evaluate the robustness of the final population pharmacokinetic model, and 500 replicates were performed to generate 95% confidence intervals of parameter estimates.

Simulation

To estimate the exposure of deutenzalutamide at steady state, the plasma concentrations of deutenzalutamide at steady state at different covariate levels were simulated after covariate screening.

Results

Summary of Analysis Data Set

A total of 24 subjects were included in the population pharmacokinetic analysis, who contributed total 543 deutenzalutamide concentrations. There were 4% data for deutenzalutamide below the lower limit of quantification, which were handled via the Beal M1 method. 19 Median age (range) at baseline in patients with mCRPC was 69.5 (55‐80) years. A summary of demographics, covariates, and baseline characteristics is shown in Table S3.

Exploratory Data Analysis

Initially, an exploratory data analysis was carried out to reveal the intrinsic characteristics of the data set. In the range of 40‐200 mg, after oral administration of deutenzalutamide soft capsules at different doses once a day in patients with mCRPC, deutenzalutamide (Figure 1) reached steady state on Day 56. The plasma concentration and exposure level (area under the concentration‐time curve from time 0 to infinity) in each dose group (40, 80, 160, and 200 mg) increased with the increase of dose. 12 The average peak time of multiple administration was about 1 h (0.5‐2 hours). The peak time was not affected by the change of dose level during multiple administration. It should be noted that deutenzalutamide had a high degree of accumulation when it reaches a steady state.

Population Pharmacokinetic Modeling

A 2‐compartment model for deutenzalutamide with first‐order absorption and elimination was chosen. The pharmacokinetic parameters of the structural model included absorption rate constant (ka), central compartment apparent volume of distribution, clearance rate, peripheral compartment apparent volume of distribution (V2/F), transport rate constant between central and peripheral compartment. The between‐subject variability of the parameters was fitted with an exponential model, while the residual variability was fitted by proportional model. In order to evaluate the influence of covariate on deutenzalutamide pharmacokinetic parameters, the covariate model was constructed by the method of forward inclusion and backward elimination. Model Evaluation

The GOF plot of deutenzalutamide for the final population model is shown in Figure 2. There was a good correlation between the predicted values and the observed values of plasma deutenzalutamide concentration, of which the trend line was basically close to the unity line. The trend of conditional weighted residual with population prediction and postadministration time was not obvious and was basically distributed homogeneously on both sides of y = 0, which indicated that the final model well fitted the observed values of deutenzalutamide. Prediction‐corrected visual predictive check was carried out to test the predictive performance of the final model with 1000 simulations, shown in Figure 3. The observed values of deutenzalutamide basically fell within the 90% prediction interval, suggesting that the final model had good predictive performance. Bootstrap analysis sampled a total of 500 times, and 476 samples were successfully minimized. The median value of the parameters from the bootstrap analysis is similar to the typical population value estimated, and the 95% confidence interval of the parameters was within the reasonable range (Table 1).

Discussion

A 2‐compartment model was constructed to describe the pharmacokinetic characteristics of deutenzalutamide. The steady‐state pharmacokinetic characteristics of deutenzalutamide can be fitted well by the final model. Judging from the pcVPC results (Figure 3), the absorption portion of the final model after the first dose was not well simulated (Figures S2, S3), which may be related to the small sample size included in this modeling. In this study, data from a Phase I clinical trial were used to examine the internal and external factors influencing the pharmacokinetics of deutenzalutamide, with the goal of providing valuable insights for subsequent clinical investigations. The small sample size inherently introduces variability and may affect the precision of our covariate effect estimates. This limitation is particularly relevant when assessing factors that might have subtle or moderate effects on the pharmacokinetics of deutenzalutamide. Regarding the variability of ka, various covariates were tested to identify potential predictors of ka, shown as Table S4, but none were found to be statistically significant. However, since deutenzalutamide is a high‐accumulation drug, more attention is paid to the state when homeostasis is reached. Therefore, the shortcomings of this model will not have an impact on the final conclusion. Deutenzalutamide is a deuterated compound of enzalutamide, both of which are highly accumulating drugs. The plasma concentration of deutenzalutamide after stabilization was 16.5 times that of a single dose, while the plasma concentration of enzalutamide after stabilization was 8.3 times that of a single dose. 12 , 16 , 20 Therefore, the comparison of the exposure of deutenzalutamide and enzalutamide in patients with prostate cancer at steady state is more instructive for clinical trials. The results of the simulations were consistent with the clinically observed that deutenzalutamide reached steady state at about Day 56, 28 days longer than enzalutamide, 21 which is related to the properties of the deuterated drug. Considering the kinetic isotope effect, carbon‐deuterium (C‐D) bonds are shorter than carbon‐hydrogen bonds and 6‐10 times more stable, making it more difficult to break. 14 The molecular weight of deuterium in deutenzalutamide is heavier than hydrogen in enzalutamide, and the C‐D bond has a lower vibrational frequency and 0‐point energy, which means that the C‐D bond need to be broken with a higher activation energy and reduces the rate of cleavage of C‐D bonds. This rate effect is the deuterium isotope effect, expressed as the ratio of the carbon–hydrogen bond to the cleavage rate of the C‐D bond. 22 Tetrabenazine, a drug used to treat movement disorders such as Huntington disease and tardive dyskinesia, undergoes metabolic processes in the liver that include dehydrogenation. The deuterated form of tetrabenazine, known as deuterated tetrabenazine (DTBZ), has been studied to explore the impact of deuteration on its pharmacokinetics. Studies have shown that deuterated tetrabenazine has a reduced clearance rate compared with the nondeuterated version. This reduction is primarily due to the kinetic isotope effect, where the substitution of hydrogen with deuterium slows down the enzymatic processes involved in drug metabolism. As a result, DTBZ remains in the system for a longer period compared with tetrabenazine. The slower metabolic rate of DTBZ contributes to a longer half‐life. 23 , 24

The simulation results showed that patients with increased body weight took longer to achieve steady‐state exposure. This is consistent with the results of modeling, and the final covariate included the effect of body weight on the V2/F. The main reason for this phenomenon is that body weight has an effect on the volume of distribution of the drug in the body. The volume of distribution reflects how widely a drug disperses throughout the body tissues compared to the plasma. As body weight increases, the number of body tissues and fluids, including blood volume, extracellular fluid, and adipose tissue, typically increases as well. This can lead to a larger volume of distribution because the drug has more compartments to distribute into since the drug can be dispersed in these tissues and fluids. When the patient gains weight, the total internal mass in the body also increases, and therefore the volume of distribution also increases. This means that the same administered dose is dispersed into a larger volume, resulting in a relative dilution of the concentration of the drug in the blood, which increases the time it takes deutenzalutamide to achieve steady state. Concurrently, deutenzalutamide is a lipophilic drug. Our analysis demonstrated that increased body weight, presumably due to more adipose tissue, leads to a larger volume of distribution for deutenzalutamide. This relationship is crucial because an increase in the volume of distribution results in a longer elimination half‐life, assuming clearance remains unchanged. 25 , 26 Consequently, with body weight gain, the attainment of steady state after the initiation of multiple dosing will be delayed, as depicted in Figure 4. Similarly, drug washout will be prolonged following the cessation of chronic dosing. However, the actual steady‐state concentration, once reached, will remain unaffected by the changes in the volume of distribution, provided that there are no alterations in the drug's clearance rate. 25 Compared with patients with typical body weight, when 80 mg of deutenzalutamide is administered once daily, the fluctuation range of steady‐state exposure in subjects weighing 50‐80 kg was 96.2%‐110.6%, which could be considered to be no clinically significant difference. It is further suggested that there is no need to adjust the dose according to the body weight.

For patients with impaired liver or kidney function, the extrapolation performance of this model has limitations and should be carefully considered. The results of [14C] deutenzalutamide human mass balance and biotransformation study showed that deutenzalutamide was mainly excreted via urine, and the total radioactivity excreted through urine from 0 to 1224 hours was 85.42% of the administered amount. Therefore, renal function‐related indicators may have an impact on the pharmacokinetics of deutenzalutamide. The effects of liver function and kidney function–related indicators on the pharmacokinetics of deutenzalutamide were investigated, the final model did not include these covariates according to the principle of covariate inclusion. Verify the accuracy and predictive power of the model by comparing it with actual clinical data. The final model has been able to accurately predict the steady‐state pharmacokinetic profile of deutenzalutamide (Table 1, Figure 3), and it can be assumed that these biochemical indicators may not be necessary for the results of this model. This means that aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and serum creatinine did not have a large impact on the model. According to the instructions for enzalutamide, mild, moderate, and severe kidney injury and liver injury have no significant effect on the pharmacokinetics of enzalutamide, and there is no need to adjust the dose in the above patients. However, these indicators were within the normal range due to the included participants. If extrapolated to subjects with hepatic and renal insufficiencies, the predictive performance will be reduced. Therefore, this model did not predict the pharmacokinetic characteristics of this special population. Further clinical studies are needed in the future.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a population pharmacokinetic model of deutenzalutamide was characterized by a 2‐compartment model with first‐order absorption and elimination. Although body weight can affect the apparent volume of distribution, there is no significant difference in the change in steady‐state exposure associated with this effect. Weight‐based dosing may not need to be considered. Overall, the findings of the current population pharmacokinetic analysis on deutenzalutamide can be instrumental to support the future clinical application of mCRPC treatment.
