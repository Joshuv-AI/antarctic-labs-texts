Associations of Fine Particulate Matter and Its Specific Components With Recurrent Stroke and Secondary Morbidity and Mortality
Authors: Zhikai Zhu, Qijiong Zhu, Pei Yu, Yongjun Wang, Tao Liu, Yong Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesAmbient particulate matter <2.5 μm (PM2.5) has been extensively associated with morbidity and mortality of stroke. However, most previous findings were derived from separate clinical phase analyses and have not focused on the specific components of PM2.5. Using a national cohort study based on multistate analysis, we aimed to evaluate the associations of PM2.5 and its specific components with several different clinical progression phases of stroke.MethodsWe analyzed data from first-ever stroke patients in the Third China National Stroke Registry, and follow-up was conducted through March 2023. Ambient PM2.5 and its components were assessed using one-year average concentrations before the end point or the latest follow-up, obtained from Tracking Air Pollution in China. Outcomes were verified through hospital records and official registries. We used multistate models to estimate the associations and applied P-splines to assess potential nonlinear associations of PM2.5 and its components. We also conducted subgroup analyses to explore potential effect modification.ResultsWe included a total of 11,491 participants with a mean age of 61.5 ± 11.5 years, of whom 7,821 (68.1%) were male. We observed positive associations of PM2.5 mass concentrations and all 5 chemical components with each stage of ischemic stroke progression. For PM2.5 mass concentration, the adjusted hazard ratios per 12.5 μg/m3 increase were as follows: 2.82 (95% CI 2.69–2.94) for the transition from first stroke to recurrence, 2.85 (95% CI 2.40–3.37) from first stroke to poststroke cardiovascular diseases (PCVDs), 2.50 (95% CI 2.28–2.73) from first stroke to death, 2.23 (95% CI 1.97–2.53) from stroke recurrence to death, and 1.93 (95% CI 1.33–2.80) from PCVDs to death. All 5 chemical components also showed strong associations across each transition stage. The exposure-response relationships followed J-shaped curves (p for nonlinearity <0.001). Of interest, patients with good functional outcomes (modified Rankin Scale score ≤3) or mild strokes (NIH Stroke Scale score <5) had a higher risk of recurrence or death, possibly because of increased exposure to outdoor air pollution.DiscussionLong-term exposure to PM2.5 and its specific components was positively associated with the risk of recurrent stroke, and secondary morbidity and mortality. Exposure assessment relied on residential addresses rather than personal measurements, which should be noted as a limitation.

Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of global mortality and the primary contributor to both morbidity and disability in China, imposing an enormous burden on the health care system.1 Despite the widespread use of contemporary secondary prevention therapies and appropriate management of modifiable risk factors, a substantial residual risk (approximately 16%–17% within 1 year, 41% within 5 years) of recurrent events exists among patients with first-ever acute ischemic stroke.2 Approximately 9% of stroke survivors will experience major adverse cardiovascular events within the first year after stroke.3 These complications include incident heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, arrhythmia, coronary artery disease, and even death.

Ambient fine particulate matter <2.5 μm (PM2.5) has gained substantial attention in recent years because of its adverse impacts on brain health.4 The proposed mechanisms include the following: PM2.5 directly elicits cerebral inflammatory changes, oxidative stress, and autonomic nervous system imbalance and triggers abnormal epigenetic changes,5 and these processes contribute to stroke progression by impairing cerebrovascular endothelium, enhancing thrombotic risk, and accelerating atherosclerotic plaque instability. As a complex mixture, the toxicity of PM2.5 is determined by its chemical composition, including black carbon (BC), ammonium (NH4+), nitrate (NO3−), organic matter (OM), and sulfate (SO42−). These constituents may exert different effects on brain health because of their different physicochemical properties.6

Currently, most existing studies have focused on the relation of PM2.5 with separate clinical phases after stroke,7,8 whereas exposure to specific components of PM2.5 and different clinical progression phases of stroke has received relatively little attention. For instance, preliminary evidence has linked higher PM2.5 level to a higher risk of stroke recurrence and poststroke cardiovascular diseases (PCVDs).9 One study8 reported positive associations between BC, NH4+, NO3−, OM, and SO42− of PM2.5 and stroke mortality. Another study discovered that exposure to OM and SO42− was significantly associated with poststroke disability, whereas no such associations were observed for other components. In addition, previous studies have been conducted in the United States, the United Kingdom, and Korea,10-12 where air pollution levels are relatively low, and there is little evidence from the developing countries with more severe pollution. From the perspectives of clinical management and public health, it is essential to identify the associations of PM2.5 and its specific components with recurrent stroke and secondary morbidity and mortality and implement multilevel societal efforts, such as expanding green spaces, promoting air purifiers, and encouraging individual mask wearing, to prevent stroke progression.

Using prospective cohort data from the Third China National Stroke Registry (CNSR-III), our study aimed to evaluate the associations of PM2.5 and its specific components with several different clinical progression phases of stroke, including the transitions from first-ever stroke to recurrence or PCVD, and subsequently to mortality.

Methods

Data Source and Study Population

We extracted data from the CNSR-III, which consecutively enrolled a total of 15,166 patients aged 18 years or older with acute ischemic stroke or TIA occurring within 7 days of onset at 201 hospitals in 22 provinces and 4 municipalities between August 2015 and March 2018; patients were followed up for up to 5 years. Baseline patient information was collected within 24 hours of admission through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained research coordinators. Participants were followed from enrollment until death, loss to follow-up, or March 31, 2023, whichever occurred first. The diagnosis of acute ischemic stroke was based on the World Health Organization criteria and confirmed by MRI or CT of the brain. A detailed description of this cohort has been published elsewhere.13 For the purposes of this study, we excluded participants with TIA and a history of recurrent strokes at baseline (n = 3,531). To reduce potential confounding by severe comorbidities and competing risks, we excluded patients with cancer (n = 101) or those who died or developed other cardiovascular diseases (n = 43) on the day of enrollment, in accordance with many cohort studies.11,14 Ultimately, a total of 11,491 individuals were enrolled in our study (eFigure 1). eTable 1 illustrates the distribution of the regions included in the study.

Environmental Data and Exposure Assessment

We collected data on PM2.5 and its chemical components, including BC, NH4+, NO3−, OM, and SO42−, from the Tracking Air Pollution in China (TAP).15 Integrating observed concentration of air pollutants, satellite retrievals, meteorological reanalysis data, land use, and population distribution, the TAP developed machine learning models to predict daily PM2.5 concentration with a spatial resolution of 10 × 10 km. Furthermore, on the basis of 10-km spatial resolution data set, taking PM2.5 concentration as the whole constraint, daily PM2.5-specific component information was obtained using revised commercial Congestion Mitigation and Air Quality–simulated conversion factors at a daily scale to partition the whole PM2.5 concentrations from TAP (R = 0.64–0.75, root mean square error = 2.35–12.67).16 In CNSR-III, residential addresses were collected at the county or district level at baseline. We overlaid these administrative boundaries with gridded pollution data sets to first calculate daily average air pollutant concentration and then calculated average concentration of air pollutants during the year before the end point or the latest follow-up for each participant as the mean exposure to PM2.5 and its components, by matching the county or district of the recorded residential addresses. This approach is consistent with well-established practice in environmental epidemiology, wherein the residential address serves as a valid proxy for estimating sustained long-term personal exposure.6,11,17

Follow-Up and Outcome Measures

Patient follow-up was conducted at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months after discharge, and annually thereafter. Each follow-up was conducted through face-to-face interviews or telephone calls by trained coordinators, who were physicians from the participating subcenter hospitals. Follow-up data were systematically collected on the occurrence and exact timing of outcome events, including mortality and its cause, stroke recurrence, PCVDs, incident stroke-related symptoms, functional disability, vascular surgery, and hospitalizations. Medication adherence to antiplatelets, anticoagulants, antihypertensives, lipid-lowering agents, and hypoglycemic drugs was also assessed at every follow-up. In this analysis, the outcome events were identified in accordance with the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision. Stroke recurrence (ischemic or hemorrhagic, I60–I64) and PCVDs, which were coded as ischemic heart disease (I20–I25) and heart failure (I50), were confirmed by the original medical records of the treating hospital, rather than only relying on patient self-report. Suspected stroke recurrences were further confirmed by CT or MRI, whereas cases without hospitalization were adjudicated by an independent end point committee. Death was confirmed by either death certificates from the attending hospital or the local citizen registry. Data monitoring was performed using an electronic data capture system by an independent contract research organization throughout the study period.

Covariates

We considered the following candidate covariates based on a priori knowledge of their associations with stroke and cardiovascular diseases18: demographic information including age (continuous, years), sex (male/female), region (South/North China, by the Qinling Mountains-Huaihe River line), and urbanization (urban/rural, urban defined as municipalities or prefecture-level cities and rural as counties or county-level cities)19; socioeconomic status including education level (below high school/high school or above) and family monthly income (≤2,300/>2,300 yuan); potential risk factors including smoking status (yes/no), drinking status (yes/no), and body mass index (calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared and categorized into obese [≥28 kg/m2] and nonobese [<28 kg/m2]); family history of stroke (yes/no); history of heart diseases (yes/no); stroke severity assessed by the NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS) at admission (range 0–42, later dichotomized by the median); functional status assessed by the prestroke modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score within 24 hours of hospital arrival (range 0 [no symptoms] to 6 [death], categorized as <3 [no symptoms to slight disability] and ≥3 [moderate disability to death]); tissue-type plasminogen activator (tPA) treatment (yes/no); and biomarker levels including homocysteine (HCY), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), and interleukin 6 (IL-6) (all dichotomized into low and high levels by their medians). All covariates were collected at baseline, concurrent with or before exposure assessment, through baseline questionnaires, clinical examinations, or laboratory tests. A directed acyclic graph (DAG) was used to generate the minimally sufficient adjusted set, including age, sex, education, family income, and region (eFigure 2).

Statistical Analysis

Participant characteristics were summarized using means with SDs for continuous variables and numbers with proportions (%) for categorical variables. Student t tests and Pearson χ2 tests were used to compare differences in characteristics among groups with or without stroke recurrence, PCVDs, and death. Missing values for education level (14.2%), household income (24.7%), history of heart disease (1.1%), and family stroke history (4.6%) were incorporated as a separate “unknown” category using dummy variables, whereas all other covariates were complete.

In the primary analyses, we used multistate regression models to investigate the associations of PM2.5 and its components with different transitions, that is, (1) baseline (first-ever stroke morbidity) to stroke recurrence; (2) baseline to PCVD; (3) baseline to death; (4) stroke recurrence to death; and (5) PCVDs to death. The proportional hazards assumption was confirmed by Schoenfeld residual plots. For participants who progressed through multiple states in the same day, we redefined the occurrence time of the former state by subtracting 0.5 days, according to previous studies.11,14 We fitted both nonlinear and linear associations of PM2.5 and its components for 5 transitions. We applied P-splines with 3 degrees of freedom to assess potential nonlinear associations and plotted exposure-response curves. Linear associations were estimated by removing the spline function from the models, and main results from linear associations are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) per each SD increase in the concentration of PM2.5 and its components. We also estimated transition probabilities for each state under low-level and high-level air pollution exposure, separately for men and women.

In addition, several stratified analyses were conducted to explore potential modifications, and Cochrane Q tests were used to test the differences among subgroups. We calculated the E-value to assess the effects of unmeasured confounding factors.20 Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of our results: (1) assigning different time intervals for participants who progressed through multiple states in the same day (0.5, 1, and 1.5 years); (2) removing participants who progressed through more than 1 state in the same day; (3) including participants with cancers at baseline; (4) further adjusting for urbanization; (5) further adjusting for obese status, smoking, drinking, history of heart diseases, family stroke history, tPA treatment, mRS score, NIHSS score, level of HCY, level of hsCRP, and level of IL-6; and (6) further adjusting for hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes based on model 5.

All statistical analyses in study were performed in R software (version 4.2.2, R Core Team 2023), and the “mstate” package was used to construct multistate regression models. Overall, a 2-sided p value of <0.05 was assumed statistically significant.

Standard Protocol Approvals and Patient Consents

The study was approved by the ethics committee at Beijing Tiantan Hospital (KY2015-001-01) and all participating hospitals. Written informed consent was obtained from the participants or their legally authorized representatives.

We used the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline21 to draft this article and the STROBE reporting checklist when editing.

Data Availability

Data not provided in the article because of space limitations may be shared (anonymized) at the request of any qualified investigator for purposes of replicating procedures and results, by directly contacting the corresponding authors.

Results

Descriptive Results

During 51,538 person-years of follow-up, we identified 1,534 cases of stroke recurrence, 188 cases of PCVDs, and 992 deaths (Figure 1). The mean concentration of PM2.5 was 37.4 μg/m3 (SD 12.5 μg/m3) in all participants. The mean age of 11,491 participants was 61.5 ± 11.5 years. Of those, 7,821 (68.1%) were male, 1,581 (13.8%) were obese, 8,123 (70.7%) resided in northern China, 5,532 (48.1%) were smokers, 5,196 (45.2%) were drinkers, and 1,447 (12.6%) reported heart disease in the baseline survey. Participants who experienced adverse events (recurrent stroke, PCVD, and death) were more likely to have been exposed to elevated levels of PM2.5 and its specific components; be older; have a history of heart disease; and have higher levels of HCY, hsCRP, and IL-6. Patients with recurrent stroke and/or mortality were likely to have family history, worse functional independence (mRS), and more severe stroke (NIHSS) (Table 1). The participants excluded from the study (n = 3,675) had similar characteristics to those included (eTable 2). eTable 3 summarizes the correlations among the pollutants.

Multistate Regression Results and Exposure-Response Curve

We estimated the roles of PM2.5 and its specific components in the clinical transitions of stroke in multistate analyses (Table 2). In the crude model, exposure to PM2.5 concentrations and all 5 chemical components was significantly associated with an increased risk of recurrent stroke, PCVDs, and death. The results in the multivariable model were stable when we further adjusted for age, sex, education, family income, and region. Generally, we observed positive associations between PM2.5 and all 5 chemical components and each stage of stroke progression. The adjusted HRs per SD increase in PM2.5 were as follows: 2.82 (95% CI 2.69–2.94, E-value = 5.09) for the transition from first stroke to recurrence; 2.85 (95% CI 2.40–3.37, E-value = 5.15) from first stroke to PCVD; 2.50 (95% CI 2.28–2.73, E-value = 4.44) from first stroke to death; 2.23 (95% CI 1.97–2.53, E-value = 3.89) from stroke recurrence to death; and 1.93 (95% CI 1.33–2.80, E-value = 3.27) from PCVDs to death. All 5 PM2.5 constituents exhibited strong and statistically significant HRs (all p values < 0.001) across each transition stage, as presented in Table 2. Among these components, BC and OM exhibited the 2 highest HRs. Specifically, the magnitudes of associations between PM2.5 and its specific components and both recurrent stroke and PCVDs were stronger than those with the transitions to mortality. In addition, the exposure-response curves between pollutants and the 5 transitions of stroke are displayed in Figure 2 and eFigure 3. The exposure-response relationships followed J-shaped curves, with relatively flat risks at lower concentrations that increased rapidly beyond a threshold (p for nonlinearity <0.001).

Discussion

In this prospective national study based on the CNSR-III, we comprehensively evaluated the associations of PM2.5 and its specific components with recurrent stroke, PCVDs, and death. Our main findings revealed that long-term exposure to PM2.5 and its components, including BC, NH4+, NO3−, OM, and SO42−, was positively associated with the risk of ischemic stroke progression. Specifically, an increase in concentrations of PM2.5 and its specific components was significantly associated with a higher risk of transitions from first-ever stroke to recurrence, PCVDs, and subsequent death. Subgroup analyses also showed that age, sex, mRS score, and NIHSS score modified the associations of PM2.5 and its components with stroke progression.

Most previous findings were derived from separate clinical phases. Our study used multistate regression models to distinguish the associations of PM2.5 and its specific components with different clinical progression phases of stroke. Our study findings are inconsistent with those of previous studies conducted in the United States10 and the United Kingdom,11 where air pollution levels are relatively low. One retrospective cohort study of 96,582 stroke or myocardial infarction survivors (aged ≥18 years) from Kaiser Permanente Northern California (2007–2016) found that a 10-μg/m3 increase in 1-year mean PM2.5 exposure was associated with 20% higher cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.20, 95% CI 1.11–1.30) but showed no significant association with stroke recurrence (HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.82–1.05).10 In another study of 318,752 healthy participants aged 40–69 years from the UK Biobank, no association was identified between 1-year PM2.5 data (2010) and the risk of subsequent progression stages after incident stroke11 (from first stroke to PCVD, HR 1.12, 95% CI 0.94–1.33; from first stroke to death, HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.64–1.28; from PCVD to death, HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.64–1.16). Our study was conducted in China, where the annual average PM2.5 concentration (52.3 ± 27.0 μg/m3) was notably higher than that in the United States (10.2 ± 2.0 μg/m3; 12.4 ± 6.6 μg/m3 in another study including 652 cities22) and the United Kingdom (9.98 ± 1.06 μg/m3). Our analyses revealed stronger associations with stroke recurrence (HR 2.35, 95% CI 2.26–2.45) and subsequent progression stages. These discrepancies may also be explained by different study populations (11,491 Chinese patients with first-ever stroke in our study vs 96,582 American survivors of stroke or myocardial infarction vs 318,752 healthy UK population), exposure windows (the year preceding the end point vs 2000–2016 long-term average vs single-year 2020), magnitude of PM2.5 increments (12.5 vs 5 vs 10 μg/m3), follow-up length (5 vs 10 vs 12 years), and age structure (61.5 ± 11.5 vs 65% ≥65 years vs 56 ± 8 years).

Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies on stroke mortality from Korea,12 India,23 and China,17 but the point estimates we reported are substantially stronger. This difference could be explained by either the higher exposure concentration in our study, compared with those reported in Korean and Indian studies, or our exposure window being closer to the outcome onset relative to another Chinese study. In a study of 45,513 older stroke survivors (≥ 65 years) between 2008 and 2016 across 7 Korean metropolitan cities (with an average PM2.5 concentration of 27.9 μg/m3), a 10-μg/m3 increase in the 1-month and 2-month moving average PM2.5 exposures was associated with mortality HRs of 1.07 (95% CI 1.05–1.09) and 1.06 (95% CI 1.03–1.08), respectively.12 Similarly, an Indian study (1999–2014; average PM2.5 24.4 μg/m3)23 conducted in the general population reported a 9% increased mortality risk per 10-μg/m3 increase in PM2.5, whereas another Chinese study (2007–2008; average PM2.5 79.5 μg/m3)17 focusing on stroke patients observed a 3% increased risk for the same exposure increment. Moreover, these studies failed to account for competing mortality risks. Given that air pollution increases mortality from stroke and other causes, censoring participants who died during follow-up may bias the estimated morbidity risk. To address these issues, we applied a multistate model that incorporates competing risks and disease-stage transitions.

The results in our study enriched the evidence linking exposure to specific components of PM2.5 with adverse outcomes after stroke. Previous studies based on Chinese data reported inconsistent findings, potentially because of heterogeneity in study design, population characteristics, and clinical end points. One study included 3,069,093 hospitalized stroke patients found that each interquartile range (IQR) increment in BC (odds ratios [ORs] 1.11, 95% CI 1.09–1.13; IQR 0.71 μg/m3), OM (OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.07–1.10; IQR 3.47 μg/m3), and SO42− (OR 1.07, 95% CI 1.05–1.08; IQR 2.81 μg/m3) was significantly associated with in-hospital stroke mortality.24 Another ecological study in Guangdong province used a time-series analysis and found that NH4+, NO3−, and SO42− were significantly associated with stroke mortality among urban residents.8 The third study,25 which focused on poststroke functional disability, discovered that each IQR increment in OM (7.92 μg/m3) and SO42− (6.28 μg/m3) was significantly associated with mRS scores among 28,085 stroke patients, whereas no such associations were observed for other components.

The calculated E-values indicate that any unmeasured residual confounder would need to be associated with both the exposure and stroke mortality by a HR of at least 4.44 to fully nullify our findings. This threshold is substantially higher than the effect sizes previously reported for low diet quality (HR 1.23, 95% CI 1.13–1.34),26 absence of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (HR 1.31, 95% CI 1.21–1.42),27 or poor medication adherence (HR 1.85, 95% CI 1.58–2.17)28 in relation to stroke mortality risk, as well as those reported for comorbidities such as diabetes (HR 1.26, 95% CI 1.22–1.31)29 and hypertension (HR 1.17, 95% CI 1.10–1.24) in association with PM2.5 exposure. The E-values for other transitions similarly suggested that it is unlikely that such comorbidities or behavioral or health care–related factors could fully explain the observed association between PM2.5 and its components and stroke progression.

We observed increased susceptibility to PM2.5 and its specific components in the younger group (age <65 years), male individuals, the good functional outcome group (mRS score ≤3), and the mild stroke group (NIHSS score <5). They were more likely to experience outdoor air pollution due to better mobility, a higher frequency and intensity of outdoor activities,30 and increased commuting exposure. By contrast, their counterparts may have reduced environmental exposure because of more limited mobility, higher rates of comorbidities, and more time spent indoors. Beyond behavioral factors, the underlying biological vulnerability must be considered. Younger adults with structurally intact blood-brain barrier express higher tight junction proteins and are thus more susceptible to functional disruption by PM2.5-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) and proinflammatory cytokines.31 Possibly owing to the lack of estrogen's protective effect against oxidative stress, male individuals have more metabolic pathways disrupted by long-term PM2.5 exposure than female individuals.32 In patients with good functional outcomes or mild strokes, the preserved myelin integrity33 and neurovascular unit function maintain responsiveness to PM2.5 and its components, leading to amplified neuroinflammation and sympathetic activation.34 By contrast, those with severe strokes often exhibit preexisting cerebrovascular dysfunction or dysautonomia, resulting in a ceiling effect that attenuates their physiologic response to exposure. Furthermore, treatment disparities may play a role, as patients with milder strokes often receive more aggressive secondary prevention measures, potentially modifying their response to air pollution.

Regarding the mechanism underlying PM2.5-induced ischemic stroke recurrence, PCVDs, and subsequent death, accumulating evidence indicates that PM2.5 exerts its effects through direct damage to the cerebrovascular system, systemic inflammation spreading from the pulmonary to the cardiovascular system, and blood pressure changes mediated by the autonomic nervous system.34,35 PM2.5 exposure elevates ROS levels and amplifies the activity of proinflammatory and procoagulant factors.36 This process directly impairs blood-brain barrier integrity, disrupting its permeability, vasomotor regulation, and reparative capacity.37 Endothelial dysfunction drives coagulation disorders35 and thrombosis risk while accelerating cerebral atherosclerotic progression by promoting lipid core expansion and fibrous cap thinning, thereby increasing plaque instability.38 This instability is a critical trigger for recurrent stroke and PCVDs because unstable plaques readily rupture or erode to occlude cerebral and cardiac vessels. In addition, PM2.5 raises plasma fibrinogen and von Willebrand factor levels and reduces red blood cell deformability, further enhancing thrombotic potential in cerebrovascular beds.39 It disrupts the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, causing sustained cortisol elevation and sympathetic overactivation40; these effects may exacerbate hypertension and plaque vulnerability. Finally, they may indirectly increase the risk of stroke progression by influencing cerebral vascular risk factors such as hypertension, hyperglycemia, and atrial fibrillation and induce epigenetic changes such as platelet mitochondrial DNA methylation41 and changes in endothelial cell miRNAs.42

As a complex mixture, the toxic effect of PM2.5 may mainly depend on its chemical components.43 BC, a tracker of older diesel fuels, amplifies poststroke inflammation and oxidative stress by downregulating DNA methylation (activating ischemic/thrombotic mechanisms),44 increasing systematic inflammatory markers, and impairing vasodilation, all of which increase recurrence risk and disrupt cerebral perfusion critical for recovery.43 OM, especially polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, penetrates the poststroke disrupted blood-brain barrier to trigger microglial activation and proinflammatory cytokine release, perpetuating neuronal damage and hindering neuroplasticity. The inorganic components (NH4+, NO3−, SO42−) also contribute to blood inflammation and coagulation45 and are associated with elevated blood pressure and the prevalence of hypertension.46 Regarding their sources, NO3− is derived mainly from the photochemical transformation of NO2, whereas NH4+ is formed by the neutralization of atmospheric nitric and sulfuric acids by ammonia. Regarding mechanisms, NH4+, NO3−, and SO42− disrupt the hypothalamic-pituitary adrenal axis through neuroendocrine dysregulation47 to compromise poststroke stress adaptation.

Our findings highlight the critical need for targeted protection of stroke survivors. We propose three-tiered preventive measures: (1) behavioral changes, including limiting outdoor activities, wearing masks outdoors if necessary, and promoting indoor air purifiers; (2) enhanced clinical surveillance of cardiovascular risk factors (e.g., blood pressure and lipid profiles); and (3) environmental modifications through urban green space expansion to reduce local exposure. It is also urgent to develop air pollution control standards and specific measures and to improve the understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying the health associations of PM2.5 and its chemical components.48 The concerted efforts by the whole society are needed to provide clean air.

Our study has several strengths. First, we used data from a large-scale, nationwide prospective cohort, which ensured the representativeness of the study population. Second, we used multistate analysis to distinguish the associations of air pollutants with different phases of stroke progression with less bias than the conventional Cox model and comprehensively considered various covariates through a DAG to control for confounding factors, thus making the results more reliable. Third, we not only investigated the associations of PM2.5 concentration but also its specific components, providing a more in-depth understanding of the impact of air pollutants on stroke progression. Fourth, we further investigated the associations of additional modifications, which have a significance for identifying vulnerable populations and implementing targeted preventive measures.

However, there are also some limitations. First, exposure assessment relied on residential address estimates rather than personal monitoring data. This approach could not account for individual activity patterns or mobility characteristics, potentially introducing exposure misclassification that could lead to either underestimation or overestimation of true association.49 Future studies could integrate personal activity logs or wearable air quality monitors to refine exposure assessment. Second, under the reasonable assumption that the spatial pollution contrasts did not change substantially during recruitment and follow-up in China, we used the average concentration of air pollutants at 1 year before the end point or the latest follow-up for each participant as a proxy for the long-term exposure in line with most air pollution studies.11 However, related research suggested that the PM2.5 concentrations in China during the winter of 2020 exhibited a reduction of 23.4% (14.68 μg/m3) compared with the same period in 2016.50 Third, the sample size for the transition from PCVDs to death was small, and only 60 cases were observed. Although a significant association was found, the small sample size compromised the precision of the effect estimate. Fourth, although this study adjusted for numerous potential confounders, there is likely residual bias due to unmeasured confounding such as industrialization. Finally, the higher risk of recurrence or death in patients with good functional outcomes or mild strokes may be due to survivor bias. This could occur if patients with severe strokes experienced early outcomes, thereby being excluded from follow-up and leading to an overestimation of risk in the surviving subgroup.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that exposure to PM2.5 and its specific components is a significant risk factor of recurrent stroke, PCVDs, and death. These findings underscore the urgent need for coordinated societal efforts to improve air quality and reduce ambient air pollution.
