Introduction

Groove pancreatitis (GP) is a rare variant of pancreatitis that affects the pancreaticoduodenal groove (PDG), which is a small anatomical space between the head of the pancreas, the medial wall of the second part of the duodenum, and the common bile duct [1]. Three distinct forms of GP have been identified in the literature: a pure variant confined solely to the groove region, typically occurring at an early stage; a segmental variant where the inflammatory process extends towards the pancreatic head; and a diffuse variant that encompasses the entire pancreas, manifesting the characteristic features of chronic pancreatitis. In addition to these pancreatic changes, the duodenal wall often exhibits thickening, fibrosis, induration, and occasionally cyst formation [2].

Diagnosis is frequently challenging, which may be a contributing factor to its relatively low incidence. The precise aetiology of this condition is still unclear. Blockage of the minor papilla and Brunner gland hyperplasia, which causes stasis of pancreatic enzyme in the dorsal pancreas, are thought to be possible pathogenesis of this condition [3]. Chronic alcohol consumption is believed to be a strong risk factor in the pathogenesis of GP, as alcohol causes viscid secretion and duct blockage that initiates the inflammatory process [4].

The clinical presentation includes upper abdominal pain, postprandial vomiting and weight loss [3]. Imaging features such as sheet-like hypodensity in the PDG, medial duodenal wall thickening, and cystic changes in the duodenal wall favour the diagnosis of GP [5]. GP can typically be treated successfully with conservative medical treatment, and surgery is reserved for patients with persistent and severe clinical symptoms or a definitive exclusion of malignancy [2].

This case report details the management of a rare presentation of groove pancreatitis in a non-alcoholic female. This case report was prepared in line with the SCARE criteria [17].

Presentation of case

A 55-year-old female, known to have gallstone disease, presented with severe upper abdominal pain for five days duration. It was non-radiating, constant pain associated with nausea and non-bilious vomiting. Meals aggravated the pain. As the day progressed, the pain intensified, prompting her to seek emergency medical attention. The patient denied a history of alcohol consumption. Upon arrival at our facility, her vitals were within normal range. On abdominal examination, there was tenderness over the epigastrium and right hypochondrial area without guarding. The rest of the systemic examination was within normal limits. The blood investigations revealed a significant increase in white blood cells and inflammatory markers, mildly elevated serum amylase (140 U/L), and normal liver function test.

The ultrasound scan revealed the presence of echogenic fluid around the second part of the duodenum, situated between the duodenum and the head of the pancreas. This finding raised the possibility of GP. An abdominal contrast enhanced computed tomography (CECT) scan showed oedematous thickening and cystic changes in the wall of the duodenum, oedematous changes in the pancreatic head and uncinate process with minimal contrast enhancement compared to the rest of the pancreas. Additionally, fluid in the para duodenal/pancreatic head region and evidence of inflammatory fluid and strands were noted. The rest of the pancreas appeared normal. The main pancreatic duct and biliary system appeared normal. The peripancreatic vessels showed no obstruction or encasement (Fig. 1).

Also, the patient underwent an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy study and was reported as normal. After contemplating and combining the clinical and imaging findings, the case was interpreted as GP.

The patient was on conservative medical management with analgesics, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), pancreatic enzyme supplements and normal balanced diet. Follow-up assessment demonstrated favourable treatment outcomes. After five days, the patient exhibited notable improvement, as evidenced by normalising inflammatory markers and serum amylase levels. Subsequent ultrasound imaging revealed the absence of localised fluid collection around the duodenum, indicative of a positive response to conservative management.

Subsequently, a laparoscopic cholecystectomy was performed to mitigate the associated risk factor for gallstone disease. Patient was asymptomatic on 6 months follow up.

Discussion

Groove pancreatitis was first described in 1973 by Becker in German literature [6]. However, the term “groove pancreatitis” gained wider usage and was introduced by Stolte and his team in 1982 [7]. GP is characterised by inflammation of the pancreaticoduodenal groove, a potential anatomic space between the dorso-cranial part of the pancreatic head, the duodenum, and the common bile duct [1]. There are three forms of GP, the pure form is confined purely to the PDG, the segmental form involves the groove area with extension into the head of the pancreas, and the diffuse form involves the whole pancreas [2].

This condition predominantly affects males in their 4th or 5th decades with a history of chronic alcoholism [8]. In our case, we observed an unusual occurrence of GP where the affected individual was female with no alcohol consumption history. This was a deviation from the normal pattern, highlighting the significance of considering gender variations in presentation of this condition.

The precise aetiology remains unclear; however, certain studies propose a potential link to chronic alcohol consumption as a precipitating factor. Chronic alcohol use increases protein content, which causes increased viscosity of pancreatic secretion, which leads to blockage of pancreatic ducts and minor papilla that initiates the inflammatory process. Extravasation of pancreatic enzymes into PDG causes GP [9]. However, the patient denied any history of alcohol consumption in our case but was diagnosed with gallstones, suggesting an alternative pathway for the development of GP.

Common presentation includes chronic intermittent post-prandial abdominal pain, recurrent nausea, vomiting, severe weight loss from impaired intestinal mobility, and duodenal stenosis [3]. In contrast, our patient presented with acute abdominal pain accompanied by nausea and vomiting. Since it was an acute presentation, no significant weight loss was observed. This variation in presentation implies considering GP as a potential differential diagnosis in the case of acute presentation.

CT/MRI remains the main non-invasive imaging modality to diagnose GP. Sheet-like hypodensity in the PDG, medial duodenal wall thickening, and cysts in the duodenal wall or the PDG are typical findings in CECT [5]. Our case also exhibited similar findings, including oedematous thickening, cystic changes in the duodenal wall, and evidence of inflammatory fluid in the paraduodenal and pancreatic head region. Since it involves the duodenum and pancreatic head, our case represents a typical example of a segmental form of GP.

The possible differential diagnosis of GP includes pancreatic head carcinoma, periampullary carcinoma, pancreatic groove neuroendocrine tumour, duodenal cystic dystrophy and acute pancreatitis with phlegmon along the groove. Distinguishing between GP and pancreatic carcinoma can be intricate, especially in cases of scirrhous adenocarcinoma of the pancreas due to similar imaging characteristics [10,11]. Some imaging features will help in the differentiation. GP appears as a sheet-like mass, while pancreatic cancer exhibits an irregular mass. In GP, the stenosis of the distal common bile duct is smooth and long, whereas in pancreatic cancer, it appears abrupt and short. Cystic dystrophy of the duodenal wall is more frequently observed in GP compared to pancreatic cancer. Also, the absence of major vessel encasement will favour GP [5,8]. Our case imaging findings were consistent with GP and did not show carcinoma features. Additionally, the serum amylase level was not significantly elevated, which possibly ruled out the other differential diagnosis of acute pancreatitis with phlegmon along the groove.

Despite these imaging findings, the definitive diagnosis of GP is usually confirmed through histopathological assessment [12]. Endoscopic ultrasound remains the preferred method for diagnosing and confirming the disease through endoscopic fine-needle aspiration or biopsy (EUS-FNA/FNB) [13].

Traditionally, GP had been treated with surgical interventions due to diagnostic uncertainties. However, recent advancements in imaging modalities have provided greater diagnostic certainty, allowing for the successful management of GP through conservative measures and minimally invasive endoscopic interventions [14]. In a retrospective study conducted across seven hospitals in Finland, almost half of the patients underwent conservative management, and all remained asymptomatic during the five-year follow-up period [15]. Another recent study conducted in Italy yielded promising results regarding conservative management. There were no differences in quality of life or pain management in their relatively large cohort, who underwent either surgery or conservative treatment. These findings further advocate for conservative treatment as the preferred initial approach for GP [16]. Our patient was also managed using conservative approaches and remained asymptomatic for six months of follow-up.

Conclusion

Groove pancreatitis should be considered in females without a history of alcohol consumption. It can also manifest as an acute condition. Diagnostic certainty of imaging modalities reduces the need for unnecessary invasive interventions in its management, and it can be effectively and safely managed through conservative measures.
