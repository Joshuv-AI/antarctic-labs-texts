Relationship Between Food Security and Walking for Transportation: Findings by Region and Rurality
Authors: Jessica Stroope, Marilyn E Wende, Robert E Anderson, Laura E Balis, Karin Valentine Goins, Christina N Bridges Hamilton, M Renée Umstattd Meyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeLack of transportation increases the risk of food insecurity. However, little is known about how food security relates to active transportation when considering rurality and the US region of the country. A better understanding could lead to active transportation interventions that connect individuals with low food security to high‐quality food access points.MethodsTo examine the relationship between food security and days per week of transport walking, we employed a negative binomial regression analysis with survey weights using data from the 2022 National Health Interview Survey (N = 27,651). Background

Food insecurity affects more than 44 million people in the United States. Defined as inconsistent access to sufficient and nutritious food to support a healthy and active life, food insecurity is a significant public health challenge, associated with higher rates of chronic diseases, including hypertension, diabetes, and stroke [1]. Food security status encompasses both financial and non‐financial barriers to accessing healthy food, including perceived barriers such as food availability, quality, and access in a given area.

Over three‐fourths of incorporated areas in the United States have under 5000 residents [2]. Rural communities in the United States have significantly higher rates of food insecurity (15.4%) than suburbs (11.7%) and the national average (13.5%) [3]. Rural areas with high food insecurity are characterized by chronic poverty rates, lack of transportation, and lack of affordable housing [4]. Lack of geographical and financial access to sufficient and nutritious food leads to lower dietary quality and increased risk for chronic disease and other comorbid conditions [5, 6].

Household income impacts transportation choices, with low‐income households most likely to report walking to destinations as a mode of transportation (i.e., transport walking) [7]. Specifically, low‐income households report walking or biking for transportation in order to save money more than higher‐income counterparts, regardless of urban, rural, or suburban location [8]. This is concerning, as pedestrian injury and death in rural and urban areas disproportionately impact minority and low‐income populations whose transport walking is more often driven by need (i.e., without access to vehicle transport) instead of choice [8, 9, 10, 11].

There is conflicting evidence as to whether rural areas have lower levels of transport walking, with evidence from the 2017 National Household Travel Survey and the 2015 National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) indicating that walking for transportation is less common in rural areas [12, 13], where there is generally less supportive infrastructure [14, 15]. However, when considering Census Block Group density, differences in active transportation (e.g., biking, walking, rolling places) between urban and rural communities subside [12, 16]. Other evidence using the 2007–2011 American Community Survey data indicates walking to work levels to be higher in rural areas (compared to urban areas), which may in part be due to lower access to public transportation [17]. Small towns in rural areas often have sufficient density to support clusters of destinations and housing, making active transportation feasible [18]. However, need‐based transport walking forces rural residents to walk in unsafe conditions. Although there are fewer total rural active transportation fatalities, the rate of rural fatalities is similar to that of urban fatalities in national data (1.82 rural vs. 1.99 urban pedestrian/bicycle fatalities per 100,000), with some states showing higher rates of rural pedestrian fatalities [10, 19].

The United States also has regional differences in active transportation behaviors, with transport walking less frequently practiced in Southern and Midwestern regions [12, 13], likely due to weather and lack of supportive infrastructure [20, 21]. In regions such as the Mississippi Delta, Appalachia, and American Indian reservations, lower‐income households are often clustered in rural areas with high rates of poverty [22], making transport walking for food acquisition at times a necessity and viable (although potentially dangerous) option, especially if grocery stores are located proximally.

Little is known about how food security relates to active transportation when considering rurality and region of the country. A better understanding could lead to active transportation interventions that connect individuals with low food security to high‐quality food access points. Using nationally representative data, this study examines the following hypotheses:

H1: Food insecure people will rely on transport walking more than food secure people.

H2: The relationship between food security and transport walking will vary by region and rurality.

Methods

Data

The NHIS is a yearly in‐person interview survey conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's National Center for Health Statistics. The NHIS provides nationally representative data to estimate the health of the people in the United States. We use cross‐sectional data from the 2022 NHIS (N = 27,651). This study was deemed exempt from Institutional Review Board review by the first author's institution.

Measures

Outcome

The outcome variable is days per week of walking for transportation. This variable was created by combining two questions, “In the past 7 days, did you walk for transportation” and “In the past 7 days, how many days did you walk for transportation,” to create a single continuous 0–7 days per week measure.

Predictor

The predictor is a binary food security variable (food secure vs. food insecure) derived from a four category composite variable created by the NHIS (very low food security, low food security, marginal food security, and high food security) by combining 10 questions measuring access to sufficient food, such as “we worried whether our food would run out before we got money to buy more” and “did you ever cut the size of your meals or skip meals because there wasn't enough money for food.” In our binary variable, high food security is coded as food secure, and marginal food security, low food security, and very low food security are combined into a single food insecure category to measure any exposure to food insecurity, following previous work using the NHIS [23, 24].

Modifiers

Modifiers include region and rurality. Regions are categorized by the U.S. Census Bureau (Midwest, Northeast, South, and West), included to assess regional differences in health and the built environment. The Northeast serves as the reference category due to its position as the region in which transport walking is the most predominant [25]. Rurality is measured by a dichotomous variable: Rural (nonmetropolitan) and urban (metropolitan). This measure uses a state‐defined level of density to ensure a representative sample, and what is considered urban or rural varies by state (according to the state‐level stratification) [26].

Covariates

Covariates include age, sex, education (less than high school, high school/GED, technical school/some college, college, advanced degrees), race/ethnicity (non‐Hispanic American Indian/Alaskan Native, non‐Hispanic Asian, non‐Hispanic Black/African American, non‐Hispanic White, Hispanic, other), and self‐rated health.

Statistical Analysis

To examine the relationship between food security and days per week of transport walking, we employed a negative binomial regression analysis with survey weights using svyset in Stata 18.0 using data from the 2022 NHIS (N = 27,651). Negative binomial regression accounts for excess zeros and allows for greater variance in a count model [27]. The survey weights account for nonresponse and sampling probabilities, and were provided by the National Center for Health Statistics [28]. Respondents with missing data on transport walking were dropped (n = 1128), with the remaining sample (n = 26,523) missing data on 2% of cases. After listwise deletion, the analytical sample was n = 25,925. Bivariate relationships between transport walking and region, rurality, and food security are respectively presented. Interaction terms are tested to determine whether rural‐urban status or region modifies the relationship between food security and transport walking; only significant interactions will be maintained in the final model. Interaction tests of first and second differences are presented. In addition to rural‐urban status and region, adjustment variables also included age, sex, education, race/ethnicity, and self‐rated health. Marginal effects of individual variables are presented.

Discussion

This paper assessed the relationship between food security and transport walking, while considering region and rurality as potential moderators. Results supported Hypothesis 1: Food insecurity was associated with more days per week of transport walking. This finding aligns with previous work demonstrating that low‐income households rely on active transportation to save money [8]. Hypothesis 2 was unsupported; in examining the relationship between food security and transport walking, we found no significant differences in the strength of the relationship by region of the country or urban‐rural status. People experiencing food insecurity walk for transportation more than food secure persons, in all parts of the country, regardless of rural residence. These findings align with three needs for rural communities: (1) walkable access to food outlets, (2) innovative transportation solutions, and (3) implementation strategies.

First, prioritizing safe and walkable access to rural food outlets is critical, especially considering the high rates of rural pedestrian fatalities [10, 19]. Lack of transportation options increases the risk of food insecurity [30, 31, 32], and improving neighborhood walkability may reduce barriers to food access [32, 33]. Future research must identify pragmatic and low‐cost pedestrian environment improvements that facilitate transport walking, which can inform ongoing policy efforts to address food insecurity. However, recent research indicates more fundamental change is needed to address the land use paradigm that puts essential destinations in locations inaccessible without a personal vehicle [34].

Second, food insecurity and low food access in rural areas are in part due to a lack of access to affordable and safe transportation options, including public transit. Accessible public transit is associated with lower food insecurity [35]. Rural communities under 2500 are the least likely to have any form of public transit, including door‐to‐door services, and the least likely to include food access as a part of transportation plans [36]. Ensuring that food access is a part of rural transit plans is critical to reduce food insecurity, with particular consideration needed for parts of town with reduced vehicle access (senior housing, low‐income neighborhoods) [36]. Rural public transit is challenging to implement, especially in low‐income rural areas, because of the smaller scale and lower efficiency as compared to urban areas [37]. However, solutions exist, such as on‐demand bus services [38, 39]. Fixed‐route bus services can also be effective, especially if financially supported by local organizations, which then benefit from increased access [38]. Although rural communities may be viewed as not having enough demand to support public transportation, there are predictable demand patterns based on when EBT (electronic benefit transfer) cards for food assistance programs are loaded, which vary by state [40]. Low‐income residents report paying friends or family to get a ride to the nearest discount grocery store (e.g., Walmart) soon after benefits are loaded [41]. Local solutions, such as church vans that provide weekly transport to discount grocers, could help bridge transportation gaps in communities with no transit available. Research is needed to assess the feasibility and scalability of rural transit interventions.

Finally, implementing complex policy and environmental interventions can be challenging. Implementation strategies address the gap between evidence‐based interventions and real‐world implementation [42, 43], such as a demonstration project that allows a community to temporarily experience a proposed change (e.g., a new bus route or crosswalk) and provide feedback [44]. Strong community partnerships, including coordination between transportation and public health departments, are another promising strategy [45, 46]. Particularly in rural communities, coalitions incorporating youth engagement and community leaders such as Cooperative Extension agents, librarians, and faith leaders can serve as partners in transportation planning, grant writing, and implementation [18, 47, 48]. This community engagement is critical because, although some Federal funds for active transportation infrastructure prioritize rural communities, such as the Transportation Alternatives Program, rural towns struggle to navigate the application process and end up leaving funding designated for rural communities unused [18].

Limitations

Although NHIS 2022 bases the rural/urban dichotomous variable on density, the level of density varies by state, and functions only at the county level. Our data lack a measure of census block density; although we assess rurality, it is unknown whether respondents live in a small town or a remote rural area. However, it is likely that most rural respondents are residents of towns. Solutions improving food access in rural towns are likely vastly different than remote rural locations.

In addition, although NHIS data measures transport walking, it is unknown what destinations are frequented. Future research should include food security measures along with destinations frequented by walking (and where respondents would like to walk) to better inform infrastructure planning.

Conclusions

Regardless of urban or rural residence, people with food insecurity rely on transport walking at higher levels than food‐secure individuals. In all regions of the United States, individuals experiencing food insecurity engage in transport walking at higher rates than food‐secure persons. Future research should move beyond documenting this relationship to identifying which environmental, transportation, and policy interventions most effectively support safe and reliable access to food via active transportation, particularly in low‐resource and rural settings. From a practice and policy perspective, efforts to reduce food insecurity should explicitly incorporate transportation and built environment strategies, ensuring that food access is embedded within transportation planning at local and regional levels. Cross‐sector collaboration between public health, transportation, and community organizations are essential to design feasible, community‐informed solutions and to improve uptake of existing funding opportunities—particularly in rural communities where resources often go unused.
