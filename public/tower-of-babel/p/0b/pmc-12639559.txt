The prognostic role of tumor-associated macrophage and cancer-associated fibroblast interactions in soft tissue sarcoma microenvironments
Authors: Jian Zhou, Michinobu Umakoshi, Yingjie Ren, Na Zhang, Yunjie Wang, Zhuo Li, Akiteru Goto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummarySoft tissue sarcomas (STS) are rare and heterogeneous cancers with unpredictable clinical outcomes. Existing prognostic models based on histology and staging often fail to capture tumor dynamics, especially in the era of immunotherapy. Within the tumor microenvironment (TME), crosstalk between tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) plays a central role in promoting immunosuppression, therapy resistance, and metastasis. This bidirectional interaction occurs via cytokines, exosomes, direct contact, and metabolic coupling, facilitating immune evasion and matrix remodeling. Evidence suggests that quantifying TAMs-CAFs interactions using immunohistochemical or gene expression signatures correlates with poor prognosis, offering a potential tool for risk stratification. However, technical and standardization challenges, patient heterogeneity, and incomplete mechanistic understanding limit clinical translation. Future progress will require AI-integrated multimodal analysis and personalized frameworks combining stromal interaction metrics with clinical variables to enable dynamic monitoring and precision therapy in STS.Subject areas: immunology, cancer

Introduction

Soft tissue sarcomas (STS) present substantial clinical challenges as a group of rare yet highly aggressive mesenchymal malignancies.1,2 Their clinical management is complicated by several factors: pronounced pathological heterogeneity encompassing over 100 distinct subtypes, a high propensity for local recurrence and distant metastasis, and limited treatment options.3 Despite the use of multimodal therapies including surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy the prognosis for patients with advanced or metastatic STS remains poor, with five-year survival rates below 65%.4,5 This sobering outlook is further exacerbated by the limited efficacy of conventional treatments and the generally disappointing outcomes of immunotherapy in STS, highlighting an urgent need to identify new biological drivers of disease progression.

In this context, the tumor microenvironment (TME) has emerged as a critical regulator of STS pathogenesis, immune evasion, and therapy resistance.3 Current prognostic models, which rely primarily on histologic grade and clinical staging, fail to adequately capture the dynamic and spatially heterogeneous interactions within the TME a significant shortcoming in the era of immunotherapy.6 Growing evidence indicates that immune-related gene expression signatures and specific cellular ecosystems within the TME correlate strongly with patient outcomes, suggesting their potential as novel prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Indeed, TME-focused interventions have shown promising results in clinical trials.7,8,9 However, translating TME features into clinically actionable tools still faces major obstacles, including technical standardization, validation across diverse STS subtypes, and integration into personalized treatment frameworks.

Within the TME, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and CAFs function as core stromal components. They collaboratively shape an immunosuppressive microenvironment and drive tumor progression through functional diversity and cellular crosstalk.10 TAMs exhibit significant heterogeneity, with a dynamic balance between M1 and M2 polarization states.11,12 Pro-inflammatory M1-type TAMs possess anti-tumor potential, while anti-inflammatory M2-type TAMs support tumor development by promoting angiogenesis, matrix remodeling, and immune evasion.13,14 Single-cell technologies reveal that TAMs spatiotemporal heterogeneity is influenced by epigenetic regulation and metabolic reprogramming, with M2 polarization significantly associated with poor prognosis.14,15 Our previous study demonstrated that a higher density of TAMs in the marginal region of soft-tissue sarcomas was significantly associated with poorer disease-free survival (DFS).16 CAFs also display high heterogeneity. Multiple functional subsets have been identified, including myofibroblast-like CAFs (myCAFs), and inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs).17,18,19 These subsets contribute to tumor metastasis and drug resistance by secreting cytokines. In well-studied solid tumors like pancreatic cancer, TAMs and CAFs form a bidirectional network that fosters an immune-excluded microenvironment and therapy resistance.20,21,22 Although this interaction is a compelling model for stromal collaboration, its specific mechanisms and significance in STS are not yet fully established and warrant dedicated study. Strategies targeting TAMs reprogramming and CAFs subset-specific targeting offer new avenues for reversing TME immunosuppression. In previous study, we evaluated the expression of CAFs marker in both the intertumoral and peripheral tumor regions and found that the high CAFs marker expression group, similar to the high TAMs group, had a negative impact on DFS in Figure 1.23 In addition, we identified a correlation between CAFs expression and the number of TAMs in each respective region. However, the interaction between CAFs and TAMs in sarcoma remains unclear.

This review aims to comprehensively and deeply explore the latest research progress regarding the prognostic application of TAMs-CAFs interactions within the TME for STS. While some studies have investigated the impact of this crosstalk on tumor development, research specifically focused on STS prognosis remains insufficiently systematic and comprehensive. Therefore, this article will delve into the intrinsic link between TAMs-CAFs interactions and STS patient outcomes, identifying key molecules and underlying mechanisms. This effort will contribute to developing more accurate and effective prognostic indicators and models for STS. These tools will provide a scientific basis for clinical treatment decisions, guiding physicians in formulating individualized therapeutic plans to improve patient survival rates and quality of life. Furthermore, this work will offer new directions and perspectives for fundamental STS research, fostering further advancement in this field.

Tumor microenvironment plays a central role in tumor progression

Recent research demonstrates that the TME functions not merely as a growth substrate for tumor cells but constitutes a critical factor influencing tumor behavior and therapeutic response. This environment holds particular importance in STS. The TME represents a dynamic network comprising tumor cells, non-malignant cells such as immune and stromal cells, and a complex extracellular matrix.24 Through intercellular interactions and secretory signaling molecules, this network regulates tumor proliferation, invasion, and metastasis. Within this structure, the TME contains two central cellular elements: TAMs and CAFs25 in Figure 2.

Regulatory role of TAMs

TAMs represent one of the most abundant immune cell populations within the TME. Their regulatory functions are crucial for tumor progression, metastasis, and therapy resistance.

TAMs exhibit significant heterogeneity in function. They are primarily classified into pro-inflammatory M1-type TAMs, which demonstrate anti-tumor activity, and anti-inflammatory M2-type TAMs, which exhibit pro-tumor properties. In advanced tumors, M2-type TAMs typically dominate. These cells support tumor growth by secreting immunosuppressive factors such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), along with growth factors including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and endothelial growth factor (EGF)26,27 in Figure 2. TAMs originate from two distinct precursor cell types. One type comprises tissue-resident macrophages, exemplified by resident tumor-associated macrophages (Res-TAMs) found in lung interstitial.28 These TAMs are closely associated with direct tumor growth. The other type consists of C-C motif chemokine receptor-2 (CCR2)-dependent macrophages derived from recruited monocyte-derived TAMs (Mo-TAMs), which primarily facilitate the tumor metastasis process.29 Research indicates that conditions such as hypoxia and nutrient competition within the TME can induce metabolic alterations in TAMs. For instance, methionine metabolism regulates TAMs polarization, subsequently influencing gastric cancer progression. Recent studies have progressively elucidated the complex role of TAMs in regulating tumor cell initiation and development within STS.30,31 One investigation utilizing single-cell RNA sequencing technology demonstrated the critical function of tumor-expressed macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) in shaping macrophage heterogeneity.32,33 This finding provides new perspectives for understanding the diversity and functions of TAMs in STS.

TAMs exert pro-tumor regulatory mechanisms through several distinct pathways. Immunosuppression mediated by M2-type TAMs involves suppressing CD8+ T cell function and upregulating checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1, thereby establishing an immune evasion barrier.34 Additionally, TAMs contribute to tumor metastasis by secreting matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) that degrade the extracellular matrix.35,36,37 This degradation promotes the dissemination of circulating tumor cells (CTCs), particularly driving metastasis in STS.38,39,40,41 Furthermore, TAMs facilitate angiogenesis through the secretion of pro-angiogenic factors like VEGF. This process enhances tumor cell chemoresistance via paracrine signaling molecules including IL-6.42,43,44

Pro-tumorigenic effects of CAFs

CAFs represent another crucial component of the TME in soft tissue sarcomas (STS-TME). As primary effector cells involved in establishing a pro-tumorigenic niche, CAFs account for a substantial proportion of cells in most human sarcomas.45 They originate from diverse cellular sources, including resident quiescent fibroblasts, bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells, and cells that have undergone epithelial-mesenchymal transition.46,47 CAFs are highly heterogeneous and can be categorized into distinct subtypes, such as myCAFs and iCAFs, which perform different functions within the TME. MyCAFs mainly promote tumor growth and metastasis by secreting extracellular matrix components and growth factors, whereas iCAFs influence the immune microenvironment through the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines.33 The response of different CAFs subpopulations to TGF-β signaling exhibits significant TME-dependent variability, with its core regulatory function manifested in the dynamic shaping of CAFs heterogeneity and function. TGF-β serves as a key driver promoting the differentiation of fibroblasts into myCAFs that exhibit high α-SMA expression.48,49,50 Activation of this subpopulation directly leads to extracellular matrix (ECM) stiffening and the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. However, studies such as single-cell RNA sequencing have further revealed that the intensity of TGF-β signaling can directly regulate the proportion and functional states of CAFs subpopulations.48,49,50 For instance, intervention with a TGF-β-trapping agent can alter CAFs subset composition, shifting their functional state from expressing the immunosuppressive factor chemokine ligand-5 (CCL-5) to producing CCL7 and CCL8, which enhance anti-tumor immunity.48,49,50 This indicates that TGF-β signaling acts not only as a master regulator of myCAFs differentiation but also as a central switch that dynamically modulates CAFs heterogeneity. Variations in TGF-β signaling intensity can drive CAFs to transition between immunosuppressive and potentially immunostimulatory phenotypes. CAFs promote tumor progression through multiple interconnected mechanisms. By secreting a variety of growth factors, ECM proteins, and matrix-remodeling enzymes, they enhance tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastatic dissemination.51 Additionally, CAFs recruit TAMs via the Janus kinase 2/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (JAK2/STAT3) pathway, forming an immunosuppressive axis that links CAFs, TAMs, and tumor cells. While studies in other cancers suggest that CAFs may also promote angiogenesis and chemoresistance in sarcomas through activation of Wnt signaling via receptors such as N-cadherin, it remains uncertain whether this mechanism operates in STS.52,53,54 CAFs further remodel the ECM to facilitate tumor cell invasiveness and modulate vascularization to support nutrient supply and metastatic spread.55 In STS, CAFs activity is strongly correlated with accelerated tumor growth and enhanced invasive potential; however, the underlying regulatory mechanisms are still not fully elucidated.56

Interactions between TAMs and CAFs in the tumor microenvironment

Current research demonstrates multiple interaction modalities between macrophages and fibroblasts. Cytokine-mediated signaling represents a significant pathway wherein macrophage-derived TGF-β accelerates fibroblast proliferation and activation, while fibroblast-secreted chemokines such as CCL12 and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) influence macrophage recruitment and polarization57 in Figure 2. Regarding extracellular matrix remodeling, MMPs secreted by macrophages degrade matrix components, subsequently modifying fibroblast behavior.58 Simultaneously, fibroblast-synthesized extracellular matrix constituents like collagen provide feedback regulation to macrophages. Furthermore, direct intercellular contact and exosome-mediated molecular exchange may contribute to their bidirectional crosstalk. These interactions exhibit cancer-type specificity. In breast cancer studies, macrophages activate fibroblasts through cytokines including TNF-α, transforming them into CAFs. Subsequently, CAFs promote breast cancer cell invasion and metastasis via MMPs secretion57 in Figure 2. Other investigations reveal CAFs recruit macrophages through MCP-1 and SDF-1, inducing M2 polarization.58 In lung cancer, macrophages potentially facilitate CAFs formation through specific signaling pathways, while in colorectal cancer, macrophage-fibroblast interactions establish a tumor-promoting microenvironment by regulating extracellular matrix remodeling and cytokine secretion, ultimately enhancing tumor cell proliferation and angiogenesis. In STS, CAFs promote tumor invasion by secreting MMPs. This activity may be sustained by TAMs via the IL-6/STAT3 signaling pathway, forming a mutually reinforcing cycle within the TME.24,59,60

These findings indicate substantial progress in understanding TAMs-CAFs interactions across various cancers, providing crucial insights into tumor development mechanisms and establishing foundations for mechanistic and translational research58 in Figure 2. However, investigations specifically focused on STS remain relatively limited. Notable deficiencies persist regarding the precise interaction mechanisms, implicated signaling pathways, and prognostic implications in this sarcoma context.

Subtype heterogeneity of TAMs-CAFs dynamic interactions

In the TME of soft tissue sarcoma, TAMs and CAFs collaboratively establish an immunosuppressive milieu through a complex dynamic network, exhibiting remarkable subtype specificity61,62,63 in Figure 3. CAFs promote tumor proliferation and suppress chemosensitivity by secreting pro-inflammatory factors, while cooperating with TAMs via secreted phosphoprotein-1 (SPP1)-mediated paracrine signaling to drive extracellular matrix remodeling and immune escape a mechanism, particularly prominent in rhabdomyosarcoma.24,64,65 Under hypoxic conditions, the hypoxia inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α)-mediated CD73-adenosine pathway is upregulated, not only promoting metabolic reprogramming of CAFs but also inducing polarization of TAMs toward an immunosuppressive phenotype.66,67 Subtype analysis reveals that in liposarcoma and synovial sarcoma, myofibroblastic CAFs and SPP1-positive TAMs promote angiogenesis and therapy resistance through endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), while single-cell transcriptomics confirm that HIF-1α coordinates metabolic coupling between the two by activating PDK1/3.24,68,69 In aggressive subtypes, stathmin-1 (1STMN1)-positive pro-carcinogenic fibroblasts dominate the microenvironment alongside Treg cells,65 whereas chronic inflammation reinforces immunosuppression via myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSC)-like macrophages and CCL-2 regulated fibroblasts [8]. Therapeutically, targeting the CAFs secretome can reduce CAFs abundance and induce macrophage M1 polarization,67,70,71 while hyaluronic acid (HA) degradation can reverse the immunosuppressive phenotype of TAMs.72,73 Mechanistically, CAFs promote MDSC differentiation and TAMs polarization via the TGF-β/Smad2/3 pathway,66,74 while fibroblast activation protein (FAP) -positive CAFs further enhance immunosuppression by inhibiting T cell function.38,75 Notably, the co-localization of CAFs and TAMs is significantly associated with inadequate T cell infiltration,76 suggesting their synergistic suppression of anti-tumor immunity. In summary, combined strategies targeting multiple pathways such as SPP1, HIF-1α, and TGF-β, along with metabolic reprogramming may offer promising therapeutic directions for soft tissue sarcoma67,70,71,77,78 in Figure 3.

Metabolic reprogramming-mediated TAMs-CAFs interaction is a central driver of soft tissue sarcoma progression; however, significant research gaps remain, including a lack of sarcoma-specific data, insufficient understanding of metabolic crosstalk between specific cell subsets, limitations in existing models to recapitulate TME complexity, and challenges in targeted therapy delivery and toxicity. Future studies should leverage spatial multi-omics and advanced organoid models to decipher the complexity of metabolic interactions, identify subtype-specific metabolic vulnerabilities, and develop nanotechnology-based delivery systems combined with multi-target therapeutic strategies to translate fundamental insights into effective clinical interventions.

Modes of dynamic interaction between TAMs and CAFs

Research demonstrates that within the TME, TAMs, and CAFs cooperatively drive tumor progression through multidimensional dynamic interaction networks. First, regarding direct intercellular communication, contact-dependent signaling mediated by immune checkpoints such as CD47-signal-regulatory protein-α (SIRPα) and PD-1/PD-L1 promotes immune evasion. TAMs induce pro-fibrotic phenotypes in CAFs through factors including TGF-β, while CAFs further reinforce the immunosuppressive microenvironment by secreting PD-L157,79,80 in Figure 3. Second, within paracrine signaling axes, inflammatory cytokine circuits comprising IL-6/STAT3 and CCL2/CCR2 establish positive feedback loops. These mechanisms drive TAMs polarization toward the M2 phenotype while activating ECM remodeling functions in CAFs. The TGF-β signaling pathway critically shapes the immunosuppressive TME by synchronizing the phenotypic interplay between TAMs and CAFs.48,81,82 It not only promotes macrophage polarization toward an immunosuppressive state but also activates fibroblasts, driving extracellular matrix remodeling and the formation of a fibrous barrier through mechanisms, such as the Smad/Ras responsive element binding protein-1(RREB1) pathway, which further facilitates epithelial-mesenchymal transition and fibrosis. These combined actions foster an immune-excluded tumor milieu.48,81,82 The dual blockade of TGF-β and PD-L1 effectively disrupts both the physical and immunosuppressive barriers, enhancing T cell infiltration and reactivating antitumor immunity48,81,82 in Figure 3. For STS, which commonly present with high TGF-β activity and an immunologically cold phenotype, a promising therapeutic approach involves employing TGF-β/PD-L1 bispecific antibodies, potentially in combination with immune-stimulating agents or targeted inhibitors, to overcome resistance and advance toward personalized immunotherapy.48,81,82

Third, at the metabolic level, CAFs provide energy substrates to TAMs via lactate shuttle mechanisms. Simultaneously, arginase-mediated metabolic reprogramming collectively suppresses T cell functionality.83,84 This metabolic symbiosis exhibits spatial heterogeneity as confirmed by single-cell studies. Regarding cooperative matrix remodeling, TAMs and CAFs collaboratively degrade basement membranes through coordinated secretion of matrix metalloproteinase 2 and 9 (MMPs2/9).85 Concurrently, collagen cross-linking networks generated by CAFs physically impede therapeutic drug penetration while promoting pre-metastatic TME formation through signaling molecules such as Wnt family member 16 (Wnt16).86

Notably, spatial transcriptomics reveals that localized interactions between specific CAFs subsets and TAMs shape immunological barriers.87 The resulting ecological niches show significant correlation with poor immunotherapy response in patients.21 These discoveries provide molecular foundations for developing combination therapies targeting cellular interactions within the TME and refining patient survival strategies. TAMs and CAFs collaboratively drive poor clinical outcomes through multiple interaction mechanisms: direct checkpoints, paracrine signaling, metabolic coupling, and joint ECM remodeling. These spatially structured networks promote immunosuppression, therapy resistance, and metastasis, which correlate strongly with advanced disease and reduced survival. Targeting these interactions offers promising strategies for prognostic biomarker development.21,76,88

Based on the foregoing discussion, it is evident that TAMs and CAFs do not function in isolation but engage in extensive and multidimensional crosstalk within the TME. Their interactions, spanning direct contact, paracrine signaling, metabolic symbiosis, and cooperative matrix remodeling, collectively establish a robust immunosuppressive and pro-tumorigenic niche. These dynamically intertwined networks significantly contribute to immune evasion, therapy resistance, and ultimately, poor clinical outcomes. Consequently, disrupting these critical cellular interactions presents a promising and rational strategy for developing novel combination therapies aimed at overcoming treatment resistance in STS.

Interaction mechanisms between TAMs and CAFs in the STS microenvironment

Cytokine secretion and effects

Within the TME of STS, macrophages and fibroblasts mutually influence each other through the secretion of diverse cytokines, thereby playing critical roles in sarcoma initiation and progression. Macrophages secrete cytokines including tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-1 beta.89 Tumor necrosis factor-alpha stimulates fibroblast proliferation and induces increased secretion of MMPs, such as matrix metalloproteinase 2 and matrix metalloproteinase 9.90,91 These proteases degrade the extracellular matrix, creating favorable conditions for tumor cell invasion and metastasis, consequently accelerating soft tissue sarcoma development. Simultaneously, IL-1 beta activates fibroblasts to express greater quantities of adhesion molecules.92 This enhancement strengthens tumor cell adhesion to stromal cells, promoting tumor cell growth and survival. Fibroblasts secrete cytokines such as platelet-derived growth factor and TGF-β, which significantly impact macrophages.93 Platelet-derived growth factor recruits macrophages to tumor sites and promotes their activation.52 Activated macrophages subsequently secrete additional tumor-promoting factors, establishing a vicious cycle that accelerates soft tissue sarcoma progression.94 Furthermore, TGF-β modulates macrophage polarization states, driving their transformation into pro-tumor phenotypes.95 Macrophages exhibiting this phenotype suppress the body’s anti-tumor immune response, thereby providing a sanctuary for tumor growth.

The reciprocal cytokine crosstalk between macrophages and fibroblasts in STS significantly contributes to poor prognosis. Macrophage-secreted TNF-α and IL-1β promote fibroblast-induced ECM degradation and tumor-stroma adhesion, facilitating invasion and metastasis. Conversely, fibroblast-derived PDGF and TGF-β recruit and polarize macrophages into immunosuppressive, pro-tumor phenotypes. This vicious cycle enhances immunosuppression, therapy resistance, and metastatic spread, correlating strongly with advanced disease stage and reduced survival, highlighting its value as a prognostic indicator and therapeutic target.

Regulatory role of growth factors

Growth factors play crucial regulatory roles in the interactions between macrophages and fibroblasts, profoundly influencing the invasion and metastasis of STS.96 VEGF secreted by fibroblasts represents one key regulator of this process. VEGF not only promotes tumor angiogenesis to provide adequate nutrition and oxygen supply for tumor growth but also acts upon macrophages, facilitating their accumulation and activation within the TME. Activated macrophages secrete additional pro-angiogenic factors that synergize with VEGF, further exacerbating tumor vascularization.93 This process establishes the foundation for soft tissue sarcoma invasion and metastasis.

On the other hand, epidermal growth factor secreted by macrophages exerts significant regulatory effects on fibroblasts. Epidermal growth factor stimulates fibroblast proliferation and differentiation, inducing increased secretion of extracellular matrix components, such as collagen and fibronectin.52 These components alter the physical and chemical properties of the TME, thereby providing a suitable stromal environment for tumor cell invasion. Furthermore, epidermal growth factor upregulates the expression of specific receptors on fibroblast surfaces, enhancing their sensitivity to growth factors. This mechanism establishes a positive feedback loop that continuously promotes the invasion and metastasis of STS.

In summary, growth factors mediate a critical cross-talk between macrophages and fibroblasts that drives soft tissue sarcoma progression. VEGF from fibroblasts promotes angiogenesis and macrophage activation, while EGF from macrophages stimulates fibroblast proliferation and extracellular matrix remodeling. This cooperative interaction establishes a self-reinforcing TME that facilitates invasion and metastasis, ultimately contributing to poorer clinical outcomes. Targeting this signaling loop may represent a promising therapeutic strategy to improve prognosis.

Alternative interaction pathways

Beyond cytokine and growth factor signaling, macrophages and fibroblasts engage through additional interaction mechanisms, with substantial research supporting these pathways. Direct intercellular contact constitutes one significant mode. Studies demonstrate that macrophages and fibroblasts interact via specific membrane proteins to regulate mutual cytoskeletal reorganization and signaling pathways.97,98 For example, certain integrins on macrophage surfaces bind to corresponding ligands on fibroblasts, activating relevant kinases within fibroblasts. This activation subsequently promotes the secretion of tumor-promoting factors.97 Furthermore, exosomes critically contribute to their bidirectional communication. Research indicates that macrophage-derived exosomes contain abundant bioactive molecules, such as microRNAs and proteins. Fibroblasts internalize these molecules, thereby modulating fibroblast gene expression and functionality.99,100 Similarly, fibroblast-secreted exosomes influence macrophage functional states by altering polarization status and cytokine secretion profiles, ultimately impacting soft tissue sarcoma disease progression.101 Beyond cytokine signaling, macrophages and fibroblasts interact via direct membrane contact-such as integrin-mediated kinase activation-and exosome exchange containing microRNAs and proteins. These mechanisms reprogram cellular functions and promote tumor progression. Targeting these direct interactions may disrupt pro-tumor signaling and improve clinical outcomes in STS.

Unresolved questions and future directions

Despite growing understanding of the interactions between TAMs and CAFs in the STS microenvironment, several key questions remain unresolved, providing clear directions for future research. Although current studies have confirmed diverse interaction networks between TAMs and CAFs, such as cytokine and growth factor signaling, direct contact, and exosome-mediated communication the precise molecular details of these mechanisms within the STS-specific context remain unclear. For instance, the identity and function of specific integrin-ligand pairs mediating direct intercellular communication across STS subtypes are still undefined, necessitating the application of proteomics and high-resolution imaging to map STS-specific interaction interfaces and elucidate their downstream signaling pathways. Furthermore, while single-cell and spatial transcriptomics have revealed spatial co-localization of TAMs and CAFs subpopulations and their formation of “ecological niches,” how these spatial structures dynamically regulate cellular functions particularly across different STS subtypes, such as rhabdomyosarcoma and liposarcoma remains poorly understood. Future efforts should integrate spatial multi-omics with live imaging techniques to dynamically dissect how specific TAMs-CAFs pairs collaboratively drive immunosuppression and therapy resistance in distinct tumor regions, such as the invasive front and hypoxic core. More importantly, although “metabolic symbiosis” has been proposed, causal evidence linking this metabolic coupling directly to immunosuppression and disease progression in STS is still lacking. This gap calls for the development of metabolic tracing technologies and genetically engineered co-culture models to functionally validate whether disrupting specific metabolic crosstalk can reverse immunosuppression and inhibit tumor growth. Finally, at the translational level, a major challenge lies in achieving safe and precise drug delivery to specific cellular subsets within the TME while avoiding off-target toxicity. Future research should focus on developing intelligent nano-delivery systems capable of recognizing specific surface markers such as FAP and TREM2 on TAMs or CAFs. In summary, the research paradigm must shift from describing correlations to validating causality and addressing targetability. Only by combining advanced technologies to deeply reveal STS-specific mechanisms and innovating targeted strategies can basic discoveries be translated into effective therapies that improve patient outcomes.

Current research status of prognostic assessment in STS

Prognostic evaluation for soft tissue sarcoma patients constitutes a multifactorial integrated system, with core elements encompassing traditional pathological parameters, molecular biological characteristics, and predictive tools.

Traditional pathological parameters

Tumor size exceeding 5 cm and high histological grade, as determined by systems such as the Fédération Nationale des Centers Lutte Contre Cancer (FNCLCC) grading system, represent independent adverse prognostic factors102 in Figure 4. This association proves particularly significant for sarcomas located in the extremities and trunk wall. Distinct histological subtypes including rhabdomyosarcoma, liposarcoma, and solitary fibrous tumor exhibit markedly divergent biological behaviors.103 The presence of anaplastic pathological features in rhabdomyosarcoma may indicate unfavorable prognosis, though the precise clinical significance requires further validation.104 Anatomical location profoundly influences outcomes, evidenced by substantial prognostic differences between retroperitoneal sarcomas, such as liposarcoma and intra-abdominal sarcomas including gastrointestinal stromal tumors.103,105 Prognosis for head and neck STS also correlates with location, where tumors involving craniofacial bones typically demonstrate poorer outcomes.

Molecular biological characteristics

The significance of molecular biomarkers in prognostic assessment for STS continues to increase. The World Health Organization’s updated 2020 classification criteria for soft tissue tumors explicitly state that molecular features are crucial for accurate tumor classification and prognosis determination106 in Figure 4. Specifically, sarcomas harboring SWI/SNF related, matrix associated, actin dependent regulator of chromatin, subfamily b, member-1 (SMARCB-1) deletions, such as epithelioid sarcoma and malignant rhabdoid tumor, typically exhibit highly aggressive behavior. Similarly, sarcomas with B cell lymphoma-6 (BCL-6) co-repressor (BCOR) gene alterations, including fusions or internal tandem duplications, constitute distinct aggressive subtypes associated with poor prognosis.107 Genomic instability has also been established as an important prognostic indicator, manifested through specific copy number variations. A genomic index of 35 or higher, gain of the short arm of chromosome 5, or deletion of the short arm of chromosome 13 containing the retinoblastoma-1 (RB1) gene show significant associations with adverse outcomes in leiomyosarcoma patients.108 Regarding immunotherapy biomarkers, STS demonstrate relatively low incidence rates of high tumor mutational burden and microsatellite instability-high status.21 This characteristic potentially limits the therapeutic benefit of immune checkpoint inhibitors for these tumors.

Within the TME, combined assessment of tumor-associated macrophage density and FAP expression levels reflects the intensity of interaction between these cell types.21 Clinical evidence indicates that high TAMs density coupled with elevated FAP expression typically predicts poor prognosis, correlating with their synergistic promotion of tumor proliferation, angiogenesis, and immune evasion.109 Consequently, another important prognostic assessment marker involves dual evaluation of TGF-β secreted by TAMs and extracellular matrix proteins produced by CAFs.110 This approach is mechanistically grounded in TGF-β′s capacity to induce CAFs activation and stimulate matrix protein secretion, collaboratively establishing a metastasis-favoring microenvironment.111

Current risk stratification strategies employ immunohistochemical scoring of dual TAMs markers or utilize relevant gene signatures. These approaches demonstrate association between marker overexpression and reduced DFS and overall survival. However, the pronounced heterogeneity of STS must be acknowledged, potentially causing differential prognostic significance of TAMs and CAFs across subtypes.23 Furthermore, most existing data originates from pre-treatment tissue samples, lacking systematic investigation into post-radiotherapy or post-chemotherapy dynamic changes within the TME.112 To fully harness the potential of TAMs-CAFs interactions for precise STS prognostication, future efforts require standardized detection systems and validation studies spanning diverse STS subtypes.113,114

Bioinformatics prediction

For prognostic prediction tools in soft tissue sarcoma, dynamic nomogram models integrate multiple variables including patient age, tumor size, invasion depth, histological grade, and subtype115,116 in Figure 4. These models enable individualized survival rate or metastasis risk prediction, demonstrating superior predictive efficacy compared to traditional American Joint Committee on Cancer staging systems. Consequently, current strategies for optimizing STS prognostic assessment necessitate integrating traditional pathological parameters, key molecular features, and dynamic predictive models. Notably, advancements in molecular biology techniques continue to reveal the tumor microenvironment’s critical role in STS pathogenesis and therapeutic response, particularly regarding immune cell composition and spatial distribution117 in Figure 4. This domain is emerging as a vital research focus with promising prognostic implications.

Regarding specific modeling methodologies, multivariate regression-based models incorporate clinical characteristics and interaction biomarkers. These models employ statistical methods to determine factor weights and compute comprehensive prognostic risk scores.118 Ultimately, they stratify patients into distinct risk categories, thereby providing objective evidence for clinical decision-making.119,120 Conversely, emerging machine learning models demonstrate enhanced capacity for processing high-dimensional complex data. Such models autonomously identify intricate patterns within datasets and exhibit unique advantages in capturing potential nonlinear relationships inherent in TAMs-CAFs interactions.119,120 Consequently, they hold significant promise for improving prognostic accuracy in STS.

In summary, prognostic assessment systems based on TAMs-CAFs interactions offer substantial advantages over traditional clinicopathological indicators. By elucidating key cellular crosstalk mechanisms within the TME, these systems provide deeper insights into the biological behavior of STS. Quantitative assessment of TAMs infiltration density and spatial distribution reveals tumor immune evasion capacity and invasive potential-microscopic features undetectable by conventional staging systems. Detection of specific molecular markers enables identification of patient subgroups with similar clinicopathological features but divergent prognoses, facilitating refined risk stratification. Furthermore, this assessment framework effectively guides therapeutic decisions, as high-risk patients may benefit from targeted combination therapies or novel immunotherapies, potentially improving survival outcomes. Nevertheless, certain limitations persist in current applications of the TAMs-CAFs interaction framework for STS prognostication.

Challenges and strategies for TAMs-CAFs interactions in STS prognostic assessment

Core challenges in the assessment system

Technical and methodological challenges

Current detection technologies face significant challenges in precisely resolving the dynamically changing interactions between TAMs and CAFs in Figure 4. Although immunohistochemistry enables spatial localization of cells and visualization of specific protein expression,121 its static nature prevents the capture of transient molecular interaction events such as ligand-receptor binding which occur on millisecond to minute timescales. Conventional two-dimensional in vitro culture models lack key components of the TME (e.g., extracellular matrix, fluid shear stress, three-dimensional spatial structure), leading to substantial discrepancies between the functional states and interaction patterns of TAMs and CAFs observed in vitro and those occurring under physiological conditions.122 Traditional in vitro culture models, such as transwell co-culture systems, can simulate interactions among fewer than five cell types,123 which falls far short of replicating the complex network of over 20 cell types present in the in vivo TME.124 Single-cell RNA sequencing technologies, like the 10× Genomics platform, provide heterogeneity data at the whole-transcriptome level.125 However, the approximately 60% mRNA loss rate during library construction leads to insufficient detection sensitivity for key signaling pathway molecules, such as TGF-β and CCL5.21,126 Spatial transcriptomics technologies, such as the Visium platform, can improve resolution to 55 μm but still cannot precisely localize intercellular communication at the individual synapse level.127 Mass cytometry, like CyTOF, can simultaneously detect over 40 protein markers, yet the equipment costs exceed $3 million, and sample throughput remains low at fewer than 100 samples per week.128 Regarding the standardization of biomarker detection, the coefficients of variation for TAM markers like CD68+/CD163+/CD204+ reach up to 35% across different laboratories.121 This technical heterogeneity reduces the reliability of direct comparisons of TAMs-CAFs interaction data, such as CCL5-CCR5 axis activation levels across different research institutions, with a confidence level as low.21,126 These limitations simultaneously obstruct researchers’ ability to effectively extract critical prognostic features from vast datasets. Furthermore, standardized detection methods for TAMs and CAFs-related biomarkers remain unestablished. Results obtained from different technological platforms exhibit variations, compromising the reliability of prognostic evaluations and impairing data comparability across studies.

The challenge of individual heterogeneity

Differences in patients’ genetic backgrounds and immune statuses can influence the intensity of interactions between TAMs and CAFs through genetic polymorphisms58 in Figure 4. This inter-individual variability reduces the applicability of standardized assessment metrics, which in turn significantly impacts the accuracy of evaluation results. Current prognostic models struggle to effectively integrate such dynamic individual variables. For example, distinct interaction patterns between TAMs and CAFs within the TME have been observed in elderly patients compared to younger patient cohorts.10 Furthermore, factors such as patient lifestyle and comorbidities further increase the variability in prognostic assessment outcomes. This underscores the complex impact of individual differences on the accuracy of evaluations.

Limitations in translational understanding

Current research on the interactions between TAMs and CAFs is primarily focused on a limited number of molecular signaling pathways, such as those related to TGF-β and FAP. However, other potential interaction mechanisms, such as the role of cellular metabolic reprogramming within the TME and its impact on prognosis, have not been systematically explored and validated. This limitation hinders our comprehensive understanding of the prognostic significance of TAMs-CAFs interactions.129,130

Multidimensional solutions

Technological innovation strategies

To enhance the detection accuracy of interactions between TAMs and CAFs, it is necessary to integrate multiple complementary technological modalities131 in Figure 4. For instance, combining immunohistochemistry with mass spectrometry imaging enables simultaneous spatial localization analysis of cells and the profiling of multiple protein interactions.132 Developing patient-derived organoid models can better reconstruct and simulate the dynamic characteristics of the in vivo TME. Applying advanced artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms allows for the efficient mining of prognostically valuable features from large datasets generated by techniques like high-throughput sequencing, while also helping to reduce the cost of data analysis.

Individualized assessment framework

It is essential to construct customized prognostic assessment models capable of integrating patients’ genetic information and clinical parameters. Such frameworks should dynamically adjust the weights of different assessment indicators based on individual-specific genetic polymorphisms and incorporate important covariates like lifestyle factors and comorbidities in Figure 4. Enhancing the model’s adaptability to individual characteristics can significantly improve the reliability of prognostic predictions. Furthermore, to optimize overall prognostic prediction efficacy, it is recommended to combine indicators reflecting TAMs-CAFs interactions with traditional clinicopathological parameters (such as tumor size and histological grade). This integrated strategy preserves the mature framework of traditional assessment systems while simultaneously supplementing critical biological information from the TME level. Ultimately, this provides a more comprehensive and reliable decision-making basis for formulating individualized treatment plans.

Clinical translation pathway

The pathway for translating the assessment of TAMs-CAFs interactions from a research tool into clinical practice should encompass key steps in Figure 4. The primary step involves enhancing the resolution and accuracy of interaction detection through the multimodal technology integration described previously. Subsequently, patients should be stratified based on the identified key biomarkers, and specific prognostic models tailored to different soft tissue sarcoma subtypes should be developed. The ultimate goal is to achieve longitudinal monitoring of these interactions and use this information to guide adjustments in treatment decisions. Following this pathway will effectively promote the integration of TAMs-CAFs interaction-based assessment methods into clinical service.

In summary, current technologies face challenges in accurately capturing the dynamic interactions between TAMs and CAFs, limiting the extraction of reliable prognostic biomarkers. Patient-specific genetic and immune heterogeneity further complicates prognostic modeling. To improve clinical outcome prediction, integrated multimodal technologies, AI-assisted data mining, and personalized assessment models that incorporate both TAMs-CAFs interaction features and traditional clinicopathological factors are essential. These strategies promise to enhance prognostic accuracy and support individualized treatment decisions in soft tissue sarcoma.

Future research directions

Application of emerging technologies

The rapid advancement of technology offers new opportunities for studying macrophage-fibroblast interactions and prognostic assessment through emerging techniques like gene editing and single-cell sequencing. Gene editing technologies, such as the CRISPR-Cas9 system, enable precise knockout, insertion, or replacement of specific genes.133 This allows researchers to selectively modify key genes within macrophages or fibroblasts, facilitating in-depth investigation into the specific functions of these genes in mediating their interactions, as well as their mechanistic roles in the initiation, progression, and prognosis of STS.134 For example, by knocking out a cytokine-secretion-related gene in macrophages, researchers can observe the subsequent effects on fibroblast behavior and alterations in the TME, thereby revealing critical nodes within their interaction network more clearly. Single-cell RNA sequencing has greatly advanced our understanding of cellular heterogeneity within the TMEs, particularly in key components such as CAFs and macrophages.135,136 Standard bioinformatics workflows-including Seurat clustering, differential expression analysis, pseudotime trajectory inference, and cell-cell communication analysis-have been successfully applied to tumors such as melanoma, allowing accurate identification of diverse cell types, including multiple CAFs subpopulations.137 These approaches systematically uncover unique phenotypic features among different CAFs subtypes. For example, C0 MYH11+ and C2 GDF15+ subpopulations with respect to gene expression patterns, metabolic activity, stemness properties, and underlying transcriptional regulatory networks. Furthermore, single-cell RNA sequencing helps identify genes specifically expressed during cellular crosstalk, such as between macrophages and fibroblasts, offering valuable biomarkers for prognostic assessment.137,138 It also reveals functional differences among cellular subpopulations involved in these interactions, thereby refining our mechanistic insight into TMEs dynamics. Looking forward, research built on single-cell RNA sequencing inferred regulatory networks such as those predicting key transcription factors via tools like pySCENIC will increasingly integrate spatial multi-omics. For instance, combining single-cell RNA sequencing with single-cell ATAC-seq enables simultaneous profiling of gene expression and chromatin accessibility, directly testing the regulatory roles of candidate transcription factors. Such integration will elevate inferred networks from computational predictions to mechanistically grounded conclusions, ultimately uncovering the upstream molecular switches that drive specific CAFs phenotypes, such as enhanced oxidative phosphorylation or distinct pathway interactions. Simultaneously, it aids in discovering differences among various cellular subpopulations involved in these interactions, further refining our understanding of the underlying mechanisms and offering a more comprehensive perspective for prognostic evaluation.

Optimizing the prognostic assessment system

Although current prognostic assessment systems for soft tissue sarcoma based on macrophage-fibroblast interactions have achieved some success, there remains significant room for optimization.139 On one hand, further exploration is needed to identify more biomarkers associated with their interactions and incorporate them into prognostic indicators. Beyond known factors like cytokines and growth factors, emerging technologies such as proteomics and metabolomics can be leveraged to screen for additional potential key molecules, enhancing the accuracy of prognostic assessment.140 On the other hand, constructing more robust prognostic models is crucial. Existing models can be optimized by integrating multi-dimensional data-including clinical features, imaging data, and molecular biology information-using machine learning algorithms. For instance, deep learning algorithms can be employed to comprehensively analyze clinical data, cell interaction-related indicators, and outcomes from large patient cohorts, thereby establishing more precise predictive models to better guide the formulation of individualized treatment plans.140 Additionally, dynamic updating of the prognostic assessment system is essential. As treatment progresses, macrophage-fibroblast interactions may evolve; real-time monitoring of these changes and updating the assessment system accordingly can more accurately reflect the patient’s prognostic status.

Multidisciplinary collaborative research

The advancement of prognostic assessment for soft tissue sarcoma greatly benefits from the integration of multiple advanced technologies. Single-cell RNA sequencing provides high-resolution characterization of cellular heterogeneity within the TME, particularly the distinct subtypes of CAFs and macrophages.141 Spatial transcriptomics further adds the crucial dimension of spatial context, enabling precise mapping of cellular interaction networks.137,138 The application of machine learning algorithms, such as graph neural networks, allows for quantitative modeling of dynamic intercellular communication and prediction of signaling pathway activities in specific spatial configurations.137,138 This technological integration is further strengthened through multi-omics approaches. Combining chromatin accessibility data with deep learning-based pathological image analysis helps validate regulatory mechanisms and facilitates the development of clinical diagnostic tools using routine tissue sections.137,138 Such multidimensional data integration enables the construction of more accurate prognostic models and supports the identification of reliable biomarkers. Multidisciplinary collaboration plays an indispensable role in this process. Close cooperation between medical experts, biologists, computational scientists, and engineers ensures comprehensive investigation from diverse perspectives. Medical professionals provide clinical samples and real-world data, while biologists reveal underlying molecular mechanisms. Computational scientists develop advanced analytical models, and engineers create novel detection technologies. Together, these collaborative efforts break through the limitations of single-discipline research, providing new insights into macrophage-fibroblast interactions and their prognostic significance in soft tissue sarcoma. Ultimately, this integrated approach will lead to more effective prognostic tools and improved clinical outcomes for sarcoma patients.

Conclusion

This review highlights the prognostic significance of macrophage-fibroblast crosstalk within the TME of soft tissue sarcoma. These interactions play a critical role in tumor progression by influencing cellular functions such as proliferation, polarization, and recruitment, and they offer a promising basis for novel prognostic indicators. Leveraging such markers may overcome the limitations of traditional approaches and improve prognostic accuracy.

Future research should focus on identifying key molecular mediators and signaling pathways involved in this cellular crosstalk to inform targeted therapies. The integration of advanced technologies like single-cell sequencing and artificial intelligence will further elucidate the relationship between cellular interactions and clinical outcomes. Interdisciplinary collaboration across medicine, biology, and computational science will be essential to translate these findings into refined prognostic tools and improved patient care.
