Perspectives of Anganwadi workers on early childhood mental health: A qualitative study from rural Karnataka and hilly Uttarakhand
Authors: A Nandhini, Radhakrishnan Govindan, Channveerachari Naveen Kumar, Keerti Kaushik, K M Rajendra, K Latha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Anganwadi workers (AWWs) are central to India’s integrated child development services (ICDS) and play an important role in early childhood care and development (ECCD). However, limited evidence exists regarding how AWWs understand and respond to early childhood mental health concerns across diverse rural settings. This study explored AWWs’ perspectives on early childhood mental health, including barriers, existing practices, and training needs.Methods:A qualitative descriptive design informed by Sandelowski’s approach was employed. Five focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted with 35 AWWs from Belur taluk, Karnataka (n = 22), and Munsyari block, Uttarakhand (n = 13). Discussions were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed inductively using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis approach. Coding was independently undertaken by two researchers using ATLAS.ti version 9.0.Results:Five interrelated themes emerged: understanding child mental health; community participation and parenting practices; innovations and training needs; operational challenges affecting psychosocial engagement; and delivery of nutrition and health services within community contexts. AWWs demonstrated strong motivation and contextual understanding of children’s developmental and emotional needs; however, recognition of less visible emotional and regulatory concerns remained limited. Parenting stress, migration, economic hardship, administrative workload, geographical barriers, and limited referral pathways constrained early identification and psychosocial engagement. Participants expressed a preference for practical, skills-based training and simple referral guidance integrated within routine ICDS activities.Conclusion:AWWs occupy an important position within rural early childhood care systems. The findings suggest that practical, context-responsive training and clearer referral pathways may strengthen the early identification and support of children with developmental and emotional concerns in rural settings.

INTRODUCTION

Early childhood is a critical period for cognitive, emotional, and social development, and disruptions during this stage can have long-term implications for mental health and overall well-being across the lifespan.[1] Adverse early-life experiences, including chronic stress, developmental difficulties, malnutrition, and limited psychosocial stimulation, have been associated with poorer educational, behavioral, and mental health outcomes later in life.[23]

In India, the integrated child development services (ICDS) programmed plays a central role in supporting early childhood health and development. Anganwadi Workers (AWWs), who are embedded within village communities, deliver services related to child nutrition, growth monitoring, preschool education, immunization support, and caregiver counselling.[4] Through routine activities such as home visits, growth monitoring, and caregiver interaction, AWWs regularly observe children’s developmental progress, behavior, communication, feeding patterns, and caregiver–child interaction. These routine touchpoints provide important opportunities for early identification of developmental and psychosocial concerns, particularly in rural and resource-constrained settings where specialized services are limited.

Evidence from low- and middle-income countries suggests that frontline community workers can contribute meaningfully to early identification and referral of child developmental and mental health concerns when appropriately trained.[256] These priorities are also reflected in global frameworks such as the WHO Nurturing Care Framework[7] and mhGAP,[8] which emphasize early identification, caregiver support, and community-based approaches for promoting child development and mental health. Existing evidence suggests that frontline workers are generally more confident in recognizing visible developmental difficulties and nutrition-related concerns than less visible emotional and behavioral problems.[59] Existing ICDS activities also primarily focus on observable developmental functioning, nutrition monitoring, and preschool readiness that may influence how children’s mental health concerns are recognized within community settings. In addition, multiple responsibilities, documentation requirements, and limited mental health training may constrain AWWs’ capacity for systematic psychosocial observation and referral.[610]

Despite the growing global emphasis on early childhood mental health, there remains limited qualitative evidence from India exploring how AWWs conceptualize and respond to early childhood emotional and developmental concerns within routine ICDS practice. Limited Indian qualitative evidence has comparatively explored AWW perspectives across ecologically distinct rural plains and hilly settings. Understanding these perspectives is important because geographical, infrastructural, and sociocultural contexts may shape both caregiving environments and frontline service delivery experiences.

Accordingly, the present study was undertaken in Belur taluk, Karnataka, representing a rural plain setting, and Munsyari block, Uttarakhand, representing a geographically remote hilly region. The study aimed to explore AWWs’ perspectives on early childhood mental health, including their observations of developmental and emotional concerns, perceived barriers to identification and referral, existing community-based practices, and training and support needs.

METHODOLOGY

Study design and setting

This study was conducted as part of the NIMHANS–Ashraya Hastha Trust Comprehensive Mental Health Action Program (NAMAN), a pilot taluk-level community mental health initiative aimed at strengthening rural mental healthcare systems through integrated and context-responsive approaches. The programmed is being implemented across geographically and socio-culturally distinct rural settings in India. The present study served as a foundational qualitative needs assessment to understand AWWs’ perspectives regarding early childhood mental health within routine ICDS settings.

A qualitative descriptive design informed by Sandelowski’s approach was employed to obtain practice-oriented and contextually grounded insights into AWWs’ experiences, perceptions, and training needs related to early childhood mental health.[11] The study did not follow a grounded theory or theory-testing framework; rather, themes were inductively derived from participant narratives using thematic analysis.[12]

The study was conducted in two geographically and socio-culturally distinct rural regions in India: Belur taluk in Hassan district, Karnataka, representing a rural plains ecology in South India, and Munsyari block in Pithoragarh district, Uttarakhand, representing a geographically remote hilly region in North India. These sites were purposively selected within the NAMAN implementation framework using a maximum-variation approach to capture contextual differences in healthcare access, ecological constraints, caregiving environments, and frontline service delivery experiences across plains and hill settings.

Belur taluk is characterized by relatively better road connectivity and access to primary healthcare services, whereas Munsyari is marked by mountainous terrain, seasonal inaccessibility, and limited specialist referral services. Both settings are served through the ICDS programmed and primary healthcare systems, where AAWs play an important role in nutrition services, preschool education, growth monitoring, and community-based child health activities.

Participants and sampling

Purposive criterion-based sampling was used to recruit AWWs from ICDS centers across both study sites. Eligible participants included AWWs with at least one year of work experience within ICDS and active involvement in child-related service delivery activities. All participants were women, consistent with the ICDS cadre structure.

Administrative permission for participant recruitment was obtained from the respective child development project officers (CDPOs) and ICDS authorities. Eligible participants were approached directly by the research team during scheduled ICDS meetings and field visits. Supervisors, CDPOs, and administrative personnel were not present during the focus group discussions (FGDs) to minimize hierarchical influence and facilitate open discussion.

A total of 35 AWWs participated in five FGD conducted between [April–July 2024], including 22 participants from Belur and 13 participants from Munsyari. Participants represented varying educational backgrounds and years of ICDS experience.

Development of the focus group discussion guide

A semi-structured FGD guide was developed based on literature review, existing community mental health frameworks, and discussions with experts in psychiatric nursing, community psychiatry, and child mental health. The guide explored participants’ understanding of child mental health, recognition of developmental and emotional concerns, caregiving and community influences, operational challenges, referral experiences, and perceived training needs.

The guide was reviewed by subject experts for content relevance and contextual appropriateness. Prior to formal data collection, mock FGDs were conducted within the research team to assess question flow, clarity, cultural appropriateness, and facilitation consistency. Based on these discussions, minor modifications and refinement of probes were undertaken to improve clarity and contextual relevance. The final focus group discussion guide is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Data collection

All FGDs were facilitated by a multidisciplinary research team consisting of psychiatrists, psychiatric nurses, psychologists, and mental health researchers. Discussions were conducted by trained facilitators and moderators, while designated note-takers documented field observations and non-verbal interactions. Both male and female researchers were involved in the FGDs. Before data collection, mock FGDs and team debriefing sessions were conducted to standardize interviewing approaches and sensitize the research team to qualitative interviewing procedures.

The primary researcher was a female PhD scholar in psychiatric nursing with prior experience in community mental health and qualitative research. The research team included mental health professionals with experience in psychiatric and community-based care. Although no prior formal relationship existed with participants, reflexive discussions were undertaken throughout data collection and analysis to minimize interpretive bias and maintain contextual sensitivity.

All FGDs were conducted in local vernacular languages (Kannada in Belur and Hindi in Munsyari) and lasted approximately 45–60 min. Discussions were audio-recorded with participant consent, and field notes documenting contextual observations and group dynamics were maintained during and after each session.

Participants were informed about the study objectives, the voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality safeguards, and their right to withdraw at any stage without consequences. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in their preferred language. No participant who consented to participate withdrew from the study.

Data collection and analysis occurred concurrently, and sample adequacy was guided by thematic saturation, with no substantially new themes emerging during the later FGDs.

Transcription, translation, and analysis

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim within 48 hours of each FGD. FGDs in Belur were conducted by the first author in Kannada, while FGDs in Munsyari were conducted by the fifth author in Hindi. Debriefing discussions were conducted within the research team immediately after each FGD to document contextual observations, culturally specific expressions, and emerging interpretations. Audio recordings were initially transcribed in the original languages and subsequently translated into English by bilingual researchers familiar with the local sociocultural context. Translation accuracy, semantic consistency, and contextual meanings were reviewed collaboratively within the research team during analysis. Local dialect expressions were discussed during interpretation to preserve conceptual and cultural accuracy. Formal independent back-translation was not undertaken; however, iterative team discussions and contextual review were used to maintain interpretive consistency across translated transcripts.

Data were analyzed inductively using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis approach.[12] Two researchers independently coded the transcripts using ATLAS.ti version 9.0. Initial codes were compared across researchers, and coding discrepancies were resolved through iterative consensus discussions within the research team.[13] The codebook was refined progressively throughout the analytical process as new insights emerged from the data. Themes and subthemes were developed inductively from participant narratives rather than being predetermined by the FGD guide domains. Field notes were used both contextually and analytically during interpretation of findings. Formal member checking was not undertaken due to logistical constraints and geographical dispersion of participants.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committees of the participating Institute Ethical Clearance from NIMAHNS{No. NIMHANS/EC (BEH.SC.DIV.)MEETNG/2024} dated 15/04/2024 and AIIMS Risikesh{Letter No- AIIMS/IEC/25/04, Date:17.01.2025}.

Administrative permissions were also obtained from the respective ICDS authorities and DCPOs before data collection.

The study reporting was guided by the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist, and the completed checklist is provided as Supplementary File 2.[14]

RESULTS

Thematic analysis of the FGDs generated five interrelated themes reflecting AWWs’ perspectives and experiences related to early childhood mental health within ICDS settings. While several concerns were shared across both study sites, differences emerged in relation to ecological context, service accessibility, and operational realities.

A total of 35 AWWs participated in five FGDs, including 22 participants from Belur, Karnataka, and 13 from Munsyari, Uttarakhand. Participants had between 3 and 38 years of ICDS work experience and educational qualifications ranged from SSLC to a Bachelor’s degree. Themes, subthemes, and illustrative participant quotations

Theme 1: Understanding and addressing child mental health

Subtheme 1.1: Mental health as observable functioning

Participants across both settings commonly understood child mental health through observable functioning, including play, communication, social interaction, and the ability to participate in routine activities.

“We feel that a mentally healthy child plays, runs around, laughs, and talks with others.” (P-M1.1, Munsyari)

Some participants also associated mental well-being with independence in carrying out age-appropriate daily activities.

Subtheme 1.2: Recognition of emotional concerns

Participants expressed greater confidence in recognizing visible developmental concerns such as hyperactivity, speech delay, aggression, and poor interaction than emotional difficulties perceived as less obvious.

“Adults can speak when they have a problem, but children can’t express it the same way.” (P-M1.2, Munsyari)

Several participants described relying primarily on behavioral observation to identify emotional distress in children.

“They sit in one place and look sad. You can see it on their face.” (P-M2.1, Munsyari)

Subtheme 1.3: Difficulty in distinguishing concerning behaviors

Participants described uncertainty in distinguishing age-appropriate behavior from concerns requiring referral or professional attention.

“Some children are shy, some stubborn—how to know when they need help?” (P-M2.4, Munsyari)

A few participants reported difficulty discussing such concerns with caregivers because parents often perceived these behaviors as normal childhood variations.

Theme 2: Community participation, parenting practices, and social environment

This theme reflects participants’ perceptions regarding the influence of family relationships, caregiving practices, and social conditions on children’s emotional and behavioral well-being.

Subtheme 2.1: Family stress and caregiving practices

Participants frequently linked children’s behavior and emotional well-being with parental stress, conflict, migration, and livelihood pressures.

“Arguments between parents and lack of emotional bonding affect children a lot.” (P-B1.3, Belur)

Economic hardship was also perceived as affecting caregiver supervision and interaction with children.

“When fathers do not earn enough, stress comes inside the home, and children show that stress in behavior.” (P-B2.1, Belur)

“Children stay with grandparents most of the time, and there is less stimulation at home.” (P-B2.4, Belur)

Subtheme 2.2: Technology overuse and reduced social interaction

Excessive mobile phone use among children was repeatedly described as a growing concern in Belur. Participants felt that prolonged mobile phone exposure reduced social interaction, outdoor play, and caregiver–child engagement.

“Even while eating, children are using mobile phones continuously.” (P-B2.2, Belur)

Subtheme 2.3: Environmental influences on parental anxiety

Participants from Munsyari highlighted how difficult terrain and environmental risks affected parental anxiety and children’s movement outside the home.

“Parents fear children going alone because of hills and weather. Their tension shows in the child also.” (P-M1.6, Munsyari)

Participants noted that such concerns often restricted outdoor play and social interaction among children.

Theme 3: Innovations, training needs, and adaptive practices

This theme reflects adaptive strategies used by AWWs and their perceived need for practical support and training related to child mental health and development.

Subtheme 3.1: Play-based engagement strategies

Participants described using songs, storytelling, sand play, games, and fine motor activities to improve children’s participation and interaction within Anganwadi centers.

“Children enjoy playing—we should teach them through play.” (P-M2.1, Munsyari)

Several participants felt that playful learning methods improved communication and group engagement among children.

Subtheme 3.2: Need for practical mental health training

Across both sites, participants consistently expressed interest in practical and community-based training related to child mental health and developmental concerns.

“We really want to learn more about mental health.” (P-M1.1, Munsyari)

Participants preferred simple and practical materials to support identification, referral, and communication with caregivers.

Subtheme 3.3: Preference for simple and visual learning materials

Participants preferred simple, locally understandable, and visually oriented learning materials over lengthy manuals or theoretical content.

“Prefer videos to watch the symptoms and learn… discussion and pamphlets are easier for us.” (P-B3.1, Belur)

Several participants also preferred locally conducted training sessions integrated within routine work settings.

“Training sessions are helpful—especially if they happen in the village.” (P-M1.1, Munsyari)

Theme 4: Daily operational challenges affecting psychosocial engagement

Subtheme 4.1: Documentation burden limiting child interaction

Participants from Belur described increasing documentation and reporting responsibilities that reduced opportunities for direct interaction and observation of children.

“The paperwork for all schemes takes so much time that we hardly sit with children individually to observe or talk to them.” (P-B2.5, Belur)

Several participants felt that administrative tasks occupied a substantial portion of their routine workday.

Subtheme 4.2: Geographical barriers and healthcare inaccessibility

Participants from Munsyari described how poor roads, difficult terrain, and seasonal inaccessibility disrupted continuity of services and follow-up.

“The roads are bad. From going to school and coming back home, both children and parents are stressed.” (P-M2.2, Munsyari)

Limited access to healthcare services in remote settings was also repeatedly highlighted.

“We do not have access to doctors nearby.” (P-M2.1, Munsyari)

A minority of participants expressed concern regarding whether additional mental health-related responsibilities could realistically be incorporated within their existing workload.

Theme 5: Delivering nutrition and health services within community contexts

This theme reflects how routine nutrition and health service delivery intersect with opportunities for developmental observation, caregiver interaction, and psychosocial follow-up.

Participants described how irregular attendance, migration, and nutrition-related challenges reduced opportunities for consistent developmental observation, emotional engagement, and early identification of psychosocial concerns.

Subtheme 5.1: Nutrition-related barriers and caregiver engagement

Participants from Belur described resistance toward supplementary nutrition and feeding recommendations as barriers affecting caregiver engagement.

“Parents ask why their children should eat foods they never ate. Children refuse, and they complain to us.” (P-B3.2, Belur)

Some participants felt that such resistance affected continuity of interaction with families.[15]

Subtheme 5.2: Migration and disrupted developmental follow-up

Migration and irregular attendance were commonly reported in Munsyari, limiting continuity of developmental observation and follow-up.

Several participants reported that long travel distances discouraged families from seeking further assessment or follow-up care.

“Even if we suspect delay, parents don’t take the child to hospitals because the nearest one is very far.” (P-M1.4, Munsyari).

DISCUSSION

This qualitative study explored AWWs’ perspectives on early childhood mental health across two contrasting rural contexts in India. The findings highlight that AWWs possess substantial contextual understanding of children’s developmental and family environments and demonstrate willingness to engage in mental health-related activities. However, their ability to identify and respond to early psychosocial concerns is shaped by developmental understanding, existing ICDS priorities, workload pressures, and broader contextual constraints. The study also provides insight into how ecological and infrastructural realities influence community-based child mental health engagement across plains and hilly settings.

A key finding was that AWWs primarily understood child mental health through visible and functional indicators such as speech, play, activity level, social interaction, and school readiness. This observation is consistent with earlier Indian studies showing that frontline workers are more confident in recognizing developmental delays and externalizing behavioral concerns than less visible emotional difficulties. However, the present study extends existing Indian AWW literature by comparing these perspectives across ecologically distinct rural settings and by examining how service-delivery environments shape recognition practices.

Importantly, the findings should be interpreted within a developmental framework appropriate for children aged 0–6 years, the primary population served under ICDS. Emotional difficulties in early childhood may not present in clearly differentiated DSM-style forms such as “anxiety” or “depression.” Rather, distress is often expressed through dysregulation in sleep, feeding, attachment, irritability, clinginess, excessive crying, behavioral dysregulation, or reduced social engagement. In this context, AWWs’ uncertainty in recognizing less visible emotional and regulatory difficulties may reflect not only limited mental health literacy but also the developmental complexity of identifying psychosocial distress during early childhood. This aligns with developmental mental health frameworks such as ZERO TO THREE/DC: 0–5 approaches[16] that emphasize relational and regulatory patterns rather than adult-oriented psychiatric categorization.[17]

The structure of ICDS itself may also influence the strong behavioral and functional framing observed among AWWs. Routine Anganwadi activities and reporting systems predominantly emphasize nutrition monitoring, preschool readiness, developmental milestones, immunization support, and observable developmental functioning. Consequently, AWWs may be more attuned to concerns that visibly affect behavior, communication, feeding, or school participation than to subtle emotional or relational difficulties. Cultural idioms of distress may further shape interpretation, with emotional concerns frequently normalized as temperament, stubbornness, shyness, or parenting-related behavior rather than recognized as indicators of psychosocial vulnerability.

At the same time, AWWs demonstrated substantial awareness of how caregiving environments influence children’s well-being. Participants consistently linked parental stress, migration, economic hardship, harsh discipline, and environmental adversity with changes in children’s behavior and emotional expression. These findings align with broader developmental evidence linking caregiver stress, social adversity, and early childhood emotional regulation.[18] The findings also suggest that AWWs may already possess an intuitive ecological understanding of child mental health, even when lacking formal mental health terminology or structured frameworks.

An important contribution of the study lies in highlighting AWWs’ strong motivation for practical and context-sensitive capacity building. Participants preferred brief, skills-oriented, locally delivered training focused on identifying developmental and emotional concerns, communicating with caregivers, and navigating referral pathways. Similar observations have been reported in low- and middle-income countries where frontline workers, including Lady Health Workers and community mental health workers, demonstrate improved confidence and role clarity when training is experiential, community-based, and integrated into existing service structures. Evidence from task-sharing initiatives in adult mental health in India similarly suggests that community workers can contribute meaningfully to early identification and referral when supported through structured supervision and simplified tools.

Operational realities emerged as a major determinant of psychosocial engagement. In Belur, administrative workload and extensive documentation requirements reduced opportunities for child-centered observation and interaction.[13] In contrast, AWWs in Munsyari described how geographical isolation, difficult terrain, and seasonal inaccessibility disrupted continuity of services and follow-up. These findings suggest that barriers to early identification differ across ecological settings, even when broader systemic constraints are shared. The study therefore highlights the importance of context-responsive approaches rather than uniform models of mental health integration within ICDS.

The findings also indicate that early childhood mental health integration may be most feasible when embedded within existing community platforms rather than introduced as a separate vertical programme.[19] Strengthening linkages between AWWs and existing systems such as Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK) Mobile Health Teams, Village Health Sanitation and Nutrition Days (VHSNDs), Health and Wellness Centers, and District Mental Health Programmed (DMHP) services may improve opportunities for early identification and referral.[20] Training support may potentially be delivered through collaborative partnerships involving DMHP teams, Community Health Officers, pediatrics services, and academic institutions. Such integration aligns with broader policy directions under the National Mental Health Programmed (NMHP), community mental health initiatives, and early childhood development frameworks in India.

Together, these findings suggest that AWWs represent an important but under-supported community resource for early childhood mental health promotion. While the study does not suggest that AWWs function as mental health diagnosticians, it highlights their potential role in observing developmental and relational concerns, supporting caregiver engagement, and facilitating early referral within routine ICDS practice.

IMPLICATIONS FOR PRACTICE

The findings suggest that AWWs are willing to contribute to the early identification of developmental and emotional concerns but require practical support within their existing roles. Participants consistently expressed the need for simple, community-based training using visual learning materials, demonstrations, and practical guidance to improve recognition of child mental health concerns. Training should also include strategies for communicating with caregivers regarding children’s developmental and emotional needs.

The findings further indicate that administrative workload and geographical barriers should be considered when planning additional mental health-related responsibilities for AWWs. Participants also highlighted challenges in accessing referral services, suggesting the need for clearer referral pathways and improved coordination with existing health services to facilitate timely assessment and follow-up of children requiring further care.

STRENGTHS, LIMITATIONS, AND CONCLUSION

Strengths

This study provides contextually grounded insights into AWWs’ perspectives on early childhood mental health across two geographically and ecologically distinct rural settings in India. Inclusion of both plains and hilly service-delivery contexts enabled exploration of how infrastructural, environmental, and operational realities shape community-level engagement with child mental health concerns. The study also contributes to the limited Indian qualitative literature examining early childhood mental health within routine ICDS systems and highlights practical implementation considerations relevant to rural and resource-constrained settings.

Limitations

However, several limitations should be considered. As a qualitative study, the findings are context-specific and are not intended to be statistically generalizable to all AWWs in India. Data collection through FGDs may have introduced social desirability bias and may have limited the expression of divergent opinions. All participants were women, consistent with the AWWs cadre structure under ICDS. Perspectives of supervisors, parents, and children were not included, and parental triangulation was not undertaken. Formal member checking was not conducted due to logistical and feasibility constraints. Additionally, translation and interpretation across Kannada, Hindi, and English may have resulted in minor contextual meaning loss despite efforts to preserve semantic accuracy. The researchers’ professional mental health backgrounds and involvement within the broader project context may also have influenced participant responses and introduced potential researcher–participant power asymmetry during discussions. Finally, the findings reflect a single-time-point perspective; longitudinal research may further clarify how seasonal, administrative, and policy-related changes influence AWWs’ capacity to identify and respond to psychosocial concerns over time.

CONCLUSION

AWWs occupy an important position at the interface of early childhood development, family environments, and community health. However, structural constraints, limited mental health literacy, and contextual service-delivery challenges restrict their ability to identify and respond to early psychosocial concerns consistently. Despite these barriers, AWWs demonstrated strong motivation, contextual understanding, and adaptive community-based engagement, suggesting readiness for greater involvement in early childhood mental health promotion.

The findings highlight the need for practical, context-responsive mental health training and simple referral pathways within existing health services. Future implementation research may help identify feasible and scalable approaches for integrating early childhood mental health support within routine community-based services across diverse rural contexts.
