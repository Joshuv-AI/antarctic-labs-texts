Remnant cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease: a cohort study
Authors: Di Zhou, Mengge Zhou, Shuohua Chen, Chenlu Yang, Yanhong Wang, Yang Zhou, Shutong Wu, Shouling Wu, Li Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent studies highlighted the independent role of remnant cholesterol (RC) on the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD). We explored interrelationships among low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), RC, and ASCVD in individuals with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD).MethodsAnalyzing 25,418 MASLD and 57,501 non-MASLD ASCVD- free Chinese adults from the Kailuan Study (2006–2011), we used Fine-Gray models to evaluate LDL-C and RC associations with ASCVD across varying cut-offs, incorporating China-PAR (Prediction for Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Risk in China) 10-year risk.ResultsThe MASLD group had comparable LDL-C but elevated RC levels compared to the non-MASLD. In MASLD individuals, associations of LDL-C (J-shaped) and RC (linear) with ASCVD were observed, contrasting with the monotonically increasing relationships in non-MASLD individuals. Even with guideline-recommended LDL-C levels, the MASLD group with elevated RC alone showed a higher ASCVD risk. Among individuals with low ASCVD risk evaluated by the China-PAR model, MASLD patients with LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L and RC < 0.6 mmol/L had comparable ASCVD risk to the non-MASLD group with LDL-C < 3.4 mmol/L and RC < 0.6 mmol/L (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.17; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.55–2.47). For those with moderate to high predicted ASCVD risk, the MASLD with the same lipid levels (LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L and RC < 0.6 mmol/L) had a similar risk of ASCVD to that of the non-MASLD with LDL-C < 2.6 mmol/L and RC < 0.6 mmol/L (HR: 1.15; 95% CI: 0.71–1.86).ConclusionsThe findings indicate that MASLD alters the relationship between lipids and ASCVD, underscoring the necessity for integrated and stricter management of both LDL-C and RC to mitigate risk in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02772-7.Keywords: Remnant cholesterol, LDL-C, ASCVD, MASLD

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly known as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [1], has emerged as a predominant chronic liver condition [2], affecting approximately one in three individuals globally, including China [3]. Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) imposes a substantial disease burden on the MASLD population [4], underscoring the importance of identifying high-risk subgroups and implementing targeted ASCVD prevention strategies within this demographic.

Atherogenic dyslipidemia, characterized by elevated total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [5], is an essential contributor to ASCVD risk in both the general population and individuals with MASLD. The primary therapeutic goal for ASCVD prevention in the general population is the management of elevated LDL-C levels contingent upon absolute ASCVD risks [6], Additionally, remnant cholesterol (RC) [7–9], which constitutes the cholesterol content within lipoproteins beyond high-density lipoprotein and low-density lipoprotein [10], is independent of conventional lipid profiles and is positively correlated with adverse cardiovascular events in both general [8, 9, 11, 12] and MASLD populations [13], indicating the potential significance of RC in ASCVD prevention.

Although several guidelines have advocated specific LDL-C management targets based on ASCVD risks [6, 14, 15], including those for MASLD [16, 17], the question of whether the LDL-C treatment target for MASLD should differ from that for the general population remains unresolved. Furthermore, the combined management of LDL-C and RC warrants further investigation.

This study aims to examine the individual and combined associations of LDL-C and RC with ASCVD in MASLD and to compare those associations with those in non-MASLD populations with varying ASCVD risks. The findings of this study provide evidence for lipid management in MASLD populations.

Methods

Study population

The Kailuan Study (trial identification: ChiCTR-TNRC-11001489) is an ongoing prospective cohort initiated in 2006 in North China [18]. A total of 119,813 participants aged 18 years and older were enrolled in the cohort between 2006 and 2011, all of whom had complete serum lipid profiles and abdominal ultrasound data. Exclusions were made for individuals with [1] a history of ASCVD (n = 4,179); [2] excessive alcohol consumption (men: ≥30 g/d or women: ≥20 g/d ethanol) [2] or missing alcohol consumption data (n = 29,077); [3] positive hepatitis B surface antigen (n = 2,898), liver cirrhosis (n = 464) or a history of malignancy (n = 276). Ultimately, 82,919 participants were included in the study (25,418 MASLD and 57,501 non-MASLD) (Figure S1). Fatty liver was diagnosed by an abdominal ultrasonography scan (HD-15; Philips, the Netherlands). MASLD was defined as the presence of hepatic steatosis with one or more cardiometabolic risk factors, and no other discernible cause of steatosis, followed by the guidance from the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases [2]. The cardiometabolic risk factors include: [1] BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2 (Asian) or WC >90 cm (male) 80 cm (female); [2] fasting serum glucose ≥ 5.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL) or type 2 diabetes or treatment for type 2 diabetes; [3] blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or specific antihypertensive drug treatment; [4] plasma triglycerides ≥ 1.70 mmol/L (150 mg/dL) or lipid lowering treatment; [5] plasma HDL-cholesterol ≤ 1.0 mmol/L (40 mg/dL) (male) and ≤ 1.3 mmol/L (50 mg/dL) (female) or lipid lowering treatment.

The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Kailuan General Hospital and the Institute of Basic Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Endpoints

The details for the collection of ASCVD and death have been described previously [19]. Briefly, the endpoint was the first occurrence of ASCVD, including myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, and revascularization therapy (percutaneous coronary intervention and coronary artery bypass grafting), which were collected through the Kailuan Social Security Information System and the local medical insurance system annually or by self-report through questionnaires biennially. Myocardial infarction (I21) and ischemic stroke (I63) were identified by the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision codes. Coronary artery bypass grafting and percutaneous coronary intervention were determined by the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, and Clinical Modification codes. Then, the medical records were extracted and validated by trained professionals. Information on death was obtained from the local vital statistics offices. Follow-up started from the enrolled date and ended at the date of death, ASCVD occurrence, or December 31, 2020, whichever came first.

Serum lipid profiles

Overnight eight-hour fasting venous blood samples were collected. Serum TC and triglyceride (TG) were measured enzymatically, and HDL-C and LDL-C levels were measured using the direct test method [20] at the central laboratory of Kailuan General Hospital. RC was calculated by TC minus HDL-C minus LDL-C.

Covariates

Questionnaire surveys were conducted to collect age, sex, smoking, alcohol intake, physical activity, and medical history. Height, weight, waist circumference, and blood pressure were measured. Body mass index was calculated by weight divided by the square of height (kg/m2). Fasting blood glucose, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), C-reactive protein (CRP), serum creatinine, and hepatitis B surface antigen were also measured at the same central laboratory as lipid profiles. Overweight or obese, central obesity, hypertension, elevated systolic blood pressure, diabetes, impaired fasting glycemia, ALT abnormality, CRP abnormality, and chronic kidney disease were defined as previously [21, 22].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (P25, P75), and categorical variables were presented as numbers (%). The differences in the characteristics between MASLD and non-MASLD were evaluated by standardized mean difference (SMD) to adjust for sample size [23], with SMD >10% as a significant difference.

Cut-off values for LDL-C and RC were determined using a hybrid approach. Clinically relevant cut-offs were primarily selected based on the Chinese Guidelines for Lipid Management (2023) [15], the American Association of Clinical Endocrinology guidelines for lipid management in MASLD patients [2], and previous literature [9]. Age-adjusted restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with three knots (5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles) were also performed to examine the dose-response relationship between LDL-C, RC, and ASCVD in both MASLD and non-MASLD populations, in order to assess whether the guideline-recommended cut-offs aligned with data-driven inflection points. Subsequently, the Nelson-Aalen analysis was applied to estimate the cumulative incidence of ASCVD, while the Fine-Gray models (univariate, age and sex-adjusted, and further lifestyle factor-adjusted) were employed to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) and evaluate the association of LDL-C and RC with ASCVD risk, both individually and jointly, considering non-ASCVD deaths as a competing risk. When analyzing the relationship between different groups of LDL-C or RC levels and ASCVD risk, to assess the linear trend across categories, we assigned the median value of each group to all individuals in that group and modeled this variable as a continuous term in the Fine-Gray models. The corresponding P-value was reported as the P for trend. Additionally, a subgroup analysis was conducted based on different predicted 10-year ASCVD risk levels (low-risk of < 5% vs. moderate-to-high-risk ≥ 5%) as determined by the China-PAR (Prediction for Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Risk in China) 10-year ASCVD risk prediction model [24], with varying lipid cut-off values set according to the risk level.

Data were analyzed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina), and R version 4.3.2 (https://www.r-project.org/), with two-sided p < 0.05 as statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

In this study, 82,919 participants with a mean (standard deviation) age of 49.6 (13.4) years were included, among whom 30.7% were diagnosed with MASLD. The MASLD group was characterized by older age and elevated body mass index, waist circumference, blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, CRP, and ALT levels while exhibiting lower eGFR compared to the non-MASLD group (all SMD > 0.1). The prevalence of current smoking, alcohol consumption, and engagement in physical exercise was similar between the two groups (Table 1). Both groups presented normal LDL-C distributions, yet right-skewed RC distributions (Fig. 1). Participants with MASLD exhibited elevated TC, TG, and RC levels while maintaining comparable LDL-C and HDL-C levels relative to non-MASLD (Table 1).

LDL-C, RC, and ASCVD by MASLD status

Following a median follow-up period of 13.7 years, 6,869 participants (8.28%) were diagnosed with ASCVD, including 1,829 (7.20%) MASLD and 2,483 (4.32%) non-MASLD. Among individuals with MASLD, the age-adjusted RCS demonstrated a J-shaped association between LDL-C and ASCVD (Fig. 1A, Poverall < 0.001, Pnonlinear < 0.001), whereas a linear relationship was observed between RC and ASCVD (Fig. 1C, Poverall < 0.001, Plinear = 0.041). In contrast, for participants without MASLD, a monotonically increasing non-linear relationship was observed between LDL-C (Fig. 1B), RC (Fig. 1D), and ASCVD.

We then categorized LDL-C levels into four groups (< 1.8, 1.8–2.5, 2.6–3.3, and ≥ 3.4 mmol/L) and RC levels into three groups (< 0.6, 0.6–1.2, and ≥ 1.3 mmol/L) based on the lipid-lowering targets recommended by the Chinese Guidelines for Lipid Management (2023) [15] for LDL-C, a previous publication [9] and RCS results for RC. The associations of these categories with ASCVD were examined concerning MASLD status (Table 2). The MASLD group exhibited a similar proportion of LDL-C categories compared to the non-MASLD group; however, there was a 10% higher prevalence of RC ≥ 1.3 mmol/L (44.1% vs. 32.8%) in the MASLD group under the specific cut-off points.

In both univariate and multivariate analyses, an increased risk of ASCVD was associated with elevated levels of LDL-C and RC levels (all P for trend < 0.001). At equivalent levels of LDL-C or RC, individuals with MASLD exhibited a higher annual average ASCVD incidence rate compared to those without MASLD. In comparison to the lowest LDL-C (1.8 mmol/L) or RC group (< 0.6 mmol/L), the HRs did not reach statistical significance among the groups with LDL-C levels of 1.8–2.5 mmol/L or RC levels of 0.6–1.2 mmol/L within the MASLD group across all models.

Assessment of ASCVD risk in combined LDL-C and RC groups by MASLD status

In alignment with the LDL-C management objectives for the general population [15] and prior research findings [9], LDL-C was stratified at a threshold of 3.4 mmol/L and RC at 0.6 mmol/L for both MASLD and non-MASLD populations (Table 3). It was observed that LDL-C < 3.4 mmol/L and RC ≥ 0.6 mmol/L constituted the largest proportion of participants for both the MASLD (71.2%) and non-MASLD (59.8%) groups. Generally, under equivalent LDL-C and RC levels, individuals with MASLD exhibited a higher 10-year cumulative incidence of ASCVD compared to their non-MASLD counterparts. Compared to non-MASLD individuals with LDL-C < 3.4 mmol/L and RC < 0.6 mmol/L, elevated RC alone (RC ≥ 0.6 mmol/L), elevated LDL-C alone (LDL-C ≥ 3.4 mmol/L) and concurrent elevations in both LDL-C and RC were all correlated with an increased ASCVD risk across all models. Notably, MASLD with LDL-C < 3.4 mmol/L and RC < 0.6 mmol/L also demonstrated an elevated ASCVD risk (model 3: HR: 1.61; 95% CI: 1.43–1.82).

Subsequently, an LDL-C cut-off of 2.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL) was established for the MASLD group (Table 3), in accordance with the LDL-C target for primary ASCVD prevention as per the American Association of Clinical Endocrinology guidelines [17]. MASLD individuals with LDL-C < 2.6 mmol/L and RC < 0.6 mmol/L exhibited a reduced 10-year cumulative ASCVD incidence, yet it remained above 5% (indicating moderate risk) and continued to be significantly associated with increased ASCVD risk (model 3, HR: 1.53; 95% CI: 1.31–1.79).

Combined LDL-C, RC, and ASCVD by MASLD status and different 10-year ASCVD risks

The cut-off value of LDL-C was adjusted based on the China-PAR’s 10-year predicted ASCVD risk and MASLD status: <3.4 mmol/L for the non-MASLD groups with low ASCVD risk and < 2.6 mmol/L for the other groups, following risk-stratified management recommendation [15]. The RC cut-off value of 0.6 mmol/L was still chosen based on the previous publication [9] and the RCS results (Figure S2).

The China-PAR model demonstrated robust predictive capability for both MASLD and non-MASLD participants (Table S1). Among individuals classified as low-risk, elevated RC alone, but not elevated LDL-C alone, was independently associated with increased ASCVD risk in both MASLD and non-MASLD participants (Table S2). Among the participants with moderate to high ASCVD risk, both elevated LDL-C alone and elevated RC alone were linked to ASCVD in non-MASLD subjects; however, these associations were not observed in MASLD subjects across all models. In the moderate-to-high risk MASLD group, a significantly increased ASCVD risk (adjusted HR: 1.61; 95% CI:1.004–2.59) was observed only when both LDL-C and RC were elevated, using thresholds of 1.8 mmol/L and 0.6 mmol/L, respectively (Table S2).

For participants with low ASCVD risk, MASLD individuals with LDL-C < 2.6 mmol/L and RC < 0.6 mmol/L exhibited a higher ASCVD risk compared to non-MASLD individuals with LDL-C < 3.4mmol/L and RC < 0.6 mmol/L (model 3, HR: 1.43; 95% CI: 1.07–1.91). When the LDL-C cut-off value for MASLD was set at 1.8 mmol/L, the MASLD with LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L and RC < 0.6 mmol/L exhibited a similar ASCVD risk to the non-MASLD reference group (model 3, HR: 1.17; 95% CI: 0.55–2.47) (Table 4, Figure S3). Among those with moderate to high risk, MASLD individuals with LDL-C < 2.6 mmol/L and RC < 0.6 mmol/L had a higher risk of ASCVD compared to non-MASLD individuals with the same LDL-C and RC level (model 3, HR: 1.64; 95% CI: 1.33–2.02). When the LDL-C cut-off value for MASLD was set at 1.8 mmol/L, the MASLD with LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L and RC < 0.6 mmol/L exhibited a moderate 10-year cumulative ASCVD incidence (9.04%) and a similar ASCVD risk to non-MASLD with LDL-C < 2.6 mmol/L and RC < 0.6 mmol/L (model 3, HR: 1.15; 95% CI: 0.71–1.86). However, this was not the case for those with LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L alone or RC < 0.6 mmol/L alone.

Discussion

In a comprehensive cohort study conducted in China, the lipid profile distribution between individuals with and without MASLD was compared. The findings indicated that individuals with MASLD exhibited elevated levels of RC while maintaining similar levels of LDL-C compared to their non-MASLD counterparts. An increasing trend was observed in the association of LDL-C and RC with ASCVD in both groups. Notably, under equivalent LDL-C and RC levels, the individuals with MASLD consistently demonstrated a higher incidence of ASCVD compared to those without MASLD. Among participants with a low 10-year ASCVD risk, elevated RC alone but not elevated LDL-C alone was independently associated with increased ASCVD risk in both MASLD and non-MASLD groups. Conversely, in participants with moderate to high ASCVD risk, both elevated LDL-C alone and elevated RC alone were associated with ASCVD in the non-MASLD group, but the associations were not observed in the MASLD. When low-risk MASLD had LDL-C levels below 1.8 mmol/L and RC levels below 0.6 mmol/L, and moderate to high-risk MASLD had LDL-C levels below 1.8 mmol/L and RC below 0.6 mmol/L, their ASCVD risk was comparable to that of the lowest-risk non-MASLD counterparts.

The study observed comparable LDL-C between MASLD and non-MASLD groups, consistent with findings from Targher’s study [25]. However, the dose-response relationship for LDL-C and ASCVD differed between the two groups. Unlike a monotonically increasing correlation between LDL-C level and ASCVD risk in the non-MASLD population, a J-shaped relationship was observed in the MASLD. Several potential explanations may account for this non-linear association. Firstly, advanced MASLD or concomitant illness may impair lipid metabolism [26, 27] and lead to both lower LDL-C and higher ASCVD risk, where there may exist the potential for reverse causality. Secondly, there may exist the “cholesterol paradox” or “risk factor paradox” similar to that observed in other conditions like death. For example, the ChinaHEART cohort revealed a U-shaped association of LDL-C with both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [28], and a nationwide Korean cohort study (2.4 million) found a J-shaped relationship with myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, and ASCVD among participants without medication [29].

The American Association of Clinical Endocrinology guideline recommends a primary prevention LDL-C target of under 2.6 mmol/L for individuals with MASLD [17]. Further examination of the guideline’s reference revealed that the LDL-C goal for MASLD was initially set at 2.6 mmol/L based on the general population [30], which lacks specific research evidence from the MASLD population. A monotonically increasing association between RC and ASCVD was identified in both non-MASLD and MASLD groups, even after multivariable adjustment. Similar findings were reported by large cohort-based studies of general populations, which demonstrated a monotonic increase in CVD risk with rising RC levels, even after adjusting for LDL-C and other traditional risk factors, in both Korea and the United States [8, 9].

Our study further assessed the association of varying LDL-C and RC levels with ASCVD risk based on 10-year ASCVD risk stratification, a topic seldom addressed in previous research. In our study, a central and counterintuitive finding was that elevated LDL-C or elevated RC were significantly associated with ASCVD only in low-risk, but not in moderate-to-high-risk individuals with MASLD. This suggests that the relationship between dyslipidemia and ASCVD in MASLD is complex and may be context-dependent. In individuals with established moderate-to-high-risk MASLD, the risk factors of insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, and associated endothelial dysfunction act synergistically, making it difficult to disentangle the individual effect of LDL-C or RC statistically. This hypothesis is supported by the results that the participants with both elevated LDL-C and elevated RC had a higher risk of ASCVD in the moderate-to-high risk MASLD population.

It was observed that all groups with elevated RC levels were associated with a higher ASCVD risk compared with the non-MASLD with both low LDL-C and low RC, indicating that maintaining only a low LDL-C level is insufficient for ASCVD prevention. The results were corroborated by previous studies in the general population [7–9], including Quispe’s study, which revealed that individuals with high RC (≥ 0.6 mmol/L) had an increased cardiovascular risk, regardless of LDL-C level, underscoring the importance of incorporating RC into lipid management. Given that individuals with MASLD consistently exhibit higher ASCVD incidence than those without MASLD at equivalent LDL-C and RC levels, it is imperative to identify strategies to mitigate ASCVD risk in the MASLD population. Our study found that low-risk MASLD with LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L and RC < 0.6 mmol/L exhibited a similar ASCVD risk level to the non-MASLD population with LDL-C < 3.4 mmol/L and RC < 0.6 mmol/L. For those at intermediate and high risk who require lipid-lowering treatment, an LDL-C level of < 1.8 mmol/L is insufficient. The ASCVD risk of the MASLD with LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L and RC < 0.6 mmol/L was found to be comparable to that of the non-MASLD population with LDL-C < 2.6 mmol/L and RC < 0.6 mmol/L.

Our findings regarding the association between RC and ASCVD risk are of physiological significance. Firstly, RC is defined as the cholesterol content of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TRLs) [7], which are capable of penetrating the intima but are too large to re-enter circulation, resulting in their entrapment within the artery wall [31]. The cholesterol present in TRLs is resistant to hydrolysis and accumulates in the arterial wall, where it is absorbed by macrophages, leading to foam cell formation and atherosclerosis [32]. Secondly, RC may stimulate the production of inflammatory mediators, such as interleukin-8, interleukin-1, CRP, and tumor necrosis factor α [7, 32]. These inflammatory mediators can activate the clotting cascade via prothrombin, thereby increasing the risk of cardiovascular disease [7]. Given the established role of low-grade systemic inflammation in the pathogenesis of ASCVD [33], it is plausible to hypothesize that it may partially contribute to the observed association between RC and ASCVD. Consequently, the elevated RC levels and the associated increased ASCVD risk in the MASLD population warrant further attention. MASLD-induced hepatic dysfunction, characterized by steatosis, insulin resistance, mitochondrial dysfunction, and chronic inflammation, disrupts hepatic lipid homeostasis, leading to reduced clearance of circulating lipids. This contributes to the accumulation of lipids such as RC in circulation, thereby augmenting cardiovascular risk [34]. Furthermore, elevated levels of serum RC can serve as an indicator for assessing the severity of hepatic steatosis in patients with MASLD, emphasizing its potential contribution to impairment of liver metabolic function and progression of ASCVD [35].

Emerging therapeutic approaches, such as angiopoietin-like 3 (ANGPTL3) and apolipoprotein C-III (Apo C-III), offer promising avenues for further reducing ASCVD risk by potentially lowering RC levels [36]. ANGPTL3, a glycoprotein produced by the liver, inhibits lipoprotein lipase (LPL) and endothelial lipase [37]. Similarly, Apo C-III acts as an inhibitor of LPL and presents in plasma, where it plays a role in TG hydrolysis [38]. Both ANGPTL3 and Apo C-III present emerging therapeutic targets for reducing TRLs and atherosclerotic risk in patients who exhibit tolerable responses to lipid-lowering therapy [39]. Activation of LPL has also been demonstrated to effectively mitigate the severity of hepatic steatosis, which may confer additional benefits for metabolic and cardiovascular diseases [40, 41]. However, there are currently no clinical trials investigating these drugs in the MASLD population. Consequently, it remains uncertain whether these drugs are effective in reducing RC, improving hepatic lipid status, and decreasing ASCVD risk in MASLD populations, necessitating further research.

Although our primary interest centered on LDL-C and RC levels, we fully accounted for the multifactorial nature of ASCVD. In our multivariable models, we adjusted for a broad range of established cardiovascular risk factors beyond lipid abnormalities, including lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking, drinking). These adjustments were made to minimize confounding and to better understand the association between LDL-C, RC and ASCVD risk in the MASLD population. Nonetheless, as an observational study, our findings do not imply a causal relationship, nor can they support the conclusion that reducing LDL-C and RC would necessarily lower ASCVD risk for MASLD. Randomized controlled trials are further required to determine whether targeted RC-lowering interventions could provide clinical benefit beyond established lipid-lowering strategies in the MASLD.

The present study acknowledges several limitations. Firstly, rather than employing direct measurement of RC, an indirect calculation method was utilized to estimate RC, a common approach in most epidemiological studies [7–9, 42]. Previous research indicated that estimated RC exhibited a robust correlation with directly measured values [43]. The calculation of RC from a standard lipid profile, without incurring additional costs, also renders it suitable for clinical application and further research. Secondly, as our analysis was restricted to the Kailuan cohort to identify potential cut-off values for LDL-C and RC, the findings may be subject to optimism bias. However, we used a hybrid method to balance statistical evidence with clinical practice and previous literature. Our choice of cut-offs was primarily guided by existing clinical guidelines [2, 15] and previous literature [9], which are based on evidence from multiple sources and multiple populations. The RCS analysis served a secondary, supportive role to visualize the continuous risk relationship and to verify that the clinically relevant thresholds were consistent with inflection points in our data. This hybrid method of cut-off selection indicated the robustness of our results. Thirdly, ultrasonography was employed as a diagnostic tool for MASLD in this extensive community-based cohort, as opposed to liver biopsy, due to the latter’s invasiveness and impracticality. Although ultrasonography exhibits low sensitivity for diagnosing fatty liver disease when liver fat content is below 20% [16], all radiologists responsible for diagnosing fatty liver disease were well-trained, resulting in non-differential misclassification of MASLD and underestimation of HR. Additionally, our study applied the China-PAR model to stratify 10-year ASCVD risk, which may not fully capture the complexity of cardiometabolic risk in the MASLD. Emerging evidence suggests that additional markers, such as the aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index [44], the atherogenic index of plasma [45], abdominal obesity [46], and hyperhomocysteinemia [47], may further improve the discrimination of high-risk individuals. Future research integrating these novel indicators into ASCVD risk stratification could help to more precisely identify MASLD patients at greater risk and thereby support more tailored LDL-C target levels. Last but not least, the study focused on all ASCVD, a composite endpoint encompassing myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, and revascularization therapy, but did not analyze the effects separately due to insufficient outcomes for each endpoint. Previous studies have confirmed that elevated RC is independently associated with a higher risk of myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke in the general population [48–50]. Future research could investigate the association between RC and the component outcomes of ASCVD in the context of MASLD.

Conclusions

Within the MASLD population, a significant increase in ASCVD risk was primarily observed only when both LDL-C and RC were elevated, especially among individuals with moderate-to-high baseline risk. When benchmarked against the non-MASLD, our study suggested that achieving effective risk reduction requires stricter control of both lipids than current guidelines recommend for the general population.
