Dynamic morphological staging of Drosophila pupal development
Authors: Xu Liwen, Chen Mei, Zhang Yumeng, Pang Xuan, Tang Jiaxu, Zhang Chenchen, Geng Junhua, Xie Wei, Gan Guangming
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study provides a comprehensive analysis of the morphological-developmental characteristics of the pupal stage in Drosophila, a model organism for metamorphosis research. We have delineated the critical stages of pupal development, from the late third-instar larvae to adult emergence, through continuous photography and time-lapse imaging. Our findings include the identification of the head-movement phase and head-static phase as distinct transitional states preceding the pupal stage. We document pupal shell dynamics, including color transitions and spiracle development as staging markers, along with internal transformations like larval tracheal degeneration, mouthpart retraction, and the formation of the compound eyes, wings, appendages, and bristles. In conclusion, we have identified several critical periods: P12 serves as a definitive marker for the onset of segmentation during the pupal stage, P40-P50 represents an optimal period for observing the initiation of changes in eye and wing coloration, and P70 marks the beginning of the investigation into the morphological changes of the adult within the Drosophila pupal case. We also found that compared with Canton-S (CS) wild-type flies, dneurexin (dnrx) and dneuroligins (dnlgs) mutants showed some differences in development time and pupal tissue organization. These observations offer a detailed developmental map of the Drosophila pupal stage, which is essential for accurate experimental staging and sampling. This study’s findings not only contribute to the fundamental understanding of insect metamorphosis but also provide a valuable resource for researchers utilizing Drosophila as a model system to study gene expression, development, and behavior.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45291-w.Keywords: Drosophila, Metamorphosis, Pupal Development, Morphological StagingSubject terms: Developmental biology, Evolution, Zoology

Introduction

Metamorphosis encompasses drastic morphological and physiological transformations across an organism’s life cycle1. This process involves different growth stages, accompanied by drastic changes in the organism’s appearance and internal structure, in order to adapt to the demands of a variable ecological environment and specific living habits. Metamorphosis is not uncommon in the biological world; it occurs in insects2, amphibians3, certain fish species4, and some marine invertebrates5. Among them, the existence of metamorphosis in insects is the most widespread6. It is estimated that over 98% of insect species undergo varying degrees of metamorphosis during their developmental cycle7.

Currently, insects are generally classified into three main categories based on their metamorphic development: ametabolous, hemimetabolous, and holometabolous8. Ametabolous insects refer to a group that undergoes only changes in body size during development, without any change in shape, such as Entognatha and Apterygota, which continue to molt throughout their lives9. Hemimetabolous development includes three main stages: the egg, nymph, and adult. During hemimetabolous development, the nymph gradually increases in size through successive molts, with externally visible wing pads that expand across instars until finally molting into the adult stage10. Crucially, this process lacks a pupal phase; instead, adult organs develop directly from nymphal tissues without undergoing the complete metamorphic remodeling seen in the “larva-pupa-adult” transformation of holometabolous species. Grasshoppers11 and cockroaches12 are commonly used animal models for studying hemimetabolous development. In contrast, the metamorphosis of bees13,14, ants15 and butterflies16 begins with a fertilized egg and goes through four stages: the egg, larva, pupa, and adult. The larval stage focuses mainly on feeding and nutrient accumulation. The pupal stage involves extensive and intense changes in cellular organization, as well as the remodeling of body shape and organs, ultimately transforming into the adult form. After developing into an adult, it will complete the functions of reproduction and gene transmission, maintaining survival through activities such as flying and foraging. This type of metamorphosis, characterized by marked differences in morphology and behavior across developmental stages, is known as complete metamorphosis17.

Among all the metamorphic development types of insects, holometabolous development accounts for the largest proportion18. Holometabolous insects, which exhibit the derived trait of complete metamorphosis, are the most species-rich group within Insecta and thus the primary focus of insect developmental research19,20. Within this group, Drosophila has emerged as a key model organism due to its experimental tractability21,22. Currently, research on Drosophila mainly focuses on the larval and adult stages23. In contrast, the pupal stage, which lasts approximately 100 h and constitutes about 40% of the entire life cycle, remains relatively understudied due to the unresolved details of its internal development. Nevertheless, the pupal stage is critically important: nutrients accumulated during larval feeding are mobilized and utilized, while key processes such as imaginal disc differentiation and nervous system remodeling take place24. If key morphological transitions during pupal development can be systematically mapped onto a temporal lineage using stereomicroscopy, the precision of experimental sampling timing would be significantly enhanced, thereby advancing research on Drosophila pupal development.

As a classic research subject among holometabolous insects, Drosophila exhibits significant differences at various developmental stages, similar to other holometabolous insects. Therefore, it is essential to conduct detailed staging when performing in-depth mechanistic studies25. Currently, mainstream experimental protocols define the absolute time after pupation by hours after puparium formation (APF) to represent the staging of pupal development26,27. However, considering the differences in developmental speed, genetic background and microenvironment among individual pupae, it is challenging to ensure that all pupae are at the same developmental stage under absolute time conditions. As development progresses towards the later stages of pupation, the synchronization among individuals decreases28. Sole reliance on temporal staging compromises accuracy and reproducibility, highlighting the need for a continuous morphological map of pupal development. Currently, many visualization imaging techniques have been applied to the study of insect metamorphosis, some of which have also been used in Drosophila29. Considering the advantages and disadvantages of various imaging techniques, due to the semi-transparent nature of Drosophila’s cuticle, it is relatively convenient to observe the overall morphology of organ development during the pupal stage using optical microscopy after moistening the specimen. Correspondingly, there is indeed a substantial amount of research that applies morphological changes during the pupal stage to the study of metamorphic development mechanisms30,31. Classifying the Drosophila life cycle using morphologically defined developmental stages offers a distinct advantage over relying solely on a temporal scale, as it provides more reliable results when determining the developmental stage of different individuals32. However, most experiments observing the pupal stage of Drosophila involve removing the experimental animals from their pupal cases for filming and recording33,34, during which the documentation of the characteristics of the pupal cases may be lost.

Currently, the foundational framework for morphological staging of the Drosophila pupal period remains the detailed system established by Bainbridge & Bownes (1981). This study delineated 24 developmental stages based on a sequence of 51 observable morphological changes, by statistically analyzing the frequency of individuals at each stage within a mixed-age population. In recent years, many studies have continued to use the staging method from this literature35. However, due to its early publication date and the relatively limited imaging technology available at the time, the classification of early pupal stages remains relatively coarse. In fact, during the prepupal stage, the central nervous system synapses of Drosophila have already begun to degenerate36, but earlier literature did not provide a relevant classification for this period. In recent years, some researchers have used scanning electron microscopy to create external morphological atlases of Drosophila37. However, these atlases do not capture the dynamic progression of development and lack detailed morphology of internal organs. Therefore, based on existing research progress, we utilized a high-resolution stereo microscope to observe and record the complete developmental changes of the wild-type Drosophila melanogaster pupal stage through continuous photography and time-lapse imaging, aiming to provide a basis for accurately defining the developmental stages of the pupa and for precise sampling. On this foundation, we further aim to investigate: for mutants known to have specific nervous system developmental defects, whether corresponding observable abnormalities manifest in their external morphological development. In the Drosophila central nervous system, transmembrane proteins encoded by the Neurexin and Neuroligins genes are located at the presynaptic and postsynaptic membranes, respectively. Their interaction is significant for the formation of synaptic connections and maintaining the normal structure and function of synapses38. Current research has clearly shown that the nervous systems of dnrx and dnlgs are inevitably affected during their development39. However, several key questions remain: first, whether there are morphological differences during the pupal stage between mutant and wild-type Drosophila; and second, at which specific pupal stage certain homozygous lethal mutant strains fail to continue normal development. Therefore, we used the method of observing the morphological development of wild-type Drosophila pupae to simultaneously observe the morphological development of the mutant pupae, recording the characteristic morphological features of dnrx and dnlgs during their development, and further linking these features to the poor development of their nervous systems.

In summary, this study aims to establish a more detailed continuous morphological staging atlas for the pupal stage of wild-type Drosophila melanogaster using high-resolution stereomicroscopy and time-lapse imaging. On this basis, it further seeks to investigate whether observable abnormalities occur in the external morphological development during the pupal stage of fruit flies with specific nervous system developmental defects, such as dnrx and dnlgs mutations.

Materials and methods

Fly stocks and rearing conditions

The flies used in this experiment include CS, dnrx27340,41, dnlg2KO7042, dnlg3KO12743 and dnlg4KO1039,44. We placed 30 flies (half females and half males) into a large glass bottle or fly vial containing standard culture medium. The flies were reared at 25 °C and exposed to a 12 h:12 h light-dark cycle. The rearing room was equipped with air conditioning and a humidifier to maintain constant conditions (25.0 ± 0.5 °C, 60% relative humidity).

Sampling and photography methods during the pupal stage

We designed the following experiment utilizing the characteristic of late third-instar larvae stopping feeding and crawling up the tube walls:

We began the experiment by introducing more than 15 adult flies, with an equal ratio of males to females, that had eclosed for 3–7 days into culture tubes, with approximately 20–30 tubes used in total (Fig. 1A). These tubes were cultured under standard conditions for 2 days, after which the adult flies were extracted, and the tubes were cultured for an additional 3–4 days (Fig. 1B). During this period, we observed the presence of stationary white pupae, brown pupae, and larvae on the walls of the tubes (Fig. 1C).

Egg laying for 2 days (A,B). Embryo development (B,C). Appearance of larval stage and pupal stage flies in the tube (C). To systematically track the development, we used a marker to number each culture tube (A1, B1, C1, etc.) and marked the white pupae on the walls of each numbered tube, recording the initial marking time as T1 (Fig. 1D,E,H). The marked tubes were then placed back into the incubator. After 1 h, the tubes were removed, and new white pupae that had appeared on the tube walls were marked with a marker. The marking time was recorded as T2 (T2 = T1 + 1 h), along with the corresponding number of newly marked pupae. This process was repeated every hour, with each marking session kept within a 10-minute window, until the required quantity of samples for the experiment was reached (Fig. 1D–G).

It is important to note that the white pupae (P0) collected at T1 were not used for the experiments. Instead, the culture tubes containing different batches of P0 collected after T1 were placed in an incubator for continued cultivation. Pupae that had been cultivated for 2 h were marked as P2, and similarly, those cultivated for 4 h were marked as P4. The entire pupal period was divided into 24 stages: P0, P2, P4, P6, P8, P10, P12, P14, P16, P20, P24, P30, P35, P40, P45, P50, P55, P60, P65, P70, P75, P80, P85, and P90.

Based on the temporal records, samples were collected from the vial walls using a brush at the corresponding time points and sequentially arranged on microscope slides (with a sample size of n ≥ 8 per stage; Figure S4). Subsequently, the consistent developmental characteristics exhibited by over 50% of the individuals within the same stage were documented.

To obtain continuous developmental videos while avoiding potential developmental perturbations caused by brush stimulation during sample transfer, four to five cleaned and dried microscope slides were inserted into the culture medium of each large glass bottle (Fig. 1I). These were then incubated under standard conditions for two to three days to facilitate larval pupation on both sides of the slides. When third-instar larvae were observed to be immobile on the slides, mechanical stimulation was applied using a soft-bristled brush. If the larvae resumed crawling, they were allowed to continue development. If they remained immobile, they were designated as having entered the white pupal stage (P0). The slides were subsequently removed using forceps, and any larvae, pupae, and food residue on one side were wiped off with a moist tissue. Excess food residue on the observation side was also cleared. The slide was then positioned under a high-resolution stereomicroscope (OLYMPUS SZX7). With the bottom and side light sources adjusted and a magnification of 1.6× selected, continuous video recording was initiated.

The assembly of video recording system (Fig. 1J)

A high-resolution stereomicroscope (OLYMPUS SZX7) was employed for observation. A piece of filter paper was placed on the bottom light source to prevent interference from the background color. A petri dish (90 mm in diameter) was placed on a hollow support, the hollow bottom of which increases air flow and thereby reduces warming of the petri dish. Inside the petri dish, a glass slide was placed on top of a 3 cm high support, and the polypropylene film containing the sample was positioned on the glass slide. The petri dish was then sealed with transparent cling film, and a 1 cm × 1 cm breathable hole was cut directly above the sample to prevent water mist interference during recording and to maintain humidity. Finally, an appropriate amount of deionized water was added into the petri dish; the water level did not cover the glass slide but maintained humidity throughout the recording process.

Results

Definition of the head-movement phase, head-static phase, and white pupa stage

According to our aforementioned method, individuals at different developmental stages can be observed within the same field of view (Fig. 2A–E, Video S1). In addition to the wandering third instar larvae (which we refer to as L3w) (Fig. 1A) and the stationary brown pupae (Fig. 2E), three other types of developmental individuals can also be seen. Two of these exhibit a shortened larval form, which we tentatively name the head-movement phase (Fig. 2B) and the head-static phase (Fig. 2C) based on their external morphology and behavioral characteristics. The third type resembles the brown pupa but has a white exterior, which we call the white pupa (Fig. 2D, Figure S4-1A-A’’).

The whole body adheres to the slide and the mouthparts retract to the inner surface of the body during the head-static phase (C). Head-movement phase In the L3w stage before the head-movement phase, feeding has stopped, and individuals crawl around on the slide and the walls of the tube, aiming to find a suitable position to pupate. During the head-movement phase, the body of the individual shortens, and the cuticle on the surface of the abdomen hardens and adheres to the slide. The mouthparts of the thorax extend and retract, causing the individual to sway back and forth in place (Fig. 2B), with the mouthparts not fully retracted into the body. When illuminated with a light source or moved with a brush, individuals in the head-movement phase will crawl again on the slide, relocating to other positions to pupate.

Head-static phase During this period, the entire cuticle of the individual hardens and shrinks, completely adhering to the slide, with both the head and tail unable to move (Fig. 2C), and the mouthparts fully retracted into the body. When subjected to slight brush stimulation or weak light stimulation, the head and tail still do not move. If water is dripped and the stimulation time or intensity is extended, the head will first exhibit forward and backward movements, while the tail remains adhered to the slide. Intense stimulation can cause some individuals to crawl a short distance.

White pupal stage (P0) The epidermis has completely hardened into a milky white pupal shell; the mouthparts retract into the pupal shell’s rostrum, and the spiracles extend from the body and protrude on both sides of the rostrum (Fig. 2D). There is no extension or crawling response to light or brush stimuli. Since the duration of the white pupal stage lasts for about 1 h45, this paper considers the white pupal stage as the beginning of the pupal stage, marked as P0 (Fig. 3A–C).

Morphological changes of pupal shell

The hallmark characteristic changes of pupal stage included changes in pupal shell morphology and pupal tissue, among which the pupal shell morphology mainly included changes in spiracle and pupal shell color.

Spiracles change

The spiracles were branched, hidden in the body during the head-movement and head-static phases (Fig. 2B,C), and protruded on both sides of the snout during the white pupal stage (P0), with 4–7 branches of about 0.05 mm in length on each side. Thereafter, no significant morphological changes were observed in the spiracles until the pupal stage (Fig. 2D,E). The changes in the location characteristics of the spiracle can provide a reference for distinguishing the white pupal stage from the head-static phase and head-movement phase.

Pupal shell color change

The pupal shell color can change significantly within 2 hours. At the white pupal stage, the pupal shell was milky white (Fig. 3A). As development progressed, the pupal shell color gradually deepened from the posterior spiracle of the tail. As observed by serial photography, the pupal shell color changed to light brown about 1 hour after the fly entered the white pupal stage (Fig. 3C). After 2 hours, the pupal shell color deepens to brown (Fig. 3E, Figure S4-1B-B”). Thereafter, until the pupal body emerged as an adult, the color of the pupal shell remained brown without undergoing significant change (Fig. 3F–L). Pupal shell color changes rapidly in the early stage of pupal development and is an important basis for judging pupal P0 stage.

Morphological changes of pupal tissues

In the pupal stage, the shape of pupal shell changes less, but the pupal tissue morphology in pupal shell changes significantly with the development process. During this period, most larval cells underwent autophagy in the pupal stage, while adult tissues and organs gradually developed or regenerated. The pupal tissues and organs were divided into static and dynamic morphological changes according to the criterion of whether they underwent obvious positional migration during metamorphosis. The former referred to the tissues and organs that changed morphological characteristics at the same position in the pupal body during the whole pupal stage, including tracheae, mouthparts, compound eyes, wings, appendages, and bristles. The latter were the tissues and organs that showed characteristic changes during the whole pupal stage and dynamic position migration, including pupal shape, bubbles, Malpighian tubules, and yellow body.

Degeneration of the dorsal tracheae of the larva

The tracheae are structures for gas exchange between Drosophila and the outside world, and the dorsal tracheae of Drosophila larvae will show obvious morphological changes during the pupal stage. In the larval and white pupal stages (P0), the tracheae were curved in vivo (Fig. 4A,A’,B,B’), but the curved tracheae straightened out after the P10 stage (Fig. 4B,C,B’), and then completed the extinction process in two ways.

There were two patterns of degeneration in the pupal stage: one was curved during pupation (A), followed by rapid bulge and straightening (A–C), then curved again, and straightened again after reaching the maximum curvature (D,E). The straightened tracheae developed into a blur after ten hours, leaving a small segment at each upper and lower end of the tracheae (F-H). The second pattern was a rapid bulge and straightening of the tracheae after pupation (A’,B’), which was not curved during subsequent development until it became blurred after 10 h, leaving a small segment at each end of the tracheae (C–H). Black arrows indicate larval tracheae on both sides of the pupal body. Scale Bar: 0.5 mm.

In the first way, after the tracheae became straight, the tracheae were curved again, and the bending position was random, which might be the upper end, middle end or lower end (the bending position of the sample was the lower end again). The degree of bending was different in different individuals, and this re-bending could be observed more than one hour after entering P0 stage. Maximal bending was observed after approximately 2 h of development (Fig. 4D). The time it took to straighten again varied from individual to individual (Fig. 4E), and after P10 the tracheae became very blurred (Fig. 4F). These blurred tracheae were disturbed by the movement of bubbles within the pupa shell within 10 to 20 min, and most of them disappeared within a short time. Only a small segment of the upper and lower end of the tracheae of the original larvae were preserved (Fig. 4G-H). The preserved upper and lower end tracheae are connected to the spiracle and posterior spiracle exposed outside the pupal shell, respectively, giving a relatively uniform appearance of the pupal shell throughout the pupal stage.

In the second way, after the tracheae became straight, it remained straight until P10, until the blur disappeared (Fig. 4C’–H’). The first type of tracheal degeneration accounted for only 20%, but most pupae showed the second type of tracheal degeneration(Figure S4-1B, Figure S6A). The time of tracheal disappearance can be used as a reference for P10.

Pupal stage mouthparts retraction and degeneration

The black mouthparts of Drosophila larvae begin to retract after entering the head quiescent stage, and will completely retract and degenerate within 12 h after entering the pupal stage. The process can be observed from the dorsal and ventral sides, which can be divided into four stages: slowing down of the mouth parts, shrinking of the mouth parts, immobile of the mouth parts, and abscission of the mouth parts. At L3w, the black mouthpiece at the top of the larva’s head expanded and expanded to feed (Figs. 2A and 5A,A’). After entering the head-movement phase, the mouthparts were still above the snout, continuously stretching back and forth and driving the chest movement, but the expansion of the mouthparts slowed down significantly during this period (Figs. 2B and 5B,B’; Figure S6B). During the head-static phase, the mouthparts were retracted into the larval body (Figs. 2C and 5C,C’). Then, during the development from the head-static phase to the white pupal stage (P0) and 30 minutes into P0 stage, the mouthparts in the pupal body reclused continuously, and the distance from the pupal shell to the rostral end gradually increased (Figs. 5C–E,C’-E’). The mouthparts stopped constricting 30 min after entering the pupal stage and were quiescence after a period of in situ fibrillation (Figs. 5E–F, E’-F’). After stage P10, the black mouth parts separated from the newly formed pupae to complete abscission, and thereafter remained in the same position in the pupal shell until emergence (Figs. 5G–L,G’–L’). The changes of mouthpiece retraction during the pupal stage provided the basis for distinguishing the head-movement phase from the head-static phase, and was also a major feature of P10 in the pupal stage under standard culture conditions.

Compound eye formation and eye color changes

Drosophila melanogaster had compound eyes. After 10 hours of development from P0, with the formation of the head, thorax and abdomen of the pupal body, the position of the compound eyes could be identified through the semi-transparent pupal shell, which was located on both sides of the head (Fig. 6A,A’). The compound eyes are largely formed by stage P4046. At this stage, removal of the pupal shell revealed that the pupal body was enveloped by a transparent membrane known as the pupal cuticle (Figure S5-1G). During this period, there was no pigment deposition in the compound eyes, and the eye color was consistent with the pupal shell (Fig. 6C-D, C’-D’). Pigmentation only began to appear around the contour of the compound eye (Fig. 6A-E, A’-E’). During the next 10 hours, the pigment gradually deepened from the periphery to the middle of the compound eye until the entire compound eye became orange (Fig. 6E-H, E’-H’; Figure S4-2O’; Figure S4-3Q’). At this time, the pupa developed for about 55 hours. During the next 5 hours, the orange compound eye darkened to brownish red (Fig. 6H–J,H’-J’; Figure S4-3R’); After 10 hours of development to P70, the eye color changed from brown to red (Fig. 6J–N,J’–N’; Figure S4-3S’,T’); Then it entered the late pupal stage. Due to the gradual deepening of the pupal shell color, the color of the compound eye observed through the pupal shell also gradually deepened until it became red and black before emergence (Fig. 6N–R,N’-R’; Figure S4-3U-X’’).

Wing formation and color change

At stage P14, the wing contours could already be observed on the pupal body after removal of the pupal shell (Figure S5-1 A-A’’). At this stage, the wings were located under the pupal cuticle, close to the sides of the body, and the wings were transparent. However, they could not be directly visualized through the outer cuticular layer of the pupal shell (Figure S4-1 H-H’’). From P65-P70, the ends of the wings showed color and gradually deepened to gray, while the other parts of the wings showed no color change (Fig. 7E-H). By stage P70, wings could be relatively easily visualized through the pupal case in 83.3% of the individuals (Figure S4-3 T’’, Figure S6C). With the development, the wing color gradually deepened. At 75 h of development, the end of the wing color deepened to black, and the root of the proximal end of the wing became gray (Fig. 7H-K). After that, the color of the wings did not change again until emergence (Fig. 7K-N).

Appendage formation and maturation

Similar to the wings, the newly formed appendages were difficult to observe through the pupal shell. At stage P14, when the pupal shell was removed, the three pairs of appendages were closely juxtaposed from top to bottom on the ventral side of the pupal body (Figure S5-1A’-A’’). The development of appendages was slow, and the landmark morphological changes of legs did not occur until after the P65 stage. Between P65 and P70, legs became brown in color and began to appear segmented (Fig. 8A,B). At P75, the leg color was still brown, but the segments became prominent (Fig. 8C). From P75 to P80, the legs grew black fine hair, and the leg color was black at this time (Fig. 8D, D’). During this period, the sex combs were observed on the first tarsus of the foreleg of the male individual (Fig. 8D), appearing as black patches. After development to P80, the sex of Drosophila could be distinguished by sexual comb. At P90, the legs of males and females developed differently, and the legs of males did not show obvious changes (Fig. 8D-F). However, at this time, the legs of females developed faster than those of males, and the bristles on the legs became hard (Fig. 8D’-F’). If the pupal shell was removed at this time, the females were capable of walking.

Formation and hardening of bristles

Pupal bristles were classified into three categories based on the position of adult bristles: head bristles, chest bristles, and abdominal bristles. The morphological changes of the three types of bristles occurred in the late pupal stage, about P65-P85. Head bristles were first observed between P65 and P70 (Fig. 9A-B). Chest bristles were observed from P70 to P75 (Fig. 9B-C). Abdominal bristles were last observed between P75 and P80 (Fig. 9C-D). All three types of bristles developed and matured within the next 5 h. The bristles were black and hard (Fig. 9E-F).

Morphological changes of pupal bristles (dorsal view). Bristle formation occurs in the late pupal stage, and the head bristles are first formed during P65-P70 (A,B). Secondly, thoracic bristles form between P70 and P75 (B,C), and finally abdominal bristles appear between P75 and P80 (C,D), and all three types of bristles become black and hard by P85 (E), at which point they develop into mature bristles. Pupal head, thorax and abdomen segments and their morphological displacement changes

At P0 stage, the newly formed pupal body was not separated from the pupal shell and retained most of the larval structure (Fig. 10A). After development to P10, the pupal body first moved to the snout as a whole under the action of bubbles at the lower end of the shell (Fig. 10B). Within 10 min, the pupal body moved to the tail to complete the separation of the pupal body from the pupal shell and form the head, thorax and abdomen. At this time, the outline of each part was not obvious (Fig. 10B-C). The whole pupal body gradually moved downward until it was about 1/4 from the upper end of the pupal shell, during which the outline of the head, thorax and abdomen gradually appeared (Fig. 10C-G). One hour before emergence, a pronounced axial elongation of the developing adult body occurred, accompanied by a relative anterior displacement of the head within the pupal shell. The pupal shell was poked and the pupal shell was left behind by the emerging adult (Fig. 10H-N). The separation of the pupal body from the pupal shell to form the head, thorax and abdomen was one of the most significant temporal morphological changes in the pupal stage. At stage P12, segmentation was completed in 77.8% of the Drosophila individuals (Figure S4-1G, Figure S6D). Based on this morphological change, it could be quickly inferred whether the pupal development time was before or after P12.

Appearance and displacement of bubbles in pupae

In Drosophila pupae, bubbles were observed both intra-pupally and extra-pupally, with both populations exhibiting distinct characteristics of expansion and migration. Bubbles in the pupal body appeared earlier and were divided into lateral and central pupal bubbles according to the location of their appearance. Pupal side bubbles appeared below the abdominal unilateral (or left or right) tracheae shortly after the pupal shell color darkened to brown and after P2 development, at which point the bubble diameter was smaller than the spacing of the two tracheae (Fig. 11A). In the subsequent development, the bubbles gradually expanded, and the diameter of P8 bubbles was close to the middle distance between the two sides of the tracheae (Fig. 11B–D). Over the next 2 h, the bubble moved toward the middle and reached its maximum diameter, which was larger than the middle distance between the tracheae on each side (Fig. 11D-F). At P10-P12, the bubbles disappeared (Fig. 11G).

When the pupal central bubble developed to P2, it appeared in the central position of the pupal body. Because the initial bubble had a small diameter and would be blocked by the tissue remnants in the pupal body, only when the pupal shell was punctured, the small bubbles would escape from the pupal body and float on the dissection fluid. Therefore, the central bubbles in the pupa were observed through the pupal shell more slowly than the ventral bubbles appear. There were differences in the time when the central bubbles were observed among different individuals, but during P8 and P10 stages, due to the large size of the central bubbles (Fig. 11D’-E’), large central bubbles could be observed through the pupal shell in all the samples observed. The bubbles in the central and side of the pupal body disappeared about 11 h after development (Fig. 11G’).

Outer pupal bubble formation and displacement

The outer pupal bubble referred to the air bubble between the pupal shell and the pupal body, which formed later. The bubble diameter reached the maximum in the body, and appeared on the day before the pupal body separated from the pupal shell, at which time the development was about 10–12 h. The specific process was as follows (Video S2): after the air bubbles inside the pupa expanded to the maximum, the outer air bubbles appeared in the pupa shell and the pupa tail and expanded and moved rapidly, pushing the pupa body to the head as a whole, so that the pupa tail and the pupa shell were separated (Fig. 12A). Then the outer pupal bubble moved forward and continued to expand from the side (or left or right) of the pupal body, moved upward and broke off at the pupal chest to form two bubbles. One bubble continued to move toward the head and disappeared at the snout, and the other bubble moved downward back to the tail of the pupal shell (Fig. 12C-E), continued to expand forward and move, and divided into two bubbles at the chest again. The two bubbles again flowed toward the rostral and caudal portions of the pupal shell, respectively (Fig. 12E-F). The repeated migration and rupture of bubbles outside the pupal body, and the repeated flow through the head, thorax and abdomen of the pupal body are the necessary conditions for the separation of the pupal shell from the pupal body to form the head, thorax and abdomen.

Appearance and migration of Malpighian tubules and yellow body

Malpighian tubules are excretory organs in Drosophila, which contain two pairs of tubular structures. During metamorphosis, two pairs of Malpighian tubules will show obvious morphological changes and positional migration. Malpighian tubules appeared at the thoracoabdominal junction within 10–20 min after the pupal body separated from the pupal shell to form the head, thorax and abdomen, and two pairs of Malpighian tubules were elongated in morphology (Fig. 13A-B). As development progressed, the elongated Malpighian tubules gradually blurred at the thoracoabdominal junction and developed to P13, when the Malpighian tubules were not visible through the pupal shell. According to the literature, the Malpighian tubules were obscured by internal tissues at this time and entered the white Malpighian tubule stage (Fig. 13B–D), which lasted for 7 to 8 h (Fig. 13D-F). In some of the individuals observed during this period, the occlusion of the Malpighian tubule was incomplete and still indistinct. After P20, the occluded Malpighian tubules gradually appeared from the abdomen (Fig. 13G). Over the next 5–10 h, two pairs of Malpighian tubule will move either symmetrically down or asymmetrically toward the tail in different individuals. Symmetrically down meant that in some individuals two pairs of Malpighian tubules moved down a certain distance at the same time and stopped, while asymmetrically down meant that in some individuals one pair of Malpighian tubule moved down first and then the other pair moved down. The two pairs of Malpighian tubules were placed one up and the other down on the abdomen, but the two pairs of Malpighian tubules eventually showed symmetry (Fig. 13M) in the late stage of development. Malpighian tubules appeared again after P20, becoming shorter and thicker in morphology with positional shift, and gradually becoming darker and more obvious in color (Fig. 13H-M). Malpighian tubules disappeared from the abdomen about ten hours before emergence (Fig. 13N).

The appearance time of the yellow body structure varied greatly among different individuals. The yellow body structure was first observed in the individuals during the period of P24 to P30, located above Malpighian tubules, at the thoracoabdominal junction (Fig. 14A,A’). It was observed in individuals between P45 and P50 at the latest, at the middle and upper ends of Malpighian tubules, ventrally (Fig. 14B,B’). Differences in morphological changes between mutant and wild-type fruit flies during the pupal stage

We selected four mutant fruit flies and the CS wild-type flies, all at the P0 stage, and observed their pupal development under a stereomicroscope. We found differences in developmental time and pupal tissue morphology between the mutants and the wild type.

Differences between dnrx273 and CS during pupal development

The dnrx273 (the genotype is homozygous.) exhibited a long-body phenotype and only had larval and pupal stages, with no adult form. When we synchronously observed the development of the dnrx273 and the CS, both exhibited similar pupal tissue changes such as mouthpart retraction and tracheal bending. At the P8 stage, the internal bubbles in both expanded to their maximum size, then gradually moved to the center, and outer bubbles began to form. In the wild-type Drosophila, outer bubbles flowed between the rostrum and the pupal case tail, rapidly completing the separation of the pupal case from the pupal body and the formation of the head, thorax, and abdomen. However, in the dnrx273, the head, thorax, and abdomen failed to form, and the outer bubbles expanded from the rostrum to the tail, with the pupal contents continuously leaking out (Video S3). As the CS continued to develop, the dnrx273 pupae showed no further changes. Therefore, we concluded that the dnrx273 was lethal during the pupal stage, with death occurring approximately between P8 and P12.

Differences between dnlg2KO70 and CS during pupal development

Compared to the CS, the dnlg2KO70 exhibited similar timing in head, thorax, and abdomen segmentation, and in some cases, the time from P0 to segmentation was even shorter. However, the developmental timing of various tissues within the pupa was inconsistent. The appearance of Malpighian tubules in dnlg2KO70 was delayed, making them more difficult to observe from outside the pupal case, and the yellow body within the tubules was also hard to detect. Additionally, the timing of compound eye and eye color changes was prolonged in dnlg2KO70. As the three types of bristles appeared sequentially, the eye color gradually deepened to a vermilion hue, whereas the wild-type Drosophila exhibited a deep red color (Video S4).

Differences between dnlg3KO127 and CS during pupal development

In the early pupal stage, the slender Malpighian tubules were difficult to observe in dnlg3KO127. However, after P20, the tubules and yellow body became clearly visible. Along the Z-axis, the yellow body in dnlg3KO127 was positioned closer to the dorsal side compared to the Malpighian tubules. As development progressed, the yellow body moved from the side of the tubules near the rostrum to the side near the tail, with vigorous shaking (Video S5).

Differences between dnlg4KO10 and CS during pupal development

Compared to the CS, the dnlg4KO10 developed more slowly, with a longer developmental time. Its Malpighian tubules appeared later than those in the CS, and its compound eye color also differed. When observed through the pupal shell, the eye color changed slowly and eventually exhibited an orange-red hue (Video S6).

Discussion

Definition of head-movement phase and head-static phase

It is known that the insect pupal stage undergoes drastic morphological degeneration, and thus this transitional period may also have a corresponding continuity of morphological degeneration. The pupal stage of insects can be divided into prepupal stage, cryptocephalous pupal stage, and phanerocephalic pupal stage, such as Eristalinus aeneus and Eristalis tenax47. The prepupal stage refers to the stage when the final instar larvae of whole insects shorten their bodies, stop feeding, search for a suitable place, and enter the stage of inactivity before pupation. In Drosophila, the true pupal stage begins after the epidermal cells of the pupa separate from the larval cuticle (puparium). The initial phase of the pupal stage, which morphologically resembles the prepupa, is termed the “cryptocephalic pupa”. Continued development leads to the complete eversion of the head, legs, and wings, at which point the pupa is referred to as the “phanerocephalic pupa”.28.

In fact, the period from the late third-instar larval stage to the prepupal stage in Drosophila can be further subdivided into the head-movement phase and the head-static phase, as proposed in Sect. 3.1. Based on our observations, under normal circumstances, the duration from the head-movement phase to P0 phase is generally 1–2 h. The head-static phase is relatively brief, during which the retraction of mouthparts and the extension of spiracles usually occur simultaneously. However, both brushing and light exposure can stimulate individuals in the head-static phase to resume crawling. If continuous brushing stimulation is applied, the transition time between these two phases will be prolonged. We have experimental evidence demonstrating significant phenotypic differences in the nervous and muscular systems across these three stages (the head-movement phase, head-static phase, and P0 stage), which will be detailed in a separate publication. This finding also explains the necessity of implementing refined morphological staging.

Definition and collection of P0 stage (high synchronization, 1 h duration)

The pupal shell was milky white and the epidermis was completely indurated; the spiracles extended from the body surface and protruded on both sides of the snout; there was no stretching and peristalsis of the body when stimulated (such as light stimulation and brush stimulation). In this article, we also define white pupae as pupa 0 h, which is P0. The characteristics of P0 that we observed are consistent with, and supplemented by, multiple literatures that have identified the white pupae with eversion of spiracles and shortening of the body48,49. The white pupa is generally considered to be the earliest stage of pupal development50. At this time, it has already formed a hardened cuticle, but the internal structure of pupa is similar to that of larva and has not yet begun to undergo drastic changes. Therefore, we also determined P0 as the starting point of pupation, and the determination of the starting point of pupation is crucial for the fine division of pupal stage.

The experiment was divided into time-point sampling observation and continuous video recording. The experimental methods used in each observation mode are innovative.

For time-point sampling observations, we cultured pupal-stage individuals in Drosophila observation tubes at consistent time intervals, designating the 2-hour timepoint as P2. This protocol was implemented because transfer operations during the P0 stage might affect pupal ontogeny. Starting from P0, we established 24 developmental stages (P0, P2, P4, P6, P8, P10, P12, P14, P16, P20, P24, P30, P35, P40, P45, P50, P55, P60, P65, P70, P75, P80, P85, P90) for comparative analysis. At each stage, we systematically recorded the emergence timing and morphological characteristics of the tracheae, mouthparts, compound eyes, wings, appendages, bristles, pupal body structures, bubbles, Malpighian tubules, and yellow bodies. We found that the majority of these morphological features exhibited high consistency within the same stage. For the minority of features that showed variation, statistical analysis was performed (Figure S6), and the characteristics shared by more than half of the individuals were defined as the common features for that specific stage. This finding underscores the importance of not only collecting samples based on developmental time but also focusing on the typical morphology representative of that period, while excluding individuals with extreme deviations. This approach thereby enhances the accuracy and reliability of sampling.

For continuous video recording, we took advantage of the characteristic that the third instar larvae will climb up the tube wall in the late stage to find a suitable position for puparium formation. A pathological slide was inserted into the medium, so that the larvae could climb up the support by themselves, which was conducive to the subsequent removal of the support and the observation of the individual larvae distributed on the support through the bottom light source. This method has not been reported in the literature before. Compared with directly selecting white pupae from the tube wall28, the intelligent use of support reduces the physical stimulation of the brush to the experimental animals and the possibility of contamination of the culture tube. Furthermore, a high degree of concordance was observed between these two observation modalities, with no contradictions or omissions identified in key morphological information.

Different developmental characteristics of organs are helpful for the study of the organs themselves and even the study of the nervous system. We take the post-pupal development time as a certain reference to divide the morphological-developmental characteristics at different time points, and on the premise of basically referring to the post-pupal development time, we provide morphological evidence to support it, which can effectively improve the efficiency and accuracy of the experiment.

The pupal shell of Drosophila serves as a protective and developmental environment during its life cycle. During the white pupal stage, the pupal shell gradually hardened and became milky white, and gradually deepened after 1 h until it became tan at the late pupal stage. The gas exchange between flies and the outside world is done by the respiratory system. During the development from the silent stage to the white pupa stage, branched spiracles developed on both sides of the snout, one end extending from the body surface, and the other end connected with the internal tracheae. As the development progressed, the tracheae began to become blurred at about P10. During the next 30 min to 1 h, the connection between the tracheae and the breathing hole is gradually disconnected and the tracheae are eliminated. In the larval stage, fruit flies mainly feed on rotten fruits and other organic matter, and the mouthparts are mainly masticatory. After the head quiescence stage, the black mouthparts of the larva begin to retract. After experiencing four stages of slowing down, retraction, immobile and abscission, the degeneration of the larval mouthparts is completed in P10.

The three-segment outline of head, thorax and abdomen of Drosophila adult was clearly formed after P12. After that, many structures also underwent dramatic changes in the interior of Drosophila: Malpighian tubules for excretion appeared at the thoracoabdominal junction within 10–20 min after the adult outline was formed, underwent white Malpighian tubules and yellow Malpighian tubules, and disappeared from the abdomen about ten hours before eclosion. During this period, the yellow body, which supplies nutrients to the pupae, can be observed above the Malpighian tubule, but its appearance and migration vary greatly among individuals. It will become blurred along with Malpighian tubule within ten hours before emergence and eventually disappear from the abdomen. From P14, the contour of adult tissues has formed, and the pupal cuticle covered the tissue. The two wings were located under the pupal cuticle and were transparent in color. The three pairs of appendages were closely juxtaposed from top to bottom on the ventral side of the pupa. Both compound eyes and wings become progressively darker as development progresses. After P65, the legs turned brown and began to segment. After P80, the sex of flies could be distinguished by whether the sex combs were observed on the first tarsus of the foreleg, and bristles began to form on the head, chest and abdomen. After P90, the female flies were able to walk after removing the pupal shell.

Thanks to these organs and structures, Drosophila can adapt to various environments and complete various stages of its life cycle. Our demonstration of the pupal stage enables researchers to use this ideal model organism to explore biological questions such as gene expression, development and behavior.

Based on the published literature, research on Drosophila pupation is particularly abundant for three key developmental windows: stages P8-P1251,52, P40-P5027,53 and P7026. In order to make the selection of stages more accurate in the future research, the typical developmental characteristics of these stages are briefly described as follows:

P8-P12: pupal segmentation is the most significant change in this period, and the overall morphology of the larval stage is gradually divided into head, thorax and abdomen. Whether segmentation is visible can serve as a marker to determine the P12 stage. The bubbles in pupae developed gradually from P8 to P10 to the largest volume. At P8, the bubble diameter was slightly smaller than the distance between the two sides of the tracheae. At P10, the bubble moved to the middle and reached the largest diameter, which was larger than the middle distance between the two sides of the tracheae. The outer pupal air bubbles began to appear at P10 to make up for the reduction in pupal shell volume after segmentation. This stage corresponds to stage P4(ii) in the 1981 article28, but it occurs at a relatively later time point.

P40-P50: In this period, for non-white-eyed flies, eye color will be a good characteristic observation point. At P40, adult tissues such as compound eyes have been formed, the compound eyes are covered with a transparent film, and transparent wings have appeared. From P45, there is pigmentation around the compound eyes. This stage corresponds to stage P7 in the 1981 article28, and the conclusions are relatively consistent.

P70: At this point, the form of the adult in the pupal shell of the Drosophila becomes more pronounced. The color of the compound eyes had deepened to red, and the ends of the bilateral wings were gray. The legs were brown in color and already segmented. Head bristles can already be observed, and chest bristles are beginning to appear. In fact, from stage P68 onward, the color of the wings can already be observed through the pupal case. By stage P70, the wing coloration has become a common characteristic of this stage. This stage corresponds to stage P11(i) as described by Bainbridge and Bownes28, although the time point specified in the article is again relatively delayed.

To facilitate a better comparison with the staging system of Bainbridge and Bownes, and to promote greater consistency in Drosophila research moving forward, we performed a cross-comparison of the two staging systems (Table S1). We found that stages L1 and L2 correspond to what we have termed HMP and HSP; however, due to the relatively coarse nature of the feature classification in the earlier system, some specific characteristics are misaligned, preventing a more detailed one-to-one correspondence. Stages P1 through P10 generally align with the staging system proposed in this paper. For stages P11(i) through P15(ii), the Bainbridge and Bownes system defines stages based on distinct morphological features, whereas our system follows a chronological timeline, making a stage-by-stage correspondence unfeasible.

We identified that the core difference between the two systems lies in their underlying logic: the Bainbridge and Bownes system is event-driven, defining stages based on the “clear completion points” of morphological events. In contrast, our study employs a time-driven “consecutive hourly stage” system (P2, P4, P6…). Consequently, the same morphological event (e.g., wing pigmentation, bristle emergence) is assigned different stage numbers between the two systems. Our system typically captures an earlier onset time for these events, facilitating earlier and more refined analysis of the developmental process. Furthermore, our staging framework offers greater continuity, making it better suited for precise, hourly experimental sampling. It also provides more detailed descriptions of early developmental phases (e.g., head movement phase and head static phase) and the dynamics of internal structures (e.g., bubbles, tracheae, and Malpighian tubules).

Morphological clues from mutant pupal development

During the concurrent observation of morphological development in mutant and wild-type Drosophila pupae, we made several notable discoveries. In 1996, researchers had already identified that dnrx273 exhibited homozygous lethality, although the underlying cause of death was not reported54. Consequently, in the current literature, only Drosophila larvae have been used as subjects for studying the homozygous dnrx27340. In our study, we observed that the homozygous lethality of dnrx273 occurred approximately between pupal stages P8 and P12. During this period, wild-type fruit flies exhibit characteristic morphological changes, including the gradual disappearance of internal bubbles, separation of the pupal case from the pupal body, and segmentation of the head, thorax, and abdomen. Based on these observations and the functional background of Neurexin — which is known to play critical roles in synapse formation, neuronal adhesion, and nervous system development55, we will next refine the lethal time window to an hourly resolution and conduct anatomical analyses of samples from corresponding developmental stages. Following dissection, priority will be given to examining morphological and structural changes in the central nervous system. Given that insect metamorphosis is primarily regulated by the ecdysone signaling pathway, we will further investigate the regulatory relationships between nrx gene expression and the ecdysone pathway, as well as the apoptosis pathway.

Furthermore, we observed that dnlgs mutants generally exhibited a prolonged overall developmental duration. This was most notably characterized by delayed pigmentation changes and reduced pigment deposition in the compound eyes. Based on the findings of this study and previous studies demonstrating that Neuroligin mutations typically lead to synaptic dysfunction56,57, we hypothesize that Neuroligin mutations may disrupt the overall neural coordination of developmental timing. For example, the release of or feedback signaling for hormones that regulate metamorphosis, such as ecdysone, could become dysregulated due to impaired neural circuitry function, thereby slowing the overall developmental pace. Concurrently, mutations in Neuroligin, a membrane protein, may interfere with broader intracellular membrane trafficking networks, indirectly affecting pigment synthesis and transport, which could result in the observed lighter eye color.

Conclusion

This study established a dynamic morphological staging atlas of Drosophila pupal development based on high-resolution continuous imaging (Figure S1-S3, Video S7). Compared to the classical system, it enables finer subdivision of early events such as the head-movement and head-static phases, and precisely defines key developmental milestones including P8 - P12, P40 - P50, and P70. With higher temporal resolution and morphological sensitivity, this staging system not only provides accurate sampling guidance for developmental biology research but also offers a morphological basis for analyzing synaptic protein mutants. It serves as a reliable morphological reference framework for investigating Drosophila pupal development and related gene functions.
