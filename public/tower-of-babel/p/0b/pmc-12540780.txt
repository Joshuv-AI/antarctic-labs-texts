Evaluation of biomass and vegetative characteristics of mesquite (Prosopis juliflora) afforestation in arid area of Iran
Authors: Jaafar Hosseinzadeh, Mehdi Heydari, Ahmad Ehsani, Masoud Bazgir, Daniel C. Dey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this research, three afforested stands of mesquite at the ages 15, 27, and 34 years were selected in a dry climate region in Iran. In each of the stands, two density classes (180 and 250 trees per ha) were located and 4 square sample plots (40 × 40 m) were randomly selected. After measuring the tree height, crown height, collar diameter and crown area, aboveground biomass was calculated using experimental and semi-empirical allometric equations. The soil quality index was determined at two depths based on the measured physical, chemical and biological characteristics of the soil samples. The results showed that stand age, stand density and their interaction significantly affected stem number, crown height growth and soil quality index. The average number of stems was the highest in the 15-year-old stand and the lowest in the 34-year-old stand. Height, collar diameter, basal area growth, biomass, and competition between trees were significantly affected by stand age and its interaction with stand density (p < 0.01). The average annual growth of tree collar diameter and height with age was lowest (4.1 mm per year and 17.7 cm per year, respectively) in the 34-year-old stand, compared to average annual growth rates of 7.2 mm and 31 cm for collar diameter and height, respectively, in the 15-year-old stand. In general, high density mesquite afforestation plantings in this dry and desert area improves soil quality and increase vegetative and productivity characteristics of mesquite, especially as stands age.

Introduction

Today, afforestation is considered a valuable approach for increasing carbon sequestration by forests, mitigating the effects of climate change and improving other ecosystem services. For these reasons, the area of forest plantations around the world has increased by more than 105 million hectares between 1990 and 20151. Forest plantations play a role in providing quality, reliable water to communities, regulating the hydrological processes, protecting the soil, controlling erosion and improving the landscape2. Estimating the biomass of trees and forest plantations is important to evaluate productivity and nutrient cycling in these ecosystems3, their role and contribution in the global carbon cycle4, their delivery of ecosystem services5 and the benefits they provide to local residents4.

The goals of afforestation, according to the existing conditions in an area, may include: stabilization of sand and desertification, wood production, improvement of soil conditions and characteristics, prevention of soil erosion, protection or creation of wildlife habitat, supply of fuel and animal feed and creation of windbreaks in soil conservation6–8. To choose suitable tree and shrub species for establishing forests as plantations in dry areas, it is necessary to know the local site conditions, consider socio-economic factors and be able to predict future growth and effects on soil resources. Vegetation, whether coniferous or broadleaf trees and shrubs, or grass, affects the surrounding environment, and these effects are different depending on the type of vegetation, its age and management practices9–11. Attempting to establish vegetation on loose sands and sand dunes in dry areas causes changes in the physical and chemical properties of the soil, including increases in organic matter and soil nutrients (e.g., phosphorus, nitrogen and potassium) and the percentage of clay and silt, all of which create a better environment for the activity of microorganisms, thus accelerating soil development12,13. Of course, the degree of these improvements in the soil depends on various factors including the species used in afforestation, and the density and age of the plantation14. Suitable species and optimal plantation density still need to be evaluated in different regions.

Afforestation is the planting of trees to reestablish forests in areas where the tree cover was lost. The soil is often poor in these areas and this challenges plant survival and growth15. In such a situation, one of the most important stages of forest planting in desert areas is the selection of the appropriate primary species. This choice is particularly important, because it affects the climate and soil characteristics of the region and affects the lives of the residents of the neighboring desert areas. Although species selection is the most important and fundamental measure for changing the landscape and future of a region16–18, However, beyond species choice, the success of restoration projects also hinges on appropriate planting methods and management strategies, including spacing, thinning, and density regulation, which influence both structural complexity and functional resilience19. These management interventions directly affect early survival, growth performance, soil interactions, and ultimately the ecological stability of the stand.

In the first World Conference on Desertification (UNCCD, 1977) in Nairobi, Kenya, the phenomenon of desertification was introduced as the main cause of destruction of natural ecosystems through reduction of biological production and soil degradation. These negative impacts can be mitigated by restoring vegetative cover through afforestation20. Protection of existing and recovery of former forests and pastures are important for sustainable development of any country experiencing desertification and other ecosystem degrading phenomenon. In arid and semi-arid regions, protecting, revitalizing, expanding and strengthening vegetation is vital to ecosystem health, productivity and sustainability, and to the well-being of people who live in these regions21,22.

Establishing permanent vegetation through afforestation protects soil and increases biodiversity and increases soil carbon and nitrogen storage23. Thus, afforestation is considered a primary method for restoring degraded lands and an important first step in the long-term process of improving ecosystem health, productivity and resilience24,25. In particular, afforestation in desert ecosystems is a key land management approach to combat desertification and ecosystem degradation20–22.

Stand age plays a pivotal role in shaping ecosystem processes, particularly through its influence on soil development and vegetation dynamics. As forest stands mature, increased litterfall leads to the accumulation of soil organic matter, fostering microbial activity and enhancing nutrient cycling and soil structure26,27. Structural changes in canopy cover and root systems over time also affect soil pH, moisture regimes, and biogeochemical fluxes. Concurrently, vegetation composition shifts due to successional processes, often resulting in greater species diversity and functional differentiation in intermediate-aged stands28. These transformations reflect a transition from rapid resource acquisition in early stages to more conservative strategies in older stands, which together regulate both aboveground productivity and belowground ecosystem functions.

Stand density is a critical structural variable that governs forest microclimates and mediates key ecological processes. High density stands generally exhibit greater canopy closure, reducing solar radiation reaching the forest floor and buffering diurnal fluctuations in soil temperature. These conditions, combined with lower evapotranspiration rates, contribute to higher soil moisture retention—especially valuable in dry environments29. Such microclimatic modifications can promote the establishment and survival of understory vegetation. However, increased density can also intensify intraspecific and interspecific competition for essential resources such as light, water, and nutrients, which may suppress individual plant growth. Hence, stand density regulates the balance between facilitation and competition, shaping understory diversity, soil nutrient dynamics, and overall forest resilience and productivity.

Today, assessing the success rate of growth and effectiveness on the one hand and deciding whether to sustain or replace pioneer afforestation in the other hand, in order to accelerate the improvement of the ecosystem in arid and semi-arid regions of the world are considered important issues in the management of these primary forests30. Quantitative assessment of biomass production and carbon storage in forests established by afforestation is one of the important measures used to evaluate success and effectiveness in ecosystem change. Accurate estimation of tree biomass and carbon storage requires cutting and harvesting a sufficient number of trees and weighing them, which is destructive and costly31. Alternatively, allometric equations can be used as a non-destructive and low-cost method to evaluate aboveground biomass of trees and stands at regional, national, continental and global levels32–34. A review of studies conducted to estimate biomass with different experimental, semi-empirical and theoretical methods showed that theory-based methods do not provide a better estimate of biomass in some species, including mesquite trees35. In this research, we used the modified semi-experimental method by West36 and Chojnacky’s experimental method37 to estimate mesquite biomass based on collar diameter.

Mesquite (Prosopis juliflora) is an important species that has been used in many afforestation projects around the world, including in the desert areas of Iran38–40. However, our knowledge about the success of establishment and growth of this species and its functions in different ages and climatic regions in relation to planting techniques, including different planting densities, is still incomplete.

Accurate estimation of biomass is fundamental for assessing carbon storage, evaluating renewable energy potential, and understanding ecosystem productivity, particularly under the pressures of climate change. In arid and semi-arid regions, Prosopis juliflora has received growing attention due to its ability to tolerate drought, fix atmospheric nitrogen, and contribute to soil fertility. These traits make it especially useful in afforestation efforts aimed at restoring degraded lands. Studies such as Navar et al.41 have shown that regional allometric models significantly improve the accuracy of aboveground biomass (AGB) estimations for mesquite species. The development of “fertility islands” beneath P. juliflora canopies (characterized by increased organic carbon, nitrogen, and microbial activity) has been documented by Archer et al.42, highlighting its role in improving soil quality. Furthermore, field evidence from southern India43 suggests that dense stands of this species not only enhance carbon sequestration but also effectively reduce erosion compared to native vegetation. These findings underscore the importance of considering both stand structure and soil depth when studying biomass-soil interactions in dryland ecosystems.

Despite these ecological benefits, knowledge gaps persist regarding how stand-structural variables (such as age and density) interact to influence both aboveground and belowground biomass accumulation over time under arid conditions. Younger mesquite stands often exhibit rapid biomass accumulation per hectare, thanks to higher density and growth rates, whereas older or denser stands may experience competitive stress, slow per-tree biomass gains and altering allocation patterns. Quantifying these dynamics across developmental stages is crucial for optimizing afforestation strategies and ecosystem restoration in dryland regions.

A clearer understanding of biomass accumulation patterns under different stand ages and densities is essential for optimizing Prosopis juliflora afforestation and agroforestry strategies in dryland ecosystems. These insights help guide decisions on site preparation, spacing, and management to maximize carbon sequestration and soil recovery. Moreover, the application of species-specific, field-derived allometric models enables more accurate and cost-effective monitoring of aboveground biomass over time, particularly where destructive sampling is impractical. Chave et al.44 emphasized the importance of integrating tree-specific traits and environmental variables into biomass models to improve accuracy across ecosystems, while Lu45 highlighted the complementary role of remote sensing techniques in monitoring biomass dynamics over larger spatial and temporal scales.

Recent advancements in biomass estimation, such as LiDAR and machine learning models, have improved accuracy, but semi-empirical methods like those of West et al.36 remain vital for field studies in remote areas. This study innovates by (1) integrating soil depth-specific quality indices with biomass metrics, and (2) challenging the paradigm that high-density plantations uniformly enhance biomass in arid zones, using age-density interactions as a novel lens. No comprehensive research has been done in the desert areas of Iran regarding changes in vegetative characteristics in stands and soil quality due to stand density and age of plantation. This research seeks to answer the following hypotheses:

1) Planting age has a greater effect on the improvement of vegetative characteristics than planting density.

2) Soil quality increases with the increase in the age of afforestation regardless of initial planting density.

3) Increasing age of mesquite afforestation is associated with continued annual biomass growth.

Maximum rainfall occurs in the months of January and February. There is a 7-month dry period from the middle of April to the end of November in the region based on the Ambro thermic curve. Generally, this area consists of hot, dry desert plains with a sparse cover of annual herbaceous plants and some perennial bushes.

Experimental design and sampling

Prosopis juliflora (family Fabaceae), a deep-rooted, spiny-branched plant native to Central and South America, was selected by the government for its heat and drought tolerance, nitrogen-fixing capacity, and suitability for sand dune stabilization, making it valuable in dryland afforestation.

Afforestation with mesquite in the Einkhosh region of Dehloran over many decades has provided an opportunity to assess plantings that span a diversity of ages. We selected three afforestation stands of different ages (15, 27 and 34 years old) for this research. In each of the stands, two density classes were identified: thin (average 180 trees per hectare) and dense (average 250 trees per hectare). The area was treeless before afforestation, but some of the planted seedlings were lost due to natural factors (such as flooding) and human factors (livestock grazing). Two square plots (40 × 40 m) were located randomly in each stand. In each of the plots, the characteristics of the trees including the total height and the starting height of the crown (using Sunto clinometer to the nearest cm), collar diameter (at the ground level) and crown dimensions (using tape measure with cm accuracy) were measured. Annual diameter growth, height growth and crown growth were determined by dividing the current total measure of the characteristic by the age of the planting. The age of stands was confirmed based on their historical planting records. Basal area (BA, in cm2) was calculated as BA = π × (Db/2)2, where Db is the collar diameter at ground level. Aboveground biomass (AGB, in kg) was estimated using two allometric equations: a modified semi-empirical model36 and an empirical model37, both based on collar diameter. All variable names and units used in statistical tables are defined accordingly. Then, the average annual growth of collar diameter, height, crown area and aboveground biomass (by West and Chojnacky methods) were calculated according to Chojnacky et al.37.

In each of the areas, 4 random soil samples were taken from two depths of 0–15 and 15–30 cm (a control area was also sampled next to each site, 20 samples in total) and their soil characteristics including: chemical properties (pH, EC, percentage of organic carbon, percentage of total nitrogen and available phosphorus), physical properties (sand, silt, clay, saturated moisture and bulk density) and biological properties (basal respiration) were evaluated by standard methods in the laboratory (see47). A part of each sample was kept at 4 °C to check soil respiration. The total data method (Rahmanipour et al., 2014) was used to calculate the soil quality index.

Statistical analysis

The mean, standard deviation and coefficient of variation were used to summarize the data and validate model assumptions. We analyzed the distribution of all key dependent variables using the Shapiro–Wilk test in SPSS. The results showed that the majority of variables conformed to the assumption of normality (p > 0.05). Furthermore, diagnostic plots (histograms, Q-Q plots, and residual analyses) confirmed that GLM model assumptions, including normality and homoscedasticity of residuals, were adequately met. GLM analysis was used to test for significance in the effect of stand density and age, and their interaction on the response of vegetative characteristics. Duncan’s mean comparison test was used to compare differences among the means of the vegetative variables measured in the different afforested stands. Although climate data (e.g., rainfall and temperature) are reported to characterize the site, they were not included as variables in statistical models because all plots were located within a homogeneous climatic zone. Instead, stand age and stand density were treated as the primary explanatory variables influencing biomass and soil properties, and their effects were analyzed using GLM.

A two-way general linear model (GLM) was employed to analyze the effects of stand age (three levels) and stand density (two levels) as fixed factors. This approach enabled the separation of the main effects of each factor as well as their interaction, allowing for a clearer understanding of how each variable contributes to variations in vegetative and soil characteristics. All statistical analyses, for the dependent variables (e.g., height, collar diameter, biomass, soil quality index), and the derived growth parameters, were performed using IBM SPSS ver. 22 and all graphs were drawn using Excel 2013 software.

Discussion

Vegetative characteristics

The high number of multi-stems in early ages can be attributed to the presence of livestock in afforestation areas and the activation of lateral buds due to grazing and cutting the terminal bud of seedlings48. While grazing pressure is most pronounced in spring and autumn, and mostly by local goats and sheep, exists in all stands, field observation and historical management records suggest that the youngest stands experienced more active grazing, which may have led to increased stem number due to repeated browsing. The change and decrease in the number of tree stems in the following years has disrupted the process of changes in the average basal area and the average aboveground biomass of the stands, so that the biomass in the 27-year-old stand is more than that of the younger and older stands. The average aboveground biomass of 15-year-old and 27-year-old mesquite trees in this study was 98 and 139 kg per tree, respectively, which is higher than the 58 and 111 kg presented by James Ansley et al.49 for Prosopis glandulosa. Stand age is also an important driver for biomass and productivity50,51. Stand age can affect biomass and productivity through increasing tree size52–55. In our study, the highest biomass was in the intermediate-aged stand (27 years), and declined slightly at 34 years. This nonlinear trend may reflect stem mortality, resource limitation, or shift from growth to maintenance in older trees.

Higher stand density increases soil carbon storage and wood production due to more complete crown coverage and more efficient light use56,57. Changes in trunk diameter can increase or decrease productivity due to changes in light distribution between individual trees55,58,59.

In arid and semi-arid regions, high stand density can reduce evapotranspiration losses, increase shading, and stabilize soil microclimate, all of which may enhance plant water-use efficiency and early-stage growth. This facilitative effect is more pronounced in young to mid-aged stands where competition is not yet dominant. Dense canopies often buffer extreme temperature fluctuations and reduce direct solar radiation at the soil surface. This helps retain moisture and supports higher photosynthetic activity and biomass accumulation at the stand level. Although individual trees may grow slower due to competition, the higher number of stems per hectare can lead to greater overall stand-level productivity, especially in fast-growing species like Prosopis juliflora29.

The basal area at the collar showed a positive and strong correlation with the total height, crown height, degree of competition and crown area, which indicates the presence of space to grow into and reflects the dynamics of stand growth. At low densities, interactions between trees will not occur or will be weak60. As stand density increases, competitive interactions become more intense, trees occupy more growing space and use more resources such as light, water, and nutrients29,61). Recent studies based on large forest inventory datasets show that stand density has stronger effects than diversity on forest productivity60,62,63. The lack of significance between stand density and many of the independent variables is due, in part, to the relatively low tree densities sampled in this study and lower levels of inter-tree competition for light even in the higher density portions of the stands. Although the results indicate the importance of changes in the stand age compared to that in stand density in these areas, the effect of density on crown height growth (P = 0.02) and soil quality index (P = 0.000) was significant.

Considering the shape and dimensions of mesquite trees, measuring the diameter of the collar rather than the diameter at breast height is a better basis for measurement and comparison, and also some previous researchers35,64 have used this trait to estimate the biomass, so our research was also carried out on this basis. In this regard, the comparison of the two equations of Chojnaky and West shows that the biomass estimation results are close in different situations, but Chojnaky’s method has shown less standard error and standard deviation and is therefore preferred.

Growth rates

The results of this research showed that the average growth in total height, collar diameter, crown height, basal area and biomass of mesquite trees decreased with increasing age. It is obvious that the growth rate of trees changes with their age, due to physiological changes such as reduction in photosynthesis rate, leaf productivity and transfer of carbon sources to different parts of the plant, leaf size and gas exchange rate65. The decrease in tree growth over time can be attributed to changes in the supply of required resources (light, nutrients, water), changes in the balance between photosynthesis and respiration, increased hydraulic resistance, reduced nutrient supply, or genetic changes with meristem age4. However, it should be noted that long-term growth patterns vary greatly between and within tree species66,67. Reference68 reported that the biomass growth of acacia trees in Chinese forest plantations was rapid (from 1.60 to 185.01 tons/ha) during the first 7 years, and then decreased (to 188.69 tons/ha) during the next 27 years.

Soil quality

The results of this research showed that the average surface soil quality index increased from about 0.52 in the 15-year-old low-density stand to about 0.83 in the 34-year-old dense stand. The increase of surface soil quality index in forest plantations has been reported in many studies. Reference68 showed an increase in soil fertility during the first 34 years in Acacia plantations in China. In fact, soil quality index of the surface soil increases with increasing age and density of forest plantations, due to the increase in vegetation cover and root development that increases the accumulation of organic matter in the surface soil, and hence, the levels of nutrients and chemical elements that improve soil quality69 and 201734,70). The increase in soil fertility can also be related to the increase in nitrogen fixation71. Fu et al.72 and Yi et al.73 reported that during the growth and aging process, soil microorganisms and nematode communities play a key role in improving soil physical, chemical and biological properties and certainly the soil quality. The strong positive correlation between soil quality index and vegetative parameters of the stands shows that by increase in stand growth, use of the soil from biological activities of trees and their remains has increased.

The decrease in soil quality index in the subsurface soil (15–30 cm) sampled in this study may be due to, the amount of total phosphorus becomes a limiting factor for carbon sequestration as the afforestation stand ages74. In general, studies have shown that hot and dry climate areas have less potential for the accumulation of soil organic carbon, total nitrogen, and total phosphors below the 20 cm soil depth75,76. The negative correlation between soil quality index and the average growth of vegetative characteristics of mesquite trees is related to the increase in the age of the trees and the decrease in the average growth.

Conclusion

A novel aspect of this study is the simultaneous evaluation of biomass accumulation and soil quality at two depths (surface and subsurface) in response to stand structure (age × density) in an arid environment. Unlike most studies that focus solely on surface soil or single-age plantations, our approach allows a more comprehensive understanding of vertical soil recovery processes in long-term afforestation. This contributes to improved monitoring strategies for afforestation in drylands. After 27 years, we observed that height growth rate decreased due to increased competition. The significant increase in collar diameter, basal area, biomass, and surface soil quality index in the 34-year-old stand compared to the 27-year-old stand indicates there may be an opportunity to increase the volume and density of trees in the stand into the future. In general, higher density plantings (250 stems per hectare) of mesquite in afforestation plantings in this dry desert area, improve soil quality and promotes vegetative development and production as stands develop over time, and therefore mesquite is a suitable species for revival of dry and sparsely covered areas.
