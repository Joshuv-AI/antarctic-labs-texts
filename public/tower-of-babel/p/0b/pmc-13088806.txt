Presumptive mivacurium chloride-associated histamine release resulting in overt tissue edema during endoscopic thyroidectomy: a report of two cases

Background

In recent years, there has been a marked increase in the number of endoscopic thyroid surgeries. The benzylisoquinoline derivative, mivacurium chloride, a highly selective non-depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agent (NMBA), with a short duration of action, has been favored for endoscopic thyroid surgery requiring neuromonitoring [1, 2]. Notably, histamine release is associated with its use [3]. Histamine release varies among patients, and may cause skin flushing, rashes, bronchospasm, hypotension, and even cardiovascular collapse/arrest [1, 4]. Here we report two cases of clearly discernable tissue edema and fluid exudation during endoscopic thyroidectomy associated with mivacurium chloride administration, which have not been reported before.

Case presentation

Case 1: A 41-year-old, 61 kg female admitted in March 2023 was diagnosed with left lobe thyroid papillary carcinoma, and scheduled for a left lobe and isthmus resection via a dual-port trans-subclavian endoscopic thyroid procedure (registered in Clinical Trials under number NCT-06398795) [5]. She had no food/drug allergies or comorbidities. Her pre-operative thyroid function and plasma albumin levels were normal.

After the patient was admitted to the operating room, routine monitoring was initiated: a five-lead electrocardiogram, non-invasive blood pressure (BP) measurement, pulse oximetry, bispectral index (BIS), and end-expiratory carbon dioxide (PETCO2 ).

Her pre-anesthesia BP was 157/102 mmHg. Anesthetic induction consisted of intravenous (IV) alfentanil 2 mg, propofol 100 mg, and mivacurium chloride 10 mg (0.16 mg kg− 1) facilitated placement of a 6.0 G-electromyogram endotracheal tube (TriVantage®/REF 8229306, Medtronic, Florida, USA). Her BP decreased to 104/62 mmHg 5 min after induction, and then gradually recovered. No marked skin flushing, rash on her trunk or face, and no significant changes in airway pressure or bronchospasm were noted. Also, there were no signs of subcutaneous emphysema on the face, neck, or upper chest. Anesthesia was maintained by 1% sevoflurane inhalation in oxygen (2.0 L/min), combined with continuous infusion of remifentanil and propofol. Additional mivacurium chloride was administered, totaling 16.5 mg (0.27 mg kg− 1). The PETCO2 was maintained at 35–45 mmHg and the BIS readings were 45 to 60. During anesthesia, her heart rate varied ± 30%, and BP varied more than ± 30%. A total of 500 ml lactated ringer’s solution was infused and no antibiotics were administered. Her position was the same as in conventional open thyroidectomy.

During the procedure, the surgeon noted obvious edema in the subcutaneous tissue after trocar insertion (approximately 22 min after mivacurium chloride administration). With the establishment of the operative cavity, the surgeon noticed marked edema from the thyroid and surrounding tissue. On dissection of the thyroid gland, continuous exudation of clear tissue fluid was evident (Fig. 1A; Video 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1). This was noticeably different from a non-edematous thyroid gland and surrounding tissue(Fig. 1B).

This surgical method requires continuous carbon dioxide (CO2) insufflation (3–6 mmHg and 10 L/min). During surgery, the lens frequently fogged (in the presence of edema), requiring constant clearing and recurrent replacement of the ultrasonic knife with an aspirator to improve surgical field visibility. Additionally, the hemostatic efficiency of the ultrasonic knife was reduced in the presence of edema, therein greatly affecting the facility and safety of the procedure. In an attempt to counteract such conditions, the anesthesiologist administered dexamethasone 5 mg intravenously (76 min after the surgery began), but the surgical field did not improve. The total operative time was 157 min, which was longer than a typical case, secondary to the tissue edema.

Case 2: A 69-year-old 51 kg female was admitted in March 2023 with a diagnosed left lobe thyroid nodular goiter, and scheduled for a left lobe and isthmus resection, which was also performed via the dual-port trans-subclavian endoscopic thyroid approach. She had no allergies, but reported hypertension and hyperlipidaemia. Her thyroid function and plasma albumin levels were normal.

After the patient was admitted to the operating room, routine monitoring was initiated. Her pre-anesthesia BP was 166/74 mmHg. The anesthetic was induced with IV etomidate 16 mg, alfentanil 2.5 mg, and mivacurium chloride 12 mg (0.24 mg kg− 1) then followed by tracheal intubation with a 6.0 G-electromyogram endotracheal tube. Anesthesia was maintained using sevoflurane with remifentanil and propofol. Her BP decreased to 125/64 mmHg after induction. During anesthesia, her heart rate varied ± 30%, and the BP varied more than ± 30%. PETCO2 was maintained at 35–45 mmHg and BIS readings remain within normal range. The operative time was 95 min, and a total of 500 ml lactated ringer’s solution was infused. No antibiotics were administered, and no additional mivacurium chloride was required. Her position was the same as in conventional open thyroidectomy.

During the surgery, the subcutaneous tissue and thyroid gland demonstrated overt edema after trocar insertion (approximately 21 min after mivacurium chloride administration). On dissection of the thyroid gland, continuous exudation of clear tissue fluid was evident (Fig. 1C; Video 2, Supplemental Digital Content 2). The anesthesiologist administered IV methylprednisolone 40 mg 13 min after the surgery began, but with no significant clinical effect. No marked skin flushing, rash, no significant changes in airway pressure or bronchospasm was noted. Also, there were no signs of subcutaneous emphysema on the face, neck, or upper chest.

Discussion and conclusion

NMBAs are commonly used in surgery, and may be associated with histamine release. They can induce anaphylactic or anaphylactoid reactions, which cause smooth muscle contraction, increased vascular permeability, and increased serous secretions [4]. Histamine-induced hyperpermeability can primarily be attributed to the NO-induced blood flow increase, and partially to endothelial barrier disruption [6]. Mivacurium chloride is the shortest-acting non-depolarizing NMBA with a histamine-releasing side effect. Che et al. reported that mivacurium chloride induced mast cell degranulation in a dose-dependent manner [7]. The mivacurium chloride dose our patients received for endotracheal tube placement was less than the recommended dose of 0.25 mg kg− 1. However, their BPs decreased > 30% after induction or during anesthesia, and may have contributed to the overt tissue edema and fluid exudation during the procedure, leading to our speculation that histamine release occurred secondary to mivacurium administration.

Endoscopic thyroid surgeries have gained popularity for the treatment of thyroid tumors due to patient demand for a better cosmetic result through minimally invasive surgical methods [8, 9]. While shortcomings remain in the current mainstream endoscopic techniques used in thyroid surgery, the application of this technology in thyroid surgery will undoubtedly advance the field into a new era. During endoscopic thyroid surgery, intraoperative neuromonitoring of the recurrent laryngeal, superior laryngeal, and vagus nerves is required [10, 11]. Mivacurium chloride’s short duration of action has led to its use in endoscopic thyroid surgery requiring neuromonitoring. The histamine release and subsequent tissue response (edema) will have a more pronounced effect on endoscopic thyroid surgery than that of conventional open thyroidectomy. Endoscopic thyroid procedures require insufflation, and large amounts of water vapor are generated during tissue dissection with high-energy instruments (such as ultrasonic knives) in edematous regions. This results in a blurring of the surgical field, and a decreased hemostatic efficiency of instruments, which greatly affects the procedure.

H1 and H2 receptor blockers and corticosteroids represent the most commonly used effective treatments in the prevention of histamine release and the amelioration of its effects [12]. Naguib et al. found that the plasma histamine concentrations increased significantly 1 to 3 min after the administration of 0.2 mg kg− 1 mivacurium chloride, and returned to normal at 5 min [3]. In our first case, the anesthesiologist administered dexamethasone 5 mg 76 min after surgery began, which may not have been the optimal timing for corticosteroid administration, thereby resulting in an ineffective clinical result. In our second case, the anesthesiologist administered methylprednisolone 13 min after the procedure began, but still with little clinical effect. The increase in plasma histamine concentration may be short-lived, but the tissue edema and fluid exudation can persist for an extended period. Zhou et al. reported that preoperative treatment with promethazine significantly reduced histamine release induced by administration of mivacurium and provided more stable hemodynamics. Promethazine (an H1 receptor antagonist) can provide protective effects after IV administration of mivacurium, not only by significantly reducing histamine release, but also by decreasing capillary permeability preventing telangiectasis caused by allergic reactions [12]. Regrettably, in our cases, promethazine was not administered. The efficacy of promethazine or corticosteroids in treating adverse reactions caused by histamine release related to mivacurium administration needs further investigation.

The cornerstone of treatment is adrenaline in treating severe capillary leakage caused-by histamine releasing [13]. Unfortunately, in our case, the anesthesiologists did not use low-dose adrenaline in time to try to change the outcome.

Female patients account for the majority of those undergoing endoscopic thyroid surgery, and it has been reported that female patients are more prone to anaphylactic and anaphylactoid reactions after receiving NMBAs [4]. Unfortunately, we did not collect the patient’s plasma samples in time to determine the concentrations of histamine and trypsin, which was a major limitation. These concentrations were necessary to confirm our speculation that histamine release occurred secondary to mivacurium chloride administration.

Clinical cases of NMBA-induced histamine release are not uncommon, but there have been no reports of overt tissue edema and fluid exudation caused by mivacurium chloride-induced histamine release during endoscopic thyroid surgery. The thyroid gland has an abundant blood supply. Therefore, the impact of histamine release, which leads to increased vascular wall permeability and tissue fluid exudation, may be even greater on the thyroid. Due to anatomical limitations and limited space in the neck, the use of mivacurium chloride in endoscopic thyroid surgery may contribute to overt edema affecting the surgeon’s field of vision. While the question of edema with the initiation of insufflation and irrigation may arise, there was no irrigation by the surgeon in either case before the specimen was removed, also the CO2 insufflation parameters we used were low, the surgical team concluded they did not contribute to the problem. In its entirety, this report of a novel finding in the use of mivacurium chloride causing edema, that obscures the surgical field in endoscopic thyroid surgery, is novel and worthy of further investigation.

However, during endoscopic thyroid surgery, factors such as high pressure of CO2 insufflation and prolonged operative time may increase the occurrence of subcutaneous tissue or thyroid edema. Thermal spread caused by high-energy instruments may also provoke inflammatory edema in the operative field. Besides, in certain types of endoscopic thyroid surgery, patients are required to abduct arms, and the surgeons may retract the clavicle and scapular muscle regions, which may transiently impair venous return from the neck and upper chest, leading to soft-tissue edema. Therefore, if overt tissue edema is observed during endoscopic thyroid surgery, surgeons and anesthesiologists need to promptly rule out the above causes and implement improvements. Furthermore, when overt edema is found in thyroid and surrounding tissue, anesthesiologists should also be alert to the possibility of concurrent edema around the trachea or larynx, and remain vigilant about extubation readiness.

In conclusion, anesthesiologists should be vigilant in the selection and use of NMBAs during endoscopic thyroid surgery. At the same time, anesthesiologists need to pay attention to the histamine release effects caused by NMBAs, and enhance their skills in addressing this side effect.
