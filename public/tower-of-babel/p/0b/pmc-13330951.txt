Intestinal neutral ceramidase, microbial metabolites and epithelial fucosylation in MASH
Authors: Cristina Llorente
Published: 2026

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and its inflammatory progression to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) represent a major global health burden with a complex, multifactorial pathogenesis.1 While hepatic steatosis remains a defining feature of MASLD, disease progression to steatohepatitis and fibrosis reflects the interplay of multiple pathogenic processes. Among these, gut–liver axis dysfunction, characterised by intestinal dysbiosis, dysregulated microbial metabolism and impaired barrier integrity, has emerged as a key contributor.

In this issue, the study by Ting Wang et al2 establishes a mechanistic link between intestinal ceramide metabolism and the microbiota–metabolite–epithelium axis in MASH progression. The authors show that intestinal epithelial cell (IEC)–specific neutral ceramidase regulates disease severity by controlling the production of the microbial-associated metabolite 2-hydroxyhippuric acid (2-HHA). Loss of epithelial neutral ceramidase reduces luminal 2-HHA levels, which in turn relieves suppression of aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) signalling in IECs. Reactivation of AHR enhances epithelial fucosylation. Epithelial fucosylation is not just a surface modification; it is a functional regulator of microbial ecology and epithelial homeostasis.3 Surface-exposed fucosylated glycans serve as selective adhesion sites and nutrient sources for symbiotic microbes. Certain commensal species express α-fucosidase activity, which enables the cleavage of fucose residues from these glycans.3 This positions epithelial fucosylated glycans as active regulatory elements within the microbiota–metabolite–epithelium axis. Ting Wang et al2 found that loss of epithelial neutral ceramidase enhances epithelial fucosylation; functionally, this restores mucosal metabolic and inflammatory balance, improves gut barrier function and protects against MASH-associated liver injury.2

The role of gut neutral ceramidase in MASH pathogenesis

Ceramide metabolism has long been implicated in metabolic disease, particularly through hepatic lipotoxicity and insulin resistance. However, the current study shifts the focus towards intestinal ceramide turnover as an upstream regulator of systemic disease. IEC-specific deletion of Asah2, which encodes neutral ceramidase, protects mice from diet-induced fibrosis and inflammation without significantly altering steatosis, suggesting that intestinal ceramidase acts primarily by modulating inflammatory signalling rather than lipid accumulation per se.

This dissociation between steatosis and injury is particularly relevant to current debates in MASH pathogenesis, where multiple studies now support a ‘two-hit–independent’ framework in which fibrosis and inflammation can be uncoupled from hepatocellular fat content.

Microbial metabolites as intermediates of epithelial signalling

A central finding of the study is the identification of microbiota-associated 2-HHA as a key mediator downstream of intestinal ceramidase activity. Asah2 deletion alters microbial composition and reduces faecal 2-HHA levels, which in turn relieves inhibition of epithelial AHR signalling. One limitation is that 2-HHA is unlikely to be purely microbial in origin. Consistent with broader metabolic literature, 2-HHA should be considered a product of host–microbe cometabolism, likely arising from microbial production of salicylate-related precursors followed by host glycine conjugation.4 This interpretation aligns with emerging consensus in the field that many circulating microbial metabolites represent integrated metabolic networks rather than unidirectional microbial outputs.

This nuance is important, as it places 2-HHA within a broader class of metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids, bile acids and aromatic amino acid derivatives, that reflect dynamic host–microbiome biochemical coupling rather than microbial exclusivity.

AHR as a central epithelial integration node

The study further positions AHR as a key epithelial sensor linking microbial metabolites to barrier function. AHR is now well established as a multicellular regulator in the gut, acting in both epithelial and immune compartments. In IECs, it controls barrier integrity, antimicrobial peptide expression and epithelial differentiation; in immune cells, it regulates cytokine production and tissue immune tone.5

Fucosyltransferase 2 (FUT2) is an enzyme encoded by the Fut2 gene that catalyses the addition of fucose residues to glycans in an α1,2-linkage. This modification is crucial for the synthesis of α1,2-fucosylated glycans. In the intestinal epithelium, FUT2 activity contributes to the fucosylation of mucosal glycans, which in turn shapes host–microbiome interactions by influencing microbial colonisation, nutrient availability and immune signalling.3

By demonstrating that 2-HHA suppresses AHR signalling and downstream FUT2-dependent fucosylation, the authors propose a mechanistic cascade whereby microbial metabolites directly shape epithelial glycosylation programmes. This is conceptually consistent with prior work showing that microbial ligands and dietary components regulate AHR activity and that AHR signalling promotes epithelial barrier integrity through interleukin 22 (IL-22) dependent and independent mechanisms.

However, the study also raises an important unresolved question: to what extent are the observed effects driven by epithelial versus immune cell AHR? Given the broad expression of AHR in both compartments, future cell-type-specific rescue experiments will be essential to fully delineate compartment-specific contributions.

Fucosylation: protective barrier programme or disease amplifier?

Perhaps the most conceptually provocative aspect of this work is the positioning of epithelial α1,2-fucosylation as a protective barrier mechanism in MASH. IEC-specific Asah2 deletion enhances Fut2 expression and mucosal α1,2-fucosylation, which strengthens barrier integrity, reduces lipopolysaccharide (LPS) translocation and attenuates hepatic inflammation.

This finding integrates well with prior studies demonstrating that intestinal fucosylation is dynamically regulated by diet and microbiota and plays a central role in host–microbe mutualism.3 In alcohol-associated liver disease, chronic ethanol exposure downregulates Fut2 and intestinal α1,2-fucosylation, thereby contributing to epithelial barrier dysfunction and dysbiosis.6 Loss of FUT2 impairs colonisation by beneficial taxa and reduces the abundance of microbes expressing nicotinamidase, thereby altering intestinal nicotinamide–nicotinic acid metabolism and exacerbating ethanol-induced liver injury.7 Loss of FUT2 simultaneously promotes overgrowth of Enterococcus faecalis.6 Cytolysin-positive E. faecalis, which produces a two-subunit pore-forming exotoxin (cytolysin), readily translocates across the ethanol-compromised intestinal barrier and drives hepatocyte death and steatohepatitis in both mice and humans. The presence of cytolysin in the faeces of patients with alcohol-associated hepatitis strongly correlates with disease severity and mortality.8 9 Therefore, fucosylation plays a key role in shaping intestinal homeostasis.

The finding by Ting Wang et al,2 however, also highlights the potential complexity of the role of epithelial fucosylation in disease contexts, particularly in MASLD, where its function may vary depending on the specific pathogenic milieu. A previous study has shown that FUT2-dependent fucosylation promotes obesity and steatohepatitis under Western diet conditions, while FUT2 deficiency is protective. Fut2-deficient mice were protected from Western diet-induced features of obesity and steatohepatitis despite an increased caloric intake whereas supplementation with α1-2-fucosylated glycans exacerbated obesity and steatohepatitis in these mice.10 Notably, the present study does not employ Fut2-deficient mice in the context of AHR. Hence, further studies addressing the role of fucosylation in the setting of epithelial AHR deletion (AhRΔIEC) and neutral ceramidase deletion (Asah2ΔIEC) may yield important outcomes and clarify the causal mechanisms underlying these observations. Furthermore, dietary fucosylated oligosaccharides such as 2′-fucosyllactose have been reported to exacerbate MASLD. 2′-fucosyllactose, a prebiotic abundant in human milk and not directly usable by the host, can be metabolised by intestinal bacteria to release L-fucose. In Western diet-fed mice, but not in control diet-fed animals, 2′-fucosyllactose supplementation has been shown to increase body and liver weight, worsen hepatic injury and promote steatosis. These observations further underscore the complexity of interpreting the metabolic consequences of dietary fucosylated substrates in disease settings.10 This appears to contrast with findings obtained on fucoidan supplementation in AhRΔIEC mice, where fucoidan ameliorates MASH and restores intestinal fucosylation and barrier function. Importantly, fucoidan may still be partially absorbed or generate bioactive metabolites that could directly influence host metabolic pathways, adding a layer of complexity to its interpretation. Although fucoidan may exert additional host-directed effects, it is increasingly recognised that it also modulates the gut microbiota, promoting the expansion of fucose-using and fucose-producing bacterial populations. This microbial remodelling may, in turn, influence luminal fucose availability and reinforce epithelial fucosylation, thereby contributing to improved epithelial integrity and metabolic outcomes.

Collectively, these studies suggest that the impact of dietary fucosylated compounds in metabolic disease is highly context-dependent and likely shaped by their differential effects on microbial metabolism, epithelial fucosylation and host signalling pathways. These seemingly contradictory observations strongly suggest that epithelial fucosylation is not inherently protective or pathogenic but instead operates as a context-sensitive metabolic interface, shaped by microbial composition, substrate availability, host metabolic state, neutral ceramidase and AHR expression. The current study contributes to this debate and suggests that the functional outcome of fucosylation depends on upstream regulatory cues, namely a neutral ceramidase–microbiota–AHR axis.

A gut–liver–lung axis extending systemic relevance

An additional strength of the work is the extension of findings beyond the liver to include lung inflammation. Both neutral ceramidase and AHR epithelial deletion models modulate pulmonary immune responses, supporting the concept that intestinal metabolic programmes shape distal organ immunity. This aligns with growing recognition of a gut–lung axis in metabolic disease, where microbial metabolites and endotoxemia contribute to systemic immune reprogramming.

However, the contribution of lung-resident microbiota and tissue-specific metabolite production remains unresolved and warrants further investigation.

Therapeutic implications and limitations

From a translational perspective, the identification of a neutral ceramidase–microbiota–AHR–fucosylation axis opens several therapeutic avenues, including microbiota-targeted interventions and modulation of epithelial glycosylation depending on the gut context (microbiota, neutral ceramidase and AHR expression). The beneficial effects of fucoidan supplementation further suggest that dietary or prebiotic approaches may restore epithelial barrier function in metabolic disease in the context of AHR deficiency.

Nevertheless, further studies are warranted to address these outstanding questions. A more detailed understanding of the potential off-target or systemic effects of fucoidan will be important to fully delineate its microbiota-mediated mechanisms of action. Likewise, the use of genetic FUT2 loss-of-function models would provide valuable causal insight into the specific contribution of epithelial fucosylation. The dual host–microbial origin of 2-HHA further highlights the opportunity for more refined metabolic flux analyses to precisely define its sources and pathways. In addition, resolving the cell-type-specific roles of AHR, as well as the broader systemic consequences of altered intestinal ceramide metabolism, will be essential to complete the mechanistic framework. Collectively, these avenues of investigation offer an important opportunity to further refine and expand our understanding of the Asah2–microbiota–AHR–fucosylation axis in metabolic disease.

Conclusion

This study provides a compelling multi-layered model in which intestinal ceramidase shapes microbial metabolism, which in turn regulates epithelial receptor signalling and glycosylation programmes that ultimately control systemic inflammatory injury. By placing epithelial fucosylation at the intersection of lipid metabolism, microbial ecology and immune regulation, it reframes MASH as a disease of integrated metabolic communication rather than isolated hepatic dysfunction. More broadly, it reinforces a central paradigm emerging in metabolic disease research: that intestinal epithelial biology is not merely a passive barrier but an active metabolic and immunological interface governing systemic homeostasis.
