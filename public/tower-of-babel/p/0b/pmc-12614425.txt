Filling the Stiff Heart: Insights From Stress Echocardiography
Authors: Hee Jeong Lee, In-Cheol Kim
Published: 2025

Exercise intolerance is a major symptom across the spectrum of heart failure (HF), regardless of the ejection fraction (EF) phenotype. Traditionally, the classification of HF has been based on EF—reduced (HFrEF), preserved (HFpEF), mildly-reduced (HFmrEF), and improved—which has guided both diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.1) However, numerous studies have demonstrated that EF alone does not sufficiently explain symptom severity or functional limitations, particularly in patients with HFpEF or HFmrEF.2)3) The central mechanism underlying exercise intolerance involves cardiac, pulmonary and skeletal dysfunction4) (Figure 1). This physiological derangement is often occult at rest but is unmasked only during stress. In this context, stress echocardiography and cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) serve as complementary modalities for revealing diastolic dysfunction in patients with HF. When combined, these tools facilitate the phenotyping of exertional dyspnea beyond conventional parameters.

The study by Huang et al.5) provides important insights into the hemodynamic mechanisms of exercise intolerance across the EF spectrum of patients with HF. In a cohort of 66 patients who underwent simultaneous CPET and exercise echocardiography, they investigated the determinants of impaired peak oxygen consumption (peak VO2 <14 mL/kg/min) and ventilatory inefficiency (ventilatory equivalent for carbon dioxide slope >34). Remarkably, peak medial E/e′ normalized to cardiac output (E/e′-to-CO ratio) emerged as the strongest predictor of severely reduced exercise capacity, with an areas under the curve of 0.87. Additionally, resting indices of diastolic dysfunction were associated with reduced exercise tolerance; however, their sensitivity for detecting exercise-induced hemodynamic changes is limited, particularly in HFpEF. Conversely, parameters of systolic function—such as left ventricular (LV) EF and global longitudinal strain—correlated more closely with ventilatory inefficiency than with peak VO2 itself.

These findings emphasize that diastolic reserve, not resting systolic function, is a pivotal determinant of exercise limitation across all HF phenotypes.6) Diastolic reserve is particularly important in HFpEF and HFmrEF, where patients often present with significant exertional dyspnea despite “normal” LVEF at rest. The authors highlighted that exercise intolerance stems from a rapid increase in LV filling pressure relative to cardiac output, indicating poor diastolic reserve. Clinically, this suggests that resting echocardiographic assessment may underestimate the hemodynamic burden in symptomatic patients. While CPET remains the gold standard for assessing global exercise physiology, the addition of stress echocardiography allows real-time visualization of the pathophysiology related to diastolic dysfunction. The peak E/e′-to-CO ratio serves as a practical, non-invasive surrogate for pulmonary capillary wedge pressure during exercise, providing diagnostic and prognostic value without requiring invasive catheterization. This physiology-centered framework enables more tailored therapeutic strategies, and patients with pronounced diastolic responses may benefit more from therapies targeting the preload and LV compliance (e.g., sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors and angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitor), whereas those with poor ventilatory efficiency may derive greater benefit from exercise training and pulmonary rehabilitation.

This work not only deepens pathophysiological understanding but also paves the way for future research and clinical applications. Future studies with larger population need to validate the E/e′-to-CO ratio as a prognostic marker and treatment response indicator. Its potential integration into transplant or mechanical circulatory support candidacy algorithms is especially promising for advanced HF populations, where the existing cutoffs for peak VO2 may not be appropriate. In clinical practice, streamlined protocols that integrate CPET and stress echocardiography could become the solution for patients with unexplained exertional symptoms, particularly those with HFpEF. Moreover, digital tools and machine learning approaches could enhance the reproducibility of E/e′-based measures during exercise, facilitating broader adoption.7)8) Ultimately, this study serves as a reminder that “not all HF is created equal,” and neither is exercise intolerance.

While CPET remains the gold standard for assessing global exercise physiology and stress echocardiography well visualizes cardiac hemodynamics, we need to acknowledge that both modalities have limitations. CPET requires specialized equipment and patient cooperation, whereas stress echocardiography is operator-dependent and subject to technical variability. Furthermore, current evidence stems from relatively small cohorts, and standardized protocols are lacking, especially in HFpEF. Future research with larger and more diverse populations is needed to validate these tools and integrate them into routine clinical practice.
