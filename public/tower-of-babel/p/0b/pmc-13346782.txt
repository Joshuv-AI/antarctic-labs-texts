Evaluating the safety of large language models in healthcare and dentistry: adversarial testing approaches
Authors: Fahad Umer, Muhammad Muthar Shaikh, Absar Ur Rahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe emergence of large language models (LLMs) provides new avenues for clinical support in healthcare and dentistry. However, these models often exhibit unpredictable behaviours when challenged by adversarial or misleading inputs. Recent data indicate that nearly 20% of LLM outputs contain safety risks or biases, necessitating rigorous evaluation prior to clinical use. This review examines AI red teaming, a systematic approach for identifying system vulnerabilities through simulated attacks. It details methodological approaches and outcome measures while proposing a structured framework to integrate these safety evaluations into the clinical AI lifecycle.MethodsThis review focuses on prompt-based attacks, such as prompt injection and jailbreaking, which are highly relevant in medical settings. Introduction

The rapid expansion of large language models (LLMs) has opened up fresh opportunities for healthcare delivery across multiple domains including dentistry [1–4]. Their capacity to interpret natural language prompts and generate contextually relevant responses has positioned them as potential clinical decision-support tools, assisting clinicians in gathering information, patient communication, and navigating clinical guidelines [1, 2]. However, their behaviour may be unpredictable under certain conditions, particularly when exposed to ambiguous, misleading or adversarial inputs [5, 6]. Recent studies have demonstrated that approximately 15–20% of LLMs responses contain safety issues or biases, underscoring the urgent need for rigorous evaluation methodologies before widespread clinical integration [7]. Importantly, unlike cybersecurity contexts where adversarial attacks are typically executed by technical experts, in healthcare settings unsafe outputs may be triggered by ordinary users, including patients, caregivers, or junior clinicians, whose limited judgement and over reliance on model-generated information may increase the likelihood that harmful outputs influence real-world clinical decisions. Beyond unintentional misuse, these systems may also be deliberately exploited to extract unsafe clinical guidance [8, 9].

Artificial Intelligence (AI) red teaming is a structured method for proactively identifying vulnerabilities in these systems. Borrowed from cybersecurity practice, red teaming involves deliberate and systematic attempts to expose system weaknesses through adversarial simulation [6]. In healthcare, red teaming includes probing for unsafe clinical recommendations, testing responses to ethically sensitive scenarios, evaluating bias across demographic subgroups, and assessing robustness to manipulative or malicious prompts [7, 10]. LLM red teaming primarily targets alignment mechanisms, reasoning pathways, and instruction hierarchies that govern how models interpret and respond to prompts [6, 11].

Despite the increasing reliance on red teaming for AI security evaluation, important gaps remain in understanding how to design, implement, and interpret these assessments effectively within healthcare settings. Red teaming activities have already demonstrated real-world relevance by identifying harmful outputs in LLMs, including incorrect image interpretations, hallucinated clinical information, overly optimistic prognostic estimates, and inaccurate risk assessments [7]. Nevertheless, there remains limited guidance on standardised methodologies, outcome measures, and reporting frameworks tailored specifically to healthcare applications. Furthermore, clarity is lacking on how red teaming findings should be incorporated into clinical governance structures and professional standards in dentistry and broader healthcare disciplines.

Therefore, this review describes the methodological approaches, evaluation strategies, and outcome measures relevant to LLM red teaming in healthcare and dentistry, proposes a structured deployment framework and examines the associated methodological limitations and governance considerations for clinical implementation.

Guardrails in large language models

LLMs are increasingly deployed with a set of built-in guardrails intended to ensure safe, ethical, and contextually appropriate outputs. These include content filtering, toxicity detection, refusal mechanisms, and alignment techniques such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), all of which are designed to prevent LLMs from generating harmful, biased, or misleading responses [12, 13]. In healthcare, these guardrails are expected to uphold clinical safety standards by restricting outputs that could result in incorrect diagnoses, inappropriate treatment recommendations, or the disclosure of sensitive patient information [7, 12, 14]. However, it is important to distinguish between two tiers of guardrail behaviors. Hard refusals represent absolute content blocks triggered by requests falling into extreme harm categories, such as biological weapon synthesis or malicious code generation, where the model declines to provide any substantive output [15]. In contrast, soft guardrails are more commonly observed in healthcare contexts. Rather than refusing outright, models may still comply with potentially unsafe clinical requests while appending generic cautionary statements, such as advising users to consult a qualified healthcare professional [9]. This behavior may create a misleading impression of safety, as clinically harmful or inappropriate information is still delivered and may subsequently be acted upon by patients or clinicians who overlook or discount the disclaimer [8, 9]. Guardrails are, in either case inherently imperfect; they are trained on finite datasets, may fail to generalise across diverse clinical contexts, and can be deliberately circumvented through adversarial inputs [6, 9, 12].

Evolution of LLM guardrails

LLM safety guardrails have improved significantly across model generations [11]. Early models had little to no safety alignment and were easily manipulated. Later models introduced RLHF, which improved safety but remained vulnerable to roleplay and persona-based attacks; tactics commonly seen in healthcare settings [6]. Current versions of frontier models like GPT-5, Claude Haiku 4.5 now resist most standard jailbreaking attempts through advanced adversarial training [11].

Despite this progress, two important risks remain. First, many healthcare institutions deploy older, open-source, or fine-tuned clinical models that carry the vulnerabilities of earlier generations, often without clinical governance teams being aware of this [16]. Second, as guardrails have improved, attack methods have also evolved in parallel with newer techniques such as indirect prompt injection and cross-modal manipulation posing risks that even frontier models are not fully protected against [17]. Clinical red teaming must therefore always target the specific model version in deployment and be updated continuously, rather than assuming safety findings from one model apply to another [9]. While all five attack types pose meaningful risks, this review will focus exclusively on prompt-based attacks, as these represent the most commonly documented and clinically relevant attack vector in healthcare settings, requiring no access to model architecture or training pipelines to elicit harmful outputs [7, 18, 19]. For a comprehensive account of all five attack types and their associated techniques, please refer to the study by Jabbar et al. [11].

Prompt based attacks

Prompt-based attacks exploit the way LLMs process and respond to natural language instructions [9]. Rather than targeting the model’s internal architecture, these attacks manipulate the text inputs presented to the model, taking advantage of its tendency to follow instructions literally without genuine semantic understanding [9]. Because they operate entirely at the input level, they are accessible to a wide range of adversaries and represent a particularly persistent challenge for safe LLM deployment in clinical environments [9, 20].

Unlike traditional cybersecurity attacks, where adversarial actors deliberately seek harmful outputs for personal gain, in healthcare the user is typically attempting to obtain more information than the model permits. Such interactions arise from patients testing medication limits, caregivers seeking dosing guidance, drug-seeking individuals probing for prescribing information, individuals attempting insurance fraud; none of whom may recognise they are circumventing safety guardrails, yet whose interactions can produce clinical consequences identical to those of a deliberate attack [8, 9, 21].

Outcome measures

Outcome measures in LLM red teaming are designed to quantify vulnerability, safety alignment, and potential clinical harm under adversarial conditions [6, 9, 22]. Rather than focusing solely on general accuracy, these metrics assess unsafe compliance, appropriateness of refusals, hallucination frequency, harm severity, and differential behaviour across demographic contexts [7, 8]. Together, they provide a structured framework for evaluating how reliably a model maintains safety guardrails when challenged by malicious or misleading prompts. Evaluation strategies

Evaluation strategies in LLM red teaming refer to the structured methods used to design, carry out, and interpret adversarial testing [6, 22]. These strategies are different from outcome measures, which focus on counting or grading the model’s failures [6, 17]. Instead, evaluation strategies explain how the testing is organised and assessed. A comprehensive adversarial evaluation framework typically incorporates three complementary approaches:

A proposed framework for adversarial evaluation

The safe integration of LLMs into clinical practice requires a structured evaluation approach that extends beyond one-time pre-deployment testing [26]. Rather than treating adversarial evaluation as a single checkpoint, it should be embedded as a continuous process spanning the entire deployment cycle from initial safety testing through to live monitoring and iterative review. This review proposes a framework that adapts principles from cybersecurity red-blue-purple teaming and applies them to the lifecycle of clinical AI systems. In dentistry specifically, this process is particularly important because LLMs may increasingly be integrated into patient triage systems, radiology support tools, treatment-planning assistants, postoperative guidance chatbots, insurance documentation systems, and educational platforms [2]. Failures in these settings may directly influence patient safety, clinical decision-making, prescribing behaviour, informed consent, and medico-legal accountability [12, 15].

Adversarial evaluation in dentistry is inherently multistakeholder. Clinicians and specialists define realistic failure scenarios and acceptable standards of care; patients and caregivers represent the most common real-world users; AI developers and safety researchers design and stress-test guardrails; and ethicists, legal experts, and regulatory bodies address liability, consent, and deployment standards [7, 11, 15]. Ensuring representation across these groups is particularly critical in dentistry, where safety failures can directly affect irreversible decisions including opioid prescribing, sedation protocols and much more [2, 12].

The framework consists of three operational phases

In pre deployment phase, the red team should identify high-risk clinical scenarios and systematically test the model against adversarial inputs in a controlled environment. Based on the findings, the blue team should iteratively refine guardrails until the model meets acceptable safety standards. The purple team should establish a formal registry of unacceptable outputs, categorised by type and severity and explicitly linked to evaluation metrics that will serve as the operational safety benchmark for subsequent phases. Prior to live release, the purple team should conduct structured audits to verify that all identified vulnerabilities have been satisfactorily addressed. Deployment should only be authorised upon satisfactory audit performance and documented safety clearance.

During the deployment phase, the red team should continuously monitor live model outputs against the predefined registry of unacceptable outputs, with ongoing surveillance encompassing the detection of behavioural changes, emerging failure patterns, and input distribution shifts relative to pre-deployment benchmarks. The purple team, operating through a designated clinical AI oversight committee comprising clinicians, AI safety specialists, and institutional representatives, should provide oversight and coordinate real-time red and blue team responses to any newly identified safety concerns.

Finally, during the iterative review phase, the red team should conduct a formal review of all flagged outputs and safety deviations, systematically identifying underlying causes and characterising emerging failure patterns. The blue team should apply targeted corrective measures and update the defensive registry accordingly. The model should only be reauthorised once the purple team has overseen a comprehensive safety re-evaluation confirming that all identified issues have been resolved. Limitations of red teaming

Despite its growing role in evaluating LLM safety, red teaming carries several important limitations in clinical contexts. First, the absence of standardised protocols means that methods, evaluators, and outcome measures vary considerably across studies, limiting reproducibility and cross-institutional comparability. Second, most exercises are conducted by technical teams with limited clinical input, meaning subtle but clinically significant vulnerabilities such as context-specific prescribing errors may go undetected. Third, controlled red teaming settings cannot fully replicate the complexity of real-world clinical environments, leaving gaps in risk identification. Fourth, adversarial prompting techniques evolve rapidly. As new jailbreak and prompt-injection strategies emerge, vulnerabilities identified in earlier red teaming exercises may quickly become outdated. Fifth, there is a temporal mismatch risk; findings from testing one model generation may not transfer to the version ultimately deployed clinically. Given the pace of LLM development, a healthcare institution evaluating an AI tool during procurement may find that the production version deployed months later has materially different guardrail behaviours. Red teaming must therefore be conducted on the exact model version and configuration entering clinical deployment and repeated following any model updates.

From a regulatory standpoint, governance of LLM red teaming in healthcare remains underdeveloped [1]. The FDA has not yet authorised any LLM for clinical use but has acknowledged the need for specialised assessment tools suited to clinical deployment contexts [12]. Its 2025 draft guidance on AI-enabled device software functions recommends that manufacturers disclose intended use, training data, and error rate estimates, while the 2024 transparency guidance emphasises that healthcare providers and patients must receive clear information about a device’s limitations and performance [27, 28]. Most recently, the 2026 revised guidance on Clinical Decision Support Software expanded the scope of AI-enabled tools to include potentially LLM-based applications that fall outside premarket device regulation, provided clinicians can independently review recommendation outputs [29]. While this reflects a shift toward innovation-friendly enforcement discretion, no specific red teaming requirements have yet been mandated for clinical LLM deployment, leaving a critical gap between identified vulnerabilities and actionable regulatory standards [9].

Future directions

Future research should prioritise the development of standardised, healthcare-specific red teaming frameworks, with particular emphasis on dentistry, that incorporate multidisciplinary input from clinicians, ethicists, and security specialists to ensure that evaluations reflect real-world clinical complexity. There is a need to move beyond prompt-based attack evaluation toward comprehensive assessment frameworks that address the full spectrum of attack vectors, including data manipulation, model exploitation, and information extraction, particularly as LLMs are increasingly integrated into high-stakes clinical workflows. Red, blue, and purple teaming should not be treated as isolated activities but integrated into a collaborative, continuous, and coordinated cycle in which vulnerabilities identified through red teaming are systematically mitigated through blue teaming defences and jointly validated through purple teaming processes. Automated and iterative red teaming pipelines should be adopted to enable continuous vulnerability monitoring rather than one-time pre-deployment testing. Equally important is the development of domain-specific benchmarks and evaluation datasets tailored to healthcare and dentistry contexts, which are currently absent from the literature.

While adversarial evaluation and red teaming strategies have been increasingly implemented across various healthcare domains, their application within dentistry remains markedly limited. Future studies should prioritise the development of dentistry-specific red teaming frameworks, utilising real clinical prompts drawn from common dental scenarios. Such work would provide an empirical foundation for understanding how LLMs perform under adversarial conditions within dental practice specifically and enable meaningful comparison of safety profiles across model generations in a clinically relevant context. Finally, closer collaboration between AI developers, clinical institutions, and regulatory bodies such as the FDA is essential to translate red teaming findings into enforceable governance standards, ensuring that identified vulnerabilities are systematically addressed before LLMs are deployed in patient-facing or clinician-support roles.

Conclusion

The safe and responsible integration of LLMs into healthcare and dentistry demands rigorous adversarial evaluation, of which red teaming represents a foundational component. While red teaming offers a structured means of identifying vulnerabilities before real-world deployment, its effectiveness is contingent upon standardised methodologies, multidisciplinary collaboration, and iterative evaluation processes that current practice has yet to fully establish. Crucially, the adversarial risk landscape in healthcare differs fundamentally from cybersecurity contexts; unsafe outputs are more likely to originate from well-intentioned but vulnerable users than from technically sophisticated adversarial actors, and the consequences are measured in patient harm rather than data breach. Bridging these gaps through domain-specific frameworks, regulatory guidance, and continuous adversarial testing will be essential to ensure that LLMs deployed in clinical environments are not only capable but also safe, trustworthy, and fit for patient care.
