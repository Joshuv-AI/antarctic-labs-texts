Primary Care Integrated Management of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (PRIM-COPD): study protocol for a cluster-randomised controlled trial
Authors: Yiwen Yu, Xiaoxia Ren, Xu Chu, Jisong Yan, Feng Guo, Quan Wan, Ke Huang, Jieping Lei, Xingyao Tang, Xing Qi, Junsheng Zheng, Yong Li, Fang Fang, Jun Pan, Cunbo Jia, Ting Yang, Chen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with China bearing a particularly high burden. Major gaps persist in early diagnosis, guideline-based treatment and long-term disease management, especially in primary care. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy and feasibility of the National Essential Public Health Services (NEPHS) COPD management approach and to generate evidence to inform the optimisation of national COPD care standards.Methods and analysisThe Primary Care Integrated Management (PRIM) trial is a prospective, multicentre, cluster-randomised controlled study conducted in seven pilot regions in China, with primary healthcare institutions as the unit of randomisation. Participants are adults aged ≥35 years with spirometry-confirmed COPD, recruited either through screening or from existing patient databases. Institutions will be randomised in a 1:1 ratio to the intervention (PRIM) or usual care arms using a computer-generated allocation sequence, stratified by urban-rural location. The intervention comprises quarterly general practitioner (GP)-led follow-ups, health education and disease management according to pilot NEPHS COPD guidelines, supported by a three-tier oversight structure involving the National Center for Respiratory Medicine, leading tertiary hospitals and primary healthcare institutions. Co-primary outcomes are the annual number of acute exacerbations and health-related quality of life (European Quality of Life-5 Dimensions and 5 Levels). Secondary outcomes include smoking status, health status assessed by the COPD Assessment Test (CAT), dyspnoea score and GP knowledge. Analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle, using multivariable regression models adjusted for clustering and prespecified baseline covariates. Cost-effectiveness analyses of the PRIM intervention in the Chinese population are also planned.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval has been granted by China-Japan Friendship Hospital (approval number 2024-KY-163).Trial registration numberNCT06573580.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Recent estimates from the Global Burden of Disease 2021 study indicate that COPD accounted for approximately 213.4 million prevalent cases, 3.7 million deaths and 79.8 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) globally in 2021.1 In China, the burden remains substantial, with recent estimates suggesting 50.6 million prevalent cases, 4.4 million incident cases, 1.29 million deaths and 23.6 million DALYs in 2021.2

COPD is associated with progressive respiratory impairment, reduced quality of life and substantial healthcare expenditure. Because respiratory symptoms often become evident only after significant disease progression, many patients miss the opportunity for early diagnosis and timely intervention. Acute exacerbations of COPD further worsen prognosis by increasing the risks of respiratory failure and death, accelerating lung function decline and contributing to poorer quality of life, more frequent hospitalisations and higher healthcare costs.36

Against this background, major shortcomings remain in COPD detection and management in China, particularly in primary care.7 Recent national evidence highlights substantial gaps across the COPD care cascade, from spirometry testing and diagnosis to treatment and disease control. In one national study, only 41.0% (95% CI 40.4 to 41.6%) of patients with COPD had ever undergone spirometry testing, 17.6% (95% CI 17.1 to 18.0%) had received a prior diagnosis, 8.5% (95% CI 8.2 to 8.8%) were receiving treatment and only a small minority achieved adequate exacerbation control.8 These findings indicate that a considerable proportion of patients with COPD are lost at each stage of care and that the overall quality of COPD management in the general population is likely to be inadequate.

In response to this situation, there is an urgent need for China to strengthen COPD management. Consequently, in September 2024, COPD was officially included in the National Essential Public Health Services (NEPHS) package, and COPD management services have since been rolled out at the primary care level. The key challenge now is to translate this policy into consistent, high-quality COPD care nationwide. Although the pilot NEPHS COPD management guidelines have been published,9 drawing on prior clinical experience as well as the NEPHS programmes for hypertension and diabetes, their efficacy and feasibility still require further validation to guide broader regional and national scale-up.

To address these knowledge and practice gaps, the Center for Respiratory Medicine in China initiated the Primary Care Integrated Management (PRIM) of COPD trial, a multicentre cluster-randomised controlled study designed to evaluate the efficacy and feasibility of the COPD management approach currently adopted under the NEPHS and to generate evidence for the refinement of national COPD care standards.

Method and analysis

Study design

The PRIM trial for COPD is a prospective, multicentre, cluster-randomised controlled trial with two arms. Each community-level primary healthcare clinic is considered a cluster. The trial will be conducted across pilot regions in China, including Beijing, Tianjin, Cangzhou, Quzhou, Wuhan, Datong and Hohhot. The selection of pilot regions provides diverse representation across China’s economic and geographic spectrum.

In each pilot region, a designated leading tertiary hospital will be responsible for overseeing the implementation of the trial. These hospitals must have a dedicated respiratory department and COPD specialists to ensure their capacity to participate. They should also be members of the National Respiratory Specialist Alliance, with extensive experience in providing COPD-related professional education and implementing standardised COPD care pathways. This expertise ensures they are well qualified to train GPs at participating primary healthcare institutions. As members of the Respiratory Specialist Alliance, these hospitals are also well positioned to work with the National Center for Respiratory Medicine to ensure adherence to the trial protocol across all participating institutions.

Study population and recruitment

We aim to recruit participants aged 35 years and older with a confirmed diagnosis of COPD, primarily from two sources:1 (1) individuals with a prior confirmed diagnosis of COPD and (2) newly diagnosed patients identified through screening. The diagnosis must be confirmed by a qualified healthcare institution through pulmonary function testing (PFT), with a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio of ≤70%, and the exclusion of other pulmonary diseases based on appropriate imaging, such as a chest X-ray or CT scan.

Newly identified eligible individuals will be approached by GPs immediately after diagnosis and asked whether they are willing to participate in the trial. Those with a prior diagnosis of COPD, identified through existing databases, will be contacted by their GPs via phone to confirm their interest. For individuals who express willingness to join, an offline appointment will be scheduled for informed consent signing and baseline assessment. This appointment may be carried out either as a home visit or an outpatient visit.

Randomisation and data acquisition

Within each region, participating institutions will be allocated in a 1:1 ratio to the intervention or control arm using a computer-generated random allocation sequence, stratified by urban–rural location. The randomisation sequence will be generated by an independent statistician who is not otherwise involved in the project using SAS V.9.2 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA). Allocation to the intervention or control group will be concealed until each institution has completed participant enrolment and baseline assessment. Group allocation will then be communicated to the participating institutions by the study coordination team via an official letter, and the institutions will be asked to inform participating patients. During the randomisation process, newly enrolled participants will be cross-checked against existing participant records to identify and exclude any potential duplicates. Because of the nature of the intervention, blinding of patients and care providers will not be feasible; however, outcome assessors and data analysts will remain blinded to treatment allocation.

A baseline assessment will be conducted immediately after the cluster randomisation and prior to the initiation of the intervention. Data collection for this study will be conducted using a secure electronic data capture (EDC) application, ensuring confidentiality through password protection. The baseline questionnaire will collect comprehensive data, including demographic information, healthcare utilisation, smoking status, COPD-related history, medical history, physiological and psychological health indicators, economic expenditures and physical examination results, such as PFTs and bronchodilator responsiveness. The data sources included medical records, patient-reported outcomes and various validated questionnaires, such as the COPD Assessment Test (CAT), the Modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) scale, the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) for mental health and the EQ-5D-5L for quality of life. Study intervention

Primary Care Integrated Management arm

During the study, patients with COPD in the intervention arm will receive quarterly follow-up visits, which will be conducted by their GPs at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months (T1–T4) after baseline (figure 1). During each follow-up visit, GPs will assess the patient’s symptoms, medication use and any acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) episodes and promptly record the information into the EDC system. Follow-ups may be conducted via telephone, home visits or outpatient visits; the 12-month follow-up must be conducted via home or outpatient visit. Based on the collected information, GPs will evaluate each patient’s condition, adjust the treatment plan as needed and determine appropriate follow-up actions. All interventions will be carried out in strict accordance with the pilot NEPHS COPD management guidelines,9 published by the National Health Commission. Each follow-up visit also provides COPD patients an opportunity to communicate with their GPs, enabling them to discuss current disease management and raise any questions or concerns. At the same time, GPs are instructed to use these interactions to reinforce key messages and offer personalised guidance (figure 2).

We also provide health education to patients during COPD management. At baseline, all patients receive a set of COPD educational materials distributed by their GPs, including a series of booklets titled Knowledge Pocket and a comprehensive health education book (see online supplemental figure 1 for details). The Knowledge Pocket series covers six core topics: prevention and management of acute exacerbations, proper medication use, healthy lifestyle practices, smoking cessation strategies and benefits, mental health support and vaccination. The health education book serves as a more detailed companion to the booklets, providing in-depth guidance on COPD treatment and self-management. These materials are not primarily text-based; rather, they include substantial visual content, such as illustrations and explanatory captions, to facilitate understanding and increase engagement among older patients. For patients with very limited literacy or who are unable to read, health education will be delivered primarily through verbal explanation by GPs at participating primary healthcare institutions.

Overall, the intervention will be implemented by GPs at participating primary healthcare institutions and overseen through a coordinated three-tier structure. First, the National Center for Respiratory Medicine will be responsible for developing and disseminating standardised educational materials, providing initial protocol training to designated tertiary hospitals and participating primary healthcare institutions before study initiation, and conducting ongoing quarterly supervision throughout the study to ensure standardised implementation. Training will cover the intervention protocol, follow-up procedures, data entry requirements and key principles of COPD management. In addition, the National Center will conduct competency assessments based on predefined indicators of study quality and implementation fidelity, including recruitment performance, completeness and accuracy of data entry through the EDC system, and adherence to the intervention protocol. Comparative performance reports will be generated at both the pilot-region level and the institution level to support benchmarking and quality improvement. The National Center will also conduct periodic site audits of designated tertiary hospitals to assess whether affiliated primary healthcare institutions are being trained and supervised as required. These audits will also serve as opportunities to identify implementation challenges, provide centralised feedback and facilitate communication between the National Center and participating tertiary hospitals or primary healthcare institutions. Issues identified during these exchanges will be collated by the National Center, and the corresponding clarifications will be shared across participating regions to promote consistent implementation.

Second, designated leading tertiary hospitals, after receiving central training, will supervise affiliated primary healthcare institutions, provide intensive continuing medical education to GPs before study initiation using materials developed by the National Center and continue to provide less frequent training and support throughout the study period. They will also conduct on-site supervision of participating institutions and regularly review implementation quality and data completeness, with at least one review every 3 months. Feedback on protocol adherence and data quality will be provided to primary healthcare institutions through the study coordination system.

Third, participating primary healthcare institutions will serve as the frontline implementers of the intervention and will be responsible for quarterly follow-up, delivery of health education and disease management, and timely and accurate data entry into the EDC system.

Due to the nature of the intervention, neither patients nor care providers can be blinded to group allocation. However, outcome assessors and data analysts will remain blinded to treatment allocation.

Usual care (control arm)

Participants in the control arm will continue to receive their usual care, including any routine check-ups or reviews. No additional follow-up or COPD-related education will be provided in this arm. If patients in the control arm subsequently receive components of the PRIM intervention outside the trial protocol, they will remain in the primary analysis according to the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle. Such deviations from the assigned intervention will be documented, and their potential impact will be explored in sensitivity analyses. Patients in the control arm will be scheduled for a single follow-up at 12 months, which will be conducted either as a home visit or an outpatient visit. The 12-month follow-up will use the same predefined endpoint assessment procedures, outcome definitions and measurement instruments as those applied at the 12-month assessment in the intervention arm, in order to ensure comparability between arms.

Study outcomes

Details of all clinical assessment and outcomes measures are provided in table 1. GPs are informed of patients’ participation in the trial and any relevant results. The main study outcomes will be assessed at baseline and at the end of 12 months. They are listed below.

Primary outcomes

There are two prespecified co-primary outcomes of equal importance, with no hierarchical ranking between them. The first is the COPD management outcome, measured by the number of AECOPD over a 12-month period. AECOPD will be defined and graded according to the 2021 Chinese COPD guideline.10 Specifically, mild exacerbations are those requiring short-acting bronchodilator treatment only; moderate exacerbations are those requiring short-acting bronchodilators plus antibiotics and/or oral corticosteroids; and severe exacerbations are those requiring an emergency department visit, hospitalisation or ICU treatment. Accordingly, exacerbation events will be identified based on predefined criteria reflecting the level of treatment escalation and healthcare utilisation. In practice, AECOPD events will be ascertained through participant self-report at follow-up assessments by asking whether the participant has experienced any acute exacerbations since the last assessment, and participant-reported events will then be verified against available institutional medical records where feasible. This approach is intended to improve the completeness and consistency of outcome ascertainment by capturing exacerbation events that may not have been recorded in medical records, including those managed outside formal healthcare settings. Although the intervention group additionally receives routine quarterly GP-led follow-up as part of the intervention, the primary outcome of AECOPD will be determined using the same prespecified criteria and ascertainment approach in both groups, thereby minimising the risk of differential measurement bias.

The second primary outcome is self-rated health-related quality of life (HRQoL). HRQoL data will be measured using the European Quality of Life-5 Dimensions (EQ-5D).11 12 The EQ-5D defines health in terms of five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain or discomfort and anxiety or depression. In each dimension, five levels of severity can be chosen: no problems, slight problems, moderate problems, severe problems and extreme problems.

Main secondary outcomes

Smoking status

One important component of our patient education is smoking cessation. Therefore, through follow-up visits, we will assess whether COPD patients who smoke can successfully quit smoking with the help of education, and whether those who have already quit are able to maintain long-term abstinence.

Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease Assessment Test

The CAT is a validated, short (eight items) and simple patient-completed questionnaire and assesses globally the impact of COPD (cough, sputum, dyspnoea and chest tightness) on health status. The CAT is scored 0–5 with a range of 0–40; scores of 0–10, 11–20, 21–30 or 31–40 represent mild, moderate, severe or very severe clinical impact, respectively.

Degree of Dyspnoea

Dyspnoea will be measured using the Modified Medical Research Council Dyspnea Questionnaire, which assesses the level of breathlessness during daily activities. The scale ranges from 0 to 4, with a score of 4 indicating severe breathlessness with minimal activity.

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease knowledge of the general practitioners

We will assess improvements in knowledge among GPs. The Knowledge Test for general health practitioners consists of a 30-item questionnaire (see online supplemental context 1), which is completed by primary healthcare providers at baseline and again at 12 months. The questionnaire evaluates their knowledge across several domains, including risk factors, prevention, detection and treatment of COPD.

Health economic evaluations

We will conduct a within-trial cost-effectiveness analysis from a broad societal perspective, accounting for both direct (medical and non-medical) and indirect costs associated with COPD. The direct medical costs will be obtained from hospital information systems and patient-completed questionnaires. Indirect costs will be analysed using the human capital approach,13 which quantifies productivity losses from absenteeism or reduced work productivity caused by work disability. Health outcomes are measured in HRQoL derived from the EQ-5D-5L.14 15 Both cost and health outcomes will be discounted at an annual rate of 5%, which was selected as the base-case discount rate in accordance with the China Guidelines for Pharmacoeconomic Evaluations (2020).1618 Sensitivity analyses using alternative discount rates will be conducted to assess the robustness of the cost-effectiveness results.

Statistical justification for sample size

The sample size was determined based on the co-primary outcome that required the larger sample size. Among the two co-primary outcomes, HRQoL yielded the more conservative estimate under the prespecified assumptions and was therefore used for the formal sample size calculation. Assuming a minimally clinically important between-group difference of 0.03 in HRQoL, a SD of 0.22 based on previous literature,19 a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and 90% power, we estimated that 1130 participants would be required per group (2260 in total). Allowing for a 20% dropout rate increased the required sample size to 1413 participants per group (2825 in total). As this is a cluster-randomised trial, we further adjusted for clustering at the primary healthcare clinic level. Assuming an intracluster correlation coefficient of 0.01 and an average cluster size of 17, we estimated a design effect of 1.16.20 After applying the design effect, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 3277 participants.

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics will be summarised using means and SD, medians and IQRs, or frequencies and percentages, as appropriate. To evaluate the efficacy of the intervention, outcomes at follow-up will be compared between the intervention and control groups using both unadjusted and adjusted analyses with corresponding 95% CIs. Given the cluster-randomised design, clustering at the level of the primary healthcare institution will be accounted for in all regression analyses. For continuous outcomes, multivariable linear regression models will be used; for binary outcomes, multivariable logistic regression models will be used. Adjusted analyses will include prespecified baseline covariates, including urban–rural location as the stratification factor, age, sex, smoking status, baseline disease severity (eg, GOLD stage and/or mMRC category) and baseline values of the corresponding outcome where applicable. The primary analyses will follow the ITT principle. The trial will be considered to have met its primary objective only if the intervention shows benefit on both co-primary outcomes. If benefit is observed for only one of the two co-primary outcomes, the trial will not be considered to have met its primary objective, although such discordant findings will be reported and interpreted cautiously. Missing outcome data will be handled using multiple imputation under the missing-at-random assumption, and sensitivity analyses, including complete-case and per-protocol analyses, will be conducted to assess the robustness of the findings.

The primary analyses will be conducted based on the ITT principle, ensuring that all participants are analysed in the groups to which they were originally assigned, regardless of adherence to the intervention. Ancillary per-protocol analyses will also be conducted in sensitivity analyses. Contingency table analysis and analysis of variance will be used to compare categorical and continuous variables, respectively, between the two arms. The descriptive statistics will be used to examine differences in AECOPD incidence and HRQoL between two arms. Statistical software, including SAS V.9.2 and R V.3.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), will be used.

Quality assurance

Data monitoring will be conducted in three stages. The first stage will be carried out by GPs at the primary healthcare institutions. When submitting the questionnaires and clinical assessment forms, GPs will be responsible for performing preliminary logical checks according to the data validation guidelines issued by the National Center for Respiratory Medicine to ensure data accuracy.

The second stage will be managed by regional programme leads at the designated leading tertiary hospitals. They will regularly review the data uploaded by the GPs in the EDC system. Any data that do not meet quality standards will be returned to the corresponding GPs for verification with the patients, and verified data will then be forwarded by the regional leads to the National Center for Respiratory Medicine for the final stage of review.

The final stage will be conducted by an independent Data and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB), established by the National Center for Respiratory Medicine. The DSMB will comprise experts in respiratory medicine, biostatistics, epidemiology, ethics, health economics and pulmonary function testing and will be responsible for overseeing data at the third level and providing recommendations on trial continuation, modification or termination. Interim analyses will be performed in collaboration with the DSMB. The DSMB will have exclusive access to unblinded interim data and will apply prespecified stopping guidelines related to safety and feasibility. No formal interim efficacy analyses are planned. Based on the DSMB’s recommendations, the Trial Steering Committee will make the final decision regarding early termination or continuation of the trial.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public will not be involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics approval has been granted by China-Japan friendship hospital (approval number 2024-KY-163). The trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (registration number: NCT06573580). The study is conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki (Stronghold Version, Brazil, October 2013) and in accordance with the protocol and the relevant legal requirements.

All patients in the study must sign an informed consent form before any procedure. The participants can withdraw their consent at any time without altering their relationship with their physicians. The first participant has been enrolled in August 2024. Data collection is expected to end in December 2025.
