Ultrasonographic and CBCT analysis of foreign bodies and dental materials
Authors: Hasret Yuce, Gozde Acikgoz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo reveal the radiodensity and sonographic characteristics of various foreign bodies and dental materials to aid in the diagnosis of these materials.MethodsThe study included 41 objects consisting of various foreign bodies and dental materials. The objects were examined by embedding them in bovine gelatin, an in vitro environment and their radiodensity was evaluated using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT). Additionally, their visibility and posterior acoustic behaviors were examined using ultrasonography (USG).ResultsWhen the radiodensity of the objects was examined in CBCT images, 27 objects (65.9%) were found to be hyperdense, while 14 objects (34.1%) were identified as isodense. All foreign bodies and dental materials included in the study were successfully visualized using USG. When the acoustic behaviors of the objects were examined, it was found that 24 materials (58.5%) caused posterior acoustic shadowing on USG. While no artifacts were detected in eight materials (19.5%), comet tail artifacts were observed in seven materials (17%), and reverberation artifacts were seen in two materials (5%).ConclusionsCBCT is an important technique for imaging foreign bodies and dental materials. Nonetheless, in an in vitro environment, USG can visualize objects that cannot be seen with CBCT. These findings may assist clinicians in selecting the most appropriate imaging method for identifying accidentally ingested or embedded foreign bodies and dental materials, especially in emergency or surgical settings.Trial registrationWe would like to clarify that our study is an in vivo experiment that does not involve human participants. Therefore, it does not fall within the scope of clinical trial registration requirements under current international guidelines.Keywords: Artifacts, Dental materials, Diagnostic imaging, Soft tissue

Background

Foreign bodies are defined as substances that enter the body through various means and are not normally present given location. These bodies enter the body primarily through aspiration into the respiratory tract, accidental ingestion into the digestive system, entrapment within the skin and tissues, or retention in body cavities [1, 2]. In addition to trauma, traffic accidents, and gunshot wounds, the medical materials used during a medical intervention may also unintentionally penetrate body tissues. While this may not cause any issues, in some cases, foreign bodies may enter the airways, necessitating urgent intervention and potentially leading to life-threatening situations [3–6].

The aspiration, ingestion, or tissue penetration of foreign bodies and dental materials is rare during routine dental treatments, but it carries a significant risk of serious complications [2–6]. These foreign bodies and dental materials can also escape into soft tissues, the sinuses, and cavities and can localize close to important vessels or nerves [4–7]. In such cases, it is crucial to accurately determine the localization of foreign bodies and dental materials by combining the patient’s medical history with clinical and radiological examination [8].

In the radiological evaluation of foreign bodies and dental materials, conventional radiographs, computed tomography (CT), cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and ultrasonography (USG) are commonly used. While conventional radiographs, CT, and CBCT are preferred for imaging radiopaque objects, MRI and USG can be utilized for visualizing non-radiopaque objects [9].

Among these techniques, the primary imaging method used for the detection of radiopaque objects is conventional radiography. However, the main disadvantages of this technique include the superimposition of tissues along the path of the X-ray beam and, in some cases, the presence of radiolucent objects that cannot be detected in soft tissue [10]. On the other hand, compared to conventional CT, CBCT is the ideal imaging technique for cases involving suspected foreign bodies in the maxillofacial region due to its lower cost and reduced radiation exposure [11–13].

USG is a diagnostic tool that uses sound waves and does not carry the risk of ionizing radiation. Acoustic impedance, which is dependent on the density and elasticity of a tissue, interacts with the ultrasound waves, resulting in the characteristic echogenic properties of tissues. While the use of USG in dentistry is thought to be limited to the evaluation of salivary glands, cervical lymph nodes, post-operative edema and hematoma, the masticatory muscles, the tongue, and the soft tissues of the head and neck region, recent advances in high-resolution devices have expanded its application in various areas of dental radiology [14]. Today, USG’s role in imaging non-radiopaque objects is also growing. For this purpose, high-frequency (7–12 MHz) USG probes are preferred for examining superficial objects, while low-frequency (3–5 MHz) probes are used for deeper objects. Additionally, modern USG devices, combined with high-frequency (7.5–12 MHz) linear probes, can provide high-resolution images in multiple planes of the maxillofacial region [14–17]. This study was intended to examine various foreign bodies and dental materials using USG and CBCT and contribute to the clinical diagnosis of such materials in cases of complications.

Studied foreign bodies and dental materials

Some of the dental materials, such as filling materials and acrylic resin, were shaped for the study, while others, such as gutta-percha or silk suture, were used directly (Fig. 1). Efforts were made to use objects of similar sizes. One-cm-thick bovine gelatin [3, 32], which was prepared with cold water according to the manufacturer’s instructions, was poured into the base of the containers to be used for the study. After the bovine gelatin hardened, the objects were placed. Then, to cover the objects in bovine gelatin, an additional layer of approximately one cm of bovine gelatin was poured over them and allowed to harden (Fig. 2). Care was taken to avoid the formation of air gaps during these steps.

Appearance of objects embedded in bovine gelatin

Radiological examinations

After the preparation of the specimens, CBCT and USG examinations were performed by an oral and maxillofacial radiologist (H.Y.) First, a CBCT image was obtained using a Newtom 5G XL (Cefla, Imola, Italy) device with a 21 × 19 FOV (110 kVP, 3.00–8.73 mA, 3.6–5.4 s scan time, and voxel size of 0.125–0.250 mm). The radiodensity of the specimens embedded in bovine gelatin was evaluated and recorded using the proprietary NNT software of the CBCT system (Newtom, Verona, Italy) and categorized radiographically. Subsequently, Adobe Photoshop Elements (Adobe Systems, Inc., San Jose, CA, USA) was utilized to crop the CBCT images so that they would fully encompass the specimens (Figs. 3 and 4). Ultrasonographic examinations were performed using an Esaote MylabTMSeven (Esaote Medical Systems, Genoa, Italy) device with a 12-MHz linear probe. The visibility of the objects; their echogenicity, if visible; and their posterior acoustic behaviors were noted and categorized as ultrasonographic (Figs. 5 and 6). Posterior acoustic behaviors are characteristic findings observed on ultrasound images posterior to the specimens. These include acoustic shadowing, which appears as a dark area behind dense structures due to the attenuation of sound waves; reverberation, characterized by repeated linear echoes resulting from multiple reflections; and comet tail artifact, seen as bright, short linear echoes typically caused by small, highly reflective structures.

Results

When the visibility of the objects in CBCT was evaluated, alginate, porcelain, zirconia, interdental brush, calcium hydroxide, zinc phosphate cement, glass ionomer cement, dental resin composite, amalgam, canal paste, endodontic H-file, metal post, fiber post, gutta percha, dental crown, nickel-titanium orthodontic wire, wooden-graphite pencil, piece of chicken bone, tooth root, natural stone, bottle glass, fishbone, orthodontic bracket, window glass, dental injector needle, steel orthodontic wire, and pin were visualized in CBCT. On the other hand, warm acrylic, cold acrylic, gauze, wax, wedge, cardboard, paper-point, chickpea, orthodontic rubber band, latex, wooden toothpick, dental floss, silk suture, and plastic were not visible in CBCT. When the radiodensity of the objects was examined, 27 (65.9%) objects were found to be hyperdense, while 14 (34.1%) objects were identified as isodense.

Discussion

Foreign bodies can enter the body involuntarily through various routes, such as aspiration, ingestion, or penetration into the skin or tissue. In such cases, they often lead to emergency department visits [18]. Depending on the type and location of the foreign body, different imaging modalities, such as conventional radiography, CT, CBCT, or USG, can be used for detection. In radiological evaluation, accurately determining the characteristics of foreign bodies, such as their location, shape, size, and density, is crucial for their safe removal. Therefore, selecting the appropriate radiological technique plays a critical role [19]. In radiological imaging, the visibility and radiodensity of materials are critical. Radiodensity is a measure of the degree to which a substance absorbs X-rays. In CBCT, radiodensity largely depends on the density and composition of the scanned material. However, factors such as the voxel size and X-ray energy used in CBCT also influence radiodensity [20, 21].

CBCT is widely preferred for evaluating the radiodensity of dental materials and foreign bodies due to its high spatial resolution [12, 22]. In our study, when the radiodensity of the objects was evaluated using CBCT, alginate, porcelain, zirconia, interdental brush, calcium hydroxide, zinc phosphate cement, glass ionomer cement, dental resin composite, amalgam, canal paste, endodontic H-file, metal post, fiber post, gutta-percha, dental crown, nickel-titanium orthodontic wire, wood-graphite pencil, a piece of chicken bone, tooth root, natural stone, bottle glass, fish bone, orthodontic bracket, window glass, dental injector needle, steel orthodontic wire, and pin were observed to be hyperdense compared to bovine gelatin. This finding is consistent with the results reported by Isman and Isman [12], who demonstrated that most orthodontic materials appeared highly radiodense on CBCT [12]. Similarly, Demiralp et al. [8] also observed that amalgam, dental composites, metal objects, and teeth presented with high radiodensity in CBCT images [8].

Conversely, warm acrylic, cold acrylic, gauze, wedge, cardboard, paper point, chickpea, plastic, wax, orthodontic rubber band, latex, wooden toothpick, dental floss, and silk suture exhibited radiodensity similar to or the same as bovine gelatin and were thus considered isodense. This observation aligns with the findings of Isman and Isman [12], who reported that plastic and organic-based materials typically show isodense characteristics in CBCT [12]. Moreover, Demiralp et al. [8] also noted that materials such as wood, acrylic, and plastic appeared with low density in CBCT imaging [8].

The ability to visualize a material radiographically also depends on the surrounding environment. In other words, the structures surrounding the material, both above and below, play significant roles in its radiographic visibility [23]. Halverson and Servaes [23] found that plastic foreign bodies were visible on radiographs when surrounded by air, but not when surrounded by fluid. This indicates that materials can exhibit different imaging characteristics and radiodensities depending on the body environments in which they are located [23]. Certainly, this represents a limitation of the current study. However, the aim of this study is not only to determine the radiodensity of the objects but also to identify the ultrasonographic characteristics of all the foreign bodies and dental materials used, including those that could not be visualized in CBCT due to isodensity.

USG is a safe, inexpensive, portable, and readily accessible imaging method for the visualization of foreign bodies and dental materials, and it has the advantage of not involving the use of ionizing radiation [24]. For imaging the head and neck region, rectangular, flat-surfaced linear probes are the most suitable option, as they are beneficial in terms of visualizing superficial structures and small objects [25]. Additionally, high-frequency ultrasound probes (7–12 MHz) are more effective for examining superficial materials, while low-frequency probes (3–5 MHz) are preferred for assessing deeper materials [16]. This is because, according to the principles of USG, as the frequency decreases, the penetration of the ultrasound waves increases, but the superficial resolution decreases [9, 14]. However, the evaluation of foreign bodies using ultrasonography (USG) also presents certain limitations, including operator dependency, difficulties in visualizing objects located deep within tissues or behind gas-containing structures, artifact formation, and the inability to distinguish some materials due to their low acoustic impedance [26, 27]. Additionally, the angle of the probe and the positioning of the material during USG imaging directly affect image quality. These factors indicate that USG may have diagnostic limitations in certain cases [26, 27].

In this study, materials embedded in superficial bovine gelatin were examined using a high-frequency (12-MHz) linear probe. The results of the evalution indicated that all foreign bodies and dental materials were successfully visualized with USG. The inability of materials such as warm acrylic, cold acrylic, gauze, wax, wedge, cardboard, paper-point, chickpea, orthodontic rubber band, latex, wooden toothpick, dental floss, silk suture, and plastic to be visualized in CBCT, yet being visible in USG, stems from the different physical principles of these two imaging techniques [8]. CBCT relies on the absorption of X-rays by dense structures, which can limit its ability to detect low-density materials. In contrast, USG measures the reflection of sound waves based on tissue density, enabling clearer visualization of such materials. This highlights USG’s superior imaging capability, especially in soft tissue-like environments [3]. However, CBCT offers significant advantages in the detailed localization of high-density objects in deep tissues and in three-dimensional assessment. Therefore, the complementary use of both techniques provides a more comprehensive and accurate clinical evaluation [3, 28].

While the literature reports that studies on the detection of materials predominantly identify similar imaging characteristics on the part of these materials, one potential reason for the occasionally contradictory findings of these studies may be the use of materials of varying sizes [3, 8, 9, 29]. In our study, although the sizes of the materials used were prepared similarly to those used in previous studies, care was taken to ensure that they had comparable dimensions. Another reason for the discrepancies seen in studies related to material detection is the use of homogeneous backgrounds, such as bovine gelatin, cadaver thighs, and meat cubes, which requires the identification of only a single tissue interface. In contrast, heterogeneous backgrounds, such as a sheep’s head, necessitate the identification of multiple tissue interfaces (e.g., muscle, bone, fat, and tendon) [3, 8, 9, 29]. Given close proximity, one interface can obscure or eliminate a second interface with its acoustic shadow [8]. Additionally, Valizadeh et al. [10] report that while various foreign bodies in the tongue and soft tissue–bone interfaces can be detected and localized using USG, they were not identifiable in air-filled spaces [10].

In a study conducted by Aras et al. [9] using sheep skulls in a laboratory setting, they examined both radiopaque and radiolucent foreign bodies ultrasonographically using a single linear probe, finding that all foreign bodies appeared to be hyperechoic within the muscle tissue. They also reported that foreign bodies between the bone surface and muscle tissue could be visualized but that those in air-filled cavities, such as the sinuses, could not be detected. They suggested that CT is a more effective imaging modality with which to visualize foreign bodies in these regions [9].

In a similar study conducted by Demiralp et al. [8] in a laboratory setting using sheep skulls, they examined the diagnostic accuracy of intraoral and extraoral USG examinations performed using linear and convex probes. They reported that linear probe USG should be the first choice in diagnosing low-radiodensity foreign bodies in soft tissue. However, they noted that CBCT is a more sensitive technique than USG in terms of detecting high-radiodensity foreign bodies in air-filled areas and bone tissue [8]. In their study, they also utilized relatively few dental materials, as in our research. Comparable results were obtained for foreign bodies in dental materials as well, but there are limited studies in the literature that focus on the use of dental materials [3, 8]. In this context, our study included of a wide variety of dental materials, and it was found that all dental materials appeared to be hyperechoic on USG.

During the visualization of objects via USG, various posterior acoustic phenomena can affect the image, including acoustic shadowing, acoustic enhancement, reverberation, ring-down artifacts, and comet tail artifacts [3, 15]. These posterior acoustic behaviors can negatively impact image quality; however, they can also assist in the diagnosis of certain objects. For instance, calcified bodies can be easily distinguished by their acoustic shadowing effects [3]. In this study, materials such as warm acrylic, cold acrylic, gauze, wax, calcium hydroxide, zinc phosphate cement, glass ionomer cement, cardboard, canal paste, dental crown, wooden-graphite pencil, chickpea, a piece of chicken bone, natural stone, wooden toothpicks, and plastic exhibited acoustic shadowing, which facilitated their differentiation. The formation of acoustic shadowing by these objects is due to their high density or rigid structure. Such objects either do not allow sound waves to pass through or reflect them to a large extent, leading to the reflection of sound waves and causing the formation of an acoustic shadow behind them [14]. Horton et al., [15] who investigated the posterior acoustic behaviors of foreign bodies, similarly reported that objects such as wood, plastic, and stone create acoustic shadows on USG, while glass and metal produce ring-down artifacts [15]. In our study, comet tail artifacts were observed in materials such as alginate, porcelain, zirconia, metal posts, and nickel-titanium orthodontic wires. Comet tail artifacts are reflections caused by closely spaced, highly reflective surfaces, such as colloid nodules, calcifications, and surgical clips. These artifacts result from the repeated occurrence of these echoes [30]. Additionally, reverberation was observed in bottle glass and window glass. In a study conducted by Caglayan and Yozgat Ilbas [3], as in our research, a relatively small number of foreign bodies and dental materials embedded in bovine gelatin were used to examine the sonographic characteristics of these objects. The results of their study support our findings, although they classified only the suture material as anechoic on USG. In contrast, our study shows that the silk suture material appears to be hyperechoic. This suggests that the visibility of objects in USG can be influenced by their size or the angle of the probe, even when placed in similar environments. In our study, the interdental brush, fiber post, paper point, gutta-percha, orthodontic rubber band, fishbone, dental floss, dental injector needle, silk suture, and pin appeared not to produce echoes based on our examination. A common characteristic of these objects is their very fine nature, which prevents them from reflecting sound waves. The results indicate that the size of an object can affect its posterior acoustic behavior in USG [3, 14, 31]. For example, when metallic objects, such as dental injector needles or pins, are positioned in the cross-section through which the ultrasound wave passes, they block the ultrasound due to their high reflectivity, creating acoustic shadows in the image. Additionally, reverberation artifacts may occur depending on the position of these objects, the angle of the probe, or their placement between reflective surfaces [3, 32].

The literature reports that various foreign bodies and dental materials, such as rubber dam clamps, files, gutta-percha, endodontic instruments, extracted teeth and root fragments, orthodontic wires, and brackets, can enter the body during dental treatments [33–38]. For example, the fragments of amalgam, dental resin composite, or cement may migrate into the soft tissues during a restorative procedure [33, 36]. To prevent such complications, the use of protective measures particularly rubber dams during restorative and endodontic procedure is of great importances [2]. Additionally, surgeons may sometimes accidentally leave foreign bodies, such as gauze or metallic objects, in a patient’s body during surgical interventions, such as dental implant placements [38]. In such cases, USG can provide extremely valuable information [33–38]. Furthermore, because USG can be utilized not only for the examination of superficial soft tissues but also for the endoscopic and endobronchial assessment of the respiratory and digestive tracts, it can be valuable for the detection of radiolucent foreign bodies [39, 40].

A significant limitation of this study is that the materials used were not examined within live tissue. Additionally, the use of only a single sample for each material limits the ability to assess potential variations in their ultrasonographic and CBCT characteristics. Live tissues, due to their vascularity, can be of great clinical value in the assessment of foreign bodies. Conclusions.

In conclusion, all materials included in the study were successfully visualized using USG. This study, to the best of our knowledge, has been conducted using a greater number and variety of objects compared to similar studies. The use of different types of objects allowed for the evaluation of sonographic and CBCT imaging characteristics over a broader spectrum, thereby providing a detailed explanation of the sonographic properties of foreign bodies and dental materials. This has made a significant contribution to the literature, particularly with the support of CBCT images for high radiodensity materials. The diversity of materials used has enhanced the generalizability of the obtained data and provided guidance for a wider range of potential clinical scenarios. In this context, it is recommended that future studies include a variety of materials and be conducted on live tissues. This would allow for a better simulation of real clinical scenarios, leading to more reliable and practical outcomes.
