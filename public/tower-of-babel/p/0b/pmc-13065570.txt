The Critical Role of Oxygen Supplementation in Epithelium-On Corneal Cross-Linking: A Narrative Review
Authors: Christopher J. Rapuano, Kenneth A. Beckman, Rajesh Rajpal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Keratoconus is a progressive corneal disorder characterized by thinning and steepening of the cornea, leading to visual impairment and reduced quality of life. Although epithelium-off corneal cross-linking has emerged as the gold-standard therapy, it can be associated with postoperative pain, delayed healing, and increased risk of complications due to removal of the corneal epithelium. To overcome these limitations, epithelium-on cross-linking was developed as a less invasive alternative. In epithelium-on cross-linking, three essential components must be present to maximize the photochemical reaction: oxygen, riboflavin formulation with permeation enhancers, and high-irradiance pulsed ultraviolet-A (UV-A) light. Oxygen plays an especially critical rate-limiting role, as it interacts with riboflavin and UV-A irradiation to produce the reactive oxygen species (ROS) that promote covalent cross-links, thereby strengthening the corneal stroma. Given that the intact epithelium during epithelium-on cross-linking can act as a barrier to oxygen diffusion, in addition to riboflavin penetration, enhancing oxygen availability during epithelium-on cross-linking has been shown to improve treatment outcomes. Oxygen-enriched epithelium-on cross-linking is poised to emerge as a mainstay therapy for keratoconus. This narrative review examines the influence of oxygen supplementation, a key rate-limiting factor, in enhancing the efficacy of epithelium-on cross-linking.

Introduction

Keratoconus is a progressive corneal disorder characterized by thinning and steepening of the cornea, resulting in irregular astigmatism and eventually visual impairment [1–3]. Keratoconus typically emerges during early adolescence and can continue to progress into older age groups, affecting patients’ quality of life [1, 4–7]. Globally, the prevalence of keratoconus was estimated to be approximately 1.4 per 1000 individuals (0.14%), although rates vary widely because of the demographic, ethnicity, and diagnostic methods used [8, 9]. In the USA, the prevalence of keratoconus was 0.03% in 2016 and 0.04% in 2019, and highest among individuals aged 18–39 years [7].

Treatment decisions for keratoconus are strongly influenced by the severity of the disease and rate of progression [1, 10]. Mild to moderate symptoms in keratoconus are often treated with eyeglasses, soft contact lenses, and, in some patients, specialized contact lenses, such as rigid gas-permeable, hybrid, or scleral lenses; however, these refractive corrections neither halt disease progression nor preserve visual acuity in the long term. In more advanced cases, corneal collagen cross-linking; refractive procedures such as intracorneal ring segments, corneal allogenic intrastromal ring segments, or photorefractive keratectomy; and corneal transplantation are considered [1, 11, 12].

Among the surgical options for the treatment of keratoconus, cross-linking surgery remains the primary treatment of choice for progressive keratoconus in the US. It has demonstrated the ability to halt or slow disease progression, making it the gold-standard treatment for keratoconus [13–19].

The epithelium-off cross-linking surgical method involves mechanical removal of the central corneal epithelium to allow adequate riboflavin absorption into the stroma and ultraviolet-A (UV-A) irradiation to achieve the cross-linking process [20, 21]; however, this procedure requires > 60 min to complete [21]. To reduce patient discomfort, continuous accelerated UV irradiance was introduced to shorten procedure time, but the accelerated oxygen consumption in the corneal stroma at the elevated irradiance levels using this approach resulted in reduced corneal stiffening [22, 23], indicating that the cross-linking process is strongly oxygen-dependent [24, 25]. This is further supported by findings from Wang et al., wherein enhanced oxygen concentration significantly improved the stiffness of ex vivo corneas [26]. Furthermore, pulsed crosslinking was introduced to address oxygen depletion associated with continuous UV-A exposure using an intermittent UV light pattern. Accelerated cross-linking protocols with pulsed UV-A intensities can enhance clinical efficiency and improve patient compliance by shortening treatment duration to approximately 20–25 min [27–29]. Despite its effectiveness, epithelium-off cross-linking is associated with several disadvantages and potential complications because of the removal of the corneal epithelium. These include significant postoperative pain; delayed epithelium healing and visual recovery; and increased risk of corneal infection, endothelial damage, sterile infiltrates, and subepithelial haze or scarring [30, 31]. Moreover, this procedure may not be feasible in patients with very thin corneas [32]. To minimize the risks associated with epithelial debridement, transepithelial or epithelium-on cross-linking was developed [13, 32, 33]. A key challenge of epithelium-on cross-linking is that the intact epithelium acts as a lipophilic barrier, hindering riboflavin penetration and oxygen replenishment into the corneal stroma [34]. To overcome these limitations, studies have explored techniques to enhance riboflavin penetration into the deeper corneal layers (iontophoresis-assisted cross-linking or chemical enhancers), the use of pulsed, higher-intensity UV-A irradiation to slow oxygen consumption, and supplemental oxygen to maintain an oxygen-rich environment to improve treatment efficacy [35–37]. This review aims to explore the impact of supplementing oxygen in enhancing the efficacy of epithelium-on cross-linking for the treatment of keratoconus.

Search Strategy

The objective of this review is to describe the available evidence about the role of oxygen in corneal cross-linking. Here, we focus on studies investigating the effects of oxygen supplementation in enhancing the efficacy of epithelium-on cross-linking for the treatment of keratoconus. We also discuss epithelium-off cross-linking, including its disadvantages. Relevant studies were identified from peer-reviewed journals using PubMed and Google Scholar, including peer-reviewed, published research reports, systematic reviews, and review articles. The following keywords were included as search terms: “corneal cross-linking,” “keratoconus,” “oxygen,” “riboflavin,” and “pulsed ultraviolet A.” The references in each publication were also reviewed for potential relevance. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Biochemistry of Corneal Cross-Linking

Cross-linking is a complex biochemical procedure that uses a riboflavin (vitamin B2) formulation containing permeation enhancers that temporarily loosen cell junctions, combined with UV-A irradiation and oxygen, to strengthen the cornea [38–40]. To maximize treatment effect, all three components should be optimized (riboflavin formulation, UV-A irradiation, and oxygen). The underlying mechanism of cross-linking involves two types of photochemical pathways: type 2 (aerobic) and type 1 (anaerobic) [24, 41–43]. In type 2 (oxygen-dependent) reactions, riboflavin, upon exposure to UV-A irradiation in the presence of oxygen, undergoes a photochemical reaction that triggers the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as singlet oxygen [24, 25, 44], which induce intra- and intermolecular covalent bonds between collagen fibers and proteoglycans, thereby enhancing stromal stiffness and strengthening the cornea [41]. After oxygen depletion, a type 1 reaction occurs, where riboflavin absorbs UV-A radiation, gets excited to the triplet state, and interacts with stromal proteins to generate ROS [39], which further interacts with collagen proteins to form covalent cross-links, thereby enhancing corneal stiffness (Fig. 1) [21, 45–47]. In addition, crosslinking of collagen fibers significantly enhances the cornea’s resistance to enzymatic digestion [48, 49]. This is particularly relevant in conditions like keratoconus, where collagen degradation caused by enzymes contributes to corneal thinning [38, 48].Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the conventional cross-linking procedure. Multiple studies have highlighted the importance of maintaining a high stromal oxygen concentration, as it promotes the production of singlet oxygen radicals. These radicals enhance photochemical cross-linking reactions, resulting in the formation of more covalent bonds and ultimately strengthening the cornea [24, 25, 34, 50]. Furthermore, by stabilizing the corneal structure, corneal cross-linking plays a crucial role in halting keratoconus progression, preserving visual function, reducing the need for corneal transplantation, and improving long-term outcomes for patients with progressive keratoconus [18, 51]. This finding underscores that oxygen availability within the corneal stroma is a key limiting factor in the cross-linking photochemical reaction. However, maintaining adequate oxygen availability during epithelium-on corneal cross-linking remains a significant challenge.

Oxygen Challenge in Epithelium-on Cross-Linking

The rate of cross-linking reaction is oxygen-dependent and depends on the diffusion of atmospheric oxygen into the stroma [41, 52]. During the epithelium-on cross-linking procedure, intact epithelium acts as a natural barrier, limiting the penetration of riboflavin, UV-A radiation, and oxygen-3 components, which are essential for effective cross-linking [34, 53]. Notably, the corneal epithelium absorbs nearly ten times more oxygen than the corneal stroma [36]. Upon UV-A irradiation, stromal oxygen levels are rapidly depleted to zero within 15 s [43, 54].

During accelerated UV irradiation, atmospheric oxygen diffuses into the stroma more slowly than it is consumed by the UV-riboflavin reaction, which limits the biomechanical effect of cross-linking, indicating that oxygen plays a pivotal role in the procedure [41, 52]. Previous studies have shown that performing cross-linking in a hypoxic environment reduces its biomechanical effectiveness, whereas higher oxygen availability enhances treatment efficacy [43, 53]. To address these limitations, novel strategies have been proposed to enhance oxygen availability in the corneal stroma and thereby improve treatment efficacy. These include oxygen supplementation to increase corneal oxygen diffusion rate [35, 43, 53] and pulsed UV-A radiation to reduce stromal oxygen consumption [55, 56].

Oxygen Supplementation Technologies and Approaches

Supplementing oxygen to the corneal surface during cross-linking has been shown to enhance the strength and depth of cross-linking [52, 57]. In addition, pulsing UV-A irradiation enhances the biomechanical effect of cross-linking by maintaining higher stromal oxygen concentration [34, 58]. Evidence suggests that combining oxygen goggles with pulsed, high-intensity UV-A irradiation enhances the overall outcomes of epithelium-on cross-linking compared with continuous UV-A irradiation without supplemental oxygen [35].

Another device used is an aspirating eyelid speculum, which is inserted after sterile draping of the eye to deliver oxygen through a humidifier bottle. Oxygen administered at a regulated flow rate of 2 l/min is directed to the ocular surface through holes in the eyelid speculum [60]. A protective frame is placed around the eye to increase corneal exposure to a continuous flow of concentrated oxygen while minimizing oxygen leakage. Alternatively, a facial mask is placed under the adhesive drape to deliver supplemental oxygen. ​The mask is connected to an oxygen source, providing oxygen at a rate of 5 l per minute during the UV-A irradiation phase of the accelerated CXL procedure [61].

Pulsed Ultraviolet-A Irradiation

Pulsed UV-A irradiation is another approach aimed at enhancing cross-linking efficacy. Unlike continuous high-intensity UV-A irradiation, which rapidly depletes stromal oxygen levels, pulsed UV-A irradiation allows oxygen replenishment between cycles, enabling a more effective aerobic process that increases the production of free radicals and enhances the biomechanical effect of cross-linking [34, 58]. A study by Yousif et al. compared pulsed light cross-linking (PL-CXL) and continuous light cross-linking (CL-CXL) protocols in patients with mild to moderate keratoconus. Although no significant differences were observed between the two protocols in terms of visual acuity, refractive outcomes, or keratometric parameters postoperatively, the demarcation line was slightly deeper in the PL-CXL group than in the CL-CXL group [62]. Sun et al. conducted an interventional case series using accelerated pulsed UV-A irradiation (45 mW/cm2) for epithelium-on cross-linking to enhance stromal oxygen availability and observed significant improvements in visual acuity and stable corneal parameters without complications during 1-year follow-up [56]. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Qureshi et al. concluded that PL-CXL improves visual acuity and keratometry outcomes in keratoconus, although differences in maximum keratometry (Kmax) and corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) were not statistically significant between PL-CXL and CL-CXL [58].

Although these strategies increase the oxygen availability in the corneal stroma, it is important to monitor oxygen concentration during corneal cross-linking, as oxygen is a key substrate in the photochemical reaction that strengthens corneal collagen fibers through the generation of ROS. Insufficient oxygen reduces the efficiency of cross-link formation, limiting treatment effectiveness and long-term stability [52]. This underscores the importance of closely monitoring and optimizing oxygen availability throughout the procedure.

Real-Time Oxygen Monitoring During Cross-Linking

Real-time monitoring of intrastromal oxygen levels during cross-linking can be performed using specialized oxygen meters. In one approach, oxygen levels can be measured with a fiber-optic oxygen microsensor (NTH-PSt7, PreSens Precision Sensing GmbH; Regensburg, Germany), which is housed in a stainless-steel needle and connected to a compatible oxygen meter (OXY-4, PreSens Precision Sensing GmbH). The sensor is inserted into the corneal stroma through a tunnel created using a femtosecond laser (Ziemer Ophthalmic Systems AG, Port, Switzerland) at a consistent depth of 300 μm in each cornea [52, 53, 63]. In an alternative approach, an oxygen meter is used to measure oxygen levels within the goggles positioned over the treated eye, ensuring that the oxygen level remains above a certain percentage; the goggles are connected to a bubble humidifier linked to a medical-grade oxygen source, delivering oxygen at a specific controlled flow rate [59].

Advancement in corneal collagen cross-linking has revolutionized the management of keratoconus. Emerging evidence suggests that the use of supplemental oxygen in corneal cross-linking enhances treatment effectiveness in keratoconus. Although numerous studies have evaluated epithelium-off cross-linking, only a limited number of clinical studies have assessed the effectiveness of oxygen-supplemented epithelium-on cross-linking protocols.

Clinical Evidence Supporting Oxygen Supplementation

Clinical evidence suggests that oxygen supplementation enhances the effectiveness of epithelium-on cross-linking by improving keratometry and visual outcomes without causing any serious adverse events (Table 3) [43, 54, 57, 64]. A meta-analysis by Borchert et al., evaluating the effect of increased oxygen availability on the corneal stroma during cross-linking demonstrated that enhancing the oxygen concentration during epithelium-on cross-linking improved visual acuity (0.08 logMAR; 95% CI 0.02–0.13; P = 0.01) and reduced keratometry (– 1.2 D; 95% CI 0.2–2.3; P = 0.02) at 6 months without causing any serious adverse events [57]. In a randomized clinical trial, Faramarzi et al. evaluated the effectiveness of combining systemic oxygen supplementation with accelerated cross-linking in patients with progressive keratoconus. Studies on epithelium-on corneal cross-linking reported inconsistent results. For example, Choo et al. and Cronin et al. (2022) observed no significant changes in Kmax, while others showed improvements in Kmax and CDVA [35, 54]. Similarly, Choo et al. reported a mean demarcation line depth (DLD) of 367.3 ± 89.8 µm, while Matthys et al. reported a mean DLD of 316 ± 63 µm [43, 54]. These may be attributed to differences in patient populations or measurement techniques. Moreover, these studies had several limitations such as differences in the oxygen delivery methods (e.g., goggles, facemasks, and systemic oxygen), shorter durations (6–12 months), and smaller sample sizes. The results from short-term follow-up may be less clinically relevant, given the time required for corneal remodeling after the corneal cross-linking procedure. Future research should focus on large-scale, randomized controlled trials with longer follow-up periods to validate the efficacy and safety of epithelium-on cross-linking.

Studies comparing epithelium-on with epithelium-off corneal cross-linking for progressive keratoconus reported no significant difference in efficacy as measured by Kmax and CDVA [13, 30, 65–67]. Epithelium-off showed more improvement in uncorrected distance visual acuity and higher reduction in central corneal thickness than epithelium-on [65, 68]. However, multiple studies reported higher rates of adverse events with epithelium-off corneal cross-linking than epithelium-on [30, 66]. The evolving innovations of corneal cross-linking demonstrate a consistent drive towards developing safer, quicker, and more efficient treatments. While the original Dresden protocol provided a solid foundation, the future direction of corneal cross-linking focuses on less or non-invasive methods, faster protocols, broader application, and customization.

Future Directions in Oxygen-Enriched Cross-Linking

Cross-linking is advancing rapidly as the gold-standard treatment for keratoconus and other corneal ectasias, with innovations focused on improving efficacy, safety, and patient comfort. The advent of epithelium-on cross-linking techniques marked a significant step forward, offering a less invasive alternative to conventional epithelium-off cross-linking protocols. In recent years, substantial advancements have been made to improve the effectiveness of epithelium-on cross-linking by addressing the key challenges of limited riboflavin stromal penetration and restricted oxygen availability. An oxygen-enriched epithelium-on cross-linking method was designed to overcome the limitations of conventional epithelium-on cross-linking protocols by optimizing riboflavin penetration, oxygen diffusion, and UV-A irradiation (Fig. 2). The epithelium-on cross-linking method recently approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) utilizes a proprietary, novel, bio-activated riboflavin formulation containing a permeation enhancer together with supplemental oxygen and high-irradiance pulsed UV-A exposure to enhance cross-linking efficacy [75]. Epithelium-on cross-linking offers a more comfortable and efficient alternative to conventional epithelium-off cross-linking, with reduced procedure time, improved patient comfort, and shorter recovery time [76]. The epithelium-on oxygen-enriched cross-linking method is designed to create optimal clinical workflow and patient compliance. Figure 3 summarizes the clinical workflow for the FDA-approved epithelium-on oxygen-enriched corneal cross-linking protocol [77]. In addition, unlike epithelium-off cross-linking, the FDA indication for the currently-approved epithelium-on oxygen-enriched cross-linking treatment does not require disease progression to be demonstrated prior to treatment, facilitating earlier intervention. Another emerging approach is the customized, topography-guided (TG) procedure, which customizes UV-A energy delivery based on corneal topography, concentrating higher doses on the keratoconic apex to strengthen the weakest areas of the cornea [34]. Similarly, phototherapeutic keratectomy (PTK)-assisted customized cross-linking (PACE) combines tailored epithelium-on and PTK-assisted epithelium-off cross-linking to individualize the corneal flattening and improve visual outcomes [78]. The future of oxygen-enriched cross-linking involves personalized protocols that adjust oxygen delivery and UV-A intensity based on individual corneal features such as thickness, biomechanics, and disease progression, aiming to improve effectiveness and reduce risks. Integration of real-time oxygen monitoring and artificial intelligence-driven treatment planning [79, 80] could further refine the precision of oxygen-enriched cross-linking, ensuring optimal outcomes for each patient.

Technological innovations are rapidly progressing toward novel approaches that not only broaden the potential uses of corneal cross-linking but also highlight the critical role of ongoing research in refining and extending its applications.

Expansion to Other Indications

While cross-linking is primarily used to halt the progression of keratoconus, its applications are widening across various corneal pathologies. It has shown efficacy in managing post-laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis ectasia (PLE), with several studies reporting that cross-linking can safely stabilize corneal biomechanics in PLE, halting progression and improving visual function [81, 82]. Epithelium-on cross-linking may be particularly helpful in this group, as they already have thinner corneas due to LASIK, and ectasias are more likely to occur in LASIK patients who start with thinner corneas. Another potentially important application is photoactivated chromophore for infectious keratitis-corneal cross-linking (PACK-CXL), which has emerged as an alternative to antimicrobial therapies for treating infectious keratitis (bacterial or fungal) [83, 84]. PACK-CXL leverages the synergistic antimicrobial effect of UV-A irradiation and riboflavin. UV-A irradiation disrupts microbial DNA and RNA, inhibiting microbial replication, while photoactivated riboflavin generates ROS that damage cell membranes and nucleic acids [85].

Cross-linking has also been used in the treatment of corneal neovascularization, an important risk factor for immune-mediated rejection of corneal allografts [86, 87]. Clinical evidence suggests that performing cross-linking before transplantation significantly improves allograft survival [87]. Corneal cross-linking induces regression of blood and lymphatic vessels through reaction of riboflavin and ultraviolet A light, which releases reactive oxygen radicals, causing apoptosis of vascular endothelial cells and keratocytes. Additionally, corneal cross-linking reduces neovascularization, thereby improving graft survival in high-risk penetrating keratoplasty [87–89]. In addition, cross-linking has been used in the treatment of bullous keratopathy to reduce stromal edema and alleviate pain. Through photopolymerization, it increases corneal stromal rigidity and exerts an antiedematous effect, thereby preventing or reversing bullous keratopathy [90–93]. Pretreatment of donor corneas with cross-linking strengthens stromal collagen fibers, making the tissue more resistant to enzymatic degradation and keratolysis, which are common causes of corneal melting, thereby limiting graft failure after Boston keratoprosthesis [94, 95].

Conclusion

Over the past 2 decades, the therapeutic landscape for keratoconus has evolved considerably, fueled by innovations in cross-linking techniques. Although the original Dresden protocol laid a strong foundation for halting keratoconus progression, current research is steering the field toward more sophisticated, technology-integrated, and patient-tailored approaches to provide the benefits of cross-linking to the most patients who can benefit. Modifications to the treatment approach include the development of specialized riboflavin formulations that enhance stromal penetration. Oxygen supplementation has emerged as a pivotal advancement in epithelium-on cross-linking, addressing one of the primary biochemical limitations of this less invasive technique—the depletion of stromal oxygen during UV-A irradiation. By increasing oxygen availability, supplemental technologies such as oxygen goggles and oxygen-emitting eyelid speculums, along with pulsed UV-A protocols, improve the efficiency of the aerobic photochemical pathway, thus enhancing biomechanical strengthening of the cornea. Clinical studies consistently demonstrate that epithelium-on oxygen-enriched cross-linking is safe and effective, with significant improvements in keratometric stability and visual acuity. These advances affirm the role of epithelium-on oxygen-enriched cross-linking as a mainstay therapy not only for keratoconus but also for other corneal disorders.
