Local transmission of arboviral infections in the south of France and prevention paradigms - status quo August 2025
Authors: Philippe Gautret, Patricia Schlagenhauf
Published: 2025

Transmission of arboviruses by local Aedes aIbopictus is not uncommon in metropolitan France, resulting notably in a significant number of autochthonous dengue cases in recent years [1]. The Aedes mosquito has now colonised 81 of the 96 departments of metropolitan France [2]. First Ae. albopictus were observed in south-east France in 2004 and the first cases of autochthonous dengue and chikungunya were reported in the South of France in 2010 [3]. From 2010 to 2021, 48 cases of local dengue and 31 cases of local chikungunya were notified, corresponding to a mean annual number of 4.0 and 2.6 cases, respectively. In 2022 to 2024 a notable increase in dengue cases was observed with 194 notified cases (64.7/year), while a single case of chikungunya was reported [3]. Only 3 cases of autochthonous Zika were documented so far, all in 2019 [3]. The vast majority of these arboviral diseases were acquired in the South of France mainly on the French Riviera [3].

In May this year, the intensified seasonal surveillance system was started in France. As of the August 5, 2025, local transmission of 9 cases of dengue and 63 cases of chikungunya were reported by Santé Publique France [3]. This large 2025 increase in the number of locally acquired chikungunya cases is likely the result of the epidemic in the Reunion Island with 704 cases imported to mainland France from 1 May to 5 August 2025 out of a total of 892 imported cases over this period of time [3]. Infected travellers, returning from French Overseas Territories where outbreaks of arboviral infections are ongoing, constitute viremic hosts for local French Ae. albopictus mosquitoes. The number of chikungunya cases in la Reunion is now largely decreasing [4].

From May 1st to August 5th, 2025, 698 imported dengue cases were notified in mainland France, mostly acquired in the French Caribbean Islands and in French Polynesia [3]. Currently, the epidemic situation in the French Caribbean is inter-epidemic with sporadic cases in Martinique and isolated foci in Guadeloupe [5]. The number of cases in French Polynesia tends to decrease but it is still significant [6].

The transmission season for dengue and chikungunya in mainland France goes May to October and reaches its peak in September [1]. This article reflects the situation in mid August 2025 and further locally transmitted cases of dengue and chikungunya are to be expected. Mosquito spraying programs are implemented in the vicinity where local transmission is detected to avoid further cases. Nevertheless, the successful adaptation of the vector, particularly in the South of France will likely favor the rise of locally acquired arboviral infections, as already observed during the past 3 years. Given the number of international tourists visiting the French Riviera, travel-associated cases where dengue or chikungunya are acquired in France, may also occur. This situation has already been observed in a paper describing cases of dengue in Dutch tourists who visited the Var department, in the South of France, in 2020 [7].

Autochthonous dengue and/or chikungunya transmission has also been reported in other European countries beyond France, including Italy, Spain and Croatia [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. The ambient temperature is one of the most important environmental factors influencing the mosquito-borne transmission viruses. The optimal daily average temperature for dengue and chikungunya virus transmission by Ae. Albopictus, in temperate zones of the northern hemisphere is at 24–26 °C; however, transmission might occur within the temperature range of 12–30 °C [12]. Such temperatures are very common during summer in these countries. Furthermore, annual rainfall of above 500 mm is conducive to the establishment of Ae albopictus and this species has also adapted to temperate winters and thrives in small reservoirs such as plant containers common in urban areas. Public health measures and integrated vector management programmes are important measures to combat mosquitoes but are resource consuming and their effectiveness varies [13].

On an individual protection level, conventional live-attenuated dengue vaccines, while partially efficacious, face critical hurdles including serotype imbalances and antibody-dependent enhancement [14]. Currently only one vaccine, Qdenga, with a two-dose schedule is available. In December 2022, Qdenga received EMA approval for prevention of dengue in travellers aged 4 years travelling to high-risk areas irrespective of prior exposure to infection. In May 2024, the World Health Organisation prequalified Qdenga and recommended it's use in children aged 6–16 in areas with high intensity of dengue transmission. Travel medicine advisory groups have made dengue vaccine recommendations for their travellers that vary from country to country but that may mandate some evidence of a prior dengue infection [15] while others are more liberal.

With regard to the live, attenuated, single-dose, chikungunya vaccine, IXCHIQ, this vaccine received EMA approval in May 2024 for persons >12 years of age. Use of the vaccine was temporarily contra-indicated in persons over the age of 65 years by the European Medicine Agency, due to severe adverse events (SAE) observed notably in elderly people vaccinated in la Reunion. This temporary restriction was lifted in July 2025 but the live attenuated vaccine should be given only when there is a significant risk of chikungunya infection and after a careful consideration of the benefits and risks. This recommendation is for people of all ages [16]. As of August 22nd, 2025, the US FDA's Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research has suspended the license for IXCHIQ based on safety concerns related to chikungunya-like illness in vaccine recipients. The FDA has mandated certain label updates that will curtail the use of the vaccine [17].

Thus, the prevention of arboviral diseases with vaccination is possible but there are caveats and restrictions. Vector control and mosquito-bite prevention with repellents remain cornerstones in the curtailment of these invasive infections. Surveillance of travellers returning from outbreak areas constitutes key intelligence in strategy planning and in pre-empting possible local transmission.

Finally, in patients with febrile illness living in or returning from vacations in the South of France during the period, May to October, physicians should consider the possibility of dengue and chikungunya infections acquired in France.
