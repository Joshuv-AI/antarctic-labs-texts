Chemokine levels in the murine brain from early postnatal development to aging
Authors: Danillo Pereira Dantas, Raquel Maria Pereira Campos, Rômulo Gonçalves Agostinho Galvani, Adriana Bonomo, Pedro Moreno Pimentel-Coelho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cytokines and chemokines are key mediators of immune-brain communication and play important roles in central nervous system development, homeostasis, and aging. While individual cytokines and chemokines have been implicated in neurodevelopmental and age-related processes, a systematic characterization of their expression in the brain across the lifespan is lacking. Here, we performed a multiplex analysis of cytokine and chemokine levels in whole-brain homogenates from C57BL/6 mice spanning early postnatal life to advanced aging (postnatal day 3 to 24 months). Using a multiplex immunoassay, we quantified 21 chemokines and 9 cytokines and identified distinct age-associated expression patterns. Several chemokines exhibited elevated levels during early postnatal development, whereas others were selectively increased in the aged brain. Notably, a subset of chemokines displayed biphasic expression, with higher levels during both early life and aging. Cytokine analysis revealed increased levels of IL-1β and IL-2 during early postnatal development, while aging was associated with elevated TNF-α, IL-2, and IL-4. Together, these data provide a descriptive resource of cytokine and chemokine expression in the mouse brain across the lifespan, highlighting life stage-specific immune signatures that may differentially influence immune cell trafficking, neuron-glia interactions, and brain function during neural development and aging.

Introduction

Cytokines serve as a common language between the immune and nervous systems, with chemokines constituting a superfamily of small chemotactic cytokines recognized for their ability to control leukocyte migration. Beyond immune responses, cytokines and chemokines regulate several processes in the central nervous system (CNS). During neural development, they regulate processes such as neurogenesis, cellular migration and differentiation, synapse formation, and myelination (Goines and Ashwood, 2013; Trettel et al., 2020). In adulthood, they are necessary for brain homeostasis and cognitive function, whether produced locally or derived from sources such as the bloodstream and brain borders. For instance, IL-17 secreted from meningeal-resident γδ T cells is required for short-term spatial working memory (Ribeiro et al., 2019).

Their diverse functions require tight regulation of their levels, and dysregulation of cytokine and chemokine signaling has been implicated in a wide range of disorders throughout life. Understanding how their expression patterns change across the lifespan is therefore particularly important. In the human post-mortem brain, cytokine expression has been characterized during postnatal development of the prefrontal cortex (Sager et al., 2023). In mice, widely used to investigate neuroimmune mechanisms and neurological diseases, comprehensive analyses of cytokine and chemokine levels across multiple postnatal life stages remain limited. To address this gap, we screened chemokine and cytokine expression in the mouse brain from early postnatal development to aging. We hypothesized that distinct life stages would be associated with specific signatures reflecting developmental, homeostatic, and aging-related processes.

Methods

Animals

A total of 49 C57BL/6 mice of both sexes were used, with ages ranging from postnatal day 3 (P3, considering P0 as the day of birth) to 24 months. For each age group, 5 animals per sex were used, except for the P3 group, which included 4 females and 5 males. Each experimental unit (n) represents a single animal derived from an independent litter. Animals were housed in a temperature-controlled room on a 12:12 h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to water and chow. Pups were kept with their dams until P21 in standard laboratory cages. All procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Federal University of Rio de Janeiro (protocol number 43/20).

Multiplex immunoassay

Animals were deeply anesthetized by intraperitoneal ketamine/xylazine injection (100/15 mg/kg), followed by transcardial perfusion with ice-cold 0.9% saline. The left hemisphere, after removal of the brainstem and olfactory bulb (with the cerebellum preserved), was immediately frozen and stored at −80 °C. Samples were then rapidly thawed on ice and homogenized in buffer (150 mM NaCl, 25 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 1% Triton X-100) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Promega, Cat# G6521). Total protein concentration was determined using the Pierce™ BCA protein assay kit (Thermo-Fisher Scientific, Cat# 23225). The Bio-Plex Pro™ Mouse Chemokine Panel 31-Plex kit (Bio-Rad, Cat# 12009159) was used to quantify 9 cytokines and 22 chemokines following the manufacturer's instructions. Plates were read on a Luminex 200™ system (Millipore). All analyte levels were subsequently normalized to total protein concentration, and final concentrations are expressed as pg/mg of total protein. For each analyte, samples with concentrations below the assay limit of detection were excluded from the corresponding statistical analysis. In most samples, CCL4 levels were below the limit of detection, and this chemokine was excluded from further analysis.

Statistical analysis

Unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis was performed after log2 transformation of cytokine and chemokine concentration values. Clustering was conducted using the average-linkage method, with Pearson correlation distance applied to columns and Euclidean distance to rows. Principal coordinate analysis was performed using classical multidimensional scaling. Differences in global cytokine and chemokine profiles among age groups were assessed by PERMANOVA using a Pearson correlation-based distance matrix, with 9999 permutations. Homogeneity of multivariate dispersion among age groups was evaluated using betadisper followed by permutation testing. Pairwise PERMANOVA comparisons between age groups were performed, and p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Data manipulation, visualization, clustering, ordination, and multivariate statistical analyses were performed in R (4.6.0) and RStudio (2026.1.0.392, “Apple Blossom" release) using the ComplexHeatmap (2.28.0), circlize (0.4.18), RColorBrewer (1.1.3), ggplot2 (4.0.3), dplyr (1.2.1), tibble (3.3.1), and vegan (2.7.3) packages.

Individual analyte levels were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (9.0.0, GraphPad Software), with each age group compared with the adult group (3-5 months; 3m). Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and homogeneity of variances using the Brown-Forsythe test. The Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn's multiple comparisons test was used for nonparametric data, whereas one-way ANOVA with Dunnett's test or Welch's ANOVA with Dunnett's T3 test was used for parametric data, depending on variance homogeneity. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Brain chemokine and cytokine expression signatures distinguish different life stages

We quantified the levels of 21 chemokines and 9 cytokines in the mouse brain at five ages: early postnatal development (P3), weaning (P21), adulthood (3m), and aging (18 and 24 months; 18m and 24m). Hierarchical clustering analysis revealed clear age-dependent clustering of samples, despite an apparent overlap between the 18m and 24m groups (Fig. 1a). Notably, all groups remained statistically distinct, indicating that cytokine and chemokine expression profiles continue to change during advanced aging. However, the magnitude of the differences between age groups decreased with increasing age, suggesting a progressive convergence of inflammatory profiles across later life stages (Fig. 1b–c). Chemokines associated with early postnatal development

To identify age-dependent changes, we compared the levels of each analyte at different life stages relative to adulthood. Several chemokines were selectively elevated during early life (Fig. 2a). Four chemokines were significantly increased only at P3: CXCL11, CXCL12, CCL11, and CCL24. Among these chemokines, CXCL12 has well-established roles in neural development, including regulation of cortical interneuron migration in embryos and inhibitory circuit formation in neonatal mice (Wu et al., 2017). It also contributes to neuroplasticity by modulating dendritic spine dynamics and remodeling (Ho et al., 2025).

In contrast, whether CCL11, CCL17, CCL24, and CXCL11 are involved in early postnatal development remains unclear. In vitro studies have shown that CCL11 modulates the proliferation of murine embryonic and human fetal neural stem/progenitor cells (Krathwohl and Kaiser, 2004; Wang et al., 2017). Moreover, systemic or intrahippocampal injections of CCL11 inhibit hippocampal neurogenesis in adult mice (Villeda et al., 2011). These observations suggest that the elevated levels of CCL11 during early postnatal life may be linked to the regulation of neurogenesis.

Regarding CCL17, this chemokine is expressed by hippocampal neurons in adult mice and affects synaptic transmission. CCL17 knockout mice exhibit reduced numbers of hippocampal microglia, which display an altered morphology (Fülle et al., 2018). Therefore, the peak expression of CCL17 at P21 may be associated with microglial maturation and synaptic regulation.

Chemokines associated with aging

Five chemokines were selectively increased in the aged brain (18m, 24m, or both): CXCL16, CCL12, CCL22, and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors CCL1 and CCL5 (Fig. 2b). While CCL5 showed a gradual increase from P3 to 24m, CCL1 was selectively increased at 18m (Fig. 2b).

CXCL16 has been reported to be increased in the cerebrospinal fluid of cognitively impaired individuals, correlating with markers of neurodegeneration. CXCL16-CXCR6 signaling has been implicated in regulating T cell entry into the intrathecal space (Piehl et al., 2022). Additionally, CXCL16 levels are increased in the brains of patients and in microglia isolated from a mouse model of Alzheimer's disease. In this model, CXCR6 deficiency decreases the number of CD8+ T cells in the CNS, disrupting microglia-T cell interactions and exacerbating cognitive deficits (Su et al., 2023). Together, these findings raise the possibility that the age-associated increase in CXCL16 may represent a compensatory response to maintain beneficial microglia-lymphocyte interactions in the aging brain.

Chemokines with biphasic expression patterns across the lifespan

Besides the monophasic patterns described above, some chemokines exhibited a biphasic expression pattern across the lifespan. CCL3 and the SASP factor CCL2 were elevated both at early stages (P3 and P21) and during aging (18m and 24m) compared with adulthood. CXCL13 and the SASP factor CCL7 were increased at P3 and during aging (18m and 24m; Fig. 2c).

Consistent with our findings, CXCL13 is expressed in the choroid plexus and meninges of P1 mice. Intracerebroventricular administration of anti-CXCL13 antibodies in embryos reduced brain colonization by B cells, required for proper oligodendrogenesis (Tanabe and Yamashita, 2018). CXCL13 mRNA levels are also increased in the aged murine brain, where this chemokine has been implicated in neurogenesis (Fritze et al., 2020). Notably, increased CXCL13 signaling may be detrimental, as a single intracerebroventricular injection of CXCL13 induces cognitive deficits in adult mice (Shen et al., 2020).

CCL2, CCL5, and CCL7 were previously shown to be upregulated at the mRNA level in hippocampal microglia from aged mice. Systemic inhibition of CCR3, a receptor for CCL5 and CCL7, reduces T cell numbers in the brain and improves cognitive and motor functions (Rege et al., 2023). These findings suggest that age-associated changes in microglial states may contribute to the altered chemokine profile in the aging brain.

No significant changes were observed for the remaining chemokines (CX3CL1, CXCL1, CXCL10, CCL19, CCL20, and CCL27; Fig. 2e).

Cytokine expression across postnatal development and aging

Among 9 cytokines analyzed, IL-1β was increased solely at P3 (Fig. 2d), in accordance with its involvement in several neurodevelopmental events (Zengeler and Lukens, 2021). Mice lacking its receptor (IL-1R1) exhibit behavioral alterations and excessive glutamatergic synapses as early as P20 (Borreca et al., 2024). We did not observe changes in IL-1β levels in whole-brain homogenates from aged animals, whereas previous studies reported increases in the hippocampus but not in other brain areas (Katharesan et al., 2016; Porcher et al., 2021).

TNF-α was lower at P3 than in adulthood and progressively increased with age, peaking at 24m (Fig. 2d). While physiological TNF-α signaling contributes to synaptic homeostasis and plasticity, chronic elevations have been associated with synaptic dysfunction (Yang et al., 2025).

IL-4 levels tended to be slightly elevated at P3 (p = 0.0542) and were significantly increased at 24m, with no changes from P21 to adulthood (Fig. 2d). A recent study showed that IL-4 levels increase from P10 to adulthood in the cerebellum and that intraperitoneal IL-4 administration during this period alters neural circuitry and behavior (Guedes et al., 2023). As our analysis used whole-brain homogenates, these differences likely reflect region-specific IL-4 regulation during development. Regarding aging, while T cell-derived IL-4 has beneficial effects on cognition in adult mice (Derecki et al., 2010), increased IL-4 production in the choroid plexus during aging has been associated with adverse outcomes (Baruch et al., 2013).

IL-2 was elevated at both P3 and 18m (Fig. 2d), although overall levels were very low, consistent with previous reports (Yshii et al., 2022). IL-2 modulates neurotransmitter release and influences the survival and growth of neural cells, and has been implicated in hippocampal development (Petitto et al., 1999; Prinz et al., 2008). In adult mice, IL-2 is produced by midbrain neurons, and IL-2 overexpression in the brain drives the expansion of regulatory T cells (Yshii et al., 2022). Considering that IL-2 delivery to the brain counteracts some effects of aging on glial cells and cognition (Lemaitre et al., 2023), its slight elevation at 18m may reflect a compensatory attempt to protect the brain via regulatory T cells.

Finally, GM-CSF, IL-6, IL-10, IL-16, and IFN-γ did not show significant changes at any age (Fig. 2e).

Conclusions

This study provides a reference dataset of cytokine and chemokine levels in the mouse brain across key life stages.

We identified age-related changes in chemokines whose roles in brain development or aging remain poorly understood, including CCL11, CCL17, CCL24, and CXCL11 (early postnatal life), CCL1, CCL12, and CCL22 (aging), and CCL3 (both stages). Aging was also associated with higher expression of mediators linked to neuroinflammation and brain dysfunction, such as TNF-α, CXCL13, CCL5, and CCL7, but also with mediators with potential protective functions, such as IL-2 and CXCL16.

The interpretation of these findings should consider some limitations. First, whole-brain homogenates provide a global overview of cytokine and chemokine expression but cannot capture region-specific differences. Second, it was not possible to assess longitudinal changes within individuals. Finally, although both sexes were included in accordance with current recommendations, the study was not powered to detect sex-dependent differences.

In conclusion, each life stage is characterized by a distinct cytokine and chemokine expression signature, including mediators associated with early postnatal life, aging, or both. Understanding their physiological roles and alterations in pathological conditions may guide strategies to preserve brain health and advance our knowledge of neurodevelopment, aging, and neurological disorders.
